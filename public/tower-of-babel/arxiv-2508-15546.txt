Robust Self-Testing of Multiqudit Supersinglet Slater States via Constant Number of Binary Measurements
Arturo Konderak, Wojciech Bruzda, Remigiusz Augusiak

Abstract
Self-testing is a powerful device-independent technique that enables one to deduce the forms of both the quantum state and the measurements involved in a physical experiment based solely on observed correlations. Although numerous schemes for self-testing multipartite entangled states have been proposed, they are typically difficult to implement experimentally, as their complexity increases significantly with the number of subsystems or the local dimension. In this work, we introduce the first self-testing scheme of a relevant class of multiqudit genuinely entangled states that exploits only a constant number of binary measurements per observer, which significantly reduces the experimental effort to implement the scheme. Specifically, it enables the self-testing of multipartite Slater (or supersinglet) states composed of d d qu d its with odd d d using only four two-outcome measurements per observer. Moreover, we prove that our scheme is robust to noise and experimental imperfections. For systems of even local dimension d d , we also provide an adapted version of the scheme that requires only d d binary measurements per observer.

Introduction

Bell nonlocality—the existence of correlations that cannot be reproduced by means of local hidden variable models
[
1
]
—is one of the most distinctive features of quantum theory
[
2
]
. Indeed, the pioneering experiments
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
to verify the existence of this phenomenon were awarded a Nobel Prize in Physics in 2022. In addition, beyond its fundamental interest, Bell non-locality has been recognized as a valuable resource for various applications within device-independent quantum information processing
[
6
]
, where observers process information without placing trust in the devices themselves. One of such applications that has recently gained substantial interest, driven by the recent rapid development of new quantum technologies such as quantum cryptography systems or quantum computing, is the device-independent certification of entangled quantum states and the measurements performed on them, commonly referred to as self-testing
[
7
]
(see also Ref.
[
8
]
for a review). Self-testing allows one to almost completely characterize (up to certain well-known equivalences) the distributed quantum state and the measurements performed on its local subsystems solely from the observed non-local correlations. It thus requires making only minimal assumptions about the internal working of the devices under study, which makes DI certification schemes immune to hacking exploiting particular specifics of the devices.

Notably, apart from its role in certifying entangled states, self-testing has also found other interesting applications. For instance, self-testing results are often used in device-independent (DI) certification of randomness generated from quantum states
[
9
,
10
]
, and also to prove separation between various sets of quantum correlations in certain Bell scenarios (see, e.g., Ref.
[
11
]
and references therein).

For these reasons self-testing has attracted a lot of attention in recent years and a plethora of DI certification schemes for pure quantum states (both bipartite and multipartite) and quantum measurements have been designed. In the bipartite case a general scheme allowing to self-test an arbitrary pure entangled two-qudit state was introduced in Ref.
[
12
]
. However, this scheme is not universally optimal in terms of the number of measurements that the parties must perform or the number of outcomes, and more optimal schemes for the maximally entangled states have been proposed (see, e.g., Refs.
[
13
,
14
]
). At the same time, in the multipartite case very general methods to certify any arbitrary multiqubit states
have been proposed first in Ref.
[
15
]
, which is based on quantum networks, and then in Ref.
[
16
]
in the standard Bell scenario.
Then, several other schemes have been proposed for various specific classes of multipartite states such as multiqubit graph states
[
17
]
, the
W

[
18
]
, or the Schmidt states of arbitrary local dimension
[
19
]
.

However, the common problem permeating most of the existing schemes is that their complexity in terms of the number of measurements or outcomes grows with the system size (either the local dimension or the number of subsystems or both). On one hand,
the schemes designed to certify states of arbitrary local dimension such as
[
12
,
19
,
13

require performing measurements whose number of outcomes grows system’s local dimension and, on the other hand, as far as multiqubit states are concerned, the method of Ref.

requires

the scheme of Ref.
[

requires doubling the number of parties in order to certify a state. At the same time, for practical applications of self-testing, the experimental effort required for their implementation needs to be reduced as much as possible. Only recently, a novel approach to DI certification was proposed in Ref.

, exploiting certain algebraic methods, which enables designing a robust self-testing scheme for maximally entangled states of arbitrary local dimension based on constant-sized statistics. In this scheme, both parties need to perform only four two-outcome measurements to certify the state, regardless of its local dimension. However, no such scheme exists in the multipartite scenario.

In this paper we address the above problem and prove that it is possible to design a scheme allowing to self-test multipartite states of arbitrary local dimension and number of subsystems just from a constant number of binary measurements performed by each observer. More precisely, we show how to self-test the so-called Slater (determinant) states

of odd local dimension, by locally measuring only four two-outcome observables. These states are defined in the antisymmetric tensor product of

In order to derive our results, we generalize the techniques of Ref.

2
Preliminaries

In this Section, we provide a comprehensive overview of the key definitions and properties underlying Bell non-locality and self-testing. We then introduce the mathematical definition of Slater states and discuss their symmetry properties.

2.1
Bell scenario

Bell non-locality provides a certification technique to ensure that two or more spatially separated parties share a quantum resource
[
34
]
. Let
n
parties share a quantum state
\ket{\Psi}
, belonging to the composite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{H}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{n-1}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space associated to the
k
th party. Each party
k=0,\dots,n-1
has access to a measurement device performing one of
N
possible measurements, each described by a two-element POVM:
\{E_{\mu,0}^{(k)},E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}\}
, where
\mu=0,\dots,N-1
. The measurement elements
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}
and
E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}
are positive operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
that sum up to the identity
\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. For simplicity, we consider measurements with only two outcomes (corresponding to indices
0
and
1
in the measurement operators), although the formalism extends naturally to more outcomes.

Based on a family of inputs
(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n-1})\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{n}
, each party
k
performs the measurement
\{E_{\mu_{k},0}^{(k)},E_{\mu_{k},1}^{(k)}\}
, thus obtaining a total probability distribution

{p}(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}|\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n-1})=\matrixelement{\Psi}{E_{\mu_0,a_0}^{(0)}\otimes\dots\otimes E_{\mu_{d-1},a_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}}{\Psi}

(1)

with
(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. In order to ease the notation, we write
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{n-1})
and
E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}={E_{\mu_{0},a_{0}}^{(0)}\otimes\dots\otimes E_{\mu_{d-1},a_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}}
. We also denote
[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\dots,b-1\}
for any pair
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a<b
; in the particular case of
a=0
it reduces to
[b]=[0,b]
. The collection of probability distributions
\{p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})\}
is called a quantum correlation generated by the state
\ket{\Psi}
and the measurements
\{E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\}
.

The correlation
p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
is said to be Bell non-local if it cannot be reproduced using only local hidden variable models (LHV). Explicitly, it cannot be written in the form

p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})=\int_{\lambda\in\Omega}d\lambda\ \omega(\lambda)\ p_{0}(a_{0}|\mu_{0},\lambda)\cdots p_{N-1}(a_{N-1}|\mu_{N-1},\lambda),

(2)

with
p_{k}(a_{k}|\mu_{k},\lambda)
representing a local strategy, and
\lambda
a shared randomness distributed according to a probability density
\omega(\lambda)
. Correlations admitting LHV models as in (
2
) are also called local or classical. Thus, the existence of non-local correlations between spatially separated parties serves as proof of the quantum nature of a system.

2.2
Self-testing

There are scenarios in which Bell non-locality not only certifies the quantum nature of correlations but also fully (up to certain equivalences) characterizes the underlying entangled quantum state and measurements. This phenomenon is known as self-testing
[
7
]
(see also Ref.
[
8
]
for a review). To formalize this concept, suppose a certain reference state
\ket*{\tilde\Psi}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{0}\otimes\ldots\otimes\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{n-1}
and a reference family of measurements
\{\tilde{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\}
generate the correlations
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
as in Equation (
1
). Suppose these are the same correlations generated by a Bell experiment involving an unknown quantum state
\ket{\Psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{n-1}

and unknown quantum measurements
\{E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\}
. Then, the definition of self-testing reads as follows.

Definition 2.1
(Self-testing)

We say that the observed correlations
p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
obtained from a quantum state
\ket{\Psi}
and measurements
\{E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\}
self-test the reference quantum realization
\ket*{\tilde\Psi}
and
\{\tilde{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\}
if for all
k\in[n]
there exists some auxiliary Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{I}_{k}
and isometries
V_{k}:\mathcal{H}_{k}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\bigotimes_{k=0}^{n-1}V_{k}\right)\ket{\Psi}=\ket*{\tilde\Psi}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}},

(3)

and for all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in[N]^{n}

\left(\bigotimes_{k=0}^{n-1}V_{k}\right)E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\ket{\Psi}=\tilde{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}\ket*{\tilde\Psi}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}},

(4)

where
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}}
is some auxiliary state from
\mathcal{I}_{0}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathcal{I}_{n-1}
.

We focus exclusively on cases where all states are pure, since—thanks to dilation—this assumption can always be made without any loss of generality
[
35
]
.

We further say that the self-testing is
robust
if the relations (
3
) and (
4
) hold approximately for correlations close to
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
. Specifically, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta
such that for all quantum states
\ket{\Psi}
and measurements
\{E_{\boldsymbol{\mu}|\boldsymbol{i}}\}
producing correlations
p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
such that
\norm{p-\tilde p}_{1}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{\mu}}|p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})-\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})|<\delta
, conditions (
3
) and (
4
) hold up to an error
\varepsilon
in the one-norm. Robust self-testing is essential because it ensures that theoretical certifications of quantum systems remain valid under realistic, noisy experimental conditions.

Assuming, without loss of generality, that
\ket{\Psi}
,
\ket*{\tilde\Psi}
and
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{junk}}}
have maximal support over the reduced Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{k}
and
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, Equations (
3
) and (
4
) imply that
[
35
]
:

V_{k}E_{\mu,a}^{(k)}V_{k}^{*}=\tilde{E}_{\mu,a}^{(k)}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{I}_{k}}\qquad\text{for}\qquad k\in[n],\quad\mu\in[N],\quad a\in\{0,1\}.

(5)

In particular, if only Equation (
5
) holds, we say that the quantum correlation
p(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
self-tests the measurement.

Remark 2.2

It is worth noting that there is one more degree of freedom/equivalence that should be considered in the definition of self-testing. Namely, the state and measurements can only be self-tested up to complex conjugation
[
8
]
. However, in our case, both the state and measurements are real, and this equivalence need not be accounted for.

In the bipartite case (
n=2
), the maximally entangled two-qubit states can be self-tested via maximal CHSH inequality violation
[
36
]
, while all pure two-qubit entangled states are self-testable using tilted CHSH inequalities
[
37
,
38
]
. More generally, any pure bipartite state can be self-tested using three or four measurements per party, each with
d
outcomes (where
d
is the state’s Schmidt number), scaling linearly with Hilbert space dimension
[
12
]
.

The general self-testing statement in
[
12
]
describes a procedure of self-testing any pure entangled state shared by two parties. Nevertheless, for certain states, more effective strategies can be developed. The SATWAP inequality
[
39
]
allows for self-testing by maximal violation the maximally entangled state
[
13
]
between two parties in any dimensions with only two measurements of
d
outcomes. More recently, it has been found in
[
14
]
that the maximally entangled state can be self-tested with a bounded and independent of the local dimension of the system number of four measurements and two outcomes per observer. To achieve this, techniques developed in
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
were considered, specifically the characterization of projections based on their sum.

Some progress has been made in developing self-testing statements for the multipartite quantum systems. The current, more general self-testing statement pertains to qubit states
[
16
]
, and it demonstrates that, up to a phase conjugation, all pure qubit states can be self-tested. Other specific self-testing statements are given here for qubits. In
[
18
]
, a self-testing statement is presented for the tripartite
W
state, which is performed using two measurements and two outcomes per party. In
[
19
]
, the GHZ state is self-tested with a total of two measurements and two outcomes in any number of parties. In
[
43
]
, self-testing of all
N
parties Dicke states has been presented for many copies, and self-testing is achieved with
2
local measurements. In
[
44
]
, three local measurements with two outcomes are used to self-test the three-qubit
W
state and the three and four-qubit GHZ states. In
[
45
]
, a self-testing for graph states is presented, consisting of 2 local measurements with two outcomes. Finally, Ref.
[
19
]
presents a self-testing statement for all Schmidt qudit states, with
d
measurements per party. Recently, a procedure for self-testing the Slater state was introduced
[
31
]
, where the number of measurements is related to the number of rigid Kochen-Specker sets. In particular, the number of measurements can be found to grow exponentially with the number of parties. On the contrary, the procedure presented in this work offers a significant improvement by allowing self-testing with only
4
dichotomic measurements, regardless of the number of parties.

2.3
Slater states

Assume that each local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is
d
-dimensional, that is,
\mathcal{H}_{k}\equiv\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The set of bounded operators over
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and it can be identified with
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, set of
d\times d
complex matrices.
Consider
n=d
copies of the local
d
-dimensional system, meaning that the total Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
. Denoting by
\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0,\dots,d-1}
the standard (computational) basis in
\mathcal{H}
, the Slater state is defined as

\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{P}_{d}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\ket{\sigma(0)\otimes\sigma(1)\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma(d-1)}\in\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d},

(6)

where
\mathbb{P}_{n}
is the set of permutations of order
n
, and
\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)
is the sign of the permutation. The Slater state satisfies the following singlet property

U^{\otimes n}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}=\xi\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}},\quad\forall\ U\in\mathcal{U},

(7)

with
\xi
being a phase, and
\mathcal{U}(d)
the set of unitaries over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The Slater state is the only vector in
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
satisfying this property
[
27
]
, as we discuss in Sec.
5.1
.

The Slater states are the least entangled fermionic states when considering a partition with only one party
[
46
]
, while this is not generally true for the reduced density matrix over two parties
[
47
,
20
]
.

Due to (
7
), a change of basis results in the Slater state acquiring an overall phase factor. Indeed, this turns out to be the only state in the antisymmetric subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
.

3
Mathematical background

In this Section, we bring together several definitions and properties which are fundamental for the self-testing statement. First, we present a couple of properties regarding finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra, and discuss the notion of projections adding up to the identity times a constant.

3.1
C^{*}
-algebras

The complete abstract definition of
C^{*}
-algebra is rather intricate
[
48
,
49
]
, hence we shall reduce the considerations to the case of
C^{*}
-algebras of operators over a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a set of operators over a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, namely,
\mathfrak{A}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Then,
\mathfrak{A}
is called a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, if it is a complex vector space, closed under composition of Hermitian adjoint of an element. Furthermore, it must be closed in the uniform norm
\norm{\cdot}_{\infty}
, defined as

\norm{A}_{\infty}=\sup\{\norm{A\ket{\psi}}:\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H},\norm{\ket{\psi}}=1\}.

(8)

In particular, a structure theorem holds for
C^{*}
-algebra
[
50
, p. 75]
.

Theorem 3.1
(Structure Theorem)

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a
C^{*}
-algebra over a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then, it is possible to decompose
\mathcal{H}
as

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}\mathcal{H}_{k,1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k,2},

(9)

with
\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
\mathcal{H}_{k,1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k,2}
finite dimensional Hilbert spaces, and
k=1,2,\dots,M
. With respect to decomposition (
9
), the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
can be expressed in the form

\mathfrak{A}=0\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k,1})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{k,2},

(10)

where, for each
k=1,\dots,M
,
\mathbb{I}_{k,2}
represents the identity over the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k,2}
, while
0
is the null operator over
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

If
\mathfrak{A}
represents the set of observables of a theory, the direct sum implies the existence of superselection rules, the sets of operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k,1})
contains the coherent superposition, and finally the identities
\mathbb{I}_{k,2}
involves gauge degrees of freedom
[
51
]
.
Given a set of operators
S\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we can define its commutant
S^{\prime}

S^{\prime}=\{X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}):XY=YX,\ \forall Y\in S\}.

(11)

The bicommutant
S^{\prime\prime}
of
S
is defined as the commutant of
S^{\prime}
. For a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
in the form of (
10
), the commutant and bicommutant are given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{0})\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}_{k,1}\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k,2}),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{k=1}^{M}\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k,1})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{k,2},

(13)

with
\mathbb{I}_{0}
and
\mathbb{I}_{k,1}
the identities over
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{k,1}
respectively, for
k=1,\dots,M
.

In particular, for
\mathcal{H}_{0}=0
, we have that
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime\prime}=\mathfrak{A}
, which is the finite-dimensional version of the von Neumann bicommutant theorem
[
48
,
52
]
.

3.2
Projections summing to the identity

In this Section, we summarize a specific class of projective operators which are particularly relevant for several self-testing problems
[
53
,
14
,
54
]
. This class of was studied in
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, and here we recall the construction and the most important properties.

Following
[
41
]
, the main motivation is to find classes of orthogonal projections summing up to the identity. Specifically, let
N
be an integer, and the problem boils down to identify all scalars
x\in\mathbb{R}
for which there are
N
orthogonal projections
P_{0}
,
P_{1},\dots,P_{N-1}
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
summing up to
x\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Theorem 3.2
(Theorem 5.1 and Lemma 5.3 from
[
14
]
)

Let
\Lambda_{3}=\{3/2\}
and, define, for
N\geqslant 4
, the set
\Lambda_{N}=\{x_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
recursively as

x_{0}=0,\quad x_{k}=1+\frac{1}{N-1-x_{k-1}},\quad k\geqslant 1.

(14)

Given
x\in\Lambda_{N}
, write it as
x=b/d
, with
b
and
d
natural numbers in the lowest form. Then, we can find
N
orthogonal projections
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{N-1}\}
in
\mathcal{M}_{d}
such that

\sum_{\mu=0}^{N-1}P_{\mu}=x\mathbb{I}_{d},

(15)

with
\mathbb{I}_{d}
being the identity in
\mathcal{M}_{d}
. Furthermore, these projections are uniquely defined, up to an isometry. Specifically, suppose that there are
N
orthogonal projections
\{{E}_{1},\dots,E_{N}\}
over an Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying (
15
), namely

\sum_{\mu=0}^{N-1}E_{\mu}=x\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}},

(16)

with
\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
being the identity over
\mathcal{H}
. Then, up to a unitary transformation,
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, and for all
\mu=0,\dots,N-1
we have
P_{\mu}=P^{\prime}_{\mu}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}
.

Roughly speaking, the idea behind the self-testing statements is based on the fact that we can infer, from the quantum correlations, both that the operators
P_{j}
are orthogonal projections, and that they sum to a scalar multiple of the identity. In this sense, employing synchronous measurement is essential. The explicit form of the projections
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{N-1}\}
can be obtained by induction in the general case, see
[
14
, Appendix B]
, and it is based on the idea of constructing functors between different representations of a specific
C^{*}
-algebras
[
41
]
. It is possible to prove that the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{N-1}\}
is the whole algebra of square matrices
\mathcal{M}_{d}
.
In the following Section, we present two explicit constructions for the projections summing up to the identity, which enable explicit self-testing of the Slater state in specific scenarios.

4
Explicit construction of projections summing up to the identity

In this Section, we present the construction of projections that sum up to the identity as in Theorem
3.2
, in two situations. In the first case, we consider the construction corresponding the projections
\{P_{0},\dots,P_{N-1}\}
having rank

4.1
Case
N=d+1

The first relevant example corresponds to the case of the projections
\{P_{\mu}\}_{\mu=0}^{N-1}
having rank
1
. They exist in all dimensions
d
by choosing
N=d+1
, and
x=N/(N-1)
in Equation (
14
), with
N\geqslant 4
. Interestingly, this class of projections is employed to construct all the other representations using the functor defined in
[
41
]
.

Proposition 4.1

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and let
\{\ket{\mu}\}_{\mu=0,\dots,d-1}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Define the states

\ket{\psi_\mu}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\mu-1}\alpha_{\nu}\ket{\nu}+\beta_{\mu}\ket{\mu},\quad\mu=0,\dots,d-1,

(17)

and

\ket{\psi_d}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{\nu}\ket{\nu},

(18)

with

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mu}=-\sqrt{\frac{d+1}{d(d-\mu)(d-\mu+1)}},\qquad\beta_{\mu}=+\sqrt{\frac{(d+1)(d-\mu)}{d(d-\mu+1)}}.

(19)

Then, the rank
1
projections
P_{\mu}=\outerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_\mu}
, with
\mu=0,\dots,d
, satisfy

\sum_{\mu=0}^{d}P_{\mu}=\frac{d+1}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d},

(20)

and therefore correspond to the
d+1
orthogonal projections summing up to
(d+1)/d
.

Remark 4.2

The form of the vectors (
17
) can be inferred by observing that, from Equation (
15
) and the uniqueness condition, for
i\neq j
we have that
\tr(P_\mu P_\nu)=1/d^{2}
, which implies that
|\innerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_\nu}|=1/d
. By using the Gram-Schmidt procedure on
\{\ket{\psi_\mu}\}_{\mu=0,\dots,d-1}
, and requiring the scalar product to be
-1/d
, we obtain Equations (
17
), (
18
) and (
19
).

Proof:

First, observe that the states
\ket{\psi_\mu}
are correctly normalized as

\sum_{\nu=0}^{\mu-1}\alpha_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\mu}{d(d-\mu+1)}=1-\beta_{\mu}^{2},\quad\mu=1,2,\dots,d.

(21)

The latter can be proved by induction. For
\mu=1
it is trivial. Suppose it holds for
\mu-1
; we then have

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{\mu}\alpha_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\mu}{d(d-\mu+1)}+\frac{d+1}{d(d-\mu)(d-\mu+1)}=\frac{\mu+1}{d(d-\mu)}.

(22)

Choose
\mu<\mu^{\prime}
, and we can easily show that:

\displaystyle\innerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_{\mu'}}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\mu-1}\alpha_{\nu}^{2}+\alpha_{\mu}\beta_{\mu}=\frac{\mu}{d(d-\mu+1)}-\frac{d+1}{d(d-\mu+1)}=-\frac{1}{d}.

(23)

Finally, for all
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
, we have

\matrixelement{\psi_\mu}{\left(\sum_{\nu=0}^{d}\ketbra{\psi_\nu}\right)}{\psi_{\mu'}}=\begin{cases}-\frac{d+1}{d^{2}},\quad&\mu\neq\mu^{\prime}\\
\frac{d+1}{d},\quad&\mu=\mu^{\prime}\end{cases}=\frac{d+1}{d}\innerproduct{\psi_\mu}{\psi_{\mu'}}.

(24)

From Equation (
17
) we know that
\{\ket{\psi_\mu}\}_{\mu=0,\dots,d-1}
span all
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and consequently, Equation (
24
) implies (
20
).

\square

4.2
Case
N=4

We now present the construction of the four projections summing up to the identity. Take
N=4
in Equation (
14
), and observe that the elements in
\Lambda_{4}
can be expressed as

x_{k}=\frac{4k}{2k+1},\qquad\forall k\geqslant 0.

(25)

From Theorem
3.2
, for all
k
it is possible to find
4
projections in dimension

satisfying (
15
), with

.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a Hilbert space of dimension
d=2k+1
. We look for four orthogonal projections
\{P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\}
satisfying (
15
). It is convenient to consider the orthogonal complements
A,B,C,D
of
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
respectively, that is

\displaystyle A=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{0},\quad B=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{1},\quad C=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{2},\quad D=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{3}.

(26)

These are orthogonal projections satisfying

A+B+C+D=\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}\mathbb{I}_{2k+1}.

(27)

One can readily infer the structure of all
P_{\mu}
from
A,B,C,D
. In particular
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
can be expressed in a block-diagonal form, as a direct sum of
2\times 2
projections, and they can be arranged in such a way that
A
,
B
and
C
,
D
share the same block structure. We precede the construction by presenting the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3

Let
X
be a
2\times 2
diagonal matrix of the form

X=\begin{pmatrix}1+x&0\\
0&1-x\end{pmatrix}

(28)

with
-1<x<1
. Then,
X
can be written as the sum of two orthogonal projections
X=A+B
,
A=A^{2}=A^{*}
and
B=B^{2}=B^{*}
, as

A=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}{x_{1}}&{\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}}}\\
{\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}}}&{x_{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}{x_{1}}&-{\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}}}\\
-{\sqrt{x_{1}x_{2}}}&{x_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(29)

and
x_{1}=1+x
,
x_{2}=1-x
.

Proof:

Straightforward.

\square

To proceed with the construction of
A,B,C
and
D
, let
\{\ket{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{2k}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2k+1}
. Furthermore, define
E_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\outerproduct{\ell}{\ell'}
, with
\ell,\ell^{\prime}=0,\dots,2k
, and
E_{\ell}=E_{\ell,\ell}
. The following decomposition holds:

\displaystyle\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}\mathbb{I}_{2k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2k}\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}E_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\left(\frac{4\ell+2}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}+\frac{4k+2-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}\right)+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{4k-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}+\frac{4\ell+4}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{4\ell+2}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{4k-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}\right)+\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{4k+2-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{4\ell+4}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{4\ell+2}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}+\frac{4k-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell+1}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left(\frac{4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell-1}+\frac{4k+2-4\ell}{2k+1}E_{2\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2E_{0}+2E_{2\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}X_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}Y_{\ell}+2E_{0}+2E_{2k}.

(30)

Now, one observes that in each subspace
\ket{2\ell}\oplus\ket{2\ell+1}
, the operator
X_{\ell}
has the form described in Lemma
4.3
, while in the subspace
\ket{2\ell-1}\oplus\ket{2\ell}
, the operator
Y_{\ell}
does.

Define

\displaystyle x^{(1)}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\ell+2}{2k+1},\quad x^{(2)}_{\ell}=\frac{4k-4\ell}{2k+1},

(31)

\displaystyle y^{(1)}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\ell}{2k+1},\quad y^{(2)}_{\ell}=\frac{4k+2-4\ell}{2k+1},

(32)

so that we can write

X_{\ell}=A_{\ell}+B_{\ell}

(33)

with

A_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}x_{\ell}^{(1)}&\sqrt{x^{(1)}_{\ell}x_{\ell}^{(2)}}\\
\sqrt{x_{\ell}^{(1)}x_{\ell}^{(2)}}&x_{\ell}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad B_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}x_{\ell}^{(1)}&-\sqrt{x_{\ell}^{(1)}x_{\ell}^{(2)}}\\
-\sqrt{x_{\ell}^{(1)}x_{\ell}^{(2)}}&x_{\ell}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}.

(34)

Similarly, we can write

Y_{\ell}=C_{\ell}+D_{\ell}

(35)

with

C_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}y_{\ell}^{(1)}&\sqrt{y^{(1)}_{\ell}y_{\ell}^{(2)}}\\
\sqrt{y_{\ell}^{(1)}y_{\ell}^{(2)}}&y^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad D_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}y_{\ell}^{(1)}&-\sqrt{y_{\ell}^{(1)}y_{\ell}^{(2)}}\\
-\sqrt{y_{\ell}^{(1)}y_{\ell}^{(2)}}&y_{\ell}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}.

(36)

The previous computation shows that
A
and
B
can be written as a direct sum of matrices with support over
\ket{2\ell}\oplus\ket{2\ell+1}
, while
C
and
D
as the direct sum in the subspaces
\ket{2\ell-1}\oplus\ket{2\ell}
. Explicitly

A=\begin{pmatrix}{\framebox{$A_{0}$}}&&&&\\
&{\framebox{$A_{1}$}}&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&\framebox{$A_{k-1}$}&\\
&&&&\framebox{$1$}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}{\framebox{$B_{0}$}}&&&&\\
&{\framebox{$B_{1}$}}&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&\framebox{$B_{k-1}$}&\\
&&&&\framebox{$1$}\end{pmatrix},

(37)

and similarly

C=\begin{pmatrix}\framebox{$1$}&&&&\\
&{\framebox{$C_{1}$}}&&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\framebox{$C_{k-1}$}&\\
&&&&{\framebox{$C_{k}$}}\end{pmatrix},\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}\framebox{$1$}&&&&\\
&{\framebox{$D_{1}$}}&&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\framebox{$D_{k-1}$}&\\
&&&&{\framebox{$D_{k}$}}\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

We therefore have the following structure.

Proposition 4.4

Let
A,B,C,D
be orthogonal projections over
\mathbb{C}^{2k+1}
, satisfying

A+B+C+D=\frac{4k+4}{2k+1}\mathbb{I}_{2k+1}.

(39)

Then, it is it possible to express them in a proper orthonormal basis as

A=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{k-1}A_{\ell}\oplus E_{2k},\quad B=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{k-1}B_{\ell}\oplus E_{2k},

(40)

and

C=E_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{k}C_{\ell},\quad D=E_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{k}D_{\ell}.

(41)

Specifically, for
\ell=0,1,\dots,k-1
,
A_{\ell}
and
B_{\ell}
are given by Equation (
34
), and have support over
\ket{2\ell}\oplus\ket{2\ell+1}
, while for
\ell=0,1,\dots,k-1
,
C_{\ell}
and
D_{\ell}
are given by (
36
) and have support over
\ket{2\ell-1}\oplus\ket{2\ell}
. Observe also that the entries of
A
and
B
corresponding to
\ket{2k+1}
, and those of
C
and
D
corresponding to
\ket{0}
, are equal to one.

5
Self-testing of Slater state

We now introduce our strategy to self-test the measurements and, under certain conditions, the Slater state. Consider
n=d
parties, such that each party has access to a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
d
. Suppose that the parties share a Slater state, which can be expressed in the standard basis
\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0,\dots,d-1}
as in Equation (
6
).

On each Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a family of projections
\{P_{\mu}\}_{\mu=0,1,\dots,N-1}
summing to
x
times the identity as in Equation (
15
), with
x=b/d
an element in
\Lambda_{N}
. Correspondingly, each party has
N
quantum measurements given by:

\{P_{\mu},\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{\mu}\},\quad\mu=0,1,\dots,N-1.

(42)

Define
\tilde{E}_{\mu,0}^{(k)}=P_{\mu}
and
\tilde{E}_{\mu,1}^{(k)}=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{\mu}
, with
k\in[d]
and
\mu\in[N]
. The corresponding quantum correlation is

\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})=\matrixelement{\Psi_\mathrm{S}}{\tilde E_{\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol a}}{\Psi_\mathrm{S}},

(43)

with
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in[N]^{d}
, and
\tilde{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}=\tilde{E}_{\mu_{0},a_{0}}^{(0)}\otimes\tilde{E}_{\mu_{1},a_{1}}^{(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes\tilde{E}_{\mu_{d-1},a_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}
.

Definition 5.1

We refer to the canonical strategy for generating the correlation
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
in Equation (
43
) as the one that utilizes the Slater state
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}
along with the local measurements
\tilde{E}_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}
defined in (
42
).

In the following Subsection, we prove that the canonical quantum strategy
5.1
enables self-testing of the local measurements (
42
) for all families of
N
projections summing up to the identity. In Subsection
5.2
, we provide sufficient conditions that also ensure self-testing of the states. We show that these conditions hold for certain classes of projections whose sum is proportional to the identity, particularly in the cases
N=d+1
in Section
4.1
and
N=4
in Section
4.2
.

5.1
Self-testing of measurements

In this Section, we prove that the strategy presented in Equation (
5.1
) self-tests the measurements in Equation (
42
). We begin by introducing a special projection on the tensor product Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
, which plays a key role in self-testing, along with some properties of the Slater state. First, given an operator of the form
A=A_{0}\otimes A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d-1}
, where each
A_{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we define the action of the symmetrizer
\mathcal{S}
on
A
as
[
56
]

\mathcal{S}(A)=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{P}_{d}}A_{\sigma(0)}\otimes A_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{\sigma(d-1)}.

(44)

The action of the symmetrizer can be extended to all elements of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d})
by linearity.
Observe that
\mathcal{S}
is a projection (idempotent) on the set of operators, and its image is the symmetric algebra
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
, as we formally prove in Appendix
A
. The Slater state is the only state in
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes d}
that satisfies the condition

\mathcal{S}(A)\ket{\Psi_\mathrm{S}}=\alpha\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}\quad:\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{C},

(45)

for all
A
of the form
A=A_{0}\otimes A_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d-1}
. This is because
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}
is the only pure state in the permutation algebra
\mathfrak{B}
, which is the commutant of the symmetric algebra
\mathfrak{A}_{f}
, the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by all
\mathcal{S}(A)
, see Appendix
A
for the details.

Given
N
projections
\{P_{\mu}\}_{\mu=0}^{N-1}
in (
15
), suppose that
\rank(P_\mu)=r=b/N<d
, and consider the other projections

R_{\mu}=\underbrace{P_{\mu}\otimes P_{\mu}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{\mu}}_{r}\otimes\underbrace{(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})\otimes\dots\otimes(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})}_{d-r},\quad\mu=0,\dots,N-1.

(46)

Define the operators:

S_{\mu}=\frac{d!}{r!(d-r)!}\mathcal{S}(R_{\mu}),\quad\mu=0,\dots,N-1.

(47)

The normalization factor guarantees that permutations leading to the same configuration in (
44
) are counted only once. It is straightforward to verify that
S_{\mu}
acts as a projection, since the projections in the sum (
44
) are mutually orthogonal.

Proposition 5.2

The Slater state
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}
is in the range of all the projections
S_{\mu}
, i.e.,

S_{\mu}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}=\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}.

(48)

Remark 5.3

The property (
48
) implies that, if we know that the first
r
components of
\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}
are in the subspace
P_{\mu}\mathcal{H}
, then all the residual
d-r
components are in the orthogonal subspace
P_{\mu}\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
. This property is used in
[
31
]
in order to obtain a perfect strategy.

Proof:

From Equation (
45
), we infer that
S_{\mu}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}=\alpha\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}
, with
\alpha
either
0
or
1
. It remains to show that
\alpha\neq 1
. The operator
P_{\mu}
can be written in terms of a complete relation:

P_{\mu}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\outerproduct{j}{j},

(49)

where
\{\ket{j}\}_{j=0,\dots,d-1}
represents the standard basis, while the Slater state can be expressed as (
6
), up to a phase (
7
).

Observe that
S_{\mu}
is the sum of positive operators. In order to check that
\alpha\neq 1
, it is sufficient to check that there is one element in the sum for which the expectation value is different from zero. For example

\displaystyle R_{\mu}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=P_{\mu}\otimes P_{\mu}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{\mu}\otimes(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})\otimes\dots\otimes(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{0}=0}^{r-1}\dots\sum_{i_{r-1}=0}^{r-1}\sum_{i_{r}=r}^{d-1}\dots\sum_{i_{d-1}=r}^{d-1}\outerproduct{i_0}{i_0}\otimes\dots\otimes\outerproduct{i_{r-1}}{i_{r-1}}\otimes\outerproduct{i_{r}}{i_{r}}\otimes\dots\otimes\outerproduct{i_{d-1}}{i_{d-1}}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{r-1}\dots\sum_{i_{r-1}=0}^{r-1}\sum_{i_{r}=r}^{d-1}\dots\sum_{i_{d-1}=r}^{d-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{P}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d!}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\ket{\sigma(0)\dots\sigma(d-1)}\left(\delta_{i_{0},\sigma(0)}\dots\delta_{i_{d-1},\sigma(d-1)}\right).

(50)

A straightforward computation yields:

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}{R_\mu}{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}P_{\mu}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{\mu}\otimes(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})\otimes\dots\otimes(\mathbb{I}-P_{\mu})\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{0}=0}^{r-1}\dots\sum_{i_{r-1}=0}^{r-1}\sum_{i_{r}=r}^{d-1}\dots\sum_{i_{d-1}=r-1}^{d-1}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}_{d}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{{d!}}\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\mathrm{sign}(\sigma^{\prime})\left(\delta_{i_{0},\sigma(0)}\dots\delta_{i_{d-1},\sigma(d-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 180.0pt\innerproduct{\sigma'(0)\dots\sigma'(d-1)}{\sigma(0)\dots\sigma(d-1)}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{r-1}\dots\sum_{i_{r-1}=0}^{r-1}\sum_{i_{r}=r}^{d-1}\dots\sum_{i_{d-1}=r}^{d-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathbb{P}_{d}}\frac{1}{{d!}}\left(\delta_{i_{0},\sigma(0)}\dots\delta_{i_{d-1},\sigma(d-1)}\right)=\frac{r!(d-r)!}{{d!}}

(51)

Hence,
\alpha\neq 0
and thus
S_{\mu}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}=\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}
.

\square

The previous property implies the following corollary.

Corollary 5.4
(Synchronous correlation)

Given
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, suppose that there is an index
\mu=0,\dots,d-1
such that either
\#\{k:A_{k}=P_{\mu}\}>r
or
\#\{k:A_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{\mu}\}>d-r
, with
\#\{O\}
being the cardinality of
O
. Then:

A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d}\ket{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}=0.

(52)

Proof:

It follows directly from the Proposition
5.2
, and the observation that

A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d}\,S_{\mu}=0,

(53)

as in all terms appearing in the sum (
44
) there are exactly
r
copies of
P_{\mu}
and
d-r
copies
\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{\mu}
. Since
P_{\mu}(\mathbb{I}_{d}-P_{\mu})=0
, the claim follows.

\square

Remark 5.5

We called the previous corollary synchronous correlation as it it implies a synchronous property for the quantum correlation
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
. A bipartite correlation
p(a,a^{\prime}|\mu,\mu^{\prime})
is synchronous if for all
\mu
and
a\neq a^{\prime}
we have that
p(a,a^{\prime}|\mu,\mu)
. In the multipartite case, the synchronous property
5.4
implies that

\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})=0

with
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu,\mu,\dots,\mu)
and
\boldsymbol{a}
satisfying
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}a_{k}\neq n-r
. Similarly to the bipartite case
[
57
,
58
]
, the synchronous property carries significant implications that are foundational to the self-testing statement from this work.

From now on, we introduce the notation
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu,\mu,\dots,\mu).

Using Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
5.2
, we can proceed with the proof of self-testing for the measurement.

Theorem 5.6

The quantum correlation
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})
in Equation (

) obtained from the canonical strategy
5.1
self-tests the measurements (

).

Proof:

Consider a system of
d
parties, each with a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{H}_{1},\dots\mathcal{H}_{d-1}
. Suppose that they share a state

\ket{\Psi}\in\bigotimes_{k=0}^{d-1}\mathcal{H}_{k}

(54)

and consider
N
POVM elements in each subspace
{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
:

\{E_{\mu,0}^{(k)},E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}\}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k}),\quad\mu\in[N],\quad k\in[d]

(55)

with
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}+E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, and
\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
being the identity on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
Suppose that these POVM and the state
\ket{\Psi}
generate the same correlations
\tilde{p}
as in Definition
5.1
and Equation (
43
), that is

\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\boldsymbol{\mu})=\matrixelement{\Psi}{E_{\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol{a}}}{\Psi},

(56)

with
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in[d]^{n}
, and

E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}=E_{\mu_{0},a_{0}}^{(k)}\otimes E_{\mu_{1},a_{1}}^{(k)}\otimes\dots\otimes E_{\mu_{d-1},a_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}
.

The proof is split into three parts. First, in Part I, we prove that a synchronous property
5.4
holds for
\ket{\Psi}
. In Part II, we show that all operators
E_{\mu,a}^{(k)}
are projectors for all
\mu
,
k
and
a
. This is a consequence of the synchronous property described in Part I. Finally, in Part III, we conclude that the sum of the projections
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}
is proportional to the identity multiplied by
x
. All of this supported by the Theorem
3.2
proves the claim.

Part I

Without loss of generality, we assume that, if
\rho_{k}
is the reduced density matrix of
\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}
to the space of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and
\rho_{k}
has maximal support.

Following
5.4
, we aim to prove that taking
A_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{0}),\dots,A_{d-1}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d-1})
, and assuming that for a fixed
\mu
either
\#\{k:A_{k}=E^{(k)}_{\mu,0}\}>r
or
\#\{k:A_{k}=E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}\}>d-r
, the following equality holds:

A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes A_{d}\ket{\Psi}=0.

(57)

Indeed, suppose
A_{k}=M_{\mu}^{(k)}
for
k=\in[r]
, and
A_{k}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
for
k\in[r,n]
. Then, setting
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu,\mu,\dots\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0\}^{r}\times\{0,1\}^{n-r}}\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0\}^{r}\times\{0,1\}^{n-r}}\matrixelement{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}{\tilde E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol a}}{\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0\}^{r}\times\{0,1\}^{n-r}}\matrixelement{\Psi}{E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol a}}{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{\Psi}{\bigotimes_{k\in[r+1]}{E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},0}^{(k)}\otimes\bigotimes_{k\in[r+1,d]} \mathbb I_{\mathcal H_{k}}}}{\Psi}=\Bigg\|{\bigotimes_{k\in[r+1]}{E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},0}^{(k)\frac{1}{2}}\otimes\bigotimes_{k\in[r+1,d]}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}}\ket{\Psi}}\Bigg\|^{2},

(58)

which implies that

E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes E_{\mu,0}^{(1)}\otimes\dots\otimes E_{\mu,0}^{(r)}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{r+1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{d-1}}\ket{\Psi}=0.

(59)

Part II

We now show that all the operators
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}
are projections. We proceed with
k=0
, and begin with the following observation, which is a consequence of Equation (
57
). Consider

\displaystyle E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{k\in[1,d]}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\right)\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\bigotimes_{k\in[1,d]}(E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}+E_{\mu,1}^{(k)})\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}}E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0\}\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\
\sum_{k}a_{k}=d-r\end{subarray}}E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}\ket{\Psi}=\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\otimes\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{a}\in\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\
\sum_{k}a_{k}=d-r\end{subarray}}E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}^{[1,d]}\ket{\Psi}.

(60)

In the previous equation, we introduced, for
m>n
:

E_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{a}}^{[n,m]}:=E_{\mu_{0},a_{0}}^{(n)}\otimes E_{\mu_{1},a_{1}}^{(n+1)}\otimes\dots\otimes E_{\mu_{m-n-1},a_{m-n-1}}^{(m-1)}

(61)

If the sum in the first line was expanded, the only non-zero terms would have
r
elements equal to
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}
and
d-r
elements equal to
E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}
. Furthermore, in the last line, we can exchange
E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}
with the identity
\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
, as no contribution would come from
E_{\mu,1}^{(0)}
.
Using the condition (
5.1
), we can explicitly show that the operator
E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}
is a projection. Take
\ket{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and we claim that
(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)})^{2}\ket{\varphi}=E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\ket{\varphi}
. Explicitly

\displaystyle(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)})^{2}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)})^{2}\rho_{0}\rho_{0}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}=(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)})^{2}\tr_{[1,d]}({\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}})\rho_{0}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\tr_{[1,d]}(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{d-1}}{\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}})\rho_{1}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{a}\in\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\
\sum_{k}a_{k}=d-r\end{subarray}}E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\tr_{[1,d]}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}\otimes E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}^{[1,d]}{\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}})\rho_{1}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{a}\in\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\
\sum_{k}a_{k}=d-r\end{subarray}}\tr_{[1,d]}(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}^{[1,d]}{\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}})\rho_{1}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\tr_{[1,d]}(E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{d-1}}{\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}})\rho_{1}^{-1}\ket{\varphi}=E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\ket{\varphi}.

(62)

In the last equality we introduced the partial trace

\tr_{[n,m]}(A):=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{n+1},\dots,\mathcal{H}_{m-1}}(A).

(63)

Notice that the assumption on
\rho_{0}
having full support is necessary in order to define
\rho_{0}^{-1}
in the first line.
We can repeat the same arguments for all parties so that

E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}=(E_{\mu,0}^{(k)})^{2}=(E_{\mu,0}^{(k)})^{*},\quad E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}=(E_{\mu,1}^{(k)})^{2}=(E_{\mu,1}^{(k)})^{*}.

(64)

hold for all

and
\mu=0,\dots,N-1
.
Consequently, all the
\{E_{\mu,0}^{(k)},E_{\mu,1}^{(k)}\}
are projective measurement.

Part III

It remains to show that the projections
E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}
sum up to the identity. We mimic the proof from
[
14
]
. For
k=0,\dots,N-1
, define
E^{(k)}=\sum_{\mu=0}^{N-1}E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
. We claim that
E^{(k)}=x\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. For
k=0
one has

\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}E^{(0)})=\matrixelement{\Psi}{E^{(0)}\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_{d-1}}}{\Psi}=x.

(65)

Similarly, using (
5.1
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{(\rho_{0}(E^{(0)})^{2})}

\displaystyle=\matrixelement{\Psi}{(E^{(0)})^2\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_{d-1}}}{\Psi}=\sum_{\mu\nu=0}^{N-1}\matrixelement{\Psi}{E_{\nu,0}^{(0)}E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_1}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb I_{\mathcal H_{d-1}}}{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu=0}^{N-1}\matrixelement{\Psi}{E_{\nu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol a\in\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\\sum_{k}a_k=d-r}} E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol a}^{[1,d]}}{\Psi}=\sum_{\mu\nu=0}^{N-1}\matrixelement{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}{\tilde E_{\nu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\sum_{\substack{\boldsymbol a\in\times\{0,1\}^{d-1}\\\sum_{k}a_k=d-r}} \tilde E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol a}^{[1,d]}}{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu=0}^{N-1}\matrixelement{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}{\tilde E_{\nu,0}^{(0)}\tilde E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}\otimes\mathbb I_{d}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb I_{d}}{\Psi_{\mathrm S}}=x^{2}.

(66)

Observe that, in the last line, since the correlation generated by
E_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}
and the state
\ket{\Psi}
is the same
\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{a}|\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
as the one generated by
\tilde{E}_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\boldsymbol{a}}
and
\ket{\Psi_\mathrm S}
.
In the last line, we utilized the fact that we are obtaining a sum that is linear in the quantum correlation
\tilde{p}
, thereby yielding the same result we would obtain for the Slater state. But then

\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}E^{(0)})^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}E^{(0)2})=x^{2}.

(67)

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that

E^{(0)}=\sum_{\mu}E_{\mu,0}^{(0)}=x\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}.

(68)

The same proof can be repeated for all
k
. From Theorem
3.2
each Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
can be written as a tensor product

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\prime},

(69)

with
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and each
E_{\mu,0}^{(i)}
decomposes as

E_{\mu,0}^{(k)}=P_{\mu}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{\prime}},

(70)

which ends the proof.

\square

Conjecture 5.9

Lemma 5.11

Suppose that

Then,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime\prime}=\mathfrak{A}_{f}
.

Proof:

It is equivalent to the fact that the Lie group generated by

is the whole

[
59
]

with

the symmetrizer (

).

7
Conclusions

Slater states are antisymmetric quantum states that represent fermionic systems. Constructed as Slater determinants, these states lie in the fermionic (antisymmetric) subspace of the Hilbert space, which notably contains no product states. Consequently, all fermionic states are inherently entangled, even without interaction, due to the indistinguishability of particles
[
60
]
. In quantum computing, Slater states are important because they are invariant under global unitaries of the form
U^{\otimes d}
. This symmetry implies protection against collective noise, making them elements of decoherence-free subspaces—a key resource for fault-tolerant computation and quantum error avoidance
[
61
]
. They also play a foundational role in quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics, notably in methods like Hartree–Fock theory and density functional theory, where they approximate complex many-body ground states of many-electron systems.

Here, we have provided a self-testing scheme for this class of multiqudit states, which, importantly, is optimal in terms of the number of measurements and outcomes as compared to other existing schemes for multiqudit quantum states. In fact, in the case of odd local dimensions, our scheme requires only four binary measurements to be performed by each observer to certify the state. We also extend our result to the case of even local dimensions, however, at the cost of increasing the number of binary measurements to
d
for a quantum system of local dimension
d
. Moreover, we demonstrate our scheme to be robust to noises and experimental imperfections, which is a key feature for practical implementations of self-testing.

These findings not only deepen our understanding of multipartite quantum systems but also provide practical tools for the reliable certification of entanglement in realistic settings.

The outlook for this work naturally divides into three directions. First, it would be significant to prove Conjectures
5.7
or
5.9
for arbitrary families of projections that add up to the identity, as considered in Theorem
3.2
. As discussed in Section
5.3
, a key obstacle lies in the fact that not all irreducible Lie algebras coincide with
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, a property that holds for
C^{*}
-algebras. For instance, the Lie algebra generated by the Pauli matrices
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
is
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, the algebra of traceless
2\times 2
matrices, whereas the
C^{*}
-algebra they generate is the whole
\mathcal{M}_{2}
.
Another interesting direction is to investigate whether the results can be extended to the case of

N=4
measurements in even dimensions, analogously to the extension for the maximally entangled bipartite state presented in
[
54
]
. From a more general perspective, it would also be very interesting to explore whether the methodology used in this work can be used to
design self-testing schemes for other bipartite or multipartite states of arbitrary local dimension.
