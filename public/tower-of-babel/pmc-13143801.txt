Letter to the Editor: Citations of Retracted Publications Should Be Discounted From One’s Bibliometric Indicator
Authors: Bor Luen Tang
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Several papers on the analysis of citations of retracted publications in various contexts have recently appeared in the pages of the journal,1,2 the latest of which by Mukhamediyarov and colleagues.3 The authors analyzed retractions of PubMed-indexed social media-related papers, including geographic distribution of authors and causes for retraction and found a positive correlation between the number of citations and Altmetric Attention Score (AAS). These and other papers published elsewhere point towards the prevalence of citations of retracted papers, including pre- post-retraction citations – the latter when papers which have been retracted continue to be cited.4,5 According to the Retraction Watch’s list of the 10 most highly cited retractions as of November, 2025, some among these have an even higher number of citations after, compare to before retraction.6

Such citations, be it due to ignorance, carelessness, or a tendency towards recalcitrant/dogmatic reliance on questionable findings,7 are ethically problematic. When erroneous or fraudulent findings or bodies of work continue to be cited and thus remain influential, there are several detrimental consequences. Firstly, other researchers following up on the same or related research topic could be misled or side-tracked by the large number of pre- and post-retraction citations. On the other hand, they could be discouraged from presenting or actively pursuing alternative theories or explanations, and their dissenting yet true results could be suppressed. Also, continuous citation of retracted articles might sustain erroneous rules, applications, social policies, or even medical practices and treatments based on the retracted papers. As such, research ethics and integrity would thus be generally undermined. Despite the obvious detriment, there remains no effective means of putting an end to such malpractice. Also, despite extensive analysis of the characteristics and possible causes associated with post-retraction citations, what to do with such citations remains unclear.

An important yet not emphasized issue associated with the citations of retracted papers is that these, be it pre- or post-retraction, continue to count towards a researcher’s bibliometric indicators. The latter range from the readily calculated and widely used h-index in the award of promotion and tenure, to the more complex c-index used for the compilation of the Stanford-Elsevier compilation of World’s Top 2% scientists.8 In this regard, there is an obvious gap in terms of giving credit only when credit is due.

When a paper is retracted (or withdrawn in more euphemistic terms), its content would remain in either paper or electronic archives. Such contents, while having identified issues, might remain scholarly useful in some respects (for example, in historical/philosophical research, or in comparative analysis of ideas and findings). However, it should be clear that all academic or intellectual credits associated with the retracted paper should be thereby nullified. This would be particularly so for papers associated with various forms of research misconduct (fabrication, falsification or plagiarism) and publishing misconduct (such as fake peer-review rings, citation farming or citation rings). In other words, citations accumulated for a retracted paper through time should no longer count towards a researcher’s h-index, c-index, or any other bibliometric indicator.

A retracted publication is often or readily disregarded (either removed by oneself or ignored by others) in assessments of researcher awards and rankings, or in decisions on career advancements. However, one’s citation-based indexes often remain unscathed and unmodified. This constitutes a form of epistemic injustice, which becomes particularly egregious when perpetrators of research and/or publishing misconduct could take advantage of and reap benefits from. Discounting the citations to one’s retracted publications would promote clarity and fairness in a competitive yet meritocratic research environment and uphold the principles of research ethics.

Unlike the prevention of post-retraction citations, discounting citations associated with retracted publications could be much more readily and effectively achieved. Such a measure should thus be implemented by reviewing boards, institutions, and various authoritative bodies that calculate and use citation indexes to reward research excellence.

Author’s Response to the Letter

This letter seeks to clarify several methodological and conceptual aspects of post-retraction citations.3 It has been drafted in line with the LETTERS recommendations of the EQUATOR Network.9

The index report comprehensively analyzed PubMed-indexed retractions tagged with social media keyword, focusing on geographic distribution, retraction reasons, and AAS.3 A statistically significant positive association was revealed between citation counts and AAS, suggesting that retracted publications continue to attract potential authors’ attention. These results underscore the urgent need for rigorous post-publication monitoring and enhanced scholarly collaboration to uphold research integrity standards. Building on previous retraction analyses,10,11 the index study highlights the lasting impact of citations to retracted items, both before and after retraction, and the risks these citations pose to the integrity of science communication.

The importance of excluding citations of retracted documents from scholars' bibliometric profiles is obvious. The integrity of citation tracking is a collective responsibility of all stakeholders of scholarly publishing and science communication, including authors, reviewers, journal editors, publishers, indexers, and learned associations.12 Citation-tracking bibliographic databases such as Scopus and Web of Science bear responsibility of cleaning citation records, as their citation metrics influence the accuracy and integrity of reference lists in the indexed literature.

Within this paradigm, two specific steps require attention. Researchers who use citation management software should use tools to detect retracted publications and manually update their reference lists to avoid inadvertently citing erroneous and unethical documents. Moreover, citation-tracking databases should identify post-retraction citations and exclude them from the recording of individual researcher and journal profiles. Records of retracted articles and their corresponding citations should remain visible in author search results with appropriate labeling to uphold the integrity of the scholarly record. However, these citations should not be used for academic credits of individual researchers. This adjustment may substantially reduce mistakes in research assessment for career advancement.

To sum up, effective management of citations to retracted documents necessitates a systematic, multi-stakeholder approach, including proactive technological interventions by database moderators, and broader adoption of advanced citation management tools.
