Computability of Equivariant Gröbner bases
Arka Ghosh, Aliaume Lopez

Abstract
Let 𝕂 \mathbb{K} be a field, 𝒳 \mathcal{X} be an infinite set (of indeterminates), and 𝒢 \mathcal{G} be a group acting on 𝒳 \mathcal{X} . An ideal in the polynomial ring 𝕂 ⁡ [ 𝒳 ] \mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}] is called equivariant if it is invariant under the action of 𝒢 \mathcal{G} . We show Gröbner bases for equivariant ideals are computable are hence the equivariant ideal membership is decidable when 𝒢 \mathcal{G} and 𝒳 \mathcal{X} satisfies the Hilbert’s basis property, that is, when every equivariant ideal in 𝕂 ⁡ [ 𝒳 ] \mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}] is finitely generated. Moreover, we give a sufficient condition for the undecidability of the equivariant ideal membership problem. This condition is satisfied by the most common examples not satisfying the Hilbert’s basis property. Keywords: equivariant ideal, Hilbert basis, ideal membership problem, orbit finite, oligomorphic, well-quasi-ordering Repository: https://github.com/AliaumeL/AtomicHilbert

1
Introduction

For a field
\mathbb{K}
and a non-empty set
\mathcal{X}
of indeterminates, we use

\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
to denote the ring of polynomials with coefficients from
\mathbb{K}

and indeterminates/variables from
\mathcal{X}
. A fundamental result in commutative
algebra is
Hilbert’s basis theorem
, stating that when
\mathcal{X}
is finite,
every ideal in
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
is finitely generated
[
26
]
, where an

ideal
is a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
that is closed under
addition and multiplication by elements of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
. This property can
be rephrased as the fact that the set of polynomials
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
is

Noetherian
.
Hilbert’s basis theorem
extends to the case where
\mathbb{K}

is a ring that is itself
Noetherian

[
35
, Theorem 4.1]
.

A
Gröbner basis
is a specific kind of generating set of a polynomial ideal
which allows easy checking of membership of a given polynomial in that ideal.

Gröbner bases
were introduced by Buchberger who showed when
\mathcal{X}
is
finite, every ideal in
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
has a finite
Gröbner basis
and
that, for a given a set of polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, one can compute a
finite
Gröbner basis
of the ideal generated by them via the so-called

Buchberger algorithm

[
11
]
. The
existence and computability of
Gröbner bases
implies the decidability of the

ideal membership problem
: given a polynomial
f
and set of polynomial

H
, decide whether
f
is in the ideal generated by
H
. The theory of

Gröbner bases
has applications in very diverse areas of computer
science, including integer programming
[
51
]
, algebraic proof
systems
[
31
]
, geometric reasoning
[
13
]
, fixed
parameter tractability
[
2
]
, program analysis
[
49
]
and
constraint satisfaction problems
[
41
]
.
In automata theory it has been used for deciding zeroness of polynomial
automata
[
6
]
, reachability in symmetric Petri nets
[
42
]
,
equivalence for string-to-string transducers
[
29
]
and equivalence
of polynomial differential equations
[
12
]
.

There has been a growing interest in the last few years for computational
models that are manipulating infinite data structures in a finite way, for
instance an automaton reading words on the infinite alphabet
\mathbb{N}
, while
maintaining a finite number of states. While this idea can be traced back to
the 90s with the notion of register automata
[
30
]
, it has been revived
in with the development of the theory of
orbit finite sets
. In this
setting, one would like to consider an infinite set of variables
\mathcal{X}
. As an
example, let us consider the set
\mathcal{X}
of variables
x_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and
the
ideal

\mathcal{Z}
generated by the set
\{x_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\}
. It is
clear that
\mathcal{Z}
is not finitely generated, and we conclude that the

Hilbert’s basis theorem
(and a fortiori, the
Gröbner basis

theory) does not extend to the case of infinite sets of indeterminates.

However, in the applications mentionned above, the infinite set of
variables (data) comes with an extra structure: the behaviour of the considered
systems are invariant under the action of a group
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{X}
. The action
of this
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{X}
naturally induces an action on
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, by
renaming the variables. The typical example is the group of all permutations of

\mathcal{X}
, which corresponds to seeing
\mathcal{X}
as a set of
indistinguishable

names: one is not interested in the ideal
\mathcal{Z}
generated by the set

\{x_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, but rather in the
equivariant ideal
generated by
the set
\{x_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\}
, which is the smallest ideal that contains it
and is invariant under the action of
\mathcal{G}
. In this case, this ideal is
finitely generated by a single indeterminate, e.g.
x_{1}
. Please note that
equivariance does not imply finite generation in general: for instance, the
ideal
\mathcal{Z}
is not finitely generated as an equivariant ideal with respect to
the trivial group.

Related Research

The above-mentioned results were rediscovered in
[
3
,
4
,
27
]
. In

[
28
]
these results were used to prove the Independent Set Conjecture in

[
28
]
, the authors also showed that one can even

\mathcal{M}
of

\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{M}}}}
is a

[

]
, which gives a necessary and a sufficient condition on the

for the
Equivariant Hilbert basis property

to hold
[
21
, Theorems 11 and 12, Lemma 13]
. The necessary and
sufficient conditions are equivalent up to a well-known conjecture by Pouzet

[
46
, Problems 12]
. But to obtain decision procedures, one still lacks
a generalisation of

to the equivariant case, except
under artificial extra assumptions
[
21
, Section 6]
. Overall, a
general understanding of the decidability of the
equivariant ideal
membership problem
is still missing, and
a fortiori
, a generalisation
of
Buchberger’s algorithm
to the equivariant case is still an open
problem.

Our results are part of a larger research direction that aims at establishing
an algorithmic theory of computation with orbit-finite sets. For instance,

[
43
]
studies equivariant subspaces of vector spaces generated by

[
20
,
36
]
study solvability of orbit-finite systems
of linear equations and inequalities, and
[
43
,
20
,
47
]
study duals
of vector spaces generated by orbit-finite sets.

1.2
Contributions.

In this paper, we bridge the gap between the
theoretical understanding of the
equivariant Hilbert basis property

[
21
, Property 4]
, and the computational aspects of
equivariant
ideals
, by showing that under mild assumptions on the group action, one can
compute an
equivariant Gröbner basis
of an
equivariant ideal
, hence,
that one can decide the
equivariant ideal membership problem
. In order to
compute such sets, we will need to introduce some classical
computability
assumptions
on the group action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
, and on the set of
indeterminates
\mathcal{X}
. These will be defined in

Section

2
, but informally, we assume
that one can compute representatives of the orbits of elements under the action
of
\mathcal{G}
(this is called
effective oligomorphism
), and that one has
access to a total ordering on
\mathcal{X}
that is computable, and

compatible
with the action of
\mathcal{G}
. Please note that the ordering
on
\mathcal{X}
is not required to be well-founded, and a typical example of our
computable assumptions would be the set
\mathbb{Q}
of rationals, equipped with the
natural ordering
\leq
and the group
\mathcal{G}
would be the group of all
monotone bijections from
\mathbb{Q}
to itself.

Let us now focus on the mild semantic assumption that we will need to make
on the set of indeterminates
\mathcal{X}
and the group
\mathcal{G}
, that will
guarantee the termination of our procedures. We refer to our preliminaries
(
Section

2
) for a more detailed
discussion on these assumptions, but again informally, we ask that the set of
monomials
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
is well-behaved with respect to divisibility up
to the action of
\mathcal{G}
, which we write as the fact that
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
well-quasi-ordering
(
WQO
). It is known from that this
is a necessary condition for the
equivariant Hilbert basis property

Theorem

1
, and we will rely on a slightly stronger
condition, namely that
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
WQO
, whenever

(Y,\leq)
is one, which is conjectured to be equivalent to the first
condition. Beware that
Theorems

1
and
2

are
incomparable: the former does not talk about decidability, while the latter
only considers
equivariant ideals
that are already finitely presented, and we
will show in

Example

41
an example where
equivariant
Gröbner bases
are computable, but the
Hilbert basis property
fails.

Theorem 1

(name=
[
21
, Theorem 11]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a totally ordered set of indeterminates
equipped with a group action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
that is

compatible
with the ordering on
\mathcal{X}
.
Then,
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
WQO
, if and only if
the
equivariant Hilbert basis property
holds for
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
.

Theorem 2

(name=Equivariant Gröbner Basis,restate=thm:compute-equiv-gb)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a totally ordered set of indeterminates
equipped with a group action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
, under our
computability assumptions
.
If
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
WQO
for every

well-quasi-ordered
set
(Y,\leq)
, then one can
compute an
equivariant Gröbner bases
of
equivariant ideals
.

To prove our
Theorem

2
, we will first introduce a weaker
notion of
weak equivariant Gröbner basis
, which characterises the results
obtained by naïvely adapting
Buchberger’s algorithm
to the equivariant
case. Then, we will show that under our
computability assumptions
, one can
start from a finite set of generators
H
of an
equivariant ideal
, and
compute a well-chosen
weak equivariant Gröbner basis
, which happens to be
an
equivariant Gröbner basis
of the ideal generated by
H
. As a
consequence, we obtain effective representations of
equivariant ideals
,
over which one can check membership, inclusion, and compute the sum and
intersection of
equivariant ideals

(
Corollary

29
).

We then focus on providing undecidability results for the
equivariant
ideal membership problem
in the case where our effective assumptions are
satisfied, but the
well-quasi-ordering
condition is not. This aims at
illustrating the fact that our assumptions are close to optimal. One classical
way for a set of structures to not be
well-quasi-ordered
(when labelled
using integers) is to have the ability to represent an
infinite path
(a
formal definition will be given in

Section

8
). We prove that
whenever one can (effectively) represent an infinite path in the set of
monomials
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
, then the
equivariant ideal membership
problem
is undecidable.

Theorem 3

(name=Undecidability of Equivariant Ideal Membership,restate=thm:undecidable-paths)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a totally ordered set of indeterminates
equipped with a group action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
, under our
computability assumptions
.
If
\mathcal{X}
contain an
infinite path

then the
equivariant ideal membership problem
is undecidable.

Finally, we illustrate how our positive results find applications in numerous
situations. This is done by providing families indeterminates that satisfy our

computability assumptions
, and for which we can compute
equivariant
Gröbner bases
, and also by showing how our results can be used in the context
of
topological well-structured transition systems

[
23
]
, with
applications do the verification of infinite state systems such as orbit
finite weighted automata
[
8
]
,
orbit finite polynomial
automata
, and more generally orbit finite systems dealing with polynomial
computations.

Organisation.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In

Section

2
, we introduce formally the notions of
Gröbner
bases
,

,

. In

, we use

, and we show that it provides a way to effectively

).
We continue by showing in

that the assumptions of our

are closed under two natural operations
(

and

). The positive

).

2
Preliminaries

Partial orders, ordinals, well-founded sets, and well-quasi-ordered
sets.

We assume basic familiarity with partial orders, well-founded sets,
and ordinals. We will use the notation
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\omega}
for the first infinite
ordinal (that is,
(\mathbb{N},\leq)
), and write
X\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}+}}Y
for the
lexicographic sum of two partial orders
X
and
Y
. Similarly, the notation
X\mathbin{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\times}}Y
will denote the product of two partial orders equipped with the
lexigographic ordering, i.e.
(x_{1},y_{1})\leq(x_{2},y_{2})
if either
x_{1}<x_{2}
, or
x_{1}=x_{2}
and
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
. We will also use the usual notations
for finite ordinals, writing
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathrm{n}}
for the finite ordinal of size

n
. For instance,
{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}\mathbin{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}+}}{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathrm{1}}
is the total order
\mathbb{N}\uplus\{+\infty\}
, where
+\infty
is the new largest element.

In order to guarantee the termination of the algorithms presented in this
paper, a key ingredient will be the notion of
well-quasi-ordering

(WQO), that are sets
(X,\leq)
such that every infinite sequence

\left(x_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of elements of
X
contains a pair
i<j
such that

x_{i}\leq x_{j}
. Examples of
well-quasi-orderings
include finite sets with
any ordering, or
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
with the product ordering. We refer the reader
to
[
17
]
for a comprehensive introduction to
well-quasi-orderings

and their applications in computer science.

Polynomials, monomials, divisibility.

We assume basic familiarity with the theory of
commutative algebra, and polynomials. We will use the notation
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]

for the ring of polynomials with coefficients from a field
\mathbb{K}
and
indeterminates/variables from a set
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
for the set of
monomials in
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
. Letters
p,q,r
are used to denote polynomials,

\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}
are used to denote monomials, and
a,b,\alpha,\beta
are
used to denote coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
.

A classical example of a
WQO
is the set of monomials
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
,
endowed with the
divisibility
relation
\mathrel{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}}}
whenever
\mathcal{X}

is finite. We recall that a monomial
\mathfrak{m}

divides
a monomial

\mathfrak{n}
if there exists a monomial
\mathfrak{l}
such that
\mathfrak{m}\times\mathfrak{l}=\mathfrak{n}
. In this case, we write
\mathfrak{m}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}}}\mathfrak{n}
. Note that monomials can be seen as functions from

\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{N}
having a finite support, and that the
divisibility

relation can be extended to monomials that are functions from
\mathcal{X}
to

(Y,\leq)
, where
Y
is any partially ordered set. In this case, we write

\mathfrak{m}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}}}\mathfrak{n}
if for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\mathfrak{m}(x)\leq\mathfrak{n}(x)
. We will write
\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{Mon}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}\mathbin{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}+}}1}}}(\mathcal{X})
(resp.
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}^{2}}}}(\mathcal{X})
) for the set of monomials that are
functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}\mathbin{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}+}}{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathrm{1}}
(resp.
{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\omega}^{2}
).

Unless otherwise specified, we will assume that the set of indeterminates

\mathcal{X}
comes equipped with a total ordering
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\leq_{\mathcal{X}}}}
. Using this order, we
define the
reverse lexicographic
(revlex) ordering on monomials as
follows:
\mathfrak{n}\mathrel{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\sqsubset^{\mathsf{RevLex}}}}\mathfrak{m}
if there exists an
indeterminate
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathfrak{n}(x)<\mathfrak{m}(x)
, and such
that for every
y\in\mathcal{X}
, if
x\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}<_{\mathcal{X}}}}y
then
\mathfrak{n}(y)=\mathfrak{m}(y)
. Remark that if
\mathfrak{n}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathsf{RevLex}}}}\mathfrak{m}
, then in
particular
\mathfrak{n}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}}}\mathfrak{m}
.

We can now use the
reverse lexicographic
ordering to identify particular elements in
a given polynomial. Namely, for a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, we define
the
leading monomial

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{LM}}}(p)
of
p
as the largest monomial
appearing in
p
with respect to the
revlex ordering
, and the

leading coefficient

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{LC}}}(p)
of
p
as the coefficient of

\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}}}(p)
in
p
. We can then define the
leading term

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{LT}}}(p)
of

p
as the product of its
leading monomial
and its
leading
coefficient
, and the
characteristic monomial

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{CM}}}(p)
of
p
as
the product of its
leading monomial
and all the indeterminates appearing
in
p
. We also define the
domain
of
\mathfrak{m}
as the set

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{dom}}}(\mathfrak{m})
of indeterminates
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathfrak{m}(x)\neq 0
. Because the coefficients and monomial in question are highly dependent on
the ordering
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\leq_{\mathcal{X}}}}
, we allow ourselves to write
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}_{\mathcal{X}}}}(p)
to
highlight the precise ordered set of variables that was used to compute the

leading monomial
of
p
.

Let us briefly argue in favor of the
reverse lexicographic
ordering. In
the case of a finite set of indeterminates, one can choose any total ordering
on
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
, as long as it contains the
divisibility

quasi-ordering, and is compatible with the product of monomials.
1
1

1

This
is often called a
monomial ordering
, see
[
14
]
.
In our case,
having an infinite number of indeterminates, we rely on a connection between

\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}}}(p)
and
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{dom}}}(p)
:
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{dom}}}(p)\subseteq{\downarrow\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{dom}}}(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}}}(p))}
, where

{\downarrow S}
is the downward closure of a set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, i.e. the
set of all indeterminates
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
y\leq x
for some
y\in S
. This means that the
leading monomial
encodes a

of the polynomial, and it will be crucial in our termination arguments. This is
already at the core of the classical

[
14
, Chapter 3, Theorem

.

Ideals, and Gröbner Bases.

An
ideal

\mathcal{I}
of

\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
is a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
that is closed under
addition and multiplication by elements of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
. Given a set
H\subseteq\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, we denote by
\mathopen{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\langle}}H\mathclose{{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\rangle}}
the ideal generated
by
H
, i.e. the smallest ideal that contains
H
. The
ideal membership
problem
is the following decision problem: given a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
and a set of polynomials
H\subseteq\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, decide
whether
p
belongs to the ideal
\mathopen{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\langle}}H\mathclose{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\rangle}}
generated by
H
. We know that
this problem is decidable when
\mathcal{X}
is finite, and that it is even

\mathsf{EXPTIME}
-complete
[
42
]
. The classical approach to the
ideal
membership problem
is to use the
Gröbner basis
theory that was developed
in the 70s by Buchberger
[
11
]
.
A set
\mathcal{B}
of polynomials is called a
Gröbner basis
of
an ideal
\mathcal{I}
if,
\mathopen{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\langle}}\mathcal{B}\mathclose{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\rangle}}=\mathcal{I}
and for every polynomial
p\in\mathcal{I}
, there exists a polynomial
q\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}_{\mathcal{X}}}}(q)\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}}}\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{LM}_{\mathcal{X}}}}(p)
.

Given a
Gröbner basis

\mathcal{B}
of an ideal
\mathcal{I}
, and a polynomial
p
,
it suffices to iteratively reduce the
leading monomial
of
p
by
subtracting multiples of elements in
\mathcal{B}
, until one cannot apply any
reductions. If the result is
0
, then
p
belongs to
\mathcal{I}
, and otherwise it
does not.

Group actions, equivariance, and orbit finite sets.

A

group

\mathcal{G}
is a set equipped with a binary operation that is
associative, has an identity element and has inverses. In our setting, we are
interested in infinite sets
\mathcal{X}
of indeterminates that is equipped with a

group action

\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
. This means that for each
\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}
, we have a bijection
\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{X}
that we denote by
x\mapsto\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot x
. A set
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
equivariant
under the action of

\mathcal{G}
if for all
\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in S
, we have
\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot x\in S
. We give in
Example

4
an example and a non-example
of
equivariant ideals
.

Example 4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be any infinite set, and
\mathcal{G}
be the
group of all bijections of
\mathcal{X}
.
Then the set
S_{0}\subset\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
of all polynomials
whose set of coefficients sums to
0
is an equivariant ideal.
Conversely, the set of all polynomials that are multiple
of
x\in X
is an
ideal
that is not
equivariant
.

Proof.

Let
p,q\in S_{0}
, and
r\in\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
.
Then,
p\times r+q
is in
S_{0}
. Remark that

p,r
and
q
belong to a subset
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
of the
polynomials that uses only finitely many indeterminates.
In this subset, the sum of all coefficients is obtained
by applying the polynomials to the value
1
for every indeterminate

y\in\mathcal{X}
. We conclude that

(p\times r+q)(1,\dots,1)=p(1,\dots,1)\times r(1,\dots,1)+q(1,\dots,1)=0\times r(1,\dots,1)+0=0
, hence that

p\times r+q
belongs to
S_{0}
.
Because
0
is in
S_{0}
, we conclude that
S_{0}
is an
ideal
.
Furthermore, if
\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}
and
p\in S_{0}
, then
the sum of the coefficients
\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot p
is exactly
the sum of the coefficients of
p
, hence is
0
too.
This shows that
S_{0}
is
equivariant
.

It is clear that all multiples of a given polynomial
x\in\mathcal{X}

is an ideal of
\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
. This is not an
equivariant ideal
:
take any bijection
\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}
that does not map
x
to
x
(it
exists because
\mathcal{X}
is infinite and
\mathcal{G}
is all permutations),
then
\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot x
is not a multiple of
x
, and therefore does
not belong to the ideal.
∎

An
equivariant set
is said to be
orbit finite
if it is the
union of finitely many
orbits
under the action of
\mathcal{G}
. We denote

\mathop{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\mathsf{orbit}_{\mathcal{G}}}}(E)
for the set of all elements
\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot x
for

\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}
and

. Equivalently, an

is a set of the form
\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{orbit}_{\mathcal{G}}}}(E)
for some finite set

. Not every

equivariant subset
is
orbit finite
, as shown in

5
. However,
orbit finite sets
are
robust in the sense that
equivariant subsets
of

are
also
orbit finite
, and similarly, an
equivariant subset
of

is

whenever

is
orbit finite
and

is finite.
For algorithmic purposes,
orbit finite sets
are the ones that can be taken
as input as a finite set of representatives (one for each orbit). The notions
of
equivariance
and
orbit finite sets
from a computational
perspective are discussed in
[
9
]
, and we refer the reader to this
book for a more comprehensive introduction to the topic.

We will mostly be interested in
orbit-finitely generated

equivariant ideals
, i.e.
equivariant ideals
that are generated by an

of polynomials, for which the
equivariant ideal
membership problem
is as follows: given a polynomial

and an

\mathopen{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\langle}}H\mathclose{{\color[rgb]{0.7773,0.043,0.2734}\rangle}}_{\mathcal{G}}

.

1.

2.

There exists an algorithm that decides whether two elements

Reducts

As mentioned in the introduction, some examples of
group actions
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
do not preserve a linear order on

\mathcal{X}
, such as the set
{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathcal{A}}
of indistinguishable names without
any relations. However, there are techniques that allow us to reduce the
problem of computing
equivariant Gröbner bases
to the case where the
action preserves a linear ordering, using what is called a
reduct
of the
action. A group action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
is said to be a
reduct
of
another group action
\mathcal{H}\curvearrowright\mathcal{Y}
if there exists a bijection
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that
f^{-1}\circ\mathrm{\uppi}\circ f\in\mathcal{G}
for every

\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{H}
. It is called an
effective reduct
if
f
is

The goal of this section is to prove

Let us fix a set

7
Applications

Polynomial computations.

The fact that (finite control) systems
performing polynomial computations can be verified follows from the theory of

Gröbner bases
on finitely many indeterminates
[
44
,
6
]
.
There were also numerous applications to automata theory, such as deciding
whether a weighted automaton could be determinised (resp. desambiguated)

[
5
,
48
]
. We refer the readers to a nice survey recapitulating the
successes of the “Hilbert method” automata theory
[
10
]
. A natural
consequence of the effective computations of
equivariant Gröbner bases
is
that one can apply the same decision techniques to
orbit finite
polynomial computations
. For simplicity and clarity, we will focus on

polynomial automata
without states or zero-tests
[
6
]
, but
the same reasoning would apply to more general systems as we will discuss in

Remark

38
.

Before discussing the case of orbit finite polynomial automata, let us
recall the setting of
polynomial automata
in the classical case, as
studied by
[
6
]
, with techniques that dates back to

[
44
]
. A
polynomial automaton
is a tuple
A\triangleq(Q,\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},F)
, where
Q=\mathbb{K}^{n}
for some finite
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Sigma

is a finite alphabet,
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is a transition
function such that
\delta(\cdot,a)_{i}
is a polynomial in the indeterminates

q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
for every
a\in\Sigma
and every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state, and
F\colon Q\to\mathbb{K}
is a polynomial
function describing the final result of the automaton. The
zeroness
problem for polynomial automata
is the following decision problem: given a

polynomial automaton

A
, is it true that for all words
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
the polynomial
F(\delta^{*}(q_{0},w))
is zero? It is known that the
zeroness
problem for polynomial automata
is decidable
[
6
]
, using the
theory of
Gröbner bases
on finitely many indeterminates.

Let us now propose a new model of computation called
orbit finite
polynomial automata
, and prove an analogue decidability result. Let us fix an

effectively oligomorphic
action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
, such that there
exists finitely many indeterminates
V\subset_{\text{fin}}\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathcal{G}
acts
as the identity on
V
. Given such a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{K}
, and
given a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]
, we write
p(f)
for the
evaluation of
p
on
f
, that belongs to
\mathbb{K}
. Let us emphasis that the model
is purposely designed to be simple and illustrate the usage of
equivariant
Gröbner bases
, and not meant to be a fully-fledged model of computation.

Definition 36
.

An
orbit finite polynomial
automaton
is a tuple
A\triangleq(Q,\delta,q_{0},F)
, where
Q=\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{K}
,
q_{0}\in Q
is a function that is non-zero for finitely
many indeterminates,
\delta\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny{eq}}}}{{\to}}\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{X}]

is a
polynomial update function, and
F\in\mathbb{K}[V]
is a polynomial
computing the result of the automaton.

Given a letter
a\in\mathcal{X}
and a
state
q\in Q
, the updated state
\delta^{*}(a,q)\in Q
is defined as the function from

\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{K}
defined by
\delta^{*}(a,q)\colon x\mapsto\delta(a,x)(q)
.
The update function is naturally extended to words. Finally, the
output of an
orbit finite polynomial automaton
on a word
w\in\mathcal{X}^{*}

is defined as
F(\delta^{*}(w,q_{0}))
.

Orbit finite polynomial automata
can be used to model programs that
read a string
w\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
from left to right, having as internal state a
dictionary of type
dict[indet, number]
, which is updated using
polynomial computations. As for
polynomial automata
, the

zeroness problem
for orbit finite polynomial automata is the
following decision problem: decide if for every input word
w
, the output

F(\delta^{*}(w,q_{0}))
is zero.

Theorem 37

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a set of indeterminates that satisfies the

computability assumptions
and such that
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})

\delta

Monomial Reachability

The undecidability results we will present in this section regarding the

equivariant ideal membership problem
will use the polynomials in a very
limited way: we will only need to consider
monomials
, and there will
even be a bound on the maximal exponent used. Before going into the details of
our reductions, let us first introduce an intermediate problem that will be
easier to work with: the (equivariant)
monomial reachability problem
.

Definition 46
.

A
monomial rewrite system
is a finite set of pairs of the form

\{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\}
where
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}}}(\mathcal{X})
.
The
monomial reachability problem
is the problem of deciding whether
there exists a sequence of rewrites that transforms
\mathfrak{m}_{s}
into
\mathfrak{m}_{t}

using the rules of a monomial rewrite system
R
, where
a
rewrite step
is a pair of the form

\mathfrak{n}(\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot\mathfrak{m})\leftrightarrow_{R}\mathfrak{n}(\mathrm{\uppi}\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\text{ if }\{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\}\in R\text{ and }\mathrm{\uppi}\in\mathcal{G}\quad.

Example 47
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{G}
be the set of all bijections of
\mathcal{X}
.
Then, the rewrite system
x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}\leftrightarrow_{R}x_{1}^{2}

satisfies
\mathfrak{m}\leftrightarrow_{R}^{*}x_{1}^{2}
if and only if

\mathfrak{m}
has all its exponents that are multiple of
2
.

The following
shows that the
monomial
reachability problem
can be reduced to the
equivariant ideal membership
problem
, and follows the exact same reasoning as in the case of finitely many
indeterminates
[
42
]
. This reduction was also noticed in
[
21
, Theorem
64]
.

Lemma 48

(label=lem:mon-rewrite-red-membership,restate=lem:mon-rewrite-red-membership)
.

One can solve the
monomial reachability problem

provided that one can solve the
equivariant ideal membership problem
.

In order to show that the
equivariant ideal membership problem
is
undecidable, it is therefore enough to show that the
monomial reachability
problem
is undecidable. To that end, we will encode the Halting problem of a
Turing machine. There are two main obstacles to overcome: first, the
reversibility of the rewriting system, which can be (partially) solved by
considering a
reversible version
of a
deterministic
Turing
machines, as explained in
[
19
, Simulation by bidirected systems, p.
15]
; and second, the fact that the configurations of the Turing
machine cannot staightforwardly be encoded as monomials due to the
commutativity of the multiplication.

Proof.

Transitions of the deterministic reversible Turing machine using bounded tape size can be
modelled as a reversible string rewriting system using finitely many rules

and

over

This system is clearly complete, in the sense that one can perform a substitution

∎

Remark 52
.

The undecidability result of
Theorem

3
can be generalised
to a
relaxed
notion of
infinite path
. Given finitely
many orbits
\mathcal{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{k}
of pairs of
indeterminates, a
relaxed path
is a set of indeterminates
such that
(x_{i},x_{j})
is belongs to one of the orbits
\mathcal{O}_{k}

if and only if
|i-j|=1
for all
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 53
.

Given an
oligomorphic
set of indeterminates
\mathcal{X}
,
it is equivalent to say that
\mathcal{X}
contains an
infinite path

or to say that it contains
finite paths
of arbitrary length.

Proof.

Assume that there are arbitrarily long finite paths in
\mathcal{X}
.
Then, one can create an infinite tree whose nodes
are representatives of (distinct) orbits of finite paths, whose root is the empty path, and
where the ancestor relation is obtained by projecting on a subset of
indeterminates.
Because
\mathcal{X}
is
oligomorphic
, there are finitely many
nodes at each depth in the tree (i.e. at each length of the finite path).
Hence, there exists an infinite branch in the tree due to König’s lemma,
and this branch is a witness for the existence of an
infinite path

in
\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Example 54
.

The
Rado graph
, as introduced in
Example

10
,
contains an
infinite path

P
. Indeed, the Rado graph
contains every finite graph as an induced subgraph, and in particular,
it contains arbitrarily long finite paths.
As a consequence of
Theorem

3
,
which applies thanks to
Remark

53
,
we conclude that the
equivariant ideal membership problem

is undecidable for the Rado graph.

Example 55
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an
oligomorphic
infinite set of indeterminates.
Then
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
contains a (generalised)

as defined in

Proof.

Let
\left(x_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

\left(y_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

in
\mathcal{X}
.
Let us define

The orbits of pairs that define the successor relation
are the orbits of

where
x_{i}=x_{k}
and

, or where
x_{i}\neq x_{k}

\mathcal{X}

Let us sketch the fact that this defines a generalised path.
Consider that

((x_{i},y_{j}),(x_{k},y_{l}))

9
Concluding Remarks

We have given a sufficient condition for
equivariant Gröbner bases
to be
computable, under natural
computability assumptions
, and we have shown
that our sufficient condition is close to being optimal since the
undecidability of the
equivariant ideal membership problem
can be derived
for a large class of group actions that do not satisfy our condition.
Let us now discuss some open questions and conjectures that arise from our work.

Total orderings on the set of indeterminates

We assumed that the
indeterminates
\mathcal{X}
were equipped with a total ordering
\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\leq_{\mathcal{X}}}}
that is
preserved by the group action. This assumption seems necessary, as the notions
of
leading monomials
would cease to be well-defined without it. However,
we do not have a clear understanding of whether this assumption is vacuous or
not. Indeed, as noticed by
[
21
, Lemma 13]
, and

Lemma

14
, it often suffices to extend the structures of
the indeterminates to account for a total ordering. A conjecture of Pouzet

[
46
, Problems 12]
states that such an ordering always exists, and this
was remarked by
[
21
, Remark 14]
. Note that in this case, one would
get a complete characterisation of the group actions for which the

equivariant Hilbert basis property
holds
[
21
, Property 4]
.

Labelled well-quasi-orderings and dichotomy conjectures

As noted in

Section

8
, there are many conjectures relating the fact that

(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
well-quasi-ordering
(for every

well-quasi-ordered
set
Y
) and the presence of long
paths of some king (
43
and
45
).
In particular, Pouzet’s conjecture
[
45
]
would imply that for actions
arising from
homogeneous structures
(as in the examples given in

Section

3
),
Theorem

2
and
Theorem

3
are
two sides of a dichotomy theorem: either the
equivariant ideal membership
problem
is undecidable and there are
equivariant ideals
that are not

orbit-finitely generated
, or every
equivariant ideal
is

orbit-finitely generated
and one can compute
equivariant Gröbner
bases
. Let us note that for some classes of graphs having bounded clique
width, Pouzet’s conjecture is known to hold
[
16
,
39
]
.
This leads us to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 56
.

For every action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
of a group
\mathcal{G}
on a set
of indeterminates that is
effectively oligomorphic
, exactly
one of the following holds:

1.

The
equivariant ideal membership problem
is decidable.

2.

There exists an
equivariant ideal
that is not

orbit-finitely generated
.

Let us point out that a similar conjecture was already stated in the context of
Petri nets with data. Indeed, the condition that
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})

is a
WQO
for every
WQO

Y
also guarantees coverability of Petri nets
with data
\mathcal{X}
is decidable
[
37
, Theorem 1]
, and it was actually
conjectured to be a necessary condition
[
37
, Conjecture 1]
.

Complexity

In the present paper, we have focused on the
decidability of the
equivariant ideal membership problem
and the
computability of
equivariant Gröbner bases
. However, we have not addressed
the complexity of such problems, and have only adapted the most basic
algorithms for computing
Gröbner bases
. It would be interesting to know,
on the theoretical side, if one can obtain complexity lower bounds for such
problems, but also on the more practical side if advanced algorithms like
Faugère’s algorithm
[
18
]
can be adapted to the equivariant setting
and yield better performance in practice.

Duals of orbit-finitely generated vector spaces

An interesting
question posed by Sławomir Lasota is that whether for group actions

\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright\mathcal{X}
for which
(\mathop{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\mathsf{Mon}_{Y}}}(\mathcal{X}),\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0.1914,0.2422,0.2813}\sqsubseteq^{\mathrm{div}}_{\mathcal{G}}}})
is a
WQO

for every
WQO

Y
, the duals of orbit-finitely generated vector spaces are
also orbit-finitely generated. This is a well-known fact that finitely
generated vector spaces have finitely generated duals, and the extension to the
orbit-finite setting would find applications in solving orbit-finite linear
systems of equations and extend the results of
[
20
]
. It is already
known to hold for actions originating from the
homogeneous structures

considered in
Examples

8
and
9
, via the work of

[
43
,
20
,
47
]
.
