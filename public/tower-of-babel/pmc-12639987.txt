Female genital schistosomiasis presenting with nonspecific symptoms: diagnostic challenges of vulvar schistosomiasis in a resource-limited setting—a case report
Authors: Aweis Abdullahi Sheik, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Eid Mohamed Jama, Abdijalil Abdullahi Ali, Ahmed Muhammad Bashir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease caused by trematode parasites of the genus Schistosoma. Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS), most often due to S. haematobium, is underdiagnosed and rarely involves the vulva. Its nonspecific manifestations frequently cause misdiagnosis in resource-limited settings.Case presentationWe report a 27-year-old multiparous Somali woman with an 11-month history of recurrent vulvar itching, persistent lesions, dyspareunia, and vaginal discharge. She was initially misdiagnosed with vaginal candidiasis without improvement. Examination revealed a firm vulvar lesion with localized edema. Laboratory tests showed eosinophilia, hematuria, leukocyturia, and pyuria. Histopathology of a vulvar biopsy confirmed S. haematobium ova with granulomatous inflammation. The patient was treated with praziquantel (40 mg/kg), achieving full symptom resolution and normalization of tissue architecture on follow-up biopsy.ConclusionVulvar schistosomiasis is an uncommon FGS manifestation. This first documented Somali case underscores the importance of considering FGS in persistent vulvar lesions in endemic regions.

Introduction

Schistosomiasis, also known as bilharzia or snail fever, is a waterborne disease caused by the trematode parasite Schistosoma [1]. It presents acutely as swimmer’s itch or chronically with granulomatous inflammation to deposited ova.² Five species infect humans: S. haematobium, S. mansoni, S. japonicum, S. intercalatum, and S. mekongi, with S. haematobium and S. mansoni accounting for most cases in sub-Saharan Africa [2, 3].

Vulvar schistosomiasis is a rare form of female genital schistosomiasis, usually due to S. haematobium and less often S. mansoni [4]. While the cervix and vagina are more frequently affected, vulvar involvement is uncommon [2]. Clinical features are nonspecific, often misdiagnosed, and challenging in endemic regions due to overlap with other vulvar diseases [2, 5]. Histopathology remains the gold standard for diagnosis, and praziquantel is the treatment of choice [2].

We report a rare case of vulvar schistosomiasis in a young female, whose persistent clinical symptoms despite repeated antifungal therapy led to the correct diagnosis, representing, to our knowledge, the first documented case from Somalia and highlighting diagnostic challenges in resource-limited settings.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old multiparous woman (G7 P5) presented to our Obstetrics and Gynecology Department with a history of recurrent vulvar itching, a persistent vulvar lesion, odorless vaginal discharge, pain, and dyspareunia for more than 11 months. She had previously been diagnosed with vaginal candidiasis at another hospital, but her symptoms persisted despite treatment. A biopsy had not been performed at that time due to financial limitations and lack of access to specialized services. She had no known comorbidities and no history of allergies.

On further history, the patient reported that she had lived for the past two years in a town near Jowhar, Somalia, an area located along a river where freshwater exposure is common—an important risk factor for schistosomiasis.

On examination, there was localized edema with a firm vulvar lesion. No inguinal lymphadenopathy or systemic abnormalities were detected.

Laboratory investigations revealed eosinophilia, while other hematological and biochemical parameters were within normal limits. Urinalysis demonstrated leukocyturia, hematuria, and pyuria.

A vulvar skin biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of Schistosoma haematobium ova surrounded by granulomatous inflammation and marked inflammatory infiltrate. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of vulvar schistosomiasis was established. In accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, the patient was treated with praziquantel 40 mg/kg orally in a single day.

At the six-month follow-up, she reported complete resolution of symptoms. Repeat histopathological examination of the previously affected site showed resolution of active inflammation and absence of schistosome ova, consistent with effective praziquantel treatment; however, complete histologic restoration is not always expected, as residual fibrosis may persist following chronic infection.

Discussion

Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS) is a neglected manifestation of schistosomiasis with significant impact on women’s health in endemic regions. It is most often caused by Schistosoma haematobium; adult worms reside in the venous plexus of the urinary and genital tracts, while eggs deposited in adjacent tissues elicit granulomatous inflammation [6]. While the cervix and vagina are commonly affected, vulvar involvement is distinctly rare [2]. Our case illustrates this uncommon presentation, easily misdiagnosed in resource-limited settings.

Diagnosis of FGS is challenging because symptoms are often nonspecific and may mimic other genital infections [2]. Routine urinalysis and parasitological tests can yield false-negative results, especially in low-intensity infections [7, 8]. Therefore, a comprehensive diagnostic approach should include parasitological urinalysis and careful genital examination, with colposcopy when available [6]. Histopathology, although highly specific, is invasive and best used to confirm uncertain cases or when lesions raise concern for alternative diagnoses [6]. In our case, biopsy provided definitive confirmation by demonstrating S. haematobium ova with granulomatous inflammation.

Praziquantel remains the cornerstone of treatment, effective in symptom resolution and restoration of normal tissue architecture [2]. Adherence and follow-up are essential to ensure treatment success. Strengthening surveillance and incorporating FGS-focused diagnostic pathways are crucial in endemic settings, as timely identification and management can prevent complications and reduce missed cases [9, 10].

Although our patient was a Somali resident, this case has important implications for international travellers. Female genital schistosomiasis has been increasingly reported among women returning from endemic regions, including sub-Saharan Africa, with 38 documented traveller cases to date [11]. Recent reviews highlight that even a single freshwater exposure can lead to FGS, often with delayed and nonspecific presentations. In light of this, travellers visiting endemic areas such as Somalia are also at risk of acquiring FGS, underscoring the need for heightened awareness among clinicians evaluating returning travellers with vulvovaginal symptoms. Incorporating FGS into diagnostic considerations for this population can prevent delayed diagnosis, mismanagement, and long-term sequelae.

This case underscores the need for greater clinical awareness, better diagnostic access, and stronger public health strategies to ensure early detection and effective management of FGS in resource-limited endemic regions.

Conclusion

Vulvar schistosomiasis is a rare but clinically significant manifestation of FGS. Although FGS is generally underdiagnosed, vulvar involvement may be comparatively easier to recognize than cervical disease because lesions can be directly examined macroscopically without the need for colposcopy. Nevertheless, its nonspecific appearance still leads to frequent misdiagnosis. Our case highlights the value of histopathological confirmation in establishing a definitive diagnosis; however, despite its high specificity, it should not replace less-invasive diagnostic modalities such as urinalysis, PCR, or colposcopy—particularly in settings with elevated HIV acquisition risk, where minimizing invasive procedures is essential. The successful response to praziquantel therapy in this case demonstrates the effectiveness of early recognition and treatment. Complete regression of lesions is more likely when therapy is initiated promptly, whereas delayed treatment can result in irreversible tissue damage. Increased clinical awareness, incorporation of FGS into differential diagnoses, and improved access to affordable diagnostic modalities are essential for reducing underreporting and ensuring better health outcomes for women in endemic regions.
