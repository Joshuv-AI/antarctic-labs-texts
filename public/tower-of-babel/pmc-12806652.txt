Sex differences in efficacy/safety of anti-amyloid-beta monoclonal antibodies for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: SoHyeon Lim, Hyun-Woo Lee, Siyeon Yoon, Ji Won Han, Jae-Yong Chung, Seonghae Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia. AD exhibits notable sex-related disparities in prevalence, progression, and treatment response. With the recent approval of anti–amyloid-beta monoclonal antibodies—aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab—understanding sex differences in their clinical effects has become increasingly relevant. This review article investigates sex differences in the pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab and discusses the possible mechanism underlying the observed differences. Although sex-specific analyses were largely underreported in clinical trials, population pharmacokinetic models identified sex as a covariate affecting clearance and volume of distribution for aducanumab and lecanemab and higher exposure to lecanemab was predicted for females. Subgroup analyses of phase 3 trials revealed that males tended to experience greater benefit from aducanumab and lecanemab, whereas females showed better response to donanemab. The overall incidence of adverse events, including amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, did not show significant differences between sexes. Potential mechanisms underlying these differences include sex-related variations in blood-brain barrier permeability, apolipoprotein E4-associated neuroinflammatory responses, and baseline disease characteristics. These findings underscore the need for future AD clinical trials to incorporate sex-based analyses and to consider sex as a key factor in optimizing treatment strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Dementia is an acquired neurocognitive disorder that significantly impairs the ability to maintain daily living activities, often with a progressive course [1]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most prevalent form of dementia, accounts for 60–70% of all dementia cases [1]. Recent estimates suggest that approximately 32 million individuals worldwide have AD, and up to 416 million may be affected across the full AD continuum, including prodromal and preclinical stages [2].

AD is characterized by persistent cognitive decline, and pathologically, AD is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the brain [3]. The amyloid cascade hypothesis is the most widely accepted pathophysiologic model of AD. According to this hypothesis, sequential cleavage of amyloid precursor protein by β- and γ-secretase generates amyloid-beta peptides that aggregate into amyloid plaques, induce neurotoxicity and lead to neurodegeneration [45]. Based on this hypothesis, several therapeutic strategies have focused on targeting amyloid-beta accumulation. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that bind to aggregated amyloid-beta and facilitate its clearance (CL) from the brain have been developed as potential disease-modifying treatments. As of June 2025, 2 amyloid-beta-targeting drugs for Alzheimer’s disease—lecanemab (Leqembi®) and donanemab (Kisunla™)—have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), whereas aducanumab (Aduhelm®), which received accelerated approval in 2021, was withdrawn from the market in 2024.

Almost 65% of patients with AD are females and the risk of developing AD is known to be higher in women, even after adjusting for age, not attributable solely to their longer life span [6]. Clinical progression has also been reported to be more rapid in females, with exhibit greater tau burden [78]. Estrogen decline after menopause has been associated with altered brain metabolism and accelerated white matter degeneration [9]. Social factors such as disparities in education, healthcare access, and delayed diagnosis, may also contribute to the increased disease burden in females [9].

The ε4 allele of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is recognized as one of the most significant genetic risk factors for AD [10]. Across all age groups and genotypes, females have a higher risk of developing AD than males [11]. Notably, females with ε3/ε4 genotype demonstrate a 1.5-fold higher risk of AD compared with males with the same genotype [11]. This elevated risk in females may be attributed to APOE4 enhancing neutrophil–microglia interactions in females, amplifying neuroinflammatory responses and accelerating disease progression [12].

Before the introduction of amyloid-beta-targeting therapies, treatment of AD was primarily focused on managing the symptoms and involved cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs)—such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine—based on the cholinergic hypothesis, as well as the N-Methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist memantine, which targets glutamatergic excitotoxicity.

Females exhibited a higher area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) and maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) for memantine compared to males; however, these differences were no longer evident after dose-body weight correction [13]. Clinical efficacy outcome differed between sexes. Females tended to derive greater benefit from memantine in managing behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia [14], whereas males had a 73% higher likelihood of responding to ChEIs with cognitive improvement compared to females [15]. Adverse event (AE) profiles also differed between sexes. According to post-marketing surveillance data from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System up to 2024, females administrating memantine reported higher incidences of cardiac failure, cardiac arrest, and deep vein thrombosis, whereas bradycardia was reported more frequently in males [16].

Sex differences have been known in both the prevalence of AD and in the clinical responses to symptomatic treatments such as ChEIs and NMDA receptor antagonists, highlighting the importance of considering sex when optimizing treatment strategies for AD.

Given the potential for sex differences in response to recently approved anti-amyloid-beta mAbs, this review article aimed to investigate sex differences in the pharmacokinetics (PKs), efficacy, and safety of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab.

SEX DIFFERENCES IN ANTI-AMYLOID-BETA MONOCLONAL ANTIBODIES

Sex differences in preclinical studies

Sex differences in preclinical studies were reviewed based on the FDA non-clinical review for aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab [171819]. AUC and Cmax values stratified by sex were identified from toxicity studies in animals (Supplementary Table 1). In general, male animals showed higher systemic exposure compared to female animals. However, given the limited number of animals in each sex group, the statistical significance of the differences could not be determined. In aducanumab toxicology study, slight increases in the incidence and/or severity of meningeal and cerebral vascular inflammation and thickening were observed in female mice at mid and high dose; however, the lack of consistent dose dependency suggested that these findings were not clearly drug-related [17]. For lecanemab, increased spleen weight was observed only in female monkeys in the high dose group of the 39-week study, but these findings were resolved during the recovery period [18]. For all 3 drugs, sex differences in efficacy outcomes were not reported in preclinical studies.

Clinical trials evaluating sex differences

A literature review was conducted to evaluate the extent to which publications of clinical trial that reported sex differences in aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab. The search was performed in PubMed up to April 2025 using the following search terms: (“Aducanumab”[Title/Abstract] OR “Lecanemab”[Title/Abstract] OR “Donanemab”[Title/Abstract] OR “BIIB037”[Title/Abstract] OR “BAN2401”[Title/Abstract] OR “LY3002813”[Title/Abstract]) AND “Alzheimer*”[Title/Abstract]. The search was limited to English-language articles with available full text and included only original clinical trial data. Additional references not identified through database searches were manually added through a review of relevant bibliographies.

A total of 82 records were initially identified. Each article was assessed for the presence of sex-stratified analyses in at least one of the following domains: PKs, clinical efficacy, or safety outcomes. Based on the selection criteria, 26 clinical trial publications were included. Of these, sex subgroup analyses were presented in 2 out of 10 studies for aducanumab, and in one out of 8 for each of lecanemab and donanemab (Table 1). Most clinical trials enrolled predominantly White participants, and the proportion of female was slightly higher than males (Supplementary Table 2). None of the clinical trials reported sex-stratified analyses for PK parameters, and only one study for each drug provided subgroup analyses for efficacy, while safety data stratified by sex were reported in only one aducanumab trial. This limited reporting is in contrast to the well-documented research findings on sex differences in the prevalence and progression of AD [67891112], although evidence regarding differences in treatment response remains inconclusive.

Across all trials, the majority of participants were White, and the donanemab trial enrolled over 90% White individuals [202122]. The baseline cognitive status varied across the clinical trials. Participants in the donanemab trial showed lower Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores and higher baseline scores on both the Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog), indicating more advanced cognitive impairment [202122]. Sex was identified as a covariate affecting total CL and central volume of distribution (V1) for both aducanumab and lecanemab, and additionally the peripheral volume of distribution (V2) for aducanumab [2324].

The population PK model for aducanumab showed higher CL, V1, and V2 in males compared to females, with increases of 13.4%, 14.6%, and 12.9%, respectively [23]. In the case of lecanemab, CL and V1 were found to be lower in females compared to males, with female-to-male ratios of 0.792 and 0.893, respectively [24]. As estimated by population PK model simulations, drug exposure of lecanemab was higher in females, with simulated AUC and Cmax values increased by 26% and 20%, respectively [25]. Notably, the 90% confidence interval for the simulated female-to-male AUC ratio (1.23–1.30) did not include 1.0, suggesting a statistically significant difference in exposure between males and females [25]. In contrast, for donanemab, although sex was evaluated as a potential covariate, it was not included in the final population PK model [26].

All phase 3 clinical trials were conducted as randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies [202122]. The primary endpoint of the aducanumab and lecanemab phase 3 trials was the change in the CDR-SB from baseline, whereas the donanemab trial used the change in the integrated Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) from baseline as the primary endpoint [202122]. Notably, the phase 3 trial of donanemab, TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, was designed to switch participants to placebo in a blinded procedure if the amyloid plaque level was less than 11 centiloids on any single PET scan or between 11 and 25 centiloids on 2 consecutive PET scans—thresholds corresponding to amyloid PET negativity [22].

In the phase 3 trials of aducanumab (EMERGE and ENGAGE), males showed greater cognitive benefit than females across multiple endpoints. For the primary endpoint, the placebo-adjusted changes from baseline in CDR-SB were −0.57 (EMERGE) and −0.21 (ENGAGE) in males, whereas the corresponding values for females were −0.21 and +0.21, respectively, indicating minimal or no benefit in females [21]. Similar trends were noted in other cognitive assessments, including ADAS-Cog13 and MMSE [21]. In contrast, functional outcomes assessed by Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study–Activities of Daily Living Scale for use in Mild Cognitive Impairment (ADCS-ADL-MCI) showed inconsistent results, with males exhibiting greater improvement in EMERGE and females showing slightly more benefit in ENGAGE [21].

The results of the phase 3 trial of lecanemab (CLARITY AD) also indicated a higher efficacy in males. For the primary endpoint, CDR-SB, males had the placebo-adjusted change from baseline of −0.73, whereas females had a smaller treatment effect of −0.20 [20]. Other endpoints, including ADAS-Cog14, Alzheimer’s Disease Composite Score, and ADCS-ADL-MCI, also demonstrated numerically greater benefits in males compared to females, suggesting consistent sex differences across both cognitive and functional domains [20]. Consistently, the supplementary forest plots of CLARITY AD.

In contrast to aducanumab and lecanemab, the phase 3 trial of donanemab (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2) demonstrated better efficacy in females. For the primary endpoint, iADRS, females showed greater improvement than males. In the low/medium tau subgroup, the changes were 3.38 in females and 3.15 in males; in the combined group, which included participants with low/medium and high tau, the changes were 3.51 in females and 2.09 in males [22]. A similar trend of greater benefit in females was also observed in CDR-SB [22].

For aducanumab (EMERGE and ENGAGE), the incidence of ARIA of edema/effusions (ARIA-E) and ARIA of microhemorrhages and hemosiderin deposits (ARIA-H) was comparable in females and males [27], and hazard ratio analyses showed no significant effect of sex on the risk of ARIA [28]. However, superficial siderosis occurred nearly twice as frequently in males (18.9%) as in females (10.7%) [27]. In the CLARITY AD of lecanemab, the incidence of both ARIA-E and ARIA-H was comparable between sexes [29]. Headaches were more frequently reported in females, whereas infusion-related reactions were more commonly observed in males, occurring in 28% of males and 24% of females [29]. Donanemab in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 also showed a similar incidence of ARIA and other AEs between females and males [30].

DISCUSSION

Sex differences were observed in the response of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab. Drugs undergo the processes of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, and the resulting plasma concentration—which represents drug exposure—can vary among individuals. After administration of an amyloid-beta-targeting drug, it reaches the brain by passing through the blood-brain barrier (BBB) via blood circulation and exhibits its effect through target binding. Drug responses, such as efficacy and the incidence of AEs, may vary depending on the concentration at the target site or the degree of systemic exposure. Sex differences at these various stages may ultimately result in differences in drug efficacy and AEs between sexes. (Fig. 1).

In preclinical studies, systemic exposure was generally higher in males than in females. Although clinical trials did not report sex-stratified analyses of PK parameters, population PK models identified sex as a covariate influencing CL and volume of distribution (V) for aducanumab and lecanemab. Efficacy analyses showed that males tended to demonstrate greater benefit from aducanumab and lecanemab, whereas females showed greater improvement with donanemab. The incidence of AEs, including ARIA, was comparable between males and females.

Sex was included as a covariate for CL in the population PK models of aducanumab and lecanemab, similar to other mAbs showing lower CL in females. Sex was identified as a commonly retained covariate affecting CL in population PK models of mAbs in oncology, such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and bevacizumab [31]. In these models, CL was consistently lower in females than in males, with a median reduction ranging from 13% to 23% [31]. These PK properties of mAbs are consistent with findings from the population PK models of aducanumab and lecanemab, in which CL was estimated to be 13.4% and 20.8% lower in females, respectively [2324].

Although the covariate effects of sex on the PK parameters of aducanumab and lecanemab fall within the 80–125% [2324], which is generally considered not clinically significant, this range is not based on an established exposure–response relationship. The clinical relevance of these effects on efficacy and safety requires further evaluation through exposure–response analyses.

The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-to-plasma ratios of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab were approximately 0.5% [32], 0.29% [33], and 0.17% [34], respectively, slightly exceeding the reported CSF-to-serum Immunoglobulin G ratios of 0.1–0.2% in humans [3536].

Males have been reported to show higher CSF neurofilament light chain levels and albumin quotients [37], while females demonstrate 7–18% lower Ktrans values [38], indicating greater BBB permeability in males than in females. Such sex differences in BBB integrity may contribute to variability in target site exposure and treatment response to amyloid-beta-targeting drug (Fig. 1).

In addition, sex-specific neuroinflammatory responses associated with the APOE4 genotype may also contribute to variability in treatment response. Although the overall carrier frequency of APOE4 is comparable between males and females [39], previous studies have suggested that APOE4 enhances neutrophil–microglia interactions more prominently in females, characterized by elevated interleukin-17 signaling and immunosuppressive microglial phenotypes [12]. These changes amplify neuroinflammation and accelerate disease progression [12], which could potentially influence the effectiveness of amyloid-beta-targeting therapies, especially in APOE4-positive females.

In the CLARITY AD trial, subgroup analyses of lecanemab showed sex differences in the effect, with a 31% difference observed on the CDR-SB. Simulation analyses estimated that such a difference would arise by chance in only 0.12% of cases, suggesting that the observed sex differences are unlikely to be random [40]. A Bayesian reanalysis of the same dataset showed moderate evidence of a smaller treatment effect in women than in men, with a maximum Bayes factor of 5.97, indicating that the presence of a sex difference is nearly 6 times more likely than its absence [41]. Although these findings do not demonstrate a lack of efficacy in females, they suggest that lecanemab may have lower effectiveness in females than in males. In contrast, the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial of donanemab showed greater improvements in iADRS and CDR-SB in females compared with males [22], but the Bayesian analysis found no significant evidence supporting a sex difference in treatment effect [41]. The observed differences in sex-specific clinical efficacy across these 3 drugs suggest a possibility that it originates from the mechanistic differences, where these drugs target distinct forms of amyloid-beta aggregates (e.g., protofibrils, fibrils, and established plaques).

Although sex differences were sparsely reported in regulatory reviews, the following descriptions were documented across the reviews of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab. According to the FDA statistical review of aducanumab, the high-dose aducanumab showed minimal effect on CDR-SB at week 78 in females in both the EMERGE and ENGAGE trials [42]. In CLARITY AD, although a 31% sex difference was observed in the primary endpoint (CDR-SB), the FDA statistical review did not highlight this finding [43]. Additionally, in the statistical review of donanemab, the mean amyloid difference at week 76 was reported to be smaller in females than in males compared to placebo [44].

Interestingly, despite greater amyloid reduction observed in males, the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial of donanemab showed greater improvements in iADRS and CDR-SB in females. The phase 3 clinical trial of donanemab was designed to discontinue treatment and switch to a blinded placebo after achieving baseline amyloid CL [22]. If there were differences in the rate of amyloid CL between males and females, it is possible that differences in the actual duration of drug exposure may have occurred. Given that females showed smaller reductions in amyloid levels, it is possible that they received donanemab for a longer period, which may have contributed to the observed treatment effect. However, data on switch rates or mean treatment duration by sex were not reported, limiting further interpretation.

This study has several limitations. First, heterogeneity in study design, endpoints, and statistical methodologies across trials limited direct comparisons between drugs. Second, since there was no direct comparative data based on sex, it was difficult to evaluate the difference in exposure, and sex differences could only be predicted based on data simulated from the population PK model. Additionally, none of the clinical trials reported baseline demographic or disease characteristics stratified by sex. If males and females differed in disease severity or other clinical variables at baseline, such imbalances may have confounded the interpretation of sex differences in outcomes and treatment response. Finally, this review did not address potential sex differences in amyloid and tau CL, or changes in other CSF and plasma biomarkers across these trials. Although such biomarker responses could provide insights into the mechanisms underlying sex differences in treatment effects, they were beyond the scope of this review and warrant further investigation.

Although aducanumab and lecanemab showed slightly lower efficacy in females than in males, and donanemab showed greater improvement in females, none of these 3 drugs have demonstrated clinically established sex differences in efficacy. This review investigated potential factors underlying these sex differences from various aspects. Future clinical trials of amyloid-beta-targeting drugs should incorporate study designs that account for sex and prespecified subgroup analyses to better evaluate and understand sex differences.
