FLRT2 promotes foam cell formation and atherosclerosis by inhibiting USP22-mediated ABCA1 deubiquitination
Authors: Jing-quan Zhou, Hui-xuan Zhang, Li-zhen Guo, Jie Wang, Yan-yu Chen, En-dong Jiang, Juan Peng, Hong Gao, Yuan-hong Sun, Ming-yan Zhu, Jian-ye Peng, Gao-feng Zeng, Yuan-ye Dang, Kong-yang Ma, Xiao-yan Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the accumulation of foam cells within the arterial wall. Despite substantial progress, the molecular mechanisms governing foam cell formation remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify fibronectin leucine-rich transmembrane protein 2 (FLRT2) as a previously unrecognized regulator of macrophage-driven atherogenesis through the facilitation of foam cell formation. FLRT2 expression was markedly elevated in macrophages within both murine and human atherosclerotic plaques. Functionally, adenovirus-mediated FLRT2 overexpression aggravated atherosclerotic lesion development in Apoe−/− mice, whereas macrophage-specific Flrt2 deletion significantly reduced the plaque burden in both AAV8-PCSK9-injected and Apoe−/− atherosclerotic models. Mechanistically, the loss of Flrt2 attenuated local inflammatory responses, as indicated by lower proportions of proinflammatory Ly6Chi monocytes in the peritoneal cavity and bone marrow and fewer M1 macrophages in the spleen. Furthermore, FLRT2 promoted foam cell formation by suppressing the cholesterol efflux transporter ABCA1 at the post-transcriptional level. We demonstrate that FLRT2 enhances ABCA1 ubiquitin‒proteasome degradation by inhibiting USP22-mediated deubiquitination. Collectively, our findings identify FLRT2 as a key regulator of macrophage lipid metabolism and inflammation, revealing a novel FLRT2–USP22–ABCA1 axis that promotes foam cell formation and accelerates atherosclerosis. Therefore, targeting FLRT2 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.Keywords: atherosclerosis, macrophage, foam cell, FLRT2, ABCA1, USP22

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial wall that underlies ischemic cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and peripheral artery disease [1]. Atherosclerosis is characterized by the deposition of lipids, predominantly cholesterol, within macrophages residing in medium- and large-sized arteries. As atherosclerosis progresses, circulating monocytes infiltrate the subendothelial space and differentiate into macrophages, which internalize oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) via scavenger receptors, thereby transforming into lipid-laden foam cells [2, 3]. These foam cells constitute a hallmark of early atherogenesis and actively drive plaque progression and instability. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms that regulate macrophage foam cell formation is therefore essential for developing targeted therapies against atherosclerosis.

Fibronectin leucine-rich transmembrane protein 2 (FLRT2) belongs to a small family of transmembrane proteins (FLRT1–3) that were first identified as extracellular matrix-associated molecules [4]. Although FLRT2 has been implicated in tumorigenesis, neurodevelopment, embryogenesis, and cardiovascular development [5–11]. its role in vascular pathology remains poorly defined. Recent evidence has linked FLRT2 to angiogenesis, vascular aging [12, 13]. and ischemic stroke susceptibility [14]. suggesting its potential involvement in vascular disease. Additionally, our previous work demonstrated that FLRT2 promotes monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation through the UNC5B-Akt/mTOR signaling pathway [15]. indicating that FLRT2 plays a regulatory role in macrophage biology. Notably, single-cell RNA sequencing of the lungs of Mycobacterium tuberculosis-infected mice revealed a macrophage subset characterized by high Plin2 expression and a foamy transcriptional profile, in which Flrt2 emerged as a prominent marker [16]. These findings suggest the intriguing possibility that FLRT2 contributes to lipid handling by macrophages and foam cell formation.

Here, we identify FLRT2 as a critical regulator of foam cell formation that is upregulated in macrophages during atherogenesis in both human and murine plaques. Functional studies revealed that FLRT2 promotes atherosclerotic lesion formation by increasing macrophage accumulation and local inflammation, whereas macrophage-specific Flrt2 deficiency attenuates plaque development. Mechanistically, FLRT2 facilitates foam cell formation by accelerating ABCA1 protein degradation via inhibition of USP22-dependent ABCA1 deubiquitination. Collectively, these findings identify FLRT2 as a critical regulator of macrophage cholesterol homeostasis and atherosclerosis, providing a compelling rationale for targeting FLRT2 in therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Detailed methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Human normal artery and atherosclerotic plaque specimens

All procedures involving human tissues were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University of South China. Normal arterial tissue and atherosclerotic plaque samples were obtained from autopsies provided by the Forensic Identification Center of the University of South China. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animals

Apoe−/− mice, C57BL/6 J mice, Lyz2Cre mice, and Flrt2fl/fl mice were purchased from Shanghai Southern Model Organisms. Macrophage-specific Flrt2 knockout (Flrt2Mac-KO) mice were generated by crossing Flrt2fl/fl mice with Lyz2Cre mice [15]. and Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice were generated by crossing Flrt2Mac-KO mice with Apoe−/− mice. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 J background. All animal procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Center of Guangzhou Medical University (protocol No. GY2019-016) and conducted in the SPF-level animal facility of the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangzhou Medical University. The breeding environment was maintained at 22–26 °C with a relative humidity of 40%–70% and a 12-h light/dark cycle.

To investigate the role of macrophage FLRT2 in atherosclerosis, three animal models were used: (1) Eight-week-old male Apoe−/− mice were randomly divided into two groups and intravenously injected with 100 μL of adenovirus (1 × 10⁹ PFU/mL). One group received a control virus, and the other received an adenovirus expressing the human FLRT2 gene (Shandong Vegen Biotechnology, Shandong, China). Four weeks after the initial adenoviral injection, a second dose was administered. All the mice were fed a Western diet (Research Diet, USA) for 8 weeks. (2) Eight-week-old male Flrt2fl/fl and Flrt2Mac-KO mice were intravenously injected with AAV8-PCSK9D377Y (5 × 10¹¹ viral units; Shandong Vegen Biotechnology, Shandong, China) via the tail vein and subsequently fed a Western diet for 12 weeks. (3) Eight-week-old male Apoe−/− Flrt2fl/fl and Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice were fed a Western diet for 8 weeks.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were first assessed for normality. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test, whereas comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Comparisons involving two independent variables were conducted using two-way ANOVA. For data that did not meet the assumption of normality, nonparametric tests, including the Mann–Whitney U test, were applied. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Results

FLRT2 is upregulated in macrophages during atherogenesis

To identify novel regulatory genes involved in atherogenesis, we first intersected the sets of upregulated genes from five independent datasets in which monocytes/macrophages were treated with ox-LDL, a major pathogenic factor in atherosclerosis. This analysis revealed a single overlapping gene, FLRT2 (Fig. S1a). We next analysed the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE155513 derived from atherosclerotic plaques of Ldlr−/− mice fed a Western diet. Following dimensionality reduction, clustering, cell-type annotation, and extraction of the macrophage population, we observed a time-dependent increase in Flrt2 expression specifically within macrophages (Fig. S1b, c).

In agreement with these observations, immunofluorescence staining of both human and mouse plaque specimens revealed a marked increase in FLRT2 signal intensity, which colocalized with the macrophage marker CD68, compared with that in controls (Fig. 1a, b). In vitro, FLRT2 protein expression significantly increased in primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from C57BL/6 J mice treated with ox-LDL (50 μg/mL for 24 h) (Fig. 1c). Similarly, compared with those from chow diet-fed control mice, primary peritoneal macrophages (PMs) isolated from Western diet-fed Apoe−/− mice displayed substantially higher levels of FLRT2 protein (Fig. 1d).

Together, these findings demonstrate that FLRT2 expression is consistently upregulated in macrophages during atherogenesis in both human and murine settings.

FLRT2 overexpression aggravates atherosclerosis in Apoe−/− mice

To assess the functional role of FLRT2 in atherogenesis, Apoe−/− mice were intravenously injected via the tail vein with either an adenovirus overexpressing human FLRT2 (Ad-hFLRT2) or a control adenovirus (Ad-Vector), followed by 8 weeks of Western diet (WD) feeding to induce atherosclerosis (Fig. 2a). En face Oil Red O (ORO) staining of the aorta revealed a markedly larger atherosclerotic lesion area in Ad-hFLRT2-treated mice than in control mice (Fig. 2b). Consistently, FLRT2 overexpression resulted in a significant increase in plaque burden, as indicated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and ORO staining of the aortic root (Fig. 2c, d), indicating that FLRT2 promotes lesion formation. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that FLRT2 overexpression significantly increased macrophage infiltration within the plaques, whereas no notable changes in the vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) distribution were observed (Fig. 2e, f). Moreover, no significant differences in body weight, plasma triglyceride (TG) level, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) level were detected between the two groups (Fig. S2a, b). However, plasma total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were elevated in mice injected with Ad-hFLRT2 (Fig. S2b).

Macrophage-specific Flrt2 deletion attenuates atherosclerosis in AAV8-PCSK9-injected mice

To evaluate the specific role of macrophage-derived FLRT2 in atherogenesis, macrophage-specific Flrt2 knockout mice (Flrt2Mac-KO) were generated by crossing Flrt2fl/fl mice with Lyz2Cre transgenic mice, as previously described [15]. Atherosclerosis was induced in both Flrt2fl/fl and Flrt2Mac-KO mice by injection of a PCSK9-expressing adeno-associated virus (AAV8-PCSK9), followed by 12 weeks of Western diet feeding (Fig. 3a). As expected, compared with Flrt2Mac-KO mice, Flrt2fl/fl mice developed substantially more extensive atherosclerotic lesions, as indicated by in situ imaging of the aortic arch (Fig. 3b) and en face ORO staining of the whole aorta (Fig. 3c). However, macrophage Flrt2 deficiency significantly reduced the plaque area in the aortic root, as demonstrated by H&E and ORO staining (Fig. 3d, e). Notably, despite reducing lesion size, macrophage-specific Flrt2 deletion did not alter the collagen content within plaques (Fig. 3f), suggesting that fibrotic remodeling was not affected. Immunofluorescence staining revealed decreased macrophage infiltration (F4/80+ area) in Flrt2Mac-KO mice, whereas the VSMC distribution (α-SMA+ area) remained unchanged (Fig. 3g, h). No significant differences in body weight, TG, TC, HDL-C, or LDL-C were detected between the two groups (Fig. S3a, b). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that macrophage-specific Flrt2 deletion mitigates atherosclerosis development in AAV8-PCSK9-injected mice.

Macrophage-specific Flrt2 deficiency suppresses atherosclerosis in Apoe−/− mice

To further validate the role of macrophage FLRT2 in atherosclerosis, we established an independent mouse model by crossing Apoe−/− mice with Flrt2fl/fl Lyz2Cre mice to generate Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO and Apoe−/− Flrt2fl/fl mice. Both groups were fed a Western diet for 8 weeks to induce atherosclerosis (Fig. 4a). Consistent with our previous findings, Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice exhibited fewer atherosclerotic plaque areas than their Apoe−/− Flrt2fl/fl counterparts did, as shown by representative in situ images of the aortic arch (Fig. 4b) and en face ORO staining of the aorta (Fig. 4c). Quantitative analysis of atherosclerotic lesion areas in aortic root sections also revealed significantly reduced plaque size in Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice (Fig. 4d). Similar results were observed in ORO-stained cross-sections of the aortic root (Fig. 4e). Despite these reductions in plaque burden, the collagen content within the plaques remained unchanged between the two groups (Fig. 4f), indicating that macrophage Flrt2 deficiency inhibits atherosclerosis formation without affecting fibrotic remodeling. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of cross-sections revealed that Flrt2 deletion in macrophages reduced macrophage infiltration (F4/80+ cells), whereas the VSMC content, marked by α-SMA, remained unaffected (Fig. 4g, h). Importantly, no significant differences in metabolic parameters, including body weight, TG, TC, HDL-C, or LDL-C, were identified between Apoe−/− Flrt2fl/fl and Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice (Fig. S4a, b), excluding systemic metabolic effects as confounding factors. Together, these results confirm that macrophage-specific Flrt2 deficiency consistently attenuates atherosclerotic development.

Macrophage-specific Flrt2 deficiency ameliorates inflammation in mice

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by persistent immune activation. To determine whether macrophage-specific Flrt2 deficiency alters inflammatory status, we collected peritoneal lavage fluid, bone marrow cell suspensions, and splenic cell suspensions from Flrt2fl/fl and Flrt2Mac-KO mice following AAV8-PCSK9 injection and 8 weeks of Western diet feeding. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a marked reduction in the proportion of proinflammatory Ly6Chi monocytes in the peritoneal cavity of Flrt2Mac-KO mice compared with that in the peritoneal cavity of control mice (Fig. 5a). A similar decrease in Ly6Chi monocytes was observed in the bone marrow (Fig. 5b). Moreover, the proportion of M1-polarized macrophages in the spleen was significantly lower in macrophage-specific Flrt2-deficient mice than in control mice (Fig. 5c). Immunofluorescence staining of aortic lesions further revealed reduced IL-1β staining in Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice compared with control mice (Fig. 5d). Together, these data indicate that macrophage Flrt2 deficiency attenuates local inflammation within developing atherosclerotic lesions.

We also collected plasma samples from two independent mouse cohorts: (1) Flrt2fl/fl and Flrt2Mac-KO mice injected with AAV8-PCSK9 and fed a Western diet for 8 weeks and (2) Apoe−/− Flrt2fl/fl and Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice fed a Western diet for 8 weeks. Flow cytometric quantification of circulating proinflammatory cytokines (CXCL1, IL-18, IL-23, IL-12p70, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-12p40, and IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β1, CCL22, IL-10, G-CSF, and CCL17) revealed no significant differences between genotypes in either model (Fig. 5e, f), indicating that systemic inflammatory cytokine profiles were not affected by Flrt2 deletion.

Finally, to test whether Flrt2 directly influences macrophage polarization, we stimulated macrophages in vitro. Upon LPS challenge, Flrt2 deficiency significantly blunted the induction of the M1 marker iNOS (Fig. S5a), whereas the IL-4-driven expression of the M2 marker Ym1 was unchanged (Fig. S5b).

Taken together, these findings suggest that although macrophage-specific Flrt2 deletion does not alter systemic cytokine levels, this deletion attenuates local inflammatory responses by reducing proinflammatory monocyte and macrophage populations within key immune compartments. This localized immunomodulation likely contributes to the atheroprotective phenotype observed in Flrt2Mac-KO mice.

FLRT2 promotes foam cell formation by inhibiting ABCA1 expression

Macrophage-derived foam cell formation is pivotal for the development of atherosclerosis [17, 18]. To determine whether FLRT2 affects this process, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and peritoneal macrophages (PMs) were infected with Ad-Vector or Ad-mFLRT2 and subsequently exposed to ox-LDL. The results revealed that FLRT2 overexpression markedly increased ox-LDL-induced lipid accumulation in both BMDMs and PMs (Fig. S6a and Fig. 6a, b). Conversely, siRNA-mediated silencing of FLRT2 significantly reduced lipid deposition in response to ox-LDL in both cell types (Fig. S6b and Fig. 6c, d), indicating that FLRT2 facilitates foam cell formation in macrophages.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism involved, we assessed the expression of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1), a critical mediator of cholesterol efflux. Upon ox-LDL stimulation, ABCA1 protein levels were substantially decreased in FLRT2-overexpressing macrophages, whereas FLRT2 knockdown resulted in a marked increase in ABCA1 protein levels in both PMs and BMDMs (Fig. 6e–h). Notably, ABCA1 mRNA expression remained unchanged following either FLRT2 overexpression or silencing, regardless of ox-LDL treatment (Fig. S7a, b), indicating that FLRT2 modulates ABCA1 at the post-transcriptional level. Other key cholesterol transporters and scavenger receptors (ABCG1, SR-A, and CD36) were unaffected by FLRT2 modulation (Fig. S8a–d). Collectively, these data indicate that FLRT2 facilitates macrophage foam cell formation at least in part by suppressing ABCA1 protein levels.

FLRT2 promotes ABCA1 degradation by suppressing USP22-mediated ABCA1 deubiquitination

Given that FLRT2 affects ABCA1 protein levels but not mRNA levels, we first examined whether FLRT2 regulates ABCA1 protein stability. Cycloheximide (CHX) was used to inhibit protein synthesis. The data demonstrated that FLRT2 overexpression markedly accelerated ABCA1 protein degradation (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, the proteasome inhibitor MG-132, but not the lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), restored the reduction in ABCA1 protein levels induced by FLRT2 overexpression (Fig. 7b), indicating that FLRT2 promotes ABCA1 degradation through the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway.

To identify the enzymes responsible for ABCA1 degradation, we used the UbiBrowser 2.0 database to predict candidate ubiquitin-modifying enzymes (UBs and DUBs) involved in ubiquitination or deubiquitination (Fig. 7c). By cross-referencing these candidates with FLRT2-interacting proteins identified by mass spectrometry in FLRT2-overexpressing HEK293T cells, ubiquitin-specific protease 22 (USP22) was revealed as a potential regulator of ABCA1 stability (Fig. 7d). Protein‒protein docking analysis using the ClusPro 2.0 platform, which is based on AlphaFold-predicted structures, revealed a stable binding conformation between FLRT2 and USP22, with a docking score of –1693.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 7e). Confocal immunofluorescence further confirmed the colocalization of FLRT2 and USP22 (Fig. 7f). Notably, FLRT2 silencing significantly increased ABCA1 protein levels in PMs, whereas USP22 knockdown partially reversed this effect (Fig. S9 and Fig. 7g). Consistent with the altered ABCA1 stability, the decrease in foam cell formation caused by FLRT2 knockdown (ORO staining) was abrogated when USP22 was simultaneously silenced (Fig. 7h). These results suggest that USP22 is a critical effector of the impact of FLRT2 on ABCA1 and foam cell formation.

Next, we investigated how USP22 regulates ABCA1 stability. The results demonstrated that USP22 depletion did not affect ABCA1 mRNA levels but decreased its protein levels (Fig. 7i, j). Treatment with MG-132 partially reversed the reduction in ABCA1 protein caused by USP22 knockdown (Fig. 7k), indicating the involvement of proteasome-mediated ABCA1 degradation. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments demonstrated an interaction between ABCA1 and USP22 upon ox-LDL stimulation (Fig. 7l, m). Moreover, ubiquitination assays revealed that USP22 knockdown increased ABCA1 ubiquitination in PMs (Fig. 7n). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that FLRT2 reduces ABCA1 protein stability by inhibiting USP22-mediated ABCA1 deubiquitination.

Finally, we investigated how FLRT2 modulates USP22 function. FLRT2 expression did not alter USP22 protein abundance (Fig. S10a, b), but Co-IP experiments revealed that FLRT2 disrupted the USP22–ABCA1 interaction: FLRT2 overexpression reduced USP22 recruitment to ABCA1, whereas FLRT2 deficiency increased USP22–ABCA1 binding (Fig. S10c, d). Therefore, FLRT2 promotes ABCA1 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation by sterically or functionally interfering with the ability of USP22 to access ABCA1, thereby promoting macrophage lipid retention and foam cell formation.

Discussion

In this study, we identified FLRT2 as a novel regulator of atherosclerosis through its role in macrophage function. We demonstrated that FLRT2 expression is significantly upregulated in macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques and is further induced by common atherogenic stimuli. Functional studies using an Apoe−/− mouse model revealed that overexpression of FLRT2 exacerbates atherosclerotic lesion development, whereas macrophage-specific deletion of Flrt2 significantly attenuates plaque formation. Notably, the loss of FLRT2 also reduced inflammatory responses in the peritoneum, bone marrow, and spleen, suggesting a broader immunomodulatory role. Mechanistically, FLRT2 accelerated ABCA1 degradation by reducing the stabilization of ABCA1 in macrophages by USP22, thereby promoting foam cell formation, a key event in atherogenesis. Together, these findings establish macrophage FLRT2 as a previously unrecognized contributor to atherosclerosis, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of this disease (Fig. 8).

Graphical abstract.

In response to atherosclerotic stimuli, macrophage FLRT2 is markedly upregulated and interacts with the deubiquitinase USP22, thereby competitively reducing USP22 binding to the cholesterol efflux transporter ABCA1. Consequently, ABCA1 undergoes enhanced ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, leading to impaired cholesterol efflux, increased foam cell formation, and accelerated atherosclerosis development.

Recent research has implicated FLRT2 in angiogenesis and vascular aging [12, 13]. Epigenetic studies have identified FLRT2 as a gene associated with ischemic stroke [14]. However, the contribution of FLRT2, especially macrophage-derived FLRT2, to atherosclerosis has not been previously explored. Our study fills this critical knowledge gap. We previously showed that FLRT2 functions as an endogenous regulator of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation [15]. Specifically, the interaction between the extracellular domain (ECD) of FLRT2 and its receptor UNC5B activates the Akt/mTOR pathway, thereby promoting monocyte/macrophage development [15]. Given the role of FLRT2 in macrophage differentiation, investigating whether FLRT2 also influences macrophage function and, in turn, atherogenesis is logical. To explore this, we developed three complementary mouse models of atherosclerosis: (1) an Apoe−/− model with adenoviral overexpression of FLRT2 plus an 8-week Western diet; (2) Flrt2fl/fl and Flrt2Mac-KO mice treated with AAV8-PCSK9 and fed a Western diet for 12 weeks; and (3) Apoe−/− Flrt2Mac-KO mice fed a Western diet for 8 weeks. All three models consistently demonstrated that macrophage-specific FLRT2 promotes atherosclerotic lesion development, identifying macrophage FLRT2 as a novel factor associated with atherosclerosis. Importantly, the dyslipidemia observed in the Ad-hFLRT2 overexpression model was absent in the macrophage-specific knockout models, suggesting that the systemic lipid changes reflect FLRT2 activity in nonmyeloid tissues rather than a macrophage-intrinsic mechanism. Together, these results identify macrophage-derived FLRT2 as a novel, cell-autonomous promoter of atherogenesis and highlight that FLRT2 activity outside the myeloid compartment can perturb systemic lipid homeostasis.

Foam cell formation, especially from macrophages, is a hallmark of early atherosclerotic lesions and plays a critical role in disease progression [19, 20]. To assess the role of FLRT2 in this process, we manipulated FLRT2 expression in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and peritoneal macrophages (PMs). Overexpression of FLRT2 via adenoviral infection significantly increased ox-LDL-induced lipid accumulation, whereas FLRT2 knockdown using siRNA reduced lipid buildup in both cell types. These findings suggest that FLRT2 promotes foam cell formation in macrophages. Although statins are effective lipid-lowering agents, 1.5%–3% of users in randomized clinical trials and up to 10%–13% in observational studies experience myalgia [21–23]. These limitations highlight the need for alternative, more targeted lipid-lowering strategies. The discovery of FLRT2 as a negative regulator of ABCA1 opens a new molecular avenue for the development of such precision therapies for atherosclerosis management.

We further reported that FLRT2 inhibits the expression of ABCA1, a membrane-associated ATP-binding cassette transporter that facilitates cholesterol and phospholipid efflux to apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I) and is essential for reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) [24, 25]. ABCA1 is highly expressed in macrophages and in the liver, brain, placenta, kidneys, small intestine, and vascular endothelium and is central to maintaining cholesterol homeostasis. Additionally, ABCG1 complements this function by transporting cholesterol to mature HDL particles, further promoting RCT [26–29]. Together, ABCA1 and ABCG1 are critical for preventing cholesterol accumulation in macrophages and the development of foam cells [30–32]. Importantly, ABCA1 is tightly regulated at the post-translational level through ubiquitination and deubiquitination, which critically influence its stability and function. Several E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinases (DUBs) have recently been identified as modulators of ABCA1 turnover in diverse pathological contexts. Listerin E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (Listerin) stabilizes ABCA1 by catalyzing K63-linked polyubiquitination at lysine residues K1884 and K1957, thereby preventing ESCRT-mediated lysosomal degradation induced by oxidized LDL and promoting cholesterol efflux in macrophages [33]. In contrast, other E3 ligases such as HUWE1, HECTD1, and UBE3A act as negative regulators. HUWE1 promotes ABCA1 ubiquitination and degradation, whereas its inhibition reduces ABCA1 ubiquitination, enhances cholesterol efflux, and suppresses CD4⁺ T-cell activation in Sjögren’s syndrome [34]. Similarly, knockdown of HECTD1 increases ABCA1 protein levels and cholesterol export in macrophages [35]. whereas UBE3A stimulates ABCA1 ubiquitination and degradation in lipid-loaded brain macrophages, thereby driving foam cell formation and inflammation [36]. Conversely, the deubiquitinase USP22 functions protectively by stabilizing PPARγ, which promotes macrophage efferocytosis and cholesterol metabolism, thereby alleviating atherosclerosis progression [37]. In this study, we demonstrated that FLRT2 overexpression accelerated ABCA1 degradation, an effect that was abrogated by the proteasome inhibitor MG-132. These findings indicate that FLRT2 facilitates ABCA1 degradation through the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway. To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, we combined bioinformatics analysis, immunoprecipitation‒mass spectrometry (IP‒MS), and molecular docking, which collectively identified USP22, a deubiquitinating enzyme, as a direct binding partner of FLRT2. Notably, USP22 expression was downregulated in atherosclerotic mouse models and has been reported to regulate diverse cellular processes through its deubiquitinase activity [37–39]. We further revealed that FLRT2 did not alter the abundance of USP22 but impaired its recruitment to ABCA1, thereby preventing USP22-mediated deubiquitination of ABCA1, increasing ABCA1 ubiquitination and promoting its proteasomal degradation. These data define a novel FLRT2–USP22–ABCA1 axis in macrophages through which FLRT2 promotes ABCA1 loss, cholesterol retention, and foam cell formation, revealing a potential target for limiting lipid accumulation in macrophages during atherogenesis.

In summary, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized role of macrophage FLRT2 in promoting foam cell formation and atherosclerosis by regulating ABCA1 stability through USP22-mediated deubiquitination. This mechanism provides insights into cholesterol homeostasis and atherogenic processes and identifies FLRT2 as a promising therapeutic target for atherosclerosis. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular interactions underlying FLRT2-mediated regulation of ABCA1 ubiquitination and to validate these mechanisms in vivo.
