Introduction

Mediastinal lipomas are rare benign tumors, the size and location of the tumor presented a diagnostic challenge, as large mediastinal masses can compress adjacent structures, leading to symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or cough. However, our patient remained asymptomatic, and the mass was discovered incidentally during a routine health examination.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old male with no history of cardiovascular disease was referred to our department after a routine health screening revealed an incidental mediastinal mass. He was completely asymptomatic, reporting no chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or systemic complaints. The patient’s body mass index (BMI) was recorded at 25.87 kg/m², with a total cholesterol level of 3.58 mmol/L.Physical examination and transthoracic echocardiography showed no abnormalities. However, contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography revealed a well-circumscribed, hypodense mass measuring 11.7 × 6.3 cm in the anterior superior mediastinum, with fat attenuation, extending toward the right cardiophrenic angle (Supplementary Video S1). The patient underwent complete resection of the mass via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. Intraoperatively, a lobulated, encapsulated fatty mass was excised. Histopathological examination confirmed a benign lipoma composed of mature adipocytes without evidence of atypia, lipoblasts, or malignancy. At 6 months, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence on imaging.

Discussion and conclusions

Mediastinal lipomas are rare benign tumors, accounting for less than 1% of all mediastinal masses. They predominantly occur in middle-aged adults, with a higher incidence in males [1]. These tumors are typically asymptomatic and often discovered incidentally during imaging studies conducted for unrelated reasons. The majority of mediastinal lipomas are located in the anterior mediastinum and are composed of mature adipose tissue without evidence of malignancy [2]. The anterior mediastinum represents a predominant site for neoplastic processes in the pediatric population, with an etiological profile distinct from that observed in adults. Lymphomas, germ cell tumors (GCTs), and thymic-derived lesions constitute the most frequently encountered masses, while mesenchymal tumors such as liposarcoma are notably uncommon in this demographic. Establishing a systematic diagnostic workflow is essential, given the broad clinical spectrum—which ranges from incidentally detected lesions to masses causing critical airway compromise or vascular obstruction [3]. Among these, lymphoma is the foremost etiology, primarily comprising Hodgkin lymphoma and the highly aggressive T-lymphoblastic lymphoma. The latter typically manifests as a bulky, rapidly expanding mass that demands urgent intervention due to the potential for superior vena cava syndrome and life-threatening tracheal compression [4]. In contrast, mesenchymal tumors are rare within the pediatric anterior mediastinum. Their diagnosis ultimately depends on histopathological evaluation following biopsy or resection, as radiographic features often lack specificity [5].

Surgical resection is the treatment of choice for mediastinal lipomas, especially when the diagnosis is uncertain, the tumor is large, or there is a risk of compression of adjacent structures [6]. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery offers a minimally invasive approach with reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery compared to open thoracotomy. In our case, the tumor was successfully resected via VATS, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a benign lipoma composed of mature adipocytes without atypia or malignancy [7]. This case underscores the importance of considering mediastinal lipoma in the differential diagnosis of anterior mediastinal masses, even in young, asymptomatic patients. Early detection and surgical intervention can prevent potential complications associated with tumor growth and compression of vital mediastinal structures (Fig. 1).
