Mapping the global research landscape of perineural invasion in gastrointestinal malignancies: a bibliometric visualization analysis
Authors: Shijie Yang, ChengZhang Zhu, Hui Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Perineural invasion (PNI) is a marker of aggressive behavior and poor prognosis for gastrointestinal (GI) malignancies. Research on PNI in tumors has evolved from initially describing its pathological features to recently exploring the interaction between tumors and nerves. This study is entirely based on data obtained from the Web of Science Core Collection and conducted a bibliometric analysis of 196 articles published in the database from 1993 to 2025. Using VOSviewer and CiteSpace software, we quantified the outputs, drew a collaboration network diagram, and analyzed co-citations, keyword co-occurrences/clustering, and outbreak situations. The number of annual publications after 2013 has significantly increased, with the greatest contributions coming from China and the United States. A comprehensive analysis of the existing literature has yielded three main themes: first, the radiomics prediction of PNI; second, the prognostic value of PNI for recurrence/survival; third, the mechanism of interaction between nerves and tumors. The emergence of terms such as "radiomics", "machine learning", and "deep learning" marks a paradigm shift towards an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven, non-invasive risk stratification method. These transitions highlight the translational potential of integrating AI-based models and insights into the mechanisms of tumor-neuron interaction and applying them to clinical practice. This study depicts the evolution of PNI research from pathological identification to tumor-neuron interaction, reveals the integration of oncology and neuroscience in GI malignancies, and highlights emerging directions. Future work should focus on the prospective construction of AI tools and multi-center multimodal validation, deeper mechanism analysis, and the inclusion of underrepresented cancer subtypes to provide a roadmap for the development of precision oncology for GI malignancies.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) malignancies are common malignant tumors that pose a great threat to human health and a huge burden worldwide [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Cancer 2020 report, colorectal cancer (10.0%) and gastric cancer (5.6%) are the leading causes of new cases combined, with mortality rates ranked 2nd and 4th respectively [3]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) has an important impact on tumor development and has attracted the attention of oncology researchers worldwide [4]. The peripheral nervous system (PNS), as part of the TME, has emerged as a research hotspot, and there has been much evidence of its interaction with tumors [5].

Perineural invasion (PNI) is characterized by tumor cell infiltration along the nerve trunk and dynamic bidirectional interactions between malignant cells and nerve components [6]. Neural components are indispensable drivers of tumor progression, and cancer cells can hijack the nervous system to grow and spread [7, 8]. In addition, neuropathic changes in the local nervous system not only cause pain, but also are closely related to tumor recurrence and poor prognosis [9, 10]. PNI has been observed in various non-neurological tumors. However, in different types of tumors, the mechanisms change differently, and the focus of attention varies. In pancreatic cancer, PNI has a very high incidence rate and is closely related to local recurrence, distant metastasis and lymph node metastasis, etc. [11]. In head and neck tumors, prostate cancer, and basal skin cancer, PNI indicates enhanced local invasion ability, but its ability to predict distant metastasis is relatively weak [12–14]. In gastric and colorectal cancers, PNI is associated with a poorer prognosis and may provide an important infiltration channel for cancer cells to break through the serosa, which may provide navigation services for the infiltration of cancer cells [15, 16].

Despite growing interest in PNI in GI oncology, the current research remains fragmented and often disease-specific. Studies predominantly focus on clinical correlations, such as their prognostic significance, or isolated molecular pathways [17, 18]. However, the broader research landscape, including collaborative networks, thematic trends, and emerging hotspots remains unclear. Moreover, no comprehensive evaluation has been conducted to map the temporal evolution of PNI-related research on GI malignancies. This lack of macro-level understanding hinders the identification of knowledge gaps, cross-disciplinary opportunities, and translational directions.

Compared with systematic and scoping reviews and other types of literature studies, bibliometric analysis allows a quantitative analysis of published literature to present a more visual structure of knowledge and the current state of research in a field, enabling faster and more accurate research on trends and hotspots. Bibliometric analysis has been widely used in various fields [19–21]. In this context, a bibliometric approach is well suited to systematically explore the development of PNI research in GI malignancies over the past two decades. Bibliometric studies to date have mainly looked at GI malignancies from an oncologic perspective in isolation. What makes this study different is that by focusing on PNI in GI cancer, it provides a unique dual-perspective analysis of the intersection between oncologic outcomes and neural mechanisms, thereby providing a more integrated view of how tumor-neural interactions have shaped the research landscape.

The objective of this study was to conduct a comprehensive bibliometric and visual analysis of global research related to PNI in GI malignancies, spanning the years 1993–2025. By leveraging data from the Web of Science Core Collection and utilizing visualization tools such as CiteSpace and VOSviewer, this study aimed to: (1) analyze publication outputs, author collaborations, and institutional contributions; (2) identify research hotspots, intellectual clusters, and thematic shifts over time; and (3) provide insights into emerging mechanisms and future research directions involving PNI in GI malignancies.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategies

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) on June 18, 2025, focusing on studies of PNI in GI malignancies. The scope of the analysis was limited to GI malignancies (esophageal, gastric, and colorectal). Pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers were excluded because they are distinct oncology entities with unique epidemiology, biology, and clinical management and are often studied separately in the context of PNI [22, 23]. The search strategy was developed using subject terms and author keywords relevant to the field. As WoSCC does not index MeSH terms, these were not included. Synonyms were not systematically tested; instead, the most representative and widely accepted keywords were selected to balance precision and coverage. This approach ensured the completeness of the dataset and its consistency with bibliometric research practices.

The following Boolean search formula was used:

((TS = ("perineural invasion" OR "perineural spread" OR "nerve invasion")) AND TS = ("gastrointestinal cancer" OR "gastrointestinal tumor" OR "gastric cancer" OR "stomach cancer" OR"colorectal cancer" OR "colon cancer" OR "rectal cancer" OR"small intestine cancer" OR "small bowel cancer" OR"esophageal cancer" OR "esophagus cancer")) NOT TS = ("pancreatic cancer" OR "liver cancer" OR "hepatocellular carcinoma" OR "bile duct cancer" OR "cholangiocarcinoma" OR "gallbladder cancer").

The document type was restricted to original research and review articles published in English. Based on this search strategy, 1487 articles were retrieved.

Subsequently, a two-stage screening process was implemented. In the first stage, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to assess their eligibility. In cases of disagreement, a third reviewer evaluated the disputed articles to reach consensus. Only studies directly related to PNI in GI malignancies were included. After full-text screening and relevance assessment, 196 publications were deemed eligible for inclusion in this analysis. The final dataset was exported in “full record and cited references” format and saved as tab-delimited text files for further analysis using bibliometric software tools. The data in "tabs separate files" were imported into the bibliometric online analysis platform (http://bibliometric.com) to analyze the relationship between collaborating countries/regions. Keyword analysis

Keyword co-occurrence analysis

Keyword co-occurrence analysis is a crucial bibliometric method used to interpret thematic structure, developmental trajectory, and emerging trends within a research domain [26]. By analyzing the frequency and co-presence of keywords, this method helps uncover the core topics and their interrelationships over time [27].

We performed a co-occurrence analysis of keywords extracted from 196 publications related to PNI in GI malignancies (Fig. 4a). In the network visualization, nodes of the same color represent clusters of keywords that frequently co-occur, indicating thematic relevance. The size of each node reflects the frequency of the keyword’s appearance: the larger the node, the more frequently the keyword appears across the dataset. The links between nodes represent co-occurrence relationships, where denser connections indicate stronger thematic associations. The visualization revealed that keywords such as “perineural invasion,” survival, “carcinoma,” neural invasion, “recurrence,” and “adjuvant chemotherapy were among the most frequently appearing terms in the field. Cluster analysis revealed four themes of PNI research in GI malignancies: (1) clinical prognosis, focusing on survival and recurrence; (2) exploring the mechanisms of tumor-nerve interaction, including the role of neurotransmitters; (3) application of emerging technologies, especially radiomics and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven prediction models; (4) clinical management and interventional treatment strategies, such as metal stents. Together, these clusters illustrate a clear trajectory of the evolution of research in this field—from descriptive clinical associations to mechanistic insights and, more recently, to advanced computational methods for noninvasive prediction, accompanied by parallel developments in clinical support and management. This progress reflects the growing interdisciplinary integration of oncology, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence while also highlighting the importance of clinical translation.

Keywords time trend analysis

Both the keyword timeline and time zone visualizations provide insight into the temporal distribution and evolution of research themes in this field [29]. Figure 4d, e illustrates the chronological arrangement of keywords within each cluster. By clustering along a temporal axis, these visualizations help reveal the historical development and shifting focus of PNI research in GI malignancies.

In this study, keywords such as “perineural invasion,” “lymphovascular invasion,” “carcinoma,” “survival,” and “prognostic factor” were identified as high-frequency and high-centrality terms. Their prominence underscores a sustained research focus on the association between PNI and clinical prognosis, pathological indicators, and tumor progression in GI malignancies.

The evolution of keyword usage over time reflects changing research priorities in the field. Prior to 2007, studies primarily concentrated on case reports and the initial characterization of nerve invasion in GI malignancies, along with early analyses of influencing factors. Between 2007 and 2013, researchers began to formalize the concept of PNI, with an increasing focus on its association with overall survival, clinicopathological staging, lymphovascular metastasis, and its role in tumor aggressiveness and prognosis.

From 2013 to 2017, attention shifted toward the relationship between PNI and treatment strategies, particularly the impact of chemoradiotherapy and surgical interventions on PNI occurrence and outcomes. The research landscape has evolved from 2017 to 2022, with a growing interest in the biological mechanisms of PNI. Investigations have begun to explore the crosstalk between GI malignancies and the peripheral nervous system, including the role of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), cytokine regulation, and immune system involvement. Since 2022, the emergence of AI, machine learning, and big data analytics has ushered in a new era of research. These technologies have been increasingly applied to develop high-performance prediction models for assessing the risk and prognosis of PNI in GI malignancies. Techniques such as radiomics, multi-omics integration, and machine learning algorithms have been utilized to analyze large-scale clinical datasets, enabling the identification of key prognostic markers and construction of multi-modal, high-sensitivity predictive tools.

Overall, the keyword time trend analysis revealed a progressive shift from clinical characterization to mechanistic exploration and technological integration in PNI research, highlighting its dynamic evolution and growing interdisciplinary relevance.

Hot spot analysis of keywords

Keyword burst analysis is a valuable method for identifying terms that experience a sudden increase in frequency, thereby revealing emerging research hotspots and shifts in academic focus [30]. As illustrated in Fig. 4f, prior to 2017, keywords such as “carcinoma,” “curative resection,” “therapy,” and “high risk” demonstrated strong citation bursts. This suggests that during this earlier period, research on PNI in GI malignancies was primarily concentrated at the clinical level, particularly in relation to surgical treatment, risk stratification, and prognosis. This focus aligns with the fact that PNI was first observed and recognized in routine clinical pathology and surgical specimens and is closely linked to survival outcomes and recurrence risk.

Since 2017, there has been a notable shift in research emphasis. Keywords such as “metastasis” and “lymphovascular invasion” began to show significant bursts, indicating growing interest in understanding PNI in the context of advanced-stage GI malignancies. This marks a transition from purely clinical observations toward more mechanistic investigations, including the biological pathways underlying PNI progression. Recent studies have increasingly integrated clinical data with molecular and cellular insights, reflecting a multidisciplinary approach that bridges clinical oncology and tumor biology.

This temporal evolution of burst keywords highlights the dynamic trajectory of the field, from initial clinical characterization to a deeper mechanism-based understanding of PNI, particularly in the context of tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and treatment resistance.

In the literature burst analysis, two landmark studies were identified with strong citation bursts, reflecting their significant impact on the field of GI tumor research (see Fig. 6). In 2009, Liebig et al. [31] published a pivotal article in the Journal of Clinical Oncology with a burst strength of 13.03, the highest among all included references. This study retrospectively analyzed tumor pathology specimens from 269 consecutive colorectal cancer patients, with a blinded re-evaluation of PNI status. This is the first systematic demonstration of PNI as an independent prognostic factor in colorectal cancer. The findings were striking: the 5-year disease-free survival rate for PNI-positive patients was only 16%, and the overall survival rate was 25%, which was significantly lower than that for PNI-negative patients (P < 0.0001). The high methodological quality of the continuous patient cohort, blinded pathological review, and rigorous statistical analysis made this study a cornerstone in the field. It has since been widely cited and has provided core evidence for clinical guidelines and multiple systematic reviews.Fig. 6Hight cited burst analysis in the field of PNI and GI malignancies (1993–2025)

In 2010, Peng et al. [32] published a complementary study on Cancer focusing specifically on the clinical implications of PNI in pT3N0-stage rectal cancer. The authors emphasized that even in early stage patients without lymph node involvement, the presence of PNI could serve as a critical indicator of recurrence risk and may supersede traditional staging systems in guiding prognosis. They proposed using the PNI as a determinant for adjuvant therapy decisions, thus influencing treatment pathways. This study extended the findings of Liebig et al. by emphasizing the translational value of the PNI, bridging pathological assessment, clinical oncology, and therapeutic decision-making. It also contributed to the incorporation of PNI status into the pathological classification and clinical stratification framework.

Together, these two publications have been extensively cited and are widely recognized for establishing the prognostic significance of the PNI in GI malignancies. Their clinical relevance, methodological rigor, and translational implications underscore their lasting impacts on both research and practice.

In 2023, Zheng et al. [33] published a landmark article in Radiographics, which marked the most recent breakthrough in this research domain. This study introduced a radiomics-based model for the preoperative prediction of PNI in gastric cancer by integrating multimodal clinical variables with multiple machine-learning algorithms. The resulting predictive models were thoroughly constructed and validated, demonstrating significant clinical value in preoperative risk stratification and adjuvant therapy decision making.

This work not only bridges tumor pathology, medical imaging, and AI but also exemplifies the evolving trend of precision oncology. Its innovative methodology and strong translational potential have attracted widespread attention, positioning it as a representative study at the intersection of radiology and oncology.

Discussion

Research highlights and evolutionary trajectory

The PNI has long been recognized as a critical pathological feature of GI malignancies. This bibliometric analysis, for the first time, comprehensively mapped the intellectual landscape of PNI research within the context of GI malignancies. A thorough investigation into the development of the field was conducted using both keyword timelines and burst analysis techniques. This comprehensive analysis yielded a discernible developmental trajectory, providing a comprehensive overview of the field’s evolution.

During the early phase (1993–2012), research primarily focused on the pathological identification and clinical prognostic correlations of PNI, particularly in colorectal and gastric cancers. Landmark historical studies have established a foundation for understanding PNI in GI malignancies. Shirouzu et al. [34] (1993) showed that stage III colorectal cancer patients with PNI had higher local recurrence and poorer survival, Tanaka et al. [35] (1994) demonstrated its strong prognostic impact in gastric cancer, even in T2 tumors without serosal invasion, and later extended these findings to esophageal cancer [36]. Collectively, these studies positioned PNI as an independent prognostic factor and potential staging sub-classifier, laying the foundation for subsequent research on mechanisms, diagnostic criteria, and predictive modeling. The rise in annual publications since 2013 reflects increasing recognition of its clinical and biological relevance.

During the mid-stage period (2013–2017), significant advancements were made in tumor staging, leading to a more precise understanding of the role of PNI in tumor recurrence, survival, and metastasis. In the early stages of this period, two highly representative studies made an important contribution to the refinement of GI tumor staging by studying PNI in detail. Ueno and Hideki et al. [37] (2013) conducted a multicenter study on 2845 cases of colorectal cancer and proposed the PNI3 grading system. The results showed that considering PNI in addition to vascular invasion could improve the accuracy of prognosis. Similarly, Jiang et al. [38] (2014) analyzed 518 patients with gastric cancer. The results indicated that integrating PNI into the TNM staging system was helpful in enhancing prognosis classification, especially in identifying high-risk patients after radical resection. This initiated the inclusion of PNI in the staging system for subdividing the GI tumor staging, marking the beginning of precise diagnosis and treatment.

Based on the preliminary research results, the subsequent studies further focused on the role of PNI in the malignant progression of GI malignancies, especially in the complex clinical environment involving multiple disease stages and treatment options. This phase saw a strategic shift towards integrating the PNI with other key prognostic indicators to develop predictive tools. Landmark studies exemplified this evolution: Dhadda et al. [39] (2014) pioneered a novel prognostic index (NRPI) incorporating PNI alongside treatment response and surgical margin factors for rectal cancer patients receiving preoperative therapy, demonstrating its superiority over conventional staging.

Chablani et al. [40] (2017) confirmed that PNI has a consistently independent predictive value for survival outcomes (disease-free survival/disease-free mortality) in patients with locally advanced rectal cancer who received neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and surgery. This was true even after adjusting for multiple pathological and treatment variables, thereby reinforcing its application value in risk stratification. Yokoyama et al. [41] (2017) further distinguished PNI as a treatment-resistant pathological factor significantly linked to aggressive disease behavior (distant recurrence, poor prognosis) in patients with stage II/III rectal cancer undergoing preoperative chemoradiotherapy. Overall, these studies have all emphasized the significant prognostic significance of PNI. That is, the role of PNI goes far beyond the basic staging stage. It has the potential to become a key component of risk assessment models and to identify high-risk patients, especially in the era of multimodal treatment.

Concurrent with clinical and predictive studies, research into the mechanism of PNI in GI malignanciess has emerged. In 2015, Qijun et al. [42] demonstrated that VCAM1 is markedly upregulated in gastric cancer, mediating bidirectional interactions between tumour and nerve cells and thereby promoting PNI. Subsequent studies have highlighted neurotransmitters, particularly acetylcholine (ACh), as key drivers of this process. As demonstrated by Wang et al. [43] ACh has been shown to enhance tumour cell proliferation and survival via M3R-mediated EGFR/ERK/AKT signalling. In addition, Ting et al. [44] revealed its role in invasion and migration through the M3R/AMPK/MACC1 axis and EMT regulation. Building on this, Huangfei et al. [45] further demonstrated that gastric cancer cells possess the capacity for autonomous synthesis and secretion of ACh, thereby establishing an autocrine signalling loop. These insights have given rise to novel therapeutic perspectives: M3R antagonists, such as 4-DAMP and darifenacin, have been shown to exert a dual action in cancer therapy. In addition to suppressing the viability of tumour cells, these agents have been observed to enhance chemosensitivity and promote apoptosis. Research in this field has evolved from the identification of molecular mediators such as VCAM1 to the elucidation of neurotransmitter-driven mechanisms of PNI. This suggests that targeting these pathways may represent a promising strategy for the management of gastric cancer.

The current phase (2018–2024) is characterized by interdisciplinary integration, especially radiomics and AI, leading to the emergence of predictive models for preoperative risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making while further exploring the molecular mechanisms. Huang et al. [46] extracted radiomics features from CT images of 346 colorectal cancer patients and combined them with serum CEA levels to construct a predictive model for PNI using a training cohort. Their study demonstrated that the integration of radiomics and clinical biomarkers could effectively predict the risk of PNI and assist in preoperative clinical decision making. This represents a typical early phase application of radiomics for constructing predictive models of PNI.

As the demand for processing high-dimensional and multimodal data has increased, recent studies have incorporated advanced technologies, such as machine learning (ML) and AI, to enhance model performance. For instance, Liu et al. [47] retrospectively analyzed the CT images and clinicopathological data of 268 colorectal cancer patients from two medical centers. By extracting a wide array of radiomics and clinical features and employing ML techniques for feature selection and model construction, their study exemplifies the integration of radiomics and ML in predictive modeling. This reflects the current shift in oncology toward a data-driven paradigm for tumor assessment.

Radiomics, a high-throughput imaging analysis technique, enables the extraction of numerous quantitative indicators that capture tumor heterogeneity, morphology, and texture from standard medical images [48]. The incorporation of ML facilitates the efficient identification of statistically significant features, whereas irrelevant variables are progressively eliminated through model training, preserving only the most informative feature combinations [49]. In real-world scenarios characterized by limited sample sizes and high data heterogeneity, the combination of radiomics and ML can significantly enhance the model performance, improve generalizability, and increase the feasibility of clinical implementation.

Between 2018 and 2024, further studies were conducted on the subject of molecular mechanisms, and it was revealed that PNI is an active process regulated by neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF and BDNF), neurotransmitters, adhesion molecules (e.g., L1CAM and NCAM), MMPs, and interactions with the immune microenvironment (e.g., promoting immune evasion) [50, 51]. Compared to high-PNI cancers such as pancreatic carcinoma (>70% incidence), GI tumor PNI research remains nascent, characterized by insufficient mechanistic depth (unclear regulatory networks of key molecules such as M3R, BDNF, and β2-AR), a lack of specific models (particularly targeting the enteric nervous system), and delayed clinical translation (no PNI-targeted drugs in clinical trials). Unique advantages include complex triple innervation by the sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric nervous systems, a solid foundation in immune microenvironment research, and the application of multi-omics technologies (e.g., single-cell/spatial omics). Future efforts should focus on establishing standardized PNI assessment protocols, developing GI-specific PNI models, elucidating neural-immune crosstalk mechanisms, integrating multi-omics to delineate the PNI microenvironment, and accelerating the validation of therapeutic targets (e.g., TrkA, M3R, and L1CAM) for clinical translation, thereby advancing PNI from a prognostic biomarker to a viable therapeutic target.

In the 2024–2025 period, research on PNI in GI malignancies has entered a new phase. Clinical models based on AI and deep learning are becoming increasingly sophisticated, with growing validation through both internal and external datasets. The enhancement in the size and variety of available datasets has further increased the robustness and generalisability of these models, thereby improving their potential clinical applicability [52, 53]. At the mechanistic level, recent studies have revealed that PNI profoundly influences the immune landscape of the TME, contributing to immunotherapy resistance [54]. Furthermore, the structural characteristics of PNI have been systematically evaluated, including measurements of nerve density and diameter. Investigators have initiated the exploration of the impact of these perineural features on tumour progression and patient prognosis using a mouse chemical denervation model. Such studies signify a novel and promising direction, whereby the focus is transitioned from descriptive pathology to functional mechanisms, thereby unveiling new avenues for therapeutic intervention [55].

Emerging research themes and clinical applications

The keyword clustering and co-citation network highlighted three main emerging themes: radiomics-based PNI prediction, which extracts multiple quantitative features from standardized imaging data through radiomics, and then applies ML to process these high-throughput and multimodal data for preoperative PNI or prognosis prediction, as well as preoperative identification of GI malignancies. The high specificity of these models enables early risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.

PNI as a biomarker for invasive diseases: The frequent occurrence of terms such as recurrence, adjuvant chemotherapy, and poor prognosis once again confirms the independent role of PNI in predicting outcomes, especially in those lymph node-negative patients who would otherwise be classified as having a low risk [56].

Neuro-immune and stromal crosstalk: Although mechanistic studies are less represented, the rising frequency of keywords such as EMT, immune infiltration, and TRPV1 suggests a growing interest in the biological underpinnings of PNI, including the tumor-nervous system interface and its modulation by inflammation and cytokines. Together, these themes underscore the translational potential of PNI research, from histopathological descriptions to dynamic, personalized clinical interventions.

Global contributions and collaborative landscape

China and the United States are significant contributors to research related to PNI in digestive tumors, with institutions such as Nanfang Hospital, Fudan University, and Harvard Medical School being particularly prominent. Nevertheless, collaboration remains largely regionalized. It is imperative that enhanced international collaboration, especially between AI research centers and clinical oncology centers, is facilitated to accelerate the development and validation of predictive tools. The most frequently cited authors, Hao Liu and Bihi D Lee, represent the basic and modern contributions, respectively, establishing a connection between traditional pathology and modern computational medicine. It is noteworthy that Liu’s team exemplifies the evolution of the field from small-scale imaging genomics studies to multi-center, multi-modal, AI-enhanced modelling efforts.

Strengths and contributions of the study

This study provides the first bibliometric visualization specifically focused on PNI in GI malignancies, providing a data-driven synthesis of the field by placing GI malignancies at the intersection of oncology and neuroscience. With the help of a dual bibliometric analysis platform and multi-dimensional analysis, we not only map global research trends, but also highlight the value of integrating the perspectives of oncology and neurobiology, and show the historical progress and emerging frontiers in this field. This interdisciplinary focus highlights the novelty of our work and points to future directions. AI-driven models can support noninvasive risk prediction and treatment decisions, while mechanistic analysis of tumor-neural dialogue may guide new therapeutic strategies, which can jointly guide the clinical management strategy of PNI in GI malignancies, providing a clear and detailed roadmap for future scientific and clinical work.

Future directions

We propose the following priorities for advancing PNI research in GI oncology: (1) Clinical validation of AI-based models: Integration of radiomics models into prospective clinical trials and real-world settings is essential to assess their practical utility. Recent studies suggest that AI-based radiomics models can serve as non-invasive tools for predicting PNI with moderate to good accuracy [57, 58]. Nonetheless, current evidence is limited by retrospective designs, geographically concentrated datasets, and methodological heterogeneity. Future research should prioritize prospective studies, methodological standardization, and the integration of advanced ML techniques to enhance clinical applicability and reliability [59]. Additionally, image-based ML models and deep learning architectures such as convolutional neural networks (CNN) have promising applications in non-invasive neural invasion prediction. However, the "black box" nature of these models remains a major obstacle for clinical application. Although their decision-making process is often difficult to explain, rigorous validation of these models using large-scale, multicenter datasets is both necessary and feasible to establish their reliability and improve clinical acceptance. (2) Molecular dissection of PNI pathways: mechanistic studies exploring neurotrophic factors, the immune microenvironment, and extracellular vesicles could uncover new therapeutic targets. Increasing evidence indicates the importance of neuroimmune pathways, including the acetylcholine-M3R-EGFR/ERK signaling pathway, the stress response mediated by adrenergic receptors, and the immune suppression network in the TME. Beyond molecular signals, future research should also consider the chemical and physical characteristics of the tumor-neural microenvironment, such as extracellular matrix stiffness, ion gradients, and metabolic conditions, which can affect neural invasion and tumor progression. (3) Multimodal data integration: fusion of imaging, genomics, and clinical data; combining validated artificial intelligence models with multi-scale insights from tumor-neurobiology will accelerate the development of precision oncology strategies for GI malignancies with PNI features. (4) Pan-GI perspective: PNI in less-studied GI malignancies, such as esophageal and small bowel tumors, deserves greater research attention; (5) Standardization of definitions and detection: Efforts should be made to harmonize pathological criteria and imaging thresholds for PNI to improve reproducibility and interoperability across centers.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations. Firstly, it is important to note that this analysis was based exclusively on the WoSCC database. Whilst this approach provides a standardised data set, it may exclude relevant studies covered by other sources, such as Scopus and PubMed. It is recommended that future studies consider integrating multiple databases in order to enhance the comprehensivity of the bibliometric results. Concurrently, the research strategy may have resulted in the omission of pertinent non-English or non-indexed studies, particularly those from lower-resource settings. Second, bibliometric approaches tend to prioritize the number of citations, which may not be a reliable indicator of content quality or methodological rigor. Third, the utilization of keyword-based clustering can occasionally result in redundancy or ambiguity in interpretation, owing to alterations in terminology. We did not include pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers in the study, which somewhat Narrows the generalizability. Of note, PNI is almost universal in the pancreatic (70 to 100% incidence) and has a significant prognostic impact in hepatobiliary cancers [60]. We avoided the confounding effects of the distinct anatomical, biologic, and clinical patterns inherent to these neurotropic cancers. Future bibliometric analysis could focus specifically on pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers to provide comparative insights. Furthermore, while this study delineates future trends, it does not provide an in-depth qualitative assessment of the methodology or the level of evidence of individual studies. A complementary systematic review or meta-analysis is necessary to provide a comprehensive overview of the existing literature on this subject. Notwithstanding the aforementioned limitations, the conclusions of this study are not affected because they are based on real-world analysis, and the results are reliable.

Conclusion

This study employed bibliometric methods to depict the knowledge structure and evolution of PNI research in GI malignancies, revealing its gradual shift from traditional pathological and prognostic studies to interdisciplinary integration of radiomics, AI, and neuro-oncology. Based on this trend, future efforts should focus on multi-center, multi-modal prospective validation of AI prediction models, in-depth exploration of tumor-neuron interaction mechanisms, and expanded research in rare cancer types such as small intestine and esophageal cancers. Strengthening international cooperation and multi-modal data integration will further promote clinical translation in these directions, providing strategic guidance for PNI research in GI oncology.
