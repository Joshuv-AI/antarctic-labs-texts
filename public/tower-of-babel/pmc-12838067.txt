Machine learning-based prediction of mortality and multidrug-resistant infection risks in ICU patients with suspected infection: a prospective national multicenter cohort study
Authors: Shuguang Yang, Yao Sun, Ting Wang, Chenxiao Hao, Hua Zhang, Wei Sun, Youzhong An, Huiying Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuspected infection can progress to sepsis or septic shock, contributing to a high mortality rate among patients admitted to ICU. However, the characteristics of suspected infection remain incompletely defined. We aimed to develop and validate predictive models to identify independent risk factors for mortality and multidrug-resistant infection in patients with suspected infection upon ICU admission in mainland China.MethodsWe prospectively collected medical data from patients with suspected infection admitted to ICUs across mainland China between July 2021 and December 2022. Patients were randomly allocated to a training cohort and a validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio. Using machine learning algorithms, we identified risk factors and constructed predictive models for mortality and multidrug-resistant infection. The performance of models developed by logistic regression, random forest, extreme gradient boosting, and gradient boosting machine was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), the Youden index, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsA total of 2963 patients from 67 hospitals in mainland China were enrolled. The most common infection sites were the lung (79.28%), bloodstream (17.11%) and abdomen (16.54%). The overall mortality rate was 10.90%. The logistic regression model demonstrated the best performance for mortality prediction (AUC 0.87) and identified the following risk factors: surgery (2.39, 95CI% 1.60–3.56, p < 0.01), APACHE II score (1.51, 95CI% 1.44–1.58, p < 0.01), and bloodstream infection (2.08, 95CI% 1.40–3.10, p < 0.01) were independent risk factors of mortality. For multidrug resistant infection prediction, the logistic regression model also showed the highest discriminative ability (AUC 0.86). Independent risk factors included APACHE II score (1.06, 95%CI 1.03–1.1, p < 0.01), bloodstream infection (1.82, 95%CI 1.26–2.61, p < 0.01), urinary infection (3.42, 95%CI 2.20–5.30, p < 0.01), Klebsiella pneumoniae (11.67, 95%CI 7.91–17.21, p < 0.01), Acinetobacter baumannii (85.22, 95%CI 50.03-145.18, p < 0.01), and Enterococcus faecium (22.10, 95%CI 8.58–56.93, p < 0.01).ConclusionsPneumonia was the most common infection among ICU patients in mainland China. Introduction

Epidemiological and therapeutic studies indicate that severe infections often develop during hospitalization in the ICU [1, 2]. For ICU patients, infections are associated with increased costs, morbidity, and mortality [3].

Antibiotics are among the most frequently prescribed medication in the ICU, which reflects the high risk of infection due to patients’ underlying conditions, impaired immunity, and the use of multiple invasive devices [4]. Studies report that 50%–70% of patients acquire infections during their ICU stay and receive antibiotics [1, 3]. Epidemiological research has established a correlation between antibiotic use in the ICU and the emergence of resistant strains. Furthermore, the frequent overprescribing and misprescribing of antibiotics exacerbate the challenge of drug-resistant bacterial [5, 6]. A study of ICU infections at Ruijin Hospital in Shanghai identified Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus as the predominant respiratory tract pathogens, with a high prevalence of decrease antibiotic susceptibility [7]. Among mechanically ventilated ICU patients, the incidence of drug-resistant infection increased from 62.5% in 2018 to 71% in 2022 [8].

However, multicenter data on the clinical features and microbiology of infections in adult ICU patients remain scarce and are often limited to single regions or countries. Infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria pose a serious threat to ICU patients worldwide. To address this gap, we conducted a national-level observational study to explore the clinical characteristics and outcomes of suspected infection in adult ICU patients in China. To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and validate predictive models for mortality and multidrug-resistant infection in patients with suspected infection at the time of ICU admission.

Methods

Study design and sampling

We conducted a prospective analysis of patients admitted to the ICUs across mainland China between July 2021 and December 2022 (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (no. 2021PHB020-001) and all participating hospitals, with a waiver of informed consent. The study was registered on July 14, 2021 (NCT04966390||https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/).

Flow chart of hospital recruitment and patient enrollment. Provinces in mainland China were divided into seven geographical regions. A total of 2,963 patients from 67 hospitals were included in this study

Patients

The onset of a suspected infection episode was defined as the first occurrence of either antibiotic administration (oral or parenteral) or body fluid culture collection, subject to the following timing rules:

If antibiotics were administered first, a culture must be obtained within 24 h.

If antibiotics were administered first, a culture must be obtained within 24 h.

If a culture was obtained first, antibiotics must be started within 72 h.

If a culture was obtained first, antibiotics must be started within 72 h.

The time of this initial event was designated as time zero for the episode [9].

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) antibiotic therapy was not administered or its duration was less than three days, (2) age under 18 years, or (3) incomplete clinical data.

Microbiological testing

Bacteria pathogens isolated from clinical specimens (including blood, urine, ascites, and pleural fluid) were identified using standard microbiological methods. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI) [10]. All procedures were performed under quality-controlled conditions.

Definitions of infections

A sample was considered culture-positive if one or more fluid cultures yielded positive results. Patients could be included more than once in the analysis for distinct infection episodes. Diagnostic criteria for infection types were defined as follows:

Pneumonia: Require the presence of new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography, plus at least two of the following criteria: (1) fever (> 38.5 °C) or hypothermia < 36 °C), (2) a positive culture from tracheobronchial or aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or significant bacteriological counts in respiratory secretions, (3) leucocytosis ≥ 12 × 109/L [11–14].

Pneumonia: Require the presence of new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography, plus at least two of the following criteria: (1) fever (> 38.5 °C) or hypothermia < 36 °C), (2) a positive culture from tracheobronchial or aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) or significant bacteriological counts in respiratory secretions, (3) leucocytosis ≥ 12 × 109/L [11–14].

Bloodstream infection: fever (> 37.3 °C) or sign of systemic infection with at least one positive blood culture [15].

Bloodstream infection: fever (> 37.3 °C) or sign of systemic infection with at least one positive blood culture [15].

Intra-abdominal infection: clinical evidence (e.g., abdominal pain or rigidity) with a positive culture from drainage or puncture fluid [16].

Intra-abdominal infection: clinical evidence (e.g., abdominal pain or rigidity) with a positive culture from drainage or puncture fluid [16].

Urinary infection: lower urinary tract symptoms with a quantitative count of ≥ 105 colony forming units per millilitre (CFU/ml) and positive urine culture [17].

Urinary infection: lower urinary tract symptoms with a quantitative count of ≥ 105 colony forming units per millilitre (CFU/ml) and positive urine culture [17].

Skin/Soft tissue infection: spectrum from mild to life-threatening, diagnosed by a positive culture from lesional skin [18].

Skin/Soft tissue infection: spectrum from mild to life-threatening, diagnosed by a positive culture from lesional skin [18].

Central nervous system infection: anatomically classified (e.g., meningitis, encephalitis, myelitis) with a positive cerebrospinal fluid culture [19].

Pelvic infection: uterine, tubal, or ovarian infection diagnosed by pelvic pain or effusion with a positive culture from pelvic drainage or vaginal/uterine secretions [20].

Pelvic infection: uterine, tubal, or ovarian infection diagnosed by pelvic pain or effusion with a positive culture from pelvic drainage or vaginal/uterine secretions [20].

Patients were assigned to the drug-resistant group if ≥ 1 isolate demonstrated resistance during ICU admission. Drug resistance was defined as non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 antibiotic class, with multidrug-resistant infection indicating non-susceptibility to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories [21].

Data collection

We collected clinical data from patients via an electronic data capture (EDC) system (https://edc2.cttq.com). The data included demographic characteristics, underlying medical condition, diagnoses, laboratory results, imaging findings, inflammatory markers and microbiological tests. Data on infections and in-hospital mortality were also recorded. All researchers received thorough training, and the participating hospitals were equipped to perform standard microbiological methods and antibiotic susceptibility tests proficiently.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM, SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviations (SD) and were compared using the student’s t-test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages (%) and were compared using the chi-squared test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model development

We developed predictive models using four machine learning algorithms: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), and gradient boosting machine (GBM) with the R software PMS package (version 4.0.3). Patients were randomly divided a training cohort (70%) and a validation cohort (30%). Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve of receiver operating characteristic (AUC), Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Youden’s index, net reclassification improvement index (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement index (IDI). Internal validation was performed with 1000 bootstrap iterations. Additional, decision curve analysis (DCA) and confusion matrices were used for assessment. The AUC value greater than 0.60 indicated good predictive value, with closer value to 1 indicating better model performance. A variable was considered an independent risk factor if it maintained statistical significance (p < 0.05) in the final model. If logistic regression demonstrated superior performance, a nomogram would be developed based on its results.

Handling of missing data

We first identified missing, Not-a-Number (NaN), and infinite values using R’s `is.na ()`, `is.nan ()`, and ‘is.infinite ()` functions, respectively. After assessing the patterns and causes of missingness, we implemented the following procedures. We removed variables with Missing Not At Random (MNAR) data that had high missingness (> 80%), low coverage, and low importance. For variables with Missing Completely At Random (MCAR)/ Missing At Random (MAR) data, low missingness, and lower importance—where deletion risked bias—we used R’s `mice ()` function for imputation. For instance, a blank hypertension field was imputed as “no history.”

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 67 hospitals from 16 provinces across China participated in the study. Of the 3,412 patients with suspected infection, 2,963 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Patient characteristics are presented in Additional file Table S1. The mean Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score was 15.75 (range 7 to 37) and the mean age was 64.34 years (range 18–103). The mean length of the ICU stay was 19.02 days. Most patients (27.36%) were from East China (Additional file Figure S1), and 92.51% (n = 2,742) were admitted to Grade III hospitals (Additional file Figure S2). Among the documented infections (with some patients having more than one type), pneumonia was the most prevalent (87.19%), followed by bloodstream infection (17.11%) (Additional file Figure S3).

Microbiological data were obtained from 11,433 samples, including sputum smears, cultures, and next-generation sequencing of blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, drainage or puncture fluid, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Among 1,661 patients, 2194 positive culture were identified. A total of 1,077 Gram-negative bacterial isolated were obtained. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most frequently isolated Gram-negative bacterium, and Staphylococcus aureus was the most prevalent Gram-positive isolate (Additional file Figure S4). Fungi were isolated from 253 patients, with candida accounting for 55.86% of the fungal isolates.

Risk factors and predictive model for multidrug resistant infection

Among the infection episodes, the incidence of multidrug resistant infection was18.06%. Univariate analysis identified 23 indictors, including age (p < 0.01), female (p < 0.01), region (p < 0.01), hospital grade (p < 0.01), surgery (p < 0.01), APACHE II score (p < 0.01), comorbidities (hypertension[p = 0.03], Cerebrovascular disease[p < 0.01], COPD[p = 0.03]), infection site (pneumonia[p < 0.01], bloodstream infection[p < 0.01], urinary infection[p < 0.01], skin infection[p = 0.02], central system infection[p < 0.01]), and specific pathogen (Klebsiella pneumoniae[p < 0.01], Acinetobacter baumannii[p < 0.01], Stenotrophomonas maltophilia[p < 0.01], Escherichia coli[p < 0.01], Pseudomonas aeruginosa[p < 0.01), Staphylococcus aureus[p < 0.01], Staphylococcus epidermidis[p < 0.01], Enterococcus faecium[p < 0.01], and Candida albicans[p = 0.04]).

In the validation set, the AUCs for LR, RF, XGB, and GBM algorithms were 0.86 (0.83–0.90), 0.86 (0.82–0.89), 0.85 (0.81–0.88), and 0.85 (0.82–0.89) (Fig. 4). Performance metrics of the AUC, Brier score, threshold-specific sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, NRI, and IDI were showed in Table 4. The LR algorithm was selected for the final model. A nomogram for predicting multidrug-resistant infection was created based on independent risk factors identified by the LR model: APACHE II score (1.06, 95%CI 1.03–1.1, p < 0.01), bloodstream infection (1.82, 95%CI 1.26–2.61, p < 0.01), urinary infection (3.42, 95%CI 2.20–5.30, p < 0.01), Klebsiella pneumoniae (11.67, 95%CI 7.91–17.21, p < 0.01), Acinetobacter baumannii (85.22, 95%CI 50.03-145.18, p < 0.01), and Enterococcus faecium (22.10, 95%CI 8.58–56.93, p < 0.01) (Fig. 5). Discussion

We conducted a prospective study of suspected infection in patients admitted to the ICUs across 16 provinces in China. The results indicated that pneumonia and bloodstream infection were the most common infection, primarily caused by Gram-negative bacteria, particular Klebsiella pneumoniae. Among the machine learning algorithms tested, logistic regression (LR) was selected for the final predictive model due to its perfect performance. The LR model identified independent risk factor for mortality (surgery, APACHE II score, and bloodstream infection) and for multidrug resistant infection (APACHE II score, bloodstream infection, urinary infection, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterococcus faecium.

In this study, surgery, APACHE II score, and bloodstream infection were independent risk factors for mortality in patients with suspected infection upon ICU admission. Bloodstream infection, which have a 30-day mortality rate of 15%, usually develop into severe sepsis, septic shock and multiple organ dysfunction, frequently necessitating ICU care [22]. This finding aligns with our model’ results. Previous studies demonstrate that Gram-negative bacteria are the most common isolates from bloodstream infection, and their incidence is increasing, especially among elderly patients [23]. Enterococcus species often associated with urinary tract infection, intra-abdominal infection, device infection, and endocarditis, represent the third leading cause of nosocomial bloodstream infection in the US [24]. A study focusing on the mortality of critically ill surgical patients found that abdominal infections emerged as the most prevalent site of infection but genitourinary infections correlated with the highest mean lactate and the highest proportion of patients experiencing septic shock [25].

We found that APACHE II score, bloodstream infection, urinary infection, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterococcus faecium were independent risk factors of multidrug resistant infection in these patients. According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 70% of patients receive antibiotics during their stay in the ICU [1]. Inappropriate antibiotic use promotes the emergence of infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria, which prolongs the length of hospital stay and the duration of mechanical ventilation [26]. The prognosis for patients who develop multidrug-resistant infection is poor, leading to increase economic burden and higher mortality rates [27, 28]. The pathogens identified in our model are well-known challenges. Klebsiella pneumoniae is the second most common cause of Gram-negative bacteraemia even as an opportunistic pathogen [29]. It often acquires resistance to many antimicrobials through plasmids encoding extended-spectrum beta-lactamases and carbapenemases [30]. Similarly, carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae are associated with serious infections, including pneumonia, meningitis, urinary tract infection, central venous catheter-related infection, and wound infection. Multidrug resistant infection complicate therapy, extends its duration, and escalates its cost [14, 31, 32].

In recent years, artificial intelligence applications in healthcare have predominantly centered on machine learning methodologies. Contemporary techniques such as neural networks, support vector machines, and random forests have been increasingly utilized to develop predictive models and identify risk factors [33, 34]. While these advanced methods excel at handling large volumes of complex, unrefined data, conventional statistical methods like logistic regression can perform equally well in scenarios with amore constrained set of well-defined predictor variables. In our study, LR demonstrated performance comparable to the other three predictive models. This is likely because the predictor variables available for patients with suspected infection were limited in number and type, a situation where parametric models like LR often remain robust and effective. We anticipate that machine learning algorithms will continue to evolve as powerful tools for predicting complex clinical outcomes, holding significant promise for future applications.

This study has several limitations. First, while we employed a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling procedure to enrolled a broadly representative sample from across mainland China, the predominant inclusion of Grade III A hospitals means our cohort may not fully represent patients treated in lower-level centres. Second, we were unable to calculate the precise rate of suspected infection in the source population due to incomplete screening data for all ICU admissions. Third, differentiating between community-acquired and hospital-acquired infections was challenging and will be a focus of future research. Fourth, the reported mortality rate might be underestimated due to the exclusion of patients who received less than 72 h of antibiotic therapy. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to use machine learning algorithms to identify independent risk factors for both mortality and multidrug-resistant infection in patients with suspected infection at the time of ICU admission on a national scale in China.

Conclusion

In conclusion pneumonia was the most common infection leading to ICU admission. Using machine learning techniques, we identified independent risk factors for mortality (surgery, higher APACHE II score, and bloodstream infection) and for multidrug-resistant infection (higher APACHE II score, bloodstream infection, urinary tract infection, and infected with Klebsiella pneumoniae or Acinetobacter baumannii or Enterococcus faecium in patients with suspected infection at ICU admission.
