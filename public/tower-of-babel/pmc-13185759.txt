Markedly abnormal semen findings caused by urospermia: Diagnosis by pre-ejaculatory bladder emptying
Authors: Gen Sukegawa, Kazuma Nakano, Yuji Tsuji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of urospermia presenting as markedly abnormal semen findings. A 35-year-old man was referred for extremely large ejaculate volume (21.3 mL), markedly acidic semen pH (5.0), and only one sperm observed in the entire microscopic field. After the patient voided immediately before ejaculation, repeat semen analysis showed normalization of semen parameters, including an ejaculate volume of 5.2 mL, pH 7.5, and sperm concentration of 111 million/mL. This case highlights urospermia as an underrecognized cause of abnormal semen findings and demonstrates that simple bladder emptying before ejaculation can yield clinically valuable information.Keywords: Urospermia, Acidic semen pH, Ejaculatory disorder, Male infertility

Introduction

Urospermia refers to urine contamination of the ejaculate and has been reported mainly in association with prostate surgery.1 In routine clinical practice, markedly abnormal semen findings such as unusually large ejaculate volume or acidic semen pH may raise suspicion of ejaculatory disorders2; however, urine contamination of the ejaculate may also produce misleading semen parameters and remain underrecognized. Although urospermia has not been well described as an independent clinical condition in humans, sporadic reports have appeared in veterinary medicine.3 We report a case of urospermia diagnosed through simple clinical observation, emphasizing the diagnostic value of bladder emptying before semen analysis.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old man was referred to our clinic following a semen analysis performed at a gynecology clinic, which revealed a watery, urine-like ejaculate. He had no history of urological surgery, trauma, or endocrine disorders. The patient had been diagnosed with overactive bladder and had received vibegron, a β3-adrenoceptor agonist, for three years.

Transrectal ultrasonography revealed no abnormalities of the bladder neck, prostate, or seminal vesicles. Endocrine assessment indicated normal concentrations of follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone, testosterone, and estradiol.

Semen analysis was conducted according to the WHO laboratory manual for the examination and processing of human semen (6th edition).4 The semen sample was obtained after 2 days of sexual abstinence and collected by masturbation in the clinic. Semen analysis at the initial visit revealed an abnormally large ejaculate volume of 21.3 mL with a markedly acidic pH of 5.0. Only one sperm was observed in the entire microscopic field (Table 1). The ejaculate appeared unusually watery and emitted a urine-like odor, raising suspicion of urine contamination.

To evaluate possible urine contamination, the patient was instructed to void immediately before ejaculation. Repeat semen analysis, performed after 4 days of sexual abstinence, showed a significant reduction in ejaculate volume to 5.2 mL, normalization of semen pH to 7.5, and restoration of sperm concentration to 111 million/mL with 8% motility (Table 1). Analysis of post-ejaculatory urine revealed no evidence of retrograde ejaculation. Following instruction to void immediately before ejaculation, the patient subsequently achieved spontaneous pregnancy, suggesting restoration of normal semen quality after elimination of urine contamination.

Discussion

Urospermia has rarely been described as an independent clinical condition in humans and is most commonly reported after prostate surgery.1 In contrast, abnormal semen findings associated with urine involvement are frequently attributed to retrograde ejaculation in routine clinical practice.2 The present case was clearly distinguishable from retrograde ejaculation because antegrade ejaculation was preserved and very few sperm were detected in post-ejaculatory urine.

The extremely large ejaculate volume and markedly acidic pH observed at the initial examination strongly suggested urine contamination. Importantly, these abnormalities normalized dramatically after simple bladder emptying before ejaculation, indicating that impaired spermatogenesis or ejaculatory duct obstruction was unlikely.

Although biochemical confirmation such as measurement of semen creatinine5 was not performed, the combination of markedly increased ejaculate volume, strongly acidic semen pH identical to urinary pH, watery appearance with urine-like odor, and dramatic normalization of semen parameters after bladder emptying strongly supports urine contamination of the ejaculate. Despite the initial abnormal semen findings were documented in a single semen analysis, the striking normalization of semen volume, pH, and sperm concentration after bladder emptying further supports urine contamination rather than spermatogenic failure.

According to the WHO laboratory manual for the examination and processing of human semen (6th edition),4 normal semen pH is typically ≥7.2. Therefore, the markedly acidic pH observed in this case (pH 5.0) is highly unusual for human semen and strongly suggests contamination by acidic fluid such as urine. In the present case, the pH of the patient's urine was also 5.0, which was identical to the pH measured in the initial semen sample, further supporting urine contamination as the cause of the acidic semen. Moreover, the absence of abnormalities on transrectal ultrasonography and normal endocrine findings suggests that structural or hormonal causes of semen abnormalities were unlikely.

A plausible mechanism in this case is altered coordination between bladder and ejaculatory function. Under normal conditions, closure of the bladder neck prevents urine leakage during ejaculation. In this patient, a history of overactive bladder may have contributed to impaired coordination, allowing urine to enter the urethra and mix with the ejaculate, thereby explaining the markedly acidic semen pH and increased ejaculate volume. A similar mechanism has been proposed in female coital urinary incontinence,6 which is associated with detrusor overactivity,7 suggesting that overactive bladder may represent a shared underlying pathophysiology.

The subsequent spontaneous pregnancy supports the interpretation that spermatogenesis was preserved and that the initial abnormal semen findings were primarily due to urine contamination. Elimination of urine admixture by pre-ejaculatory bladder emptying likely restored the functional quality of semen, thereby enabling natural conception.

Conclusion

In summary, this case demonstrates that urine contamination of the ejaculate can mimic severe semen abnormalities and may lead to an erroneous suspicion of impaired spermatogenesis. The combination of markedly increased ejaculate volume, strongly acidic semen pH, watery appearance with urine-like odor, and dramatic normalization of semen parameters after pre-ejaculatory bladder emptying strongly supports the diagnosis of urospermia. This case highlights that urospermia should be considered in patients presenting with unusually large semen volume and acidic semen pH, and that a simple maneuver - bladder emptying before ejaculation - may provide a practical and informative diagnostic approach in routine clinical practice.
