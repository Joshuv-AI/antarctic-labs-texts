Comparative effects of metformin and lifestyle therapy on bone metabolism markers: A 6-month pilot, open-label randomized-clinical trial
Authors: Mohammed Almosfer, Shaun Sabico, Kaiser Wani, Malak Nawaz K. Khattak, Ibrahim S. Almarashad, Nicola Veronese, Nasser M. Al-Daghri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe metabolic effects of Metformin (Met) and lifestyle modification in early type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) management are well established, but their influence on bone remodeling remains uncertain in under-investigated populations, particularly during early treatment when weight loss and metabolic shifts may transiently affect skeletal turnover. This study examined the short- and mid-term effects of metformin, lifestyle intervention, and their combination on bone turnover markers (BTMs) in treatment-naïve Saudi adults with T2DM.MethodsIn this 6-month pilot, open-label multicenter randomized controlled trial, 114 treatment-naïve Saudi adults with newly diagnosed T2DM (90 males, 24 females; mean age ± SD 53.6 ± 8.4 years; mean BMI 30.3 ± 3.9 kg/m2; mean HbA1c 7.0 ± 0.6) were assigned to Met (1000 mg/day), lifestyle modification, or combined therapy (n = 38/group). Serum markers of bone resorption (C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen [CTX], primary outcome) and bone formation (procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide [P1NP], osteocalcin), along with sclerostin (SOST), were measured at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months. Anthropometric, glycemic, lipid, renal, and hepatic parameters were assessed as secondary outcomes.ResultsAfter adjustment for baseline P1NP levels, only the lifestyle group demonstrated a significant reduction in CTX after 6 months (p < 0.05), while osteocalcin, SOST and P1NP showed no significant changes across all interventions. All interventions were associated with improvements in anthropometric and glycemic measures; most pronounced in the combined intervention group.ConclusionIn treatment-naïve adults with T2DM, lifestyle intervention alone was associated with a significant reduction in CTX after 6 months, while metformin-based interventions showed no significant effects on bone turnover markers. These exploratory findings may suggest short-term modulation of bone turnover but should be interpreted cautiously given the pilot design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-026-03423-2.

Introduction

Metformin is a well-established agent for improving insulin sensitivity and lowering hepatic glucose output, and the most common first-line pharmacological approach in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and prediabetes management [1]. A recent umbrella review also documented that structured lifestyle interventions, which often include caloric restriction, increased physical activity, and behavioral counseling, are similarly effective in improving glycemic indices and reducing cardiometabolic risk, although notable anthropometric benefits appeared to be lacking in combination with metformin [2]. While their metabolic benefits are well-documented, less is known about their effects on bone turnover and bone-related biomarkers, particularly in the context of weight loss. A synthesis of evidence done on animal models with T2DM nevertheless demonstrated that metformin can improve bone density [3]. In human trials, evidence from a meta-analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (n = 4,914) participants indicated that although anti-T2DM therapies as a group were associated with higher bone mineral density (BMD) at the lumbar spine and femoral neck compared with placebo, metformin showed a relative advantage over other anti-T2DM agents, with direct comparisons showing that overall treatment effects favored metformin [4]. Notably, the absence of clinically meaningful changes in bone turnover markers (BTMs) associated with metformin, together with the high heterogeneity across the included studies [4], further reinforces the variability and overall inconclusiveness of metformin’s effects on BTMs [5].

Bone remodeling is influenced by endocrine, metabolic, and mechanical factors [6]. Weight reduction, for instance, alters mechanical loading and can modify circulating bone markers [7], including osteocalcin, C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX), Procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), and sclerostin (SOST) [8]. Osteocalcin and P1NP are markers of bone formation, whereas CTX reflects bone resorption. SOST is a Wnt-signalling antagonist that regulates osteoblastic activity and may also respond to metabolic shifts [9].

Understanding how lifestyle and pharmacological interventions influence bone metabolism is clinically relevant, especially in genetically predisposed populations at risk for both metabolic and skeletal disorders. Despite the lack of convincing evidence in humans, it has been proposed that metformin influences bone turnover primarily through its canonical AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, which reduces oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence within the bone marrow niche. By suppressing reactive-oxygen species (ROS) production and pro-inflammatory nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B (NF-κB)-dependent cytokines, metformin limits osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption-related markers [10]. Simultaneously, existing literature suggested that AMPK-mediated inhibition of mammalian targets of rapamycin (mTOR) enhances autophagy and osteogenic signalling in mesenchymal stem cells, promoting bone formation and a favourable bone turnover profile [10].

In this 6-month, pilot, open-label, randomized controlled trial (RCT), we evaluated for the first time in a treatment-naïve Saudi population with T2DM, the independent and combined effects of metformin and lifestyle modification on bone turnover markers with CTX as the primary outcome, alongside secondary assessments of anthropometric parameters, cardiometabolic biomarkers, and renal and hepatic function. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first RCT in the Arabian Gulf region to assess changes in BTMs among treatment naïve adults with T2DM, filling a significant gap among under represented ethnic groups where both T2DM and osteoporosis are highly prevalent [11].

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was an open label, randomized, multicenter, 6-month pilot clinical trial conducted at primary healthcare centers across the Hail and Qassim regions, Saudi Arabia. Ethical approvals were obtained from the Ministry of Health Institutional Review Board in Hail Health Cluster (MOH IRB Log No. 2.24-6, February 6, 2024) and Qassim Health Cluster (MOH IRB Log No. 607/46/3015 September 25, 2024), Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Ethical approval was also obtained from King Saud University (No. E-24-1470, August 05, 2024). This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT06439758).

Participants

Among 208 individuals screened for T2DM, diagnosis was confirmed in 114 participants by collaborating primary care physicians using the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Confirmed treatment-naïve T2DM participants with written informed consent were then randomly allocated to receive either metformin (1000 mg), lifestyle intervention only, or both. Anthropometric measurements and fasting blood samples were collected by trained personnel at participating centers at baseline, 3 months and 6 months. Participants attended study visits after overnight fasting. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria included non-Saudi nationality, age < 25 or > 65 years, pre-existing T2DM on medication, osteoporosis, major cardiovascular, renal, hepatic or psychiatric disease and morbid obesity.

Intervention

Participants were randomized into three groups for 6 months: (A) metformin 1000 mg/day; (B) lifestyle modification (based on structured diabetes prevention program conducted in local primary-care settings and included ≥ 5% weight reduction, moderate physical activity (150 min/week), reduced fat intake, and increased dietary fiber (15 g/1000 kcal). In brief, the program was conducted in Arabic and carried out by certified dietitians and diabetes instructors, covered prediabetes risk, nutrition and weight management, carbohydrate awareness, food label interpretation, physical activity (≥ 30 min of moderate exercise, 5 days/week), and essential self-monitoring practices, with ongoing follow-up support for lifestyle modification [12]; (C) metformin plus lifestyle modification. The number of education sessions are provided in supplementary Table 1.

Data collection

Baseline data were obtained using a standardized questionnaire capturing demographics, medical history, general health status, and osteoporosis risk. Anthropometric measurements included height (cm), weight (kg), neck (cm), waist (cm) and hip (cm) circumferences, and blood pressure (mmHg) (average of two readings).

Laboratory analysis

Fasting blood samples were collected at baseline and follow-up visits. Routine biochemical parameters [fasting glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase, ALT and aspartate aminotransferase, AST), renal function test (urea and creatinine), and lipid profile (triglycerides, total cholesterol, high density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol)] were analyzed at regional primary healthcare laboratories using the Dimension EXL 200 analyzer (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The remaining fasting serum samples were labeled, stored and transported to the Chair for Biomarkers of Chronic Diseases (CBCD), Biochemistry Department, College of Science, King Saud University (KSU), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, following standardized biospecimen handling and transport procedures set by the Ministry of Health.

The Cobas e 411 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) was used for the assessment of CTX and P1NP, and the corresponding electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) kits supplied by the manufacturer. The intra- and inter-assay CV were 4.7% and 5.7%, respectively, for CTX and 3.0% and 4.1%, respectively, for P1NP. The LIAISON XL automated quantitative analyzer (DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy) was used for chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA)-based analysis of osteocalcin. The intra- and inter-assay CVs were (8% and 9%, respectively). Serum sclerostin concentrations were determined using the SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) in conjunction with a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The intra- and inter-assay CVs were (2.1% and 10.8%, respectively). All assays for BTMs were performed at CBCD, KSU, according to manufacturer instructions with appropriate quality control.

Sample size estimation used results reported by Mori et al. [13] where they reported the mean change from baseline to 3 months in CTX values of pioglitazone (15.8 ng/mL) and metformin groups (-13.6 ng/mL), with the highest SD at 36.6, effect size > 0.4. The present study used a conservative effect size of 0.3 for the primary analysis of three parallel groups with three repeated measurements. Given the expected very high correlation among repeated measures, repeated-measures ANOVA was used with α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power = 0.80, the calculated sample size was n = 37 participants per group. We expected 5% attrition and targeted 40 participants per group.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0; Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and as median (25th–75th percentiles) for non-normally distributed (non-Gaussian) data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Non-Gaussian variables were log-transformed prior to parametric analysis. Repeated measure analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and the Friedman test were performed to compare mean and median differences, respectively, in Gaussian and non-Gaussian variables among the lifestyle, Met, and Met + lifestyle groups, accounting for group effects, time effects, and group × time interactions, while adjusting for baseline P1NP levels. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation analysis, as appropriate. Primary analysis was intention-to-treat principle for within and between group comparisons as prespecified, using last observation carried forward (LOCF) to account for missing data. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 208 participants recruited and screened, 114 treatment-naïve Saudi adults aged 25–65 years with newly diagnosed T2DM were randomized into the intervention groups. After 6 months, 63 participants completed the intervention, corresponding to an attrition rate of 45% (Fig. 1). The mean age of the randomized cohort (n = 114) was 53.6 ± 8.4 years; mean BMI 30.3 ± 3.9 kg/m2; and mean HbA1c 7.0 ± 0.6. All groups were comparable at baseline in terms of age, anthropometrics, glycemic and lipid profiles, renal biomarkers, liver enzymes, and bone markers except for P1NP levels which were significantly lower in Met+lifestyle group as compared to Met group. Primary and secondary endpoints

Changes in BTMs

In Table 2, within-group comparisons adjusted for baseline P1NP revealed a significant decrease in median CTX values after 6 months in the lifestyle group only (p-value < 0.05). No significant difference was observed in the combined group as well as within-group comparisons. On the other hand, osteocalcin showed no significant changes in all groups after 3 months post-intervention. Modest but non-significant increases were noted in osteocalcin levels in all groups after 6 months. Within and between-group comparisons in SOST levels showed no significant changes in all groups. Finally, P1NP levels showed a modest but non-significant increase across all groups after 6 months. Post-hoc power analysis for within-group differences in the metformin group showed the achieved statistical power of CTX, osteocalcin, and P1NP were 0.1, 0.91, and 0.28, respectively, for baseline and 3-month comparisons.

Changes in Anthropometrics

Within and between-group comparisons adjusted for baseline P1NP showed a significant decrease in weight, BMI, and neck circumference in all groups after 6 months, with the Met+lifestyle group having the most reductions and significant interaction effects (p-values < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in both mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures over time (Table 2).

Changes in Glycemic parameters

Both fasting glucose and HbA1c declined significantly over time in all groups (p-values 0.004 and < 0.001, respectively), with the Met+lifestyle group showing the greatest fasting glucose (-2.7) and HbA1c reductions (–1.2) after 6 months post-intervention, both of which were significant (p-values < 0.01) (Table 2).

Changes in Lipid, Renal, and hepatic profiles

HDL increased significantly in the Met group after 3 months as well as in the lifestyle group after 6 months (p-values < 0.05). Triglycerides in the Met group significantly increased after 6 months (p = 0.02) while total cholesterol showed no significant changes overtime in all groups. Creatinine showed a significant interaction effect (p = 0.04), with the Met+lifestyle group showing a significant increase 6 months post-intervention. Lastly, urea, AST and ALT showed no significant changes over time in all groups (Table 2).

Correlations with changes over time

At 3 months, moderate positive correlations were observed among BTMs across all participants. Changes in CTX were significantly correlated with Δ osteocalcin (r = 0.44, p < 0.01) and ΔP1NP (r = 0.51, p < 0.01). Additionally, Δ osteocalcin was correlated with Δ creatinine (r = 0.44, p < 0.05). In the lifestyle group, ΔWHR showed significant positive correlations with ΔCTX and Δ osteocalcin (r = 0.62 and 0.57, p < 0.01). Δ P1NP was positively correlated with Δ weight, Δ BMI and Δ ALT (r = 0.44, 0.45, 0.49; p-values < 0.05), but inversely correlated with ΔSBP (r = − 0.46; p < 0.05). Within the Met group, ΔCTX was positively correlated with Δ osteocalcin and Δ weight (r = 0.51 and 0.44, p-values < 0.05) with significant and strong inverse correlations with Δ HDL and Δ total cholesterol (r= -0.75, -0.58, p-values < 0.01). In the same group Δ osteocalcin was positively correlated with both Δ P1NP and Δ creatinine (r = 0.52, 0.54 p-values < 0.05) and inversely with Δ HDL (r = − 0.53; p < 0.05). Discussion

The present pilot, open-label trial is the first to evaluate the short- and mid-term effects of metformin monotherapy, lifestyle modification, and their combination on BTMs in treatment-naïve Saudi adults with T2DM, with secondary assessment of anthropometric and metabolic outcomes. At baseline, all groups were well matched in terms of demographic and cardiometabolic parameters, minimizing confounding factors and allowing meaningful comparisons in changes to the interventions. The primary finding was a temporal but non-significant reduction in CTX after 6 months observed in all groups, possibly indicating suppressed bone resorption over time. The reduction pattern in CTX is consistent with recent studies from other ethnic groups investigating the effects of metformin monotherapy in T2DM subjects [14, 15]. This finding is consistent with improved metabolic control and reduced inflammatory signaling, both of which are known to downregulate osteoclast activity in T2DM [16, 17]. The absence of a similar reduction in the combined metformin+lifestyle group may reflect inter-individual variability or a more balanced remodeling state resulting from concurrent anabolic and anti-resorptive influences. Importantly, CTX changes were positively correlated with osteocalcin and P1NP at both time points, reinforcing the coupled nature of bone remodeling in this cohort.

In the present study, most bone formation markers demonstrated a distinct temporal pattern. More notably, P1NP, the reference marker of collagen synthesis, increased modestly but non-significantly across all groups by 6 months. This reversible pattern supports the interpretation of transient remodeling adaptation rather than persistent impairment of osteoblast function, as some of the well proposed mechanisms of metformin in bone remodeling is the activation of AMPK and Wnt signaling pathways that promote osteoblast differentiation [18]. It is worthy to note that the low post-hoc power for P1NP and CTX at early time points further suggests that larger samples or longer follow-up may be required to fully characterize these early skeletal responses. Lastly, SOST levels remained unchanged throughout the study, possibly indicating that the observed alterations in bone turnover were either unlikely mediated through the canonical Wnt–β-catenin inhibition pathway [19], or under powered sample size. Nonetheless, the observed inverse correlations at 6 months between sclerostin and P1NP, as well as lipid and select anthropometric parameters, suggest a possible indirect role of metabolic status in modulating osteocyte signaling over time [20]. Cumulatively, the acute effects of all the interventions compared in the present study with regards to BTMs reinforce previous findings from other populations, and while short term changes appear promising, longer follow-ups are still required to capture the full remodeling cycle [21, 22].

Beyond the skeletal outcomes, the secondary endpoint results revealed all interventions led to favorable anthropometric changes, with the combined Met+lifestyle group demonstrating the greatest and clinically meaningful reductions in weight, BMI, and neck circumference, highlighting the additive benefits of pharmacologic and behavioral interventions in this population even in primary care settings [23–27]. Glycemic control improved significantly in all groups, with the combined intervention yielding the largest reductions in fasting glucose and HbA1c, again aligning with current evidence supporting multimodal strategies in early T2DM management [2, 28].

Metabolic improvements were further reflected in lipid and renal biomarkers. HDL increased modestly in the Met+lifestyle groups, while other lipid fractions remained stable, suggesting a selective cardioprotective effect rather than broad lipid remodeling. Renal and hepatic parameters remained largely stable, supporting the short-term safety of all interventions. Correlation analyses revealed consistent associations between changes in BTMs and anthropometric, glycemic, lipid, and hepatic markers, underscoring the close interplay between skeletal remodeling and metabolic health in T2DM [29]. Notably, inverse associations between osteocalcin or P1NP and glycemic indices at 6 months reinforce the emerging concept of bone as an active metabolic organ [30, 31]. It is also interesting to note that an increase in creatinine was observed in the combination group, and while the magnitude of change remained within the clinically normal range, even subtle variations in renal function may influence circulating sclerostin concentrations [32]. Notably, adjustment for creatinine did not alter the observed trends, suggesting that the findings are unlikely to be driven solely by renal clearance.

The strengths of this study include its randomized design, inclusion of treatment-naïve T2DM participants, simultaneous assessment of resorption, formation, and regulatory bone markers, and repeated measurements allow for temporal interpretation in primary care settings. The focus on a heterogeneous Arab population also addresses a critical regional knowledge gap. However, limitations should be acknowledged. First is the small sample size primarily due to high attrition rate. The achieved sample size was lower than initially planned, reducing the statistical power and increasing the risk of a type II error. However, while the absence of statistically significant between-group differences may in part reflect underpowering, the findings should not be interpreted solely in terms of statistical significance. The direction and magnitude of the observed effects, together with the consistency of trends across outcomes, provide preliminary insights into potential biological responses in this population. Therefore, while some findings reached statistical significance, their clinical relevance should be interpreted considering effect size and established thresholds for meaningful change. Conversely, certain non-significant trends may still hold clinical importance, particularly given the pilot nature of the study. Accordingly, the results are best interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, consistent with a pilot study design, and warrant confirmation in larger adequately powered trials. Other limitations include male predominance, absence of BMD or microarchitectural assessment, and exclusion of individuals over 65 years as well as patients with osteoporosis, which limits the applicability of the exploratory findings to populations at highest risk for both T2DM and fragility fractures. Additionally, longer follow-up is required to determine whether the observed acute biochemical adaptations translate into structural or fracture-related outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, although no significant between-group differences were observed for CTX or other BTMs following the intervention, exploratory within-group analyses in the metformin arm revealed modest reductions in P1NP and osteocalcin at 3 months, as well as SOST at 6 months, suggesting temporal and short-term modulation of bone turnover, potentially reflecting early adaptive responses to improved metabolic status rather than sustained alterations in skeletal remodeling. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the pilot nature of the study, the limited statistical power, and the absence of consistent between-group effects. Larger, adequately powered trials with longer follow-up and incorporation of structural skeletal endpoints are required to confirm these observations and clarify their clinical significance.
