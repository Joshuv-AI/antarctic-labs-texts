Multi-Messenger Predictions for T Coronae Borealis: Probing Particle Acceleration in Novae
Prantik Sarmah, Sovan Chakraborty, Xilu Wang

Abstract
The MAGIC detection of near-TeV gamma-rays from the 2021 outburst of the recurrent nova RS Ophiuchi (RS Oph) has established it as a TeV-scale particle accelerator. However, the underlying production mechanism– hadronic versus leptonic –remains uncertain due to the non-detection of coincident neutrinos at IceCube. Indeed, the neutrino flux predicted by the hadronic model for RS Oph was below IceCube sensitivity. T Coronae Borealis (T CrB), a nova similar to RS Oph, is anticipated to undergo an outburst soon. Being closer to Earth ( 0.8 0.8 kpc versus 2.45 2.45 kpc for RS Oph), T CrB is expected to yield a higher neutrino flux, making the upcoming outburst a once in a lifetime opportunity to test—and potentially detect—nova neutrinos. In this work, we present the first model-based estimates of the hadronic secondary fluxes from T CrB and assess their detectability with gamma-ray (LHAASO, Fermi-LAT, MAGIC, H.E.S.S., MACE, and HERD) and neutrino (IceCube and KM3NeT) telescopes. We adopt two proton-acceleration mechanisms: (i) an external shock (ES) driven mechanism at the interaction ( 10 13 10^{13} cm) of nova ejecta and the red giant wind, and (ii) magnetic reconnection (MR) near the white dwarf surface ( 10 9 10^{9} cm). The latter, arising deep inside the nova system, will fully absorb gamma-rays while allowing only neutrinos to escape. This could potentially produce neutrino signals hours before the ES-origin photons or neutrinos—a unique temporal delay signature. For our benchmark ES model, gamma-rays are detectable across all facilities, while the neutrino detection prospect is poor. Only a tiny upper part of the ES model parameter space is above IceCube/KM3NeT sensitivity. In contrast, both observatories have significantly better prospects for detecting neutrinos in the MR scenario.



I
Introduction

A nova is a transient astrophysical phenomenon arising in close binary systems, where a temporary outburst can result in shock acceleration and subsequent secondary production of gamma-rays and high energy neutrinos.
In a typical nova system, the accreted hydrogen-rich material on the surface of a white dwarf undergoes a thermonuclear runaway, producing a sudden increase in luminosity by several orders of magnitude. There are primarily two classes of novae namely
classical
and
recurrent
(also referred to as symbiotic). The companion star in classical novae is generally a low mass main sequence late-type star
(
20
;
19
)
, while recurrent novae host massive red giant stars
(
56
;
55
)
. Due to the massive red giant companion, recurrent novae undergo periodic eruptions. The period of eruption can vary over a wide range from
10-100
years depending on the characteristics of the nova system.
Novae were long considered as GeV-scale accelerators
(
6
;
17
;
29
)
, until the recent detection of TeV gamma-rays from RS Ophiuchi (Oph) by the MAGIC
(
5
)
and HESS
(
22
)
observatories. RS Oph is a recurrent nova in the Ophiuchus constellation at a distance of
\sim 1-3
kpc. These gamma-rays are produced via either hadronic (inelastic
pp
interaction) or leptonic (inverse Compton) processes. Although both observations prefer the hadronic origin over the leptonic one
(
5
;
22
)
, the gamma-ray origin is still not settled due to the non-detection of correlated hadronic high-energy neutrinos. The hadronic process is expected to create both neutral and charged pions, decaying to gamma-rays and high-energy neutrinos, respectively. Indeed, the non-detection of such correlated high-energy neutrinos from RS Oph in the running neutrino detectors like IceCube, is consistent with the low neutrino flux inferred from gamma-ray data
(
5
)
, indicating that a nova must be significantly closer to Earth than RS Oph to be detectable in the currently operating high energy neutrino detectors.

T Coronae Borealis (T CrB), a recurrent nova similar to RS Oph, has been expected to undergo eruption anytime soon
(
59
;
43
;
51
)
. The past eruptions of this nova (1866 and 1946) suggest a recurrence period of
80
years and thus, the next eruption is due in 2026. T CrB is only about
0.8
kpc away located in the constellation Coronae Borealis. Due to its closer distance from Earth, the chance of neutrino detection is substantially higher than RS Oph, creating a once in lifetime opportunity for detecting the rare nova neutrinos
(
3
;
61
;
4
;
52
;
12
)
. Therefore, in this work we estimate the fluxes of high-energy neutrinos and gamma-rays from the upcoming outburst of T CrB and explore the detection possibilities in various currently operating neutrino and gamma-ray telescopes. For neutrinos, we focus on IceCube and KM3NeT, while for gamma-rays we consider LHAASO, Fermi-LAT, HESS, MAGIC, MACE and HERD.

For these estimates, we look into two possible proton acceleration models for gamma-rays and neutrino production in novae. In particular, we adopt the external shock (ES) model for proton acceleration proposed for RS Oph gamma-rays
(
22
;
5
;
21
;
68
)
. The ES is generated by the collision of the nova ejecta with the RG wind. This ES mechanism has been found to be successful in explaining the observed gamma-ray flux up to a fair degree of accuracy
(
5
;
22
;
21
;
68
)
. Magnetic reconnection (MR) produced by nova fast winds close to the WD surface is another possible mechanism for proton acceleration in novae
(
12
)
. Since this acceleration takes place in the WD wind which is much denser than the RG wind, the gamma-rays created in
pp
interaction are expected to be completely absorbed. While the associated high-energy neutrinos can easily escape and, therefore are the smoking gun signals of this mechanism. We also explore this model and point out the distinctions from the ES model that will be essential for probing the origin of the neutrinos from T CrB, if detected.
Note that, apart from these two models, alternative models such as internal shock and multiple shocks have also been proposed to explain the RS Oph Gamma-ray observations
(
23
;
60
)
.

For both models mentioned above, the secondary signals observed at earth depend heavily on the choice of the model parameters. Because the information on the characteristics of T CrB is not readily available, we consider the recent gamma-ray observations of RS Ophiuchi as our benchmark case
(
22
;
5
)
. Keeping in mind the potential variability of these model parameters among nova systems, we adopt a range of uncertainties based on different observations
(
5
;
66
;
22
;
35
;
48
;
64
;
44
;
18
)
. For the conventional ES model, we estimate both the gamma-ray and muon neutrino flux for T CrB. The gamma-ray flux is found to be detectable in all the currently running gamma-ray telescopes (Fermi-LAT, LHAASO, HERD, MAGIC, HESS and MACE). Whereas, detecting the associated neutrino is challenging, only the upper limit of the predicted flux range is likely to be within the reach of both IceCube and KM3NeT. In contrast, the MR region being opaque to gamma-rays, one can only estimate the muon neutrino flux. We analyze the MR neutrino detection prospects in both IceCube and KM3NeT. At above
1
TeV, the neutrino flux from the MR model can be substantially larger than that of the ES model, enhancing the chance of detection. Indeed, at the highest energies the MR benchmark model based on RS Oph becomes nearly detectable in KM3NeT. Thus, any detection of the correlated high energy neutrinos and gamma-rays from T CrB will reveal the details of the particle acceleration mechanism in novae. In fact, even the non-detection of these neutrinos in IceCube and KM3NeT will place stringent constraints on the MR mechanism in novae. In principle, under an optimistic scenario, both MR and ES processes could operate during the nova explosion, creating unique phenomenological signatures.
For example, spatial separation between the ES and MR emission regions can result in a time delay of a few hours between their respective signals.
For a typical wind velocity of
(2000-4000)
km/sec, the delay can be in the range of several hours, depending on the details of the nova system. For our typical benchmark T CrB model, with a wind velocity of
3000
km/sec this delay could be about
(9-10)
hours.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we describe the ES model and the associated production and propagation of neutrinos and gamma-rays. The detectability of these fluxes with various telescopes is analyzed in Sec.
III
. In Sec.
IV
, we briefly describe neutrino production via the MR mechanism and the corresponding detection prospects. Finally, we conclude in Sec.
V
.

II
Gamma-ray and Neutrino Flux Model

For the recurrent novae in binary systems, protons are believed to be accelerated in the forward shock created by the interaction of high velocity ejecta with the circumbinary medium
(
21
)
.
Consequently, secondary gamma-rays and neutrinos will be produced in the interaction of shock accelerated protons with the circumbinary medium. In the following, we describe the model adopted for the evolution of accelerated proton spectrum and the production of secondary gamma-rays and neutrinos.

A nova explosion in an RG+WD binary system is triggered by the thermonuclear burning of material accreted onto the surface of the white dwarf from the RG companion. The explosion releases a high-velocity ejecta, which subsequently collides with the dense RG wind, giving rise to a nova shock.
This shock driven nova mechanism is also termed as the external shock (ES) model
(
21
;
5
;
68
)
and is schematically depicted in our Fig.
1
. The RG star is shown in red at the center and the WD is depicted in bright white color. The yellow colored arrows around the WD star represent the shock formed due to the nova outburst.
This ES mechanism has been found to be successful in explaining the multi wavelength data (up to GeV) from many novae observations. In fact, the TeV gamma-ray flux ( up to
1
TeV) observed from the recent nova RS Oph
(
5
;
22
)
has also been well explained by the ES based model.
(
5
;
21
)
.

In this external shock model, the interaction of the nova ejecta with circumbinary medium is assumed to be similar to the circumstellar mdedium (CSM) interaction in supernovae (SNe)
(
50
)
. However, the circumstellar medium in SNe generally has a spherically symmetric geometry, in contrast to the circumbinary medium in novae where the asymmetry arises due to the binary nature of the system.
This asymmetry is usually found to be large at smaller radii, i.e., close to the white dwarf (WD) star. However, at radii larger than the semi-major axis of the binary system, the circumbinary medium becomes nearly spherically symmetric, thus mimicking the SNe-CSM interaction
(
21
)
. The circumbinary medium is generally composed of both the, WD wind and RG wind but expected to be dominated by the RG wind due to the larger mass-loss rate of RG. Typically, WD winds vary in the range
(10^{-13}-10^{-6})~\rm M_{\odot}~yr^{-1}

(
66
)
, whereas for RG winds the mass-loss rates lie in the range
(10^{-8}-10^{-5})~\rm M_{\odot}~yr^{-1}

(
34
;
26
)
. Note that
the WD wind may become comparable to the RG wind during the nova phase
(
37
;
36
)
. However, the contribution of such large wind to the circumbinary medium is still negligible due to the short duration of this WD wind, i.e., a few weeks to a few months
(
37
;
36
)
.
Therefore, the circumbinary medium is commonly modeled with only the RG wind, which mostly consists of Hydrogen and some Helium. Then the number density of these RG wind protons as a function of the shock radius,
r
, is given by,

n_{\rm RG}(r)=n_{\rm RG,i}\left(\frac{r_{\rm i}}{r}\right)^{2},

(1)

where
n_{\rm RG,i}=\frac{\dot{M}_{\rm RG}}{4\pi r_{\rm i}^{2}v_{\rm RG}\mu_{\rm p}m_{\rm p}}
is the density at the initial radius
r_{\rm i}
, where the spherical symmetry begins in the circumbinary medium. We take
r_{\rm i}\approx 10^{13}~\rm cm
which is typical for the binary separation in recurrent novae
(
14
)
. The proton mass and the mean number of protons in the wind are denoted as
m_{\rm p}
and
\mu_{\rm p}
, respectively. We assume
\mu_{\rm p}=1.4
which corresponds to
10\%
of Helium abundance in the wind
(
49
)
. For a typical RG wind with mass-loss rate,
\dot{M}_{\rm RG}=5\times 10^{-7}~\rm M_{\odot}/yr
and wind velocity,
10~\rm km/s
,
n_{\rm RG,i}\sim 10^{10}~\rm cm^{-3}
.

Once the fast moving ejecta from the WD collide with this RG wind, the nova shock is formed and may consist of both forward and reverse fronts.
The forward shock then propagates into the RG wind to accelerate the charged particles (electrons and protons). An efficient particle acceleration requires the shock to propagate in a medium that is optically thin to Thomson scattering, i.e,
\tau_{\rm T}\ll 1
. This condition is achieved at
r<r_{\rm i}
during the free expansion phase, when
the shock velocity remains nearly constant
(
50
)
. The shock then enters the decelerating phase also known as the Sedov-Taylor (ST) phase when the mass of the material swept up by the shock becomes equal to the mass of the ejecta
M_{\rm ej}
, implying
r_{\rm ST}\sim 6r_{\rm i}
. During this phase, the shock radius and velocity vary with time
t
as,
r\propto t^{2/3}
and
v_{\rm sh}(t)\propto t^{-1/3}
, respectively. The parameters of our ejecta model, i.e., the evolution of the number density of the ejecta (
n_{\rm ej}
) with the shock radius, are considered to be consistent only with this free expansion and ST phase. As finally, the shock enters the radiative cooling phase (
r\propto t^{1/2}
and
v_{\rm sh}(t)\propto t^{-1/3}
) when particle acceleration is suppressed. Note that the boundaries of these phases vary with the choice of
n_{\rm RG}
and
n_{\rm ej}
. We choose
r_{\rm i}\approx 10^{13}~\rm cm
and the maximum shock radius,
r_{\rm max}\approx 6r_{\rm i}
throughout this work. These choices confine the estimations while the shock is in the free expansion and ST phase. To be conservative, we follow the variation of
r
and
v_{\rm sh}
according to the ST phase between
r_{\rm i}
and
r_{\rm max}
.

We adopt the following calculation for the number density of the nova ejecta
(
5
;
21
)
,

n_{\rm ej}(r)=\frac{M_{\rm ej}}{4\pi hr^{3}m_{\rm p}}.

(2)

Here, we assume that the width of the ejecta is
\sim 10\%
of the shock radius, i.e., the ejecta is concentrated in a region with width
hr
,
h=0.1
. For a typical ejecta mass of
M_{\rm ej}=10^{-6}~\rm M_{\odot}
at
r=r_{\rm i}
, the
n_{\rm ej}\sim 10^{12}~\rm cm^{-3}
. The velocity of the ejecta in the symbiotic nova system can be a few
1000
km
(
5
;
60
)
.

Given this nova shock ejecta model, diffusive shock acceleration mechanism will result in accelerated protons, which are responsible for the secondary particles.
The energy distribution of these protons due to the shock acceleration process can be described by a power-law spectrum with an exponential cut-off. The cut-off proton energy can be obtained from the maximum energy of the accelerated protons,
E_{\rm p,max}(r)
, which depends on the acceleration timescale (
t_{\rm acc}
) and the timescales of different loss processes (
t_{\rm los}
) that hinder acceleration. The acceleration timescale in the Bohm limit is given by,
t_{\rm acc}(r)\approx 6E_{\rm p}c/eB(r)v_{\rm sh}^{2}(r)

(
25
)
. The magnetic field is
B(r)=\sqrt{32\pi n_{\rm RG}(r)E_{\rm th}}
,
E_{\rm th}
is the post-shock thermal energy with
E_{\rm th}=\kappa_{\rm B}T_{\rm RG}
, where
\kappa_{\rm B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
T_{\rm RG}(\sim 10^{3}~\rm K)
is the temperature of the RG wind. For the
t_{\rm los}
, the relevant loss processes include dynamical loss and
pp
collision loss. The dynamical loss timescale is
t_{\rm dyn}=r/v_{\rm sh}
. The
pp
collision timescale is given by
t_{\rm pp}(r)=(\kappa_{\rm pp}n_{\rm RG}(r)\sigma_{\rm pp}c)^{-1}
, where
\kappa_{\rm pp}=0.5
is the proton inelasticity and
\sigma_{\rm pp}\approx 3\times 10^{-26}~\rm cm^{2}
is the
pp
interaction cross-section. For our calculation, we adopt the energy dependent cross-section from
(
38
)
.

The evolutions of shock velocity and wind density at different timescales lead to the radial (or temporal) evolution of the accelerated proton number density (
N_{\rm p}(E_{{}_{\rm p}},r)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{\rm p}}\left[\frac{E_{\rm p}N_{\rm p}(E_{{}_{\rm p}},r)}{r}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle+\frac{N_{\rm p}(E_{{}_{\rm p}},r)}{v_{\rm sh}(r)t_{\rm pp}(r)}=N_{\rm p}^{\rm inj}(E_{\rm p},r),

N_{\rm p}^{\rm inj}(E_{\rm p},r)
given by,

N_{\rm p}^{\rm inj}(E_{\rm p,r})=f_{p}E^{-\alpha_{\rm p}}\exp{\left[-\frac{E_{\rm p}}{E_{\rm p,max}(r)}\right]}.

(4)

Here the normalization factor
f_{p}
is given by
(9\pi/32)\epsilon_{\rm p}n_{\rm RG}(r_{\rm i})\beta_{\rm sh}^{2}r_{\rm i}^{2}\times(\alpha_{\rm p}-2)
, where
\epsilon_{\rm p}
is the fraction of the shock kinetic energy deposited in the RG wind,
\beta_{\rm sh,i}
is the initial shock velocity,
v_{\rm sh,i}
in unit of
c
and
\alpha_{\rm p}
is the power-law index. We take
\alpha_{\rm p}\geq 2
based on the gamma-ray observation of RS Oph
(
5
)

The solution of Eq. (
3
) gives the distribution of accelerated protons as a function of
E_{\rm p}
and

Note that in principle the pions can undergo dynamical, synchrotron and hadronic interaction losses. However, the timescales of such losses are much smaller than the pion decay timescale, and hence these losses are negligible. The secondary electrons (positrons) from pion decay suffer from synchrotron and inverse Compton losses producing low energy gamma-rays below

The gamma-rays and neutrinos produced at the nova are anticipated to get attenuated during the propagation in the source ejecta and to the Earth. The dominant propagation effect for gamma-rays above 10 GeV is the pair production on the low energy photons in the source as well as in the interstellar medium. Whereas, the weakly interacting neutrinos are not affected by such losses but may undergo flavor oscillation during the propagation. Note that we are interested in the energies above
10
GeV only, and therefore, we do not consider low energy propagation effect, e.g., synchrotron radiation.

The attenuation of gamma-rays due to pair production can be estimated by the factor
e^{-\tau_{\rm opt}(E_{\gamma},r)}
, where the optical depth of the gamma-ray photons interacting with optical photons
\tau_{\rm opt}(E_{\gamma},r)=r\int_{\varepsilon_{\rm min}}^{\varepsilon_{\rm max}}\mathrm{d}\varepsilon n_{\rm opt}(\varepsilon,r)\sigma_{\gamma\gamma}(E_{\gamma})

(
54
)
. The optical photon density is
n_{\rm opt}(\varepsilon)=f_{opt}\varepsilon^{2}/(e^{\varepsilon/\kappa_{\rm B}T_{\rm opt}}+1)
, where
T_{\rm opt}
is the average temperature of the optical photons. The normalisation factor
f_{opt}
is obtained from
\int\varepsilon n_{\rm opt}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{d}\varepsilon=L_{\rm opt}/(4\pi cr^{2})
, with a given optical luminosity. The values of
L_{\rm opt}
and
T_{\rm opt}
are listed in Tab.
1
.

On the other hand, the secondary neutrinos are immune to such absorption due to their weakly interacting nature, but can undergo flavour conversion during the propagation between the source and the earth. This flavour oscillation depends on the baseline (distance between the source and the earth) and the energy of the neutrinos. Since the secondary neutrino spectra from such novae may extend from GeV to PeV, the oscillation scenario may differ in different energy range. In particular, for the high-energy neutrinos (above
10

(

52

However, flavor oscillation may still be possible for these Galactic distances high energy secondary neutrinos due to the effect of Earth matter, i.e., the MSW (Mikheyev–Smirnov–Wolfenstein) effect while propagating through the Earth. The declination (
\delta

The flavour oscillation resulted in a slightly lower muon neutrino flux than the un-oscillated one. Thus, though small, the oscillation effects can still have effects on the high energy neutrino (

Accelerated protons that escape the reconnection region produce neutrinos and gamma-rays through the inelastic
pp
collisions with ambient protons in the equatorial wind. Subsequent interactions of these gamma-rays with ambient thermal photons (average energy
\sim
200 eV) within the source can then generate electron-positron pairs.
The efficiency of these
\gamma-\gamma
interactions also depends on the number density of the thermal photons,
n_{th}
. We model
n_{th}

with an
r^{-2}
radial profile, normalized to
10^{19}~\rm cm^{-3}
at
R_{\rm WD}
.
As the optical depth of these thermal photons is
>>1
, the gamma-rays would be completely absorbed in the source
(
12
)
. While neutrinos escape the dense region without significant attenuation due to their weak interactions. The resulting neutrino flux is therefore determined by the distribution of the accelerated protons given by
N_{\rm p}(E_{\rm p})\propto E_{\rm p}^{-\alpha_{\rm p}}\exp{\left(-E_{\rm p}/E_{\rm p,max}\right)}

\int_{m_{\rm p}}^{\infty}E_{\rm p}N_{\rm p}(E)\mathrm{d}E_{\rm p}=E_{\rm p,total}

E_{\rm p,total}
is the total energy of the accelerated protons given by
E_{\rm p,total}=8.6\times 10^{41}L_{38}\epsilon_{\rm p,-1}t_{10}\Omega_{-1}
ergs, where
\epsilon_{\rm p}
is the acceleration efficiency and
\Omega
is the solid angle of the reconnection region, and both are taken to be of the order of
10^{-1}
. The duration of the reconnection process,

is assumed to be similar to the duration of gamma-ray emission (
\sim 14

The interaction of these accelerated protons with the matter deposited by the WD wind produces the neutrinos. The number density of matter in this WD wind is taken as by,
n_{\rm W}\approx 10^{18}L_{38}/(v_{3}^{3}r^{2})~\rm cm^{-3}

(
12
)

, the accelerated protons can undergo complete cooling due to interaction with the matter. The decay of each charged pion (
\pi^{+}
or
\pi^{-}

pp

N_{\nu_{\mu}}\approx 2\mu N_{\rm p}

where, spectral distribution function

\int\mathrm{d}\phi_{\nu_{\mu}}(E_{\nu})E_{\nu}=N_{\nu_{\mu}}^{\rm total}

V
Conclusion

Detection of TeV gamma-rays by the MAGIC and HESS from RS Oph has established symbiotic novae as a new source class of TeV-PeV scale particle accelerators. However, due to the non-detection of any correlated high energy neutrinos, the origin of these gamma-rays, i.e., whether hadronic or leptonic is not yet clear. T CrB, another recurrent nova of similar nature, is expected to erupt again soon. T CrB, being closer to the Earth, may produce a higher flux than RS Oph and thus, improving the chance of high-energy neutrino detection with detectors such as IceCube and KM3NeT. In this work, we primarily focus on the detection prospects of the high energy neutrinos (
>1
TeV). Any such detection—or even a non-detection—when correlated with high-energy gamma-rays would be crucial to constrain particle-acceleration models and the novae properties. This would complement the existing studies in literature that focus on the
1
-
10
GeV neutrinos and related mechanisms. Furthermore, we present the first analysis of two different hadronic emission models for T CrB, followed by a detailed comparison of their phenomenological prospects with currently operating gamma-ray and high-energy neutrino observatories.

Primarily, we have explored the external shock model to explain the MAGIC and HESS observed gamma-rays for the recent nova RS Oph. In this model, protons are accelerated in forward shock produced by the interaction of nova ejecta with the RG wind. These accelerated protons colliding with cold protons ahead of the shock in the RG wind create high-energy neutrinos and gamma-rays via inelastic
pp
interaction. The predicted fluxes are found to be strongly dependent on various parameters such as wind density, shock velocity, fraction of shock kinetic energy and spectral index. For the prediction of T CrB, we calibrate our reference model based on the RS Oph observations. However, the possible variations of these model parameters based on other nova observations result in large uncertainties (about
3
orders of magnitude) in the secondary fluxes.
Despite the large uncertainties for the typical reference model, all the currently operating gamma-ray telescopes such as Fermi-LAT, MAGIC, HESS, LHAASO and MACE will be able to detect the T CrB gamma-rays. Detection of gamma-rays at lower energies by Fermi-LAT will be crucial in understanding parameters like wind density and fraction of shock kinetic energy. Similarly, detection at the highest energy part of the gamma-ray flux with LHAASO can help probe the maximum proton energy
E_{\rm p,max}
. In contrast to gamma-rays, the possibility of high energy neutrino detection for the reference ES acceleration model is found to be low, both at IceCube and KM3NeT. Only the upper end of the neutrino flux band is compatible with these presently running detector sensitivities. Nevertheless, non-detection of such neutrinos will also put stringent constraint on the neutrino flux and the nova models.

In addition to ES model, we also consider the magnetic reconnection model where the protons are accelerated beyond TeV energies due to the strong magnetic fields close to the WD surface. The typical maximum proton energy (
\sim 10^{2}
TeV) resulting from the MR can be substantially larger than that of the ES model (
\sim 1
TeV). Thus, larger TeV energy neutrino and gamma-ray production is expected from the magnetic reconnection region compared to the external shock region.
Like the ES, the neutrino flux resulting from this mechanism also has large uncertainties due to uncertainties in the MR model parameters. At GeV energies, the neutrino flux is comparable to that of the ES model. For energies above
1
TeV, the neutrino flux from ES model drops rapidly, whereas, the MR model produces a significantly larger flux detectable at both IceCube and KM3NeT. In contrast the gamma-rays from the MR region are expected to be completely absorbed due to the high density of matter and radiation close to the WD and can only be probed via the neutrino detection.
Thus, any future observation of gamma-rays from T CrB can only be explained through either hadronic or leptonic emission at the conventional nova shock mechanism.
It is interesting to note that the emission regions of MR and ES mechanisms being spatially separated, can lead to a delay in the arrival of neutrinos/gamma-rays from ES mechanism relative to MR-produced neutrinos. The delay, potentially of several hours depending on the model parameters, would provide distinctive phenomenological diagnostics of recurrent-nova explosion mechanisms.

Note that for the MR mechanism, the neutrino emission is typically assumed to be isotropic due to the unsaturated proton acceleration. For an anisotropic emission in the case of saturated acceleration, the detection chances will be dependent on the alignment of the MR region with observer’s line of sight. Modeling this anisotropic distribution is complex and beyond the scope of the present work. Also, for the extreme MR models with a steep index (
>2.4
), the resulting neutrino flux would be heavily suppressed, potentially leading to the non-detection of these early-phase MR neutrinos.

Although the ES model explains the RS Oph observation well, alternative models–such as internal shock or multiple shocks–have also been proposed.
Though these models may not have any substantial impact on the overall flux predictions, but can be important for the detailed temporal evolution of the nova shock and add to the understanding once the observational light curve data is available. In addition to the detection of gamma-rays and neutrinos, future observations at other wavelengths such as radio, X-rays and optical will be crucial in constraining all these different models.
