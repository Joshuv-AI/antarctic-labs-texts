Dilated ascending aorta in neonates with congenital complete heart block: An interesting observation worth exploring
Authors: Lydia James George, Lamk Kadiyani, Shivaprasad Pannasamudra Mohankumar, Saurabh Kumar Gupta, Sivasubramanian Ramakrishnan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAscending aorta dilatation has been reported in some cases of congenital complete heart block (CHB). Although maternal autoantibodies are implicated in the causation, the exact mechanism, significance, and management are not fully understood. In this report, we describe dilated ascending aorta in two neonates with congenital CHB and their short-term follow-up results.

INTRODUCTION

Ascending aorta dilatation is a well-known entity in cases with bicuspid aortic valve and connective tissue disorders such as Marfan’s syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome, arterial tortuosity syndrome, and Ehler–Danlos syndrome.[1] Aneurysmal dilatation or rapid increase in the diameter of the ascending aorta increases the risk of aortic dissection or rupture, thus necessitating prophylactic root replacement. In recent years, a similar dilatation of ascending aorta has also been reported in children with congenital complete heart block (CHB). The exact mechanism is unclear, although maternal antibodies have been implicated in its causation. We report two neonates with congenital CHB having ascending aorta dilatation.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

An 8-day-old asymptomatic male neonate with congenital CHB was referred with a heart rate of 58 beats/min. The electrocardiogram (ECG) also revealed a QTc duration of 483 ms as against an upper limit of normal of 460 ms in neonates. The mother tested positive for anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies. The ECGs of parents and siblings were normal.

An echocardiographic evaluation showed a structurally normal heart, including a normal trileaflet aortic valve, aortic root, and normal ventricular function. However, the ascending aorta was dilated, measuring 11 mm (z-score + 3) [Figure 1].[2] Considering the prolonged QTc and the need for long-term beta-blocker therapy, he underwent an epicardial pacemaker implantation (Ventricle paced- ventricle sensed- Inhibitory- Rate responsive [VVIR]). The child was started on oral propranolol at 2 mg/kg/day. The ascending aorta dilatation persisted in the immediate postoperative period. At 1-year follow-up, the patient showed a paced rhythm at 80 bpm, longest QTc of 502 ms, normal ventricular function, and ascending aorta dimension of 17 mm (z-score + 4.4).

Case 2

A 1-month-old male neonate presented with complaints of low heart rate. The ECG confirmed CHB with QRS rate of 60/min. The mother was positive for anti-Ro (SSA) antibodies. Holter recording revealed a mean heart rate of 64 bpm and a normal QTc [Figure 2]. Echocardiography showed normal ventricular function and no structural heart disease except a 5 mm fossa ovalis atrial septal defect. The aortic valve was trileaflet, but the ascending aorta was markedly dilated, measuring 15.8 mm (z-score + 6) [Figure 3].[2] Considering a stable heart rate above 55 bpm and normal ventricular function, the child was followed up without a pacemaker. At 6-month follow-up, the child remains asymptomatic with normal growth and a stable heart rate of 55 bpm. The ventricular function was normal, and the ascending aorta continued to be dilated, measuring 20.5 mm (z-score + 6.3).

DISCUSSION

Over the past decade, there have been reports of dilated ascending aorta in children with congenital CHB. In a retrospective study of 60 children with congenital CHB, 52% had dilated ascending aorta despite having no structural heart disease. The median z-score of the ascending aorta was 2.2.[3] More importantly, the ascending aortic dilatation persisted on follow-up. The authors attributed ascending aortic dilatation to the autoinflammatory state related to maternal antibodies. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction was more frequently observed among those with ascending aorta dilatation.

Davey et al. retrospectively studied the association of maternal antibodies and ascending aortic dilatation in 62 children. The ascending aorta was dilated in all patients with positive maternal serology compared to 5% with negative serology. Further, the dilatation persisted in 96% of those with positive maternal serology compared to 10% with negative maternal serology.[4]

Besides the immune-mediated inflammatory state, ascending aorta dilatation in these patients may be related to increased stroke volume to compensate for reduced heart rate. In a retrospective study of 17 patients, Altit et al. demonstrated normalization of ascending aortic size following implantation of a dual chamber permanent pacemaker.[5]

Like other reports, both of our patients also had immune-mediated congenital CHB. On follow-up after 1 year and 6 months in the two patients, the z-scores of the ascending aorta seem to have increased, irrespective of pacemaker insertion. Currently, there is a paucity of data to hypothesize the cause of the dilatation or guide the management of ascending aortic dilatation in congenital CHB. There are no prior reports of complications related to ascending aortic dilatation in patients with congenital CHB. Nonetheless, we believe that the presence of ascending aortic dilatation possibly merits a careful long-term follow-up.

CONCLUSION

Ascending aortic dilatation is sometimes associated with immune-mediated congenital CHB. Although this observation is of unclear significance, it may have serious implications for the future. Larger prospective observational studies with long-term follow-up are needed to understand the pathophysiologic mechanisms and clinical consequences of ascending aortic dilatation among patients with congenital CHB.
