Illuminating the Performance of Electron Withdrawing Groups in Halogen Bonding
Authors: Daniel P. Devore, Thomas L. Ellington, Kevin L. Shuford
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThroughout the halogen bonding literature, electron withdrawing groups are relied upon heavily for tuning the interaction strength between the halogen bond donor and acceptor; however, the interplay of electronic effects associated with various substituents is less of a focus. This work utilizes computational techniques to study the degree of σ‐ and π‐electron donating/accepting character of electron withdrawing groups in a prescribed set of halo‐alkyne, halo‐benzene, and halo‐ethynyl benzene halogen bond donors. We examine how these factors affect the σ‐hole magnitude of the donors as well as the binding strength of the corresponding complexes with an ammonia acceptor. Statistical analyses aid the interpretation of how these substituents influence the properties of the halogen bond donors and complexes, and show that the electron withdrawing groups that are both σ‐ and π‐electron accepting form the strongest halogen bond complexes.

Introduction

Noncovalent interactions, such as halogen bonding, have been shown to have a large impact in the fields of catalysis,[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] bio‐molecular studies,[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] crystal engineering, and supramolecular chemistry.[ 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] The high tunability of halogen bond (XB) interactions affords their versatile nature for a wide variety of applications. These interactions are formed by a nucleophilic region in a molecule interacting with the electrophilic region associated with a halogen atom in the same or differing molecular entity. [13] This electrophilic region, or area of positive electrostatic potential (ESP) referred to as the σ‐hole, is molded by the anisotropic redistribution of electron density upon the formation of a covalent bond between a halogen and some other atom.[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] XB interactions have been modified by varying the (i) strength and location of electron withdrawing groups (EWGs),[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] (ii) size and polarizability of the halogen atom,[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] and (iii) hybridization of the atom covalently bonded to the halogen. [20] A thorough historical background on halogen bonding can be found in the review article by Cavallo et al. [23]

Lapp and Scheiner have demonstrated that both the strength of the electron withdrawing substituents and the distance between the EWGs and the halogen atom are highly important. [24] They showed that the XB interaction is enhanced with the addition of an EWG at the end of an alkyne chain; however, that effect was reduced when the EWG is pushed further away from the halogen atom. It has also been shown that the electron withdrawing substituent placed ortho to the halogen in an iodobenzene XB donor results in stronger interactions with NH3 than when the EWG is meta or para to the halogen. Naghani et al. have demonstrated how the position and number of fluorine substituents affect the interactions between an iodobenzene derivative and HCN. [25] They have shown that the interaction energy between the iodobenzene XB donor and the HCN acceptor becomes more negative upon increasing the amount of fluorine substituents added to the XB donor. The position of the fluorine substituent was found not to significantly alter the interaction energy of the complex. Furthermore, the addition of more fluorine substituents to the benzene ring and the modification of where those substituents lie on the ring have been shown to have non‐additive effects on the strength of the F‐substituted iodo‐benzene⋅⋅⋅NCH complex.[ 25 , 26 ] Scheiner and Hunter have found the halogen bond interaction strengths depend on the position of the electron donating and electron withdrawing groups in a benzene ring, and that this tracks relatively well with the meta and para Hammett constants assigned to each substituent. [27] These studies are a representative subset of the investigations that have examined the positional dependence of EWGs and the effects to the properties of XB donors as well as their respective complexes.

Unsaturated compounds (e. g., alkynes and benzenes) possess the unique property of having σ‐ and π‐bonds within the molecule. This permits EWGs to be π‐electron accepting or donating, in addition to σ. The Hammett constants, derived by Hammett in 1937, [28] have been a highly utilized approach for the determination of substituent effects, such as steric, inductive, and resonance effects.[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] While Hammett constants have been proven to have a strong relationship with the strength of halogen bond interactions, [27] these constants are strictly predicting the meta and para effects in benzene rings, leaving out the ortho position due to the introduction of steric effects. Another descriptor that has been utilized to determine the σ‐ and π‐electron contributions from EWGs in alkane, alkene, and alkyne systems is the use of the σ‐ and π‐electron donating accepting parameters (sEDA and pEDA, respectively). [33] These parameters utilize the natural populations of valence s and p orbitals in the atoms impacted by the EWG (e. g., carbon in methane or benzene rings) for the determination of how strong an electron donating or electron withdrawing group is. This is accomplished by taking the difference of the relevant natural orbital populations in a substituted molecule and an unsubstituted derivative to provide a quantitative metric describing the strength of an electron withdrawing substituent relative to hydrogen.

Herein, we conduct a thorough analysis of 495 XB donors complexed with ammonia. The goal of this study is to gain a basic understanding of how the strength and position of EWGs in a vast number of XB donors affect the electronic, structural, and spectroscopic properties of the donor and corresponding complexes. The σ‐ and π‐electron withdrawing capacities are utilized to determine the overall strength of the EWG and observe their impact on the σ‐hole magnitude of the XB donor and the stability of the corresponding complex. Statistical analyses are employed to provide additional information regarding the trends that are found between the halogen bonding properties studied. Through these statistical analyses, we see that the sEDA and pEDA descriptors for carbon atoms in the backbone of the XB donors are not good sole predictors for the strength of the XB complexes that form. However, these descriptors used in conjunction with each other and the natural populations of the principal halogen atom are a much more effective approach to predicting the strength of the complexes, as well as finding the most effective substituent and position on a benzene ring. The ‐NO2, ‐CN, and ‐CF3 substituents are found to have the most influence on the magnitude of the σ‐hole as well as interaction strength between the halo‐alkyne XB donors and the ammonia acceptor. The ‐NO2, ‐CN, and ‐CO2H substituents modify the halo‐benzene‐ammonia complex strength the most, while the ‐NO2, ‐CN, and ‐CHO substituents have the greatest impact on the halo‐ethynyl benzene systems. The ortho position in halo‐benzene systems is the most favorable location for EWGs to generate the strongest interaction strength in the complex. However, this result changes when an ethynyl linker is added to benzene, producing the halo‐ethynyl benzene derivatives. The favored position for the EWG is found to be the meta position in the halo‐ethynyl benzene systems. Thus, the complete identity of the interacting system is important to consider for supramolecular assembly and crystal design.

Results and Discussion

We examined a large set XB donors with a total of eight EWGs, three π‐electron donating and five π‐electron accepting. The analogous molecules terminated with hydrogen were also included as a baseline reference. These donors were used to determine the effects that different types of EWGs have on halogen bonded systems, and to develop a ranking for the groups that have the strongest influence on the VS,max and interaction strength of the XB donors and corresponding complexes. A study conducted by Ozimiński and Dobrowolski on the σ‐ and π‐electron contributions of substituents in carbon systems reports a ranking of ‐H<‐CHO<‐CO2H (−COOH)<‐CF3<‐CN<‐Cl<‐NO2<‐O2C2H3 (−OOCCH3)<‐F in terms of σ‐electron accepting ability in a benzene ring and a ranking of ‐F<‐Cl<‐O2C2H3<‐H<‐CF3<‐CN<‐CO2H<‐NO2<‐CHO in terms of π‐electron accepting ability, where the ‐F, ‐Cl, and ‐O2C2H3 substituents are π‐electron donors and ‐CF3, ‐CN, ‐CO2H, ‐NO2, and ‐CHO are π‐electron acceptors. [33]

In this study, the σ‐ and π‐electron contributions for each substituent were obtained for every halo‐alkyne, halo‐benzene, and halo‐ethynyl benzene donor. This was accomplished by using the sEDA and pEDA parameters for the valence electrons in the carbon atoms and the principal halogen atom. As depicted by the upper images in the left panel of Figure 1, the σ‐electron contribution is obtained by summing the natural population of valence s‐ and p‐orbitals that, upon hybridization, overlap head‐on in the bonding region (blue orbitals). These are the orbitals that contribute to the formation of σ‐bonds between carbon atoms. The π‐electron contribution is found by summing the natural population of the p‐orbitals that lie orthogonal to the bonding axis for the halo‐alkynes and the orbital out of the ring plane for the halo‐(ethynyl)benzene systems (red orbitals in lower images, left panel of Figure 1). These orbitals are responsible for the formation of π‐bonds between carbon atoms. The contributions for the principal halogen atom differ slightly. The σ‐electron contribution is calculated analogously (blue orbitals, right panel of Figure 1); however, since there are no π‐bonds between halogen and carbon atoms, there is no π‐electron contribution. That being the case, the valence p orbitals of the halogen atom do still interact with the unhybridized p‐orbitals of the carbon atoms in the alkyne systems and the benzene ring. Therefore, the natural populations for p‐orbitals of the halogen atom that lie orthogonal to the bonding axis (red orbitals labelled p‐orbital, right panel of Figure 1) are reported separately. Once the σ‐ and π‐electron contributions are obtained for each halogen bond donor, the sEDA and pEDA descriptors can be computed by taking the difference between the σ‐ or π‐electron contributions of the substituted and unsubstituted XB donors.

Halo‐Alkyne Systems

The halo‐alkyne XB donors are one of the simplest systems to compare the σ‐ and π‐electron donating/accepting ability of EWGs. This is due to the fact that only one substituent can be changed on the opposite end of the molecule from the halogen atom. Thus, a direct observation of how EWGs affect the σ‐ and π‐bonds between carbon atoms, in conjunction with the natural population of the halogen atom, can be made. These observations are determined by the sEDA and pEDA parameters for both the carbon and halogen atoms. As stated earlier, sEDA (C) is determined by summing the population of natural atomic orbitals (NAOs) that are rotationally symmetric along the bonding axis of the carbon atoms in the substituted halo‐alkyne donor (top left image of left panel in Figure 1) and subtracting the analogous sum of NAOs for the unsubstituted donor. That is, (1) sEDAC=∑i=12σXCCRi-∑i=12σXCCHi

where σXCCRi is the population of the valence s and p NAOs of carbon atom i in the substituted halo‐alkyne, and σXCCHi is the population of the analogous NAOs of carbon atom i in the unsubstituted halo‐alkyne. The sEDA (X) parameter is found in a similar way to sEDA (C); however, the NAOs of the principal halogen atom are used instead of the carbon, and there is only a single halogen atom present. Thus, (2) sEDAX=σXCCR-σXCCH

where σXCCR is the population of the valence s and p NAOs contributing to the halogen σ‐bond (top image, right panel of Figure 1) in the substituted donor, and σXCCH is the analogous sum of NAOs in the unsubstituted donor. In addition to the σ‐bonds, the substituents can also affect the π‐bonds that form between the carbon atoms. The pEDA (C) parameter can be computed by summing the population of the remaining valence p NAOs for the unsubstituted halo‐alkyne (p‐orbitals orthogonal to the bonding axis; bottom left figure of left panel in Figure 1) and subtracting this from the analogous NAOs in the substituted donor. That is, (3) pEDAC=∑i=12πXCCRi-∑i=12πXCCHi

where πXCCRi is the population of the valence p NAOs of carbon atom i in the substituted halo‐alkyne donor, and πXCCHi is the analogous sum of orbitals for carbon atom i for the unsubstituted donor. The halogen atom, however, does not have any π‐bonds. While the halogen atom does not have an analogous pEDA parameter, the nonbonding valence p‐orbitals do interact with the unhybridized p‐orbitals of the carbon atoms and therefore are still affected. Hence, the ΔΣ p‐orbital descriptor is defined as the difference in population between the sum of the p‐orbitals perpendicular to the molecular axis in the substituted halo‐alkyne donor (middle image labelled p‐orbital in right panel of Figure 1) and the analogous NAOs in the unsubstituted donor.

All electron withdrawing substituents shown in Table 1 have negative sEDA (C) values. This indicates that the EWGs are able to accept σ‐electron density regardless of the fact that the principal halogen atom is a good σ‐electron acceptor itself. The ‐F substituent is the strongest σ‐electron acceptor for all halo‐alkyne XB donors studied, which agrees with the study by Ozimiński and Dobrowolski on methane, ethene, and benzene systems. [33] However, as the left‐most scatter plots in Figure 2 as well as the poor monotonic and linear correlation values (rs,r<0.3 ) show, the sEDA (C) parameter is not highly correlated with VS,max (fourth row, left column of left panel Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13) or E bind (fourteenth row, fifth column of left panel Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13). In fact, sEDA (C) is shown to have poor correlations (rs,r<0.3 ; fifth column and fourth row, left panel and fourth column and third row, right panel, Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13) with all other properties observed in this study with the exception of the pEDA descriptor, which exhibits a weak to moderate correlation (rs,r<0.6 ; fifth column, fifth row left panel of Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13). So while not much better, pEDA (C) does aid in the determining the more effective EWGs by taking into account the π‐electron accepting/donating ability of the substituent (0.5<rs,r<0.65 ; fifth row, first column, and eleventh row, sixth column of left panel in Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13).

The ‐Cl, ‐F, and ‐O2C2H3 substituents are observed to be π‐electron donating groups for all principal halogen atoms based on the positive values for pEDA (C) in Table 1, with ‐Cl being the most donating and ‐O2C2H3 being the least donating. The back‐donation of π‐electrons from the EWGs to the carbon atoms in the alkyne assist in our understanding of why these substituents form the least positive VS,max values when compared to the other substituents (Tables 1, S2, and S7). This is due to the electron density around the alkyne carbon atoms increasing by virtue of the π‐electron donating ability of the substituents. The forces of repulsion between the alkyne carbon atoms and the principal halogen atom are thus enhanced, preventing the influx of charge from the Lewis base from being accepted into the σ* orbital of the C−X bond. The unsubstituted XB donors generate the second least positive VS,max (Tables 1, S2, and S7), which also shows that, while small, the σ‐accepting nature of the electron withdrawing substituents plays a key role in increasing the electrophilicity of the XB donors. Thus, more charge is allowed to transfer from the NH3 acceptor to the XB donor compared to the unsubstituted donor (Tables S3–S6, S8).

The ‐CN, ‐NO2, ‐CF3, ‐CO2H, and the ‐CHO (except for in the bromo‐alkyne donors) substituents are of a π‐electron withdrawing nature. The ‐CHO substituent has the strongest π‐electron accepting capabilities for the fluoro‐, iodo‐, and astato‐alkyne XB donors (Tables 1, S2, and S7), whereas the carboxylic acid is the most π‐electron withdrawing for the chloro‐ and bromo‐alkyne donors (Table 1). The weakest π‐electron withdrawing substituent is the ‐CN group for the chloro‐, bromo‐, iodo‐, and astato‐alkyne XB donors, while the ‐NO2 substituent is the weakest π‐electron accepting group for the fluoro‐alkyne donors. The π‐electron withdrawing substituents have a tendency to have more positive VS,max values and form stronger interactions (E bind in Table 1), when compared to the π‐electron donating substituents. However, the strongest π‐electron withdrawing groups do not have the most positive VS,max nor do they generate the strongest interactions (BSO in Tables S3–S6, S8; E bind in Tables S1, S2, and S7). Rather, the weaker π‐electron accepting groups generate the most attractive XB donors and strongest complexes. This evidence is supported by the moderate Spearman (0.5<rs<0.65 ) and Pearson (0.5<r<0.65 ) correlations in Figures S1, S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13, showing that the stronger (weaker) the π‐electron accepting capability of the EWG, does not necessarily mean that a more attractive (repulsive) XB donor will result. Meaning that while the pEDA (C) is indeed helpful in determining the EWG with the most influence on the VS,max and E bind for the XB donors and complexes, it cannot be used as a sole determining factor for finding the VS,max or E bind of the XB systems.

As stated previously, the EWGs that have an average σ‐electron withdrawing capability and are on the weaker side of π‐electron withdrawing seem to have the strongest impact on the VS,max and E bind of the XB donors and complexes. Therefore, the total amount of electrons being withdrawn, calculated by summing the sEDA (C) and pEDA (C) values together, was considered (C‐total, Table 1). Figure 3 shows the comparison between the total population of electrons being withdrawn from the NAOs of the carbon atoms to VS,max (first row, first column) and E bind (second row, first column). The scatter plot in Figure 3 and the correlations in Figures S2, S5, S8, S11, and S14 show that the total amount of electrons being withdrawn/accepted by the carbon atoms and the VS,max or E bind is not linearly or monotonically correlated. Rather, the total electron population is observed to be related to the VS,max and E bind by a polynomial function, as shown in Figure 3. A grouping can be seen in the middle of the graphs that represent the most positive σ‐holes and the most negative binding energies. The cluster that is centralized around −0.2 e − in Figure 3 is consisted of mainly the strong π‐electron withdrawing groups (i. e., ‐CHO, ‐CO2H, and ‐CF3). The ‐CN and ‐NO2 substituents are found between the cluster of strong π‐electron acceptors and the very strong σ‐electron acceptors. This demonstrates that the EWG should maintain a balance of accepting σ‐electron density and π‐electron density without one overpowering the other. The population of the carbon atoms alone does not paint the full picture as to what EWGs influence the VS,max and E bind of the XB systems the most. Therefore, in addition to the sEDA (C) and pEDA (C), the halogen equivalent sEDA (X) was calculated.

Scatter plot comparing the magnitude of the σ‐hole (V S,max ) and binding energy (E bind ) of the corresponding complexes to the total depletion of the natural population of the carbon atoms and the principal halogen atom in the halo‐alkyne XB donors.

The principal halogen atom is shown to be σ‐electron accepting in the presence of the ‐CHO (astatine being the exception) and ‐O2C2H3 substituents (Tables 1, S2, and S7), whereas the principal halogen atom donates the σ‐electrons in the presence of the remaining EWGs. This would partially explain why the ‐O2C2H3 substituent generates the least positive VS,max and weakest complexes. However, the ‐CHO substituent forms stronger interactions than the ‐Cl and ‐F substituents for all principal halogen atoms, according to the value for E bind in Table 1. The sEDA (X) parameter also varies from weak to strong Spearman and Pearson correlations with the VS,max and E bind (0.30<rs,r<0.87 ; Figures S3, S4, S6, S7, S9, S10, S12, and S13) with the increase in the size of the halogen atom. These trends are visualized in the third column of Figure 2. The change in the natural population of the p‐orbitals that lie orthogonal to the bond axis (ΔΣ p‐orbital), which has moderately strong Spearman (rs≈0.70 ) and Pearson (r≈0.60 ) correlations with VS,max and E bind , can therefore be used to further elucidate the best electron withdrawing substituent. Note, the ΔΣ p‐orbital for the BrCCCHO XB donor is a true outlier (greater than 2.7 standard deviations from mean) and was removed from the correlations (Figures S6 and S7) and plots in Figure 2. ΔΣ p‐orbital is observed to increase in the presence of the ‐F and ‐Cl substituents. The p‐orbital population of the principal halogen atom increasing in the presence of the ‐F and ‐Cl substituents also aids our understanding of why the σ‐hole generated is only slightly more positive and the complexes are less than 0.60 kcal mol−1 stronger than that of the unsubstituted XB donor (Tables 1, S2, and S7). ΔΣ p‐orbital is most depleted in the presence of the ‐CHO, ‐CN and ‐NO2 groups, respectively. The ‐CN and ‐NO2 substituents also deplete the principal halogen atom of the most σ‐electron density compared to the rest of the EWGs. This provides compelling evidence as to why the ‐NO2 and ‐CN substituents form the most positive VS,max and the strongest complexes. By summing the sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital values together as X‐total, the comparison between VS,max /E bind and the total depletion of the principal halogen atom's electron population can be made (Table 1). The second column of Figure 3 shows that as the principal halogen atom is increasingly depleted of electron density, the magnitude of the σ‐hole (binding energy of the complex) becomes more positive (negative). This is consistent with the Spearman and Pearson correlations found in the first row, first column (third row, second column) of Figures S2, S5, S8, S11, and S14. Hence, it is imperative to thoroughly consider the complete reduction of the electron population surrounding the principal halogen atom.

The most effective electron withdrawing groups are σ‐electron accepting and are slightly π‐electron withdrawing from the perspective of the carbon atoms. The substituents that draw most of the principal halogen atom's σ‐ and p‐orbital electrons away from the halogen atom tend to have the largest impact on the magnitude of the σ‐hole and overall binding strength of the XB complex. The groups that donate σ‐ and/or p‐orbital electron density (or slightly withdraw p‐orbital density) back to the principal halogen atom form less positive VS,max values and weaker complexes when compared to the groups that are purely withdrawing. Therefore, the rough ordering of EWGs from most to least effective is ‐NO2≈‐CN>‐CF3>‐CHO>‐CO2H>‐Cl≈‐F>‐H>‐O2C2H3 in the halo‐alkyne XB donors.

Halo‐Benzene Systems and Halo‐Ethynyl Benzene Systems

The halo‐alkyne systems are useful for introducing the σ‐ and π‐electron donating/accepting nature of electron withdrawing substituents and their effects on halogen bonding. However, resonance effects associated with molecules like halo‐(ethynyl)benzene are not represented in the halo‐alkyne systems. To explore how resonance and induction effects impact the EWG's influence on the magnitude of the σ‐hole and the strength of the corresponding XB donor‐acceptor complex, the sEDA and pEDA descriptors were utilized in the halo‐(ethynyl) benzene XB donors. As shown by the right‐most figures in the left panel in Figure 1, the blue s‐ and p‐orbitals that lie in the plane of the benzene ring will contribute to sEDA parameters (Eqs. (4) and (5)), and the red p‐orbitals that are orthogonal to the ring will contribute to the pEDA (C) (Eq. 6): (4) sEDAC=∑i=16σXC6HmRni-∑i=16σXC6H5i (5) sEDAX=σXC6HmRn-σXC6H5 (6) pEDAC=∑i=16πXC6HmRni-∑i=16πXC6H5i

Here, σXC6H5i is the natural population of the s‐ and p‐orbitals on carbon atom i that contribute to the σ network in the unsubstituted halo‐(ethynyl)benzene donor, and the analogous orbitals of carbon i for the substituted halo‐(ethynyl)benzene donor are represented as σXC6HmRni . πXC6H5i is the natural population of p‐orbitals on carbon atom i that contribute to the π network of the unsubstituted halo‐(ethynyl) benzene donors, and πXC6HmRni is the analogous sum of orbitals of carbon atom i for the substituted halo‐(ethynyl)benzene donors. σXC6H5 represents the natural population of the principal halogen atom's s‐ and p‐orbitals that lie along the C−X bonding axis for the unsubstituted XB donor, and σXC6HmRn is the analogous sum of orbitals for the principal halogen atom in the substituted XB donor. As stated before, the halogen atom does not form π‐bonds, and therefore does not have a pEDA descriptor. However, the halogen atom's p‐orbitals that lie orthogonal to the bonding axis do interact with the unhybridized p‐orbitals of the carbon atoms (sp 2 carbon in the benzene ring, sp carbon in ethynyl benzene). Thus, the change in the population of the halogen's p‐orbitals are taken into account via the ΔΣ p‐orbital descriptor.

Note, Tables 2 and 3 are representative sets of the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors. The fluorine‐ (Tables S9, S17, and S18), chlorine‐ (Tables S10, S11, S19, and S20), iodine‐ (Tables S13, S14, S22, and S23), and astatine‐containing (Tables S15, S16, S24 and S25) benzene and ethynyl benzene donors are displayed in the Supporting Information. With the exception of the chlorine substituents in the chloro‐benzene XB donor (Table S10), all EWGs are σ‐electron accepting, which is represented by the negative values for the sEDA (C) parameter in Table 2. A similar observation is made in the halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors (Table 3), where all electron withdrawing substituents are σ‐electron accepting for all halo‐ethynyl benzene donors. The strongest σ‐electron withdrawing group (i. e., most negative sEDAC ) is the fluorine substituent for all halo‐(ethynyl)benzene XB donors (Tables 2 and 3). The ‐F substituent occupying the ortho and meta positions are shown to be the most σ‐electron withdrawing (Tables 2 and 3). Note that all halo‐(ethynyl)benzene systems are symmetric, meaning that both ortho (meta) positions relative to the principal halogen atoms are occupied when the substituent is mentioned to be placed in the ortho (meta) position. Because of this fact, the sEDA (C) for the EWGs in the ortho or meta positions is approximately two times larger than that of the sEDA (C) of the EWG para to the principal halogen atom. This shows that there is an impact on the sEDA (C) with the quantity of EWGs in the XB donor. Similar to the halo‐alkyne donors, Figure 4 and the weak Spearman (0.36<rs<0.52 ) and Pearson (0.30<r<0.49 ) correlations with VS,max and E bind (left panels of Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29; first column, fourth row and fifth column, twelfth row) show that sEDA (C) alone does not do a great job predicting the EWGs that yield the most positive VS,max or most negative E bind values in the halo‐benzene systems. A similar observation is made for the halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors (Figures 5, S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46). Therefore, the π‐electron donating/withdrawing ability of the EWGs must also be taken into account.

The ‐F, ‐Cl, and ‐O2C2H3 substituents in the meta, ortho, or para positions were found to be π‐electron donating for all halo‐(ethynyl)benzene donors (Tables 2 and 3). The ‐Cl substituent donated the most π‐electron density, followed by the ‐F group, with ‐O2C2H3 donating the least amount of π‐electron density back to the benzene ring (Tables 2 and 3). This observation agrees with Frenking et al., that more polarizable halogen atom substituents are the stronger π‐electron donors in neutral and cationic compounds. [34] The ‐Cl and ‐F substituents ortho to the principal halogen atoms are shown to be the most π‐electron donating, followed by the groups meta to the halogen, then para. This is opposite to what is observed for the ‐O2C2H3 substituent, which is found to donate the most π‐electrons while occupying the para position in the iodo‐ and astato‐benzene donors (Tables S13 and S15) in addition to all halo‐ethynyl benzene donors (Tables 3, S19, S22, and S24) except when fluorine is the principal halogen atom (Table S17). In contrast, the ‐O2C2H3 group meta to the principal halogen in the halo‐benzene XB donors, with the exception of when iodine or astatine are the principal halogen atoms (Tables S13 and S15), is shown to be more π‐electron donating when compared to the ortho or para positions (Table 3). The fact that these substituents are π‐donating gives a reasonable explanation for why these groups generate a VS,max (E bind ) that is only marginally more positive (negative) than that of the unsubstituted halo‐(ethynyl)benzene donors. This also helps explain the poor correlations between the pEDA descriptor and the VS,max (E bind ) found in Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29 (first column, fifth row; sixth column, twelfth row of left panel) and S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46 (first column, fifth row; sixth column, fourteenth row of left panel).

The remaining substituents were found to be π‐electron withdrawing in nature (Tables 2 and 3). This dual σ‐ and π‐electron withdrawing character of the EWGs assist in rationalizing the very large positive VS,max in the XB donors and strong corresponding complexes that are formed. The strongest π‐electron accepting group observed is the ‐CHO substituent in the meta position in the halo‐benzene (Table 2) and halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors (Table 3). The ‐CN, ‐CF3, and ‐NO2 substituents ortho to the principal halogen atom accept the least amount of π electrons in the halo‐ethynyl benzene donors (Table 3) in addition to the halo‐benzene donors (Table 2), with the iodo‐ and astato‐donors being the exception (‐CN and CF3 groups in para position; Tables S13 and S15). However, unlike the halo‐alkyne systems, there is no monotonic or linear correlation between pEDA (C) and the VS,max (fifth row, first column of left panel) and E bind (twelfth row, sixth column of left panel) in the halo‐benzene (Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, and S29) and halo‐ethynyl benzene (Figures S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46) systems. This can also be seen visually in the scatter plot (second column) in Figures 4 and 5. Even when considering the sum of the sEDA (C) and pEDA (C) descriptors to get the total depletion of electron density in the carbon atoms (C‐total), the monotonic and linear correlations found in Figures S18, S21, S24, S27, S30, S35, S38, S41, S44, and S47 are non‐existent (visualized in first column of Figure 6). Therefore, a much better indicator for determining how effective an electron withdrawing substituent is in the halo‐(ethynyl)benzene groups is by the change in the σ‐electron and p‐orbital population of the principal halogen atom.

Scatter plot comparing the magnitude of the σ‐hole (V S,max ) and binding energy (E bind ) of the corresponding complexes to the total depletion of the natural population of the carbon atoms and the principal halogen atom in the halo‐benzene (top row) and halo‐ethynyl benzene (bottom row) XB donors.

The sEDA for the halogen atom (sEDA (X)) has a range of moderate to strongly monotonic (0.62<rs<0.98 ) and linear (0.62<r<0.99 ) correlations with the VS,max (first column, first row of left panel) and E bind (second column, twelfth (fourteenth) row of left panel) for all halo‐(ethynyl)benzene XB donors (Figures S17, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29, S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46). Note, the iodobenzene, chloro‐ and iodo‐ethynyl benzene structures with the CHO substituent para to the principal halogen atom have been removed from the correlations in the heat maps found in the SI as well as Figures 4 and 5 due to being greater than 2.77 standard deviations away from the mean for the sEDA (X) or ΔΣ p‐orbital data. Similarly, the natural population of the halogen atom's p‐orbitals orthogonal to the C−X bonding axis (ΔΣ p‐orbital; red orbitals in bottom right image, right panel in Figure 1) has strong monotonic and linear correlations with the VS,max (first column, third row of left panel in Figures S17, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29, S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46) and E bind (fourth column, twelfth row S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29 and fourth column, fourteenth row S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46). The chloro‐benzene systems have much weaker monotonic and linear correlations (Figures S19 and S20) due to the donors with chlorine EWGs giving vastly different sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital values (data points offset from main group in Figure 4) compared to the other chloro‐benzene systems. These correlations can be verified visually from the third and fourth columns in Figures 4 and 5. Therefore, utilizing the combination of the sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital descriptors provides a more complete picture of how influential the EWGs are on the σ‐hole and interaction strength in halogen bonding. This is confirmed by the moderate to strong monotonic (0.67<rs<0.95 ) and linear (0.62<r<0.97 ) correlations between sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital (third row, second column of Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29, S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46). Further confirmation can be seen from the X‐total vs. VS,max and E bind scatter plots in Figure 6 as well as the correlation heat maps in the SI (Figures S18, S21, S24, S27, S30, S35, S38, S41, S44, and S47), where X‐total is the sum of sEDA (X) and the total depletion of electron density from the halogen atom's p‐orbitals (ΔΣ p‐orbital), thus representing the depletion of the total electron density from the principal halogen atom.

An interesting point to be made about the halo‐benzene donors, is that with a few exceptions (−CHO, ‐CO2H, ‐O2C2H3, and ‐F in the larger principal halogenated systems), the EWGs meta to the principal halogen atom generate the most positive VS,max values (Tables 2, S9, S10, S13, S15). This would lead us to predict that donors with electron withdrawing substituents meta to the principal halogen atom form the strongest complexes. However, rather than the meta position, the EWG ortho to the principal halogen atom forms the strongest complexes (Table 2). This result is corroborated by the sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital descriptors, where the EWGs ortho to the principal halogen atom are responsible for depleting the principal halogen atom of the most σ‐ and p‐orbital electrons. The ‐CHO and ‐CO2H groups also participate in intramolecular hydrogen bonding with the halogen atom, thereby “pulling” electron density away from the p‐orbital that lies parallel to the plane of the ring. Therefore, the ‐CHO, ‐CO2H, and ‐O2C2H3 groups in the ortho position form the most positive VS,max and strongest complexes when compared to the same substituents being meta or para to the halogen atom. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds that occur with ‐CHO and ‐CO2H groups (and other substituents) in halogen bond donors have been reported throughout the literature to enhance the halogen bond interactions.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 ] Given that EWGs ortho to the principal halogen atom form the strongest XB complexes in halo‐benzene donors, the positional ordering of the electron withdrawing substituents is established as the ortho position as the strongest, followed by meta, and finally the para position (i. e., ortho>meta>para). These results are in complete agreement with the findings of Lapp and Scheiner. [24]

The halo‐ethynyl benzene donors, however, do not exhibit the same trends as the halo‐benzene molecules due to the ethynyl linker creating a “barrier” around the halogen atom, also referred to as a steric factor. EWGs are prevented from interacting directly with the principal halogen atom as a result. With the exception of the ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 substituents, EWGs meta to the halogen atom generate both the most positive VS,max and most negative binding energies. The ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 substituents are able to form hydrogen bond‐like interactions with the electronegative belt around the acetylene linker. Thus, the linker is slightly polarized, which increases the polarization of the halogen atom. This can be seen from the ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 groups ortho to the halogen atom having a more negative sEDA (X) and ΔΣ p‐orbital compared to the groups being in the meta or para positions (Table 3).

Interestingly, in addition to the ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 substituents, the ‐CN and ‐CF3 groups ortho to the principal halogen atom yield the most depletion of σ‐electrons from the halogen atom compared to when these groups are meta (Table 3). These substituents also cause the p‐orbitals of the halogen atom to be the most depleted when they occupy the ortho positions. While this may not be reflected in the monotonic and linear correlations (Figures S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46), part of the reason ortho positioning of ‐CHO, ‐O2C2H3, ‐CN, and ‐CF3 groups deplete the halogen atom of σ‐ and p‐orbital electrons may be due to the fact that these groups deplete the benzene ring of the most σ‐electrons compared to when they are in the meta or para positions. Depletion of the halogen atom's electrons would lead one to believe that ‐CHO, ‐O2C2H3, ‐CN, and ‐CF3 groups ortho to the principal halogen atom would then form the most positive VS,max and strongest corresponding complexes. However, besides the ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 substituents, the remaining EWGs form the most positive VS,max values and strongest complexes when occupying the meta position. A possible explanation for this may be that EWGs in the meta position are more π‐electron withdrawing (less donating) compared to when occupying the ortho position. This causes there to be more electron density in the benzene ring when the EWGs are ortho to the principal halogen atom than when they are in the meta position. This extra electron density then increases the forces of repulsion when electron density is transferred from the Lewis base (i. e., ammonia) to the XB donor. Therefore, the XB donor does not accept as many electrons when compared to the EWGs being meta to the principal halogen atom, which draws away more electron density from the benzene ring.

The ‐NO2, ‐CO2H, ‐Cl, and ‐F substituents in the meta position deplete the principal halogen atom of the most σ‐electrons compared to when the groups are ortho to the halogen atom (Table 3). These groups also deplete the halogen atom of electron density from the p‐orbitals and accept more (donate less) π‐electron density from the benzene ring. This combination of effects provides insight as to why EWGs in the meta position of the halo‐ethynyl benzene donors generates a more positive VS,max and stronger complexes compared to when the electron withdrawing substituents are ortho or para to the principal halogen atom. Through these observations, an optimal ordering for the position of the EWGS in halo‐ethynyl benzene donors appears. The position in which the EWGs form the strongest complexes (with the exception of the ‐CHO and ‐O2C2H3 substituents) is the meta position, followed by the ortho then para (i. e., meta>ortho>para). This is opposite of what was found for the halo‐benzene donors, showcasing the effect that the ethynyl linker has on the halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors.

As mentioned earlier, the π‐electron donating groups (−F, ‐Cl, and ‐O2C2H3) generate the weakest complexes for the halo‐(ethynyl)benzene systems. This is partially due to the fact that these substituents are increasing the density of the π‐electrons in the ring, which increases the forces of repulsion between the electron density of the benzene ring and the halogen or ethynyl group. This prevents the EWGs from redistributing the electron density away from the halogen atom too much. The π‐electron accepting groups, on the other hand, deplete the benzene ring of the π‐electron density; however, they also deplete the ring of a smaller amount of σ‐electron density when compared to the π‐electron donating groups. This trend – the more σ‐electron withdrawing a substituent is, the more π‐electron donating the group – can be seen in the fifth row, fifth column of the heat maps in Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, S29, S33, S34, S36, S37, S39, S40, S42, S43, S45, and S46, where the Spearman (0.75<rs<0.82 ) and Pearson (0.75<r<0.82 ) correlations are moderately strong. The π‐electron withdrawing groups decrease the total delocalization of the electron density in the benzene ring. Thus, the π‐electron accepting substituents facilitate the depletion of the halogen atom's σ‐electron and p‐orbital population. The diminution of electron density from the halogen atom aids in the formation of a more positive σ‐hole. However, as seen in the fourth and fifth rows of the second column in Figures S17, S19, S20, S22, S23, S25, S26, S28, and S29, the σ‐electron population of the principal halogen atom correlates weakly with the depletion of σ‐ (sEDA (C)) and π‐electron (pEDA (C)) population of the benzene ring (rs,r<0.65 ) in the halo‐benzene XB donors. The natural population of the halogen atom's p‐orbitals does not monotonically or linearly correlate with the depletion of the σ‐ and π‐electron population of the benzene ring at all in the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene systems. Therefore, the depletion of the principal halogen atom's electrons must be thought about in terms of both the σ‐ and π‐electron depletion together. However, even when considering the relationship between the sum of the σ‐ and π‐electron depletion of the benzene ring (C‐total) and the total electron depletion of the halogen atom (X‐total), there is no monotonic or linear correlation that can be seen (Figures S18, S21, S24, S27, S30, S35, S38, S41, S44, and S47). Although there is no monotonic or linear correlation between C‐total and X‐total, there appears to be a polynomial relationship between C‐total and VS,max and E bind (Figure 6). The EWGs that accept too much σ‐electron density or too much π‐electron density seem to be the substituents that generate the least positive VS,max and weakest complexes. However, the grouping in the middle can be seen to primarily have the most positive VS,max and most negative E bind with some exceptions. Therefore, the groups that tend to weakly accept π‐electron density and are of moderate strength for accepting σ‐electron density, like the ‐NO2 and ‐CN substituents, tend to “pull” the most σ‐electrons and electrons from the p‐orbitals of the principal halogen atom. A similar occurrence is seen in the halo‐ethynyl benzene systems. Hence, a general ranking for the EWG's impact on the strength of the XB interactions is ‐NO2>‐CN>‐CO2H>‐CF3>‐CHO>‐Cl≈‐F>‐O2C2H3 for the halo‐benzene systems, and ‐NO2>‐CN>‐CHO>‐CF3>‐Cl≈‐F>‐CO2H>‐O2C2H3 for the halo‐ethynyl benzene systems.

There is slight difference between the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene systems in terms of influential EWGs. The ‐CO2H substituent has a higher impact on the formation of the σ‐hole and XB complexes in the halo‐benzene donors than in the halo‐ethynyl benzene donors. The order of the ‐CHO and ‐CF3 substituents are inverted when comparing the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene derivatives. The ‐CO2H can generate hydrogen bond interactions with the halogen atom in the halo‐benzene donors. Therefore, the ‐CO2H group is able to enhance the halogen bond due to these intramolecular interactions that are not able to manifest in the halo‐ethynyl benzene donors. Similarly, the hydrogen bond‐like interactions that occurr between the halogen atom and the hydrogen of the ‐CHO group are very weak, and these additional interactions are not able to enhance the halogen bond much, if at all. However, the ethynyl linker, with a more diffuse electronegative belt, is able to interact with the hydrogen atoms of the ‐CHO group more effectively and repel the fluorine atoms of the ‐CF3 substituent more, hence the rank ordering that is observed.

Conclusions

We systematically studied 495 halogen bond donors with varying electron withdrawing substituents. The effects that these substituents have on the magnitude of the σ‐hole and the strength of the corresponding XB complex were examined. A statistical analysis was performed to provide further insight regarding the trends of the data and ascertain how effective the substituents (i. e., ‐NO2, ‐CN, ‐CF3, ‐CO2H, ‐CHO, ‐O2C2H3, ‐Cl, ‐F, and ‐H) were in strengthening the halo‐alkyne, halo‐benzene, and halo‐ethynyl benzene complexes formed. The natural population of valence orbitals in the carbon backbone and the principal halogen atom were utilized to determine the strength of the electron withdrawing groups by considering the depletion of the σ‐ and π‐electrons.

The EWGs that were found to be both σ‐ and π‐electron accepting formed the strongest XB complexes in all systems considered. Amongst the σ‐ and π‐electron withdrawing groups, the weakest π‐electron acceptors (i. e., ‐NO2, ‐CN, and ‐CF3) were found to be the most effective EWGs forming the strongest XB complexes. The ‐CO2H and ‐CHO substituents had a stronger influence on the magnitude of the σ‐hole and strength of the corresponding complexes than ‐CF3 in the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene systems, respectively, due to the additional hydrogen bond‐like interactions that were able to coalesce. The ‐F, ‐Cl, and ‐O2C2H3 π‐donating groups, on the other hand, were found to form the weakest complexes. In addition to finding the EWGs that modified the electronic environment of the XB donors and the strength of the XB interactions, the positional dependence of the substituents was also taken into account for the halo‐benzene and halo‐ethynyl benzene XB donors. It was found that the EWGs generated the strongest complexes while occupying the ortho positions in the halo‐benzene donors due to the ability to directly interact with the principal halogen atom. However, the addition of the ethynyl linker, which prevented the direct interactions between the EWGs and the halogen atom, was found to form stronger complexes with the substituents in the meta position. These results demonstrate that a thorough understanding of the type, strength, and position of electron withdrawing substituents is essential when considering the applications of XB complexes.

Computational Details

All XB donors and their corresponding complexes with the ammonia acceptor were fully geometry optimized. In addition, harmonic vibrational frequency computations and natural bond orbital (NBO) analyses[ 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 ] were conducted on the XB systems at the M06‐2X/aug‐cc‐pVDZ (denoted as M06‐2X/aVDZ) level of theory.[ 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 ] Relativistic pseudo‐potentials were employed on bromine, iodine, and astatine centers (i. e., M06‐2X/aVDZ for period 1–3 atoms; M06‐2X/aVDZ‐PP for Br, I, and At).[ 51 , 52 ] This level of theory has been used previously for noncovalent systems.[ 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 ] Basis set superposition error was accounted for through the execution of Boys‐Bernardi counterpoise corrections. [57] The complex's binding energy was determined by finding the energy difference between the optimized structures of the complex and the isolated donor and acceptor molecules, expressed as E bind =E complex −(E donor +E acceptor ). To confirm the stability of each structure, harmonic vibrational frequency computations were conducted, ensuring they represented an energy minima (i. e., ni=0 ) on the M06‐2X/aVDZ‐PP potential energy surface. The local force constants and the intra/intermolecular interaction strengths are directly correlated through the Badger rule. [58] Therefore, local mode analysis (LMA), which decouples the normal modes into their local stretching counterparts, was administered to further characterize the bond and interaction strengths of the C−X, C≡C, and X⋅⋅⋅N bonds in the XB donors and complexes. Through the generalized Badger rule,[ 59 , 60 ] the local mode force constants can be transformed into bond strength orders (BSO) using a power relationship: (7) BSOn=A*(ka)B

where A and B are constants calculated from the known BSO and local adiabatic force constants (k a ) of two reference molecules, I2 (BSO=1.0; k a =1.882 mDyn/Å) and I3 − (BSO=0.5; k a =0.651 mDyn/Å), used in a previous study. [61] A review by Kraka and coworkers provides a more thorough presentation of LMA. [62]

All geometry optimizations, harmonic vibrational frequency computations, NBO analyses, and where applicable, the gradients and Hessians therein, were performed using the Gaussian 16 software package. [63] The Multiwfn software program [64] was employed for the analysis of the electron density topology in the molecules using the QTAIM method.[ 65 , 66 , 67 ] Additionally, the electrostatic potential surfaces were calculated with a total electron isodensity of 0.001 electrons/Bohr3 as described by Politzer and Murray.[ 68 , 69 , 70 ] The LModeA code, developed by Kraka and co‐workers, was used to procure the local mode force constants and dipoles for each XB donor and corresponding complex.[ 62 , 71 , 72 , 73 ] Electron accepting/donating ability was determined via Natural Population Analysis of the carbon and halogen valence orbitals. [43] The shift in the σ‐ and π‐electrons that results from the addition of the electron withdrawing substituents was assessed through the sEDA and pEDA parameters. [33] The degree of covalency of the halogen bond interactions was determined from the total energy density (H(r)) at the critical point between the X⋅⋅⋅N bond. The Cremer‐Kraka criterion for the degree of covalency was applied.[ 74 , 75 ] This criterion states a negative, and therefore stabilizing, total energy density (Hr<0 ) is of dominant covalent character, whereas a positive, destabilizing, total energy density (Hr>0 ) is primarily electrostatic character. Data analysis was conducted using the pandas Python package,[ 76 , 77 ] and the correlation heat maps, scatter, and regression plots were generated using the seaborn and matplotlib python libraries.[ 78 , 79 ]

A comprehensive statistical analysis was conducted for all XB systems in this study. A detailed list and definition for the XB properties analyzed is provided in Table S1 (see Supporting Information, SI). Spearman rank correlations and the analogous Pearson correlation heat maps are provided in the SI. The correlation heat maps are partitioned into electronic, energetic, and spectroscopic properties in the left panel of the figures and electronic and structural properties in the right panel of the figures. Spearman correlations, denoted as rs, indicate the extent to which a monotonic function can represent the connection between two variables, rather than reflecting the overall dispersion of the data. [80] Pearson correlations, symbolized as r, characterize the linearity of the association between two variables. [81] The relationship between two variables increasing in a monotonic (linear) fashion would be represented as a positive Spearman (Pearson) correlation, while a negative value would mean that the relationship between the two variables is monotonically (linearly) decreasing. As a reference point, a robustly monotonic or linear correlation is considered to be present when rs>0.9 or r>0.9 , respectively. In contrast, when rs=0 and r=0 , there is no correlation between the two variables. Exponential and step functions are better characterized through Spearman correlations, which allow for a more comprehensive depiction of the connection between variables. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the Pearson correlations also offer valid and valuable insight into the association between two variables, therefore both the Spearman and Pearson correlation heat maps can be found in the SI.
