Federated ADMM from Bayesian Duality
Thomas MöllenhoffRIKEN Center for AI ProjectTokyo, Japan thomas.moellenhoff@riken.jp, Siddharth SwaroopHarvard UniversityCambridge, United States siddharth@seas.harvard.edu Finale Doshi-VelezHarvard UniversityCambridge, United States finale@seas.harvard.edu Mohammad Emtiyaz KhanRIKEN Center for AI ProjectTokyo, Japan emtiyaz.khan@riken.jp

Abstract
ADMM is a popular method for federated deep learning which originated in the 1970s and, even though many new variants of it have been proposed since then, its core algorithmic structure has remained unchanged. Here, we take a major departure from the old structure and present a fundamentally new way to derive and extend federated ADMM. We propose to use a structure called Bayesian Duality which exploits a duality of the posterior distributions obtained by solving a variational-Bayesian reformulation of the original problem. We show that this naturally recovers the original ADMM when isotropic Gaussian posteriors are used, and yields non-trivial extensions for other posterior forms. For instance, full-covariance Gaussians lead to Newton-like variants of ADMM, while diagonal covariances result in a cheap Adam-like variant. This is especially useful to handle heterogeneity in federated deep learning, giving up to 7% accuracy improvements over recent baselines. Our work opens a new Bayesian path to improve primal-dual methods. Code is at https://github.com/team-approx-bayes/bayes-admm .

Introduction

Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) forms the backbone of many federated deep-learning algorithms
[
1
,
64
,
24
,
59
,
40
,
65

, and all such algorithms continue to use ADMM in more or less the original form proposed in the 1970s
[
22

21

Federated ADMM is derived by using a Lagrangian which constrains the server parameters
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to be equal to the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
of the client
k
by using dual vectors
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}

The resulting algorithm consists of three steps: (1) local optimization of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
at each client, (2) local update of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
, and (3) a global server update of
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
using the aggregate
\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}}
of all the
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
.
The optimality condition then yields a closed loop as shown in
Fig.

1
, connecting the two dual spaces of the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\mathbf{v}
vectors.
This core ADMM structure continues to be used to this day, not only in deep learning but also in many other fields such as image processing, inverse problems, science, economics and finance; see
[
10
,
47
]
for surveys.
It is clear that ADMM and its core structure are a foundational idea for federated, distributed, and decentralized optimization.

In modern times, we need extensions that can solve the most challenging issues in deep learning, for example, new methods that can deal with heterogeneity and uncertainty arising due to distributed nature of the problem.
This would make AI development cheaper (saving compute and money) while also improving privacy, security, and sustainability.
However, existing ADMM variants do not fundamentally depart from the original ADMM updates
[
16
,
9
,
61
,
5
,
58
,
57
, e.g.,]
.
In this work, we propose a new Bayesian way to derive and extend federated ADMM.
This allows us to use Bayesian federated learning
[
3
,
34
,
25
,
62
,
37
,
46
]
, which use fundamentally different methodologies to ADMM, to directly address heterogeneity and uncertainty in modern federated deep learning.

Here, we present a fundamentally new Bayesian way to derive and extend federated ADMM. We reformulate the original federated problem as a variational-Bayesian (VB) problem where the goal is to learn a posterior distribution
\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
instead of the point estimate
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. This lifting gives rise to a new duality structure associated with the solutions of the VB objective, which not only resembles the ADMM structure but naturally extends it
(
Fig.

1
). We call this the Bayesian Duality structure, and show that it naturally recovers the original ADMM when isotropic Gaussian posteriors are used in the VB objective. New variants are also automatically obtained by simply choosing different kinds of exponential-family posterior forms. We use multivariate Gaussians to obtain a new Newton-like variant which can exploit posterior covariance to handle heterogeneity. Unlike regular ADMM, the Newton-like variant can find the optimum in a single communication round on quadratic objectives. Such results do not exist in the ADMM literature so far. Using diagonal covariances, we get a cheap Adam-like version
of ADMM, and this improves over existing baselines across a variety of federated deep learning benchmarks.
Our work exploits the power of Bayesian methods to extend ADMM for federated deep learning, opening a new Bayesian path for distributed optimization.

2
Federated ADMM and Connections to Bayes

Federated learning aims to train a global model with parameters
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
at a central server by communicating with
K
clients without ever gaining access to their local data. The aim is to obtain the optimal
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
that minimizes the sum over each client’s loss, denoted by
\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, computed over their local data. This problem is solved by introducing local
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
at each client and by forming a Lagrangian, which uses dual vectors
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
to enforce the
constraint
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
. More formally, the goal is to solve the problem shown below in the left, and federated ADMM uses the Lagrangian shown on the right where the inner-product term
\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle
appears due to the equality constraints,

\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=\arg\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\,\,\sum_{k=0}^{K}\ell_{k}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\,\implies\,\,\mathcal{L}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:K},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{1:K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})\!+\!\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k},(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\!-\!\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\rangle\right]+\ell_{0}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(1)

For notational convenience, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:K}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{1:K}
the set of all
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
for
k=1
to
K
. We also use
\ell_{0}
to denote the regularizer which in the Lagrangian is defined over
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

The two formulations are equivalent because
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
can be recovered by the optimality condition of the Lagrangian minimized with respect to
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:K})
and maximized with respect to
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{1:K}
. Below, we give expressions for the optimality conditions of the original objective (shown on the left) and the Lagrangian (the four equations on the right) for the case when
\ell_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}
:

\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla\ell_{k}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*})\,\,\implies\,\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{*}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*},\quad\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{*}=-\nabla\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}^{*}),\quad\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}}^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{*},\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}}^{*}.

(2)

All equations are straightforwardly obtained by simply taking the derivatives of the objectives and setting them to 0. For the Lagrangian, we have used an additional vector
\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}}
to highlight the closed loop shown in
Fig.

1
. The four equations on the right indicate the four respective edges in the loop.

Federated ADMM closely follows this loop, implementing an algorithm to obtain the optimality condition of the Lagrangian. The core algorithm is shown in
Figure

2
. To derive the algorithm, first, we collect the terms depending on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
, and add a quadratic proximity term to optimize the objective
\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mbox{$\frac{\rho}{2}$}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2}
with
\rho>0
as the (inverse) step-size. We locally optimize this to update
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
, which is shown in the first line of

Fig.

2
. Then we do the same with all
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
to get the update in the second and third lines respectively. The second line simply accumulates the discrepancy between
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, while the third line computes averages and sum of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:K}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{1:K}
respectively.
An interesting property is that, after the updates in lines 1 and 2, the duals are equal to the gradients:
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}=-\nabla\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})
. As a result, optimality is achieved in the third line when
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, that is, we get
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\text{\tt{Sum}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k})
at convergence.

As discussed earlier, the ADMM structure in
Fig.

1
and the core algorithm in
Fig.

2
continues to be used to this day in more or less the same form in a variety of fields. Then, a natural question arises: Are there more general ways to derive and extend ADMM? Existing literature is full of many new variations and extensions of ADMM, but they more or less use the same derivation as the original method. For instance, a long line of research is concerned with accelerating the convergence of ADMM, such as by considering overrelaxation
[
16
,
11
]
or momentum
[
9
,
12
,
11
]
.
These approaches simply introduce additional variables but do not alter much the form of the main updates. Another line of research considers preconditioned variants where the quadratic proximal terms are replaced by scaled (Mahalanobis) norms
[
48
,
23
,
20
,
61
,
5
]
or even more general Bregman distances
[
58
,
57
]
. This changes the form of the updates but the overall structure still remains the same.

Ideally, we want extensions that can solve the most pressing issues for federated deep learning, for instance, methods that can deal with heterogeneity and uncertainty arising from the distributed nature of the problem. With such applications in mind, we turn to Bayesian methods, as they are naturally suited to handle such issues. Bayesian methods have a rich history for distributed learning, starting with algorithms such as Kalman filtering
[
28
]
, forward-backward algorithm
[
50
]
, expectation maximization
[
14
]
, and their junction-tree versions
[
35
]
.
These have been used to solve decentralized and distributed problems in signal processing
[
42
,
15
]
, but work on federated deep learning is not extensive yet. Existing works in Bayesian federated learning
[
3
,
34
,
25
,
62
,
37
,
46
]
use fundamentally different methodologies to ADMM.
Our goal here is bridge this gap, so that Bayesian ideas can be naturally incorporated in ADMM and used to improve it.

Our work is inspired by a recent work by
Swaroop et al. 2025
who show a connection between federated ADMM and a variational Bayesian method called Partitioned Variational Inference (PVI)
[
6
]
. They show a line-by-line correspondence between the ADMM and PVI updates, and use it to derive new extensions.
The server update in
[
54
]
is different from that used in ADMM and corresponds what is used in the Alternating Minimization Algorithm (AMA)
[
55
]
(we discuss these details in
Appendix

A
).
As a result, their framework is not able to derive and extend ADMM.
In the next section, we describe our new proposal to fix these issues, as well as provide further understanding of the intriguing similarities between PVI and ADMM using the Bayesian duality structure from
Figure

1
.
These insights will lead to algorithms that are both computationally cheaper and empirically better than
Swaroop et al. 2025

3
A New Bayesian Duality Structure

Here, we present our new Bayesian duality structure shown in
Figure

1
(right), and then use it to derive the BayesADMM algorithm shown in
Figure

2
. Bayesian duality follows from the optimality condition of a VB objective which lifts
Equation

1
to the space of exponential-family (EF) distributions
\mathcal{Q}
,

\bar{q}^{*}=\underset{{q\in\mathcal{Q}}}{\argmin}~\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+{\KLop}{}\left(q\,\|\,\pi_{0}\right){},\,\text{ where }\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\exp\left(\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle-A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

(3)

Here, the second term is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between an exponential family
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and the prior
\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\propto e^{-\ell_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
.
The distribution
q
is defined using natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, sufficient statistics
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and an inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle
between them.
The log-partition function
A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is a convex function to ensure that
q
normalizes to one, but it also defines a dual-coordinate system which is important for us to derive Bayesian Duality. Essentially, we can define a dual-coordinate
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}=\nabla A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, also known as the expectation parameter. The pair
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
constitutes a dual-coordinate system
[
4
]
which we use to define the two space shown in
Figure

1
(right).
A self-contained introduction to EFs is in
Appendix

B
and more details are in
Wainwright et al. 2008
.

3.1
Bayesian Duality and BayesADMM

We start by writing the stationary condition for the VB objective, which takes a similar to the stationarity condition in
Eq.

2
. This is derived by taking the derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
,

\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}{\KLop}{}\left(\bar{q}^{*}\,\|\,\pi_{0}\right){}=\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}^{*}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\quad\implies\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}=-\sum_{k=0}^{K}\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}^{*}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\bigl|_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}$}=\text{$\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}^{*}$}}.

(4)

The simplification at the right follows as shown by
Khan & Rue 2023
. They also show that the gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is the natural-gradient with respect to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}

[
31
, Eq. 4]
.
An important aspect of the condition is that it exploits the
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
coordinate system where gradients computed in the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space are mapped to the
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-space. This is analogous to the use of the
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$},\boldsymbol{\theta})
pair in
Eq.

2
, where the gradient in
\mathbf{v}
-space is mapped to an element in the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-space. For VB, the gradient is
replaced by natural-gradients
\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}
and the two spaces
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
are duals of each other, with the map
\nabla A
.

We will exploit the dual-coordinate system to generalize the Lagrangian from
Equation

1
. We denote the server and client distributions by
\bar{q}
and
q_{k}
respectively, and their respective dual-pairs are denoted by
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}})
and
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k})
. Then, instead of the constraint
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}
, we constrain the respective expectation parameters
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}=\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}
. This choice of coordinates is essential to our proposal. Exponential families are derived using expectation constraints, and natural parameters are Lagrange multipliers (see
Appendix

B
). Using
\boldsymbol{\mu}
as a primal variable aligns with this duality of exponential families. We will also see that the use of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-coordinates in the primal yields addition and subtraction in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-space in the updates which is natural as it corresponds to multiplication or division of exponential family distributions. This property is used to obtain mirror-descent formulations of Bayesian learning
[
31
, Eq. 22]
. With these considerations in mind, we propose the following Lagrangian,

\mathcal{L}(\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{1:K},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{1:K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\!+\!\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}\!-\!\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}\rangle\right)+{\KLop}{}\left(\bar{q}\,\|\,\pi_{0}\right){},

(5)

which is analogous to the one shown in
Equation

1
but now formulated in dual-coordinates. There are four main differences: the loss
\ell_{k}
is replaced by its expectation, the inner product corresponds to the one used to define
q
, Lagrange multipliers are denoted by
\smash{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}}
, and instead of
\ell_{0}
we now have a KL term to the prior
\pi_{0}
.
As before, by taking the derivative of the Lagrangian over the individual arguments, we get the first three conditions,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}^{*}=\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}^{*},\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}^{*}=-\nabla_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}]\big|_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}$}=\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}^{*}$}},\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}^{*},\quad\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}^{*}=\nabla A(\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}),

(6)

and the last equation is the EF’s dual map. These four equations together give us the Bayesian Duality structure shown in
Figure

1
. The structure mirrors the ADMM one but uses the more general
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
coordinates. The four edges of Bayesian Duality can be implemented in the same manner as ADMM. Lagrangian formulations of variational-Bayesian problems have been used by Khan et al.
[
32
,
29
]
for dual optimization of Gaussian latent models, but they do not exploit the duality of exponential families, rather using mean-covariance parametrizations of Gaussians. Similar to us,
Adam et al. 2021
propose a Lagrangian in
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
-coordinates but with a different goal of improving SVGPs.

An algorithm to solve the optimality condition in
Eq.

6
is shown in
Fig.

2
. We call it BayesADMM because it follows the same steps as ADMM but is derived from the VB objective. The updates of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
follow by collecting all their terms in the Lagrangian, and then adding a proximal term. One main difference is that instead of using the quadratic proximity term, we use a KL term. The resulting updates are shown below:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}\leftarrow\argmin_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}$}}\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}\rangle+\rho{\KLop}{}\left(q_{k}\,\|\,\bar{q}\right){},

(7)

[

, and (ii) there are learning rates

The derivation of line 2 of BayesADMM fundamentally departs from the standard procedure. Typically, we would simply maximize the Lagrangian with respect to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
, but we do not do this. We view
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-coordinates in the dual update, as they play a similar role (see
Appendix

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\leftarrow\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}+\rho(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\quad\implies\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}=-\widetilde{\nabla}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}].

(9)

The second equation follows from the optimality condition of lines 1 and 2. This update step also marks a fundamental departure from other generalizations of ADMM used in the optimization literature
[
58
,
57
,
7
,
44
]
, which use increments in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
-space, and also from nonlinear augmented Lagrangian approaches
[
38
,
17
,
8
,
45
]
. In practice, these differences also improve convergence of our method, see
Figures

3a
and
3b
for a comparison to BregmanADMM
[

]
.

We also note that this matches with the (damped) dual update in PVI
[
6
,
54
]
which we explain in
Appendix

.
In PVI, the updates follow a Bayesian framework without any connection to duality, Lagrange multipliers or ADMM-like methods. PVI can diverge without an additional damping term and when only damping in the dual update is used
[
6
]
, convergence is still slower.

that the dual update in PVI with damping is related to a local variant of the Bayesian learning rule (BLR)

]
.

]

3.2
Deriving Federated ADMM from Bayesian Duality

We now show that BayesADMM naturally recovers the original ADMM when isotropic Gaussian posteriors are used in the VB objective, and later extend ADMM by using a more flexible posterior form. Given the form of the posterior, the procedure is straightforward. We just do the following:

1.

Identify the EF’s
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
pairs and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, for example, using
Nielsen & Garcia 2009
.

2.

Derive the form of the natural gradient, for example, using
Khan & Rue 2023
.

We illustrate the procedure to derive ADMM by using isotropic Gaussians
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$})
. For this choice, we can write all the necessary quantities for step 1 and 2 below,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\quad\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\quad\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\theta},\quad\nabla_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\nabla\ell_{k}].

(10)

The last equality is due to Bonnet’s theorem
[
51
]
which implies that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
lies in the gradient space, similarly to
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
. Because the KL divergence between two Gaussians with identity covariance is simply the squared Euclidean distance, we can write line 1 of BayesADMM as,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k}\leftarrow\argmin_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k}$}}\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k}-\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\|^{2}.

(11)

Line 1 in ADMM (
Figure

2
) is recovered by approximating
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\nabla\ell_{k}]\approx\ell_{k}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})
. This is the so-called delta approximation
[
31
, Sec. 1.3.1]
, which is also used by
Swaroop et al. 2025
to derive connections to ADMM. In general, we can perform a delta approximation in the optimality condition of line 1 of BayesADMM to obtain a method which solves the original objective in
Equation

1
, which for Gaussians is related to the Laplace approximation method. Derivation of other lines follow in a similar fashion. A detailed derivation is in
Appendix

C
. It is also possible to keep the expectation in the update, in which case this will use a Gaussian loss smoothing.
This is closely related to sharpness-aware minimization (SAM)
[
19
,
41
]
which works well in federated learning
[
49
,
18
]
. Our new BayesADMM naturally includes such ideas.

3.3
Extending Federated ADMM using Bayesian Duality

We show a simple extension by considering a Gaussians with diagonal covariances which then leads to a fast and scalable variant of ADMM with Adam-like diagonal adaptive preconditioning. We consider such extensions to exploit posterior covariance and better handle heterogeneity. Denoting
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{diag}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$})^{-1})
where
\mathbf{s}
is the precision vector, we can write the following,

\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}),\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}^{2}+1/\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}),\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2}),\,\nabla_{\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}]=\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{k}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}$}_{k}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k},\,\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}$}_{k}\right).

(12)

Here, all multiplication (such as
\mathbf{s}
\mathbf{m}
) and divisions (such as
1/\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}
) are element wise. The last equation is obtained from
Khan & Rue 2023
where we denote the expected gradient and Hessian by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\nabla\ell_{k}]
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}$}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\mbox{$\mbox{diag}$}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{k})]
, respectively. Using these in line 1-3 of BayesADMM gives us a new Adam-like extension of ADMM shown in
Algorithm

1
. A detailed derivation is in
Appendix

C
.

We now briefly describe the algorithm to show that its implementation is nearly identical to the standard ADMM and as easy. Line 4 in
Algorithm

1
implements the first line in BayesADMM. This is obtained by essentially expanding defining
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k},-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k})
with an additional dual variable
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}
to handle the constraints in the precision
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}_{k}
. Therefore, the dual term becomes
\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}\boldsymbol{\theta})/2
. The second term is new and is highlighted in red in line 4. Similarly, the KL divergence between the Gaussian gives rise to a scaling of the quadratic term by
\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}}
which is also highlighted in red. We propose to implement line 4 by using the IVON optimizer
[
53
]
which automatically yields both
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}_{k}
for the VB training. It has no additional overhead over standard training with Adam.

Line 5 and 6 implement the second line in BayesADMM and they are straightforwardly obtained by simply plugging the definitions from
Eq.

12
. Line 6 updates the dual variable of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}_{k}
but just involves addition and subtraction which are easy to implement. Finally, line 9-11 implement the third line of BayesADMM and these are simple additions of natural parameters and the dual variables. These are also easy to implement. In line 6, we use a different step-size
\gamma
for the dual
update which we found to give better results (also a choice that is common in the literature
[
57
]
).

To summarize, the new extension has almost the same cost as Adam and is as easy to implement. It adds three new lines (6,10,11) all of which involve simple addition and subtraction, while the line 4 can be implemented by IVON whose cost is the same as Adam. Communication cost is doubled compared to federated ADMM as we send both a mean and diagonal covariance vector, similarly to other preconditioned or Bayesian methods
[
54
]
. However, this is expected to help in cases with
heterogeneity.

On the toy illustrations in
Figures

3a
and
3b

Appendix

C
. Similar to message passing algorithms
[
60
,
33
]
, this method can converge in a single round of communication, which is unlike ADMM and BregmanADMM
[
58
]
.

5
Discussion

We introduce a Bayesian Duality structure, from which an extension of ADMM that learns distributions
\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
instead of point estimates
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
naturally follows. For Gaussians with fixed variance, we recover standard ADMM and general Gaussians give Newton-like methods. These show good performance when compared to recent baselines. Other approximating distributions may lead to new interesting splitting algorithms, and more generally, our work opens up new research path to extend and improve primal-dual splitting optimization algorithms using Bayesian ideas.

There are some limitations of the approach. ADMM is usually formulated for general linear (or even nonlinear) constraints, whereas here we only consider the ‘consensus’ (federated) setting. To make the method easily scalable for deep learning, we used diagonal covariances. While this performed better than federated ADMM (which uses a fixed scalar covariance) and all other baselines, more general structured covariances are expected to give larger benefits.

Appendix A
Partitioned Variational Inference (PVI)

Here, we discuss the difference between BayesADMM and PVI. Unlike PVI, BayesADMM in
Figure

2
uses the learning rate
\rho
in all three updates (
\alpha
is also derived from
\rho
), whereas PVI does not use any learning rates in its raw form
[
6
]
. The learning rate is essential for convergence and it is well-known that splitting algorithms can diverge if learning-rates are not carefully chosen
[
55
, Eq. 3.4d]
. To get a method that works well in practice,
Swaroop et al. 2025
used a learning rate
\rho
in the dual update with a justification of damping (similarly to
Ashman et al. 2022
) but they did not use it in the update of
q_{k}
.

To see the above, we rewrite PVI as introduced in
Ashman et al. 2022
, bringing it closer to BayesADMM. The first step is a local update of the distribution
q_{k}
which takes the following form,

\displaystyle q_{k}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\argmax_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}~\int q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\frac{\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{k}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

(13)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}~\mathbb{E}_{q}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\log\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+{\KLop}{}\left(q\,\|\,\bar{q}\right){},

(14)

where for the equality step, we switched from maximization to minimization and replaced the likelihood with a loss
\ell_{k}
. We can see that the second step for
\log\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle
matches line 1 in
Figure

2
, but as discussed above, there is no step-size
\rho
in front of the KL-term.
\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are so-called site-functions
[
6
]
which aim to approximate the likelihood, but as we can see, they play a similar rule to Lagrange multipliers.

Ashman et al. 2022
then update
\hat{t}_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leftarrow\frac{q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\quad\implies\quad\log\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leftarrow\log\hat{t}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+(\log q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\log\bar{q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})).

(15)

Taking the log, we can see that this is the dual update step in line 2 of
Figure

2
, since we can write the update purely in terms of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. But again, there is no step-size.
Ashman et al. 2022
also consider a damping factor, which then recovers exactly line 2 and matches
Equation

9
.

Finally, the server update in
Ashman et al. 2022
is given by,

\displaystyle\bar{q}^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto\bar{q}^{(t-1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\hat{t}_{k}^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\hat{t}_{k}^{(t-1)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\propto\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\hat{t}_{k}^{(t)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(16)

where the last step holds when
\hat{t}_{k}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=1
and
\bar{q}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which is the initialization condition in
Ashman et al. 2022
. The last step can be seen by recursively applying the definition of
\bar{q}
and noticing that successive terms in the product cancel out in a telescoping fashion.
Again, taking the log, this recovers line 3 in
Figure

2
when
\alpha=1
.

The use of
\alpha=1
in the server update of PVI makes the method more similar to the Alternating Minimization Algorithm (AMA) of
Tseng 1991
than ADMM which uses a general
\alpha
(derived from
\rho
). AMA does not use a proximal-term on the server, which is similar to the PVI update. However, AMA also uses a step-size in the primal and dual updates, whereas PVI only uses a step-size in the dual (when damping is enabled). From the convergence proof of AMA, it is clear that step-sizes are required to obtain a convergent method, see
[
55
, Eq. 3.4d]
.

Finally, we connect PVI with damping to the Bayesian learning rule.
Taking the optimality condition of
Equation

14
for an exponential-family
q
and inserting it into the dual step
Equation

15
with damping, we get:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{k}+\rho\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[\ell_{k}]\bigl|_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}=\text{$\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}$}}.

(17)

This is a reformulation of the Bayesian learning rule
[
31
]
in terms of local parameters, see
Khan & Rue 2023
(see also
Khan & Lin 2017
), except that the natural gradients are evaluated at
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{k}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

Appendix B
Background on Exponential Families

Many commonly used distributions such as Gaussians, Categorical or Gamma belong to an exponential family.
Viewing them as such provides us with a rich dual structure which we exploint in this paper.
Table

3
provides an overview over all exponential families used in this paper.
Here, we give a short introduction and refer the interested reader to
Wainwright et al. 2008
for more details and a rigorous treatment. Following
Jaynes 1957
, we introduce exponential families as maximum-entropy solutions of a constrained optimization problem. We assume a base measure
h(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and then search for a distribution
q
in the space of all possible distributions which has maximum entropy (relative to
h
) and whose expectation under sufficient statistics
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
matches some given moments
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. There are many distributions which share the same moments
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, but only one has the maximum entropy (see
Figure

5a
for an example with uniform base-measure
h(\boldsymbol{\theta})=1
). The maximum entropy distribution is given as the solution to the following optimization problem:

q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\argmin_{q(\boldsymbol{\theta})}~{\KLop}{}\left(q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\|\,h(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right){}\,\text{ s.t. }\,\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$},\,q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0,\,\int q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}=1.

(18)

Similar as in
Jaynes 1957
, introducing Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
for the moment constraints and another Lagrange multiplier
\alpha
for the normalization constraint, we get the Lagrangian,

\mathcal{L}(q,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\alpha)={\KLop}{}\left(q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\|\,h(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right){}+\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]-\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}\rangle+\alpha\left(1-\int q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\right).

(19)

Taking the first variation of the Lagrangian with respect to
q
and setting it to zero, we arrive at,

q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=h(\boldsymbol{\theta})\exp(\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle-\alpha),

(20)

which for
\alpha=A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\log\int\exp(\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle)h(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the definition of the exponential family as provided in the main text in
Equation

3
, where we did set
h(\boldsymbol{\theta})=1
. We also see that the Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are the natural parameters of the exponential family.

The Lagrangian we used in the main text to derive our BayesADMM method closely follows this dual structure of the exponential family. The above derivation provides an additional motivation why one should use the natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
in the dual update of our method in
Equation

9
: They live in the same space of Lagrange multipliers to expectation constraints.

As mentioned in the main text, Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and moments
\boldsymbol{\mu}
form a dual coordinate system, where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}=\nabla A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
. The map
\nabla A:\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
maps from the space of valid natural parameters
\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}:A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})<\infty\}
to the space of valid moments
\mathcal{M}=\{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]:q\in\mathcal{Q}\}
. The inverse is given by
\nabla A^{*}:\mathcal{M}\to\Omega
, where
A^{*}
denotes the convex conjugate of
A
. For an illustration of the two spaces
\Omega
and
\mathcal{M}
on the example of Gaussian distributions, see
Figure

5b
.

Some Background on Federated ADMM

For reader’s convenience, we provide a short derivation of federated ADMM as shown in
Figure

2
. The method is a standard application of ADMM to the consensus problems with regularization in
Equation

1
, see
Boyd et al. 2011
. Compared to the algorithm presented by
Boyd et al. 2011
, we reorder the variables so that the dual update is performed directly after the client step. The reordering is still a valid instance of ADMM and falls within the reach of standard theory (the role of the two functions in the splitting can be reversed). The updates are as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\leftarrow\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}}\,\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}+\smash{\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|^{2},}

(21)

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}\leftarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}+\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(22)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\,\ell_{0}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}\|^{2}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right).

(23)

The first two steps are the same as in Federated ADMM as shown in
Figure

2
.

To see how the third step matches the one in
Figure

2
we use
\ell_{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}
. Then, the server
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
-update has a simple solution:

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\rho(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}\right)=0\quad\implies\quad(1+K\rho)\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}K\rho\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}.

(24)

Dividing the above equation by
1+K\rho
we get,

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(1-\alpha)\,\text{\tt{Mean}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1:K})+\alpha\,\text{\tt{Sum}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{1:K}),

(25)

where
\alpha=1/(1+K\rho)
. This is the third step in
Figure

2
.

Appendix D
Details on using
Algorithm

2
to Implement BayesADMM (
Algorithm

1
)

Here, we provide additional details on how line 4 in
Algorithm

1
is implemented in our experiments. As commonly done, we consider a tempered version of the variational-Bayesian problem in
Equation

3
,

\bar{q}^{*}=\underset{\bar{q}\in\mathcal{Q}}{\argmin}~\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}[\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]+\tau{\KLop}{}\left(\bar{q}\,\|\,\pi_{0}\right){},

(39)

where
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter. Dividing the whole objective by
\tau
does not change the minimizer and gives rescaled client losses
\ell_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\tau
on the original objective
Equation

3
.

For the tempered problem, the local client optimisation problem in BayesADMM for Gaussians with diagonal covariance reads as follows,

\argmin_{q_{k}}\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\ell_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\tau+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]+\smash{\rho{\KLop}{}\left(q_{k}\,\|\,\bar{q}\right){}},

(40)

which when pulling the KL-terms into the first expectations gives us the modified loss in
Algorithm

1
.
\ell_{k}^{(i)}
denote the per-example loss functions and
N_{k}
is the number of data examples on the client
k
.

We now bring this problem into a form that allows us to directly apply
Algorithm

2
. Dividing by
\rho
we have the equivalent form:

\argmin_{q_{k}}\,\frac{N_{k}}{\rho\tau}\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}\left[\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\ell_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\tau}{N_{k}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}-\frac{\tau}{N_{k}}\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]+\smash{{\KLop}{}\left(q_{k}\,\|\,\bar{q}\right){}}.

(41)

Matching the forms of the above equation with
Equation

30
gives us
\lambda=N_{k}/(\rho\tau)
,
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}=(\tau/N_{k})\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}
,
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}=(\tau/N_{k})\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}
. For each client subproblem in line 4 in
Algorithm

1
we are calling
Algorithm

2
as a subroutine with the following inputs:
\ell_{k}
,
(\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}},\bar{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$}$}}^{2})

\frac{N_{k}}{\rho\tau}

\frac{\tau}{N_{k}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{v}$}$}_{k}

\frac{\tau}{N_{k}}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{u}$}$}_{k}

2
then returns the needed (
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{k},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{s}$}$}_{k}
