Evaluation Hallucination in Multi-Round Incomplete Information Lateral-Driven Reasoning Tasks
Wenhan Dong Tianyi Hu Jingyi Zheng Zhen Sun, Yuemeng Zhao Yule Liu Xinlei He Xinyi Huang

Abstract
Multi-round incomplete information tasks are crucial for evaluating the lateral thinking capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Currently, research primarily relies on multiple benchmarks and automated evaluation metrics to assess these abilities. However, our study reveals novel insights into the limitations of existing methods, as they often yield misleading results that fail to uncover key issues, such as shortcut-taking behaviors, rigid patterns, and premature task termination. These issues obscure the true reasoning capabilities of LLMs and undermine the reliability of evaluations. To address these limitations, we propose a refined set of evaluation standards, including inspection of reasoning paths, diversified assessment metrics, and comparative analyses with human performance.

The Mirage of Performance: Unpacking the Shortcomings of Output-Centric Evaluation

The use of LLM as a Judge has been widely adopted due to its demonstrated high reliability and accuracy across various scenarios, achieving strong alignment with human judgment
[
11
]
.
However, when applied to multi-turn interactive reasoning tasks, this approach faces a key limitation:
it focuses on the final output while overlooking whether the reasoning process aligns with human logic and common sense.

This limitation becomes especially critical in tasks that require lateral thinking, where the reasoning path is as important as the final answer.
Yet, this aspect remains insufficiently explored, leaving a clear research gap.
To address this, we examine how representative final answers indeed are in multi-turn reasoning tasks and argue that a comprehensive evaluation must consider both final outputs and reasoning processes.

Specifically, we observe that relying solely on the final output for evaluation may fail to adequately capture potential issues within the reasoning process of the evaluated model.
In particular, the tested model might adopt shortcut strategies or prematurely abandon complex reasoning tasks.
These behaviors are often invisible in the final output, which can lead to inaccurate or unfair assessments of a model’s actual reasoning ability.
Despite the existence of evaluation metrics that focus on intermediate reasoning processes
[
14
,
8
]
, these metrics still fail to capture the shortcut behaviors discussed in this study.

The Phenomenon of Taking Shortcuts by Tested Models

In our experiments, we identified two distinct types of shortcut behaviors exhibited by LLMs during multi-turn reasoning tasks.
These behaviors can lead evaluators to overestimate the models’ true reasoning abilities, as they produce seemingly correct answers without genuine logical inference.
We categorize these shortcut behaviors into two main types:

Players Inducing Judges to Leak the Answer and Repeating Responses.

One common shortcut behavior involves the model prompting the judge to directly or indirectly reveal the correct answer, which the model then simply repeats to complete the task without engaging in genuine reasoning.

The example above illustrates the following pattern:

•

Player Questioning Strategy
: Although the judge’s guidelines explicitly prohibit providing specific information, players still elicit critical clues about the answer through intentional or unintentional leading questions.

•

Repeated Responses
: After obtaining the solution, the player repeats the content provided by the judge to complete the task without engaging in complex reasoning and lateral thinking.

By exploiting the judge’s leaked answer rather than reasoning, the model bypasses the task’s intent and leads to an overestimation of its true reasoning ability.

One notable issue is that the tested model may attempt to replace the question provided by the judge with a new one, thereby misleading the judge into positively evaluating its response.
For example, in the case shown in Figure 1, the player suddenly introduces a new question during the game and guesses the answer themselves.
Due to the high similarity in form between the new and original questions, the judge may fail to identify this substitution, leading to an incorrect judgment of the player’s response as correct.

Surrender Phenomenon in High-Difficulty Tasks

In addition to the aforementioned behavior where models attempt to obtain answers by surrendering and then repeating those answers, we also observed another distinct phenomenon: some models give up without making any attempt to acquire the answer.
This behavior differs fundamentally from the former, as it reflects a complete cessation of reasoning effort rather than a strategic choice to bypass complexity.
Specifically, when confronted with high-difficulty problems, these models often give up early, responding with phrases like “I don’t know the answer” or “I cannot continue”.
This phenomenon is particularly prominent when tasks involve ambiguous clues or complex reasoning requirements.

The tendency of models to surrender instead of attempting further reasoning may reflect limitations in their task engagement or exploratory inclination.
However, it is important to note that this surrender behavior should not be interpreted as a lack of reasoning ability.
Instead, it may reveal more about the model’s strategic preference when dealing with uncertainty or complex problems.
Although this phenomenon has not been thoroughly analyzed in the present study, it suggests a valuable direction for future research, particularly focused on improving models’ sustained reasoning capabilities and exploratory motivation in high-difficulty tasks.
Further studies could focus on designing more refined task frameworks to encourage models to demonstrate greater reasoning potential and adaptability when facing challenging tasks.

Conclusion and Call for Future Research

The issues mentioned above may be partially mitigated through techniques such as Prompt Engineering.
However, preliminary tests conducted so far have not provided conclusive evidence or experiments to completely eliminate these problems.
We call for future benchmarks based on LLMs as Judges to incorporate these considerations.
Additionally, we urge researchers conducting multi-turn reasoning tasks to strengthen the inspection of test results in their experimental designs.
Specifically, researchers should focus on evaluating whether the reasoning process aligns with human expectations and include the inspection results as an essential component of research reports to enhance the comprehensiveness and reliability of evaluations.

For the surrender phenomenon observed in high-difficulty tasks, we recommend that future research further explore the performance of models in terms of task focus, stress resistance, and exploratory willingness to gain a more comprehensive understanding of their behavioral patterns.
This will not only contribute to improving model design but also provide new perspectives for the evaluation methods of multi-turn reasoning tasks.

The Illusion of Competence: Exploring Unknown Influences in Lateral-Driven Reasoning

In this section, we explore several potential issues that may affect the evaluation of LLMs’ performance in multi-round lateral-driven reasoning tasks.
These issues include potential contamination of training data, the role of luck versus systematic reasoning, and the rationality of datasets in testing human and model capabilities.
By addressing these challenges, we aim to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the limitations in current evaluation methods and propose directions for future research.

Suspected Contamination of Training Data and Its Impact on Model Performance

In multi-round reasoning tasks, we observe that certain models may exhibit anomalous reasoning capabilities, potentially due to the suspected inclusion of test questions in their training data.
This phenomenon is particularly evident in Deepseek, which accurately guesses multiple key elements within a minimal number of rounds without querying core information.
Such behavior suggests that the model might rely on memorized patterns from its training data rather than engaging in genuine reasoning or lateral thinking.
However, due to the lack of transparent access to the model’s training data and the challenges associated with retrieving specific contents, direct verification of whether these questions exist in the training data remains unfeasible.

The observation highlights that the model’s superior performance may partially stem from potential contamination of its training data rather than reflecting its intrinsic reasoning abilities.
To address this issue, we recommend that future research prioritize the design of dynamic test sets or adopt adversarial testing methods to mitigate the influence of training data on model performance.
For instance, generating new puzzles or significantly modifying existing puzzles can help construct a more challenging testing environment, enabling a more accurate evaluation of the model’s true capabilities.

Success May Be Attributable to Luck Rather Than Reasoning

In some cases, LLMs may arrive at correct answers not through structured reasoning, but through repeated guessing.

In multi-turn reasoning tasks, we observe that models frequently become fixated on a single hypothesis, testing minor variations of the same idea across many rounds without exploring alternative possibilities.
This behavior resembles a brute-force or trial-and-error approach, rather than a logical or creative reasoning strategy.
For instance, a player model might fixate on one clue, asking over ten related questions in a row without exploring alternative possibilities.
This constraint restricts the ability to address challenges and obscures actual performance in multi-turn interactions.
Moreover, success in identifying the correct answer may reflect random guessing rather than logical deduction or creative reasoning.
Humans, by contrast, rarely repeat questions with such high frequency.
For example, the player model asks 14 consecutive questions about “who took the daughter away”, while humans typically shift focus after a few attempts.

Addressing the Question of Human Solvability in Lateral-Driven Datasets

In previous academic discussions, including feedback from OpenReview, there have been questions about whether lateral-driven datasets, such as Situation Puzzles, can genuinely test the lateral thinking and reasoning abilities of LLMs.
Additionally, whether human players can solve these puzzles within a reasonable timeframe has become a topic of debate.
If the puzzles themselves are overly complex or exceed human cognitive capacity, the resulting evaluations may be questioned, thereby undermining the credibility of the research conclusions.
To address this issue, we suggest that future dataset designs include comparative analyses with actual human test results.
Specifically, during the experimental design phase, a certain number of human participants can be invited to engage in testing, and their performance on the same tasks can be recorded, including metrics such as success rates, the number of rounds required, and the questioning strategies employed.
By comparing the performance of human players with that of models, the rationality of the puzzles can be validated, and a more scientific benchmark for assessing model capabilities can be established.

Summary and Call for Future Research

In summary, this section examines three critical issues related to model performance in multi-round reasoning tasks: potential contamination of training data, the possibility that success may be attributed to luck rather than reasoning, and whether lateral-driven datasets, such as Situation Puzzles, can genuinely assess the lateral thinking reasoning abilities of both humans and models.
These issues not only reveal shortcomings in current evaluation methods but also provide important directions for future research.
First, to address the issue of potential training data contamination, we urge researchers to adopt stricter measures when designing test sets, such as dynamically generating new puzzles or modifying existing ones in-depth, to reduce the influence of training data on model performance.
Second, to address the phenomenon of success potentially being attributed to luck, we recommend introducing new evaluation metrics to measure the model’s performance throughout the process, quantifying its comprehensive capabilities in complex lateral-driven reasoning tasks.
Finally, to address the rationality of datasets, we suggest that future experimental designs incorporate accurate test results and process performances of human players to enhance the scientific rigor and reliability of the evaluation framework.
Due to the high cost of human participants in multi-round interactions, future test sets can maintain a smaller scale but should demonstrate superior quality.

Quantifying Consistency: LLMs as Reliable Judges Based on Final Response

In the previous chapter, we analyzed the limitations of current evaluation methods based on final outputs in multi-round, incomplete information reasoning tasks in detail.
Specifically, these methods fail to effectively identify potential shortcut-taking behaviors adopted by models during the reasoning process, such as inducing answer leakage, repeating responses, or substituting questions to mislead judges.
Such behaviors can distort evaluation results, thereby masking the true reasoning capabilities of the models.
However, it is important to note that these limitations do not stem from the role of judges themselves, but rather from the inadequacy of the current evaluation framework, which fails to fully account for the complexity and dynamism of multi-round interactive reasoning tasks.

In the Situation Puzzle game, LLMs serving as judges must demonstrate high levels of consistency and information control to ensure both fairness in rule enforcement and the intellectual challenge of the reasoning process.
To evaluate the reliability of LLMs as Judges, we assessed their performance across two key phases:“questioning” and “guessing”.

Consistency Analysis in the Questioning Phase.

During the questioning phase, players engage in multi-turn interactions with the judge by posing questions.
In the judge’s prompts, only one of the following responses can be selected to respond: “Yes”, “No”, “partially”, or “unknown”.
To assess the performance of LLMs in this phase, we randomly sampled their responses across 500 questioning scenarios and compared them with answers provided by human annotators.
The results revealed that 462 out of 500 responses (
92.4%
) were consistent with those of the annotators.
This high level of consistency indicates that LLMs can provide answers aligned with annotators in multiple-choice question scenarios, thereby ensuring the coherence of game logic and the accuracy of information transmission.

Furthermore, among the remaining 38 inconsistent responses, a closer examination revealed that 11 were nonetheless acceptable.
Although these responses differed from the annotators’ choices, they demonstrated reasonable inferential capabilities within the specific context and adhered to the problem’s contextual constraints.
This suggests that LLMs exhibit a degree of flexibility and robustness when handling closed-ended multiple-choice questions.

Consistency Analysis in the Guessing Phase.

During the guessing phase, when players choose to make a guess, the judge evaluates the correctness of their answers.
To assess the reliability of LLMs in this phase, we randomly selected 183 guessing instances and organized four annotators to evaluate these instances.
The responses from LLMs were then compared with the annotations provided by the annotators.

Summary.

Our analysis demonstrates that LLMs exhibit a high level of consistency and reliability when serving as judges in Situation Puzzle games, particularly in scenarios involving closed-ended question-and-answer interactions and correctness evaluations.
These findings confirm the feasibility of using LLMs as judges in multi-round reasoning tasks.
However, the limitations identified in the previous chapter, such as the inability to detect shortcut-taking behaviors or premature task abandonment, are not inherent flaws of LLMs as judges but rather reflect deficiencies in the design of the current evaluation framework.

To address these challenges, future research should focus on refining evaluation methods to better capture the nuances of reasoning processes.
By strengthening the inspection of reasoning paths and incorporating more diversified metrics, we can enhance the comprehensiveness and scientific rigor of evaluations in complex interactive scenarios.
These efforts will pave the way for more robust benchmarks in evaluating the lateral thinking reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

Call for New Benchmarks

In current research, although automated evaluation methods based on response have been widely adopted and demonstrate high reliability and accuracy, their application in multi-turn interactive reasoning tasks still reveals significant limitations.
Specifically, these methods primarily focus on the model’s responses, particularly the final outputs, while neglecting whether the reasoning process aligns with human-accepted logic and common sense.
This limitation is particularly pronounced in tasks that involve lateral thinking and multi-turn reasoning, where the reasoning path itself holds significant importance.
However, existing research in this area remains insufficient, presenting a notable research gap.

To address this challenge, we propose the establishment of new benchmarks to comprehensively evaluate the lateral thinking and reasoning capabilities of LLMs under incomplete information.
Below are the core criteria we propose:

1.

Diversified Metrics

Current evaluation methods predominantly rely on metrics, which fail to capture the model’s performance in multi-turn interactions.
Future research should adopt diversified metrics, such as questioning strategies and question appropriateness, to construct a multidimensional evaluation framework.

2.

Sampling and Inspection of Reasoning Paths

Relying solely on the final answer may overlook critical issues in the reasoning process, such as shortcut strategies or premature abandonment.
These behaviors often manifest in the reasoning path rather than the final output, undermining evaluation fairness and comprehensiveness.
To address this, we recommend incorporating sampling to inspect reasoning paths, coupled with manual inspection, ensuring their rationality and diversity.

3.

Comparison with Human Results

To validate the effectiveness of the dataset and the scientific rigor of the evaluation method, we recommend comparing human and model performance in tasks such as success rates, interaction rounds, and questioning strategies.
Special attention should be paid to reasoning paths rather than just final answers.

4.

Cross-Disciplinary Collaboration

To ensure the dataset effectively tests lateral thinking abilities, we recommend collaborating with experts in psychology.
Integrating psychological theories can inform experimental design and validate model performance in complex scenarios, thereby enhancing both scientific rigor and the persuasiveness of research.

5.

Similarity and Dynamism of Questions

To mitigate training data contamination, we recommend dynamically modifying questions to reduce reliance on memorized information.
Questions should exhibit both similarity and variability to test generalization capabilities while remaining within the bounds of human cognitive capacity.

Conclusion

In this paper, we examined the limitations of current methods for evaluating the lateral thinking reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs) in multi-round tasks with incomplete information.
Our findings indicate that current evaluations often fail to ensure that reasoning aligns with human-accepted paths.
Key issues, such as shortcut-taking behaviors, premature task termination, and training data contamination, reduce evaluation reliability.
To address these issues, we propose a refined evaluation standard that includes inspecting reasoning paths, utilizing diversified metrics, and comparing model performance with human reasoning.
By introducing criteria for dynamic question generation and improved sampling of reasoning paths, our study guides the design of future benchmarks and improves evaluation systems for LLMs’ lateral reasoning under incomplete information.
