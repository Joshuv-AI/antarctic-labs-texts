Study protocol for the Healthier Wealthier Families (HWF) cluster randomised controlled trial: testing the effectiveness and cost-benefits of offering financial counselling to families with young children who report financial hardship at well-child health home visits
Authors: Anna Price, Skye Frazer-Ryan, Jonathan Turk, Anneke C. Grobler, Lisa Gold, Sharon Goldfeld, Emma Elcombe, Rachael Beswick, Joanne Trentin, Domenique Meyrick, Claerwen Little, Lynn Kemp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHousehold income is a social determinant of children’s health and development. In Australia, one in three families are unable to afford material basics, and the prevalence of poor mental health triples for these caregivers. Healthier Wealthier Families (HWF) connects well-child healthcare with community-based financial counselling to provide an early intervention for financial hardship. Adapted from a Scottish model and piloted in the Australian states of Victoria and New South Wales in 2020-22, this protocol describes an evaluation of the HWF model embedded in well-child home visits in the state of Queensland. Aims are to evaluate (1) HWF effectiveness in reducing financial hardship and improving caregiver mental health, (2) cost-benefits of HWF, and (3) implementation factors necessary for broader scale-up.MethodsThis study is a pragmatic, cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) involving 336 clients receiving well-child home visiting services across all active service regions in Queensland. 24 service areas (clusters) are allocated to either immediate (‘now’) or delayed (‘next’, after 12 months) screening for financial hardship by health home visitors. Caregivers who report financial hardship and consent to referral will be contacted by a financial counsellor. Aim 1 will compare the financial hardship and mental health outcomes between the randomised groups at 13–24 months. Using these outcome data and routine financial counselling data, Aim 2 will assess the costs of the program (measured through partner reports of time and materials used in provision of the intervention) against change in outcomes in the form of a cost-consequences analysis from a government perspective. For Aim 3, qualitative interviews with caregivers and service providers will explore experiences, barriers, and enablers of implementation.DiscussionEthical approval has been granted by the Western Sydney University (#H13834) and UnitingCare Queensland (#202504) Human Research Ethics Committees. Results will be disseminated to partners and policymakers, through meetings, reports, and through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Findings will inform if and how financial counselling can be embedded and scaled within universal well-child healthcare services state-wide and nationally.Trial registrationThe trial is registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12625001337460).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28551-2.

Introduction

Financial hardship and early childhood

Household income is a well-established social determinant of health, particularly during the first 1,000 days of life (conception to 2 years) when the brain develops more rapidly than any other time [1, 2] During this period, household income is a protective factor that contributes to children’s optimal health and development [3]. In contrast, low household income (e.g. poverty) can compromise brain growth [4], increase the risk of poorer socioemotional functioning and school failure, and increase lifelong risks of chronic disease, mental illness, reduced economic opportunity and intergenerational adversity [1, 2, 5, 6].

The complex mechanisms underpinning the relationship between household income and child outcomes are conceptualised through two pathways. According to the ‘investment’ pathway, household income benefits children directly through better food, stable housing and healthcare. According to the ‘family stress’ pathway, household income affects children indirectly through improved caregiver mental health and parenting capacity [3]. In Australia, where this study is being conducted, one in three caregivers with young children cannot afford material basics that are essential for good health. Poor mental health is three times as common for caregivers experiencing material deprivation [7–9]. The current trial measures financial hardship and caregiver mental health as the outcomes representing the investment and family stress pathways, with material deprivation selected as the primary outcome.

Australia’s social welfare system

To offset financial hardship, Australia’s government entitlements are available to support families, roughly halving the levels of poverty that would otherwise exist [10]. However, low take-up of entitlements is common across high income countries where an estimated 20–60% of eligible recipients miss out [11]. 1.3 million Australians were found to be missing out in 2004, when this issue was last comprehensively addressed [12]. In 2023-24, 62% of low income households had not applied for a concession to their energy bills [13]. Across high-income countries, common explanations for missing out are lack of awareness, system complexity, and stigma [13, 14]. That our systems are designed in ways that prohibit access to entitlements is both unethical and preventable.

Financial wellbeing services help clients access entitlements, manage debt and build financial literacy. These services are funded by Australian state and federal governments and delivered through non-profit and community organisations. The core service provision is financial counselling, a specialised profession that requires accreditation with a state-based peak body, a supervised training period, and ongoing professional development (e.g. trauma-informed care, responding to family violence). Financial counsellors work on a client’s behalf and are not mandated to report client activities to government [15, 16].

There is limited published evidence of the benefits and cost benefits of financial counselling. One evaluation from 2012 (n = 139) found that over two-thirds of clients who accessed financial counselling avoided legal action, accessed creditor hardship programs, and said their financial situations and literacy improved [16] 63% reported benefits to mental health and wellbeing, and 45% said their relationships with their families and children improved [16]. A cost comparison found that every $1 invested into financial counselling delivered a financial benefit of $5 to clients [16]. As referrals are typically via allied health or self-referring clients during periods of financial crisis, the services are focused on treatment and are missing opportunities for earlier intervention and more efficient delivery.

Addressing financial hardship through universal healthcare

Australia’s Child and Family Health Services (CFHS) deliver well-child healthcare (universal prevention) to families with young children from birth to school entry [17]. The multi-disciplinary workforce predominately comprises nurses, as well as Aboriginal health workers and practitioners, and social care practitioners. Governance is at the state/territory and local level, and CFHS are commonplace in other high-income countries with universal healthcare such as the UK, Sweden and Canada [17]. Australia’s many CFHS recognise financial hardship as a social risk factor for families’ health and wellbeing, yet none systematically identify and respond to it. Australian research shows that families experiencing adversity want to be asked about the social circumstances affecting their health and wellbeing, where it is done safely and respectfully [18]. However, health practitioners need training and support to feel confident and capable to do this effectively [19].

Our systematic review shows a precedent for addressing financial hardship through healthcare [20]. We identified four international models for families with children aged 0–5 years, although they lack randomised controlled trial (RCT) evidence of benefits [20]. The most substantial benefits were reported by the Scottish ‘Healthier Wealthier Children’ program [21, 22]. In 2010, the Scottish Government funded a partnership between the National Health Service, local government and community sector to support the universal early years workforce to identify financial hardship and refer caregivers to money advice workers. The two pre-post evaluations (2010-13) reported average annual gains per client of £1661 (n = 2516) and £1919 (n = 2289). By 2020, the initiative had generated over 26,000 referrals and over £36 million in financial gain for families, mostly through government entitlements that were being missed. The initiative was subsequently cited as a requirement of Scotland’s Child Poverty Action Plan [23].

Pilot feasibility work that informs this protocol

In 2020-22, we conducted a pilot that adapted the Scottish model to CFHS in Victoria and New South Wales (NSW), known as ‘Healthier Wealthier Families’ (HWF) [15, 24]. As the pilot coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and extensive lockdowns in the states of Victoria (2020-21) and NSW (2021), we encountered many design and implementation challenges. Using quality improvement processes, we iterated the model with service providers in four regions across these two states before developing a feasible model in outer-metropolitan Victoria (see Fig. 1) [15, 24]. In the feasibile model, 66/69 clients seen by the CFHS provider reported financial hardship and 44/66 (67%) engaged with the financial counsellor. Of the 44 who used the service, 75% were born overseas, 52% spoke primary languages other than English.

In the pilot pre-post evaluation, income from government entitlements increased by $6,504 AUD (£3177 GBP, 2022 exchange rates) annually on average ($125 or £61 GBP p/week), plus $784 AUD (£383 GBP) from other sources such as concessions, grants, brokerage, and debt waivers [24]. This increase is comparable to that seen in Healthier Wealthier Children in Scotland [21, 22], and is substantial given that 80% of households earned less than $1,000 AUD (£489 GBP) per week. Financial counselling also helped clients avoid loss of utilities (52%) and legal action (11%), stabilise housing (14%), and conduct financial literacy activities (66%).

In the pilot, caregivers were unanimous in finding HWF beneficial (Fig. 1). In interviews, they described the immense stress of financial hardship (‘I was going crazy to be honest. … all of that built on me so much that I was like thinking I just wanted to be gone. Finished with this life’). They said that HWF reduced their stress (‘It’s reducing stress…help us for my budget… when she fixed the bills for me, it’s save our budgets. We could save $600, and more…nearly $1,000 is a saving in this situation… it’s very helpful’), HWF provided immediate solutions (‘they send me always notice, notice…if they cut electricity, it’s hard because winter, cold, kids. So hard…So, I was so happy when I get [help]’); HWF increased their financial knowledge (‘she really helped with things that we have no idea existed. Can’t find information on.’), and HWF increased empowerment and wellbeing (‘I know now I am safe. I know nobody will slap me. I know that if I am having no food, I can call someone’) [24].

Setting and participants

HWF will be embedded into Queensland’s nurse-led home visiting CFHS, which is an evidence-based model of care known as the Maternal Early Childhood Sustained Home-visiting (MECSH) Program. MECSH offers 25 home visits with the same specially-trained nurse or health practitioner (termed ‘MECSH provider’ throughout) from pregnancy until children are 2–3 years old, and at least one visit with a social care practitioner. MECSH is designed to develop the parenting capacity that underpins children’s learning and development [25]. The HWF research team has led a multi-site RCT of MECSH embedded in usual service delivery since 2012 (known as ‘right@home’), which has demonstrated immediate and sustained benefits to parenting and family relationships (2–6 years), maternal mental health and wellbeing (3–6 years), and children’s learning and behaviour (5–6 years) [26–30].

The state government of Queensland is expanding home visiting state-wide (2024-28) to ‘families and children who need it most, responding to the needs of local families and building strong connections with communities and local services’ [31]. The state is divided into 16 regions that are each supported by a Hospital and Health Service. Most regions comprise multiple MECSH service areas, which act as clusters for the HWF trial. All service areas delivering MECSH will be the setting for this RCT. Potential participants will be all MESCH clients.

Consumer and end-user involvement

Implementation of MECSH involves local adaptation and co-design with MECSH providers so that, in addition to core MECSH content, additional foci can support issues relevant to each community (e.g. substance use, family violence, mental health). HWF exists in a program of consumer (families) and end-user (service providers) involvement because it is adapted to each local context. In the pilot protocol and the development of this protocol, the processes were iteratively co-developed with CFHS providers and their clients, and between CFHS providers and financial counsellors [15, 24].

Methods for testing HWF benefits (aim 1)

Design and allocation

The design is a pragmatic, cluster RCT with two groups: Group 1 (‘now’) and Group 2 (‘next’). Active MECSH service areas are randomly allocated 1:1 to the two groups using computer-generated block randomisation with allocation concealment until after randomisation. Allocation can occur while MECSH sites become active, which is anticipated to be around 2 years (Table 1). For 12 months following allocation, Group 1 (‘now’) are offered HWF. During months 13–24, clients in both Groups are offered HWF (Fig. 3). Table 2 describes the HWF financial hardship items, which are used for screening and outcome measurement.

This pragmatic design is different to a delayed or withheld intervention because clients are not screened for financial hardship until the point that financial counselling can be offered. It is not known yet whether embedding the HWF referral in MECSH is beneficial. In this study, randomisation enables a systematic approach to choosing who is asked about financial hardship and subsequent referral. In the pilot’s original RCT design, usual care involved provision of educational material designed and published online by the Australian government. The pilot showed that randomising the referral to financial counselling after the financial screen was unfeasible [24]. Health providers did not want to ask families about financial hardship unless they could respond with financial counselling, which was not possible for families receiving usual care. The processes also required a researcher to contact eligible clients for consent and randomisation, which prevented a direct referral from the health provider to the financial counsellor. By randomising MECSH service areas to an immediate or delayed screen in the current protocol, the design tries to overcome the feasibility issues encountered in the pilot RCT.

Measures

Table 2 describes the HWF measures. MECSH providers collect routine surveys at enrolment (pregnancy to 8 weeks postpartum), 6 and 22–23 months postpartum. When HWF commences in a service area, the HWF financial hardship items will be embedded into the routine MECSH surveys for all clients, plus additional HWF-specific surveys asked at 12 and 18 months or at the MECSH practitioner’s discretion (e.g. if a client is experiencing hardship and the MECSH provider wants to offer a referral). All MECSH and HWF routine surveys collect caregiver-reported mental health using the Matthey Generic Mood Questionnaire (Table 2).

MECSH providers and financial counsellors will receive training in HWF (~ 2 h) before the intervention starts, and ongoing support from the implementation team to build and maintain trusted working relationships. Like MECSH providers, financial counsellors work with multilingual service providers, interpreters, or support persons as needed. The primary mode of financial counselling delivery is by phone. However, it depends on what is available in the region and preferred by the client, e.g. by phone, in a local financial counselling office, or another location (library, CFHS clinic, as agreed by all parties). In the pilot, which was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic and lockdown periods, only phone consults were possible, and these were shown to be feasible and acceptable for clients and the financial counsellor [24].

The MECSH home visiting service model provides the comparator. For 12 months following allocation (Fig. 3), the MECSH surveys for clients in the Group 2 service areas do not include the HWF financial hardship items in Table 2. After 12 months, clients in both Groups are asked the financial hardship items. This trial is low risk because it connects two existing services and clients can stop or recommence services at their discretion. There are no criteria for discontinuing or modifying the intervention, and no rules regarding concomitant care or other permitted or prohibited interventions during the trial.

Recruitment and procedure

For caregivers who report financial hardship, the MECSH provider describes the financial counselling available and asks if the caregiver agrees to the HWF referral. Recording a client’s agreement in the online MECSH database automatically triggers a referral email to the financial counsellor, to contact the client and do a routine intake assessment. For clients who agree to a referral to financial counselling, the MECSH provider asks if the caregiver consents to data sharing for the purposes of evaluating HWF. These processes were used in the feasibility pilot, where all referred clients consented to data sharing [24].

Outcome data will be collected approximately 13–24 months after allocation to assess differences between the randomised groups (Table 1, Fig. 3). We will co-develop direct data collection to address potential gaps, e.g. phone calls or texts to collect outcome data from clients who stop MECSH in both groups. Our mixed methods approach will capture other biases or threats to validity (see Methods for Aims 2 and 3 below).

Sample size

Sample size was determined for a two-group cluster RCT with 24 clusters (12 per group), and powered to detect a reduction in material deprivation of 7% assuming the rate in the Group 2 (‘next’) was 100%. Assuming a mean cluster size of 11 participants (inflated to 14 per cluster to allow for 17% loss to follow-up), an intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.02 (with exchangeable correlation structure), two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, the total target sample size is 336 participants (168 per group).

Blinding

It is impossible to blind MECSH providers and families to HWF (the intervention). However, the design minimises exposure of families in Group 2 (‘next’) to the intervention, and minimises selection bias from MECSH providers and financial counsellors by automating both when the financial hardship items are asked, and the referral from the MECSH provider to the financial counsellor. In the HWF pilot, none of the caregivers who reported financial hardship were existing clients of financial counselling, suggesting it is unlikely that MECSH Group 2 caregivers will self-refer to financial counselling in the first 12 months of intervention [24]. However, the financial wellbeing partners will record referral source to enable analysis of change over time (e.g. an increase due to contamination). The statistician responsible for conducting the outcomes analysis will also be blinded to allocation until analysis.

Analysis

The analysis will follow the Intention To Treat principle of including all randomised clusters and all eligible clients in all clusters. Baseline characteristics and outcomes will be described by group. A mixed effects logistic regression, adjusted for baseline characteristics identified a priori, such as mental health and sociodemographic characteristics (as fixed effects), will be fitted with financial hardship and poor mental health as outcome variables. The randomised treatment group will be a fixed effect and randomised cluster will be a random effect to account for the clustering within MECSH service area. We will use marginalised probabilities to calculate risk differences.

If missing data are determined to be recoverable (that is able to be estimated without bias from the observed data alone – missing at random) and more than 10% of data are missing, multiple imputation will be used to handle the missing data in all outcomes. Imputations will be generated using chained equations, also known as fully conditional specification, with 50 imputed datasets and 10 iterations between each imputation. Ideally this will be conducted using a single regression model for all outcomes, although it may be necessary to impute incomplete outcomes using separate models. Imputation will be conducted separately by group, to ensure that any treatment effects are maintained. Baseline characteristics as well as any variables that are predictors of missingness and/or associated with the incomplete outcomes will be included as auxiliary variables in the imputation model as well as all variables included in the analysis. Estimates of interest will be obtained using Rubin’s rules, and will be reported with 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

Supplementary analysis

Two supplementary analyses are planned. First, the material deprivation score (range 0–4) will be analysed as a count outcome using mixed-effects Poisson regression. This model will mirror the primary mixed-effects framework described above but will specify a Poisson rather than a logistic link function, with the count score treated as the dependent variable instead of the binary outcome. The second will apply a hypothetical strategy to address the intercurrent event of not engaging with financial counselling. Multiple imputation will be used to estimate the effect of HWF on financial hardship and mental health under the assumption that all participants experiencing financial hardship engaged with financial counselling in the intervention arm.

Methods for testing cost effectiveness (aim 2)

Design, measures and procedure

The routine service data provided by financial counsellors will include frequency of caregiver contact and compliance, and activities and advice provided.

Analysis

Using the outcome data (Aim 1), and the financial counsellor service delivery data, a cost-effectiveness analysis will assess the costs of the program (measured through partner reports of time and materials used in provision of the intervention) against change in outcomes in the form of a cost-consequences analysis from a government perspective. A critical outcome for clients is the change in financial outcomes through use of financial counselling. These will be evaluated in a cost-benefit analysis from the client perspective, including the net value (project financial benefits (compared to Group 2) less project costs) and benefit-cost ratio. This analysis replicates the financial analysis of the Scottish initiative [21, 22], and the only published Australian cost-benefit analysis [16], to enable comparison to these previous evaluations.

Methods for understanding implementation factors and other impacts (aim 3)

Design

Mixed-methods evaluation of barriers and facilitators to implementation, and other impacts additional to the primary outcomes. This will be guided by the program logic (Fig. 2) and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [33].

Measures and procedure

Table 3 describes the implementation measures. Caregiver-reported capacity to adapt and self-manage, and enablement and satisfaction, are collected in routine MECSH surveys. Qualitative interviews will also be conducted with a purposive sample of approximately 15 caregivers, and one MECSH provider and one financial counsellor from each service region, to understand the mechanisms driving the potential impacts (Fig. 2).

Analysis

Clients’ capacity to adapt and self-manage, and their enablement and satisfaction, will be compared between groups. Qualitative interviews will be digitally audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. After establishing initial codes, they will be thematically analysed [38]. We will use the data collected across Aims 1–3 to evaluate the implementation outcomes using the taxonomy proposed by Proctor et al., of acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, fidelity, coverage, cost, sustainability [39].

Data management

All participants are assigned a unique numerical identifier for use throughout the study. Secure electronic databases record all participant data. Hardcopy materials that cannot be transferred to electronic documents are stored in locked cabinets. Other than the service providers who have access to their clients’ data, only project staff directly involved in analysis can access data. All project data are stored for the required period, that is, indefinitely if the participant consents to providing their data for data pooling or, otherwise, for 7 years after the completion of the study. After that time, hardcopy materials are destroyed by shredding, and any password-protected electronic archives are permanently deleted.

Data monitoring and auditing

The HWF Translational Reference Group incorporates key stakeholders and experts to direct and advise on the technical design, implementation, and interpretation of the trial. To ensure high-quality data, the research team monitor data collection for completeness, accuracy, and consistency with the protocol. This includes regular checks of recruitment logs, referral data, and research datasets, as well as spot-checks of transcripts and consent records. Any issues will be discussed within the research team and escalated to the Translational Reference Group if needed. No formal audits are planned, although the governing ethics committee and funders may audit this research as part of their own processes. No interim analyses or stopping rules will be applied, although the study may be terminated early if recruitment or intervention delivery is not feasible due to an epidemic such as COVID-19 or another crisis.

Managing potential harms

Adverse events are recorded from the time the participant consents until 30 days after the last study visit. It is anticipated that adverse events will most likely relate to psychosocial, substance abuse, home safety and child protection issues. Where an incident that requires a child protection notification occurs during a MECSH or financial counsellor visit, the MECSH provider and financial counsellors respond in accordance with their local mandatory reporter guidelines. For any direct contact with researchers (e.g. qualitative interviews), the trial’s Safety and Communication Protocol provides researchers with a structured set of responses to participant-disclosed risks including support needs, safety related and emergencies. Support and debriefing for research staff are provided by the investigators and employee assistance programs.

Ancillary and post-trial care

None is planned as the research is low risk; the intervention is an existing service that is already freely available and designed for individuals that include the families targeted by this research; identification of eligible families is conducted by a healthcare service that has as part of its core business the identification of issues and referral to relevant agencies; the comparator is usual care; and the project team is experienced in considering and minimising the risks of this type of intervention research for the participant group.

Dissemination

As study findings emerge, the consumer advisors and Translational Reference Group will inform the development of resources tailored for different users. Trial results will be communicated to partners and policymakers, through meetings, reports, and through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Data and document sharing is decided on a case-by-case basis at the discretion of the custodians and consistent with ethical approval and institutional requirements. Authorship is defined using the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines and specified according to the CREDIT statement. There is no intention to engage professional writers for academic publications.

Discussion

This protocol describes a pragmatic, cluster randomised trial of the Healthier Wealthier Families (HWF) model, embedded within Queensland’s nurse‑led Maternal Early Childhood Sustained Home‑visiting (MECSH) program. The study responds to a well‑established gap between the recognition of financial hardship as a key social determinant of health and the limited availability of systematic, evidence‑based mechanisms to identify and address financial hardship within universal child and family health services.

HWF builds on international and Australian evidence suggesting that healthcare provides a trusted and equitable platform for early identification of financial hardship, and that timely referral to specialist financial counselling can yield meaningful financial and psychosocial benefits for families [20–22, 24]. Unlike most previous initiatives, which have relied on pre-post designs or pilot evaluations, this study applies random allocation at the service‑area level to test whether embedding financial hardship screening and referral into routine well‑child home visiting improves caregiver financial hardship and mental health outcomes at scale. By randomising service areas to immediate or delayed screening, the design directly addresses feasibility issues encountered in earlier trials while maintaining rigour and ethical acceptability in a real‑world service setting.

The trial is intentionally pragmatic. HWF connects two existing services, and its delivery is integrated into routine practice rather than imposed as a parallel research activity. This approach enhances external validity and maximises the relevance of findings for policymakers and service systems considering statewide or national implementation. The mixed‑methods design allows the study to capture effectiveness and cost consequences, as well as implementation processes, mechanisms, and contextual factors that influence uptake, sustainability, and scalability. Understanding these factors is particularly important given the diversity of communities served by MECSH, including families experiencing intersecting social and structural adversity.

A further strength of the study is its explicit focus on outcomes that reflect established pathways linking income and child health. The primary outcome, material deprivation, is sensitive to change and meaningful to families [15, 24]. Measuring caregiver mental health captures the family stress pathway through which income insecurity affects parenting capacity and child development [3]. All measures are intentionally brief and low‑burden. The inclusion of economic evaluation responds to longstanding calls for policy‑relevant evidence that can inform decisions about resource allocation within constrained public systems [16, 21, 22].

The study also has limitations. Blinding of providers and participants is not possible due to the nature of the intervention, although automated screening and referral processes are used to minimise selection bias. As with other service‑embedded trials, outcomes rely on caregiver self‑report collected through routine surveys, which may be influenced by engagement with services over time. Variation in local implementation contexts may also contribute to heterogeneity in effects, although this is an intended feature of a pragmatic design and will be explored through the implementation and qualitative analyses.

If effective, HWF has the potential to strengthen the role of universal child and family health services in addressing the social determinants of health, by moving beyond identification of social risk to a systematic and supported response [17]. Findings from this trial will inform whether, how, and under what conditions financial counselling can be sustainably embedded within well‑child healthcare in Queensland and more broadly across Australia, contributing to ongoing efforts to reduce child and family inequities through earlier and more integrated intervention.
