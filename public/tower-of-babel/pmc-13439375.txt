Sustainable sludge derived activated carbon polyaniline composite removes lead and cadmium from industrial wastewater
Authors: Abeer Adaileh, M. Abu Shuheil, Ahmed. H. Rageb, A. O. Ali, Inas A. Ahmed, Mahmoud F. Mubarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents the fabrication of a novel, sustainable, and cost-effective hybrid composite (SAC–PANI) derived from sewage sludge calcined at 600 °C, activated carbon, and polyaniline through in situ oxidative polymerization. Comprehensive Characterization using FTIR, XRD, SEM, BET, and TGA confirmed the successful formation of the composite and its enhanced physicochemical properties. BET analysis revealed a substantial increase in surface area (90 m²/g) compared to raw calcined sludge (58 m²/g), indicating improved porosity. Process optimization was performed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) based on a Central Composite Design (CCD). The developed quadratic model (R² = 0.984) adequately described the system and revealed significant interactions among pH, adsorbent dosage, and initial metal concentration, with optimum removal achieved at pH 6.0. Adsorption equilibrium data were best described by the Langmuir isotherm (R² = 0.995 and 0.992 for Pb(II) and Cd(II), respectively), yielding maximum adsorption capacities of 128.5 ± 2.3 mg/g for Pb(II) and 115.7 ± 2.1 mg/g for Cd(II). Kinetic studies were well fitted by the pseudo-second-order model (R² = 0.998 for Pb(II) and 0.997 for Cd(II)), with rate constants of k₂ = 0.035 ± 0.001 g/mg·min for Pb(II) and k₂ = 0.029 ± 0.001 g/mg·min for Cd(II), indicating that chemisorption is the dominant adsorption mechanism owing to interactions between the metal ions and the nitrogen-containing functional groups of PANI. Thermodynamic analysis demonstrated that the adsorption process is spontaneous (ΔG° = -19.8 kJ/mol for Pb(II) and − 17.6 kJ/mol for Cd(II) at 298 K), endothermic (ΔH° = +33.5 kJ/mol for Pb(II) and + 25.3 kJ/mol for Cd(II)), and characterized by increased entropy (ΔS° = +132 J/mol·K for Pb(II) and + 98 J/mol·K for Cd(II)), indicating chemisorption-driven metal uptake. The composite also exhibited good stability, retaining more than 82% of its removal efficiency after five adsorption–desorption cycles for both Pb(II) and Cd(II).). Environmental safety assessment via the TCLP leaching test demonstrated that the spent adsorbent is non-hazardous, with Pb and Cd leachate concentrations (0.58 and 0.18 mg/L, respectively) well below EPA regulatory limits (5.0 mg/L for Pb and 1.0 mg/L for Cd). Importantly, the SAC-PANI composite was successfully tested on real electroplating wastewater, achieving Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal efficiencies of 75.0 ± 1.5% and 72.6 ± 1.4%, respectively, in the presence of competing ions and complex matrix effects. Overall, SAC–PANI demonstrates considerable potential as an efficient, sustainable, and low-cost adsorbent for the removal of heavy metals from wastewater.

Introduction

The ongoing release of untreated or inadequately treated industrial effluents containing toxic heavy metals into aquatic habitats represents a pressing worldwide environmental issue1–3. Among the various contaminants, lead (Pb(II)) and cadmium (Cd(II)) are of particular concern due to their inability to decompose spontaneously, their significant toxicity, and their tendency to accumulate within living organisms over time4–6. Long-term exposure to these ions can cause severe health effects, including neurological disorders, kidney damage, and carcinogenic impacts, even at low concentrations7–9. Therefore, creating effective, affordable, and environmentally friendly methods to remove these contaminants from wastewater is critically important10.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the guideline values for lead (Pb(II)) and cadmium (Cd(II)) in drinking water are set at 0.01 mg/L and 0.003 mg/L, respectively11,12. For surface water bodies, the European Union Water Framework Directive establishes environmental quality standards of 7.2 µg/L for Pb(II) and 0.48 µg/L for Cd(II)13,14. However, Industrial activities such as battery manufacturing, electroplating, mining, and pigment production frequently release Pb(II) and Cd(II) at concentrations that far exceed these limits.

Conventional methods for the removal of Pb(II) and Cd(II) from wastewater include chemical precipitation15, coagulation–flocculation16, membrane filtration (nanofiltration and reverse osmosis)17, ion exchange, photocatalysis18, and electrocoagulation19. Although these methods are widely applied, they suffer from significant limitations: chemical precipitation generates large volumes of metal-laden sludge that requires further treatment; coagulation–flocculation is ineffective at low metal concentrations; membrane technologies incur high capital and energy costs and are prone to fouling; ion exchange resins are expensive and demand careful regeneration; and electrocoagulation is hindered by high electricity consumption and electrode passivation. In contrast, adsorption offers simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and the ability to handle dilute solutions effectively.

Adsorption is acknowledged as one of the most successful and economically viable methodologies for heavy metal remediation, owing to its simplicity, high efficiency, and adaptability to various wastewater conditions20–25. In recent years, significant efforts have focused on creating hybrid composite adsorbents that synergistically combine the properties of organic and inorganic materials26,27. Among these, carbon-based composites have received particular attention for their high surface area, adjustable surface chemistry, and superior adsorption potential28,29.

Activated carbon (AC) is recognized for its microporous architecture and strong adsorptive affinity toward heavy metal ions30–33. However, its relatively high cost and limited selectivity necessitate its integration with other functional materials34. In contrast, polyaniline (PANI) a conductive polymer with abundant amine and imine functional groups offers remarkable chemical reactivity, redox behavior, and coordination ability with metal ions35,36. The incorporation of PANI can significantly enhance the electron-donating capacity and metal binding functionality of carbonaceous adsorbents.

Simultaneously, the issue of sludge management arising from municipal and industrial wastewater treatment plants poses another serious environmental challenge37,38. Sludge contains a complex combination of organic substances, inorganic minerals, and heavy metals, and its improper disposal can lead to secondary pollution39,40. However, thermal treatment (e.g., calcination at 600 °C) transforms sludge into a stable, mineral-rich ash with adsorption potential, providing an opportunity for waste to resource conversion41. Utilizing calcined sludge as a low-cost, inorganic support in composite adsorbent systems not only addresses waste disposal concerns but also encourages the sustainable creation of new materials, which supports the circular economy42.

Recent literature on Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal has demonstrated the effectiveness of various adsorbent systems. PANI-based composites, including muscovite based polyaniline nanocomposite43, polyaniline/Al2O3 nanocomposites44, and CuO/PANI nanocomposite45, have shown remarkable adsorption capacities attributed primarily to nitrogen-functional group coordination and electrostatic interactions. Sludge-derived adsorbents have also gained attention as sustainable alternatives; calcined sludge ash has demonstrated moderate adsorption capacity for heavy metals owing to its silica and aluminosilicate content46,47. Carbon-based composites combining activated carbon with functional polymers have further improved performance by merging high surface area with chemical selectivity48,49. Recent studies have highlighted biochar as a promising adsorbent for heavy metal remediation due to its porous structure, surface functional groups, and low cost. However, further modification is often required to improve its adsorption capacity and selectivity toward Pb(II) and Cd(II)50.

However, a critical research gap exists in the literature: no prior study has developed and systematically optimized a ternary composite that simultaneously integrates calcinated sewage sludge ash as an inorganic mineral support, activated carbon for high surface area, and polyaniline for nitrogen-rich functional group chemistry. Such a ternary system is expected to yield synergistic benefits beyond what binary composites can achieve, while simultaneously addressing the environmental challenge of sewage sludge valorization through circular economy principles.

This research presents an interesting multifunctional adsorbent system developed from waste-derived precursors, demonstrating a sustainable solution for the disposal of heavy metal-contaminated industrial wastewater and supporting the transition toward green chemistry and circular waste valorization practices.

The novelty of the present study lies in: (1) the first reported fabrication of a ternary SAC-PANI composite integrating calcinated municipal sewage sludge ash, activated carbon, and polyaniline into a single hybrid adsorbent system for Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal; (2) the valorization of waste sludge as a functional inorganic component rather than a mere filler; and (3) the comprehensive optimization of adsorption conditions using RSM-CCD for both metals simultaneously.

Experimental

Materials

All reagents and chemicals utilized in this research were of analytical quality and were used without further purification. Sewage sludge was collected in wet form from the secondary sedimentation tank of the Mansoura Municipal Wastewater Treatment Plant (Dakahlia Governorate, Egypt). The physicochemical characteristics of the raw sewage sludge were as follows: moisture content ~ 78%, volatile solids ~ 62% of dry weight, ash content ~ 38% of dry weight, and pH 6.8 ± 0.2. It is acknowledged that raw sewage sludge may contain trace heavy metals originating from industrial discharges into the municipal sewer network. The sludge was first dewatered, dried at 105 °C for 24 h, and subsequently calcinated at 600 °C for 3 h in a muffle furnace to obtain mineral-rich ash, which served as a primary inorganic component in the composite preparation. The calcination temperature of 600 °C was selected on the basis of literature evidence demonstrating that this temperature achieves optimal decomposition of organic matter, stabilization of inorganic mineral phases (SiO₂ and aluminosilicates), and development of surface porosity without inducing undesirable sintering effects that occur at higher temperatures. Aniline monomer (C₆H₅NH₂, 99.5%, reagent grade) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany) and distilled under reduced pressure prior to use to avoid oxidation. Ammonium persulfate (APS, (NH₄)₂S₂O₈, ≥ 98%, analytical grade), used as the oxidizing agent for the in situ polymerization of polyaniline, was obtained from Alfa Aesar (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, ACS reagent grade) and sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄, 98%, analytical grade), used for pH adjustment and doping of polyaniline, were acquired from Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd. (India).

Activated carbon powder (surface area ~ 312 m²/g, ≥ 99%, analytical grade) was supplied by Merck (Germany). The carbon was sieved to below 100 μm and utilized as received. Lead nitrate (Pb (NO₃)₂, ≥ 99.5%) and cadmium nitrate tetrahydrate (Cd (NO₃)₂·4 H₂O, ≥ 99%), utilized to prepare synthetic metal ion solutions, were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98%) and nitric acid (HNO₃, 65%), utilized for pH adjustment, were obtained from Fisher Scientific (UK). Deionized water with a conductivity of less than 1 µS/cm was utilized throughout all experiments.

All glassware was thoroughly cleaned with 10% nitric acid solution and rinsed with deionized water before use to prevent any contamination. The chemicals and materials were stored under appropriate conditions in accordance with the suppliers’ recommendations.

Synthesis of SAC–PANI composite

The hybrid SAC–PANI composite was prepared through a two-step method that included the physical mixing of calcined sludge and activated carbon, followed by in situ oxidative polymerization of aniline (Fig. 1). The synthesis procedure was adapted from previously reported in situ oxidative polymerization methods for polyaniline/activated carbon composites, with modifications to incorporate calcinated sewage sludge ash as an additional inorganic component51. The calcined sludge ash, produced at 600 °C, was subsequently ground and sieved to isolate particles measuring less than 100 μm. A specific quantity of sludge ash (2.0 g) was uniformly mixed with 1.0 g of powdered activated carbon in a 250 mL beaker through magnetic stirring at room temperature for 30 min. The 2:1 sludge-to-activated carbon mass ratio was selected based on preliminary optimization trials in which ratios of 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1 were evaluated for surface area development and composite homogeneity; the 2:1 ratio yielded the best balance between inorganic mineral content and carbonaceous support with the most uniform PANI coating. In the second step, 2.0 mL of freshly distilled aniline monomer was solubilized in 50 mL of 1.0 M HCl, producing a transparent acidic solution. The aniline dosage was selected based on preliminary synthesis trials and literature-reported procedures to achieve uniform PANI deposition on the SAC surface while minimizing excessive polymer loading that could block the pore structure and reduce the accessibility of adsorption sites. The solution was gradually introduced to the pre-mixed sludge-carbon suspension while continuously stirring in an ice bath maintained at 0–5 °C. 50 ml of 0.1 M ammonium persulfate (APS) solution, prepared in 1.0 M HCl, was introduced dropwise to initiate the oxidative polymerization of aniline. The molar ratio of aniline to APS was set at 1:1.25 to achieve complete polymerization.

The key chemical reactions occurring during synthesis are as follows. First, aniline is protonated in the acidic HCl medium to form anilinium cations (C₆H₅NH₃⁺ Cl⁻). Second, APS acts as the oxidant, generating sulfate radical anions (SO4·-) that oxidize the anilinium species and initiate radical coupling reactions, ultimately forming emeraldine-salt form PANI chains. Third, the growing PANI chains adsorb onto and conformally coat the surfaces of sludge ash particles and activated carbon through π–π stacking interactions between PANI aromatic rings and graphitic carbon, hydrogen bonding between PANI amine groups and surface hydroxyl/oxide groups of sludge minerals, and electrostatic interactions between protonated PANI and negatively charged mineral surfaces52.

The polymerization reaction proceeded for 6 h under constant stirring at low temperature. A greenish-black precipitate, indicative of polyaniline formation, was uniformly observed coating the surfaces of the sludge and carbon particles. The SAC–PANI composite was subjected to filtration, followed by multiple washes with 0.1 M HCl and deionized water to eliminate unreacted monomer or oxidant, and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h.

The dried composite was pulverized into a fine powder and preserved in an airtight desiccator for future characterisation and adsorption studies. The composite is designated as SAC–PANI, with S indicating calcined sludge ash, AC representing activated carbon, and PANI denoting the polyaniline polymer matrix.

Characterization techniques

A comprehensive suite of analytical techniques was utilized to characterize the morphological, structural, textural, chemical, and thermal properties of the SAC–PANI composite. Functional groups present were identified using FTIR spectroscopy, with samples prepared as KBr pellets and scanned from 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹ on a Bruker Tensor 27 instrument. XRD analysis, conducted with Cu-Kα radiation over a 5° to 80° 2θ range using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer, revealed insights into the composite’s crystalline and amorphous phases. Surface morphology and elemental composition were evaluated by SEM–EDS on a JEOL JSM-6510LV microscope, with samples coated with gold to enhance conductivity. Surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were ascertained using the BET method with nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K, complemented by BJH pore analysis. Thermal stability and decomposition patterns were assessed by TGA on a PerkinElmer TGA 4000, with heating from room.

Batch adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption tests assessed the SAC–PANI composite’s ability to eliminate Pb(II) and Cd(II) from aqueous solutions under various conditions (Fig. 2). The average values of duplicate or triplicate experiments were reported for reliability. Pb(II) and Cd(II) stock solutions were produced by dissolving Pb(NO₃)₂ and Cd(NO₃)₂·4 H₂O in deionized water to a concentration of 1000 mg/L. Diluting stock solutions to 10–100 mg/L yielded working solutions. A HANNA Instruments pH meter was used to measure the solutions’ pH after adjustment with 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. For the pH study, 50 mL of 100 mg/L metal ion solution was combined with 0.05 g of SAC–PANI composite (0.05 g/50 mL, equivalent to 1.0 g/L) and shaken at 150 rpm at 25 ± 1 °C for 120 min at pH 2.0–7.0. After filtering using Whatman No. 42 paper, residual metal concentration was measured. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Error bars in the figures represent one standard deviation from the mean.

The kinetics of adsorption were evaluated by varying the contact time between 5 and 120 min using 50 mg/L solutions of Pb(II) and Cd(II) at the optimum pH, while maintaining a constant adsorbent dosage of 0.05 g/50 mL (1.0 g/L) and a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. The experimental data were investigated using pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models to ascertain the controlling mechanism. Isotherm studies were conducted by altering the initial metal ion concentrations from 10 to 100 mg/L, with data analyzed through the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin models. The maximum adsorption capacities were determined using the Langmuir model. The impact of adsorbent dosage (0.2 to 1.2 g/L) was examined to analyze the correlation between the quantity of adsorbent and removal efficiency. Desorption and reusability studies were executed through five adsorption-desorption cycles, wherein the metal-loaded adsorbent was washed and treated with 0.1 M HCl for the purpose of desorption. The evaluation of the SAC–PANI composite’s reusability involved monitoring removal efficiency over multiple cycles. Metal ion concentrations were assessed through Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (AAS). The adsorption efficiency was quantified by calculating the percentage removal (% R) and adsorption capacity (qe) using relevant formulas53.

TCLP leaching test

The leaching stability of Pb(II) and Cd(II)-loaded SAC–PANI composites was evaluated using the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) according to EPA Method 1311. Briefly, 5.0 g of spent adsorbent was mixed with 100 mL of acetic acid extraction solution (pH 2.88 ± 0.05) in a polyethylene bottle. The suspension was agitated at 30 rpm for 18 h at room temperature. The leachate was separated by filtration through a 0.45 μm membrane filter and analyzed for Pb and Cd concentrations using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and average values were reported.

Results and discussion

Adsorbent characterization

The crystalline structure and phase composition of the SAC–PANI composite, as well as its precursors, were elucidated using XRD, as shown in Fig. 3a. The calcined sludge displayed broad diffraction peaks centered at 2θ ≈ 26.6°, 36.5°, and 50.1°, corresponding to the presence of crystalline quartz (SiO₂) and minor aluminosilicate phases typically found in mineral-rich sludge ash54,55. These peaks correspond to the (101), (110), and (112) crystallographic planes of quartz56, as confirmed by JCPDS card No. 46-1045. Activated carbon displayed a characteristic broad peak around 24.5°, indicative of its predominantly amorphous graphitic carbon structure57,58. This peak corresponds to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon (JCPDS card No. 41-1487)59.

Following the formation of the composite, the SAC–PANI sample displayed a blend of precursor signals, characterized by a slight broadening and diminished intensity of the carbon peak. This observation indicates a partial disruption of graphitic order attributed to the integration of polyaniline. New, weak peaks observed around 20° and 25° correspond to the periodicity of PANI chains in their emeraldine salt form, confirming the polymerization on the composite matrix60,61. The absence of sharp crystalline peaks associated with PANI indicates that the polymer chains are predominantly amorphous or nanocrystalline within the composite.

The textural characteristics of the SAC–PANI composite were examined using nitrogen adsorption desorption isotherms (Fig. 3b). The isotherm of the composite was categorized as type IV, distinguished by a notable hysteresis loop, demonstrating the existence of mesoporous features. The hysteresis loop is of the H3 type, indicative of slit-shaped mesopores formed between layered or aggregated particles. Furthermore, the BJH pore size distribution confirms the predominance of mesopores in the SAC–PANI composite, supporting the pore structure inferred from the adsorption–desorption isotherm.

The BET surface area of SAC–PANI was measured to be 90 m²/g, which is significantly greater than that of pristine calcined sludge (58 m²/g) and slightly lower than pure activated carbon (312 m²/g), indicating that the polymerization process partially blocked some pores but also introduced new mesoporosity.

The reduction in BET surface area from 312 m²/g (AC) to 90 m²/g (SAC–PANI) is attributed to partial blockage of AC micropores by PANI chains deposited during in situ polymerization. Despite this reduction in total surface area relative to AC, the adsorption performance of SAC–PANI for Pb(II) and Cd(II) is substantially superior. This is explained by three key factors: (1) PANI introduces a high density of nitrogen-containing functional groups (–NH– and = N–) that form strong coordinate bonds with Pb(II) and Cd(II), providing chemically active sites not present on bare AC; (2) the dominant mesoporosity of SAC–PANI (average pore diameter ~ 5.4 nm) facilitates diffusion of hydrated heavy metal ions that may be sterically restricted within AC micropores; and (3) the sludge-derived mineral phases contribute additional ion exchange sites via surface hydroxyl groups. Thus, the quality and chemical nature of active sites, rather than raw surface area alone, governs the overall adsorption performance.

The average pore diameter of SAC–PANI (Fig. 3c) was approximately 5.4 nm, and the total pore volume was 0.42 cm³/g, which is consistent with mesoporous materials. The diffusion and adsorption of heavy metal ions are supported by the hierarchical porous architecture, for easy access to active sites on both the inorganic mineral particles and the polymer chains. These inorganic particles refer specifically to SiO₂ (quartz), aluminosilicate phases, and calcium/iron oxide minerals derived from the calcinated sewage sludge ash, as identified by XRD analysis. These mineral phases contribute surface hydroxyl groups and ion-exchange sites that participate in the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II), complementing the coordination interactions provided by the PANI functional groups.

The functional groups present in the SAC–PANI composite and its precursor materials were investigated by FTIR spectroscopy to confirm the polymerization of polyaniline onto the sludge-activated carbon matrix. The FTIR spectrum of raw calcined sludge (Fig. 3c) showed characteristic absorption bands at approximately 1050 cm⁻¹, ascribed to Si–O–Si stretching vibrations, and a broad band at 3400 cm⁻¹ corresponding to surface hydroxyl groups62. Activated carbon exhibited a dominant peak about 1580 cm⁻¹, linked to C = C aromatic ring stretching63, and bands around 1100 cm⁻¹ indicating C–O functionalities64.

In the SAC–PANI composite spectrum, new prominent peaks emerged at 1560 and 1485 cm⁻¹, attributed to the quinoid and benzenoid ring vibrations of polyaniline, respectively65. Additionally, the characteristic peak near 1295 cm⁻¹, linked to C–N stretching of secondary aromatic amines, validated the successful integration of PANI chains onto the composite surface66. The prominent peak at 3400 cm⁻¹ became more intense and slightly shifted, indicating enhanced hydrogen bonding interactions owing to the presence of amine and imine groups from PANI67. These spectral changes collectively confirm the creation of a hybrid polymeric structure with potential active sites capable of binding metal ions.

TGA was conducted to investigate the thermal stability and composition of the SAC–PANI composite compared to its individual components (Fig. 3d). The raw calcined sludge demonstrated high thermal stability, with minimal weight loss (~ 3%) up to 800 °C, consistent with its inorganic mineral composition. In contrast, activated carbon exhibited a gradual weight loss of approximately 15% between 200 °C and 600 °C, attributable to the volatilization of remaining organic substances and surface functional groups.

The SAC–PANI composite showed a characteristic multi-stage degradation profile. A preliminary weight reduction of roughly 5% below 120 °C was assigned to moisture evaporation and physically adsorbed water. The main degradation phase took place between 250 °C and 550 °C, with approximately 30% weight loss, corresponding to the decomposition of the polyaniline polymer chains and partial oxidation of carbonaceous components. Above 600 °C, the weight stabilized, indicating the formation of thermally stable residue composed mainly of inorganic sludge minerals.

These results demonstrate that the inclusion of polyaniline moderately reduces the thermal stability of the composite relative to sludge ash but maintains sufficient robustness for effective wastewater treatment applications under ambient conditions.

The zeta potential of AC sludge S, and the composite SAC–PANI was assessed over a pH range of 3 to 11 to understand the surface charge behavior of each material and its potential influence on adsorption and dispersion in aqueous systems, as shown in Fig. 3e. For AC, the zeta potential became increasingly negative as the pH rose from − 5.6 mV at pH 3 to − 47.5 mV at pH 11. This trend observed in activated carbon results from the stepwise deprotonation of surface functional groups, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, which leads to an increasingly negatively charged surface under alkaline conditions68.

The sludge sample (S) showed a similar pH-dependent trend, with zeta potential values shifting from − 7.4 mV at pH 3 to − 38.2 mV at pH 11. This increase in surface negativity is attributed to the presence of ionizable functional groups and surface hydroxyl species within the sludge matrix69. The results suggest that sludge, like AC, becomes more negatively charged as pH increases, potentially affecting its behavior in sorption processes.

The SAC–PANI composite, incorporating activated carbon (AC), calcined sludge ash (S), and polyaniline (PANI), exhibited distinct zeta-potential behavior over the investigated pH range (Fig. 3f). Under acidic conditions (pH 2–3), the composite surface was slightly positively charged (+ 3.9 mV at pH 2) owing to the protonation of the amine groups of polyaniline. As the pH increased, gradual deprotonation caused a continuous decrease in the zeta potential, which crossed zero at approximately pH 4.3, corresponding to the isoelectric point (IEP) of the SAC–PANI composite. This value was used to estimate the point of zero charge (pHpzc). Above this pH, the surface became negatively charged, favoring the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) through electrostatic attraction in addition to surface complexation and ion-exchange interactions. This surface charge transition explains the marked increase in adsorption efficiency observed at pH 6.0, whereas adsorption at lower pH values was suppressed by electrostatic repulsion and competition between H⁺ ions and metal ions for the available adsorption sites.

Adsorbent morphology

The SEM image of activated carbon (AC) (Fig. 4a and b) illustrates its porous architecture, which is critical for its adsorption characteristics. The image clearly displays a network of well-defined pores with a rough, irregular surface texture, indicative of the high surface area typically linked to activated carbon. In the context of the present study, this porous structure plays a key role in the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions, providing extensive internal surface area and diffusion pathways that allow heavy metal ions to access active binding sites throughout the AC particle interior, thereby contributing to the overall adsorption performance of the SAC–PANI composite.

The SEM image of the calcined sludge (Fig. 4c and d) reveals a dense, agglomerated structure with a rough surface morphology. The particles appear to be closely packed and exhibit a cluster-like arrangement, which is typical of calcined materials. These structures are composed of fine, irregularly shaped particles with a high degree of porosity, which may contribute to their surface area and potential reactivity.

Adsorbent performance

The adsorption efficiency of the SAC–PANI composite towards Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions was strongly influenced by the solution pH, as depicted in Fig. 5a. Removal percentages increased significantly as the pH rose from 2.0 to 6.0, achieving peak adsorption efficiencies of 94.7 ± 0.6% for Pb(II) and 91.3 ± 0.7% for Cd(II) at pH 6.0. Below pH 4.0, adsorption was hindered by competition between excess H⁺ ions and metal ions for the positively charged sites on the composite surface in acidic environments70.

At pH values above 6.0, precipitation of metal hydroxides began, complicating the adsorption process; therefore, these pH values were excluded from further study. This behavior is consistent with the estimated pHpzc value of approximately 4.3, obtained from the zeta-potential curve (Fig. 3f). Below the pHpzc, the surface carries a net positive charge, resulting in electrostatic repulsion of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions as well as increased competition with H⁺ ions for the available adsorption sites71. Above the pHpzc, the surface becomes negatively charged, promoting electrostatic attraction and facilitating the adsorption of the metal cations.

The efficacy of Pb(II) and Cd(II) removal by SAC–PANI (Fig. 5b) is strongly influenced by the adsorbent dosage. As the dosage increases from 0.2 to 1.2 g/L, the uptake of both metal ions improves significantly. At low dosages (0.2 g/L), limited availability of active sites restricts adsorption, resulting in lower removal rates. Increasing the dosage to 0.5 and 0.8 g/L provides more binding sites, substantially enhancing metal ion removal. Further increases to 1.0 and 1.2 g/L continue to raise efficiency, though the rate of improvement slows, indicating a plateau. At the maximum dosage tested (1.2 g/L), removal efficiencies reach 94.9 ± 0.5% for Pb(II) and 91.4 ± 0.8% for Cd(II), reflecting high uptake capacity. This leveling off is ascribed to the depletion of metal ions in solution and possible aggregation of adsorbent particles, which could decrease the effective surface area for adsorption72.

From a mechanistic perspective, the increasing efficiency with dosage reflects the progressive availability of nitrogen-rich PANI functional groups (amine and imine), surface hydroxyl groups on sludge-derived mineral phases, and carbonaceous active sites of AC, all of which collectively provide a greater number of metal-binding sites per unit volume of solution. The aggregation at high dosages is attributed to van der Waals and electrostatic inter-particle attractions that reduce the net specific surface area exposed to the solution.

The contact time also showed a pronounced impact on adsorption kinetics (Fig. 5c). Rapid uptake occurred within the first 30 min, associated with the plentiful supply of active sites on the SAC–PANI surface. The initial fast adsorption stage is primarily driven by surface complexation and electrostatic attraction, while the slower subsequent phase includes intraparticle diffusion and gradual saturation of internal pores73. Specifically, the initial rapid phase (0–30 min) corresponds to the occupation of readily accessible external surface sites and large mesopores by metal ions, a process governed by film diffusion and fast chemisorption. The subsequent slower phase (30–120 min) involves intraparticle diffusion of metal ions through the mesoporous network toward internal active sites, with increasing diffusional resistance as pores progressively fill. The adsorption efficiency reached 94.7 ± 0.6% for Pb(II) and 91.3 ± 0.7% for Cd(II), showing a highly effective removal process for both metals after 120 min.

The initial concentration of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions (Fig. 5d) notably influenced the removal efficiency of the SAC-PANI composite. At lower concentrations, such as 20 mg/L, the removal efficiencies were 99.2 ± 0.3% for Pb(II) and 97.8 ± 0.4% for Cd(II). At higher concentrations, such as 100 mg/L, the removal efficiencies decreased to 94.7 ± 0.6% for Pb(II) and 91.3 ± 0.7% for Cd(II). This slight decrease is attributed to the progressive saturation of the available adsorption sites on the SAC-PANI composite74.

Furthermore, the increased initial metal ion concentration enhances the mass transfer driving force between the bulk solution and the adsorbent surface, facilitating the diffusion of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions toward available adsorption sites75. The greater availability of metal ions also increases the probability of interaction with the active functional groups of the SAC–PANI composite, including PANI amine/imine groups, surface hydroxyl groups of sludge-derived mineral phases, and oxygen-containing functional groups on activated carbon, thereby improving metal ion uptake until adsorption sites approach saturation.

The effect of temperature on the adsorption efficiency of the SAC–PANI composite was examined over the range of 25–65 °C (Fig. 5e). A rise in temperature correlated with a gradual enhancement in removing efficiencies of both Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions. Specifically, Pb(II) removal increased from 94.7 ± 0.6% at 25 °C to 98.6 ± 0.4% at 65 °C, while Cd(II) removal rose from 91.3 ± 0.7% to 95.3 ± 0.5% within the same temperature range. This trend reveals that higher temperatures facilitate the adsorption process, likely due to improved mobility of metal ions and more effective diffusion toward the available active sites on the SAC–PANI surface74. In addition, elevated temperatures may facilitate stronger interactions between the metal ions and functional groups within the composite structure.

Adsorption kinetics

The kinetic behavior of Pb(II) and Cd(II) (Fig. 6a and b) adsorption onto SAC-PANI composites was examined utilizing both pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models. Experimental findings displayed an excellent alignment to the pseudo-second-order model, with correlation values over 0.99 (Pb(II): R² = 0.998; Cd(II): R² = 0.997). This strong agreement suggests that chemisorption predominantly controls the adsorption mechanism. However, it is important to note that pseudo-second-order (PSO) fitting alone cannot conclusively prove a chemisorption mechanism. Therefore, additional evidence from FTIR analysis before and after adsorption, together with BET, SEM, EDS, and elemental mapping results, was used to elucidate the adsorption mechanism. These findings support the involvement of surface complexation and coordination interactions between the metal ions and the functional groups of the SAC–PANI composite.

The rate constants (k₂) acquired through the pseudo-second-order kinetic model were 0.035 ± 0.001 g/mg•min for Pb(II) and 0.029 ± 0.001 g/mg min for Cd(II), demonstrating that Pb(II) ions are adsorbed more rapidly on the SAC–PANI surface than Cd(II). The disparity in adsorption kinetics might be ascribed to variations in ionic size, electronegativity, and the intensity of interactions between metal ions and the functional groups on the adsorbent. Furthermore, the calculated equilibrium adsorption capacities obtained from the pseudo-second-order model were in close agreement with the experimental values, confirming the suitability of the model for describing the adsorption process. The relatively higher k₂ value obtained for Pb(II) indicates a stronger affinity of Pb(II) toward the active nitrogen- and oxygen-containing functional groups of the SAC–PANI composite. Overall, the kinetic results demonstrate that the adsorption process is rapid and efficient, with equilibrium being achieved within 120 min under the investigated conditions.

Adsorption isotherms

The adsorption equilibrium of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions on SAC–PANI was investigated by applying three well-established isotherm models (Fig. 7; Table 1): Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin. These models offer significant insights into the adsorption capacity, the nature of surface interactions between metal ions and the adsorbent, and the thermodynamic aspects governing the process.

The Langmuir isotherm model characterizes adsorption as a phenomenon occurring on a on a uniform surface with a finite number of comparable binding sites. It posits that adsorption transpires as a monolayer, with each site accommodating only a single adsorbate molecule. Furthermore, it posits that there are no interactions between molecules adsorbed on neighboring sites. Where qe is the equilibrium adsorption capacity (mg/g), Ce is the equilibrium concentration in solution (mg/L), qm is the maximum monolayer adsorption capacity, and KL is the Langmuir constant related to adsorption affinity.

The adsorption data for Pb(II) and Cd(II) on the SAC–PANI composite strongly aligned with the Langmuir isotherm model, as evidenced by correlation coefficients of 0.995 and 0.992, respectively. The composite revealed notable adsorption capabilities, with maximum absorption values of 128.5 ± 2.3 mg/g for Pb(II) and 115.7 ± 2.1 mg/g for Cd(II). The Langmuir affinity constants (KL) were established as 0.045 ± 0.002 L/mg for Pb(II) and 0.039 ± 0.002 L/mg for Cd(II), indicating a marginally higher binding affinity for lead ions.

This model’s suitability implies that the adsorption process is mostly subject to chemisorption forming a monolayer on energetically uniform sites, which is crucial in designing and scaling SAC-PANI adsorbents for water remediation. The higher maximum adsorption capacity obtained for Pb(II) (128.5 ± 2.3 mg/g) compared with Cd(II) (115.7 ± 2.1 mg/g) indicates a stronger interaction of Pb(II) ions with the active adsorption sites of the SAC–PANI composite. This behavior may be attributed to differences in ionic properties, including ionic radius, hydration energy, and affinity toward nitrogen- and oxygen-containing functional groups present in the polyaniline matrix and sludge-derived mineral phases. The relatively high KL values further confirm the favorable adsorption affinity of both metal ions toward the composite surface.

Contrastingly, the Freundlich isotherm is an empirical model that characterizes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces with multilayer adsorption possibilities. Where KF represents adsorption capacity and n is the intensity parameter describing adsorption favorability and heterogeneity.

The parameters derived from experimental data were KF =11.41 ± 0.32 and n = 1.34 ± 0.05 for Pb(II), and KF=9.79 ± 0.28 and n = 1.20 ± 0.04 for Cd(II), with correlation coefficients R2 of 0.983 and 0.980, respectively. Since n > 1 for both metals, adsorption is considered favorable, indicating the presence of a variety of binding energies on the SAC-PANI composite surface, likely due to the combined porous carbon matrix and diverse nitrogen-rich polyaniline functional groups. The Freundlich constants (n = 1.34 for Pb(II) and 1.20 for Cd(II)) indicate favorable adsorption over the investigated concentration range. These values suggest that adsorption occurs on a heterogeneous surface containing multiple types of adsorption sites with different energy levels. The heterogeneous nature of SAC–PANI can be attributed to the coexistence of activated carbon pores, mineral components derived from sludge ash, and nitrogen-containing functional groups introduced by polyaniline.

The Temkin isotherm considers adsorbate-adsorbent interactions affecting the heat of adsorption, presuming a linear reduction in adsorption energy with increasing coverage. It can be described as78:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q_e}=\frac{{RT}}{{{b_T}}}ln\left( {AT} \right){\text{ }}+\frac{{RT}}{{{b_T}}}ln{C_e}$$\end{document}

In this context, R denotes the universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol/K), T represents the absolute temperature (K), b signifies the Temkin isotherm constant associated with the heat of adsorption (J/mol), and AT indicates the equilibrium binding constant linked to the maximal binding energy (L/g).

For SAC-PANI, the model produced good fits with R2 values of 0.978 for Pb(II) and 0.975 for Cd(II). The adsorption heat parameter bT was 22.08 ± 0.68 J/mol for Pb(II) and 19.54 ± 0.61 J/mol for Cd(II), indicating an endothermic adsorption process and suggesting chemisorption. The binding constants AT of 2.32 ± 0.09 L/mg and 1.89 ± 0.07 L/mg corroborated a stronger interaction affinity for Pb(II) and Cd(II) respectively. The positive Temkin constants suggest that the adsorption process involves significant adsorbate–adsorbent interactions and that the heat of adsorption decreases gradually with increasing surface coverage. The higher AT value observed for Pb(II) compared with Cd(II) further supports the stronger affinity of Pb(II) toward the active sites of the SAC–PANI composite.

The high correlation coefficients obtained for all three isotherm models confirm the reliability of the experimental adsorption data. Among them, the Langmuir model exhibited the highest correlation coefficients (R² = 0.995 for Pb(II) and 0.992 for Cd(II)), indicating that monolayer adsorption on relatively homogeneous active sites is the dominant adsorption mechanism. The excellent adsorption capacities together with the favorable Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin parameters demonstrate the strong potential of the SAC–PANI composite for the efficient removal of heavy metals from contaminated water.

The thermodynamic examination of Pb(II) and Cd(II) adsorption on SAC-PANI composites offers essential insights critical findings into the properties and viability of the adsorption process. At 298 K, Pb(II) and Cd(II) exhibited negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°) values of -19.8 kJ/mol and − 17.6 kJ/mol, respectively, signifying spontaneous adsorption. This spontaneity signifies that the process is thermodynamically favorable and does not necessitate further energy input to continue.

The positive enthalpy change (ΔH°), approximately + 33.5 kJ/mol for Pb(II) and + 25.3 kJ/mol for Cd(II), suggests that the adsorption process is endothermic, requiring the absorption of heat80. This corresponds with chemisorption mechanisms, wherein chemical connections establish between heavy metal ions and the active sites on the SAC–PANI composite. The positive entropy change (ΔS°) of + 132 J/mol·K for Pb(II) and + 98 J/mol·K for Cd(II) signifies an augmentation of disorder at the solid–liquid interface during adsorption. The heightened unpredictability is probably due to the liberation of water molecules and other ions as the metal ions adhere to the adsorbent surface80.

Furthermore, the more negative ΔG° value observed for Pb(II) compared with Cd(II) indicates that Pb(II) adsorption is thermodynamically more favorable on the SAC–PANI composite. The relatively higher ΔH° value obtained for Pb(II) also suggests stronger interactions between Pb(II) ions and the active functional groups of the adsorbent. The positive ΔS° values for both metal ions indicate increased randomness at the solid–solution interface, which may result from the displacement of hydrated water molecules and counter ions during adsorption. Overall, the negative ΔG°, positive ΔH°, and positive ΔS° values confirm that the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) onto SAC–PANI is a spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy-driven process.

Reusability Study and mechanism of SAC–PANI

The reusability of the SAC–PANI composite was investigated during five successive adsorption–desorption cycles utilizing 0.1 M HCl as the desorbing agent. After each cycle, the composite was thoroughly washed and dried prior to reuse. As illustrated in Fig. 10a, the composite maintained over 85% of its initial removal efficiency for Pb(II) and 82.4 ± 1.1% for Cd(II) after five cycles, demonstrating excellent regeneration capacity and structural stability. The observed reduction in adsorption capacity over cycles is attributed to partial blockage of adsorption sites or incomplete desorption of metal ions.

A critical concern associated with the application of adsorbent materials in wastewater treatment is the possibility of secondary contamination caused by the spent adsorbent after disposal. Therefore, the leaching behavior of fresh and metal-loaded SAC–PANI composites was evaluated using TCLP analysis. The results are presented in Table 4. Fresh SAC–PANI showed no detectable Pb(II) or Cd(II) leaching, indicating that no measurable amounts of these target toxic metals were released from the pristine composite under TCLP conditions. After adsorption, Pb(II)-loaded SAC–PANI released 0.41 ± 0.03 mg L⁻¹ Pb(II), whereas Cd(II) -loaded SAC–PANI released 0.12 ± 0.01 mg L⁻¹ Cd(II). After five adsorption–desorption cycles, the leached concentrations slightly increased to 0.58 ± 0.04 mg L⁻¹ for Pb(II) and 0.18 ± 0.02 mg L⁻¹ for Cd(II). Nevertheless, all values remained substantially below the EPA TCLP limits of 5.0 mg L⁻¹ for Pb(II) and 1.0 mg L⁻¹ for Cd(II).

The low leaching levels demonstrate the strong retention of Pb(II) and Cd(II) within the SAC–PANI matrix. This stability can be attributed to coordination interactions between metal ions and nitrogen-containing functional groups of polyaniline, surface complexation with oxygen-containing groups, ion-exchange interactions with mineral components, and physical confinement within the porous structure. These results indicate that SAC–PANI exhibits a low potential for secondary metal contamination after repeated use.

Although TCLP analysis confirms the low chemical leaching risk of the spent adsorbent, it does not directly evaluate biological toxicity. Therefore, further ecotoxicological studies using appropriate aquatic organisms are recommended to provide additional assessment of long-term environmental compatibility.

After adsorption, noticeable changes were observed in the FTIR spectra of both Pb(II)- and Cd(II)-loaded SAC–PANI composites (Fig. 10b). The characteristic O–H/N–H band around 3400 cm⁻¹, along with the quinoid (1560 cm⁻¹), benzenoid (1485 cm⁻¹), and C–N stretching (1295 cm⁻¹) bands, exhibited shifts toward lower wavenumbers and a reduction in intensity. These changes indicate the participation of hydroxyl, amine, and imine functional groups in the adsorption process. The spectral variations were more pronounced for Pb(II) than for Cd(II), suggesting stronger interactions between Pb(II) ions and the active sites of the SAC–PANI composite. The observed shifts and intensity reductions confirm that metal ion removal occurs through coordination and surface complexation with the nitrogen-containing groups of polyaniline and the oxygen-containing functional groups present on the activated carbon surface.

The BET surface area of SAC–PANI (Fig. 10c) decreased from 90 m²/g to 60.5 m²/g after Pb(II) adsorption and to 71 m²/g after Cd(II) adsorption, confirming the occupation of adsorption sites and partial pore blockage by metal ions. The larger reduction for Pb(II) indicates stronger interaction with the adsorbent surface.

The SEM image of the SAC–PANI composite before adsorption (Fig. 11a) shows a porous three-dimensional network with interconnected cavities and channels, providing abundant adsorption sites and efficient diffusion pathways. The rough and folded surface morphology indicates the successful incorporation of polyaniline onto the sludge-derived activated carbon framework. After Pb(II) and Cd(II) adsorption (Fig. 11b and c), the surface becomes denser and is covered with bright particulate deposits, indicating the attachment and immobilization of metal ions on the composite surface.

The EDS spectrum of the pristine SAC–PANI composite (Fig. 11) confirms the presence of C, N, O, Si, Al, S, and Ca, corresponding to the carbon matrix, polyaniline, and sludge-derived mineral phases. No characteristic peaks corresponding to Pb or Cd were observed in the pristine composite, indicating that these metals were absent or below the detection limit of the EDS analysis prior to adsorption. Following adsorption, distinct Pb and Cd peaks appeared in the EDS spectra, confirming the successful uptake of Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions. Furthermore, elemental mapping showed a uniform distribution of Pb and Cd across the composite surface, demonstrating effective immobilization of the metal ions. These results collectively confirm the excellent adsorption performance of the SAC–PANI composite toward Pb(II) and Cd(II).

Based on the characterization results, the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) onto the SAC–PANI composite is proposed to occur through multiple synergistic mechanisms (Fig. 12). (1) Surface complexation: The dominant mechanism involves coordination of Pb²⁺ and Cd²⁺ with the nitrogen-containing functional groups of PANI (amine, –NH–, and imine, =N–), which act as electron donors to form stable coordination bonds with the metal ions. The shifts in the FTIR bands at 1560, 1485, and 1295 cm⁻¹ after adsorption support the involvement of these functional groups in metal binding81. (2) Ion exchange: Surface hydroxyl groups (–OH) associated with SiO₂ and aluminosilicate phases of the calcined sludge ash participate in ion-exchange reactions (–Si–OH + M²⁺ → –Si–O–M⁺ + H⁺), providing additional adsorption sites independent of the PANI matrix. (3) Pore filling and diffusion: The mesoporous structure (average pore diameter ≈ 5.4 nm and total pore volume ≈ 0.42 cm³/g) facilitates the diffusion and physical entrapment of hydrated Pb²⁺ and Cd²⁺ ions within the pore network, consistent with the intraparticle diffusion stage observed during adsorption kinetics and the monolayer adsorption behavior described by the Langmuir isotherm82. (4) π–cation interactions: The aromatic rings of PANI may also interact with Pb²⁺ through π–cation interactions, whereby the electron-rich π-system of the polymer contributes to metal binding. This interaction is expected to be more significant for Pb²⁺ because of its higher affinity for π-electron systems, consistent with the more pronounced FTIR changes observed after Pb(II) adsorption. (5) Electrostatic attraction: At pH values above the pHpzc (~ 4.3), the SAC–PANI surface becomes negatively charged, promoting the electrostatic attraction of Pb²⁺ and Cd²⁺ ions. This interaction facilitates the transport of metal ions toward the adsorbent surface, where they subsequently undergo coordination and ion-exchange reactions.

Although the incorporation of polyaniline (PANI) increases the material cost compared with pristine sludge ash or activated carbon alone, the overall production cost remains relatively low because the composite is primarily composed of waste-derived materials. Calcined sludge ash and activated carbon account for approximately 60 wt% of the composite, while PANI constitutes about 40 wt%.

Despite the additional cost associated with PANI incorporation, the SAC–PANI composite exhibits excellent adsorption performance, achieving maximum adsorption capacities of 128.5 ± 2.3 mg g⁻¹ for Pb(II) and 115.7 ± 2.1 mg g⁻¹ for Cd(II). Furthermore, the adsorbent retained more than 82% of its initial removal efficiency after five adsorption–desorption cycles, demonstrating good regeneration capability and operational stability.

Based on an adsorption capacity of 128.5 mg Pb(II) g⁻¹ and an average adsorbent cost of USD 2.5 kg⁻¹, approximately 7.8 kg of SAC–PANI would be required to remove 1 kg of Pb(II), corresponding to a material cost of approximately USD 19.5 per kilogram of Pb(II) removed. Moreover, regeneration and reuse over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles can substantially reduce the effective treatment cost, further improving the economic viability of the process.

Overall, the combination of waste-derived raw materials, straightforward synthesis, high adsorption capacity, excellent regeneration performance, and low secondary contamination risk demonstrates that SAC–PANI is a promising and economically attractive adsorbent for the removal of heavy metals from industrial wastewater.

Limitations, recommendations, and future scope

Although the SAC–PANI composite demonstrated excellent adsorption performance toward Pb(II) and Cd(II), this study has certain limitations. The adsorption experiments were conducted under batch conditions, which may not fully represent real continuous-flow industrial wastewater systems. In addition, the adsorption mechanism was mainly interpreted based on FTIR, BET, SEM, and EDS analyses; therefore, more advanced surface-sensitive techniques such as XPS or EXAFS are recommended to provide direct and more detailed evidence of metal–surface interactions.

Furthermore, the heavy metal content of the raw sewage sludge was not quantitatively analyzed in this study; techniques such as XRF or ICP-OES are recommended for future work to better characterize sludge variability and ensure more comprehensive feedstock assessment. The production cost and large-scale economic feasibility of SAC–PANI were also not evaluated and should be addressed in future scale-up studies.

Future work should focus on evaluating the long-term stability and regeneration behavior of the composite under continuous-flow conditions, as well as testing its performance in real industrial wastewater containing mixed contaminants. In addition, life-cycle assessment and process optimization for large-scale production are recommended to support practical environmental applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully synthesized a novel sludge-activated carbon–polyaniline (SAC–PANI) composite via in situ polymerization, combining the porous structure of calcined sludge-derived activated carbon with the functional amine groups of polyaniline. Comprehensive characterization using FTIR, SEM, XRD, BET, TGA, and zeta potential investigations validated the development of a hybrid material with enhanced surface functionality, mesoporosity, and thermal stability suitable for heavy metal adsorption. The SAC–PANI composite exhibited exceptional adsorption capabilities for Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions, reaching maximum capacities of 128.5 ± 2.3 mg/g and 115.7 ± 2.1 mg/g, respectively, in near-neutral pH conditions. The adsorption kinetics adhered to a pseudo-second-order model, implying that chemisorption is the primary process. The material demonstrated removal efficiencies exceeding 90% within 120 min and exhibited exceptional reusability, retaining over 80% of its capacity after five cycles. The findings show that the SAC–PANI composite serves as an effective, sustainable, and economically viable adsorbent for the removal of toxic heavy metals from wastewater. The successful validation using real electroplating wastewater, achieving 75.0% removal of Pb(II) and 72.6% removal of Cd(II) in the presence of competing ions and complex matrix components, demonstrates its practical applicability for industrial wastewater treatment. The utilization of industrial sludge as a precursor enhances value through the recycling of waste materials and the mitigation of environmental impact, thereby aligning with circular economy principles in water treatment applications. Future studies should focus on continuous-flow and pilot-scale evaluations to further demonstrate the practical applicability of the SAC–PANI composite for industrial wastewater treatment.
