Characterization, optimization and emission analysis of manually-made charcoal dust briquettes with starch, paper and algae binders
Authors: George Lomunyak, Booker Osodo, Francis Njoka, Emmanuel Kombe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Utilization of loose waste biomass such as charcoal dust remains popular in low-to mid-income countries due to increased energy demands, poverty and heighten efforts to mitigate climate change. This study sought to investigate the effects of starch, paper and algae binders on the physical, mechanical and combustion characteristics and emission levels of manually-made acacia charcoal dust briquettes. Acacia charcoal dust is collected and mixed with different binders at binder proportions of 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 25 % and 30 % of the total weight for individual samples. Briquette samples are produced using a manually-operated briquetting machine. Briquettes are dried, characterized, and analyzed. Response Surface Methodology is used to investigate the effect of binder type and binder proportions on calorific value, ignition time and burning rate of the briquettes. Results show that the density of the produced briquettes from Charcoal-Starch (CS), Charcoal-Paper (CP) and Charcoal-Algae (CA) samples ranged between 571.5 kg/m3- 678.1 kg/m3, 495.8 kg/m3-534.4 kg/m3, 535.6 kg/m3-578.2 kg/m3, respectively. A mean shatter resistance of 91.3 %, 97.9 % and 95.2 % is obtained for CS, CP and CA briquettes, respectively. Moisture content, volatile matter, ash content and fixed carbon for all the samples ranged between 4.7 % and 7.1 %, 30 % and 46 %, 14 % and 32 %, 23 % and 56 %, respectively. Moreover, optimization response results show that a 30 % starch binder proportion gives the best calorific value, ignition time and burning rate. The highest emissions for CO, NOx and SO2 are, respectively, 389 ppm, 2.86 ppm and 2.26 ppm for CS samples, 345.3 ppm, 5.46 ppm, and 2.66 ppm for CP, and 422.1 ppm, 5.46 ppm 3.06 ppm for CA samples. Those results show that it is practically feasible to manually-make quality acacia charcoal dust briquettes with starch, paper and algae as binders. Such briquettes hence provide a quality source of fuel curbing deforestation and waste dumping, reducing fuel shortages in cities and forming a source of livelihood especially in developing countries.

Introduction

One of the renewable sources of energy considered to play a critical role in mitigating climate change, among other resources is biomass energy [1]. When used sustainably, it can supply most of the world's energy needs [2]. Biomass briquetting, a densification technology, is one of the technologies used in improving the quality of loose biomass primarily for household or commercial heating and power generation applications [3].

Use of charcoal especially in Sub-Sahara Africa is quite prevalent in urban areas where both middle- and low-income classes heavily depend on it for cooking. Whereas the production of charcoal takes place in rural areas, sale of charcoal is often complicated as conversely, it is one of the main causes of deforestation. Hence, middlemen, usually found on city peripheries, serve as intermediaries due to occasional bans by governments. Transportation and handling of charcoal results in up to 20 % losses as fine dust particles [4]. As a result and for lack of cheap sources of cooking fuel, metropolitan dwellers and urban poor find themselves mixing this accumulated dust with soil, animal dung, etc. to recreate alternative cooking balls/fuel. Similar practices mainly by environmentalists and pro-climate change women or youth groups, are observed in rural areas near charcoal production and/or storage facilities/sites. There is no appropriate technology of using this waste as the present concepts are based on rudimentary practices. The effectiveness of densification process depends greatly on cohesion of particles [5] something that is not adhered to in most cases.

Biomass in general contain naturally-occurring structural binders such as lignin and proteins that are activated as binding agents when biomass is densified at relatively high temperature and pressure [6,7]. Attainment of such conditions call for the use of motorized briquetting machines which requires electricity leading to increased cost of production [8]. For manual briquette making, external binding agents can be used to facilitate extrusion of loose biomass at low temperatures and pressures. The choice of the binder is critical in improvement of the quality of briquettes. There is hence intense efforts being made to get cheap, available and more sustainable binders for briquette production [9]. Besides, binders affect physical and combustion characteristics, and emission levels of briquettes [10,11].

Extensive studies have been done on the effects of binders on quality of briquettes. Starch is frequently preferred as a biomass binder due to its availability, good mechanical strength and high energy content [7,12,13]. Also, starch forms strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules [14]. For instance, Borowski et al. [15] used starch as a binder in charcoal briquettes production and reported that the produced briquettes had good mechanical characteristics. Also, Velusamy et al. [12] found that the density of briquettes formed improved significantly. This was also noted by Aransiola et al. [16]. In other studies, Lubwama et al. [13] suggested that cassava starch binder produced briquettes with better calorific values than clay binder. Most office papers are thrown out or burnt [17]. In efforts to reduce solid municipal wastes, waste paper has been used to make briquettes [18] and has also been commonly converted to paper pulp to act as a binder in briquetting [19]. Waste paper improves some properties of briquettes. For example, Kebede et al. [20] used waste paper pulp as a binder in coffee pulp residues briquettes and established that though the binder had high volatile matter, there was an increase in fixed carbon and calorific value of the selected residues. Also, 30 % waste paper pulp binder in saw dust briquettes had comparatively high compressive strength and low calorific value than 30 % wheat starch binder [8].

Algae is a vast group of photosynthetic, heterotrophic, single-celled organisms forming many taxonomic groups. According to Nagarajan et al. [21], algae can be used as a biomass binder due to its high protein and low lignin content. In addition, algae inhabit fresh water and sea water ecosystems, they possess faster growth rate and are easy to cultivate than most plant biomass [22]. Moreover, algae can survive in diverse climates that are unsuitable for agriculture unlike most terrestrial biomass [23]. Due to these properties, Marangon et al. [24] admitted that there is need for development of microalgae biomass briquette as a means of realizing a more sustainable energy product with very little pressure in the environment. In the production of briquettes from a blends of rice husks, corn cobs and bagasse with algae acting as binding agent, Muazu & Stegemann [25] noted that the binder increased durability, density and energy value of the briquettes. Besides this, Olugbade et al. [26], reported that microalgae binder was more durable than other binders because of its low lignin content.

Utilization of energy-rich charcoal dusts often discarded as waste in commercial kiln zones and charcoal selling and/or storage yards present a unique opportunity to exploit waste biomass reducing both energy poverty and climate change challenges. From the reviewed literature, there are limited studies that attempted to vigorously compare the effects of starch, waste paper and algae on acacia charcoal dust. Comparison of such common binders is critical since they are linked to the resultant briquette combustion and mechanical properties. Furthermore, no optimization attempts have been made to arrive at the best binder-acacia charcoal dust admixtures. This study therefore set to investigate the influence of starch, waste paper and algae binders on physical and mechanical properties, combustion characteristic and emissions levels of manually-made acacia charcoal dust briquettes. The study further optimized some parameters that would yield quality briquettes.

Materials and methods

Test samples collection and preparation

Charcoal dust from acacia tree species (Acacia nilotica) which is mostly preferred for woodfuel in dry land areas in Kenya [1] was obtained from a traditional charcoal kiln yard in Kiserian, Kajiado County, Kenya. Wheat flour was used as starch binder. Waste papers were collected from solid municipal waste dumpsite in Kiserian while algae was collected from a pond in the same area.

The large foreign materials in the charcoal dust were handpicked and the resulting dust sieved through a 2 mm mesh to obtain a uniform feedstock. Algae biomass binder was dried and ground then passed through the 2 mm mesh sieve. Starch solution and algae paste were prepared by adding the binders in distilled water in a mass ratio of 2:3 for 5 min [27]. Starch was added to a warm water to provide a gel like paste. Waste paper was shredded into small pieces and soaked in cold water at room temperature for three days and converted to pulp by ponding manually using pestle and mortar. For each binding agent, five samples were prepared where charcoal dust with binder proportions of 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 25 % and 30 % of the total weight was homogenously mixed. The blended charcoal dusts with different binders were then filled up to the edge of a locally-made briquetting machine mold and pressed. The formed briquettes were extruded and then dried naturally in a well-ventilated room at room temperature for 30 days. Physical and mechanical characterization

The mass (m) of dried briquette samples was determined using an electronic balance while the volume (v) of the sample was calculated by measuring length, width and height of samples using Vanier calipers. A set of three sample briquettes from each admixture was used and the values determined from the averages. Briquette density (ρ) was determined as the ratio of mass and the volume. Shatter resistance of the briquettes was determined based on ASTM D440-49 standard applied by Waluyo et al. [10]. The briquette samples were dropped from a height of 1.8 m and falling on a solid surface 3 times. The mass of the larger pieces of broken samples were weighed and used to get the resistance index according to equation (1):(1)shatterresistance=100%−[mo−mfmo]x100%where mo is the initial mass of briquette while mf is final mass of briquette.

Fourier-Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis was done to determine the trend and main functional groups in the solid briquette samples as adopted by Ngangyo et al. [28]. A Shimadzu FTIR fitted with Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) sampling technique was used with a scan range of 400 cm−1 – 4000 cm−1 and 21 scans with a resolution of 4 cm−1. The FT-IR spectra were performed using Lab solution software.

Water resistance (WR) was determined by immersing briquette samples in a glass containing distilled water for 120 s as adopted by Davies et al. [29]. The percentage of water gained was given by equation (2).(2)%waterabsorbedbybriquette=(M2−M1)M1x100where M1 is the initial weight of briquette before immersion while M2 is the final weight of briquette after immersion. Thereafter, water resistance capacity was obtained using equation (3).(3)waterresistancecapacity%=100%−%waterabsorbed.

Proximate analysis and combustion characteristics

In determining the moisture content of the samples, dried briquettes were weighed before placing them in an oven for 24 h at 104 °C (ASTM D2444-16). The moisture content of a samples was determined as the difference in briquette weight before and after placing them in the oven as shown in equation (4).(4)M%=Mo−MfMox100%where M% is the percentage moisture content, Mo is the mass of briquettes before placing in the oven while Mf is the mass of briquettes after being placed in the oven.

Moreover, ash content (AC) of the briquettes was determined according to ASTM D1762-84 standard test. Sample briquettes were burned until all the carbon contents were consumed and the residue attained a constant weight. The ash content was determined from equation (5).(5)%AC=P−QRx100%where P is the mass of crucible plus ash residual (g), Q is the mass of empty crucible (g) and R is the mass of sample used (g)

In the determination of volatile matter (VM), 1 g of oven-dried briquettes from the different binder types were weighed in a crucible covered with a lid before heating in a furnace at a temperature of 950 °C for 7 min [30]. The residues were then cooled in a desiccator before taking their respective weights. The loss in weight is then considered the weight of the volatile matter and calculated as in equation (6)(6)%VM=Δmmix100%where Δm is the change in weight in grams while mi is initial weight in grams.

After this, fixed carbon content was determined from equation (7)(7)%FC=100−(%AS+%VM)

The amount of heat generated by burning a unit mass of briquettes (calorific value, CV) was determined using the formula in equation (8) as also adopted by Ref. [28]:(8)CV=354.3FC+170.8VM

The ignition time of the samples was determined using a stopwatch to measure the time taken for each sample to catch fire. The edge of the base of a sample was burnt using a Bunsen burner until a first visible flame was noted Kebede et al. [20]

Burning rate was determined using a stopwatch to measure time and an electronic weighing balance to obtain the mass of the briquette as used by Oyelaran [31] and calculated as shown in equation (9).(9)burningrate=totalmassofbriquette(g)burningtime(min)

Briquette quality optimization

Response Surface Methodology (RSM), a central composite design method, was adopted to determine the combined effect of binder type and binder proportion factors on calorific value (CV), ignition time (IT) and burning rate (BR). Binder type and binder proportions were chosen as independent variables (factors) and CV, BR and IT were the response variables. All the three binder types, that is, starch (1), paper (2), algae (3) were used while the binder proportions were selected as, 10 %, 15 %, 20 %, 25 % and 30 % of the total weight. An experimental design was generated, regression analysis done and a response function developed. From the parametric runs, contour plots, and surface plots were generated and optimal conditions determined.

Flue gas emissions monitoring during combustion

An aluminum-made chimney of 1500 mm was fabricated in such a way that the larger bottom diameter fitted seamlessly on top of a modified Kenyan energy saving charcoal cooking stove. On the one side of the chimney and at 1000 mm above the stove, a 10 mm diameter opening was made to allow for fitting of a probe for the gas analyzer (ECA 450 Environmental emission analyzer). The amounts of flue gases such as oxides of sulfur (SOX), oxides of nitrogen (NOX), oxides of carbon (COX), released during combustion of the briquettes were determined by first weighing certain amount of the briquette samples made from each of the individual binders. The samples were ignited and then placed in the stove. The flue gases emitted by each briquette sample type were analyzed after every 2 min for 30 min. At least three sets of runs were made for each sample.

Results and discussions

Physical and mechanical characterization

Variations in the density of the briquette samples is presented in Fig. 2. The figures shows that, the density of briquettes varies with both the binder type as well as binder proportion in the total weight of the briquettes as also observed by Olugbade et al. [26]. Starch binder concentration increased briquette density (Fig. 2(a)) as also found by Sarafa et al. [30], Narzary et al. [32,33]. The same is observed for algal bound briquettes. For CP briquettes, the density decreased with increase in paper binder concentrations. From the mean densities of samples of different binders (Fig. 2(b)), CS briquettes had the highest average density of 616.2 kg/m3. This is because starch reduces the void spaces present in the briquettes. This is due to high adhesive nature of starch unlike paper and algae binders [32,34].Fig. 2Variations in briquette density: (a) Effect of variation of binder proportion on density of manually-made charcoal dust briquettes, (b) mean density of briquettes made of different binders.Fig. 2

CA and CP briquettes had mean densities of 563.3 kg/m3 and 510.5 kg/m3, respectively. Waste paper binder concentration decreased briquette density due to low bulk density and low moisture content of paper. Overall, it is worth noting that all briquettes from the three binders at least met the British standards’ minimum density of 480 kg/m3 [35].

Variations in shatter resistance which is indicative of the durability of briquettes as suggested by Tambuwal et al. [36] is presented in Fig. 3(a) and (b). The shatter resistance of CP samples increased as the binder proportion increased (Fig. 3(a)) due to paper's low moisture content as also noted by Olorunnisola [37] and tight interlock between particles. Also, increase in binder proportions increased shatter resistance for CA and CS briquettes up to 25 %. Generally (except for CS 30 %) the more the binder, the less friable the briquettes become as suggested by Zanella et al. All samples obtained greater than 80 % mean shatter resistance (SR) which is the minimum acceptable resistance [38]. The binders can therefore be used to manually make stress resistant briquettes from charcoal dusts.

The next step was to examine the internal structure of the so formed briquettes. FT-IR spectra of the different samples is shown in Fig. 4. Worth noting is the broad and intense –OH bands at 3200 cm−1 and CHs at 2924 cm−1 for all the samples (Fig. 4(a), (b) and (c)) which correspond to the main functional groups of cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin [39]. Also, there is presence of carbon in most of all functional groups of the samples. This signals that the briquettes from all the three binders are ideal for burning. In Fig. 4(c), the spectra show variations in transmittance percentage for the functional group C-O (of cellulose and lignin) due to increased lignin from the algae as its percentage increases.Fig. 4FT-IR-ATR overlays for charcoal dust briquette samples with varying binder ratio (10%–30 %): (a) starch; (b) paper pulp and (c) algae.Fig. 4

FT-IR-ATR overlays for charcoal dust briquette samples with varying binder ratio (10%–30 %): (a) starch; (b) paper pulp and (c) algae.

The water resistance of briquettes also varied with different proportions of binders as shown in Fig. 5. Water resistance of charcoal-starch (CS) samples increased from 57.1 % at 10 % starch binder to 94.2 % at 30 % of the binder. This is because the starch binder coats the surface of the briquettes decreasing their solubility in water [9]. Starch contains high amounts of hydrogen bonds which cannot be broken by water molecules at room temperatures. Charcoal-paper (CP) samples recorded the lowest mean water resistance of 24.7 % (±5.71). This is because paper is made of cellulose which is highly hydrophilic. This means that briquettes made with paper pulp as a binder absorb moisture easily and should be stored in dry places and/or extra caution must be taken when transporting them. It is important to note that briquettes containing algae as a binder also had considerably low mean water resistance of 25.3 % (±1.05) but better than CP especially at higher binder concentrations. This is attributed to large pores in those samples where water accumulates.Fig. 5Effect of binder proportion on water resistance of manually-made charcoal dust briquettes.Fig. 5

Proximate and combustion characteristics

Proximate analysis parameters

The behavior of proximate parameters was observed at different binder proportions for the various samples as shown in Fig. 6. From Fig. 6(a), charcoal-starch (CS) samples had the highest average moisture content of 6.27 % (±0.530) while CP and CA briquettes had 4.83 % (±0.081) and 5.64 % (±0.043), respectively. The trend in moisture content for CS briquettes agrees with findings by Deshannavar et al. [34] and Aransiola et al. [16]. High MC for CS samples can be attributed to high hygroscopic nature of the starch binder. CP samples had the lowest moisture content due to high porosity of paper binder that allow easy loss of water during drying. For CA briquettes, the more the algae proportion as a binder the more porous the briquettes and the less the water is retained in the briquettes after drying.Fig. 6Proximate parameter variations with binder proportions: (a) moisture content (MC); (b) volatile matter (VM); (c) fixed carbon (FC) and (d) ash content (AC).Fig. 6

Proximate parameter variations with binder proportions: (a) moisture content (MC); (b) volatile matter (VM); (c) fixed carbon (FC) and (d) ash content (AC).

Fig. 6(b) shows volatile matter variation. The mean volatile matter (VM) was 38.4 %, 40.9 % & 39.5 % for CS, CP & CA samples, respectively. The low mean volatile matter of CS samples compared to those of CP and CA samples can be attributed to high density and low porosity of CS samples. Low VM for CA briquettes was also noted by Costa et al. [23].

From Fig. 6(c), the fixed carbon (FC) in CS samples increased with increase in starch binder proportions. Similar trends were observed by Ige et al. [40] but contrary to what Deshannavar et al. [34] found. More than 25 % paper binder concentration significantly lowered the fixed carbon of the resulting CP briquettes. In a study of characteristics of woody waste briquettes with waste paper as a binder Ayunina et al. [41] presented similar trends in fixed carbon content of the briquettes produced. The fixed carbon in CA briquettes decreased with increase in algae composition in the sample. This result is akin to what Costa et al. [23] reported in their study of energetic potential of algae biomass.

On the ash content (Fig. 6(d)), CA samples showed high mean ash content of 28.24 % which is similar to what Haykiri-Acma et al. [22] observed in their study of carbonized algae briquettes. The ash content is significantly high and different from CS and CP samples which had a mean AC of 16.2 % and 17 %, respectively. This is ascribed to high inorganic content of algae biomass and also contamination of algae binder by diatoms and sandy particles. It is important to also note that increase in starch binder proportion decreased the ash content of CS samples which is coherent with the trend presented by Narzary et al. [32] where taro starch was used as one of the binders.

The short ignition time (IT) of CP samples compared to those of CS and CA samples at each proportion can be adduced to the high volatile matter of the CP samples, low density and large porosity that allowed faster penetration of oxygen. Conversely, long ignition time of CS samples is due to strong compaction of particles by the starch binder inhibiting easy aeration. The results on ignition time for CS samples are in agreement with what Rapheal et al. [43] observed. Oladeji [44] and Palanisamy et al. [45] also support the observed trends by suggesting that ignition time increases with increase in density of briquettes which minimizes porosity and spaces for mass diffusion of air in the briquettes.

On the other hand, burning rate (BR) for CS samples decreased with increase in starch binder proportion. This trend is consistent with results reported by Oyelaran [31] on the same parameter. CP samples had the highest mean of BR (0.129 g/min) meaning they burn faster. This is attributed to the high volatile matter and porosity of the samples which allow easy infiltration of air during burning [46]. The low mean BR for CS samples can be associated to their higher density and close packing of particles.

Gas emissions monitoring during combustion

The constituent flue gases produced during the combustion of the briquettes and obtained from the fabricated chimney are analyzed to further observe the effects of the binders. From Fig. 10(a), CO emissions increased with increase in starch binder from 96.7 ppm at 10 % to 389 ppm at 20 % binder. The mean CO emissions from the CS samples was 233.3 (±142.4). This was lower compared to the mean CO emissions for CP (258 ppm) and CA (333.1 ppm) samples. This can be associated with the high temperature and smooth burning of CS samples.Fig. 10Effects of binder and binder proportions on emission of flues gases: (a) CO emissions; (b) NOx emissions and (c) SO2 emissions.Fig. 10

Effects of binder and binder proportions on emission of flues gases: (a) CO emissions; (b) NOx emissions and (c) SO2 emissions.

The high CO emissions by CA briquettes means that there was poor mixing of fuel and air. There was no clearly observable general trend on CO emissions among different CP samples. This can be due to various factors such as, changing combustion temperatures, insufficient supply of air, briquette packing, etc. Also, Rabaçal et al. [49] noted that CO levels can also be affected by mechanical properties of the fuel.

According to Jamradloedluk & Wiriyaumpaiwong [50], the largest part of flue gases is mostly nitrogen because it is the highest component of air (79 %). There are various sources of NOx in flue gases: thermal NOx is formed at temperatures greater than 1300 °C, prompt NOx is obtained at flame front while fuel-NOx is obtained from fuel. According to Roy et al. [51], combustion of charcoal in domestic stove only result to formation of fuel NOx.

Apart from briquettes with 10 % starch binder, the amount of NOx emitted increased among the CS samples from 0.6 ppm at 15 % to 2.86 ppm at 30 % starch proportion as shown in Fig. 10(b). This may suggest that as the amount of starch increased in the samples, the nitrogen content also increased. Similar results were obtained by Narzary et al. [32] when studying rice straw charcoal briquettes using different binders. Among the CP samples, NOx levels also increased with increase in paper binder proportion from 0.8 ppm at 10 % to 5.46 ppm at 30 % binder. Moreover, NOx levels emitted by CA samples were higher (5.46 ppm) at 25 % algae concentration and lowest at 10 % (0.933 ppm). A similar trend was observed by Kucukvar & Tatari [52]. On the contrary, Magida et al. [53] reported a decrease in NOx emissions with increase in algae proportion when studying coal-microalgae co-firing in fixed bed combustor. On average CA samples recorded the highest NOx emissions which might suggest that algae binder contain higher levels of nitrogen than starch and paper binders.

During combustion, SO2 is derived from oxidation of sulfur content in the fuel [[54], [55], [56]]. On average, CP samples recorded the highest SO2 levels during combustion (2.04 ppm). Among the CA samples, emission of SO2 increased with increase in algae binder from 0.266 ppm at 10 % to 3.06 ppm at 25 % algae composition as displayed in Fig. 10(c). Apart from briquettes with 10 % starch binder that emitted 0.933 ppm, the SO2 content in CS samples increased with increase in starch binder from 0.8 ppm at 15 % to 2.26 ppm at 25 % binder concentration. Generally, biomass has low sulfur content (0.01%–0.5 % wt.) and hence low SO2 emissions by the briquettes [57]. Also Otieno et al. [58] and Shirai et al. [59] explained that during combustion of a fuel some SO2 released reacts with H2 to form H2S and H2O hence lowering emission of SO2 gas. The upper acceptable exposure limit for CO, NOx and SO2 flue gases as suggested by Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA) are 200 ppm, 25 ppm (NO) and 5 ppm, respectively for 8 h per day, 5 days a week for many years [60].

Conclusions

The effect of binders (starch, paper and algae) on physico-mechanical, and combustion characteristics and emission levels of acacia charcoal dust briquettes were investigated. Charcoal dust briquettes produced with 30 % starch binder had the highest density of 678.1 kg/m3. Nevertheless all the binders produced briquettes with densities greater than the minimum 480 kg/m3 as per the British briquette density standards. CP briquettes showed a higher stress resistance than CS and CA samples. FT-IR analysis showed almost similar chemical groups though their intensities varied. Paper binder briquettes had the lowest water resistance hence more hygroscopic. The results indicate that both binder type and binder proportion affect the calorific value, ignition time and burning rate of charcoal dust briquettes. A 25.1 % starch binder ratio is the best condition to produce briquettes with high calorific values, shortest ignition time and low burning rate. The findings revealed that flue gases emitted by briquettes made from the different binders are below the minimum threshold level as set by OSHA. However levels of NOx are relatively higher in CA briquettes than CS and CP briquettes. The study therefore finds that starch, paper and algae binders can be used as binders to produce charcoal dust briquettes at low pressures. This can go a long way in utilizing the energy-rich charcoal dusts which are often discarded as waste in kilns and charcoal selling/storage yards. However, unlike waste paper and algae that are readily available in dumpsites and rivulets, respectively, wheat starch could be more expensive for large-scale applications. Hence, to reduce production costs, waste paper or algae may be a better option for cheaper charcoal dust briquettes.
