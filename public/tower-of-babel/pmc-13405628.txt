Introduction

The ever-increasing frequency and intensity of natural hazards and emergencies necessitate advancing regional public health preparedness and response (PHPR) data capabilities that link data, policy, and action among health jurisdictions, academics, disaster researchers, nonprofits, and health care entities.3 However, the regional data landscape faces challenges created by disparate data silos, limited data access, inconsistent data formats and standards, and policy and legal guardrails that prevent the rapid sharing of data across jurisdictions.1,4 Historically, these limitations have led to uncoordinated response efforts and created considerable delays in response time, inefficient resource allocation, and a fragmented picture of regional situational awareness.5 A regional data ecosystem approach can bridge these divides through increased partnerships in PHPR practice and research.

Data Ecosystem Requirements and Possible Solutions

We first define the meaningful use of the data ecosystem in Region 10 to delineate its expected role in the PHPR landscape. In the subsequent 4 sections, we explore the ecosystem's design requirements (ie, the minimum conditions needed to fulfill the ecosystem's expected role) and possible solutions across 4 domains: 1) data, 2) governance, 3) policy and logistics, and 4) infrastructure.

Meaningful Use and Application of Data Ecosystem

At the intersection of 1) the US congressional authorization language6 for this work and 2) the CoP's data needs for their PHPR work, we define the data ecosystem as a framework to connect people, platforms, and processes in Region 10 for improved PHPR data and uptake of evidence-based strategies and interventions (EBSIs).1,2 This regional approach seeks to complement and leverage evolving data modernization efforts at federal and state, Tribal, local, and territorial (STLT) levels, thereby contributing to a broader public health ecosystem.7 It aims to minimize duplicate efforts and create synergies across jurisdictional boundaries and practitioner-researcher barriers. Data Considerations

We considered 1) information and data sources; 2) essential elements of information; 3) data standards, data elements, and metadata requirements; and 4) information and minimum data-related needs. These considerations aimed to inform which information and data sources the regional data ecosystem development process should accommodate.

Information and Data Sources

Our design process sought to identify PHPR information needs through understanding data sources that informed past PHPR activities across hazards, to thereby delineate the scope of data that the data ecosystem should accommodate.

We used 4 use cases (tracking hazards, health outcomes, population characteristics, and facility capacity) to anchor search terms for an environmental scan to create a compendium of PHPR-related data sources used for past and current disasters or emergencies (see Appendix A, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B714). PHPR decisions involve not only public health data but also hazard characteristic data, critical infrastructure data, demographic data, and many other types of data. A data ecosystem that sufficiently addresses PHPR information needs will incorporate data of diverse spatial and temporal resolutions, data standards, and collection methods. The compendium of PHPR-related data sources helped identify specific standardization, integration, and interoperability issues that should be addressed at every stage of the data pipeline (Figure. 1).

Essential Elements of Information

To prioritize information needs in building the regional ecosystem, we identified essential elements of information (EEIs; also called minimum datasets) that have been used to support timely decision making during past disasters and/or public health emergencies in Region 10.8 We then assessed the availability of existing data for these EEIs (see Supplementary Document for extended discussion, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713).

EEIs defined by CoP members as relevant to their PHPR work went beyond CDC-defined Core Data Sources,9 including environmental data, critical infrastructure data, population data, and resource/inventory data. Data Standards, Data Elements, and Metadata Requirements

Data standards, data elements, and metadata requirements comprise the technical elements needed to understand the components from diverse data sources that need to be standardized and used in an interoperable environment and context. The CoP identified data standardization as foundational for the regional PHPR ecosystem development process because it underpins the efficiency of each ecosystem function.

Broad tasks expected of the data ecosystem use PHPR data that are characteristically 1) health outcome–related, 2) geospatial, and/or 3) emergency response–useable. These characteristics are central to PHPR data standardization. For example, such standardization should consider 1) how health outcome data are linked with and interpreted relative to other information (eg, spatio-temporal hazard tracking) and 2) how the necessary timeliness for emergency response may deprioritize some standardization considerations. The ecosystem should also consider the need to incorporate data from nontraditional data streams, such as new data partners, community scientists, or data not originally collected for public health purposes. Care needs to be taken to obtain sufficient documentation without imposing undue burden on the partners that collect and manage these data. Relevant metadata requirements are discussed in Sections 3.2d and 3.3c of the Supplementary Document, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713.

Information and Minimum Data-Related Needs

Trade-offs are inevitable in meeting information and minimum data-related needs. This section explores what trade-offs exist between data sources for PHPR tasks and how the data ecosystem can be built to maximize the usability of data to meet the needs. Clear data evaluation criteria are important to both allocate resources for relevant data ecosystem functions (ie, data collection, storage, preparation, analysis, and sharing) and support PHPR EBSIs. Understanding the trade-offs (eg, between accessibility and quality, granularity and cost, and standardization and timeliness) informs key cross-cutting design considerations such as interpretation limitations, cost-effectiveness of data acquisition, and data triangulation techniques.

The CoP discussed the criteria for evaluating whether or not data are useable, useful, or appropriate for PHPR, as depicted at the center of Figure 4 (see Finding 3.3.D.1 in the Supplementary Document for detail, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713). Using the criteria, we evaluated 10 example datasets (5 classes of EEIs as identified by the CoP) to determine how existing data sources compare with essential information needs. The evaluations highlighted the importance of the complementary nature of multiple data sources in meeting the PHPR information and minimum data-related needs. Generally, no data source was deemed better than all the others in every evaluation criterion. This implies the need for PHPR practitioners and researchers to be aware of different data sources' characteristics to decide which one to use for specific PHPR tasks and for routine public health needs. See Appendix B, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713 for detailed evaluations of the 10 example datasets.

Governance Considerations

Governance and regulatory challenges restrict the visibility of shareable data and data sharing efficiency. Additional difficulties arise in data integration, interoperability, and other coordination efforts across STLT jurisdictions.

As emergencies frequently cross jurisdictional boundaries, multiple jurisdictions and their community partners need data governance (ie, a set of policies, processes, and standards to manage data) in place before the onset of emergencies, to facilitate data deposition, information retrieval, and situational awareness.1,10,11 Without clear governance, many data systems are unprepared to authorize partners' timely access to data, eg, via data use agreements (DUAs).

The ideal PHPR data ecosystem should not be limited to traditional public health data sources (eg, public health surveillance systems, health care facilities, and wastewater monitoring programs). Potential sources of PHPR data include veterinary practices, agricultural sites, daycare centers, school systems, social services, community-based organizations, and hospitality venues. Urban, rural, and Tribal communities may directly contribute data to and receive benefits from the ecosystem. Effective partnerships with these diverse entities require an inclusive governance structure.

Many CoP members advocated for incorporating and prioritizing CARE (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics) principles into the data governance plan. These principles respect the autonomy of Indigenous peoples and Tribal partners to share their data as they see fit and as they stand to benefit from the use of the data that they provide.12 Engaging Tribal representatives with expertise in data management, data privacy, and data sovereignty from the outset is critical to the success of the data ecosystem. Data-sharing agreements should recognize the unique needs of each Tribe and ensure their respective sovereignties are respected.

A data governance framework should also incorporate Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles. Appropriate reuse of data increases efficiency and avoids waste, so interoperability is key. An important caveat is that FAIR principles should support, not compromise, the CARE principles.13

Some data sources contain sensitive or high-risk information, meriting deeper consideration of information security, privacy risk disclosures, and risk mitigation steps.14 Frontier areas (ie, the most sparsely populated and geographically remote rural areas) in Region 10 include large unincorporated geographies, requiring additional consideration to robustly protect personally identifiable information (PII). Several studies we reviewed recommend first establishing a governance focus with drafted policies that specify the intended spatial scale and geospatial-temporal data use policies governing the permissible spatial scale of accessible data.15,16

Overall, data governance discussions to form agreements should include but are not limited to how the data ecosystem may display data, how they may be requested in ways that respect Tribal data sovereignty, and how to make data, models, and code FAIR for the ecosystem users and their use-cases.

Policy and Logistical Considerations

We also identified policy and logistical obstacles to data standardization, integration, and sharing based on PHPR professionals' experience. All the obstacles described below are applicable across all STLT levels of the ecosystem to varying extents and complexities.

Obstacles to Data Sharing

Individual and organizational barriers, including trust, competitive environments, professional incentive structures, and data privacy concerns. Some organizations may not be able to release data because of complicated rules surrounding the sharing of data. Even if information is not legally protected, organizations may not share data because of uncertainty about the rules, lack of trust, worries about losing a competitive advantage, or because they view it as a breach of trust with the individuals who provided the data. Data exposed in a security breach can cause serious risks, especially for populations at heightened risk of stigma, discrimination, or personal harm (eg, individuals receiving substance use disorder treatment and survivors of domestic violence). Relatedly, the high costs and technical expertise required to achieve HIPAA-compliant data security can be prohibitive. Over time, governing bodies and their corresponding policies change. Because these changes can shift logistical requirements for data standardization, integration, and sharing, the ecosystem should incorporate proactive planning and ongoing policy dialogue to help ensure that future policy changes are responsive to regional data ecosystem needs.

Infrastructure Considerations

Data ecosystem development must consider requirements for and assessment of potential technical infrastructure solutions. Further, they must be appraised alongside considerations of data, governance, policy, and logistics to fully appreciate socio-technical infrastructure challenges and solutions for data integration and sharing.

Assessment of Platforms, Applications, and Middleware Solutions

Our environmental scan generated a list of software applications and middleware (Supplementary Document, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713, Table 2) that provide support for PHPR data ecosystem functions across jurisdictions. We used a case study approach to assess the software/technical needs, identifying the following 4 themes (see the Supplementary Document's Section 3.4c for detail, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B713): 1) establish processes to characterize populations and geographies that may be disproportionately affected during emergencies, including those facing communication, transportation, language, or accessibility barriers, and determine how to reach them; 2) identify data layers and complementary technologies that integrate with Geographic Information Systems (GIS) software, which is commonly adopted across jurisdictions; 3) consider training resources for remote-sensing spatial data and data platforms; and 4) consider nuances of different spatial layers for downstream analysis and modeling.

In sum, the recommended regional data ecosystem approach may build bridges between existing data systems and provide a gateway 1) for researchers to codevelop the PHPR evidence base and EBSIs with practitioners, 2) for the practitioners to coimplement the EBSIs with the communities they serve, and 3) for the communities to coevaluate the effectiveness of the EBSIs with the researchers, using data that are more accessible and integrable through the ecosystem.Implications for Policy & Practice■ Anticipated barriers to data sharing and integration for PHPR activities include capacity issues (eg, staff time, funding, technical expertise), coordination challenges (eg, lack of clear documentation, lack of data standardization within and between organizations), and legal and organizational hurdles (eg, privacy concerns, intellectual property restrictions, inconsistent statutes and legislation across jurisdictions).■ Overcoming these barriers will require multifaceted approaches that combine technical, legal, and organizational solutions (eg, policies and practices that minimize redundant staff efforts across jurisdictions while leveraging recent advancements in encryption, distributed computing, and artificial intelligence to ensure data sovereignty, privacy, and security).■ Fostering trust will be central to the data ecosystem development. CARE and FAIR principles support the data ecosystem's long-term viability.
