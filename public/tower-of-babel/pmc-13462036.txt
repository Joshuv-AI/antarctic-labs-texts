Gut microbiota modulation of systemic immunity and cancer immunotherapy: mechanisms, evidence, and therapeutic implications
Authors: Bader S. Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota plays a critical role in regulating systemic immune responses and has emerged as a key determinant of therapeutic efficacy and toxicity in cancer immunotherapy. Accumulating evidence indicates that specific microbial taxa and microbiota-derived metabolites modulate antitumor immunity by shaping immune cell maturation, cytokine signaling, and the tumor microenvironment. In particular, gut microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and inosine influence immune checkpoint inhibitor responses by regulating T-cell activation, dendritic cell function, and immune homeostasis beyond the intestinal compartment. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that alterations in gut microbiota composition are associated with variability in immunotherapy outcomes, including treatment resistance and immune-related adverse events. Importantly, microbiota-targeted interventions such as dietary modulation, probiotics, prebiotics, antibiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation have shown promise in enhancing immunotherapy efficacy and reducing toxicity. This review synthesizes current mechanistic insights and clinical evidence linking the gut microbiota to systemic immunity and cancer immunotherapy outcomes, highlighting microbiome modulation as a potential therapeutic adjuvant to optimize immunotherapy response and support precision oncology.

Introduction

Human gut microbiota (GM) is a complicated and dynamic micro-ecosystem composed of trillions of host-specific microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other eukaryotic as well as microbial species. Among these species, bacteria largely occupy the human gut’s microbial ecosystem, comprising more than 99% of the total composition. Around 2172 species of GM have been identified through the comprehensive analysis of MetaHit and the Human Microbiome Project (Hugon et al. 2015). The four major bacterial species of GM account for over 98% of GM, including Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinomycetes, and Proteobacteria (Guo et al. 2022). GM plays an important role in various aspects of human physiology, including diet, immune homeostasis, immune system maturation, maintenance of a balanced immune response, intestinal barrier protection, pathogen resistance, and metabolism (Zhao et al. 2023). Disrupted GM composition has been associated with the development of cancer via various mechanisms, including modulation of immune evasion approaches, changes in tumor microenvironment (TME), and genomic instability (Ansaldo et al. 2021). Cancers or malignant tumors are considered the second leading cause of death worldwide. Traditional cancer treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, have contributed in the improvement of patient outcomes, their efficiencies are often limited because of adverse side effects, recurrence, and resistance to cancer chemotherapy (Zhang and Zhang 2020; Lei et al. 2025).

Multimodal therapy is required for the management of most solid cancers, which usually involves surgery and/or the use of a combination of multi-chemotherapeutic agents (Pan et al. 2020). GM is highly dynamic and individualized in terms of both its structures and functions. GM shows substantial functional plasticity, which is weakened during cancer treatment, which can take place because of exposome, alterations in diet, neoadjuvant, xenometabolites, and adjuvant agents that disturb the structures and functions of GM (Chrysostomou et al. 2023).

Immunotherapy (IMT) can significantly change the course of cancer treatment, which has revitalized the tumor immunology field. IMT regimens harness the patient’s own immune system to fight against cancer. So far, various immunotherapeutic strategies, including divalent antibodies, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), cytokine therapies, cancer vaccines, oncolytic virus therapies, and adoptive cell transfer, have gained popularity. Among these immunotherapeutic strategies, the use of ICIs has been identified as an auspicious strategy to treat cancer patients because of their improved overall survival (OS) as well as tolerance, and long-lasting effects in comparison with the conventional cancer treatments (Zhang and Zhang 2020). ICIs are monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that can enhance immune responses mediated by T cells through targeting inhibitory ligands and receptors (Shiravand et al. 2022; Kiousi et al. 2023).

Immune checkpoint blockade by using ICIs is increasingly becoming popular in cancer treatment. Immune checkpoint blockade involves the use of mAbs to specifically inhibit checkpoint proteins and ligands, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death-1 (PD-1), and PD-ligand 1 (PD-L1), which eventually results in the prevention of immune evasion through tumor cells (Robert 2020). It has been demonstrated that the use of IMTs has resulted in promising outcomes in cancer patients, however its objective response rate can significantly vary among the ICI-receiving patients, which was found to be 30% in some cases (Qin et al. 2020). In addition, the use of ICIs can result in overactivation of the immune system, which can result in various adverse events ranging from mild to life-threatening conditions (Dora et al. 2023). The success of ICIs usually relies on the features of each patient and their cancer types. Therefore, it is essential to adopt a personalized approach to harness optimum therapeutic outcomes, however there are challenges in optimizing therapy monitoring, treatment planning, and diagnosis. Growing evidence strongly suggests that there is an important link between GM and the effectiveness of immunotherapy (Lione et al. 2021; Reens et al. 2021).

Some studies have revealed that certain GM can enhance the functions of dendritic cells (DCs), which can result in improved cytotoxic T cell priming and their buildup in the TME (Sivan et al. 2015). In addition, GM-primed DCs were found to enhance CD8 + T-cell priming capacity during immune checkpoint blockade, whereas GM profiling showed that this effect, along with enhanced antitumor activity, is linked with Bifidobacterium (Sivan et al. 2015). Interestingly, administration of Bifidobacterium was found to restore the effectiveness of PD-L1 blockade via mediating the infiltration and maturation of DCs in tumor niches and enhancing their capacity to prime for the activation of CD8+ T-cells (Xie et al. 2025). Several microbiome-targeting approaches, including probiotics, prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have been found to potentially increase therapeutic outcomes on IMTs in patients (Huang et al. 2022a, b). In this review, the role of GM in systemic immunity as well as cancer IMT, and various preclinical and clinical studies regarding the link between GM and IMT have been explored. Various approaches to optimize cancer IMT outcomes through GM modulation have also been discussed. This review also discusses applications of various microbial biomarkers to predict therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, this review identifies future research areas and potential clinical translation where GM is likely to result in a positive outcome.

Search strategies

Multiple widely recognized databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Cochrane Library have been searched. Searches were conducted in these databases up to May 2026 using individual or combined search terms, such as gut microbiota, intestinal microbiota, systemic immunity, immune responses, mechanisms, therapeutic implications, immune checkpoint inhibitors, cancer, and tumor. After the systematic search, articles were manually screened; those meeting the inclusion criteria were reviewed in full and selected articles were cited accordingly.

Overview of gut microbiota and their antitumor functions

GM in the human GI tract is a complex and diverse collection of around 100 trillion microorganisms (Gomes et al. 2018). GM goes through a dynamic variation from infancy to old age (RodrÍguez et al. 2015). Various factors affect the diversity and composition of GM, including diet, stress, exercise, antibiotic administration, age, and host genetics. The dynamic community of GM involves species from bacteria, viruses, archaea, protists, and fungi (Matijašić et al. 2020). Among them, Firmicutes (for example, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Clostridium) and Bacteroidetes (for example, Bacteroides) are the most common (90% of the total population) phyla in the GM (Wang et al. 2023). Various other phyla, including Verrucomicrobia, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria also exist in the human gut (Wang et al. 2023). GM has the capacity to interact with host cells to control multiple physiological mechanisms, including nutritional acquisition, host defense against infections, immune system function, and safeguarding the integrity of intestinal barrier (Nishida et al. 2018). Furthermore, GM can affect the absorption and bioavailability of orally administered drugs and lead to the generation of pharmacologically active metabolites that can further impact the efficacy or side effects of these drugs (Spanogiannopoulos et al. 2016). Dysbiosis is the alteration in the composition and function of GM, which is associated with the pathogenesis of multiple diseases, particularly cancer (Fan et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021). Gut microbiota and systemic immunity

The impact of GM in the development of both systemic and intestinal immune systems was first demonstrated in studies involving germ-free animals that revealed various immunological impairments in the intestinal mucosa and lymphoid structures at systemic sites. It was observed that germ-free mouse models also have abnormalities in T-cell trafficking, expressions of anti-microbial molecules, generation of immunoglobulins (Ig), and clearance of pathogens following systemic infections (Lo et al. 2021). GM significantly contributes to shaping the mammalian immune system, which mediates host protection or resistance against infectious diseases (Panwar et al. 2021). Numerous studies have already revealed the contribution of GM in the regulation of systemic immunity to provide protection against infections beyond the gut (Jordan and Clarke 2024). GM interacts with the human immune system, induces maturation and functions of immune cells (D'Alessandro et al. 2020). GM regulates these activities via 3 major mechanisms, including induction of mucin generation via intestinal goblet cells to preserve the integrity of mucosal layer structure and plays role as an important and dynamic barrier, stimulation of the development of intestinal mucosa-related lymphoid tissues, as well as induction of the maturation and differentiation of immune cells, predominantly via GM-mediated modifications that induce the development of isolated lymphoid follicle for innate defense and activation of naive B cells and T cells (Zhao et al. 2023).

The interaction between T cells and GM-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) is an emerging research field, revealing how these metabolites affect T cell functions, differentiations, and overall immune response (Kim et al. 2014). SCFAs can play an important role against cancer via activating G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and suppressing histone deacetylase (HDAC). SCFA-mediated HDAC suppression is linked with cell cycle arrest (Cheng et al. 2021). The microarray studies of human colonic epithelial cells showed that most butyrate-related genes are also involved. Butyrate has the capacity to decrease the expressions of pro-apoptotic protein Bax and anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 (Cheng et al. 2021). In addition, propionic and butyric acids were also found to mediate cancer cell apoptosis in adenoma via inducing the expressions of p53 and p21. Propionic and butyric acids can also influence cancer cell differentiation, suppress in vitro colon cancer cell migration, and decrease colon cancer cell invasiveness. Activated GPR109a and GPR43 exert anti-tumor activities via suppressing proliferation and mediating the apoptosis of colon cancer cells that are independent of HDAC inhibition (van der Beek et al. 2017). High expressions of SCFAs receptors on immune cells suggest that SCFAs can influence the generation and expansion of Tregs via suppressing HDAC or SCFA-sensing GPCR, thus contributing to intestinal immune regulation and regulation of colon cancer development (Johansson et al. 2015).

Indeed, it is important to understand the development of inflammatory diseases, autoimmune diseases, and cancer SCFAs, like other GM-derived metabolites, exhibit time-dependent effects that are particularly important during crucial developmental windows (Tang et al. 2025). Growing evidence suggests that SCFAs have a significant contribution in shaping metabolic and immune pathways during early stages of life, including weaning and neonatal periods, which are extremely sensitive to dietary and microbial inputs (Pakroo et al. 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest the significance of temporal SCFA exposure for proper immune education as well as long-term health outcomes. SCFAs were also found to have contribution in controlling cytokine formation by T lymphocytes (Liu et al. 2023). Various studies have revealed that SCFAs can improve the function of regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Hu et al. 2022).

Various studies have demonstrated that SCFA-generating Bifidobacterium dentium, a gut bacterium, colonized germ-free mouse models, improved the structure and functions of the intestinal mucosal barrier by increasing the production of mucin (Engevik et al. 2019). It has also been reported that germ-free mouse models displayed immature intestinal lymphoid tissues, such as isolated lymphoid follicles and crypt nodes, along with reduced levels of serum Ig (Zhao et al. 2023). Nonetheless, GM colonization in the intestines of germ-free mouse models resulted in enhanced lymphocyte levels as well as Ig germinal center proliferation in the lamina propria and small cysts, which further enhanced serum Ig levels (Zhao et al. 2023). Collectively, these findings suggest the importance of GM in mediating maturation of the immune system. In addition, folic acid production by Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus was found to elevate the level of DNA methylation as well as mRNA N6-methyladenosine in intestinal cells. Furthermore, butyrate-altered histone acylation is mediated by eubacteria, Clostridial clusters, and anaerobic bacteria. GM also has the capacity to mediate immune homeostasis and gut development through epigenetic alterations of intestinal tissues (Wu et al. 2022a, b).

GM has a significant contribution in maintaining human immune system balance and mediating the maturation of the immune system in the intestine and beyond. Indeed, enteroendocrine cells express toll-like receptors (TLRs) and initiate responses mediated by nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) when exposed to microbial-associated molecular patterns (Burgueño and Abreu 2020). Indeed, this response results in the release of enteroendocrine peptides and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Watnick and Jugder 2020). Moreover, this response involves increased transcription of the genes encoding DH31, tachykinin, and antimicrobial peptides. Such transcriptional alterations were found to be mediated via IMD pathway signaling in enteroendocrine cells that express tachykinin in the anterior midgut (Watnick and Jugder 2020). Adaptive immune responses are also regulated by GM, especially the differentiation and development of CD4+ cells and CD8+ T cells. It has been observed that Lactobacillus activates and mediates T regulatory cells (Tregs), whereas Clostridium perfringens induces differentiation and proliferation of Tregs as well as Th17 cells, which can result in interleukin (IL)−17 generation through intestinal Th17 cells (Song et al. 2022). On the other hand, Bifidobacterium was found to induce B cells to produce and also secrete secretory IgA (sIgA). GM can also affect various other immune cells and Ig via inducing regulatory B cells (Bregs) to synthesize various inhibitory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, which eventually inhibit inflammation (Kim 2022).

GM can also generate substances that indirectly affect immune functions. SCFAs are absorbed and partly metabolized via intestinal epithelial cells and transported into the circulatory system. It has been reported that SCFAs can modify the host immune responses via suppressing histone deacetylase to exert anti-inflammatory actions (Lavelle and Sokol 2020). Indeed, the maintenance and balance of intestinal immune homeostasis depends on both direct activation of the innate and adaptive immune system by the GM and the indirect effect of GM-derived metabolites on the immune responses (Zhao et al. 2023). Impacts of gut microbiome in cancer immunotherapy

IMTs are promising therapeutic agents that are revolutionizing the therapeutic outcomes of a range of cancer types and since the GM can robustly impact host immune responses, therefore there is a growing research interest in the capacity of GM to modulate the effectiveness of IMTs (Lo et al. 2021). CpG oligodeoxynucleotides have potent immunostimulatory effects and can improve the anticancer activities of various anticancer agents (Zhang et al. 2022a, b). GM can significantly affect the effects of CpGs, as confirmed by a reduction in the effectiveness of CpG in non-antibiotic-receiving EL4-containing mouse models in comparison with germ-free mice or antibiotic-receiving mice. Enhanced suppression of tumor growth and survival rates were found to be mediated by enhanced generation of tumor necrosis factor as well as CD8+ T cell amplifications in the TME (Chrysostomou et al. 2023). Further studies revealed that Alistipes shahii plays an important role in inducing the generation of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) via tumor-associated myeloid cells (Chrysostomou et al. 2023).

An alternative strategy to mediate anticancer IMT is to block the immune checkpoints, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, to suppress T-cell functions. Ipilimumab is an anti CTLA-4 mAb, which is currently being studied in several clinical trials to evaluate its efficacy in the treatment of colorectal cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and melanoma (Chrysostomou et al. 2023). It has been observed that the effectiveness of ipilimumab relies on GM-immune interactions. In addition, decreased tumor sensitivity to ipilimumab was observed in both germ-free as well as broad-spectrum antibiotic usage in mouse models of colon cancer, melanoma, and MCA205 sarcoma. Firmicutes, particularly the Faecalibacterium genus, has also been linked with CTLA-4 responses. In individuals with metastatic melanoma, Increased levels of Gemmiger formicilis and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii have been linked with increased responses to anti-CTLA-4-based therapies. Enrichment of GM with Firmicutes was found to result in beneficial clinical outcomes. Firmicutes was also linked with colitis-related phenotypes in melanoma patients. Interestingly, Bacteroides including Parabacteroides distasonis, Bacteroides uniformis, and Bacteroides vulgatus, were found to be associated with poor therapeutic outcomes; nonetheless, individuals with increased levels of Bacteroidetes were linked with absence of colitis (Chaput et al. 2017).

GM has also been associated with ipilimumab-linked intestinal colitis and toxicity (Sehgal and Khanna 2021). It was reported that ipilimumab-mediated intestinal toxicity in melanoma patients was found to be linked with GM-mediated synthesis of vitamin B and transport of polyamine (Dubin et al. 2016). Following anti-CTLA-4 therapy, Vancomycin-receiving mouse models experienced severe intestinal colitis, which can be reversed via Bifidobacterium administration (Wang et al. 2018).Tumor cells can evade host’s immune surveillance through PD-L1 expression, which can bind and induce PD-1. It has been observed that the use of mAbs against PD-L1 ligand (anti-PD-L1) and PD-1 receptor (anti-PD-1) can prove beneficial in case of numerous cancer types (Liu et al. 2021). Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the studies that aimed at finding the link between GM and anti-PD-1 as well as anti-PD-L1-based therapies (Chrysostomou et al. 2023). In spite of these heterogenous findings, it is increasingly becoming evident that GM is closely associated with the regulation of these checkpoint interactions.

Along with anti-PD-L1 therapy, administration of Bifidobacterium increased drug effectiveness in mouse models through the buildup of CD8+ T cells, which is an action mediated by the activation of DCs (Sivan et al. 2015). Gut epithelial barrier plays an important role as an initial defense against pathogenic microbes. Nonetheless, its integrity is substantially disrupted in cancer, along with microbial dysbiosis characterized by reduced beneficial commensals and enhanced pathogenic bacteria. Some pathogenic bacteria (such as Bacteroides fragilis) and various lifestyle factors including cigarette smoking and high-fat or Western diet can mediate gut barrier disruption (Yang et al. 2022). After the disruption of gut barrier, various pattern recognition receptors including RIG-I-like receptors, NOD-like receptors, and TLRs are activated. These receptors can detect GM-derived pathogen-associated molecular patterns and host cell-derived damage-associated molecular patterns, which possess alarm signals because of exogenous and endogenous damage mediated by exposure to hydrophobic portions (Ma et al. 2024). Indeed, activation of these receptors can induce chronic inflammation, inducing oncogenic signaling cascades including cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and NF-κB (Man and Jenkins 2022; Xie et al. 2025).

Moreover, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG was linked with improved effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy, via the activities of interferon gamma (IFN-γ)-generating DCs and IFN-β generation through the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase/STING/TANK binding kinase 1/IFN regulatory factor 7 axis (Si et al. 2022). Enhanced levels of Collinsella aerofaciens, Enterococcus faecium, and Bifidobacterium longum have already been linked with improved efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in melanoma patients; nonetheless, the exact mechanism of this finding is yet to be fully revealed (Matson et al. 2018a, b). In a similar manner, increased levels of Akkermansia muciniphila were linked with the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy following evaluation of clinical fecal samples obtained from patients with RCC and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). This finding was further confirmed by utilizing IMT obtained from responders to antibiotic-treated or germ-free mouse models resulted in enhanced reaction to anti-PD-1 therapy. Furthermore, Enterococcus hirae and Akkermansia muciniphila enhanced the anticancer property of anti-PD-1 therapy via inducing DCs found in lymph nodes resulting in the secretion of IL-12 (Routy et al. 2018).

Studies involving germ-free mice provided further insights regarding the role of GM in the regulation of IMT. Administration of a mixture of IFN-γ-generating bacteria (11-strain mixture) resulted in enhanced effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy through the suppression of tumor growth in germ-free MC38 tumor-bearing mice. These activities were facilitated via increased levels of IFN-γ-generating CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, while depletion of CD8 + T cells following treatment with anti-CD8+ antibody, led to improved bacteria-driven protective activities (Tanoue et al. 2019). Various studies have already demonstrated the effect of GM on therapeutic toxicity of ICIs. GM profiling of melanoma patients after treatment with combinations of IMTs, including anti-PD-1 as well as anti-CTLA-4, exhibited no substantial alteration in the alpha diversity between patients who experienced these adverse effects and those who did not. Nonetheless, individuals experiencing severe toxicity had substantially increased levels Bacteroides intestinalis and IL-1β upregulation. Immune cell profiling revealed that expressions of surface receptors CD27 and CD28 on circulating CD4+ and CD8+ T-effector cells was markedly reduced in individuals who did not develop severe toxic effects (Andrews et al. 2021).

Along with the direct effects of GM to modulate the efficacy of chemotherapy and IMT, GM-derived metabolites can also significantly affect cancer treatment. Microbial cometabolites, including inosine, bile acids, and SCFAs, have the ability to propagate to the tumor sites through the bloodstream and exhibit their effects in the TME. A major challenge with SCFAs is their inconsistent roles across different IMTs, which causes conflicting results in terms of their capacity to improve efficacy of IMTs. Increased levels of fecal propionic acid obtained from anti-PD-1 therapy-receiving patients have been linked with increased antitumor responses, which can result in prolonged disease-free survival (Botticelli et al. 2020). In addition to this, butyric acid suppresses histone deacetylases, which are usually expressed in several cancer types. Inhibition of histone deacetylases subsequently results in enhanced expressions of CD8+ T cells and reduced Tregs in liver cancer. Butyrate mediates suppression of histone deacetylase by the upregulation of inhibitor of DNA binding 2, which can lead to the upregulation of IL-12R expressions in CD8+ T cells, infiltrating the TME (He et al. 2021). In contrast, increased serum levels of butyrate in individuals with anti-CTLA-4 are linked with poor cancer treatment outcomes (Coutzac et al. 2020a, b). Interestingly, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum-derived inosine (a metabolite) can improve the antitumor actions of ICIs through the activation of human adenosine receptors (A2ARs) on T cells (Mager et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that GM plays an important role in determining the efficacy of IMTs. Nonetheless, more studies are required to reveal the detailed mechanisms of GM metabolic capacity in anticancer drug metabolism.

Preclinical and clinical studies regarding the link between gut microbiota and immunotherapy

Preclinical studies

Preclinical studies involving mice revealed that specific GM populations can influence the therapeutic outcomes of IMTs. Antibiotic-treated or germ-free mouse models with depleted GM showed decreased responses to CTLA-4 suppression in comparison with mouse models with intact GM (Vétizou et al. 2015). However, the response to IMTs in these mouse models was restored by introducing specific bacteria, including Bacteroides fragilis in association with Burkholderia cepacian or Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (Vétizou et al. 2015). Various other studies also reported multiple bacterial strains including Lactobacillus johnsonii, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, and Olsenella species derived from ICI-receiving tumors that may substantially improve the effectiveness of ICIs in mice (Mager et al. 2020). Among these strains, Bifidobacterium was found to improve the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 IMT via enhancing tumor infiltration and improving immune cell activities (Lee et al. 2021; Yoon et al. 2021). In a similar manner, various other isolated strains including Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens ZW18 and Lactobacillus paracasei sh2020 were found to be effective in improving the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy (Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Zhao et al. 2022).

Various preclinical studies have revealed the importance of GM metabolites in immune checkpoint blockade. Trimethylamine N-oxide was found to induce immune activation and improve the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade in pancreatic cancer mouse models (Mirji et al. 2022). In addition, butyrate generated by Roseburia intestinalis was linked with enhanced efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy against mouse models of colorectal cancer (Kang et al. 2023). Various other Clostridiales strains, including Anaerostipes caccae, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Eubacterium hallii, was found to improve activation of CD8+ cells and their infiltrations into tumors, which further enhanced anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy against solid tumors (Montalban-Arques et al. 2021). Furthermore, Lactobacillus gallinarum-derived indole-3-carboxylic acid improved efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in mice with unique microsatellite instability statuses (Fong et al. 2023). Clinical studies

A range of human studies have also been carried out to study the link between the composition of GM and IMT outcomes (Aron-Wisnewsky et al. 2020; Tao et al. 2020). Multiple bacteria were detected as potential biomarkers in numerous types of cancers, which can be used as an indicator of responders in thoracic cancer, RCC, NSCLC, and hepatocellular carcinoma (Derosa et al. 2022; Newsome et al. 2022). Increased levels of Faecalibacterium were linked with improved responses to ICIs in different cancer types, including NSCLC, hepatocellular carcinoma, and melanoma (Newsome et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2022a, b). A meta-analysis involving individuals with melanoma showed that the existence of Lachnospiraceae species is linked with an improved clinical outcome, whereas the existence of Streptococcaceae species is associated with an unfavorable outcome (McCulloch et al. 2022). In a different study, it was reported that various bacterial taxa are distinctively increased in patients with melanoma who responded to the combination of PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade, including Fournierella massiliensis, Parabacteroides distasonis, and Bacteroides stercoris (Andrews et al. 2021). Increased levels of Enterococcus faecium, Collinsella aerofaciens, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, as well as Bifidobacterium longum were observed in metastatic melanoma patients treated with ICIs (Matson et al. 2018a, b). Interestingly, among the ICI responders, a certain subgroup experienced significantly higher levels of Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Prevotella, whereas further studies with shotgun metagenomes revealed that GM has the capacity to produce SCFAs, including Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Eubacterium, which are positively linked with the responses to anti-PD-1/PD-L1-based therapy across multiple types of gastrointestinal cancer (Peng et al. 2020).

Increased levels of Alistipes sp Marseille-P5997 and Lachnospiraceae bacterium-GAM79 have been observed in patients with advanced hepatobiliary cancers receiving anti-PD-1 therapy, where these patients exhibited longer OS and progression-free survival (PFS), and individuals with increased levels of Erysipelotichaceae bacterium-GAM147 and Ruminococcus calidus also exhibited enhanced PFS (Mao et al. 2021). It was observed that hepatocellular carcinoma patients-receiving pembrolizumab or nivolumab showed enhanced levels of Veillonella, Lachnospiraceae, as well as Lachnoclostridium, along with an increased level of ursocholic acid and ursodeoxycholic acid, which was found to be linked with Lachnoclostridium (Kang et al. 2024). Increased levels of explicit bacterial taxa including Agathobacter and Ruminococcaceae UCG-013 were observed in Japanese patients with NSCLC, where these patients experienced favorable objective response to IMT (Jin et al. 2019). In a different study, increased levels of Clostridium and Lactobacillus were observed in Japanese patients with NSCLC treated with ICIs, where these patients took longer period to reach treatment failure (Katayama et al. 2019).

An abundance of Phascolarctobacterium was detected in Spanish patients with NSCLC who were treated with ICI therapy, where the patients exhibited extended PFS (Zhang et al. 2021). In another study, an abundance of Prevotella copri, B. longum, and Alistipes putredinis was observed in Chinese NSCLC patients-receiving treatment with nivolumab (Jin et al. 2019). An increased level of fecal Streptococcus, especially Streptococcus salivarius, was observed in prostate cancer patients-receiving pembrolizumab (Peiffer et al. 2022). Interestingly, a lower level of β-diversity and increased levels of Prevotellaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, Turicibacteraceae, as well as Lachnospiraceae were observed in individuals with HER2-positive breast cancer, who showed unresponsiveness to trastuzumab (Di Modica et al. 2021). Furthermore, the variety of fecal microbiome in these individuals was linked with immune signatures associated with tumor-infiltrating immune cells (Di Modica et al. 2021). Along with gut bacteria, gut fungi can also play a role in ICI responses. It was observed that these fungi may also serve as potential biomarkers for the prediction of ICI responses (Huang et al. 2023). In this regard, it was reported that the major fungus Schizosaccharomyces octosporus in ICI responders might result in fermentation of starch into SCFAs, which may further enhance efficacy of ICIs (Huang et al. 2023). However, the impact of gut viruses on ICI responses is still undefined. In a study, a link between fecal enterococcal prophage and therapeutic benefits of prolonged PD-1 blockade has been revealed in patients with lung and renal cancers, which further suggested the potential of certain phages to improve ICI efficacy and induce host’s immune system (Fluckiger et al. 2020).

Limited data are available regarding the effect of GM on adoptive T cell therapy (ACT) in comparison with ICIs, however increasing evidence indicates that GM also impacts therapeutic outcome of ACT (Schubert et al. 2021). It was revealed that previous exposure to antibiotics prior to chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy is linked with poor therapeutic outcomes. In contrast, increased levels of Faecalibacterium, Bacteroides, and Ruminococcus were found to be linked with improved responses to CD19 CAR-T cell therapy (Smith et al. 2022). Dietary intervention

It has been reported that dietary interventions can markedly affect immune responses, bacterial metabolites, and microbial composition (Wastyk et al. 2021). In addition, various preclinical studies reported that dietary interventions including targeted micronutrient supplementation, ketogenic diets, high-fiber diets, intermittent fasting, and caloric restriction can improve IMT via redesigning the composition of GM (Ferrere et al. 2021; Pomatto-Watson et al. 2021). In mouse models, dietary restrictions were found to mediate the buildup of memory T cells in the bone marrow, which further strengthened immune reactions against tumors and infections (Collins et al. 2019). It was also observed that GM uses host-released mucus glycoproteins as a source of nutrition during intermittent or chronic fiber deficiency, which can disrupt colonic mucosal barrier as well as reduce the abundance of Bifidobacterium and increase the abundance of Mucococcus (Desai et al. 2016). A decreased level of responses was observed with anti-PD-1 therapy in mouse models that consumed a low-fiber diet (Spencer et al. 2021). On the other hand, a ketogenic diet as well as its primary metabolite, 3-hydroxybutyrate, were found to ameliorate the effectiveness of anti-PD-1-based therapy via averting the upregulation of PD-L1 in cells of bone marrow and mediating CXCR3+ T cell expansion. Furthermore, a diet free from glycine or serine slowed colorectal cancer while mediating the buildup of cytotoxic T cells to improve anti-tumor immune responses. Interestingly, blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway improved the effectiveness of this dietary intervention, which indicates the significance of combination of glycine/serine-free diet with IMT (Tong et al. 2024).

Various clinical studies also indicate the significance of dietary interventions in therapeutic outcomes of IMTs. Fermented food and high-fiber diets were found to affect GM and immune responses through different mechanisms. High-fiber diets elevated the levels of carbohydrate-active enzymes associated with glycan metabolism along with moderate activities on microbial variety, whereas fermented diets improved variety of GM and decreased inflammatory markers (Koh et al. 2016). Indeed, a correlation was observed between increased level of dietary fiber intake and improved clinical outcomes, especially in individuals with enhanced bacterial diversity (Simpson et al. 2022). Moreover, dietary fiber generates SCFAs, including butyrate, which can can cause direct activation of tumor-fighting CD8+ T cells and improve efficacy of anti-PD-1-based therapy (Morrison and Preston 2016). Nonetheless, conflicting study findings indicate that an increased level of SCFA in peripheral blood might restrict the effectiveness of anti-CTLA-4-based therapy, which suggests that the effects of metabolites in antitumor immunity might differ across tissues (Coutzac et al. 2020a, b). Thus, it is crucial to precisely select the dietary interventions for the optimization of therapeutic outcomes of IMTs. Probiotics

Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide health benefit, when ingested in adequate amounts. Various preclinical studies demonstrated that supplementation with certain gut microbes, including Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, Enterococcus, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus, can improve tumor IMT (Cascone et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2021). The probiotic bacteria Bifidobacterium pseudolongum can generate inosine, which can further activate the T cell adenosine A2A signaling to improve efficacy of IMTs (Martins et al. 2019). Enhanced immune responses against TME was observed through enhanced activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and increased release of IL-2 and IFN-γ following supplementation with B. bifidum K57, which further provided synergistic action along with anti-PD-1-based therapy to decrease tumor burden (Lee et al. 2021). Next-generation sequencing has detected Akkermansia as a potential probiotic that can mediate IMT (Corb Aron et al. 2021). Furthermore, commensal bacterial strains have significant contribution in the case of IMT via maintaining gut homeostasis. In a study, eleven bacterial strains were obtained from healthy donors that mediated the generation of IFN-γ through CD8+ T cells, which was found to improve efficacy of ICI in murine tumor models (Tanoue et al. 2019).

Various clinical studies have also revealed that probiotics can modulate GM and improve antitumor immune responses in individuals with cancer. Several probiotic strains with mucosal-protective and anti-inflammatory properties, including Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, were found to reduce radiotherapy- and chemotherapy-induced toxicity (Das 2019; Jiang et al. 2019). In a study, it was revealed that probiotic supplementation markedly decreased IL-10, IL-1β, and IL-23A mRNA levels in colonic mucosa, which further resulted in decreased occurrence of postoperative infections (Consoli et al. 2016). In a different clinical study, supplementation with Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium lactis elevated butyrate-generating bacteria, especially Clostridiales and Faecalibacterium spp., in fecal, mucosal, and tumor microbiota, which indicate the significance of probiotics in improving antitumor immunity (Hibberd et al. 2017). A range of clinical studies are also currently ongoing that are assessing the efficacy and safety of probiotics in combination with ICI therapy. MRx0518 is an orally administered, flagellin-producing probiotic that causes activation of NF-κB and TLR5 signaling pathways to exert antitumor activities (Lauté-Caly et al. 2019). Administration of the combination of pembrolizumab and MRx0518 in a phase 1/2 trial was found to meet its primary efficacy endpoint in individuals with RCC. On the other hand, as compared to 20% response rate with ICI alone, Clostridium butyricum CBM588 markedly improved PFS in metastatic RCC patients in a phase I clinical trial, along with a response rate of 58% in combination with an ICI (Dizman et al. 2022). Collectively, these findings indicate the significance for the development of GM-based interventions in optimizing cancer IMTs.

Prebiotics

Prebiotics are primarily composed of non-digestible fibers, predominantly polysaccharides and oligosaccharides, that specifically mediate the growth of beneficial GM (Becerril-Alarcón et al. 2019). In a study, it was reported that an oral inulin gel, developed to selectively get released in the colon, significantly modulated GM, induced systemic responses of memory T cells, mediated SCFA metabolism, and improved the efficacy of anti-PD-1-based therapy (Zhang et al. 2021). In addition, pectin improved efficacy of ICI by using several processes and enhanced responses of anti-PD-1-based therapy in colorectal cancer via elevating the level of butyrate-generating gut flora and modulating infiltration of T cells into the TME350. Pectin was also found to be associated with enhanced occurrence of mucophilic bacilli, which generated cyclic AMP and activated STING signaling pathway, which further led to type I IFN-mediated immunity (Lei et al. 2025). Various other prebiotics including polysaccharides derived from medicinal plants were also found to be effective. It was observed that ginseng polysaccharides can improve efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy via altering tryptophan metabolism, remodeling the composition of gut metabolites, and inhibiting responses of Treg cells (Huang et al. 2022a, b; Lei et al. 2025).

Targeted use of antibiotics

Individuals with malignant tumors, antibiotics are mainly used to treat treatment-associated infections and postoperative prophylactic infections. Nonetheless, these interventions can alter GM and can potentially affect therapeutic outcomes of IMTs (Kim et al. 2019; Domzaridou et al. 2023). Both preclinical as well as clinical studies have indicated that the use of antibiotics, predominantly broad-spectrum antibiotics, might impair the efficacy of ICIs. Antibiotics including neomycin, metronidazole, ampicillin, and vancomycin disturbed IL-17-generating γδT17 cells in mouse models of melanoma and NSCLC (Lei et al. 2025). Similarly, clinical studies have observed decreased level of reduced PFS and OS in individuals with advanced NSCLC and RCC who were treated with antibiotics before ICIs (Derosa et al. 2018). The effects of antibiotics in tumor IMTs are linked with the duration of antibiotic exposure in patients. In a multicenter prospective study, it was observed that use of antibiotics before ICI therapy was linked with lower poor therapeutic outcomes and reduced OS (Ribas et al. 2021).

In spite of the concern regarding the negative effects of antibiotics in IMTs, some antibiotics might improve therapeutic outcomes when utilized carefully. Vancomycin mediated migration of natural killer T cells and production of IFN-γ, which further suppressed both primary as well as metastatic HCC growth in mouse models (Ma et al. 2018; Cai et al. 2022). In addition, antibiotics might counteract tumor-related microbial effects that have the capacity to impair therapies (Pernigoni et al. 2021). It was observed that antibiotic regimens containing vancomycin might improve efficacy of androgen deprivation therapy via eradicating these bacteria (Terrisse et al. 2022). Although antibiotics may offer therapeutic advantages in modulating tumor-related microbiota, use of antibiotics in cancer therapy must need to be sensibly considered. In this regard, various factors including antibiotic resistance, TME, tumor genetics, and a patient’s immune status ought to be carefully considered (Lei et al. 2025).

Fecal microbiota transplantation

FMT is a direct strategy that involves GM manipulation, which includes the delivery of stools from a healthy donor to the recipient through infusion in the colon or through upper gastrointestinal tract via frozen or lyophilized capsule administration or through direct delivery via gastroscopy or colonoscopy (Kang et al. 2024). The potential of FMT in cancers emerged from the observations of preclinical studies confirming that germ-free mouse models treated with FMT from IMT-responsive individuals showed stronger anti-tumor immunity in comparison with the individuals receiving transplants from non-responders (Matson et al. 2018a, b). Two clinical trials (NCT03341143 and NCT03353402) first confirmed that FMT can overcome resistance to ICI therapies in metastatic melanoma (Davar et al. 2021). These clinical studies also indicated favorable immune alterations, such as reduced IL-8-expressing myeloid cells and enhanced activations of CD8+ T-cells. Microbiome analysis showed a reduction in potentially detrimental species including Bacteroides and enrichment of various commensal bacteria (Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Bifidobacterium longum). In order to improve antitumor efficacy, future studies ought to focus on integration of FMT with complementary approaches, identification of optimal donor-recipient pairings, and improving microbiota transfer protocols (Lei et al. 2025).

Future perspectives

Various study findings have already demonstrated the crucial role of GM in antitumor immunity and cancer immunotherapy, particularly in ICI-based therapies. Insights into the underlying mechanisms generated from both preclinical and clinical studies have mediated the discovery of microbiota-based therapeutic approaches including dietary interventions, probiotic supplementation, and FMT. Furthermore, in-depth exploration of mechanisms underlying GM activities has laid important foundations for the development of targeted therapeutic approaches, suggesting that well-defined components of the GM can be harnessed to generate desired effector T cell programs that confine growth of tumors (Wu et al. 2026). Various study findings already demonstrated the importance of understanding the impact and regulation of GM on the success of IMTs, which offer crucial research directions in improving antitumor IMTs. These study findings indicate that individuals with certain GM profiles might get more benefitted from IMTs (Yan et al. 2024). Nonetheless, the bacterial taxa influencing responses of IMTs are found to be dependent on contexts and diverse mechanisms might be associated with different tumor types or IMT types (McCulloch et al. 2022; Davar et al. 2024; Derosa et al. 2024). Furthermore, different bacterial taxa and processes might be associated with the modulation of responses to IMTs in different cohorts or patients owing to the heterogeneity of human GM because of geography or other GM-affecting factors, therefore more studies are required for more precise prediction (McCulloch et al. 2022; Clavijo-Salomon and Trinchieri 2025).

Despite its promise, GM modulation suffers from several important clinical challenges. Many clinical studies involve small sample sizes, which further limits a study’s statistical power and generalizability of findings to broader populations. Another major challenge is the standardizations, since GM composition varies extensively among individuals because of various factors, including environmental exposure, medication use, and diet (Lei et al. 2025). Furthermore, reproducibility is affected by the lack of uniform protocols for microbiome analysis. Widespread clinical adoption is further complicated by safety concerns, particularly approaches like FMT might not consistently restore commensal bacteria, and unintended microbial shifts might result in adverse effects (Ianiro et al. 2022). GM-based techniques or therapies also involve unique regulatory challenges. In some microbiome-targeting approaches (for example- FMT), exact active ingredient(s) is not known. It is quite impossible to identify whether the active ingredient is consistent in each dose, without a clearly defined active ingredient (Bloom and Chung 2025). Another regulatory challenge involved in GM-based therapies is whether the therapy will be viewed as a medicinal product, human tissue product, or biological agent. Each of these categories are differently regulated within each country and regulations are likely to differ from one governing body to another (Merrick et al. 2020). Indeed, it is important to address these issues for successful clinical translation of GM-based approaches.

Advancements in microbiomics, metabolomic, and genomics are mediating more precise and robust microbiota research (Kato et al. 2017). Various emerging tools, such as ingestible biosensors and artificial gut models, are likely to improve real-time microbiota research (Lucchetti et al. 2024; Rehan et al. 2024). In addition, multi-omics techniques combining metabolic, immune, and microbiome data might detect important microbial factors affecting the responses of IMTs, which can mediate targeted interventions. It has recently been demonstrated that GM can significantly affect efficacy of various vaccines, specially indicating the important roles of Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium (Jordan et al. 2022). It was observed that these bacteria have the capacity to interact with DCs, which are important for the initiation of anti-tumor immunity. GM modulation through targeted microbial supplementation or dietary interventions might improve the capacity of hosts to identify and provide response against tumor antigens presented by vaccines (Jing et al. 2023).

Integration of GM modulation with cancer vaccines signifies an important therapeutic approach. The way research is currently advancing, personalized cancer IMTs might become a reality in near future. Individuals with imbalanced GM may be benefitted from interventional approaches, for example probiotics, dietary modifications, or even engineered bacterial therapies, whereas individuals with a favorable GM profile linked with positive immunity might need only minor alterations to harness optimum therapeutic outcomes. Other than GM, microbes present in TME, skin, and oral cavity might also affect immune responses, extending the scopes of personalized therapeutic approaches (Choudhry 2021; Issrani et al. 2022; Lau et al. 2023). Furthermore, achievement of clinical translation in this field will necessitate interdisciplinary collaboration among oncologists, immunologists, data scientists, and microbiologists. In order to obtain further clinically relevant insights, more clinical trials, as well as standardized data collection approaches are required. Machine learning and artificial intelligence might also ameliorate stratification of patients and help detection of GM-driven processes that affect therapeutic outcomes. More studies involving GM can significantly contribute in the development of precision medicine for cancer treatment through integrating expertise across several fields (Lei et al. 2025).

Conclusion

GM has significant contribution in positively affecting cancer IMTs, which further contributes in therapeutic efficacy and patient responses. The advances in sequencing technologies and GM modulation approaches for the the optimization of cancer IMTs, including dietary modifications dietary interventions, probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT have opened the opportunities for the improvement of IMT outcomes. Nonetheless, there are several challenges in translating these study findings into clinical settings, predominantly because of inter-individual variability as well as the intricacy of host-GM interactions. Future studies are required involving artificial intelligence and multi-omics approaches to demonstrate causal relationships between GM composition and IMT responses. Indeed, there is a potential to ameliorate treatment efficacy, overcome drug resistance, and personalize IMT regimens by harnessing GM potential. More studies are required to unleash the full potential of GM-targeted interventions, which is likely to mediate the development of more personalized and effective cancer therapies.
