Quercetin modulates the aggregation behavior of bovine hemoglobin
Authors: Alexandra Busuioc, Vlad Tudor Popa, Aurica Precupas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The effect of different concentrations of quercetin (QUER) on bovine hemoglobin (BHb) conformation and thermal stability was investigated using differential scanning microcalorimetry (µDSC), circular dichroism (CD), dynamic light scattering (DLS), Thioflavin T, Congo Red, and ANS binding assays. Both QUER concentration and incubation temperature modulate the aggregation behavior of BHb. Low concentrations of QUER stabilize the protein structure, while high concentrations promote protein aggregation, as µDSC measurements revealed for the samples incubated at 277 K. With increasing concentration, non-bound QUER acts as a promoter of protein aggregation after incubation for 96 h at 310 K. The changes in the secondary structure of BHb, evaluated by CD, point to an increase in β structure after incubation at 310 K, indicating aggregate formation in the presence of QUER. DLS measurements highlight the aggregation effect of polyphenol on the unfolded protein structure after thermal incubation. Congo Red (CR), Thioflavin T (ThT), and 8-anilino-1-napthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) assays support the formation of aggregates. The results provide valuable insights into the polyphenol-mediated effect on protein thermal behavior and contribute to the development of hemoglobin-based biomaterials, protein stabilization strategies, and biopharmaceutical formulations.

Introduction

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a heme-containing globulin found in red blood cells involved in multiple physiological functions, including modulation of intracellular pH, enzymatic and metabolic processes, erythrocyte aging, drug interactions, and oxygen transport and storage.1–3 Structurally, it is a tetrameric globular protein consisting of two αβ dimers, containing a heme prosthetic group embedded within a hydrophobic pocket in each polypeptide chain.4 Structural or functional anomalies in Hb can lead to severe disorders such as thalassemia, sickle cell disease, and iron-deficiency anemia.5 Due to its multifunctionality, Hb has attracted considerable attention in healthcare6 and food science, where it is explored as an acellular oxygen carrier, a bioavailable iron source, and a natural colorant or flavoring agent. The interaction of natural polyphenols with proteins is extensively studied due to their relevance in food and health applications.7–10 Bovine hemoglobin (BHb) is used as a model protein11 for investigating polyphenol–protein interactions and the effect of ligands on protein biological function.12–15 Ligand–protein interactions are influenced by numerous external and physiological factors that can alter the bioavailability of the active compound and the protein structure and functionality.

Quercetin (QUER) is one of the most abundant flavonoids among dietary polyphenols; it is studied for its anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antiviral, and antifungal properties.16,17 High consumption of polyphenol-rich fruits and vegetables has been associated with reduced risk of chronic conditions, including cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and cancer.18–21 Previous investigations reported the inhibitory effect of QUER on β-amyloid aggregation,22 BSA fibrillation kinetics, preventing amyloid-like aggregation,23,24 and the effect on turkey Hb-mediated lipid oxidation.25 Only a few studies reported the interaction of quercetin with bovine hemoglobin using spectroscopic methods. The study of Tian et al.26 reported from fluorescence data a 1 : 1 interaction between QUER and BHb with a binding constant of 1.25 × 104 M−1 at 298 K. S. Das et al.27 reported a binding stoichiometry QUER : BHb slightly above one and increasing with the rise in temperature. To the best of our knowledge, the present study reports for the first time the effect of different QUER concentrations on the aggregation behavior of hemoglobin. The concentrations of QUER have been chosen to provide information about both the effect of the bound ligand (for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 molar ratio) and that of free excess ligand (for QUER : BHb 3 : 1 molar ratio) on the protein thermal behavior. Previous studies of our group reported the effect of some polyphenols on BHb thermal stability: naringenin28 promotes protein thermal aggregation, as µDSC and DLS revealed, while rutin29 inhibits the growth of fibril-like protein structures and promotes the formation of disordered aggregates or protofibrils. The present work provides new insights into the concentration- and temperature-dependent dual role of quercetin in modulating BHb thermal stability and aggregation behavior. Corroboration of calorimetric and spectroscopic approaches provides a comprehensive understanding of the polyphenol influence on hemoglobin aggregation, which may be further applied in biochemistry, biophysics, and pharmaceutical research.

Materials and methods

Materials

Bovine hemoglobin, BHb, (H2500, ≥98% purity), and quercetin dihydrate, QUER, (Q0125, ≥98% purity), Congo Red (CR, C6767), Thioflavin T (ThT, T3516) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 8-Anilino-1-napthalenesulfonic acid (ANS, BD16291, 95% purity) was obtained from Lead Sciences. The chemical compounds were used without further purification. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used to prepare a stock solution of QUER due to its low solubility. The stock solution of QUER was diluted with 30 mM sodium phosphate buffer (NaH2PO4 and Na2HPO4), pH 7.2 to prepare working solutions for µDSC. The concentration of DMSO in all working solutions was 1% of the total volume. The experiments were carried out for different QUER : BHb molar ratios: 0 : 1, 1 : 1, and 3 : 1. The QUER–BHb systems were incubated under the following conditions: (i) for 24 h at 277 K, and (ii) for 96 h at 310 K. The samples were brought to room temperature before µDSC, CD, and DLS measurements.

Methods

Differential scanning microcalorimetry (µDSC)

The µDSC measurements were performed by a µDSC 7evo microcalorimeter (Setaram). The effect of different concentrations of QUER on BHb thermal aggregation was evaluated in duplicate for QUER : BHb 0 : 1, 1 : 1, and 3 : 1 molar ratios in the temperature range 298 K–363 K using a heating rate of 1 K min−1. The BHb concentration was maintained constant at 3.1 × 10−5 M. All thermograms showed an exothermic signal, assigned to the aggregation process of BHb. The corresponding thermodynamic parameters, the aggregation temperature (Tagg) and enthalpy change (|ΔHagg|) were determined using Calisto v2.16 software with a sigmoid baseline. The per mol quantities were calculated from the absolute values (Joules), taking into account the sample volume and the protein concentration.

Circular dichroism (CD)

A Jasco J-815 spectrometer was used to monitor the conformational changes in the protein secondary structure after incubation at different times and temperatures, in the absence and presence of QUER. The CD spectra were measured at 298 K between 190 nm and 260 nm, using a 0.05 cm quartz cell. The concentration of BHb was maintained constant (2 µM in 30 mM phosphate buffer solution, pH 7.2), while the concentration of QUER was varied from 2 µM for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 molar ratio to 6 µM for QUER : BHb 3 : 1 molar ratio. The scan rate was 100 nm min−1, and the final spectrum was averaged over three scans. All CD spectra were baseline-subtracted for the buffer solution and the corresponding QUER concentration for QUER–BHb samples. The secondary structure content was obtained using the BeStSel webserver30 with an NRMSD value ≤ 0.1.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The size distribution of BHb after thermal incubation in the absence and presence of QUER was determined using DLS measurements on a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Filtered samples of QUER–BHb systems using a 0.22 µm pore-sized microfilter were loaded in standard disposable cuvettes, and the hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) and the polydispersity index (PDI) were determined and reported as the mean value ± standard deviation. Measurements were performed at 298 K, and the results are the average of five measurements reported as intensity and number size distributions.

Congo red assay

The formation of aggregates was evaluated by UV-vis measurements using a Carry Varian 100Bio spectrophotometer with a 1 cm path length cell. The absorbance of Congo Red (CR) was monitored between 400 and 700 nm. For this assay, 1400 µL aliquots of the protein solutions were withdrawn and mixed with 1560 µL of a solution containing 20 µM CR in 30 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.2.

ThT fluorescence measurements

A stock solution of native BHb (300 µM) in the absence and presence of QUER at different molar ratios (QUER : BHb 0 : 1, 1 : 1, and 3 : 1) was prepared in 30 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.2. The samples were incubated for 96 h at 310 K. ThT fluorescence measurements were performed in duplicate using FP-8250 Spectrofluorometer (Jasco). 50 µL of the incubated sample was mixed with 2950 µL ThT (25 µM in 30 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.2) and incubated for 30 min. Samples were excited at 440 nm, and the emission spectra were recorded in the range of 460–600 nm. The excitation slit was set at 5 nm, and the emission slit was set at 10 nm. For each sample, the inner filter correction for QUER absorption at 440 nm was taken into consideration.

8-Anilino-1-napthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) binding measurements

BHb (300 µM) samples incubated at 310 K in the absence and presence of QUER at different molar ratios were taken out after 96 h. Aliquots of 15 µL incubated protein were mixed with 2985 µL of ANS (0.5 µM), and the final protein concentration was 1.5 µM. Samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min. The emission spectra were recorded in duplicate in the range of 400–600 nm with excitation and emission slits of 5 and 5 nm, respectively, using the excitation wavelength of 380 nm. To remove interference from polyphenol, the spectra were corrected for the inner filter effect.

Results and discussion

Quercetin modulates the thermal aggregation of bovine hemoglobin

The µDSC thermograms of QUER–BHb systems (Fig. 1) incubated under different time and temperature conditions display an exothermic peak corresponding to protein aggregation, both in the absence and presence of increasing concentrations of QUER. The corresponding thermograms exhibit a broad peak associated with protein aggregation due to intermolecular hydrophobic interactions between (partially) unfolded protein regions exposed to the solvent.31 The presence of DMSO, even at low concentration, may alter both the denaturation34 and aggregation32 of proteins. Therefore, the BHb 1% DMSO system was taken as control, and studied samples, all containing 1% DMSO, were labeled accordingly: QUER : BHb 0 : 1, QUER : BHb 1 : 1 and QUER : BHb 3 : 1.

µDSC scans of BHb thermal aggregation in 30 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, in the absence and presence of increasing concentrations of QUER after incubation at different temperatures and times: (A) 24 h at 277 K, and (B) 96 h at 310 K.

After 24 h incubation at 277 K the thermograms presented in Fig. 1A evidence a dual, opposing action of the polyphenol: for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 the aggregation peak is shifted to a higher temperature, pointing to a stabilization effect of the bound polyphenol; on the contrary, for QUER : BHb 3 : 1 the aggregation peak exhibits a sizable shift to lower temperature, indicating the aggregation promoting effect of free (unbound) polyphenol present in solution. A comparison between thermograms obtained after incubation at 277 K and 310 K reveals significant shifts of the aggregation peaks to higher temperatures (Fig. 1B). For 96 h 310 K samples, the dynamic (noniso)thermal aggregation is delayed. Moreover, broader exothermic peaks are obtained for all samples incubated at 310 K, pointing to the presence of various size aggregates produced during incubation. The thermodynamic parameters of the aggregation process (Tagg and |ΔHagg|) were presented in Fig. 2. One may easily notice the regular behavior of aggregation peak temperatures: (i) higher temperature (310 K) incubated samples aggregate at a higher temperature than lower temperature (277 K) incubated ones, pointing to a general stabilization produced by 310 K incubation. (ii) For 310 K incubated samples, there is a monotonous decrease of peak temperatures with the increase of QUER content, indicating the destabilization produced by the ligand. (iii) For 277 K incubated samples, there is a slight increase in the peak temperature for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 ratio, which signs for the stabilizing effect of the bound ligand; the excess of ligand (i.e., its presence in solution) leads to protein destabilization (a decrease in aggregation peak temperature). The adsorption of free polyphenol on the protein surface could cause conformational changes in the protein structure that further promote aggregation.33 A similar action was previously reported for morin and sinapic acid binding with bovine serum albumin.34,35

Thermodynamic parameters (the mean value ± data range) obtained from µDSC scans for QUER–BHb systems.

Less regularity is manifested by values of aggregation thermal effects. This may be due to integration errors of broader peaks obtained for 310 K incubated samples. However, the important difference in aggregation heats of QUER : BHb 0 : 1 samples may be ascribed to the presence of larger aggregates formed during incubation at 310 K of ligand – free BHb samples.

The calorimetric results prove that QUER modulates the thermal aggregation pathway of BHb in a concentration-dependent manner. Similar effects were reported for other polyphenol – BHb systems. Naringenin (NAR) modifies the thermal stability and aggregation behavior of BHb, with µDSC revealing complex, multistep aggregation processes, while the combined µDSC, CD, and DLS results indicated that free dissolved NAR acts as an aggregation promoter.28 More recently, a concentration-dependent effect was also reported for rutin in BHb, where calorimetric, spectroscopic, and microscopic analyses showed that the polyphenol modulates the aggregation pathway and favors disordered aggregates or protofibrillar structures while limiting the development of highly ordered fibrils.29 These observations are consistent with the present results indicating that polyphenols modulate protein aggregation rather than exerting a uniform stabilizing or destabilizing effect. A related dual effect was also reported for other polyphenol–protein systems. For example, quercetin modifies the thermal denaturation profile of bovine serum albumin (BSA) in a concentration-dependent manner, with different QUER binding modes producing different effects on protein stability.36 Similarly, gallic acid was shown to increase the thermal stability of BSA while simultaneously promoting the formation of larger aggregates from a partially unfolded protein fraction.37 Together, these studies support the different effects of polyphenol–protein interactions on native protein stability and on the aggregation of partially unfolded species. In the present QUER–BHb system, this difference is evident at 277 K: the 1 : 1 ratio increases the aggregation temperature, consistent with a stabilizing contribution from protein-bound QUER, whereas the excess ligand at the 3 : 1 ratio decreases the aggregation temperature, indicating an aggregation-promoting effect of free QUER. Under prolonged incubation at 310 K, the progressive decrease in aggregation temperature with increasing QUER concentration further substantiates the free ligand contribution to the destabilization and aggregation of BHb. Thus, the µDSC results identify an important feature of the QUER–BHb system: the effect of QUER is not a simple stabilizing or destabilizing one, but depends on the relative amounts of protein-bound and free ligand and on the thermal incubation conditions. This thermal behavior triggers the conformational changes and heterogeneous aggregate formation supported by CD and DLS measurements, and CR, ThT, and ANS binding assays in the following sections.

Quercetin induces changes in the protein secondary structure

Circular dichroism was used to investigate the effect of QUER on the secondary structure of incubated QUER–BHb systems. The CD spectra of native BHb present two negative minima in the UV region, at 209 (due to π–π* transition of α-helix) and 222 nm (due to π–π* transition for both α-helix and random coil),38 and also the strong positive band near 195 nm. Changes in the protein ellipticity occur in the presence of QUER. The CD spectra of the samples incubated for 24 h at 277 K (Fig. 3) present a low decrease of the signal for QUER : BHb 1 : 1, as a result of protein unfolding.

CD spectra of BHb in the absence and presence of QUER after incubation.

The enhancement of the negative ellipticity at higher ligand concentration (QUER : BHb 3 : 1) reveals the stabilizing effect of the ligand on the native structure of the protein. The CD spectra of the samples incubated for 96 h at 310 K retain the characteristic shape of a predominant α-helix structure. Lower ellipticity values were obtained, more evident in the presence of QUER, indicating changes in the secondary structure related to protein unfolding. The secondary structure content obtained from BeStSel deconvolution of CD spectra has been presented in Fig. 4.

The secondary structure content of BHb, in the absence and presence of QUER after incubation for (A) 24 h at 277 K, and (B) 96 h at 310 K.

A dual action of QUER was evidenced for the samples incubated for 24 h at 277 K (Fig. 4A): lower α-helix content and higher β structure for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 (NRMSD = 0.00467) point to an aggregation promoter effect of bound polyphenol, while higher concentrations of polyphenol (QUER : BHb 3 : 1) (NRMSD = 0.00560) induced an increase in the α-helix content, pointing to a stabilization effect on the protein structure (NRMSD = 0.00474). For the samples incubated for 96 h at 310 K (Fig. 4B), lower values of α-helix content and the increase in β structure, obtained in the presence of the ligand, were associated with the unfolded structure of the protein (NRMSD = 0.00566). QUER acts as a promoter of BHb aggregation (QUER : BHb 1 : 1 – NRMSD = 0.00496 and QUER : BHb 3 : 1 – NRMSD = 0.00607), as µDSC measurements revealed. The secondary structure changes obtained from BeStSel deconvolution provide conformational insights that explain the aggregation behavior observed by µDSC and further demonstrate the concentration-dependent effect of QUER on BHb. At the 1 : 1 molar ratio, the decrease in α-helix content and the increase in β-structure are consistent with partial unfolding and conformational changes that favor intermolecular association. Under prolonged thermal incubation at 310 K, this transition becomes more pronounced in the presence of QUER, indicating that the ligand promotes the change of the predominantly α-helix BHb structure toward β-rich conformations associated with aggregation. Similar α-helix to β-structure transitions induced by polyphenol binding were reported for several globular proteins. In human serum albumin, interactions with formononetin, hesperetin, and naringin were associated with changes in secondary structure involving a reduction in α-helix content and an increase in β-structured conformations.39–41 Conformational changes were reported for ovalbumin in the presence of curcumin and epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), indicating that polyphenol binding can modify the secondary structural organization of globular proteins toward β-rich conformations.42,43 A comparable concentration-dependent structural effect was described for β-lactoglobulin interacting with curcumin derivatives, where increasing ligand concentrations produced more pronounced changes in the protein secondary structure.44 These previous findings support the ability of polyphenols to induce α-helix to β-structure transitions in globular proteins. The present results extend this observation by showing that the structural effect of QUER on BHb is strongly dependent on both ligand concentration and thermal treatment. In particular, the distinct response of the 1 : 1 and 3 : 1 molar ratio systems after incubation at 277 K indicates that QUER can exert different conformational effects depending on the ligand concentration, whereas prolonged incubation at 310 K shifts both systems toward β-rich, aggregation-prone conformations. Thus, QUER does not simply induce a uniform destabilization of BHb, but modulates its unfolding pathway according to the physicochemical conditions of the protein–ligand system. The effect of QUER on the thermal stability of protein incubated for 96 h at 310 K was further evaluated using DSL measurements, CR, ThT, and ANS assays.

DLS reveals concentration-dependent changes in BHb aggregate size

DLS measurements were performed to detect and characterize the protein aggregates formed after incubation for 96 h at 310 K (Fig. 5), in the absence and presence of polyphenol.

DLS measurements for QUER–BHb systems. (A) The intensity size distribution of BHb, in the absence and presence of QUER after incubation for 96 h at 310 K, (B) the Dh and PDI mean values ± standard deviation, and (C) number size distribution; inset: detailed view of the 1–15 nm region showing the monomeric population.

The intensity size distribution of QUER : BHb 0 : 1 (Fig. 5A) reveals the presence of two populations: a low-size one centered around Dh1 ∼ 8 nm, corresponding to the monomeric form of hemoglobin, and a second population with Dh2 in the 100–300 nm range, associated with larger size aggregates. In the presence of QUER, significant changes in the aggregates' population were observed. For the QUER : BHb 1 : 1, the aggregates population presents increased intensity and a narrower distribution with Dh2 = 127.50 nm, indicating enhanced size aggregates formation promoted by QUER. At higher polyphenol concentration (QUER : BHb 3 : 1), the intensity size distribution corresponding to protein aggregates becomes broader and is shifted toward higher values of the hydrodynamic diameter (Dh2 = 187.40 nm), indicating the formation of heterogeneous aggregates. The results are in good agreement with µDSC and CD measurements. The higher values of PDI (Fig. 5B) obtained in the presence of QUER support the results. Moreover, the number distribution presented in Fig. 5C displays a regular behavior: the monomer size slightly increases with increasing QUER content. This may be interpreted as a partial unfolding of the monomer population that scales with the ligand concentration and accounts for aggregation thermodynamic parameters obtained by µDSC.

The DLS results provide further evidence that QUER modifies the aggregation behavior of BHb and that this effect depends on ligand concentration. The increase in Dh and PDI in the presence of QUER indicates the formation of larger and more heterogeneous protein aggregates, particularly at the QUER : BHb 3 : 1 molar ratio. These findings are consistent with the structural changes detected by CD and with the aggregation behavior observed by µDSC, suggesting that QUER promotes the association of partially unfolded BHb molecules into heterogeneous assemblies. Previous studies showed that polyphenols can modulate the size of protein aggregates. A recent multi-technique study of mutant huntingtin aggregation reported that myricetin, curcumin, resveratrol, and EGCG reduced oligomer growth and restricted hydrodynamic increase, with myricetin showing the strongest effect.45 In contrast, in the present study, QUER promotes aggregate growth, as evidenced by the increased Dh and PDI values. This difference emphasizes the complex effects of polyphenols on protein aggregation that depend on the nature of the protein, ligand concentration, and interaction environment. The obtained DLS results corroborate the calorimetric and spectroscopic data and demonstrate that the concentration-dependent structural effects of QUER are reflected not only in protein conformation but also in the size and heterogeneity of the resulting aggregates.

Congo red absorbance assay supports QUER-induced conformational changes and aggregation of BHb

CR assay was used to further confirm the BHb aggregates in the presence of increasing concentrations of QUER. The absorption spectrum of the native BHb-CR system presents a maximum at 498 nm (Fig. 6), revealing CR binding to the protein.46

CR absorption spectra of BHb (1.5 µM) in 30 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, in the presence of increasing concentrations of QUER, after incubation for 96 h at 310 K.

For the incubated protein samples at 310 K for 96 h, in the absence and presence of QUER, the value of CR absorbance increases and is associated with a red shift from 490 to 498 nm for QUER : BHb 0 : 1, to 496 nm for QUER : BHb 1 : 1, and to 494 nm for QUER : BHb 3 : 1 in the absorption spectrum. These results point to CR binding to the extended conformation of the protein. During incubation, the hemoglobin tetramer undergoes partial unfolding or transition into early-stage amorphous aggregates, exposing hydrophobic groups that were previously buried in the native state. A higher increase in absorbance was observed for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 molar ratio. These results support the conformational changes and the aggregation of BHb induced by QUER obtained by using µDSC, CD, and DLS data for the incubated samples at 310 K. Similar changes in CR binding were reported during the aggregation of hemoglobin, where the transition from partially unfolded or molten-globule states toward aggregated species was characterized using CR and ThT.46 The study evidenced the change of hemoglobin from an altered conformational state toward aggregated structures, highlighting the importance of these complementary probes for distinguishing conformational changes associated with protein aggregation. In the present study, the increased CR absorbance and wavelength shifts observed after thermal incubation indicate increased accessibility of binding sites in BHb, consistent with the formation of partially unfolded protein conformations. The pronounced increase in CR absorbance observed for the QUER : BHb 1 : 1 molar ratio is consistent with the structural changes detected by CD and the aggregation behavior evidenced by µDSC and DLS. These results support the formation of partially unfolded and heterogeneous protein assemblies.

ThT binding assay indicates the formation of predominantly non-fibrillar aggregates

Thioflavin T (ThT) is a fluorescent dye used to detect amyloid-like fibrillar aggregates due to its selective interaction with cross-β-sheet-rich structures formed during protein aggregation.47 Native QUER–BHb systems present low ThT fluorescence intensity (Fig. 7A), indicating the absence of fibrillar structures in the native protein conformation.

(A) ThT fluorescence spectra in the absence and presence of increasing QUER : BHb molar ratio, (B) normalized ThT fluorescence mean value ± data range as error bars.

Incubation at 310 K induces conformational changes in protein structure, and partial unfolding that favors intermolecular association and formation of aggregates with enhanced β-sheet content.48 The normalized fluorescence of ThT for QUER–BHb systems was presented in Fig. 7B.

The restrained increase in ThT fluorescence after thermal incubation suggests the formation of limited ordered β-structured regions rather than highly ordered amyloid fibrils, as reported for glyoxal addition to bovine hemoglobin.46 The enhancement of ThT fluorescence for incubated QUER–BHb systems at 310 K compared to 277 K is more pronounced for QUER : BHb 1 : 1 and 3 : 1, and signs for the aggregation effect of polyphenol, as confirmed by the µDSC and DLS results.

Although the CD results indicate an increase in β-structure during thermal incubation, the limited ThT response suggests that these structural changes do not lead to extensive formation of highly ordered amyloid fibrils. Instead, QUER appears to favor the formation of less ordered and predominantly non-fibrillar aggregates. This behavior is particularly interesting when compared with previous studies showing that the effect of QUER on protein aggregation differs depending on the protein and experimental conditions. For example, Wang et al.49 reported that QUER inhibited amyloid fibrillation of bovine insulin in a concentration-dependent manner and converted preformed insulin fibrils into amorphous aggregates, indicating an anti-fibrillogenic effect of the polyphenol. In contrast, the present results show that QUER promotes the aggregation of thermally stressed BHb, while the relatively weak ThT response indicates that the resulting aggregates are not extensively organized into amyloid fibrils. A similar difference between aggregation and fibril formation was reported for rutin – BHb systems, where the polyphenol promoted disordered aggregates or protofibrillar structures while limiting the formation of highly ordered fibrils.29 These differences suggest that polyphenols may modulate the aggregation pathway rather than simply inhibit or promote aggregation. In the present BHb system, QUER appears to favor protein association and aggregate growth without extensive development of amyloid-like structures. The broader aggregate size distribution observed at the higher QUER concentration in DLS, together with the restrained ThT response, suggests that QUER promotes the formation of heterogeneous aggregates without development of extended highly ordered amyloid structures. The CR and ThT results provide further evidence that QUER affects not only the extent of BHb aggregation but also the structural organization of the resulting aggregates.

ANS binding assay reveals polyphenol effect on the hydrophobic domains involved in protein aggregation

ANS binds non-covalently to exposed hydrophobic residues of proteins and is used to evaluate conformational changes, partial unfolding, and aggregation processes in proteins. The exposure of hydrophobic regions during partial protein unfolding enhances ANS fluorescence and induces a blue shift in the emission peak. The fluorescence spectrum of ANS (Fig. S1A in SI), in phosphate buffer containing 1% DMSO, exhibits two emission maxima, at 493 and 540 nm, corresponding to Kosower's non-planar and charge transfer, respectively.50 The ANS fluorescence increases for the native and incubated QUER : BHb 0 : 1, and the maximum slightly shifts to a lower wavelength (491 nm), indicating the interaction of the charged group of lysine and arginine with the sulfonate group of ANS.51 The spectra were deconvoluted into two components using PeakFit software with two HVL functions (Fig. S1B–D), and the influence of QUER concentrations on ANS fluorescence was evaluated for the first peak (491 nm) assigned to ANS bound to protein (Fig. 8A).

(A) ANS fluorescence spectra for native and incubated QUER–BHb systems, (B) normalized intensity of ANS fluorescence mean value ± data range as error bars.

The changes in ANS emission for the native and incubated QUER–BHb systems were assessed after correcting for the inner filter effect and normalizing the fluorescence values (Fig. 8B). Lower ANS fluorescence values were obtained for both native and incubated QUER : BHb 1 : 1. The polyphenol preserves the compact protein structure and the inner hydrophobic regions remain buried. The increase in ANS fluorescence for native and incubated QUER : BHb 3 : 1 reveals a higher exposure of the hydrophobic regions to the solvent, thus promoting intermolecular interactions and subsequent aggregation. This dual effect suggests that QUER induces concentration-dependent conformational changes in the protein structure. For QUER : BHb 1 : 1, these changes promote partial protein compaction and/or the shielding of hydrophobic domains involved in intermolecular interactions, whereas at higher concentrations, QUER exhibits a pronounced aggregation-promoting effect. Similar effects have been described for flavonoid–protein systems, where polyphenol binding alters the structure of proteins through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding.52 These findings support the hypothesis that polyphenol modulates the structural conformation and surface properties of BHb, influencing the exposure of hydrophobic domains involved in aggregation.

The concentration-dependent behavior recorded by the ANS assay explains the dual behavior observed in the microcalorimetric and spectroscopic experiments. At QUER : BHb 1 : 1 molar ratio, the quenching of ANS fluorescence points to the burial of hydrophobic sites, the bound ligand prevents both dye access and intermolecular self-assembly. In contrast, the rise in ANS emission at a 3 : 1 molar ratio proves that excess free ligand induces protein unfolding and exposure to the solvent. Similar concentration-dependent conformational effects were reported for flavonoid–protein systems. Studies on human serum albumin showed that flavonoid binding can modify the protein secondary and tertiary structure, including changes in α-helix content and the environment of hydrophobic binding sites, with effects on protein stability and aggregation behavior.53 Quercetin binds within the hydrophobic cavity of human serum albumin, inducing measurable conformational changes.54 These observations are consistent with the present ANS results, which indicate that the structural response of BHb to QUER depends on ligand concentration and is associated with changes in the accessibility of hydrophobic regions. The ANS results support the structural changes observed by CD and are consistent with the larger and more heterogeneous aggregates detected by DLS.

This study systematically demonstrates that QUER exerts a dual, concentration-dependent action on the structural stability and aggregation kinetics of bovine hemoglobin under sustained thermal stress. A 1 : 1 QUER : BHb molar ratio favors a more compact conformation of the protein by limiting the exposure of hydrophobic regions, whereas excess free QUER promotes structural rearrangement, increases hydrophobic surface exposure, and enables intermolecular association. The present results connect the concentration-dependent conformational changes to the thermal aggregation behavior and the size of protein aggregates. The increased β-structure content, enhanced hydrophobic exposure, increased particle size and polydispersity, together with the limited ThT response, indicate that QUER excess favors the formation of heterogeneous, predominantly non-fibrillar aggregates rather than highly ordered amyloid structures. This outcome is important because previous studies of polyphenol–protein interactions emphasized the protective or aggregation-suppressing effects of these compounds, whereas the present data demonstrate that increasing the concentration beyond the 1 : 1 molar ratio can reverse this effect and promote aggregation. Thus, the ligand state (free or protein bound) represents an important parameter for understanding and controlling the structural form of polyphenol–protein systems under thermal stress. The concentration-dependent behavior has practical implications for the formulation of protein-based therapeutics and biotechnological systems. Therefore, controlling the free concentration of hydrophobic small molecules may be critical for maintaining protein stability and preventing unwanted precipitation. In food science, the observed concentration-dependent aggregation may also provide a basis for developing controlled approaches for the separation or recovery of heme proteins from complex food matrices.

Although the present study provides valuable insights into the interaction between bovine hemoglobin and quercetin, it is limited by the use of simplified in vitro experimental conditions that may not fully reflect the complexity of physiological environments. Further investigations integrating high-resolution structural techniques, molecular dynamics simulations, and complementary assays performed under physiologically relevant conditions are necessary to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this interaction and to assess its biological significance.

Conclusions

This work provides a comprehensive characterization of the effect of quercetin on the thermal stability and aggregation behavior of bovine hemoglobin using calorimetric and spectroscopic approaches. The results demonstrate that quercetin acts as a concentration- and temperature-dependent modulator of protein aggregation: after 24 h incubation at 277 K, a low concentration of polyphenol (QUER : BHb 1 : 1) slightly stabilizes the protein structure, whereas higher concentrations promote aggregation. Following prolonged incubation at physiological temperature (96 h at 310 K), QUER acts as a promoter of BHb aggregation, as evidenced by calorimetric, spectroscopic, and DLS analyses. Although the native α-helix structure of the protein remains largely preserved, the increase in β-sheet content observed in the presence of polyphenol indicates structural changes associated with aggregate formation. The presence of oligomeric species and larger aggregates detected by DLS, together with the results of Congo Red, ThT, and ANS assays, indicates that QUER influences both protein unfolding and the subsequent aggregation. Overall, these findings highlight the complex correlation between protein–ligand interactions, conformational stability, and the aggregation process. These insights enable the design of stable nutraceutical delivery systems and predict hemoglobin-polyphenol behavior in biological environments.
