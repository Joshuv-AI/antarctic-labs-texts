The association of quality of life and fear of progression in patients with pulmonary hypertension: a cross-sectional network analysis
Authors: Mengqi Liu, Yijin Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe quality of life (QoL) of patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH) is severely compromised. Fear of progression (FoP) may be an important predictor of compromising the QoL in PH patients. Therefore, our study aimed to investigate the association between QoL and FoP among PH patients.MethodsA cross-sectional study comprising 255 patients was conducted using convenience sampling, using the Self-administered socio-demographic information questionnaire, the World Health Organization quality of life questionnaire abbreviated version, and the Chinese version of the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form. Network analysis was completed using R software.ResultsIn the current network model, the QoL in the psychological domain had the highest node centrality (strength = 1.155). The highest bridge centrality node was physiological health-related fear (bridge strength = 0.303). The edge between physiological health-related fear and social family-related fear has the highest strength (edge weights = 0.596).ConclusionsThis study revealed that the psychological domain of QoL is likely to be the most dominant in the overall QoL of PH patients. It can serve as a primary focus for QoL management strategies to improve the overall QoL of PH patients. Furthermore, physical health-related fear is an urgent mental health concern to be alleviated in PH patients' FoP. As a bridge variable, physical health-related fear can be used as a potential clinical intervention target for developing and utilizing intervention programs to alleviate the FoP in PH patients, which may help maintain or even enhance the psychological and overall QoL of PH patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02205-7.

Background

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is defined as a clinical and pathophysiological syndrome characterized by increased pulmonary vascular resistance and pulmonary artery pressure caused by structural or functional changes in the pulmonary vasculature due to a variety of heterogeneous diseases (etiology) and different pathogenetic mechanisms [1]. A retrospective study [2] stated that the annual incidence of adult PH has risen from 99.8 cases/100,000 population in 1993 to 127.3 cases/100,000 population in 2012. In 2018, surveys showed that adult PH patients had mortality rates of 13.0%, 36.4%, and 62.4% at 30 days, 1 year, and 5 years, respectively [2]. PH has become a substantial global health problem that requires urgent attention from medical professionals [3].

Quality of life (QoL) reflects the extent to which diseases and their treatments affect patients' daily functioning [4], serving as an indicator of treatment efficacy [5]. The symptoms of poor exercise tolerance and fatigue associated with pulmonary hypertension (PH) [6] not only severely impair patients' physical capabilities, work, and economic viability, increasing their reliance on medications, therapies, and care services [7], but also restrict their social interactions [8], ultimately diminishing their QoL [2, 6]. Consequently, healthcare professionals continuously advocate for an expansion of the management approach for treating PH to focus on the overall improvement of patients' quality of life [4, 9]. Identifying needs related to QoL and developing effective care strategies based on these factors is crucial for enhancing the overall quality of life for patients with PH.

Cognition significantly influences the somatic symptoms and stress relief processes [10]. Among various cognitive factors, fear of progression (FoP) emerges as a predominant concern [11]. Fear of progression (FoP) [12] is defined as a reactive, consciously perceived fear that arises from a serious, potentially life-threatening, or disabling illness or its treatment. Takita et al. [9] conducted interviews with 13 patients suffering from moderate to severe anxiety or depression. They found that, regardless of the cause, these 13 PH patients frequently experienced FoP due to excessive concerns about treatment risks and potential physical disabilities associated with disease progression. A cross-sectional study by Kuang et al. [13] involving 102 pulmonary disease patients demonstrated that increasing FoP negatively impacts patients' QoL. Thus, FoP may serve as a critical predictor of compromised QoL in PH patients. Exploring the relationship between FoP and QoL could provide valuable insights for healthcare professionals in developing targeted QoL management strategies, ultimately enhancing the overall QoL of PH patients. However, to date, studies exploring the correlation between fear of progression (FoP) and quality of life (QoL) have predominantly focused on patient populations with cancers such as lung cancer [13] and breast cancer [14], with only a limited number of studies addressing this issue in patients with pulmonary hypertension.

In recent years, network analysis (NA) has frequently been employed to study comorbidity and symptom overlap, but its application is not limited to these areas [15]. More recently, network analysis has begun to be utilized in the field of nursing research, with promising prospects for revealing the connections between psychological symptoms and health conditions, informing personalized nursing interventions, and visualizing dynamic changes. For several reasons, it has demonstrated performance advantages over traditional factor correlations. First, NA allows for the examination of interactions between dimensions, considering the interplay between QoL and FoP [16], while also visualizing the probability of symptom transfer [17]. This approach is more aligned with real-world complexities than traditional factor analysis methods [18]. Second, NA identifies nodes with the highest centrality [19] and bridge strength [20], facilitating targeted interventions to improve the QoL for PH patients.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the correlation between FoP and QoL in patients with PH, providing a scientific basis for healthcare professionals to develop effective quality management strategies that address the needs of these patients.

Materials and methods

Participants and sample size

Applying a cross-sectional descriptive study design, 255 patients with PH who attended a tertiary general hospital in REDACTED from July 2021 to February 2022 were selected as respondents using a convenience sampling method. Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients whose condition met the diagnostic criteria within the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pulmonary Hypertension (2021) [21] and who were diagnosed with PH by right cardiac catheterization; (2) Age > 18 years; (3) Informed diagnosis; (4) Consciousness and certain reading and writing abilities. Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe hearing and communication impairment; (2) History of previous psychiatric disorders; (3) Combination of severe diseases of other systems. In this study, a GLASSO network model containing six nodes was constructed using NA, which required the estimation of 21 parameters (six threshold parameters plus 6*5/2 pairwise association parameters) [22]. Following practices of at least 3 ~ 5 individuals per parameter, our sample size was sufficient for exploratory data-driven analyses [23]. Then, the sample size was at least 63 to 105 patients with PH. Recruitment conditions and information were posted within the team's online patient group to attract patients to participate in the study by offering a medical consultation as a preferential condition. We ultimately included 247 patients with PH, which met the sample size requirement.

Procedures

This study was approved by the medical ethics committee of the host institution, which determined that no intervention involving human biological samples was conducted and agreed that the study would collect data based on the verbal consent of the respondents (Approval number: 2021-R-154). Before the survey, the subjects were informed of the purpose, content, and data privacy of this study. After obtaining informed consent from the patients, the questionnaire was distributed to them, who were instructed to complete it following a standardized guideline. The researcher collected and checked the questionnaires on the spot, and the information was kept strictly confidential. During the period, the researcher provided the subjects with the opportunity to answer questions at any time to ensure the quality of questionnaire completion and reduce invalid questionnaires. This study adhered to the RECORD statement (i.e., a checklist of items extended from the STROBE statement) when writing this paper. The distribution of questionnaires and data collection were jointly conducted by a graduate nursing student and an assistant to a clinical expert specializing in pulmonary hypertension.

Measures

We used a self-administered questionnaire to collect social and disease information on patients’ gender, education level, frequency of social activity participation, monthly per capita household income. The cardiac function was assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification [24], which includes four classes: Class I—No symptoms and no limitation in ordinary physical activity, e.g., shortness of breath when walking or climbing stairs; Class II—Mild symptoms (mild shortness of breath and/or angina) and slight limitation during ordinary activity; Class III—Marked limitation in activity due to symptoms, even during less-than-ordinary activity, e.g. walking short distances (20—100 m).Comfortable only at rest; Class IV—Severe limitations. Experiences symptoms even while at rest. Mostly bedbound patients. The World Health Organization quality of life questionnaire abbreviated version (WHOQOL-BREF) was used to measure the status of QoL. WHOQOL-BREF [25] contains 4 domains (physiological, psychological, social relationships, and environmental) and consists of 24 entries and 2 independent analysis questions, using a Likert 5-point scale with a score range of 4 to 20 for all 4 domains, the higher the score, the better the QoL [26]. WHOQOL-BREF has been widely used in various populations in mainland China and has proven to be a validated measurement tool [27]. The reliability analysis conducted in this study indicated that the WHOQOL-BREF exhibited an acceptable degree of internal consistency in the measurement of the QoL of PH patients (Cronbach's ɑ = 0.908). The Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF), which was translated into Chinese by Wu et al. [28], was used to measure the level of FoP. FoP-Q-SF includes 2 dimensions, physiological health-related fear, and social family-related fear, with 12 entries, and uses a Likert 5-point scale. The higher the score, the higher the level of fear [29]. The original FoP-Q-SF uses a score greater than 34 as a criterion for determining the presence of FoP in an individual, but whether the Chinese version of the FoP-Q-SF uses a score of 34 as a cut-off value has not been confirmed by research. FoP-Q-SF has been validated to be a valid instrument for measuring FoP in Chinese patients (Cronbach's ɑ = 0.883) [30]. The reliability analysis conducted in this study indicated that the FoP-Q-SF scale exhibited an acceptable degree of internal consistency in the measurement of the FoP of PH patients (Cronbach's ɑ = 0.929). Statistical analysis

Network analysis

Statistical analysis was completed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0 (SPSS Inc.). Frequency and percentage are used to describe ordinal and categorized variables, such as gender, education level, and other sociodemographic characteristics. The means and standard deviations (M ± SD) were used to describe continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, such as QoL. The t-test was used to evaluate if there was a statistically significant difference in WHOQOL-BREF or FoP-Q-SF scores between the two groups that were divided by gender and other sociodemographic characteristics. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the differences in PTGI-SF scores between three or more groups, that were divided by education level, number of social activities in the past week alike.

In this study, network analysis was completed using R (Version 4.1.1) software, assessing the degree of correlation of nodes in the network model using the R package qgraph. The network analysis model has four important metrics, which are node centrality, edge weights, bridge centrality, and correlation stability (CS). We declare that the data, code/script materials mentioned in the "Methodology" section of this study are available.

Node strength is a quantitative indicator of node centrality, which represents the sum of the weighted values of all the connections of a node, indicating the importance of the node in the model. The higher the node strength, the more the node is at the center of the network [31]. Given that Bringmann et al. [32] showed that closeness and betweenness are not applicable to psychological networks, node strength was used as the only indicator of node centrality [33]. In addition, our study estimated predictability using the R package mgm (Version 1.2–11) [34], which reflects the extent to which a particular node is predicted by all its neighbors. Edge is the line connecting any two nodes in the network model. Edge strength represents the intensity and directionality (i.e., positive/negative correlation) of the line connecting any two nodes in the network model, while also taking into account other variables and correlation strengths in the overall network model. Bridge nodes are nodes that serve as potential links between broader communities of nodes. Bridge centrality, also known as bridge strength, represents the sum of the weighted values of all the links between a node in the first group and all the factors in the second group, which is used to evaluate the connectivity between the two groups of nodes.

Network stability and accuracy

R package bootnet was used to evaluate network stability and accuracy: the case dropping bootstrap procedure was used to calculate the Correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) to demonstrate the maximum percentage that could be removed without affecting the data to evaluate stability; non-parametric bootstrapping was used to calculate confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the accuracy of the edge-weighted values. According to Epskamp and colleagues’ research recommendation, the CS-C needs to be at least 0.25 and preferably greater than or equal to 0.5 to indicate that the network model is highly stable [22].

Predictability and different test

The predictability index shows that the QoL in the psychological domain (66.9%) was the most predictable node, followed by QoL in the physiological domain (64.1%) and QoL in the environmental domain (61.5%). On average, 58.65% of the variation in the network model per node could be explained by the adjacent nodes (M predictability = 0.59 ± 0.10), as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Supplementary Figure S2 showed the result of Bootstrapped difference test for each edge strength. Strength shows no statistical difference among the following edges: the edge psychological domain QoL (QoL2)—environmental domains QoL (QoL4), physiological domain QoL (QoL1)—psychological domain QoL (QoL2), physiological domain QoL (QoL1)—environmental domain QoL (QoL4), and the edge psychological domain QoL (QoL2)—social relationships domain QoL (QoL3). Nevertheless, all four edges were significantly less weighted than the edge between physiological health-related fear and social family-related fear.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study was one of the few epidemiological studies that determined and examined the variables of PH patients' fear of progression in Mainland China, and the first to characterize the QoL and FoP network of PH patients. The mean score of four domains in WHOQOL-BREF was lower than the scores reported in a Chinese study comprising 777 normal people with a comparable instrument [35]. The results showed that the QoL of PH patients was severely impaired, especially in the physical health domain, in agreement with the previous findings [4]. Additionally, consistent with recent findings [13], we found a significant negative correlation between FoP and QoL in PH patients.

The study revealed that the node QoL in the psychological domain had the highest centrality and was closely related to all other nodes in the network, in partial agreement with the findings of previous studies [36–38]. Recent studies [36] noted that intervention programs targeting cognition improved participants’ somatic functioning and pain, providing side evidence that there is a strong association between patients’ psychological and physical health. In addition, the Evolutionary Theory of Loneliness (ETL) [39] states that social connection is a basic human need, and that long-term lack of such social connection can have adverse mental health consequences. Empirical studies [40] promoted social support as an important psychological first aid and recovery strategy to improve the social isolation of populations during epidemics. As for the environmental domain, previous studies [38, 41] showed that improvements in health services, knowledge, information, and skills acquisition, and housing environment can reduce the level of social isolation in patients and improve their subjective well-being by improving the QoL in the environmental domain. In short, previous studies have provided theoretical and empirical evidence for the association between QoL in the psychological domain and other domains, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Finally, the study also found that QoL in the psychological domain was the most predictable and could be used to indirectly improve patients’ psychological health by intervening in nearby nodes with which it is strongly correlated, such as QoL in environmental and physiological domains. This suggests that healthcare professionals should pay attention to the mental health status of PH patients; regularly provide patients with disease knowledge through public and expert lectures, improve the conditions of community healthcare resources; form multidisciplinary collaborative teams in rehabilitation, nursing, and social psychology to develop personalized rehabilitation strategies to improve patients’ somatic activity endurance. QoL in the psychological domain is indirectly improved by regulating QoL in the environment and physiological domains.

With suboptimal node strength, physiological health-related fear was identified as another core node that urgently needed to be highlighted. Node physiological health-related fear was closely related to other domains in QoL and social family-related fear, in line with the findings of Hanprasertpong et al. [42]. Physiological aspects: sustained somatic stress such as hypoxia and susceptibility to fatigue cause the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) and sympathetic nerve system (SNS) secretion disorders in the organism, triggering dysfunction of the entire neuroendocrine system, leading to impaired emotion regulation and other functions, inducing physiological fear [43]. Psychological aspects: Due to the progressive development of PH, patients are unable to stop the progression of the disease even with regular medication, resulting in great psychological stress. This negative preoccupation leads patients to reevaluate stressors, reducing their psychological tolerance and causing fear. Social relationship aspects: positive emotions motivate individuals to build social networks to meet their needs [44], while activity limitations and negative emotions due to somatic symptoms can overwhelm positive emotions in patients. This impairs the patient’s social skills and leads to chronic social isolation and panic [45]. It can be seen that physiological health-related fear is related to the QoL in the physiological, psychological, and social relationship domains, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Among them, the psychological domain QoL was most significantly correlated with physiological health-related fear, followed by the social relationship domain QoL, and the physiological domain QoL. In turn, fear also negatively affects patients’ QoL: (1) If the symptoms happen not to attack after patients adopt fear-related defensive behaviors (i.e., avoidance of physical exercise), such experience would exacerbate avoidance behaviors (i.e., fear of movement) and in the long run will lead to the patient's physical dysfunction or even disability, trapping patients in a vicious cycle of fear and somatic disablement [46]; (2) The prolonged presence of fear can cause hyperactivity of the HPA axis [47], which inhibits Hypothalamic-Pituitary–Gonadal (HPG) secretory function [48]. This would cause fluctuations in sex hormone concentrations and mood in patients, promoting the release of antidiuretic hormones aggravating water and sodium retention in patients, and negatively affecting their cardiac function.

It was noted that physiological health-related fear and social family-related fear were the most strongly correlated in our model, in line with the findings of previous studies [49, 50]. Based on the biopsychosocial model of suffering in cancer patients, some scholars [51, 52] suggested that the onset of somatic symptoms can be perceived by patients as a precursor to a poor prognosis if they lack disease knowledge or have disease information processing bias, even without a doctor's confirmation. This can mislead patients to worry excessively about the malignant effects of the disease on their families' physiological health and social development, resulting in negative emotions such as anticipatory sadness and FoP. On grounds of this theory, it is reasonable to assume that physical health and social family-related fears are fundamentally rooted in somatic symptoms caused by the disease. This may be the reason why physiological health-related and social family-related fear are most strongly correlated in the network.

The study revealed that node physiological health-related fears have the strongest bridge centrality in the network, followed by social family-related fears. Accordingly, we hypothesized that targeting physiological health-related fear as an intervention would improve both FoP and QoL of PH patients, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. Professor Butow [53] developed a Conquer Fear intervention with fear of progression variables as a target based on a theoretical model. Under such intervention, cancer-specific distress (total) remained more improved at 6-month follow-up, confirming the superiority of interventions targeting FoP in alleviating patients' long-term psychological distress. This laterally validates the findings of this study, suggesting that FoP may be a potential key target for improving the long-term QoL in patients with chronic diseases (i.e., PH patients).

Our findings provide novel insights that likely contributed to the evolution of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Supportive Experiential Therapy (SET), and other programs to improve the efficacy of interventions based on the original ones. As social family-related fear are also negative emotions, they also hurt patients' QoL, but their bridging centrality is less than that of physiological health-related fear. This may be because the negative impact of the patient’s fear of illness on his or her family and social relationships is rooted in the fear of a significant decrease in the patient’s physical functioning, work, and economic independence as a whole result of the illness. For Chinese people, moral responsibility is one of the essential requirements for civic development. Therefore, Chinese PH patients generally have the trait of having a strong sense of social and family responsibility. This trait has a subtle influence on patients’ cognition and is sufficient to make the fear caused by somatic symptoms evolve into social family-related fear. If this were not the case, patients' level of social family-related fear would likely be lower than those of the present study population. Accordingly, it is reasonable to assume that alleviating patients’ physiological health-related fear to a safe level could fundamentally alleviate patients’ social and family fear. This may be the reason why the bridge centrality of node physiological health-related fear is stronger than that of social family-related fear.

Additionally, we found that there is no relation between FoP2 (social and family fear) and QoL3 (social relationships domain of QoL). This finding is somewhat different from the results of previous studies. Mehnert et al. [54], in a prospective study, found that toxic interpersonal relationships and low levels of social support may exacerbate the overall level of FoP in cancer patients one year after recovery. Zheng et al. [55], on the other hand, found that adequate social support can alleviate the severity of FoP in postoperative patients with lung carcinoma. A possible explanation for the discrepancy between the results is that the aforementioned previous studies did not take into account the substructure of FoP and only explored the superficial relationship between FoP overall and social support. Therefore, it is highly likely that the results mask potential associations and differences between the FoP substructure and the social relationships domain of QoL. In addition, QoL3 is an instant assessment of a patient's satisfaction with either the psychosocial aspects of his/her relationships and the social support he/she receives [56]. FoP2, on the other hand, assesses the patients' perceived fear that the deterioration of the disease will prevent them from having a normal social and family life, focusing on the family's economic well-being and the health of the offspring [57]. It is clear that although both involve social dimensions, the connotations are quite different. Taken together, our results may suggest that support from psychosocial dimensions, such as relationships, may not have a significant mitigating effect on the fear of damage to family finances and offspring health caused by disease progression. This finding compensates for the lack of attention to FoP sub-structures in previous studies and provides new evidence for constructing accurate psychological care. However, this finding has not been reported in previous studies, which may be unique to our sample and needs to be further explored in the future.

Strength and limitations

The present study used an innovative network analysis method to characterize the network of FoP and QoL in PH patients. This not only remedies the shortcomings of the traditional method of rating the importance of factors by scores only but also provides novel insights for healthcare professionals to improve specific life quality management strategies, which are highly innovative and have clinical application value. Methodologically, our study used networked stability analysis and NCT test to ensure the stability of the network model. However, the study still acknowledges the existence of limitations. First, this study is a cross-sectional study and cannot confirm the long-term dynamic changes and causal relationships between variables. Second, traditional psychological factors such as anxiety and depression were not included in this study to investigate the differences in the pathways and intensity of their effects on the QoL of patients about FoP. In addition, no clinical biochemical indicators other than the NYHA cardiac function classification were included in this study, which limits the generalizability of this study in constructing QoL management strategies. In the future, the potential of clinical biochemical indicators, psychological indicators, and other multifaceted indicators in predicting, intervening, and managing QoL in PH patients could be further included and explored; Finally, the results obtained may be limited to the specific sample of this study and have limited generalizability as NA is essentially a data-driven exploratory analysis.

Conclusions

To conclude, this study revealed that the psychological domain of QoL is likely to be the most dominant in the overall QoL of PH patients. It can be adopted as a dominant intervention direction for QoL management strategies to improve the overall QoL of PH patients. Furthermore, physical health-related fear is an urgent mental health concern to be alleviated in PH patients' FoP. As a bridge variable, physical health-related fear can be used as a potential clinical intervention target for developing and utilizing intervention programs to alleviate the FoP in PH patients, which may serve to maintain or even promote the psychological and multi-domain life quality of PH patients. Lastly, significant gender differences in the FoP variable need to be validated and explained by more rigorous future studies.

Relevance to clinical practice

This study demonstrated that patients with PH have a poor quality of life and fear of disease progression. This provides an opportunity for healthcare professionals to provide patients with the necessary support and services promptly to alleviate their fear of disease progression and enable improvement in their quality of life. In addition, this study highlights the dominance of the psychological domain in quality of life and physical health-related fears in the network, which may provide key innovations for clinical patient quality of life management policymakers to upgrade the effectiveness of existing intervention programs. Furthermore, it is essential to offer psychological support to female patients of childbearing age.

Statement

The relevancy of our research to nursing

PH is now considered a chronic disease with a high mortality rate, and the presence of FoP in patients with this disease deserves as much attention as the psychological disorder of fear of recurrence in cancer patients. Nursing is a humanistic discipline with the important disciplinary task of preventing disease and promoting and maintaining human health. The improvement of patient's QoL has also been one of the elements of nursing. This study provides new insights for nursing staff to improve life quality management strategies for PH patients, and provides promising targets for later intervention practices, which are closely related to maintaining patient safety and the development of nursing discipline.
