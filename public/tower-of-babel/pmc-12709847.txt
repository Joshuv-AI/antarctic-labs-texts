When the hospital becomes an option again: a qualitative analysis of women’s and their partners’ experiences with a designated clinic for women who request different care than recommended in the guidelines
Authors: Marene Dimmendaal, Floor Opdam, Jeroen van Dillen, Marieke de Vries, Martine Hollander
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasingly, women with high-risk pregnancies or previous traumatic birth experiences decline care recommendations or seek alternatives because they feel that current maternity care services do not align with their personal preferences or needs. In the Netherlands, a designated clinic was set up to support women who decline care recommendations in making well-informed health decisions and to prevent them from making a negative choice to birth outside the system. The objective of this study was to learn from parents’ experiences with the clinic: what made them choose for a hospital birth while initially being apprehensive about this idea?MethodsA total of eight semi-structured interviews were conducted, which took place between May 2019 and August 2019 in Nijmegen, the Netherlands.ResultsAfter open, axial and selective coding of the transcripts following constructivist grounded theory approach, three themes were constructed from the data: ‘An unexpected positive experience’, ‘Mutual effort and flexibility’ and ‘Together but on my terms’. One overarching theme was identified: ‘A healing and empowering experience’.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that the approach of the designated clinic can be successful in meeting parents’ needs such as trust and autonomy during pregnancy and birth, and in preventing women from making a negative choice to birth outside the system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08243-2.Keywords: Maternity care, Personalized care, Woman-centred care, Shared decision making, Care outside the guidelines, Treatment refusal, Autonomy, The netherlands

Background

The right to make autonomous choices during pregnancy, childbirth and the postpartum period is highly valued by women. Moreover, woman-centred care, where the woman’s wishes and needs are prioritised, is recognised as a fundamental concept in midwifery [1–3]. Woman-centred care entails respectful maternity care (RMC), which refers to care that maintains the dignity and privacy of women and enables informed choice. This concept is included in the recommendations for intrapartum care issued by the WHO in 2018 [1]. Even though women-centred care and RMC have recently received quite some attention through research and implementation projects, women still experience a lack of choice and informed consent during childbirth [4–9]. Additionally, current maternity care services are experienced to not always align with personal preferences and needs [4, 10–13]. As a result, women may decline care recommendations and seek alternatives such as birthing without assistance [14–17].

Over the past decade, several articles have been published on women’s motivations to decline care recommendations in maternity care. The recurring theme, as Jackson et al. (2020) described, is: pursuing what’s - in the eyes of women themselves - best and safest. Women trust their ability to give birth naturally and perceive birth as an intimate experience where following their intuition is key. The parts of care that they decline are considered to be unnecessary and to be potentially impeding the body’s ability to birth naturally and safely [10, 11, 14, 18]. Other women have had a traumatic birth experience in the past, during which they experienced a lack of choice and control [18]. Thereafter, their focus is to protect themselves against a repeated experience of loss of autonomy. They may do so by striving for ultimate control over their birth process and therefore declining certain aspects of care which contributed to the feeling of being powerless in their previous, traumatic, birth [10, 12, 18, 19]. Situations where the care provider does not support a woman´s choice can escalate into manipulation, coercion and conflict. Furthermore, these forms of escalation are shown to affect a woman’s trust in the care system [4, 20–22].

In the Netherlands, women with a low-risk pregnancy receive prenatal and intrapartum care from community midwives. They have a choice for intended place of birth: at home, in a birth centre or in the hospital. Women with risk factors or complications during pregnancy or birth are referred to the hospital, where they receive obstetrician-led care [23]. In principle, all maternity care providers in the Netherlands act according to the principles of RMC by performing shared decision making (SDM), as the Dutch law prescribes SDM as the norm in care [24]. During SDM, a woman and her care provider jointly engage in sharing information and in interpreting medical evidence and options in light of what matters most from the woman’s perspective [25, 26]. However, this does not necessarily mean that the options offered to women in regular maternity care include options that go against medical advice as per national guidelines. Therefore, even though most if not all maternity care providers deliver respectful care and practice SDM, they are not always open to a woman’s request to explore care outside recommendations. Hollander et al. (2018) reported that more than 80% of Dutch midwives and obstetricians received at least one request for less care than recommended in the year preceding the study. These requests included, among others, declining testing for gestational diabetes, opting for a home birth in case of a high-risk pregnancy and declining intrapartum fetal monitoring during labour [13]. These results correspond with international studies, where testing for gestational diabetes and intrapartum fetal monitoring are shown to be declined most frequently, in addition to induction of labour and oxytocin administration [20, 27, 28].

Holten et al. (2018) performed a multiple case study on defining moments for Dutch women who chose to birth at home with high-risk pregnancies (breech, twins, previous caesarean section), thereby going against medical advice. Ten cases were analysed. The most striking finding of this study was that nine of these women initially opted for a hospital birth, but eventually decided to birth at home because they experienced a lack of autonomy, choice and flexibility during pregnancy and/or birth. Their experiences left them with the impression of having no choice but to leave the regular maternity care system: a ‘negative choice’. A negative choice is defined by Holten et al. (2018) as a decision against something rather than for something.

In 2015, care providers working at the university hospital in Nijmegen, the Netherlands, started a designated clinic “Tailored Maternity Care” (previously referred to as the “Clinic for Maternity Care Outside The Guidelines”) [29]. The clinic was set up to increase expertise and improve care for women who decline care recommendations and seek alternatives. Any pregnant woman in the hospital’s catchment area can be referred to the clinic by their primary care midwife, by in-house colleagues or by hospital staff elsewhere. The number of visits that are needed depends on the woman and what she needs. The consultations at the clinic are aimed at having a constructive and open dialogue about women’s plans and motivations for certain birth wishes. The primary goal of the consultations is not to arrive at a birth plan within the protocol, but to stimulate and support women in making well-informed autonomous decisions, by respectfully counselling them about different care options. Between January 2015 and December 2017, a total of 55 women visited the designated clinic. van der Garde et al. (2019) analysed these 55 cases and found that most women opted for the recommended care, after being counselled by their obstetrician. In all cases, an acceptable compromise was reached. All outcomes were good, with no perinatal deaths, no neonatal intensive care unit admissions and no severe maternal morbidity. Over the past five years, approximately five women per year have visited this designated clinic in Nijmegen. We have found the number of women referred annually to be declining a little, reflecting, we believe, an increased willingness by other providers to move with women’s requests when the deviation from protocol is small.

In 2021, a qualitative analysis of maternity care providers’ experiences with this clinic was reported [19]. A total of ten care providers who worked with or within the clinic were interviewed for that study. The resulting article reports why, in the eyes of maternity care providers, the designated clinic´s approach was successful in preventing women from making negative choices. According to the interviewees, the hospital became an option again for these women because of the approach of building trusting relationships with women, supporting them in making informed choices and guaranteeing their autonomy throughout pregnancy and childbirth. In the current study, we aim to investigate the experiences from women and their partners in the designated clinic and to learn about their motives to choose to birth at the hospital and not make a negative choice.

Materials and methods

For describing data collection and data analysis, the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) was used [30].

Research team and positionality

Interviews were conducted by LVDB, a psychologist who was a research assistant at the Institute for Computing and Information Sciences at Radboud University until August 2019. In preparation for this study, she received training in interviewing techniques. None of the women or partners were known to the interviewer prior to the interviews. The first author (MD) is a doctoral candidate at the Institute for Computing and Information Sciences at the Radboud University and graduated on the use of artificial intelligence to support the process of SDM. The second author (FO) is a community midwife and has been researching personalized maternity care over the past three years. MdV is a medical decision psychologist with an interest in SDM and birth trauma. MH and JvD are both obstetricians who work in the Radboud University Medical Centre and set up the designated clinic “Tailored Maternity Care” in 2015. They both advocate for SDM by conducting research about this topic and applying it in their daily clinical work. To minimize potential bias in interpretation of the results due to their affiliation with the studied clinic, these authors were not involved in data collection, formal data analysis or writing the original draft. All authors believe in the importance of RMC and SDM, which may have affected the results.

Study design

In this explorative single-centre qualitative study, a constructivist grounded theory approach, as suggested by Charmaz, was followed [31]. Grounded theory enables the discovery of new theories during the collection of qualitative data. A constructivist grounded theory approach emphasizes the subjective interrelationship between the researcher and the participant, and their co-construction of meaning [32]. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with women and their partners who visited the designated clinic in Nijmegen, the Netherlands. Interviews with their care providers were carried out simultaneously and have previously been published [19]. For this study, participants were purposively sampled from the population of women and partners who visited the designated clinic multiple times during their pregnancy (see Table S1 in the supplementary files). The information of visitors is stored with their permission by MH. This information was used to contact potential participants by telephone or email. All women who were approached agreed to participate. Some included their partners. All female participants gave birth at the Radboud University Medical Centre less than a year before the interviews were conducted and consented to their quotes being used in this article.

Data collection

Data was collected through in-depth semi-structured interviews conducted between May and August of 2019, after verbal and written consent was obtained. Before the interviews were conducted there had been e-mail contact with all participants to address issues regarding confidentiality and to explain the aim and methods of the study. The interviews took place at a location the participant preferred. A topic list was used to guarantee consistency between the interviews. The topics that were addressed in the interviews are shown in Table S2 of the supplementary files. On average, the interviews lasted between 30 and 90 min and were audio-recorded and transcribed into Dutch verbatim. Confidentiality and anonymity of participants during data collection, analysis and sharing of the results was maintained. The audio recordings and transcripts were encrypted and stored anonymously in a password protected university storage system. Quotes were translated from Dutch into English by the first author (FO). Data collection stopped after eight interviews. Data saturation was reached after six interviews as no new themes arose from the data.

Data analysis

Collected data was analysed through a constructivist grounded theory approach [31]. Once the interviews were transcribed, they were read several times and notes were taken for data familiarisation. All eight transcripts were coded by the first author (FO). The initial open coding was followed by axial coding and selective coding. Memos that clarified codes and relations between different codes facilitated the axial and selective coding process. Codes with similar patterns were grouped into themes and subthemes. To safeguard intercoder reliability, a second researcher (MD) randomly and independently coded four transcripts. Throughout the analysis, any discrepancies between MD and FO were discussed to ensure the reliability of coding. After reaching consensus, three core categories and one overarching theme were found. The code tree can be found in Table S3 of the supplementary files. The research team discussed which quotations expressed the subthemes best. Subsequently, MD and FO presented them in a qualitative descriptive manner. The analysis was performed using Atlas.ti software (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany).

Results

A total of six cases involving nine participants who received care at the clinic were included in this study. Interviews were either women-only (Nw = 5), partner-only (Np = 2) or couples (Nc = 1). Due to interviewee preference, one couple simultaneously participated in the interview and one woman was accompanied by her partner during her interview. In the latter case, the partner was there to support his wife and tried to take part as little as possible during her interview. Table S1 in the supplementary files provides a summary of the participants, their care requests before visiting the clinic, the final birth plan, and the medical outcomes. The quotes that are used to illustrate the findings are labelled with either ‘W’ or ‘P’ to represent respectively women or their partners.

Three major themes emerged from the interviews: “An unexpected positive experience”, “Mutual effort and flexibility” and “Together but on my terms”. One overarching theme was identified: “A healing and empowering experience”. Development of the themes and overarching theme can be found in Table S3 of the supplementary files.

An unexpected positive experience

Most participants who were interviewed visited the clinic because they wanted their upcoming childbirth experience to be different from their previous traumatic birth experience. Because of their past, they felt that they needed to be firm about their birth wishes in their current pregnancy to make sure that they would be respected by their care provider.

“I thought that it was necessary to explicitly clarify what I wanted and what I didn´t, because I knew I wasn´t able to fight for my wishes during my delivery. I mean, you can try to scream, you can say ‘no’ to procedures. But if your care provider still proceeds despite your objections, you´re powerless. My worst nightmare was that this would happen again.” (W1).

“After the experience with our first, I had learned that I have to firmly assert these [our wishes], because they [the hospital] disregarded these the first time. And that made me think, ‘that’s not going to happen again, we have to be clear what our wishes are and how we see things’, and then we will see what happens and how much space there actually is. And she [gynaecologist 1] was very clear about that. Yes, she was willing to give us that space. We could make use of it.” (P3).

For all participants, the interactions with the obstetricians at the designated clinic were different and better than expected. In contrast to the care providers they had met in the past, women encountered open-minded and empathic care providers at the designated clinic. Furthermore, they perceived that their ideas about the hospital were nothing like reality this time.

“I was really prejudiced… I did not like the idea of giving birth in the hospital (…) I was surprised that there were possibilities, that they (care providers at the clinic) thought along with me. In the hospital they listened to me, and I have never had the idea that I was judged about my ideas, ideas that were deviant from their protocols. This was a real eye-opener.” (W1).

Mutual effort and flexibility

The open-minded attitude of the clinicians at the clinic was something new for many of the particpants. They reported that there was room for their fears, wishes and questions to be discussed, without any predetermined judgment. Participants found it important for care providers to be curious and ask questions, to build an understanding of why a woman has certain wishes. For many participants, this also opened a door to listen to the clinicians’ wishes and preferences.

“I really wanted to know what my care provider thought of the situation. (…) What her opinion and estimation was. I wanted to give her space as well, because she took my wishes seriously and gave me space to make my own decisions. You’re the professional, I’m the mother, it’s my body so I decide in the end, but I want to know about my potential risks.” (W5).

“It may very well be possible that that played a role, that so much mutual trust had been created, that following the advice became easier.” (P3).

Participants stated that after several conversations at the clinic, they had a better understanding of the care providers’ perspective. They described to be more willing to consider certain care recommendations to meet the care provider’s wishes as well once the relationship of trust between their care provider and them had started to develop.

“I think they really made an effort in convincing her [partner] that she had freedom of choice, but within certain boundaries of medically responsible actions. And that is also important to indicate, like I said before, to argue why certain things are impossible, and why other things are [possible]… Yes, maybe that is… I like to know why something is advised and if I get a good understanding of it I am more willing to accept it.” (P1).

The willingness to receive and act upon the care provider’s information did not merely originate from the idea of reciprocity. Participants trusted that the provider would only recommend a procedure if it was the best fit for the situation. It was perceived that care providers were sincerely concerned about the woman’s and her child’s wellbeing and they presented their care recommendations as an expert on the matter instead of a representative of the protocol.

“I noticed that my obstetrician was really straightforward when it came to discussing their concerns and ideas, like ‘this is my concern, I worry about this, this is really important’. This concern came from them as a person (…). They communicated it as a worry they had as a professional, without being too scientific or imposing facts on us.” (W6).”

The participants eventually fully supported their birth plan, though they sometimes had made some concessions in comparison to their initial birth plan. However, even though participants became more flexible in their demands regarding their birth plan, initial wishes were not always discarded. Not fulfilling their initial wishes was sometimes even mourned. For example, one participant explained how she felt in conflict with her final birth plan. After having had several conversations at the clinic and being advised to birth at the hospital, an informed decision to birth at the hospital was made. Nevertheless, the feeling of wanting to birth at home remained strong.

“Nothing changed about my wish. My wish was still to birth at home (…). But you know, everyone advised me to birth in the hospital. This made me feel like I had to. It felt really irresponsible to birth at home when everybody told me not to. Even though the chance was really high that everything would go well at home.” (W4).

Looking back on the consultations at the clinic, parents concluded that being open to have a dialogue with your care provider about the different perspectives is important in making a well-informed decision. The positive interactions were a starting point in regaining trust in care providers.

“In the end, you’re not as far apart from each other as you think. A hospital birth was unthinkable to me, but it was better than expected. By communicating, discussing and learning about each other’s frame of reference… Clients know so little about the medical world, causing them to have little knowledge about why certain procedures or interventions are preferable. Informing women is really important.” (W1).

Together but on my terms

The second and third theme are closely linked, but not identical. The third theme describes how women and their partners who participate in this study distinguish autonomy and control as a fundamental condition for a safe childbirth, and to what extent they are willing to hand over control to the care provider, based on mutual trust and respect.

Birth plan: on my terms

The interviews revealed an overarching urge among the participants to experience (the journey towards) their birth on their own terms.

“Being taken seriously… That is incredibly important during birth (…) Childbirth is not a medical experience, it’s something you only do one/two/three times in your life. You’ll keep remembering it for the rest of your life. I really wanted to do it on my own terms.” (W4).

The participants stated that they wanted to be in touch with and listen to their own body during birth. In addition, women and their partners repeatedly mentioned that they wanted to have no interference as long as there were no signs of impending complications. Women in this study expressed that they had great trust in their body’s ability to birth healthily and estimated the chance of their body not meeting this expectation as very unlikely. Participants were open to consider care recommendations regarding interventions from a care provider as long as the medical reasoning behind the suggestion was shared.

“For us it was clear that we want to do it by ourselves and we only need medical staff if complications occur. That is the moment that we can’t do it by ourselves anymore and we need your expertise which we did not study for.” (W6).

“If something was not possible, it was well-argued and clearly explained, so it was never like ‘You are in the hospital, so we decide how this will go’, and I think that is actually really good.” (P1).

Before visiting the designated clinic, most participants experienced a dilemma regarding the place to give birth. In their eyes, a home birth was the perfect place to give birth because of its intimate character and the idea of ultimate autonomy. However, it was not perceived as a good option considering their obstetric history (for instance VBAC). Most women did not feel that hospital birth was a good option either. They learnt from previous experience that giving birth in the hospital involves medicalization, being attended by too many and unknown care providers and little attention for personal values and needs. Participants explained to feel more confident and safer with their own known community midwife. At the clinic, the option of giving birth with their own community midwife was presented to the women, despite protocol recommendations stating that in case of a history of one or more caesarean sections, intrapartum care is solely given by obstetricians or hospital-based midwives. This suggestion made the hospital an option again.

“The other possibility was to birth at home or birthing unassisted, but that’s definitely not something I prefer. From the beginning, I knew I did not want to birth at home, but I also knew I didn’t want to birth at the hospital. I thought those were the only options and eventually my midwife referred me to the clinic. (…) I did not want to take risks, but to birth at the hospital with hospital staff is also risky to me because then I’m at risk of interventions. We figured out a perfect solution, having a hospital birth with my own community midwife.” (W1).

Most participants preferred a home birth after their first negative birth experience. They described how they anticipated to feel safe and relaxed in the comfortable surroundings of their own home, as opposed to the hospital where they perceived that intimacy, comfort and privacy are lacking. Eventually, every woman in this study chose a second hospital delivery, but for all of them, this childbirth experience was very different from their first. One important difference was the preparation for birth. Women prepared for their second birth by asserting their needs, fears, wishes in a very precise way and identified the features that contributed to their first negative experience. Primarily, this helped their care providers to adjust their communication and care to the client, but it also portrayed what the environment in a delivery room should be like in order for the woman to feel as comfortable as possible. Eventually, women were surprised to find out that what they were looking for was possible at the hospital after all.

“I pursued the romantic picture of birth: birthing is natural, I don’t need anybody but myself, I need a relaxed atmosphere in order to avoid adrenaline release in my body, and things like that. My expectation was that those things are not possible in the hospital. My expectation turned out to be wrong.” (W1).

The second important difference is that women experienced that they had given birth on their own terms. This is elaborated on in the following subtheme.

Birth: on my terms

After their first hospital delivery, participants lost their trust in maternity care providers. This was caused by experienced disrespect of care providers towards their personal boundaries. In their second pregnancy, the participants regained trust in their care providers through being reassured that their personal boundaries would be respected at all times during delivery. No procedure would be carried out without consent.

“The longer ago it [the previous birth] was and the more we reflected on it, the more we realised that actually many things had happened that were not discussed with us (…) And here at the clinic, warm and caring care providers… Not once did we feel pressured.” (P2).

“During my second delivery care providers really listened to me. They respected my boundaries. At a certain moment, I had to go with my legs in the stirrups again. I didn’t want that because it evoked negative memories. Then they (care provider) said ‘OK we won’t do it then’.” (W2).

Because of the trusting relationship that had been developed during the prenatal consultations at the clinic, women believed that their care provider would only intervene during delivery if they had a valid reason to do so.

“I let go because I truly believed that if they said ‘I want to give you the oxytocin injection because I think in this situation, it is important’, I trusted that it was medically necessary and that they weren’t planning on giving the injection because they wanted to adhere to the protocol.” (W3).

By engaging in SDM and deliberating in a structured way about possibilities, values and intentions, participants were stimulated by their care provider to take on a pro-active role in weighing risks and choosing for or against certain care recommendations during delivery. This gave women a sense of peace and control.

Because of my past, I became really ‘anti-hospital’. I only wanted to give birth in my own way and you (care providers) are not allowed to get involved in any way. (…) My obstetrician explained that everything is possible but they wanted to make me aware of the risks that were involved if you birth without fetal monitoring. Eventually I got the feeling that I made an informed choice about this, without being forced by anyone.” (W5).

This SDM process was an ongoing process that does not only take place during the consultations at the clinic, but also during birth. One participant explained how she viewed complications during her birth as unavoidable and not traumatic because she felt supported and included by care providers:

“To a certain extent I would have liked the outcome to be different and not with my legs in the stirrups with a vacuum extractor, but in a bath or on the birthing stool. But I feel that we did everything to achieve my ideal birth. During the delivery they gave me sufficient space to do it in my own way. (…) Things did not go as planned, but everything was discussed with me. Eventually, I felt that we couldn’t go on like this, she (the baby) had to come out and if I wanted to avoid serious problems, the use of the vacuum was unavoidable. Nevertheless, I had the feeling that I was involved in making that decision. It’s not something that happened to me, but something I was part of. That’s what matters.” (W4).

Overarching theme: a healing and empowering experience

The participants in this study described their care trajectory at the clinic in Nijmegen and their childbirth experience as healing and empowering experiences because they gave birth and felt that this time they were in charge, without having the process being taken over by others.

“To me, a good birth is a birth where you have the feeling that you are not overruled. That you make the decisions yourself, that you are able to do it in your own way as much as possible. Interventions were performed, yes I did not want them, but I don’t look back on it with the feeling of being coerced or overruled. I look back on it and think, we went in a certain direction and I had an active role in doing so." (W4)

Women and their partners described how important it was for them to reclaim control over the process of giving birth. To them, being in control involved experiencing complete autonomy in all choices surrounding birth and the ability to weigh medical risks yourself. They wanted their caregivers to provide them with a recommendation, to jointly discuss the reasons behind it and to support them in making decisions for or against certain medical care.

“Protocols are important, but you can deviate from them and every situation is different. It is important to think about the client in making medical decisions. You become, together with your care provider, owner of the decision-making process. (…) I gained more self-confidence because of the whole care process at the clinic.” (W6).

“Such a protocol, it is nice and clear for everyone, but it may not be necessary for some people. We did not like the approach of ‘let’s do it because the protocol says so’. Then we realised that you can simply deviate from the protocol… After all, it’s your child and your birth.” (P2).

Participants shared that the type of care that the clinic facilitated made their birth an empowering experience. By having open and honest conversations with and being taken seriously by the care providers at the clinic, the hospital became an option again. Then, during their hospital birth, the participants experienced the respectful care and flexibility as was discussed in their consultations at the clinic: their autonomy was respected at all times and care providers were sensitive to their personal needs and preferences. This experience strengthened the participants’ confidence, which reinforced their trust in the hospital.

“I don’t know much about medicine, but I do know how protocols work, so I can imagine that you’re not supposed to go around them without reason. If you however still make an effort because you think it could positively contribute to someone’s experience and therefore the outcome, then I think it’s great that you are willing to go for that. I’m glad such people exist.” (P1).

“Thanks to my obstetricians, my trust in care providers is back. I don’t feel nervous anymore entering the hospital where I had my first birth (…) Now I think: I just met the wrong person back then, I don’t have to be afraid that it happens again.” (W3).

Being able to birth without interference and allowing birth to take it’s natural course, fostered their self-confidence and sense of self-esteem.

“For me, this was a good birth because I stayed in charge, I felt powerful. No one overruled me, I was supported instead of belittled. If certain procedures were necessary which I might not like, they explained the importance to me and asked me how I felt about them.” (W5).

Discussion

This article aimed to elucidate women’s and their partners’ experiences with the designated clinic “Tailored Maternity Care” in the Netherlands, and how the clinic’s approach resulted in a positive choice made by women to birth at the hospital. Three core categories were identified: “An unexpected positive experience”, “Mutual effort and flexibility” and “Together but on my terms”. “A healing and empowering experience” surfaced as overarching concept throughout the parents’ journey. The major findings of this study will be discussed in a structured form, following the three themes and the overarched theme that were identified. Thereafter, strengths and limitations of this study are discussed and finally, implications for practice are shared.

Making sure this birth will be different

The previous experience of participants in this study with giving birth had an impact on how they felt in this pregnancy. Most participants had a strong aversion towards the hospital and hospital care. In fact, the hospital had become a traumatising place. Negative or traumatic birth experiences have shown to have a negative effect on the transition to parenthood and are associated with decisions to avoid contact with the healthcare system in future pregnancies [20]. It is estimated that 9% to 44% of women experience giving birth as traumatic [33]. Factors that contribute to this experienced trauma during birth are type of birth, complications and/or interventions during the process, perceived threat, dissociation or poor support by care providers [33, 34]. The interviews show that most participants felt that they wanted to do anything they could to avoid repeat of the trauma. Educating themselves extensively about possible interventions and procedures during birth and planning to decline specific unwanted interventions or procedures was their way to reclaim control over their birthing experience and ensure that this birth was “not like last time”. This corresponds with findings by Niles et al. (2021) and Yuill et al. (2020) who observed that women consider knowledge as an empowering means to remain rooted in their health decisions [21, 35]. Similarly, several studies found that women armed themselves with a huge amount of information and felt that they had to be firm about their wishes. They approached prenatal appointments as if each might be a battle to assert their rights [10, 22, 36].

The search for a leading role: shared decision making

The clinic focuses on SDM and its aim is to identify and display care scenarios and to jointly explore what feels best for the woman instead of merely telling her what is best (without taking her personal preferences and context into account). The results of this study show that women experienced a regained control over their pregnancy and birth because they made autonomous decisions regarding their health and were stimulated and supported in doing so. In maternity care, there is a growing interest in SDM from client advocacy and professional societies, the government and healthcare insurers. Recently, in the Netherlands, the law on the medical treatment agreement was updated. In this update, the requirement of ‘informed consent’ has been adjusted to SDM as a prerequisite for client-care provider encounters [24]. This adjustment entails that the care provider’s role is not merely informing their client, but also to acquire information from the client such as their needs or wishes, with the aim of integrating these in medical decision-making. In SDM, care providers ought to take full responsibility for providing women with understandable, good quality information to support clients in weighing different care options. Moreover, the care provider should encourage clients to express their needs and to make health decisions that are right for them [25, 26]. In the current study and previous study about the clinic, we found that approaching a woman’s birth in accordance with the principles of SDM results in more nuanced birth plans. This makes it less likely for discussions or conflicts to arise during birth. However, various barriers have been identified that inhibit the implementation of SDM and therefore do not result in the intended patient-participation in the process [37]. SDM refers to a shared process towards the final decision and does not imply a shared say in the final decision. The woman and her partner decide, after weighing all the care options, which treatment to choose. This may result in choosing less care than recommended, or no care at all [25, 38].

In the previous study of this clinic, maternity care providers stated that guaranteeing women’s autonomy is the most important goal of working with and at the clinic [19]. In the current study, trust in the care system was shown to be rebuilt, mainly because the care providers at the clinic explicitly emphasized the woman’s right to refuse care at all times. Women reported that, by knowing this, they could completely focus on giving birth instead of constantly guarding their boundaries. This contributed to their positive birth experience. By experiencing shared decision making and true autonomy at the clinic, women and their partners felt in control of their own pregnancy and birth.

The hospital becomes an option again

Literature shows that if recommended care is declined, tension and strife between women and their care providers may ensue [4, 20–22]. This can result in mistrust in the healthcare system and therefore making the decision to reject any type of care, for example by birthing unassisted. Feeley and Thomson (2016) discovered that conflict between a woman and her care provider appeared to be an important factor in the choice to birth outside the system. Holten et al. (2018) conducted an in-depth analysis of cases where women chose a home birth in a high-risk pregnancy or an unassisted childbirth, with the aim to determine if there was a pattern, through which the decision to go against medical advice was reached. For most participants in that study, the patterns involved a trajectory of preceding trauma, self-education, and conflict over the birth plan. That conflict led to a negative choice for a birth outside of the system, where the hospital was no longer perceived as a safe option [22]. Like many of the women interviewed in our study, the women interviewed in the study reported by Holten et al. (2018) had developed an aversion to hospitals and medical staff, as a consequence of a previous traumatic birth experience. They had experienced that their care provider was not open to their wishes and did not show respect for their autonomy during consultations. Those interactions made them feel that the risks of giving birth in the hospital outweighed the risks of a home birth. These patterns are also seen in other research about care outside the guidelines.

Overall, women in this study highlighted that they want what is best for them and their baby. They want a safe birth and are open to medical assistance if complications arise. Parents expect their care provider to explain why something is recommended, for them to understand its importance and consequently make a well-informed decision about whether or not to follow the provider’s advice. Incorporating these elements in prenatal consultations, as the designated clinic does, enables women to regain trust in the care system and refrains them from making a negative choice. The results of Van der Garde and colleagues (2019) show that for all parents who have received care at the clinic, the hospital became an option again and only positive choices for place of birth were made [27]. These experiences are in contrast to the study of Holten et al. (2018) where women felt that the hospital is a place where true autonomy is not possible and therefore chose to birth outside the maternity care system. The women in this study, who entered the consultations with the same aversion and with similar preferences as the women in the study by Holten et al., considered the hospital to be an option again after discussing their wishes at the designated clinic.

Preventing negative choices and fostering empowerment

The results of this study correspond with the global development that women increasingly desire agency and control over giving birth and the care they receive while giving birth [21]. Opting for a home birth in a country where home birth is not well integrated in the maternity care system and birthing without medical assistance (freebirthing) are examples of this development [10, 11, 16, 39]. According to some researchers, these developments are associated with the idea that the current maternity care services do not adequately meet the needs of women [10, 12, 16, 28, 40]. In our study, participants often mentioned the need for control, which is in line with what participants in other studies sought [10–12, 14, 18]. Having a sense of control is related to a positive and satisfying birth experience [10, 41]. Seeking full control over the nature of childbirth might not be achievable, whereas ‘external control’, that implies having access to information, having a leading role in decision-making and actively addressing what is required for you to feel safe during birth, can be endeavoured [10, 42]. In the context of a subsequent pregnancy after a traumatic birth, factors that are of importance to women to experience healing and a positive subsequent birth were explored in a recent scoping review by Pidd et al. (2023). The overview of results in this review shows similar findings to our study. The included studies revealed themes such as choice and control, support, trust, communication, information, acknowledgement and understanding to be important key factors [43].

These aspects are being achieved in various ways: choice and control is often being illustrated by seeking control in requesting or even demanding certain procedures without medical indication (i.e., caesarean on maternal request or freebirthing) and by being supported by a known midwife or doula during a subsequent birth to help women feel more empowered [36, 44, 45]. Rebuilding trust is often reported to be achieved by requesting continuity of care with known carers and by receiving sensitive and flexible individualized care that is needs-based [36, 45–47]. A factor that is being mentioned in various studies but not explicitly in ours is the wish of women to be acknowledged and understood in her prior traumatic experience [45]. Lastly, not always do women report to have healed from their previous experience; sometimes, they report to just want to have a ‘better experience than last time’. However, some women, especially those achieving a vaginal birth after a caesarean, mentioned to have healed from their previous traumatic birth and to have felt a sense of pride in what they had achieved [44]. In addition, the themes that were found in the qualitative case study by Horan et al. (2023) express similar needs as the women in our study. The study explored healing birth trauma by acting according to the principles of trauma-informed care - a framework for care. In this context specifically, the themes that were found to be most important to achieve healing and empowerment were to establish a therapeutic patient-provider relationship, to prioritize communication, respect and knowledge in person-centered care, and finally engaging in Shared Decision Making. The study participant indicated that the trusting relationship with her physician was central to her physical and emotional healing, as well as being able to have a voice in her subsequent birth, by sharing ideas during birth in terms of birth position, and the team respecting her voice and input as well [48].

Increasingly, women use a birth plan, where they outline their intentions for their upcoming birth [49]. However, these outlines might not be supported by their care providers, resulting in tension and even conflict between patient and care provider. Prior research made several recommendations on how to prevent conflict in situations where women and care provider disagree on the birth plan [20, 21]. For example, conflict can be prevented by providing high-quality evidence and discussing options ahead of time [20]. Furthermore, presenting procedures or interventions as options rather than mandatory practices can make clients feel more empowered and pave the way for a mutually respectful and constructive SDM process [21]. In general, it seems of vital importance to explicitly reassure women that they are autonomous during birth and can decline any measure or intervention if they prefer to. Women in this study have made several statements about their experience in the designated clinic that show that the consultations are in line with these recommendations.

In the paper about the perspective of care providers affiliated with the clinic described here, which was published in 2021, care providers explained how listening, investing time, pursuing continuity of care provider and showing flexibility in exploring alternative care options are essential in RMC [19]. The participants of the current study acknowledged that this is what they need from their care provider to trust them, and that they valued these needs being met at the clinic.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is that this is the first study to explore the perspectives of women and their partners who visited a designated clinic for tailored maternity care. The interprofessional research team is also considered as a strength of this study, providing different perspectives on the results and the subject matter of this study. One of the limitations of this study is the representativeness of the women and partners in this study. Only a small number of women who decline care recommendations are referred to a designated clinic. Most women address their preferences in regular consultations with a care provider they are assigned to (such as a primary care midwife in a low-risk pregnancy), and find an appropriate comprimise for their wishes. Therefore, not many women declining care recommendations are referred to a clinic such as this one. In addition, the small sample size of this study is a limitation. However, data saturation was reached and additional interviews were conducted after reaching data saturation but no new themes occurred. Finally, two of the co-authors of this study work at the clinic, and their experience may therefore have influenced the interpretation of the results of this study. However, their personal experience and specialisation in this topic may also enable them to place the results in the correct context.

Implications for practice

Despite the current efforts in maternity care to engage in RMC and SDM, some pregnant women experience opposition in the care system when they share their preferences and needs and do not feel heard. It is important to not abandon these women to their fate of a high risk homebirth, but to work with them and to be willing to make concessions to protocolised care, in order to prevent a negative birthing experience or a negative choice to birth outside the hospital from happening. The results of this paper show that the approach of the designated clinic can be successful in meeting parents’ needs such as trust and autonomy during pregnancy and birth but also in preventing women from making negative choices to birth outside the hospital. Care should however be taken that women don’t feel forced or constrained in their autonomy. The care providers at the clinic succeeded in regaining women’s trust in the care system by listening, getting an understanding of the woman’s perspective and recognising women’s autonomy and their right to decline care. Moreover, care providers took responsibility for guiding women in making well-informed decisions by discussing evidence-based protocols in a respectful way and by explicitly noting that ultimately, the woman decides whether or not to consent to recommended care options. Care providers in general could take on a more flexible attitude by reconsidering everyday interventions and by being (more) open to adjust their care to a woman’s individual situation. Being aware of parents’ responsibility for their own decisions once they have been informed correctly is a necessary condition for being able to deviate from protocol. Centralising care outside the guidelines in designated clinics is not the preferable solution if care providers want to enable every pregnant woman to take ownership of her own perinatal care, as it will inherently limit access to this form of personalized care. On the other hand, realising such designated clinics as part of every care facility asks for changes in agreements and responsibilities within care facilities. We advocate to educate professionals in the approach that is being performed in this clinic, to ensure that every woman receives respectful maternity care and is being heard and empowered throughout their pregnancy and childbirth trajectory. Then, clinics like these are no longer needed. Not incorporating these consult strategies in regular care will only result in friction at a later stage of pregnancy or birth, and consequently in negative choices and traumatic birth experiences. The investment in training and adjustments will take time initially, but it will save time in the end.

In line with our findings, existing research shows that women clearly value certain aspects traditionally associated with home births, such as privacy, intimacy, the ability to move around and the ability to create an optimal environment, as they experience it, to give birth in [11, 18]. By offering variation in birthing options in a hospital setting, such as water births, wireless waterproof fetal monitoring and alternative birthing positions, the hospital becomes a place where women are enabled to birth in the way they perceive is best for them and their baby.

Conclusions

This paper reports the experiences of six women and three partners who received care at the “Tailored Maternity Care” clinic in Nijmegen, the Netherlands. For most participants in this study, their journey started with a previous traumatic birth experience, after which they decided they needed to reclaim their autonomy by arming themselves with a birth plan that did not align with standard care recommendations. During the consultations at the clinic, the woman, her partner, and the obstetrician exchanged views on the care recommendations and the woman’s request. Gradually, they agreed upon a birth plan that was acceptable to everyone involved and no negative choices to birth outside the hospital were made. The next childbirth experience of the participants of this study took place in a setting where communication was attuned to the woman and her partner and where personal boundaries were respected. Interacting with care providers and giving birth in the hospital after visiting the designated clinic became a healing and empowering experience for them and contributed to rebuilding their trust in the (maternity) health care system. These conclusions add to the existing knowledge of parents’ experiences with RMC and contribute to information about implementation of RMC and SDM, and its importance.
