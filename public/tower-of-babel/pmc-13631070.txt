Uncovering the Genetic Landscape of Pediatric Hearing Loss Along the Texas–Mexico Border
Authors: Desiree Lanehart, Michelle Gray, Roberta Sierra, Camille Maldonado, Daniel Brooks, Hongzheng Dai, Aaly Rahimtoola, Sandy Magallan, Sarah Rodriguez, Jose Gamez, Pattie Rosenlund, Surya N. Mulukutla, Alberto Allegre, Lori Berry, Brendan Lee, Daryl A. Scott, Blake Vuocolo, Seema R. Lalani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChildren in Health Professional Shortage Areas (HPSAs), including the US border regions, experience delays in the diagnosis of hereditary hearing loss (HL), driven primarily by limited access to specialized genetic services. Although approximately 60% of congenital HL has a genetic etiology, many affected children in these communities remain undiagnosed, delaying timely intervention. Project GIVE is an NIH‐funded virtual genomics program that expands access to genome sequencing (GS) for children with undiagnosed multisystemic conditions along the Texas–Mexico border in the Rio Grande Valley (RGV) and El Paso regions of Texas, USA. Children (0–18 years) with suspected rare diseases were referred by regional healthcare professionals through a virtual portal, Consultagene, and underwent comprehensive virtual clinical genetics evaluation and trio GS. Among 23 Hispanic/Latino children evaluated for HL, 16 (~70%) received a molecular diagnosis. Of these, 56% had changes to medical management. Our findings demonstrate that high diagnostic yield for pediatric HL can be achieved in under‐resourced populations when genomic testing barriers are addressed. As targeted therapies for hereditary HL emerge, broader implementation of comprehensive genetic testing in HPSAs is crucial to ensure timely medical interventions.

Introduction

Hearing loss (HL) affects approximately one to three per 1000 newborns and represents one of the most common congenital sensory impairments [1]. In developed countries, approximately 66% of prelingual HL cases are attributable to genetic causes, while the remainder arise from environmental exposures or gene–environment interactions [1]. Hereditary HL is broadly classified into syndromic and nonsyndromic forms. Nonsyndromic HL accounts for roughly 70% of hereditary cases and typically involves isolated auditory impairment. Over 150 causative genes have been implicated in hereditary nonsyndromic HL encompassing autosomal recessive (AR), autosomal dominant (AD), X‐linked, and mitochondrial inheritance patterns [1, 2]. The remaining 30% of hereditary cases are syndromic, associated with more than 600 multisystem disorders such as Waardenburg, Pendred, and Usher syndromes [3]. Because extra‐auditory features may be subtle or delayed in presentation, syndromic forms of HL can be difficult to recognize clinically [3, 4].

When paired with genetic counseling, molecular testing can inform prognosis, guide medical management and cascade testing recommendations, and clarify recurrence risk for future pregnancies. Elucidating a genetic diagnosis early can also inform appropriate time‐sensitive interventions, such as cochlear implantation [5] and emerging gene‐targeted therapies; crucially, delays in administering such interventions can influence speech, language, and neurodevelopmental outcomes [6]. Accordingly, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMGG) recommends that all children with confirmed HL undergo a comprehensive clinical genetics evaluation [7]. The widespread implementation of next‐generation sequencing, including targeted gene panels and exome (ES) or genome sequencing (GS), has transformed the diagnostic landscape of hereditary HL, with reported diagnostic yields ranging from 30% to 48% depending on cohort and methodology [4, 7, 8, 9, 10], although substantially higher rates have been reported in select populations.

Despite advances in next‐generation sequencing, diagnostic yields remain disproportionate among pediatric HL populations in the United States [11]. While similar rates of HL are observed across populations, underrepresentation of certain ancestral groups in genomic reference databases may complicate variant interpretation and lead to differences in diagnostic yield [8, 11]. Additionally, structural barriers to accessing genomic care, including limited access to local clinical genetics services and geographical distance from specialized genomic centers [12], can delay receiving a molecular diagnosis. Children in HPSAs, including predominantly Hispanic/Latino populations living along the US‐Mexico border [13], are particularly susceptible to such barriers. The landscape of genetic determinants of HL in border communities remains underexplored despite the ongoing advances in genomic medicine, potentially delaying diagnosis and access to disease‐specific treatments.

In 2022, Project GIVE (Genetic Inclusion by Virtual Evaluation), an NIH‐funded initiative at Baylor College of Medicine, implemented a virtual genomics service to provide GS and clinical evaluation to children with suspected rare diseases along the Texas–Mexico border in the RGV and El Paso regions [14]. To date, over 200 pediatric patients with heterogeneous phenotypes have undergone GS testing through Project GIVE; ~10% of these patients had HL. Here, we examine the genetic etiologies, diagnostic yield, and clinical impact of GS in 23 children with isolated and syndromic HL. Findings will not only enhance understanding of hereditary HL in Hispanic/Latino communities at the Texas–Mexico border but also highlight implementation and testing strategies to improve access to genomic care in HPSAs.

Materials and Methods

Patient referral and recruitment processes in Project GIVE were previously described in detail [14]. Briefly, children aged 0–18 years residing in the RGV or El Paso in Texas who were suspected of having rare diseases were eligible for Project GIVE (ClinicalTrials.govNCT05318222). Referrals were submitted by regional healthcare professionals via the HIPAA‐compliant Consultagene portal (https://www.consultagene.org/). Between June 2023 and December 2025, Project GIVE evaluated a clinically heterogeneous cohort of 23 children with isolated or syndromic HL. The cohort included 12 males and 11 females aged 5 months to 18 years (median age = 9 years). All participants self‐identified as Hispanic/Latino with Mexican descent (Table 1). A comprehensive virtual clinical evaluation was completed, and GS was performed using buccal samples processed at CLIA‐certified laboratories (Baylor Genetics or GeneDx). Interpretation followed the ACMGG variant classification guidelines (Table S3). Additional methods can be found in the Supporting Information Material.

Results

Demographic information and audiologic characteristics of the pediatric participants in the HL cohort are described in Table 1. The cohort included nine children (39%) with isolated HL; of these, eight had SNHL and one family had auditory neuropathy. Fourteen children (61%) had syndromic HL characterized by additional extra‐audiologic manifestations including seizures, neurodevelopmental concerns, dysmorphic features, short stature, and/or congenital birth defects. Children with syndromic presentations had SNHL, conductive HL, or mixed HL.

Sixteen of 23 participants received a definitive diagnosis, achieving a ~70% diagnostic yield (Table 2). The median age of diagnosis was 2.5 years old (mean = 6.4 years, range = 4 months to 18 years). The average time from Consultagene referral to the return of results was 144 days (4.7 months). Molecular diagnoses included copy number variants (CNVs) in two subjects and single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) in 14 participants. AR conditions represented most of the diagnoses (8/16, 50%), with the remainder being AD (6/16, 37%) and X‐linked (2/16, 13%).

Eight of the 14 children with syndromic presentations (57%) received a diagnosis, including 16p11.2 deletion syndrome, CHARGE syndrome (CHD7), Ehlers–Danlos syndrome kyphoscoliotic type 2 (FKBP14), Kabuki syndrome (KMT2D; n = 2), Norrie disease (NDP), and CASK‐related disease. Notably, one child with multisystemic manifestations was found to have a likely pathogenic variant in PTPRQ, consistent with nonsyndromic HL. No additional variants were identified to explain his extra‐audiologic features.

Eight of the nine children with isolated HL (88%) received a diagnosis. Three had GJB2‐related HL, accounting for ~18% (3/16) of all molecularly diagnosed patients. Two had AR STRC‐related HL; one patient harbored a homozygous 15q15.3 deletion encompassing the STRC and CATSPER2 genes, which is associated with deafness and male infertility syndrome. Other isolated HL diagnoses included AR OTOF and MYO15A‐related HL and AD ACTG1‐related HL.

Nine of the 16 children who received a molecular diagnosis had changes to their medical management (56%), including recommendations for additional imaging studies/exams (4/9), subspecialist referral (2/7), specialized management considerations (e.g., interventions, medications) (3/9), and cascade testing of relatives (3/7) (Table S1). Recurrence risks and reproductive options were discussed with multiple families.

Seven of the 23 participants with HL (30%) remained undiagnosed (Table S2). A novel 0.27 Mb de novo 19p13.3 deletion (HG38: chr19:566635‐832069del) not previously linked to HL was classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

Discussion

Diagnostic Yield and Implications for Border Populations With Limited Access to Genomic Care

This study demonstrates a high diagnostic yield of GS for pediatric HL in the Hispanic/Latino population along the Texas–Mexico border. The ~70% diagnostic yield exceeds previous reports [9] and suggests that underdiagnosis in this population is driven primarily by limited access to genomic testing rather than a reduced genetic burden. This study advances the understanding of HL in this group and identifies pathogenic variants across a diverse set of genes. By integrating virtual genetics evaluation with GS, Project GIVE successfully delivered diagnostic genomic care to families living in HPSAs and substantially reduced the diagnostic odyssey for many participants.

Importantly, GS findings prompted meaningful changes in medical management for over half of diagnosed participants, and some participants were also identified as candidates for emerging gene therapy trials. Our findings support the importance of early, comprehensive genetic testing in pediatric HL evaluation, particularly as critical precision medicine approaches continue to evolve.

Genetic Etiologies Identified in the Cohort

Connexin‐related HL, the most common cause of nonsyndromic HL, was observed in our cohort with a prevalence of ~18%, which is consistent with reports from both European and Latin American populations [15, 16]. Notably, two participants (13%) had STRC‐related disease, the second most common cause of hereditary SNHL after GJB2‐related HL [17]. One of these individuals had a contiguous 15q15.3 deletion involving both STRC and CATSPER2, which had important counseling considerations regarding future reproductive implications associated with deafness‐infertility syndrome [17]. Detection of these CNVs highlights an important advantage of GS, as structural variants may be missed by more limited testing approaches.

The identification of actionable variants in genes like OTOF further illustrates the growing importance of precise molecular diagnosis of HL. Recent clinical trials of AAV‐mediated OTOF gene therapy show significant auditory recovery, reinforcing the value of early genetic diagnosis for therapeutic eligibility in addition to prognostic and counseling considerations [18]. Equitable access to genetic testing is essential to prevent existing gaps in healthcare from extending into precision medicine.

Furthermore, 8/16 (50%) of diagnosed patients were found to have syndromic forms of HL, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive genomic testing to detect multisystemic conditions that may not be apparent at the time of clinical evaluation.

Case‐Level Insights and Clinical Management Implications

Representative cases illustrate how access to GS informed family counseling and contributed to ongoing gene discovery efforts. Case 013 (16‐year‐old male, Table 2) was found to have a maternally inherited pathogenic variant in NDP, consistent with X‐linked Norrie disease. Genetic counseling emphasized the importance of carrier testing for his three unaffected sisters who were of reproductive age. Case 021 (12‐year‐old female, Table S2) was found to have a 0.27 Mb de novo 19p13.3 deletion encompassing 12 protein coding genes, including PTBP1. Pathogenic start‐loss and missense variants in this gene have been associated with Skeletal dysplasia, Tooth anomalies, and Developmental delay (STAD) syndrome [19]. While HL has not yet been described in STAD syndrome, RNA‐binding proteins, such as PTBP1, may play a role in hair cell formation and inner ear development [20]. Further studies are needed to determine if this gene is involved in auditory function.

In summary, this study demonstrates that high diagnostic rates for pediatric HL can be achieved in Hispanic/Latino populations when access to advanced genomic testing is available. By uncovering a wide range of syndromic and nonsyndromic etiologies, this work highlights genetic contributors that may be overlooked with limited testing strategies. As precision‐based treatments for hereditary HL continue to advance, expanding inclusive genomic programs will be crucial to ensure timely access to emerging interventions and to support broader implementation of genomic medicine.
