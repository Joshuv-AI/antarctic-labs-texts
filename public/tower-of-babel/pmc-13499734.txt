Cost-Effectiveness and Budget-Impact Analysis of Semaglutide in Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction and Obesity in the German Health-Care System
Authors: Bent Estler, Hanna Fröhlich, Tobias Täger, Norbert Frey, Lutz Frankenstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is common, debilitating, and has limited availability of disease-modifying therapies. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) such as semaglutide improved symptoms and weight in the Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity and HFpEF (STEP-HFpEF) trial but their economic value in HFpEF is unclear.MethodsWe developed a cohort state-transition (Markov) model based on STEP-HFpEF to evaluate semaglutide versus placebo in patients with HFpEF and obesity from the German statutory health insurance (SHI) perspective. Health states were defined by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire–Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ-CSS) quartiles and death. Outcomes included costs, quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). Deterministic, probabilistic, time-horizon, and structural KCCQ trajectory scenario analyses were performed. A 1-year prevalence-based budget impact analysis estimated additional SHI expenditure under alternative eligibility and uptake scenarios.ResultsOver 5 years, semaglutide increased total costs (2025 €) (17,690.5 € versus 12,748.6 €) and QALYs (3.371 versus 3.208), yielding an ICER of 30,443 €/QALY and a positive incremental net monetary benefit. Over 1 year, the ICER was 82,237 €/QALY. At the base-case price, the probability that semaglutide is cost-effective at 100,000 €/QALY was 0.911, rising with further price reductions. The 1-year budget impact ranged from approximately 168 million € to 864 million €, depending on eligibility and uptake.ConclusionsSemaglutide appears cost-effective for HFpEF with obesity under the base-case assumptions and over longer analytic horizons. Yet it generates substantial budget impact, indicating that affordability and pricing will be critical for its sustainable implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40273-026-01621-4.

Key Points

Semaglutide improved quality-adjusted outcomes in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and obesity, but at higher cost than placebo from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance.Cost effectiveness was unfavorable over 1 year but became more favorable over longer analytic horizons, highlighting the importance of long-term treatment effect assumptions.Despite potentially acceptable cost effectiveness, the estimated short-term budget impact of semaglutide is substantial, making affordability a key consideration for decision makers in Germany.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a highly prevalent and debilitating syndrome, accounting for approximately half of all heart failure cases and associated with substantial mortality, morbidity, and healthcare resource use [1, 2]. Patients with HFpEF frequently experience severe functional limitation, reduced exercise capacity, and impaired health-related quality of life, while effective disease-modifying treatment options remain limited compared with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [1]. In contemporary HFpEF cohorts, symptom burden and functional impairment are often assessed using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire–Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ-CSS), which has been shown to correlate strongly with prognosis, healthcare utilization, and patient-centered outcomes [3, 4]

Obesity is highly prevalent among patients with HFpEF and represents a key pathophysiological driver and therapeutic target in this population [5]. Excess adiposity contributes to hemodynamic congestion, systemic inflammation, impaired diastolic function, and cardiorespiratory limitation, and is consistently associated with worse symptoms, reduced exercise capacity, and poorer quality of life in HFpEF [5, 6]. Despite this, there are currently no widely implemented, evidence-based pharmacological strategies specifically targeting weight reduction and symptom improvement in patients with HFpEF and obesity. Standard heart failure therapies and lifestyle interventions often provide only modest improvements and are frequently limited by tolerability, adherence, or insufficient efficacy in achieving sustained weight loss [7].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) such as semaglutide have emerged as effective agents for inducing substantial and sustained weight loss in people with obesity, with additional benefits on cardiometabolic risk factors and quality of life [8]. In the STEP-HFpEF trial, once-weekly semaglutide significantly improved KCCQ-CSS, 6-min walk distance, and body weight compared with placebo in patients with HFpEF and obesity, demonstrating clinically meaningful gains in symptoms and physical limitations over 52 weeks [9]. These findings suggest that semaglutide may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for this high-risk population, with the potential to improve both patient-centered outcomes and longer-term prognosis. However, semaglutide is associated with substantial drug acquisition costs, and its use in HFpEF has not yet been comprehensively evaluated from an economic perspective.

In publicly financed healthcare systems such as the German statutory health insurance, decisions about the adoption of novel therapies require not only evidence of clinical efficacy, but also robust information on their economic value and affordability [10]. Cost-effectiveness analyses provide a framework to relate incremental costs to gains in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), but a therapy deemed cost-effective at a given willingness-to-pay threshold may still have considerable budgetary consequences when applied to a large eligible population [11]. With rising healthcare expenditure and increasing pressure on pharmaceutical budgets in Germany, understanding both the cost-effectiveness and the budget impact of high-cost, innovative treatments such as semaglutide is therefore crucial for sustainable resource allocation [11].

In this context, we aimed to evaluate the economic consequences of using semaglutide in patients with HFpEF and obesity in the German healthcare system. Specifically, we assessed the cost-effectiveness of semaglutide compared with placebo over different time horizons and explored the potential budget impact of its adoption from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance payer.

Methods

Model Overview

We developed a cohort state-transition (Markov) model to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of semaglutide compared with placebo in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and obesity, based on the STEP-HFpEF trial population [9]. The model was developed de novo for the present analysis, while prior health-economic models in HFpEF and HFrEF informed the general modelling framework [12, 13]. Disease severity was represented using Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire–Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ-CSS) quartiles, as KCCQ-CSS provides a prognostically relevant and patient-centered measure of health status that is more granular than the traditional New York Heart Association classification [3, 14, 15]. This approach is consistent with recent economic evaluations in both HFpEF and HFrEF that have used KCCQ-based quartile health states for decision modelling [12, 13]. Patients could transition between KCCQ quartiles and death at each cycle. The model used monthly cycles and a 5-year time horizon (60 cycles). The 5-year horizon was chosen as a pragmatic base-case horizon consistent with IQWiG methodological guidance [10], which emphasizes that the time horizon should be long enough to capture decision-relevant differences in costs and outcomes while limiting uncertainty from extrapolation beyond the observed trial period. Because STEP-HFpEF provided 52-week follow-up only, but semaglutide in HFpEF is expected to have cost and health consequences beyond 1 year in a chronic disease setting, a 5-year horizon was considered an appropriate compromise between relevance and extrapolation uncertainty. To assess the impact of this assumption, additional scenario analyses with 1-year and 10-year horizons were performed.

The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance payer, including only direct medical costs. Costs and health outcomes were discounted at 3% per annum in line with German Health Economic Guidelines [10]. Model outputs included total discounted costs, total discounted quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), incremental costs and QALYs, and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER; €/QALY).

All analyses, including model implementation, deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses, and generation of plots (cost-effectiveness plane, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves, tornado diagrams, time-in-state plots), were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Version R 4.4.1). All input parameters are displayed in Table 1. The model structure is shown in Fig. 1. The analysis was conducted under the guidance of the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS) [16].

Patients and Baseline Distribution

The target population reflected patients enrolled in STEP-HFpEF, which included adults with symptomatic HFpEF and obesity [9]. Key inclusion criteria comprised age ≥ 18 years, left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 45%, New York Heart Association class II–IV symptoms, body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2, KCCQ-CSS < 90 points, and 6-min walk distance ≥ 100 m, together with objective evidence of HFpEF such as elevated natriuretic peptides and echocardiographic abnormalities, elevated filling pressures, or a recent HF hospitalization. Major exclusion criteria included diabetes (or glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%), substantial recent weight change, and previous or planned bariatric surgery [9]. Baseline characteristics in STEP-HFpEF (median age 69 years, 56.1% women, median BMI ~37 kg/m2, median baseline KCCQ-CSS 58.9) were used to describe the modelled cohort [9].

For the Markov model, baseline health-state occupancy was defined using four KCCQ-CSS categories: Q1 (0–39), Q2 (40–54), Q3 (55–69), and Q4 (70–100). Because STEP-HFpEF did not report the proportion of participants within these four categories at baseline, the initial health-state distribution was reconstructed for model initialization rather than directly observed. Specifically, category midpoint values were assigned to each KCCQ-CSS state, and category probabilities were specified such that the weighted average reproduced the reported mean baseline KCCQ-CSS of the trial population. The plausibility of this reconstructed distribution was further assessed against the published subgroup analysis of STEP-HFpEF, which reported baseline KCCQ-CSS tertile cutoffs and tertile-specific central values, confirming that the reconstructed distribution was consistent with the overall pattern of baseline health-status impairment observed in the trial [15]. This approach yielded baseline proportions of 0.210, 0.236, 0.263, and 0.290 for Q1–Q4, respectively, which were applied identically to both treatment arms at model entry. In probabilistic sensitivity analysis, uncertainty in the initial health-state distribution was represented using a Dirichlet distribution centered on the base-case vector. As quartile-level baseline frequencies were not reported in STEP-HFpEF, this represented a structural approximation required for model initialization.

Clinical Input and Transition Probabilities

The primary driver of clinical benefit was the change in KCCQ-CSS observed in STEP-HFpEF. From baseline to week 52, mean KCCQ-CSS improved by 8.7 points in the placebo group and 16.6 points in the semaglutide group, yielding a between-group difference of 7.9 points [9]. We assumed a linear trajectory for mean KCCQ-CSS between baseline and 52 weeks in each arm and derived monthly target mean KCCQ-CSS values by linear interpolation. Beyond 12 months, we assumed that the treatment effect plateaued and mean KCCQ-CSS remained stable in both arms. This assumption was informed by longer-term semaglutide data in obesity without diabetes, in which the main between-group weight-loss effect was established during the first year and then largely stabilized over subsequent follow-up [17]. Given that a substantial part of the clinical benefit of semaglutide in HFpEF with obesity is likely mediated through weight loss, we considered a post-year-1 plateau to be a clinically informed extrapolation in the absence of HFpEF-specific long-term data.

To obtain KCCQ-quartile transition probabilities, we used an exponential tilting approach. At each monthly time point during the first year, the baseline KCCQ-quartile distribution was reweighted such that the resulting distribution matched the arm-specific target mean KCCQ-CSS for that month, using quartile-specific representative KCCQ values. The resulting monthly distributions were used to construct arm- and time-specific 4×4 transition matrices, where each row corresponded to the next-cycle distribution. From month 13 onwards, we applied a 4 × 4 identity matrix, implying no further transitions between KCCQ states and allowing only mortality to change cohort composition. This should not be interpreted as implying that HFpEF is clinically static but rather, it represents the simplifying assumption that the between-group difference observed at 12 months is maintained but does not widen further.

Mortality was modelled as background all-cause mortality without a direct treatment effect. Sex-specific remaining life expectancy at age 69 (14.86 years for men and 17.65 years for women) and the STEP-HFpEF sex distribution (56.1% women) were used to estimate a weighted remaining life expectancy of approximately 16.4 years. Assuming an exponential survival function, this corresponds to an annual mortality hazard of 0.0609, an annual death probability of approximately 5.9%, and a monthly death probability of 0.00506 applied equally to both arms. In each cycle, the 4 × 4 KCCQ transition matrix was embedded into a 5-state matrix by multiplying KCCQ transitions by (1 − p_death,month) and allocating p_death,month to the death state, which was absorbing.

Serious adverse events (SAEs) were modeled as pooled, all-cause transient events rather than as separate event-specific categories. This approach was chosen because STEP-HFpEF showed a clear between-group difference in the overall SAE rate, whereas most individual SAE categories were infrequent and were not intended to support adequately powered comparative estimation at the event-type level [9]. Accordingly, use of separate category-specific SAE parameters was considered likely to introduce unstable estimates and overinterpretation of sparse subgroup counts. The model therefore used the aggregate SAE incidence reported for each trial arm (23.4 versus 50.1 events per 100 person-years for semaglutide and placebo, respectively) as the most robust summary measure of SAE burden. This yielded monthly SAE probabilities of 0.0193 in the semaglutide arm and 0.0409 in the placebo arm. The expected number of SAEs per cycle was obtained by multiplying these probabilities by the number of patients alive at cycle start

Resource Utilization and Cost Data

The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance and included direct medical costs only. All costs were expressed in 2025 Euros. Background HFpEF management costs were implemented as a per-patient monthly cost of €99, applied equally to both arms, representing outpatient visits, chronic HF medication, and routine monitoring in a stable HFpEF population. This estimate was derived from the German ALDO-DHF trial (“Economic impact of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction: insights from the ALDO-DHF trial”) [18]. Because serious adverse events in STEP-HFpEF were reported in broad categories rather than as diagnosis-specific inpatient episodes, a direct event-level costing approach was not feasible. We therefore derived a pooled average German inpatient SAE cost by assigning DRG-based proxies to the main differential SAE categories, using F62C for cardiac disorders and E79C for respiratory/thoracic events, while infections/infestations were valued using the official average German inpatient hospital cost. These costs were weighted by their observed frequency in STEP-HFpEF and applied equally to both model arms. Using German 2025 DRG-based inpatient cost proxies and official German hospital cost statistics, this yielded a weighted mean pooled SAE cost of approximately €4014 per event [19].

Semaglutide acquisition cost was set at €255.81 per month in the base case, reflecting the list price for the type 2 diabetes indication in the German Rote Liste 18 after a fixed pharmacy rebate of €1.77 per month and an additional 7% mandatory discount. Because the aim of this analysis was to inform future reimbursement and pricing decisions in HFpEF with obesity, this adjusted price was used as the base-case monthly acquisition cost. Placebo acquisition cost was assumed to be zero, and drug costs were applied monthly over the entire 5-year model horizon. To align drug-cost accrual with treatment exposure observed in STEP-HFpEF, semaglutide costs in the base case were weighted by a time-varying on-treatment proportion reflecting approximately 16% discontinuation over 52 weeks, assuming a constant monthly discontinuation hazard during year 1 that was gradually tapered thereafter. To explore the potential impact of alternative reimbursement conditions, scenario and probabilistic analyses varied semaglutide cost using deterministic multipliers of 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2, corresponding to 80%, 50%, and 20% of the base-case monthly price.

Utility Data

Health-related quality of life was modelled as a function of KCCQ-CSS quartiles and expressed as EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) utilities. Utilities for each KCCQ-CSS quartile were derived using published mapping algorithms from KCCQ to EQ-5D [20, 21] applied to quartile midpoints, and were subsequently adjusted and cross-checked against trial-based utilities from the EMPEROR-Preserved economic evaluation to ensure that the resulting values were plausible and consistent with heart failure populations [12]. The German EQ-5D-5L value set [22] was used to reflect preferences in the German healthcare context. This approach represents a pragmatic approximation in the absence of directly observed German EQ-5D data for a STEP-HFpEF-like population.

To capture the short-term impact of serious adverse events, we applied a 1-month disutility of − 0.10 for patients experiencing an SAE, based on the incidence and severity of events reported in STEP-HFpEF [9]and consistent with recommendations for incorporating adverse events in economic evaluations [23].

Sensitivity Analyses

Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses were performed to explore the influence of individual parameters on cost-effectiveness results. Parameters varied included semaglutide monthly cost, routine HFpEF management cost, SAE cost, arm-specific SAE probabilities, background mortality, quartile-specific utilities, and SAE disutility. Low and high values were typically defined as ± 1 standard deviation (for parameters with an SD) or ± 20% of the base value (for costs without directly observed variability). Results were summarized using a tornado diagram showing the effect on incremental net monetary benefit at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €100,000/QALY, used for visualization rather than as an official German decision threshold.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) used 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations per semaglutide price scenario (base case, 80%, 50%, and 20% of base-case price). In each simulation, baseline KCCQ distribution (Dirichlet), event probabilities (beta), utilities (beta), costs (gamma), and SAE disutility (truncated normal) were jointly sampled. For each simulation and price scenario, the model produced total discounted costs and QALYs, incremental costs and QALYs, and incremental net monetary benefit (NMB) over a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds (0–150,000 €/QALY). These results were summarized in cost-effectiveness planes and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves over a wide range of willingness-to-pay values, and the probability that semaglutide is cost-effective at an illustrative threshold of €100,000/QALY was additionally reported for each price scenario. In addition, we performed an exploratory subgroup analysis to assess potential heterogeneity in cost-effectiveness according to baseline health status. Patients were stratified by baseline Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Clinical Summary Score (KCCQ-CSS) tertiles using published STEP-HFpEF subgroup data [15]. For each tertile, a simplified cohort state-transition model with monthly cycles over 5 years was run using tertile-specific changes in KCCQ-CSS from baseline to week 52 for semaglutide and placebo. Mean KCCQ-CSS was assumed to improve linearly during the first 12 months and to plateau thereafter. Because the prespecified STEP-HFpEF subgroup analysis did not demonstrate statistically significant interaction across baseline KCCQ-CSS tertiles, this analysis was considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Finally, to assess structural uncertainty in long-term KCCQ extrapolation, we conducted two additional deterministic scenario analyses. In an optimistic KCCQ scenario, the semaglutide benefit achieved at 1 year was maintained while placebo gradually returned toward baseline KCCQ-CSS by year 5. In a pessimistic KCCQ scenario, placebo remained stable after year 1 whereas the incremental semaglutide KCCQ benefit waned linearly to zero by year 5.

Model Validation

We assessed model validity in several steps. First, internal validity was evaluated by checking whether the model reproduced key STEP-HFpEF outcomes. Specifically, we compared model-predicted mean changes in KCCQ-CSS and the distribution of patients across KCCQ-CSS quartiles at 12 months in both arms with the observed trial data and iteratively refined the transition structure within clinically plausible bounds until close agreement was achieved. Full cycle-specific base-case Markov traces for both treatment arms are provided in Supplementary Table 7.

Second, clinical and health economic experts in HFpEF reviewed the model structure, assumptions on KCCQ trajectories, serious adverse events, mortality, and the implementation of costs and utilities for face validity and methodological consistency. Finally, we assessed external validity by comparing the magnitude of survival, quality-of-life, and cost patterns generated by the model with published HFpEF and heart failure economic evaluations in comparable populations, and no major inconsistencies were identified.

Budget Impact Analysis

For the budget impact analysis, we used a prevalence-based framework to estimate the additional pharmaceutical expenditure associated with introducing semaglutide for patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and obesity in Germany over a 1-year time horizon. The analysis was aligned with the structure and assumptions of the base-case cost-effectiveness model, including a 3% annual discount rate for costs and the use of 2025 Euros from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance (SHI) payer [10]. The budget impact was therefore driven by the incremental 1-year per-patient cost of semaglutide versus placebo from the deterministic cost-effectiveness analysis.

Because no robust epidemiological data are available on the prevalence of HFpEF with obesity in Germany, we derived the target population indirectly from broader heart failure data. We assumed a German population of 84 million [24] and applied a heart failure prevalence of 3.4% [25]. Of all heart failure cases, 50% were assumed to have HFpEF [26, 27]. Among patients with HFpEF, we further assumed that 35% are obese [5, 6]. Sequential application of these proportions yielded an estimated population of 2,856,000 individuals with heart failure, 1,428,000 with HFpEF, and 499,800 patients with HFpEF and obesity who could in principle be candidates for semaglutide.

We then defined two alternative clinical eligibility scenarios on the basis of the STEP-HFpEF trial. In the more restrictive scenario, we approximated real-world eligibility by applying the ratio of randomized to screened participants in STEP-HFpEF (529/817, corresponding to an eligibility fraction of approximately 0.647) to the HFpEF + obesity population [9]. This approach assumes that, in practice, only a subset of patients with HFpEF and obesity would fulfil key trial inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the more permissive scenario, we assumed that all patients with HFpEF and obesity would be eligible for semaglutide, effectively setting the eligibility fraction to 1.0.

For each eligibility scenario, we modelled three alternative adoption rates of semaglutide among eligible patients: 30%, 50%, and 100%. The number of treated patients in each scenario was calculated as the product of the HFpEF + obesity population, the eligibility fraction, and the assumed adoption rate. The cost input for the budget impact analysis was taken from the deterministic base-case cost-effectiveness analysis, using the discounted incremental 1-year cost per patient of semaglutide versus placebo (1729.2 € per patient). This incremental cost reflects differences in drug acquisition costs, HF-related routine care, and events as captured by the Markov model over 1 year. For each scenario, total incremental 1-year costs (budget impact) were obtained by multiplying the per-patient incremental cost by the number of treated patients. Results

Base-Case Analysis

In the base-case analysis over a 5-year time horizon (German payer perspective), which incorporated a discounted semaglutide acquisition cost and discontinuation-adjusted drug-cost accrual, semaglutide was associated with higher costs and greater health benefits compared with placebo. Total discounted costs were 12,748.6 € for placebo and 17,690.5 € for semaglutide, yielding incremental costs of 4,941.9 €. Total discounted QALYs were 3.208 with placebo and 3.371 with semaglutide, corresponding to an incremental gain of 0.162 QALYs. This resulted in an ICER of 30,443 €/QALY for semaglutide versus placebo. At an illustrative willingness-to-pay threshold of 100,000 €/QALY, the incremental net monetary benefit of semaglutide was 11,291.3 €, indicating that semaglutide is cost-effective compared with placebo under the assumed threshold. The results of the base-case analysis are displayed in Table 3.

Sensitivity Analyses

The tornado diagram of one-way sensitivity analyses identified background mortality as the most influential driver of incremental net monetary benefit, with death risk in both treatment arms being the top two parameters. The monthly semaglutide acquisition cost was the next most influential driver, followed by the risk of serious adverse events. Other parameters had comparatively smaller impacts on the cost-effectiveness results, suggesting that uncertainty in mortality, drug cost, and SAE risk is most relevant for decision making. In probabilistic sensitivity analyses, semaglutide showed a high probability of being cost-effective at an illustrative willingness-to-pay threshold of 100,000 €/QALY across all price scenarios. At the base-case price (100%), the probability that semaglutide was cost-effective versus placebo was 0.911. This increased to 0.929 when the semaglutide price was reduced to 80% of the base-case value, 0.961 at 50% of the price, and 0.975 at 20% of the price, indicating increasing decision robustness with lower drug prices.

Scenario Analysis

In a scenario analysis restricted to the 1-year trial follow-up horizon, semaglutide was associated with higher costs and only a modest gain in QALYs compared with placebo (Supplementary Table 1). Total discounted costs were €3030.4 for placebo and €4759.6 for semaglutide, resulting in incremental costs of €1729.2. Total discounted QALYs over 1 year were 0.743 for placebo and 0.764 for semaglutide, corresponding to an incremental gain of 0.0210 QALYs. This yielded an ICER of €82,237 per QALY gained. At a willingness-to-pay threshold of €100,000 per QALY, the incremental net monetary benefit (INMB) over 1 year was positive (€373.5), indicating that semaglutide remained within the illustrative threshold when the analysis was limited to the within-trial time horizon.

When the time horizon was extended to 10 years, semaglutide remained more costly but generated substantially larger QALY gains (Supplementary Table 2). Total discounted costs were €20,859.6 for placebo and €26,137.1 for semaglutide, with incremental costs of €5277.5. Total discounted QALYs were 5.266 for placebo and 5.546 for semaglutide, resulting in an incremental gain of 0.280 QALYs. The corresponding ICER was €18,830 per QALY gained, and the INMB at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €100,000 per QALY was €22,749.3, suggesting that semaglutide is highly cost-effective over a longer-term horizon.

These findings are consistent with the base-case 5-year analysis. Taken together, the 1-, 5-, and 10-year scenarios show a clear pattern: semaglutide was least favorable over the short, trial-based horizon but became increasingly cost-effective as the time horizon was extended, reflecting the accumulation of health benefits over time relative to the upfront drug costs. The results of the time-horizon scenarios are displayed in the Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Additional deterministic scenario analyses examined structural uncertainty in the long-term KCCQ trajectory. In the optimistic KCCQ scenario, semaglutide remained associated with an incremental gain of 0.2290 QALYs, while incremental costs remained 4941.9 €, resulting in an ICER of €21,583/QALY and an INMB of €17,955.2 at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €100,000/QALY. In the pessimistic KCCQ scenario, the incremental QALY gain decreased to 0.1019, with incremental costs unchanged at 4,941.9 €, resulting in an ICER of €48,476/QALY and an INMB of €5252.6. Thus, the cost-effectiveness of semaglutide remained sensitive to assumptions regarding post-year-1 KCCQ trajectories. The structural scenario analyses are presented in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

In an exploratory heterogeneity analysis stratified by baseline KCCQ-CSS tertiles, the cost-effectiveness of semaglutide varied substantially according to baseline symptom burden. In patients with the lowest baseline KCCQ-CSS (T1, ≤ 48.4), semaglutide was associated with an incremental gain of 0.2238 QALYs and an ICER of €22,077/QALY. In the intermediate tertile (T2, 48.4–66.7), the incremental gain was 0.1661 QALYs, corresponding to an ICER of €29,755/QALY. In contrast, in the highest baseline tertile (T3, > 66.7), the incremental QALY gain was only 0.0137, resulting in an ICER of €359,901/QALY. In a weighted aggregation across tertiles, the overall incremental QALY gain was 0.1355 and the ICER was €36,467/QALY. These findings suggest that semaglutide may provide greater economic value in patients with worse baseline health status, whereas cost-effectiveness appears substantially less favorable in patients with less symptomatic disease at baseline. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the underlying treatment-effect heterogeneity was exploratory and based on subgroup estimates without statistically significant interaction. Full results are shown in the Supplementary Table 6.

Budget Impact Analysis

In the prevalence-based budget impact analysis, introducing semaglutide for patients with HFpEF and obesity in Germany led to substantial increases in 1-year pharmaceutical expenditures across all eligibility and adoption scenarios (Table 4). In the more permissive “no exclusion” scenario, in which all 499,800 patients with HFpEF and obesity were considered eligible, treating 30%, 50%, and 100% of eligible patients with semaglutide resulted in 149,940, 249,900, and 499,800 treated patients, respectively. Compared with a status quo of continued placebo use only (€1,514,605,808), total costs increased to €1,773,877,515, €1,946,725,320, and €2,378,844,831, corresponding to incremental 1-year costs of approximately €259.3 million, €432.1 million, and €864.2 million.

Discussion

In this economic evaluation based on STEP-HFpEF, semaglutide for patients with HFpEF and obesity yielded clinically relevant gains in quality-adjusted life-years but at substantial additional cost from the perspective of the German statutory health insurance payer. Over a 5-year time horizon, semaglutide was associated with higher costs and greater QALY gains than placebo, resulting in an ICER around €30,443/QALY and a positive incremental net monetary benefit at an illustrative willingness-to-pay threshold of €100,000/QALY. Because Germany has no formally adopted cost-effectiveness threshold, this value should be interpreted as a visualization aid rather than a reimbursement cutoff [28]. These findings suggest that semaglutide may be considered cost-effective when long-term benefits on symptoms and health-related quality of life are considered. In contrast, when the analysis was restricted to the 1-year trial follow-up, the incremental QALY gain was modest, the ICER was €82,237/QALY, and the incremental net monetary benefit remained small. This pattern indicates that semaglutide is less economically attractive when its value is judged solely on short-term, within-trial outcomes. Its economic case depends on the assumption that benefits on KCCQ-CSS and utilities are sustained beyond the first year.

Our exploratory subgroup analysis further suggests that economic value may also depend on baseline symptom burden: semaglutide appeared more cost-effective in patients with lower baseline KCCQ-CSS and substantially less favorable in those with higher baseline KCCQ-CSS, consistent with greater room for improvement and potential ceiling effects among less symptomatic patients.

Affordability is a central concern in the context of a highly prevalent syndrome such as HFpEF, especially when considering a high-cost pharmacological intervention that is likely to be used chronically and in addition to existing standard of care. Our prevalence-based budget impact analysis shows that the widespread introduction of semaglutide in HFpEF with obesity would lead to considerable additional annual expenditure for the German statutory health insurance, even under conservative assumptions. Depending on whether all patients with HFpEF and obesity are considered eligible or only a STEP-HFpEF-like subgroup, and depending on adoption rates between 30% and 100%, the incremental 1-year budget impact ranged from approximately 168 million € to 864 million €. These figures illustrate that, although semaglutide may be cost-effective at a high willingness-to-pay threshold, its overall affordability remains a relevant concern when applied to a large eligible population in a publicly financed health system with growing financial pressures [29].

When interpreting these findings, it is informative to consider other pharmacological options for patients with heart failure and, in particular, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. To date, no cost-effectiveness analysis of SGLT2 inhibitors in HFpEF has been published for the German healthcare system, but evaluations from other settings provide useful context. In a USA analysis of empagliflozin added to standard care in patients with HFpEF, treatment increased costs by approximately $26,000 and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) by only 0.06, resulting in an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of about $437,000 per QALY gained at a monthly Federal Supply Schedule price of $327. Even when using typical Medicare Part D prices, empagliflozin remained in the “low value” range, with ICERs exceeding $500,000 per QALY, and cost-effectiveness was highly sensitive to drug price, health-related quality-of-life gains, and the magnitude of any mortality benefit [12]. By contrast, a UK lifetime model pooling individual patient data from DAPA-HF and DELIVER across the full spectrum of left ventricular ejection fraction, with health states defined by Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire symptom score quartiles, found dapagliflozin to be consistently cost-effective. The base-case ICER was about £6500 per QALY gained, below a £20,000 per QALY willingness-to-pay threshold and remained below this threshold even in older patients with preserved ejection fraction, while approaching dominance (higher QALYs, lower costs) in patients with poorer renal function and reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction [30]. Taken together, these studies illustrate that cost-effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors is generally more favorable in heart failure with reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction than in HFpEF, where the economic case remains challenging. In HFpEF, even interventions that meaningfully improve symptoms or reduce hospitalizations often do so at relatively high costs per QALY, and our findings for semaglutide fit into this pattern, underscoring that price and budget impact are central determinants of value in this phenotype.

Pricing is thus a key driver of the value proposition of semaglutide in HFpEF. Our sensitivity analyses show that the probability of semaglutide being cost-effective at 100,000 €/QALY rises substantially as the monthly drug price is reduced. Lower price scenarios (for example, 80%, 50% or 20% of the base price) were associated with higher incremental net monetary benefit and greater decision robustness in probabilistic analyses. These results underline that current list prices may be at the upper margin of what can be justified, even under generous thresholds, and that price reductions achieved through negotiation, rebates or indication-specific agreements could move semaglutide into a range more consistent with established cardiometabolic interventions [31]. From a policy perspective, conditional reimbursement strategies that tie broader access to price concessions and the generation of longer-term outcome data may be a pragmatic way to reconcile clinical benefits with budgetary constraints.

This study has several important strengths. To our knowledge, it represents the first cost-effectiveness and budget impact analysis of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in HFpEF, and specifically the first evaluation of semaglutide in HFpEF with obesity. It thus addresses a major evidence gap at the intersection of heart failure, obesity and health economics, at a time when GLP-1–based therapies are rapidly expanding their cardiovascular indications. Methodologically, the model is closely aligned with the clinical phenotype of HFpEF by structuring health states around KCCQ-CSS quartiles, thereby directly linking the primary patient-reported outcome of STEP-HFpEF to utilities and costs. This KCCQ-centered design recognizes that symptomatic burden and functional limitation are central outcomes in HFpEF and allows their improvement to be translated into QALY gains in a transparent way. Furthermore, the analysis is tailored to the German healthcare context using country-specific unit costs and the German EQ-5D value set, and follows international standards for health economic evaluation, including thorough probabilistic sensitivity analysis and scenario exploration [10]. Internal validation indicated good agreement between model-predicted and observed KCCQ distributions at 12 months, lending credibility to the transition structure and assumptions. In addition, we explicitly explored whether cost-effectiveness differed by baseline symptom severity using published subgroup data, thereby addressing potential heterogeneity in treatment value across patient profiles.

At the same time, several limitations must be acknowledged. A key limitation of the budget impact analysis is the uncertainty surrounding the true prevalence of HFpEF with obesity in Germany. In the absence of robust epidemiological data for this specific subgroup, we derived the target population indirectly from overall heart failure prevalence and assumed proportions of HFpEF and obesity, based on published sources [6, 26]. This indirect approach inevitably introduces uncertainty into the absolute budget impact estimates. To address this, we varied the effective number of patients treated through combinations of two eligibility scenarios. A “no exclusion” scenario including all HFpEF patients with obesity and a more restrictive STEP-HFpEF-like scenario and adoption rates of 30%, 50%, and 100%. These analyses demonstrate how sensitive total expenditure is to real-world uptake, but they do not eliminate the underlying uncertainty about true population size. As such, the reported budget impacts should be interpreted as scenario-based estimates rather than precise forecasts.

Beyond epidemiology, the long-term extrapolation of treatment effects represents another important source of uncertainty. Our base-case and 10-year analyses assume that the gain in KCCQ-CSS observed over 52 weeks with semaglutide is maintained over the longer term, with a plateau after year 1 and no further transitions between KCCQ states other than mortality. This is a structural simplification made in the absence of long-term GLP-1 receptor agonist data in HFpEF. However, the assumption was informed by longer-term semaglutide follow-up data in obesity without diabetes, in which the main between-group weight-loss effect was established during the first year and then largely stabilized over subsequent follow-up [17]. Given that a substantial part of semaglutide’s clinical benefit in HFpEF with obesity is likely mediated through weight loss, we considered this a clinically plausible extrapolation, although uncertainty remains. This uncertainty was additionally explored in optimistic and pessimistic KCCQ trajectory scenario analyses.

We also explored heterogeneity in cost-effectiveness according to baseline KCCQ-CSS tertiles. Although this analysis suggested more favorable ICERs in patients with worse baseline health status, it should be interpreted cautiously. The subgroup estimates were derived from published aggregate data rather than individual participant data, and the prespecified subgroup analysis in STEP-HFpEF did not show statistically significant interaction across baseline KCCQ-CSS tertiles. Accordingly, these findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence for differential treatment value across subgroups. Future analyses using individual participant data would be better suited to evaluate quartile-specific transitions and patient-level heterogeneity more robustly.

A further limitation is that the model was based on STEP-HFpEF, in which semaglutide was compared with placebo and background SGLT2-inhibitor use was low (3.6%), whereas SGLT2 inhibitors now represent an important component of contemporary standard care in HFpEF, so future analyses using active-comparator or add-on data would be valuable once such evidence becomes available [9].

The analysis also focused on direct medical costs from the German statutory health insurance perspective and did not include indirect costs, productivity impacts or informal care, which may be relevant, particularly for younger or working-age patients with HFpEF and severe obesity [32]. Improvements in functional capacity and body weight might translate into gains in work ability or reductions in caregiver burden that are not captured in our base-case perspective; including such broader societal effects could make semaglutide appear more attractive economically. Finally, the STEP-HFpEF population was selected and excluded individuals with diabetes and other common comorbidities, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader real-world HFpEF populations and to patients receiving combination treatment with both SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists [33].

Taken together, our findings suggest that semaglutide has the potential to be a cost-effective option for patients with HFpEF and obesity in Germany when assessed over medium- to long-term horizons and under relatively high willingness-to-pay thresholds, but that its affordability at current list prices is problematic if used broadly. Compared with established HFpEF therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors, which combine lower acquisition costs with robust benefits on hard cardiovascular outcomes, semaglutide occupies a complementary role as a potent symptomatic and weight-loss therapy with high budgetary impact. Future research should therefore focus on long-term outcomes, real-world utilization patterns and better epidemiological characterization of HFpEF with obesity, while policy efforts will need to address pricing and reimbursement to ensure that the clinical benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists can be realized without jeopardizing the sustainability of publicly financed healthcare systems.

Conclusions

Semaglutide may be cost-effective for patients with HFpEF and obesity in Germany when benefits are assessed over longer time horizons and at relatively high willingness-to-pay thresholds, but it appears less favorable over a 1-year horizon and entails substantial budget impact at current pricing assumptions. Exploratory analyses further suggest that value may be greater in patients with worse baseline health status, although this heterogeneity requires confirmation. Given its likely use as an add-on to existing HFpEF therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors, the affordability and real-world implementation of semaglutide will depend critically on pricing, reimbursement conditions, and future evidence on long-term clinical outcomes in this population.
