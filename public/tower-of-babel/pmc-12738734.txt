Radiographic and clinical outcomes at 12 months following full endoscopic interlaminar decompression for grade 1 degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis
Authors: Yusuf Bayram, Muhammed Enes Karataş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) frequently coexists with degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS) in the elderly population, and the standard treatment commonly involves decompression with fusion. However, fusion procedures are associated with increased morbidity and may not always be necessary, particularly in Grade I DS. Full-endoscopic unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (LE-ULBD) has gained attention as a motion-preserving alternative, yet its impact on postoperative stability remains insufficiently studied. Primary objective was to evaluate 12-month improvements in patient-reported outcomes (leg-pain VAS, ODI) after LE-ULBD; radiographic stability was assessed as a key secondary endpoint. This retrospective cohort study included 32 patients with Meyerding stable Grade I DS and symptomatic LSS who underwent single-level lumbar LE-ULBD between January 2023 and October 2024. Pre- and 12-month postoperative radiographs were analyzed for segmental instability, defined as sagittal translation > 4 mm or angular motion > 10°. Clinical outcomes were assessed using Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and Macnab’s criteria. Subgroup analysis was performed based on slip progression. Mean vertebral slip percentage showed no significant change postoperatively (13.4% ± 3.1 vs. 13.8% ± 3.4; p = 0.27). Only one patient (3.1%) developed new radiographic instability. VAS improved from 7.1 ± 1.2 to 1.4 ± 0.8 and ODI from 64.3% ± 9.1 to 18.5% ± 4.7 (p < 0.001 for both). According to Macnab’s criteria, 90.6% of patients reported Excellent or Good outcomes. Slip progression was observed in 9 patients (28.1%), but this was not associated with inferior clinical results. Complications occurred in 2/32 (6.3%) one incidental durotomy and one epidural hematoma with reoperation in 1/32 (3.1%); new radiographic instability in 1/32 (3.1%). LE-ULBD appears to be a safe and effective treatment option for selected patients with Grade I DS and LSS, achieving significant symptomatic relief while preserving segmental stability in the majority of cases. These findings support its consideration as an alternative to fusion in carefully selected individuals. LE-ULBD short-term (12-month) radiographic stability and clinically meaningful improvement in patients with stable grade-I degenerative spondylolisthesis; longer-term follow-up is required to determine durability and late instability risk.

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common health problem in the elderly and is frequently associated with degenerative spondylolisthesis (DS)1. The prevalence of DS in individuals over the age of 60 is estimated to be approximately 10–15%2. The treatment of this condition presents several challenges due to the necessity of preserving existing spinal stability in addition to achieving adequate neural decompression, as well as considerations related to the advanced age of the patient population. Fusion combined with decompression remains the standard approach in many DS cases3,4.

However, current fusion procedures have several disadvantages, including prolonged operative time, excessive intraoperative blood loss, extended hospital stays, and the risk of adjacent segment disease5. Recent randomized trials have shown that decompression alone can offer similar outcomes to fusion in selected DS patients, while reducing complications and recovery time6. Endoscopic techniques are increasingly used as promising motion-preserving alternatives for lumbar decompression. Nevertheless, the application of these techniques in patients with spondylolysis remains controversial7.

The interlaminar endoscopic surgical technique offers several theoretical advantages for patients with DS. Specifically, the full endoscopic technique provides access to the spinal canal through a highly minimally invasive approach, enabling effective spinal canal and neural decompression while preserving the facet joints, posterior longitudinal complex, and paraspinal muscles such as the multifidus8,9. Prior to the era of endoscopic surgery, studies have reported postoperative instability rates ranging from 12% to 15% in patients undergoing open decompression without fusion10. Despite being highly minimally invasive, endoscopic surgeries still lack the volume of clinical evidence observed in open or fusion surgeries, particularly in patients with Grade I DS and concomitant LSS. Although a limited number of studies have reported favorable outcomes of full-endoscopic unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (LE-ULBD) in LSS patients, radiological and clinical data in DS patients—regardless of the presence of instability—remain scarce11.

In this retrospective single-center study, we investigated whether LE-ULBD preserves segmental stability while providing effective neural decompression in radiographically stable Meyerding Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis. We enrolled patients without preoperative dynamic instability on standing flexion–extension radiographs and hypothesized that a tissue-sparing, facet-preserving endoscopic approach would yield clinically meaningful improvements in VAS and ODI while maintaining radiographic stability in appropriately selected cases. Accordingly, the study was powered on the clinical endpoints (12-month changes in VAS/ODI), with radiographic stability specified as a key secondary outcome.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study has been approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Hisar Intercontinental Hospital (Approval date: 30 May 2025; Meeting No: 25 35) and all patients gave written informed consent. The methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the ethics committee. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed during the study.

We reviewed all consecutive stand-alone LE-ULBD procedures for degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis between January 2023 and October 2024. The cohort was restricted to radiographically stable Meyerding Grade I disease; patients with preoperative dynamic instability on standing flexion–extension radiographs (translation > 3 mm) were excluded12. Eligibility required neurogenic claudication and/or radiculopathy concordant with MRI-documented central/lateral recess stenosis after ≥ 3 months of conservative care, with complete pre- and 12-month post-operative data. To avoid phenotype heterogeneity, mechanical back pain was defined as axial pain worse with extension/standing or rotation and improved with flexion/unloading, without dermatomal radicular features; patients with predominant mechanical back pain lacking clinicoradiographic concordance for neural compression were excluded. Additional exclusions were < Grade I spondylolisthesis, isthmic/traumatic listhesis, prior index-level surgery, coronal deformity > 20°, and active infection13,14.

The primary clinical endpoints targeted leg-dominant symptoms (leg-pain VAS) and disability (ODI), consistent with the inclusion of neurogenic claudication/radiculopathy. Axial back-pain VAS was not a primary endpoint in this retrospective cohort and will be collected prospectively to enable phenotype-specific analyses (axial vs. radicular).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position on a radiolucent table. Thoracic and pelvic bolsters were used to preserve physiological lumbar lordosis and reduce intra-abdominal pressure. After fluoroscopic localization of the target level, a paramedian (~ 8 mm) skin incision was made and sequential dilation was performed to introduce the endoscopic working cannula into the interlaminar window.

A unilateral interlaminar approach with an over-the-top technique was used to achieve bilateral decompression. On the ipsilateral side, a limited laminotomy and flavectomy were performed to expose the lateral recess. The endoscope was then medially angulated beneath the spinous process for contralateral sublaminar undercutting, decompressing the contralateral lateral recess and traversing root while preserving the facet capsule and posterior ligamentous complex (Fig. 1). Bony removal was intentionally facet-sparing to minimize the risk of iatrogenic destabilization.

Decompression proceeded under continuous saline irrigation, which maintained visualization and assisted with debris clearance. Hemostasis was achieved with bipolar radiofrequency/coagulation as needed. After confirming adequate bilateral neural decompression and meticulous hemostasis, instruments were withdrawn. The skin was closed with a single absorbable suture; no drains were required. All patients followed a standardized postoperative pathway with early mobilization on the day of surgery15.

In cases with MRI-concordant central/lateral recess stenosis, interlaminar LE-ULBD was the default; a limited, targeted endoscopic discectomy was added only when an intraoperatively confirmed focal disc fragment was the primary compressive source. Resection was kept conservative to preserve stability.

This approach is consistent with previously validated criteria in the literature1,2.

Secondary outcomes comprised the change in leg-pain VAS (0–10) and ODI v2.0, global functional status by modified Macnab’s criteria, operative time, and perioperative complications. Measurement of slip percentage was also included as a radiologic parameter, defined as the ratio of the forward displacement of the upper vertebra over the length of the inferior vertebral endplate on neutral lateral radiographs. All radiographic measurements were independently performed by a fellowship-trained spine surgeon blinded to clinical outcomes. Clinical outcomes were recorded at baseline and at 6- and 12-month follow-ups. At 12 months, global patient satisfaction was assessed using modified Macnab’s criteria (Excellent/Good/Fair/Poor) based on pain relief, function, and return to activities.

MCID definitions and clinical success. To complement analyses of continuous change, minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were pre-specified as follows: for ODI, an absolute improvement of ≥ 12–12.5 points or a ≥ 30% relative improvement; for VAS (0–10), an absolute improvement of ≥ 2.0 points or a ≥ 30% relative improvement. The primary clinical success definition was meeting MCID for either ODI or VAS at 12 months; sensitivity analyses reported dual-responder status (meeting MCID for both ODI and VAS) and Macnab Excellent/Good as complementary global measures of patient-reported success.

Statistical analysis

Primary endpoints were the within-subject changes in leg-pain VAS and ODI at 12 months, analysed independently as co-primary continuous endpoints (not a composite). The a priori paired-design sample-size calculation (G*Power v3.1.9.7) used a common, conservative effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.65), α = 0.05 (two-sided), and power = 0.80, yielding n = 30; n = 32 were enrolled. This design targeted ≥ 80% power for each endpoint. MCID-based responder analyses were pre-specified for clinical context but not used in sample-size determination and were treated as exploratory without multiplicity adjustment.

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD (if approximately normal) or median (IQR) otherwise; categorical variables as n (%) with 95% CIs (Wilson for single proportions). Normality of change scores (ΔVAS, ΔODI) and other continuous endpoints was assessed via Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homoscedasticity for between-group comparisons was checked with Levene’s test; when violated, Welch’s t was used.

For within-subject comparisons (preoperative vs. 12-month VAS/ODI), paired t-tests (normal) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (non-normal) were applied. For between-group comparisons (e.g., slip-progression yes/no), independent-samples t (Welch-adjusted if needed) or Mann–Whitney U were used. Categorical outcomes (e.g., Macnab Excellent/Good, complication rates) were compared using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for small expected counts. Associations between BMI and continuous changes (ΔODI, ΔVAS) were examined with Spearman’s ρ (or Pearson’s r when approximately normal).

We report two-sided p-values and 95% confidence intervals. Effect sizes accompany hypothesis tests: Cohen’s d (or dz for paired designs) for parametric comparisons, rank-biserial r for non-parametric comparisons; for proportions, risk difference/ratio with 95% CIs where informative. All patients completed 12-month follow-up; analyses were complete-case. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3); power calculations used G*Power (v3.1.9.7).

Interobserver reliability. Two independent spine surgeons evaluated all flexion-extension and neutral lateral radiographs. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for translation and angular motion were 0.87 and 0.91, respectively, indicating excellent reproducibility.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort included 32 consecutive patients (20 female, 12 male) with a mean age of 65.2 ± 8.4 years (range 49–79 years). The mean follow-up duration was 13.7 ± 1.1 months, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months for all patients.

All surgeries were successfully performed using the full endoscopic interlaminar approach without conversion to open surgery. The most frequently operated level was L4–5 (24 cases, 75%), followed by L3–4 (6 cases, 18.8%) and L5–S1 (2 cases, 6.2%).

The average operative time was 68.3 ± 12.7 min (range 48–92 min), and all procedures were associated with estimated blood loss of less than 50 mL. No intraoperative complications occurred. All patients were mobilized within 6 h postoperatively, and the mean hospital stay was 1.3 ± 0.5 days (range 1–3 days).

In exploratory analyses, Body mass index (BMI) was similar between patients with vs. without slip progression (28.2 vs. 27.4 kg/m²; mean difference 0.83 kg/m²; Cohen’s d 0.17; p ≈ 0.66, 95% CI − 2.86 to 4.53). These analyses were underpowered and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Radiographic outcomes

In this stable Grade I cohort, preoperative static radiographs revealed a mean slip of 13.4% ± 3.1% (range, 8–19%), corresponding to an absolute displacement of 5.4 ± 1.2 mm (range, 3–8 mm). At 12-month postoperative follow-up, the mean slip was 13.8% ± 3.4% (range, 9–20%), equivalent to 5.5 ± 1.3 mm (range, 3–8 mm). The mean change in slip was 0.1 ± 0.6 mm (p = 0.35), indicating that although a slight increase was observed, the degree of vertebral translation remained radiographically stable following endoscopic decompression (Table 2). Postoperative analysis revealed that vertebral slip percentage increased in 9 patients (28.1%) at 12-month follow-up. The largest increase in slip was observed in a 69-year-old female patient who underwent L4–5 decompression, where the slip progressed from 13.6% preoperatively to 16.9% postoperatively, representing a 3.3% increase. A subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate whether patients with postoperative increases in vertebral slip percentage (n = 9) demonstrated different clinical outcomes compared to the rest of the cohort (n = 23). No statistically significant differences were found in postoperative VAS scores (1.5 ± 0.9 vs. 1.4 ± 0.8; p = 0.63), ODI scores (19.3% ± 5.1 vs. 18.1% ± 4.6; p = 0.48), or Macnab outcome distribution (p = 0.71). Dynamic radiographs obtained preoperatively demonstrated a mean translational motion of 2.1 ± 0.9 mm (range 1.3–2.9 mm), and the radiographic outcomes at follow-up were as follows: One patient (3.1%) developed new-onset instability, defined as translational motion > 4 mm (measured at 4.2 mm). No patient exceeded the angular-motion threshold of > 10° at 12 months. (Table 3).

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were evaluated at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively. Statistically and clinically significant improvements were observed in both pain and disability scores, with changes exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds established for lumbar spine interventions (≥ 2.0 points for VAS and ≥ 12.5% for ODI). Mean VAS scores for leg pain improved from 7.1 ± 1.2 preoperatively to 1.4 ± 0.8 postoperatively. (p < 0.001) Similarly, ODI scores decreased from 64.3% ± 9.1 to 18.5% ± 4.7. (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Complications

Two complications occurred (6.3%, 2/32; 95% CI 1.7–20.1%). One incidental durotomy was identified intraoperatively and repaired endoscopically with fibrin glue and a synthetic dural substitute; no postoperative CSF leak or pseudomeningocele ensued. One symptomatic epidural hematoma presented on postoperative day 1 with acute neurological decline and was evacuated endoscopically on an emergency basis; the patient achieved full neurological recovery by 6 weeks. No nerve-root injury, surgical-site infection, or mortality occurred.

Discussion

This retrospective study demonstrates that LE-ULBD can effectively treat Grade 1 degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis while preserving segmental stability in 96.9% of cases. The single instability occurrence (3.1%) compares favorably with reported rates of 12–15% following open decompression without fusion10, suggesting the endoscopic approach may offer superior stability preservation.

Our short-term observations are consistent with randomized evidence indicating that decompression alone is noninferior to decompression with fusion for degenerative spondylolisthesis at 2 years (NORDSTEN-DS), notwithstanding a modestly higher reoperation rate after decompression alone. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses likewise show comparable functional outcomes for decompression alone versus decompression plus fusion in low-grade spondylolisthesis—without a clear long-term advantage to routine fusion—although reoperation rates may be slightly higher in decompression-only cohorts20,21. These data, together with the tissue-sparing characteristics of LE-ULBD, support decompression-first strategies in carefully selected, radiographically stable Grade I cases, while emphasizing the need for extended follow-up to assess late instability and durability22.

In addition, comparative series and recent systematic reviews comparing full-endoscopic with traditional microscopic/open decompression consistently show similar functional improvement with a lower perioperative burden—notably reduced blood loss and shorter length of stay—supporting a tissue-sparing decompression strategy in this population23,24.

Several technical factors likely contribute to these favorable outcomes. First, the minimally invasive nature of the procedure allows for precise decompression while minimizing disruption of stabilizing structures. Unlike open techniques that often require complete laminectomy and facetectomy, the endoscopic approach enables targeted removal of only the pathologic compressive elements25. Second, preservation of the posterior ligamentous complex, including the facet capsules and interspinous ligaments, helps maintain segmental stability26. Third, the tubular retractor system minimizes paraspinal muscle injury, potentially reducing postoperative dynamic instability27.

The clinical outcomes in our series compare favorably with fusion literature. The mean ODI improvement of 45.8% surpasses the 28% improvement reported in the fusion cohort of the landmark SPORT trial28. This suggests that many patients with Grade 1 spondylolisthesis may achieve excellent outcomes without subjecting them to fusion-related morbidity. Our complication rate is 6.3%, which is comparable to surgeries performed using similar techniques, but it is also quite good when compared to the complication rates reported for fusion surgery (15–20%)29,30.

Although vertebral slip percentage increased postoperatively in 9 patients (28.1%), this did not correspond to worse clinical outcomes. VAS, ODI scores, and Macnab’s criteria remained comparable between patients with and without slip progression. Consistent with prior reports, radiographic slip progression in low-grade DS does not necessarily translate into worse function: after minimally invasive decompression, Ravinsky et al. found no correlation between slip progression and ODI, and recent series likewise observed that modest increases in slip% were not linked to inferior patient-reported outcomes. This aligns with our subgroup analysis showing no differences in VAS, ODI, or Macnab across patients with versus without slip progression31,32.

The single case of postoperative instability in our series occurred in a patient who underwent two-level decompression. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting multilevel decompression may increase instability risk33. Other potential risk factors in this case included relatively greater facet resection (estimated at 35–40%) and preexisting facet degeneration. Notably, our results differ somewhat from previous endoscopic series reporting higher instability rates34. The importance of facet preservation is supported by biomechanical studies demonstrating that resection exceeding 30–40% significantly increases segmental mobility35. Our technique of medial facet undercutting while preserving the articular capsule appears effective in maintaining stability.

It is important to distinguish between interlaminar and transforaminal endoscopic approaches: whereas interlaminar ULBD permits bilateral decompression via unilateral, facet-sparing undercutting with preservation of stabilizing osseoligamentous structures (posterior ligamentous complex and facet capsule), transforaminal foraminotomy/foraminoplasty typically entails partial resection/undercutting of the superior articular process and facet margins to enlarge the foramen, which—particularly in borderline-stable segments—can carry a greater risk of destabilization if over-resected. Our findings therefore apply specifically to the LE-ULBD technique36,37.

Our findings are also supported by recent data from de Van Isseldyk et al., who employed a patient-specific tailored endoscopic foraminotomy approach in both isthmic and degenerative spondylolisthesis cases. Their results demonstrated that endoscopic decompression through transforaminal or interlaminar approaches achieved satisfactory outcomes without causing postoperative instability38. This further reinforces the concept that individualized endoscopic strategies can maintain segmental integrity while effectively relieving radicular symptoms.

In addition, a recent case series by Dolas et al. reported favorable outcomes in 24 patients treated with endoscopic interlaminar decompression using a unilateral approach, including multi-level cases. They observed significant reductions in both VAS and ODI scores, with no compromise of stability, highlighting that adequate decompression with minimal tissue disruption is achievable even across multiple levels39. These findings support the notion that endoscopic decompression may be safely extended to select patients requiring broader decompression.

Our results align with previous studies emphasizing the potential of full-endoscopic decompression to provide effective neural decompression while preserving spinal stability. In particular, Siepe et al. detailed the surgical technique and rationale of the LE-ULBD approach, highlighting its feasibility, clinical effectiveness, and lower complication rates compared to microsurgical and other MIS methods. Their experience supports our findings, particularly regarding the role of precise over-the-top decompression in elderly and multimorbid patients, where reduced soft tissue trauma, minimized infection risk, and early mobilization are paramount advantages40. Furthermore, the authors emphasized that LE-ULBD may be especially beneficial in obese individuals due to its minimal access requirements, a consideration increasingly relevant in modern spine practice.

The clinical implications of this study are potentially significant. For carefully selected patients with Grade 1 degenerative spondylolisthesis - particularly those without preoperative dynamic instability - full endoscopic interlaminar decompression may represent a viable alternative to fusion. Emerging series of LE-ULBD in low-grade spondylolisthesis demonstrate favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes, suggesting that pars- and facet-preserving decompression may mitigate iatrogenic instability; nevertheless, the long-term durability of these benefits remains to be established41.

In strictly selected patients with Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis without preoperative dynamic instability, LE-ULBD provides short-term radiographic stability, clinically meaningful improvements exceeding MCID thresholds, and excellent perioperative recovery. Unlike previous minimally invasive decompression series, this study demonstrates that a full-endoscopic approach can preserve stability while optimizing outcomes, supporting its role as a stability-preserving alternative to fusion.

Limitations

This retrospective, single-surgeon cohort is susceptible to selection and performance bias despite strict inclusion/exclusion criteria and a standardized LE-ULBD workflow; accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The modest sample size (n = 32) curtailed subgroup analyses, and the 12-month follow-up precludes firm inferences regarding late-onset instability, adjacent segment disease, or reoperation, so conclusions are restricted to short-term radiographic stability and clinical improvement. External validity is confined to radiographically stable Grade I degenerative spondylolisthesis and should not be generalized to higher-grade or dynamically unstable cases. The absence of a comparator arm (e.g., fusion or microscope-assisted MIS) limits statements about comparative effectiveness or learning-curve effects. Several sources of unmeasured heterogeneity remain: facet arthropathy was not quantitatively graded, paraspinal muscle quality (e.g., fatty infiltration) was not systematically assessed, and we relied on standing flexion–extension radiographs rather than weighted/functional dynamic imaging, which may detect subtler forms of instability but is not yet standardized. Consistent with our inclusion criteria (neurogenic claudication/radiculopathy), clinical endpoints prioritized leg-dominant symptoms (leg-pain VAS, ODI); axial back-pain VAS and generic PROMs were not collected, which limits phenotype-specific and health-utility comparisons. To address these gaps, we have initiated prospective registry follow-up with planned analyses at 24 and 60 months, incorporating broader PROMs, dynamic radiography, and spinopelvic/segmental alignment metrics, while future multi-surgeon, multi-center studies are needed to evaluate reproducibility and durability. The study was underpowered to detect BMI-related differences in subgroup outcomes; BMI findings should be interpreted cautiously. Occupational exposure (e.g., heavy manual work or professional driving/whole-body vibration) was not systematically captured, precluding adjustment for work-related load; these data will be collected prospectively using standardized categories.

Conclusion

This study highlights the clinical value of LE-ULBD as a compelling motion-preserving alternative to fusion in patients with Grade I DS and symptomatic LSS. With a radiographic stability preservation rate of 96.9% and significant improvements in pain and disability, LE-ULBD offers both safety and efficacy without the morbidity associated with traditional fusion procedures. These findings underscore the importance of precise patient selection—particularly those without dynamic instability and with predominantly radicular symptoms. In an era increasingly focused on minimally invasive and cost-conscious care, LE-ULBD stands out as a viable, tissue-sparing solution. Prospective studies with long-term outcomes are warranted to further validate and expand its role in the surgical treatment of degenerative lumbar pathology.
