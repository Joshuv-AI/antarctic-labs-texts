Arousal effects on oscillatory dynamics in the non-human primate brain
Authors: Shashank A Anand, Fatih Sogukpinar, Ilya E Monosov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractArousal states are thought to influence many aspects of cognition and behavior by broadly modulating neural activity. Many studies have observed arousal-related modulations of alpha (~8 to 15 Hz) and gamma (~30 to 50 Hz) power and coherence in local field potentials across relatively small groups of brain regions. However, the global pattern of arousal-related oscillatory modulation in local field potentials is yet to be fully elucidated. We simultaneously recorded local field potentials in numerous cortical and subcortical regions in the primate brain and assessed oscillatory activity and inter-regional coherence associated with arousal state. In high arousal states, we found a uniquely strong and coherent gamma oscillation between the amygdala and basal forebrain. In low arousal rest-like states, a relative increase in coherence at alpha frequencies was present across sampled brain regions, with the notable exception of the medial temporal lobe. We consider how these patterns of activity may index arousal-related brain states that support the processing of incoming sensory stimuli during high arousal states and memory-related functions during rest.Keywords: arousal, coherence, local field potentials

Introduction

Arousal represents a fundamental axis of internal states that describe general or nonselective attentiveness and responsiveness (e.g. from wakeful to sleepy or conscious to unconscious) (Brown et al. 2011; Jawinski et al. 2019). Disrupted arousal states are associated with several psychiatric disorders such as major depression and schizophrenia (Williams et al. 2004; Schmidt et al. 2017). Arousal effects cognition, emotion, memory, and behavior in a temporally precise and coordinated manner (Pfaff et al. 2008) by simultaneously influencing the neural activity across many brain areas in response to external stimuli and changes in internal state.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings have long observed widespread low-frequency (~<15 Hz) neocortical oscillatory activity during low arousal states, such as increased alpha rhythms (8 to 15 Hz) during eye closure or rest (Berger 1929; Adrian and Matthews 1934; Barry et al. 2007) and an “anteriorization” of alpha rhythms (loss of occipital alpha with emergence of frontal alpha) during anesthesia (Tinker et al. 1977; Feshchenko et al. 2004; Breshears et al. 2010; Purdon et al. 2013). One theory of alpha rhythms suggests that they emerge from or index inhibitory thalamocortical circuitry that may serve as a gating mechanism (Klimesch et al. 2007; Jensen and Mazaheri 2010), suppressing sensory attention (Foxe and Snyder 2011), and/or regulating top-down control over frontal executive function (Weiner et al. 2023). However, an understanding of the spatial structure of alpha rhythms, specifically alpha synchrony within and between large numbers of cortical and subcortical areas, and their relationship to arousal-related behavioral states remain understudied.

Gamma oscillations (~30 to 50 Hz) have been extensively studied in tasks involving the processing of incoming sensory stimuli and attention such as selective spatial attention (Fries et al. 2001a; Liang et al. 2005), sensory perception (Pritchett et al. 2015), and working memory (Yamamoto et al. 2014; Lundqvist et al. 2016). They are often associated with multi-unit activity at the recording site (Ray and Maunsell 2011, 2015). A few studies also related gamma oscillatory activity to arousal states. Stimulation of mesopontine cholinergic neurons and basal forebrain, two areas involved in modulating arousal, potentiated cortical EEG at gamma frequencies in cats and rats, respectively (Steriade et al. 1991; Cape et al. 2000; Cape and Jones 2000), and gamma oscillatory activity in human basal ganglia disappeared during drowsiness (Jenkinson et al. 2013). Such arousal-related gamma rhythms are thought to index feedforward information flow and online cognitive processing (Garcia-Rill 2017). To date, the effect of arousal on gamma oscillations and their cross-region coordination has not been assessed in primate models across large networks of brain regions.

Arousal states are known to impact neural computation and behavior (Cardin and Schmidt 2003; Vinck et al. 2015); however, the mechanisms and related biomarkers of these interactions are poorly understood. While many previous reports have utilized drugs to induce specific arousal states, naturally occurring fluctuations in arousal remain underexplored, particularly in primates. We simultaneously recorded task-free local field potentials (LFPs) from many cortical and subcortical brain regions in two non-human primates (NHPs) using a semichronic high channel count recording drive. This revealed novel markers of high arousal and low arousal (rest-like) states. First, two strongly connected (Mesulam et al. 1983b; Russchen et al. 1985; Koliatsos et al. 1988) brain areas involved in processing the salience of external incoming sensory stimuli, the amygdala (AG) and the basal forebrain (BF) (O’Neill et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019, 2021), displayed a uniquely strong and coherent oscillation in ~30 to 40 Hz range during high arousal states. Second, we found that most cortical and subcortical brain areas showed relatively increased alpha (8 to 15 Hz) activity and coherence during low arousal states (Barry et al. 2007; Weiner et al. 2023). However, strikingly, medial temporal areas involved in distinct forms of declarative memory encoding and retrieval did not strongly increase alpha coherence with other regions during the low arousal state. We conclude with a discussion of how these patterns of low- and high-frequency oscillatory coherence could reflect the wide effects of arousal on cognitive functions and processing of external stimuli.

Materials and methods

General

Two adult male rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta; 6 and 9 years old) were used for the electrophysiological recordings analyzed in this study. MRI-compatible head-holders and chambers were surgically placed under general anesthesia and sterile conditions. The placement of the chambers was driven by pre-operative structural MRIs (3 T; Siemens). After estimation of electrode path, 124-channel semi-chronic high channel count recording drives (LS124; Gray Matter) were implanted and lowered immediately below dura (to minimize effect of dural thickening on recording quality). The arrays only had single contact electrode shanks, which were spaced 1.5 mm apart. The electrode recording process is described in a separate Methods section.

Operationally defining arousal epochs

Eye position data were collected with an infrared video camera (Eyelink; SR Research). Saccade rate was calculated in 5-s bins across the task-free recording session using a 20°/s velocity threshold on eye position data. The distribution of saccade rate when the eyes were open was clustered into two groups using k-means clustering, and the saccade rate threshold that separated the two groups was calculated. The two-group threshold was imposed because the saccade rate distribution seemed bimodal and passed the Hartigan’s Dip Test (P < 0.05) in 276 out of 292 sessions (94.5%).

We operationally defined the high arousal state as epochs when the NHP’s eyes were open and had a saccade rate continuously higher than the threshold set from the k-means clustering (the mean saccade rate in this state was 1.9 saccades per second). We operationally defined the low arousal (rest-like) state as epochs when the NHP’s eyes were continuously closed for at least 30 s. Each session had high and low arousal epochs of varying durations. Sessions without both a high and low arousal epoch >30 s were not used in subsequent state analyses. High and low arousal time windows with the longest duration were selected for each session. Next, the longer time window was truncated to create equal time windows for the two arousal states. The majority of arousal-state windows were between 30 and 60 s, with 79% of task-free sessions having epochs between 30 and 60 s, and 96% of sessions having epochs between 30 and 90 s (Supplemental Fig. S8). An example session with the selected low/high arousal state windows is also shown (Supplemental Fig. S10A).

We also simultaneously obtained heart rate and eye tracking in six task-free sessions. Heart rate (HR) was obtained by a pulse sensor (World Famous Electronics, LLC) attached to the monkey’s earlobe, and short-term heart rate variability was calculated using the root mean square of successive beat differences. Operationally defined high arousal states had higher heart rate and lower short-term heart rate variability than operationally defined low arousal states (Supplemental Fig. S10B).

Because the purpose of our study was to compare brain states across relatively longer timescales, we chose epochs of similar durations across sessions and used unbiased screens for state-related discrimination across the frequency domain (see receiver operator characteristic (ROC) analysis). Our approach averaged out rapid state changes within high and low arousal epochs, ensuring they did not affect our results. This specification is essential as we could not use ultraslow LFPs like delta (~0.5 to 2 Hz) to cluster the low arousal state into microstates on the order of seconds (Liu et al. 2021).

We verified that saccades were not solely responsible for the main effect of the paper by repeating key analyses after eliminating 300 ms time around every saccade onset during high arousal epochs. Identical time windows were removed from low arousal epochs to maintain equal time windows between arousal states. This removed, on average, 50% of the data per session. However, the remaining data were sufficient to repeat our key analyses (see Results, Supplemental Fig. S1, Supplemental Fig. S4, Supplemental Fig. S5).

Electrophysiology recordings and LFP preprocessing

Task-free recordings were collected independently (not during intertrial intervals). LFPs were acquired using a Plexon recording system with a high-pass cutoff ~1 Hz. Wideband signal was downsampled to 1000 Hz and subsequently low-pass filtered at 200 Hz. Due to the limitations in the Plexon data acquisition system, we did not consider slow oscillations like delta (0.5 to 2 Hz). Electrode channels that did not meet the following criteria were removed from subsequent processing: (1) post-op CT images that showed that the electrode was in neural tissue and followed a reconstructable trajectory; (2) electrode produced single units during the history of array neuronal recordings, since the channels which did not produce single units were highly likely to be noisy; (3) electrode impedance >0.2 MΩ or observable single unit activity (for details, see Ogasawara et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). The raw signals from all remaining electrode channels were re-referenced using a common average reference filter, where the mean signal across all channels was subtracted from every individual channel, to reduce common mode noise and any influence of the original cranial reference that was placed over V1. The number of electrodes in an analysis represents the number of recordings that were from the area of interest, independent of whether the electrodes were intentionally moved prior to the recording.

Anatomical localization of recording sites

Recording site locations were determined using a combination of histology, imaging, and classical electrophysiology methods. Postsurgical CT scans were acquired and registered to structural MRI scans to visualize electrode locations. During recording sessions, functional and anatomical landmarks were used to verify electrode locations (for details, see Ogasawara et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). The MRI parcellations were defined as in previous studies (Markov et al. 2014; Dotson et al. 2017, 2018; Mao et al. 2021; Ogasawara et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). 45B and 8v were grouped. Ventral medial temporal cortex (VMTC) was widely defined along the anterior–posterior axis, and included perirhinal cortex, temporal pole, and medial TE (medial inferior temporal cortex). VMTC was separated into anterior and posterior regions using a threshold of 3 mm posterior to the anterior commissure (forming regions AVMTC and PVMTC, respectively). Ventral pallidum was excluded from this current analysis because of the proximity to basal forebrain. For a full list of included regions, their anatomical location referenced to the anterior commissure, and number of recording sites, see Supplemental Tables S1 and S2.

Histology

Euthanasia, perfusion, and brain extraction procedures were as in Ogasawara et al. (2021). Briefly, the monkey was deeply anesthetized after which sodium pentobarbital (25 to 30 mg/kg) was injected. This procedure was performed with the Division for Comparative Medicine in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols. The monkey was then perfused with formaldehyde. Next, the brain was extracted, blocked, and equilibrated with sucrose. Frozen sections were cut every 50 μm in the coronal plane. After thorough washes in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and inactivating the endogenous peroxidase activity with hydrogen peroxidase, sections were incubated free-floating in 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline containing Triton X-100, blocking serum, and the primary antibody (Anti-Choline Acetyltransferase Antibody; 1:500) for 67 h at 4°C. The immunoreaction was visualized accordingly using avidin–biotin complex method using the Vectastin elite ABC kit (Vector Lab., Burlingame, CA) and 3′,3′-diaminobenzidine as a chromogen with nickel ammonium sulfate. After thorough washes, all sections were mounted on microscope slides, dehydrated in ethanol, cleared in xylene, and cover slipped.

Power

Welch’s power spectral density estimate (pwelch function in MATLAB, 1-Hz bins, eight Hamming windows with 50% overlap) was used to calculate the strength of electrical activity across frequencies. To determine whether power differences between the two arousal states were statistically significant for a given region, we used a nonparametric paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (signrank in MATLAB).

Power-law fit of power spectra

Power-law fit was determined using the fit function in MATLAB using a one-term power series model, defined by , to describe a region’s mean power spectrum region across all recording sites. A statistically significant increase from the expected power-law fit was determined using a one-sample t-test (ttest in MATLAB) between the distribution of computed power across all recording sites and the value of the estimated fit at a given frequency, with a significance threshold of 0.05.

Coherence

Magnitude-squared coherence (mscohere in MATLAB, 1-Hz bins, eight Hamming windows with 50% overlap) was used to calculate the correspondence of frequency content between two signals over broad spectral scales.

Here, x and y represent different signals, C represents coherence between x and y, Pxx and Pyy represent the individual power spectral densities of x and y, and Pxy represents the cross power spectral density between x and y.

Phase-locking value (PLV) was used to calculate the pure phase correspondence between two signals over narrow spectral scales.

Here, ΔΦn represents the phase difference between the two signals at sample n. Instantaneous phase (Φ) was calculated from the phase angle (phase function in MATLAB) of the Hilbert transform (hilbert in MATLAB).

To determine whether coherence and PLV differences between the two arousal states were statistically significant for a given region-pair, we used a nonparametric paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (signrank in MATLAB). To determine whether arousal state–related PLV differences were statistically significantly different between region-pairs, we used a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test between the distributions of state-related PLV changes between region-pairs. We corrected for multiple comparisons by multiplying the P-value by the total number of region-pairs. The statistical significance threshold was set to 0.05 for the corrected P-value (Bonferroni correction).

Wavelet coherence (wcoherence in MATLAB) was used to calculate a time course of coherence around state transitions (eyes closed to eyes open and vice versa).

Here, C(a, b) represents the continuous wavelet transform of the signal at a specific scale and position while S represents a smoothing function in time and scale. To determine whether coherence differences were statistically significantly different after state transition, we used a nonparametric paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (signrank in MATLAB).

Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) analysis

Discriminability between power spectra for an individual region was calculated using an ROC analysis between distributions of mean power at a given frequency (1-Hz bins) during high and low arousal epochs across all sessions. This generated a single area-under-curve (AUC) value for each frequency bin. These values were between 0 and 1 (with 0.5 representing chance, or no discriminability). The meaning of AUC values above 0.5 or below 0.5 are indicated in each relevant figure.

Mean discriminability between coherence during high and low arousal epochs across all electrodes was obtained by averaging the AUC of every single electrode pair. The P-value assessing the reliability of the discriminability (provided by the AUC)was determined using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (ranksum in MATLAB), with a significance threshold of 0.05.

Spring-embedded graphical network

An undirected graph object (graph function in MATLAB) was generated from the mean coherence matrix with a coherence threshold of 0.2 (all values <0.2 were set to 0). This threshold was approximated from the median coherence across all region-pairs in both arousal-state epochs (0.21 and 0.20 in high and low arousal epochs, respectively). Arousal state–dependent graphical networks were visualized using a force-directed layout (plot function in MATLAB). Degree centrality (degree function in MATLAB) and average edge weight for each region was calculated from the graph object for each arousal state.

Results

Arousal state is associated with frequency-specific changes in local field potentials

The LFP contains activity across a broad frequency range with specific spectral windows hypothesized to have different physiologic origins and possibly unique links to behavior and cognition (Engel et al. 2001; Buzsáki and Draguhn 2004). Therefore, we sought to understand the effect of arousal state (see Methods for details on arousal state definitions) on electrical activity in sampled brain regions across frequencies. During low arousal epochs, we observed discriminable increases in the strength of electrical activity (power) at frequencies < 12 Hz across all brain regions (Fig. 1A; also see Supplemental Fig. S1 for the individual power spectra of each region). This power increase during low arousal epochs was especially apparent in frontal cortical areas like BA 6 and BA 8d (ROC between high and low arousal power at 10 Hz; BA 6: AUC = 0.16, P < 0.001; BA 8d: AUC = 0.18, P < 0.001). Additionally, during high arousal epochs we observed particularly strong increases in power in the AG and BF at canonical gamma band frequencies (30 to 50 Hz) (Fig. 1A, ROC between high and low arousal power; AG at 35 Hz: AUC = 0.80, P < 0.001; BF at 34 Hz: AUC = 0.68, P < 0.001).

Gamma oscillations in amygdala and basal forebrain are modulated by arousal state

LFP power displays aperiodic (1/f-like) properties, on top of which periodic oscillations appear as narrow-band increases in power above expected scale-free power (Freeman and Zhai 2009; Donoghue et al. 2020). These oscillations are thought to reflect intrinsic cellular and circuit properties that are associated with specific functional states (Buzsáki and Draguhn 2004). Because we observed discriminable gamma (30 to 50 Hz) power changes in the AG and BF during high arousal epochs, we next studied the power spectrum of AG and BF to reveal whether these areas showed any frequency-specific periodic oscillations during high arousal epochs, especially within gamma frequencies. The power spectra of AG and BF revealed large power increases above expected power-law fit from standard scale-free LFP power (Fig. 1B/D; one-sample t-test, P < 0.05). This putative gamma oscillation was observed between ~ 30 and 40 Hz, and was related to arousal state, emerging primarily in high arousal epochs (Fig. 1C/E, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001).

Amygdala–basal forebrain gamma activity is more coherent in the high arousal state

Gamma coherence has long been studied as an indicator of inter-regional communication (Buzsáki and Schomburg 2015) during cognitive and perceptive processes, like sensory integration (Gray et al. 1989; Gray and Singer 1989) or selective attention (Fries et al. 2001). While it faces several challenges that can lead to difficulties with interpretation, such as contamination by spiking activity or emergence from disparate mechanisms (Ray and Maunsell 2011, 2015; Buzsáki and Wang 2012; Schneider et al. 2021), gamma coherence has emerged as an important index of brain states (independent of whether it has a causal influence on those states).

A study in cats found cortical gamma coherence during alert wake states disappeared during REM sleep (Castro et al. 2013), indicating that arousal state might be associated with gamma coherence. Stimulation of arousal-related areas such as peribrachial and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, brainstem reticular formation, and basal forebrain may cause such coherent gamma activity in the cortex (Moruzzi and Magoun 1949; Steriade et al. 1991; Cape et al. 2000; Cape and Jones 2000).

We investigated whether the arousal-related gamma oscillations observed within AG and BF were also synchronized, specifically in high arousal epochs. Indeed, we observed that during high arousal epochs AG and BF coherence prominently increased at canonical gamma frequencies, with a maximum increase of 64% at 35 Hz (Fig. 1F; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001, also see Supplemental Fig. S6). AG and BF coherence also increased during low arousal epochs at lower frequencies (<15 Hz), with a maximum increase of 22% at 8 Hz (Fig. 1F; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001). The gamma coherence increase was not a result of phasic bursts during state transitions (Supplemental Fig. S12).

We examined whether AG and BF oscillations were phase locked in order to confirm that AG–BF coherence resulted from phase synchrony between periodic oscillations and not from a purely amplitude-related phenomenon. We calculated the PLV between 32 and 39 Hz based on the narrower spectral range of the BF oscillation (Fig. 1D). AG and BF oscillations were also phase locked between 32 and 39 Hz during high arousal epochs (Fig. 1G; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001). Furthermore, this large, arousal state–related, phase coherence was specific to AG–BF. PLV between AG and BF increased during high arousal epochs more than any other region-pair; this increase was statistically significant for all region-pair comparisons with AG–BF except anterior entorhinal-9/46D (Fig. 1H; Kruskal–Wallis test, Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction, corrected P < 0.001 for all region-pairs except anterior entorhinal-9/46D which was not significant, corrected P > 0.05).

Finally, we explored the relationship of the magnitude of arousal-related synchrony between AG–BF and the structure of their anatomical connectivity. It is known that the BF strongly projects to the relatively lateral regions of the AG. We divided the AG into medial and lateral parts based on a standard macaque stereotaxic atlas (Saleem and Logothetis 2012), and our own cholinergic staining that dissociated the basolateral AG from the medial AG (Fig. 1I). Next, we normalized arousal-related PLV changes across all AG–BF pairs within each individual NHP, and for every sampled 0.1-mm3 volume in AG displayed the average PLV change (Fig. 1J, Supplemental Fig. S9). We found that lateral regions of the AG that receive densest BF inputs display larger arousal-related increases in synchrony with BF as compared to medial nuclei (Fig. 1I, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < 0.001).

Alpha coherence broadly increases in the low arousal state, except for medial temporal regions

Few studies have characterized changes in alpha coherence in local field potentials across areas with arousal state in primates. In humans, change in frontal–occipital alpha coherence was reported between eye closure and sleep (Cantero et al. 1999) and a coherent frontal alpha rhythm was linked to loss of consciousness after propofol anesthesia (Feshchenko et al. 2004; Ching et al. 2010; Vijayan et al. 2013; Weiner et al. 2023). Since alpha rhythms are thought to reflect functional inhibition or regulation (Klimesch et al. 2007; Jensen and Mazaheri 2010; Foxe and Snyder 2011), coherent alpha rhythms across cortical areas may prioritize certain processes, such as memory encoding over attentional processing of incoming sensory stimuli. However, the specific role of coherent alpha rhythms in functional inhibition has not been adequately studied, and even any spatiotemporal relationship between alpha rhythms and attentional or sensory gating remains unclear (Morrow et al. 2023).

We calculated alpha coherence between many sampled brain regions to determine whether alpha coherence between regions differs across arousal states (Fig. 2, also see Supplemental Fig. S6). Most sampled brain regions showed, on average, increased alpha (8 to 15 Hz) coherence during low arousal epochs (mean ROC between high and low arousal coherence across all region-pairs; AUC = 0.44 to 0.48; Supplemental Fig. S2). Specifically, these regions were BA 9/46D, dorsal pallidum, BA 9/46V, BA 6, BA 8d, OFC, BA 3ab, striatum, BA 45B/8v, insula, and basal forebrain (Fig. 2C; one-sample t-test, Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction, corrected P < 0.001). The general trend for alpha coherence increases appeared to be global, observed across most brain regions, whether they were nearby or distant (Fig. 2A). Additionally, global alpha coherence was higher at the beginning (first 15 s) compared to the end (last 15 s) of the low arousal state. Thus, alpha coherence may index upcoming state transitions (Fig. 2A inset, Supplemental Fig. S13).

Green hues label specific medial temporal regions. 9/46D, dorsal pallidum, 9/46V, 6, 8d, OFC, 3ab, striatum, 45B/8v, insula, and basal forebrain increased alpha coherence on average during low arousal epochs (one-sample t-test, Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction, corrected P < 0.001). Anterior-ventral medial temporal cortex, hippocampus, anterior entorhinal cortex, and posterior-ventral medial temporal cortex all decreased alpha coherence on average during low arousal epochs (one-sample t-test, Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction, corrected P < 0.001). Probability density functions (right panel) show distribution of average coherence difference across regions. No visible relationship was seen between distributions in alpha coherence differences and distributions in beta/gamma coherence differences. D) Spring-embedded graph network for visualization of alpha coherence during high and low arousal states. Each blue line represents a weighted edge of the network and indicates a coherence value >0.2 while every black dot marks a node in the network, representing a specific region. The degree centrality of a node quantifies the number of edges a particular node contributes to and the change in degree centrality between arousal states (high–low arousal) is indicated in parentheses next to the node in the low arousal state graph. The boxed inset shows changes in average edge weights between high and low arousal states for all nodes, with green hues representing specific medial temporal regions. Negative changes in degree centrality indicate that regions have more “connections” (coherence >0.2) during the low arousal state, while negative changes in edge weights indicate that the mean alpha coherence between "connections" increases during the low arousal state. A general clustering is observable during the low arousal state, quantified by negative changes in degree centrality and average edge weights for most regions. Meanwhile, medial temporal regions pull away from the main cluster during the low arousal state. Anterior-ventral medial temporal cortex and hippocampus show no change in degree centrality and posterior entorhinal cortex, anterior entorhinal cortex, and posterior-ventral medial temporal cortex all show positive changes in degree centrality. All medial temporal regions show positive changes in average edge weights between high and low arousal states. Supplemental Table S3 lists the degree centrality and average edge weights for all regions in both arousal states. High and low arousal states were operationally defined (see Methods). High arousal states reflect periods of high saccade rate, which tended to correspond with periods of higher heart rate and lower short-term heart rate variability.

However, some regions deviated from the general pattern of increased alpha coherence during low arousal epochs. Namely, the anterior-ventral medial temporal cortex, hippocampus, anterior entorhinal cortex, and posterior-ventral medial temporal cortex all showed decreases in average alpha coherence during low arousal epochs (Fig. 2C; one-sample t-test, Bonferroni multiple comparisons correction, corrected P < 0.001). Notably, medial temporal regions generally displayed similar increases in alpha power during low arousal as other brain regions (ROC between high and low arousal alpha power; mean AUC = 0.40 to 0.50; Fig. 1A, Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. S1), showing a deviation most notably in alpha coherence measures. This spatial pattern of arousal-related coherence changes was specific to lower frequencies (Fig. 2C, Supplemental Fig. S3).

Overall, these patterns of alpha coherence can be visualized on a spring-embedded graphical network representing each arousal state (Fig. 2D), with nodes representing individual regions, and edge weights representing coherence values (see Methods). Qualitatively, most regions cluster together during low arousal state compared to high arousal, but the medial temporal areas seem to pull away. We quantified state-related graph network changes using degree centrality and average edge weights (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Table S3). For any region, degree centrality represents total number of connections, while average edge weight represents the average alpha coherence of those connections. The degree centrality of all medial temporal regions either remained unchanged or decreased during low arousal epochs when compared to high arousal epochs, indicating that these regions had fewer "connections" (alpha coherence >0.2; Methods) during low arousal epochs. Additionally, the average edge weight for all medial temporal regions also decreased during low arousal epochs, indicating that these regions had reduced alpha coherence with connected regions during low arousal epochs. Meanwhile, average edge weight for all other regions increased during low arousal epochs, representing how these regions increased alpha coherence during low arousal epochs (Fig. 2D, boxed inset).

Saccades are not solely responsible for arousal state–related coherence changes

Numerous brain regions respond to and/or contribute to the control and generation of saccades in the non-human primate (Boch and Goldberg 1989; Moschovakis et al. 1996; Hikosaka et al. 2000; Snyder et al. 2000; Munoz 2002; Scudder et al. 2002) and display changes in motor-related brain rhythms (Reva and Aftanas 2004; Yuval-Greenberg et al. 2008). Since we used saccade rates to index behavioral arousal states, we sought to ensure that our observed arousal-related coherence changes were not solely due to saccades and associated sensorimotor processes. We removed 300-ms windows centered around every saccade during the high arousal state and found that the key patterns in arousal-related coherence remained. Most regions still showed qualitatively global increases in alpha coherence during low arousal epochs, with the same notable exception of medial temporal areas (Supplemental Fig. S4, also see Supplemental Fig. S1). Similarly, amygdala and basal forebrain still showed a large increase in gamma coherence (30 to 50 Hz) during high arousal epochs with a maximal increase of 53% at 35 Hz (Supplemental Fig. S5; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001), and an increased coherence at lower frequencies (<15 Hz) during low arousal epochs with a maximal increase of 21% at 5 Hz (Supplemental Fig. S5; Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this report, we describe multiple electrophysiological features characterizing changes in arousal state in non-human primates. Interestingly, we found a coherent oscillation between AG and BF within gamma frequencies that arose or increased during the high arousal state and a generally global increase in alpha coherence with the notable exception of medial temporal regions during the low arousal state.

The use of a common reference (CR) might have artificially generated coherence between two areas (by conferring a common subtracted signal). We addressed this confound through multiple avenues. First, we subtracted the common average signal from all signals on a session-by-session basis, seeking to remove any effect of CR. We further restricted our key analyses to those that would not be biased by CR in the first place. We used paired tests between arousal states during the same session, thus effects of CR in any given session should have affected both states similarly. Coherence might have also emerged nonphysiologically from intrinsic volume conduction in the brain. Volume conduction cannot explain AG–BF gamma coherence because it should have caused a general coherence in a spatial area around the source, which is not the case (as evidenced by the specificity of AG–BF coherence over other areas in similar proximity, e.g. BF–dorsal pallidum). Volume conduction is also not likely to explain the exclusion of medial temporal areas from a network of increased alpha coherence during low arousal because numerous medial temporal areas show increased alpha power at low arousal states (Fig. 1A, Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. S1).

Our definition of low and high arousal states was based on task-free physiological variables (saccade rates, which tended to correspond to changes in heart rate and heart rate variability; Supplemental Fig. S10, Supplemental Fig. S11). However, there are several other potential methods to define arousal states, including engagement in active performance of cognitive tasks. Distinct operational definitions may produce different results and necessitate further clarification of how the field defines arousal states. Other methods will also be required to further understand how arousal dynamics over many timescales mediate coherence. Additionally, we did not perform any sleep staging during this study, and therefore could not distinguish between eyes closed rest and any stage of sleep. However, eyes closed epochs were usually quite brief (<60 s), which makes it unlikely that the low arousal state consists of a large number of epochs with any deeper stage of sleep (Supplemental Fig. S8).

Gamma rhythms (~>30 Hz) are thought to index important circuit dynamics like feedforward information processing (Buzsáki and Wang 2012; Van Kerkoerle et al. 2014). Furthermore, the inter-regional coupling of gamma activity, often measured through coherence, is thought to reflect coordinated interactions between distant and nearby brain areas responsible for behavior and cognition (Bressler et al. 1993; Chrobak and Buzsáki 1998; Engel et al. 2001; Buzsáki and Schomburg 2015).

In our study, AG and BF showed relatively strengthened gamma oscillations during high arousal states (Fig. 1B–E). The emergence of arousal-related gamma oscillations in the AG and BF is supported by previous studies that reported gamma oscillations in both the AG and BF (Sterman and Clemente 1962a, 1962b; Feng et al. 2019; Headley et al. 2021). Specifically, gamma oscillations were found in AG during fear expression and vigilance (Stujenske et al. 2014; Kanta et al. 2019) and in BF during high arousal that diminished with quiescence (Sterman and Clemente 1962a, 1962b).

The gamma oscillations in AG and BF were uniquely coherent and showed relatively greater increases in phase-locking during high arousal states than any other sampled region-pair (Fig. 1F–H). This AG–BF coupling could emerge in part through the structural connectivity between the two regions, which is supported by our observations that arousal-related PLV increases were largest in the lateral regions of the AG (which have known connectivity with the BF) (Fig. 1I–J, 25 to 27). However, this synchrony may also emerge from a common input to both AG and BF, or from neurons that project both locally and between the two regions (Schneider et al. 2021). Both the AG and BF process the salience of external sensory stimuli (O’Neill et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019, 2021) and have distinct projections. While the AG projects strongly to temporal and occipital cortical areas that process visual stimuli (Amaral and Price 1984; Iwai and Yukie 1987), the BF projects widely across cortex (Mesulam et al. 1983a; Rye et al. 1984; Saper 1984; Luiten et al. 1987), including cortical areas with sparse AG projections (Amaral and Price 1984; Barbas and de Olmos 1990; Baizer et al. 1993). Therefore, AG–BF coherence may reflect or index a coordinated arousal modulation of stimulus-locked events (Monosov 2020), like the attentional processing of incoming sensory stimuli. One prior study investigated how the AG–BF pathway guides reward-driven attention (Peck and Daniel Salzman 2014). They showed that BF might receive motivational significance of visual stimuli from the AG while the BF could convey this information to frontal and parietal cortical areas not strongly innervated by the AG.

A large body of work has contributed to a hypothesis that alpha oscillations index a form of selective inhibition or regulation of online cognitive processing (Jasper 1936; Volavka et al. 1967; Legewie et al. 1969; Klimesch et al. 2007; Van Kerkoerle et al. 2014; Chapeton et al. 2019), where the influence of sensory processes on behavior could be modulated (Foxe and Snyder 2011) and/or context/behavior irrelevant circuits are inhibited (Jensen and Mazaheri 2010). Alpha power is often inversely correlated with spiking activity (Haegens et al. 2011; Chapeton et al. 2019), BOLD fMRI signal (Goldman et al. 2002), and is associated with the suppression of gamma power (Spaak et al. 2012). One human EEG study reported that alpha power differences between eyes open and eyes closed states showed global changes that closely reflected autonomic arousal (Barry et al. 2007).

Our results indicate that the low arousal (rest-like) state, in which the monkeys’ eyes were closed, is indexed by a broad increase of alpha coherence (Fig. 2A/C) in many brain areas including prominently frontal cortical regions involved in control of online behavior, such as attention and motor planning (Weinrich and Wise 1982; Weinrich et al. 1984; Szczepanski et al. 2010). It was particularly striking that medial temporal areas did not show similar strong relative increases in alpha coherence, despite having some increases in alpha power during low arousal states (Fig. 2A/C, Supplemental Fig. S1). Of note, power quantifies oscillatory strength measured at an electrode site, while coherence captures the relationship of this activity between sites. Therefore, even if alpha power increases during low arousal states in several regions, it is not a requirement that the power increase is coherent between regions. These medial temporal regions are involved in declarative memory encoding and retrieval (e.g. hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, perirhinal cortex), for which arousal state plays an important role (Stickgold 2005). Differentiation of these regions from a general pattern of increased alpha coherence may thereby reflect memory-related processing during low arousal states. In this framework, arousal-related modulations may generate a coordinated network state associated with inhibitory alpha rhythms during low arousal that suppress sensory processing and motor output, prioritizing declarative memory functions.

Interestingly, recent modeling studies and intracranial EEG studies in humans have shown that thalamocortical alpha rhythms after propofol-induced unconsciousness (Ching et al. 2010; Vijayan et al. 2013) can be structured into spatial networks, with a frontal network showing coherent alpha oscillations that increase sharply after unconsciousness (Weiner et al. 2023). The authors hypothesize that these coherent alpha dynamics contribute to the unconscious state by interfering with top-down control of memory, attention, and executive function, which may parallel our discussion on the functional consequences of the coherent alpha network that indexes the naturally occurring low arousal state in non-human primates.

However, we do not make any functional claims regarding LFP rhythms in our report. In our own view, any potential role of alpha oscillations in the coordinated inhibition of numerous, specific cognitive processes remains unclear. Nor do we make claims about other low-frequency bands (Supplemental Fig. S7). In general, concrete interpretations of oscillations and coherence will require extensive additional investigation. In particular, whether their circuit origins are similar across different brain areas and/or how they impact neural computations remains to be fully elucidated.

Arousal state is regulated by the brain through multiple, diffuse, widely projecting pathways that subsequently play important roles in modulating sensory perception, motor activity, and emotional expression (Pfaff et al. 2008). These pathways consist of ascending and descending components that result in cortical activation and sensory-motor responsiveness (Jones 2003). The major anatomical regions involved in these pathways are located in the brainstem (e.g. “reticular formation”), hypothalamus, thalamus, and basal forebrain. Manipulating arousal state or arousal circuitry causes very broad effects on neural activity across many brain regions (Moruzzi and Magoun 1949; Cape and Jones 2000). For example, inactivating basal forebrain reduced the global component of cortical BOLD signal ipsilaterally in an arousal-dependent manner (Turchi et al. 2018), caffeine was associated with striking differences in overall network structure of BOLD functional connectivity (Laumann et al. 2015; Poldrack et al. 2015), and arousal activity has even been claimed to be closely related to brain-wide BOLD functional connectivity structure (Raut et al. 2021). Our results provide further evidence and insight into the importance of arousal state in modulating activity and coherence across many regions, which may have additional implications for task-free analyses.
