Preoperative gut microbial network alterations and BCAA-Related metabolic disturbance in postoperative delirium after cardiac surgery: a prospective matched multi-omic study
Authors: Congmin Lyu, Ziying Wang, Ruojin Zhao, Haiping Zhao, Sheng Liu, Hong Lian, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostoperative delirium (POD) is a frequent neuropsychiatric complication after cardiac surgery, yet the biological basis of individual susceptibility remains unclear. In this prospective cohort study, 317 adults undergoing elective on-pump cardiac surgery were enrolled and followed for POD during the first 7 postoperative days. Thirty patients who developed POD were then matched 1:1 with 30 non-POD controls by age, sex, and primary diagnosis for multi-omic analyses. Preoperative fecal samples were collected from the first bowel movement after admission and before prophylactic antibiotic administration, and postoperative fecal samples were collected from the first postoperative bowel movement. Paired fecal samples underwent shotgun metagenomic sequencing, and perioperative serum samples underwent untargeted metabolomic profiling. Preoperatively, α- and β-diversity were comparable between groups, but patients who subsequently developed POD exhibited a less connected and less integrated microbial network structure. Postoperatively, gut microbial composition differed significantly between groups (PERMANOVA R² = 0.053, P < 0.001). Metagenomic profiling identified 35 differentially abundant species and 16 differentially enriched KEGG level 3 pathways, with POD-associated features showing inferred functional shifts toward amino-acid catabolism, including branched-chain amino acid (BCAA)-related pathways. Untargeted metabolomics demonstrated marked perioperative remodeling in both groups, but POD was associated with a 27-metabolite panel characterized predominantly by lower postoperative levels or impaired recovery, with pathway enrichment converging on valine, leucine, and isoleucine metabolism. Integrative analyses further linked POD-associated microbial taxa with amino-acid catabolic pathways and lower levels of BCAA-related serum metabolites. These findings suggest that POD is associated with preoperative alterations in microbial network organization and a postoperative microbiome–metabolome disturbance pattern centered on amino-acid metabolism, particularly the BCAA axis.Subject terms: Comparative genomics, Prognostic markers, Clinical genetics

Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common and clinically significant neuropsychiatric complication that typically occurs within the first week after surgery. Its incidence is particularly high following cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, affecting approximately 25–50% of patients and contributing to prolonged hospitalization, long-term cognitive impairment, and increased mortality [1–3]. Although POD is widely recognized as a multifactorial syndrome, its biological determinants remain incompletely defined. Proposed mechanisms include neuroinflammatory activation, neurotransmitter imbalance, disruption of blood–brain barrier integrity, and systemic metabolic stress [4]. Consistent with the potential role of perioperative inflammation in postoperative brain dysfunction, interventional evidence from cardiac surgery has shown that attenuation of the inflammatory response may reduce the risk of early postoperative cognitive decline [5]. However, the upstream factors that shape individual vulnerability to these perioperative insults remain poorly understood.

The gut microbiome has emerged as an important regulator of host metabolism, immune tone, and barrier function, and is increasingly implicated in gut–brain interactions relevant to perioperative neurocognitive disorders [6–8]. Through microbial metabolites and functional interactions, the intestinal ecosystem can influence systemic substrate availability, inflammatory signaling, and host physiological resilience. In surgical settings, accumulating evidence suggests that cardiopulmonary bypass and perioperative stress can perturb gut microbial composition and metabolic output. Nevertheless, the contribution of gut microbial ecology to POD remains insufficiently characterized, particularly with respect to how microbial community organization may relate to host metabolic disturbances.

Importantly, prior investigations have largely relied on 16S rRNA sequencing and cross-sectional postoperative sampling, limiting taxonomic resolution and constraining functional interpretation. [9, 10] Most studies have focused on community-level diversity measures or differential taxa, without examining structural properties of microbial interaction networks or integrating inferred microbial functional potential with host metabolic trajectories. As a result, it remains unclear whether susceptibility to POD may be preceded by a distinct preoperative ecological configuration of the gut microbiome, and whether postoperative microbial restructuring is linked to metabolic pathways relevant to host neurocognitive vulnerability. In particular, diversity and composition do not necessarily capture the organizational properties of microbial communities that may determine their stability under stress.

In this prospective cohort study with a 1:1 matched comparison of patients undergoing elective on-pump cardiac surgery, we performed shotgun metagenomic sequencing of paired preoperative and early postoperative fecal samples together with perioperative serum metabolomic profiling. By integrating taxonomic, functional, metabolic, and ecological network analyses, we sought to identify microbiome–metabolome features associated with susceptibility to POD. We hypothesized that patients who develop POD would exhibit distinct preoperative and postoperative gut microbiome features, accompanied by coordinated host metabolic alterations.

Methods

Study design, patient selection, and POD assessment

This prospective cohort study with a 1:1 matched comparison was conducted at Fuwai Hospital, Beijing, China, between September 2022 and April 2023. Consecutive adult patients undergoing elective on-pump cardiac surgery were screened for eligibility. Eligible participants were aged >18 years and scheduled for elective cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, including coronary artery bypass grafting, valve surgery, or septal myectomy. Preoperative cognitive status was assessed during enrollment based on medical records, clinical history, and clinical interview, with family-reported information used when needed. Patients with a documented history of delirium, dementia, cognitive impairment, or communication disorder were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included recent antibiotic or probiotic use, pre-existing psychiatric disease or antipsychotic treatment, major gastrointestinal disease or prior gastrointestinal surgery, recent intensive care unit admission or mechanical ventilation, previous cardiac surgery, significant renal or hepatic dysfunction, and other conditions judged unsuitable by the investigators.

All eligible participants provided written informed consent upon admission and were prospectively enrolled before biospecimen collection. A total of 317 patients were included in the prospective cohort. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital (Approval No. 2021-1466) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

POD was assessed twice daily during the first 7 postoperative days using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU). [11, 12] Before each assessment, the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) was used to determine assessability; patients with RASS scores of −4 or −5 were reassessed when the RASS score was ≥−3. Additional CAM-ICU evaluations were performed when delirium-like symptoms were observed. Assessments were conducted by trained clinicians who had completed formal CAM-ICU training and were independent of downstream biological analyses.

All enrolled patients were followed prospectively for POD during the first 7 postoperative days. After outcome ascertainment, 30 patients with POD were identified and assigned to the POD group. A 1:1 matched non-POD group was then selected from the remaining patients based on sex, primary diagnosis, and age. Matching was performed after sample collection and before multi-omic analyses.

Surgery, anesthesia, and cardiopulmonary bypass management

All patients underwent elective open-chest cardiac surgery under general anesthesia with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), including coronary artery bypass grafting, valve surgery, or septal myectomy, according to the primary diagnosis. Anesthetic induction, maintenance, and intraoperative monitoring were performed according to routine institutional protocols at Fuwai Hospital. CPB was managed using standard institutional procedures, with aortic cross-clamping and myocardial protection applied as required by the specific procedure. Overall intraoperative management followed routine institutional practice across the study cohort.

Clinical data and biospecimen collection

Perioperative clinical data, including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and operative variables, were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical record system.

Biospecimen collection was performed prospectively in the enrolled cohort according to the predefined study schedule. Preoperative fecal samples were collected from the first bowel movement after admission and before prophylactic antibiotic administration. Postoperative fecal samples were collected from the first postoperative bowel movement. All fecal samples were immediately snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C until metagenomic sequencing.

Peripheral venous blood was collected in the fasting state on the morning of the second hospital day before surgery and again on postoperative day 1. After clotting and centrifugation, serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until untargeted metabolomic profiling. Only samples meeting predefined quality-control criteria were included in downstream analyses.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatic processing

Total microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the OMEGA Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and quality were assessed using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 2000 platform with paired-end 150-bp reads. Raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession number PRJNA1164032.

Raw reads were quality filtered and stripped of adapter sequences to generate high-quality clean reads. Host-derived reads were removed by alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38). Taxonomic classification of non-host reads was performed using Kraken2 [13] against a RefSeq-derived database, and species-level relative abundance profiles were used for downstream analyses.

For functional profiling, metagenomic assembly was performed with MEGAHIT [14], followed by gene prediction using MetaGeneMark [15]. Predicted coding sequences were clustered into a nonredundant gene catalog using MMseqs2 [16]. High-quality reads were mapped back to predicted genes using Salmon [17] to estimate gene abundance. Functional annotation was performed against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, and gene abundances were aggregated to KEGG level 3 pathways for downstream analyses.

Untargeted serum metabolomics

Untargeted serum metabolomic profiling was performed by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC–HRMS; Q Exactive HF-X, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Serum samples were extracted by organic solvent precipitation with an internal standard and analyzed in both positive and negative ionization modes according to standardized procedures. Pooled quality-control samples and reagent blanks were included throughout the analytical run to monitor signal stability and technical variation, and sample injection order was randomized between study groups.

Raw LC–MS data were processed using Progenesis QI for peak detection, retention-time alignment, and peak integration. Metabolite annotation was based on MS and MS/MS spectral matching against public and in-house libraries, including HMDB and METLIN. Annotated metabolite features passing quality-control filters were retained for downstream analysis.

Statistical and integrative analyses

Clinical and microbiome analyses

Clinical analyses were performed using SPSS and R. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Alpha diversity was assessed using the Chao1 and Shannon indices. Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Group-level differences were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), and homogeneity of dispersion was assessed by PERMDISP. Differentially abundant taxa and KEGG pathways were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) [18], with thresholds of Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.05 and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score >2.0. Preoperative and postoperative comparisons were performed separately.

Species-level co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for each group and time point using the 50 most abundant species. Only robust associations (|r | >0.6 and P < 0.05) identified by FastSpar with 1000 bootstrap iterations were retained. Networks were visualized in Cytoscape, and topological properties were calculated using the igraph package in R.

Untargeted metabolomic analyses

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was used to visualize overall metabolomic variation. Postoperative between-group comparisons were performed to identify differential metabolites in the postoperative cross-sectional analysis. To assess differences in perioperative metabolic trajectories, change values were calculated for each metabolite as postoperative minus preoperative abundance (Δ = postoperative − preoperative), and between-group comparisons were performed on these Δ values.

Metabolites identified in both the postoperative cross-sectional and trajectory-based analyses were retained for downstream interpretation and KEGG-based pathway enrichment. Enrichment results were summarized using pathway-level statistics and visualized as bubble plots.

Integrative analyses

Integrative analyses were performed using selected postoperative differential taxa, differentially enriched KEGG level 3 pathways, and retained POD-associated serum metabolites. Microbiome abundance data were transformed after addition of a small pseudocount to account for compositionality, and metabolite intensities were log-transformed and scaled before analysis. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess species–pathway and species–metabolite associations across all subjects. Correlation patterns were visualized as clustered heatmaps using the pheatmap package in R.

Result

Cohort assembly, matching, and baseline comparability

Among 317 prospectively enrolled patients who underwent biospecimen collection according to the predefined study schedule, 30 developed POD within 7 postoperative days. After outcome ascertainment, 30 non-POD controls were selected from the remaining cohort by matching on sex, age, and primary diagnosis, yielding 30 patients in each group for analyses (Table 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly comparable between groups. The mean age was 64.7 ± 6.9 years in the non-POD group and 65.2 ± 7.8 years in the POD group, with 13 females (43.3%) in each group. The distribution of primary diagnoses, including ischemic heart disease, valvular heart disease, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, was similar between groups, and the proportions of major surgical procedures were also balanced. CPB and aortic cross-clamp (ACC) times were numerically longer in the POD group, but these differences did not reach statistical significance (CPB: 151.8 ± 43.0 vs. 126.2 ± 47.5 min, P = 0.053; ACC: 110.2 ± 29.7 vs. 93.0 ± 35.3 min, P = 0.071).

Perioperative changes in microbial diversity and community composition

Metagenomic sequencing generated high-quality datasets for 27 preoperative and 26 postoperative non-POD samples, and for 28 preoperative and 25 postoperative POD samples, with detailed sequencing metrics provided in Supplementary Table 1. Across all samples, taxonomic annotation identified 30 phyla, 398 genera, and 1177 species-level taxa. The gut microbiota was dominated by Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota, which together accounted for more than 98% of the total community abundance.

At baseline, neither α-diversity nor β-diversity differed significantly between POD and non-POD patients, indicating comparable preoperative microbial diversity and overall community composition (Fig. 1A, B). After surgery, α-diversity remained broadly similar between groups, whereas β-diversity showed significant group-wise separation (PERMANOVA, R² = 0.0527, P < 0.001; Fig. 1C, D). PERMDISP analysis did not detect differences in dispersion (P = 0.603), supporting that the observed separation reflected compositional divergence rather than unequal within-group variability.

Perioperative gut microbial diversity and community composition in patients with and without postoperative delirium (POD).

A, B Preoperative microbial profiles. A α-diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon) did not differ significantly between POD and non-POD patients. B Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances showed no significant difference in preoperative community composition between groups (PERMANOVA, R² = 0.02, P = 0.624). C, D Postoperative microbial profiles. C Postoperative α-diversity remained comparable between groups. D PCoA showed significant separation in postoperative community composition between POD and non-POD patients (PERMANOVA, R² = 0.0527, P < 0.001).

Reduced preoperative network integration and greater postoperative fragmentation in POD

To further characterize microbial community organization beyond conventional diversity metrics, species-level co-occurrence networks were constructed using the top 50 most abundant taxa in the POD and non-POD groups at the preoperative and postoperative timepoints (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S2).

Preoperative and postoperative species-level gut microbial co-occurrence networks in patients with and without postoperative delirium (POD).

Species-level co-occurrence networks were constructed using FastSpar based on the top 50 most abundant species in each group and timepoint (|r | > 0.6, P < 0.05). A, B Preoperative networks of the non-POD and POD groups, respectively. C, D Postoperative networks of the non-POD and POD groups, respectively. Node colors indicate bacterial phyla, and edges represent significant pairwise correlations between species. Compared with the non-POD networks, the POD networks showed lower connectivity preoperatively and a more contracted and fragmented topology postoperatively.

Preoperatively, the POD and non-POD networks were similar in size (34 vs. 37 nodes), but the POD network contained fewer edges (58 vs. 81), with a longer average path length (3.51 vs. 2.53) and a larger diameter (6.75 vs. 3.37). Betweenness centrality was also lower in the POD network than in the non-POD network (2775.86 vs. 6553.57), consistent with reduced network integration.

Postoperatively, the non-POD microbiome maintained a larger and more connected network structure (42 nodes and 94 edges), whereas the POD network was markedly contracted, comprising only 18 nodes and 14 edges. Compared with the postoperative non-POD network, the postoperative POD network also showed lower betweenness centrality (68.00 vs. 7840.40) and transitivity (0.375 vs. 0.472), together with higher modularity (0.741 vs. 0.653) and a greater number of clusters (6 vs. 4), consistent with greater network fragmentation.

Together, these findings suggest that network-level disorganization was already detectable before surgery in patients who later developed POD and became more pronounced postoperatively.

Taxonomic shifts and inferred functional changes toward amino-acid catabolism in POD

Differential taxa associated with POD

To identify microbial taxa underlying the postoperative divergence in community composition, differential abundance analysis was performed using LEfSe. In total, 64 taxa across multiple taxonomic ranks differed significantly between POD and non-POD patients (P < 0.05, LDA score > 2.0; Supplementary Table S3). Their hierarchical relationships and effect sizes are shown in the cladogram and corresponding LDA plots (Fig. 3).

Differentially abundant taxa between postoperative delirium (POD) and non-POD groups identified by LEfSe analysis.

A Cladogram showing the phylogenetic distribution of taxa differentially enriched between groups. Blue and orange nodes indicate taxa enriched in the non-POD and POD groups, respectively. B Bar plot of linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores (log10) for taxa with LDA score > 2.0. Taxonomic ranks are abbreviated as follows: k kingdom, p phylum, c class, o order, f family, g genus, s species.

At the species level, 35 discriminatory taxa defined distinct postoperative microbial signatures between groups. The POD group was enriched in several species associated with dysbiotic or opportunistic microbial states, including Escherichia coli, Enterococcus hirae, Desulfovibrio fairfieldensis, and Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum. In contrast, the non-POD group showed higher abundance of several commensal taxa commonly linked to fermentative metabolism and gut microbial homeostasis, including Coprococcus comes, Anaerostipes hadrus, as well as Bifidobacterium and Prevotella species.

Together, these findings indicate that the postoperative divergence between POD and non-POD patients was accompanied by distinct species-level taxonomic configurations.

Inferred functional shifts toward amino-acid catabolism in POD

To examine whether the observed taxonomic differences were accompanied by shifts in inferred microbial functional potential, metagenomic features were annotated to KEGG Orthologs (KOs) and summarized at the pathway level. At the community-wide level, β-diversity based on KO functional profiles did not differ significantly between POD and non-POD patients (PERMANOVA, R² = 0.02, P = 0.724; Fig. 4A), indicating that overall functional composition remained broadly similar despite taxonomic divergence. To identify finer-scale functional differences, LEfSe analysis was subsequently performed at the pathway level and identified 19 differentially enriched KEGG pathways across levels 1–3 between groups, including 16 level 3 pathways (LDA score > 2.0, P < 0.05; Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S4; Supplementary Figure S1).

Blue and orange bars indicate pathways enriched in the non-POD and POD groups, respectively. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores (log10) indicate the effect size of each pathway.

Notably, the POD-associated microbiome showed enrichment of amino-acid catabolic pathways, including valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation and lysine degradation, consistent with greater inferred potential for amino-acid catabolism. Several additional pathways annotated to KEGG categories related to stress responses or disease processes were also more abundant in the POD group. In contrast, the non-POD group was enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, thiamine metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and polyketide sugar unit biosynthesis, consistent with a comparatively preserved biosynthetic and energetic functional profile.

Taken together, these results suggest that, despite limited global functional separation, the POD-associated microbiome displayed pathway-level shifts consistent with greater amino-acid catabolic potential, particularly along the branched-chain amino acid–related axis.

Perioperative metabolomic remodeling identifies a refined POD-associated metabolite panel

To determine whether the inferred microbial functional shifts were accompanied by corresponding host metabolic alterations, untargeted serum metabolomics was performed. A total of 647 annotated metabolites were retained for downstream analyses, and QC samples showed stable analytical performance and tight clustering throughout the batch run (Supplementary Figure S2A–C). At baseline, unsupervised PCA did not reveal evident separation between patients who subsequently developed POD and non-POD controls (Fig. 5A), indicating broadly comparable preoperative metabolic profiles.

Perioperative serum metabolomic profiling identifies a refined POD-associated metabolite panel.

A Principal component analysis (PCA) of preoperative serum metabolomic profiles showed no evident separation between POD and non-POD patients. B PCA of postoperative serum metabolomic profiles showed partial separation between groups. C Venn diagram showing the overlap between 58 metabolites with differential perioperative trajectories and 80 metabolites with nominal postoperative between-group differences, yielding a refined panel of 27 metabolites associated with POD. D KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the 27 metabolites. Bubble size indicates the number of mapped metabolites, and bubble color indicates the enrichment significance level.

Both POD and non-POD patients exhibited marked perioperative metabolic remodeling, with clear separation between preoperative and postoperative samples in multivariate space (Supplementary Figure S2D–E), consistent with a strong systemic effect of surgical stress on circulating metabolites.

In contrast, postoperative cross-sectional analysis demonstrated divergence between POD and non-POD patients. PCA showed partial separation between groups (Fig. 5B). Univariate analysis identified 80 nominally differential metabolites (P < 0.05, Supplementary Table S5).

To distinguish POD-associated metabolic signals from the broader perioperative response to surgery, perioperative change values (Δ = postoperative − preoperative) were calculated for each metabolite. This trajectory-based analysis identified 58 metabolites with differential perioperative responses between POD and non-POD patients (Supplementary Table S6).

Intersection of the trajectory-associated metabolite set with the postoperative differential metabolite set yielded a refined panel of 27 high-confidence metabolites associated with POD across both analytical frameworks (Fig. 5C; Table 2). All 27 metabolites showed concordant directions of change across the two analyses, with 26 showing lower postoperative levels or impaired recovery in POD patients.

Pathway enrichment analysis of this 27-metabolite panel identified valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis as the most significantly enriched pathway, while several additional pathways related to amino-acid, lipid, and bile acid metabolism showed nominal enrichment (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S7). Given that most metabolites in this panel were reduced or showed impaired postoperative recovery in POD patients, these findings suggest that POD was associated with a more specific metabolic signature primarily involving disruption of BCAA-related metabolic homeostasis.

Integrated associations among POD-associated taxa, inferred microbial pathways, and serum metabolites

To assess whether the taxonomic shifts, inferred microbial functional changes, and host metabolic alterations identified above were aligned along a common biological axis, integrative correlation analyses were performed.

Correlation analysis between the 35 differentially abundant species and the 16 differentially enriched inferred KEGG level 3 pathways revealed distinct association patterns for POD-enriched and non-POD–enriched taxa (Fig. 6A). Several POD-enriched species, including Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Ligilactobacillus saerimneri, and Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum, were positively associated with valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, and some also showed positive associations with lysine degradation. In contrast, several taxa enriched in non-POD patients, including Anaerostipes hadrus, Prevotella buccalis, and Phocaeicola plebeius, showed weaker or opposite associations with these catabolic functions and were more positively linked to pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, thiamine metabolism, and arginine biosynthesis. Overall, these association patterns were directionally aligned with greater BCAA-related catabolic potential among POD-associated taxa.

We next examined whether these 35 differential species were also associated with the 27 refined serum metabolites identified by both postoperative cross-sectional and trajectory-based analyses. Across all subjects, structured association patterns were observed between microbial taxa and circulating metabolites (Fig. 6B). Several POD-enriched taxa showed inverse correlations with BCAA-related metabolites, including isoleucine and N-acetyl-leucine, whereas several non-POD–enriched taxa were positively associated with metabolites related to lipid and bile acid metabolism. Overall, the taxonomic pattern was directionally aligned with both greater inferred BCAA-related catabolic potential and lower levels of BCAA-related serum metabolites.

Together, these integrative analyses suggested a BCAA-centered microbiome–metabolome association pattern in POD, linking POD-associated taxa with greater inferred amino-acid catabolic potential and lower levels of BCAA-related metabolites.

Discussion

This study provides an integrated longitudinal analysis of gut microbial alterations and host metabolic changes in patients undergoing on-pump cardiac surgery. The results suggest that POD is associated with a pattern characterized by preoperative alterations in microbial network organization, more pronounced postoperative network fragmentation, inferred pathway-level shifts toward amino-acid catabolism, and disturbance of BCAA-related host metabolism. Taken together, these findings link microbial community organization with host metabolic remodeling in POD and suggest that microbiome-associated vulnerability may already be present before surgery.

A notable finding in this study was that preoperative differences between groups were apparent in microbial network topology, but not in conventional diversity measures. This distinction is important because diversity-based metrics primarily describe richness, evenness, and overall compositional dissimilarity, whereas co-occurrence networks capture organizational features of microbial communities that are relevant to stability under perturbation. In the present study, patients who subsequently developed POD showed gut microbial networks with fewer connections, lower centrality, and reduced overall integration before surgery, despite broadly comparable preoperative α- and β-diversity. From this perspective, the preoperative POD microbiome was not characterized by overt dysbiosis in taxonomic composition, but rather by a less integrated ecological configuration. After surgery, this pattern became more pronounced, with marked contraction and fragmentation of the POD-associated networks, consistent with reduced structural stability. Similar disturbances in microbial network architecture have been described in critically ill populations and after major surgical interventions [19, 20], although most previous studies have emphasized diversity changes rather than topological organization. Our findings therefore suggest that preoperative alterations in microbial network organization may represent an underrecognized ecological state associated with postoperative microbial disorganization and host metabolic perturbation in POD.

The postoperative microbial changes observed in POD were notable not simply because taxa differed between groups, but because these shifts appeared to follow a consistent ecological pattern. Taxa enriched in POD, including Escherichia coli, Enterococcus hirae, and Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum, have been linked in previous studies to dysbiotic or metabolically stressed gut environments [21–23], whereas taxa enriched in non-POD patients are more often associated with fermentative or homeostasis-associated communities. Most previous perioperative microbiome studies have interpreted postoperative dysbiosis primarily in taxonomic terms [9, 24], whereas the present findings suggest that, in POD, taxonomic restructuring may be more meaningfully understood through its functional orientation. In this context, the concurrent enrichment of inferred amino-acid catabolic pathways in POD is particularly notable. These findings suggest that the postoperative microbiome associated with POD showed inferred pathway-level shifts consistent with greater amino-acid catabolic potential, especially along the BCAA-related axis. This interpretation may also help explain why taxa such as Lacticaseibacillus spp., although often regarded as beneficial commensals, do not necessarily indicate a protective ecological state in this setting; their roles appear to be context dependent and may differ under inflammatory or metabolically constrained conditions. [25–29] Although these pathway-level findings remain inferential, their consistency with the taxonomic pattern suggests that postoperative microbial changes in POD were aligned with amino-acid catabolic bias and may be relevant to the host metabolic disturbances observed subsequently.

The metabolomic data further suggest that POD was associated with a selective disruption of amino-acid homeostasis rather than a nonspecific postoperative metabolic response. Although both groups underwent marked perioperative metabolic remodeling, the refined 27-metabolite panel showed a clear directional profile, with most metabolites displaying lower postoperative levels or impaired recovery in POD. Within this panel, BCAA-related metabolites were particularly prominent, and pathway enrichment repeatedly converged on valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, because pathway enrichment in untargeted metabolomics does not necessarily imply directional activation of that pathway, but rather indicates that the observed abnormalities cluster within the corresponding biochemical network. Considered together with the predominantly downward pattern of the identified metabolites, these findings are more consistent with disruption of BCAA-related homeostasis than with activation or suppression of a single biosynthetic program. This distinction is important because circulating BCAAs influence cerebral amino-acid precursor availability by competing with aromatic amino acids for transport across the blood–brain barrier via LAT1, [30–32] and have also been implicated in immunometabolic signaling under stress conditions. [33–35] In this context, the metabolomic changes observed in POD appear to converge on a biologically relevant axis linking peripheral amino-acid balance to processes potentially related to neurocognitive vulnerability. The multi-omic pattern observed here was similarly directionally consistent: POD-associated microbial restructuring was aligned with inferred amino-acid catabolic bias, while the host metabolomic profile pointed toward lower levels of BCAA-related metabolites. Although these data do not establish causality or define the direction of influence between microbial and host compartments, they support the possibility that altered BCAA balance represents an important biochemical component of POD. By contrast, the relatively preserved lipid- and bile acid–related associations observed in non-POD patients may reflect a more stable perioperative metabolic state. [36–38]

From a clinical and translational perspective, these findings highlight two directions that may warrant further study. First, preoperative microbial network architecture may capture vulnerability that is not apparent from conventional diversity measures alone and could therefore be explored as a candidate feature for perioperative risk stratification in larger, prospective cohorts. Second, the convergence on BCAA-related microbial and host metabolic alterations suggests that amino-acid homeostasis warrants further mechanistic and interventional study. An important next step will be to determine whether perioperative nutritional strategies or microbiome-directed approaches can modify the metabolic trajectories associated with POD. At present, however, these implications should remain provisional. Whether such strategies can influence delirium risk or improve postoperative neurocognitive outcomes will require validation in adequately powered prospective studies with mechanistic readouts.

Additional analyses of postoperative cytokines, intestinal barrier function markers, and SCFAs are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Most of these measures did not show clear between-group differences within the early postoperative sampling window, although serum butyrate and valerate were higher in the POD group.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the modest sample size and single-center design limit generalisability and may affect the stability of network-based and cross-omic analyses. Moreover, because formal neuropsychological screening was not performed preoperatively, undiagnosed mild cognitive impairment could not be completely ruled out. Second, the functional results derived from metagenomic annotation reflect inferred microbial potential rather than direct measurements of transcriptional activity or metabolic flux. Third, serum metabolomic profiles and fecal metagenomic features arise from different biological compartments, and the correlations observed here do not establish the direction of influence between them. In addition, central neurochemical readouts, blood–brain barrier function, and other direct measures of brain-related pathophysiology were not assessed. Postoperative microbiome features should also be interpreted in the context of shared perioperative antibiotic exposure and other in-hospital factors that may have influenced postoperative microbial composition. Finally, as an observational study, the present work cannot determine whether the microbial and metabolic alterations identified here are causal contributors to POD or secondary features of the perioperative stress response. Future studies incorporating denser longitudinal sampling, metatranscriptomic or flux-based approaches, and experimental validation will be needed to clarify the mechanistic significance of this axis.

In summary, POD in this cohort was associated with preoperative alterations in microbial network organization, greater postoperative network fragmentation, and a BCAA-centered microbiome–metabolome disturbance pattern. These findings suggest that microbiome-associated vulnerability to POD may be reflected not only in postoperative taxonomic shifts, but also in the preoperative organization of the microbial community and its relationship to host amino-acid homeostasis. This integrated perspective provides a focused basis for future mechanistic studies of how microbial community organization and amino-acid homeostasis may shape susceptibility to POD in the perioperative setting.
