Development of a Three-Source Convergent Risk Assessment Tool for Amyloidosis Screening in Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Patients
Authors: Komatsu David Edward, Badalamente Marie Anne, Grabowski Kathryn, Paulus Megan, Muhlrad Samantha, Meo Ann Marie, Gorevic Peter Dov, Vlay Stephen, Hurst Lawrence Colwyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAmyloid transthyretin cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a progressive and frequently fatal disease that is under-recognized. A diagnosis of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) elevates the risk of receiving a diagnosis of ATTR-CM, with a diagnosis of CTS often recognized 5–10 years before ATTR-CM is clinically diagnosed. The recent development of the availability of disease-modifying therapies, which appear to be most effective during the early deposition phase of amyloidogenesis, has underscored the need to define the “red flags” that may signal the diagnosis of ATTR-CM. This study sought to develop a risk assessment tool to identify CTS patients at elevated risk for amyloidosis.MethodsWe developed a 34-item questionnaire and administered it to three cohorts: (1) cardiac amyloid patients; (2) CTS patients; and (3) general orthopedic patients. Demographics, symptoms, and diagnoses were compared using chi-squared tests. Subsequently, three independent evidence streams were prespecified for weight assignment: (1) absolute prevalence differences from the primary cohort; 2) risk differences from an 800,662-subject TriNetX study; and 3) prospective tenosynovial biopsy data. Prespecified tier criteria were applied to assign point weights, with concordance across streams assessed as the primary methodological validity metric. A five-tier weighted scoring system was developed, comprising four progressively higher risk categories and a highest risk category that unequivocally recommends tenosynovial biopsy.ResultsEighteen risk factors demonstrated statistically significant prevalence differences between the amyloidosis and CTS cohorts (P < .05). The highest were irregular heartbeat/arrhythmia, chronic fatigue, and shortness of breath. Concordance between the questionnaire-derived risk hierarchy and the TriNetX study was 95%, and a four-tier scoring system was established.ConclusionsWe developed an evidence-based, weighted risk assessment tool to identify CTS patients at elevated risk for amyloidosis and guide tenosynovial biopsy decisions during carpal tunnel release.Clinical relevanceThis tool has the potential to identify CTS patient at risk for cardiac amyloidosis that could benefit from biopsy at the time of surgery.

Systemic amyloidosis is a protein misfolding disease caused by extracellular deposition of fibrillar proteins, leading to progressive dysfunction, organ failure, and death.1,2 Amyloid transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis presents as either wild-type (ATTRwt) or hereditary (ATTRv) caused by mutations, notably the V122I variant of which 3% to 4% of African Americans are carriers.3,4 ATTR cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) can result in heart failure and ultimately death.5 The approval of tafamidis in 2019 represented the first disease-modifying therapy for ATTR-CM, with significant reductions in mortality and cardiovascular events reported in the pivotal ATTR-ACT trial.6 However, tafamidis and newer drugs (eg, acoramidis, amvuttra) cannot reverse amyloid deposition. As such, clinical benefit is maximized when initiated before significant cardiac fibrosis or remodeling.7 Unfortunately, time to diagnosis remains a major barrier, with mean delays of 6.1 years for ATTRwt-CM and 5.7 years for ATTRv-CM.8

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) has emerged as an important early clinical marker associated with systemic ATTR amyloidosis. Sperry et al9 published a landmark study showing that 10.2% of patients ≥50 years undergoing carpal tunnel release had amyloid deposition within the tenosynovium, transverse carpal tunnel ligament, or local vasculature, with bilateral CTS present in 100% of biopsy-positive cases. Subsequent studies have confirmed amyloid prevalences from 10% to 29% in CTS populations.10,11 Although these observations demonstrate a strong relationship between CTS and ATTR amyloidosis, they do not establish a causal relationship. Additionally, most patients who have a positive tenosynovial biopsy do not go on to have cardiac involvement, prompting the need for further risk stratification of this subset of patients.12 Nevertheless, CTS is frequently recognized 5–10 years before the clinical diagnosis of ATTR amyloidosis and may serve as a critical clinical clue prompting consideration of ATTR amyloidosis in appropriate patients.13,14

In a large epidemiological study, Grabowski et al15 examined 800,662 CTS patients from the TriNetX research network and identified 30 pre-existing conditions associated with an increased likelihood of an amyloid diagnosis within 6 years of a CTS diagnosis. In males ≥50 years, heart failure conferred the greatest risk, followed by atrial fibrillation, family history of heart disease, and lumbar spinal stenosis. These data provide a quantitative foundation for evidence-based risk stratification. Despite the growing evidence linking CTS to amyloidosis, no validated screening tool currently exists to guide biopsy decisions during surgery. Universal tenosynovial biopsy of all CTS patients aged ≥50 years would detect the most cases, but at the cost of ∼90% unnecessary biopsies, creating a substantial economic and pathological burden.16 Conversely, reliance on clinical decision making alone results in inconsistent screening and missed diagnoses. A structured, evidence-based scoring instrument is needed to identify which patients are at highest risk and would most benefit from biopsy at the time of surgery.

The purpose of this study was to develop a weighted risk assessment tool, integrating primary questionnaire data from three patient cohorts with large-scale epidemiological data. We hypothesized that a three-source convergent evidence synthesis approach could produce a scoring system capable of substantially reducing the number needed to biopsy while maintaining high diagnostic sensitivity.

Materials and Methods

Study design and data sources

This study employed a three-source convergent evidence synthesis design integrating three independent data streams: (1) a primary 34-item questionnaire given to three patient cohorts during 2025; (2) sex-stratified risk difference data from a TriNetX study of 800,662 CTS patients; and (3) tenosynovial biopsy prevalence data from Sperry et al.9

Patient cohorts and enrollment

Three patient cohorts were enrolled from academic hand surgery, orthopedic, cardiology, and rheumatology practices at a single academic medical center. Institutional review board approval was obtained before patient enrollment.

Amyloidosis cohort (n = 25)

Patients with confirmed systemic amyloidosis were recruited from cardiology and rheumatology clinics. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of amyloidosis by Congo red staining and/or mass spectrometry. The cohort was predominantly male, elderly, and Caucasian. This cohort represented the reference standard against which risk factor prevalence was measured.

Carpal tunnel syndrome cohort (n = 100)

Patients with CTS requiring surgical release were enrolled from a hand service. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of CTS based on clinical examination and/or electrodiagnostic studies. Patients with diagnosed amyloidosis were excluded. The cohort was predominantly female, younger than the amyloidosis cohort, and predominantly Caucasian.

Orthopedic control cohort (n = 100)

Patients without CTS or amyloidosis were recruited from a general orthopedic practice as a control group. This cohort was predominantly female. Seventeen percent of orthopedic controls reported a history of CTS.

Questionnaire administration

After obtaining informed consent, a paper copy of the 34-item questionnaire was completed by the subjects in the waiting area. The questionnaire was organized into six domains: (1) demographics and CTS (ie, age, self-reported sex, race, hand dominance, bilateral CTS, prior CTS surgery, duration of symptoms); 2) cardiac (ie, shortness of breath, irregular heartbeat, bilateral lower extremity swelling, orthostatic dizziness, fatigue, weakness, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, coronary artery disease, valvular disease, pacemaker); (3) musculoskeletal (ie, lumbar or cervical spinal stenosis, trigger finger, rotator cuff disease, biceps tendon rupture, joint replacement history, chronic back pain); (4) neurological (ie, peripheral neuropathy, tingling feet, muscle weakness); (5) constitutional and systemic (ie, unexplained weight loss, sleep apnea, eye floaters, lingual nodules or macroglossia); and (6) personal and family (ie, diabetes, thyroid disease, family history of heart disease, irritable bowel syndrome/gastrointestinal symptoms). Binary items were coded as yes/no. Data were entered using double-entry verification into an Excel spreadsheet.

Statistical analysis and three-source convergence methodology

The primary statistical approach was descriptive epidemiology with prevalence comparison. For each risk factor, the proportion of patients reporting a positive response was calculated for each cohort. Prevalence differences between the amyloidosis cohort and the CTS cohorts were computed as absolute percentage differences. Statistical significance of group differences was assessed using Pearson chi-square analysis with Fisher exact correction applied when any expected cell frequency was less than 5. All tests were two-tailed with P < .05 considered significant. Risk factors were ranked by absolute prevalence difference (amyloidosis minus CTS).

Weight assignment for the scoring system used a triangulation methodology combining the three sources: (1) absolute prevalence differences from primary cohort; (2) absolute risk differences per 100,000 from the TriNetX study; and (3) prospective biopsy data.9,15 Because the amyloidosis cohort comprised only 25 patients, formal logistic regression was not pursued. The widely cited minimum of 10 events per predictor variable would require 250 amyloidosis cases for a 25-item model.17 Weight tiers were assigned using prespecified, mutually exclusive criteria, with each factor placed in the highest tier for which it qualified by either the prevalence-difference or the TriNetX criterion: Tier 1 (5 points, prevalence difference ≥ 30% or TriNetX > 600/100,000), Tier 2 (4 points, 25% to <30% or >300 to ≤600/100,000), Tier 3 (3 points, 20% to <25% or >200 to ≤300/100,000), Tier 4 (2 points, 10% to <20% with P < .05 or >100 to ≤200/100,000), and Tier 5 (1 point, <10% with P < .05, significant in TriNetX only, or 50 to ≤100/100,000). The criterion bands are contiguous and nonoverlapping, so that any given prevalence difference or absolute risk value maps to exactly one tier. Concordance between questionnaire-derived rankings and TriNetX epidemiological rankings was assessed by calculating the percentage of factors with consistent directional placement within one tier.

Three-source convergence and concordance assessment

The scoring system was developed using a prespecified three-source convergence methodology to address the fundamental statistical limitation of rare disease tool development at a single. Rather than relying on a single underpowered cohort or assigning weights by clinical judgment, we triangulated across the three independent datasets. Tier criteria were defined before final data analysis. A concordance metric was defined as the percentage of factors for which both Stream 1 and Stream 2 produced independent tier placements within one tier of each other. This metric quantifies the degree to which two methodologically orthogonal evidence sources, which differ in sample size by a factor of 3,581, agree on the relative importance of each risk factor. Under a binomial model with conservative assumptions (50% per-factor chance concordance), observing 18 or more concordant results in 19 independent comparisons yields P < .001, establishing that observed concordance cannot be attributed to chance.

Bilateral CTS was present in 52% of amyloidosis patients compared with 68.7% of CTS patients. Although the direction of the difference was reversed (amyloidosis patients were less likely to report bilateral CTS than CTS patients as a group), this is explained by the higher overall CTS prevalence in the CTS cohort by definition and by the Sperry et al9 finding that bilateral CTS is the cardinal feature of biopsy-positive cases, underscoring that in the CTS context, bilateral involvement is the high-risk signal, not unilateral CTS. Factors that did not demonstrate significant prevalence differences between the amyloidosis and CTS cohorts included hypertension, thyroid disease, rotator cuff tear, and pacemaker. These factors were retained in the lower scoring tiers based on significance in the TriNetX analysis.

Automatic biopsy override criteria were proposed for three high-specificity findings regardless of score: (1) confirmed cardiac amyloidosis or positive amyloid biopsy at any site; (2) confirmed ATTR genetic mutation (eg, V122I); and (3) new diagnosis of heart failure in males ≥65 years with CTS and bilateral digit involvement. These criteria reflect findings where the a priori probability of systemic amyloidosis is sufficiently high to justify immediate cardiac amyloidosis workup.

Discussion

This study reports on our development of an evidence-based, weighted risk assessment tool to guide tenosynovial biopsy decisions during carpal tunnel release. By integrating primary questionnaire data from 225 patients across three cohorts with independent data from over 800,000 CTS patients and a landmark biopsy study, we developed a scoring system with 95% concordance and an estimated capacity to reduce the NNB from 10 to 3–5 at the high-risk threshold.

The findings from our primary cohort are consistent with prior literature on risk factors for ATTR-CM. The predominance of cardiac symptoms, including irregular heartbeat/arrhythmia, shortness of breath, lower extremity swelling, orthostatic dizziness, heart failure, and cardiomyopathy, aligns with the known pathophysiology of ATTR-CM, in which amyloid deposition within the myocardium and cardiac conduction system results in cardiac dysfunction prior to heart failure.20 The higher prevalence of these symptoms in the amyloidosis cohort suggests that our questionnaire is capturing the expected clinical phenotype of ATTR-CM.

The strong signal for musculoskeletal factors, particularly lumbar spinal stenosis, trigger finger, biceps tendon rupture, and joint replacement, is consistent with the known predilection of amyloid fibrils for connective tissue and tendon sheaths throughout the body.21 However, many of these conditions are also common in older adults, and their increased prevalence in our cohort may reflect a combination of amyloid-related pathology, age-related degenerative changes, and comorbid disease burden. The observation that 84% of amyloidosis patients reported chronic back pain compared with 54% of CTS patients and 50% of orthopedic controls may reflect the high background prevalence of lumbar spinal stenosis in the elderly amyloidosis cohort. Lumbar spinal stenosis is a recognized manifestation of ATTR amyloidosis because of amyloid deposition in the ligamentum flavum and surrounding connective tissues, and epidural amyloid deposition has been identified at autopsy in a large majority of patients with ATTRwt.22 Although lumbar spinal stenosis has been associated with ATTR amyloidosis, the present study was not designed to distinguish symptoms attributable to amyloid deposition from those related to aging or other musculoskeletal comorbidities.

Our finding that eye floaters had a 35% increased prevalence in the amyloidosis cohort compared to the CTS cohort is of particular interest. Vitreous amyloid deposition occurs in ATTRv amyloidosis because of local TTR production by the retinal pigment epithelium, and floaters are a recognized symptom of ocular involvement, particularly in hereditary forms.23,24 The inclusion of eye floaters as a Tier 4 risk factor represents a novel addition to current screening algorithms. Although our study cannot determine if eye floaters in our patient cohort are due to amyloid deposition or posterior vitreous detachment from aging, our finding is still valid to include in our risk assessment algorithm.

Another important observation is the reversal of the bilateral CTS signal in our primary analysis. Although bilateral CTS was present in 52% of amyloidosis patients, 68.7% of CTS patients also reported bilateral involvement, yielding a negative prevalence difference. This appears paradoxical but is explained by the structure of our cohort. Specifically, in the CTS cohort, all patients have CTS, and many present bilaterally. The clinically relevant question is not “does bilateral CTS distinguish amyloidosis patients from CTS patients overall?” but rather “within a CTS patient population, does bilateral involvement increase the probability of amyloidosis?” Sperry et al9 definitively answered this question, demonstrating that 100% of biopsy-positive patients had bilateral CTS. The three-source convergence methodology is specifically designed to resolve exactly this type of cohort-structure paradox: the Sperry biopsy finding and the TriNetX signal together override the cohort-level comparison, appropriately assigning bilateral CTS to Tier 2.

The triangulation methodology addresses the fundamental statistical challenge of risk tool development for rare diseases. With 25 confirmed amyloidosis patients, we only achieved one-tenth of the logistic regression minimum for the 25-variable model, making formal regression-derived weights statistically infeasible.17 Therefore, three-source convergence is a reasonable alternative. Rather than assigning weights by clinical judgment or relying on a single underpowered cohort, we triangulated across three independent datasets. The 95% concordance between the primary questionnaire-derived hierarchy and the TriNetX-derived hierarchy is not a descriptive summary statistic, it is a formal measure of convergent validity. Under a binomial model, the probability that two methodologically independent risk hierarchies would agree in tier placement 18 of 19 times by chance is P < .001, regardless of whether the chance concordance rate is assumed to be 20% (uniform random across five tiers) or 50% (generous). This convergent validity retroactively anchors the 25-patient primary cohort result: the risk hierarchy it produces is 95% identical to the hierarchy produced by 800,662 patients followed for 6 years.

This work builds directly on the tiered diagnostic algorithm proposed by Donnelly et al25 and the risk factor quantification by the TriNetX study.15 Donnelly et al25 proposed a two-tier clinical algorithm based on demographics and comorbidities, whereas the TriNetX study provided the first large-scale quantification of individual risk factor contributions. Our contribution is the translation of this evidence base into a practical, weighted, point-of-care scoring tool. The recently described algorithm by Ruesch et al26 evaluated similar criteria in a pilot study using Congo red staining of intraoperative tissue and identified six patients with positive staining, though the study concluded that neither routine biopsy nor early disease-modifying treatment was currently cost-effective. Our tool is specifically designed to address the cost-effectiveness concern by identifying the subset of patients most likely to have positive biopsies.

Several study limitations should be noted. The amyloidosis cohort was small, and its predominantly male, older, and Caucasian composition may not be generalizable to the ATTR-CM population. Further, the predominantly Caucasian composition of all three cohorts limits assessment of race-specific risk, particularly for African Americans with the V122I variant. In addition, the scoring weights have not yet been validated against biopsy outcomes. The cross-sectional design also prevents assessment of temporal relationships between symptoms and diagnosis, and the additive scoring model assumes risk factor independence. However, several variables, particularly cardiac symptoms and diagnoses, are clinically correlated, which may result in our model overweighing patients with multiple manifestations of the same underlying disease process. Additionally, several “red flag” symptoms included in our questionnaire are common age-related conditions that may coexist with ATTM-CR independent of amyloid deposition. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causality. Additionally, patients in our CTS cohort were identified based on clinician diagnosis as documented in the medical record by treating orthopedic surgeons and/or electrodiagnostic testing, which reflects real-world practice patterns, but does not ensure uniform use of electrodiagnostic studies. Therefore, although unlikely, there is potential for patients in this cohort to not truly have CTS.

Future directions include a prospective multicenter validation study with universal tenosynovial biopsy at the time of carpal tunnel release. This design would generate the first prospective dataset to calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for the scoring instrument. However, this validation study could evaluate collinearity among variables and determine whether alternative weighting schemes improve model performance. Additionally, future studies could examine whether the incorporation of demographic data, such as age and sex, into the scoring system improves its validity. Currently, our tool should be considered a risk stratification framework derived from available observational evidence. Prospective evaluation is needed to determine its clinical utility, generalizability, and predictive performance in diverse patient populations.

Hand surgeons occupy a unique and potentially life-saving position in the diagnostic pathway for ATTR-CM. The brief interval of elective carpal tunnel surgery represents a critical window during which systematic risk screening and selective tenosynovial biopsy could enable timely initiation of disease-modifying therapy before irreversible cardiac damage occurs and the validation of this tool would represent a major advance for their patients.
