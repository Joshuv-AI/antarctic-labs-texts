Noise-adapted Quantum Error Correction for Non-Markovian Noise
Debjyoti Biswas, Shrikant Utagi, Prabha Mandayam

Abstract
We consider the problem of quantum error correction (QEC) for non-Markovian noise. Using the well known Petz recovery map, we first show that conditions for approximate QEC can be easily generalized for the case of non-Markovian noise, in the strong coupling regime where the noise map becomes non-completely-positive at intermediate times. While certain approximate QEC schemes are ineffective against quantum non-Markovian noise, in the sense that the fidelity vanishes in finite time, the Petz map adapted to non-Markovian noise uniquely safeguards the code space even at the maximum noise limit. Focusing on the case of non-Markovian amplitude damping noise, we further show that the non-Markovian Petz map also outperforms the standard, stabilizer-based QEC code. Since implementing such a non-Markovian map poses practical challenges, we also construct a Markovian Petz map that achieves similar performance, with only a slight compromise on the fidelity.

When a quantum system undergoes decoherence

, which occur due to either drift

In this work, we study the efficacy of standard QEC and approximate, noise-adapted QEC in the presence of non-Markovian noise. We consider the approach of channel-adapted QEC based on the Petz recovery map

,

, wherein the recovery operation exactly adapts to the error (or, Kraus) operators. We first show that this approach leads to a generalization of the known approximate QEC conditions for the case of not completely positive (NCP) noise maps. This then motivates us to study the efficacy of the Petz map in correcting for non-Markovian errors.

We then move on to the specific example of non-Markovian amplitude damping noise, which is an important physical example of an NCP map

VI

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Non-Markovianity of quantum dynamical maps

We begin with a brief discussion on the physical origins for non-Markovianity in the evolution of open quantum systems. There are a number of approximations that go into formulating a master equation that describes open system evolution, namely, (i) the initial factorization assumption, where the joint system-environment (S-E) state is assumed to be of the product form (ii) the Born-Markovian approximation, in which a large separation between system and environment time-scale is assumed and (iii) the rotating-wave approximation (RWA), in which fast (or, counter)-rotating terms in the interaction Hamiltonian are ignored. Relaxing (i) or (iii) leads to the system evolution map being non-completely-positive (NCP)
[
7
]
.

However, retaining (i) and (iii) but relaxing (ii) leads to a time-dependent master equation for the system density operator
\rho(t)
, that takes the form of a time-dependent GKSL-like equation (in the canonical form)
[
31
]
.

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(t)[\rho(t)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\Gamma_{j}(t)\bigg(L_{j}(t)\rho(t)L_{j}^{\dagger}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}(t)L_{j}(t),\rho(t)\}\bigg).

(1)

where
\{L_{j}(t)\}
are called the jump operators,
\{\Gamma_{j}(t)\}
are the canonical decay rates, both of which maybe time-dependent in general, and
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
is the anti-commutator for any two operators
A
and
B
.
\mathcal{L}(t)
denotes the time-dependent Lindbladian operator associated with the open system dynamics. The process in Eq. (
1
) is termed time-
in
homogeneous Markovian when all the canonical decay rates
\Gamma_{j}(t)
are positive for all times, in which case all the approximations from (i)-(iii) are assumed to be in place, implying that the environment operators are
not
delta-correlated in time
[
6
,
7
]
.

The solution to the master equation is given by a dynamical map of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{T}\exp\left\{\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\mathcal{L}(\tau)d\tau\right\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator and
\tau
is intermediate time. When (ii) is lifted, the map may be non-Markovian with at least one of the canonical decay rates
\Gamma(t)
becoming negative at certain interval of time-evolution
[
30
,
1
,
4
,
31
]
. The quantum map
\mathcal{E}
corresponding to a given Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
is also often referred to as a quantum noise channel.

Given a space of bounded operators
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, a linear map
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be completely positive (CP) if and only if its corresponding
Choi
matrix
\chi
is positive:

\displaystyle\chi(t_{2},t_{0})=\left(\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})\otimes I\right)[\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}]\geq 0,

(3)

where
\ket{\Psi}=\ket{00}+\ket{11}
is an unnormalized Bell state. When this is guaranteed, the map can be written in the Kraus form as

\displaystyle\rho(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)[\rho(0)]=\sum_{j}E_{j}(t)\rho(0)E^{\dagger}_{j}(t),

(4)

where
E_{j}(t)
are the Kraus operators, and the initial time
t_{0}=0
for simplicity. Throughout the paper, time-dependence of Kraus operator is implicit and we drop the parameter in parenthesis henceforth.

One of the ways to look at non-Markovian dynamics is via the notion of
divisibility
of the quantum dynamical maps corresponding to the noise
[
4
]
. A quantum map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})
, taking a density matrix from
t_{0}
to
t_{2}
, is divisible if it can be concatenated as,

\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})=\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{1})\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{0})

(5)

for all
t_{2}\geq t_{1}\geq t_{0}
. This equality breaks down for non-Markovian noise in the sense that the intermediate map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{1})=\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})\mathcal{E}^{-1}(t_{1},t_{0})
is NCP. A prototypical example of such a channel is non-Markovian amplitude damping discussed in Appendix
A
. For this noise channel, the full map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})
from
t_{0}
to
t_{2}
is CPTP, but the intermediate map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{1})
is not. For the noise model we consider in this work, even though the intermediate map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{1})
is NCP, it is still linear, Hermiticity-preserving and trace-preserving (HPTP).

It is important to mention that there exists a hierarchy of divisibility conditions for CPTP maps
[
32
]
. Two main conditions relevant for non-Markovian dynamics are CP-indivisibility and P-indivisibility. A CPTP map is said to be P-
in
divisible if the intermediate map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{1})
is not even positive. In this sense, a P-indivisible noise is necessarily CP-indivisible but the converse may not be true
[
33
]
. However, for a channel with single jump operator
L
in Eq. (
1
), for a system of dimension two, it is known that CP-divisibility and P-divisibility coincide
[
6
,
32
,
34
]
, which suffices for us in the present work. This concept becomes relevant to us in Section
V.2
.

II.2
Quantum Error Correction and the Petz map

Quantum error correction (QEC) proceeds by encoding the information into a subspace of a larger Hilbert space. A QEC protocol is characterized by the codespace
\mathcal{C}
into which the information is encoded, as well as the recovery operation
\mathcal{R}
which is in general a CPTP map.

A codespace
\mathcal{C}
is said to be
perfectly correctable
for a noise channel
\mathcal{E}
with Kraus operators
\{E_{i}\}
if there exists a recovery map
\mathcal{R}
such that
(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E})[\rho]\propto\rho
, for all states
\rho
on the codespace. The well known Knill-Laflamme (KL) conditions provide a set of necessary and sufficient conditions for a noise channel to be perfectly correctable on a codespace
\mathcal{C}
, as follows
[
35
]
.

\displaystyle PE_{i}^{\dagger}E_{j}P

\displaystyle=\alpha_{ij}P,

(6)

where
P
is the projector onto the codespace
\mathcal{C}
and
\alpha_{ij}
are complex scalars that form the elements of a Hermitian matrix.

On the other hand, when there exists a recovery map
\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}[\rho]
is close in fidelity to the original state
\rho
, for all states in a codespace
\mathcal{C}
, the noise channel
\mathcal{E}
is said to be
approximately correctable
on
\mathcal{C}

[
13
]
. The search for good approximate quantum codes naturally leads to the idea of
noise-adapted
QEC, where the code and recovery map are tailored to a specific noise channel, in contrast to standard quantum codes that are designed to perfectly correct for Pauli errors. Noise-adapted QEC serves as an interesting route to resource-efficient protocols, when a specific noise is known to be dominant in a given physical setting.

Several variants noise-adapted recovery maps have been proposed in the literature, based on analytical
[
13
,
15
]
and numerical
[
36
]
constructions. Here, we will primarily focus on a specific form of the recovery channel known as the Petz map
[
37
,
12
]
. Corresponding to a quantum code
\mathcal{C}
and noise map
\mathcal{E}
, the Petz recovery channel is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{P,\mathcal{E}}[.]

\displaystyle=P\,\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}\,\left[\mathcal{E}[P]^{-1/2}(.)\mathcal{E}[P]^{-1/2}\right]\,P

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}PE_{i}^{\dagger}\left(\mathcal{E}[P]^{-1/2}(.)\mathcal{E}[P]^{-1/2}\right)E_{i}P,

(7)

where
P
is the projector onto the codespace
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{E}[P]=\sum_{i}E_{i}PE_{i}^{\dagger}
denotes the action of the noise map on the codespace. This map is known to provide a universal and near-optimal strategy for any code used and noise channel
[
15
,
38
]
. The near-optimality of the Petz recovery channel is stated with respect to the worst-case fidelity on the codespace, defined as,

\displaystyle F^{2}_{\rm min}

\displaystyle=\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\,\,F^{2}(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}[\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\,],|\psi\rangle)

(8)

where
\,F^{2}(\rho,|\psi\rangle)=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle
is the fidelity between the states
\rho
and
|\psi\rangle
.

II.3
QEC for HPTP noise maps

We now briefly review some of the recent studies on QEC for non-Markovian errors. In a Markovian process, the noise strength reaches maximum only in the asymptotic time limit, ensuring that we are within the assumptions of standard quantum error correction
[
39
]
. Conversely, in a non-Markovian process, the noise strength can fluctuate significantly, from zero to its maximum value, within a finite time domain, causing the majority voting scheme, and subsequently the error correction protocol, to fail. In Sec.
V
we show that this is indeed the case, using the example of non-Markovian amplitude damping.

In this work, we specifically focus on HPTP noise channels, for which the map could NCP at intermediate times. We first note that for any HPTP map, there exists an operator sum-difference representation
[
18
,
40
,
20
,
21
]
of the form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\rm HPTP}_{\tau\rightarrow t}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}{\rm sign}(i)E_{i}\rho E^{\dagger}_{i},

(9)

for
t\geq\tau
, where the index
i
corresponds to
i^{\rm th}
eigenvalue of the Choi matrix (Eq. (
3
)) of the corresponding dynamical map. Once again, we note that all operators are time-dependent unless otherwise stated.

A simple example of such a map would be the inverse of a phase-flip channel, which is an NCP-HPTP map
[
18
]
. In fact, any linear NCP map can be represented as a difference of two CP maps
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}

[
41
]
. For example, one can find maps
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
that lead to an operator sum-difference decomposition for the non-Markovian amplitude damping channel
[
40
]
.

It was recently argued
[
21
]
that the original Knill-Laflamme conditions given in Eq. (
6
) are sufficient for non-Markovian errors characterized by Kraus operators
\{\text{sign}(i),E_{i}(t)\}
. This argument implicitly assumes that the matrix of coefficients
\alpha_{ij}
in Eq. (
6
) is always diagonalizable. However, it has been shown earlier
[
20
]
that the diagonal form of the KL conditions
PF^{\dagger}_{i}F_{j}P=d_{ii}\delta_{ij}P
would not be satisfied if
\mathcal{E}_{2}[P\rho P]\neq 0
, that is, the code space is mapped outside the positivity domain of the noise map, thus causing the errors to be indistinguishable. Moreover, the
\alpha_{ij}
matrix becomes pseudo-Hermitian for NCP noise and there is no guarantee that there exists a (pseudo-)unitary operator that diagonalizes it
[
42
]
, thus invalidating the conclusions of Ref.
[
9
]
.

Finally, we make a few remarks regarding the foundational issues surrounding the notion of quantum non-Markovianity and the dynamical map formalism of open quantum systems. Recent developments
[
28
,
43
]
suggest that an
operational
notion of (non-)Markovianity renders the two-time map approach described above insufficient in describing non-Markovian stochastic process in full generality. However, for our current purposes it suffices to note that the full map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})
describing the overall timescale of the non-Markovian process is still a CPTP map and can be viewed as a black box. Furthermore, for the non-Markovian amplitude damping considered in this paper, there exists an exactly solvable time-dependent GKSL-type equation (
1
) thanks to conservation of photon numbers, which means that we have exact time-dependent Kraus operators at our disposal. In this light, given the full noise profile, we will see that the above discussed operational issues have no negative effects on channel-adapted QEC.

III
Fidelity bounds on exact QEC for non-Markovian noise

We begin by studying the effects of non-Markovian noise within the framework of standard, general-purpose quantum error correction. Since the non-Markovian noise regime involves rapid decay and recurrence in the population and coherence levels in the density matrix, this in turn implies that the noise strength rapidly reaches its maximum strength within a short time, thus raising the question of the efficacy of QEC in this regime. We first write down an explicit expression for the worst-case fidelity, within the framework of
perfect
QEC where some subset of errors are assumed to be perfectly correctable by the code.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a HPTP map
\mathcal{E}[.]=\sum\limits_{k}{\rm sign}(k)A_{k}[.]A_{k}^{\dagger}
and a codespace
\mathcal{C}
with associated projector
P
. Let
\{E_{i}\}
denote the subset of errors perfectly correctable by the code
\mathcal{C}
and
\{F_{j}\}
denote the subset of errors not correctable by the code
\mathcal{C}
. The worst-case fidelity after QEC is then given by,

\displaystyle F^{2}_{\rm min}=1-

\displaystyle\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}\left(\langle\psi|M^{\dagger}_{kl}M_{kl}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|M_{kl}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right),

(10)

with scalars
\{\alpha_{kk}\}
and matrices
\{M_{kl}\}
defined by,

\displaystyle PE_{k}^{\dagger}E_{l}P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{kl}P,

\displaystyle PE_{k}^{\dagger}F_{l}P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle PM_{kl}P.

(11)

Proof.

We first rewrite the noise process
\mathcal{E}
in terms of the correctable and un-correctable errors as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[.]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k}{\rm sign}(k)E_{k}[.]E_{k}^{\dagger}+\sum\limits_{l}{\rm sign}(l)F_{l}[.]F_{l}^{\dagger}.

(12)

For the correctable errors
\{E_{k}\}
, we can construct a CPTP recovery map
\mathcal{R}
with the Kraus operators
R_{k}=PU_{k}^{\dagger}
, where
\{U_{k}\}
are unitaries appearing from the polar decomposition of the operators
E_{k}P=U_{k}\sqrt{PE_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}P}
. Therefore, the recovery operators
R_{k}
take the form,

\displaystyle R_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{kk}}}PE_{k}^{\dagger}.

(13)

The recovered state corresponding to an initial encoded state
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
is thus given by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}\,[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\,]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}PE_{k}^{\dagger}E_{l}P|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|E_{l}^{\dagger}E_{k}P

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}PE_{k}^{\dagger}F_{l}P|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|F_{l}^{\dagger}E_{k}P.

(14)

From Eq. (
14
), we can derive the fidelity between the error-corrected state and the initial state as,

\displaystyle F^{2}\left(\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}[\,|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\,,|\psi\rangle]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)|\alpha_{kl}|^{2}}{\alpha_{kk}}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}|\langle\psi|M_{kl}|\psi\rangle|^{2}.

(15)

This equality follows directly from Eqs. (
11
). The trace preserving (TP) condition for the noise process implies,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k}{\rm sign}(k)E_{k}^{\dagger}E_{k}+\sum\limits_{k}{\rm sign}(k)F_{k}^{\dagger}F_{k}=I

(16)

Using this and the TP condition
\sum\limits_{k}R_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}=I
for the recovery, we get,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k,l}\left({\rm sign}(k)E_{k}^{\dagger}R_{l}^{\dagger}R_{l}E_{k}+{\rm sign}(k)F_{k}^{\dagger}R_{l}^{\dagger}R_{l}F_{k}\right)=I.

(17)

Using the form of the recovery operators in Eq.(
13
) and Eqs. (
11
) the above equation takes the form,

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=P\sum\limits_{k,l}\left({\rm sign}(k)E_{k}^{\dagger}R_{l}^{\dagger}R_{l}E_{k}+{\rm sign}(k)F_{k}^{\dagger}R_{l}^{\dagger}R_{l}F_{k}\right)P,

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)|\alpha_{kl}|^{2}P}{\alpha_{kk}}+\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}M_{kl}^{\dagger}M_{kl}.

(18)

Therefore, using the Eq. (
18
), we can rewrite the fidelity expression in Eq. (
15
) to get the desired expression for the worst-case fidelity,

\displaystyle F^{2}_{\rm min}=1-

\displaystyle\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}\left(\langle\psi|M^{\dagger}_{kl}M_{kl}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|M_{kl}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right).

(19)

∎

The fidelity loss, defined as
\eta=1-F^{2}_{\rm min}
, therefore has the form,

\displaystyle\eta=\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\sum\limits_{k,l}\frac{{\rm sign}(l)}{\alpha_{kk}}\left(\langle\psi|M^{\dagger}_{kl}M_{kl}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|M_{kl}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right).

(20)

From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we observe that the expression in the parenthesis in the Eq.(
20
), remains positive for all the time. The
{\rm sign}(l)
term however, becomes negative for some error operators during those intermediate times for which there is information back flow. This clearly shows that the fidelity (or, correspondingly, the fidelity loss) exhibits an oscillatory behavior in the presence of non-Markovian noise, even within the framework of perfect QEC.

We can see this explicitly, by noting that for certain time intervals, the infidelity
\eta
takes the following form,

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\sum\limits_{k,l_{1}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{kk}}\left(\langle\psi|M^{\dagger}_{kl_{1}}M_{kl_{1}}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|M_{kl_{1}}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\underset{|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm min}\sum\limits_{k,l_{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{kk}}\left(\langle\psi|M^{\dagger}_{kl_{2}}M_{kl_{2}}|\psi\rangle-|\langle\psi|M_{kl_{2}}|\psi\rangle|^{2}\right),

(21)

where
{\rm sign}(l_{1})>0
and
{\rm sign}(l_{2})<0
. In the above expression the second term corresponds to a drop in the fidelity loss and hence a revival of fidelity. The first term, on the other hand, corresponds to damping of the peaks in the revivals. We demonstrate this with an example in Sec.
V
, while performing the stabilizer-measurement based recovery for non-Markovian amplitude-damping noise.

Finally, one may easily verify that as the noise strength approaches its minimum value, the fidelity loss reaches its minimum. In fact, it was previously shown that any stabilizer code would meet a similar end for non-Markovian decoherence if a stabilizer recovery is performed in a continuous time QEC setting
[
17
]
. In what follows, we show how our work points to the possibility of error correcting non-Markovian noise without having the fidelity drop to zero at finite times.

IV
Noise-adapted QEC for non-Markovian errors

We now consider two kinds of noise-adapted recovery maps tailored to non-Markovian noise and characterize their performance via the worst-case fidelity. Note that since HPTP errors are not accessible experimentally during intermediate times when the map in non-CP, a noise-adapted recovery for HPTP maps is not practically implementable. However, in what follows, we will show how the Petz recovery map adapts to the
full
map
\mathcal{E}(t_{2},t_{0})
and corrects for all intermediate HPTP errors in such a way that it takes advantage of the information backflow occurring naturally due to non-Markovian decoherence over a period of time. The recoverability of Petz channel in such a case can be attributed to the fact that Petz-based recovery does not rely on syndrome extraction via a feedback loop.

One might be led to assume that a recovery operation such as the Petz channel, when adapted to a non-Markovian noise, must also be non-Markovian in order to achieve a good fidelity. However, as we show later, even a Markovian Petz channel suffices for recovery with pretty good fidelity, although in this case the Petz channel does not exactly adapt to the noise in the sense that it adapts to the Kraus operator structure but not to the time-dependent noise parameter. The price to pay, however, is that the total
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}
channel need not be unital. The worst-case fidelity therefore suffers a drop, an aspect which is further discussed in Sec.
V.1
.

Recall from Eq. (
7
) that for a noise channel with Kraus operators
\{E_{i}\}
and a codespace with projector
P
, the Petz channel is the map with Kraus operators
R_{i}=PE_{i}^{\dagger}(t)\mathcal{E}[P]^{-1/2}
. In the case of non-Markovian noise, we may consider two variants of the Petz recovery map. Naively, we could let the Petz recovery operators exactly adapt to the noise operators at all times. This would result in a
non-Markovian Petz channel
, denoted as
\mathcal{R}^{NM}
, one that violates the CP-divisibility condition in Eq. (
5
) (
\mathcal{R}(t_{2},t_{0})=\mathcal{R}(t_{2},t_{1})\mathcal{R}(t_{1},t_{0})

\displaystyle E_{1}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\

V.1
Consequences of the non-unitality of the QEC channel

Here, we explain the early recurrence of worst-case fidelity, before the point in time at which information backflow begins, observed in Figs.
1
and
2
, for the Markovian Petz recovery map. This additional oscillation in the fidelity is due to the combined effect of the structure of the code and the non-unitality of the QEC channel and is not due to non-Markovianity. This is demonstrated in Appendix
B

\mathcal{R}^{NM}\circ\mathcal{E}
channel is unital. These conclusions become apparent when viewed via the matrix representation of a CPTP map as follows.

We consider the composite CPTP map
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{E}\equiv\Phi
. The action of
\Phi
on a density operator
\rho
, can be represented by a matrix
M
with the matrix elements

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}[O_{i}\Phi[O_{j}]],

(38)

where
\{O_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d^{2}-1}
forms an Hilbert-Schmidt basis.

The matrix
M
is real because the the operators
O_{i}
are hermitian and the map
\Phi
is CP. We can write any state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{C}
in the Hilbert-Schmidt basis as

\displaystyle\rho=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}=\frac{1}{d}(I+\vec{O}.\vec{r}),

(39)

where

\vec{r}
is a real
(d^{2}-1)

[
44
]

38

and
\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle F^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}(r^{\rm T}.M.r).

(40)

Therefore, for a code
\mathcal{C}
of dimension

M
becomes a
d\times d
matrix with following form

\displaystyle M=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&0\\
\hline\cr\vec{\tau}&\quad T_{{d^{2}-1}\times{d^{2}-1}}\\
\end{array}\right).

Note that if the channel is unital onto the codespace
\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
\Phi[P]=P
, then
\vec{\tau}=\vec{0}
. Therefore in that case the fidelity will depend upon the eigenvalues of the matrix
T_{{d^{2}-1}\times{d^{2}-1}}

[
44
]
. However the fidelity for a non-unital channel is

\displaystyle F^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}(r^{\rm T}.T.r+\vec{\tau}.\vec{r}).

(43)

Now for a qubit code (
d=2

O_{i}

[
44

and

– the projector onto
\mathcal{C}

from the Eq.(

\Phi

VI
Conclusion

We study the problem of quantum error correction for non-Markovian noise, specifically focusing on the class of Hermiticity-preserving trace-preserving (HPTP) maps which are not completely positive (CP) for certain intermediate times. We first provide fidelity bounds for both standard QEC as well as noise-adapted QEC for this class of noise maps.

We then consider a specific example of a physically motivated noise model, namely a non-Markovian amplitude damping channel, and compare the performance of exact and noise-adapted QEC schemes for this noise process. We see that the non-Markovian Petz channel adapts to the entire noise map and effectively corrects all intermediate HPTP errors in the low noise limit and preserves the code space in the highest noise limit. In other words, the Petz channel adapted to a fixed timescale of non-Markovian noise leverages information backflow. It uniquely safeguards the worst-case fidelity against the rapid fluctuations of information flow between the system and environment, a capability not shared by other recovery operations.
Our findings further strengthen the known results on the near-optimality of the Petz channel for Markovian noise
[
15
]
.

However, implementing such a non-Markovian Petz channel could be nontrivial and somewhat resource intensive. In fact, writing down a quantum circuit for CP-indivisible channel remains an open problem in the literature to our knowledge. Here, we overcome this hurdle by showing that a Petz channel can be inexactly adapted, meaning adapted to only the Kraus operator structure but not to the time-dependent damping parameter, so that a CP-divisible Petz channel can correct for CP-indivisible amplitude damping channel. However, the price we pay is that the total channel seizes to be unital causing a small reduction in fidelity, for a brief period of time, below the worst-case limit of
0.5
.

Going forward, a couple of open questions are in order. In this work, we considered the cases of the
4
-qubit code and the
[[5,1,3]]
code with the Petz channel as recovery.
It would be an interesting open question to explore the relationship between the eigenvalue spectra of the QEC superchannel and the optimality of the code to correct non-Markovian errors in general. It would be also interesting to further explore the relationship between the distance of a code and non-Markovianity of QEC superchannel
[

]
. Finally, it would be an interesting challenge to study the efficacy of the Petz recovery map beyond the class of CP-indivisible maps and work towards a general theory of noise-adapted QEC for arbitrary forms of non-Markovian noise.

Appendix A
Non-Markovian amplitude damping channel

Let us consider damped Jaynes-Cummings (JC) model
[
30
,
6

of a two level system interacting with a dissipative bosonic reservoir at zero temperature. The total system-reservoir Hamiltonian, in the RWA, is

\displaystyle H_{\rm tot}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}}{2}+\sum_{j}\omega_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}+\sum_{j}(g_{j}\sigma_{+}a_{j}+g^{\ast}_{j}\sigma_{-}a^{\dagger}_{j}),

g_{j}

\sigma_{-}=|0\rangle\langle 1|

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{\rm SE}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H_{\rm tot},\rho_{\rm SE}].

(46)

The above model has a Lorentzian environmental spectral density
[
30
]

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma_{0}b^{2}}{2\pi[(\omega_{0}-\Delta-\omega)^{2}+b^{2}]},

(47)

where
\Delta=\omega-\omega_{0}

\omega
of the qubit from the central frequency
\omega_{0}
of the bath.
\omega_{0}
represents the energy gap between ground state
\ket{0}
and exited state
\ket{1}

\Gamma_{0}
quantifies the strength of the system-environment coupling and

the spectral bandwidth. Note that the spectral density in Eq.(
47

colored
memory kernel, giving rise to non-Markovian dynamics. This particular model indeed has well-defined Markovian and non-Markovian regimes, thus allowing manipulation of control parameters in the Kraus representation as follows.

Assuming that the environment starts out in the grounds state, tracing out the environment degrees of freedom from Eq. (
46
) in the interaction picture, gives rise to the GKSL-like equation (
1
) for the model considered with single jump operator
L_{j}=L=\sigma_{-}

30

1
]

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\Gamma(t)\bigg(\sigma_{-}\rho(t)\sigma_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-},\rho(t)\}\bigg).

(48)

The solution to the above master equation then is given by an amplitude damping (AD) channel with the time-
dependent
Kraus operators

\displaystyle E_{1}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&\sqrt{1-\gamma(t)}\\
\end{array}\right)\quad;\quad E_{2}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\sqrt{\gamma(t)}\\
0&0\\
\end{array}\right),

\rho(t)=\mathcal{E}(t)[\rho]=\sum_{j}E_{j}(t)\rho E^{\dagger}_{j}(t)

The density matrix of the open system after the action of the channel is given by
[
47

\rho_{S}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho_{11}(0)G(t)&\rho_{10}(0)\sqrt{G(t)}\\
\\
\rho_{01}(0)\sqrt{G(t)}&\rho_{00}(0)+\rho_{11}(0)(1-G(t))\\
\end{array}\right),

(53)

where the function

\frac{d}{dt}G(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau f(t-\tau)G({\tau})\,,

(54)

f(t-\tau)=\int\mathrm{d}\omega J(\omega)\exp[i(\omega_{0}-\omega)(t-\tau)]\,.

(55)

The time-dependent decay rate given in Eq. (
48

\Gamma(t)=-\frac{2}{G(t)}\frac{dG(t)}{dt}

\mathcal{E}
with associated Kraus operators
\{E_{i}\}
. Let
p_{l}
and
p_{l}\lambda_{l}
respectively be the maximum and the minimum eigenvalues of the operators
PE_{l}^{\dagger}E_{l}P

that The code
\mathcal{C}
approximately corrects for the errors

\displaystyle(p_{l}-\lambda_{l}p_{l})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

\displaystyle PU_{l}^{\dagger}U_{k}P

\displaystyle=\delta_{lk}P,

(56)

where

are the unitary operators obtained from the polar decomposition of the error operators on the codesapce

\mathcal{R}_{L}

with Kraus operators

.

In order to construct a noise-adapted recovery for non-Markovian noise in a similar fashion, we consider two different forms of the Leung recovery
\mathcal{R}_{L}
. First we consider the näive approach, where the recovery operators are constructed as above, using the unitaries obtained via polar decomposition of the noise operators. In other words, the recovery is
exactly adapted
to the non-Markovian noise and we refer to this scheme as the non-Markovian Leung recovery, in our discussions below.

In the second scenario we consider a Markovian Leung recovery by constructing the recovery operators using unitary operators that are obtained via polar decomposition of the noise operators whose noise strength is adapted to the Markovian regime of the noise. The two approaches obviously yield different bounds on the worst-case fidelity.

C.1
Non-Markovian Leung recovery

Lemma 2
.

Consider a HPTP map
\mathcal{E}(t)\sim\{sign(l),A_{l}(t)\}

\mathcal{C}
with projector
P

Proof.

To appear in the upper bound of the fidelity we consider the recovered state first. The recovered state is the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L}\circ\mathcal{E}[|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|]

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{lk}E_{l}^{\dagger}R_{k}^{\dagger}R_{k}E_{l}+\sum\limits_{l}N_{l}^{\dagger}P_{E}N_{L}=I,

(l)
