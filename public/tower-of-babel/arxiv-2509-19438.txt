Formation and Diagnostic Use of Carbon Lines in Stripped-Envelope Supernovae 2025 Formation and Diagnostic Use of Carbon Lines in Stripped-Envelope Supernovae –
Stan Barmentloo , Anders Jerkstrand

Abstract
Carbon is one of the main end products of nucleosynthesis in massive stars. In this work, we study the emission signatures of carbon in spectra of stripped envelope supernovae (SESNe). A grid of model nebular spectra is created using the NLTE radiative transfer code SUMO , with stellar evolution- and explosion models as inputs. In the models, [ C i ] λ \lambda 8727 and [ C i ] λ ​ λ \lambda\lambda 9824, 9850 are identified as the only significant optical carbon lines, with contribution from both the O/C and He/C zones. To obtain estimates of L [C I] λ 8727 L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}} , which is blended with the Ca II triplet , we introduce and apply the CaNARY code, a publicly available Monte Carlo scattering code . We study carbon lines in a sample of SESNe, and find that luminosities of [ C i ] λ ​ λ \lambda\lambda 9824, 9850 relative to the optical spectrum increase with time, just as in our model grid. However, the relative luminosities of both [ C i ] λ ​ λ \lambda\lambda 9824, 9850 and [ C i ] λ \lambda 8727 are overproduced in our models. Multiple explanations for this discrepancy, such as too high carbon abundances in the stellar evolution models and underestimated cooling through molecule formation, are investigated. For those SNe where both lines are clearly observed, we use an analytical formalism to constrain their ejected carbon masses to the range ∼ \sim 0.2 – 2 M ⊙ M_{\odot} . However, several SNe yield upper limits of 0.05 M ⊙ M_{\odot} . We also show that [ C i ] λ ​ λ \lambda\lambda 9824, 9850 is a useful line to diagnose both carbon mass and the extent of the He/C zone. We strongly encourage observers and instrumentalists to target [ C i ] λ ​ λ \lambda\lambda 9824, 9850 in future SN observing campaigns.

Introduction

Once they run out of nuclear fuel, massive stars (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}\gtrsim
8
M_{\odot}
) end their lives as a core-collapse supernova (CCSN)
(
Ekström 2025
;
Jerkstrand et al. 2025
)
. In this explosion, these stars eject large amounts of heavy elements created through nucleosynthesis in their cores. In this way, CCSNe are the main astrophysical source for the majority of elements with
Z\lesssim 36

(
Timmes et al. 1995
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020
;
Sieverding et al. 2023
)
.

One of the elements to which CCSNe contribute significantly is carbon. However, there still remains uncertainty as to the relative importance of massive stars (
through
CCSNe) on the one hand, and low- and intermediate mass stars (
through
AGB-stars) on the other
(
Franchini et al. 2020
;
Farmer et al. 2021
)
. The
relative importance of
these contributions, and thus when and where most carbon is formed, has important consequences for the star formation history and IMF of galaxies
(
Matteucci 2012
;
Jeřábková et al. 2018
;
Romano et al. 2020
)
. Obtaining better constraints on carbon yields from CCSNe will improve our understanding in these areas.

Another reason to study carbon yields in massive stars is the
12
C(
\alpha,\gamma
)
16
O reaction rate. This reaction directly influences the abundance of
12
C left at the end of He-burning
(
Laplace et al. 2025
)
. This abundance in turn sets the compactness of the star
(
Chieffi & Limongi 2020
)
, which governs its explodability
(
Sukhbold & Woosley 2014
;
Xin et al. 2025
)
. Despite its importance, the
12
C(
\alpha,\gamma
)
16
O reaction rate has historically been, and today still is, relatively uncertain (for a good overview, see section 2.2 in
Pepper et al. 2022
).

Studying SNe provides a valuable avenue for constraining nucleosynthetic yields in massive stars, as their spectra do not only contain signatures from the surface layers, but also, during the so-called
nebular phase
(
\gtrsim
100 – 150 d post explosion), the interior regions
(
Heger et al. 2003
;
Hillier & Dessart 2012a
;
Jerkstrand 2017
)
. Using models of the elemental compositions of exploded stars (e.g.
Limongi & Chieffi 2018
;
Ertl et al. 2020
;
Ma et al. 2025
) as inputs, synthetic nebular spectra can be created with
spectral formation
codes (e.g.
SUMO

(
Jerkstrand 2011
;
Jerkstrand et al. 2012
;
Jerkstrand et al. 2014
)
,
CMFGEN

(
Hillier & Miller 1998
;
Dessart & Hillier 2010
;
Hillier & Dessart 2012b
)
and
EXTRASS

(
Jerkstrand et al. 2020
;
van Baal et al. 2023
;
van Baal et al. 2024
)
) that solve for the physical conditions (temperature, excitation- and ionisation structure) and radiative transfer in the nebula. Comparing these synthetic spectra to observed ones in turn allows for estimations of the nucleosynthetic yields
and progenitor mass estimates. The results can increasingly often be compared with analysis of progenitor star detections
in pre-explosion images
(
Smartt 2015
;
Van Dyk 2017
;
Van Dyk et al. 2023
)
.

While carbon emission has been modeled and studied for individual SNe, such as the Type Ib SN 1985F
(
Fransson & Chevalier 1989
)
, the Type Ic SN 2007gr
(
Mazzali et al. 2010a
)
and three Type IIb SNe
(
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
)
, a comprehensive study of carbon in a large sample of SNe is still lacking. In part, this is due to the difficulties associated with
observing and
measuring the two significant nebular carbon lines, [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850. [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 is part of the Ca II NIR triplet-complex, a region which is known to host (at least) five more non-negligible lines in nebular SESNe, some of which (e.g. the Ca NIR triplet itself) are optically thick and thus further complicate estimates of line luminosities. On the other hand, [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 is an effectively isolated feature, but its wavelength region is often not covered in spectrographs used for obtaining nebular SESN spectra. When the region is covered, it often suffers from poor S/N, as it is close to the edge of the spectrograph. For ground-based observations, the blue half of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 also suffers non-negligible atmospheric absorption (
\sim
20%).

In this work we attempt to face these challenges and study carbon line formation in a sample of CCSNe. To this end, we analyse a variation of the sample of spectra presented in
Barmentloo et al. 2024
, which contains 123 nebular spectra of 41 SESNe (Stripped Envelope SNe, i.e. SNe of stars that lost their hydrogen envelope,
and in some cases also the He envelope
). To better understand the formation of carbon lines for such SNe, we compute a grid of NLTE radiative transfer models using
SUMO
.

The paper is built up as follows: in Section
2
, we start with a short overview of the theory on the formation of the two most important carbon lines in SESNe, [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850. Then, Section
3
describes the SESN sample that is studied in the remainder of the work. Section
4
covers how the synthetic model spectra were simulated, as well as how we estimated
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
and
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda\lambda$9824, 9850}}
for our sample. These luminosity estimates are presented and compared to the
SUMO

for the observed SNe. Section
6
discusses potential explanations for the lack in carbon luminosity observed in the section before. Finally, the paper is closed of with a summary and outlook in Section
7
.

Theory on Carbon Line Formation

To better understand the formation and use of carbon lines in nebular spectra, it is important to understand under which conditions the different lines form. During the nebular phase, the decreasing densities and temperatures mean that only low lying levels (
\sim
a few eV) in the atom can be effectively populated and cause strong emission lines. For
C
i
, these levels are (besides the triplet ground state 2s
2
2p
2
(
3
P) ) the 2s
2
2p
2
(
1
D) level at 1.26 eV and the 2s
2
2p
2
(
1
S) level at 2.68 eV (see Figure
1
). Of the lines connecting these levels, only [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 have been robustly observed and modeled (e.g. in
Fransson & Chevalier 1989
;
Mazzali et al. 2010a
;
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
;
Dessart et al. 2023
). The doublet line [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
4622, 4627 has a transition strength
>
100 times lower than the competing transition [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727, making this line insignificant. For
C
ii
(which is present in significant quantities throughout the nebular phase) the first excited level resides at 5.33 eV, and no lines from this or any of the higher levels have been observed or found
to be strong
in spectral models in nebular-phase SESNe.

Besides their upper levels being effectively populated, [
C

]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 are of particular interest and use for another reason. As these lines are forbidden, their transition rates are low at
A=6\times 10^{-1}
s
-1
and
A=2.9\times 10^{-4}
s
-1
, respectively.
This means that collisional (de-)excitation tends to dominate, bringing about LTE

for these levels with respect to the ground state
. For such transitions, the luminosity
L
in the line can be described (in the optically thin case, valid for both our lines; see Table 1 in
Jerkstrand 2017
) using Equation
1
(eq. 42 in
Jerkstrand 2017
):

L=M_{ion}\left(\mu m_{p}\right)^{-1}Ah\nu\frac{g_{u}}{Z(T)}e^{-E_{u}/kT},

(1)

where
\mu m_{p}
is the weight of a carbon atom,
E_{u}
is the energy of the upper level,
g_{u}
is the statistical weight and
Z(T)
is the partition function. This equation has only two unknowns (
M_{ion}
and
T
), meaning that if we can extract
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
and
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda\lambda$9824, 9850}}
from the spectrum, we can isolate
T
by taking the ratio of these luminosities. In turn, this allows us to calculate
M_{ion}
. We can thus estimate the mass of
C
i
in the ejecta from a nebular spectrum. As will be discussed in Section
5.4
, the ionisation fraction of neutral carbon is not expected to vary much with time or progenitor mass, meaning that an estimate of
M_{\text{C\,{i}}}
can, in turn, be translated to an estimate of
M_{\text{C }}
without too much
uncertainty
.

The decrease of temperature and density in the ejecta over time means that eventually, the upper level of [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 is no longer
dominantly depopulated
by thermal collisions, and the analytical formalism starts to break down.
This happens for [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 well before for [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 due to its much higher A-value
,
meaning that it has a much higher critical electron density
n^{crit}_{e}
(2.5
\times
10
7
and 2.6
\times
10
4
g cm
-3
for the
1
S and
1
D levels, respectively; see section 3.2 in
Jerkstrand 2017
)
. In such an NLTE case, the luminosity in a line is instead given by Equation
2
(eq. 50 in
Jerkstrand 2017
):

L=\left(\mu m_{p}\right)^{-1}h\nu\times M_{f}Q_{uf}(T)\frac{g_{u}}{g_{f}}e^{-\left(E_{u}-E_{f}\right)/kT}n_{e},

(2)

where
Q_{uf}
the collision rate in cm
3
s
-1
between the upper level of the transition
u
and the feeding level
f
and
n_{e}
the electron density.
For the
C
i
lines, the main feeding state is always the ground term, which is close to the
C
i
mass
. In this case there are then three unknowns (
M_{\text{C I}}
,
T
,
n_{e}
), so that we can no longer determine
M_{\text{C I}}
from just the
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
and
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda\lambda$9824, 9850}}
line ratio. It is thus of the utmost importance to know at what epoch the LTE assumption breaks down.
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
applied the LTE formalism to the line ratio of [
O
i
]
\lambda
5577 / [
O
i
]
\lambda\lambda
6300, 6364,
which form analogously to [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850
, and found that for [
O
i
]
\lambda
5577 (which has an upper level of 4.2 eV above the ground state) this occurs around
\sim
150d post explosion. If and when this break down occurs for [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 for the models in our grid is investigated in Section
5.4
. A further assumption that is made when using Equation
1
, is that
the carbon emission can be well described by a single zone, single temperature model, an ansatz with unknown accuracy
. Again, the impact that this assumption has on our
M_{\text{C }}
estimates is discussed in Section
5.4
.

3.2
Extinction

None of the photometry above has yet been corrected for extinction. We corrected for extinction by the Milky Way (MW) using the catalogues of
Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011
, accessed through the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (NED). Extinction by the host is more difficult to consistently take care of. For many of our objects, authors have used relations between the equivalent width of the
Na
i
D line and
E(B-V)_{\text{host}}
, and then assumed a reddening law for the host using
R_{V}
= 3.1. While this is common practice, multiple studies have shown that for the resolutions typical in SN spectra,

i
D is a poor proxy for extinction
(
Blondin et al. 2009
;
Poznanski et al. 2011
)
3
3

3

However, for spectra with sufficiently high resolutions, the relations may still hold
(
Poznanski et al. 2012
)
. Furthermore, setting
R_{V}
to 3.1 assumes the dust in the host galaxy to behave as MW dust, which is not necessarily the case
(
Stritzinger et al. 2018
)
.

A more reliable method for estimating extinctions is using multi-color light curves of minimally reddened SNe
as templates.
Stritzinger et al. 2018

were able to obtain estimates for the host extinction

for a sample of SESNe using this methodology. For their sample, typical

The total extinctions adopted for our NIR sample are shown in the seventh column of Table

4.1.2
Molecules

An additional effect that required modelling in our NLTE spectra compared to the models in
Barmentloo et al. 2024
is the

of molecules in the SN nebula. The molecule CO has been identified in a large number of Type II SNe (e.g.
Spyromilio & Leibundgut 1996
;

). While a simplified treatment of

was present in
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
, the detailed creation and emission of molecules was added to

by

. In our models, molecule formation and cooling was treated as described in those works. The effects of molecule formation on carbon emission are analysed in Section
5.1.2
.

4.1.3
Filling Factors

The heating of the radioactive decay of
56
Ni and
56
Co causes the inner Fe/He and Si/S zones to expand during the first days after explosion, which is known as the "Ni-bubble effect"
(
Herant & Benz 1991
;
Gabler et al. 2021
)
. This leads to a density contrast between these zones and the remaining macroscopically mixed zones. As this effect is not present in the ejecta models, it needs to be parameterised when preparing our ejecta models for
SUMO
. Estimates of the resulting densities (or filling factors, i.e. the fraction of the core-volume that is occupied by a specific core zone) have been made for individual SNe
(
Jerkstrand et al. 2012
, e.g. for SN 2004et,)
, but a systematic study of filling factors in SNe is still lacking. Filling factors should thus be treated as an unknown parameter.

The effect of filling factors on
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda\lambda$9824, 9850}}
and
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
was studied for three different model sets. These had density contrasts 1-5-20-20-20-20 (model
\chi=20
), 1-10-30-30-30-30 (model
\chi=30
) and 1-10-100-100-100-100 (model
\chi=100
). Here, the density contrast is defined as the overdensity compared to the Fe/He zone. So, in model
\chi=30
, the density in the Si/S zone is ten times that of the density in the Fe/He zone, and for the O/Si/S, O/Ne/Mg, O/C zones and the mixed-in part of the He/C zone, the density is thirty times that of the Fe/He zone.
The closing constraints are that the total mass in the macroscopically mixed core is unchanged, just as the mass of each individual zone in the core and the total volume of the core. With this in mind, the equation to determine the filling factor ff
i
for a core zone
i
with mass
M_{i}
becomes:

4.3
Estimating
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}

The spectral region where [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 is located is filled with at least six well known emission lines (see Table
3
). This fact makes inferring
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}

challenging

(
Fransson & Chevalier 1989
;
Mazzali et al. 2010a
;
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
)
. In theory, one could use
SUMO
to obtain an estimate for
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
: by creating a sufficiently large grid of models with different carbon abundances and comparing each model Ca II NIR triplet complex line profile to the observed one, one could find the input model giving the best fit and obtain how much of the Ca II NIR triplet complex luminosity was caused by [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727.
This approach however becomes limited by inaccuracies in the Ca II modeling, in addition to C I. It is also
very computationally expensive, with a single
SUMO
epoch requiring on the order of 100 CPU hours to converge. Creating a grid with sufficient free parameters (as well as covering nebular epochs with sufficiently small intervals) thus becomes intractable. Furthermore, if a reader would want to use this method themselves but their desired parameter combination is not present in the initial grid, extending the grid would be cumbersome.

In this work we present a novel approach to estimating
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
. We created
a new, simple and fast, Monte Carlo transfer code
,which we dub CaNARY
8
8

8

CaNARY will be made publicly available for anyone to use at
https://github.com/StanBarmentloo/CaNARY
. This code solely simulates the physics relevant for the Ca II NIR triplet region, reducing the CPU time for creating a single Ca II NIR triplet complex line profile from 100 CPU hours (as part of a full
SUMO
model) to a mere 30 seconds.

In short, the code simulates the ejecta as an expanding sphere of material with a single outer-velocity. The material in the sphere is assumed to consist only of the elements mentioned in Table
3
, and these elements can either emit photons (at a given luminosity) or scatter them (with a given optical depth), or both. The resulting simulation has a total of six free parameters (significantly fewer than fitting six Gaussians) and still includes much of the relevant physics to the problem. For this work, we used the code to create a spectral library of
\sim
3000 Ca II NIR triplet complex line profiles. Subsets of these were compared to each observed spectrum by determining a
\chi^{2}
-score. By finding the minimum in the
\chi^{2}
vs.
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
curve, an estimate for
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
was then determined. A more detailed description of CaNARY is given in Appendix
B
.

5.2
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda\lambda$9824, 9850}}

We performed our methodology for estimating (upper limits on)

f_{[\text{C\,{i}}]\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}=\frac{\int F_{[\text{C\,{i}}]\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}}{\int^{8000\ \text{\r{A}}}_{5000\ \text{\r{A}}}(F_{\lambda}-F_{\text{pseudo}})d\lambda}\times 100,

C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 line,
F_{\lambda}
is the observed flux in the spectrum and
F_{\text{pseudo}}
is the pseudo-continuum. This pseudo-continuum is defined as the average of the three lowest mean fluxes found in the regions 5740 – 5790 Å, 6020 – 6070 Å, 6850 – 6900 Å and 7950 – 8000 Å (see appendix A3 in
Barmentloo et al. 2024
).
The reason for removing this pseudo-continuum is that in most observed spectra, there will be an unknown amount of background emission from the host galaxy. Naturally, the model spectra will not have this additional emission. For most spectra the host contribution is low, but to assure an as equal as possible comparison between observations and models, we remove the pseudo continuum for all spectra. Comparing
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
results with the pseudo continuum subtraction turned on and off (not shown here), we find that none of our general conclusions in this work are altered.

). On the other hand, the resulting

and

, due to the added uncertainties in NLTE departure and distance and extinction towards the host.

6.1.1
Binarity

For more than a decade now, it has been the consensus
(
Sana et al. 2012
;
Smith 2014
;
Moe & Di Stefano 2017
)
that the majority of massive stars are not single stars, but that they rather are in binaries. Motivated by this fact, there have been recent studies (e.g.
Woosley 2019
;
Laplace et al. 2021
;
Farmer et al. 2021
;
Schneider et al. 2021
) to investigate the differences in stellar evolution between single and binary stars. Besides the clear difference caused by the loss of the hydrogen envelope through RLOF, an important finding that has been done in
Woosley 2019
,
Laplace et al. 2021
and
Schneider et al. 2021
is that binary-stripped stars retain a higher carbon mass fraction
X_{\text{12C}}
at central carbon ignition than (effectively) single stars (see figure 15 in
Laplace et al. 2021
).

However, this may not necessarily translate into a higher final
M_{\text{C }}
; in fact, for
M_{\text{preSN}}

\lesssim
4.5
M_{\odot}
(which holds for all our progenitors except the he8p0 model),
Farmer et al. 2021
find that single stars end up with slightly higher
M_{\text{C }}
than their binary-stripped counterparts. This suggests that the discrepancy in relative carbon luminosities between our SESN sample and the stellar models by
Woosley 2019
can not be explained through a large part being single star progenitors.

Finally, one can consider the reduction of carbon in the final ejecta through RLOF of the helium envelope. For helium stars with
M_{\text{He,i}}

\lesssim
3.5
M_{\odot}
, the helium envelope is expected to expand upon core helium depletion (see e.g. figure 4 in
Woosley 2019
). For sufficiently close binaries (
P_{\text{orb}}

\lesssim
10 – 20d
(
Tauris et al. 2015
;
Yoon et al. 2017
)
), this expansion ushers in a second phase of RLOF, stripping the He/N layer and significant parts of the He/C envelope. Reminding the reader of Figure
3
, this could lead to significant carbon luminosity reductions. This expansion did not occur in our model grid. However, the majority of the SNe in our sample are not expected to originate from such low mass progenitors (see e.g. table 2 in
Barmentloo et al. 2024
), and the sample of objects with necessary ejecta masses of
\lesssim
0.5 – 1.0
M_{\odot}
found in the literature is also small.

6.1.2
Rotation

Another important parameter in stellar evolution is rotation
(
Langer 1992
;
Heger et al. 2000
;
Meynet & Maeder 2000
)
, as the mixing it induces increases the extent of the core burning region.
Limongi & Chieffi 2018
studied rotation
11
11

11

Note that the stellar evolution code is in 1D, so that rotation is treated using a mixing efficiency parameter. See their section 3 for details on this calibration
for a large grid of single stars. In this study, three different values for the initial rotational velocity (
v=
0, 150 and 300
~\mathrm{km~s^{-1}}
) were considered. The authors find that for the lower mass stars (
M_{\text{CO}}

\lesssim
5

\sim

to
\sim

with rotational velocity (see their table 8).

As the grid in
Limongi & Chieffi 2018

However, except for the tightest binaries (which lose their envelopes already on the MS), MS-evolution of single and binary stars should be very similar, and one would thus expect the effect of rotation on the MS to also occur for stars in binaries. Most importantly, including rotation would lead to larger helium cores for the same
M_{\text{ZAMS}}
in our grid.

6.1.3
Metallicity

Ma et al. 2025
recently presented a study where they investigated the effect of metallicity on carbon yields from massive stars (both single and in binaries), by simulating the same stars for SMC, LMC and solar metallicity. They find that the influence of metallicity is dominant, being larger than the effect of e.g. binaries or other stellar parameters, with stars producing relatively more carbon with increasing metallicity. It should be noted that these increased yields only occur from
M_{\text{ZAMS}}

\gtrsim
18
M_{\odot}
(or
M_{\text{CO}}

\gtrsim
4.5
M_{\odot}
), so that a non-negligible fraction of our sample would be excluded (see e.g. the progenitor mass estimates in
Barmentloo et al. 2024
.

Recent studies of CCSNe metallicities
(
Anderson et al. 2010
;
Ganss et al. 2022
;
Xi et al. 2024
)
find the quantitiy 12 + log(O/H) to range between 8.1 and 8.7, with mean metallicities of 8.4 – 8.5 and 68% of the population within 0.1 – 0.15 from the mean. Assuming a solar value of 12 + log(O/H) = 8.69
(
Asplund et al. 2021
)
, this means that the majority of CCSNe have metallicities of
\sim
0.4 – 1.0
Z_{\odot}
. The solar metallicity models from
Woosley 2019
are thus likely to have a somewhat higher metallicity than typical in our observed sample, which especially for higher (
M_{\text{ZAMS}}

\gtrsim
18
M_{\odot}
) mass progenitors could lead to overestimation of carbon yields.

6.1.4
Shell Mergers

Another mechanism that might decrease carbon abundance in massive stars is a C-O shell merger
(
Rauscher et al. 2002
;
Meakin & Arnett 2006
;
Rizzuti et al. 2024
)
. In this process, which occurs in the final days of the star’s life, carbon and neon are ingested into the oxygen burning shell. Among other things, a C-O shell merger leads to the destruction of the carbon in the inner half of the O/C-layer, and would thus have a similar order effect on carbon emission as molecules. While the mechanism has been found in both 1D and multidimensional simulations, there remains large uncertainty on how common these mergers are, especially because observational evidence is still lacking. A recent study by
Roberti et al. 2025
found that for a large collection of single-star literature models,
\sim
20% experience a C-O shell merger, with the majority having low
M_{\text{CO}}
(
\lesssim
5
M_{\odot}
) and central
X_{\text{12C}}
(
\lesssim
0.28) at the end of core helium burning. The stellar models that we studied in this work experienced no such merger, which can largely be explained by the fact discussed before in Subsection
6.1.1
, namely that binary-stripped stars have higher
X_{\text{12C}}
than their single star counterparts. In fact, the four models studied in this work all have
X_{\text{12C}}

>
0.28. A similar argument can be made for the C-Ne shell mergers observed in the single-star models by
Laplace et al. 2025
; in their simulations, these occur for models with low
X_{\text{12C}}
(0.17) and masses above the first compactness peak (
M_{\text{CO}}

\gtrsim
6.5
M_{\odot}
), a parameter space which is not covered by the stellar models in our grid. All in all, we deem it thus unlikely shell mergers are relevant for a significant part of our sample.

As was noted in Section
5.3
, Figure
8
shows that the Type Ic SNe have on average the highest
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda 8727}
values in our sample. However, in the canonical picture Type Ic SNe lack a helium layer, and thus a He/C layer. Naively, we would thus expect them to have the lowest relative carbon luminosities.

One potential explanation for this discrepancy could be that the He/C extent in the
Woosley 2019
and
Ertl et al. 2020
models is larger (or has a larger carbon abundance) than typical in the universe. Between different stellar evolution models, the characteristics of this zone vary appreciably, as its formation is influenced by many uncertain factors, such as rotation and (wind- and binary) mass loss (see sections 4.2 and 5.1 in
Limongi & Chieffi 2018
). For example, while the ratio of the O/C to He/C zone masses for our models is similar to those studied in
Jerkstrand et al. 2015
, the carbon abundance in the He/C zone of their 17
M_{\odot}
model is 0.045, where it is 0.39 in our he8p0 model (which comes closest in ejecta mass).

While determining the abundance of carbon in the He/C-zone from the carbon lines themselves is not possible, their line widths might turn out to be of use. As was noted by
Fransson & Chevalier 1989
, carbon lines in nebular spectral synthesis models turn out wider than other strong nebular lines such as e.g. [
O
i
]
\lambda\lambda
6300, 6364 and [
Ca
ii
]
\lambda\lambda
7291, 7323. The reason for this is that the carbon lines originate partly from the He/C-layer, which has a higher velocity than the macroscopically mixed core. The more the carbon line width thus differs from e.g. the [
O
i
]
\lambda\lambda
6300, 6364 line width, the larger the extent of the He/C-layer. While the complex blend of [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 makes it complicated to determine line widths accurately, the effectively isolated [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 serves as a great tool for such a comparison.

and

This fact combined with the small sample size makes drawing any general conclusions difficult, but these comparisons do indicate that the He/C-layer extent for some SNe may indeed be smaller than in the stellar models studied in this work. Furthermore, they show once again that high quality observations of [

7
Conclusions

In this work, we presented the first systematic study of carbon emission lines in SESNe in the nebular phase. We created a grid of
SUMO
models based on
KEPLER
helium star models, and found [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 and [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 to be the only carbon lines of significance. For this grid, we studied the effects on carbon emission of uncertain input physics, namely the filling factors of the core zones and the effect of molecule formation. Next, we compared the carbon line luminosities in a sample of SESNe nebular spectra to our model grid. To extract
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
, a line that is part of a complex blend of at least six emission lines, we created the CaNARY code, a trimmed-down version of
SUMO
that focuses solely on the line formation physics for this blend. Our main findings are as follows:

[I]

1.

In our model grid, carbon lines are only effectively formed in the O/C and He/C zones. The relative importance of these zones varies with helium core mass, with increasing core mass leading to increased importance of the He/C zone. This is caused by an increased ratio of He/C to O/C zone mass, as well as a heightened neutral carbon fraction in the He/C zone.

2.

Carbon line luminosities are only mildly (
\lesssim
10
\%
) sensitive to uncertainties in filling factor and molecule formation. We do note that the models in our grid form only a small mass (
\lesssim
5
\times
10
-6

M_{\odot}
) of carbon monoxide compared to existing SESN literature models (
\sim
10
-4

M_{\odot}

(
Liljegren et al. 2023
)
) and observational estimates (
\sim
10
-4
– 10
-3

M_{\odot}

(
Banerjee et al. 2018
;
Rho et al. 2021
)
). We attribute this low mass to a comparatively small mass of the O/C zone in our ejecta models, as well as lower densities in this zone.

3.

The relative strength of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 compared to the optical spectrum,
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
, is found to increase strongly with time, from
\sim
1 at 150d up to 10 – 20 at 400d. Furthermore,
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
is found to increase with helium core mass, albeit not monotonically. Combined with the fact that in all models and at all epochs [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 is well-treated as an LTE line, this makes [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 an extremely useful line to study carbon nucleosynthesis in SNe, especially when observed at epoch
\gtrsim
300d.

For the majority of spectra in our sample, the wavelength region of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 was either not included in the spectrum, or the quality of the spectrum was too poor. Nonetheless, when comparing the relative luminosity
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
in models to our observational sample, we find that more than half of observed spectra have
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
below even our lowest model, indicating an overestimation of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 emission in our models.

4.

The evolution of
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda 8727}
in the model grid is not as pronounced in time or mass, decreasing from
\sim
15 to
\sim
10 between 150d and 400d. Importantly, we also find that [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 is not well treated as an LTE line, with departure coefficients at 150d already down to
\sim
0.7 – 0.9, decreasing to 0.15 – 0.2 at 400d. Comparing the model grid values with the estimates of
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda 8727}
obtained by our newly developed CaNARY code, we again find most observations to have lower values than the model grid. The discrepancy is the largest for Type IIb SNe and still clear for Type Ib SNe. For Type Ic SNe, observations are not incompatible with the model values.

5.

We provide estimates and upper limits of the neutral carbon mass through an analytical, LTE formalism. However, the non-LTE nature of [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 leads to our LTE formulae largely overestimating these masses. The added uncertainties in host extinction and distance to the SN make these mass estimates highly complicated. Therefore, we advise readers to only use this formalism at early epochs (where the LTE departure is smallest), and for objects with well known host extinction and distance. In fact, the higher densities in their ejecta and consequently smaller LTE departures should make Type II SNe significantly better candidates to apply the formalism to than SESNe, and we therefore encourage such a study.

6.

Multiple potential explanations for the found discrepancies in
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda 8727}
and
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
are discussed, including stellar evolution modeling, binary fractions, rotation, metallicity and C-O shell mergers. From our limited analysis, we find that there are still appreciable differences in carbon yields for different stellar evolution codes, and at the same time we deem metallicity and shell mergers to be unlikely causes of the discrepancy.

7.

When studying the line widths of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 in observed spectra, we find indications that they are not significantly different from the line widths of [
O
i
]
\lambda\lambda
6300, 6364. This hints that the extent of the He/C zone may be smaller than expected from models, where line widths of [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 are clearly larger than those of [
O
i
]
\lambda\lambda
6300, 6364. However, the limited sample of detected [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 in observations does not allow us to conclude anything definitively.

Based on the conclusions presented here, we would like to close off this work by joining a call to observers that was also present in the recent work by
Liu et al. 2025
, i.e. to put increased attention into obtaining high quality [
C
i
]
\lambda\lambda
9824, 9850 observations of SNe. Its LTE nature makes it a comparatively simple line to model and analyse, and the close to monotonic
increase of
f_{\text{[\text{C\,{i}}]}\lambda\lambda 9824,9850}
with helium core mass for a given epoch also gives it potential as a progenitor mass diagnostic. Furthermore, its linewidth can be used as a probe for the extent of the He/C zone, which in turn can provide valuable information to stellar evolution modelers.

\sigma

Validating these upper limits is a non trivial undertaking, as the whole premise is that there is no signal to be checked. What we can do is judge by eye whether or not the limits look credible. A collection of upper limit signals we obtained is presented in Figure

Simulating Ca II NIR triplet line profiles

Here we describe the CaNARY code described in Section
4.3
in further detail. The code tracks a total of
10^{6}
photons from creation to escape.

B.1
Photon Creation

When a photon is created, it has an initial wavelength
\lambda_{\text{init}}
, radius
r_{\text{init}}
and angle
\mu_{\text{init}}
.

The wavelength
\lambda_{\text{init}}
is drawn randomly from the six lines in Table
3
.
12
12

12

In our simulations, the line Fe I
\lambda
8688 also showed non negligible optical depths, but we do not include it in the CaNARY modeling. This choice was made as we wanted to keep CaNARY as simplistic as possible, and the potential minor gain in accuracy did in our eyes not justify the increase in complexity.
The probability of each line’s wavelength being selected is a free parameter and should be understood as the line’s luminosity generated by thermal processes.

We remove two of the six free parameters here by always assuming the same ratio of luminosities for the three individual Ca II NIR tripletlines. We justify this as follows. The luminosity of a line,
per unit volume
, in the Sobolev approximation
(
Sobolev 1957
)
is given by Equation
5
:

L=n_{u}A\beta_{S}h\nu,

(5)

where
n_{u}
is the number density in cm
-3
of the upper level,
A
is the transition rate in s
-1
and
\beta_{S}
is the escape probability in the Sobolev approximation. For an optically thick line (which the Ca II NIR triplet lines are at all times, see Section
4.3
),
\beta_{S}\sim 1/\tau_{S}

(
Jerkstrand 2017
)
, which in turn is given by (
ignoring the stimulated emission correction
):

\tau_{S}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\frac{g_{u}}{g_{l}}A\lambda^{3}n_{l}t.

(6)

Inserting Equation
6
into
5
and rearranging, we find that:

L=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^{4}t}\frac{n_{u}}{n_{l}}\frac{g_{l}}{g_{u}}.

(7)

In other words, the ratio of luminosities for individual lines in the triplet is given by (ignoring the factor
\left(\frac{\lambda_{\text{line1}}}{\lambda_{\text{line 2}}}\right)^{-4}
, which is close to unity as the wavelengths are close):

\displaystyle\frac{L_{8498}}{L_{8542}}=\frac{n_{3}}{n_{2}}\frac{g_{2}}{g_{3}},

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{L_{8498}}{L_{8662}}=\frac{n_{5}}{n_{4}}\frac{g_{4}}{g_{5}},

\displaystyle\frac{L_{8542}}{L_{8662}}=\frac{\frac{n_{5}}{n_{4}}\frac{g_{4}}{g_{5}}}{\frac{n_{3}}{n_{2}}\frac{g_{2}}{g_{3}}}.

Here it is important to realise from what levels the Ca II NIR triplet lines originate (see Figure
18
). The Ca II ion has a singlet ground state (referred to as
{}^{2}\text{S}
in Figure
18
), followed by a doublet (
{}^{2}\text{D}
) and another doublet (
{}^{2}\text{P}\textdegree
). If one assumes that statistical equilibrium for levels within a multiplet is obtained faster than the multiplet is depopulated, one can say that
n_{5}/n_{4}=g_{5}/g_{4}\times e^{-\frac{E_{5}-E_{4}}{k_{B}T}}
and
n_{3}/n_{2}=g_{3}/g_{2}\times\frac{E_{3}-E_{2}}{k_{B}T}
. Plugging this result into Equation
8
, one finds that all components of the triplet have the same luminosity (barring the Boltzmann factor, which is close to unity).

For levels 2 and 3, this equilibrium holds to low densities, because the radiative deexcitation can occur by forbidden lines only. Thus, it is robust to take
L_{8498}/L_{8542}=1
. Levels 4 and 5 are, however, radiatively deexcited by allowed transitions. While the transitions are generally optically thick, the resulting
A\beta_{S}
values are still larger than the collisional depopulation rates, so equilibrium cannot be assumed. Some subtlety now enters the argument. The emissivity ratio we are seeking refers to emission following populating processes other than absorption of triplet lines themselves (that process is what the code simulates). The two main drivers for this are thermal excitation (from ground and/or
2
D) and HK-line absorption. The first process tends to dominate at early nebular phases, the second later on. Starting with the second, because the HK lines have the same A-value, the photoexcitation flows through them to
n=4
and
n=5
will tend to be similar
, as long as the diffuse UV field does not strongly differ blueward of 3933 Å compared to 3968 Å. For thermal excitation, the twice as high statistical weight of level 5 means it will receive twice as high flow as level 4. Thus, for the HK fluorescence case a ratio of 1 for
L_{8498}/L_{8662}
is the best choice (same as in equilibrium), and for thermal excitation case a ratio of 2 is the best choice. Tests were carried out showing that choosing 2 instead of 1 did not significantly impact the C I luminosity estimate.

B.2
Photon Travel

Once a photon is created, it starts traveling through the nebula at its initial direction, continuously redshifting (in the comoving frame) along the way. If the photon redshifts to one of the six wavelengths in Table
3
, it interacts with this line following the Sobolev approximation. Effectively this means that the interaction surface with a line is infinitely thin, so that once a photon redshifts past the line’s exact wavelength, it will never interact with it again. To obtain the result of an interaction, all we need to know is the Sobolev optical depth
\tau_{S}
of the line; at each interaction, there is a probability of
\exp{-\tau_{S}}
that the photon moves through the line unharmed. In the remaining
1-\exp{-\tau_{S}}
cases, the photon interacts with the line. We treat this interaction as an isotropic scattering event. In other words, we assume that the radiative re-emission of the photon occurs on a much shorter timescale than the collisional (de-)excitation to other energy levels within the atom;
this is based on that
A\beta_{S}
for the (allowed) triplet lines are much larger than the collisional deexcitation rates, even when the lines are optically thick
.

In our simulation, we assume the optical depth for the Ca II NIR triplet lines to always be
\tau_{S}

\gg
1. This is supported by our
SUMO
simulations, where at epochs
<
300d
13
13

13

As already discussed in Section
B.1
, CaNARY estimates for the regime
\gtrsim
300d are less reliable
,
\tau_{S}

\gtrsim
a few in the core. On the other hand, we assume [
C
i
]
\lambda
8727 to always be optically thin with
\tau_{S}

\ll
1. With a transition rate a factor
\sim 10^{7}
lower than the Ca II NIR triplet this is justified, and once again the
SUMO
simulations show that this assumption holds well for all masses and epochs considered here. For the two remaining lines, we leave
\tau_{S}
as a free parameter, as they cross the
\tau_{S}\sim 1
regime within our considered time range.

Finally, we are left with six free parameters for creating a single simulated line profile. These are given in Table
3
. Note how the table lists seven parameters, but that because our code only generates ’relative’ luminosities, one effectively eliminates one of the luminosity parameters (e.g. one can put one of the luminosities to 1, and define the others as a ratio to this luminosity).

B.3
Post Processing & Model Selection

With the wavelength at escape counted for each photon, a synthetic output spectrum is created. By varying the six free parameters in Table
3
, a grid of
\sim
3000 such synthetic spectra was simulated. This grid covers
V_{\text{out}}
values between 3 000 and 7 000
~\mathrm{km~s^{-1}}
at intervals of 500

/

from 0 to 0.8, roughly at intervals of 0.05.

Once the spectral library is created, it is time to compare to the observed spectrum. First, any underlying quasi-continuum in the observed spectrum is removed by simply subtracting the average of the flux levels between 8200 – 8300 Å and 9000 – 9100 Å, which during the nebular phase are free of emission lines (see Figures
19
and
22
–

). The remaining integrated absolute luminosity within the Ca II NIR triplet,

is stored, as this will later be used to determine
L_{\text{[C I] $\lambda$8727}}
. Then, the spectrum is normalised to its peak flux to allow comparison to the synthetic spectra. To use as much of the spectral information as possible, the user is then allowed to indicate the following:

1.

Is there any sign of
Mg
i

\lambda

2.

Is there any sign of
O
i

\lambda

3.

V_{\text{out}}
?
