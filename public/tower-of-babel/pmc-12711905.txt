Complexity analyses of geomagnetic and solar activity indices time series
Authors: Mohsen Javaherian, Saeid Mollaei, Neda Zolghadr, Hooman Moradpour, Mehdi Yousefzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The universal properties of the geomagnetic/solar indices critically describe their time series’ dynamics. Here, we investigate the complexity behavior of the time series of the geomagnetic and solar activity indices Kp, DST, Ap, F10.7, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Lyman}_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, AE, AL, AU, pc, and R. To do this, we apply multiscale entropy (MSE) analysis, detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), rescaled range (R/S) test, and visibility graph (VG) to the time series of indices recorded daily from January 1st, 1996 to January 1st, 2020. MSE analysis reveals that AE exhibits the lowest entropy (minimal complexity), followed by F10.7, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Lyman}_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R. Ap shows intermediate complexity, while AL, Dst, AU, and pc maintain higher entropy values. The range of Hurst exponents between 0.5 and 1 extracted from DFA and R/S analyses confirms the long-term memory of geomagnetic indices. In contrast, solar activity indices exhibit Hurst exponents consistent with pink noise or non-stationarity. VG analysis identifies AU, Dst, pc, and Kp as possessing “small-world” characteristics—corroborating strong long-range correlations. Synthesized results indicate that the indices Kp, Dst, AL, AU, and pc exhibit stronger evidence of long-term correlated memory, while the solar indices, along with the geomagnetic index AE, are comparatively suitable for studying transient phenomena.

Introduction

In recent decades, most of the many body systems with non-linear dynamical behaviors are studied in the framework of complex systems theories. Since the linear analyses employed for finding regular structures in data sets are not responsible for stochastic phenomena, the nonlinear analyses have emerged to find self-similar behaviors in irregular structures. The irregular behaviors are originated from random external inputs and/or the nature of chaotic and nonlinearity of a system1. The ways of analyzing irregularities can lead to finding comprehensive patterns and universal characteristics in complex systems.

The solar corona, the outermost layer of the Sun’s atmosphere, with millions of kilometers extending into space, is known to be a highly dynamic region with a complex environment. Numerous phenomena are occurring on various timescales, making it hard to understand the behavior of its events for predicting space weather and its impact on Earth. Solar coronal indices provide critical tools for characterizing the behavior of the solar corona. Many indicators have been introduced and monitored to find some trends of solar changes and quantify various aspects of solar activity, such as geomagnetic storms, solar flares, and coronal mass ejections (CMEs). These geospace and solar-terrestrial indices are significant for focusing on space weather changes, geomagnetic activity, and solar influences on Earth. K index of planets, measured by a magnetometer with a time lag of three hours, presents an estimation of the power of a geomagnetic storm2. It shows the level of disturbances that occurred in the horizontal orientation of the Earth’s magnetic field. In this regard, the rate of magnetic field variations near Earth is computed by the weighted mean of the K index known as Kp3. The measurements are done in mid-latitude locations which are obtained by 13 connected geomagnetic ground-based observatories. In addition, the Ap index shows the amplitude of magnetic variations determined by the mean of eight values of the K index in one day that is measured in a fixed location4. So, both indices Kp and Ap represent the geomagnetic activity which are characterized in different ranges. Since phenomena like CMEs affect the Earth’s magnetic field, DST index quantifies the strength of such disturbances5. F10.7 is defined as the solar radio flux at 10.7 cm indicating solar EUV index6, because the main variable components of both spectral bands originate in the same solar regions: the chromosphere and solar corona. F10.7 has been used as a proxy for EUV and its impacts on Earth’s upper atmosphere. Using high-resolution radio observations, it was found that 10.7-cm flux are rooted in both compact bright sources and diffuse regions related to magnetic activity7. The strong correlation between F10.7 and other solar activity indices suggests that the flux is primarily due to thermal (free-free) emission, making it a suitable criterion for estimating the total magnetic flux emerging from the solar photosphere. Also, the reader can refer to8 for details on the instrumentation, measurement process, and calibration approaches employed for the F10.7 solar flux index. In analysis9, it was unveiled that there is a considerable shift in the relationship between sunspot number (SSN) activity and F10.7 started after the peak of cycle 23, with some possible signatures dating back to 1980. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Lyman}_{\alpha }$$\end{document} quantifies hydrogen emission at 121.6 nm10,11 indicating solar UV which varies with solar cycle12. For tracking auroral currents, the AE index is employed indicating auroral electrojet. The occurrence of auroras in the northern and southern hemispheres are demonstrated by AL (westward electrojet with negative values) and AU (eastward electrojet with positive values), respectively, which both are components of AE13. To track the solar wind coupling, pc index is used which is indicative of the polar cap magnetic activity14. The index R is representative of sunspot number (e.g., see15) exhibiting solar radio bursts at 15.4 GHz. In researches16,17, the two main forms of sunspot number data are addressed comprising of the International Sunspot Number (initiated by R. Wolf in 184918) and the Group Sunspot Number (constructed by Hoyt and Schatten in the late 1990s19). Additionally, the authors provide a comprehensive review of the calibration and correction of biases in the sunspot series to resolve discrepancies between the two classifications of SSN.

Numerous studies have focused on the relationship between solar activity and geomagnetic parameters. For example, a comprehensive review on the impact of solar activity and geomagnetic disturbances on the Earth’s ionosphere—particularly the utility of mentioned indices (i.e., Kp, Dst, Ap, F10.7, AE, AL, AU, Pc, and R) in predicting and understanding ionospheric behavior, and their relevance to high-frequency (HF) radio propagation, satellite communications, and GPS systems—was presented by20. In work21, it was revealed that the 2008–2009 solar minimum was included low solar wind parameters and geomagnetic activity (the lowest in the space era) leading to transferring minimum energy from the solar wind to Earth’s magnetosphere. In other investigation, cycle-by-cycle analyses of the Ap and F10.7 indices suggest that Solar Cycle 25 may be substantially weaker than historical averages22 which was subsequently confirmed by modeling study23. Furthermore, the cycle’s solar activity characteristics has been discussed in24 based on sunspot properties (number and areas), magnetic field intensity, flare occurrences, CMEs, and solar radio flux distributions. There are a number of external factors identified that affect the solar activity and geomagnetic-related variables: solar wind structure (which are dependant to solar phenomena like high-speed solar wind streams, coronal holes, and CMEs), interplanetary magnetic field (particularly its orientation and strength), sunspot cycle phase and amplitude, long-term (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>11$$\end{document}-year) periods (such as the Gleissberg cycle and other multi-decadal variations16), cosmic ray flux, and Earth’s orbital and magnetic geometry (including seasonal variations and the orientation of Earth’s dipole field)25. We should mention that the impact of solar activity and geomagnetic disturbances on technological infrastructure and communication systems is important and inevitable, often leading to disruptions in power grids, satellite operations, navigation systems, and communication networks (e.g., see26–32).

One of the ways for complexity analysis of a system is using entropy. Recently, new techniques were developed to compute the entropy of time series. In research33, wavelet analysis was employed to determine how wavelet entropy of time series (signal complexity) varies over time. It was found that there is the multi-peaked structure of maxima of Solar Cycles 21, 22, and 23 compatible with the Gnevyshev gap phenomenon. Also, in another study conducted by34, wavelet-based multiscale techniques was used to analyze long-term variations in solar activity (SSN, F10.7 radio flux, and the Mg II index) from 1979 to 2022. Their results show that the mentioned indices are strongly correlated especially around the 11-year solar cycle. After the 2014 maximum activity, even during the declining phase of the solar cycle, there is nonlinearity in multiscale trend with increasing in complexity (entropy). One of the robust methods which is independent of the time series length is multiscale entropy (MSE) analysis. MSE can evaluate the entropy (or irregularity) across multiple temporal scales. This method was first introduced for analyzing the complexity of signals related to heart rate35, and then, it was widely applied to biological signals36,37 and traffic time series38. Using the concept of entropy, robust results can be obtained about the complexity of a system, along with a quantitative interpretation of the system’s long-term memory39.

The Hurst exponent is proposed for distinguishing between stochastic (irregular) time series and signals with long-range interaction within system components (self-affinity), which was initially introduced by40. In this approach, a time series \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[y_t]$$\end{document} exhibits self-affinity when different parts of the signal can be explained by the equation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_{\alpha t} = \alpha ^{-H}y_t$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and H imply a positive coefficient and the Hurst exponent, respectively (e.g., see41). The Hurst exponent within the range of (0.5,1) indicates the presence of long-term memory in the system of interest. Various methods have been developed to estimate the Hurst exponent, including detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA)42, rescaled range (R/S) test43, and wavelet44,45.

Among different ways of complexity analysis of time series, one of the known approaches is visibility graph (VG) to construct a network46. This allows us to study a complex system via focusing on network parameters (such as shortest path length, clustering coefficient, and degree of nodes) illustrating characteristics of the system. The properties of the VG can be analyzed to extract meaningful information about the time series data in different fields from biological systems to climate time series47–49.

In this paper, we employed MSE analysis, DFA, R/S test, and VG approach to investigate the complexity and self-affinity of time series of geomagnetic and solar activity indices. Moreover, we presented the Pearson and Spearman correlation values between each pair of indices. Section “Description of methods” outlines the methodology of MSE, DFA, R/S test, and VG construction with its parameters. In Section “Results and discussions”, we present the results and discussions. Data analysis

The data center OMNI (On-line Mean) is basically designed to record information about the solar wind. This center which keeps constantly long-term record of space weather data has two main versions: Low Resolution OMNI (LRO) and High Resolution OMNI (HRO).

The type LRO is the summary version recorded from various spacecraft orbiting Earth or locating at the L1 Lagrange point (like Advanced Composition Explorer (ACE)). So, gathering the data of solar wind’s magnetic field and its plasma is not relatively affected by the Earth’s magnetic field lines. This is the massive collection of hourly-average data which is continuously updated from 1963. Using cross-comparison and cross-normalization, the collected data from different sources has been carefully revisited and adjusted to preserve the accuracy and consistency. LRO also provides valuable data including proton flux and indices of geomagnetic and solar activity. Furthermore, the measurements of the number of protons with different energies (1, 2, 4, 10, 30, and 60 MeV) is recorded from interplanetary magnetic field (IMP) and GOES satellites. The LRO data is publicly available at different resolutions: hourly, daily, and even 27-day averages (useful for seeing solar rotation effects). It can all be found at https://omniweb.gsfc.nasa.gov/ow.html.

The type HRO is the detailed version of datasets. It provides data in finer scales, with measurements taken every minute or every 5 min. This allows for studying instant dynamical changes in the solar wind. Like LRO data, this type of data comes from a similar set of spacecraft (IMP 8, Geotail, Wind, and ACE) but focuses on the magnetic field and plasma parameters near the Earth’s magnetosphere. Description of methods

Here, we focus on different methods that can provide information about self-similarity and complexity of time series. MSE analysis is one of the newly approved methods that can evaluate complexity of a system over multiple time scales (different resolutions) using two steps of sequential coarse-graining and sample entropy. DFA is the other method suggested for analyzing data to find patterns and correlations in time series. Recently, it has been widely used across various fields of studies to understand complex systems and their dynamics. R/S test provides a powerful tool for analyzing the structural pattern of time series, where understanding the scaling laws seems to be crucial for modeling and forecasting. VG is an approach to transform time series into a graph representation. This method allows to extract the complex patterns and structures from data. By exploiting these methods, researchers can be able to explore the underlying dynamics of the time series.

MSE analysis

MSE analysis is an approach to investigate the complexity of time series. Its efficiency has been proven in a large number of studies on biological systems and it is being widely used in various fields of study such as physiology35,51, seismology52, economy53, and cosmology54. Time series that include different characteristics can provide valuable information about the corresponding physical systems. For example, the system with more self-similarity is scale invariance over a broader range of scales. Thus, its components are constructed to be more correlated than the other systems. Accordingly, it seems to be observable a footprint of these types of characteristics in the corresponding time series of the underlying system. Since entropy is proportional to the amount of information required to describe a system’s state, it may be considered a measure of complexity. However, randomized time series exhibit higher entropy values than their original counterparts, despite the randomizing process eliminating correlations and thereby reducing complexity. Consequently, using entropy to directly compare the complexity of different time series appears problematic. In such a situation, MSE solves the problem. Imagine a time series with N numbers, like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{x_1, x_2, \cdots , x_i, \cdots , x_N\}$$\end{document}. A coarse-grained time series like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{y_j^{(\tau )}\}$$\end{document} with a scale factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} can be produced from the original time series, as follows1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y_j^{(\tau )} = \frac{1}{\tau } \Sigma _{i=(j-1)\tau +1}^{j\tau }~~ x_i,~~~~~~~~1 \le j \le \frac{N}{\tau }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}One can see that for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau = 1$$\end{document}, the above relation produces the original time series. If we construct coarse-grained time series for different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} and calculate their entropies, investigating how entropy changes as a function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} reveals an important phenomenon. Although stochastic time series exhibit higher entropy values than complex ones at small scales, their entropy decreases rapidly as the scale factor increases due to correlation loss during coarse-graining. In contrast, complex time series show a slower decrease in entropy or maintain constant values across scales36.

The entropy calculation in the mentioned procedure is sample entropy (SampEn). The process of studying a time series calculating sample entropy for different scales is called MSE analysis. The procedure is applied to the whole time series during the explained process. The definition of SampEn and its properties can be found in the literature vastly35,36,39. Although wavelet-based entropy analysis has been used in solar studies33,34 (as we mentioned in Section “Introduction”), the approach suffers from critical limitations: for instance, the type of wavelet function (e.g., Haar, Daubechies) and parameters related to the selected wavelet (e.g., thresholding value and decomposition levels) make it challenging wherein inappropriate choices can lead to inaccurate entropy assessments. Actually, there is no universal rule for choosing the optimal wavelet function, scale, and accepted protocol for wavelet-based usage, resulting in potential inconsistencies. Moreover, the complexity measure at one scale may be affected by entropy at other scales, complicating the interpretation of specific features. Boundary effects at the beginning and end of the time series can influence on the wavelet coefficients. The computational complexity is high, especially for long signals or fine-scale analysis. Noise sensitivity and limited ability to handle non-stationary signals, especially when dealing with rapid transient events, are common limitations of this method and MSE analysis (e.g., see55–60). Moreover, the MSE estimation is independent of the signal length and is more interpretable. Therefore, the MSE approach appears to be the better choice for our analysis.

DFA and R/S test

We employed the DFA as one of the statistical methods to test the long-range correlated behavior and self-similarity in time series. It is known for finding self-similar behavior in time series data, which can be found in various fields such as finance (see61 and references therein), neuroscience62, and environmental science63,64. The DFA uses the basic idea of removing trends at various time scales of time series fluctuations41. We start with the original time series x(t), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t = 1, 2, \ldots , N$$\end{document} and N is the total number of data points. The integrated time series y(t) is computed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y(t) = \sum _{i=1}^{t} (x(i) - \bar{x})$$\end{document}, wherein \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{x}$$\end{document} is the mean of the original time series. Then, the integrated series y(t) is divided into non-overlapping windows of equal length s. The detrended procedure is applied to each window by fitting a polynomial (usually linear) trend. So, the trend is removed from the integrated series. The detrended signal for each window k can be expressed as, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_k(t) = y(t) - P_k(t)$$\end{document}, wherein \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_k(t)$$\end{document} is the fitted polynomial for the k-th window. The fluctuations are calculated for each window by computing the root mean square fluctuation F(s), which provides the average fluctuation of the detrended time series as follows2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F(s) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_s} \sum _{k=1}^{N_s} \left( \frac{1}{s} \sum _{j=1}^{s} y_k(j) \right) ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_s$$\end{document} is the total number of windows of length s.

Finally, to find scaling behavior, the procedure is repeated for different window lengths s to identify the relationship between F(s) and s. R/S test, or rescaled range analysis, is a statistical method used to extract the long-range dependencies and self-similarities from time series data in different fields of studies (e.g., see65,66). It specifies the degree of variability in a dataset during various time scales which gives information about fractality properties in the data67. We first compute the mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{X}$$\end{document} of a given time series X(t) with N observations. Then, the cumulative deviation from the mean is obtained by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y(t) = \sum _{i=1}^{t} (X(i) - \bar{X})$$\end{document}. For each time scale n, the time series is segmented into k non-overlapping windows of length n (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = \frac{N}{n})$$\end{document}. The range R for each window is computed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_j = \max (Y(t)) - \min (Y(t)) \quad \text {for } j = 1, 2, \ldots , k$$\end{document}. In the next step, the standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_n$$\end{document} of the windows is calculated as follows4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_n = \sqrt{\frac{1}{k} \sum _{j=1}^{k} \left( Y_j - \bar{Y} \right) ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_j$$\end{document} is the mean of the cumulative deviations in the window.

Finally, the rescaled range R/S is then obtained by5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} (R/S) = \frac{\langle R \rangle }{S_n}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle R \rangle$$\end{document} is the average range over all windows.

The relationship between the rescaled range and the time scale can often be expressed as following scaling behavior:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} (R/S) \sim n^{H}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where H is the Hurst exponent, indicating the degree of being persistent or anti-persistent in the time series as mentioned in DFA method. VG construction

VG analysis is a method used to transform a time series into a graph representation, where the nodes of the graph correspond to the data points of the time series46,68,69. This approach allows to study the complex structures and patterns in the data by focusing on the properties of the resultant graph. The visibility graph is constructed from a time series \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X = \{X_1, X_2, \ldots , X_N\}$$\end{document}. Each point in the time series \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_i$$\end{document} is a node in the graph. An edge is drawn between two nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_j$$\end{document} (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i < j$$\end{document}) if the visibility criterion is satisfied. Based on this criterion, if there is a line of sight between the two data points (i.e., all points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_k$$\end{document} (where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i< k < j$$\end{document}) do not obstruct the view of points), there will be an edge between two nodes in the graph. In the point of mathematical view, this criterion is expressed as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X_k< \frac{X_j - X_i}{j - i} (k - i) + X_i \quad \text {for all~~} k~~ \text {such that~~} i< k < j. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Actually, the visibility condition implies whether two points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_j$$\end{document} are connected or not. If this condition is satisfied for all intermediate points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_k$$\end{document}, an edge is drawn between nodes i and j.

After applying the visibility condition through all pairs of nodes in an iterative process, a graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = (V, E)$$\end{document} is constructed where V is the set of nodes (vertices) and E is the set of edges (connections established based on the visibility condition).

Common metrics that can give the meaningful interpretation about the systems include shortest path length, clustering coefficient, and degree distribution. The average shortest path length between pairs of nodes represents the efficiency of information transfer within the network. Clustering coefficient quantifies the degree to which nodes tend to cluster together, giving an idea of the local connectivity (or forming a triangle) in the graph. The clustering coefficient reflects the likelihood that whether a node’s neighbors are interconnected or not. In degree distribution, the degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_i$$\end{document} of a node i is the number of edges connected to it. The degree distribution gives significant information about the structure and connectivity of the graph. The details of their computations are as follows:

As we noted, the path length in a graph is the number of edges in the shortest path which connects two nodes. In an unweighted graph, we can obtain the path length by counting the edges between the two nodes. To compute the average path length L of a graph, we use the following formula:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L = \frac{1}{N(N-1)} \sum _{i \ne j} d(i, j), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N is the number of nodes in the graph and d(i, j) is the shortest path length between nodes i and j.

The clustering coefficient quantifies the likelihood that the neighbors of a node are also connected to each other. For a specific node v, the local clustering coefficient C(v) is calculated as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C(v) = \frac{2E_v}{k_v(k_v - 1)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_v$$\end{document} is the number of edges between the neighbors of node v, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_v$$\end{document} is the degree of node v (the number of connections it has). So, the global clustering coefficient can be estimated by averaging the local clustering coefficients:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{v \in G} C(v). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The mean degree of a graph is the average number of edges connected to each node. The mean degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\langle k \rangle$$\end{document} can be computed as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \langle k \rangle = \frac{2E}{N}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where E is the total number of edges in the graph, and N is the total number of nodes in the graph. Results and discussions

Looking at Fig. 3, we can see that the most of geomagnetic indices including Kp, pc, AU, AL, and Dst have the similar trend with very close values of SampEn. They take the maximum values of SampEn in different scales. The indices Ap, R, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and F10.7 show the similar trend with less values of SampEn than the above-mentioned indices. Except for AE, all indices approximately show a gradual ascent in SampEn over the initial ranges of the scale, and a gradual decrease begins from the middle ranges of the scale. It seems that the SampEn of all indices (except of AE) approaches to the range of 0.5–1.5 on scale 20. Among the indices, AE shows different behavior than the others. AE displays a gentle descent trend in Fig. 3 from the beginning ranges of scales with quantities very close to zero. In research39, the authors carried out the comprehensive quantitative investigation about the relation between the value of Hurst exponents and the outputs of MSE analysis. They found that time series with more long-range correlations have higher values of MSE particularly in greater values of scales which had been qualitatively hinted by35,36. So, according to Fig. 3, it seems that the Kp index has the more long-range correlated behavior in time series. On the other hand, the MSE plots demonstrate that AE index has the lesser values of SampEn through all scales indicating lower correlated behavior in its time series.

When a time series is dominated by structured activity—for example, a periodic or highly regular pattern—entropy values tend to be low. Such a condition is representative of a low level of complexity, and it typically implies the presence of coordinated or deterministic processes. On the other hand, when a time series contains many overlapping, independent, or noisy processes, there is increases in irregularity and unpredictability of the system leading to reaching a maximum entropy. It suggests a high level of complexity, mostly associated with nonlinear dynamics, stochastic behavior, or physiological disorders in specific applications. In summary, entropy values will take a minimum quantity when an organized activity characterizes the signal, and a maximum value when there are many overlapping, independent, or noisy processes. Thus, we can say that time series with more complexity are probably due to modulation effects (e.g., how activity is distributed or how energy transfers among solar regions) rather than a change in the laws governing by underlying physics. So, for instant, changing activity distribution can modify the measurements (like solar radiation) without any actual changes in the core physical processes inside the Sun. Therefore, since F10.7 exhibits stable and comparatively lower values within the solar group, we can conclude that this index is more influenced by deterministic processes than others. In the group of geomagnetic activity indices, this type of characteristic belongs to AE index. In reference9, the authors showed that a 365-day uniformly weighted running standard deviation of daily SSNs yields higher values compared to the noon measurements of the F10.7 index. In the wavelet analysis of monthly averages of SSN and F10.7 presented by34 for the period 1979–2022, it was shown that the entropy values of F10.7 and the SSN are quite similar; however, in our results F10.7 takes entropy values lower than those obtained for SSN. Furthermore, they found that the F10.7 index provides a more stable measure over extended periods, which corresponds well with our results.

Here, we would like to start a fascinating discussion about the relation between graph parameters and long-term correlation. One might ask whether the graph parameters such as shortest path length and clustering coefficient can tell us about long-term correlations in events. There is not a simple answer with “yes” or “no”. It needs more careful discussion.

As we know, the shortest path length tells us how the information can be transferred quickly through the graph. A great value of this parameter implies that we deal with a more sparsely connected graph with slow spreading through the network. Now, we explain that how this parameter provides information indirectly about long-term correlated behavior in the system.

Imagine we have a system with long-term memory. There are interactions caused by repeated collaboration and/or frequent communication. In this case, because the nodes are closely connected and interacted more easily, the shorter path lengths can be representative of stronger long-term correlations. Mentioning that the shorter value of this graph parameter does not guarantee that there must be long-term memory in a system. For example, assume that there might be a short path between two nodes with sporadic interaction. This can not show the possibility of long-term correlated behavior.

The greater values of clustering coefficients suggest that the nodes’ neighbors are highly interconnected. The graph with the characteristics of tightly knit clusters indicate that there are strong local relationships between nodes implying the existence of long-term correlations. Assume that we zoom in a real network (such as a social networks) with highly collaborative behavior. As it seems there are highly clustered groups (a vast number of triangles) in a graph, so it might be representative of a stable team with enduring correlation.

If our graph includes both small average value of path length and large average value of clustering coefficient, we deal with a “small world” network. This type of network is prone to have long-range correlation for two reasons: (1) local interactions which lead to stability, and (2) global connectivity which facilitates information transfer through the network.

So, we must find an index with smaller shortest path and higher clustering coefficient in Table 2. It is true that the Ap index has the smallest shortest path length, but it has a low clustering coefficient. Since the choice of this indices is hard, first we focus on indices with higher clustering coefficient. So, the solar activity indices F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R, and also, geomagnetic indices AE and Ap show smaller values of clustering coefficient leading to focus on the other parameters. It seems that AU index with values of 6.08 and 0.18 for shortest path length and clustering coefficient, respectively, satisfies both conditions. After AU, the geomagnetic indices DST, pc, and Kp take smaller values of shortest path length and greater values of clustering coefficient, respectively (Table 2) which signify the “small world” characteristics.

The distribution of nodes degree can give us crucial information about the type of network and properties of the graph. Figures 4 and 5 (solid lines) show distributions of nodes degree extracted from time series of indices. Before we move on to fit different functions to distributions, let’s first talk about the properties of power-law function for characterizing network graphs. Many real-world networks like social networks follow the power-law distribution. This type of frequency degrees of the nodes, known as scale free network, illustrates high connections (hub) for a small number of nodes, and a few number of degrees for a vast number of nodes which are far from average. This type is usually expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(k) \propto k^{-A}$$\end{document}, where P(k) and A are the probability of a node with k degrees and exponent with a positive value, respectively. The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-A$$\end{document} dictates that the shape of the fitted function which is significant for describing the behavior of the network.

To display the power-law distribution function in a linear form, we convert the scales into logarithmic one. We start from the relation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(k) = Ck^{-A}$$\end{document}, wherein C is the normalization constant. By taking logarithm of both sides (e.g., see70–72), we can obtain12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \log (P(k)) = \log (C) - A \log (k). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}As this relation is in the form of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = \alpha x + \beta$$\end{document} (wherein \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y = \log (P(k))$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x = \log (k)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-A = \alpha$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta = \log (C)$$\end{document}), we can use a linear fit for distributions in a log-log plot (see Figs. 4 and 5, dashed lines). Depending on the intervals of A, we are faced with different cases73–75:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A \le 2$$\end{document}: As the average of degrees becomes infinite, we will see divergence in the sum of probabilities P(k) for all k. This case is physically impossible. In this case, a vast number of nodes treats as hubs and it is very sensitive to removing hubs.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2< A < 3$$\end{document}: Many network graphs that we deal with in real world take values from this range indicating scale-free network. The heavy tail tells that the hubs still important, but not as dominant as previous case. Actually, there is a robustness in a graph that causes not to be relatively affected by removing hubs.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A \approx 3$$\end{document}: This type of network is a universality class for critical phenomena. Hubs exist but are less dominant and the variance of degree diverges. Actually, fluctuations dominate system behavior.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A > 3$$\end{document}: When the exponent of the power-law function takes values from this range, the network treats very close to a random network. We can say that with increasing A, the role of hubs decreases. There will be a transition to a more homogenous structure with a more uniform distributed case.

We should note that the method of fitting and the existing different types of noises can have influences on fitting results and their uncertainties. Furthermore, because of the existence of some stochastic procedures in the system of interest, researchers deal with the mixture of various distributions, making them hard to model and extract the appropriate physical interpretation for their behavior.

In Table 4, we present the Pearson and Spearman correlation values computed between each pair of indices, shown in bold and italic, respectively. As is well known, Pearson correlation measures linear relationships between pairs of parameters and assumes normality, while Spearman correlation determines monotonic relationships (not necessarily linear) and performs well with non-normal data or ranked variables. The approximate ranges of [0, 0.09], [0.10, 0.39], [0.40, 0.69], [0.70, 0.89], and [0.90, 1.00] correspond, respectively, to no relationship, weak correlation, moderate correlation, strong correlation, and perfect correlation for both Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients. Negative values within these ranges indicate an anti- (inverse) correlation between pairs of parameters.

We found that there are all classified ranges of correlations between pairs of indices (see Table 4). We first focus on pairs of parameters indicating strong positive correlations. The Kp and Ap indices show high values for both Pearson (strong association with a value of 0.85) and Spearman (perfect association with a value of 0.99) correlations, exhibiting very similar behavior between these indices. Likewise, Kp and pc indices show strongly correlated behavior, with Pearson and Spearman correlation values of 0.86 and 0.83, respectively. As expected, Ap and pc also display strong positive correlations, with Pearson and Spearman values of 0.82 and 0.83, respectively. Since F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R (SSN) represent solar activity, there is a strong positive linear relationship between F10.7 and Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document} (Pearson = 0.77), with a higher Spearman correlation of 0.97. As expected, the F10.7 and R indices demonstrate a strong linear correlation (0.76) and a near-perfect monotonic relationship (0.95), consistent with findings reported by8,9,25,34. Furthermore, the auroral electrojet indices AE and AU exhibit near-perfect correlations, with Pearson and Spearman values of 0.93 and 0.96, respectively. AU and pc also demonstrate strong monotonic and linear relationships (Pearson = 0.80, Spearman = 0.81), implying that they are influenced by similar geomagnetic events. Next, we consider pairs of parameters with strong negative correlations. The Kp and AL indices demonstrate a strong inverse relationship, with Pearson and Spearman values of − 0.91 and − 0.92, respectively. As expected, AE and AL exhibit an almost perfect anti-correlation behavior with a Pearson value of − 0.98 and a Spearman value of − 0.99, returning their physical definitions as components of auroral electrojet activity with which are measured in opposite directions.

Solar activity indices like SSN and F10.7 are commonly utilized as a global estimation of solar activity (e.g., see8,9,25), though they actually return to localized processes. Despite this, there is strongly correlated behavior between indices, probably because they are influenced by the solar activity cycle. The findings suggest that this may reflect the importance of modulation across various solar regions, rather than changes in the underlying physics of the Sun9,34. We see that while some indices represent strong temporal correlations (in both Pearson and Spearman methods), their complexity behavior differs during the various scales employed in this study.

Some pairs of indices exhibit correlation values that lie on the boundary between moderate and strong correlation (or anti-correlation) ranges. For instance, the Dst and AL indices show an approximately strong Pearson correlation (0.74), while their Spearman correlation is equal to 0.69 (slightly lower than of Pearson value), indicating a moderate monotonic relationship. The Ap and AU indices also reflect a moderate to strong positive association, with a Pearson correlation of 0.70 and a higher Spearman correlation of 0.87. Furthermore, the Dst and AU indices exhibit a moderate anti-correlated behavior in both Pearson and Spearman values, each with a value of − 0.55. The Dst and pc indices also show an inverse relationship, with Pearson and Spearman values of − 0.72 and − 0.66, respectively—indicating that higher Dst values (associated with weaker geomagnetic storms) tend to correspond with lower polar cap activity.

Moderate correlation values typically return to complex underlying processes in which the indices interact through overlapping; while, they do not have similar physical drivers. In such cases, although there is a statistical relationship between the indices, but other impacts—such as nonlinear dynamics or external factors—may meaningfully affect their trends. The obtained results from both strong and moderate correlations (and anti-correlations) give an important insight into the underlying process of phenomena: a high correlation between two indices does not necessarily guarantee that they exhibit identical behavior in MSE analysis. While correlation quantifies the degree of statistical association between time series, MSE reveals differences in the underlying complexity of their dynamics. This suggests that even highly correlated indices may display distinct patterns of variability and information content across different temporal scales.

The combined use of entropy, fractal, and network-based analyses reveals that geomagnetic and solar indices differ significantly in complexity, memory, and structural behavior. This multidimensional approach enhances our insight by identifying which indices are better suited for modeling long-term magnetospheric dynamics (e.g., Kp, Dst, AU) versus those that reflect short-term variability (e.g., AE, F10.7). It also shows that although some indices exhibit strong linear correlations, their MSE profiles reveal different complexity behaviors—indicating that statistical correlation alone is insufficient to capture the dynamics of underlying processes. The aim of this paper is not to propose an approach for predicting solar activity or forecasting its impact on geomagnetic indices. As discussed in34,76, accurate prediction of solar activity and related phenomena (such as high-speed solar streams), and also, space-weather forecasting, necessitates the use of new forecasting methods along with more solar data and geomagnetic parameters and detailed numerical modeling. We intend to present possible methods for estimating the long-term correlation between some solar and geomagnetic activity indices. This work provides a baseline for future studies focused on time series prediction using complexity analysis.

Conclusions

In this work, we focus on the complexity of time series of geomagnetic and solar activity indices of Kp, DST, Ap, F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, AE, AL, AU, pc, and R. We collected LRO data sets from OMNI2 platform recorded from January first, 1996 to January first, 2020. Methodologically, our paper involves combining MSE, DFA, R/S, and VG analyses on daily geomagnetic and solar indices, allowing cross-validation between entropy-based, Hurst exponent, network-topology, and correlation-based measures. This integrated approach complements time-local wavelet entropy studies (e.g.,33,34) by providing a robust MSE analysis, self-affinity recognition framework, and network perspective. Since all methods carry inherent uncertainties, employing diverse approaches serves as a superior strategy to achieve robust and comprehensive results.

More complexity means having more correlations in multiple scales. A time series with complex structures across multiple scales demonstrates slower correlation loss by coarse-graining during MSE analysis, so it has higher values of entropy across the scales. Considering this, we can interpret what is shown in Fig. 3. As the values of entropy for AE are lower than those of all other indices, we can claim it has a lower degree of complexity. F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R, respectively, have higher values than AE in the range of scales, and Ap is above all of these four indices. The indices of AL, Dst, AU, and pc have values close to each other after the scale of four and drop to values lower than 1.5 by the scale of 20, but still are higher than Ap. In comparison, AE, F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R exhibit the lowest entropy values across all scales. This lower complexity suggests weaker correlated behavior in their time series relative to other indices.

Using both DFA and R/S test, we found that the Hurst exponents of the time series belonged to the geomagnetic indices (i.e., Kp, DST, Ap, AE, AL, AU, pc) take values in the range (0.5,1). This signifies that they have long-range correlation in their signals, reflecting long-term memory. Such self-similarity corresponds to elevated fractal dimensionality in the system. This implies a fractal system where subsets encode global information77, characteristic of scale-free dynamics. Such behavior suggests that the time series of the geomagnetic indices exhibit self-organized criticality—demonstrating scale-invariance and autonomous self-tuning during system evolution. On the other hand, the ranges of the Hurst exponents extracted from the time series of solar activity indices (i.e., Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R) shows that they are probably classified in the group of pink noise or non-stationary signals.

The correlation analysis of geomagnetic and solar activity indices reveals that many pairs exhibit strong positive or negative linear and monotonic relationships, consistent with their underlying connections. Indices such as Kp, Ap, and pc, as well as solar activity indicators F10.7, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, and R (SSN), significantly exhibit strongly correlated behavior indicating the modulation effect of solar cycle activities. However, moderate correlations mostly reflect more complex interactions affected by interconnecting phenomena, while owning separate physical drivers. So, high correlation coefficients may exist between time series of phenomena, but their complexities across different time scales can still vary substantially. MSE analysis represents that there might be highly correlated behavior between time series of indices, while their underlying dynamics are completely different, emphasizing the need to estimate both correlation and complexity factors to comprehensively grasp solar and geomagnetic variability.

Here, we present mathematical tools to analyze the complexity and correlated behavior of geomagnetic and solar activity indices. Methods like DFA and R/S analysis determine the Hurst exponent range (0.5–1) for long-term memory. However, when comparing multiple time series within this range, alternative techniques are required to identify which exhibit stronger correlations. MSE and VG approaches provide frameworks for ranking time series by long-term memory intensity. Notably, some methodologies may fail to detect existing long-term memory, highlighting that no single technique can universally segregate long-range correlations. On the other hand, some approaches may suggest scale-invariance of systems (as observed in some node degree frequencies), while actually returning computational artifacts or inappropriate methodological choices. Thus, the complexity of the system necessitates a probabilistic approach to assess self-similarity in time series of events. Based on the combined results from the MSE, DFA, R/S, and VG analyses, indices such as Kp, Dst, AL, AU, and pc exhibit stronger evidence of long-term correlated behavior compared to others, implying they are relatively more informative for model building. In contrast, R, Lyman\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}, F10.7, and AE show comparatively weaker evidence of such behavior, suggesting they may be less informative for studying long-term memory under the applied methods. Actually, these indices contain stronger contributions from short-term or localized processes and may be better suited for studying transient phenomena. We emphasize that these are relative assessments tied to the sampling and period of our study; long duration analyses (longer records or time-local methods) will be necessary to generalize these assessments beyond the frameworks considered here.

In future work, we intend to investigate the complexity and long-range behavior of solar and geomagnetic activity indices during the rising and declining phases of Solar Cycles 23 and 24 (particularly during Gnevyshev gap33) using the proposed techniques. Furthermore, the other entropy-based methods (such as Tsallis, Kaniadakis, and Fuzzy) will be used in time series to compare results and provide a deeper understanding of the underlying physical processes of event-based analysis. Interpreting variations in the extracted parameters will provide a foundation for forecasting changes in solar activity and geomagnetic indices.
