Point of Order : Action-Aware LLM Persona Modeling for Realistic Civic Simulation
Scott Merrill, Shashank Srivastava

Abstract
Large language models offer opportunities to simulate multi-party deliberation, but realistic modeling remains limited by a lack of speaker-attributed data. Transcripts produced via automatic speech recognition (ASR) assign anonymous speaker labels (e.g., Speaker_1 ), preventing models from capturing consistent human behavior. This work introduces a reproducible pipeline to transform public Zoom recordings into speaker-attributed transcripts with metadata like persona profiles and pragmatic action tags (e.g., [propose_motion] ). We release three local government deliberation datasets: Appellate Court hearings, School Board meetings, and Municipal Council sessions. Fine-tuning LLMs to model specific participants using this “action-aware” data produces a 67% reduction in perplexity and nearly doubles classifier-based performance metrics for speaker fidelity and realism. Turing-style human evaluations show our simulations are often indistinguishable from real deliberations, providing a practical and scalable method for complex realistic civic simulations.

Introduction

Figure 1:

Public Zoom recordings are converted into speaker-attributed transcripts using a speaker-linking pipeline that aligns anonymous diarization labels with persistent real-world identities across videos. From these transcripts, we automatically extract structured metadata: topics, action tags, and speaker profiles; which condition PEFT fine-tuned LLMs for persona modeling. We evaluate these LLM personas on perplexity, classifier fool rate, and speaker attribution accuracy.

Deliberative forums such as courtrooms, city councils, and school boards shape high-stakes decisions in law, infrastructure, and education. These settings involve structured debate, negotiation, and strategic interaction among identifiable participants whose roles and goals meaningfully influence outcomes. Large Language Models (LLMs) create a promising opportunity for realistic simulation of these complex, multi-agent interactions. Such simulations enable the exploration of “what-if” scenarios: how outcomes shift with different agendas, participants, or procedural rules; and provide a risk-free environment for analyzing institutional decision-making. Figure
1
summarizes our approach to building such simulations from real meeting data.

Achieving this vision faces a major bottleneck: although millions of public meeting recordings exist, ASR-generated transcripts typically assign only generic speaker labels (e.g.,
Speaker_1
,
Speaker_2
), preventing models from capturing consistent personas, behaviors, or interaction patterns across recordings. This impedes meaningful simulation of specific individuals. We address this challenge with a lightweight multimodal speaker-linking framework (Figure
1
, top). Our method integrates visual cues (speaker boxes, on-screen names), audio features, and textual context to assign and propagate stable identities across large collections of videos. This approach works on standard YouTube recordings and requires no special metadata beyond the meeting videos themselves. However, identity alone falls short as real-world deliberations involve ASR errors, overlapping speech, and rapid transitions between formal procedure and spontaneous debate.

To generate simulations that remain coherent under these noisy conditions, we introduce
action-aware persona modeling
(Figure
1
, middle). We fine-tune LLMs on transcripts augmented with structured metadata, including persona profiles, topics, and pragmatic action tags (e.g.,
[propose_motion]
,
[ask_clarification]
), to model speaker-specific behavior. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) makes it practical to adapt LLMs to dozens of individual speakers. Interestingly, even without fine-tuning, including action tags in natural language (e.g.,
[call_vote]
) reduces perplexity by nearly 30%.

We release the first three public, speaker-labeled datasets of government deliberations-Appellate Court hearings, School Board meetings, and Municipal Council sessions-and introduce two novel metrics for evaluating speaker fidelity in multi-agent simulations:
Classifier Fool Rate
(CFR) and
Speaker Attribution Accuracy
(SAA) (Figure
1
, bottom). Fine-tuning with structured metadata dramatically improves generation, reducing perplexity from 20.37 to 6.64 (-67%), increasing CFR from 0.29 to 0.64 (+121%), and SAA from 0.23 to 0.45 (+96%), producing simulations that human annotators often mistake for real conversations.

Our contributions are:

•

A multimodal diarization and speaker-linking pipeline enabling scalable, accurate identity assignment in public meeting videos.

•

An action-aware persona modeling framework showing that structured metadata significantly enhances simulation fidelity.

•

Three new datasets of speaker-labeled governmental deliberations spanning courts, school boards, and municipal councils covering
\sim
1.72M words in total.

•

Evaluation metrics for assessing realism and consistency in multi-agent LLM simulations.

3
Methods

Realistic dialogue simulations require large volumes of high-quality conversational data. Publicly available Zoom gallery-view recordings are especially valuable, offering synchronized audio and visual cues that make speaker diarization across videos straightforward. The gallery layout naturally supports this: active speakers are highlighted with a colored border, and participant names appear on video tiles. Transcripts derived from these recordings are enriched with layered metadata—including speaker-level profiles capturing beliefs and traits, meeting-level topics providing context, and turn-level action tags marking conversational moves. Together, these annotations form a corpus with rich local and global priors, enabling more realistic, coherent, and controllable persona-driven dialogue generation.

3.1
Data Collection

Videos were downloaded from YouTube using
yt-dlp

38
and processed at one frame per second, a rate sufficient to capture Zoom’s highlight transitions while remaining computationally efficient. Active speakers were identified by applying a color mask and contour extraction
5
to locate the highlighted tile. The lower-left corner of each tile, containing the participant’s name, was cropped and processed with optical character recognition (OCR)
9
. For low-resolution or compressed recordings, an EDSR super-resolution model
19
;
1
was applied prior to OCR to enhance legibility. This pipeline produced a per-second record of active speaker identities that remained robust across video qualities from 240p to 1080p. The full labeling workflow is illustrated in
Figure 2
.

Audio tracks were transcribed using Whisper
24
, and each segment was assigned to a speaker based on the diarization timeline. OCR-derived names were normalized and clustered using fuzzy matching to ensure consistency across recordings. The final corpus comprises time-aligned transcripts with canonical speaker labels.

From this detected speaker tile, the name-region is extracted (2) and processed with OCR to obtain the participant’s identity.

3.2
Extracting Meta-Data

Persona Profile Extraction

Persona profiles are derived from speakers’ extended monologues, as sustained turns provide the most reliable evidence of goals, policy positions, values, and rhetorical style—signals rarely recoverable from brief interjections. For each speaker, the 25 longest monologues are extracted and analyzed using
GPT-5

23
, which is prompted to extract dimensions such as goals, tone, boundaries, and recurring policy positions. These summaries are then consolidated in a second
GPT-5
pass to produce a single coherent profile (see Appendix
A
, Fig.
5
)

Topic Extraction

Meeting topics are identified through a two-step process. First, each diarized transcript is segmented into contiguous 1,024-token chunks—long enough to preserve local coherence and argument flow, yet within LLM input limits.
GPT-5
then summarizes each chunk into 2–3 concise bullet points. These chunk-level summaries are consolidated by
GPT-5
into a small set of topic labels for the meeting (see Appendix
A
, Fig.
6
)

Action Tag Extraction

A compact taxonomy of communicative action tags was developed to capture key speech acts in meetings. Each dataset uses a tailored tagset designed according to three guiding principles:

1.

Action-focused
—tags describe the speaker’s action (e.g., making a proposal, calling a vote) rather than emotion or sentiment, ensuring that annotations capture the pragmatic intent critical for modeling dialogue dynamics.

2.

Compact and informative
—a small set of 15-30 categories captures key distinctions between speech acts while remaining scalable.

3.

Utterance-based
—the utterance serves as the annotation unit because speech acts are typically expressed within single turns.

GPT-5
is prompted with each utterance and the tagset to assign labels, allowing multiple tags when an utterance serves several functions. This automated process yields consistent turn-level annotations while minimizing manual effort.

3.3
Dataset Format

Diarized transcripts are serialized into a compact ChatML-style schema
22
for model training. Each training example consists of previous speaker-labeled utterances from the transcript and a target utterance from the speaker of interest. Context is truncated at 1,024 tokens to preserve dialogue coherence while remaining within feasible processing limits. If truncation occurs within a speaker’s current sentence, the speaker label is preserved, and the visible text is prefixed with an ellipsis to indicate omitted material. Action tags are prepended to each utterance and sorted alphabetically (see Appendix
B
, Fig.
7
).

4.4
Dataset Validation

Dataset quality was assessed through three human evaluation tasks on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), each completed by three independent workers with standard attention checks.

Speaker Identification
(Appendix
F
, Fig.
9(a)
) required workers to match single utterances to the correct speaker from a predefined list, yielding Speaker Identification Accuracy.
Speaker Consistency
(Appendix
F
, Fig.
9(b)
) evaluated cross-video speaker linking by asking whether two utterances from different meetings were produced by the same speaker.
Transcription Quality
(
9(c)
) asked workers to rate each utterance on a 5-point Likert scale for accuracy and readability. To reduce labeling noise, each example was assigned a single label by majority vote: if three workers labeled an utterance and two agreed while the third disagreed, the majority label was used.

Across the three datasets, utterances were correctly attributed 98.5% of the time (Appendix
F
, Fig.
10(a)
), and cross-video speaker links were correctly identified 95.3% (Appendix
F
, Fig.
10(b)
) of the time. Transcription quality ratings were highly positive, with 55.9% “Strongly agree” and 29.8% “Agree,” and few negative ratings (8.4% Neutral, 3.7% Disagree, 0.3% Strongly disagree) (Appendix
F
, Fig.
10(c)
). These results confirm the accuracy and consistency of the transcripts, establishing a reliable foundation for modeling and evaluation.

5
Experiments

5.1
PEFT Fine-Tuning

To study the impact of fine-tuning across architectures, three state-of-the-art transformer-based LLMs were selected:
LLaMA-3.1-70B

31
,
Qwen-2.5-72B

37
, and
GPT-OSS-120B

28
.
LLaMA-3.1-70B
and
Qwen-2.5-72B
are dense decoder-only transformers differing in pretraining data and multilingual coverage, while
GPT-OSS-120B
is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model optimized for reasoning-intensive tasks.

Two fine-tuning scenarios were evaluated: using transcripts with action tags and without tags (see Appendix
B
, Fig.
7
). Fine-tuning employed QLoRA
7
, which applies quantization-aware low-rank updates while retaining full-precision gradients. Learning rate and LoRA
\alpha
were optimized via grid search. Each model was trained for five epochs, with intermediate checkpoints saved for evaluation, and the best checkpoint selected based on perplexity on a held-out test set. Performance was compared to baseline full-precision pretrained models using only structured system prompts (Section
5.2
) for persona modeling (no task-specific fine-tuning).

5.2
System Prompts

In addition to fine-tuning, structured
system prompts
were used to guide model behavior at inference time (see Appendix
I
, Fig.
12
). These prompts integrate five layers of information to support role-consistent dialogue:

1.

Persona Description:
A concise summary of the agent’s defining traits, policy positions, and communication style (Section
3.2
).

2.

In-Context Examples:
Representative utterances from training transcripts illustrating the speaker’s lexical choices, rhetorical style, and argumentation patterns.

3.

Micro-Profile:
Quantitative behavioral summaries derived from training transcripts—e.g., question rate, directive rate, and average sentiment—grounded in natural language.

4.

Conversation Context:
A list of meeting topics (Section
3.2
) and participants.

5.

Instruction Prompt:
Explicit guidance specifying the agent’s role.

Ablation studies evaluate the contribution of each component of the system prompt.

5.3
Simulations

To explore the creation of realistic multi-party simulations, trained models acted as conversational agents. Each dataset began with agents receiving a seed prompt, agenda topics, participant lists, and brief instructions (see Appendix
I
, Fig.
13
). An artificial clock advanced one minute per utterance plus one minute per 100 words, with simulations running until the meeting duration was exceeded. Responses were generated in a round-robin order and the full conversation history truncated to 1,024 tokens, consistent with the training setup. Simulated agendas for each dataset are shown in Appendix
H
.

Two simulation variants were tested. In the
time-unaware
baseline, agents had only the agenda and discussion context. In the
time-aware
variant, agenda items were timestamped and agents were instructed to prepend each utterance with the simulated time. This setup enabled evaluation of temporal cues in maintaining topic coherence over long, multi-party discourse.

6
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate both individual fine-tuned models and the multi-agent simulations they produce.

6.1
Model Evaluation

We assess models on a held-out set of transcripts across three dimensions: linguistic quality, persona fidelity, and identity distinctiveness.

Perplexity (PPL)

PPL measures linguistic quality by quantifying how well a model predicts a held-out sequence of tokens. Lower PPL indicates a better fit to the target persona’s linguistic patterns.

Classifier Fool Rate (CFR)

CFR measures persona fidelity. For each speaker
s\in S
, a one-vs-all classifier
C_{s}
is trained to distinguish
s
’s utterances from all others (details in Appendix
G.1
). Let
N_{s}
be the number of utterances generated by agent
s
. CFR is the fraction of agent-generated utterances
\{x_{i}^{(s)}\}
that
C_{s}
mistakes for real utterances from
s
,

CFR(s)=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbb{I}[C_{s}(x_{i}^{(s)})=1]
.
A high CFR suggests the agent’s outputs are stylistically convincing and emulate the target persona’s tendencies.

Speaker Attribution Accuracy (SAA)

SAA measures persona distinctiveness. A multi-class classifier
A
(see Appendix
G.2
) predicts the speaker identity for each generated utterance
x_{i}^{(s)}
. SAA is the fraction of utterances correctly attributed to the speaker
s
the agent is simulating:

SAA=\frac{1}{\sum_{s\in S}N_{s}}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}\mathbb{I}[A(x_{i}^{(s)})=s]

Evaluating both CFR and SAA is critical. CFR measures plausibility, but an agent could achieve a high CFR by using generic, domain-specific phrases common to all speakers. SAA is a stricter test, as the classifier must distinguish between similar speakers in the same meeting, relying only on unique stylistic and rhetorical cues.

6.2
Simulation Evaluation

We assess whether simulations are realistic, coherent, and achieve conversational goals.

Human Evaluation (Turing Test):

Crowdworkers were shown two short, four-turn conversation excerpts side-by-side: one from a real transcript and one from a simulation. They were asked to identify the human-generated conversation (see Appendix
K
, Fig.
16
). The resulting human-identification accuracy measures the perceived realism and interactional plausibility of the simulated dialogue.

Automatic Evaluation:

We evaluate the functional performance of simulations based on provided agenda topics. We measure (1)
Topic Coverage
, the proportion of agenda topics meaningfully addressed, and (2)
Goal Achievement
, whether discussions culminate in a collective decision (e.g., a vote), reflecting purposeful interaction.

7.2
Simulation Evaluation

Human Evaluation (Turing Test)

In Turing tests, MTurk workers correctly identified human versus simulated transcripts only 45.5% (below the 50% expected by random guessing; see Appendix
K
, Fig.
16
). This indicates that simulations are largely indistinguishable from real transcripts.
Recognition varied by model:
GPT
simulations were detected 60% of the time, compared to only 36% for
LLaMA
, and 42% for
Qwen
. This likely reflects stylistic factors:
GPT
’s more structured, chain-of-thought–style reasoning produces discourse that appears coherent but more polished and more uniform than natural human dialogue.
LLaMA
and
Qwen
generate less structured exchanges that appear more natural to evaluators.

Temporal Grounding

Time-aware simulations can improve topic coverage and goal achievement rates, though gains vary by model (see Appendix
K
, Fig.
15
).
For instance, topic coverage changes slightly for
GPT
(85.1% to 83.1%) but increases substantially for
LLaMA
(71.4% to 94.4%) and
Qwen
(81.4% to 99.2%) when temporal cues are provided.
Temporal grounding also increases consensus decisions on agenda items (28.4% to 40.5%), particularly helping instruction-tuned models that otherwise struggle to reach later items. Providing explicit temporal cues helps anchor discussions to the meeting’s evolving structure, offering a lightweight yet effective mechanism for sustaining deliberative closure.

Emergent Procedural Behaviors

Figure 4
illustrates the opening dialogue of two representative simulations: the Albemarle County School Board meeting (Fig.
4
a) and a DC Court of Appeals hearing (Fig.
4
b). In both cases, the generated dialogues remain coherent, topically focused, and exhibit realistic turn-taking, referencing prior statements in ways characteristic of real meetings.

Beyond surface-level plausibility, the simulations display emergent procedural and structural regularities that closely mirror authentic institutional interactions. In the school board simulation, the conversation naturally progresses through a moment of silence, the reading of a mandated statement, a roll call, and participants introducing themselves along with their represented districts. Similarly, the courtroom dialogue unfolds in a logical order: an initial greeting, opening statements, and a deliberation period through a structured exchange of questions and answers, including verification of representation status.

Across the broader corpus of simulated meetings (more examples provided in Appendix
K
), additional recurring procedural elements also emerge, such as pledges of allegiance, amendments to the agenda, formal acknowledgments, and other common institutional rituals. These repeated features indicate that models have internalized latent organizational norms, temporal conventions, and the general structure of real-world institutional interactions. Quantifying the prevalence of such procedural markers offers a promising direction for assessing institutional simulation realism.

8
Discussion

This work introduces a simple, MTurk-verified diarization and speaker-linking pipeline that can accurately label multi-party meeting transcripts at scale. Using this method, we produce and release three speaker-labeled datasets of government deliberations. Fine-tuning LLMs on this data improves fidelity across complementary dimensions: linguistic quality (PPL), persona fidelity (CFR), and identity distinctiveness (SAA). Human evaluations further show that crowdworkers are unable to reliably distinguish short excerpts of simulated dialogue from real transcripts, underscoring the realism of our generated deliberations.

Our analysis yields several insights for generating realistic simulations of institutional discourse. First, structured metadata is critical. We find that
action tags represented in natural language
are particularly effective, improving generation quality even for baseline models that are not explicitly fine-tuned on them. Second, the effectiveness of persona prompting depends strongly on the model’s training regime: non-fine-tuned models benefit from rich prompts that explicitly supply persona and agenda context, whereas fine-tuned models—which already internalize these traits—perform best with concise prompts that avoid conflicting signals. Finally, temporal anchoring (time-aware simulation) helps maintain agenda coherence, improves topic coverage, and increases the rate at which discussions reach deliberative closure.

The practical value of this work is a reproducible framework for generating realistic simulations of government deliberation. This framework enables systematic,
in silico
exploration of counterfactuals—such as changing agenda order, participation rules, or individual strategies—and offers a tool for both policy analysis and leadership training. Future work should go beyond round-robin speaking orders to model richer interaction dynamics, such as next-speaker prediction and interruptions, extend to diverse institutional contexts and video layouts, and align action-aware simulations with deliberative democracy theory.

9
Limitations

The method requires access to Zoom meetings recorded in gallery view, which limits its applicability. Many real-world deliberations, collaborative environments, or public events are not recorded this way, restricting the universality of the approach. That said, gallery view recordings exist across a wide range of domains—including workplace teams, academic seminars, public-interest groups—providing substantial breadth for empirical study. These data can meaningfully inform practical questions, such as how workplace teams might be structured to improve coordination or how lectures and discussions could be organized to strengthen student engagement and learning outcomes.

The approach also depends on Whisper ASR transcriptions. Real-world meetings frequently include overlapping speech, variable audio quality, and background noise, all of which can reduce transcription accuracy. This issue is amplified in less structured environments—such as debates or public hearings—where interruptions and crosstalk are common. In such settings, additional preprocessing steps may be required. Thus, while Whisper performs well in many contexts, the reliance on ASR-generated text imposes practical constraints, particularly for noisy or high-overlap audio.

Our work enables realistic simulation of deliberative processes, which has both promising and concerning implications. On the positive side, simulations could support civic participation, governance training, and policy exploration by democratizing access to realistic deliberation dynamics. However, realistic persona models also carry risks: generated transcripts could be misused to spread misinformation, manipulate public opinion, or displace genuine human participation in democratic processes. We release datasets and code for research purposes with the expectation that users will consider these risks. Future work should establish community guidelines for responsible deployment of generative civic models, particularly in high-stakes domains like policy-making or public engagement.

Appendix C
Finding Datasets

The proposed method focuses on “Zoom gallery-style” recordings. While such videos may seem rare, a substantial number are publicly accessible. To identify them systematically, the YouTube Data API was queried using three terms:
“zoom meeting,” “virtual meeting,”
and
“zoom webinar,”
targeting content uploaded in 2020—a period marked by a surge in remote meetings due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Only videos longer than 20 minutes were considered, as longer recordings are more likely to capture full multi-participant meetings rather than brief tutorials or unrelated content. Searches were conducted in one-month increments to ensure comprehensive coverage, yielding roughly 10,000 candidate videos.

Not all candidates depicted gallery-style meetings. To refine the selection, a ResNet50 model
13
was fine-tuned to classify whether a video thumbnail represented a Zoom gallery-style meeting. Positive examples were sampled from frames of 100 curated gallery-style meetings, while negatives were drawn from thumbnails of diverse YouTube content, including news, comedy, and entertainment. Applying this model reduced the candidate set by roughly 40%, resulting in approximately 6,000 gallery-style videos.

The 6,000 videos identified represent only a small fraction of the full corpus of gallery-style recordings. The search was limited to three queries, 2020 uploads, videos longer than 20 minutes, and thumbnail-based classification. The goal of this exercise, however, is not to quantify the total population, but rather to demonstrate the diversity of meetings that can be diarized and simulated. Even within this subset, the videos encompass a wide range of meeting types, participant configurations, and interaction patterns. Channel IDs and descriptions were extracted and categorized using
GPT-5
, producing the distribution shown in Figure
8
, which highlights many meetings suitable for studying deliberation, group dynamics, individual behavior, and multi-agent interactions.

Appendix E
Action Tagging Taxonomies

This appendix presents the action-tag taxonomies used for our three meeting corpora: DC Court of Appeals (Table
4
), Albemarle County School Board (Table
5
), and Waipā District Council (Table
6
). Each table lists the dataset-specific tags, their corresponding unified category, a short definition, and a canonical example.

The taxonomy was designed to capture communicative acts central to each dataset’s institutional context while remaining compact and practical for both human annotation and downstream computational modeling. When constructing the taxonomy, we followed several guiding principles to ensure clarity, consistency, and analytical utility:

•

Action-focused
— Tags capture the speaker’s communicative intent (speech act or function) rather than emotional stance or opinion outcomes. Focusing on actions rather than sentiment allows for consistent comparisons across speakers and institutions, and ensures that models trained on these tags learn to predict functional behavior rather than subjective interpretation.

•

Compact and informative
— Each dataset uses a concise tagset (typically 15–30 tags) that maximizes analytical signal while minimizing cognitive load for annotators. A smaller, focused set of tags reduces ambiguity, ensures faster and more reliable annotation, and allows downstream models to more effectively learn patterns of interaction without being overwhelmed by an excessive number of classes.

•

Utterance-based
— The primary unit of annotation is the utterance, reflecting the natural granularity of spoken or transcribed meetings. Multiple tags can be applied when an utterance serves multiple functions, while span-level annotations are reserved for specialized cases such as legal citations. This design ensures that annotations align closely with how participants actually communicate, making both human and automated analysis more interpretable.

•

Cross-context applicability
— While each corpus comes from a different institutional setting, the taxonomy is designed to be flexible enough to capture similar communicative acts across contexts. This supports comparative studies of deliberation, interaction dynamics, and procedural management across institutions, while preserving dataset-specific richness through the mapping to unified categories.

•

Practical for computational modeling
— The taxonomy was constructed with downstream modeling in mind. Categories are mutually intelligible, semantically coherent, and of sufficient frequency to allow robust learning. Prepending tags to the text of each utterance (as shown in Figure
7
) facilitates easy integration with models for classification, prediction, and generation tasks.

To facilitate cross-dataset analysis, the dataset-specific tagsets were mapped to a set of unified categories:

•

INFO
— providing or clarifying factual or procedural information.

•

ASK
— requesting information, clarification, or explanation.

•

ARG
— making arguments, expressing opinions, or persuading others.

•

CITE
— explicit reference to authoritative sources such as cases, statutes, or reports.

•

PROC
— procedural moves that manage session flow, agenda, or turn-taking.

•

INTERACT
— short, interactive acts such as acknowledgments, agreement/disagreement, interruptions, or backchannels.

•

ACTION
— calls for action or assignments of concrete tasks.

•

LEGIT
— statements oriented toward legitimacy, reassurance, or public accountability.

For efficient and consistent annotation, utterances were passed to
GPT-5
one at a time along with the corresponding tag set, and the model was prompted to assign the appropriate tags to each utterance.

Appendix G
Classifier Training for Style and Identity Evaluation

This appendix provides full implementation and evaluation details for the classifiers used to compute the
Fool Rate
and
Speaker Attribution Accuracy
metrics described in Section
6
. Both classifiers are based on fine-tuned
DeBERTa-v3-large
models and are designed to assess whether generated utterances exhibit realistic, persona-consistent linguistic style.

G.1
Fool Rate Classifier (One-vs-All)

The
Fool Rate
classifier evaluates whether a fine-tuned agent can reproduce a target speaker’s stylistic patterns closely enough to “fool” a discriminative model. For each speaker
s
, a separate binary classifier
C_{s}
is trained to predict whether an utterance
x
originates from that speaker (
positive
) or from any other (
negative
). High fool rates indicate that an agent’s generated outputs align strongly with the target persona’s stylistic and lexical characteristics.

Each utterance in the dataset is treated as an independent text sample, with only content-rich utterances (more than ten words) included to ensure sufficient stylistic signal. Data are split 70/30 into training and test sets. All available training utterances from the target speaker serve as positive examples, while an equal number of randomly sampled utterances from other speakers form the negative class. Each one-vs-all classifier is initialized from
microsoft/deberta-v3-large
. Training employs the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
, and early stopping is applied based on validation F1 score with a patience of 5.

All classifiers achieve strong discriminative performance, confirming their sensitivity to stylistic features. Detailed per-speaker precision, recall, accuracy, and F1 scores for the Albemarle County School Board, DC Court of Appeals, and Waipā District Council datasets are reported in
Table 7
.

G.2
Speaker Attribution Accuracy (Multi-Class)

The
Speaker Attribution
model assesses whether generated utterances remain uniquely identifiable to their respective personas. In contrast to the
Fool Rate
classifier, which measures how convincingly an agent imitates a single target speaker, this model evaluates the distinctiveness of each persona’s linguistic and stylistic signature across the entire speaker set. High attribution accuracy indicates that persona-specific characteristics are preserved and consistently detectable in a multi-speaker context.

Only utterances longer than twenty words are included to ensure sufficient linguistic context for classification. To address class imbalance, the dataset is upsampled so that each speaker contributes an equal number of examples, followed by a 70/30 train–test split.

Each model is initialized from
microsoft/deberta-v3-large
and fine-tuned for multi-class classification, assigning a unique label to each speaker. Training employs
Focal Loss
with class-balanced weights to mitigate residual imbalance, optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-6}
. Models are trained with early stopping using a patience of 5 based on macro-averaged precision.

The multi-class classifier achieves strong discriminative performance, confirming that persona-specific linguistic features remain recoverable across speakers. Detailed per-speaker precision, recall, accuracy, and F1 scores for the Albemarle County School Board, DC Court of Appeals, and Waipā District Council datasets datasets are presented in

Temporal Grounding.

We further analyze how explicit temporal cues affect deliberative performance by measuring topic coverage and goal achievement rates (Fig.
15
). Topic coverage quantifies how fully a simulated meeting reaches and discusses each agenda item. To compute this automatically at scale, we use
GPT-5
to evaluate whether each agenda topic was substantively discussed within the corresponding transcript segment. Time-unaware simulations frequently stall after the first item for
LLaMA
and
Qwen
. When temporal cues are added, topic coverage improves substantially: from 71.4% to 94.4% for
LLaMA
, from 81.4% to 99.2% for
Qwen
, and remains comparatively stable for
GPT
(85.1% to 83.1%), consistent with
GPT
’s stronger inherent planning abilities.

Goal achievement is measured using the distribution of votes per agenda item, which captures whether discussion culminates in a collective decision. Because each dataset involves a different numbers of voting agents, raw vote totals are not directly comparable; we normalize outcomes by placing vote counts into three
equal-width buckets
: low, medium, and high vote participation.
GPT-5
is used to automatically extract the number of agents who cast a vote. The
high
bucket corresponds to items where nearly all agents cast a vote (indicating strong participation), whereas the
low
bucket captures cases where zero or very few agents vote (failure to reach a decision).

In time-unaware simulations, models consistently reach high participation on the first agenda item but fail to maintain engagement for subsequent items, indicating difficulty progressing through the meeting structure. Temporal grounding keeps agents aligned with the agenda and supports more consistent decision-making across the full meeting. Aggregated across models, the rate at which agents reach nearly full participation increases from 28.4% in time-unaware settings to 40.5% with temporal cues.
