Assessment of BMI percentages of obesity thresholds and cardiometabolic risk factors in children and adolescents: a retrospective cross-sectional study in Beijing
Authors: Ling Xiao, Yin Zeng, Si Zheng, Xiao-Yan Wang, Zhen Guo, Ying Cui, Rui Tang, Lin-Lin Guo, Jiao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of pediatric obesity, particularly severe obesity, is rising rapidly in China. Few studies have examined the relationship between body mass index (BMI) expressed as percentages of obesity thresholds (%BMI) and cardiometabolic risks in Chinese pediatric population. This study aims to evaluate the dose-response relationship between %BMI and cardiometabolic risk factors among children and adolescents in Beijing.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study utilized electronic health records (EHRs) of children and adolescents aged 7–18 years with obesity attending a specialized clinic. Anthropometric and laboratory data were extracted and validated. Individuals were categorized into class 1 obesity (BMI 100–120% of the obesity thresholds), class 2 (120–140%), and class 3 (≥ 140%). Logistic regression assessed the associations between obesity subcategories and four cardiometabolic risk factors, including prediabetes, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance (IR), and elevated liver enzymes. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to investigate the dose-response relationship between %BMI and cardiometabolic risk factors.ResultsAmong 1,761 included individuals, class 3 obesity was associated with significantly higher odds of prediabetes (OR = 1.49 [95% CI: 1.01–2.19] for boys; OR = 2.91 [95% CI: 1.57–5.41] for girls), IR (OR = 4.40 [95% CI: 2.66–7.51] for boys; OR = 3.52 [95% CI: 1.63–8.35] for girls), and elevated liver enzymes (OR = 2.65 [95% CI: 1.81–3.93] for boys; OR = 2.97 [95% CI: 1.65–5.47] for girls) compared to class 1. Linear dose-response relationships were observed between %BMI and these factors. Dyslipidemia and its components (e.g., low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and high triglyceride) exhibited complex dose-response relationships that varied by sex and Tanner Stage.ConclusionsAmong obese children and adolescents in Beijing, higher %BMI might be linearly associated with greater likelihood of prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes, while dyslipidemia exhibits sex- and puberty-specific patterns. Further researches are required to characterize cardiometabolic risks across the obesity spectrum in Chinese pediatric population to inform targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02940-3.

Background

The rising prevalence of pediatric obesity has emerged as a critical global public health challenge. In China, obesity prevalence among children and adolescents has sharply increased from 0.1% in 1985 to 9.6% in 2019, with projections that this upward trend will continue without effective intervention [1]. Severe obesity has risen even more sharply, from 0.2% to 4.0% between 1991 and 2015, according to the China Health and Nutrition Survey [2]. These alarming trends underscore the urgency of evaluating the health risks of Chinese children and adolescents across the obesity spectrum. Although the adverse health impacts of pediatric obesity as a uniform condition are well established [3–5], evidence describing the dose-response relationships between obesity severity and specific cardiometabolic disorders is limited. For example, a study of children and adolescents in United States reported that individuals in extended BMI percentile categories had higher prevalences of abnormal cardiometabolic biomarkers than those classified as overweight [6]. Similarly, a population-based survey in China found a higher prevalence of abnormal fasting blood glucose (FBG) among children with severe obesity (BMI z-score > 5) [7]. However, conventional BMI z-scores may inadequately standardize very high BMIs for sex and age due to compression effect, where z-scores for extreme values converge into a very narrow range. Besides, variability in z-score at extreme values largely reflects properties of the Lambda-Median-Sigma (LMS) transformation rather than differences in BMI severity, which can be misleading in longitudinal follow-up of children with severe obesity [8]. Expressing BMI as a percentage of the sex- and age-specific 95th percentile (%BMI) preserves variation among children with extremely high BMIs and better differentiates degrees of severe obesity, which is substantial for stratifying severe obesity in pediatric populations [9]. Several studies have evaluated dose-response relationships between obesity severity and cardiometabolic risks in European populations [6,10]. However, corresponding evidence in China remains limited, despite differences in body fat distribution and cardiometabolic susceptibility across populations [11]. Addressing this gap may improve understanding of heterogeneity in obesity and support risk-stratified interventions.

This study aimed to evaluate associations between obesity severity and four cardiometabolic risk factors (prediabetes, dyslipidemia, IR, and elevated liver enzymes) among children and adolescents in Beijing, and to assess dose-response relationships between %BMI and these factors. By delineating these patterns, this study seeks to enhance our understanding of the cardiometabolic risks across the obesity spectrum in pediatric population.

Methods

Data source and cleaning

This retrospective cross-sectional study utilized anonymous EHR data from the obesity clinic at the Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University. The Capital Center for Children’s Health is a tertiary-level hospital in Beijing, with approximately 48,000 inpatient admissions and 2.5 million outpatient visits in 2025. Children and adolescents attended the obesity clinic after being brought by their parents because of weight-related concerns. As an EHR-based sample, the study population may differ from children in population- or school-based surveys, and selection bias cannot be excluded. However, the dataset includes a high proportion of children with obesity and contains clinical variables not routinely available in large-scale surveys, such as Tanner stage and the presence of acanthosis, enabling more detailed assessment of associations between obesity severity and cardiometabolic risk factors, particularly at extremely high BMI levels. Given the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic for outpatient attendance, we considered the post-pandemic clinic visits between 2022 and 2025. Only the first visit record of the same individual was included in this single center study; therefore, clustering or repeated observations were not applicable in the following analyses.

The sample selection process was summarized in Figure S1. The cardiometabolic biomarkers and clinical notes of visits were extracted from EHRs. For individuals with multiple visits, the records of the first visit were selected. Anthropometric metrics (e.g., weight, height, and waistline) and physical examination results (e.g., acanthosis, purple striae, and Tanner Stage) were extracted from the clinical notes and manually checked by trained clinicians; minor discrepancies were rectified where possible and unresolved errors were coded as missing. Cardiometabolic biomarkers were drawn from the structured records of fasting blood tests in EHRs and included fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin, triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT). Blood pressure data was excluded due to low recording prevalence (25%), despite its importance for cardiometabolic diseases.

Our target population is school-age children and adolescents with obesity. Therefore, individuals aged 7–18 years were included in this study, resulting in 2,244 children and adolescents. Records with extreme values (exceeding ± 5 SD from the mean) were treated as missing for potentially erroneous records. Then children and adolescents with BMIs exceeding sex- and age-specific obesity thresholds were included, according to the Chinese National Health Industry Standard WS/T 586–2018 [12]. Professional clinicians excluded individuals with nephrotic syndrome, Cushing’s syndrome, hypothyroidism, hepatitis, diabetes or use of diabetes medication, according to the diagnoses, disease histories, and laboratory or radiological examinations in EHRs. This finally resulted in 1,761 individuals. Given the retrospective nature of this study and the well anonymous status of the data, written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University (No. SHERLLM2024044).

Definition of obesity subcategories

%BMI was defined as BMI expressed as percentages of sex- and age-specific obesity thresholds for Chinese children and adolescents, which was a continuous measure of the obesity severity. All eligible individuals were classified into three obesity subcategories based on the %BMI: class 1 obesity (BMI ≥ sex- and age-specific obesity thresholds [equivalent to 28 kg/m2 for Chinese adults] to < 120% of the thresholds [equivalent to 32.5 kg/m2 for Chinese adults]), class 2 obesity (≥ 120% of the thresholds to < 140% [equivalent to 37.5 kg/m2 for Chinese adults]), and class 3 obesity (≥ 140% the thresholds) [12–14], which serves as a categorial measure of the obesity severity.

Definition of cardiometabolic risk factors

We considered four cardiometabolic risk factors, including prediabetes, dyslipidemia, IR, and elevated liver enzymes. Prediabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or FBG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L [15]. Dyslipidemia was defined as TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, LDL-c ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, HDL-c < 1.0 mmol/L, TG ≥ 1.1 mmol/L (< 10 years) or ≥ 1.5 mmol/L (≥ 10 years), or non-HDL-c ≥ 3.7 mmol/L [13]. Non-HDL-c was calculated as the TC minus HDL-c. Elevated liver enzymes was defined as elevation in ALT, AST, or GGT, using thresholds from Chinese National Health Industry Standard [16]. We used homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) as a proxy of the IR. The HOMA-IR was calculated as (fasting insulin [µIU/mL] × FBG [mmol/L])/22.5, and pediatric IR was defined as a HOMA-IR ≥ 2.6 for prepubertal children or HOMA-IR ≥ 3.2 for pubertal adolescents [17]. Thresholds for all biomarkers were provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages) and compared across obesity subcategories using χ2 test. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD (normal distribution) or median (IQR) (non-normal), with comparisons using ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test.

In the primary analyses, we used logistic regression models to examine the associations between obesity subcategories and each cardiometabolic risk factors. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to evaluate the dose-response relationships between %BMI and these risk factors. Given the substantial biological differences by sex and developmental stage in pediatric population, all analyses were performed stratified by sex and Tanner Stage. Because of the limitation of the data source, available covariates include age and sex. Models for sex-stratified analyses were adjusted for age; Tanner-Stage analyses collapsed Tanner Stage into I (prepubertal) versus II-V (pubertal) to balance physiological heterogeneity and statistical power, with adjustment for age and sex within these subgroups. To examine the interaction between %BMI and stratified variables, product-terms of %BMI and sex or Tanner Stage were included in the logistic regression models, with %BMI as the independent variable. In interaction models involving Tanner Stage, age was not additionally adjusted due to its collinearity with Tanner Stage.

For exploratory analyses, we used the same analytic framework described above to assess the associations between individual dyslipidemia components (high TG, TC, LDL-c, non-HDL-c, and low HDL-c) and obesity subcategories, as well as %BMI, given the heterogeneity of dyslipidemia across sex and Tanner Stage. In addition, recognizing that cardiometabolic dysfunction develops along a continuous spectrum, we analyzed cardiometabolic biomarkers as continuous variables within sex- or Tanner Stage-specific subgroups. RCS models were employed to characterize the dose-response relationships between %BMI and continuous biomarkers. For associations where the RCS models indicated linearity, with P-overall < 0.05 and P-non-linear ≥ 0.05, linear regression models were conducted to quantify these relationships.

Unlike standard linear models that assume a constant change in the log-odds (or mean) per unit increase in the predictor, RCS models partition the continuous variable into segments defined by knots. Within each segment, a cubic polynomial is fitted to the data. These polynomials are constrained to be smooth and continuous at the knot locations. In this study, all RCS models were implemented using the “rms” package. We specified 4 knots for each continuous variable, placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution, as recommended by Harrell [18]. For binary outcomes, RCS models were performed using the “lrm” function and the departure from linearity was assessed using Wald χ2 test; for continuous biomarkers, the “ols” function was used and the non-linearity was assessed using F-test. P-overall < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant association, and P-non-linear < 0.05 indicated evidence of non-linearity.

1,761 individuals were included. Approximately 70% of individuals had available cardiometabolic biomarkers, and the availability of these biomarkers increased across obesity subcategories (e.g., FBG was available in 68.53% of individuals with class 1 obesity, 69.31% with class 2, and 74.27% with class 3) (Table S2). Due to the high missing rate of key variables, including prediabetes (30.15%), IR (31.63%), dyslipidemia (29.87%), elevated liver enzymes (30.27%), and Tanner Stage (6.30%), several strategies were applied to address missingness. In the primary analyses, pairwise deletion was used to handle missing data in order to retain a larger number of individuals and improve statistical power in the analyses. To be specific, individuals with missing values for variables required in a given model were excluded from that specific analysis. Given the patterns and substantial proportion of missing laboratory test results, two sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the main findings. First, complete-case analyses were performed by excluding individuals with missing biomarker data or Tanner stage information. Second, multiple imputation by chained equations was implemented. Following established methodological guidelines [19], we generated 30 imputed datasets and utilized 20 iterations per dataset to ensure model convergence. All analyses were performed in R 4.4.2. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses, P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Overview of the clinical characteristics

A total of 1,761 children and adolescents were included. The median age was 10.30 (8.92, 11.76) years and 31.91% were girls. The median %BMI was 1.24 (1.15, 1.36). 699 (39.69%) individuals were categorized as class 1 obesity, 720 (40.89%) were class 2 and 342 (19.42%) were class 3. The proportion of girls declined across obesity subcategories from 36.77% in class 1 obesity to 23.98% in class 3. Prevalences of acanthosis and purple striae increased from 53.08% and 26.18% in class 1 obesity to 82.75% and 60.53% in class 3 obesity. Class 3 obesity had the highest prevalences of prediabetes (40.08%), IR (86.18%), dyslipidemia (66.93%), and elevated liver enzymes (69.69%), followed by class 2 and class 1 (Table 1). Similar patterns were observed for continuous biomarkers such as insulin, HbA1c, TG, ALT, and GGT, while median HDL-c declined with increasing obesity subcategories (Table S3).

Association of obesity severity and cardiometabolic risk factors

Figure 1 showed the age-adjusted OR of cardiometabolic risk factors across obesity subcategories by sex. The odds of having prediabetes were significantly higher in class 3 obesity for boys (OR = 1.49, [95% CI: 1.01–2.19]) and girls (OR = 2.91, [95% CI: 1.57–5.41]) compared with class 1 obesity, while no significant difference was observed between class 1 and class 2 obesity. The patterns for IR, and elevated enzymes was similar in both boys and girls. Odds of IR or elevated enzymes increased with obesity subcategories, and class 3 obesity was associated with substantially higher odds compared with class 1 (Fig. 1). RCS analyses showed linear dose-response relationships between %BMI and prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes in both sexes (P-overall < 0.01; P-non-linear >0.05) (Fig. 2), which indicated that the log-odds of these cardiometabolic risk factors generally increased constantly with each unit increase in %BMI. In contrast, the association between %BMI and dyslipidemia varied by sex. The odds of dyslipidemia increased incrementally with higher obesity subcategories in boys, with class 2 and class 3 showed 1.37 (95% CI: 1.01–1.87) times and 2.07 (95% CI: 1.42–3.04) times odds than class 1 obesity, while girls in class 2 obesity showed highest odds of dyslipidemia (OR = 1.85, [95% CI: 1.17–2.93]) (Fig. 1). The RCS analyses also showed linear dose-response relationship in boys (P-overall < 0.001; P-non-linear = 0.369) and non-linear associations in girls (P-overall = 0.019; P-non-linear = 0.037) (Fig. 2). However, the interaction between sex and %BMI was not statistically significant for dyslipidemia (P = 0.527) (Table 2).

When stratified by Tanner Stage, the associations of obesity subcategories with prediabetes and elevated liver enzymes were similar across subgroups (Fig. 3), while those of dyslipidemia and IR varied. In Tanner Stage Ⅰ subgroup, the odds of dyslipidemia significantly and incrementally increased with obesity subcategories, with adjusted OR = 1.61 (95% CI: 1.14–2.28) for class 2 obesity and 2.32 (95% CI: 1.49–3.65) for class 3 compared to class 1. This trend was also observed in Tanner Stage Ⅱ-Ⅴ, although the association was not statistically significant. A similar pattern was observed for IR. RCS models showed linear associations of %BMI with prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes in both Tanner Stage subgroups (Fig. 4) (P-overall < 0.01; P-non-linear > 0.05). However, dyslipidemia exhibited a non-linear association in Tanner Stage Ⅱ-Ⅴ (P-overall = 0.006; P-non-linear = 0.016), and the test for non-linearity in Tanner Stage Ⅰ was marginally significant (P-overall < 0.001; P-non-linear = 0.079) (Fig. 4). The interaction between %BMI and Tanner Stage was statistically significant for IR (P = 0.038) but not for dyslipidemia (P = 0.350) (Table 2).

Exploratory analyses of obesity severity and dyslipidemia components

Given the heterogeneity in the dose-response relationships of dyslipidemia across sex and Tanner Stages, we conducted further analyses to investigate the association between obesity severity and specific dyslipidemia components, namely high TC, LDL-c, TG, non-HDL-c, and low HDL-c.

Overall, the association between high TC, LDL-c, and non-HDL-c and obesity subcategories showed different patterns in boys and girls, but these associations were not statistically significant (Fig. 1). Specifically, the odds of high LDL-C and non-HDL-C increased with higher obesity subcategories in girls, whereas the odds were similar across obesity subcategories in boys. Interaction analyses between %BMI and sex indicated that boys were associated with lower odds of high LDL-c and non-HDL-c (P < 0.05, OR < 1) (Table 2). However, the RCS models showed no significant linear or non-linear associations between %BMI and high TC, LDL-c, or non-HDL-c in either sex (P-overall > 0.05, P-non-linear > 0.05), suggesting that these results may not be robust (Figure S2). Low HDL-c and high TG were observed significant heterogeneity across obesity subcategories. As shown in Fig. 1, compared with class 1 obesity, the odds of low HDL-c in class 3 obesity were higher in boys (OR = 2.93, [95% CI: 1.88–4.59]), whereas the increase was smaller and not statistically significant in girls (OR = 1.85, [95% CI: 0.95–3.53]). The odds of high TG increased significantly with higher obesity subcategories in boys, while the odds were similar in girls. RCS analyses showed linear associations between %BMI and low HDL-c and high TG in boys (P-overall < 0.05; P-non-linear > 0.05), while no significant association was observed in girls (P-overall > 0.05; P-non-linear > 0.05) (Figure S2). The interaction between %BMI and sex was marginally significant for high TG (P = 0.076) but not for low HDL-c (P = 0.400).

When stratified by Tanner Stage, high TC, LDL-c and non-HDL-c were relatively homogeneous across obesity subcategories, and no significant associations with %BMI were observed in RCS models (P-overall > 0.05; P-non-linear > 0.05) (Fig. 3 and Figure S3). The odds of low HDL-c increased with obesity subcategories and showed linear associations with %BMI in both Tanner Stage Ⅰ and Ⅱ-Ⅴ (Fig. 3 and Figure S3). The odds of high TG increased significantly with higher obesity subcategories in Tanner Stage Ⅰ, whereas the odds were similar across obesity subcategories in Tanner Stage Ⅱ-Ⅴ. Consistently, RCS analyses indicated linear associations between %BMI and high TG in Tanner Stage Ⅰ (P-overall < 0.01; P-non-linear > 0.1) and no significant association in Tanner Stage Ⅱ-Ⅴ (Figure S3). The interaction between %BMI and Tanner Stage was also statistically significant for high TG (P = 0.039) (Table 2).

Exploratory analyses of %BMI and continuous cardiometabolic biomarkers

RCS models indicated linear associations between %BMI and HbA1c, HOMA-IR, and HDL-c across sex and Tanner stage subgroups. The estimated coefficients for HbA1c were similar across subgroups, suggesting similar linear associations between %BMI and HbA1c across sexes and Tanner Stages, which is consistent with the observed association between prediabetes and %BMI (Table S4). For HOMA-IR, the estimated coefficient was 7.92 (95% CI: 6.64–9.19) in Tanner stage I and 9.85 (95% CI: 7.64–12.06) in Tanner stages II-V. HDL-c was inversely associated with %BMI in all subgroups (Table S4). TG were positively associated with %BMI in boys (β = 0.37, [95% CI: 0.13–0.61]) and in Tanner stage I (β = 0.51, [95% CI: 0.26–0.76]), but corresponding associations were not statistically significant in girls or in Tanner stages II–V. For liver enzymes, only ALT or GGT were significantly associated with %BMI, while AST was not associated with %BMI in any subgroups.

Sensitivity analysis

Overall, the sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the main conclusions. Complete-case analyses (n = 1,157) (Figures S4-S7; Table S5-6) and analyses after multiple imputation (Figures S8-S9) yielded similar results to the primary analyses, with modest changes in effect estimates and confidence intervals. The odds of prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes increased with obesity subcategories in both sexes and Tanner Stages (Figure S4-S5; Figure S8-S9), and the dose-response relationships between %BMI and these cardiometabolic risk factors were broadly linear (Figure S6-S7). The interaction between %BMI and Tanner stage for IR was similar to that in main analyses (P = 0.049) (Table S5). Low HDL-c was linearly associated with %BMI in boys and both Tanner Stage subgroups, and high TG showed a linear association only observed in boys and Tanner Stage Ⅰ (Figure S6-S7).

Discussion

In this EHR based cross-sectional study of obese children and adolescents in Beijing, greater obesity severity, measured both categorically and continuously, was associated with higher risks of prediabetes, insulin resistance, and elevated liver enzymes. These associations exhibited approximately linear dose-response relationships when obesity severity was measured by %BMI. However, associations between %BMI and dyslipidemia were more complex and varied by sex and Tanner Stage.

The anthropometric profiles of our study population align with a previous hospital-based cross-sectional study in southern China, with both studies indicating a high prevalence of physical signs suggestive of cardiometabolic risks [20]. Most of the individuals in our study exhibited central obesity (WHtR = 0.59 ± 0.05) according to established thresholds for Chinese children [21]. WHtR is a validated predictor of obesity-related comorbidities and long-term health outcomes, such as IR, metabolic syndrome, and all-cause mortality [22–24]. Another high-risk clinical marker of metabolic dysfunction was acanthosis, whose prevalence achieved 65.53% in our study and even 82.75% in class 3 obesity. Obesity-associated acanthosis has been described as a cutaneous marker of hyperinsulinemia and may indicate increased risk of glucose and lipid abnormalities [25]. Early identification of this cutaneous marker may facilitate timely diagnosis and intervention of metabolic diseases within the pediatric obese population.

We observed significant associations between obesity severity and cardiometabolic risk factors, including prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes. These findings echo previous reports indicating that the prevalence of pediatric metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was much higher among overweight or obese children (40.5–45.5%) than the overall pediatric population (8.1%) [26]. A school-based cross-sectional study of 7,519 children aged 6–17 years reported a crude prevalence of prediabetes (defined by FBG or HbA1c) of 9.72% in normal-weight children and 10.3% in those with obesity [27]. Pediatric obesity is also closely associated with insulin resistance, an important driver of metabolic disorders [28–31]. Hospital-based cross-sectional study showed that youth with severe obesity (BMI percentiles corresponding to ≥ 35 kg/m² at age 18 years) exhibited 30–50% higher levels of proinsulin, insulin, and triglyceride than those with moderate obesity (BMI percentiles corresponding to 30–35 kg/m² at age 18 years) [32]. Additionally, in our dose-response analyses using RCS models, we observed a linear association between %BMI and the odds of prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes, suggesting that these risks tend to increase progressively across higher %BMI values. These findings indicate that cardiometabolic risks may accumulate continuously rather than emerge at several cut-points. Notably, while class 3 obesity carried the highest odds of prediabetes, the odds was comparable between class 2 and class 1 obesity. This pattern is consistent with findings from an EHR-based study, which reported similar prevalences of HbA1c ≥ 5.7% among individuals with BMI in the 95-98th and 98-99th percentiles (corresponding to class 1 and 2 obesity), at 16.9% and 18.9%, respectively. The prevalence was much higher among those with BMI in the 99-99.9th and ≥ 99.9th percentiles (corresponding to class 3 obesity), at 23.8% and 31.6%, respectively [6]. National survey data also reported that the prevalence of prediabetes or type 2 diabetes was substantially higher in expended obesity classes 4–5 (46.77%) than in classes 1–3 (15.10%) [10]. These data suggested a rapid escalation in prediabetes prevalence at extremely high %BMI levels.

Dyslipidemia and its components showed more complex relationships with %BMI across sex and Tanner Stage subgroups. We observed the associations between obesity severity and high TG or low HDL-c varied by sex and Tanner Stage. Previous NHANES and EHR-based studies have indicated that boys are associated with higher prevalence of low HDL-c and high TG [6,30,33]. Our findings indicated that the associations between obesity severity and these lipid dysfunctions were more pronounced in boys than in girls. These sex-specific differences might be related to distinct patterns of adipose sequestration and hormone secretion reported in previous studies. It has been suggested that boys tend to accumulating more visceral adipose tissue, whereas girls often exhibit greater subcutaneous fat depots [34]. Visceral adipose tissue has been linked in the literature to increasing free fatty-acid flux, promoting triglyceride-rich lipoprotein production and lowering HDL. Besides, lean mass might represent another contributing factor to this heterogeneity. Findings from a multi-ethnic Asian cohort suggested that higher lean mass may attenuate high FBG and diastolic blood pressure in girls with high fat mass index [35]. Literatures identified several pathways, such as skeletal muscle’s role in glucose disposal and myokine secretion, that could theoretically mitigate the metabolic consequences of excess adiposity [36]. However, the concurrent presence of high lean mass and intramyocellular lipids in boys, which has been associated with insulin resistance, adds further complexity to these relationships [37]. Pubertal stage also appears to modulate lipid metabolism. Studies have shown that, rising testosterone might activate hepatic lipase, which decreases HDL-c and sometimes raises TG [38,39]; estradiol increases apolipoprotein-A1 expression and reduces the production of TG through potential mechanism like modulating the expression of SR-BI and hepatic lipase [40]. Additionally, we observed that the association between obesity severity and insulin resistance was stronger in prepubertal children (Tanner stage I) than in pubertal adolescents. This observation aligns with the physiological IR naturally occurring during puberty [41], which might lead to a “ceiling effect” that weakens the detectable association between obesity and IR in pubertal adolescents. Proposed mechanisms, such as growth-hormone-driven lipolysis and elevated free fatty acids during puberty, may potentially mask or overlap with obesity-related metabolic changes [42]. We also observed potential sex- and Tanner Stage-specific associations between obesity severity and other dyslipidemia components, such as high LDL-c and non-HDL-c; however, these results were not consistent across models. Therefore, the results of these exploratory analyses should be interpreted with caution. Given the complex interplay among obesity severity, sex, and pubertal development, further longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate these potential hypotheses.

The findings of this EHR-based study align with recent clinical guidance that recommends focused evaluation of obesity-related organ dysfunction in children with severe obesity [13]. In particular, boys, children with class 3 obesity, those with central obesity or marked clinical stigmata of insulin excess (e.g., acanthosis) merit priority for metabolic evaluation. Among children with milder forms of obesity who are asymptomatic, early metabolic alterations such as IR may already be present. In this context, behavioral and psychological interventions, structured physical activity, and medical nutrition therapy remain first-line approaches to improve cardiometabolic health [14]. Pharmacologic agents, including metformin and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, might promote weight loss and improve insulin sensitivity. However, to date, no anti-obesity medications have been approved for use in children or adolescents in China. Additional clinical trials are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of pharmacologic therapies for pediatric obesity in Chinese populations [14].

Several limitations should be considered and findings should be carefully interpreted [45]. First, this hospital-based cross-sectional study primarily included children and adolescents who attended an obesity clinic, which inevitably introduced selection bias and children with more severe obesity or related complications may be overrepresented. Nevertheless, the proportion of girls in our sample was 31.91%, which was comparable to the 32.7% reported for girls among obese children and adolescents aged 7–18 years in the national cross-sectional community health survey between 2017 and 2019 [43]. Besides, the demographic and anthropometric characteristics of our sample were broadly consistent with those reported in previous large-scale, hospital-based studies, suggesting relatively good representativeness for outpatients at obesity clinics. Second, the pattern of missing laboratory test results may have introduced additional selection bias, as individuals with more severe obesity or poorer health status were more likely to undergo laboratory testing [44]. Third, only basic covariates were considered in our study, including sex and age. Important potential confounders, such as lifestyle factors and socioeconomic status, were absent. In the context of urban China, modern dietary patterns and high sedentary time are significant drivers of both obesity and cardiometabolic dysfunction [45]. And the impacts of socioeconomic status are extensive, varying from attitudes and beliefs towards obesity to lifestyles. Children of families with a low socioeconomic status are more likely to consume unhealthy food and are physically less active [46]. A survey in southern China showed that lower parental education levels were associated with underestimation of children’s weight status, which may affect timely clinical evaluation [47]. Besides, other confounders, such as genetic predispositions and intrauterine exposures could influence both obesity severity and metabolic outcomes, were also absent in the EHRs. Fourth, several key variables in this study represent proxy measures of complex biological processes, which may introduce misclassification and require cautious interpretation. %BMI and BMI-based obesity subcategories, which were the principal exposure metrics, are pragmatic clinical indicators but imperfect proxies for adiposity. BMI does not directly quantify body fat mass, does not capture fat distribution, and does not reflect heterogeneity in adipose tissue depots [48]. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations between BMI-based severity and cardiometabolic risk factors. Another proxy variable is HOMA-IR, which correlates as high as 0.91 with clamp measures in nondiabetic children but mainly reflects hepatic insulin sensitivity [49]. Fifth, the cross-sectional design permits assessment of statistical associations only and does not support causal inference. Sixth, as a real-world study based on EHRs, this study had a relatively limited sample size, leading to wide confidence intervals for the estimated parameters. Seventh, the absence of blood pressure measurements precluded assessments of this key cardiometabolic risk factor.

Conclusion

This study examined the association between obesity severity and cardiometabolic risk factors among children and adolescents with obesity in Beijing. %BMI may be linearly associated with greater likelihood of prediabetes, IR, and elevated liver enzymes. Associations between %BMI and dyslipidemia varied by sex and pubertal stage. Additional population-based studies are needed to further delineate cardiometabolic risks across the obesity spectrum in Chinese pediatric populations and to inform precise interventions for this heterogeneous population.
