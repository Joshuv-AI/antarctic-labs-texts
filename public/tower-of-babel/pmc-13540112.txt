A phase II multi-institutional single arm trial evaluating TG4010 vaccine plus nivolumab in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Charles X Wang, Guillaume Luxardi, Hidetoshi Mori, Emanual M Maverakis, Alexander A Merleev, Alina I Marusina, Alexander D Borowsky, Mamta Parikh, Jonathan W Riess, Ala Moshiri, Lyudmila Bazhenova, Matthew Gubens, Marianna Koczywas, Joseph D Butner, Jonathan Huang, Soo Kyoung Kim, Logan V Vick, Yaohui Sun, Robert J Canter, William J Murphy, Megan E Daly, Karen Kelly, Arta M Monjazeb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) therapy, but only 25% of patients have durable responses. TG4010, a mucin 1 (MUC1) vaccine, has demonstrated activity in NSCLC. We conducted a multi-institutional study to determine the safety and activity of nivolumab plus TG4010 in ICI naïve NSCLC patients (NCT02823990). The target accrual was 29 evaluable patients. The primary endpoint was the response rate requiring at least 10/29 responders. The study was terminated early due to slow accrual after enrolling 13 patients. The treatment was well tolerated. The most common adverse effects were fatigue (grades 1–3) and injection site reactions (grades 1–2). TG4010+ICI had limited benefit in NSCLC with 1 of 12 (8.3%) evaluable patients responding, 2 with disease control, and 9 progressing on therapy. The median overall survival was 7.23 months. One patient with a HER2 driver mutation (which prognosticates poor ICI response) achieved an ongoing durable complete response. This exceptional responder had significant baseline upregulation of GSEA pathways linked to interferon signaling, which may be critical for generating vaccine directed immune responses. In general, therapy decreased MUC1 expression in tumors and PD-1 on T cells and upregulated pathways of adaptive and innate immune responses within tumors and circulating immune cells. However, TCR sequencing demonstrated no T cell clonal expansion or epitope spreading, which may explain the lack of clinical benefit. TG4010+ICI appears to have limited benefit. Further study is needed to optimize the clinical efficacy, which may include a role in NSCLC with driver mutations or in combination with cytotoxic therapy.Keywords: Lung cancer, immunotherapy, cancer vaccine, immune checkpoint inhibitor

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the treatment of non-small lung cancer (NSCLC) and are now the standard of care across early-stage, locally advanced and metastatic settings. Despite the remarkable progress made, durable response is limited to 25% of patients 1 indicating that novel therapeutic strategies are still desperately needed. Mechanisms for primary resistance to ICIs include poorly antigenic tumors and/or tumors with minimal T cell infiltrate, 2 and novel strategies should seek to address these mechanisms. 3 Cancer vaccines induce tumor-specific antigen responses that activate an innate and adaptive response culminating in the production of cytotoxic lymphocytes. We hypothesized that vaccination against tumor associated antigens (TAA) can increase the number and tumor microenvironment (TME) infiltration of tumor directed T cells and thereby overcome primary resistance to ICI.

TG4010 is a modified, Vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA), a non-propagative significantly attenuated Vaccinia virus strain that contains sequences coding for the human MUC1 (human mucin 1) antigen and interleukin 2 (IL-2). 4 MUC1 is a TAA which is aberrantly glycosylated in cancer cells compared to normal cells. This hypoglycosylation makes MUC1 more vulnerable to processing by the proteasome in APCs (antigen presenting cells) and tumor cells. As a result, MUC1 peptides (epitopes) are presented on the tumor cell surface by MHC class 1 molecules. In lung cancer, MUC1 expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC) is 80%. 5 , 6 TG4010 also encodes IL-2, a potent stimulator of T and natural killer (NK) cells, to solicit a local effect. The mechanism of action of TG4010 is to induce MUC1 antigen expression in a non-tumor environment with a functional immune system to (1) induce MUC1 specific immunity via MUC1 tumor antigen presentation to T cells through MHC class I and II molecules and (2) to induce a nonspecific activation of the innate and adaptive immune systems via the Vaccinia virus and IL-2. The vaccine was found to have limited toxicity in the phase I setting as monotherapy. 7 Two randomized phase II trials evaluating TG4010 plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in untreated advanced stage NSCLC met their primary endpoint of progression free survival (PFS). 8 , 9 Based on this efficacy signal, its minimal toxicity profile, and its potential to enhance ICI activity, we conducted a pilot study to determine the antitumor activity of nivolumab plus TG4010 in ICI naïve lung cancer patients with advanced disease after failure of a platinum doublet. Nivolumab was selected as the ICI to study in combination with TG4010 due to a partnership Bristol-Myers Squibb and Transgene SA. This study was performed before ICI moved to the first line setting.

Methods

Ethics statement

This multisite study was conducted at four NCI-designated Cancer Centers (University of California (UC) Davis, UC San Diego, UC San Francisco and City of Hope) from December 14, 2016, to February 24, 2021. Patients were enrolled between March 2017 and April 2018, and the last patient came off study in February 2021. The study was authorized and approved by the local institutional review boards of all centers (UC Davis IRB# 885316). The study design and conduct complied with all relevant regulations regarding the use of human study participants and was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practices and the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent prior to any study procedures. The trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02823990).

Patient eligibility

Eligible patients had tumors with adenocarcinoma histology and stage IIIB or IV NSCLC (AJCC 7th edition) who progressed on systemic first-line platinum-based chemotherapy. Patients had to have an ECOG performance status of 0–1, adequate hematological, hepatic, and renal function per the protocol requirements, and be capable of providing informed consent. Patients had to have at least one site of measurable disease by RECIST version 1.1. The exclusion criteria included active central nervous system metastases; prior cancer immunotherapy; chronic immunosuppressive medications; other active malignancy; active autoimmune disease; tissue allograft; hypersensitivity reactions to components of the study drugs; active human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis B, or hepatitis C; idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; and severe cardiovascular disease, including unstable angina and New York Heart Association class III or greater congestive heart failure. EGFR and ALK mutational testing was required for patients whose tumor histology was adenocarcinoma, and those with EGFR and ALK actionable mutations were excluded.

Study design

This was a multi-institutional single-arm open-label phase II trial. Patients were treated with a weekly subcutaneous dose of 108 plaque-forming units of TG4010 and nivolumab 240 mg IV every two weeks for 3 cycles. In subsequent cycles, patients received TG4010 q 2-weekly with nivolumab. All patients were treated until progression, intolerable toxicity, or an unwillingness to continue therapy. A limited physical exam, laboratory assessment, and toxicity evaluation occurred with every cycle. Cross sectional imaging to assess treatment response was planned for every 3 cycles (about 6 weeks). The trial schema is presented in Supplemental Figure 1.

The primary endpoint of this study was objective response by Response Evaluation in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1. 10 The null hypothesis for the response rate was set at 20% (based on historical controls), and the alternative hypothesis of efficacy was set at 40%. Responses were required from at least 10 of the 29 evaluable patients to reject the null hypothesis, with a one-sided type I error rate of 5% and 80% power. An interim analysis for futility and efficacy was planned after 15 evaluable patients. If less than 3/15 patients (20%) were responders, the study would stop for futility; if more than 8 patients responded, the trial would stop for efficacy, otherwise enrollment would continue. The secondary endpoints included safety and toxicity by CTCAEv4.0, and the efficacy endpoints including overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Exploratory biological correlative studies on tumor tissue and peripheral blood were also included. The full details of the study design, dosing, dose modifications, correlative studies and statistical methodology can be found in the study protocol (Study Details|NCT02823990|TG4010 and Nivolumab in Patients With Lung Cancer|ClinicalTrials.gov).

Correlative sample collection

For correlative studies, mandatory core needle tissue biopsy was required pretreatment and at cycle 4 (week 8), with an optional biopsy at progression (Supplemental Figure 1). Blood sampling for corelative studies was required pretreatment, at cycle 4 (week 8), at cycle 8, and at cycle 16, with an optional blood sample at progression (Supplemental Figure 1). Correlative analyses were performed on tumor tissue and blood in a batched manner after the completion of trial enrollment. The tissue samples were formalin fixed, paraffin embedded and stored for batch analysis. Tissue IHC and IF were performed using previously described methods. 11 PD-L1 staining utilized antibody clone 28-9 (Dako). If sufficient tissue was available, an aliquot of at least 10 mg of fresh tissue was snap frozen in RNA later and stored at −80 °C. Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes as well as one 5 ml cyto-chex tube. Blood from the EDTA tubes was separated into was generally processed within 1 h into peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and plasma. Aliquots of PBMCs were cryopreserved, snap frozen, or stored in RNA later for future analysis. The plasma was snap frozen for future analysis. The Cyto-chex tubes were shipped directly to Covance on the day of collection for analysis of CD16, CD56, and CD69 triple-positive activated lymphocytes (TrPAL). 8

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were immunophenotyped using flow cytometry as we have previously described. 11 , 12 Briefly, PBMCs cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and incubated with Fc-block (BD Bioscience, Franklin Lakes, NJ) on ice for 15 min. Then, the cells were stained with an antibody panel (see Supplemental data Table 1) for 1 h on ice, followed by Aqua-LIVE/DEAD (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were washed after each step using PBS containing 0.5% BSA and before being processed on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, Franklin Lakes, NJ). The data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.6.2 (Tree Star Inc. Ashland, OR). The analysis of TrPAL was performed by Covance as previously described. 8

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed as we have previously described. 11

RNA sequencing: RNA was extracted from cryopreserved PBMCs and sequenced for RNA expression and TCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted using Quick-RNA MagBead Kit (Zymo Research) from cryopreserved PBMCs. RNA concentrations and quality were then quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen), and the Agilent TapeStation (Agilent). Indexed libraries were constructed using the SMARTer® Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 (Takara Bio) per the manufacturer's protocol. Library quantification was assessed by Qubit and the quality by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries' concentrations were validated by qPCR for pooling. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform using PE150 chemistry (Illumina).

RNA sequencing alignment: Raw RNA sequencing data were aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome GRCh38 using the STAR aligner (version 2.7.11b). 13 Default parameters were utilized. Gene expression quantification was subsequently performed using the same STAR software.

Differential expression analysis: The DESeq2 R package (version 1.40.2) 14 was used to conduct differential expression analysis. Raw p-values from the differential expression tests were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini‒Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Genes exhibiting an FDR-adjusted p-value less than 0.05 and an absolute fold change greater than 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA): GSEA was carried out using the fgsea R package (version 1.26), employing a pre-ranked gene list based on log₂ fold changes from the differential expression analysis. Gene sets were obtained from the Reactome pathway database. 15 Pathways with adjusted p-values less than 0.05 were deemed significantly enriched.

Data visualization: visualization of the data, including plots for differential expression and gene set enrichment, was performed using the ggplot2 R package (version 3.4.4). 16

Immune repertoire profiling: unique complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences were identified from the RNA-Seq data using MiXCR (version 3.0.13). 17 MiXCR was operated under default settings to profile the adaptive immune repertoire.

Statistics and reproducibility

Descriptive statistics (including frequency, proportion, median, range) were used to summarize patient demographics, tumor characteristics, toxicity and response status. OS and PFS were analyzed by the Kaplan‒Meier method 18 using GraphPad Prism version 10. The sample size for correlative studies was based upon the presence of sufficient material to perform the studies. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size for correlative studies. No data were excluded from the analyses. This trial did not include randomization, and the investigators were not blinded during the experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analysis of the IHC/IC data was performed using descriptive statistics. Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using Flowjo software (v. 6.10.2). Percent cells were examined for normality and log transformed when necessary. Statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical package (v.4.1.1). 19 A two-sided t-test was used to compare two groups of patients or different time points, and p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Since these analyses were hypothesis generating in nature, we did not control for multiple comparisons (with the exception of transcriptomic analyses).

Results

Enrollment

Thirteen patients were enrolled between March 2017 and April 2018, and the last patient came off study in February 2021. The study was stopped early because of slow accrual resulting from FDA approval of ICI for first-line therapy with platinum-based chemotherapy, irrespective of PD-L1 status. Patient characteristics are described in Table 1, with additional details for each patient provided in Table 2. Notable characteristics include median age of 66 (range 56–79) and 69% females. Seven patients were non-smokers and six were former smokers. The majority of patient's tumors had no (N = 7) PD-L1 expression, 2 patients and PD-L expression of 1% and 2%, and 4 patients had PD-L1 expression of 10% (2 patients), 30% and 40% of tumor cells. Seven patients had tumors with actionable mutations [HER2 (N = 4), METexon 14 (N = 2), and KRAS (N = 1)].

Safety and toxicity

Combining TG4010 and Nivolumab was well tolerated. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) are summarized in Table 3. Aside from the injection site reaction, TG4010 did not appear to add toxicity beyond what would be expected from nivolumab alone. The most common treatment-related adverse effects were fatigue in 8 patients (4 – grade 1, 2 – grade 2, 2 – grade 3) and injection site reaction in 8 patients (7 – grade 1, 1 – grade 2). Four patients experienced grade 3 toxicity (1 patient with anorexia; 1 patient with fatigue; 1 patient with hyponatremia; and 1 patient with fatigue, hyperhidrosis, and hypoxia). One patient experienced grade 4 myocarditis and discontinued the study. There were no grade 5 treatment-related adverse events.

Outcomes

Among the 13 patients enrolled, 13 were evaluable for survival, and 12 patients were evaluable for response. One patient came off trial because of an adverse event and was not evaluable for response. One patient had a complete response (confirmed), no patients had a partial response, two patients had stable disease (confirmed), and 9 patients had disease progression as their best response (Table 2). The nine progressing patients had evidence of progression at the time of the first study scan post-cycle 3 and the median time to progression was 41 d (Figure 1A). The two patients with stable disease developed progression after 124 and 130 d. At the time of study closure, all but one patient had expired, and the median overall survival was 7.23 months (Figure 1B). The one patient with a complete response completed two years of treatment (51 cycles). This patient had a durable complete response and came off study at the time of study closure. This patient was alive and disease free with no further systemic therapy after completing the clinical trial. Interestingly, this patient had a HER2 driver mutation (exon 20 insertion: A775_G776insYVMA), which generally portends a poor response to immune checkpoint inhibition in NSCLC. However, this patient had a tumor PD-L1 expression level of 30%, and we cannot rule out that this exceptional response was due to nivolumab alone. Since the trial did not meet its accrual goal of 29 evaluable patients, we tested the effectiveness of the therapy in the 12 evaluable patients using an exact binomial test. With 1 of the 12 patients responding the response rate of 8.3% is significantly less than the 40% threshold set for a positive trial result (exact two-tailed p = 0.035).

Correlative science

Correlative analysis was performed on all available samples with sufficient tissue for analysis. Previous trials employing TG4010 suggested that baseline levels of NK cell activity are inversely correlated with therapeutic response. Low baseline levels of “triple positive activated lymphocytes” (TrPAL; CD16+, CD56+, and CD69+) correlated with improved treatment response and clinical outcome. 8 , 9 Unfortunately, given the limited patient number and limited number of responses in this trial, TrPAL could not be meaningfully correlated with outcomes. Interestingly, examining the one patient with a durable complete response, we found that at baseline, there was an initial high TrPAL value, which subsequently decreased continuously with treatment (Supplemental Figure 2).

To investigate putative gene signatures associated with treatment response, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on baseline tumor biopsies and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with sufficient samples (n = 8). In a hypothesis generating manner we compared the baseline transcriptome of single responding patient to the non-responders. Interestingly, in the PBMCs, several GSEA pathways linked to interferon signaling, antiviral responses, and RNA translation were upregulated in the responder compared to the non-responders (Figure 2A). Individual genes with significantly different expressions in these immune responding pathways are characterized in a heat map (Figure 2B). These findings suggest that increased baseline expression of pathways linked to interferon signaling, antiviral responses, and RNA translation—pathways that are also likely critical to generating an immune response to the TG4010 vaccine—may correlate with treatment response and should be evaluated in future studies.

Examination of the transcriptome within the TME was less informative. While there was differential expression of interferon alpha/beta signaling and the regulation of TLR by endogenous ligand GSEA pathways, most differentially expressed pathways were not distinguishing of vaccine or anti-tumor immune responsiveness (Supplemental Figure 3A). We were also able to generate a baseline tumor gene expression panel that strongly predicted the treatment response (Supplemental Figure 3B and C), but the differentially expressed genes did not fall into a pattern that could readily generate a hypothesis.

In a hypothesis generating manner, we next examined the biological effect of therapy using paired pre- and post-treatment samples. Using quantitative IHC, we performed MUC1 staining on paired pre- and post-treatment samples from 4 patients for whom paired FFPE samples with sufficient tissue were available. We observed a significant reduction in MUC1 protein expression in tumors post-treatment in all 4 patients (Figure 3A and B). We next interrogated treatment induced transcriptomic changes in the TME or peripheral blood. We conducted a paired analysis of bulk RNA sequencing on 6 patients for whom paired pre- and post-treatment samples were available (12 samples total). We found that there was substantial treatment induced transcriptome changes in the tumor (Figure 3C) and PBMCs (Figure 3D). Specifically, we observed the upregulation of numerous GSEA pathways of both adaptive and innate immune response, antigen presentation, and T cell activation. We also evaluated changes in the frequency and phenotype of circulating immune cells in response to therapy by flow cytometry. We observed a decreased frequency and MFI of PD-1 on circulating CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations, which have been reported by others after ICI and have been linked to improved outcomes with ICI (Figure 3E). 20 , 21 TCR sequencing demonstrated no evidence of T cell clonal expansion or epitope spreading within the tumor or peripheral blood, with no significant changes in the proportion of dominant TCRB clones (Figure 3F) and a trend toward increased variability and diversity of TCRB as measured by Shannon entropy (Supplemental Figure 4).

Discussion

The evaluation of rational novel immune combinations to build upon the efficacy of ICIs is necessary for therapeutic advancement. 22 Combining ICI with tumor directed vaccines is one such strategy. Unfortunately, we were unable to demonstrate the significant anti-tumor activity of nivolumab plus TG4010 because the rapidly changing treatment landscape for advanced NSCLC leading to study closure. Based on our data, one could forecast that the trial was likely to be discontinued owing to futility at interim analysis given that 9/12 evaluable patients progressed at the first assessment scan and that only one patient had an objective response. To meet the interim analysis endpoint, 3 more patients would have needed to be accrued, and 2 of those patients would have needed to experience objective responses. Our data do not compare favorably to historical response rates with ICI alone in metastatic NSCLC. 23 , 24 However, this may be related to patient selection. The majority of patients were never or former light smokers whose tumors did not express PD-L1. Both factors are associated with a lack of ICI activity, and this may have been too challenging to overcome. While we observed an impressive durable complete response in one patient, we cannot rule out that this was due to nivolumab alone given a tumor PD-L1 expression level of 30%. However, this patient had a tumor with a HER2 driver mutation, which, like other driver mutations in NSCLC, is associated with significantly reduced response rates to ICI. Analogous to EGFR (HER1) mutated lung cancer, the clinical activity is very low with single agent ICI in HER2 mutated NSCLC. In a retrospective study, response rates were less than 10%, and de novo progressive disease was noted in over 60% of patients who received single agent ICI. 25 One study reported no ICI responses in patients with an HER2 amplified NSCLC even among patients with high PD-L1 expression and high tumor mutational burden. 26 Another study reported an ORR of 7% to ICI monotherapy in 29 patients with an HER2 amplified NSCLC. 25 The exceptional responder whose tumor harbored a HER2 mutant lung cancer is noteworthy and suggests further evaluation of this approach in a population whose tumors have a driver mutation. However, given the low response rate and limited patient numbers, it cannot be concluded that TG4010+ nivolumab is superior or inferior to nivolumab alone. In the general this combination does not warrant further study.

Two randomized phase II trials evaluating TG4010 plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone in untreated advanced stage NSCLC 8 , 9 met their primary endpoints, suggesting that TG4010 enhances the effect of first line chemotherapy in NSCLC. The reason for the disparate results between our trial and these previous trials is not immediately clear but correlative studies suggest a hypothesis.

In previous trials, low circulating TrPAL levels correlated with improved treatment response and clinical outcome. 8 , 9 We observed no meaningful correlation between TrPAL and treatment outcomes, likely due to the limited numbers of patients and response. Interestingly, examining the one patient with a durable complete response, we found that at baseline, there was an initial high TrPAL value, which subsequently decreased continuously with treatment (Supplemental Figure 2). In the current trial, there is evidence of a biologic effect of therapy. In our responding patient, we observed increased baseline expression of pathways linked to interferon signaling, antiviral responses, and RNA translation—pathways that are also important for generating anti-vaccine responses. We also observed that treatment induced a decrease in MUC1 expression in tumor cells, suggesting eradication of high MUC1 expressing cells. We found substantial treatment-induced transcriptome changes in tumors (Figure 3C) and PBMCs (Figure 3D), with the upregulation of numerous GSEA pathways linked to the immune response. In circulating PBMCs, we saw that treatment decreased cell surface PD-1 levels on circulating CD8+ and CD4+ T cell populations, which has been previously linked to treatment response with ICI (Figure 3E). 20 , 21 It is unclear if the biological changes we observe are due to ICI, TG4010, or both.

Of note, correlative data from the above mentioned previous trials demonstrated that patients who generated T cell responses against the vaccine also experienced epitope spreading with broadening of T cell responses directed against non-vaccine tumor associated antigens which was strongly linked with improved survival. 27 Unlike these prior trials of TG4010+ chemotherapy, in our trial, TCR sequencing demonstrated no evidence of clonal expansion of top T cell clones or epitope spreading within the tumor or peripheral blood. These previous trials incorporated platinum-based chemotherapy doublets, which can induce immunogenic cell death of cancer cells. 28 Thus, a clear possibility is that TG4010 is best coupled with agents such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy (or even mechanical ablation approaches 29 that induce immunogenic cell death so that the vaccine can induce an immune response, 30 which spreads beyond vaccine epitopes and is better able to eradicate tumor cells, even those that adapt to express lower levels of MUC1, as we observed in this study.

Further studies are needed to understand the mechanisms of TG4010 in the clinic and to maximize its efficacy. In combination with single agent ICI, TG4010 appears to have limited benefit, although it may play a role in NSCLC patients with driver mutations. We hypothesize that combining TG4010 with radiotherapy or platinum-based chemotherapy doublets will provide a superior vaccine effect and epitope spreading and will better synergize with ICI.
