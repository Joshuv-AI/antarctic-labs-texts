Implementation of a unit-specific quality improvement process for prevention of hospital-acquired pressure injuries
Authors: Beth K Kern
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) occur in 3%–34% of patients admitted to hospitals worldwide. Early intervention of an HAPI prevention process can help prevent or reduce HAPIs. The aim of this study was to develop a hospital unit-specific quality improvement process (QIP) focused on sacral HAPI prevention. A sudden increase in sacral HAPIs within an acute care intensive care unit (ICU) during 2022 at a 308-bed acute care hospital prompted concern with current practices in patient turning and repositioning. Within a Plan-Do-Check-Act format QIP in an adult ICU-specific QIP, the following areas were addressed (1) assessment planning using staff surveys, fishbone diagram, run chart data collection and failure mode effect analysis, (2) do processes with staff education, product utilisation that included glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and drypads, and flow revamp, (3) ongoing data review through run chart interpretation and (4) acting within the multidisciplinary team to hold the gain. Following initial implementation of the 30° offloading wedges during January 2023, the rate of sacral HAPIs decreased from 18 to 4.6 per 1000 patient days per month. During a 3-month period that included full implementation of an ICU-specific QIP and use of glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and drypads, the total number of sacral HAPIs decreased to one. Overall, the mean rate of sacral HAPIs per 1000 patient days per month decreased from 4.49±5.31 during 2022 to 2.30±2.21 during 2023, representing an approximate decrease of 50%. The total number of sacral HAPIs decreased from 24 in 2022 to nine in 2023. The improvement has been sustained with one HAPI noted for 2024 and zero HAPIs for 2025 through April. The implementation of an ICU-specific QIP and combined use of friction-reducing glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and full body drypads was successful at decreasing the sacral HAPIs.Keywords: Evidence-Based Practice, Safety culture, Quality improvement, Pressure Ulcer, PDSA

Problem

A sudden increase in sacral hospital-acquired pressure injuries (HAPIs) at an acute care hospital prompted concern with current practices in patient turning and repositioning. The aim of this quality improvement process (QIP) study was to develop a hospital unit-specific QIP focused on sacral HAPI prevention.

Patients most susceptible to pressure injuries (PIs) include those patients with reduced mobility, poor health, chronic diseases and overall frailty.1 The most commonly affected areas are the sacrum, buttocks, heels and trochanters. The supine position of the patient or the use of wheelchairs increases the risk for PIs to develop in different areas of the body.1 This was critical to focus on within our QIP, as our data drilldown revealed the harmful injury of hospital-acquired sacral pressure injuries.

This QIP was conducted at the Regional Hospital of Scranton (Scranton, Pennsylvania, USA), a 308-bed facility that is part of a national for-profit healthcare system consisting of 71 network hospitals in 15 states in the USA. In the study described, the intensive care unit (ICU) showed a significantly higher sacral PI rate of up to 8 cases per 1000 days per month in 2022 and 18 cases per 1000 days per month during December 2022, compared with the historical hospital rate of 1.1 per 1000 days per month. The increase prompted an evaluation of current practices that included the implementation of a QIP.

Background

PIs are defined as localised skin damage that can occur as a result of pressure alone or in combination with shear.1 Definition of stage 1 PIs involves non-blanchable skin erythema, stage 2 involves partial-thickness skin loss of the epidermis and dermis, stage 3 involves full-thickness skin loss and stage 4 involves full-thickness skin loss and tissue loss.2

A systematic review of 35 articles reported that the worldwide incidence of PIs in hospitals was estimated at 12% of hospital inpatients (range: 1.3%–34.4%).3 The stages of PIs were stage 1 (45%), stage 2 (45%), stage 3 (4%) and stage 4 (4%), indicating that 90% of PIs were in the earlier stages at diagnosis. The incidence of PIs in USA hospitals ranged from 8% to 40%.3

The incidence of PIs can vary widely among institutions and is a direct measure of the quality of skin care provided.4 Prevention of PIs is essential beginning at the time the patient is admitted to a facility.5 In general, “skin inspection should be a high priority and performed as soon as possible following admission to a healthcare service” (expert opinion). Skin care should include a regular regimen and any changes in skin care should be documented.6 This is aligned to 2019 International Guidelines that indicate the completion of “a comprehensive skin and tissue assessment for all individuals at risk of pressure injuries should occur as soon as possible after admission/transfer to the healthcare service, as a part of every risk assessment, periodically as indicated by the individual’s degree of pressure injury risk, and prior to discharge from the care service”.2

In a review by Jocelyn Chew et al,7 the turning frequency of hospitalised patients to prevent HAPIs was evaluated.7 One study reported that a 3-hour turning interval with a 30° tilt was more effective than a 6-hour turning interval with a 90° tilt. Another study reported that a 4-hour turning interval was as effective as a 2-hour turning interval. Best practices from within the review recommend a 2-hour turning frequency on a standard mattress and a 4-hour turning frequency on a pressure redistribution mattress.7

In a different study, an electronic healthcare recording system was used to closely monitor the number of admission-related pressure injuries (APIs) and HAPIs.8 Out of nine hospital departments monitored, the university hospital intensive care unit (ICU) had the most APIs and HAPIs for a total of 176.93 PIs per 1000 episodes, which was approximately four times greater than the next highest department, which was a medical unit of a large community hospital at 42.55 PIs per 1000 episodes. Patient nutrition status, mobility, perfusion, hydration, skin problems and patient age were significantly associated with HAPIs (p<0.001). These results suggest that closer monitoring of HAPIs and development of best practices can possibly lead to better HAPI reduction.8

Typically, the Braden scale is used to determine the severity of PIs, with lower scores indicative of a greater risk of the patient developing PI.9 Risk factors that contribute to patients’ lower scores include completely limited sensory perception, constantly moist, bedfast, completely immobile, poor nutrition, or friction and shear caused by frequent repositioning with maximum assistance.9 Severe risk scores are ≤9, with mild risk scores of 15–18. A moderate risk for developing PIs is a score of 13–14 and a high risk is a score of 10–12. A score of <15 includes patients with a moderate to severe risk of developing PIs.9

Moisture-associated skin damage (MASD) is characterised as inflammation and erosion of the skin due to prolonged exposure to moisture such as urine, stool, perspiration, wound exudate and mucus.10 11 Wet skin is more susceptible to friction and shear, rendering the skin more susceptible to incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD) and intertriginous dermatitis (ITD), which are two types of MASD.11 Patients with urinary and faecal IAD are approximately five times more likely to develop PIs compared with those without IAD.12 Prevention and treatment of MASD includes the use of specialised hospital equipment, customised linens, dressings, topically applied cleansing agents and moisturisers, and protective skin barriers.10 11

The incidence of MASD was evaluated in 145 ICU patients.13 MASD was reported in 23.8% of patients, IAD was reported in 26.2% of patients and ITD was reported in 15.9% of patients. IAD was caused by faecal incontinence, number of stools, liquid stools and length of hospital stay. ITD was associated with obesity and lower Braden scores.13 In a multivariate analysis of acute care patients, both IAD and immobility were associated with a significant increase in developing full-thickness sacral PIs.14

Body mass index (BMI) and association with ICU-associated PIs was evaluated in a review by Chen et al.15 The occurrence of PIs increased gradually for BMIs above 26.9 kg/m2. However, underweight ICU patients (<18.5 kg/m2) had a significantly higher incidence of sacral (coccyx) HAPIs. The lowest risk of PIs in ICU patients was at a BMI of 27.5 kg/m2.15 Similarly, in another study, it was shown that the PI prevalence was 9.0% for non-obese ICU patients compared with 6.1% for obese ICU patients.16

In a study by Labeau et al,17 a total of 13 254 patients from 1117 ICUs in 90 countries were evaluated for ICU-acquired PIs. A total of 59% ICU-acquired PIs (3995/6747) were identified with a mean ICU-acquired prevalence of 16.2% in ICU hospitalised patients. The leading anatomical sites of PIs were the sacral region and the heels at 34.9% and 19.4%, respectively. The back of the shoulders and back of the head also ranked high in percentage of ICU-acquired PIs at 5.8% and 4.7%, respectively.17

Measurement

Pre-implementation, facility baseline data were gathered for sacral HAPIs for the entire year of 2022 that helped to identify gaps in turning and repositioning protocols, potential failure points and limitations. A baseline fishbone completed at this time clearly defined turning and positioning issues as a key component to address for improvement. To align with this finding, a questionnaire was completed by all nursing staff regarding knowledge related to sacral HAPIs and standard skin care and offloading procedures. The goal of the QIP was to identify the immediate root cause of the increase in sacral HAPIs in the past 12 months (2022) while focusing on long-term sustainability.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected on all HAPI using the electronic medical records and the internal hospital safety data reporting system. Data were collected and entered in Microsoft Excel and then imported and analysed using R-Studio statistical software package. We calculated the rate of reportable HAPIs per 1000 patient days for the ICU sample each month, during both the pre-implementation and post implementation phases.

We examined the run chart patterns indicating a sustained decrease during the post implementation phase. The HAPIs per 1000 patient days by year and patient days were summarised for the ICU sample by year using the descriptive statistics: mean, median, SD. Counts were collected for the pre-QIP implementation survey and post-QIP implementation survey and a frequency and percentage were reported for each survey response.

The total number of ICU-related sacral HAPIs decreased from a total of 24 (baseline) in 2022 to 9 in 2023 (excluding November and December 2023). The mean rate of HAPIs per 1000 patient days per month showed approximately a 50% reduction between 2022 and 2023 (4.49 vs 2.30) while the median rates were similar (2.31 vs 2.44), respectively.

Design

The QIP involved (1) education (including sacral offloading best practices), (2) planning tools (including staff surveys and root cause analysis), (3) data collection, data interpretation tools (run control chart), (4) failure modes and effect analysis (FMEA) and (5) Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA).

Strategy

The purpose of this project was to develop a hospital ICU-specific QIP for sacral HAPI prevention by implementing an improved turning and repositioning evidence-based practice change supported by the ComfortGlide System (Medline Industries LP, Northfield, Illinois, USA) that included friction-reducing glide sheets, full body drypads and 30° offloading wedges. The Scranton facility participated in a collaboration with Medline to implement a QIP that focused on the reduction of sacral HAPIs by 50%.

When we reviewed our HAPI prevention practices, we found that our staff were practising with little or no evidence-based technologies. This finding was validated during a pre-improvement staff meeting and baseline staff survey. Once we had identified the root cause, we organised a formal QIP that included:

We established ongoing data collection using our internal event reporting system with our project goal of reducing HAPIs by 50%. A washout period was necessary to establish a clean start, as we had had some initial utilisation of tools that we wanted to focus on with our project actions. We completed a staff member survey to gauge how practice was currently being done by our frontline staff, how they imagined what they needed, and how we would focus on the following (1) pre- and post-practice norms, (2) teamwork and (3) clinical decision making.

Using a cause-and-effect fishbone diagram (figure 1), we were able to move towards the planning and action (or doing) part of our project. We walked through the factors that lead to pressure injury development, focusing on materials, people and equipment/materials. Major areas of concern that emerged were patient method, equipment, material and environment. For many of these areas, there was no awareness of patient’s/family’s preference, lack of staff education, lack of knowledge of equipment (pillows instead of 30° offloading wedges), no pressure sensing devices, small room size/storage space and perceived low staffing. The findings of the gap analysis and PDCA process led to changes and improvements in patient care using the friction-reducing glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and full body drypads. We knew tools to facilitate HAPI prevention had to be immediately available along with consistent education on the tools.

During the PDCA process, a fishbone diagram was completed to help identify gaps in patient turning, offloading and repositioning concerns that may lead to sacral HAPIs. As our multi-disciplinary team reviewed our first set of data (staff feedback and pressure injury data), a formal cause-and-effect diagram was done. We then completed staff surveys with an anonymous virtual survey that took less than 3 min to complete (to promote staff participation). Based on the survey results, we found a variability in the staff’s understanding of when to turn and reposition patients. Weighing, turning and positioning, along with respiratory instability, was also identified. We also found that staff did not know which patients were at greatest risk for sacral PI development or how to best meet the needs of critically ill patients. As we planned education, we set a minimum goal of 85% staff completion on the product and process before our data collection go live. We defined a 3-month change time after our washout period. We had to act fast to push for improvement to prevent further patient harm and provide safe patient care.

The run and control chart graphs helped to show the median between the HAPI rates and show a SD away from the mean. It allowed us to see if a single month’s HAPI rate increase was within standard limits, or higher.

PDCA step 2: Do

We took all the information and data we had gathered and created a potential implementation plan. Examining the ways that the plan could fail, we solidified everyone’s roles during the go-live. We reviewed how staff members would select a patient that qualifies for the system, how they would order the product/s, how they would be used and then what they would do when the patient was discharged or moved to another care unit as they got better. Working with our wound care nurse as a critical member of our QIP, we defined a process map for use of the comfort glide and positioning wedges that supplemented our decision tree for care based on Braden scores (that were reliable within our specific ICU).

FMEA tools were used for identifying where the project may fail and the severity of that failure to our initiative. Appropriate utilisation was a major part of this initiative as underutilisation of tools can contribute to negative outcomes. We decided to estimate the appropriate product usage on a monthly basis and compare that to what our actual product usage was. We also reviewed the potential of a staff member ‘miss-coding’ a pressure injury. We decided our teams would do a root cause analysis on all reported sacral pressure injuries to learn why it happened and to work through preventing a similar injury in the future. We did not want to include injuries that were coded in different anatomical areas (ie, buttocks). Additionally, our wound ostomy care nurse would become the subject matter expert on defining HAPIs and their stage.

We identified key areas that we would put continuous focus on during QIP implementation, along with appropriate product usage review to monitor outcomes and stay ‘on-budget’. We identified an ICU team member who took on the responsibility for tracking daily stock and re-ordering product as needed.

There were still concerns that staff were not familiar with which patients qualify for a turning and positioning system, and which did not. A special population subset was the obese and morbidly obese patient within the ICU. As noted, we made a product decision tree for frontline staff, educated them on it and deployed it where the product was stored. We tracked the product usage on a monthly basis against our patient population. We also recruited a staff member who knows how to use the product consistently. To make sure there was always an expert at the bedside, we created a super-user group. We made sure product training was always available by offering posters and videos. We also had Medline team members come out on a quarterly basis to educate/re-educate on the product.

Our frontline staff member training included explaining to staff the decision tree and having staff teach back how to use the product. For the super users’ group, we did advanced training explaining the pathophysiology of pressure injury development from a friction, shear, pressure and moisture point of view. We also taught them common questions from new users of turning and repositioning systems so they could answer ad-hoc questions from their peers.

PDCA step 3: Check

Monthly meetings were conducted to review the data using the run and control charts and the monthly product usage. While our HAPIs were low, we still solicited feedback from staff on the initiative. As we checked our results, we knew from our monthly meetings that anecdotally, our numbers were positively decreasing. We did meet our goal of a 50% reduction in HAPIs. This was also extrapolated out on our hospital system clinical scorecard that showed a notable improvement in ranking following QIP implementation.

PDCA step 4: Act

Once improvement was made and shared, we worked to hardwire the utilisation of the positioning products, maintaining a supply of expert staff (superusers) within the ICU as reference peers, and on staff using the decision tree. Keeping the data in front of our staff and our leadership through display on a clinical scorecard promoted pride in keeping values low to zero. The improvement has been sustained with one HAPI noted for 2024 and zero HAPIs for 2025 through April.

Most staff members, 95.8% (n=23), knew that the correct positioning for offloading was above and below the sacrococcygeal area.

After full QIP implementation during a 3-month period from 1 August to 31 October 2023, that included the use of friction-reducing glide sheets, full body drypads and 30° offloading wedges, the team was enthralled that only one sacral HAPI was observed for the hospital ICU. One HAPI that occurred in August 2023 was from a sacral acquired deep tissue injury (DTI) and was defined as an incident with no medical intervention needed.

The mean patient days per month were slightly higher during 2022 compared with 2023 (442±54 vs 400±49, respectively). The total number of ICU-related sacral HAPIs decreased from a total of 24 in 2022 to 9 in 2023 (excluding November and December 2023). The mean rate of HAPIs per 1000 patient days per month for 2022 was 4.49±5.31 versus 2.30±2.21 indicating a 50% reduction between 2022 and 2023. The team felt it was important to consider patient days to account for census fluctuations. Positively, no incidence of MASD was reported during the 3-month period with full implementation of the ICU QIP and use of the glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and drypads.

Lessons

Addressing an increase in patient harm with HAPIs, we followed a PDCA QIP to facilitate improvement. A gap analysis was initiated that helped to identify major areas of concern that focused on the person (patient), methods, equipment, material and environment. Lack of nursing staff education and awareness of the proper equipment used in patient offloading were areas that needed attention. Pre-implementation nursing staff surveys also helped to identify areas that required attention.

Based on the pre-QIP implementation survey results, it was revealed that only 16.7% (n=4) of staff were able to adequately offload a patient using pillows while 83.3% (n=20) were not. This was demonstrated despite repositioning using two staff members in 79.2% (n=19) of cases. However, our post-QIP implementation nursing staff survey results revealed that 100% (n=28) of the ICU nursing staff were able to properly offload patients using the 30° offloading wedges rather than pillows.

The findings of the gap analysis and PDCA process resulted in ICU nursing staff training and patient care certification that led to improvements in patient offloading including turning and repositioning. The 30° offloading wedge was introduced in January 2023, resulting in a reduction in the rate of sacral HAPIs from 18 per 1000 patient days/month in December 2022 to 4.6 per 1000 patient days/month in January 2023.

A multidisciplinary support team was organised to develop and monitor protocols for the prevention of ICU-acquired sacral HAPIs. As part of the team, collaboration with Medline consultants helped to bring awareness of the tools that could be used to prevent sacral PIs from occurring. A timeline and a step-by-step implementation plan helped to ensure QIP success.

The initial implementation of the 30° offloading wedges led to approximately a 50% reduction in the mean rate of sacral HAPIs between 2022 and 2023 from 4.49±5.31 to 2.30±2.21 sacral HAPIs per 1000 patient days/month, respectively. However, for the 3-month period from August to October 2023, full QIP implementation with the combined use of the friction-reducing glide sheets, 30° offloading wedges and full body drypads resulted in only one sacral HAPI acquired from a DTI that was defined as an incident with no medical intervention needed.

Limitations

The findings of this study are based on a 2-year period of time that included a 1-year baseline period (2022) followed by use of a 30° offloading wedge for a 7-month period. Only 3 months of data collection were available following full QIP implementation and use of friction-reducing glide sheets, full body drypads and 30° offloading wedges. Additional evaluation in a hospital setting is necessary.

Conclusion

Pressure injuries are defined as localised damage to the skin caused by a combination of moisture, pressure, friction and shear.1 It has been shown that the prevention of sacral HAPIs can be accomplished through the use of appropriate pressure-relieving support surfaces and frequent repositioning of the patient.1 In our ICU, Stryker hospital beds that were 14 years old were used in this study. Mattress wear and tear is always a concern; however, we were still able to reduce HAPIs. Prior to the QIP study, the staff were using pillows in order to support and reposition patients. Additionally, preventable PI sacral dressings, off-loading of heels support and nutritional support consults were routinely used in ICU.

Overall, this QIP project was deemed successful in relation to addressing our initial concern of increased sacral HAPIs identified in our ICU during 2022. The development of the QIP led to successful implementation and impact on patient-related sacral HAPI outcomes. In addition, we achieved our goal of a 50% reduction in the mean rate of HAPIs per 1000 patient days per month between 2022 and 2023 following QIP implementation.

The implementation of a hospital unit-specific QIP has provided a method to track the continued improvement of HAPIs. We observed a consistent improvement in sacral HAPI reduction post-QIP implementation. Implementation of the QIP proved beneficial in order to prevent HAPIs in the hospital acute care ICU facility.

The results of this study warrant continued implementation of a hospital unit-specific QIP with the combined use of 30° offloading wedges, friction-reducing glide sheets and full body drypads in order to help prevent or reduce HAPIs in the hospital acute care ICU facility. Education and training were essential to the outcome of this study that provided team building for improved patient safety and product effectiveness.
