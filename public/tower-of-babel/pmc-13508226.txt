Drivers of the paradigm shift in norovirus diagnostics: technological innovation, contextual demands, and collaborative synergy
Authors: Zhou Peng, Xinlan Zhang, Xiyan Deng, Defu Hou, Rushi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper systematically reviews paradigm shifts in diagnostic technologies for norovirus. Given the substantial burden of acute gastroenteritis caused by this virus and the current lack of licensed antiviral therapies, early and accurate diagnosis is of paramount importance. Diagnostic technologies have evolved from electron microscopy and immunological assays to molecular detection methods, with reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) currently recognized as the gold standard for molecular diagnostics. However, RT-qPCR is constrained by complex operational procedures, the need for specialized equipment, and limited suitability for rapid point-of-care applications. Emerging technologies, including isothermal amplification and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based technologies, have significantly improved diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and turnaround time. Nevertheless, challenges related to standardization, quality control, and contamination prevention remain to be addressed before their widespread implementation. Furthermore, this review proposes a stratified, scenario-adaptive diagnostic framework in which rapid immunochromatographic assays are used for preliminary screening in community settings, highly sensitive PCR-based or multiplex molecular methods are employed for clinical diagnosis in healthcare facilities, and digital PCR (dPCR) is applied for precise quantification in food safety and environmental monitoring. Looking ahead,, advances in norovirus diagnostics will increasingly rely on interdisciplinary collaboration to accelerate the development of integrated and intelligent point-of-care testing (POCT) platforms. In particular, biosensors based on molecular recognition technologies and microfluidic platforms, which offer advantages such as, simple operation, rapid processing and high stability, are among the promising approaches for achieving instrument-free, on-site “sample-in, result-out” diagnostics.Keywords: Norovirus, Diagnostic technology, CRISPR-Cas system, Microfluidic technology, Scenario adaptation

Background

Human Norovirus (HuNoV) was first identified in a stool specimen during an outbreak of acute gastroenteritis in Norwalk, Ohio, USA, in 1968, making it the first confirmed pathogen capable of causing viral gastroenteritis [1]. Infection with this virus not only manifests as diarrhea and vomiting but is also frequently accompanied by a series of clinical symptoms, including nausea, abdominal pain, fever, fatigue, and decreased appetite. Severe cases may lead to dehydration and systemic reactions, posing a persistent threat to public health [2]. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study, HuNoV infections caused approximately 130,000 deaths worldwide in 2019, among which 43,000 and 54,000 cases occurred in children under 5 years old and the elderly over 70 years old, respectively. This highlights the high pathogenicity and mortality risks of HuNoV in vulnerable populations [3]. In recent years, the epidemic intensity of HuNoV has exhibited an upward trend. The NoroSTAT surveillance system established by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recorded 2,675 outbreak events between August 2024 and July 2025, nearly double the number reported in the previous cycle (1,478 cases). This upward trend reflects intensified norovirus transmission dynamics and the escalating severity of epidemic prevention and control challenges.

At present, there are no specific antiviral drugs or licensed vaccines available for norovirus infection. Although the bivalent virus-like particle vaccine based on the yeast expression system has entered Phase III clinical trials, its immunogenicity, long-term protective efficacy, and safety remain key issues that need to be further addressed [4]. Against this backdrop, early and accurate laboratory diagnosis has become a critical step in interrupting transmission chains and controlling outbreaks. An ideal detection method should integrate high sensitivity, strong specificity, rapid response, and ease of operation to meet application requirements across diverse scenarios. However, existing detection methods still have notable limitations: commercial ELISA kits lack sufficient sensitivity for samples with low viral loads. At the same time, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) requires specialized equipment limiting itsrapid on-site deployment.

Based on the aforementioned challenges, this review systematically summarizes the developmental trajectory of norovirus diagnostic technologies, with a critical focus on evaluating the trade-offs among sensitivity, specificity, and detection efficiency across different analytical methods. Furthermore, a scenario-oriented technical implementation framework is established in this work. Virus structure and functional characteristics

Norovirus is a non-enveloped, spherical viral particle with a diameter of approximately 27–35 nm. Its genome consists of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA molecule, with a full-length ranging from 7.3 to 7.7 kb [5]. The 5’ end of the genome is covalently linked to a viral genome-linked protein (VPg), while the 3’ end carries a polyadenylated tail. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the norovirus coding region contains three open reading frames (ORFs): ORF1 encodes a polyprotein precursor that can be cleaved by the viral protease into multiple non-structural proteins (including the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, RdRP); ORF2 encodes the major capsid protein VP1; and ORF3 encodes the minor capsid protein VP2 [6]. VP1 is the key protein that constitutes the viral capsid and determines its antigenicity, and it can be further divided into two major structural domains: the relatively conserved shell (S) domain and the highly sequence-variable protrusion (P) domain. Among these, the P domain is exposed on the surface of the viral particle; it not only mediates cellular attachment by binding to host receptors such as histo-blood group antigens (HBGAs) but also serves as the primary target of neutralizing antibodies, thus constituting the major antigenic determinant of the virus [7]. It is precisely the continuous antigenic drift in the P domain (especially the P2 subdomain) that drives the periodic evolution of globally dominant genotypes such as GII.4. New variants emerge approximately every 2–4 years, leading to recurrent infections in human populations and posing a persistent challenge to the sensitivity of existing detection methods [8].

Structure of the genome and distribution of detection targets for Norovirus

Based on VP1 sequence diversity, noroviruses are systematically classified into 10 genogroups (I–X), which can be further subdivided into nearly 50 genotypes. Among these genotypes, GII.4 has long been the globally dominant strain and is the primary pathogen responsible for large-scale outbreaks of acute gastroenteritis [9].

In terms of pathogenic mechanisms, the virus initiates infection by binding the VP1 protein to specific receptors on the surface of host intestinal epithelial cells. After entering the host cell, the non-structural proteins encoded by ORF1 are responsible for viral genome replication and employ multiple strategies to antagonize the host innate immune response. For example, the VPg protein can interfere with the translation of host mRNA. In contrast, the non-structural protein p22 (NS4) has been confirmed to disrupt vesicular trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus. These actions collectively induce cellular dysfunction and innate immune suppression, ultimately triggering the clinical manifestations of acute gastroenteritis, characterized by watery diarrhea and vomiting [10–14].

The evolution path of norovirus detection technology

Traditional detection methods

Morphological detection technology

During the 1970s and 1980s, the clinical diagnosis of norovirus relied primarily on electron microscopy (EM) and immune electron microscopy (IEM) techniques [1]. EM enables direct visualization of viral particles in stool or vomitus samples; however, it has a relatively high limit of detection (LOD) of approximately 10⁶ particles per milliliter, making it suitable only for detecting samples with high viral loads during the acute phase of infection. In addition, with a diameter of approximately 27 nm and a morphology of small round structures, norovirus particles are difficult to distinguish effectively from other pathogens, such as sapoviruses and astroviruses, under electron microscopy. This results in a high rate of misdiagnosis and significant inter-observer variability [1, 15]. IEM utilizes tracer antibodies to agglutinate and label viral particles, followed by electron microscopy, which greatly improves sensitivity compared with conventional EM [16]. Nevertheless, IEM requires the prior preparation of specific sera matched to circulating strains, involves a lengthy experimental cycle, and imposes extremely stringent requirements on operators, equipment, and antibody raw materials. Consequently, it is difficult to implement in routine clinical laboratories and is now largely obsolete.

Immunological methods

Despite multiple evolutions over time, immunological diagnostic methods for norovirus have never fully met the requirements of long-term clinical application due to inherent limitations in their core performance. In 1978, the immune adherence hemagglutination assay (IAHA) was developed; it exhibited improved sensitivity compared with IEM and did not rely on expensive equipment, making it suitable for large-scale seroepidemiological surveys [17]. However, IAHA only detects antibody responses and cannot directly identify viral particles, rendering it unsuitable for etiological diagnosis during the acute phase of infection and leading to its rapid replacement by subsequent technologies. The radioimmunoassay (RIA), established in the same year, enabled the simultaneous detection of antigens and antibodies on a single reaction plate, offering advantages such as ease of operation, high sensitivity, and strong specificity [18]. Nevertheless, its dependence on radioactive labels poses safety risks and suffers from the drawback of short half-lives. Moreover, it is prone to missing low-concentration antigens or antibodies, resulting in false-negative results. Introduced in 1985, the biotin-avidin immunoassay (BAI) eliminated the need for radioactive labels while retaining the ability to detect antigens and antibodies concurrently on a single plate [19]. However, the fecal matrix can induce false-negative results, and endogenous biotin may compete with avidin, leading to false-positive outcomes. In addition, its sensitivity is inferior to that of subsequent molecular methods, and its specificity is compromised by antigenic variations of norovirus [20, 21]. For these reasons, BAI has gradually been replaced by nucleic acid-based technologies such as reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), which fail to meet the demands for high sensitivity and specificity in diagnosis during outbreak scenarios.

In enzyme immunoassays (EIAs), norovirus detection has evolved from human antibody sandwich assays and virus-like particle (VLP)-based antigen preparation to broad-spectrum polyclonal antibody double-antigen sandwich assays, which have enabled initial antigen standardization and broad-spectrum recognition. Nevertheless, this technology has failed to effectively address the core bottleneck of insufficient genotyping specificity over the past two decades [22–25]. At present, the market for fecal norovirus antigen detection is dominated by three mainstream ELISA kits. IDEIA (Dako) has relatively low detection sensitivity, which tends to result in false-negative outcomes. SRSV(II)-AD (Denka) shows poor specificity and is prone to causing false-positive results. While RIDASCREEN Norovirus Assay offers advantages such as high specificity (> 98%), ease of operation, and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for preliminary outbreak screening, its sensitivity is only 86%, requiring a relatively high viral load for detection. Negative samples thus need to be retested by RT-qPCR, indicating that this assay cannot replace molecular diagnostic methods [26–30].

In the realm of immunochromatographic assays, Takanashi et al. first developed a colloidal gold lateral flow strip targeting intact viral particles in 2008; this assay enabled detection within 15 min, but its low sensitivity necessitated confirmation by RT-PCR [31]. In 2016, researchers developed a nanobody-based lateral flow assay targeting the VP1 protein. By adopting a directional biotin-streptavidin-colloidal gold bridging strategy, this method enhanced thermal stability, achieved uniform labeling, amplified detection signals, and reduced non-specific adsorption [32]. In 2021, Meng Xu et al. further optimized the detection target to the S domain of VP1, expanding the detection range to 9 genogroups and improving sensitivity to 1 × 10⁵ copies per reaction.This platform exhibits wide cross-genogroup recognition capacity among existing rapid antigen test strips. However, this sensitivity is still far lower than that of RT-qPCR (10² copies), leading to a risk of missed detection of over 30% in patients with early-stage infections [33]. Notably, the sensitivity of immunochromatographic assays has only improved by approximately 10-fold over the past several years. While targeting conserved epitopes enables moderate cross-genogroup recognition, such assays largely lose the capacity to distinguish viral genotypes, restricting their utility for epidemiological outbreak tracing. Most commercial immunochromatographic test strips require subsequent RT-PCR verification to rule out false-negative results, forming a limitation when high sensitivity is required. Therefore, future research may prioritize integrated “immunocapture-nucleic acid amplification” pipelines over independent optimization of single immunochromatographic platforms. The four norovirus colloidal gold rapid test kits with relatively high global market share include SD Bioline Norovirus, RIDAQUICK Norovirus, NOROTOP, and ImmunoCardSTAT [34–37]. Although the manufacturer of SD Bioline claims that this POCT boasts high-performance parameters, along with the advantages of time-saving and ease of operation, studies have demonstrated that its detection sensitivity for RVA-RT-qPCR-positive samples is merely 46.46%. Despite a high specificity of 96.6%, this still indicates that nearly half of the infected cases may be missed. Changhee Ha et al. found that the sensitivity of RIDAQUICK for norovirus detection was significantly higher than that of the established IDEIA ELISA kit (69% vs. 45%, p < 0.01) [34]. Overall, the immunochromatographic kits currently available on the market tend to have low sensitivity for norovirus detection, making it difficult to meet stringent testing requirements. Further optimization is therefore required in this technical field to develop detection kits with both high sensitivity and high specificity.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

The development of molecular diagnostic technologies for norovirus reflects an ongoing optimization process, balancing targeting strategies, detection throughput, and sensitivity. In 2004, Xiaoli Pang et al. established the first single-channel TaqMan RT-qPCR assay based on the LightCycler platform. By targeting the highly conserved ORF1–ORF2 junction region of the viral genome, this assay enabled broad-spectrum detection and quantification of GI and GII noroviruses within 2 h, thereby becoming the gold standard for outbreak investigations and clinical diagnosis at that time [38]. However, this assay utilized only a single FAM fluorescence channel, requiring separate well amplification for GI and GII. This not only limited the detection throughput but also increased the risk of cross-contamination. In addition, as the virus evolves, mutations in the original primer and probe binding regions have gradually reduced assay coverage, leading to a lag between primer design and the dynamic genetic variation of the viral genome.

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, in 2020, D. Liu et al. redesigned the primer-probe sets by updating the sequence database, introduced a universal reverse transcription primer for both GI and GII and dual TaqMan probes (FAM/VIC), and established a single-tube duplex RT-qPCR assay, which shortened the detection time to 75 min [39]. While this strategy improved detection throughput and efficiency, and reduced the risk of cross-contamination through physical isolation of fluorescence channels, it still suffered from issues such as primer-dimer formation, competitive inhibition caused by high-concentration templates, and missed detection of GIV noroviruses. Notably, Batule et al. reported an HRP-mediated colorimetric PCR assay in 2018 that achieved low-level detection of norovirus in shellfish samples, with a limit of detection down to 1 copy per milliliter [40]. This method eliminated the reliance on sophisticated temperature control and optical detection systems, yet it exhibited certain limitations in quantitative accuracy due to the non-linear amplification of colorimetric signals.

RT-qPCR is widely accepted as a standard method for norovirus detection. Nevertheless, this technique has intrinsic technical limitations. Genotypic mutations of norovirus can trigger primer mismatches, and false-negative results are generated during testing; unstable detection data may produce biased benchmarks for evaluating novel diagnostic platforms. Commercially available primer sets fail to identify rare genogroups including GIV, which brings potential risks to epidemic surveillance [41]. Colorimetric PCR eliminates the demand for specialized detection devices. However, its quantitative accuracy and uniform readout performance are sacrificed, and significant deviations will be generated when results are judged visually by different operators. The delayed update of primer sequences is not only a technical issue; this problem mainly arises from inconsistent update cycles, as the mutation speed of norovirus exceeds the official approval progress of detection reagents, and relevant viral gene databases should be updated continuously by regulatory authorities to keep pace with viral variation [42].

Digital PCR [42]

Digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) enables absolute quantification of target nucleic acids via single-molecule partitioning and endpoint fluorescence counting, eliminating the need for standard curves. In recent years, it has demonstrated significant value in the field of norovirus detection [43]. The core workflow of this technology involves partitioning diluted nucleic acid samples into thousands to tens of thousands of independent reaction units using microfluidic chips or emulsification techniques, ensuring that each unit contains, on average, no more than one template molecule. After PCR amplification, units containing the target nucleic acid generate fluorescent signals. By counting the proportion of positive units and applying the Poisson distribution principle, the absolute copy number of nucleic acids in the sample can be directly calculated [44–46]. dPCR offers advantages, including a low LOD high accuracy, and strong anti-interference capability in complex matrices. However, the high cost of the technology platform and associated consumables, such as chips and droplet cartridges, most of which are disposable, has limited its large-scale application in routine clinical diagnosis and resource-constrained settings. Therefore, dPCR is currently primarily used for the precise analysis of high-value samples in scientific research, reference laboratories, and for outbreak traceability [47, 48]. For instance, in one study, reverse transcription dPCR (RT-dPCR) was used to quantify norovirus RNA in frozen raspberry matrices, achieving detection sensitivities of 3 copies per reaction for GII and 14 copies per reaction for GI genotypes. Nevertheless, its qualitative detection sensitivity for GI remained inferior to that of conventional RT-qPCR, reflecting the limitations of this technology in terms of consistent detection performance across different genotypes in real food matrices [49], The reason for this result might be that frozen raspberries interfere a lot with the GI RT-dPCR system; primers and probes don’t work very well for GI under digital PCR conditions; and low-copy templates can easily get lost when samples are divided into droplets.The PCR principle is shown in Fig. 4.

Emerging molecular revolution: the integration of isothermal amplification and CRISPR

Isothermal amplification technology

Isothermal amplification technologies are a class of methods that achieve efficient nucleic acid amplification at a constant temperature, including loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), nucleic acid sequence-based amplification (NASBA), and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) [50]. In recent years, isothermal amplification-based detection technologies for norovirus have undergone remarkable evolution, with breakthroughs particularly detection speed and portability. Between 2023 and 2025, LAMP technology incorporated a nanomagnetic bead enrichment strategy, reducing the LOD to 10¹ copies per tube. Furthermore, a handheld fluorescent reading platform capable of completing detection within 15 min was developed, which facilitated the leap of this technology from laboratory-controlled isothermal environments to on-site POCT. During the same period, a combined NASBA-CRISPR/Cas13a detection system (2024) and an RPA-Cas genotyping POCT platform (2023) were developed in succession. The former reduced the LOD to 10 copies per microliter via a single-tube trans-cleavage reaction and showed potential for visual detection; the latter achieved accurate genotyping of norovirus GI and GII at 37 °C within 35 min [51]. Together, these technologies have pioneered a new paradigm of integrated “amplification-recognition” detection.

However, the high sensitivity of isothermal amplification technologies also entails a risk of false-positive results. For example, the amplification products of LAMP and RPA are highly prone to forming aerosol contamination. Owing to the vigorous nature of the reaction and the frequent need to open tubes during detection, even trace amounts (10⁻18g) of contaminated nucleic acids may yield false-positive outcomes. To address this challenge, uracil-DNA glycosylase (UNG) can be incorporated into the reaction system. This enzyme degrades contaminating templates from previous amplification products and controls the amplification trigger threshold to below the femtogram level (10⁻15g), thereby significantly reducing the risk of false positives [52]. But it’s worth pointing out that the UNG enzyme can only partially control cross-contamination from amplified products. In grassroots, open settings without laboratory cleanliness, the risk of aerosol contamination is still uncontrollable. At the same time, isothermal amplification systems rely on a combination of multiple enzymes, which not only makes reagent costs 2 to 3 times higher than ordinary PCR, but also results in poor enzyme stability at room temperature. Most studies only test the performance of freshly prepared systems and completely ignore storage stability. This is also the core engineering bottleneck that has made it difficult for isothermal amplification technology to truly be applied in grassroots POCT.

CRISPR-cas detection system

The CRISPR-Cas system originated in bacteria’s adaptive immune system and has evolved into a class of programmable molecular diagnostic tools through rational engineering. Its detection principle is based on a three-step cascade reaction of “sequence-specific recognition–trans-cleavage activity–signal transduction”: CRISPR RNA (crRNA) guides the Cas protein to specifically bind to target nucleic acids, thereby activating its non-specific collateral cleavage activity, which in turn cleaves the reporter probe to release a detectable signal (e.g., fluorescence or lateral flow assay signal) [53]. The CRISPR-Cas system can be categorized into two classes: Class 1, which relies on multisubunit effector complexes, and Class 2, which utilizes a single effector protein. Owing to its simpler architecture and ease of operation, (Tables 1 and 2) Class 2 is more widely applied in bioengineering and molecular diagnostics [54]. The CRISPR principle is shown in Fig. 5.

NASBA norovirus (NoV) detection, the efficacy of the CRISPR-Cas system is highly dependent on the characteristics of Cas proteins and their compatibility with the type of target nucleic acids [61]. Cas12a primarily recognizes double-stranded DNA (dsDNA); thus, viral RNA must first be converted into dsDNA via reverse transcription RPA (RT-RPA) or reverse transcription LAMP(RT-LAMP) [62]. In contrast, Cas13a can directly target RNA and can be combined with various isothermal amplification technologies to achieve efficient detection of viral RNA [63]. Given the global prevalence of GII.4 norovirus genotypes and the severe clinical symptoms they induce, the development of detection technologies specific to this subtype is particularly crucial. In 2021, Tanfen Fang et al. first integrated RT-RPA with CRISPR/Cas12a to establish a real-time fluorescent and visual nucleic acid detection method suitable for GII.4 norovirus. However, the crRNA was designed against the conserved region of the RdRp gene of the GII.4 genotype. As a result, this method is highly specific to GII.4. Its capacity to detect other norovirus genotypes is limited, and it does not support multiplex detection [55].This preliminary strategy implemented RPA amplification and CRISPR detection as separate steps, which not only complicated the operational workflow but also increased the risk of aerosol contamination. To optimize this process, in 2022, L. Duan and X. Yang et al. reported a single-tube RPA-Cas13a detection system that integrates reverse transcription, amplification, and CRISPR recognition into a single reaction system. While maintaining a high sensitivity of 5 copies per reaction, this system significantly reduces the probability of cross-contamination. The results of this method can be interpreted via fluorescence readout and lateral flow strips (LFS), eliminating reliance on large-scale instrumentation and making it more suitable for rapid on-site detection [59].

In addition to RPA, NASBA, an isothermal RNA amplification technique relying on continuous enzymatic reactions, has also been applied for norovirus detection. In 2024, the Zefeng Mao team developed a combined platform based on NASBA and CRISPR/Cas13a. By targeting the conserved sequence in the ORF1–ORF2 junction region of norovirus and leveraging the collateral cleavage activity of Cas13a to trigger fluorescent signals, this platform achieved a detection sensitivity of 10 copies/µL [60]. However, the NASBA reaction depends on thermally unstable enzymes, must be carried out at 42 °C for 2 h, which is not only time-consuming but also prone to primer-dimer formation and non-specific amplification, thereby increasing the risk of false positives. Moreover, NASBA exhibits strong dependence on the length of target sequences (120–250 nt), which limits the flexibility of primer design [64].

It is worth noting that both RPA and NASBA rely on multiple enzymes in the reaction. This not only leads to high costs but also makes trace contamination of enzyme preparations more prone to inducing non-specific amplification. To address this issue, researchers in 2025 combined the enzyme-free catalyzed hairpin assembly (CHA) amplification system with CRISPR/Cas12a detection. In this design, viral RNA triggers the CHA reaction, which in situ generates a large amount of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) to activate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a. Cas12a then cleaves the fluorescently labeled single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) reporter molecules, achieving dual-stage signal amplification and significantly improving detection sensitivity [56].

In terms of signal readout, traditional CRISPR-based detection primarily relies on single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) probes labeled with fluorophore-quencher (FQ) pairs. Their one-to-one signal release mode suffers from an insufficient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at low viral load. In 2024, a recentstudy introduced aggregation-induced emission luminogens (AIEgens) into the CRISPR/Cas12a system for the first time: AIEgens bind to long-chain nucleic acids via electrostatic and non-covalent interactions, enabling a one-to-many signal transduction mode that can greatly improveenhances fluorescence intensity,, However, the anti-interference capacity of this AIE-based sensing system in complex faecal matrices has not been comprehensively validated yet [57]. The following year, the Zefeng Mao group further developed a dual-mode CRISPR sensing platform based on platinum@metal-organic framework (Pt@MOF) nanomaterials. This material simultaneously exhibits peroxidase-like activity and alkali-induced fluorescence recovery properties, employing an “off-signal” detection logic. Upon target nucleic acid-induced activation of Cas12a, the “magnetic bead–DNA–Pt@MOF” (MDM) probe is cleaved, resulting in a synchronous reduction in both chromogenic and fluorescent signals. This allows dual-channel verification at a detection sensitivity of 10 copies/µL, thereby improving the reliability of the result [58]. However, the steric hindrance caused by the micron-scale size of the MDM probe in this strategy has, to some extent, limited the cleavage efficiency of Cas12a and prolonged the reaction time. Also, the ‘signal-off’ mode requires a very stable background, and in complex samples like stool, there are interfering substances like bilirubin, which can easily lead to false positives.

Although more than 10 CRISPR-based detection methods for norovirus have been reported since 2021, none have yet obtained CE-IVD or FDA certification to date. The main reason why CRISPR technology is hard to translate to POCT is the gap between R&D and commercialization: most new CRISPR methods are still stuck at the lab prototype stage, with no commercial on-site testing products available. Fragmented patents for Cas enzymes and matched amplification reagents raise licensing expenses, and limited supply of industrial Cas materials causes unstable batch performance [65]. Sample preprocessing can’t be integrated and still relies on manual nucleic acid extraction, so it doesn’t meet the real on-site rapid testing requirements. Testing costs are far beyond what grassroots or food safety scenarios can afford, and there’s no unified standardized validation process. Most current research only focuses on lab detection limits, completely ignoring issues that must be solved for practical use, like mass production stability, room temperature storage, and regulatory compliance. It’s a typical case of ‘looks good in the lab, but unusable on site [66]. To promote the standardization and practical application of CRISPR technology, it would be beneficialto establish a unified performance verification protocol and quality control standard [67]. Looking ahead, integrating the modules of nucleic acid extraction, isothermal amplification, CRISPR recognition, and signal readout into a microfluidic chip to construct an integrated detection platform with the “sample-in, result-out” workflow may serve as one important developmental trend for achieving on-site, high-throughput, and automated detection. Meanwhile, further advances in the design of multiplex MDM probes and multi-signal output strategies will facilitate the simultaneous, rapid screening of multiple pathogens in food safety and environmental monitoring scenarios.

Multiplex target parallel detection technology platform

Multiplex RT-qPCR panel

Acute viral gastroenteritis is a major etiological factor leading to hospitalization and even death in immunocompromised and immunodeficient populations, particularly children. Acute diarrhea is extremely prevalent among children under five years old, with its primary pathogenic agents including rotavirus, norovirus, astrovirus, sapovirus, and human adenovirus. Therefore, the rapid and accurate identification of pathogenic agents is valuable for guiding rational treatment and controlling transmission [68]. Multiplex RT-qPCR technology can simultaneously amplify multiple target sequences using multiple primers pairs within a single reaction, enabling the concurrent detection of multiple pathogens in a single sample with high sensitivity and specificity [69]. Previous studies have developed detection systems for common pathogens causing viral gastroenteritis in children based on this technology; its ability to identify pathogens in a timely and accurate manner can offer practical assistance for clinical diagnosis, treatment, and infection prevention and control [70]. Although multiplex RT-qPCR can markedly shorten sample volume and detection time, its primer design process is complex and lengthy, and it imposes high requirements on equipment and consumables. Thus, it is more suitable for professional laboratories with large sample volumes, relatively fixed detection targets, and access to multi-channel qPCR or chip analysis platforms.

Luminex-based suspension array

Luminex technology is a multiplex detection platform based on fluorescently encoded microspheres, enabling the simultaneous identification and quantitative analysis of multiple RNA or protein targets in a single reaction [71]. In 2012, Yan Liu et al. established the development of a Luminex-based multiplex nucleic acid detection system that simultaneously screens for 7 common enteroviruses associated with acute gastroenteritis. Compared with conventional PCR, this detection method exhibits sensitivity and specificity at least 75% for each pathogen, with a LOD ranging from 10³ to 10⁴ copies per reaction. Endowed with both high-throughput and accurate quantification capabilities, it can act as a feasible option for rapid profiling of pathogen spectra in complex clinical samples [72].

Metagenomic Nanopore Sequencing (mNGS)

Since 2023, mNGS technology has been gradually incorporated into diagnostic systems for environmental and clinical pathogens. This technology enables the simultaneous identification of multiple microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, in a single test without the need to predefine pathogen genetic information, and has enhance the classification resolution to the species and even strain levels. It thus represents an impartial and valuable technical option tool for screening unknown pathogens and for epidemic traceability [73].

Traditional diagnostic and molecular detection methods have failed to identify the causative pathogens in up to 90% of gastroenteritis cases, often resulting in diagnostic delays, irrational use of antibiotics, increased risk of nosocomial transmission, and repeated testing [74]. In 2019, scholars such as Angel et al. reported the successful application of mNGS in the routine detection of infectious diseases using fecal samples. Its efficacy in detecting common gastrointestinal pathogens is comparable to that of traditional diagnostic methods. Moreover, a single tube of sample allows for the simultaneous detection of bacteria, viruses, and parasites, as well as drug- and virulence-resistance genes, without the need for a culture process, which can supply supportive evidence for clinical decision-making [75].

However, to date, no country has approved commercialized mNGS diagnostic reagents, related instruments, or standard databases. This technology still lacks unified specifications for methodology establishment, quality control, and bioinformatics analysis pipelines. Therefore, it is currently mainly implemented as a laboratory-developed test (LDT) and has not yet been integrated into routine clinical diagnosis and treatment pathways [76].

Isothermal amplification and CRISPR-Cas systems eliminate the need for thermal cyclers. They establish the second portable molecular detection paradigm.Most combined amplification and CRISPR platforms have obvious drawbacks. Nucleic acid extraction and signal detection must be carried out separately. Fully automatic “sample-in, result-out” analysis cannot be achieved.Separate reaction steps increase the risk of aerosol contamination. Single signal outputs also show weak anti-interference ability in stool and food samples.All these unresolved limitations promote the development of miniaturized biosensors and microfluidic bedside testing platforms.

Bedside monitoring platform: biosensors and microfluidic technology

Biosensor

With distinct advantages including rapid response, facile miniaturization, and controllable detection cost, biosensors have become one promising technical candidate for point-of-care diagnosis and large-scale screening of norovirus in samples. Based on signal readout modes, norovirus-targeted biosensors are primarily categorized as optical and electrochemical. Through the rational design of recognition elements, the construction of signal amplification strategies, and optimization for adaptability to complex matrices, both types of biosensing systems can realize high-performance detection featuring favorable efficiency, sensitivity and throughput for norovirus specimens.

Optical biosensor

Optical biosensors enable quantitative analysis by detecting optical signal changes triggered by target recognition, exhibiting prominent advantages in intuitive signal readout and anti-electromagnetic interference performance. Major types of optical biosensors include colorimetric sensing, fluorescence sensing, surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) sensing, and multimodal optical sensing [77].

Colorimetric sensing enables naked-eye interpretation of results without relying on large analytical instruments, making it a one widely adopted option for rapid point-of-care screening. Traditional colorimetric strategies primarily rely on nanoparticle aggregation. As shown in Fig. 6A (a), Wen Ying Cui and colleagues developed a foldable biochip that integrates G-quadruplex structures with graphene oxide-coated microbeads, achieving streamlined operations of viral enrichment, lysis, and nucleic acid recognition within 30 min [78], This method relies only on enzyme-assisted signal amplification of G-quadruplexes and involves multiple steps where nucleic acids are lost throughout the process. It doesn’t provide exponential amplification of the target and is also limited by room temperature reactions and interference from fecal matrix backgrounds, so its sensitivity is significantly lower than RT-PCR, which can exponentially amplify viral RNA. To overcome the sensitivity bottleneck, nanozyme-based signal amplification systems have been introduced into the construction of colorimetric sensing platforms. Indra Memdi Khorisa et al. fabricated hollow PtNPs@Co₃O₄ nanozymes using ZIF-67 as a template (Fig. 6A (c)). The synergistic catalytic effect between noble metals and metal oxides substantially can greatly improve the color development efficiency of the 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidin (TMB)-H₂O₂ system, pushing the limit of colorimetric detection down to the femtogram level. However, the high loading of platinum components tends to induce nanoparticle aggregation, which reduces catalytic activity and limits the anti-interference capability of the sensing system [79]. To further balance detection sensitivity and practical applicability, dual-modal colorimetric coupling strategies have undergone rapid development. Tao Wang and co-workers synthesized core–shell structured Fe₃O₄@COF hybrid nanozymes (Fig. 6A (d)), which simultaneously endow the sensing platform with magnetic responsiveness and peroxidase-like activity. By switching probes between dispersion and aggregation states, this strategy achieves dual-modal outputs, combining naked-eye colorimetric preliminary screening with precise quantification via magnetic relaxation. The system maintains stable signal responses and satisfactory recovery rates even in complex matrices such as shellfish samples. Nevertheless, it relies on low-field nuclear magnetic resonance equipment, resulting in high detection costs and limiting its full portability [80]. In the same year, Maytawan Thanunchai et al. immobilized the norovirus S protein-targeted SMV25 aptamer on the surface of gold nanoparticles (Fig. 6A (b)). Magnesium-ion-triggered dispersion–aggregation switching produces distinct color changes, enabling visual screening for norovirus in stool specimens. This method exhibits cross-recognition capacity against major prevalent norovirus genotypes; however, its detection performance is susceptible to sample matrix interference, with sensitivity only at the nanogram leve l [81].

Schematic overview of optical and electrochemical biosensors for human norovirus (HuNoV) detection. (A) Optical biosensing platforms, including colorimetric sensing, fluorescence & surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) sensing, and multimodal signal output sensing. (B) Electrochemical biosensing platforms, including voltammetric, impedimetric (electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, EIS), and photoelectrochemical (PEC) sensing. All platforms integrate biofunctionalized nanomaterials, specific target recognition, signal amplification, and multimodal signal output to rapidly, sensitively, and specifically detect HuNoV in complex clinical, food, and environmental matrices

Fluorescence and SERS sensing offer promising alternatives for high-sensitivity detection. The combination of the CRISPR-Cas system and isothermal amplification techniques effectively mitigates background interference and poor specificity inherent to conventional fluorescence detection (Fig. 6A (e)). Weiya Wang et al. integrated RT-RPA with CRISPR-Cas12a to activate fluorescent signal output and simultaneously established a synthesis route for thiol-free spherical nucleic acid probes (Fig. 6A (f)). This strategy features low background signals and excellent salt tolerance, thereby lowering the limit of detection to 40 copies per reaction [82]. This method’s LOD is close to PCR under isothermal conditions and has CRISPR-level specificity. If they can solve the compatibility issue between RPA and Cas12a buffers, achieve closed-tube detection, and turn the fluorescence signal into a color change or lateral flow signal visible to the naked eye, its POCT value will increase a lot.

In terms of SERS sensor fabrication, Ojodomo J. Achadu et al. encapsulated molybdenum trioxide quantum dots within pH-responsive nanogels and constructed high-density sensing hotspots based on a two-dimensional hexagonal boron nitride substrate. Acidic condition-triggered probe release achieves significant signal enhancement, with a limit of detection as low as 10 copies/mL in clinical stool specimens. However, the complex material preparation process and reliance on large-scale Raman spectrometers limit the clinical adoption and practical application of this method [83].

Currently, multi-signal output modes have emerged as one valuable research direction for improving detection sensitivity, whereby mutual calibration of multiple independent signals can reduce the risk of false positives. The research team that developed the aforementioned SERS system further integrated sulfur-doped carbon dots with magnetic silver nanocubes to establish a dual-modal fluorescence–SERS sensing method (Fig. 6A (g)). This approach achieved a low LOD of 10 copies/mL in clinical stool specimens and greatly enhanced the anti-interference performance and result reliability of the sensing system [84]. Zefeng Mao et al. combined the trans-cleavage activity of CRISPR-Cas12a with the catalytic performance of Pt@MOF nanozymes to construct a colorimetric-fluorescent dual-modal sensing platform (Fig. 6A (h)). This technique enables on-site detection without bulky instruments, yet it exhibits limited accurate quantification capacity and is susceptible to ambient light interference [85]. On this basis, Weiya Wang and colleagues combined DNA hydrogels with the CRISPR system to develop a trimodal optical sensing platform integrating fluorescence, colorimetry, and SERS (Fig. 6A (r)). The limits of detection for the three sensing modes were 0.5 fM, 83 aM, and 0.16 fM, respectively, with linear ranges spanning six orders of magnitude. Nevertheless, this system lacks valid verification in clinical stool specimens. The introduction of multi-signal output increases experimental complexity and imposes stringent requirements on rational probe design [86].

Electrochemical biosensor

Electrochemical biosensors rely on variations in current, potential, impedance, and thermal parameters for signal readout. Featuring easy integration, low power consumption, and excellent portability, they can be regarded as one important developmental trend in point-of-care testing [87]. Early electrochemical sensing enabled broad-spectrum, rapid detection using screen-printed electrode arrays. Nan Wang et al. modified screen-printed carbon electrodes with gold nanoparticles and adopted protein A for the oriented immobilization of antibodies (Fig. 6B (a)). The binding between viral antigens and antibodies hinders interfacial electron transfer, thereby reducing the differential pulse voltammetry current signal and enabling the quantitative detection of norovirus GI.1 and GII.4 genotypes. This method achieves a limit of detection of 10⁴ copies/mL in clinical stool specimens within 1 h, just relies on a one-to-one ab-ag binding, without gene amplification, so it’s less sensitive than RT-PCR and only suitable for large-scale on-site screening, [88].To overcome the sensitivity bottleneck in amplification-free detection, nanohybrid materials and dual-recognition strategies have been widely adopted. Hui Zhao et al. utilized covalent organic framework and pillararene hybrid nanomaterials as signal amplification carriers. They constructed a sandwich-type electrochemical sensor by combining peptide and aptamer dual-recognition units (Fig. 6B (b)). This platform can realize highly sensitive detection without nucleic acid amplification, with alimit of detection as low as 0.84 copies/mL. However, it targets only a single capsid protein and fails to distinguish different viral genotypes and variant strains [89]. Self-powered photoelectrochemical sensing further eliminates the reliance on external power supplies. Yun Chen et al. fabricated π-electron-rich carbon nitride homojunction photoanodes to enhance light-harvesting and carrier separation efficiency. The steric hindrance generated by viral antigen binding modulates interfacial charge output, and the developed sensor exhibits satisfactory matrix tolerance and long-term stability in stool samples [90]. Molecular imprinting technology offers an alternative strategy to address the poor stability of biological recognition elements. Sarbjeet Kaur et al. synthesized molecularly imprinted nanoparticles using conserved viral epitopes as templates, which serve as artificial recognition units to replace biological antibodies (Fig. 6B (d)). These imprints are tolerant to high temperature, extreme pH conditions, and long-term storage, and are compatible with dual-mode detection based on electrochemical impedance and thermal conduction. Effective detection in food matrices such as lettuce is achieved, providing a feasible solution for norovirus monitoring under cold-chain-free conditions [91]. Sara Martorell et al. integrated synthetic biology with photoelectrochemical sensing and optimized the recognition interface by adopting virus-like particle-mimicked antigens and high-affinity single-chain antibodies (Fig. 6B (e)). The entire detection procedure for stool specimens can be completed within 45 min, and results obtained show a high correlation with RT-qPCR assays [92]. The combination of microfluidics and isothermal amplification has advanced the integrated design and ultra-high sensitivity of electrochemical sensors. In 2026, Pengda Wei et al. constructed an antibody-conjugated rolling circle amplification (RCA) electrochemical microchip (Fig. 6B (c)). This platform integrates immune recognition and in situ rolling circle amplification into a single reaction system. Through cascade nucleic acid amplification, antigen-binding events are converted into measurable electrical signals, obtaining an extremely low limit of detection of 10 fg/mL for the norovirus VP1 antigen. This technique requires only a small sample volume and holds considerable promise for high-throughput analysis. Nevertheless, constrained by the accuracy of microchip fabrication and interfacial modification procedures, its batch-to-batch stability still requires further optimization [93].

In summary, optical biosensors for norovirus detection have undergone gradual technical advancement from visual naked-eye screening to trimodal ultrasensitive quantification, with detection sensitivity improved from the nanogram level to the attomolar level. Meanwhile, electrochemical biosensors have advanced from broad-spectrum, rapid detection, amplification-free ultrasensitive sensing, and antibody-independent stable recognition, accompanied by continuous improvements in portability and integration. Both types of biosensing technologies have made progressive breakthroughs in signal amplification, recognition element optimization, and multi-scenario applicability. Nevertheless, complex matrix interference, batch-to-batch consistency, and the trade-off between analytical performance and device portability remain the central challenges restricting current applications. In the future, multimodal integration, element-free biomimetic recognition, and monolithic integrated chips are expected to become major developmental directions of norovirus sensing technologies, which may provide improved technical options for clinical diagnosis and food safety surveillance.

Microfluidics

Microfluidic technology enables the precise spatial separation of detection sites by fabricating micron-scale fluidic channels, thereby facilitating parallel analysis of multiple targets, and has emerged as a core enabling technology in modern POCT systems [94, 95]. A study integrated RPA and LAMP to develop a detection system designated DORLA, which was further incorporated into a microfluidic chip to construct a portable multiplex norovirus detection platform. This platform successfully detected HuNoV GI and GII simultaneously in clinical fecal samples, demonstrating high sensitivity, rapid response, and excellent portability. It thus can serve as a potential candidate for pathogen-screening tool suitable for on-site or bedside applications [96].

In 2025, Wang et al. reported a 3D-printed self-driven microfluidic chip. This chip innovatively employs gravity and capillary action for fluid actuation without the need for external pumps or valves, and integrates five functional modules—viral lysis, nucleic acid amplification, CRISPR reaction, glycolysis, and electrochemical detection—into a single nylon chip, which effectively reconciles the reaction compatibility of multi-enzyme systems. By converting CRISPR cleavage signals into glucose concentrations and using personal glucose meters (PGM) as readout devices, this technology enables the rapid on-site detection of noroviruses with a sensitivity of 60 copies [97]. However, the precision of fluid control in this platform is susceptible to gravity and liquid viscosity, and complex sample matrices may interfere with fluid behavior. Additionally, the operational process still requires manual reagent loading and relies on external magnetic separation and thin-film heaters, representing a gap from fully automated detection. The limited dynamic range (20–600 mg/dL) of personal glucose meters and their non-linear algorithms may also introduce readout biases in high-concentration samples. Currently, this technology remains at the laboratory-prototype stage; further advances are expected to involve deep integration of microfluidic technology with reagent pre-storage technology, as well as large-scale clinical validation, to enhance its practical utility in resource-limited settings.

Matrix interference mitigation and breakthroughs in sample preparation

Fecal specimens, shellfish digestive glands, and fresh agricultural produce matrices contain diverse coexisting inhibitory substances, including bile salts, humic acids, glycogen, and endogenous ribonucleases. Although a series of anti-interference strategies for norovirus detection have been progressively developed, these approaches vary greatly in purification efficiency and matrix tolerance. Each strategy also has inherent limitations [98].

Crude impurity removal via physical separation

Centrifugation, membrane filtration, and PEG-NaCl only eliminate insoluble contaminants, but soluble bile salts and polysaccharides cannot be removed. They merely serve as auxiliary preprocessing for highly inhibitory samples [99].Antibody/aptamer-modified magnetic beads specifically capture intact norovirus and remove glycogen/bile salts via magnetic separation, greatly reducing matrix interference. This mainstream method still leaves trace interferents after elution, which disrupt CRISPR and immunoassays [78–81, 83, 85, 90].Foldable multi-chamber microfluidics trap inhibitor-rich fecal liquids and deliver virus-carrying GO beads for centrifugation-free on-site purification. Without supporting chemical protection, obvious signal loss arises under high bile salt concentrations [78].

Solid-phase interface modification to block non-specific adsorption

BSA or glycine only blocks empty binding sites on carriers. They cannot resist high levels of humic acid and bile salts [100].PEG and polydopamine form hydrophilic layers. Negative impurities are repelled by static electricity, so anti-interference ability is significantly enhanced [101, 102].Porous COF and MOF materials create pore barriers. Glycogen and large lipid molecules are blocked through steric effects. This method integrates physical separation and surface modification [80].Molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) are made with norovirus VP1 protein. Specific cavities are built to avoid non-specific binding of foreign proteins and bile salts. But MIPs cost much to produce and show unstable results between batches [91].All these surface treatments only reduce surface adsorption. They cannot shield key enzymes like reverse transcriptase and Cas proteins from damage by small interfering molecules.

Neutralization of endogenous inhibitors by buffer chemicals

Different protective agents are supplemented into buffers to remove residual interferents post-purification.RNase inhibitors block fecal endogenous ribonucleases and stop viral RNA breakdown [103].Trehalose and betaine serve as molecular chaperones. They preserve the structure of Cas12a/Cas13a and reverse transcriptase and lessen bile salt-triggered hydrophobic denaturation [104].EDTA mixed with TCEP works as a composite chelator. Metal ions from shellfish samples are captured to relieve Cas activity suppression from metal competition [105].Each protective agent only functions within a limited concentration window. Protection declines rapidly when interferents exist at high levels.Besides, the combined use of multiple additives increases the manufacturing cost of test strips.

Integrated composite anti-interference systems

The full CRISPR system is encapsulated in microscale confined spaces by DNA hydrogels. Inhibitors cannot diffuse into these regions, so raw stool samples can be tested without purification. The drawbacks are complicated preparation and weak storage stability.Samples and enzyme reagents are isolated by liquid stratification in single-tube glycerol phase separation. The tube remains closed throughout the whole test. Precise layering is mandatory; slight vibration causes signal loss [86].Both integrated devices integrate physical isolation and buffer protection, which simplifies multi-step operations. Nevertheless, poor production stability remains an unresolved flaw.

Enzyme-free amplification for anti-interference detection

CHA enables enzyme-free signal amplification. RPA and Taq polymerases are readily deactivated by bile salts, and they are not required in CHA. Target strands are pre-concentrated via strand displacement hybridization to activate subsequent Cas cleavage. Higher detection stability is achieved in stool samples rich in humic acids [56].

All individual anti-interference approaches have clear drawbacks. Soluble enzymatic inhibitors cannot be eliminated by physical purification. Surface modification only relieves non-specific adsorption. Buffer additives cannot cope with heavily interfered samples.The three-part workflow combining magnetic separation, hydrophilic nanocoating, and composite anti-interference buffer achieves the optimal overall performance. Yet this combined scheme involves lengthy steps and higher material costs. Integrated microfluidics and DNA hydrogel reactors have great application potential. Future research on on-site norovirus detection will mainly focus on simplifying production procedures and improving storage stability.

Evaluation of the main detection methods for human norovirus

Hierarchical detection requirements and scenario adaptation

Healthcare facilities diagnosis

Hospital outpatient, emergency, and inpatient departments impose requirements for high sensitivity, high specificity, and same-day reporting of norovirus test results, to promptly implement isolation and treatment and reduce the risk of nosocomial transmission and additional medical expenditures. In settings such as tertiary hospitals, pediatric and geriatric wards, and pre-operative and pre-transplant screening, detection methods are generally required to achieve high sensitivity, distinguish between GI and GII, and deliver results within 2–3 h. For such demands, real-time fluorescent RT-PCR is the recommended technique; as the gold standard for norovirus diagnosis, this technology can simultaneously meet the requirements for high sensitivity and high specificity [108].

For cases of acute gastroenteritis with an unknown etiology in the emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU), to reduce repeated sampling and enable rapid screening, a multiplex detection platform capable of simultaneously identifying 12–22 enteric pathogens (including bacteria, viruses, and parasites) is recommended, such as FilmArray or Luminex xTAG® GPP. These nucleic acid-based qualitative multiplex detection systems can simultaneously detect 22 targets in 1–2 h, with a sensitivity of over 80% and a specificity of more than 90%. Furthermore, as closed-tube systems, they can effectively reduce the risk of nosocomial infections, making them highly suitable for rapid screening of pathogens causing acute gastroenteritis [109].

For scenarios including triage in outpatient and emergency departments, bedside testing in pediatric and geriatric departments, and sentinel surveillance in primary hospitals, POCT-adapted isothermal amplification technologies are recommended, such as the RPA-CRISPR/Cas test strip. This technical system reacts under a constant temperature of 37–42 °C, completing amplification and CRISPR cleavage within 20 min; results can be visually interpreted, with a detection sensitivity of 10¹–10² copies/µL, comparable to that of RT-qPCR. As it is independent of fluorescent PCR instruments, it is particularly suitable for rapid screening in emergency departments, pediatric night shifts, or resource-limited regions [110, 111].

In immunocompromised patients, diagnosis by RT-qPCR typically requires approximately 6 h, yet a 1-day delay in treating norovirus infection may lead to a marked increase in mortality. The use of POCT technology enables definitive diagnosis within 15 min, thereby allowing timely adjustment of immunosuppressant regimens and effectively reducing the risk of death in these patients [112].

Food industry chain

The primary transmission routes of norovirus include person-to-person transmission and foodborne transmission; the latter is often caused by viral contamination of fresh produce, shellfish, and ready-to-eat fruits and vegetables that come into contact with contaminated water sources during cultivation or aquaculture. For the detection of low viral loads in food samples, viral enrichment via PEG precipitation or hydrophilic membrane filtration is recommended, followed by quantitative nucleic acid analysis using dPCR [113]. A collaborative validation study involving 13 laboratories across 9 countries demonstrated that dPCR had a coefficient of variation (CV) of less than 8% for the quantification of GII.4 norovirus in oysters, which was significantly superior to RT-qPCR in terms of repeatability and accuracy [114]. Consequently, dPCR technology has been progressively applied to norovirus surveillance in port sampling, market recalls, and food processing plants, among other settings.

All existing norovirus detection tools carry unavoidable performance trade-offs across three application scenarios. Colloidal gold strips lack sufficient sensitivity for standalone screening; standard RT-qPCR fails to meet urgent diagnostic demands for critical patients; RT-qPCR shows poor quantification stability in food matrices, while costly dPCR and CRISPR devices cannot be widely deployed at grassroots sites. No single assay satisfies all testing demands, justifying the necessity of our stratified scenario-adaptive diagnostic framework.

Community screening

Community screening scenarios impose core requirements of rapidity, low cost, and high throughput on detection technologies. Norovirus outbreaks have traditionally been prevalent in child gathering settings such as kindergartens and primary and secondary schools. Yet in recent years, infection incidents in nursing homes and geriatric medical institutions have also shown an upward trend [106]. Norovirus outbreaks are characterized by rapid growth rates, widespread epidemic distribution, and a high incidence in collective settings, including schools. There is an urgent need for medical staff to rapidly identify clusters of cases, block superspreading events, and implement early warning systems. Fecal swabs or anal swabs are recommended to facilitate at-home sampling for infected individuals. Given the predominance of the GII.4 genotype in circulation 8, GII.4-specific colloidal gold immunochromatographic test strips can be selected as a primary screening tool. Although this method has a sensitivity of approximately 80%, it enables results to be obtained within 5 min and enables on-site primary screening with zero operational threshold; positive samples are further confirmed by PCR, effectively alleviating the testing burden on clinical laboratories [107].

Future technology integration and interdisciplinary trends

The CRISPR-Cas system is derived from the adaptive immune mechanism of bacteria and guides Cas proteins to precisely recognize and cleave specific nucleic acid sequences via programmed RNAs, having emerged as an essential tool in molecular diagnosis [115]. Beyond its aforementioned combination with isothermal amplification technologies such as RT-RPA and NASBA, the integration of this system with nanomaterials has also exhibited enormous potential in recent years [116, 117]. Although a complete industrial chain for norovirus detection based on this technology has not yet been established, the in-depth integration of the CRISPR-Cas system with isothermal amplification, fluorescent signal amplification, and other technologies is expected to achieve higher detection specificity and sensitivity in the future.

In 2024, the Soo Chung team developed an integrated detection system based on a disposable centrifugal microfluidic chip, which integrates the entire workflow of thermal lysis, PCR amplification, hybridization, and color development onto a chip platform; result interpretation is achieved via smartphone imaging, truly fulfilling the on-site detection goal of “sample in, result out”. This technology fabricates microfluidic paper-based analytical devices (µPADs) using wax printing, which directly capture norovirus particles, followed by loading antibody-conjugated submicron particles and performing imaging analysis of the aggregated signals on the µPAD surface [118]. By integrating the functions of conventional large-scale instruments into a miniaturized chip and coupling with mobile terminals for result reading, this system lays a solid foundation for the development of a “centrifugal microfluidic-smartphone colorimetric” technology suitable for temporary settings such as cruise ships, military barracks, and large gatherings. This technical approach also provides a feasible direction for translating norovirus detection into community and household scenarios, indicating that the combination of miniaturized chip devices and smartphones may enable early viral surveillance and transmission control in the future, thereby facilitating timely treatment for patients.

Discussion

Compared to the recent reviews on norovirus diagnostics [119–122], this review sets up a completely new three-stage framework for norovirus detection. It clearly explains the dual driving factors of viral antigen drift and real-world clinical/food testing needs. This perspective hasn’t appeared in previous studies. Most existing reviews just list biosensors or molecular methods individually. Here, we link gene variations in the VP1 capsid to the inherent flaws of immunoassays, revealing the root cause of frequent false negatives in commercial rapid test kits. The review also proposes a tiered diagnostic strategy suitable for three major scenarios: large-scale community screening, multi-pathogen hospital testing, and quantitative monitoring for food safety. It even provides quantitative standards for detection limits, cost, and testing speed, which can help researchers and labs gradually choose the right testing method.

Conclusion

As the leading pathogen of acute gastroenteritis, norovirus causes more than 130,000 deaths worldwide each year. With vaccines and specific therapeutics still in the research and development stage, early diagnosis has thus become a pivotal step in epidemic control. This paper systematically reviews the evolutionary course of norovirus detection technologies: the morphological and immunological methods developed in the 1970–1990 s achieved pathogen visualization and batch screening for the first time, yet their sensitivity was only at the 10⁶ particle level, and they failed to distinguish between different genotypes; RT-qPCR and dPCR, which emerged in the 1990–2010 s, pushed the limit of detection down to 10² copies, but their reliance on thermal cyclers and central laboratories precluded timely on-site detection; since 2015, the third-generation technologies centered on isothermal amplification (e.g., RPA and LAMP), the CRISPR/Cas system, and electrochemical/optical biosensors have enabled detection at the single-copy level within 20 min, and POCT has been realized via microfluidic integration and smartphone-based readout.

The introduction of novel materials, including MXenes, covalent organic frameworks (COFs), DNA hydrogels, and aggregation-induced emission luminogens, has driven the leap in signal output from a “one-to-one” to a “one-to-many” mode, with the detection sensitivity further improved to the femtomolar (fM) level. Meanwhile, the diverse demands of distinct scenarios such as community primary screening, hospital emergency care, and food monitoring have also promoted the modular development of detection technologies: colloidal gold immunochromatographic strips are suitable for 5-minute rapid primary screening, RPA-CRISPR test strips meet the requirements for emergency POCT, and dPCR is applied for the accurate quantification of viruses in food samples. However, several challenges remain to be addressed, including the asynchrony between primer-probe design and viral variation, interference from complex sample matrices, and the lack of standardization and regulatory oversight for emerging technologies.The targeted matrix interference mitigation strategies and optimized sample pretreatment approaches summarized in this review provide feasible solutions to attenuate such matrix-derived inhibitory effects.

Mobile AI-based result interpretation integrated into an all-in-one disposable microfluidic chip system is expected to achieve the ultimate goal of single-copy detection, 5-minute turnaround, and a unit cost of 1 US dollar. With the promotion of the World Health Organization’s digital health guidelines and improvements to real-time mutation databases, norovirus diagnosis is moving toward an era of full-process traceability featuring “sample in, result out, and data to the cloud”. This review provides a systematic evidence-based foundation for technological selection and public health decision-making in this field.
