Systemic inflammatory and metabolic indexes for aortic dissection: a machine learning analysis
Authors: Wenyuan Dong, Wusimanjiang Aizimaitijiang, Hongcheng Jiang, Zixuan Liu, Abudunaibi Balati, Yuxin Gong, Xingwei He, Hesong Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammation plays a crucial role in the development of aortic dissection. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) are widely used surrogate markers for systemic inflammation, while the neutrophil to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR), platelet to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR), lymphocyte to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (LHR), and monocyte to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (MHR) incorporate high-density lipoprotein, reflecting both inflammatory and metabolic status. However, there is currently a lack of studies systematically comparing the associations between different inflammatory and metabolic surrogate markers and the risk of AD. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between these six inflammatory-metabolic indexes and the risk of aortic dissection, as well as evaluate their discriminative value for this condition.ObjectiveThis study aims to clarify the relationship between the inflammatory metabolic markers and aortic dissection.MethodsThis study enrolled a total of 1,959 patients. First, Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to minimize confounding bias. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations between inflammatory indexes and AD risk. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to explore potential nonlinear dose–response relationships. The discriminative performance of inflammatory indexes was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Furthermore, six machine learning algorithms were implemented for feature importance analysis.ResultsAfter adjusting for potential confounding factors, we identified SII 1.002 (95% CI: 1.001–1.002), SIRI 1.984 (95% CI: 1.702–2.352), and NHR 1.692 (95% CI: 1.499–1.928) as independent risk factors for AD development, while LHR 0.211 (95% CI: 0.143–0.304) served as an independent protective factor. Dose–response analysis demonstrated a linear association between NHR and AD risk (P for Nonlinear = 0.02), whereas SII, SIRI and LHR showed nonlinear relationships (P for Nonlinear < 0.001). ROC curve analysis revealed that the four inflammatory indexes had AUC values ranging from 0.740 to 0.835 for distinguishing AD and its clinical subtypes. Among the six machine learning algorithms evaluated, the XGBoost model demonstrated the best performance, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.883.ConclusionAmong these six systemic Inflammatory and Metabolic indexes, the SII, SIRI, NHR, and LHR indexes demonstrate significant associations with the presence of aortic dissection. Further prospective validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted to confirm their clinical utility before integration into diagnostic workflows.

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD), a catastrophic cardiovascular emergency characterized by acute separation of the aortic wall layers, is associated with rapidly escalating mortality without timely intervention [1]. Despite advancements in diagnostic imaging and surgical management, early detection remains hindered by nonspecific clinical presentations and the absence of reliable biomarkers for identifying high-risk individuals during the pre-dissection phase [2, 3]. Current therapeutic strategies, including drug treatment and surgical intervention, are limited to mitigating complications rather than preventing dissection initiation, underscoring the critical need for discriminative tools that enable proactive drug intervention.

Although it is not traditionally classified as an inflammatory disorder, AD is now increasingly recognized to closely link to inflammatory activation and metabolic dysregulation [4, 5]. While immune cell infiltration and cytokine-mediated vascular remodeling are pivotal to AD pathogenesis [6, 7], emerging evidence highlights the contributory role of lipid metabolism disorders, particularly high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) decrease, in exacerbating aortic wall vulnerability [8, 9]. Consequently, there is a critical need to identify a composite biomarker that integrates both inflammatory and metabolic dimensions to discriminate the development of AD.

Some of the current inflammatory indexes, such as SII and SIRI, have demonstrated prognostic value in postoperative AD cohorts [10, 11]. However, they only focus on immune cell dynamics neglects the metabolic dimension during AD progression, particularly the dual role of HDL-C as an anti-inflammatory mediator as well as cholesterol transporter [12, 13]. Novel indexes integrating inflammatory cells with HDL-C, including NHR and LHR, hold promise for bridging this clinical practice gap. Preliminary studies suggest their utility in cardiovascular diseases [14], yet systematic comparisons of these indexes in AD remain absent. To further enhance the ability to capture the combined discriminative power of multi-dimensional inflammatory-metabolic indicators, this study introduces machine learning approaches.

Methods

Study design and population

This study employed a retrospective design, with data sourced from the hospital medical record system and a national survey database. Patients were rigorously screened according to the following pathways, and exclusion criteria were clearly defined.

The inclusion process for the study group was as follows: We first collected electronic medical records from all patients who underwent aortic computed tomography angiography (CTA) at Tongji Hospital affiliated with Huazhong University of Science and Technology between August 2010 and October 2021. Exclusion was performed in a stepwise manner based on predefined criteria. Initially, patients diagnosed with intramural hematoma (IMH), penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU), aortic aneurysm, those with a prior history of aortic dissection (AD) diagnosis, or those without a definitive aortic disease diagnosis were excluded. Subsequently, patients aged < 18 years, pregnant patients with AD, those with an onset time exceeding 14 days, or those with unclear records of onset time were excluded. Patients with any missing data, including incomplete routine blood test results or unclear key temporal information, were directly excluded. Ultimately, a total of 1,012 AD patients were included in the study group.

The control group was selected from 102,927 respondents in the 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. The exclusion criteria were applied sequentially as follows: participants positive for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) or hepatitis C virus antibody/RNA, pregnant women, individuals with comorbid cardiovascular diseases, those with chest pain, and those aged < 18 years. After screening, 951 participants were included in the control group.

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The retrospective component was approved by the Ethics Committee (TJ-IRB20211102) with a waiver of informed consent. The use of NHANES data was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This process aimed to minimize the influence of active inflammation, metabolic disorders, or acute symptoms unrelated to AD. We acknowledge that this approach introduces heterogeneity in ethnicity, healthcare setting, and potential laboratory assay variations compared to the AD cohort. All subsequent analyses interpret the inflammatory indexes primarily in terms of their relative discriminative capacity and associative strength within our analytical framework, rather than as sources of universal diagnostic thresholds.

Data collection and measurements

Clinical data for the control group were extracted from the NHANES database, while data for the study group were obtained from the hospital's electronic medical record system. Collected demographic information included age, gender, smoking history, diabetes history, and hypertension history. Fasting venous blood samples (3 mL) were collected from the antecubital vein after hospital admission and stored in anticoagulant tubes for subsequent analysis. The following biochemical parameters were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (Scr), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and other relevant biochemical indicators. Analysis methods and statistics

Using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width set at 0.02 standard deviations of the logit-transformed propensity score, we achieved balanced distributions of the selected features. Matching quality was verified by standardized mean differences (SMD) < 0.1 for all covariates, indicating adequate balance between comparison groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests (for normally distributed continuous variables), Mann–Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed continuous variables), or chi-square/Fisher's exact tests (for categorical variables), according to variable types. The associations between inflammatory markers and AD risk were assessed through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, with restricted cubic splines (RCS, with 4 knots) employed to explore dose–response relationships. This study employed six machine learning methods to construct diagnostic models for AD. First, the complete dataset was randomly split into a training set and an independent test set at a 7:3 ratio. We developed and compared six classic classification models based on the training set: Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, XGBoost, Generalized Linear Model, Elastic Net, and Decision Tree. All models were trained and optimized using fivefold cross‑validation. Their discriminative performance was evaluated on the independent test set, and the AUC values were compared by plotting ROC curves. To further identify key predictive features and interpret the model, SHAP values were employed to analyze the best‑performing model. All analyses were performed in R software (version 4.4.2). Statistical tests were two‑sided, and a P‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Inflammatory activation is recognized as pivotal pathophysiological mechanism across cardiovascular diseases [15], and it is also critical in AD pathogenesis [16–18]. Although AD is not classically defined as an inflammatory disease, emerging evidence reveals that immune cell infiltration in the aortic media and adventitia plays a pivotal role in AD pathogenesis [19]. Immunocyte accumulation triggers cytokine-mediated activation of signaling pathways that drive critical pathological processes, including phenotypic switching and apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), along with degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) [20–22]. Such pathophysiological mechanisms position AD as a disease closely associated with inflammation. Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have demonstrated significant enrichment of immune cells within aortic tissues during AD progression [23, 24]. Meanwhile, a pronounced upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-1β and IL-3, was demonstrably associated with AD onset [25–27], and targeted anti-inflammatory therapies demonstrate therapeutic efficacy in attenuating the progression of AD [28, 29]. Nevertheless, given the functional heterogeneity of immune cell subsets and inflammatory cytokines, it is necessary to screen out the most effective biomarkers which can optimize discriminative accuracy for AD.

Previous studies have mostly focused on the association between inflammation-related biomarkers and prognosis in patients with AD, such as SII predicts short- and mid-term survival in patients with type A aortic dissection who received surgical treatment and type B aortic dissection who underwent thoracic endovascular repair [11, 30], The preoperative SII has prognostic value for long-term outcomes in type B aortic dissection patients undergoing endovascular repair and for outcomes in acute type A aortic dissection patients undergoing surgical treatment [10, 31], Preoperative MHR can not only predict short- and long-term mortality risks in patients with acute type B aortic dissection undergoing endovascular repair, but also serve as an auxiliary diagnostic indicator for acute aortic dissection in the Chinese Han population [32] or in a Chinese han population [33]. While existing studies have confirmed the association between inflammatory markers and the prognosis of patients with aortic dissection, they mainly focus on perioperative cohorts which limits generalizability to outpatient diagnostic triage. Our study investigated the correlation between inflammatory surrogate indexes and the risk of AD. The results demonstrated that among six inflammatory indexes examined, SII, SIRI, NHR and LHR were independently associated with AD risk. The SHAP analysis based on the XGBoost model indicated that among the four inflammatory indexes, NHR demonstrated the highest feature importance. While traditional inflammatory markers such as SII and SIRI primarily reflect systemic immune activation, NHR uniquely integrates two critical biological dimensions: neutrophilic inflammation and lipid metabolism, both of which play indispensable role in AD progression [34, 35]. Neutrophils, as key mediators of acute and chronic vascular inflammation [36], contribute to the injury of VSMCs and ECM degradation, and eventually cause AD development [37]. Simultaneously, HDL-C, a lipid component with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, counteracts vascular damage by inhibiting oxidative stress and promoting cholesterol efflux [5, 38, 39]. The inverse association between HDL-C levels and AD risk aligns with its protective metabolic role [34], whereas elevated neutrophils promote inflammatory aggression. By combining both pathways, NHR provides a more holistic representation of the “meta-inflammatory” state that drives AD pathogenesis, which is a feature absent in SII or SIRI. A study based on UK Biobank data, through a 14-year follow-up, found a positive correlation between NHR and the risk of AD [40]. In summary, as a composite indicator, NHR not only demonstrates excellent discriminative ability in identifying aortic dissection risk, but its significant positive correlation with AD risk levels also suggests its potential as a long-term predictive tool. The high importance scores of Eos and Bas suggest that immune regulation may play a pivotal role in AD pathogenesis. Previous studies have shown that peripheral eosinophil counts correlate with prognosis in patients with type B aortic dissection undergoing endovascular aortic repair [41]. A lower Eos count at admission was associated with higher short-term and long-term mortality. In this study, based on SHAP analysis, Eos emerged as a key feature for identifying AD. However, the intense stress response triggered by acute AD, such as the massive release of cortisol and catecholamines, can itself lead to a rapid decline in eosinophil levels. Therefore, the observed decrease in Eos is more likely to reflect the severity of the patient's condition and the acute stress state, rather than being a direct causative factor in the occurrence of AD. Nonetheless, the strong association between this indicator and prognosis suggests that Eos levels may serve as a useful biomarker for risk stratification in AD patients. Imbalances in immune regulation may play an important role in the pathophysiological process following the onset of AD, but this requires further validation through prospective studies. Among the four inflammatory indices, NHR exerted a greater influence on AD risk than either neutrophils or high-density lipoprotein independently. This study demonstrates a positive correlation between platelet levels and the risk of AD occurrence. Furthermore, a separate multicenter retrospective study indicates that the combined effect of thrombocytopenia and elevated D-dimer levels significantly increases in-hospital mortality [42]. Additionally, although some studies have observed that dyslipidemia may be associated with a reduced risk of AD, lower incidence of AD in patients with elevated low-density lipoprotein and type 2 diabetes—this "protective" association should be interpreted with caution. Notably, diabetes is relatively uncommon in AD patients, so this seemingly protective effect is more likely to reflect selection bias in the control group rather than a genuine biological protective mechanism. A study involving 7,324 patients found that diabetes did not increase in-hospital or short-term mortality in patients undergoing surgical repair of type A aortic dissection [43]; other studies have also reported that diabetes is associated with a reduced risk of AD occurrence or progression [44]. However, these results may be influenced by the characteristics of the control population in the study design. One hypothesis suggests that the chronic low-grade inflammatory state associated with diabetes may induce adaptive changes in the aortic wall, thereby enhancing its resistance to dissection [45], but this mechanism lacks sufficient supporting evidence. Therefore, before considering systemic inflammatory states and lipid metabolism abnormalities as potential therapeutic targets for AD, it is essential to further exclude confounding factors, particularly the impact of population selection bias. The blood-based biomarkers proposed in this study are cost-effective and easy to detect, making them suitable for use in emergency departments or primary healthcare settings. They can serve as rapid triage tools to effectively identify patients at high risk of suspected aortic dissection and prioritize them for confirmatory imaging examinations. However, this blood-based model has inherent limitations: it cannot visually display the location and extent of aortic intimal tears, the morphology of true and false lumens, or branch vessel involvement, as imaging modalities do. Therefore, it cannot replace imaging for the precise classification of dissection (Type A/Type B).

Several limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. Firstly, the cross-sectional design inherently means that the results only reflect statistical associations between variables and **cannot establish causality or be used to predict future disease onset**. Secondly, there is significant heterogeneity between the case group (hospitalized patients in China) and the control group (the U.S. NHANES community population) in terms of ethnicity, health status, testing techniques, and data structure, which may introduce biases that are difficult to adjust for. Additionally, the study is based on **single-center, retrospective data with a limited sample size**, and the clinical variables included are insufficient, preventing the models from fully adjusting for all confounding factors. The study also did not conduct subtype analysis for Type A and Type B aortic dissection. Most importantly, the models **lack independent external validation**, and their generalizability and calibration performance across different populations and healthcare settings remain unknown. Furthermore, the retrospective study design inherently carries the possibility of unmeasured confounding factors. Therefore, the conclusions of this study are preliminary and exploratory. Their clinical application must undergo rigorous validation and refinement through multicenter, prospective studies with larger samples.

Conclusion

This study confirms that SII, SIRI, NHR, and LHR are significantly associated with the risk of aortic dissection. As cost-effective, inflammation-related biomarkers, they demonstrate potential clinical application value in risk stratification. It should be emphasized that before advancing clinical translation, this comprehensive prediction model must be independently validated through prospective, multicenter studies to further evaluate its robustness and generalizability in real‑world clinical settings.
