Complementary feeding practices and associated factors among mothers of children aged 6–23 months in Debre Berhan town, Ethiopia
Authors: Chalie Mulugeta, Abebaw Alamrew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Appropriate nutrition during the first 1,000 days from pregnancy to a child’s second birthday is a critical window for optimal growth, cognitive development, and long-term health, with proper complementary feeding practices playing a pivotal role. Despite this, evidence on the magnitude of appropriate complementary feeding practices and their associated factors remains limited at the local level in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study assessed the Complementary Feeding Practices and Associated Factors among Mothers of Children Aged 6–23 Months in Debre Berhan Town, Ethiopia, in 2021. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from July 1 to August 30, 2021, using a cluster sampling technique in four randomly selected kebeles, including all eligible mothers. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, and appropriate complementary feeding practice was assessed based on WHO-recommended indicators. Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with appropriate complementary feeding practice at p < 0.05. The prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding practice was 43.7%, and factors significantly associated included being a housewife (AOR = 2.53; 95% CI: 1.05–6.08), attending postnatal care (AOR = 3.18; 95% CI: 2.03–4.97), institutional delivery (AOR = 2.14; 95% CI: 1.11–4.14), and child vaccination (AOR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.15–3.12). The level of appropriate complementary feeding practice in Debre Berhan Town (43.7%) remains suboptimal compared with WHO recommendations for children aged 6–23 months. Postnatal care attendance, being a housewife, institutional delivery, and child vaccination were significantly associated with appropriate complementary feeding practice. Strengthening postnatal counseling and integrating appropriate infant and young child feeding messages into routine vaccination services may help improve complementary feeding practices.

Introduction

The first two years of a child’s life represent a critical period for survival, growth, and long-term health outcomes. Adequate nutrition during this “first 1,000 days” is essential for optimal physical growth, brain development, and immune function. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that undernutrition contributes to approximately 45% of deaths among children under five worldwide, most of which are preventable through appropriate infant and young child feeding practices1. The WHO defines complementary feeding as the provision of safe and nutritionally adequate foods and liquids in addition to breast milk, typically beginning at six months of age2.

Despite global efforts to improve infant feeding practices, most children aged 6–23 months do not receive diets that meet recommended standards for dietary diversity and meal frequency. A substantial proportion of children fail to meet core complementary feeding indicators, contributing to persistent childhood undernutrition, including stunting, wasting, and micronutrient deficiencies3.Recent WHO data show that fewer than one in four children in this age group achieve minimum dietary diversity and meal frequency2,4. Consequently, an estimated 149 million children worldwide are stunted and 45 million are wasted, largely due to inadequate complementary feeding5. During this stage, nutritional demands for energy, protein, and key micronutrients increase rapidly, and inappropriate feeding practices may compromise growth, immunity, and cognitive development.

Complementary feeding practices are influenced by multiple determinants, including maternal education6,7, household wealth8, access to health services6, cultural beliefs9, ante natal care follow up10,knowledge of the mother11,institutional delivery11,smaller family size12 and media exposure8,13. In low- and middle-income countries, inadequate sanitation, limited food availability, and insufficient nutrition counseling further exacerbate poor feeding practices14. To address these challenges, the WHO and UNICEF developed the Global Strategy for Infant and Young Child Feeding, which promotes exclusive breastfeeding for six months, timely initiation of complementary feeding at six months, and continued breastfeeding up to two years or beyond15.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, inappropriate complementary feeding practices are widespread The prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding practices among mothers of children aged 6 to 23 months in sub-Saharan African countries was 13.02%8,and and only about 21% of children receive a minimum acceptable diet16. Children from rural areas, low-income households, and families with limited maternal education are disproportionately affected. These disparities contribute to the region’s high burden of stunting and anemia, with stunting rates in East Africa remaining above 30%17.

In Ethiopia, child undernutrition continues to be a major public health concern. According to the Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS), 37% of children under five were stunted, 7% were wasted, and 21% were underweight in 201918. Evidence further indicates that only 9.8% of children aged 6–23 months meet the criteria for appropriate complementary feeding19. Previous studies have consistently identified maternal education2,20,6,7, socio-economic status, urban residence, access to health facilities, antenatal and postnatal counseling, and media exposure as important predictors of appropriate feeding practices21–23. Conversely, cultural beliefs, food insecurity, limited maternal knowledge, and poor health service utilization hinder optimal feeding behaviors23–25.

Marked regional disparities in complementary feeding practices have been documented in Ethiopia. Studies from Tigray reported that only 18.9% of mothers practiced appropriate feeding27, while a study in southern Ethiopia reported that more than half of the mothers (55.3%) provided inadequate complementary foods26.In the Amhara region, only 26.8% of mothers practiced appropriate feeding27. Other studies have reported a prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding of 32.9% in Oromia28,, 37% in Debre Tabor29, and 14.8% in Konso30. In contrast, substantially higher prevalence was observed in Addis Ababa, where 83% of mothers practiced appropriate complementary feeding, reflecting better access to health services, education, and nutrition information in urban settings31.

The period between 6 and 23 months is particularly critical, as children transition from exclusive breastfeeding to family foods. During this stage, exposure to poor-quality food, inadequate feeding frequency, and unsafe food-preparation practices increases the risk of growth faltering and nutritional deficiencies32. However, context-specific evidence on complementary feeding practices remains limited in rapidly urbanizing towns such as Debre Berhan. The town is characterized by mixed urban–rural livelihoods, variable access to markets and nutritious foods, and evolving cultural feeding norms, which may influence maternal feeding behaviors differently from larger urban centers or rural areas. Understanding these local dynamics is essential for designing effective interventions.

Improving complementary feeding aligns with Ethiopia’s National Food and Nutrition Policy and the global commitment to Sustainable Development Goal 2, which aims to end hunger and ensure access to safe and nutritious food for all33. Evidence-based, locally adapted strategies are therefore required to translate these policy goals into meaningful improvements in child nutrition. Given the persistently low prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding in Ethiopia and the limited availability of localized evidence in Debre Berhan Town, this study aims to assess complementary feeding practices and identify associated factors among mothers of children aged 6–23 months.

Methodology

Study area

The study was conducted in Debre Berhan town. It is located in the North Shoa Zone, Amara regional state, the North East part of Ethiopia. It is 130 km far from the capital city, Addis Ababa, and 695 km from the regional town, Bahir Dar. The town has nine kebeles. Debre Berhan town has a total population of 108,825, of which 59,617 were females, and the estimated number of women of child-bearing age (15-49years) was 25,661. There are 9 health posts, three health centers, one government hospital and one private hospital.

Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional study design was conducted from July 1 to August 30, 2021, to assess weaning practice and associated factors among mothers who had a child aged 6–23 months in Debre Berhan town, 2021.

Source population: All mothers with a child aged 6–23 months in Debre Berhan town were our source population.

Study population: All mothers with a child aged 6–23 months in selected kebeles of Debre Berhan town.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Mothers of children aged 6–23 months living in the selected kebeles of Debre Berhan Town during the study period. Mothers who were available and able to provide informed responses.

Exclusion criteria: Mothers of children aged 6–23 months who unable to communicate due to severe illness, had children with congenital anomalies affecting feeding, or were unable to breastfeed from birth were excluded from the study.

After adding 5% non-response rate and a 1.5 design effect, the final sample size was 444. A design effect of 1.5 was applied to the sample size to account for the intra-cluster correlation inherent in the cluster sampling technique, which increases variability compared with simple random sampling. This value is commonly used in community-based nutrition studies in Ethiopia to adjust for clustering.

Sampling techniques

Debre Berhan Town is administratively divided into nine kebeles: Slasie, Selam, Eleni, Ayertena, Shewa, Eyerusalem, Mesalemia, Gebreal, and Afework. From these, four kebeles—Selam, Afework, Shewa, and Mesalemia were selected using a simple random sampling (SRS) technique, ensuring that each kebele had an equal chance of inclusion. After selecting the kebeles, a cluster sampling approach was employed, and all eligible mothers with children aged 6–23 months residing in the selected kebeles during the study period were included. The total number of eligible mothers identified in these kebeles was 444, and all were included in the study.The distribution of participants across the selected kebeles was as follows: Selam (115), Afework (107), Shewa (118), and Mesalemia (104).

Study variables

Dependent variable

Complementary feeding practice; the main outcome of the study, assessed based on complementary feeding behavior among children aged 6–23 months.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors: Maternal education, maternal occupation, Family size, Husband’s education, and husband’s occupation.

Obstetrics and child characteristics: Parity, ANC visit, PNC, Place of delivery, and weaning advice.

Weaning-related factors: Age of initiation of complementary food, Reason for starting complementary food, Dietary Diversity/day, Age of ceasing breastfeeding, and Minimal meal frequency.

Operational definitions

Minimum meal frequency Feeding of infant and young children (IYC) that fulfills at least 2–3 times complementary feeding within 24 h for IYC age of 6–8 months and 3–4 times complementary feeding within 24 h for IYC age of 8 months and above34.

Dietary Diversity: Dietary diversity was assessed based on the number of food groups consumed in the previous 24 h, following WHO/UNICEF IYCF indicators. The seven WHO-recommended food groups include grains, roots, and tubers; legumes and nuts; dairy products; flesh foods; eggs; vitamin-A rich fruits and vegetables; and other fruits and vegetables. Children who consumed foods from four or more groups were classified as meeting the minimum dietary diversity (MDD) standard35.

Appropriate complementary feeding practice: In this study, appropriate complementary feeding practice was defined using the Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD) indicator. Children were considered to have appropriate complementary feeding if they met both Minimum Dietary Diversity (MDD) and Minimum Meal Frequency (MMF) during the previous 24 h. It was coded as Yes if both criteria were met and No otherwise35,36.

Data collection tools and methods

Data on socio-demographic characteristics, complementary feeding–related factors, obstetric history, and child characteristics were collected using a semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant literature. It was initially prepared in English and then translated into Amharic, the local language, to facilitate data collection. Finally, the questionnaire was re-translated into English by language experts for analysis purposes. Data collection was carried out by the principal investigators.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was evaluated by experts in relevant fields to ensure content validity. It was pretested on 5% of the sample in Arerti town. The pretest was used to assess language clarity, the appropriateness of the questions, the estimated time required for the interview, and to identify necessary amendments. Data collectors received training on the questionnaire, data collection procedures, and ethical considerations. During data collection, supervisors and investigators closely monitored the process to ensure data quality. Finally, the principal investigators checked all completed questionnaires for completeness, consistency, and accuracy before data entry.

Data processing and analysis

Before analysis, the data were cleaned, edited, and coded, and any inconsistencies or errors were corrected after reviewing the original data using the assigned code numbers. The cleaned data were entered into Epi-Data version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and means were computed to summarize the study variables. To control for potential confounding variables, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. A bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted for each explanatory variable, and those with a p-value ≤ 0.25 were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. In the multivariable analysis, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model diagnostics were performed to ensure validity: multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values ranging from > 1, indicating no significant multicollinearity; model goodness-of-fit was checked using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.42), showing an adequate fit.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant national and institutional guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from Debre Berhan University (Ref: 234/2021), and permission was secured from the North Shoa Zonal Health Department and the Debre Berhan Town Health Office. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential benefits. Participants were assured that their involvement was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained by using unique codes instead of personal identifiers, and all data were securely stored. The study findings will be disseminated to Debre Berhan University, local health authorities, political leaders, and relevant non-governmental organizations to support initiatives aimed at improving weaning practices.

Result

Socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics of the respondents

Among the 444 infants and young children included in the study, 57.4% were male, and 42.6% were female. Most mothers (78.2%) resided in urban areas, while 21.8% lived in rural areas. Regarding maternal occupation, 47.5% were housewives, 31.1% were government employees, 14.6% were merchants, and 6.8% were farmers. Children were aged 6–8 months (20.7%), 9–11 months (27.3%), and 12–23 months (52.0%). Most mothers attended at least one antenatal care visit (63.1%) and had delivered in a health facility (85.1%). Regarding parity, 40.1% of mothers were primiparous, 56.5% had two or more children, and 3.4% had five or more children (Table 1).

Complementary feeding practices and dietary diversity

Most mothers (83.3%) start complementary foods at 6–8 months, consistent with WHO recommendations, while a few introduced foods earlier than 4 months (1.8%) or later than 9 months (14%). Over half of children (57.7%) continued breastfeeding until 12–23 months, whereas 42.3% ceased breastfeeding before 11 months. The main reasons for starting complementary feeding were reaching an appropriate age (70.9%), breast problems (13.5%), inadequate breast milk (6.1%), and work inconvenience (7.0%). Regarding dietary diversity, only 2.7% of children received foods from a single food group, 34.9% from two groups, 33.1% from three groups, and 29.3% from four or more groups daily, indicating that less than one-third of children met the recommended dietary diversity. For minimal meal frequency, 58.7% of children aged 6–8 months, 67.7% of children aged 9–11 months, and 72.3% of children aged 12–23 months (all breastfed) received the WHO-recommended minimum number of meals per day (Table 2).

Prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding in Debre Berhan Town

In this study, the proportion of mothers practicing appropriate complementary feeding in Debre Berhan Town was 43.7%, based on the Minimum Acceptable Diet (MAD), which considers both dietary diversity and meal frequency. This indicates that more than half of the children are not receiving the recommended complementary feeding (Fig. 1).

Discussions

In this study, 43.7% of mothers with infants and young children aged 6–23 months practiced appropriate complementary feeding. This finding is comparable with reports from Sidama Zone (42%)37, Soro District (34.3%)38, Enemay district(40.5%)39,and Shashemene town (41.7%)40. However, the prevalence observed in the current study is higher than that reported in Northwest Ethiopia (23.9%)38, Wolaita Sodo (8.5%)27, Mini EDHS 2019 (9.76%)6,and Southern Ethiopia (9.5%)12. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study settings, socio-demographic characteristics of participants, access to health information and services, and variations in sample size and study period. On the other hand, the prevalence in this study is lower than findings from Southwest Ethiopia (55.3%)41 and Lasta (56.5%)13. The variation may be attributed to contextual differences, including variations in health service coverage, community-based nutrition programs, cultural feeding practices, and levels of maternal awareness in those areas.

The study tried to identify the determinants of appropriate weaning practice. Attending post-natal visit, maternal work being a housewife, child vaccination, and institutional deliveries were factors significantly associated with appropriate weaning practice. Variables such as ANC follow-up and parity were not statistically significant after adjustment, suggesting that their effects may be mediated by other factors, including postnatal care attendance, maternal education, or health service utilization.

Mothers attending post-natal visits were 3.17 times more likely to practice appropriate weaning compared with mothers who did not attend postnatal service. This finding is consistent with existing literature11–45, which emphasizes the role of postnatal care in enhancing maternal knowledge and child-feeding practices. During postnatal visits, healthcare providers often offer counseling on optimal infant and young child feeding (IYCF), including timely and appropriate initiation of complementary feeding. Such health education can empower mothers to make informed decisions about their children’s nutrition.

Maternal occupation was another significant factor. Mothers who were housewives were nearly three times more likely to practice appropriate weaning compared to mothers who were farmers. This finding consistent with earlier literature13,39,44,which found a higher likelihood of appropriate weaning among employed mothers. The higher likelihood of appropriate feeding practices among housewives compared to farmers may be explained by differences in time availability. Mothers engaged in farming activities often face heavy workloads and prolonged absence from home, which may limit their ability to prepare diverse foods and maintain recommended feeding frequency. Housewives may have greater daytime access to community health workers, such as health extension workers, who provide counseling and support on child feeding practices. Additionally, social support, exposure to health education, and the opportunity to implement recommended practices within the household may further contribute to these differences.

In addition, institutional delivery was also associated with better weaning practices. Mothers who gave birth at the hospital level were more than twice as likely to practice appropriate weaning compared to those who delivered at home. This finding is consistent with previous literature8,11,46. This may be because health facilities provide opportunities for immediate postnatal counseling and follow-up care, including nutrition education. Moreover, institutional delivery is often linked with greater exposure to skilled health professionals who can emphasize the importance of early and proper complementary feeding.

Another significant factor was the child’s vaccination status. Mothers whose children received full immunization were almost twice as likely to practice appropriate weaning as those whose children were not fully vaccinated. This report was consistent with previous literature47. This association may reflect the “contact point” effect: each vaccination visit provides an opportunity for health workers to deliver nutrition counseling and reinforce recommended complementary feeding practices. Mothers who regularly attend immunization services are therefore more likely to receive guidance on child feeding.

Limitation of the study

This study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Mothers might not accurately remember the timing of complementary feeding initiation or may report practices consistent with recommended guidelines rather than their actual behavior.

Mothers who were seriously ill during data collection were excluded, potentially introducing selection bias, as illness may be associated with suboptimal weaning practices. This could lead to a slight underestimation of inappropriate weaning prevalence. Although urban–rural data were collected, the small number of rural participants limited our ability to perform robust comparisons between settings. The predominance of urban participants may also limit generalizability to rural and semi-urban populations, where feeding practices and access to health information may differ. In addition, the “Farmer” occupational category was used as the reference group in the multivariable logistic regression analysis despite its relatively small proportion in the study population, which may have affected the stability of estimates and resulted in wider confidence intervals. Therefore, interpretation of occupational-related findings should be made with caution.

Conclusions

Less than half of mothers with children aged 6–23 months in Debre Berhan town practiced appropriate complementary feeding. Postnatal care attendance, being a housewife, institutional delivery, and child vaccination were significantly associated with appropriate weaning practices. However, this study has some limitations. Feeding practices were self-reported, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases, and mothers who were seriously ill during data collection were excluded, potentially leading to selection bias and slight underestimation of inappropriate weaning prevalence. The predominance of urban participants and the small number of rural mothers limit the generalizability of the findings to rural and semi-urban populations, and causal relationships cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design. To improve appropriate weaning practices, interventions are recommended at multiple levels: community-based nutrition education, peer support, and food demonstrations; health facility integration of structured feeding counseling into postnatal care, delivery discharge, and immunization; and policy-level improvements in training, supervision, monitoring, and access to nutritious complementary foods. Future studies using longitudinal designs and including more rural participants are needed to confirm these associations and enhance generalizability.
