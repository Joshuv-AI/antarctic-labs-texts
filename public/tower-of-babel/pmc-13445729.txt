Chronic arsenic poisoning from a realgar-containing traditional medicine causing hepatic veno-occlusive disease and polyserositis: a case report
Authors: Yanhong Liu, Xingguo Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD) is a potentially life-threatening vascular liver disorder. Its association with chronic arsenic poisoning from traditional medicines is rare, and this case highlights a unique etiology and the importance of timely intervention.Case presentationA 20-year-old Han Chinese female presented with progressive abdominal distension after self-administering Jian'er Pills (containing realgar, As₂S₂) at 15 tablets/day for one year—25% above the recommended maximum dose. Clinical, laboratory, imaging, and histopathological data were collected. Physical examination revealed palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, diffuse hyperpigmentation, hepatomegaly, and ascites. No peripheral edema was noted. Laboratory tests confirmed severe arsenic toxicity (urinary arsenic 4523.6 μg/L; blood arsenic 151.4 μg/L) and pancytopenia. Liver function tests showed ALT 78 U/L, AST 65 U/L, total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 0.4 mg/dL. Imaging showed hepatomegaly with periportal edema, massive ascites, and bilateral pleural and pericardial effusions. Liver biopsy confirmed VOD with characteristic features: sinusoidal dilatation, central venule obliteration, and hyaline microthrombi. The patient received chelation therapy with sodium dimercaptopropanesulfonate and sodium thiosulfate. Within four days, pericardial and pleural effusions resolved completely, and ascites volume decreased by > 50%. After three treatment cycles, all effusions resolved and hepatic venous flow normalized. At 7-month follow-up, arsenic levels normalized, with no recurrence of effusions and normal liver function.ConclusionsChronic excessive intake of arsenic-containing traditional medicines can precipitate life-threatening VOD with polyserositis. Early diagnosis and prompt chelation therapy are crucial for achieving complete and sustained recovery.

Introduction

Hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD), also known as sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, is a potentially life-threatening vascular liver disorder typically associated with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or high-intensity chemotherapeutic agents such as busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and melphalan [1]. VOD resulting from chronic arsenic exposure through oral traditional medicine intake is exceptionally rare. Arsenic is known to induce endothelial damage, oxidative stress, and microcirculatory disturbance, which can lead to sinusoidal obstruction [2].

Several previous case reports have described hepatic veno-occlusive disease associated with herbal or traditional medicines containing pyrrolizidine alkaloids [3–6]. However, Jian’er Pills do not contain these compounds. The present case therefore represents a distinct mechanism of VOD related to chronic arsenic exposure, highlighting a novel etiology of traditional medicine-associated liver injury.

We report a rare case of VOD with polyserositis secondary to chronic arsenic poisoning from long-term use of a realgar-containing traditional medicine, highlighting the distinctive clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, successful chelation therapy, and favorable 7-month outcome.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old Han Chinese female presented in June 2025 with a two-week history of progressive abdominal distension. She had a history of chronic constipation, for which she had been self-medicating with Jian'er Pills at 15 tablets daily for approximately one year—chronic excessive intake (25% above the recommended maximum adult dose of 12 tablets). Jian'er Pills contain realgar (arsenic disulfide, As₂S₂) as a labeled ingredient (Fig. 1A). Physical examination revealed distinctive dermatological findings: diffuse hyperpigmentation on the neck and upper chest (Fig. 1B), palmar hyperkeratosis with whitish gritty plaques (Fig. 1C), plantar hyperkeratosis showing similar changes (Fig. 1D). No peripheral edema was noted. Abdominal examination showed hepatomegaly (liver edge 4 cm below costal margin) and shifting dullness. Neurological assessment confirmed symmetric sensory reduction in both feet.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated severe arsenic toxicity: urinary arsenic 4523.6 μg/L (reference < 32 μg/L) measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) using a spot urine sample (not a 24-h collection; blood arsenic 151.4 μg/L. Complete blood count revealed pancytopenia (leukocyte count 1.97 × 10⁹/L).Liver function tests showed ALT 78 U/L, AST 65 U/L, total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 0.4 mg/dL. Coagulation profile was normal. Serological tests, including anti-HAV IgM, HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM, anti-HCV, antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA), and smooth muscle antibody (SMA), ruled out autoimmune, viral, and other common causes of hepatitis.

Abdominal ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced CT revealed hepatomegaly with periportal edema (Glisson's sheath thickening), massive ascites, and bilateral pleural and pericardial effusions. Doppler ultrasound showed dampened hepatic venous flow, suggesting outflow obstruction.

Abdominal paracentesis was performed for diagnostic purposes prior to liver biopsy, yielding transudative ascitic fluid (protein 1.8 g/dL, albumin 0.9 g/dL, white blood cell count 45/μL, lactate dehydrogenase 85 U/L), consistent with portal hypertension-related ascites. After albumin infusion and diuretic therapy, the patient’s ascites decreased markedly. Subsequently, percutaneous liver biopsy was successfully performed under ultrasound guidance.

Percutaneous liver biopsy confirmed VOD with characteristic features: marked sinusoidal dilatation and congestion, extravasation of erythrocytes into the space of Disse, subtotal obliteration of central venules by edematous fibrous tissue, and multiple hyaline microthrombi within sinusoids (Fig. 1E). Masson trichrome staining further demonstrated prominent perivenular fibrosis surrounding the obliterated central veins (Fig. 1F), confirming the chronic nature of the venous outflow obstruction and providing histopathological correlation with the clinical picture of portal hypertension.

The patient received intravenous chelation therapy with sodium dimercaptopropanesulfonate (0.25 g daily) and sodium thiosulfate (1.28 g twice daily), along with diuretics and supportive care. The clinical response was rapid: repeat abdominal ultrasound four days after treatment initiation showed complete resolution of pericardial and pleural effusions and > 50% reduction in ascites volume. After three cycles of chelation therapy, all effusions resolved completely, and Doppler ultrasound demonstrated normalization of hepatic venous flow. The cutaneous hyperkeratosis and hyperpigmentation began to fade during hospitalization. Anticoagulation was not administered, as it has not been shown to be beneficial in VOD and may increase bleeding risk in the setting of thrombocytopenia [7].

At 7-month follow-up, laboratory tests showed complete normalization of arsenic levels (urinary arsenic < 32 μg/L, blood arsenic < 32 μg/L). Abdominal ultrasound confirmed absence of recurrent ascites or effusions. Liver function tests remained normal. Palmoplantar hyperkeratosis substantially regressed with only minimal residual thickening, and neurological symptoms nearly completely resolved. No new symptoms or complications emerged. Discussion and conclusions

This case demonstrates that chronic excessive intake of an arsenic-containing traditional medicine—not just acute poisoning or high-dose chemotherapy—can precipitate life-threatening VOD with polyserositis. This finding carries significant clinical implications: even non-acute, non-lethal doses of arsenic exposure can induce severe and potentially life-threatening hepatic vascular toxicity. Traditionally, VOD has been primarily associated with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) or high-intensity chemotherapeutic agents such as busulfan and melphalan; however, this case highlights that environmental or pharmaceutical arsenic exposure is also an important etiology of VOD—one that may be clinically overlooked.

Notably, the patient exhibited the classic clinical triad of arsenic poisoning: skin hyperkeratosis with pigmentation changes, peripheral neuropathy (manifested as paresthesia and muscle weakness), and hepatic involvement (hepatomegaly, ascites, and elevated transaminases). This constellation of symptoms should strongly raise suspicion for chronic arsenic poisoning, particularly in patients without a history of HSCT or underlying liver disease. This triad can serve as a valuable clinical screening clue to facilitate early recognition.

Another key feature of this case was the favorable response to chelation therapy. Following prompt diagnosis, the patient received sodium dimercaptopropanesulfonate (DMPS) and other chelating agents, leading to rapid improvement in liver function, resolution of ascites, and avoidance of progression to multiorgan failure—without requiring defibrotide, which is currently the only FDA-approved drug for severe HSCT-associated VOD. This distinction suggests that although the pathological mechanisms of arsenic-induced VOD overlap with those of HSCT-related VOD (e.g., sinusoidal endothelial injury and microthrombosis), the core driver in arsenic-related cases is direct arsenic toxicity and oxidative stress, rather than the widespread endothelial damage caused by conditioning regimens. Therefore, etiology-targeted chelation therapy may be more effective in arsenic-associated VOD than endothelial-protective agents alone. In contrast, HSCT-associated VOD, driven primarily by chemotherapeutic endothelial injury, often requires defibrotide, a polydisperse oligonucleotide that stabilizes endothelial cells and restores the thrombofibrinolytic balance [8, 9]. The present case underscores a fundamental therapeutic principle: when a clear exogenous toxin is identified, removing the source—rather than solely mitigating its downstream effects—can lead to rapid and complete reversal of even severe VOD, potentially obviating the need for defibrotide.

Several alternative diagnoses were considered and excluded. Viral hepatitis (A, B, C) and autoimmune hepatitis were ruled out by serological testing [2]. Other drug-induced liver injuries (e.g., from acetaminophen or herbal products containing pyrrolizidine alkaloids [3–6]) were considered unlikely given the absence of typical exposure history and the specific histopathological findings. Infectious causes that can mimic VOD—such as leptospirosis, dengue, and malaria—were also considered; however, the patient had no fever, travel history, or exposure to vectors, and serological tests for these pathogens were negative. Furthermore, 7-month follow-up confirmed an excellent long-term prognosis: sustained normalization of liver function, no recurrence of ascites, partial improvement of neurological symptoms, and significant recovery in quality of life. This underscores the decisive role of early recognition and timely intervention in arsenic poisoning management. Delayed diagnosis could lead to irreversible hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, or even hepatocellular carcinoma due to ongoing arsenic accumulation [10].

This case serves as a critical clinical warning: even chronic excessive intake of arsenic-containing traditional medicines—exceeding the recommended dose by only 25%—can precipitate life-threatening VOD with polyserositis. The presence of the classic arsenic-poisoning triad—characteristic dermatopathy (hyperkeratosis and hyperpigmentation), peripheral neuropathy, and unexplained hepatic dysfunction with ascites—should prompt immediate toxicological screening, as early recognition is paramount for favorable outcomes. Crucially, this case demonstrates that etiology-targeted chelation therapy can achieve complete and sustained reversal of arsenic-induced VOD, with normalization of hepatic venous flow and resolution of multi-cavity effusions within days, underscoring that toxin removal is the most effective intervention. The updated Chinese guideline for drug-induced liver injury also emphasizes the importance of thoroughly investigating traditional medicine use in patients with unexplained liver injury [11]. These findings reinforce the need for heightened clinical awareness of drug-induced hepatic vascular toxicity and the importance of obtaining a detailed medication history, including traditional remedies, in patients presenting with unexplained portal hypertension.
