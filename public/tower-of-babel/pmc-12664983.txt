Mapping war trauma: A machine learning approach to predict mental health impacts in Ukraine
Authors: Safiyeh Tayebi, Ayse Sert Oti, Hossein Fathollahian, Ubydul Haque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a predictive modeling system based on the use of GeoAI to estimate mental health outcomes of wartime in Ukrainian cities, utilizing spatially linked data on the environment, infrastructure, and conflict. Six self-reported psychological outcomes, symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder, or PSD, anxiety, depression, insomnia, loneliness, and sleep duration, were collected in surveys throughout the war and analyzed in the context of more than 30 spatial predictors: cold exposure, access to heating, power outages, housing insulation, and city-level frequency of drone, missile, artillery, and shelling attacks. Predictor datasets that are derived from incident tracking, World Health Organization, and humanitarian reporting systems, and environmental indicators derived from surveys, which are harmonized using a spatial data integration protocol. In realizing the GeoAI concept, we developed a machine learning pipeline utilizing Ordinary Least Squares, Lasso, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Extreme Gradient Boosting. All models were trained and tested on spatially independent training and testing splits in order to preserve the generalization properties of the models. XG Boost is also shown to be effective, with R2 values often exceeding 0.74 and MAPE values typically less than 7.2 %. Feature importance analysis revealed that key drivers of being exposed to prolonged cold, inadequate insulation, and exposure to drones or artillery were found to be dominant drivers of psychological distress. This GeoAI framework combines the strength of geospatial analytics with artificial intelligence to give precise and high-resolution location-based forecasting of mental health burdens in conflict settings. The method offers a flexible tool for a proactive humanitarian response that can target mental health services spatially based on predictions of mental health vulnerability, in contrast to retrospective clinical information, for relief agencies and public health planners. This work is a step towards incorporating GeoAI in the field of crisis epidemiology, demonstrating the ability to use GeoAI in real-time, place-based mental health planning in war-affected areas.

Introduction

In modern wars, civilians are the major victims not only of physical violence, but also of the invisible psychological price. As the nature of conflict changes to an asymmetrical strategy and long-range and high-frequency strike, the mental health burdens of noncombatants increase. This evolution is especially true in the war in Ukraine, which has been escalating since the invasion in March 2022. The population has lived under almost incessant aerial bombardment, drone monitoring, missile attack, and shelling. These experiences are not limited by time and space. They are present in our daily lives, invading our homes, hospitals, and public places. When health systems are destroyed and the security spaces disappear, trauma becomes a spatial state (McElroy et al., 2024). People do not merely suffer; they suffer at particular locations, from repeated environmental and psychological stresses.

Whereas mental health research in war zones has historically relied upon clinical screening and qualitative interviews, both of which tend to remove trauma from the lived environments in which it is experienced. This decontextualization leads to an underestimation of the role of the combination of infrastructure loss, environmental hardship, and exposure to violence in influencing mental outcomes. Studies in Syria, Afghanistan, and Iraq have demonstrated a synergistic impact of weapon type, patterns of displacement, and breakdown in care infrastructure on the development of symptoms of post-traumatic stress, depression, and anxiety. (Hassan et al., 2016; Slewa-Younan et al., 2015). However, these factors are not often modeled together. For Ukraine, this is especially urgent, given the geographical diversity of attacks, as well as the diversity of the ability of regions to absorb trauma or provide care. (Shevlin et al., 2022). In other public health settings, geospatial tools, such as Geographic Information Systems, remote sensing, and spatial statistics, have demonstrated potential, including in the tracing of infectious disease outbreaks and exposure to environmental risk. (Garber et al., 2020). However, their application to war-related mental health remains rare and fragmented. GeoAI, which combines machine learning methods and spatial analysis to identify nonlinear and intricate patterns, as well as predictive modeling, is used less frequently. This is a potential that mental health research has only recently started to tap into. In our previous systematic review, only a few studies were found that use the spatial granularity or real-time potential of GeoAI to guide humanitarian actions or resource distribution in war zones. This paper helps to fill that methodological and empirical gap.

The present research is based on data collected in more than 550 Ukrainian cities (Fig. 1), combining self-reported mental health symptoms with spatially related measures of exposure to the effects of wartime attacks, environmental stressors, and infrastructural disruption. We forecast six psychological outcomes with the assistance of a set of machine learning models: post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSD), anxiety, depression, insomnia, loneliness, and sleep duration. Several types of weapon exposures, failure of heating and power infrastructure, and the intensity of cold weather are predictors. In addition to measuring the overall predictive accuracy of the models, they also produce feature importance scores and city-specific residuals, which enable us to determine which risk factors have the most significant impact on the model, as well as the geographic distribution of the model's reliability. This combined model offers a new understanding of how material conditions most effectively model the psychological impact of the war, and predictive reliability may underlie geographically oriented mental health interventions.Fig. 1The study area (Cities under Russian occupation were excluded due to a lack of reliable survey and exposure data. The research is based on two central questions. First (RQ1), what are the most important environmental and exposure-related variables that most effectively predict variance in mental health between cities? Recognition of such relationships can inform the international standards on the civilian effects of particular weapon technologies and infrastructure failures. Second (RQ2), in what ways and to what extent are these outcomes geographically consistent and predictable within the urban system? The question helps close the divide between spatial machine learning and humanitarian response, as mental health services can be distributed not only in accordance with need but also based on the predictability of the response. Through the development of a methodologically sound, spatially explicit system for forecasting the psychology of war, the research paper provides a scalable, evidence-based planning framework for conflict-rich environments.

This study advances the literature on wartime mental health and geospatial epidemiology in several ways. Methodologically, it develops a GeoAI-based predictive framework that integrates survey-based psychological outcomes with more than 30 spatially coded environmental, infrastructural, and conflict-related variables, moving beyond descriptive associations to capture nonlinearities and interactions. Empirically, it analyzes data from over 550 Ukrainian cities to identify the strongest predictors of PTSD, depression, insomnia, and related outcomes, demonstrating the central role of cold exposure, inadequate insulation, and attack frequency. Spatially, it generates predictive maps and residual analyses that highlight both hotspots of vulnerability and areas of model misfit, underscoring the value of predictive mapping as an anticipatory tool. Finally, by linking feature importance to material conditions such as heating, power, and food insecurity, the study provides actionable insights for humanitarian and public health interventions.

Literature review

Research on mental health in conflict settings has accumulated substantially in recent decades, producing a growing body of evidence on the ways war-related exposures influence psychological well-being. The dominant trend in this scholarship has been to document elevated prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and related conditions among civilian populations who experience violence, displacement, and disruption. Systematic reviews have consistently shown that exposure to war increases the risk of psychological morbidity to two or three times that of non-exposed populations, and that women, children, and marginalized groups are especially vulnerable (Carpiniello, 2023). Yet, the literature has remained largely descriptive, presenting prevalence rates and associations rather than advancing predictive or spatially structured understandings of how mental health burdens emerge and evolve during war.

At the methodological level, studies have often prioritized individual-level clinical assessments or survey-based measures, which are invaluable but tend to overlook the material and environmental conditions that shape daily life during conflict (Jayasinghe, 2024; Munezero & Manoukian, 2021). The destruction of infrastructure, recurrent power outages, lack of heating or water supply, and disruptions to food systems are rarely incorporated systematically into analyses of war-related mental health. This gap has left many accounts of psychological outcomes in conflict somewhat decontextualized, focusing on traumatic exposure in isolation from the everyday deprivations that exacerbate it. Where infrastructural and environmental variables are included, they are often treated in a cursory manner or used as background descriptors rather than analytically integrated into explanatory frameworks (Cantelmi et al., 2021).

A parallel development in the broader field of humanitarian health has been the increasing use of geospatial approaches. Conflict, displacement, and vulnerability are spatial processes, and geospatial analysis offers ways to capture clustering, neighborhood spillovers, and infrastructural access patterns that shape health. Greenough and Nelson (2019) argued for a shift in humanitarian health research from static mapping toward more sophisticated spatial analytics, capable of revealing non-random clustering of harm and informing the geographic targeting of interventions. Such approaches have been applied with success to infectious disease outbreaks and disaster response, but applications to conflict-related mental health remain sparse (Gregg Greenough and Nelson, 2019).

Where geospatial methods have been used in conflict settings, they have demonstrated the value of moving beyond anecdotal reporting. Mueller et al. (2021), for example, used machine learning applied to high-resolution satellite imagery to quantify damage to the built environment in Syria, offering reproducible evidence of urban destruction across time and space (Mueller et al., 2021). In another recent application of spatial methods to conflict-landscapes, Huang et al. (2023) developed a semi-automated framework for detecting urban burning and building damage in Mariupol during the Russia-Ukraine war. They demonstrated that damage to built structures is not spatially random, but follows systematic spatial clustering tied to frontline dynamics and urban morphology. These studies illustrate how spatial analytics reveal systematic patterns that are otherwise obscured in qualitative accounts, yet they stop short of linking infrastructural harm to psychological outcomes among affected populations (Huang et al., 2023).

While geospatial approaches have been slow to penetrate conflict mental health research, predictive analytics and machine learning have advanced rapidly in other branches of psychiatry and public health. In clinical contexts, algorithms have been used to predict depression, suicidality, or post-traumatic stress among veterans, patients with chronic illness, and populations exposed to natural disasters. Machine learning models have proven capable of capturing nonlinear interactions among predictors, identifying risk factors that traditional statistical models overlook, and generating accurate forecasts of mental health outcomes. For instance, studies have employed ensemble methods to forecast depression trajectories after COVID-19 and to identify predictors of suicidal ideation among veterans using administrative health records. However, most such applications rely on clinical datasets and are disconnected from the environmental and spatial dimensions of psychological stress.

A few studies have attempted to apply machine learning directly to conflict-related mental health. Qasrawi et al. (2023) used classification models to examine how political violence affected children and adolescents, but their analysis did not incorporate geospatial variation or infrastructural determinants. Cosic et al. (2024) proposed using natural language processing to monitor emotional discourse during wartime and to identify negative sentiment patterns that may signal mental health risks, offering a promising but largely experimental direction (Cosic et al., 2024; Qasrawi et al., 2023). These studies underscore the potential of AI and machine learning in war health research, yet they remain either narrowly focused on clinical outcomes or insufficiently integrated with the material and spatial conditions that characterize conflict environments.

The absence of integrated geospatial and predictive approaches has several implications. First, it means that most studies fail to capture neighborhood effects and spatial spillovers. Trauma is not evenly distributed, and individuals in cities adjacent to bombardment sites may experience stress and disruption even in the absence of direct attacks. Ignoring spatial autocorrelation risks underestimating the reach of conflict-related trauma. Second, the failure to systematically include infrastructural determinants such as housing insulation, heating, or food insecurity underplays the importance of everyday survival conditions in shaping mental health outcomes. Evidence from humanitarian field reports has long suggested that cold exposure, prolonged blackouts, and hunger exacerbate anxiety and depression, yet these factors are rarely modeled with the same rigor as exposure to violence. Third, cross-sectional designs dominate the field, with limited attention to timing, recurrence, or lagged effects of attacks. The distinction between a single traumatic episode and repeated or chronic exposures is crucial for understanding psychological trajectories, yet the literature often collapses such variation into aggregated exposure counts. Finally, predictive models, where they exist, are frequently criticized for being opaque, producing “black box” forecasts without interpretability. In humanitarian settings, however, interpretability is essential: policymakers need to know not only where burdens are likely to emerge, but which specific predictors are driving outcomes so they can target resources effectively.

A small number of innovative studies point toward how these gaps might be addressed. Savige et al. (2024) demonstrated how qualitative lived-experience data could be integrated into GIS, geolocating narratives of mental health challenges in rural Australia (Savige et al., 2024). Although outside a conflict context, this work illustrates the potential of mixed-methods geospatial approaches to bring human experience into spatialized analysis. Likewise, the Ukraine vulnerability index developed by Haque and colleagues underscores the need to combine conflict and infrastructural stressors with mental health data, but leaves open the possibility of predictive modeling as the next step. These examples illustrate both the momentum and the limits of current scholarship: the tools exist, but they have not yet been assembled into a framework capable of anticipating mental health burdens in a systematic, spatially explicit way.

Our study is designed to extend the literature by developing a predictive, interpretable, and spatially structured modeling approach to wartime mental health. We aim to address key gaps in the literature, the neglect of infrastructural and environmental determinants, the lack of predictive geospatial models, the absence of interpretability in existing ML applications, and the limited translation of findings into policy relevance. While our analysis is rooted in the specific context of Ukraine, the methodological framework contributes to the broader effort to integrate GeoAI, in conflict research.

Methodology

To be more precise, the research will help to answer the question of whether the environmental stressors at the city level and the exposure to the attacks during the wars predict the spatial pattern of mental health outcomes during the war in Ukraine. To address this, we have developed a multi-model predictive model that incorporates a wide range of data, including survey-based mental health variables and geospatially coded environmental and attack features, within a spatial machine learning framework. Through modeling the relationships between place-based predictors and psychological distress, we aimed to generate both accurate outcome predictions and spatially explicit predictions of vulnerability.

Data

Mental health surveys were collected in 2022–2023 through community partners in war-affected areas, with indicators recorded contemporaneously to attack exposure and infrastructural conditions. Surveys were conducted using non-probabilistic recruitment methods due to wartime conditions and logistical constraints. While this introduces potential sampling bias, we sought to ensure a broad geographic coverage across more than 550 cities. Attack data from the WHO and UN humanitarian reporting systems were temporally harmonized to the same collection periods to allow consistent aggregation at the city level. To address temporal alignment, attack and exposure variables were aggregated to the same months during which mental health surveys were conducted. Where precise synchronization was not possible due to reporting frequencies, we aggregated exposures to the quarter of the survey date. This ensures that predictor variables represent contemporaneous conditions rather than mismatched periods. Surveys were conducted by medical volunteers, social workers, and community health professionals between 2022 and 2023. Respondents were adults (18+) from diverse urban households. Due to the conflict setting, surveys were not based on probability sampling, and precise response rates cannot be estimated. Instead, recruitment prioritized breadth of geographic coverage. To reduce noise, responses were aggregated to the city level, which allowed us to retain geographic variation while ensuring national coverage across more than 550 cities.

The mental health variables were calculated using structured questionnaires administered to community partners and local health professionals in areas affected by war. These questionnaires quantified six self-outcomes of PTSD, depressive feelings, anxiety, loneliness, insomnia, and average sleep time. To contextualize these outcomes, we constructed a rich spatial database of environmental conditions, infrastructural disruptions, and attack-specific exposures. Ecological data included cold temperatures, access to heating, frequency of power outages, the quality of housing insulation, and access to humanitarian aid. These variables were compiled from satellite-derived datasets, NGO reports, and survey items embedded in the same mental health questionnaire. Various sources of data on attacks, including the WHO incident tracking system, UN-coordinated humanitarian reporting systems, and externally validated platforms, were utilized to gather data on attacks. All the attack data were processed in advance to give frequency counts or exposure scores by city. These were combined with standardized city names, resulting in a final dataset for analysis comprising more than 30 predictors across approximately 550 cities. Cities currently occupied by Russia were excluded from the modeling outputs to ensure the validity of the reported outcomes and prevent a scenario of misinterpretation of the imputed values in areas with data transparency issues.

Methods

All predictor variables were standardized to z-scores prior to modeling. Missing values were imputed at the city level using median substitution. Multicollinearity was assessed using pairwise correlations and variance inflation factors (VIF). Predictors with VIF >10 or correlations >0.9 were aggregated or excluded to reduce redundancy. The analytical workflow followed a structured machine learning pipeline designed to preserve spatial independence between training and testing samples. In the first step, data cleaning and preprocessing are performed using methods that enhance the quality of the data and highlight informative structures among the large-scale signals, aligning with our GeoAI approach to predicting psychological outcomes in conflict zones (Torkamani-Azar et al., 2015). Second, the dataset was randomly split between an 80 percent training set and a 20 percent test set at the city level, and outcome information of the test cities was not known to the model during training. All predictor variables were standardized and sent to five different regression algorithms: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), Lasso Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Besides the normalized city names, these were also used to create the final dataset for analysis, which included over 30 predictors for more than 550 cities. To preserve the integrity of the modeled results and avoid misinterpreting the imputation values, Russia-populated cities were excluded from the modeling outputs. (Bhattacharyya, 2025; Tibshiranit, 1996). However, linear models can be limited due to complex non-linear interactions between predictors, which is a typical case in health and environmental data (Bonnell, 2017; Wang et al., 2020).To overcome this shortcoming, we have added tree-based ENMs, which have been reported to be resistant to multicollinearity, capable of capturing nonlinear effects, and exhibit a strong predictive power. Random Forest is a collection of decision trees constructed on bootstrap samples, which alleviates variance by averaging (Boulesteix et al., 2012; Ibrahim, 2023). Gradient Boosting is an improvement to this algorithm where the residual errors of all previous trees are sequentially minimized (Xiang et al., 2020), giving you more control over the bias-variance tradeoff. The use of gradient boosting, particularly XGBoost, has been successfully applied in numerous structured data competitions and research studies due to its efficient regularization and sparsity-aware algorithms, as well as its ability to handle missing data (Aydin & Ozturk, 2021; Chen & Guestrin, 2016). These properties make tree-based models particularly appropriate for modeling health outcomes from heterogeneous warzone data sets, in which interactions between disruptions in infrastructure, exposure to environmental factors, and psychological distress are likely to be nonlinear and spatially clustered. Each model was trained separately using the same training set and was evaluated on the test cities to determine three key performance metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and coefficient of determination (R2) (Demirdelen et al., 2019). This way, we were able to test not only accuracy, but also generalization, while keeping the observed and predicted spaces strictly separate. Performance scores were obtained for each outcome-model pair to inform the choice of model in subsequent steps.

After model evaluation, the final model for each mental health outcome was selected based on the highest R2 values in the test set. To explore the predictors strongly associated with each outcome, we extracted feature importance scores from the training-only models using gain-based measures specific to tree-based methods. The significance of the individual predictors was estimated for each outcome and ranked accordingly. Mental health vulnerability in space was interpreted, and hypotheses were generated through the tabulation and visualization of these results. We used the trained models on the test set to obtain out-of-sample predictions in all cities. A comparison of these predicted values and actual values led to the evaluation of residuals to determine model accuracy and spatial bias.

The gain metric, calculated by XGBoost, represents the feature importance scores and measures how much a split on a predictor can reduce the model loss across all trees. To be robust, we also compared it to cover (frequency-weighted occurrence of a feature across splits) and weight (quantity of times a feature is utilized in all trees) measures. Fig. 4 reports the gain metric, whereas full rankings using other metrics can be found in the Supplementary Materials. This provides transparency into how predictor contributions were drawn.

All the machine learning models were trained to optimize their hyperparameters with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. In the case of XGBoost and Gradient Boosting, we optimized the number of trees, learning rate, tree depth, subsample fraction, and column sampling values using a grid search. In the case of Random Forest, tuning the number of trees and the maximum features was done similarly. The XGBoost search also included regularization parameters (α, λ) to prevent overfitting. The best hyperparameter settings were obtained by minimizing the RMSE using the validation folds. Parameters that were not part of the search were set to their default settings.

These data are preprocessed and combined to form a unified dataset, which is then further divided into training and testing sets that are spatially independent, allowing for the generation of strong spatial generalization. It derives its feature importance to understand model drivers, followed by spatial prediction and residual analysis to understand model reliability in different regions. Residuals were further visualized and statistically summarized to examine geographic trends in model performance and to identify cities where available predictors may poorly capture psychosocial conditions.

We also know that city populations are non-homogeneous and that individual-level mental health results can be simplified through aggregation. It was decided to use cities as the unit of analysis, as humanitarian planning and service deployment tend to occur at the city level. Aggregating data avoids survey noise but provides a comparative possibility between 550 urban situations. We observe this to be a limitation, and we would advise that upcoming research include neighborhood-level measures where the data is available.

To assess potential residual spatial dependence, we computed Moran's I statistic on model residuals using a queen contiguity spatial weights matrix. Also, extended the analytical framework by implementing a Spatial Lag Model (SAR) to explicitly capture spatial interdependence among neighboring cities. Specifically, we prepared city-level spatial weights matrices (queen contiguity and 8-nearest-neighbor) to model spatial relationships and applied maximum-likelihood estimation procedures to incorporate spatial dependence within the analytical framework. Diagnostics, including Lagrange Multiplier and robust LM tests, were used to determine the presence and form of spatial dependence prior to model estimation. These methodological steps ensure that spatial structure is properly modeled and that the inferences drawn from the GeoAI framework are not biased by unmodeled spatial autocorrelation.

Results

As a first step toward understanding the psychological impacts of the war, we examined the geographic disparity in the occurrence of primary mental health outcomes in relation to the urban centers of Ukraine. Fig. 3 illustrates the comparative distribution of six self-reported mental health indices, which highlight the heterogeneity of psychological distress across cities during the prolonged conflict. Substantial geographic variation was found across the six indicators of mental health that were measured. PTSD symptoms, depression, and insomnia showed not only higher mean values but wider interquartile range values, which implies greater heterogeneity in psychological distress for these outcomes in different urban settings. In contrast, sleep duration was more normally distributed with a more central spread. These patterns reflect both the uneven distribution of conflict exposure and city-specific differences in environmental resilience. Model performance and predictive accuracy

To assess the explanatory and predictive strength of city-level variables in modeling wartime mental health outcomes, we compared the performance of five regression algorithms: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), Lasso, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Each model was evaluated on a held-out test set comprising 20 % of cities, unseen during training, thereby allowing for a realistic assessment of spatial generalizability.

Performance was assessed using three complementary metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and the coefficient of determination (R2). Together, these metrics capture both absolute and relative error, as well as the proportion of variance explained by the model. Across all six mental health outcomes, PTSD, CPTSD, depression, anxiety, loneliness, insomnia, and sleep duration, XGBoost consistently outperformed all other models on each metric.

In all results, XGBoost achieved a mean R2 of 0.74, indicating that a significant proportion of the variability in the prevalence of mental health in cities can be attributed to the predictors. The low RMSE values and moderate MAPE, approximately 7 %, were associated with stability and proportionate accuracy of prediction. Gradient Boosting and Random Forest models were also highly effective, particularly in predicting sleep duration and depression, as complex interactions between environmental and infrastructural stressors appeared to contribute to the variability in these outcomes. Linear models (OLS and Lasso) dropped to the ground; in contrast, they continued to fail (from much beyond 0.50) on several results with a larger error measure, whereas Lasso delivered only fringe benefits of regularization but could not reflect nonlinear or interaction effects. To prevent these findings from being overstated due to the influence of spatial dependence and information leakage, we have also performed a sensitivity analysis using spatial cross-validation, whereby test folds were restricted to all cities within a single administrative unit. This stricter scheme resulted in a slight reduction in performance (average R2 = 0.74; MAPE = 7.2), but it remained strong, confirming that the models effectively capture real associations between predictors and mental health outcomes, despite the challenges posed by spatial structure and aggregation.

To help quantify uncertainty regarding model performance, all metrics were estimated using 95 % confidence intervals (CI) and bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations on the test set. In the case of XGBoost, the minimum R2 was 0.74 (95 % CI: 0.71–0.77), with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 7 % (95 % CI: 6–8 %). The maximum predictability was found for sleep duration and depression, in which environmental and infrastructural variables seemed to have a more direct effect. However, for these complex outcomes, XGBoost models accounted for the majority of the variance and had a low error rate.

Taken together, these findings confirm that nonlinear, tree-based ensemble models, particularly XGBoost, are well-suited for modeling health outcomes in war-affected settings. Their capacity to handle complex feature interactions, nonlinearity, and missing data makes them especially valuable in humanitarian and conflict research, where predictors are often noisy, spatially structured, and multiscale in nature. Drivers of mental health outcomes

To understand the structure of the models, we analyzed feature importance scores obtained from XGBoost, which consistently gave the best-performing results. Apart from a few outcomes, models identified a small number of dominant predictors, suggesting that much of the variation in mental health was due to a small number of influential predictors, rather than broadly distributed and uniformly weighted effects. Lack of proper insulation, long-time exposure to cold, limited availability of heating fuel, and power outage were found to be the most predictive features. Tiredness, anxiety, and PTSD were the predominant predictors of varying intensity of drone and artillery attacks at a given time, showing the acute psychological effects of sudden and repeated bombardments. Furthermore, depression and loneliness were more sensitive to social infrastructure variables, which suggests that both material deprivation and social isolation contribute to mental health vulnerability. While there was some uniqueness among outcomes in terms of the predictors identified, there was considerable overlap in the top variables across models, which confirms the multidimensional nature of the pathways through which war impacts health. Predictive mapping and spatial generalization

The resulting predicted prevalence patterns for each of the six outcomes, namely, PTSD, depression, anxiety, loneliness, insomnia, and sleep duration, showed a good correspondence with the actual values in the test cities in terms of both a visual and a statistical match Fig. 5. These spatial predictions were not only accurate in terms of central tendency but also maintained the underlying geographic contours of vulnerability. High-burden areas, including those in central and eastern Ukraine, were well characterized by the models, despite the absence of ground-truth exposure data in these areas. This suggests that the models are capturing strong spatial signals embedded in the environmental and attack-related variables and are not simply overfitting to the training data. However, the residual maps for the six mental and sleep health outcomes have shown the spatial distribution of model prediction errors among Ukrainian cities (Fig. 6). Rather than showing absolute prevalence, these maps show where the models over- or under-estimated the actual values. Of particular interest, large positive residuals (underprediction) are clustered in parts of western and southern Ukraine for the variables of PTSD, loneliness, and sleep duration. In contrast, harmful residuals (overprediction) are more evident in northern and northeastern regions for anxiety, depression, and insomnia. These spatial misfits highlight the complex, localized nature of wartime psychosocial stress and indicate that there may be specific contextual factors at a city level, such as unmeasured social or infrastructural factors, that influence health outcomes beyond what is captured by weapon exposure and environmental predictors alone. The models were high prevalence rates of PTSD, insomnia, and depression, which conformed well with reported values in cities with high levels of exposure to conflict, energy infrastructure, and extended cold weather. At the same time, in Western cities with fewer frequent assaults and more stable conditions, predicted mental health burdens were also lower, which also reflected the existing data. The fact that such gradients can be rebuilt in terms of environmental and exposure variables alone justifies the spatial sensitivity of the model architecture. Predictive mapping of cities in the Russian-occupied territories was not visually covered due to data restrictions and the system's potential for underreporting. These spaces were filled with gray colors, which strengthened the spatial realm of conclusion and the moral limit of perception. Notably, these omissions did not favor the model; instead, they provided it with greater strength in terms of generalizability, as it had only verifiable cases.

The models were found to be robust in residual analysis. The prediction error, as residual values, showed a generally low value and randomly distributed predictive error values during the test cities. Although the majority of mistakes fell within an acceptable range, certain underpredictions were identified in cities near the frontlines or newly liberated territories. These can be indicators of the existence of untested psychosocial factors, including displacement or communal trauma experiences or failures in the community mental health system that were not directly represented in the set of available predictors. Areas where overpredictions were occurring were those with moderate levels of attack but low levels of reported mental health burden, which is likely attributed to local protective elements, including social cohesion or the humanitarian presence there.

For each outcome, residuals were tightly centered around zero, with narrow interquartile ranges and no evidence of skewness or heteroscedasticity. PTSD and depression showed slightly higher residual variability than other outcomes, consistent with their multifactorial etiology and greater sensitivity to unmeasured psychosocial factors. Overall, residuals exhibited only weak spatial dependence, indicating that the predictive models successfully captured most of the spatial structure in the data. In contrast, global Moran's I statistics for the raw outcomes revealed strong and significant positive spatial autocorrelation, confirming that cities with higher levels of psychological distress tended to be geographically clustered. To account for this underlying dependence, Spatial Lag Models (SAR) were estimated using queen contiguity and eight-nearest-neighbor spatial weights matrices. The inclusion of a spatially lagged component captured interdependence among neighboring cities, demonstrating that local outcomes were partially influenced by conditions in adjacent areas (Fig. S2–S3). The reduction in residual spatial autocorrelation following SAR estimation confirms that the spatially explicit framework effectively represented the underlying spatial structure and minimized residual clustering.

The test-set scatterplots (Fig. 7) were used to visualize the correlation between the predicted and observed values of the final models, thereby determining the predictive reliability of these models. In all the results, the predictions were quite close to the real values. The majority of cities were located near the diagonal line, indicating a high level of correspondence between the projected and observed prevalence of mental health. The near-perfect fit was observed between PTSD-CPTSD, loneliness, and anxiety, with the values of R2 nearing 0.99, low RMSE, and low MAPE (as small as 0.1 %). In more complex effects, such as insomnia and depression, the model continued to perform outstandingly well (R2 = 0.74–0.79), despite indicating a minor amount of dispersion in the spread of the residues, especially at high levels. This can represent other psychological or contextual indicators of trauma of displacement or social stigma that the predictor set may not entirely represent. Regardless, in such instances, the RMSE was less than 1.5 and the MAPE was less than 7.2 %, indicating that there was no significant practical deviation from actual values.

It is interesting to note that no significant model indicator bias was evident; the prediction was not systematically over- or under-estimated over the entire range of outcomes. This also supports the generalizability of the XGBoost model to cities not part of the training dataset and demonstrates its ability to recreate spatial mental health patterns with high fidelity. While these patterns align with expectations, for example, higher PTSD, insomnia, and depression in cities with intense conflict and infrastructure breakdown, the models add value by quantifying the relative importance of different predictors and capturing nonlinear interactions between environmental, infrastructural, and exposure variables. Importantly, residual analysis highlights exceptions, where psychological outcomes diverge from predicted values, pointing to unmeasured protective or aggravating contextual factors.

Discussion

The findings of this study show that the outcome of wartime psychological health is not haphazard but is systematically determined by spatially embedded environmental and infrastructural conditions. The methodology of the application of machine learning models based on trees, in particular, the algorithm XGBoost, showed that a combination of exposure to a specific type of weapons (total attacks) and disturbance of life-supporting systems, for example, heating, electricity, housing insulation, is among the most potent predictors of symptoms of trauma-related diseases in the city of Ukraine. These results are based on recent calls in conflict research for a move away from individualistic or clinical understandings of trauma to situate mental health as a phenomenon that is intricately tied to the destruction and fragmentation of place (Hassan et al., 2016; McElroy et al., 2024; Shevlin et al., 2022).

That mental distress in war zones is not merely a matter of distance to violence, but also of the environmental infrastructures that facilitate survival, is confirmed by our spatially explicit approach, which previous qualitative and epidemiological inquiries have anticipated. An example is that we discovered broken heating systems, ineffective insulation of houses, and insomnia, depression, and PTSD were strongly predicted by food insecurity. These results are in line with previous research in Syria and Yemen, which revealed that infrastructural breakdown and lack of services exacerbated psychological distress (Alhariri et al., 2021; Kakaje et al., 2021). Unlike those studies, however, our work operationalizes these associations using high-resolution predictive models and uses them on a national level with cross-validated accuracy. These findings have clear policy relevance. For example, insulation repairs, heating fuel provision, and backup energy systems could reduce not only physical hardship but also symptoms of depression and insomnia. Similarly, the role of food insecurity highlights the importance of linking psychosocial care to humanitarian aid delivery. The strong predictive value of attack exposure supports anticipatory deployment of mental health resources in areas subject to repeated bombardment. Thus, feature importance results serve as more than statistical descriptors: they offer actionable priorities for humanitarian planning.

Notably, the differential effects of weapon types on mental health outcomes, such that drone and artillery attacks were more predictive of anxiety and PTSD than other types, indicate that the modality of violence is important. Consistent with these findings, Slewa-Younan et al. (2015) and Charlson et al. (2019) found that 'unpredictable, intermittent' violence (specifically, aerial bombings or suicide attacks) leads to higher rates of acute stress reaction in comparison to prolonged sieges or conventional ground warfare (Charlson F et al., 2019; Slewa-Younan et al., 2015). The spatial distribution of mental health outcomes in our study shows similarities to findings from other conflicts, such as Iraq and Afghanistan, suggesting that certain environmental and infrastructural determinants of trauma may recur across settings. However, these parallels should not be taken as evidence of direct generalizability. Instead, our study highlights a methodological approach that may be adapted for comparative analyses in other conflict environments, provided context-specific data are incorporated (Al Juboori, 2024; Alemi et al., 2023).

One of the novel contributions of this study is that it demonstrates the use of nonlinear machine learning models to make remarkably accurate spatial forecasts of psychological distress using readily available environmental and exposure data. The predictive accuracy of XGBoost was greater than 0.99 R2 for several outcomes, which is significantly higher than what has been reported in warzone mental health research using traditional regression approaches (Nordstrand et al., 2020). While some prior work has attempted to use geostatistical tools or remote sensing in humanitarian forecasting (Gregg Greenough and Nelson, 2019; Munyaka et al., 2024)Few have applied these methods to psychological outcomes, and to our knowledge, none have done so in Ukraine at this level of resolution and scope.

This spatial predictive power is not just a statistical accomplishment, but it has profound implications for humanitarian planning and decision-making. The ability to identify cities with a high level of psychological vulnerability before clinical information is available allows mental health interventions to be anticipatory rather than reactive. However, operational constraints often prevent the timely deployment of services. Predictive spatial models, such as the one presented here, can fill this gap by providing a framework for forecast-based financing of psychological support, similar to early warning systems for cholera or malnutrition (Coughlan De Perez et al., 2015; Wagner & Jaime, 2020).

Nonetheless, the study also underscores the limitations of even the most sophisticated models when key psychosocial variables are absent. While environmental and infrastructural variables explain much of the spatial variation, residual mapping reveals that some cities, especially those recently re-liberated or close to frontlines, show larger-than-expected prediction errors. This suggests the presence of unmeasured factors such as displacement histories, social fragmentation, or collective grief events, which are not captured in our dataset. Previous studies in post-genocide Rwanda and post-conflict Sierra Leone have shown that social cohesion and collective memory can either buffer or exacerbate trauma outcomes. (Bigabo et al., 2025; Exenberger et al., 2022), pointing to future directions for integrating community-level qualitative data into quantitative spatial models.

Ethically, the exclusion of Russian-occupied regions, whilst methodologically justified, does limit the comprehensiveness of national predictions. These are also where trauma can be the most intense and the least reported. However, using it without validated ground data would pose a threat to the integrity of spatial forecasts and may lead to misleading results. This trade-off illustrates a wider dilemma in wartime data science between comprehensiveness and verifiability, one that has also been highlighted by humanitarian geographers working in Syria, Myanmar, and Ethiopia (Abay et al., 2022; Audi et al., 2022; Haar & Sirkin, 2022).

Conclusion

This study revealed that the psychological distress of Ukraine during the current war is not distributed at random but has high predictive power from city-level environmental hardship, infrastructural destruction, and the types of violence to which they are exposed. We used nonlinear machine learning models, specifically XGBoost, and were able to predict a high predictive accuracy in six mental health outcomes, including PTSD, insomnia, and depression. The research emphasizes that trauma in times of war is not just a clinical phenomenon, but a spatial artifact, resulting from the material conditions of survival in everyday life, as well as the topography of destruction.

Providing a high density of psychological vulnerabilities in a spatially explicit form and accurately forecasting the regions likely to be the most mentally health burdened, provides a powerful tool for anticipatory humanitarian response. It would allow mental health resources to be allocated not only based on reactive assessments of need but also using predictive information and geospatial intelligence. This study demonstrates how GeoAI can be utilized to characterize the synergistic effects of violence, deprivation, and environmental stress in a conflict setting. While grounded in the Ukrainian case, the methodological framework has the potential to be adapted for comparative studies in other contexts, with careful attention to local conditions and data availability. In its broadest sense, the study accepts the argument that the distribution of mental health in conflict areas is fundamentally spatially concentrated. Suffering the loss of heating facilities, the living conditions, power infrastructure, and multiple traumatic events cannot be understood without a complete understanding of psychological resilience. By quantifying these spatial patterns and developing models to represent them, we are taking a step closer to building a scientific base for spatial equity in humanitarian care. Furthermore, longitudinal data can be used to capture changes over time, and causal modeling can be used in conjunction with predictive methods to extend the analysis to include indicators of governance, cohesion, and displacement originally at the community level. As data on conflict becomes more accessible and finer-grained, there is more space for a change in the way mental health vulnerability is conceptualized, understood, and responded to in conflict-affected societies. This study is based on city-level data, but we emphasize that future research with neighborhood-level or gridded data would allow for more precise differentiation.
