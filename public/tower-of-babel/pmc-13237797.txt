Patient-specific implants for joint-sparing reconstruction of segmental tibial and femoral defects
Authors: Mirka L Buist, Frank F A IJpma, Suzanne van Wees, Anne M L Meesters, Nick Assink, Paul C Jutte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsSegmental bone defects of the femur and tibia present a major challenge for reconstructive surgery, especially in joint-sparing oncological and post-traumatic cases. Patient-specific implants (PSIs), enabled by 3D imaging and additive manufacturing, offer tailored solutions. This review aimed to systematically evaluate clinical applications of PSIs for femoral and tibial shaft reconstruction in terms of mechanical survival rate, and introduce a classification framework to guide implant design and fixation, as well as communication.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed and Embase identified studies reporting joint-sparing reconstructions using PSIs for segmental femoral or tibial defects. Data were extracted on implant design, fixation method, material, biological augmentation, and clinical outcomes. A novel classification system was developed to categorize implants by defect location (unicortical, diaphyseal, meta-extended) and fixation type (screws, plates, nails, stems, hybrid). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to assess five-year implant survival across subgroups.ResultsA total of 53 studies involving 299 patients were included. Titanium was the predominant material, often featuring lattice structures to support bone ingrowth or grafting. Fixation methods varied, with hybrid fixation showing the highest five-year implant survival rate (97%), followed by stems (95%) and plate- or nail-only fixation (each 90%). Most implants remained failure-free at final follow-up; structural failure was the most common complication. The classification system enabled structured comparison across implant types. A notable concentration of studies (27/53) originated from China, suggesting regional leadership in PSI innovation, potentially linked to regulatory flexibility compared to the European Union.ConclusionThis review provides a structured overview of PSI design and fixation, and outcomes for joint-sparing femoral and tibial reconstruction. The proposed classification framework facilitates standardized reporting and interdisciplinary communication. Introduction

Defects in the diaphysis and metaphysis of long bones, whether caused by trauma, infection, tumour resection, or congenital conditions, pose a considerable clinical challenge.1 Traditional reconstructive approaches, such as bone grafting, modular prostheses, or the Masquelet technique, often face limitations in restoring mechanical function, anatomical alignment, or long-term durability, particularly in large or irregular defects.2,3 Recent advancements in medical imaging and 3D printing technologies have enabled the development of patient-specific implants (PSIs), which offer alternatives for personalized and anatomically precise reconstruction.4 These custom-designed solutions allow for the precise restoration of skeletal geometry, potentially improving implant integration, load transfer, and functional outcomes. Despite the recent technological advances, it remains unclear which reconstruction options are currently applied in clinical practice or how successful they are, as existing reports are highly diverse and often difficult to compare.

Although the use of PSIs in diaphyseal reconstructions is gaining more attention, the field remains in an early and highly exploratory phase.5 Clinical implementations are often reported as case reports or small series, with wide variability in implant design, material selection, surgical technique, and outcome reporting. The uptake of PSIs for segmental defects remains slow, and most data derive from single cases or small cohorts, emphasizing the need for standardization and larger, multicentre studies. This is similar to what Benady et al6 observed for 3D-printed scaffolds. There is currently no consensus on design principles or standardized documentation. This makes it difficult to compare outcomes across studies or draw broader conclusions about efficacy and safety. This challenge is especially relevant for defects in weightbearing bones such as the tibia and femur, where mechanical strength and long-term durability are critical for successful outcomes. This lack of standardization also complicates regulatory evaluation and hinders the development of clear legislative pathways. In Europe, the introduction of the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) has further stalled this growing momentum for PSIs by adding complexity to the approval process of medical devices.7 In addition, the terminology used to describe reconstructions that preserve native joint surfaces varies widely across the literature. Terms such as shaft, intercalary, dia- and metaphyseal, segmental bone defects, and joint-sparing are often used interchangeably, despite subtle anatomical distinctions. In this review, we considered all such reconstructions as joint-sparing, provided the native joint surface was preserved, regardless of the specific terminology used in the original publication. Together, these factors highlight the need for a comprehensive review of the current landscape to identify trends, knowledge gaps, and opportunities for greater consistency in clinical practice and research.

The objective of this literature review is to systematically identify and evaluate all reported clinical implementations of PSIs used for the reconstruction of shaft defects of long bones in the lower limb. With this review, we aim to address the following research questions: 1) In patients treated with a PSI for the reconstruction of a segmental bone defect in the tibia or femur, what design characteristics of the implant have been reported?; and 2) Which type of PSI for the reconstruction of a segmental bone defect in the tibia or femur has the most favourable clinical outcomes? This review aims to support future clinical decision-making. It promotes the use of a common terminology among clinicians and researchers. Additionally, it guides the development of a standardized framework for their design, implementation, and evaluation, by providing a structured overview of the available evidence and introducing a classification system for patient-specific orthopaedic implants.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines where applicable.8 Given the exploratory nature of this topic and the predominance of case reports and small series, a formal quality assessment and risk of bias analysis were not performed. This review was registered in the PROSPERO database.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search to identify studies reporting clinical implementations of PSIs for joint-sparing defects of long bones was performed. Two electronic databases, PubMed and Embase, were used for the literature search. The search terms were developed in collaboration with a medical librarian to ensure their accuracy and relevance. In literature, a wide range of terms is used to describe reconstructions that preserve the native joint surface, including shaft, intercalary, dia- and metaphyseal, segmental bone defect, and joint-sparing. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we included all these terms in our search strategy. The full list of search terms is provided in Supplementary Table i. All studies published between 1 January 2000 and 4 December 2025 were included.

In addition to the database search, we identified additional relevant studies (n = 9) through manual search of reference lists, expert recommendations, and relevant review articles. These were also screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study selection

The initial search results were imported into the reference management tool Rayyan (USA),9 and duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers (MLB and NA for the studies until 2024, and MLB and SW for the studies from 2025) screened the abstracts for eligibility, and conflicts were resolved by discussions between the two reviewers. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were retrieved and assessed for final inclusion based on the established criteria.

Exclusion criteria were: ex vivo or in vitro studies; animal studies; total joint replacement or defects through the joint surface; implants other than those used for intercalary defect reconstruction of tibia or femur; reviews, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses; modular prosthesis, without a custom part; custom prosthesis including a joint surface; and languages other than English, Dutch, French, or German.

Data were extracted from the included studies using a pre-designed extraction form. An overview of the sections included in the extraction form is included in Supplementary Table ii.

Data extraction and categorization

To categorize implant failures, we applied the Henderson classification,10 originally developed for endoprosthetic reconstructions in limb salvage surgery. While some aspects do not directly translate to PSIs for long bone defects, the overall framework remains relevant. Soft-tissue failures (Type 1) were considered applicable primarily when instability or wound dehiscence was reported, although joint-related issues were less relevant. Aseptic loosening (Type 2) was directly applied to cases of implant loosening. Structural failures (Type 3) included implant fractures and mechanical wear, as well as periprosthetic fractures if directly related to the implant. Infection-related failures (Type 4) were classified based on timing as described in the original classification. Since many included studies focus on tumour reconstructions, recurrence of disease (Type 5) remained relevant, particularly local recurrence. We applied the Henderson classification only to studies with a minimum follow-up of one year. For studies with a paediatric population, we also classified paediatric failure from the modified Henderson classification (Type 6) for failure due to growth arrest resulting in bone deformity or joint dysplasia.11 Implant survival was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis, following the approach described by Cabas-Geat et al.12 Because the aim of this analysis was to assess mechanical integrity, only structural failures (Henderson Type 3) were considered in the Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Results

The initial search yielded 1,385 records, 538 from PubMed and 847 from Embase. In addition, nine relevant articles were identified through other sources. After removing 319 duplicates, 1,066 unique records were screened by title and abstract. A total of 65 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 53 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. When a study included additional patients outside this scope, only the relevant subset was extracted and analyzed. In studies like Zhang et al,62 which mentioned six cases involving ‘limb bones’, only one case clearly discussed a femur. We included this case and excluded the other five. Similarly, in Tsuda et al,54 only one case was clearly identified as involving a joint-sparing prosthesis in the tibia or femur. We included only this case for analysis. The two articles from Tetsworth et al,52,53 two articles from Lodewijks et al,41,42 and two articles from Benady et al15,16 had an overlap in the reported patients, for the overlapping patients (two for Tetsworth et al, three for Lodewijks et al, and one for Benady et al). To avoid duplication, we only included these patients once, using data from the most recent publication, allowing for the longest follow-up. In total, 317 of the 488 reported patients met our inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Not all studies provided information on patient sex; however, based on the 46 articles that did, approximately 66% of patients were male and 34% were female. All but one article was published after 2018, with a peak in publications in 2022. The majority of reconstructions were performed to treat defects arising from tumour resection (26), traumatic injuries (seven), osteomyelitis (five), or other non-specified bone defects. Follow-up durations, when reported, varied considerably, ranging from no follow-up after the surgical intervention to over ten years. For some studies, such as Liu et al,40 Shehadeh et al,50 and Xu et al,60 it was unclear which patients had longer follow-ups. Therefore, for the analysis of the implant survival rate, we included data only up to the point where all patients were followed.

Classification of implants

The 53 included studies used a variety of PSI designs. We developed a classification system to systematically describe and compare implant designs across the included studies. This classification system is based on the location and extent of the bone resection (Type 1 to 3) and the fixation method used (Subtype A to E). This framework provides a consistent way to analyze the surgical and mechanical strategies reported in the literature. The full system is presented in Table II and visualized in Figure 3. Outcomes and complications

Outcome measures across the studies varied considerably. Some studies reported only complications, while others included quantitative measures such as the Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS)66 or the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS)67 scores. Of the studies that reported functional outcomes, many showed promising results, with LEFS scores increasing from around 30 preoperative to around 50 postoperative, and MSTS scores reaching as high as 90% to 100%. We applied the Henderson classification to categorize implant failure based on the reported complications. Only studies with a follow-up time of at least one year were included in this classification. Of the total 319 included patients, 299 had a follow-up of at least one year and were thus included for further analysis. Out of these 299 cases, 249 had no failure. Henderson Type 3, structural failures, were the most common with 20 cases. Infection was the second most common type of failure, with 14 instances of Type 4, followed by ten instances of aseptic loosening (Type 2). There were also eight cases of tumour reoccurrence (Type 5) and one case of soft-tissue failure (Type 1). In the studies with minors, no paediatric failure (Type 6) was reported. Some patients in the articles of Shehadeh et al50 and Lodewijks et al41,42 had multiple types of failure, causing the sum of all classified failure types to be more than the total number of analyzed patients. Discussion

Advancements in 3D imaging, design software, and manufacturing have created new possibilities for reconstructing segmental bone defects in the femur and tibia. However, it remains unclear which of these reconstruction options are actually in clinical use, as published reports are highly diverse and difficult to compare. Most publications consist of single case reports or small series, with heterogeneous reporting of implant design, materials, outcome measures, and follow-up duration. This heterogeneity makes it hard to compare studies. To address this gap, we aimed to identify the design characteristics of the reported implants, and to identify which types have the most favourable clinical outcomes. We did this by introducing a classification system for PSIs used in reconstruction of segmental bone defects in the femoral and tibial diaphysis and metaphysis. This classification categorizes PSIs according to their location and fixation method, and analyzes the reported clinical outcomes per category. Implant-survival rates differed between fixation types, with plate-only fixation showing the lowest survival, nails and stems performing similarly, and hybrid solutions performing best. This fixation-based classification system brings clarity to a heterogeneous body of literature and provides a framework for sharing knowledge between clinicians and engineers, while also enabling more meaningful comparisons in future studies.

Implant design characteristics

This review mapped the current design landscape of PSIs for femoral and tibial shaft reconstruction, aiming to identify recurring approaches and potential patterns that could guide best practices. Across the 53 studies, there was a large variation in design characteristics. Fixation methods included screws, plates, intramedullary (IM) nails, cemented and uncemented IM stems, and various combinations of these. Plate fixation was the most common method, used in 18 studies. This was closely followed by IM nails (17 studies), combinations (17 studies), and IM stems (14 studies). Titanium, either solid or with porous structure, was the predominant material, used in 42 studies. Other materials included tantalum, PEEK, and the polymers mPCL-TCP and PCL-HA. Direct comparison between studies was challenging given this diversity of the studies. Many reports were single cases or small series, and terminology used to describe implant location and extent of reconstruction was inconsistent. To address this gap, we developed a classification system for patient-specific reconstruction of segmental bone defects of the femur and tibia. This classification system divides implant design by the location – Type 1 for unicortical defects, Type 2 for diaphyseal defects, and Type 3 for defects extending beyond the diaphysis – and fixation method – A for screws, B for plates, C for IM nails, D for built-in stems, and E for hybrid solutions. This provided a systematic and logical structure to categorize implant types. Our review adds to the current literature by providing one of the first systematic mappings of PSI design characteristics for tibial and femoral shaft reconstructions. The introduced classification system allows standardized reporting and enabled us to make a comparison of the outcome assessments. Beyond that, it can facilitate a shared language between surgeons and engineers, and aid surgeons to select the right option for the individual patient.

Clinical outcomes

Functional outcomes were generally positive, with most reporting meaningful improvements in patient mobility and pain relief. However, direct comparison of functional outcomes was hampered by the use of diverse outcome measures – such as MSTS, LEFS, and several other scoring systems – and inconsistent follow-up durations. To standardize implant failure reporting, we applied the Henderson classification, which revealed that most PSIs (249 of 299) remained failure-free at last follow-up. When failures did occur, structural failures were the most common type (20 cases), followed by infection (14 cases), aseptic loosening (ten cases), and tumour reoccurrence (eight cases). We generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves, using our newly developed implant classification system. These curves demonstrated variation between fixation methods: plate-only and IM nail fixation showed the lowest implant survival rate of both about 90%, IM stems showed a performance of about 95%, and hybrid solutions had the highest implant survival rate of around 97%. Implants with a combination of an internal fixation system (stem or nail) and a plate seem to have the lowest risk of failure. This is probably attributable to these being secured for both internal and rotational stability. Implant stability is essential for long-term implant survival, as insufficient mechanical support can lead to micromotion, impaired osseointegration, and eventual failure.68 When comparing these results to published outcomes for tumour endoprosthesis, PSIs in our review performed at least comparably – and in many cases even better – than published mechanical survival rates for tumour endoprostheses. For example, a literature review from Theil et al69 found the following structural failure (Henderson Type 3) rates for conventional megaprosthetic reconstructions: proximal femoral arthroplasties range between 0% and 3% in larger series, but up to 19% in other reports; distal femoral arthroplasties range from 0% to 37%; proximal tibial arthroplasties range from 8.6% to 19%; total femoral arthroplasties range from around 8.6% to 9%; and combined femoral and tibial arthroplasties are estimated at a structural failure rate of 23.1%. In contrast, the structural failure we estimated for the PSIs is below 10% across all types, with hybrid fixation types as low as 3%, suggesting that patient-specific designs may offer mechanical reliability comparable to or better than traditional solutions. Moreover, segmental reconstructions offer the advantage of joint preservation, thereby avoiding common issues of total joint replacements such as wear, instability, and the eventual need for revision arthroplasty. Our review adds to the existing literature by providing one of the first structured comparison of PSI mechanical survival across fixation types, benchmarked against established tumour endoprosthesis data, and by demonstrating how a standardized classification framework can enable meaningful cross-study analyses despite heterogeneous outcome reporting.

Clinical application and future perspectives

To apply PSIs in a hospital setting, a multidisciplinary team is essential. In addition to surgeons experienced in oncological tumour resections, the team should include technical physicians and engineers. Together, they can create a precise 3D model of the segmental bone defect and translate it into a PSI design. Manufacturing can be performed by companies skilled in titanium CNC milling or 3D printing, depending on the implant design. Regulatory requirements vary by jurisdiction but generally involve demonstrating compliance with medical device safety standards – for example, following the MDR pathway for custom-made devices in Europe70 or FDA regulations in the USA.71 Notably, the majority of innovation in this field has come from China, where the possible absence of recently introduced restrictive regulations – such as the MDR in Europe – has allowed for continued clinical development and faster translation into practice. For joint-sparing reconstructions, conventional off-the-shelf implants are rarely suitable; alternatives such as massive allografts have historically shown high complication and failure rates. PSIs therefore fill an important niche, offering individualized solutions where standard implants are not an option. Reduced operating time, better implant fit, and potentially fewer revisions may help offset the additional initial investments in time and effort due to individualized design, fabrication, and regulatory clearance. Looking ahead, advances in preoperative virtual simulation, implant testing, and optimization of the 3D workflow may reduce both the regulatory burden and development time, making PSIs an increasingly attractive alternative to standard implants and allograft reconstructions.

While this review has provided an overview of the implant characteristics and outcomes, several limitations must be acknowledged. The classification system introduced in this review has not been validated. To our knowledge, no validated classification currently exists for this purpose; therefore, we created a conceptual framework, however validating the system was beyond the scope of this review. The risk of publication bias is a substantial concern, as only successful cases tend to be published. This may contribute to an overestimation of the effectiveness of these designs, especially considering that the observed failure rates were relatively low despite the complexity of the reconstructions. Unfortunately, due to the small sample size of patients in the included publications, it was impossible to do a meta-analysis and assess the risk of bias. Another limitation is that the included studies report little information on the costs and required infrastructure to realize PSIs. The implementation of PSIs is so far mostly done in a research setting, where costs are covered by funding sources. Therefore, it is difficult to draw conclusions on whether PSIs may be a cost-effective solution. In addition, most included studies originated from a limited number of geographical regions, which restricts the generalizability of the reported outcomes. Furthermore, the lack of standardized reporting in terms of patient populations, implant specifications, and outcome measures underscores the need for uniformity of reporting in future clinical studies.

In conclusion, this review systematically mapped the design characteristics and clinical outcomes of PSIs for the reconstruction of segmental bone defects of the femoral and tibial diaphysis and metaphysis. We introduced a classification system based on implant location and fixation method, to bring clarity to the heterogeneous literature and enable standardized comparison across studies, while also revealing differences in mechanical survival between fixation types. Notably, the majority of research in this field (27 of 53 studies) originated from China, suggesting their emergence as an innovation leader in PSI development, potentially driven by more favourable regulatory environments compared to regions like the EU where frameworks like the MDR may be constraining advancement. The most commonly reported design characteristics include titanium as the primary material, lattice structures to promote bone ingrowth or accommodate bone grafts, and fixation via plates, nails, stems, or hybrid combinations. Hybrid fixation methods – combining internal and rotational stability – demonstrated the highest mechanical implant survival rate (97% after five years). PSIs appear non-inferior to off-the-shelf implants in terms of mechanical survival, while offering the added benefit of joint preservation.
