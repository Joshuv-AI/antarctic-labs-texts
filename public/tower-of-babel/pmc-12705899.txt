Efficacy of Platelet Rich Plasma Versus Placebo for Improvement in Olfactory Function in Adults With Olfactory Dysfunction: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Ali B. Jafar, Ghabrielle Almeida, Fatima Al Mousawi, Eric Delgado Rendon, Amber U. Luong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOlfactory dysfunction significantly impairs quality of life. Conventional treatments such as corticosteroids and olfactory training offer limited and inconsistent efficacy. Platelet‐rich plasma (PRP), a biologically active agent, has shown potential regenerative effects in olfactory neuroepithelium. This study systematically evaluates the efficacy of PRP compared to placebo in improving olfactory function.MethodsA systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD 420251045079). The databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Central, and Web of Science. Seven studies (four RCTs, three non‐RCTs) involving 789 patients were included. Subjective and objective outcomes were extracted and pooled using a random‐effects model. Risk of bias was assessed using ROB‐2 and ROBIN‐I tools.ResultsPRP was significantly more effective than placebo in improving subjective olfactory scores at 1 month (SMD = 1.16, 95% CI [0.29–2.03], p = 0.009), 3 months (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI [0.07–0.50], p = 0.01), and 12 months (SMD = 1.47, 95% CI [1.06–1.89], p = 0.00001). Objective testing also favored PRP at all timepoints, including 1 month (SMD = 1.55, 95% CI [0.36–2.73], p = 0.01), 3 months (SMD = 0.44, 95% CI [0.05–0.82], p = 0.03), and 12 months (SMD = 2.42, 95% CI [0.35–4.5], p = 0.02). Subgroup analysis showed younger age (< 40 years old) was associated with greater improvements. The safety profile was favorable with no serious adverse events reported.ConclusionPRP demonstrates both short‐ and long‐term benefits in olfactory recovery compared to placebo, especially among younger adults. Given the promising results and safety profile, PRP represents a viable treatment option for olfactory dysfunction. Standardization of PRP protocols and further high‐quality RCTs need to be further explored.

Introduction

Smell disorders affect a substantial portion of the general population, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 22.2% to 24.5% across different studies [1, 2, 3]. Common etiologies of olfactory dysfunction include sinonasal disease, viral infection, head trauma, and drug toxicity; however, age, sex, and race also substantially influence its incidence, with higher incidence observed in older individuals, males, and black individuals [2, 3, 4]. Though sometimes overlooked, olfactory impairment can make a significant negative impact on quality of life. Studies have found that olfactory loss is strongly associated with psychological distress and can also impair food enjoyment, the detection of spoiled food and hazardous smoke, and even social interactions [5, 6, 7]. It has also been identified as an independent predictor of five‐year mortality [8, 9, 10].

The rate of spontaneous recovery is highly inconsistent, and many patients struggle with long‐term disorders such as anosmia, hyposmia or parosmia particularly in the setting of COVID‐19 infection [11, 12]. Therapies for olfactory dysfunction vary depending on the underlying cause. For post‐viral or inflammation‐induced cases, first‐line treatment typically involves intranasal corticosteroids or saline irrigation, sometimes accompanied by olfactory training. Although the literature generally supports the use of topical steroids and olfactory training, clinical outcomes may vary. These differences can be attributed to patient comorbidities, challenges with treatment adherence, and the possibility that improvements may be only temporary [13, 14, 15].

PRP is an autologous biological product derived from whole blood, and though it is characterized by high platelet concentration, it also contains an abundance of growth factors, leukocytes, fibrin, and other bioactive factors [16, 17]. Studies have found PRP to exhibit regenerative properties in a wide variety of clinical contexts, and its potential in the treatment of long‐standing olfactory dysfunction was first explored in a 2020 pilot study, with subsequent studies focusing on the efficacy of its application [17, 18]. This study aims to compile and evaluate existing research on the efficacy of PRP in treating olfactory dysfunction, particularly olfactory loss, to draw conclusions about its effectiveness and inform future research and applications.

Methods

Study Protocol

This pooled analysis was based on a pre‐specified protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD 420251045079). This study was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook guidelines for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses, as well as the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) statement when this study was prepared. Since this research relied on published data, institutional ethical approval was not needed.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria for our study were established using the PICO strategy (Patients, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design) and are outlined as follows: (i) participants: adults > 18 years old diagnosed with olfactory dysfunction (ii) Intervention: platelet rich plasma (iii) Comparison: control (iv) Outcomes: subjective and objective olfactory evaluation (v) study design: randomized controlled trials, non‐randomized controlled trials, and cohort studies.

Conversely, the exclusion criteria encompassed non‐adults, non‐English full texts, and studies deficient in outcomes of interest. Additionally, study types other than RCTs such as case reports, observational studies, review articles, and letters were excluded.

Study Selection and Search Strategy

We performed a bibliographic search using the following databases: PubMed, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, which were systematically screened from inception until May 2025. We used the following search strategy for our systematic search: PRP OR “Platelet rich plasma” OR “Platelet‐rich plasma” OR “growth factors” AND “olfactory dysfunction” OR “anosmia” OR “hyposmia” OR “Parosmia.”

The exact literature search reported in Table 1. Moreover, for thorough screening and to minimize the potential of overlooking pertinent articles, we rigorously reviewed the references of all enumerated studies, as well as Clinical‐trials.gov, the World Health Organization (WHO) Clinical Trials register and ResearchGate. Two co‐authors independently performed the screening process, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with the most senior author.

Data Extraction and Outcome Measurement

Using a data extraction sheet that has been previously established in accordance with our protocol for outcome extraction, we retrieved data from the selected studies. With regard to baseline characteristics of the included studies, we extracted data pertaining to the first author's name, publication year, study design, sample size of participants, country, patients' age, and study arms. Also, we extracted data specific to each study arm, mean age (years), number of participants according to gender (male and female). Finally, we extracted data pertaining to our outcomes of interest. We recorded both subjective (visual analog scale (VAS), olfactory improvement scale, QOD‐NS, ODS mean), objective (Sniffin stick test, CCCRC test, UPSIT, BSIT, SCENTinel) outcomes in terms of data collection pertaining to 1, 3, 12 months.

Quality Assessment of the Included Studies

The Cochrane tool (RoB‐Version 2) was used to assess the quality of each RCT article, and the ROBIN‐I tool was used to assess quality for non‐randomized cohort or case control studies. In the ROB‐2 tool, low risk, some concerns and high risk of bias scores were assigned to each assessed domain, followed by a corresponding score for the overall quality of the study. Each assessed domain was given a bias risk score, which ranged from low to some concern to high, and then a commensurate level for the total quality of the study. Two co‐authors did the quality evaluation separately; conflicts were resolved by discussion with the senior author.

Meta‐Analysis

The Cochrane Collaboration's Review Manager software (RevMan, version 5.4 for Mac) was used to conduct a statistical analysis. We collected dichotomous data as risk ratio (RR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and used the Mantel–Haenszel method to analyze it. We collected the continuous data as standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% CI and used the Inverse‐Variance method to analyze it. We depended on the random‐effects model for the overall analysis. Results assessed using the mean difference effect size were for outcomes measured in the same manner, whereas outcomes measured differently were appraised using the SMD effect size. When all studies used the same instrument for example UPSIT to evaluate olfactory function, we calculated the Mean Difference (MD) to represent the absolute difference in mean post‐treatment scores between PRP and control groups. However, when studies used different olfactory assessment tools (e.g., UPSIT, Sniffin Sticks (TDI), CCCRC tests etc.) we used the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) to enable pooling of results across heterogeneous scales. The SMD expresses the mean difference between intervention and control groups in Standard Deviation (SD) units, calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled SD of the outcome measure. This approach standardizes scores to a common metric and allows comparison across instruments with differing numerical ranges. The level of heterogeneity was determined using the chi‐square and I 2 tests, which involved observing the graphs on the forest plots. The chi‐squared test with p < 0.1 and I 2 > 50% was used to indicate significant heterogeneity. Statistical significance was determined as p < 0.05.

Results

Summary of the Literature Search

Digital database searches identified 86 citations, of which 12 citations were omitted due to duplications. The remaining 74 citations underwent further title and abstract screening. Only 4 RCTs and 3 non‐RCTs (cohort and/or case‐control studies) met the inclusion criteria. The PRISMA flowchart is presented for the detailed screening process and study selection is depicted in Figure 1.

PRISMA flowchart summarizing study selection. Of 86 records identified, 7 studies (4 randomized controlled trials and 3 non‐randomized trials) met inclusion criteria and were included in the meta‐analysis.

Summary of the Included Studies

A total of 4 RCTs and 3 non‐randomized trials with a total of 789 patients were included. Of these, 559 were allocated to the PRP arm, and 230 to the control arm. Three studies were conducted in the USA, two were conducted in Belgium, one in Egypt and one in Turkey. A summary of the included studies' demographics and baseline characteristics is shown in Table 1.

Summary of the Quality Assessment

Overall, 4 RCTs and 3 non‐RCTs were evaluated. All randomized controlled trials were assessed using the ROB‐2 tool. Non‐randomized studies were evaluated using the ROBINS‐I tool. Results for both assessments are available in Figures 2a,b and 3. Risk of bias was systematically assessed using the Cochrane ROB‐2 tool for randomized controlled trials and the ROBINS‐I tool for non‐randomized studies. Among the four included RCTs, two were rated as low risk of bias, while two showed “some concerns,” primarily related to allocation concealment and blinding of outcome assessors. All three non‐randomized studies were rated as having moderate to serious risk of bias, most notably due to confounding, lack of pre‐specified analysis plans, and non‐blinded outcome assessments. Despite these limitations, all studies clearly reported inclusion criteria and used validated measures of olfactory function. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved by consensus, and visual summaries of the assessments are provided in Figures 2a,b and 3. Overall, while some methodological concerns exist, the included studies offer a reasonable level of internal validity to support the study's conclusions.

Meta‐Analysis of the Endpoints

Subjective Olfactory Improvements

Our meta‐analysis evaluating subjective improvement in olfactory dysfunction 1 month following intervention showed that PRP was associated with significantly greater improvement compared to control (SMD = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.29 to 2.03, p = 0.009) as outlined in Figure 4. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I 2 = 81%, p = 0.005). While two studies (Abo El Naga 2022 and Steffens 2022) demonstrated large and statistically significant effects favoring PRP, one study (Yan 2022) did not show a significant difference.

Forest plot showing subjective olfactory improvement at 1 month following treatment. PRP was associated with significantly greater improvement compared to control (SMD = 1.16; 95% CI, 0.29–2.03; p = 0.009).

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis based on mean age was conducted using a cut‐off of 40 years, with results outlined in Figure 5. Among participants with a mean age < 40 years, PRP was associated with a significant improvement in olfactory function compared to control (SMD = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.18 to 2.02, p < 0.00001), with no observed heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%). In contrast, no significant effect was observed in participants with a mean age > 40 years (SMD = 0.17, 95% CI: −0.61 to 0.94, p = 0.67). The difference between subgroups was statistically significant (χ 2 = 10.17, p = 0.001). These findings indicate that younger individuals may experience greater subjective improvement in olfaction following PRP therapy.

Subgroup analysis of subjective olfactory improvement at 1 month by age. PRP showed significant benefit in participants <40 years (SMD = 1.60; 95% CI, 1.18–2.02; p < 0.00001) but not in those >40 years (SMD = 0.17; 95% CI, −0.61–0.94; p = 0.67).

Although subgroup analysis based on the duration of olfactory dysfunction was considered, it could not be performed as only two of the three included studies reported sufficient data on symptom duration.

At 3 months from baseline, an analysis of two studies demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in subjective olfactory function among participants receiving PRP compared to controls (SMD = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.50; p = 0.01), with no observed heterogeneity between studies (I 2 = 0%) as shown in Figure 6.

Forest plot of subjective olfactory improvement at 3 months. PRP significantly improved olfactory function compared with control (SMD = 0.29; 95% CI, 0.07–0.50; p = 0.01) with no heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%).

At twelve months, data from two additional studies revealed a substantially greater effect in favor of PRP (SMD = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.89; p < 0.00001), with no heterogeneity observed (I 2 = 0%), as outlined in Figure 7. These findings suggest that PRP may confer long‐term benefits in subjective olfactory recovery.

Forest plot of subjective olfactory improvement at 12 months. PRP showed a markedly greater effect than control (SMD = 1.47; 95% CI, 1.06–1.89; p < 0.00001) with no heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%).

Objective Olfactory Improvements

Six studies evaluated the objective changes in olfactory function between the PRP and control groups. Three of these studies (Steffens 2022, Yan 2022, and Lechien 2025) measured changes using the composite TDI score. Among the remaining three, Evman 2023 utilized the CCCRC test to assess both threshold and identification. Duffy 2023 employed the BSIT and SCENTinel scores to evaluate odor identification and intensity, while Fieux 2024 assessed odor identification alone with UPSIT.

Our meta‐analysis of three studies examined objective changes in olfactory function at 1 month following PRP treatment compared to control, with results indicating a statistically significant improvement in olfactory performance favoring PRP (SMD = 1.55, 95% CI: 0.36 to 2.73, p = 0.01). However, the analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity among studies (I 2 = 84%, p = 0.002), suggesting variability in effect sizes. Notably, while Steffens and Evman reported large and statistically significant effects (SMDs = 1.10 and 3.25, respectively), Yan 2022 reported a smaller, non‐significant effect (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI: −0.15 to 1.44). See Figure 8 below.

Forest plot of objective olfactory improvement at 1 month. PRP produced significantly greater improvement than control (SMD = 1.55; 95% CI, 0.36–2.73; p = 0.01).

A subgroup analysis stratified by age was conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity in the observed effects of PRP on objective olfactory improvement at 1 month, as seen in Figure 9. Among participants with a mean age under 40 years (Steffens 2022 and Evman 2023), PRP treatment resulted in a marginally significant improvement in olfactory function compared to controls (SMD = 2.10, 95% CI: 0.00 to 4.20, p = 0.05). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed within this subgroup (I 2 = 89%, p = 0.002). In contrast, for participants with a mean age over 40 years (Yan 2022), the effect of PRP was smaller and not statistically significant (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI: −0.15 to 1.44, p = 0.11). The test for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.20), although the overall trend suggests that younger individuals may derive greater benefit from PRP therapy. These findings indicate a potential age‐related difference in PRP responsiveness, which may partially explain the high heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis.

Subgroup analysis of objective olfactory improvement at 1 month by age. PRP showed a marginally significant effect in participants <40 years (SMD = 2.10; 95% CI, 0.00–4.20; p = 0.05) but not in those >40 years (SMD = 0.64; 95% CI, −0.15–1.44; p = 0.11).

At 3 months post‐treatment, an analysis of two studies revealed that patients receiving PRP had a statistically significant improvement in objective olfactory scores compared to controls (SMD = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.82; p = 0.03), with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 28%). See Figure 10 below.

Forest plot of objective olfactory improvement at 3 months. PRP significantly improved olfactory scores compared with control (SMD = 0.44; 95% CI, 0.05–0.82; p = 0.03) with low heterogeneity (I 2 = 28%).

At 12 months, the observed effect was more pronounced, with a pooled SMD of 2.42 (95% CI: 0.35 to 4.50; p = 0.02), although substantial heterogeneity was present (I 2 = 94%). These findings suggest that PRP may confer both short‐ and long‐term benefits in olfactory recovery. See Figure 11 below.

Discussion

This meta‐analysis confirms that PRP results in a statistically significant improvement in olfactory function in patients with persistent olfactory dysfunction compared to control interventions. All included studies enrolled adult patients with olfactory loss lasting more than 6 months who had failed conventional therapies, including intranasal corticosteroids, systemic steroids, and olfactory retraining. Improvements were observed across both subjective and objective measures of olfactory function, with benefits evident as early as 1 month post‐treatment and persisting up to 12 months.

Interestingly, the pooled effect size observed at 3 months was smaller than that at 1 and 12 months. This apparent dip should be interpreted with caution, as only two studies contributed data to the 3‐month analysis, resulting in reduced precision and statistical power. Moreover, these studies varied in patient characteristics and the olfactory assessment tools used, which may have influenced comparability and diluted the pooled estimate. A transient plateau in improvement may also reflect the biological time course of PRP, where early trophic and anti‐inflammatory effects are followed by gradual neuroregenerative changes that become more pronounced at longer follow‐up.

These findings add to the evolving therapeutic landscape for olfactory dysfunction, a condition with limited effective treatments. While corticosteroids and olfactory training remain the mainstays of therapy, results vary considerably across patients and etiologies [13, 14]. Although alternative agents such as theophylline have been explored, they lack compelling evidence to support routine use [19]. PRP, by contrast, represents a biologically plausible and promising intervention. It has demonstrated regenerative properties in otolaryngology settings such as vocal fold healing, tympanic membrane repair, and post‐surgical mucosal recovery, supporting its potential application in olfactory neuroepithelium [20, 21, 22].

PRP is an autologous biologic concentrate rich in platelets and key growth factors such as PDGF, TGF‐β, and VEGF [16, 17]. These factors likely modulate local inflammatory responses and support nerve regeneration. In the context of olfactory dysfunction, PRP may facilitate the regeneration of olfactory receptor neurons and restore epithelial integrity. These mechanisms may explain the sustained improvements observed up to 12 months. This is consistent with histologic and animal model data demonstrating peripheral neural regeneration following PRP administration [23, 24].

Subgroup analyses in this meta‐analysis revealed that age may moderate treatment response. Participants under the age of 40 experienced significantly greater improvements in both subjective and objective measures of olfaction compared to older individuals. This age‐dependent effect may reflect a higher neuroregenerative capacity in younger patients, underscoring the importance of patient‐specific factors when selecting treatment.

Safety profiles across the included studies were favorable. Six of nine studies reported no adverse events, while the remaining three noted only minor, self‐limited events such as mild epistaxis, nasal pressure, vasovagal episodes, procedural discomfort, and nasal congestion. Importantly, no serious or long‐lasting complications were reported, supporting the overall safety of PRP when used in this setting.

Limitations

While the findings of this systematic review are encouraging, several limitations must be considered. First, the total number of high‐quality randomized control trials remains limited, restricting the ability to comprehensively assess publication bias or draw firm conclusions about treatment efficacy across subgroups. Second, heterogeneity in PRP preparation protocols, dosing frequency, and delivery techniques introduces variability that complicates cross‐study comparisons. The lack of a standardized regimen limits the generalizability and reproducibility of findings, precluding the formulation of evidence‐based dosing recommendations. Additionally, although validated psychophysiological olfactory assessments were used, outcome measures varied across studies including the use of Sniffin Stick, TDI, BSIT, SCENTinel, UPSIT, and CCCRC. This variability complicates direct comparisons and may contribute to heterogeneity in analyses. Furthermore, while subgroup analyses by age helped explain some of the heterogeneity (I 2 = 81% for subjective outcomes and I 2 = 84% for objective outcomes at 1 month), further subgroup analysis based on clinically relevant factors such as symptom duration and etiology could not be assessed due to limited reporting. Moreover, most included patients had post‐COVID olfactory loss, with only one study focusing on post‐traumatic cases. Given the differing pathophysiology by etiology, findings may not be fully generalizable to other causes of olfactory dysfunction such as idiopathic, traumatic, or sinonasal etiologies.

Conclusion

This meta‐analysis revealed that platelet‐rich plasma appears to be a promising and well‐tolerated treatment for patients with persistent olfactory dysfunction. Improvements were observed across both subjective and objective olfactory measures, with statistically significant results as early as 1 month and sustained up to 12 months post‐treatment. Subgroup analysis indicated that younger individuals (< 40 years old) may experience more pronounced benefits.

However, the clinical significance of these findings should be interpreted with caution. PRP should not yet be regarded as a definitive or effective treatment, but rather a potential adjunctive option that can be discussed with carefully selected patients who have persistent olfactory dysfunction refractory to standard therapies. Further high‐quality randomized controlled trials with standardized PRP preparation protocols are needed to confirm its clinical efficacy and establish optimal treatment parameters.
