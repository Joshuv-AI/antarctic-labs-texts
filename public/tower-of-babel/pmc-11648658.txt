Hapalindole Q suppresses autophagosome−lysosome fusion by promoting YAP1 degradation via chaperon-mediated autophagy
Authors: Yali Wu, Shaonan Wang, Zhicong Guo, Min Sun, Zhen Xu, Yu Du, Fahui Zhu, Yajuan Su, Zhou Xu, Yi Xu, Xu Gong, Ruan Fang, Jiaojiao Hu, Yan Peng, Zhaowen Ding, Cong Liu, Ang Li, Weiwei He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTargeting autophagy has shown increasing promise in the treatment of various diseases. While exploring the biological functions of indole terpenoids, we identified hapalindole Q as a mechanistically intriguing macroautophagy inhibitor, which induces Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) degradation through chaperone-mediated autophagy and thus impedes autophagosome−lysosome fusion. This study introduces a YAP-dependent small-molecule approach to autophagy regulation, which merits further investigation for biomedical applications. In addition, this YAP1-binding molecule may serve as a valuable tool to explore the therapeutic implications of YAP1 degradation. From a broader perspective, this work highlights the importance of proper mechanistic characterization in uncovering the potential of functionally underappreciated natural products.

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved catabolic process responsible for the removal of unnecessary or dysfunctional cellular components and the recycling of nutrients (1, 2). There are three major types of autophagy in mammalian cells: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), of which macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) is the most prevalent (1, 2). Autophagy consists of several consecutive steps: a) initiation, b) nucleation of the isolation membrane (phagophore), c) expansion and closure of the double-membrane structure to form the autophagosome, d) fusion of the autophagosome with the lysosome to form the autolysosome, and e) degradation of the cytoplasmic components engulfed by the autolysosome (1, 2). Autophagy dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of diseases such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and infectious diseases, and therefore, autophagy modulation is considered to be a promising therapeutic strategy for these diseases (3, 4). Activating autophagy may have beneficial effects on neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases associated with defective autophagy (3, 4). Conversely, inhibiting autophagy holds the potential for suppressing the progression of tumors that rely on proficient autophagy in response to metabolic and other stresses (3, 4). Over the past decade, the discovery and mechanistic characterization of autophagy-regulating small molecules have received considerable attention (5–8). Notably, current autophagy inhibitors mostly target either autophagy initiation or lysosomal activity (5, 6); thus, mechanistically distinct inhibitors (5, 9, 10) are highly desired.

The Hippo pathway, along with its downstream effectors, Yes-associated protein (YAP, also known as YAP1) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), plays a pivotal role in the regulation of organ size and tissue homeostasis and regeneration (11–13). In recent years, the cross talk between the Hippo−YAP/TAZ signaling and autophagy has attracted increasing interest (14). Pavel et al. reported that loss of YAP/TAZ function impairs autophagosome formation and thus suppresses autophagy (15). Totaro et al. revealed the regulatory role of YAP/TAZ in autophagosome−lysosome fusion (16). These findings suggest a potential strategy to suppress autophagy via inhibition of YAP/TAZ. Although a variety of small molecules targeting YAP are being developed as therapeutic candidates (17–19), to our knowledge, those that bind directly to YAP are rather rare (20–22), presumably due to the lack of druggable pockets (19). The discovery of such molecules is expected to facilitate the development of therapeutics for both autophagy-related and Hippo−YAP/TAZ-related diseases.

The hapalindole-type natural products, including hapalindoles, fischerindoles, ambiguines, and welwitindolinones, are a large family of indole terpenoids derived from marine cyanobacteria (23–25). From a structural perspective, they share an attached ring system (26) containing a densely substituted cyclohexane moiety and an indole moiety. Since Moore and coworkers discovered the initial members in 1984 (27), this fascinating class has become an attractive target for chemical synthesis (23–25), which inspired the development of a variety of useful strategies and methods. For instance, Baran et al. developed the direct coupling of indoles with carbonyl compounds for the scalable synthesis of the hapalindole-type natural products (28–30) and established the concept of protecting-group-free synthesis in their study (31). We also developed a Prins cyclization approach (32–36) for the convenient preparation of hapalindoles and their analogs (37). Recently, the biosynthesis of hapalindoles and ambiguines has attracted increasing interest (24, 25). The Sherman (38–40) and Liu (41, 42) teams independently elucidated the mechanism of the enzymatic reaction cascade responsible for the construction of the multisubstituted cyclohexane moiety of hapalindoles; the ring-closure step in the biosynthesis is similar to the Prins cyclization step in our chemical synthesis. The hapalindole-type natural products display a wide range of biological activities including antitumor, antimicrobial, and insecticidal activities (23–25). However, the mechanisms underlying these activities remain largely unexplored, which limits the biomedical applications of this class of molecules.

On the basis of our previous efforts in natural product synthesis, we constructed a library of indole terpenoids (32–35, 37, 43, 44) (including hapalindoles) and analogs thereof (45–47). A research program has been initiated to identify autophagy modulators from this library and to characterize them from a mechanistic perspective. Herein, we report that hapalindole Q (1, Fig. 1A) (28, 37). a structural representative of the hapalindole family, suppresses autophagosome−lysosome fusion by binding to YAP1 and promoting its degradation via CMA.

Results

Compound 1 Inhibits Autophagosome−Lysosome Fusion.

We investigated the effect of compound 1, prepared in racemic form through our Prins cyclization approach (37), on autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells (Hep G2). First, the autophagy markers microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) and p62 (sequestosome 1) were examined (48, 49). LC3 is a mammalian homolog of yeast autophagy-related protein 8 (Atg8), which is essential for autophagosome development and maturation (50). During autophagosome formation, LC3-I, the cytosolic form of LC3, undergoes lipidation to generate LC3-phosphatidylethanolamine conjugate (LC3-II), which is then recruited to the autophagosome membranes (50). After autophagosome−lysosome fusion, LC3-II bound to the inner autophagosome membrane is degraded along with other engulfed components (50). Notably, LC3B, an isoform of LC3, is widely used as a marker for monitoring autophagy (50). Immunoblot analysis showed that compound 1 increased LC3B-II levels in a dose-dependent manner in Hep G2 cells (Fig. 1B). The selective autophagy adaptor p62 was also moderately upregulated (Fig. 1B). We then tested how quickly compound 1 exerted its effect on LC3B. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, LC3B-II began to increase within 6 h of treatment with 1.

We next explored whether the increase in LC3B-II levels results from stimulation of autophagy (upstream) or from inhibition of autophagosome−lysosome fusion/lysosomal degradation (downstream). The LC3B-II levels in Hep G2 cells treated with 1 alone versus in those cotreated with 1 and an autophagy inducer or a lysosome inhibitor were compared by immunoblotting (48, 49). The combination of 1 and the autophagy inducer rapamycin (RAPA) markedly upregulated LC3B-II (Fig. 1 D and E). In contrast, the combination of 1 and the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ; Fig. 1 F and G) or bafilomycin A1 (BafA1; Fig. 1 H and I) had minimal impact on LC3B-II levels, compared to 1 alone. These results indicated that compound 1 is a downstream suppressor of autophagy, consistent with the unchanged phosphorylation status of autophagy-related protein 13 (Atg13) at S355; Atg13 is a key subunit of the unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1) complex responsible for autophagy initiation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (51). The autophagic flux was monitored by using a tandem fluorescent protein-tagged LC3 reporter (48, 49). In this assay, the mCherry−eGFP−LC3B construct (52) was expressed in Hep G2 cells. Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence of LC3B-positive puncta revealed an accumulation of autophagosomes and a reduction of autolysosomes after treatment with 1 (Fig. 1 J and K), which confirmed that compound 1 inhibits the fusion or degradation process in autophagy.

We further investigated whether compound 1 impairs lysosomal function. The acidotropic fluorescent probe LysoTracker Red was employed to measure lysosomal acidity in Hep G2 cells. In contrast to BafA1, a known vacuolar-type H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) inhibitor, compound 1 demonstrated essentially no effect on the luminal pH of lysosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Lysosomal proteolytic capacity was assessed by immunoblotting for the lysosomal proteases cathepsin B (CTSB) and cathepsin D (CTSD) (53). As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D, the maturation of these proteases remained unaffected by treatment with 1. These results suggested that the observed autophagy suppression is not a consequence of lysosomal dysfunction but is likely attributable to inhibition of autophagosome−lysosome fusion.

Compound 1 Promotes YAP1 Degradation via CMA and Disrupts Proper Distribution of Rab7.

Given the potential role of YAP1 in autophagosome−lysosome fusion (16), we examined the effect of 1 on YAP1. As shown in Fig. 2A, this compound downregulated YAP1 in a time-dependent manner in Hep G2 cells. A similar decrease in YAP1 levels was observed in other human HCC cells (HCC-LM3 and SMMC-7721), human cervical cancer cells (HeLa), human ovarian cancer cells (SK-OV-3), and human triple-negative breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231) after treatment with 1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), which confirmed its role as a general YAP1 downregulator. Meanwhile, an accumulation of LC3B-II was detected in the tested cancer cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We next investigated whether the reduction of YAP1 results from transcriptional downregulation or post-translational degradation. YAP1 mRNA levels, measured by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), remained unaffected by treatment with 1 (Fig. 2B), consistent with the high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) results (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Thus, the observed YAP1 reduction should be attributable to post-translational degradation. Phosphorylation of YAP1 at S381 typically triggers canonical ubiquitin-dependent degradation; therefore, a temporary increase in phospho-YAP1 (S381) levels can serve as an indicator of the proteasomal degradation (54). However, phospho-YAP1 (S381) was even decreased by treatment with 1 in Hep G2 cells (Fig. 2A). We also examined YAP1 levels in Hep G2 cells overexpressing the proteasomal degradation-resistant YAP1-5SA mutant (54). As shown in Fig. 2 C and D, compound 1 reduced both YAP1-WT and YAP1-5SA, which indicated that the degradation is not mediated by the ubiquitin−proteasome system (UPS). On the other hand, the effect of 1 on YAP-5SA was counteracted by treatment with the lysosomal protease inhibitor leupeptin (Fig. 2 E and F). These results demonstrated that lysosomal degradation is responsible for the YAP1 reduction induced by 1.

In light of the recent discovery by Desideri et al. that YAP1 is a substrate of CMA in HCC cells (55), we tested whether CMA is involved in compound 1-induced degradation of YAP1. siRNA-mediated knockdown of the CMA receptor lysosome-associated membrane protein 2A (56) (LAMP2A) (Fig. 2 G and H) or the chaperone protein heat shock protein family A member 8/heat shock cognate protein 70 (57) (HSPA8/HSC70) (Fig. 2 I and J) significantly attenuated the effect of 1 on YAP1. Additionally, the HSC70 inhibitor VER-155008 exhibited a similar counteracting effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Desideri et al. also reported that mutation of the KFERQ-like motif, QELLR (residues 323–327), within YAP1 diminishes its interaction with HSC70 and impairs its lysosomal degradation (55). We observed that mutating the QELLR motif mitigated the effect of 1 on YAP1 (Fig. 2 K and L). These results suggested that compound 1 promotes YAP1 degradation via CMA.

We then assessed the relevance between compound 1-induced YAP1 degradation and autophagy suppression. The impact of 1 on autophagic flux was compared in Hep G2 cells transfected with YAP1 and in those transfected with an empty vector. Consistent with our previous observation (Fig. 1 J and K), treatment with 1 led to accumulation of autophagosomes and reduction of autolysosomes in the cells transfected with the empty vector (Fig. 3). In contrast, no such outcome was observed in the cells transfected with YAP1, as shown in Fig. 3. This counteracting effect of YAP1 overexpression on treatment with 1 suggested that YAP1 degradation is responsible for the inhibition of autophagosome−lysosome fusion.

We further explored the effect of 1 on Ras-related protein Rab-7a (Rab7), a member of the Rab family of small GTPases that regulate membrane trafficking. Rab7 plays a crucial role in the maturation of autophagosomes and their fusion with lysosomes, with its precise localization to specific organelles being essential for its function (58, 59). Despite minimal influence on Rab7 protein levels (Fig. 4A), compound 1 disrupted proper distribution of Rab7 and induced its clustering (Fig. 4 B and C) in Hep G2 cells. Notably, siRNA-mediated knockdown of YAP1 similarly affected Rab7 distribution (Fig. 4 D and E), which suggested an important role for YAP1 in regulating Rab7 localization. These results implied that compound 1 impairs Rab7 transport by degrading YAP1.

Compound 1 Binds to YAP1.

Having identified 1 as a YAP1 degrader, we investigated whether YAP1 is a direct cellular target of 1. Two label-free biophysical methods, the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) (60, 61) and biolayer interferometry (BLI) (62) analysis, were used to examine the direct interaction between 1 and YAP1. CETSA detects changes in the thermal stability of a protein upon ligand binding in intact cells or cell lysates (60, 61). As shown in Fig. 5 A and B, compound 1 significantly enhanced the thermal stability of YAP1. BLI analyzes the interference patterns of white light reflected from a protein-conjugated biosensor tip in the presence and absence of a ligand (62). This method confirmed the binding of 1 to YAP1 and determined the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) to be 14.83 ± 0.65 μM (Fig. 5C).

We sought to specify the enantiomeric form of 1 that binds to YAP1 and suppresses autophagy. Compound (+)-1, which shares the same absolute configuration as naturally occurring hapalindole Q, and its enantiomer [(−)-1] were prepared by using our Prins cyclization approach from enantiomerically enriched precursors, separated by HPLC with a chiral stationary phase. Interestingly, both (+)-1 and (−)-1 bind to YAP1, as shown by BLI analysis, with Kd values of 9.13 ± 0.32 and 22.78 ± 0.86 μM, respectively (Fig. 5 D and E). Consistently, compound (+)-1 significantly decreased YAP1 levels and increased LC3B-II levels in Hep G2 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B), whereas (−)-1 exhibited a weaker effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and C). Furthermore, treatment of Hep G2 cells expressing mCherry−eGFP−LC3B with (+)-1 led to autophagosome accumulation and autolysosome reduction (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), which indicated that compound (+)-1 inhibits autophagosome−lysosome fusion.

Finally, we conducted a preliminary structure−activity relationship study of 1 for YAP1 binding and degradation. The isothiocyanate group may contribute to the binding of 1 to YAP1, given its electrophilicity toward the lysine and cysteine residues of proteins (63). To test the necessity of the isothiocyanate group, analogs 2 and 3 lacking this group were prepared in racemic form and subjected to BLI analysis (Table 1). To our surprise, these compounds still bind to YAP1 with Kd values (21.17 ± 0.71 μM for 2 and 23.13 ± 0.31 μM for 3) comparable to that of 1 (14.83 ± 0.65 μM) (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Similar to 1, they downregulated YAP1 and upregulated LC3B-II in Hep G2 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). In contrast, indole derivatives 4 and 5 do not bind to YAP1 (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6) and, consistently, had essentially no effect on YAP1 and LC3B-II levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 C and D). These results suggested that the hapalindole Q scaffold, rather than the isothiocyanate moiety, is critical for YAP1 binding and degradation.

Discussion

We discovered that hapalindole Q (1), a member of the hapalindole family of indole terpenoids, is a mechanistically distinct autophagy inhibitor (Fig. 6). This compound induces YAP1 degradation via CMA, impairs proper distribution of Rab7, and impedes autophagosome−lysosome fusion. Pharmacologically superior autophagy regulators are anticipated to be identified from hapalindoles and their analogs, which can be readily accessed through established methods of chemical synthesis or biosynthesis. Given the rarity of small molecules that directly target YAP1, this class of YAP1-binding compounds may serve as a useful tool for investigating YAP1 degradation. More broadly, this work highlights the importance of proper mechanistic characterization in revealing the value of functionally neglected natural products, such as hapalindoles.
