Cultivating Enduring Changes in Assessment Through a Life Sciences Community of Practice
Authors: Ludmila Tyler, Peter J. Kennelly, Shelly Engelman, Jennifer Loertscher, Jennifer C. Bobenko, Yufeng Wei, Kirsten F. Block, Kathryn M. McCulloch, Vishwa D. Trivedi, Kimberly T. Lane, Kimberly J. Linenberger Cortes, Victoria Del Gaizo Moore, Daniel R. Dries
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo provide instructors with personally relevant, discipline‐based training in assessment, the Community for the Assessment of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology Learning (CABL) conducted a series of six participant‐centered workshops between July 2020 and January 2024. Key features of the workshops included emphasizing backward design, peer editing, and the use of participant‐generated questions and rubrics as teaching tools. Five of the workshops focused on summative assessment and one on alternative assessment, with half offered virtually and the latter half delivered in person. In total, 110 unique scientist‐educators participated. To investigate how effective these CABL workshops were in fostering changes in assessment attitudes and practices, attendees completed an Exit Survey at the end of each workshop and responded to a single Follow‐Up Survey administered in the spring of 2024. Six of the 48 respondents to the Follow‐Up Survey participated in Focus Groups. Exit Survey responses indicated a high degree of satisfaction with the structure and content of the workshops and their immediate positive impact on knowledge about assessment. The Follow‐Up Survey revealed lasting impacts: Sixty percent of respondents reported revising their overall approach to assessment, while nearly a third described specific actions they had taken to implement inclusive teaching practices. Focus Group participants noted that transformative change could occur through both adopting best practices and disseminating knowledge to their professional networks. Together, these findings suggest that the participant‐centered CABL workshops catalyzed changes in assessment perspectives and self‐reported practices among a substantive portion of attendees.

Introduction

Assessment as a Vital Component of the Teaching and Learning Cycle

Teaching and learning occur in the context of a dynamic partnership between pedagogical choices and assessments [1, 2, 3]. Over the past three decades, scholarly research into the science of teaching and learning has catalyzed a movement away from the long‐standard “sage‐on‐the‐stage” content‐delivery model to the adoption of evidence‐based, “active” learning approaches [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Yet, in many cases, the instruments employed to assess student learning have changed little from their 20th‐century antecedents.

Reliable, insightful assessment is vital for both measuring student success in achieving learning objectives and for instructors to evaluate the efficacy of their teaching practices [10, 11, 12]. Perhaps most important is the impact on students, who rely on feedback from assessments to guide their learning process [13, 14]. Thus, to support active learning and students' acquisition of high‐level conceptual knowledge and skills, a key shift toward more intentional, responsive, and integrated assessment is needed.

Nevertheless, across STEM disciplines, implementation is more advanced for research‐based instructional practices than for evidence‐based assessments [6]. Assessments remain rooted in the past for several reasons. Ease and expediency—for example, through machine‐scorable and computer‐based assessments—may be prioritized in the face of heavy workloads and the logistical challenges of teaching large classes [15, 16]. Using the same assessment tools over time and across sections taught by different instructors may be valued for perceived consistency. Concern that changes to assessment practices could compromise historic standards can also be a barrier. However, decisions to maintain the status quo are often made in the absence of substantive training in effective assessment techniques [17, 18].

In tandem, participation in teaching‐focused professional development has been found to be positively associated not only with reduced time lecturing [19] but also with the utilization of research‐based assessments, such as surveys measuring affective learning and concept inventories [16]. Process Oriented Guided Inquiry (POGIL; pogil.org; [20]), Course‐Based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs; serc.carleton.edu/curenet/index.html; [21]), and Peer‐Led Team Learning (PLTL; pltlis.org; [22]) are among the projects which have shown success in disseminating active‐learning strategies by building professional communities for instructors. Indeed, the Teacher‐Centered Systemic Reform (TCSR) model posits that a focus on instructors is critical to achieving substantive, sustained educational change [23, 24]. The American Association for the Advancement of Science's Levers for Change Report has similarly identified professional development opportunities for instructors as a potential high‐impact mechanism for improving STEM education; instructors' participation in communities of practice—with the training and support they provide through peer interactions—is one avenue for facilitating this change [6].

Because implementation is shaped by an instructor's experiences, perspectives, knowledge, and context, an opportunity exists to support life sciences educators in utilizing evidence‐based practices in assessment through a series of participant‐centered workshops [25, 26]. The Community for the Assessment of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology Learning (CABL) was created to address this very need by providing workshops for professional development and capacity‐building in assessment in the molecular life sciences [27].

The Emergence of CABL as a Community for Professional Development in Assessment Practices

The American Society for Biochemistry & Molecular Biology (ASBMB) offers an annual, national examination for students in ASBMB‐accredited undergraduate biochemistry and molecular biology (BMB) programs [28]. This assessment relies on the volunteer work of dozens of individuals in the BMB education community. During the writing and scoring of this national exam, the coordinators—many of whom are authors of this manuscript—observed firsthand that volunteers' disparate approaches reflected a need for professional development in assessment. Informed by the immersive, iterative, and feedback‐rich experiences of building the ASBMB exam, the CABL founders approached the National Science Foundation (NSF) with a proposal to fund a pilot workshop on summative assessment for BMB faculty [27]. Core elements of the workshop included (a) promoting engagement by using participant‐generated assessments and rubrics as teaching tools; (b) multiple rounds of peer editing in the construction of a learning objective, assessment, and rubric; (c) an emphasis on backward design as a guide for producing incisive assessments; and (d) promoting lasting impact through a dynamic community of practice among the participants.

To recruit a cohort representing the range of institutions which might use, contribute to, and otherwise strengthen the national ASBMB assessment and to foster durable networking relationships, CABL partnered with hosts in specific geographic regions. Atlanta, Georgia, at the heart of a rich landscape of educational institutions in the southeastern United States—including rural institutions and Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs)—was selected as the location of the pilot workshop. This successful pilot has since blossomed into an international learning community of faculty and trainees connected through the shared experience of participating in a series of NSF‐funded workshops on best practices in summative, alternative, and formative assessment [27]. Beginning with guidance on creating a learning objective, workshop participants use the principles of backward design to define a learning objective and then construct an aligned assessment item and accompanying rubric. Facilitated small‐ and large‐group discussions and extensive peer review during the workshop allow participants to refine their assessments [27]. Critically, participants create their own assessments, leaving the workshop with a completed plan shaped for their individual teaching context and retaining their own intellectual property. These structures lead to a highly engaging and immersive professional development series that empowers participants with a product for immediate deployment in their own teaching and serves as a point of nucleation for community building.

Previously, we examined CABL as a case study in using iterative feedback from participants in the pilot and two subsequent workshops to develop and refine an online workshop series [27]. Launched during the pandemic, these initial workshops were held virtually and focused on summative assessment, which tests student learning at the end of a class, unit, or course and often takes the form of a quiz or exam. We then continued to improve the workshops based on participant feedback, including responses to Exit Surveys at the end of each workshop: We introduced in‐person events; experimented with the number of contact hours; developed an asynchronous, pre‐workshop module on constructing learning objectives; and designed materials to allow participants to evaluate example assessments before revising their own. To further meet participant needs, CABL workshops also expanded to alternative and ultimately formative assessment. While summative assessment is widely recognizable, instructors typically have less experience with alternative assessment (which encompasses a range of activities beyond quizzes and exams, such as project‐, problem‐ or process‐based tasks, multi‐media presentations, and reflections) or formative assessment (which is characterized by iterations of practice and feedback to develop learning, for example, through clicker questions, worksheets, and homework).

Here, we leverage a deeper body of data consisting of survey and focus group responses generated from a larger number and variety of workshops—both virtual and in‐person—to investigate their immediate and continuing impact on participants' professional development in assessment. Our specific research question for these analyses was: To what extent have intentional, participant‐centered choices in workshop design catalyzed immediate and lasting changes in assessment perspectives and practices?

Methods

Study Subjects

The data used for this analysis were solicited from the 110 unique participants who attended one (or more) of a series of six regional workshops conducted over a span of three‐and‐a‐half years. These workshops varied in their format (virtual versus in‐person), focus (summative or alternative assessment), duration, and geographic location (Table 1). Of the 84 workshop participants for whom both Exit Survey responses and institutional information were analyzed, 60% and 40% were affiliated with public and private institutions, respectively. These institutions ranged in size from fewer than 2000 undergraduates (12% of participants) to more than 24,000 undergraduates (25% of participants). The Carnegie research classifications (https://carnegieclassifications.acenet.edu/) were similarly varied: Participants affiliated with institutions without a Carnegie research classification represented approximately 37% of the total; the next largest group, at ~30%, encompassed participants from Research Colleges and Universities (RCU) which offer few if any doctoral degrees. Participants from Research 1 and 2 (R1 and R2) institutions comprised the remaining ~17% and ~15%, respectively. Approximately 71% of participants were affiliated with minority‐serving institutions (MSIs), consistent with a workshop series which was open to all while specifically extending invitations to BMB instructors at these institutions [27]. Attendees represented institutions from all areas of the country with particular concentration in the Northeast, Southeast, and Southwest (Figure 1).

Experimental Design

As part of a mixed‐methods convergent design, Exit Surveys were administered at the end of each workshop to probe immediate changes in instructor perspectives (Figure 2). Data Collection

Exit Surveys

The Exit Survey included Likert items addressing self‐reported efficacy, structure of the workshop, and sense of community; free‐response items centered on workshop strengths and areas for improvement (Data S1). After the July 2020 pilot workshop, the Exit Survey was refined and then remained largely unchanged through 2024. For consistency, responses to Exit Surveys from the five workshops held in January 2022 through January 2024 were analyzed for this study. Per workshop, response rates for the Exit Surveys ranged from 83% to 100% of individuals who completed the workshop (Table S1). In total, 84 Exit Survey responses were collected for the 89 participants in the January 2022 to January 2024 workshops (94% overall response rate). Four participants attended two workshops in this time period; however, all 84 responses were analyzed for the following three reasons: (i) since survey responses were collected anonymously, it is not possible to determine which responses may have been submitted by these four individuals; (ii) Exit Surveys asked about participants' experiences at a particular workshop, making the workshop—not the individual—the unit of analysis; and (iii) these four represented at most 5% of the total number of responses and may, indeed, have been one or more of the survey non‐respondents.

Follow‐Up Survey

To probe lasting changes in practices, in the spring of 2024, the 110 individuals who had attended the six workshops held from July 2020 to January 2024 were sent a Follow‐Up Survey (Data S2) asking questions similar in format and content to those in the Exit Survey. Seven of these individuals had attended two workshops. The Follow‐Up Survey closed in July of 2024, with 31%–81% of workshop participants (per workshop) represented in the responses. These percentages were normalized to the original number of participants in each workshop (Table S1).

Focus Groups

To more deeply understand the participant experience, respondents to the Follow‐Up Survey were invited to participate in Focus Groups (Figure 1). A total of six respondents participated, representing 12.5% of the total number of Follow‐Up Survey respondents and 5.5% of all unique workshop participants. Five of the six Focus Group participants attended a single workshop in July 2020 (virtual, summative), February 2023 (in‐person, alternative), or March 2023 (in‐person, summative); the sixth participant represented both the July 2020 and February 2023 workshops. In addition, half of the Focus Group members attended a formative assessment workshop, which was held in person in July 2024, after the conclusion of the Follow‐Up Survey, and which likely shaped their perspective on CABL.

Following the protocol summarized in Data S3, Focus Groups were conducted as pairs over Zoom, which was used to record audio. Semi‐structured Focus Groups were centered around a set of core themes, allowing for follow‐up discussion through clarifying questions. The audio files of Zoom recordings were transcribed using Otter.ai and reviewed by the interviewers for clarity and accuracy. Transcription resulted in the de‐identification of respondents for analysis. Transcripts of responses from each individual were concatenated into a single document.

Data Analysis

Quantitative Analysis of Likert Items From the Exit Surveys and Follow‐Up Survey

Likert responses were re‐coded to a numerical scale, generally from 1 (“Poor,” “Not at All,” or “Strongly Disagree”) to 5 (“Excellent,” “Very Much,” or “Strongly Agree”). Responses from common items were aggregated and mean scores calculated to form a final data set. Likert data for pre‐ and post‐workshop items were treated as discontinuous; Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank tests were used to test the null hypothesis that the workshop activities did not alter participants' responses to compared items. When the W‐statistic equaled zero, indicating a normal data distribution, the data were treated as continuous and a paired t‐test employed as a secondary test of the null hypothesis.

Thematic Analysis of Free‐Response Items From Exit Surveys

Free‐response items from the Exit Survey were inductively coded for emergent themes within the same workshop according to the method of Saldaña [29]. Themes were subsequently validated via review by at least two other coders, with discussion to achieve consensus. After data from each workshop were analyzed independently, the full data set was further examined for themes shared across workshops.

Thematic Analysis of Follow‐Up Survey Free‐Response Items and Focus‐Group Transcripts

Free‐response items from the Follow‐Up Survey and transcripts from the Focus Groups were inductively and independently coded by two investigators for emergent themes according to the method of Saldaña [29]; validation was achieved by coding to consensus among a team of four researchers. The resulting codebook was then used to code each individual response (Follow‐Up Survey items) or transcript (Focus Groups) for the presence or absence of these themes; validity was reached through coding to consensus.

Limitations

Our conclusions are founded on multiple lines of evidence with a large majority of workshop participants over a 3.5‐year period represented by at least one of the data collection methods: Exit Surveys, Follow‐Up Survey, or Focus Groups. We acknowledge, however, the inherent limitations of our data. A primary consideration is that all of the input from participants is self‐reported. Responses to Likert‐scale questions and comments, whether on surveys or in focus groups, reflect the perspectives of the participants but cannot provide insight into changes in participants' attitudes and practices as observed through more theory‐driven lenses. Holistic evaluations of teaching advocate for combining the instructor's voice with that of peers and students [30, 31]. Thus, peer analysis of assessments implemented in course settings, classroom observations, and an investigation of the information revealed about student learning, as well as students' perceptions of assessments, could complement self‐reporting and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the extent, efficacy, and duration of workshop‐inspired changes. Additionally, because survey data were collected anonymously, longitudinally tracking the evolution over time of individuals' perspective on their CABL professional development experience is not possible; instead, we report here trends for participants as a whole.

We further recognize that each line of evidence utilized in this study has both advantages and limitations. Exit Survey responses, for example, had the highest response rates and were therefore likely to be the most representative of participants' overall CABL experience. Conversely, surveys administered at the conclusion of each workshop measured only immediate changes in attitudes. Changes in practices would require time for implementation beyond the workshop and could be elucidated by the Follow‐Up Survey and Focus Groups. These latter data collection methods, however, relied on responses from a smaller number of participants (approximately 44% and 5% of the 110 participants for the Follow‐Up and Focus Groups, respectively) over a longer period, with possibly a year or more between a given workshop and a participant's reflection on its effects.

Results

The CABL workshops were designed to provide participants—who were involved in undergraduate education at a range of institution types—with professional development in assessment via an instructor‐centered, peer learning environment [27]. To evaluate the extent to which the workshops changed knowledge about and approaches for assessment, we focused on six workshops held from July 2020 to January 2024, with 110 unique participants (Figure 2, Table 1). Responses collected from workshop participants via Exit Surveys, a Follow‐Up Survey, and Focus Groups are the primary source of data for our analyses of workshop efficacy. This mixed‐methods approach allowed us to investigate not only immediate and lasting impacts but also efficacy across workshop modalities and topics.

Exit Surveys: Participants Reported Immediate Changes in Knowledge and Skills From the Workshop

Because the Exit Survey was revised after the 2020 pilot workshop, we limited the Exit‐Survey portion of our current analysis to the responses from the five post‐pilot workshops. Across these five workshops, responses to Likert items addressing workshop efficacy and relevance regularly yielded average scores between the top two ratings on a five‐point scale (Tables 2 and 3). For instance, when asked to evaluate their ability to use backward design prior to the workshop, participants were neutral; however, following the workshop, they reported increased competence (Table 2). Similarly, average scores concerning self‐assessed ability to write rubrics rose from just below neutral pre‐workshop to nearly “Good” afterward (Table 2).

In addition and again across workshops, most participants agreed or strongly agreed that the workshops helped them to identify relevant learning objectives in BMB (88% of respondents) and to craft rubrics aligned with these objectives (98% of respondents, Table 3). Overall, 99% of respondents agreed that the workshop was relevant to their professional goals (Table 3). These results emphasize the overall effectiveness of the workshops, even across variables, such as expanding the January 2024 workshop to participants from STEM rather than BMB specifically. Thematic analysis of free‐response items on the Exit Surveys supported these results, with participants frequently citing the workshop activities and use of backward design as particularly beneficial (Table S2). Further highlighting workshop strengths, respondents often recommended that future workshops devote even more time to working with peers, as well as more intensive expert‐ and peer‐mentored activities (Table S3).

Longitudinal Follow‐Up Survey: Participants Also Reported Lasting Changes in Implementation due to CABL Workshops

Implementation of New Knowledge/Skills

In the spring of 2024, a Follow‐Up Survey designed to evaluate the long‐term impact of CABL was administered to all 110 unique individuals who had participated in one or more CABL workshops from July 2020 through January 2024 (Data S2). A total of 48 CABL alumni completed the Follow‐Up Survey, seven of whom had participated in more than one workshop (Table S1). As a whole, the responses to both the Likert‐ and free‐response items on the Follow‐Up Survey indicate that most participants in the six workshops successfully integrated new knowledge or skills acquired from CABL into their assessment practices. Analyzing the responses for each workshop separately revealed some variability.

With the in‐person 2023–2024 workshops generally showing higher scores than the virtual 2020–2022 workshops (Table 4); however, the iterative incorporation of improvements in workshop design based on lessons learned from earlier workshops may have contributed to these outcomes. Overall, on a Likert item asking to what extent participants “applied new knowledge or skills learned at the workshop(s)” in their teaching practice, most respondents indicated that they applied much of what they had learned (mean = 4.10 on a scale of 1 = “Not at All” to 5 = “Very Much;” Table 4).

Survey respondents were also asked to provide “a brief example of any new knowledge or skills that [they had] integrated into [their] teaching practice” (Table S4). Thematic analysis revealed that approximately 60% of respondents reported revising their overall approach to assessment. One participant wrote, “I believe I am more intentional when writing assessment questions. I think more about what I am actually assessing with the question and whether the question will truly address a particular lesson objective.” Approximately one‐third of respondents mentioned the use of more targeted measures of student learning. For instance, one participant noted, “The workshop provided me with a strategy to write multiple choice questions with options that capture what students may be thinking about a specific concept.” Such statements suggest that the influence of workshop participation can extend beyond the single assessment item covered to more broadly affect an instructor's approach to assessment. Encouragingly, nearly one‐third of respondents to the Follow‐Up Survey also indicated increased use of backward design, including 10% who specifically commented on incorporating rubrics as part of this process. Additionally, multiple CABL participants mentioned subsequent efforts to act as catalysts for change among their colleagues. As one respondent wrote, “I use the rubric for rubrics any time I'm developing a new one. I have also shared it with everyone.” Another wrote, “I have imparted the knowledge to my entire department which has led to better assessment practices and innovations for assessment.”

Implementation of Inclusive Teaching Practices

Each workshop emphasized accessibility as an inclusive assessment practice—from vocabulary choices to color use in visual representations to length of the prompt to cognitive load. Intriguingly, while responses on the Follow‐Up Survey indicated that participants overall had implemented inclusive practices from the workshops into their teaching, the mean evaluation score of 3.90 for this item (Table 4) was low relative to scores for the implementation of other recommended changes. We hypothesize that this somewhat lower rating could be due to a combination of factors. First, all CABL workshops emphasize the benefits to students of clear assessments that focus on evaluating BMB learning—rather than, for instance, requiring cultural understanding which may or may not be shared across student groups. One example would be to avoid referencing football as the context for a question about metabolism or respiratory biochemistry: Because the word “football” is used very differently in American and global cultures, and not all students are athletically inclined, this reference would not be universally understood and might be more confusing than clarifying. However, workshop participants may not immediately identify this approach as inclusive. Such conjecture is supported by an outlier comment on the Follow‐Up Survey: “We did not cover inclusive teaching practices to my recollection.” Second, alternative assessments—for which there was only one workshop in this data set—offer flexibility and inherently encompass multiple ways for students to demonstrate their learning. Participants may therefore more readily identify alternative assessment as inclusive. Indeed, the lone alternative assessment workshop in this set scored particularly highly for inclusive teaching (mean score of 4.31), reflecting the workshop's clear focus on integrating inclusivity (Table 4). Moreover, the second highest score (4.17) came from a March 2023 workshop in which the plenary speaker explained the value of test question templates and explicitly addressed transparency in assessment preparation as an inclusive practice [32, 33].

Nevertheless, the free responses for the incorporation of inclusive teaching practices on the Follow‐Up Survey were positive overall. For instance, thematic analysis across workshops indicated that nearly half of respondents reported incorporating inclusive practices into their pedagogy (Table S5). One respondent specifically highlighted the benefits across student groups with the statement “We are in a very diverse student body already, but the methods also help with our students that have accessibility/disability documentations and to create methods that impact a larger audience than traditional methods.” Nearly 30% of the comments reflected increased intentionality in considering inclusion when designing assessments. Responses illustrating this theme included: “I have become much more intentional and cognizant of evaluating questions on exams and homework from an equity and inclusivity perspective.” Several comments also mentioned incorporating enhanced flexibility into teaching and learning modalities, as well as clearer communication with students. Approximately 15% of respondents referenced backward design in the context of inclusion, as shown in the following comment: “I started re‐designing my class activities and exams with explicit alignment to the learning objectives‐so students had sufficient practice and there were no surprises on exams.”

Notably, respondents who had attended more than one CABL workshop scored the highest across both changes to their instructional practices and incorporating inclusion into their assessment approach, with ratings of 5.00 and 4.57, respectively (Table 4). This finding suggests that participating in multiple CABL workshops on different assessment types may be especially effective in fostering and reinforcing positive changes in teaching practices.

Focus Groups: Participants Expanded Upon Improvements in Teaching as a Motivator and Lasting Changes From the Workshop

To unpack the emergent themes from this data set via dialogue, six Focus Group members were recruited via voluntary self‐selection from the respondents to the Follow‐Up Survey. These individuals had participated in one to three CABL workshops each and together represented multiple workshops—both virtual and in‐person—and a range of institutions and geographic regions. In semi‐structured Focus Groups, participants were asked about their motivation to engage with CABL and the outcomes of their workshop experiences (Data S3). Several themes emerged (Table 5). Half the Focus Group participants cited improving their teaching practice—either in place or in transition to a teaching position—as a motivator. A majority also discussed how CABL impacted the way in which they approach teaching and assessing. Most Focus Group participants indicated that the CABL experience facilitated their professional development, with nearly all commenting positively on how the workshop(s) changed their pedagogy, including backward design and the construction of learning objectives. Indeed, five of the six participants reported using new assessment practices and perceiving improvement in student performance as a result of attending CABL workshops. As one participant observed, “It has been transformative” (Table 5). Many Focus Group participants also noted sharing best practices in teaching with colleagues, with one participant stating that they had become “an evangelist” for backward design. Together, then, the Follow‐Up Surveys and Focus Groups identified multiple aspects of lasting changes in participants' beliefs and practices in assessment.

Discussion

Contemporary educators often have many opportunities to engage in professional development workshops. But what makes a professional development workshop meaningful or valued? Here, we use a rich data stream consisting of both quantitative (Likert surveys) and qualitative (free‐response questions and focus groups) data to empirically identify aspects of the CABL workshops that participants expressly value. This mixed‐methods approach using complementary lines of evidence affords us the ability to triangulate overarching themes that recruit participants, support their professional development, and sustain their activities at their home institution and beyond. In so doing, we have identified the following four aspects as being especially valued in participant‐centered professional development workshops: (1) a discipline‐specific focus, (2) anchoring around the creation of a tangible product to increase the likelihood of adoption, (3) peer review of participants' products, and (4) flexibility in modality and scheduling to broaden access.

The Need for Discipline‐Specific Professional Development

Even at the interdisciplinary January 2024 workshop, which encompassed additional STEM disciplines, nearly 70% of the participants were members of the BMB community. These interactions enabled engagement on educational practices both generally in higher education and specifically for the content, unit dynamics, and student expectations of the molecular life sciences [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. For example, many participants reported a sense of isolation when a BMB program constitutes a sub‐discipline within a larger department, for example, Chemistry or Biological Sciences, or when the small size of the institution limits responsibility for BMB to only a few instructors or even a single faculty member. This observation has implications for programs and individuals who fund or design professional development workshops, highlighting the potential of a disciplinary focus to enhance the impact of these experiences.

Anchoring Workshops Around the Creation of a Tangible Product

In the CABL workshops, participants used backward design principles to draft their own learning objective and corresponding assessment to use throughout the workshop. In small groups, participants practiced evaluating sample objectives and assessments of varying quality, before focusing on their own assessments—with peer feedback—for the remainder of the workshop. Participant‐centered programming uses feedback to iteratively improve workshop content and strengthen connections between learning objectives, assessment, rubric design, and ultimately implementation. For example, feedback from early workshops revealed that participants entered with varying degrees of experience in developing measurable learning objectives. In response, content was developed to allow participants to asynchronously complete training related to learning objectives. This asynchronous pre‐workshop module with examples improved efficiency, especially when deployed for face‐to‐face workshops held over a shorter window of time. The availability of different workshops in the same series offered further flexibility as well as opportunities for repeat engagement, connecting professional development across distinct assessment types.

The Value of Peer Review

The central role of participant‐driven discussions and peer editing complemented grounding in the discipline and the construction of a concrete product. Both online and in‐person workshops prominently featured three‐ or four‐person breakout groups guided by a workshop facilitator; ideas were shared in follow‐up discussions with the whole group. The small groups focused on peer‐editing of learning objectives, assessment items, and rubrics generated by the participants themselves. The role of the facilitator was less to lead these discussions and more to provide prompts for launching and, if needed, redirecting the exchange of ideas. Facilitators also gave feedback tailored to the work of each participant. Consistent with the Teacher‐Centered Systemic Reform model, instructors' personal experiences and engagement with communities of peers have previously been reported to be key factors in the adoption of innovative instructional practices (e.g., [24, 25, 39]). We therefore hypothesize that the use of authentic, personal materials as learning tools in a collaborative environment can increase the efficacy of professional development.

Flexibility in Modality and Scheduling

Iterative adaptation of the CABL workshops to different contexts and in response to participant feedback allowed us to observe the trade‐offs of specific design decisions. As we investigated workshop efficacy, we noted the potential for logistical factors to shape the reach of these workshops. For example, the first three workshops covered by this study were held virtually, while the next three were held in person. While in‐person sessions offered opportunities for extended and nuanced participant‐facilitator and participant‐participant interactions, our work supports the conclusion that a virtual workshop format can improve accessibility both across geographic distance and when travel costs, work, family responsibilities, or other individual circumstances limit in‐person attendance. For instance, the pilot workshop for the CABL project was hosted by Kennesaw State in Georgia during the beginning of the pandemic, when travel was severely restricted. This workshop primarily drew participants from the southeastern United States—as expected for the recruitment area—but also attracted participants from Michigan, Wisconsin, and Alaska. One individual in particular commented that the online format spread over several days was the only way they would have been able to participate in any professional development workshop, given their caregiving responsibilities at home. In comparison, the in‐person workshops scored noticeably higher with regard to their success in fostering the development of collaborative interactions or networking among participants (Kennelly et al., manuscript in preparation). Not surprisingly, this challenge of building community is common in virtual conferences and workshops across disciplines [40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45]. We therefore suggest that the different experiences of participants in virtual versus in‐person CABL workshops primarily reflect inherent strengths and limitations of these formats rather than any CABL‐specific feature.

Scheduling decisions have similarly entailed trade‐offs. Although each of the virtual workshops was offered as a series of short sessions over multiple days, we have experimented with the number, length, and type of these online sessions [27]. The in‐person workshops analyzed for this study were held over a weekend (February 2023) or in a single day (March 2023 and January 2024). No obvious correlation between a workshop's length and its efficacy was observed: the February 2023 alternative assessment workshop, which was the longest, scored the highest in Follow‐Up Survey queries about the incorporation of inclusive teaching practices; however, the January 2024 summative assessment workshop, which scored the highest for the application of new skills or knowledge, was among the shortest of the workshops (Tables 1 and 4). Our experience with the first three workshops revealed the need to provide a foundation in backward design and the role of learning objectives; thus, we began asking registrants to complete an online module in preparation for their upcoming workshop participation. This pre‐workshop module may have helped to maximize the impact of the time dedicated to the workshop itself—whether long or short.

Evidence from all three data streams demonstrates that across formats, participants consistently valued “interacting with colleagues” and “learning from ideas shared by other participants and the facilitators.” In parallel, some respondents suggested allocating even more time to workshop activities and break‐out discussions, with comments such as “I feel I want more time,” while others noted the intensity of the workshop, especially for one‐day, in‐person offerings, with comments such as “It's too long. I'm drained.” One participant explicitly highlighted these trade‐offs with the note “The first day was a bit long … But, I wouldn't recommend changing the schedule because of travel.” In summary, offering workshops with a variety of schedules and formats is an additional way to balance competing needs and to give potential participants more choice in engaging with professional development in effective assessment practices.

CABL As a Successful Professional Development Community

In the end, the test for a workshop or any other professional training is the extent to which participants implement what they learned into their daily practice. Overall, the mean Likert scores on the Follow‐Up Survey for both the post‐workshop application of “new knowledge or skills” (4.10) and “inclusive teaching practices” (3.90) were positive. The workshops' effectiveness was underscored by the even higher ratings given by participants who attended more than one CABL workshop: 5.00 and 4.57, respectively (Table 4). These outcomes also suggest the benefits of offering opportunities for continued engagement, for example, via a workshop series rather than a single, stand‐alone event. This success of CABL was further amplified by the participants in the Focus Groups, who were able to comment in greater detail on how CABL had influenced their pedagogical practices. Consistent with broad applicability and as revealed by comments from the Follow‐Up Survey, approximately 60% of respondents reported revising their overall approach to assessment. Although there is, to our knowledge, no single metric which defines a threshold for success in professional development, based on evidence from the Surveys and Focus Groups, the CABL workshop series appears to have been successful in positively impacting the instructional viewpoints and practices of a substantial portion of its participants. Importantly, the positive outcomes of a participant‐centered, discipline‐based approach were observed to occur across workshop modality and schedule, suggesting that the trade‐offs between virtual and in‐person meetings are worthwhile if they broaden access to such professional development.

Conclusions and Implications

Self‐reporting, as we use here to investigate the experiences of workshop participants, has inherent limitations; additional avenues of inquiry, such as longitudinal examinations of implemented assessments, other teaching materials, and practices, could further elucidate concrete outcomes for both instructors and students. Nevertheless, the overall positive responses across data collection methods in this study—representing the perspectives of instructors at large, medium, and small institutions, with varying levels of research activity—suggest broad applicability of the CABL approach. We conclude that centering activities on participant‐designed content, facilitating peer interactions, and intentionally responding to participants' feedback help to ensure that instructors find personal relevance in each workshop, can connect to a broader disciplinary community, and leave with tools suited for use in their own courses and institutional contexts. Together, the data sets support the idea that the above features of the CABL series built participants' capacity for engaging in evidence‐based assessment beyond the workshops. Notably, 33 of the 110 unique participants—nearly one in three—became workshop hosts and leaders themselves [46]. Thus, this instructor‐centered approach also shows promise for empowering individuals to catalyze change in assessment activities regionally and nationally.
