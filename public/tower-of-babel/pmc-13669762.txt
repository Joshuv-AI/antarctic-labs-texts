The future of dentistry in England: A vision for 2050 – From access crisis to health creation: Prevention, integration and public value
Authors: Sara Hurley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
By 2050, dentistry could be transformed by regenerative science, artificial intelligence, remote monitoring and more integrated digital records. The central question is whether those advances translate into better oral health for all or widen existing inequalities in access and outcomes. Many of the enabling technologies are already emerging; the more important question is how they are organised, commissioned and distributed. This paper argues for a shift from episodic repair towards health creation: preventing disease, identifying risk earlier, preserving tissue and function, and connecting oral health with wider care across the life course. It contrasts two possible futures and proposes five commissioning imperatives: commission for health, build connected pathways, strengthen dental data infrastructure, use the whole workforce and translate innovation equitably. The future of dentistry will depend less on scientific capability than on how systems organise and distribute it.

Introduction

Picture a dental clinic in 2050. Remote monitoring and artificial intelligence have triaged patient-reported symptoms and images into the clinical schedule, so that history and risk are available before the patient arrives. The consultation takes place in an environment adapted to the patient’s sensory preferences. An intraoral scan of soft and hard tissues, compared by AI with previous images, sits alongside chairside salivary testing. Together, these measures may identify changing oral or systemic risks and prompt further investigation or communication with the wider healthcare team.

The clinician interprets the findings and shapes a care plan around clinical need, patient preferences and the contribution of the wider dental team. Regenerative materials support damaged tissue to repair, while a missing tooth has been biologically regrown and fitted with an embedded biosensor monitoring the stability of the regenerated tissue. The encounter is recorded contemporaneously in a structured clinical record, accessible where appropriate to the patient and the wider team involved in care. This goes beyond the paper-free, amalgam-free clinic of incremental modernisation. What is imagined here for 2050 is already beginning to take shape.

It is tempting to stop there, in the comfortable territory of what science makes possible. But picture that same clinic again and ask a quieter question: who is sitting in the chair? For patients unable to secure publicly funded dental care in 2026, the gap between what dentistry can provide and what is actually available is already tangible: difficulty finding an NHS dental practice, delayed treatment, avoidable pain and, too often, tooth loss. By 2050, that divide could take a different form. The question may no longer be simply whether a patient can obtain care, but whether they can benefit from earlier diagnosis, personalised prevention, tissue-preserving treatment and regenerative technologies. Will those advances translate into mainstream oral healthcare according to need, or remain unevenly distributed?

Answering that requires a wider ambition for health creation.1, 2 Drawing on its use in health and place-based policy, the term is used here to mean the active production and preservation of oral health and function across the life course: preventing disease where possible, identifying risk earlier, preserving tissue, maintaining dignity and reducing avoidable deterioration before crisis care is required.

What we know but have not designed for

Health creation requires oral health to be treated as part of wider health across the life course. Yet oral health remains relatively separate from NHS policy and care, despite sharing determinants with other non-communicable diseases and contributing to outcomes beyond the mouth.3, 4 Poor oral health can affect a child's education,5 tooth loss can impair oral health-related quality of life,6 and poor oral health in older adults is linked to malnutrition, aspiration risk and frailty.7, 8, 9

Despite that knowledge, the reality in 2026 is that access to NHS dental care remains difficult for many people. The irony is that constrained access sits alongside a growing dental workforce,13 while the market is shifting towards mixed and private provision.14, 15 Healthwatch England reports the corresponding patient experience: private dental use rose from 22% in 2023 to 32% in 2025 and almost doubled among people struggling financially.16 The 2023 Adult Oral Health Survey also shows continuing unmet need and marked variation in attendance by deprivation.17 The problem is therefore not simply insufficient capacity, but how that capacity is distributed through publicly funded care.

The case for integration is also clinical. Periodontitis is independently associated with cardiovascular disease and diabetes18, 19; the relationship with diabetes is bidirectional, and periodontal treatment is associated with modest improvements in HbA1c.19, 20, 21 Periodontal disease has also been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, although evidence that treatment during pregnancy improves them remains uncertain.22

Taken together, these are not separate access and integration problems. They point to the same design challenge: a system that recognises oral health too late, too episodically and too separately from wider health. Health creation therefore requires care to move upstream, connect with relevant wider pathways and organise data, workforce and technology around preserving health and function before crisis occurs. Technologies developing now could materially change what dentistry is able to prevent, detect, preserve and treat by 2050 and how oral healthcare is integrated with wider care to enable that shift.

Technology as an accelerant

Regenerative biology is beginning to challenge the assumption that lost dental tissue can only be replaced. Organoids are being developed to model dental tissues,23, 24 while pulp-dentine regeneration techniques have entered clinical trials.24 Whole-tooth regeneration remains more distant,24, 25 but biomimetic materials offer a more immediate shift towards remineralisation. Nano-hydroxyapatite and bioactive glass are already used clinically and can enhance remineralisation of early enamel lesions.26, 27 Much regenerative science remains preclinical or translational, and its eventual clinical role is uncertain.

Digital technologies are further advanced. Artificial intelligence is increasingly applied to periodontal diagnostics across radiographic and intraoral imaging.28, 29 One multi-centre study reported performance superior to periodontal specialists in detecting stage II–IV periodontitis.29 Clinical judgement remains central, with AI supporting rather than replacing clinical expertise.28, 30 Remote monitoring and teledentistry can support contact, diagnosis and triage between visits, particularly in orthodontics and paediatric dentistry.31, 32 Caries detection may approach the accuracy of conventional examination, although periodontal assessment remains more difficult remotely.30 Near-patient and salivary diagnostics may provide further, less invasive ways of assessing risk.33

Together, these advances extend the window for intervention: disease can be identified earlier and care directed more consistently towards preserving health and function. They also create an equity challenge. If innovation develops principally through private care, differences in access to early diagnosis, prevention and tissue preservation could widen. A future NHS therefore needs mechanisms to evaluate innovation in populations with substantial disease burden and unmet need.

From repair to prevention, preservation and health creation

Technology delivers value only through the system in which it is used. The greater opportunity is to organise care so that disease is prevented, arrested or biologically repaired before it results in pain, extraction or loss of function. This shifts the purpose of care upstream. The traditional model (identify disease, remove damaged tissue, restore and replace) remains necessary, but advances in diagnostics, materials, prevention and data allow greater emphasis on preserving health and function.

Public health dentistry already demonstrates that principle: supervised toothbrushing, fluoride varnish, water fluoridation where locally appropriate and evidence-based prevention advice reduce disease before it presents as pain.34 Care-home mouth-care standards provide another example of prevention embedded in routine care.35 The challenge for 2050 is to connect these approaches to a coherent prevention infrastructure and continuing care.

Measuring what matters

Moving upstream also requires a different model of value. Activity is easy to count, but does not show whether disease was prevented, risk reduced or function preserved. A future system should give greater weight to prevention, stability, continuity, function and reduced urgent care.

Much of the infrastructure already exists. SNOMED CT provides standard clinical terminology in dentistry,36 alongside electronic referral, NHS-number-based data and professional record-keeping standards.37, 38, 39 By 2050, structured records could support care across settings, with AI-enabled decision support identifying significant findings or changing risk while clinicians retain responsibility for interpretation.37, 39

With appropriate consent and governance, relevant dental information could connect with wider health records and be accessible to patients.37, 38, 40, 41 Aggregated anonymised data could also show where disease burden is concentrating, allowing commissioning and workforce capacity to respond more closely to evidenced population need.

The 2050 oral healthcare workforce

No credible 2050 vision rests on training more dentists to deliver the same model. Expansion is necessary, but numbers alone will not resolve a design problem: skills, scope and accountability need to be organised across the whole team.42, 43

Dental practices will remain central to diagnosis, treatment planning, operative, urgent and specialist care. Dentists will retain essential roles in complex diagnosis, operative care and clinical leadership, while therapists, hygienists, dental nurses, clinical dental technicians and oral health educators work to their registered capability.42, 43 Depending on role and scope, this could include prevention, risk review, periodontal care, minimally invasive treatment, monitoring and follow-up.

Shared imaging, remote assessment and digitally enabled workflows may further redistribute elements of care, with professional responsibility clearly defined. The important question is how workforce and technology are organised around patient need.

Outreach and connected care for population oral health

Prevention and early identification could increasingly occur in schools, nurseries, pharmacies, care homes and family hubs, provided that identified need connects to a dental team able to respond. This does not require those settings to become dental practices. Their role is to extend reach through commissioned activity, agreed protocols, training and clear referral routes.34, 42 Distributed prevention only works where responsibility and capacity to act are clear.

Care homes illustrate the principle. NICE expects oral health to be embedded in daily care and referral pathways,35 while the Enhanced Health in Care Homes model already provides named clinical leadership, multidisciplinary working and shared care planning.44 An identified dental partner, with agreed referral routes and domiciliary or practice-based care according to need, could extend that architecture.

Two futures for 2050

The two futures that follow are scenarios, not predictions. Both extrapolate from current trends and assume continued scientific advance; the difference lies in how capability is organised and therefore in what patients experience.

Future A: health creation

It is 2050. A woman in her mid-forties with type 2 diabetes attends her local medical practice for her annual diabetes review. Following NICE guidance, which has long recommended that periodontitis risk is discussed as part of annual diabetes care,45 her GP asks about her oral health. Her diabetes is monitored continuously through a wearable device linked to her health record, while an intra-oral biosensor tracks selected markers of oral inflammation. A change in those data, alongside deteriorating glycaemic control and her report of recent gum bleeding, generates an oral-health risk flag. The record prompts connection with her dental team, allowing assessment to be arranged before pain or acute deterioration becomes the trigger for care.

With her agreement, the dental team can see the relevant information. Periodontal findings and imaging are added to the shared record, making the deterioration visible alongside her diabetes control. The dental team manages her periodontal disease while her diabetes clinician considers the findings alongside her metabolic control. She receives treatment and follow-up proportionate to risk, with personalised prevention, monitoring and recall adjusted as her clinical status changes.

The technology is largely invisible to her. Its value lies in what the system does with the information it generates: risk is recognised earlier, information follows the patient, and a change identified in one part of the NHS creates a route into appropriate care rather than disappearing at an organisational boundary.

Future B: residual service

It is also 2050. The same woman lives in the same town with the same type 2 diabetes. Dentistry has advanced just as far as in Future A, but she has not had access to the same connected model of care.

Her diabetes is monitored through the wider health system and, at her annual review, her GP discusses oral health risk in line with NICE guidance.45 She reports bleeding gums. There is no linked dental team, no intra-oral monitoring available to her through publicly funded care, and no pathway through which the information can trigger assessment. She is advised to arrange a dental appointment.

She has not seen an NHS dentist for several years. No local practice is routinely accepting new NHS patients and no dental team holds continuing responsibility for her care.

Her symptoms progress from bleeding gums to intermittent toothache. Without a preventive plan, structured follow-up or a route back to a team that knows her, she manages the symptoms until the pain becomes difficult to control. Eventually she contacts an urgent dental service, travels, takes time off work and has one tooth extracted. Advanced periodontal disease is recorded, but continuing periodontal care cannot be secured.

Her diabetes team does not receive the dental findings, and the urgent episode does not alter her wider care plan. The immediate problem is treated; the underlying disease remains.

The science is no different from Future A. What she lacks is the connected system that makes it useful: continuing responsibility, information that follows her and a pathway able to respond when risk changes. For those dependent on a narrower public offer, the inequality is no longer simply access to a dentist; it is access to continuity, prevention and the benefits of scientific progress.

The choice between them

Both futures contain advanced science, digital dentistry and skilled professionals. The difference is how capability is organised: around prevention, continuity and need in Future A, and episodic treatment and urgent demand in Future B.

Future B can emerge incrementally, without any dramatic policy decision: persistent difficulty accessing routine care, prevention disconnected from treatment, underused workforce capability, weak interoperability and innovation diffusing faster through private than public pathways.

Future A requires deliberate design. Achieving that future would require investment ahead of some measurable benefits, alignment between national contract reform and local commissioning, interoperable data, workforce confidence, appropriate indemnity arrangements and credible measures of prevention and outcomes. Organisational boundaries, competing service pressures and variation in local capacity would also need to be managed. These are substantial implementation challenges, but they do not alter the direction of travel.

What needs to change: five commissioning imperatives

If Future B is the consequence of drift, Future A will require purposeful commissioning. Five imperatives follow:

First, commission for health, not activity. Commissioning should reward prevention, risk reduction, stabilisation, continuity and measurable outcomes. Urgent care should connect patients to continuing care.

Second, build connected pathways. Where there is clinical purpose, oral health should connect with wider care pathways rather than remain organisationally separate. Shared protocols, referral routes and appropriate record connectivity are needed to make those pathways work in practice.

Third, build dental data infrastructure. A publicly funded dental care system cannot commission effectively for need it cannot see. Existing NHS infrastructure provides a foundation for structured, interoperable patient records and better population-level intelligence.36, 37, 38, 39

Fourth, use the whole workforce. Dental professionals should work to their full registered capability where they can have greatest impact, organising care around patient need rather than inherited patterns of delivery.42, 43

Fifth, translate innovation equitably. NHS early-adopter programmes should include populations with the greatest disease burden and unmet need, rather than concentrating innovation where implementation is easiest.

These imperatives are technical, but they are also distributive choices. A system that continues to tolerate preventable dental pain, avoidable childhood extractions and widening oral health inequality is determining who benefits from progress, even when that decision is made by omission.

Conclusion: designing for public value

By 2050, progress should be judged by whether oral healthcare prevents avoidable disease, preserves health and function, connects care around the patient and reduces inequality. Science is extending what dentistry can achieve; system design will determine who benefits.

Health creation for all requires oral health to be treated as part of whole-person health, prevention as clinical work, data as infrastructure and the wider dental team as essential capacity. Restoring access to the existing model alone will not be enough.

The task is to put the mouth back in the body: clinically, digitally, contractually and politically.
