A novel 3D cardiac microtissue model for investigation of cardiovascular complications in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Jan Wolnik, Patrycja Adamska, Aleksandra Oleksy, Anna Magdalena Sanetra, Katarzyna Palus-Chramiec, Marian Henryk Lewandowski, Józef Dulak, Monika Biniecka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects not only the joints but also has significant cardiovascular (CV) manifestations. The mechanistic interplay between RA and cardiovascular complications is not yet well understood due to the lack of relevant in vitro models. In this study, we established RA cardiac microtisses (cMTs) from iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (CMs), endothelial cells (ECs) and cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) to investigate whether this fully human 3D multicellular system could serve as a platform to elucidate the connection between RA and CV disorders.MethodsPBMC and FLS from healthy and RA donors were reprogrammed to hiPSCs with Sendai vectors. hiPSCs pluripotency was assessed by IF, FACS, spontaneous embryoid bodies formation and teratoma assay. hiPSCs were differentiated to cardiac derivatives such as CMs, ECs and CFs, followed by cell markers characterizations (IF, FACS, qRT-PCR) and functional assessments. 3D cMTs were generated by aggregation of 70% CMs, 15% ECs and 15% CFs. After 21 days in culture, structural and metabolic properties of 3D cMTs were examined by IF, qRT-PCR and Seahorse bioanalyzer.ResultshiPSCs demonstrated typical colony-like morphology, normal karyotype, presence of pluripotency markers, and ability to differentiate into cells originating from all three germ layers. hiPSC-CMs showed spontaneous beating and expression of cardiac markers (cTnT, MYL7, NKX2.5, MYH7). hiPSC-ECs formed sprouting spheres and tubes and expressed CD31 and CD144. hiPSC-CFs presented spindle-shaped morphology and expression of vimentin, collagen 1 and DDR2. Self-aggregation of CMs/ECs/CFs allowed development of contracting 3D cMTs, demonstrating spherical organization of the cells, which partially resembled the cardiac muscle, both in structure and function. IF analysis confirmed the expression of cTnT, CD31, CD144 and DDR2 in generated 3D cMTs. RA cMTs exhibited significantly greater formation of capillary-like structures, mimicking enhanced vascularization—key RA feature—compared to control cMTs. Seahorse examination of cMTs revealed changes in mitochondrial and glycolytic rates in the presence of metabolic substrates and inhibitors.ConclusionsThe cMTs model may represent an advanced human stem cell-based platform for modeling CV complications in RA. The highly developed capillary-like structures observed within RA cMTs highlight a critical feature of inflammation-induced CV dysfunction in chronic inflammatory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-024-03956-1.Keywords: Human pluripotent stem cells, Cardiac microtissue, Rheumatoid arthritis, Cardiovascular disease

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by joint inflammation, synovial tissue hypertrophy, joint effusions, and degradation of articular cartilage and bone. Chronic inflammation, driven by key pro-inflammatory cytokines in RA such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, significantly upregulates angiogenic factors like VEGF. These factors promote the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones, exacerbating joint inflammation and contributing to disease progression. Despite increased angiogenesis, the new blood vessels are often immature and dysfunctional, resulting in hypoxia, which perpetuate the inflammatory cycle in RA.

In addition to joint-related symptoms, RA is associated with a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular (CV) complications. This elevated risk is driven by high levels of pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic mediators, leading to a 35–50% lifetime risk of premature mortality and a reduction in lifespan by approximately 10 years [1]. Although it has been demonstrated that these mediators contribute to heart and vascular complications, with heart failure and accelerated atherosclerosis being the most common manifestations [2, 3], the advanced insights into the mechanisms linking RA and CV complications are particularly challenging. Obtaining RA-specific cardiac cells through biopsies or invasive cardiac procedures is not routinely performed. Additionally, the limited survival of cardiomyocytes after isolation and their non-proliferative phenotype restricts their application in extensive research. While studies often rely on primary cardiomyocytes derived from murine and rat hearts, these cells fail to fully replicate human cardiac physiology. Furthermore, strain-dependent genetic variations in animal models contribute to inconsistencies in preclinical studies. These limitations underscore the importance of establishing a novel, reliable model composed of RA-specific cardiac cells to accurately recapitulate the cellular interactions between joint immunology and CV manifestations.

The utilization of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to generate components of the myocardium that are highly inaccessible has opened new avenues for disease modeling, predicting drug-related cardiotoxicity, and testing cell therapies for the heart in a patient-specific manner. The heart is a complex organ composed of several cell types, including beating cardiomyocytes and specialized somatic cells such as cardiac fibroblasts and cardiac endothelial cells. A study by Litviňuková et al. provided a more comprehensive understanding of the cellular composition of the healthy adult human heart [4]. The study revealed that cardiomyocytes (CMs) constitute the largest population, comprising 30% in atrial tissues and 50% in ventricular tissues, followed by cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) or mural cells, and then endothelial cells (ECs) and immune cells. This detailed cellular map, combined with advanced methods of directing iPSC differentiation into cardiac derivatives, enhances our ability to model the heart accurately.

Recently, there has been considerable interest in the development of human 3-dimensional (3D) cardiac organoids, driven by their promising potential applications. Incorporating stromal cells into these 3D cardiac models enhances the maturation of hiPSC-CMs, which remain immature and fetal-like in 2D monolayer cultures [5]. The inclusion of various cell types in these cardiac organoids allows more closely resemble the complex structural and functional characteristics of native human heart tissue, including tissue architecture, gene and protein expression. Additionally, this approach enables the study of intercellular interactions and physiological responses within a controlled in vitro environment.

Here, we present a novel organoid-based 3D model of RA cardiac microtissues (cMTs), incorporating CMs, ECs and CFs derived from hiPSCs. We also provide the evaluation of standard structural and functional parameters on both individual hiPSC-derived cell types and the complete cardiac microtissues. This innovative model aims to recapitulate the impact of a pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic RA environment on cardiac cell functions. Furthermore, it has the potential to greatly facilitate precision medicine by evaluating the cardioprotective or cardiotoxic effects of anti-rheumatic drugs, offering significant prognostic value and improving treatment management.

Methods

PBMCs and FLS collection and reprogramming to hiPSCs

Sample donors (n = 3) were recruited from the Clinical Department of Rheumatology and Internal Medicine, Wrocław, Poland and from the Rheumatology Department of St. Vincent’s University Hospital, Dublin, Ireland and provided as a gift. The utilization of the human biological material in this research was approved by the Jagiellonian University Bioethical Committee. Following the isolation of PBMCs and FLS from the whole blood samples and from synovial biopsies respectively (Additional file 1: Supplemental methods), the hiPSC lines (HC n = 1 and RA n = 2) were generated using CytoTune™-iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Additional file 1: Supplemental methods). The schematic representation of PBMCs and FLS reprogramming to hiPSCs is shown in Fig. 1A. The hiPSC-HC line was derived from PBMCs of 45-year-old year healthy male, the hiPSC-RA1 line was derived from PBMCs of 52-year-old year RA female and the hiPSC-RA2 line was derived from FLS of 56-year-old RA female, who underwent arthroscopy of the inflamed knee joint.

hiPSCs culture

hiPSCs were cultured on Geltrex™ (ThermoFisher Scientific)-coated plates in Essential 8™ (E8) medium (ThermoFisher Scientific). The spent medium was replaced daily. Cells were passaged at 70–90% confluency using 0.5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen) and subsequently cultured in E8 medium supplemented with 10 µM Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor Y-27632 (Abcam) for 24 h after each passage to increase cell survival.

Assessing spontaneous hiPSCs differentiation in vitro and in vivo

In vitro spontaneous differentiation of obtained hiPSCs was examined via embryoid bodies (EBs) formation assay (for details see Additional file 1: Supplemental methods). In vivo monitoring of hiPSCs pluripotency was determined by teratoma formation assay on NOD SCID mice (The Jackson Laboratory), as previously described [6] (for details see (Additional file 1: Supplemental methods). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

This was followed by the application of IWR-1, a WNT inhibitor, to drive the cells into the cardiac mesoderm. Purification of hiPSC-CMs and hiPSC-ECs

Following the completion of the cardiac and endothelial differentiation procedures, single-cell suspensions were purified using Magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS) on an autoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). For cardiac differentiation, the suspension of heterogeneous hiPSC-CMs was incubated with the PSC-Derived Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). A two-step magnetic labelling and separation process was used to isolate hiPSC-derived CMs from non-CMs, following the manufacturer's protocol. For the endothelial differentiation, the suspension of heterogeneous hiPSC-ECs was incubated with the human CD31 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) to isolate CD31+ endothelial cells according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Generation and cultivation of 3D cardiac microtissues

Before forming cMTs, the efficiency of differentiating hiPSCs into CMs, ECs, and CFs was verified using flow cytometry. Additionally, to ensure the quality and specificity of the cardiac cell populations, cell-specific markers and gene expression were assessed through immunofluorescence staining and qRT-PCR, as described below. On the day of cMT formation (Day 0), hiPSC cardiac derivatives were detached as follows: hiPSC-CMs using Multi Tissue dissociation kit 3 (MTD3) (Miltenyi Biotec), hiPSC-ECs with Accutase (ThermoFisher Scientific), and hiPSC-CFs with TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (ThermoFisher Scientific), all for 5 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Each cell line was then centrifuged for 5 min at 200 × g and counted. The three cell suspensions were combined to a total of 5000 cells per 50 mL of cMTs culture medium, maintaining a composition of 70% CMs, 15% ECs, and 15% CFs. The cMTs culture medium was composed of Endothelial Cell Growth Medium MV2 (EGM) (PromoCell) supplemented with 50 ng/mL VEGF (StemCell Technology), 10 ng/mL bFGF (StemCell Technology) and 2% B-27 supplement (ThermoFisher Scientific). Mixed hiPSC-derived CMs, ECs, and CFs were seeded into U-bottom 96-well plates (faCellitate) and centrifuged for 10 min at 300 × g. Then cMTs were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and the cMTs culture medium was replaced every third day. Further assessments were performed after 21 days in culture.

OCT cryosections of 3D cMTs

To prepare cryosections, 3D cMTs were firstly collected into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min. Next, cMTs were washed with PBS (with Ca2+/Mg2+) and fixed with 4% PFA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 60 min. After fixation, the cMTs were washed again with PBS (with Ca2+/Mg2+) and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (Sigma Aldrich). The embedded samples were frozen and sectioned into 10 µm thick slices. These sections were air-dried for at least 1 h before being processed for immunofluorescence staining or stored for further analysis at -20 °C.

Flow cytometry

To immunophenotype, the samples were prepared utilizing protocols optimized for each cell type. Detailed information on the procedures is provided in Additional file 1: Supplemental Methods. Briefly, cells were harvested, fixed with 4% PFA (20 min/RT) and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (30 min/ice) (BioShop Canada Inc.). Next, cells were stained with appropriate primary antibodies (1 h/ice) and secondary antibodies (30 min/ice in the dark) (all listed in Additional file 2: Table S1.). Following incubations, cells were counterstained with 0.2ug/mL DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich). All samples were analyzed using LSRFortessa cytometer and FACSDiva™ software (BD Biosciences).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was used on hiPSCs, EBs, hiPSC-CMs, hiPSC-ECs, hiPSC-CFs, and 3D cMTs to identify the presence of cell-specific markers. Details on the incubation conditions are provided in Additional File 3: Table S2. Briefly, hiPSCs and hiPSC-CMs were seeded on Geltrex-coated 10 mm round cover glass slides placed in 24-well plate. Then, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X-100 and washed with PBS (w/o Ca2+/Mg2+). hiPSCs-ECs and hiPSC-CFs were seeded on 10 mm glass cover slip without Geltrex, fixed with cold methanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and washed with PBS (w/o Ca2+/Mg2+). To block non-specific binding sites, cells were incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (BioShop Canada) in PBS (blocking buffer). Then, cells were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in a blocking buffer overnight in the fridge (see Additional file 4: Table S3). The next day, cells were washed in PBS, incubated with secondary antibodies (see Additional file 4: Table S3) and the nuclei were counterstained with 0.2 µg/ml DAPI. The stained cells were then mounted with Dako Fluorescent Mounting Medium (Agilent) onto microscope slides. Stained 2D cell cultures were analysed on a microscope Axio Observer (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), while stained 3D cMTs were analysed with confocal microscopy using the Axio Observer.Z1 with the LSM 880 module (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

3D cMTs confocal imaging and analysis

For excitation, a diode laser (405 nm), an argon laser (488 nm), and diode lasers (561 nm and 633 nm) were used. Z-stacking was carried out with a Plan-Apochromat 20x/0.8 M27 objective, scanning each microtissue with a step size of 1.6 µm. Depending on the organoid size, 40–70 slices were captured from each cMT. Images were then analyzed using ImageJ (FIJI) software, with 3D modeling and z-stack processing modules. The Z-Project function was utilized to generate projections from stacks, with parameters set to the standard deviation of fluorescence to enhance internal structure differences and reduce noise. To measure the capillary-like structures formed by endothelial cells in the core of the cMTs, the ImageJ 3D OC module was used. Firstly, the Z-project from each cMT was prepared, excluding any CD31-positive cells located in the slides at the top and bottom surfaces of the cMT. Next, for each cMT, area with branched CD31 positive vascular-like structures was selected, excluding any cells localized at the side surface of image to avoid false-positive signals from cells forming the surface of the cMT. The selected areas containing CD31 positive signals only from cells within the core of the cMT were then measured. The 3D Object Counter function was used to map the network of vascular structures. Thresholds were set for each cMT to ensure consistent identification of vascular elements. Measurements of the mapped structures were then summarized and related to the total selected area from each organoid (see Additional file 5: Supplemental Fig. 1). Results were presented as a percentage of vascularization in the selected area of the cMTs.

Quantitative RT-PCR

For qRT-PCR, total RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using the NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). 0.5 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed using High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA™ Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The qRT-PCR reaction mixture included the following components: 7.5 µl of AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), 0.4 µl forward and 0.4 µl reverse primers (working concentration of 0.25 µM each), 4.7 µl nuclease-free water, and 2 µl cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed on StepOnePlus thermocycler (Applied Biosystem) in StepOne 2.3 Software according to standard protocol. Primer sequences are provided in Additional file 6: Table S4. The expression levels of two reference genes, TNNT2 and EFF2, were used for normalization. Data analysis was conducted using the ΔCt relative expression method.

hiPSC-CMs electrophysiology by patch-clamp

For the patch-clamp analysis, 7500 hiPSC-CMs were seeded onto 10 mm Geltrex™-coated round coverslips in RPMI/B-27. Three days after seeding, hiPSC-CMs were treated with TNF-α (10 ng/ml) (Bio-Techne/RD System) and IL-1β (1 ng/ml) (Bio-Techne/RD System). Following 24 h stimulations, the electrical activity of hiPSC-CMs was measured using a whole-cell current clamp, as described previously [12].

Endothelial sprouting assay

The angiogenic sprouting assay was performed as previously described [9]. The basic medium used for the solidified collagen was Endothelial Cell Basal Medium-2 (EBM) (PromoCell). The complete medium was EGM, supplemented with VEGF (15 ng/mL). The embedded spheroids were treated with TNF-α (10 ng/ml). The number of sprouts per spheroid and their lengths were quantified after 24–48 h, the sprouts were captured using phase-contrast microscope.

Matrigel™ tube-formation assay

Prior to the start of the experiment, 50 µL of Matrigel™ (Merck) was added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30–60 min to allow for polymerization. hiPSC-ECs were harvested, resuspended in the complete medium EGM, seeded on top of the solidified Matrigel™ at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and allowed to attach. Tube formation was monitored periodically over the incubation time (18–24 h of culture) and captured using phase-contrast microscope.

CFs activation

Cardiac fibroblasts were cultured in chamber slides under conditions promoting activation. The culture medium used was Fibroblast Growth Medium 3 (FGM3) (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 20 ng/mL FGF2 (PeproTech) and devoid of TGF-β inhibitor. To induce activation, cells were treated with 10 ng/ml TGF-β (PeproTech) for 48 h and then stained by immunofluorescence.

Metabolic functionality of whole cMTs

Oxidative respiration and glycolytic acidification were assessed using the Seahorse XFp Analyzer (Agilent), as previously published [13]. Briefly, cMTs were collected into a 15 mL Falcon tube and washed three times in cMTs culture medium containing 0.2% BSA. Three cMTs per well were then plated onto 8-well Geltrex-coated Seahorse plates. For mitochondrial stress test, measurements were performed in assay medium (XF DMEM Medium pH 7.4), supplemented with 15 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM L-glutamine (all from Agilent Technologies). Oligomycin, FCCP, Antimycin A and Rotenone (all from Sigma-Aldrich) were diluted in assay medium to final concentrations: 35 µM, 40 µM, 20 µM and 20 µM respectively and injected. For glycolytic stress test, measurements were performed in XF DMEM Medium supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate and 2 mM L-glutamine. Glucose and 2-DG (Sigma-Aldrich) were diluted in assay medium to final concentrations: 150 nM and 100 mM respectively and injected. Normalization was carried out by lysing the cMTs with MTD3 for 2 h at 37 °C, after which the number of dissociated cells was counted. Data analysis was performed using Seahorse Wave Desktop Software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD of three independent experiments, unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance was analysed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s test for multiple comparisons. Additionally, Wilcoxon Signed Rank test or Mann Whitney was used for analysis of nonparametric data. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, p-values of less than 0.05 (*p < 0.05) were determined as statistically significant.

Results

RA-specific hiPSCs exhibit typical hiPSC morphology and express pluripotent capacity

To develop a reliable 3D model of RA cardiac tissue, two hiPSC lines were derived from FLS and PBMCs of RA patients. Additionally, PBMCs from a healthy donor were used as a control. After collecting FLS and PBMCs, the cells were transduced using Sendai vectors (Fig. 1a). Emerging hiPSC colonies were observed from the 5th day of the procedure and allowed to grow until approximately the 15th day. At this point, the pure hiPSC colonies had reached an appropriate size for transfer. The obtained control and RA hiPSC lines demonstrated the characteristic morphology of stem cells, growing in round, compact colonies with smooth, well-defined edges (Fig. 1b). The karyotype of all hiPSCs was examined and revealed a normal chromosome structure, with no changes in ploidy or signs of large rearrangements (Fig. 1c). Subsequently, all hiPSC lines were subjected to several assays confirming their pluripotency. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that over 99% of the hiPSCs were positive for OCT3/4 and SSEA4 (Fig. 1d). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining confirmed the presence of pluripotent markers in the nucleus (NANOG, OCT3/4) and cytoplasm (TRA1-81 and SSEA4) (Fig. 1e). hiPSCs spontaneously differentiated in vitro via EBs into cells originating from the three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm), as demonstrated by the strong immunofluorescent signals of NFH, VIM, and GATA4, respectively (Fig. 1f, top panel). To further provide evidence of hiPSC pluripotency, a spontaneous differentiation assay in vivo via teratoma formation was conducted on NOD SCID mice. The developed teratomas contained tissues from all three germ layers, including neuroepithelial rosettes, muscles, and glandular structures (Fig. 1f, bottom panel).

Generation and assessment of functional patient-specific hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes

In order to generate functional CMs, hiPSCs were differentiated into CMs following the cardiac monolayer protocol [7, 8] as illustrated in the scheme (Fig. 2a). The concentrations and ratios of CHIR-99021 to IWR-1 were adjusted individually for each cell line to achieve high differentiation efficiency. In both control and RA monolayer differentiations, first spontaneous contractions were observed around day 7 and from day 10 forward cells formed a contracting network-like structures. Cardiac differentiations of hiPSCs routinely yield heterogeneous populations comprised of not only hiPSC-CMs, but also hiPSC-derived non-cardiomyocytes (non-CMs) in varying proportions. For this reason, after the completion of the differentiation process, a two-step magnetic labeling and separation of hiPSC-derived non-CMs from hiPSC-derived CMs was performed using autoMACS. As a result, the purification process successfully collected a highly enriched "CardioPure" hiPSC-CM population. The purity of cardiac differentiations was further validated by flow cytometry, revealing 79.8% positivity for the cardiac-specific marker cardiac troponin T (cTnT) in the CardioPure fraction. This is in contrast to 27.4% cTnT positivity in the unsorted population (a mix of iPSC-CMs and non-CMs) and 9.3% cTnT positivity in the depleted population of non-CMs (Fig. 2b, left panel). This distinction was also reflected in the different morphologies of each cell fraction. The unsorted population exhibited a mix of uncharacterized cells with both a patchy monolayer and 3D clump morphologies. The depleted non-CM population displayed a mesenchymal-like cobblestone monolayer, while the CardioPure fraction showed an elongated cardiac-like morphology (Fig. 2b, right panel). To further assess the cardiac features of purified hiPSC-CMs, including sarcomeric organization, protein and gene expression, analysis was performed for typical cardiac markers. The morphological structures of hiPSC-CMs were confirmed through positive IF staining targeting MYL2, Cx43, Hopx, NKx2.5, ACTN2, and MYH7 (Fig. 2c), with comparable expression patterns in both HC and RA donors. Next, the quality of hiPSC-CMs was assessed by qRT-PCR, which confirmed the high expression of cardiac genes such as ACTN2, MYH7, MYL7, TnnT2, and MYL2, in contrast to their lack of expression in the originating hiPSCs (Fig. 2d), with no difference in mRNA expression among cardiomyocytes derived from HC-iPSCs and RA-iPSCs.

Electrophysiological properties of hiPSC-CMs in RA proinflammatory milieu

We hypothesized that impaired cardiac electrophysiology could be a mechanism behind CV manifestations in RA. To explore this, we investigated whether the electrophysiological properties of hiPSC-CMs are altered in the presence of major pro-inflammatory mediators associated with RA. The whole-cell patch-clamp analysis shown changed action potential (AP) kinetics in the presence of TNF-α and IL1-β (Fig. 2e). Larger values of action potential duration at 90% (APD90) indicated slower repolarization in cells stimulated with TNF-α, but lower APD90 values were measured in IL1-β treated CMs from RA than from HC. The upstroke velocity, which represents the depolarizing phase of the action potential, significantly increased in TNF-α treated cells from RA patient. In turn, the parameter reflecting hyperpolarization following the action potential spike (AP through) was reduced in hiPSC-CMs treated with TNF-α and with IL-1β in RA patient, while IL-1β also decreased AP through when compared HC vs RA. The values of APD20, APD50, and action potential peak remained unchanged in cells stimulated with TNF-α (Additional file 7: Supplemental Fig. S2). However, APD20 and APD50 values were significantly reduced following IL-1β treatment (Additional file 7: Supplemental Fig. S2). Our findings of significantly altered action potential kinetics in RA suggest that pro-inflammatory profiles may be retained during reprogramming to iPSCs and their differentiation into CMs. Additionally, this indicates a differential sensitivity to various pro-inflammatory cytokines in the RA-specific CMs. The observed upregulation or downregulation of electrophysiological properties indicates the need for a more in-depth understanding of the mechanisms behind cytokine-dependent actions of ion channels.

Morphological characterization of hiPSC-derived ECs

Both control and RA hiPSCs were successfully differentiated into hiPSC-ECs, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Figure 3b shows the typical morphology observed on days 1–5 of differentiation. By day 8, two distinct cell layers were visible: non-endothelial cells formed the top layer and endothelial cells formed the bottom layer. Differentiating ECs could be seen through holes in the top cell layer, which typically formed a patchy network (Fig. 3c, right panel). Following endothelial differentiation on day 8, the cells were subjected to magnetic separation to enrich the endothelial fraction. The purity of the separated endothelial fraction was then validated by flow cytometry, which showed 94% positivity for endothelial-specific markers, such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule (CD31) and vascular endothelial cadherin (CD144) and typical EC-like morphology, compared to 27.3% positivity for unsorted cells (Fig. 3c). Immunofluorescence results further confirmed in purified iPSC-ECs the presence of endothelial markers, including CD31, CD144, von Willebrand factor (vWF), and phosphorylated endothelial nitric oxide synthase (p-ENOS) (Fig. 3d). Additionally, hiPSC-ECs were assessed by qRT-PCR, which confirmed the expression of CDH5 and PECAM1 (Fig. 3e).

Angiogenic properties of hiPSC-ECs

The angiogenic capacity of control and RA hiPSC-ECs was assessed by two commonly used in vitro assays. The tube-formation ability of the differentiated cells was observed in all lines, regardless of the donor (Fig. 3f). Furthermore, the response of hiPSC-ECs to angiogenic stimuli (VEGF) and pro-inflammatory stimuli (TNF-α) was tested using an endothelial sprouting assay (Fig. 3g). Both control and RA hiPSC-ECs formed sprouts in the 3D collagen matrix when VEGF was supplemented in the complete cell culture medium (EGM), in contrast to fewer or no sprouts observed in a basic medium (EBM) without growth factors and with a lower FBS concentration. Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of sprouts formed per sphere increased significantly when the cells were exposed to TNF-α (p < 0.05). In addition, the total length of sprouts per sphere varied significantly between donors, with the highest values observed in RA spheres cultured with VEGF alone and in the presence of TNF-α (p < 0.05 and p < 0.005, respectively) (Fig. 3g). This observation indicates that the obtained hiPSC-ECs are functional and possess angiogenic capacity. In the context of rheumatoid arthritis, RA-derived hiPSC-ECs exhibit greater sensitivity to stimuli, making them a valuable model for investigating endothelial dysfunctions in the pro-angiogenic and pro-inflammatory joint environment.

Derivation and characterization of hiPSC-derived CFs

To establish cardiac fibroblasts, all hiPSC lines underwent the differentiation protocol as depicted in Fig. 4a. Stage-dependent morphological changes were observed, with cells reaching typical fibroblast morphology by day 32 (Fig. 4b). To evaluate the efficiency of CF differentiation, cells were stained with Discoidin Domain Receptor (DDR2), a specific CFs marker and measured by FACS. The gating strategy was established to isolate DDR2-positive CFs from other cell populations. The analysis revealed that DDR2-positive cells comprised 83.5% of the total cell population (Fig. 4c). Next, the presence of fibroblast markers: vimentin (VIM), DDR2 and collagen I (COL I) was confirmed by IF staining (Fig. 4d). The qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significantly higher expression of DDR2 and POSTN in hiPSC-derived CFs in relation to undifferentiated hiPSCs (Fig. 4e). To confirm TGF-β induced activation of CFs, markers of the activation status of CFs (alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and COL I) were verified by IF and shown markedly increase presence of α-SMA and enhanced collagen I deposition in TGF-β stimulated cells when compared to unstimulated CFs (Fig. 4f). These results provide evidence that the hiPSC-derived cardiac fibroblasts generated in this study display biological properties closely matching those of primary cardiac fibroblasts.

Formation and characterization of 3D cardiac microtissue model

After establishing highly efficient hiPSCs differentiations to cardiac derivatives, cardiac MTs were aggregated from 5,000 cells (CMs, ECs, and CFs) in U-bottom 96-well microplates (Fig. 5a). Upon initial aggregation, the cells compacted into a dense, spherical shape (Fig. 5b). Over the 21-day culture period, the spheroids maintained their 3D structure with intact cell layers, uniformly distributed throughout the MTs, and demonstrated a homogeneous composition with no signs of necrotic cores, suggesting efficient nutrient and oxygen diffusion within the spheroids. Additionally, the proliferative potential of cells forming cMTs was evidenced by the presence of Ki67 in cells actively undergoing division, such as iPSC-ECs and iPSC-CFs (Additional file 8: Supplemental Fig. S3a). The microtissues began to contract within 5–10 days after seeding and continued to do so consistently throughout the entire culture period, as indicative of functional cardiac tissue capable of mimicking the beating of native heart tissue. This observation was further supported by the presence of Cx43, a protein integral to the maturation and functional integration of hiPSC-derived cardiac cells, as Cx43 enables direct intercellular communication by facilitating the formation of gap junctions between cells (Additional file 8: Supplemental Fig. S3b). By combining immunostaining and qRT-PCR, a comprehensive verification of the cMTs content was achieved. Assessment of the morphology and cellular architecture was performed on both intact cMTs and their cryosections. Co-expression of cTnT, CD31, and DDR2 was observed in all sample types (Fig. 5c). Moreover, a high relative expression of genes specific to CMs (ACTN2 and TnnT2), ECs (CDH5 and PECAM1), and CFs (DDR2 and POSTN) was detected in the formed cMTs (Fig. 5d). This gene expression profile further confirms the successful integration of these cell types within the cMTs, indicating a robust cellular composition and the potential for functional performance that closely mimics native cardiac tissue.

Distinct vascular network between control and RA cMTs

After confirming the multicellular composition of the cMTs, our subsequent goal was to explore potential differences in spheroid outgrowth morphology between control and RA cells. We stained intact control and RA cMTs to visualize the expression and localization patterns of cTnI, CD31, Cx43, vimentin, and COL I within the spheroids (Fig. 5e and Additional file 9: Supplemental Fig. S4). Interestingly, we observed a significantly enhanced endothelial network in RA cMTs compared to those formed from control cells. The capillary-like structures in RA cMTs were more dense and highly branched, with numerous interconnected cells forming a complex meshwork throughout the spheroids. This observation was consistent across both PBMCs and FLS-derived RA microtissues, reaching statistical significance (p < 0.01) for FLS-derived RA microtissues when compared to control (Fig. 5f). This enhanced network suggests active angiogenesis and remodeling processes within the RA iPSC-cMTs, indicative of robust ECs-ECs interactions contributing to the hyperproliferative and invasive nature of rheumatoid arthritis.

Metabolic functionality of whole cMTs using Seahorse analyzer

Next, the Seahorse analyzer was employed to assess the metabolic activity of whole 3D MTs formed from both control and RA donors (Fig. 5g). Unlike most studies that measure the energy metabolism of RA cells (such as synovial fibroblasts and PBMCs) in monolayers, our protocol involved measuring oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic activity directly in the 3D format. Importantly, this approach eliminates the need to dissociate the 3D structure and replate cells as single entities. As a result, the bioenergetic profile of the cells was characterized under consistent culture conditions, providing a more accurate recapitulation of the metabolic environment. The results showed that the cMTs exhibit distinct functional metabolic pathways, with RA patients demonstrating higher levels of both oxygen consumption rate and extracellular acidification rate compared to control donor. This initial validation highlights the potential physiological relevance and applicability of cMTs in translational research, offering deeper insights into the impact of energy metabolism on cardiovascular involvement in rheumatoid arthritis.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed a novel spheroid-based 3D model of rheumatoid arthritis cardiac microtissue by integrating hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts. This model represents a promising tool for investigating the mechanisms that underlie cardiovascular manifestations in RA, and potentially other inflammatory diseases. Following cell aggregation, the outgrowing spheroids spontaneously established capillary-like structures within the cMTs. Notably, the density of the endothelial network differed between control and RA cMTs, with a greater area of CD31-positive capillary-like structures observed in the RA-derived cMTs. This may indicate an activated proliferative and migratory capacity of RA hiPSC-specific cardiac derivatives under unstimulated conditions, suggesting they may harbour an invasive phenotype. It is well known that synovial cells of RA joint release pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α), which act synergistically. This synergistic effect was also confirmed in our study, as we observed the highest number and length of sprouts formed by RA hiPSC-ECs when stimulated with both VEGF and TNF-α. Therefore, our results confirm the integral role of endothelial cells to the formation, maintenance, and function of cardiac microtissue model in RA, a disease associated with dysfunctional angiogenesis [14].

In order to create cMTs, we firstly differentiated control and RA iPSCs into cardiac derivatives. Of note, iPSC-based approaches are in the early stages of study in RA research. Firstly, the group led by Ju was pioneering in generating RA-specific hiPSCs by reprogramming fibroblast-like synoviocytes obtained from knee synovial biopsies of patients undergoing arthroscopy [15, 16] Given the pivotal role that FLS play in the pathogenesis of RA, they emerge as prime candidates for reprogramming into iPSCs to establish RA disease-specific cells in vitro. Subsequently, our group achieved another milestone successfully generating and characterizing hiPSC lines not only from RA patient-specific FLS, but also from RA patient-specific PBMCs [17]. In line with the study by Lee et al., the generation of functional cardiomyocytes from RA-derived hiPSCs has revealed the presence of spontaneously beating cells, exhibiting a typical sarcomeric structure and expression of cardiac-specific markers [18]. Notably, there were observed differences in calcium-handling properties between RA-hiPSC-CMs and the control hiPSC-CMs. These differences were reflected in lower amplitudes and shorter durations of calcium transients in RA cells, indicative of decreased contractility in CMs derived from RA patients.

In the present study, we hypothesized that one of the mechanisms contributing to increased CV complications in RA can be impaired electrophysiology of cardiac cells, an effect driven by high-grade systemic inflammation. Previous research has indicated a link between RA-associated inflammatory cytokines and the QT interval on ECG, which represents the total time for ventricular depolarization and repolarization. Furthermore, studies suggest that a lower inflammatory burden may protect against QTc prolongation observed in RA patients, thereby reducing the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias [19]. Our study also identified significant direct effects of inflammatory cytokines on cardiac electrophysiology, particularly the alteration of action potential duration, which refers to the time required to complete repolarization phase. Both prolonged and shortened APD readings, observed in the presence of TNF-α and IL-1β respectively, may contribute to an increased risk of arrhythmias due to alterations in the contraction pattern of heart muscle cells. Therefore, impaired duration and shape of the action potential may reflect dysfunctional expression and activity of ion channels. Indeed, several studies have reported consistent data showing impaired functions of potassium and calcium channels in cardiac cells overexpressing TNF-α or in cardiac cells cultured with TNF-α [20–22]. To the best of our knowledge, no research groups have yet generated or characterized ECs and CFs derived from rheumatoid arthritis iPSCs. In the context of RA, the activation of endothelial cells and angiogenesis is a complex process that is central to and may be considered a prerequisite for synovial inflammation. ECs receive cues from specific cytokines and growth factors, including VEGF, IL-1, TNF-α, and IL-8, which initiate mitosis, migration, and organization of primitive angiogenic tubes, leading to the formation of mature vasculature [23–25] and upon macroscopic examination, RA synovium is characterized by a straight, regular branching pattern [26]. More importantly, the enhanced vasculature in RA is considered a central contributor to the cardiovascular complications seen in RA due to its critical role in the development of atherosclerotic plaques [27, 28]. Indeed, elevated levels of TNF-α are linked to endothelial dysfunction, a precursor to atherosclerosis, by promoting an increased risk of plaque rupture. IL-1β promotes the proliferation and activation of vascular smooth muscle cells and increases the production of other inflammatory mediators, exacerbating cardiovascular risk. Additionally, elevated IL-6 levels are associated with increased arterial stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, and the progression of atherosclerosis. In this study, we successfully obtained and characterized RA-specific ECs at a structural and functional level that had not been demonstrated in previous reports. RA-derived hiPSC-ECs displayed significant angiogenic capabilities, including tube and sprout formation. Notably, the number and length of sprouts differed between control and RA donors, with these parameters further increased in the presence of VEGF and TNF-α. This makes hiPSC-ECs a proper tool for investigating functional changes in microvessels in RA and other angiogenesis-associated diseases.

Multicellularity is crucial for disease modeling as it enables the creation of physiologically relevant models that mimic the intricate cellular interactions present in damaged tissues. This approach allows for a more accurate representation of the cellular dialogue and the complex environment seen in disease states [13]. Given that the heart comprises three major cell types—CMs, ECs and CFs—we formed cardiac organoid-like structures to evaluate whether this fully human 3D multicellular system could serve as a promising platform to link RA with cardiovascular changes. We found that the vascular cells formed capillary-like structures within our cardiac microtissues. To determine whether there were any vascular differences related to the disease state, we compared the density of these capillary-like structures in healthy control and RA. Applied semi-quantitative analysis, which measured the ratio between the total area of the cMTs core and the area coved by capillary-like structures (visualized by CD31 positive cells) revealed profoundly developed vascularization in RA, a critical feature to model inflammation-induced CV dysfunction.

In RA, despite increased vascular supply, the synovial joints often experience hypoxia due to dysfunctional and immature vasculature, leading to abnormal blood flow [29, 30]. Endothelial cell metabolism and metabolic reprogramming are critical in vessel sprouting and angiogenesis processes [31]. In the heart, energy metabolism is finely tuned to sustain high demands of normal cardiac function, maintaining continuous and intense contractile activity. Cardiovascular disorders disrupt this balance, causing metabolic shifts that impair ATP production, contribute to myocardial dysfunction, and exacerbate disease progression [32]. Given these considerations, we aimed to assess whether our 3D cardiac microtissue model could be examined using the Seahorse cell metabolism analyzer. Preliminary findings indicated metabolic remodeling in RA-derived cMTs, characterized by increased reliance on glucose metabolism and enhanced mitochondrial function compared to healthy controls. However, further investigations are necessary, particularly to delve deeper into the metabolic profiles of spheroids cultured in the RA milieu.

Conclusion

Many chronic inflammatory diseases are associated with various cardiac and vascular manifestations. This study is the first to describe the successful construction of 3D cardiac organoids using RA-specific hiPSC-derived cardiac derivatives. These cardiac organoids formed multicellular constructs comprising cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and cardiac fibroblasts, replicating the cell composition found in the human heart. Given that inflammation is a key trigger of RA, our cMTs could be further enhanced by incorporating macrophages and T lymphocytes into these 3D cardiac organoids, offering valuable insights into the role of immune cells in CVD within the context of RA. While this enhancement would enable the exploration of additional aspects of comorbid diseases, challenges would remain in precisely controlling the type and proportion of immune cells within each organoid. Moreover, significant challenges must still be addressed to fully unlock the potential of our 3D cardiac models in RA—challenges that were beyond the scope of this study. These include the introduction of inflammatory triggers, such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and hypoxia, as well as standard RA treatments, to evaluate the future potential of cardiac microtissues in studying rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammation-driven disorders.
