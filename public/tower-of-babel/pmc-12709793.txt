Integrating transcriptome and metabolome reveals sex-dependent meat quality regulation in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens
Authors: Meilin Hao, Yuxiao Xie, Chuntao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study integrated metabolomics and transcriptomics to elucidate molecular mechanisms regulating meat quality in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens across sexes and muscle types. Male chickens exhibited significantly higher shear force in breast and leg muscles (P < 0.01) and lower cooking loss in leg muscle (P < 0.05) compared to females. Metabolomic profiling identified 24 sex-differential metabolites, enriched in amino acid and nicotinate metabolism pathways. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 377 and 486 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in breast and leg muscles between sexes, respectively, associated with steroid biosynthesis and energy metabolism. Integrative analysis highlighted histidine metabolism (characterized by anserine accumulation in females via MAOA upregulation) and nicotinate metabolism as key sex-specific pathways. Tissue comparisons demonstrated distinct regulation of linoleic acid metabolism, purine metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. These findings provide novel insights into molecular networks underlying meat quality differences and identify potential biomarkers for enhancing nutritional value in poultry breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12316-z.

Introduction

Broiler chickens are highly favored due to their superior quality, affordability, and short production cycle. Importantly, they provide consumers with a safe and reliable source of animal protein. Recently, extensive research has focused on enhancing meat quality, as consumers increasingly prioritize the nutritional value and health benefits of their food [1]. The breast muscle of broilers, in particular, has garnered attention due to its direct correlation with both muscle yield and meat quality characteristics [2]. Meat quality is a multifaceted trait influenced by various physicochemical characteristics, including pH, tenderness, meat color, intramuscular fat content, fatty acid composition, and sensory quality. However, sex also plays a crucial role in determining these physicochemical parameters, as well as the growth and slaughter performance of broilers [3, 4].

The evident impact of sexual dimorphism on the size of carcasses directly affects other parameters of the slaughter performance of chickens and the quality of meat [3–7]. Researchers have investigated the chemical composition of the breast muscles of male and female farmed pheasants. Research has revealed that sex affects the total collagen content, suggesting that the tenderness and shear force of meat may vary between sexes [8]. Dariusz et al. (2018) reported that the shear force values of the breast muscle in 112-day-old male pheasants were greater than those in female pheasants [9]. Furthermore, since sexual dimorphism is believed to influence meat quality, one explanation for the variations in meat quality could be the differences in androgen levels between sexes. These hormones significantly impact lipid metabolism in birds [4]. Śmiecińska et al. (2024) demonstrated that muscle type (leg vs. breast) significantly influences both individual fatty acid (FA) content and the overall FA profile of intramuscular fat (IMF) in poultry [10]. Furthermore, research indicates that hen muscle tissue contains higher inosine monophosphate (IMP) concentrations than rooster muscle tissue, with breast muscle exhibiting significantly greater IMP levels than leg muscle [11]. Compared to breast muscle, leg muscle displays higher pH, increased cooking loss, reduced lightness (L*), and lower drip loss.

Metabolomics quantitatively and qualitatively analyzes small-molecule metabolites. LC‒MS technology enhances chromatographic separation and enables precise metabolite mass measurement with improved sensitivity, accuracy, and precision [12]. RNA-Seq extensively characterizes genetic expression differences related to meat quality. Li et al. (2022) [13] used LC‒MS/MS metabolomics to study chicken meat composition across growth stages, revealing significant variations in metabolites like pentadecanoic acid and identifying key functional genes influencing chemical composition and metabolic pathways, such as DGAT2, CYP2D6, APOV1, PLTP and PNMT. This provided insights into metabolic changes during growth and highlighted regulatory genes affecting meat quality and flavor. The integrative analysis of the metabolome and transcriptome would be a breakthrough in identifying the function of unknown genes and determining the gene-to-metabolite correlations. These data could enhance our understanding of chicken physiology, aiding in better breeding and production practices.

The Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chicken is a distinctive indigenous breed known for its exceptional meat quality and rich flavor. Primarily raised in the southeast region of Guizhou Province, China, this breed is characterized by unique features, such as vibrant feathers and black bones. They are typically raised for 5–6 months before market sale to achieve optimal taste and texture [14]. Few studies to date have examined the effects of sex using integrated transcriptome and metabolome analyses. Furthermore, molecular characterization of their meat quality is limited. In this study, we conducted joint transcriptome and metabolome analyses to investigate metabolite level changes between female and male Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens. Additionally, we examined key pathways differentially regulated between breast and leg muscles. These findings may elucidate underlying mechanisms and identify potential biomarkers for selection strategies to enhance chicken meat nutritional value.

Materials and methods

Animals and sample collection

The animals used in this experiment were provided by the Guizhou Rongjiang Shanong Development Co., Ltd. One-day-old male and female chickens were randomly selected and subjected to identical conditions, including free access to both water and a regulated diet, until they reached 20 weeks of age. A three-phase feeding regimen was implemented following the protocol outlined in Daszkiewicz et al. (2020) [8]. The basal diet was formulated to meet the nutrient requirements of Nutrient Requirements of Chinese MeatType Yellow Feathered Chickens. Detailed information on the chemical composition of the diets administered to the chickens can be found in Table S1. Chicks were randomly distributed into pens in a conventional indoor facility. The facility contained fans to ventilate and cool the pens. Each indoor pen was the size of 1.8 m×1.8 m and contained wood shavings, water bowls, and hanging tube feeders. The chicken bred indoor pens were supplied with a constant photoperiod of 24 h. Male (roosters, n = 30) and female (hens, n = 30) Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens were slaughtered at 20 weeks of age. Prior to being slaughtered, the chickens underwent a 12-hour fasting period, although they were given free access to water. They were humanely stunned via a stun bath and subsequently euthanized by cutting. The leg muscles (gastrocnemius, soleus, and extensor digitorum longus) and breast muscles were collected for analysis. The samples were placed in sterile tubes and fast-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then they were transferred to a −80 °C ultra-low temperature refrigerator for subsequent metabolome analysis. Muscle tissue from the same chicken was collected in a sterile tube, and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80 °C for subsequent transcriptome and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. Samples were designated as: F*_T (female leg muscle, Group A), F*_X (female breast muscle, Group B), M*_T (male leg muscle, Group C), and M*_X (male breast muscle, Group D).

Meat quality measurement

Thirty individual chickens, comprising both males and females, were randomly selected for measurement of meat quality characteristics. The pH of the breast meat was measured twice: once at 45 min postslaughter and again at 24 h postslaughter. This measurement was performed via a precise two-point calibration method, employing standard pH buffers of 4.0 and 7.0, in accordance with established protocols. Drip loss was measured as a percentage of the initial weight of the meat samples compared to their weight after being stored for 24 h at 4 °C. Cooking loss was calculated by the weight difference between the fresh samples and the heat-treated samples. Meat color parameters, including lightness (L*), redness (a*) and yellowness (b*), were measured at 24 h postmortem at 4 °C with a CR-400 chromometer (Konica Minolta Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Tenderness was assessed via a digital tenderness meter (C-LM4, Tenovo, Beijing, China) operating at a speed of 200 mm/min.

Metabolite extraction and data analysis

For each experimental group (F*_T, F*_X, M*_T, M*_X), tissue samples from three independent biological replicates were analyzed. Following dissection, pectoral and leg muscle tissues were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen tissues were then sectioned into approximately 80 mg fragments on dry ice. These fragments were transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf tubes and homogenized with 200 µL of ultrapure water using five ceramic beads in a mechanical homogenizer. For metabolite extraction, precisely weighed tissue samples were placed in 2 mL centrifuge tubes containing 1000 µL of extraction solution (75% methanol: chloroform [9:1] and 25% water) along with three stainless steel beads. Homogenization was performed using a bead mill, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane into LC‒MS vials for subsequent analysis.

LC analysis was conducted via an ACQUITY UPLC System (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved with an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min, and the gradient elution was programmed as follows: 0–1 min, 95% A; 1–10 min, 95–5% A; 10–12 min, 5% A; 12–12.1 min, 5–95% A; and 12.1–15 min, 95% A for column re-equilibration. Mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed on Q Exactive(Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with ESI ion source. Simultaneous MS1 and MS/MS (Full MS-ddMS2 mode, data-dependent MS/MS) acquisition was used. The parameters were as follows: sheath gas pressure, 30 arb; aux gas flow, 10 arb; spray voltage, 3.50 kV and − 2.50 kV for ESI(+) and ESI(-), respectively; capillary temperature, 325 ℃; MS1 range, m/z 100–1000; MS1 resolving power, 70,000 FWHM; number of data dependant scans per cycle, 10; MS/MS resolving power, 17,500 FWHM; normalized collision energy, 30 eV; dynamic exclusion time, automatic.

Raw data were converted to mzXML format using MSConvert in ProteoWizard (v3.0.8789). Converted files were processed with XCMS for feature detection, retention time correction, and alignment. Peaks were identified and annotated using Metlin, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Data normalization was performed using the robust LOESS signal correction (QC-RLSC) method to mitigate systematic biases. After normalization, ion peaks with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below a threshold were selected for further analysis to ensure reliability. Metabolite identification was conducted by comparison with authentic standards and reference data. Multivariate analyses and modeling were performed using ropls software. After scaling, models were built using principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Each model effectively distinguished sample groups. Permutation testing was applied to assess overfitting risk. Descriptive performance was measured using cumulative R2X and R2Y; an ideal model achieves R2X = 1 and R2Y = 1. Predictive performance was assessed via Q2, which should be close to 1 for optimal models. Permutation testing ensured reliability, with R2 and Q2 values from permuted data significantly lower than the original. Using OPLS-DA, significant metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) scores. Metrics including P-values, VIP scores, and fold changes were evaluated. Ultimately, metabolites with a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg method) and VIP score > 1 were considered statistically significant.

RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis

For each experimental group (F*_T, F*_X, M*_T, M*_X), three independent biological replicates (n = 3) were analyzed. Total RNA was isolated from breast and leg tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and agarose gel electrophoresis, while RNA purity was measured with a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Raw sequencing data often contain adapter sequences and low-quality reads; these were removed using Cutadapt (v1.15) to generate high-quality clean data for downstream analysis. Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted via DESeq (version 1.30.0) with the following criteria: an absolute log2-fold change > 1 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P value < 0.05 to control false discovery rate (FDR). Additionally, the Euclidean method was employed to calculate the distances between samples, followed by hierarchical clustering via the complete linkage method.

Validation of RNA-seq data by qRT-PCR

To validate the results of the transcriptome data, 5 DEGs were randomly selected for qRT-PCR analysis. The specific primer sequences are as follows: aldehyde dehydrogenase 7 family member A1 (ALDH7A1, 5 F: cacccattgtccacacagag; R: caatctctgccccacttgtt), adenosine monophosphate deaminase 3 (AMPD3, 5 F: gcagaacttggcaaaatggt; R: tgggattgattgttgcttca), glutathione S-transferase theta 1 (GSTT1, 5 F: acaacatcccgttcgagttc; R: ttagcatgatgccacgagag), fatty acid elongase 1 (ELOVL1, 5 F: cttgtgttgggcatccttct; R: ccctgccatcagaaactcat), myosin light chain 9 (MYL9, 5 F: ggttcattcacgaggaccat; R: ttgtccttagctccgtgctt). Total mRNA from the samples was extracted using the Trizol Kit. cDNA synthesis was then performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the Roche Step One real-time PCR system (Roche, USA). PCR amplification of all samples was performed in triplicate. β-Actin (5 F: tgcgtgacatcaaggagaag; R: tgccagggtacattgtggta) was used as the normalization control to calculate the relative expression levels.

Statistical analysis and integrative multi-omics analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard errors. Statistical comparisons for phenotypic and targeted omics data were conducted using Student’s t-test in SPSS Statistics (v28.0, IBM), with P < 0.05 considered significant. To elucidate the molecular networks regulating carcass characteristics, we systematically integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log2FC| >1, P-adj < 0.05 from DESeq2) and significantly different metabolites (SDMs; VIP > 1 from OPLS-DA, P < 0.05) were mapped to KEGG pathways using the KEGG Mapper-Search&Color Pathway tool (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Common KEGG pathways significantly enriched (P < 0.05) by both DEGs and SDMs were identified for each comparison group using Fisher’s exact test in the clusterProfiler R package (v4.6.2). Pathways meeting this dual-enrichment criterion were designated as key regulatory pathways. Pairwise pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the abundance levels of SDMs and the expression levels of DEGs within these common pathways, using the cor() function in R (v4.3.1). Only gene-metabolite pairs with a strong correlation (|r| >0.8) and statistical significance (P < 0.05) were retained for network construction. Metabolite pathway enrichment analysis, based solely on SDMs, was performed using the Pathway Analysis module of MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca) with the Gallus gallus pathway library. Pathways with P < 0.05 from the hypergeometric test and a pathway impact score > 0.1 were considered significantly enriched. Correlation coefficients between metabolites were visualized as heatmaps using the ComplexHeatmap R package (v2.16.0).

Results

Meat quality traits by sex and muscle type

At 20 weeks, male chickens exhibited significantly higher live body weight than females (Fig. 1A). The shear force of both breast and leg muscles was greater in males than in females (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). For breast muscles, no significant sex-based differences were observed in drip loss or cooking loss (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1C and D). However, male leg muscles showed lower cooking loss (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). Regarding meat color, female leg muscles had higher brightness (L*) (P < 0.05) but lower redness (a*) (P < 0.001) and yellowness (b*) (P < 0.05) compared to males (Fig. 1E and G). In breast muscles, females exhibited lower redness (a*) (P < 0.001) and higher yellowness (b*) (P < 0.05) than males (Fig. 1F and G). Male leg muscles also displayed lower yellowness (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1G). Post-mortem pH (measured at 45 min and 24 h) did not differ significantly between sexes in either muscle type (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1H).

Metabolic profiles of the breast and leg muscles of female and male chickens

Metabolite classification and PCA/OPLS-DA analysis

To examine the metabolic differences between female and male chickens, liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC‒MS) was performed in both positive and negative ion modes. A total of 6,483 metabolites were identified in the positive ion mode, while 8,358 metabolites were detected in the negative ion mode. These metabolites, including those observed in both modes, were subsequently categorized based on their chemical taxonomy. The analysis revealed that the top five classes among the identified metabolites were carboxylic acids and derivatives (13%), endogenous metabolites: natural products/medicines (11%), organooxygen compounds (9%), benzene and substituted derivatives (8%) and fatty acyls (7%) (Fig. 2A). Amino acids, peptides, and analogs had the highest proportion among the classified subclasses (13%) (Fig. 2B). In the comparison of female and male chicken breast muscles, the PCA accounted for 26.4% of total variance by the first principal component (PC1) and 21.7% by the second principal component (PC2) (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, for the leg muscles, PCA explained 26.4% (PC1) and 21.7% (PC2) of the total variance (Fig. 2C, D). The PCA score plot revealed that 37.6% of the variation between the breast and leg muscles of male chickens could be explained by two components, whereas 35.2% of the variation between the breast and leg muscles of female chickens could be explained by two components (Fig. 2E and F). The OPLS-DA plots revealed clear distinctions in metabolite profiles across the four comparative groups (Fig. 2G and J). The OPLS-DA models exhibited strong performance, with explained variation (R2Y) and predictive ability (Q2) values both exceeding 0.5 and R2Y values surpassing 0.9. These metrics indicate that the models are robust and suitable for subsequent analysis.

Sex-specific differential metabolites

Using VIP scores from OPLS-DA models, we identified significantly different metabolites (SDMs) with the criteria VIP > 1 and p < 0.05. In the breast muscle, we observed an upregulation of 8 metabolites in male chickens, with a distinct predominance of 25-hydroxycholesterol, 1-pyrroline-2-carboxylic acid and oxidized glutathione. Similarly, in the breast of female chickens, we observed an upregulation of 16 metabolites, prominently featuring pelargonic acid and NAD (Fig. 2K and Table S2). Compared with those in F*_T, a total of 10 metabolites were upregulated in M*_T, with the majority of these upregulated metabolites being pelletierine, N-a-acetylcitrulline and L-valine (Fig. 2L and Table S3). Using the VIP scores obtained from the OPLS-DA models, we identified differentially accumulated metabolites by applying the criteria of VIP > 1 and P < 0.05. This result indicates that carboxylic acids and their derivatives are the predominant differentially abundant metabolite types distinguishing female and male chickens within the same tissue. Interestingly, within the same sex, a greater number of differentially abundant metabolites were identified between the breast and leg muscles. In M*_X, a total of 31 metabolites were differentially upregulated compared with those in M*_T. Conversely, in M*_T, 20 metabolites were differentially upregulated (Fig. 2M; Table S4). The 31 metabolites that were upregulated in M*_X were mainly identified as oxidized glutathione, 1-arachidonoylglycerol, and adenosine diphosphate ribose. On the other hand, the 20 metabolites that were upregulated in M*_T were predominantly categorized as bovinic acid and 13S-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid. On the other hand, compared with those in F*_T, 34 metabolites were upregulated in F*_X, whereas 19 were upregulated in F*_T (Fig. 2N; Table S5). The 34 upregulated metabolites in F*_X were associated with L-valine, dihydroxyacetone phosphate, gamma-L-glutamyl-D-alanine, glycerophosphocholine and NAD, whereas the 19 upregulated metabolites in F*_T were primarily anserine and thioguanine. These findings indicate that carboxylic acids and their derivatives, fatty acyls, and glycerophospholipids are the primary categories of differentially abundant metabolites when sex and tissue type are compared. The VIP scores were used to pinpoint crucial metabolites showing significant content differences. In the breast muscle of female and male chickens, the top five candidate biomarkers, namely, lysoPA, diethylstilbestrol, D-galactose, erucic acid and gabapentin, were upregulated in males compared with female chickens. Conversely, 1-pyrroline-2-carboxylic acid, aminophenazone, NAD, 25-hydroxycholesterol and myristoleic acid were downregulated in M*_X. In the leg muscle of female chickens, specific compounds such as NAD, norepinephrine, 6,8a-Seco-6,8a-deoxy-5-oxoavermectin ‘’2b’’ aglycone, methyldopa, and prostaglandin H2 were enriched. Conversely, in the leg muscle of male chickens, different compounds, including 1-arachidonoylglycerol, L-valine, p-cresol sulfate, 3-methylhistidine, dopamine, N-acetylputrescine, 4-pyridoxic acid, and N-a-acetylcitrulline, were more enriched. In the comparison of differentially abundant metabolites between M*_X and M*_T, several key metabolites were identified. Notably, oxidized glutathione, 1-arachidonoylglycerol, 13 S-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid, adenosine diphosphate ribose, and anserine were among the top differentiated metabolites. On the other hand, the five dominant metabolites in M*_T are 12,13-epoxy-9(Z)-tridecenoic acid (12,13-EpOME), undecanoic acid, guanosine monophosphate (GMP), 6-hydroxynicotinic acid, and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. In F*_X, significant metabolites included L-valine, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), and γ-L-glutamyl-D-alanine. In F*_T, there was an increasing trend for docosapentaenoic acid (22n-3), biotin, oleic acid, and 3’-adenylic acid (3’-AMP).

Pathway enrichment analysis

The biological functions of the differentially abundant metabolites were determined through KEGG pathway analysis via MetaboAnalyst 5.0. In the breast muscle between the female and male chickens, several pathways were significantly enriched (P < 0.05), including the intestinal immune network for IgA production and histidine metabolism (Table S6, Fig. 2O). In the leg muscle between female and male chickens, the differentially abundant metabolites were involved in nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism (P < 0.05) (Table S7, Fig. 2P). Regarding the differentially abundant metabolites between M*_X and M*_T, the identified pathways included linoleic acid metabolism, purine metabolism, vascular smooth muscle contraction, the intestinal immune network for IgA production, and galactose metabolism (Table S8, Fig. 2Q). Conversely, the metabolites that varied between F*_X and F*_T were predominantly associated with linoleic acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation (Table S9, Fig. 2R).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between female and male chickens

Transcriptomic profiling and 3DPCA

A total of 42,627,098–48,522,692 raw reads and 40,398,516–45,940,730 clean reads were obtained through transcriptome analysis of each muscle tissue sample (Table S10). The alignment rate of these clean reads to the reference genome varied from 93.17% to 95.30%, with each sample displaying uniquely mapped reads within this range (Table S10). Specifically, the 3DPCA (Three-Dimensional Principal Component Analysis) analysis explained 79% (F*_X vs. M*_X), 67% (F*_T vs. M*_T), 79% (M*_T vs. M*_X), and 81% (F*_T vs. F*_X) of the variability (Fig. 3A-D).

Sex-specific DEGs

We identified 211 upregulated and 166 downregulated DEGs between F*_X and M*_X (Fig. 3E; Table S11). In the DEGs of the top 10 upregulated, growth arrest and DNA damage inducible gamma (GADD45G), protogenin (PRTG), homeobox A1 (HOXA1), nyctalopin, transmembrane protein 86 A (TMEM86A), synaptosome associated protein 47 (SNAP47), bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7), caspase 10 (CASP10), CCAAT/enhancer binding protein delta (CEBPD), and solute carrier family 41 member 3 (SLC41A3) are the gene with the most significant difference. In the DEGs of the top 10 down-regulated, histidine triad nucleotide binding protein W (HINTW), phospholipid phosphatase related 4 (PLPPR4), fucosyltransferase 7 (FUT7), BARX homeobox 2 (BARX2), TNFAIP3 interacting protein 3 (TNIP3), C-C motif chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6), follistatin like 4 (FSTL4), marker of proliferation Ki-67, keratinocyte differentiation factor 1 (KDF1), and RAD51 recombinase are the gene with the most significant difference. The top ten enriched biological processes were sterol biosynthetic process, positive regulation of biological process, cholesterol biosynthetic process, secondary alcohol biosynthetic process, immune system development, system development, cellular response to chemical stimulus, response to organonitrogen compound, cellular response to organic substance, and hemopoiesis (Fig. 3F). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in metabolic pathways, including steroid biosynthesis, histidine metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, glycerophospholipid metabolism, drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 3G). By comparing the leg muscles of female and male chickens, we identified 486 DEGs, with 172 upregulated and 314 downregulated genes (Fig. 3H; Table S12). In the DEGs of the top 10 upregulated, short stature homeobox 2 (SHOX2), annexin A13 (ANXA13), cysteine and glycine rich protein 3 (CSRP3), 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1E (HTR1E), myosin light chain 3 (MYL3), solute carrier family 10 member 4 (SLC10A4), growth arrest and DNA damage inducible gamma (GADD45G), cochlin (COCH), follicle stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR), and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 1 (PCSK1) are the gene with the most significant difference. In the DEGs of the top 10 down-regulated, histidine triad nucleotide binding protein W (HINTW), ubiquitin associated protein 2 (UBAP2), 60 S ribosomal protein L17, retinol binding protein 7 (RBP7), cell death inducing DFFA like effector c (CIDEC), perilipin 1 (PLIN1), joining chain of multimeric IgA and IgM (JCHAIN), myosin VIIA (MYO7A), somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2), and polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 6 (GALNT6) are the gene with the most significant difference. These DEGs were enriched in various functional categories, including immunological memory formation process, cell surface, immunological memory process, myosin complex, cell surface receptor signaling pathway, CNTFR-CLCF1 complex, cellular water homeostasis, embryonic genitalia morphogenesis, extracellular region, and tissue development (Fig. 3I). Additionally, through KEGG analyses, we identified several top pathways that were enriched among the DEGs. These pathways included metabolic pathways, steroid biosynthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, galactose metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, one carbon pool by folate, neomycin, kanamycin and gentamicin biosynthesis, folate biosynthesis, ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis, mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism (Fig. 3J). These results suggest that these pathways play crucial roles in influencing the differences in meat quality between female and male chickens.

Tissue-specific DEGs

We also examined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these two chicken tissues. A comparison of M*_T and M*_X revealed 652 DEGs whose expression was upregulated and 1236 DEGs whose expression was downregulated (Fig. 3K; Table S13). In the DEGs of the top 10 upregulated, immunoglobulin-like and fibronectin type III domain containing 1, troponin C1 (TNNC1), SIM bHLH transcription factor 2 (SIM2), alanine–glyoxylate aminotransferase 2 (AGXT2), FERM domain containing 5 (FRMD5), plakophilin 1 (PKP1), gap junction protein beta 2 (GJB2), gap junction protein beta 6 (GJB6), four and a half LIM domains 2 (FHL2), and apolipoprotein C3(APOC3) are the gene with the most significant difference. GO analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in several biological processes and molecular functions, including oxidation‒reduction processes, generation of precursor metabolites and energy, energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds, NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) activity, NADH dehydrogenase (quinone) activity, and phosphate-containing compound metabolic processes (Fig. 3L). Furthermore, in the comparison between F*_T and F*_X, a greater number of DEGs were observed. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the DEGs were involved in metabolic pathways; glycolysis/gluconeogenesis; fatty acid degradation; pyruvate metabolism; glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism; propanoate metabolism; and valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation (Fig. 3P). These findings suggest that the DEGs between the breast and leg muscles play crucial roles in determining muscle phenotype.

qRT-PCR verification of DEGs

To provide additional evidence for the gene expression profiles recorded at the transcriptome level, the differential expression of 5 selected DEGs like ALDH7A1, AMPD3, GSTT1, ELOVL1, and MYL9 was validated by qRT-PCR. The expression pattern of the selected genes obtained by quantitative Real Time PCR in additional cohort of samples was in agreement with the RNA-seq data, although the magnitude was variable (Fig. S2).

Discussion

The quality of poultry products, including nutrient content and functional properties, is influenced by factors such as intensive selection, genotype, age, sex, and production system. Typically, males exhibit greater daily weight gain and carcass weight, correlating with other parameters [3]. This study assessed the meat quality of Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens, revealing significant sex-based differences in key physicochemical indicators. Shear force, a measure of tenderness, was significantly higher in males (P < 0.01), consistent with findings in pheasants [8, 9], suggesting inherent sex differences in muscle collagen. Water retention capacity, evaluated via cooking loss and drip loss, varied: breast muscle showed no sex difference (P >0.05), while leg muscle from males exhibited significantly lower cooking loss (P < 0.05), indicating superior water-holding capacity. Drip loss was unaffected by sex (P >0.05), aligning with studies on pH-related stability [15]. These differences influence juiciness and yield, potentially linked to intramuscular fat content and fatty acid profiles [4, 10]. Meat color, assessed by lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*), displayed sexual dimorphism: female leg muscle had higher L* but lower a* and b* than males (Fig. 1E-G); female breast muscle also showed lower a* (P < 0.001) and higher b* (P < 0.05) than male breast muscle. The elevated b* in females corroborates findings in other poultry [4, 16, 17], potentially due to lipid deposition. These color parameters serve as vital visual cues for consumer acceptance. Sex had no effect on drip loss, consistent with pH-associated parameters [15].

In this study, we compared the metabolic differences between female and male Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chicken muscles for the first time. Muscle metabolites serve as vital indicators for assessing the metabolic profile of the two muscle types in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens. Through PCA and OPLS-DA analyses, significant differences in metabolic profiles among the groups were revealed. A total of 24 metabolites were differentially regulated between M*_X and F*_X. Among these, 12 metabolites, which primarily belong to the class of carboxylic acids and their derivatives, were significantly upregulated in female chickens. KEGG analysis revealed that the differentially abundant metabolites between M*_X and F*_X, as well as M*_T and F*_T, were significantly enriched in pathways related to amino acid metabolism and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism. These pathways are crucial for amino acid biosynthesis. In our study, we observed differential regulation of several metabolites between female and male chickens. The metabolites included 25-hydroxycholesterol, 1-pyrroline-2-carboxylic acid, oxidized glutathione, pelargonic acid, NAD, pelletierine, N-α-acetylcitrulline, L-valine, 1-arachidonoylglycerol, and 4-pyridoxic acid. L-valine is one of the nine essential amino acids that cannot be synthesized de novo by higher organisms and must be obtained from the diet. This amino acid not only serves as a building block for proteins but also plays critical roles in regulating protein and energy metabolism and participating in neurotransmission [18]. The oxidation of glutathione (GSH) to its disulfide form (GSSG) is the primary mechanism by which cells regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and alleviate oxidative stress [19]. 4-Pyridoxic acid is one of the two major vitamin B6 compounds. It serves as a cofactor in the expression of sex hormone genes and the metabolism of neurotransmitters by facilitating the conversion of tryptophan to serotonin. Additionally, it acts as a cofactor in the synthesis of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [20]. N-acetylcitrulline is a metabolite of citrulline that participates in the arginine biosynthesis pathway [21]. Lycopodine is considered a potential component of Lycopodium alkaloids (LAs) and exhibits significant acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitory activity, cytotoxicity, and neuroprotective properties [22]. 25-Hydroxycholesterol is produced in the endoplasmic reticulum of most cells, where it acts as a potent regulator of cholesterol synthesis, intracellular transport, and storage. During inflammatory responses, its production is significantly increased in macrophages, dendritic cells, and microglia [23]. The differential regulation of these metabolites, alongside the involvement of amino acid metabolism, cell growth and death, and lipid metabolism pathways, may suggest potential associations with the observed growth differences between female and male chickens (Fig. 1A). Further validation is required to establish their utility as growth-related biomarkers.

In the present study, the genetic differences between the sexes were quite pronounced, and these disparities became even more apparent when the transcriptomic profiles of different muscles were compared. A total of 377 DEGs were identified between F*_X and M*_X. The majority of these DEGs are involved in various metabolic pathways, including steroid biosynthesis, histidine metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis (both the lacto and neolacto series), and glycerolipid metabolism. A total of 486 DEGs were found between F*_T and M*_T, many of which are involved in processes such as steroid biosynthesis; amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism; galactose metabolism; fructose and mannose metabolism; taurine and hypotaurine metabolism; and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. Transcriptome analysis revealed that sex affected carcass characteristics and muscle biochemical indices in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens. Upon comparing the transcriptome profiles between the breast and leg muscles, a greater number of DEGs were observed, with the majority being upregulated in the breast muscles. These DEGs were associated primarily with glycolysis/gluconeogenesis; fatty acid degradation; pyruvate metabolism; valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation; the citrate cycle; cysteine and methionine metabolism; starch and sucrose metabolism; beta-alanine metabolism; and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism. These findings indicate that the developmental processes of the breast and leg muscles are not uniform. Similarly, a comparable pattern was observed in the levels of essential amino acids in Daheng broilers [24].

The integration of metabolomic and transcriptomic datasets offers a promising approach to elucidate the critical pathways and molecular mechanisms that influence the quality of Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens. Our study revealed significant differences in histidine metabolism and nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism between female and male chickens. Histidine can combine with β-alanine to produce carnosine and anserine, both of which help guard against oxidative damage. Antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) protect muscle cell membranes from oxidative damage by free radicals, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation that causes rancidity and off-flavors in meat. Higher levels of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), GSH-Px, and SOD in muscle tissue are associated with better meat quality preservation, including reduced oxidative spoilage susceptibility and improved shelf life. Research has found that crossbreeding decreases GSH-Px activity and T-AOC in goose muscles, correlating with changes in meat quality parameters such as triglyceride (TG) content. This highlights the direct link between the muscle intrinsic antioxidant defense system and lipid stability, a key factor influencing flavor, tenderness, and overall meat quality [25]. Additionally, through decarboxylation, histidine can be transformed into histamine, a neurotransmitter involved in regulating food consumption alongside other neurotransmitters, such as catecholamines and 5-hydroxytryptamine. Research indicates that histidine reduces food intake via histamine and that supplementing diets with histidine can increase carnosine and anserine levels, subsequently increasing meat quality [26, 27]. In this study, we observed an elevated presence of monoamine oxidase A (MAOA) and a reduced presence of aldehyde dehydrogenase 7 family member A1 (ALDH7A1) and aspartoacylase (ASPA) in female chickens. This led to an increase in flavor metabolites such as anserine, which aligns with the rapid growth rate observed in male chickens. These results offer fresh perspectives and evidence that support the molecular regulatory pathways involved in muscle growth and development. Niacin is crucial for all living organisms and plays a vital role in energy metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), a key molecule in energy metabolism, is involved in multiple cellular functions [28]. Yu et al. (2017) reported through correlation and metabolic pathway analyses that the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathways are closely associated with imbalances in the intestinal flora [29]. In this study, NAD and niacinamide levels were increased in male chickens, whereas nicotinamide riboside kinase 2 (NMRK2) accumulation was decreased. These results revealed a potentially different metabolic mechanism in female and male chickens. In this study, the key differential pathways between the breast and leg muscles of Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens were also revealed. The metabolic pathways of linoleic acid, histidine, purine, and galactose, along with oxidative phosphorylation, exhibited distinct regulatory patterns between the breast and leg muscles. These differences play crucial roles in shaping the phenotypic variations observed between these muscle groups. Phospholipase A2 group IIE (PLA2G2E), aspartoacylase (ASPA) and carnosine synthase 1 (CARNS1) are highly expressed in the breast muscle, and cytochrome P450, family 2 (CYP2J21) and hexokinase 2 (HNMT) are highly expressed in the leg muscle. PLA2G2E alters the lipid composition of lipoprotein particles, which ultimately moderately facilitates fat deposition in tissues. Aspartoacylase (ASPA) catalyzes the hydrolysis of N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a molecule prevalent in the brain and considered a marker of brain health, into aspartate and acetate [30]. Carnosine synthase 1 (CARNS1), also known as ATP-grasp domain-containing protein 1, is predominantly expressed in the skeletal muscles and central nervous system of vertebrates. This enzyme catalyzes the synthesis of carnosine and homocarnosine [31]. In contrast, cytochrome P450 family 2 subfamily J member 2 (CYP2J2), the sole member of the human CYP2J subfamily, is a monooxygenase involved in regulating oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and immune responses. Additionally, the HNMT and HNMT-like genes modulate carnosine and anserine levels, respectively. Histamine, synthesized from histidine via histidine decarboxylase, is degraded into N-methylhistamine by HNMT [32]. These changes may contribute to enhanced flavor.

In the present study, the overaccumulation of phosphoribosyl glycinamide synthetase (GART), adenylate kinase 1 (AK1), adenosine monophosphate deaminase 1 (AMPD1), phosphodiesterase 3B (PDE3B), PDE7B, phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1), xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH), AMPD3 and PDE4D indirectly influenced the level of adenosine, and PDE5A, PDE1B, guanylate cyclase 1 soluble subunit beta (GUCY1B1), PDE9A, GUCY1A1, GDA, GUCY1A2, PDE3A, nudix hydrolase 2 (NUDT2), and natriuretic peptide receptor 2 (NPR2) indirectly influenced the level of inosine and adenosine diphosphate ribose. GART is a crucial folate enzyme in the de novo synthesis pathway that catalyzes the transformation of glycinamide ribonucleotides into formylglycinamide ribonucleotides. This process is essential for nucleotide formation, which in turn is vital for DNA replication [33]. DNA replication ensures the accurate transmission of genetic information that underpins meat quality. It guarantees the inheritance and expression of key genes responsible for producing proteins such as collagen and calpains (which influence tenderness), metabolic enzymes involved in fat deposition (affecting juiciness and flavor), and myoglobin (which determines color). While replication itself doesn’t directly alter meat properties, it is the essential process that preserves the genetic potential for high-quality meat by faithfully copying the instructions for these critical traits [33, 34]. AMPD1 acts as a catalyst for the hydrolysis and deamination of AMP, producing IMP and ammonium ions. The activity of this enzyme reflects the cellular energy requirements, with the level of activation determining the concentrations of intracellular metabolites such as purines and inorganic phosphate [35]. AK1 is an enzyme expressed in highly regenerative tissues such as skeletal muscle, the blood system, and the brain. It plays a crucial role in regulating adenine nucleotide metabolism. These tissues, owing to their high metabolic demands and regenerative capacities, rely on AK1 to maintain the cellular energy balance and metabolic function [36]. PGM1 is an enzyme that plays a critical role in glycogen metabolism and glucose homeostasis by catalyzing the conversion of glucose-1-phosphate to glucose-6-phosphate. Deficiency of PGM1, caused by mutations in the PGM1 gene, can lead to congenital dysglycosylation and glycogen storage disease, which are characterized by impaired glycoprotein biosynthesis and abnormal glycogen accumulation in tissues. The phosphodiesterase (PDE) enzymes, as key regulators of the cyclic nucleotide signal transduction system, offer the potential for organ-selective targeting by focusing on specific PDE isoenzymes. By selectively inhibiting or activating particular PDE enzymes, it is possible to modulate physiological activities in a specific organ or system without affecting others [37]. Xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) catalyzes the final two steps of purine catabolism, converting hypoxanthine to uric acid. Following this, soluble guanylyl cyclase (GUCY1) generates cyclic GMP (cGMP), which serves as a secondary messenger in various physiological processes. These processes include vasodilation, inhibition of platelet aggregation, and a reduction in blood pressure [38]. Guanine deaminase (GDA) is an important metalloenzyme that processes the first step in purine catabolism, converting guanine to xanthine by hydrolytic deamination [39]. NUDT2 is a crucial enzyme that hydrolyzes 5′-triphosphate RNA caps (such as NAD caps and 7-methylguanosine caps), playing a significant role in regulating mRNA stability and turnover in cells. It has also been shown to degrade viral RNAs in vitro, thus contributing to antiviral immunity [40]. Natriuretic peptide receptor 2 (NPR2) plays a key role in cartilage and bone morphogenesis. Mutations in the NPR2 gene result in acromesomelic dysplasia, Maroteaux type (AMDM). This condition is part of a broader spectrum of skeletal dysplasias that can arise from NPR2 mutations, impacting the growth and development of bones and cartilage [24]. In the present study, galactokinase 1 (GALK1) exhibited increased abundance in the leg muscle. Conversely, glucosidase alpha neutral C (GANC), galactosidase alpha (GLA), phosphofructokinase (PFKM), PGM1, and UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase 2 (UGP2) presented relatively high expression levels in the breast muscle, and dihydroxyacetone phosphate and D-mannose acid were overaccumulated in the breast muscle, whereas sorbitol presented increased abundance in the leg muscle. These results indicate that one of the primary differences between chicken breast and leg muscles lies in their amino acid and energy metabolism pathways. These pathways follow distinct developmental programs and are characterized by different muscle fiber properties. These properties can potentially serve as biomarkers for distinguishing the phenotypic differences between breast and leg muscles.

The higher shear force observed in male chickens correlates with upregulated oxidative phosphorylation DEGs (e.g., ATP5F1AW, COX7C) in male breast muscle, suggesting enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism may contribute to tougher meat texture. Similarly, the lower cooking loss in male leg muscle aligns with elevated expression of lipid metabolism genes (ELOVL1, GPCPD1) and metabolites (1-arachidonoylglycerol), implicating sex-specific regulation of intramuscular fat retention.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the molecular mechanisms underlying meat quality differences in Qiandongnan Xiaoxiang chickens, revealing that sex and muscle type significantly influence these traits. Key findings indicate that male chickens exhibit higher shear force in both breast and leg muscles than females, correlating with upregulated oxidative phosphorylation genes and enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism. Sex-specific metabolic pathways were identified, with histidine metabolism and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism critically affecting flavor and tenderness. Pronounced muscle-type differences were observed: breast and leg muscles exhibited distinct regulatory patterns in linoleic acid metabolism, purine metabolism, galactose metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. Integrative analysis identified key biomarkers (L-valine, oxidized glutathione, NAD) and genes (ELOVL1, GSTT1) associated with lipid retention and water-holding capacity. These findings establish a comprehensive molecular network for meat quality regulation, suggesting potential biomarkers for selective breeding to enhance poultry nutritional value and sensory attributes.
