Weighted, Multiphase, Volume-Preserving Mean Curvature Flow as Limit of the MBO Scheme on Manifolds
Fabius Krämer

Abstract
. The famous thresholding scheme by Merriman, Bence, and Osher (Motion of multiple junctions: A level set approach. Journal of Computational Physics 112.2 (1994): 334-363.) proved itself as a very efficient time discretization of mean curvature flow. The present paper studies a multiphase, volume constrained version on a weighted manifold that naturally arises in the context of data science. The main result of this work proves convergence to multiphase, weighted, volume-preserving mean curvature flow on a smooth, closed manifold. The proof is only conditional in the sense that convergence of the approximating energy to the weighted perimeter is assumed. These type of assumptions are natural and common in the literature. The proof shows convergence in the energy dissipation inequality, induced by the underlying gradient flow structure, similar to the work of Laux and Otto (The thresholding scheme for mean curvature flow and De Giorgi’s ideas for minimizing movements. The Role of Metrics in the Theory of Partial Differential Equations 85 (2020): 63-94.). This leads to a new De Giorgi solution concept that describes weakly the limiting flow. Estimates regarding the derivatives of the heat kernel are developed in order to pass to the limit in the variations of the energy and metric. Keywords: Mean Curvature flow, gradient flows, heat kernel, clustering Mathematical Subject Classification (MSC2020) : 53E10, 53Z50, 49Q20 (Primary), 35D30, 58J35, 49Q05 (Secondary)



1.
Introduction

Mean curvature flow traditionally arose in material science, for example as flow describing the grain boundaries of metal in the annealing process
[
33
]
. But nowadays, its natural property to (locally) minimize the perimeter as gradient flow, motivates its use in data science tasks
[
6
,
14
,
30
,
31
]
. One prime example for this is the MBO scheme on graphs by Bertozzi et al.
[
31
]
; it is a space and time discretization of mean curvature flow based on the famous MBO scheme
[
32
]
. The scheme is remarkable as, although computationally cheap, it inherits the gradient flow structure to iteratively minimize (locally), over all subsets
\Omega
of the data, the energy

\sum_{x}\sum_{y}p(h,x,y)\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega}(x)\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega^{c}}(y).

The heat kernel
p(h,x,y)
depends on the weights of the underlying graph and is an over all paths from node
x
to node
y
averaged weight. Therefore, a minimizing set
\Omega
of the energy minimizes the by the heat kernel given weights of edges that go from the set
\Omega
to its complement
\Omega^{c}
. Remember, the classical partition problem aims for minimizing the sum of weights from
\Omega
to its complement. Hence, the MBO scheme solves, locally and up to reweighing, the partition problem.

Empirical studies
[
17
]
have shown that the scheme has even better accuracy in semi-supervised clustering tasks if one adds a volume constraint to the scheme. Often the number of data points in every set is (roughly) known in advance such that one can predescribe it in the algorithm. The aim of this paper is to show that this volume-constrained MBO scheme on graph is a space and time discretization of volume-preserving mean curvature flow. To that aim Laux and the author
[
18
]
have already proven that the scheme converges to its mean field limit if the number of data points goes to infinity. This result is obtained by using the
\Gamma
-convergence
[
19
]
of a suitable rescaling of the previously mentioned energy together with convergence of the Lagrange multipliers
[
18
]
of the volume constraints to obtain convergence of the iterates as minimizers in the minimizing movement interpretation of the MBO scheme
[
11
]
. The setting for the big-data limit is given by the “manifold assumption”; it states that the data points are sampled from a probability measure that is concentrated on a lower-dimensional manifold. The idea behind the assumption is that although the data may be embedded in a high dimensional space, points in the same class or cluster are similar and thus live in a lower dimensional structure. So far the mean field limit and its partition properties are not well understood. One is especially interested in its long-time behavior, as this describes approximately the properties of the result of the discrete clustering algorithm mentioned in the beginning.

This paper is filling this gap by proving in the main Theorem
1
that the mean field limit of the multiphase volume constrained MBO scheme on the weighted data-manifold (see (
1
) below) conditionally convergences to multiphase, weighted, volume-preserving mean curvature flow on the manifold. The evolution law of the limiting flow on the interface of phase
i
is (formally) given by

V_{i}=-H_{i}-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log(\rho)\rangle+\Lambda_{i}

where
V_{i}
is the (outer) normal velocity,
H_{i}
is the mean curvature,
\mathbf{n}_{i}
is the outer unit normal on the boundary of phase
i
, respectively, and
\rho
is the probability density on the manifold while
\Lambda_{i}
is the Lagrange multiplier ensuring the preservation of volume.
The parameter for the convergence is the diffusion time
h
of the heat kernel (compare with the definition of the MBO scheme (
1
)), which is actually the step-size for the time discretization of the gradient flow. The setting yields four major difficulties: The lack of the comparison principle as there are more than two phases, the curvature of the manifold, the inhomogeneity due to the weight, and the Lagrange multiplier arising from the volume constraint.

There have been already several works regarding the convergence of the MBO scheme to mean curvature flow
[
3
,
8
,
12
,
16
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
38
]
. The only work on a
weighted manifold
so far is by Laux and Lelmi
[
21
]
where they use the powerful concept of viscosity solutions for mean curvature flow. Viscosity solutions are based on the comparison principle for mean curvature flow which is only valid for the case of two phases. The present paper is the first that proves the convergence of the scheme in the
multiphase
case on manifolds. Therefore, it introduces a new distributional solution concept for (weighted, multiphase,) volume-preserving mean curvature flow on a manifold. This weak solution idea is a natural extension of the ones introduced in
[
20
,
24
]
and based on De Giorgi’s energy dissipation inequality for gradient flows. That is, one encodes all information of the gradient flow equation
\partial_{t}u=-\nabla E(u)
in the inequality
E(u(0))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\partial_{t}u(t)|^{2}+|\nabla E(u(t))|^{2}\,dt\leq E(u(T))
stating sharp energy dissipation. In our case, the energy reads as the weighted perimeter, i.e.,
E(u)=\int_{M}\rho|\nabla u|
where
M
is the manifold,
\rho
is the weight and
|\nabla u|
is the total variation of the Gauss-Green measure of
u
. The
L^{2}
-gradient (on the boundary and weighted by
\rho
) of the weighted perimeter used in Definition
1
is, at least formally (one can make this rigorous by looking at inner variations, see
[
29
, Chapter 17]
),
\nabla E(\mathbbold{1}_{\Omega})=H+\langle\mathbf{n},\nabla\log(\rho)\rangle
where
H
and
\mathbf{n}
are the mean curvature and normal of
\Omega
, respectively.

Similar to the weight on the manifold, the work
[
10
]
regards an MBO scheme for an
inhomogeneous
(anisotropic) surface energy. Furthermore, they consider an obstacle mean curvature flow which analogously to the volume constraint yields a Lagrange multiplier. They prove
\Gamma
-convergence of the corresponding energies. Also in
[
8
]
anisotropic, inhomogeneous mean curvature flow is studied but this time with a minimizing movement scheme in the spirit of Almgren-Taylor-Wang
[
1
]
. The authors show that their discretization converges to a flat flow by combing a Hölder estimate with suitable bounds to apply the Arcelà-Ascoli theorem. In a second step they show under a similar assumption as (
8
) that their flat flows are distributional solutions.

The only proof of dynamics for a
volume-constrained
MBO scheme before this paper is given by
[
26
]
. The key in the proof is an
L^{2}
-bound of the Lagrange multiplier which yields compactness in the weak
L^{2}
topology. As their proof only applies to the case of two phases and in the Euclidean space, the bound had to be extended to the multiphase case on a weighted manifold in
[
18
]
. In
[
18
]
the bound was not used for a convergence proof but for estimating the running time of an algorithm to compute the Lagrange multiplier for the scheme.

There are also other advances in approximating mean curvature flow. The Allen-Cahn equation, the gradient flow of the Modica-Mortala functional, is a phase field approach to the problem of dealing with the singularities arising in mean curvature flow. There is a wide literature on convergence of the Allen-Cahn equation to mean curvature flow. To just name the two closest to this paper, the fundamental work of Laux and Simon
[
25
]
first proved the convergence in the multiphase, volume-preserving case to distributional solutions while the recent paper of Ganedi, Marveggio, and Stinson
[
13
]
showed the inhomogeneous case. The later work similarly as in the present proof of Proposition
2
shows the convergence of the first variations of the corresponding energies.

To tackle the mathematical challenges, the discrete energy dissipation inequality of De Giorgi (see Lemma
2
) is used which is a consequence of the minimizing movement interpretation of Esedoḡlu and Otto
[
11
]
. Though, one has to be careful as the Lagrange multiplier makes the energy dependent on the previous iteration. The energy dissipation inequality is a stable and powerful tool as it allows passing to the limit by just proving lower semicontinuity of the velocity and the curvature term (see Proposition
1
and
2
). For the proofs, Lemma
4
is crucial stating the convergence of the energy densities. The statement is framed as convergence of measures on the tangent bundle. This is a natural setup to work in; the heat kernel localizes when the diffusion time decreases. Hence, a tangential variable is necessary to describe the direction and velocity of the blow up. The lemma is an adaptation from the two phase Euclidean version in
[
24
]
and boils down to proving a localized lower-semicontinuity of the thresholding energy. So far there was only one generalization of
[
24
]
to the vectorial setting; the authors in
[
20
]
treated the multiphase, Euclidean case with arbitrary surface tensions
\sigma_{ij}
and mobilities
\mu_{ij}
, i.e. it holds
V_{ij}=-\sigma_{ij}\mu_{ij}H_{ij}
on the interface between phase
i
and
j
. To do that an on De Giorgi’s structure theorem based partition of unity is needed to localize on every single interface. The present paper provides a simpler approach for the proof of Lemma
4
in the multiphase case with constant mobilities and surface tensions that manages to avoid the geometric measure theory machinery. The simpler approach here could be easily adapted to the case of additive surface tensions
\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j}
which are induced by energies of the form
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\textup{Per}(\Omega_{i})
.

One of the main technical contributions of this paper, that could be also useful for future works, are several estimates around the derivatives of the heat kernel
p(h,x,y)
and its approximation
G_{h}(x,y)=\frac{1}{(4\pi h)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{4h}}
. For example Corollary
1
states a Gaussian bound for the mixed second derivative of the heat kernel. Also, Lemma
6
claims that if the curvature of the manifold is bounded then the Hessian of half the squared distance is up to second order equal to the identity, i.e.

\left|\big\langle\nabla_{y}^{2}\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{2}(w),v\big\rangle_{y}-\langle w,v\rangle_{y}\right|\lesssim\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)|v||w|

for any
v,w\in T_{y}M
.
One is interested in the Hessian of the squared distance in the proof of Proposition
2
as there the second derivatives of the approximate heat kernel
G_{h}
appear in the second variation of the metric.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Chapter
2
states the main result and the necessary intermediate results for its proof. Then Chapter
3
introduces basic notation and preliminaries for calculus on weighted manifold while Chapter
4
proves the above-mentioned estimates regarding the heat kernel. Finally, Chapter
5
gives the proof of the compactness of Lemma
3
, the energy convergence of Lemma
4
, the lower semicontinuity of the metric quotient of Proposition
1
and the lower semicontinuity of the metric slope of Proposition
2
.

2.
Main Result and Structure of Proof

Let a closed, smooth Riemannian manifold
M
together with a probability measure
\mu=\rho\Vol
be given, where
\Vol
denotes the volume measure on
M
.
We assume the bound
\rho>0
and denote by
p
the heat kernel on
M
(see Chapter
3
for more information).
Assume an initial partition of
M
into
P\in\mathbb{N}
phases given by the characteristic
functions
\chi^{0}_{1},\dots,\chi^{0}_{P}:M\rightarrow\{0,1\},\sum_{i=1}^{P}\chi_{i}=1
. To describe the dynamics of the evolving sets
\Omega_{i}=\{\chi_{i}=1\}
one updates the characteristic functions

(1)

\chi^{\ell}_{i}(x):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i\in\argmax_{j=1,\dots,P}\left\{\big(p(h)*\chi_{j}^{\ell-1}\big)(x)-m^{\ell}_{j}\right\}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where the Lagrange multiplier
m^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
is chosen such that
\sum_{i=1}^{P}m^{\ell}_{i}=0
and the volume of every phase is preserved, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\chi^{\ell}\,d\mu=\int_{M}\chi^{0}\,d\mu.

The Lagrange multiplier
m^{\ell}
exists and is unique if
h>0
and
\int_{M}\chi^{0}\,d\mu>0
,
see
[
17
,
18
]
for more information. Furthermore, one defines the piecewise
constant interpolation

(2)

\displaystyle\chi_{h}(t):=\chi^{\ell}\quad\text{if }t\in[\ell h,(\ell+1)h),\quad\chi_{h}(t)=\chi^{0}\text{ if }t\leq 0.

The main result of this paper states the convergence of this interpolation of the volume constrained MBO scheme to weighted, volume-preserving mean curvature flow.
Here the following weak solution concept is used for the flow.

Definition 1

(De Giorgi Solution MCF)
.

A function
\chi:M\times(0,T)\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{P}
with
\sum_{i}\chi_{i}=1\ \mu
-a.e. is called a De Giorgi solution to weighted, volume-preserving mean curvature flow if it satisfies the following three conditions a), b) and c):

a)

For every
i=1,\dots,P
there exists
H_{i}\in L^{2}(|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt)
which is the mean curvature in the
weak sense, i.e., for every
\xi\in C^{\infty}((0,T),\Gamma(TM))
it holds

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M\times(0,T)}\big(\textup{div}\,\xi-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla_{\mathbf{n}_{i}}\xi\rangle\big)|\nabla\chi_{i}|(t)\,dt=-\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M\times(0,T)}H_{i}\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\xi\rangle|\nabla\chi_{i}|(t)\,dt.

b)

For every
i=1,\dots,P
there exists normal velocities
V_{i}\in L^{2}(|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt)
such that for
every
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M\times[0,T))
one has

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\eta(t=0)\chi_{i}^{0}\,d\Vol+\int_{M\times(0,T)}\partial_{t}\eta\,\chi_{i}\,d\Vol\,dt+\int_{M\times(0,T)}\eta V_{i}\,|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt=0.

c)

De Giorgi’s inequality is satisfied, i.e.,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\tau\downarrow 0}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{(T-\tau,T)}\rho\,|\nabla\chi_{i}|(t)\,dt

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M\times(0,T)}\rho\left(V_{i}^{2}+\left(H-\Lambda_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log\rho\rangle\right)^{2}\right)\,|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt\leq\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M}\rho\,|\nabla\chi_{i}|.

with

(6)

\Lambda_{i}=\frac{\int_{M}(H+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log\rho\rangle)\rho|\nabla\chi_{i}|}{\int_{M}\rho|\nabla\chi_{i}|}.

It follows a remark showing that Definition
1
is meaningful.

Remark 1
.

Definition
1
is a natural extension of De Giorgi solutions of mean curvature flow introduced in
[
20
,
24
]
. In a weak sense, equation (
3
) enforces the Herring angle conditions which together with De Giorgi’s inequality (
5
) imply the dynamics
V_{i}=H_{i}-\Lambda_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\log\rho\rangle
. Moreover, the dynamics together with definition of the velocities (
4
) and the Lagrange multiplier
\Lambda
yield the conservation of mass
\int_{M}\chi(t_{1})d\mu=\int_{M}\chi(t_{2})d\mu
for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
. To see this assume that (
3
), (
4
) and (
5
) are satisfied for
\chi:M\times[0,T]\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{P}
with
\sum_{i}\chi_{i}(t)=1
such that the sets
\Omega_{i}(t)=\{\chi_{i}(\cdot,t)=1\}
have smoothly evolving interfaces
\Sigma_{ij}:=\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\partial\Omega_{j}
.

(1)

The Herring angle condition is a consequence of (
3
). To see this, apply the divergence theorem on surfaces (cf.
[
37
, Chapter 2.7]
) to a test function
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)

\int_{\Sigma_{i}}\textup{div}\,\xi-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla_{\mathbf{n}_{i}}\xi\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=-\int_{\Sigma_{i}}H_{i}\langle\xi,\mathbf{n}_{i}\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-1}-\int_{\partial\Sigma_{i}}\langle\xi,\eta_{i}\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-2}

where
\eta_{i}
is the inward pointing unit conormal, i.e., tangent to
\Sigma_{i}
and normal to
\partial\Sigma_{i}
. For example, when denoting with
J
the rotation by ninety degrees in the normal plane, one has
\eta_{i}=J\mathbf{n}_{i}
at any triple junction. So, equality (
3
) and the above divergence theorem on surfaces imply

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\partial\Sigma_{1}}\langle\xi,J\mathbf{n}_{1}\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-2}+\int_{\partial\Sigma_{2}}\langle\xi,J\mathbf{n}_{2}\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-2}+\int_{\partial\Sigma_{3}}\langle\xi,J\mathbf{n}_{3}\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{d-2},

such that at triple junctions it has to hold the Herring angle condition
\mathbf{n}_{1}+\mathbf{n}_{2}+\mathbf{n}_{3}=0
.

(2)

The dynamics
V_{i}=-H_{i}+\Lambda_{i}-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log\rho\rangle
are satisfied with
\Lambda_{i}
as in (
5
). This can be seen because the De Giorgi inequality (
5
) reduces in the smooth case to

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{M}\rho|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M\times(0,T)}\left(V_{i}^{2}+(H_{i}-\Lambda_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log\rho\rangle)^{2}\right)\rho|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt\leq 0.

Together with the Herring angle condition one checks that

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{M}\rho|\nabla\chi_{i}(t)|=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M}\rho V_{i}(H_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log(\rho)\rangle)|\nabla\chi_{i}|

which together with
\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M}\rho V_{i}\Lambda_{i}|\nabla\chi_{i}|=0
allows us to complete the square

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M\times(0,T)}\rho(V_{i}+(H_{i}-\Lambda_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log(\rho)\rangle))^{2}|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt\leq 0.

Hence, the dynamics
V_{i}=-H_{i}+\Lambda_{i}-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\log(\rho)\rangle
hold on the boundary of
\Omega_{i}
.

(3)

The volume is preserved, i.e., for any
T>t\geq 0
it holds
\int_{M}\chi_{i}(t)\,d\mu=\int_{M}\chi_{i}(0)\,d\mu
. This follows immediately from the dynamics
V_{i}=-H_{i}+\Lambda_{i}-\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\rho\rangle
together with the definitions (
4
) and (
6
) of
V_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}
, respectively, such that

\displaystyle\int_{M}\chi_{i}(t)\rho\,d\Vol-\int_{M}\chi_{i}(0)\rho\,d\Vol

\displaystyle=\int_{M\times(0,T)}\rho V_{i}|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{M\times(0,T)}\rho(H_{i}-\Lambda_{i}+\langle\mathbf{n}_{i},\nabla\rho\rangle)|\nabla\chi_{i}|\,dt

\displaystyle=0.

The main result of this paper states that the volume constrained MBO scheme converges to a De Giorgi solution to volume-preserving mean curvature flow given by Definition
1
.

Theorem 1

(Main Theorem)
.

Given
\chi^{0}=(\chi_{1}^{0},\dots,\chi_{P}^{0})
with
\chi_{i}^{0}:M\rightarrow\{0,1\}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{P}\chi_{i}^{0}=1

\mu
-a.e. and such that
|\nabla\chi_{i}^{0}|
is a bounded measure for every
i=1,\dots,P
. For any sequence
h\downarrow 0
the interpolation
\chi_{h}
is defined by (
1
) and (
2
). Assume there exists a limit
\chi:M\times(0,T)\rightarrow[0,1]^{P}
such that

(7)

\chi_{h}\rightharpoonup\chi\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mu\times dt).

Then it holds
\chi\in\{0,1\}^{P},\sum_{i=1}^{P}\chi_{i}=1\ \mu
-a.e. and
\nabla\chi_{i}
is a bounded measure which is equi-integrable in the time variable. If additionally one supposes that

(8)

\limsup_{h\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}E_{h}(\chi_{h}(t))\,dt\leq c_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{(0,T)\times M}\rho(x)\,|\nabla\chi_{i}(x)|\,dt

with
c_{0}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}
holds, then
\chi
is a De Giorgi solution to mean curvature flow in the sense of Definition
1
.

Similar to the works
[
20
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, which have been motivated by the work of Luckhaus and Sturzenhecker
[
28
]
, Theorem
1
is conditional and requires (
8
) to prevent the loss of interface area. Only the contrary inequality

(9)

\liminf_{h\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}E_{h}(\chi_{h}(t))\,dt\geq c_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{(0,T)\times M}\rho(x)\,|\nabla\chi_{i}(x)|\,dt

is true due to the lower semicontinuity of the energy proven in
[
19
]
. In Lemma
4
an alternative, simpler proof will be given for a localized version of (
9
).

To access the proof of Theorem
1
the following interpretation of the MBO scheme as a minimizing movement scheme, due to
[
11
]
, opens a path to use variational techniques. For a metric space
(\mathcal{M},d)
, an energy
E:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a time step-size
h>0
and initial conditions
u^{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the minimizing movement scheme is given by iterating for
\ell=1,2,\dots

(10)

u^{\ell}\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{M}}E(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d^{2}(u,u^{\ell-1}).

This scheme is the standard discretization of a gradient flow. As mean curvature flow is the
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the perimeter, it shouldn’t be of any surprise that the minimizing movement interpretation of the MBO scheme minimizes over an energy that approximates the perimeter (cf. Lemma
1
).

As here the volume constrained MBO scheme is under consideration the term incorporating the Lagrange multiplier has to be included in the minimizing movement interpretation. Therefore, the scheme is adapted by making the energy depend on the iteration which lets us hide the Lagrange multiplier in the energy, i.e.,

(11)

u^{\ell}\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{M}}E^{\ell}(u)+\frac{1}{2h}d(u,u^{\ell-1}).

The minimizing movement interpretation is now the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Minimizing Movement Interpretation)
.

The scheme (
1
) satisfies (
11
) provided one defines

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle:=\left\{u:M\rightarrow[0,1]^{P}\ measurable\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{P}u_{i}=1\ \mu-a.e.\right\},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}d_{h}^{2}(u,\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M^{2}}p(h,x,y)(u_{i}-u_{j})(x)(u_{i}-u_{j})(y)\,d\mu(y)\,d\mu(x),

(14)

\displaystyle E_{h}^{\ell}(u)

\displaystyle:=E_{h}(u)+\Lambda^{\ell}\cdot\int_{M}u\,d\mu,

where
\Lambda^{\ell}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{h}}m^{\ell}
is the rescaled Lagrange multiplier of (
1
) and

(15)

E_{h}(u):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{h}}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\int_{M}\int_{M}p(h,x,y)u_{i}(x)(1-u_{i}(y))\,d\mu(y)\,d\mu(x)

is the classical thresholding energy. Furthermore,
(\mathcal{M},d_{h})
is a compact metric space and
E_{h}^{\ell}
continuous.

Having a minimizing movement scheme (
10
) at hand, the maybe naive approach would be to plug in
u^{\ell-1}
as competitor. This leads first to

(16)

E(u^{\ell})+\frac{1}{2h}d^{2}(u^{\ell},u^{\ell-1})\leq E(u^{\ell-1})

and after successively application then to
E(u^{L})+\frac{h}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\frac{d(u^{\ell},u^{\ell-1})}{h}\right)^{2}\leq E(u^{0})
. This is the right type of equation in regard of (
5
) but a factor
2
is missing as at least heuristically
\frac{d_{h}(\chi_{h}^{\ell},\chi_{h}^{\ell-1})}{h}\rightarrow|\partial_{t}\chi|
. To solve this problem, another interpolation than the constant is introduced. The more intrinsic

(17)

u(t)\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{M}}E^{\ell}(u)+\frac{1}{2(t-(\ell-1)h)}d^{2}(u,u^{\ell-1})\quad\text{for }t\in((\ell-1)h,\ell h]

is called the “variational interpolation”. After introducing

e(t):=E^{\ell}(u(t))+\frac{1}{2(t-(\ell-1)h)}d^{2}(u(t),u^{\ell-1})

and using that
u(t)
is a critical point of
E^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2(t-(\ell-1)h)}d^{2}(\cdot,u^{\ell-1})
it holds on a heuristic level

\frac{d}{dt}e(t)=-\frac{1}{2(t-(\ell-1)h)^{2}}d^{2}(u(t),u^{\ell-1}).

Integrating in time from
(\ell-1)h

3.
Notation and Prerequisites

The heat kernel on weighted Riemannian manifolds is first introduced. A brought introduction to the most important properties can be found in
[
15
]
.
Let
(M,\mu,g)
be a closed, smooth,
d
-dimensional, weighted Riemannian manifold. That is
M
is compact and without boundary, the metric
g:TM\times TM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is smooth, and
\mu=\rho\Vol
is a measure on
M
with smooth, positive density
\rho>0
. The tangent bundle will be denoted by
TM
which roughly is the union of all tangent spaces
T_{x}M
for
x\in M
. For tangent vectors
v,w\in T_{x}M
it will be used
\langle v,w\rangle_{x}:=g_{x}(v,w)
and
|v|_{x}^{2}=g_{x}(v,v)
. The class of smooth functions
f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
while the class of smooth vector fields
\xi:TM\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\xi\in\Gamma(TM)
. For
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
the gradient
\nabla f(x)\in T_{x}M
is defined by satisfying

\langle\nabla f(x),v\rangle_{x}=D_{x}f(v)\quad\text{ for all }v\in T_{x}M

where
D_{x}f
is the differential of
f
at
x\in M
. The weighted divergence
\textup{div}_{\mu}:\Gamma(TM)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(M)
is defined by the duality

\int_{M}\textup{div}_{\mu}(\xi)f\,d\mu=-\int\langle\xi,\nabla f\rangle\,d\mu\quad\text{ for all }f\in C^{\infty}(M).

One can then check that
\textup{div}_{\mu}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\rho}\textup{div}(\rho\xi)
where
div
denotes the divergence defined analogous to
\textup{div}_{\mu}
but with respect to the volume measure
\Vol
. Consequently, the weighted Laplace–Beltrami operator is defined by
\Delta_{\mu}:=-\textup{div}_{\mu}\nabla
(the sign-convention is used to ensure the eigenvalues are positive). The solution
e^{-h\Delta_{\mu}}\chi
of the heat equation

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dh}u=-\Delta_{M}u&\text{on }M\times(0,T]\\
u(x,0)=\chi(x)&\text{in }M\end{cases}

can be expressed by convoluting with the heat kernel
p
, see
[
15
, Theorem 3.3]
and references therein. This means

(39)

e^{-h\Delta_{\mu}}\chi=p(h)*\chi:=\int_{M}p(h,x,y)\chi(y)\,d\mu(y)

and furthermore it holds the symmetry
p(h,x,y)=p(h,y,x)
, normalization
\int_{M}p(h,x,y)\,d\mu(y)=1
and the semigroup property

(40)

p(t+s,x,y)=\int_{M}p(t,x,q)p(s,q,y)\,d\mu(q)\quad\text{ for any }t,s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Throughout the paper approximations for the heat kernel will be used that are close to the Euclidean heat kernel. Therefore, the following abbreviations are introduced

\displaystyle G_{h}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{(4\pi h)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{4h}},

\displaystyle G_{h}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{(4\pi h)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4h}}.

In the following, for
x,y\in M,z\in T_{x}M
, it will be used

x_{z}:=\exp_{x}(z)
as shorthand notation for the exponential map
\exp_{x}:T_{x}M\rightarrow M
. The injective radius
\mathop{\textup{}}{inj}(M)
, describing the radius in which the exponential map is diffeomorphic, is positive as
M
is compact and smooth. Hence, the inverse
\exp_{x}^{-1}(y)
is well-defined if
\textup{dist}(x,y)<\mathop{\textup{}}{inj}(M)
. Furthermore, denote
\gamma_{x,y}
for the unit speed geodesic connecting
x
with
y
(if it exists) and

\gamma_{x,z}
for the geodesic starting at
x
with initial velocity
z
. Parallel transport from
x
to
y
along the geodesic
\gamma_{x,y}
is denoted by
PT_{x\rightarrow y}
where parallel transport will only be applied if
\textup{dist}(x,y)\leq\mathop{\textup{}}{inj}(M)
such that there exists a unique geodesic. The parallel transport is always with respect to the Levi-Civita connection which is in this paper depicted by
\nabla
(it can be distinguished from the gradient as it is applied to vector fields and not to functions).

A function
\chi:M\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is of bounded variation, or short
\chi\in BV(M,\{0,1\})
, if
\chi\in L^{1}(M)
and

\sup\left\{\int_{M}\textup{div}(\xi)\chi\,d\Vol:\xi\in\Gamma(TM),\sup_{M}|\xi|\leq 1\right\}<\infty.

As usual, the Riesz-representation theorem yields a Gauss–Green measure
\nabla\chi
such that

\int_{M}\textup{div}(\xi)\chi\,d\Vol=-\int_{M}\langle\xi,\nabla\chi\rangle\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\Gamma(TM).

Denoting by
\mathbf{n}
the measure theoretic normal satisfying
\nabla\chi=\mathbf{n}|\nabla\chi|
, where
|\cdot|
stands for the total variation, then the previous identity turns into

(41)

\int_{M}\textup{div}(\xi)\chi\,d\Vol=-\int_{M}\langle\xi,\mathbf{n}\rangle|\nabla\chi|\quad\text{for all }\xi\in\Gamma(TM).

In the following the normal
\mathbf{n}_{i}
will always be the one associated to the function
\chi_{i}
.

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}p(h,x,y)|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}_{1}}{\sqrt{h}\mu(B_{\sqrt{h}}(x))}e^{-\frac{\textup{dist}_{M}^{2}(x,y)}{\tilde{C}_{2}h}}.

The next Lemma will be crucial in the proof of Proposition
2
. It will be necessary to estimate the Hessian of the squared distance function which appear in second derivatives of the heat kernel. In the Euclidean case
M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds
\nabla^{2}_{x}\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2}=Id_{d}
. On a manifold this holds only up to second order errors due to the spreading of geodesics by curvature. The first derivative of the distance on the manifold is given by
\nabla_{x}\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)=2\exp^{-1}_{x}(y)
such that after a suitable identification of tangent spaces the second derivative is given by
D_{x}\exp^{-1}_{x}(y)
. To see that this quantity is close to the identity one shows that its differential of the inverse
(D\exp_{x})_{\exp^{-1}_{x}(y)}
is almost the identity. Now, one can already see that this is related to the Jacobi fields
J(t)=(D\exp_{x})_{tv}(tw)
satisfying the Jacobi equation
\ddot{J}(t)+R(\dot{J}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t))\dot{\gamma}_{x,y}(t)=0
which will be the main tool for the proof.

Lemma 6
.

For any
x,y\in M
with
\textup{dist}(x,y)\leq\mathop{\textup{}}{inj}(M)\wedge\sqrt[4]{\frac{1}{2d^{3}C_{R}^{2}}e^{-1-dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}}}
and
v,w\in T_{y}M
it holds the following error estimate of the Hessian of the (half) squared distance function

(50)

\left|\Big\langle\nabla_{y}^{2}\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{2}(w),v\Big\rangle_{y}-\langle w,v\rangle_{y}\right|\lesssim\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)|v||w|.

Proof.

We follow the lines of Villani
[
39
, Chapter 14]
,
[
40
, Equation (4.2)]
.
Denote by
\gamma(t):=\exp_{y}(t\exp_{y}^{-1}(x))
the geodesic from
y
to
x
with velocity
|\dot{\gamma}|=\textup{dist}(x,y)
. Further introduce the moving orthonormal frame
(e_{1}(t),\dots,e_{d}(t)),e_{1}(0)=\frac{\exp_{y}^{-1}(x)}{|\exp_{y}^{-1}(x)|}
,
with
e_{j}(t)
evolving with parallel transport. With the Riemannian curvature tensor
R
(which is bounded as
M
is compact and smooth) denote

R_{ij}(t)=\langle R(\dot{\gamma}(t),e_{i}(t))\dot{\gamma}(t),e_{j}(t)\rangle_{\gamma(t)}.

One calls a matrix
J
a Jacobi matrix if it satisfies the Jacobi matrix equation

(51)

\ddot{J}(t)+R(t)J(t)=0.

The dots represent the second covariant derivative. The Jacobi matrices are the fields describing how geodesics spread apart over time. They are closely related to Jacobi fields which describe the behavior of the differential of the exponential function.
Furthermore, introduce the two Jacobi matrices
J_{0}
and
J_{1}
that solve the Jacobi matrix equation with initial conditions given by

(52)

\begin{array}[]{cc}J_{0}(0)=0_{d}&\dot{J}_{0}(0)=I_{d}\\
J_{1}(0)=I_{d}&\dot{J}_{1}(0)=0_{d}.\end{array}

Then by
[
39
, Chapter 14]
,
[
40
, Equation (4.2)]
it holds

(53)

\langle\nabla_{y}^{2}\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{2}(w),v\rangle_{y}=\langle J_{0}(1)^{-1}J_{1}(1)\cdot w,v\rangle_{y}

where
J_{0}(1)^{-1}J_{1}(1)\cdot w=(J_{0}(1)^{-1}J_{1}(1))_{ij}w^{j}e_{i}(1)
.
The next step is to prove that
J_{k}(1)
is close to the identity for
k=0,1
. To that aim define for any Jacobi matrix
J
the auxiliary function
\phi(t):=|J(t)|_{F}^{2}+|\dot{J}(t)|_{F}^{2}
with
|\cdot|_{F}
denoting the Frobenius norm.
Using
|R_{ij}|\leq C_{R}|\dot{\gamma}|^{2}|e_{i}||e_{j}|=C_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}
,
\frac{d}{dt}|J_{ij}|^{2}=\frac{d}{dt}|\langle J_{i},e_{j}\rangle|^{2}=2J_{ij}\dot{J}_{ij}
and
\frac{d}{dt}|\dot{J}_{ij}|^{2}=2\dot{J}_{ij}\ddot{J}_{ij}
one computes

\displaystyle\dot{g}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}J_{ij}\dot{J}_{ij}+J_{ij}\ddot{J}_{ij}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}J_{ij}\dot{J}_{ij}+J_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{d}R_{ik}J_{kj}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|J_{ij}|^{2}+|\dot{J}_{ij}|^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}C_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}(|J_{ij}|^{2}+|J_{kj}|^{2})

\displaystyle=(1+dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2})\phi.

Hence, the Grönwall inequality yields
|J(t)|_{F}^{2}\leq e^{(1+dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2})t}(|J(0)|_{F}^{2}+|\dot{J}(0)|^{2})
which implies for
J=J_{k}
,
k=0,1
,
0\leq t\leq 1
that

(54)

|J_{k}(t)|^{2}\leq de^{1+dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}}.

Furthermore, due to
J_{k}(1)-I_{d}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\ddot{J_{k}}(s)\,ds\,dt
and
|R|_{F}^{2}\leq d^{2}C_{R}^{2}\textup{dist}^{4}(x,y)
it holds

\displaystyle|J_{k}(1)-I_{d}|_{F}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}|R(s)|_{F}^{2}|{J_{k}}(s)|_{F}^{2}\,ds\,dt\leq d^{3}C_{R}^{2}e^{1+dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}}\textup{dist}^{4}(x,y).

Due to the Neumann series it holds for
\textup{dist}(x,y)\leq\sqrt[4]{\frac{1}{2d^{3}C_{R}^{2}}e^{-1-dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}}}
that
|J_{0}(1)^{-1}|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{1-|I_{d}-J_{0}(1)|_{F}}\leq 2
which lets us conclude

\displaystyle|J_{0}(1)^{-1}J_{1}(1)-I_{d}|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq|J_{0}(1)^{-1}|_{F}(|J_{0}(1)-I_{d}|_{F}+|J_{1}(1)-I_{d}|_{F})

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{d^{3}C_{R}^{2}e^{1+dC_{R}\textup{diam}(M)^{2}}}\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y).

The step is finished by putting the identity (
53
) into (
50
) and using the just established bound

\displaystyle\left|\langle\nabla_{y}^{2}\frac{\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)}{2}(w),v\rangle_{y}-\langle v,w\rangle_{y}\right|=\left|\langle\left(J_{0}(1)^{-1}J_{1}(1)-I_{d}\right)\cdot w,v\rangle_{y}\right|\lesssim\textup{dist}^{2}(x,y)|v||w|.
