Systems that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit
Ole Sönnerborn

Abstract
We provide a complete characterization of all finite-dimensional quantum states that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit at arbitrary Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity. Employing a purification-based approach, we prove that mixed-state saturation occurs precisely when three structural criteria are fulfilled: the state’s support is confined to the sum of two energy eigenspaces (the ground level and a single excited level); each eigenvector of the state with nonzero weight is a fixed superposition of one ground- and one excited-state energy eigenvector (determined by the minimizer of the objective function identified by Giovannetti et al. ) and all such eigenvectors evolve in mutually orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces. These requirements impose a strict rank bound, ruling out saturation by any faithful state. For qubit systems, we derive a purity-resolved and tight Margolus–Levitin bound that reduces to the pure-state result in the limit of unit purity. Through a time-reversal argument, we further extend the dual Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit to mixed states and establish the corresponding saturation conditions.



I  Introduction

In their now-classic paper
MaLe1998
, Margolus and Levitin showed that the time
\tau
required for a system to undergo unitary evolution between two completely distinguishable pure states is bounded from below by the ratio of
\pi/2
to the system’s expected energy above the ground-state energy (throughout, we adopt units in which
\hbar=1
):

\tau\geq\frac{\pi/2}{E-E_{0}}.

(1)

This time–energy relation—commonly referred to as the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit—is a fundamental bound that complements the Mandelstam–Tamm time–energy uncertainty relation
MaTa1945
;
Fl1973
;
HoAlSo2022
. It plays a significant role in the conceptual foundations of quantum theory and in the quantitative analysis of dynamical processes central to emerging quantum technologies
LeTo2009
;
Fr2016
;
DeCa2017
;
Neetal2021
;
HoSo2023c
. Applications span a wide range of areas, including performance limits in quantum computation
Ll2000
;
Ll2002
;
AiDe2022
, precision metrology
GiLlMa2004
;
Maetal2023
;
Heetal2024
, bounds on information-processing rates
DeLu2010
;
AcDe2017
;
De2020
, the charging of quantum batteries
Bietal2015
;
Caetal2017
;
Lietal2025
, and quantum control and shortcuts to adiabaticity
CaDe2017
.

A natural question is how the speed limit in Eq. (
1
) should be modified when the requirement that the initial and final states be completely distinguishable is relaxed to allow for an arbitrary overlap
\delta
between them. The original Margolus–Levitin argument depends crucially on the condition
\delta=0
and does not indicate how the inequality should be generalized for
0<\delta<1
. Shortly after the Margolus–Levitin result appeared, however, Giovannetti
et al.

GiLlMa2003
suggested the following extension of the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit:

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{E-E_{0}},\quad\alpha(\delta)=\min_{z^{2}\leq\delta}(1+z)\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z^{2}}}.

(2)

The inequality in Eq. (
2
) can be derived directly for qubit systems, and, supported by numerical evidence, Giovannetti
et al.
conjectured that it should hold for arbitrary finite-dimensional systems as well. Remarkably, a complete analytical proof did not appear until two decades later
HoSo2023a
. This delay can be attributed in part to the fact that the Margolus–Levitin speed limit possesses a geometric character that differs fundamentally from that of other quantum speed limits, as discussed in Ref.
HoSo2023a
.

More realistic descriptions of quantum systems must allow for statistical ensembles of pure states, or mixed states. Assuming the validity of Eq. (
2
) for pure states, Giovannetti
et al.
further showed that the same bound continues to hold for mixed states provided that the overlap is replaced by the Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity
Uh1976
;
Jo1994
. Because the structure of their argument plays an important role in the developments presented here, we include in Sec.
II
a slightly adapted version of their original derivation.

In this work, we identify all systems that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit, meaning those whose dynamics achieve equality in Eq. (
2
). While Ref.
HoSo2023a
provides a complete characterization of the pure states that attain the Margolus–Levitin bound, we extend this characterization to include mixed states as well (Secs.
III
and
IV
), thereby broadening the class of systems for which the bound is tight.

From this characterization, it follows that any state saturating the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit must have a highly constrained rank. In particular, its support is confined to the direct sum of two energy eigenspaces, and its dimension cannot exceed the smaller of the multiplicities of those eigenvalues. Consequently, faithful states can never saturate the Margolus–Levitin bound. This observation is made explicit in Sec.
V
, where we present a refined Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit applicable to mixed—and therefore faithful—qubits.

In Sec.
V
, we also extend the dual Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit derived by Ness
et al.

NeAlSa2022
to mixed states and characterize the systems that saturate this dual bound. This characterization follows directly from that obtained for systems saturating the standard Margolus–Levitin bound, since the dual speed limit can be recovered from the original via a time-reversal argument.

II  The Margolus–Levitin speed limit

Consider a system in a state
\rho
undergoing unitary evolution generated by a Hamiltonian
H
. Let
E=\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)
denote the system’s expected energy, and let
E_{0}
be the ground-state energy, defined as the smallest populated eigenvalue of
H
. The time
\tau
required for the system to evolve into a state having Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity
\delta
with the initial state is then bounded from below by the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit:

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{E-E_{0}},\quad\alpha(\delta)=\min f_{\delta}(z),

(3)

where the objective function is

f_{\delta}(z)=(1+z)\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z^{2}}},\quad-\sqrt{\delta}\leq z\leq\sqrt{\delta}.

(4)

The Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity (hereafter simply
fidelity
) is a measure of distinguishability between quantum states and is defined in terms of the trace norm as

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\|\sqrt{\rho_{1}}\sqrt{\rho_{2}}\|_{\operatorname{tr}}^{2}.

(5)

The trace norm of an operator is given by
\|A\|_{\operatorname{tr}}=\operatorname{tr}|A|
, where
|A|=\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
. For pure states, the fidelity reduces to their overlap: if
\rho_{1}=|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|
and
\rho_{2}=|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
, then

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})=|\langle\psi_{1}|\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}.

(6)

That the inequality in Eq. (
3
) holds for systems in pure states was established in Ref.
HoSo2023a
. Following Giovannetti
et al.

GiLlMa2003
, we show that this result implies its validity for systems in mixed states as well.

Remark 1
.

We say that an energy level, that is, an eigenvalue of
H
, is
populated
if the support of the state is not orthogonal to the corresponding eigenspace. Equivalently, the eigenvalue occurs with nonzero probability in an energy measurement. Because the Hamiltonian is time-independent, this probability is conserved. Consequently, the support of the state is contained in the sum of the eigenspaces associated with the populated energies.

Remark 2
.

The Margolus–Levitin speed limit does not, in general, extend to systems governed by time-dependent Hamiltonians. As shown in Ref.
HoSo2023b
, no canonical extension of the Margolus–Levitin bound exists in this setting.

II.A  Purification and Uhlmann’s Theorem

Before demonstrating that the Margolus–Levitin speed limit extends to systems in mixed states, we first introduce the notion of purification and recall Uhlmann’s theorem.

For every mixed state
\rho
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a unit vector
|w\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
such that
\rho
is recovered by taking the partial trace of
|w\rangle\langle w|
over the second copy of
\mathcal{H}
:
\rho=\operatorname{tr}_{2}|w\rangle\langle w|
; see Refs.
Jo1994
;
NiCh2010
. This vector is called a
purification
of
\rho
.

The Hamiltonians
H
and
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
share the same eigenvalue spectrum, up to multiplicities, and an eigenvalue of
H
is populated by
\rho
if and only if the corresponding eigenvalue of
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
is populated by
|w\rangle\langle w|
. (Accordingly,
E_{0}
denotes the smallest populated eigenvalue for both
\rho
and
|w\rangle\langle w|
.) Moreover,
\rho
and
|w\rangle\langle w|
have the same expected energy:

\operatorname{tr}(H\rho)=\langle w|H\otimes\mathbbm{1}|w\rangle.

(7)

Any other purification of
\rho
has the form
\mathbbm{1}\otimes U|w\rangle
for some unitary operator
U
acting on
\mathcal{H}
. Uhlmann’s theorem
Uh1976
asserts that the fidelity between two mixed states equals the maximal possible overlap between their purifications. Specifically, if
|w_{1}\rangle
and
|w_{2}\rangle
are purifications of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, then

F(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\max_{U}|\langle w_{1}|\,\mathbbm{1}\otimes U|w_{2}\rangle|^{2},

(8)

where the maximization is taken over all unitary operators
U
on
\mathcal{H}
. A proof of Uhlmann’s theorem can be found in Ref.
Uh1976
and in Refs.
Jo1994
and
NiCh2010
.

II.B  The Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit for systems in mixed states

Consider a system with Hamiltonian
H
prepared in a state
\rho
. Its time evolution is given by

\rho_{t}=U_{t}\rho U_{t}^{\dagger},\quad U_{t}=e^{-itH}.

(9)

Let
|w\rangle
be a purification of
\rho
.
The corresponding time-evolved purification is defined as

|w_{t}\rangle=U_{t}\otimes\mathbbm{1}|w\rangle,

(10)

which is the solution of the Schrödinger equation generated by
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
starting from
|w\rangle
.

The vector
|w_{\tau}\rangle
is a purification of the final state
\rho_{\tau}
, and, by Uhlmann’s theorem, the overlap
|\langle w|w_{\tau}\rangle|^{2}
is upper bounded by the fidelity
F(\rho,\rho_{\tau})
. As illustrated in Fig.
1
and proven in Appendix
A
, the function
\alpha
is monotonically decreasing. Consequently,
\alpha(|\langle w|w_{\tau}\rangle|^{2})
is lower bounded by
\alpha(F(\rho,\rho_{\tau}))
. Using the Margolus–Levitin bound for pure states, we therefore obtain

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(|\langle w|w_{\tau}\rangle|^{2})}{E-E_{0}}\geq\frac{\alpha(F(\rho,\rho_{\tau}))}{E-E_{0}}.

(11)

This establishes that the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit holds for systems in mixed states as well.

Graph of the function
\alpha
, which appears in the numerator of the Margolus–Levitin bound, shown as a function of the fidelity
\delta
between the initial and final states. As evident from the plot, the function is strictly decreasing. A proof of this property is provided in Appendix
A
.

III  Systems saturating the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit

In this section, we provide a complete characterization of all systems that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit. Reference
HoSo2023a
includes a full characterization of the systems in
pure
states that attain the Margolus–Levitin bound. We begin by recalling that result.

III.A  Systems in pure states that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit

Suppose the initial state of the system is represented by the unit vector
|\psi\rangle
. This state evolves into one whose overlap with
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
equals
\delta
in a time that saturates the Margolus–Levitin bound if and only if
|\psi\rangle
can be written as a superposition of the form

|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1-z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1+z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{1}\rangle,

(12)

where
|E_{0}\rangle
is a normalized ground-state vector,
|E_{1}\rangle
is a normalized energy eigenvector corresponding to a higher eigenvalue
E_{1}
, and
z_{\delta}
is the unique value of
z
at which the objective function
f_{\delta}(z)
, defined in Eq. (
4
), attains its minimum. The existence and uniqueness of this value are established in Appendix
B
. Figure
2
plots
f_{\delta}(z)
for several values of
\delta
, with the associated minimizers indicated along the horizontal axis.

Figure 2:
Plots of the objective functions corresponding to the fidelities
\delta=0.9,0.7,0.5,0.3,0.1
. The
z
-coordinates of their minima are indicated along the horizontal axis. A proof that the objective function for a given fidelity possesses a unique minimum is provided in Appendix
B
.

This characterization follows from the observation that the system must effectively behave as a qubit, that is, evolve within the span of two eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian. This was shown in Ref.
HoSo2023a
. For pure qubit systems, it is then straightforward to determine which states saturate the Margolus–Levitin bound. An explicit proof that
|\psi\rangle
can be written in the form of Eq. (
12
) is provided in Sec.
V
, which analyzes qubit systems in detail.

III.B  Systems in mixed states that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit

If a system with Hamiltonian
H
evolves between two states with fidelity
\delta
in a time
\tau
that saturates the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit (
3
), then the following statements hold for its initial state
\rho
:

(i)

The support of
\rho
is contained within the direct sum of two eigenspaces of
H
.

Since the populations of the energy levels are conserved, the system effectively behaves as a two-level system, possibly with degenerate levels. Let the corresponding energies be
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
, with
E_{0}
denoting the lower of the two.

(ii)

Every normalized eigenvector of
\rho
with a nonzero eigenvalue can be written in the form specified in Eq. (
12
).

This property implies—and is in fact equivalent to—the statement that all eigenstates of
\rho
with nonzero eigenvalues share the same expected energy, and that each evolves between states with overlap
\delta
in a time
\tau
that saturates the Margolus–Levitin bound. Giovannetti
et al.

GiLlMa2003
noted this as well, assuming the validity of the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit for pure states. Their argument, however, relied on numerical evidence concerning the function
\alpha
. In contrast, we obtain the same result here using only analytically established properties.

(iii)

Every pair of orthogonal eigenvectors of
\rho
with nonzero eigenvalues evolves within orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces of the Hilbert space.

This property implies that the rank of the state cannot exceed the smaller of the multiplicities of
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
. Consequently, a faithful state can never saturate the Margolus–Levitin bound. In Sec.
V
, we refine the Margolus–Levitin speed limit for qubit systems in a manner that remains tight irrespective of the state’s purity.

Equivalent description of systems that saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit

As we show below, conditions (i)–(iii) are jointly necessary and sufficient for a system to saturate the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit. They admit an equivalent formulation as follows. In a spectral decomposition

\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|,

(13)

where
r
is the rank of
\rho
and thus all
p_{j}>0
, each eigenvector admits a representation of the form

|\psi_{j}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1-z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{0}^{j}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1+z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{1}^{j}\rangle,

(14)

where
|E_{0}^{j}\rangle
and
|E_{1}^{j}\rangle
are normalized eigenvectors of
H
associated with the eigenvalues
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
, respectively. These energy eigenvectors are pairwise orthogonal,

\langle E_{0}^{j}|E_{0}^{k}\rangle=\langle E_{1}^{j}|E_{1}^{k}\rangle=\delta_{jk},\quad\langle E_{0}^{j}|E_{1}^{k}\rangle=0,

(15)

which means that the eigenvectors
|\psi_{j}\rangle
evolve within mutually orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces of the Hilbert space. All pure states
|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|
have the same expected energy, namely

E=\bigg(\frac{1-z_{\delta}}{2}\bigg)E_{0}+\bigg(\frac{1+z_{\delta}}{2}\bigg)E_{1},

(16)

and each evolves in such a way that it saturates the Margolus–Levitin bound with overlap
\delta
at time
\tau
.

IV  Proof that properties (i)–(iii) are necessary and sufficient

In establishing properties (i)–(iii), we rely on certain properties of the function
\alpha
and of the trace norm. We begin by stating these properties.

IV.A  Strict monotonic decay of
\alpha

The function
\alpha
appearing in the numerator of the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit is strictly decreasing: if
\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}
, then
\alpha(\delta_{1})>\alpha(\delta_{2})
. This behavior is apparent from Fig.
1
, and a proof is given in Appendix
A
.

IV.B  Properties of the trace norm

The trace norm is unitarily invariant, meaning that
\operatorname{tr}|UAV|=\operatorname{tr}|A|
for all unitary operators
U
and
V
. Moreover,
|\operatorname{tr}A|\leq\operatorname{tr}|A|
, with equality if and only if
A
differs from
|A|
only by a unimodular factor:

|\operatorname{tr}A|=\operatorname{tr}|A|\iff A=e^{i\theta}|A|\text{ for some }\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(17)

A proof of this equivalence is given in Appendix
C
.

IV.C  Properties (i)–(iii) are necessary

Consider a system with Hamiltonian
H
that evolves between two states whose fidelity is
\delta
in a time
\tau
that saturates the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit (
3
). Let
\rho
be the initial state,
E=\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)
its expected energy, and
E_{0}
the lowest populated energy level. Let
\rho_{t}
be the time-evolved state as defined in Eq. (
9
).

Choose an arbitrary purification
|w\rangle
of
\rho
and let it evolve in time as in Eq. (
10
). The operators
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
and
H
are isospectral (up to multiplicities), and
E_{0}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
that is populated by
|w\rangle\langle w|
. Moreover,
\langle w|H\otimes\mathbbm{1}|w\rangle=E
.

Since equality is assumed to hold throughout Eq. (
11
), and since
\alpha
is strictly decreasing and therefore injective, it follows that

|\langle w|w_{\tau}\rangle|^{2}=F(\rho,\rho_{\tau}).

(18)

The first equality in Eq. (
11
) then implies that the Margolus–Levitin bound for pure states is saturated by
|w\rangle\langle w|
. From the characterization in Sec.
III
,
|w\rangle
must therefore lie in the direct sum of the eigenspaces of
H\otimes\mathbbm{1}
associated with
E_{0}
and some higher eigenvalue
E_{1}
. These eigenspaces are
\mathcal{E}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are the eigenspaces of
H
corresponding to
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
. Since

|w\rangle\in(\mathcal{E}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{H})\oplus(\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H})=(\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1})\otimes\mathcal{H},

(19)

the support of
\rho
is contained in the sum of
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
:

\operatorname{supp}\rho=\operatorname{supp}\operatorname{tr}_{2}|w\rangle\langle w|\subset\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1}.

(20)

This establishes condition (i).

Properties (ii) and (iii) follow from consequences of Eq. (
18
). According to this equation, the square root of the overlap between the purifications
|w\rangle
and
|w_{\tau}\rangle
coincides with the square root of the fidelity between
\rho
and
\rho_{\tau}
. Both admit the following representations:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt|\langle w|w_{\tau}\rangle|=|\operatorname{tr}(\rho\,U_{\tau})|=|\operatorname{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho})|,

(21a)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\sqrt{F(\rho,\rho_{\tau})}=\operatorname{tr}|\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}^{\dagger}|=\operatorname{tr}|\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}\,|,

(21b)

where in the last step of Eq. (
21b
) we have used the unitary invariance of the trace norm. Since the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
21a
) and (
21b
) coincide, Eq. (
17
) implies that

\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}=e^{i\theta}\,|\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}\,|\text{ for some }\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(22)

In Appendix
D
, we show that condition (
22
) implies that the compression of
U_{\tau}
to the support of
\rho
is proportional to the orthogonal projection onto that support, and that the proportionality constant has modulus
\sqrt{\delta}
. Thus, if
P
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the support of
\rho
, condition (
22
) yields

P\,U_{\tau}\,P=\sqrt{\delta}\,e^{i\theta}P.

(23)

To establish property (ii), let
|\psi\rangle
be any normalized eigenvector of
\rho
with a nonzero eigenvalue
p
. Equation (
23
) then implies that
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
evolves into a state whose overlap with
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
equals
\delta
at time
\tau
:

|\langle\psi|U_{\tau}|\psi\rangle|^{2}=\delta.

(24)

Let
|\psi_{1}\rangle=|\psi\rangle
and
p_{1}=p
, and expand
\rho
in a spectral decomposition as in Eq. (
13
), where
r
is the rank of
\rho
, so that all
p_{j}>0
and each
|\psi_{j}\rangle
lies in the support of
\rho
. We now show that all states
|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|
have the same expected energy. Since each of these states is supported in
\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1}
and is nonstationary, it must populate the ground-energy eigenspace. Applying the Margolus–Levitin bound together with Eq. (
24
) yields, for every
|\psi_{j}\rangle
,

\tau\big(\langle\psi_{j}|H|\psi_{j}\rangle-E_{0}\big)\geq\alpha\big(|\langle\psi_{j}|U_{\tau}|\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}\big)=\alpha(\delta),

(25)

and therefore

\langle\psi_{j}|H|\psi_{j}\rangle\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{\tau}+E_{0}=E.

(26)

Equality with
E
follows from the assumption that the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit is saturated. Because
E=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}\langle\psi_{j}|H|\psi_{j}\rangle
, each term in this sum must individually satisfy

\langle\psi_{j}|H|\psi_{j}\rangle=E.

(27)

Equations (
24
) and (
27
) together show that every eigenvector—and in particular
|\psi\rangle
—represents a state whose unitary evolution saturates the Margolus–Levitin speed limit. It follows that
|\psi\rangle
can be represented in the form given in Eq. (
12
), in accordance with the characterization of pure states that achieve equality in the Margolus–Levitin bound. This establishes condition (ii).

To establish (iii), we represent each eigenvector
|\psi_{j}\rangle
as in Eq. (
14
). We then show that the associated energy eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal, as expressed in Eq. (
15
), from which condition (iii) follows.

Let
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
be the orthogonal projections onto the eigenspaces
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, respectively. Since the support of
\rho
is contained in
\mathcal{E}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{E}_{1}
, Eq. (
23
) implies that

e^{-i\tau E_{0}}PP_{0}P+e^{-i\tau E_{1}}PP_{1}P=\sqrt{\delta}\,e^{i\theta}P.

(28)

Together with
PP_{0}P+PP_{1}P=P
, this shows that the compressions of the eigenspace projectors to the support of
\rho
are each proportional to
P
:

\displaystyle PP_{0}P

\displaystyle=q_{0}P,\quad

\displaystyle q_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{\delta}\,e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\tau E_{1}}}{e^{-i\tau E_{0}}-e^{-i\tau E_{1}}},

(29a)

\displaystyle PP_{1}P

\displaystyle=q_{1}P,\quad

\displaystyle q_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{\delta}\,e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\tau E_{0}}}{e^{-i\tau E_{1}}-e^{-i\tau E_{0}}}.

(29b)

Combining these relations with the representation in Eq. (
14
) yields, for all indices

j,k
,

\displaystyle q_{0}\delta_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{k}|P_{0}|\psi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{\delta})\langle E_{0}^{j}|E_{0}^{k}\rangle,

(30a)

\displaystyle q_{1}\delta_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{k}|P_{1}|\psi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(1+z_{\delta})\langle E_{1}^{j}|E_{1}^{k}\rangle.

(30b)

These identities show that the conditions in Eq. (
15
) are satisfied and hence that the eigenvectors
|\psi_{j}\rangle
evolve within mutually orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces.

IV.D  Properties (i)–(iii) are sufficient

In the preceding section, we showed that conditions (i)–(iii) are necessary, in the sense that they must hold whenever the Margolus–Levitin speed limit is saturated. Here, we demonstrate that these conditions are also sufficient.

Assume that a system with Hamiltonian
H
is in a state
\rho
whose support is contained in the direct sum of the eigenspaces associated with two energy eigenvalues
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
, with
E_{0}<E_{1}
. Further assume that every eigenvector of
\rho
with a nonzero eigenvalue admits a representation of the form in Eq. (
12
), so that the corresponding pure state evolves into a state with overlap
\delta
after a time
\tau
that saturates the Margolus–Levitin bound. Finally, let
\rho
have a spectral decomposition of the form in Eq. (
13
), and assume that the eigenvectors in this decomposition evolve within mutually orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces. Under these conditions,

\begin{split}\sqrt{\rho}\,U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}&=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sqrt{p_{j}p_{k}}\,|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|U_{\tau}|\psi_{k}\rangle\langle\psi_{k}|\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|U_{\tau}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\big((1-z_{\delta})e^{-i\tau E_{0}}+(1+z_{\delta})e^{-i\tau E_{1}}\big)\rho.\end{split}

(31)

Thus,
\sqrt{\rho}U_{\tau}\sqrt{\rho}
is proportional to
\rho
, which is positive semidefinite. Hence Eq. (
22
) holds, and therefore so does Eq. (
18
). From Eq. (
18
), we obtain

\sqrt{F(\rho,\rho_{\tau})}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}\,|\langle\psi_{j}|\psi_{j;\tau}\rangle|=\sqrt{\delta}.

(32)

Accordingly, the system evolves between two states with fidelity
\delta
in time
\tau
. The smallest populated eigenvalue of
H
is
E_{0}
, and the expected energy is

\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}\langle\psi_{j}|H|\psi_{j}\rangle=E.

(33)

Thus,

\tau=\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{E-E_{0}}=\frac{\alpha(F(\rho,\rho_{\tau}))}{\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)-E_{0}}.

(34)

This shows that the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit is saturated whenever properties (i)–(iii) are satisfied.

V  Discussion

The Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit applies to both pure and mixed states, and in this work we have identified the precise circumstances under which it can be saturated. Specifically, if a system evolves unitarily from an initial state
\rho
to a state with fidelity
\delta
in a time
\tau
that attains the Margolus–Levitin bound, then
\rho
must be a convex combination of pure states that populate the same two energy eigenvalues, have identical expected energy, and individually saturate the bound with overlap
\delta
at time
\tau
. Moreover, the supports of these pure states must evolve within mutually orthogonal subspaces.

An immediate consequence of these structural constraints is that the rank of
\rho
cannot exceed the degeneracy of the ground-state energy. This requirement rules out faithful states. Below we introduce a modified Margolus–Levitin bound for qubits that permits faithful states to saturate the resulting speed limit as well.

This modification naturally raises the question of whether Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity is the most appropriate extension of the pure-state overlap to mixed states in this setting. While fidelity is a standard and operationally meaningful choice, it is by no means unique; several alternative generalizations of overlap exist and may lead to distinct or potentially sharper speed limits. Investigating such alternatives will be the subject of future work.

Ness
et al.

NeAlSa2022
derived a dual version of the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit, which was later extended to arbitrary overlap in Ref.
HoSo2023a
. In the last section below we extend this dual bound to mixed states, using Uhlmann–Jozsa fidelity as the distinguishability measure, and characterize all mixed-state systems that achieve equality in this dual quantum speed limit.

V.A  Quantum bits

The rank of any state that saturates the Margolus–Levitin speed limit cannot exceed the smaller degeneracy of the two energy levels it populates. As a consequence, no mixed qubit can satisfy the Margolus–Levitin bound. In this section, we derive a qubit-specific variant of the Margolus–Levitin bound that overcomes this limitation. The derivation will also clarify the structural origin of Eq. (
12
).

Consider a qubit governed by the Hamiltonian

H=E_{0}|E_{0}\rangle\langle E_{0}|+E_{1}|E_{1}\rangle\langle E_{1}|,\quad E_{0}<E_{1}.

(35)

We represent the state
\rho
by its Bloch vector
\bm{r}=(x,y,z)
in three-dimensional Euclidean space, defined by

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\langle E_{1}|\rho|E_{0}\rangle+\langle E_{0}|\rho|E_{1}\rangle,

(36a)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=i\!\left(\langle E_{1}|\rho|E_{0}\rangle-\langle E_{0}|\rho|E_{1}\rangle\right),

(36b)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\langle E_{1}|\rho|E_{1}\rangle-\langle E_{0}|\rho|E_{0}\rangle.

(36c)

The
z
-component of the Bloch vector is determined by the expected energy
E=\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)
through

z=\frac{2(E-E_{0})}{E_{1}-E_{0}}-1,

(37)

and the length of the Bloch vector is fixed by the state’s purity
\wp=\operatorname{tr}(\rho^{2})
, which remains constant under unitary evolution:

|\bm{r}|^{2}=2\wp-1.

(38)

The dynamics generated by
H
cause the Bloch vector to rotate about the
z
-axis with constant inclination and angular speed
\omega=E_{1}-E_{0}
. Let
\bm{r}_{\tau}
denote the Bloch vector of the state at time
\tau
. During the evolution, the orthogonal projection of the Bloch vector onto the
xy
-plane rotates through an angle

\theta\geq\arccos\!\bigg(\frac{\bm{r}\cdot\bm{r}_{\tau}-z^{2}}{|\bm{r}|^{2}-z^{2}}\bigg)=\arccos\!\bigg(\frac{\bm{r}\cdot\bm{r}_{\tau}-z^{2}}{2\wp-1-z^{2}}\bigg).

(39)

An illustration is provided in Fig.
3
. The corresponding evolution time is therefore

\tau=\frac{\theta}{\omega}\geq\frac{1}{E_{1}-E_{0}}\,\arccos\!\bigg(\frac{\bm{r}\cdot\bm{r}_{\tau}-z^{2}}{2\wp-1-z^{2}}\bigg).

(40)

Figure 3:
During the evolution, the Bloch vector precesses about the
z
-axis (vertical dashed line) with fixed inclination and angular speed
\omega
. The projections of the initial Bloch vector
\bm{r}
and final Bloch vector
\bm{r}_{\tau}
onto the
xy
-plane subtend an angle
\theta
, yielding an evolution time
\tau=\theta/\omega
.

Assume that the fidelity between
\rho
and
\rho_{\tau}
is
\delta
. Hübner’s formula for the fidelity between qubit states
Hu1992
gives

\delta=\operatorname{tr}(\rho\rho_{\tau})+2\sqrt{\det\rho\,\det\rho_{\tau}}\\
=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(3+\bm{r}\!\cdot\!\bm{r}_{\tau}-2\wp\right).

(41)

Combining Eqs. (
37
), (
40
), and (
41
), we obtain

\tau(E-E_{0})\geq\bigg(\frac{1+z}{2}\bigg)\!\arccos\!\bigg(\frac{2\delta+2\wp-3-z^{2}}{2\wp-1-z^{2}}\bigg).

(42)

We now define

\alpha(\delta,\wp)=\min\big\{f_{\delta,\wp}(z):z^{2}\leq\delta+2\wp-2\big\},

(43)

where the objective function is given by the right-hand side of Eq. (
42
), or equivalently,

f_{\delta,\wp}(z)=(1+z)\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{2\wp-1-z^{2}}}.

(44)

For a qubit state of purity
\wp
, any evolution between two states with fidelity
\delta
in time
\tau
must satisfy

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta,\wp)}{E-E_{0}}.

(45)

In the pure-state limit
\wp=1
, the qubit speed limit (
45
) reduces to the Margolus–Levitin bound (
3
).

Let
z_{\delta,\wp}
denote the value of
z
at which the objective function
f_{\delta,\wp}
attains its minimum. Suppose that the Bloch vector of a mixed state
\rho
of purity
\wp
has
z
-component
z_{\delta,\wp}
, and let
\tau
be the earliest time at which the fidelity between
\rho
and
\rho_{\tau}
equals
\delta
, so that equalities hold in Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
). Then equality also holds in Eq. (
42
), yielding

\tau(E-E_{0})=f_{\delta,\wp}(z_{\delta,\wp})=\alpha(\delta,\wp),

(46)

and the bound (
45
) is saturated.

By adjusting the phases of the energy eigenvectors
|E_{0}\rangle
and
|E_{1}\rangle
, if necessary, we may take the initial Bloch vector to have
x
-component
(2\wp-1-z_{\delta,\wp}^{2})^{1/2}
and
y
-component
0
. With this choice of phases, the state assumes the form

\begin{split}\rho=&\frac{1}{2}(1-z_{\delta,\wp})|E_{0}\rangle\langle E_{0}|\\
&\,+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\wp-1-z_{\delta,\wp}^{2}}\,\big(|E_{0}\rangle\langle E_{1}|+|E_{1}\rangle\langle E_{0}|\big)\\
&\,+\frac{1}{2}(1+z_{\delta,\wp})|E_{1}\rangle\langle E_{1}|.\end{split}

(47)

In the pure-state limit,
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, where

|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1-z_{\delta,1}}{2}}|E_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1+z_{\delta,1}}{2}}|E_{1}\rangle.

(48)

This establishes the explicit representation in Eq. (
12
).

V.B  The dual Margolus–Levitin speed limit

Ness
et al.

NeAlSa2022
derived a dual version of the Margolus–Levitin quantum speed limit: If a system governed by a time-independent Hamiltonian evolves between two perfectly distinguishable pure states in a time

\tau
, then

\tau\geq\frac{\pi/2}{E_{m}-E},

(49)

where
E_{m}
denotes the
largest
populated energy eigenvalue.

This inequality can be extended to an arbitrary overlap
\delta
between the initial and final states,

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{E_{m}-E},

(50)

and it remains valid for mixed states when
\delta
is taken to be the fidelity between the initial and final density operators.

Equation (
50
) follows directly from the Margolus–Levitin bound (
3
) through a time-reversal argument
HoSo2023a
: If the Hamiltonian
H
evolves
\rho
into
\rho_{\tau}
in a time
\tau
, then
-H
evolves
\rho_{\tau}
back into
\rho
in the same time. Let
\delta
be the fidelity between
\rho
and
\rho_{\tau}
. For the reversed Hamiltonian
-H
, the smallest populated eigenvalue is
-E_{m}
, and the expected energy is
-E
. Applying the Margolus–Levitin bound to this reversed evolution yields

\tau\geq\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{-E-(-E_{m})}=\frac{\alpha(\delta)}{E_{m}-E}.

(51)

If the inequality in Eq. (
50
) is saturated, then the support of the initial state is necessarily contained within the eigenspaces of
H
corresponding to the energies
E_{0}
and
E_{m}
. Moreover, each eigenvector of
\rho
with a nonzero eigenvalue admits a representation of the form

|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1+z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1-z_{\delta}}{2}}|E_{m}\rangle,

(52)

where
|E_{0}\rangle
and
|E_{m}\rangle
are energy eigenvectors with eigenvalues
E_{0}
and
E_{m}
, respectively. Thus, each such eigenvector represents a pure state that evolves into a state with overlap
\delta
in time
\tau
, thereby saturating the dual Margolus–Levitin bound (
50
). Moreover, when the initial mixed state is expressed as a convex combination of mutually orthogonal pure states, those associated with nonzero eigenvalues evolve within mutually orthogonal two-dimensional subspaces.

Appendix A
Proof that
\alpha
is strictly decreasing

In this appendix we show that the function
\alpha
, defined in Eq. (
3
), is strictly decreasing.

Let
\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}
. Since the objective function
f_{\delta_{1}}
is continuous on its compact domain
[-\sqrt{\delta_{1}},\sqrt{\delta_{1}}\,]
, there exists a
z_{1}
at which
f_{\delta_{1}}
assumes its minimum value. The point
z_{1}
also lies within the domain of
f_{\delta_{2}}
, and because arcsine is strictly increasing, we have that

f_{\delta_{1}}(z_{1})=(1+z_{1})\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta_{1}}{1-z_{1}^{2}}}>(1+z_{1})\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta_{2}}{1-z_{1}^{2}}}=f_{\delta_{2}}(z_{1}).

(53)

Consequently,
\alpha(\delta_{1})=f_{\delta_{1}}(z_{1})>\min_{z^{2}\leq\delta_{2}}f_{\delta_{2}}(z)=\alpha(\delta_{2})
, which proves that
\alpha
is strictly decreasing.

Appendix B
Proof that the objective function has a unique minimum

In this appendix we show that the point at which
f_{\delta}
assumes its minimum value is unique. When
\delta=0
, the domain of
f_{\delta}
collapses to the single point
\{0\}
, and the claim is immediate. We therefore assume throughout that
0<\delta<1
.

We first observe that the minimum is attained on the interval
[-\sqrt{\delta},0]
, since
f_{\delta}(-z)<f_{\delta}(z)
for all
z>0
. On the open interval
(-\sqrt{\delta},0)
, the derivative of
f_{\delta}
takes the form

f_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)=\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z^{2}}}+\frac{z\sqrt{1-\delta}}{(1-z)\sqrt{\delta-z^{2}}}.

(54)

The derivative diverges to
-\infty
as
z\downarrow-\sqrt{\delta}
, and approaches the positive limit

\arcsin\sqrt{1-\delta}
as
z\uparrow 0
. Hence the minimum must occur at a stationary
point in
(-\sqrt{\delta},0)
. To establish uniqueness, it suffices to show that
f_{\delta}^{\prime}
is positive at all of its stationary points, that is, points where the second derivative vanishes, for then the graph of
f_{\delta}^{\prime}
will cross the
z
-axis exactly once.

The second derivative of
f_{\delta}
is

f_{\delta}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{(\delta+2\delta z-\delta z^{2}-2z^{3})\sqrt{1-\delta}}{(1-z)^{2}(1+z)(\delta-z^{2})^{3/2}}.

(55)

It is apparent from this expression that the stationary points of
f_{\delta}^{\prime}
coincide with the zeros of the cubic polynomial
\delta+2\delta z-\delta z^{2}-2z^{3}
. Let
z_{*}\in(-\sqrt{\delta},0)
be such a zero. (If no zero exists,
f_{\delta}^{\prime}
has no stationary point in
(-\sqrt{\delta},0)
, and the minimizer of
f_{\delta}
is unique.) Then

\delta=\frac{2z_{*}^{3}}{1+2z_{*}-z_{*}^{2}}.

(56)

Since
0<\delta<1
and
z_{*}<0
, the denominator must be negative and the fraction must be strictly less than
1
, which together imply
z_{*}<-\tfrac{1}{2}
. We use (
56
) to rewrite
f^{\prime}_{\delta}(z_{*})
as follows:

f^{\prime}_{\delta}(z_{*})=\arcsin\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z_{*}^{2}}}-\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z_{*}^{2}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{(1-z_{*})^{2}}}.

(57)

Then, to determine the sign of
f^{\prime}_{\delta}(z_{*})
we use that
\arcsin(x)>x
for
0<x<1
to conclude that

f^{\prime}_{\delta}(z_{*})>\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{1-z_{*}^{2}}}\Bigg(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{2}{(1-z_{*})^{2}}}\,\Bigg).

(58)

Since both factors on the right-hand side are strictly positive, so is
f^{\prime}_{\delta}(z_{*})
. From this follows that the minimum of
f_{\delta}
is unique. We denote the unique minimizer by
z_{\delta}
.

Appendix C
Proof of the trace-norm properties

In this appendix we show that
|\operatorname{tr}A|\leq\operatorname{tr}|A|
for any operator
A
, and that equality holds if and only if
A=e^{i\theta}|A|
for some real number
\theta
. Since the statement is trivial when
A=0
, we assume
A\neq 0
.

Let
A=V|A|
be a polar decomposition of
A
, with
V
unitary. Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, we obtain

|\operatorname{tr}A|^{2}=\big|\operatorname{tr}(|A|^{1/2}V|A|^{1/2})\big|^{2}\leq\operatorname{tr}\big(|A|^{1/2}|A|^{1/2}\big)\operatorname{tr}\big(|A|^{1/2}V^{\dagger}V|A|^{1/2}\big)=(\operatorname{tr}|A|)^{2}.

(59)

This proves that
|\operatorname{tr}A|\leq\operatorname{tr}|A|
.

Now assume that equality holds:
|\operatorname{tr}A|=\operatorname{tr}|A|
. Then equality holds in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which implies that the operators
V|A|^{1/2}
and
|A|^{1/2}
are linearly dependent.
Thus, there is a scalar
a
such that
V|A|^{1/2}=a|A|^{1/2}
. Multiplying this identity with
|A|^{1/2}
from the right gives
A=a|A|
. Finally, from
|\operatorname{tr}A|=|a|\operatorname{tr}|A|
and the assumption
|\operatorname{tr}A|=\operatorname{tr}|A|
it follows that
a
has unit modulus. Thus,
a=e^{i\theta}
for some real number
\theta
, and
A=e^{i\theta}|A|
.

Proof that Equation (
