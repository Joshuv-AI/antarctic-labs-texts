Effects of fermented palm kernel cake on growth performance, intestinal health, cecal microbiota, and ammonia emission in broilers☆
Authors: Jun Li, Zhengyang Chen, Rongyan Huang, Zhao Liu, Congcong Yan, Xiuan Zhan, Yibin Xu, Yufang Wang, Yahua Lan, Xiaodong Zhang, Yongxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to evaluate the effects of fermented palm kernel cake (FPKC) on growth performance, intestinal health, cecal microbiota, nitrogen metabolism, and ammonia emission reduction of broiler chickens. A total of 900 healthy 21-d-old Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to 5 dietary treatments with 6 replicates per treatment: a basal diet (control (CON) group) and diets containing 4% (T1 group), 8% (T2 group), 12% (T3 group), or 16% (T4 group) FPKC for a 21-d feeding period. The results showed that compared with the CON group, T1, T2 and T3 groups had no adverse effects on growth performance (P > 0.05), whereas T4 group significantly increased feed-to-gain ratio (P < 0.05). Compared with the CON group, T2 and T3 groups significantly increased villus height, villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, and digestive enzyme activities in the duodenum and jejunum (P < 0.05). Moreover, the T2 and T3 groups showed significantly lower serum d-lactate, diamine oxidase, endotoxin, fecal pH, and ammonia emissions than the CON group (P < 0.05). In contrast, compared with the CON group, T4 group impaired intestinal morphology, disrupted nitrogen metabolism, and reduced serum total protein (P < 0.05). 16S rRNA sequencing showed that FPKC inclusion altered the cecal microbiota composition by increasing Faecalibacterium, Shuttleworthia, Erysipelatoclostridium, Flavonifractor and reducing Lactobacillus, Bacillus, Eubacterium, and Bifidobacterium abundance (at genus level), which are mainly associated with the production of short-chain fatty acids and protein fermentation. Collectively, FPKC can be included in broiler diets at levels up to 12% to improve intestinal health and reduce ammonia emission without compromising growth performance.Keywords: Fermented palm kernel cake, AA broilers, Growth performance, Intestinal health, Ammonia emission reduction

Introduction

Globally, the expansion of livestock and poultry production has increased reliance on conventional feed resources, particularly corn and soybean meal, thereby raising concerns over feed cost, availability, and supply security. In China, corn and soybean meal are also the primary energy and protein sources, respectively, in compound feeds for livestock and poultry (Naeem et al., 2021). However, the rapid growth of the livestock sector, together with limited land resources and constrained domestic grain production, has markedly increased China’s dependence on grain imports from multiple countries (Hanafiah et al., 2021). In 2024, China’s corn and soybean imports reached 13.64 million and 105.03 million tons, respectively, jointly representing 66% of total grain imports (GACC, 2025). This has imposed substantial pressure on China’s food security and has given rise to a potential chokepoint risk.

Palm kernel cake (PKC) is categorized as an agricultural and industrial byproduct emanating from the palm oil extraction process (Caldes et al., 2024). The worldwide annual yield of PKC surpasses 10 million metric tons, with an annual growth rate of approximately 10% (Huang et al., 2024). PKC contains approximately 16% crude protein, is the fifth most abundant protein meal feed resource after soybean, rapeseed, sunflower, and cottonseed meals. In addition, PKC contains a higher fat content than corn and soybean meal, and is therefore often regarded as an energy source in animal feed (Olukomaiya et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2025). Furthermore, PKC is cost-effective and non-toxic, and is therefore widely used in livestock-producing regions to substitute for corn and soybean meal, helping reduce production costs (Naeem et al., 2021).

However, PKC contains a high level of crude fiber (16-18%), which monogastric animals have limited capacity to utilize, thereby reducing overall nutrient digestibility (Alshelmani et al., 2014). Besides, excessive dietary fiber may also impair intestinal villus morphology in poultry, further affecting nutrient absorption (Rajesh and Pravin, 2021). In addition, PKC contains anti-nutritional factors (such as tannic acid, phytic acid, oxalic acid, β-mannan, xylan), which may interfere with the utilization of proteins and minerals (Witida et al., 2022). The amino acid imbalance in PKC, characterized by markedly low levels of methionine, lysine, histidine, and threonine, can limit the efficiency of protein synthesis, thereby reducing production performance in livestock and poultry.

At present, the main approaches to improving the feed value of PKC include enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial fermentation. Among them, enzyme–microbe synergistic fermentation, which combines exogenous enzyme preparations with probiotic microorganisms, generally shows greater effectiveness than enzymatic treatment or microbial fermentation alone (Liu et al., 2025). During fermentation, microorganisms secrete enzymes and produce metabolites that facilitate the degradation of macromolecular organic substances. Exogenous enzymes further accelerate the decomposition of refractory components (Chen et al., 2025). In addition, the metabolic products of microorganisms can serve as substrates for enzyme preparations, and the nutrients produced by the decomposition of enzyme preparations can be utilized by microorganisms, forming a mutually promoting effect and enhancing the fermentation effect (Du et al., 2024). This technology can reduce the content of PKC anti-nutritional factors, promote the generation of small molecule nutrients, improve palatability and nutrient utilization rate, and help shorten the fermentation cycle and enhance production efficiency.

Previous studies have shown that fermented palm kernel cake (FPKC) can improve nutrient digestibility and intestinal morphology in broilers compared with PKC, particularly at moderate dietary inclusion levels (Alshelmani et al., 2016; Hanafiah et al., 2017). As increasing attention has been directed toward improving the nutritional quality of PKC through enzyme–microbe co-fermentation, it is necessary to further evaluate the feeding value of fermented PKC in broiler chickens. Therefore, in the present study, a FPKC was produced using a heat-tolerant Bacillus subtilis strain in combination with xylanase, and its effects at different dietary inclusion levels on growth performance, intestinal health, cecal microbiota, nitrogen metabolism, and ammonia emission reduction of broilers were systematically investigated. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the broader application of FPKC in poultry production.

Materials and methods

All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang A&F University, which is responsible for overseeing and regulating the ethical use of animals in research at the university.

Birds and experimental design

A total of 900 healthy 21-day-old Arbor Acres broilers with similar body weight were selected and randomly divided into 5 groups, with 6 replicates in each group and 30 broilers per replicate. The experimental treatments included a control (CON) group fed a basal diet without FPKC, and four treatment groups (T1–T4) in which FPKC (provided by Shanghai Yuanyao Agricultural Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was included at levels of 4%, 8%, 12%, and 16%, respectively. The dietary inclusion levels of FPKC were selected based on previous studies and were arranged as graded supplementation levels for broilers (Alshelmani et al., 2016; Olukomaiya et al., 2019). The nutrient compositions of PKC and FPKC were presented in Table 1, and the dietary formulas and nutritional components of each group are shown in Table 2. The formulated diets complied with the nutritional requirements for broilers recommended by National Research Council (NRC, 1994) and Chinese chicken feeding standard (NY/T33-2004).

Broilers were raised in three-layer stacked cages (length 60 cm, width 50 cm, height 45 cm), with three chickens in each cage, and were fed pellet diets under continuous lighting throughout the experimental period. The test period is 22 to 42 days. Routine feeding management and immunization. Feed consumption and chicken flock weighing are calculated as one unit for each repetition.

Growth performance

The initial body weight of broilers was recorded on day 21. On day 42, body weight and feed consumption were recorded on a replicate basis. Mortality was monitored daily throughout the experimental period. Initial average weight (IAW), final average weight (FAW), average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated for each treatment group.

Fecal PH Determination

At 42 days of age, fresh fecal samples were collected twice on a replicate basis (0800 and 1600 h) and homogenized with distilled water at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio. After equilibration for 45 min, fecal pH was measured using an HI 99121 N pH meter (Hanna Instruments, USA).

Determination of ammonia produced by fecal fermentation

On days 35 and 42, 50 g of fresh feces per replicate were collected, placed into pre-labeled 300-mL Erlenmeyer flasks, sealed, and incubated at 37°C for fermentation. At the 6th, 12th, 18th, 24th, 30th, 36th and 48th hours of fermentation, the average ammonia concentration within 5 minutes was measured using a portable ammonia analyzer (AR8500, Smart Sensor, Hong Kong, China). After each measurement, flasks were resealed and fermentation continued (Li et al., 2022).

Sample collection and preservation

Following the conclusion of the formal experimental period, two male broilers with similar body weights were randomly selected from each replicate (12 per group, totaling 60 birds). The selected birds were fasted for 8 h with free access to water. Blood was collected from the wing vein into clot-activator tubes, and serum was obtained after clotting and centrifugation and stored at −20°C until analysis. After euthanasia, the small intestine was excised. The length and weight of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were recorded. Intestinal length index was calculated as the length of each intestinal segment divided by body weight, and intestinal weight index was calculated as the weight of each intestinal segment divided by body weight. Subsequently, midpoint sections (2–3 cm) of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were gently flushed with sterile physiological saline and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological evaluation. The remaining intestinal segments were processed for mucosal sampling, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further analysis, whereas cecal contents were aseptically collected for microbiota analysis.

Cecal microbiota structure

Genomic DNA was extracted from cecal digesta using the DNAiso reagent (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using barcoded primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR was performed in a 20-μL reaction using TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase under the following conditions: 95°C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; and 72°C for 10 min (ABI GeneAmp® 9700). Amplicons were gel-purified, cleaned up, and quantified using a Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Libraries were prepared using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China). Raw reads were deposited in the NCBI SRA. Paired-end reads were quality-filtered using fastp and merged with FLASH. Denoising and ASV inference were performed using DADA2 in QIIME2, and sequences assigned to chloroplasts or mitochondria were removed. To normalize sequencing depth, data were rarefied to 20,000 reads per sample, with a mean Good’s coverage of 99.09%. Taxonomy was assigned using a Naive Bayes classifier against the SILVA v138 database, and functional prediction was performed using PICRUSt2 (v2.2.0).

Observation of intestinal mucosal permeability indicators and morphology

Serum d-lactate (D-LA), Diamine oxidase (DAO) and endotoxin were determined using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The 4% formaldehyde-fixed duodenum, jejunum and ileum tissue samples were subjected to alcohol dehydration, xylene clearing, and finally wax embedding and H-E staining. Stained sections were microscopically examined and photographed. Villus height and crypt depth were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA), and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (V/C) was calculated.

Digestive enzymes and serum nitrogen metabolism indicators

The collected duodenal and jejunal mucosal samples were accurately weighed, diluted with 0.9% saline at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio, and thoroughly homogenized. The homogenates were centrifuged at 4°C and 3,500 r/min for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for the determination of digestive enzyme activities. α-Amylase, lipase, and protease activities were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The activities of α-amylase and protease were expressed as U/mg protein, whereas lipase activity was expressed as U/g protein.

Serum uric acid (UA), xanthine oxidase (XOD) and total protein (TP) were assayed using commercially available kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), and all measurements were performed in strict compliance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analyses

The experimental data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS 26.0, and Duncan's method was used for multiple comparisons of the data with significant differences. The results were expressed as "mean ± standard deviation", and P < 0.05 was used as the criterion for determining the significance of the difference.

Intestinal development

The effects of dietary FPKC supplementation on intestinal development are shown in Table 4. No significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed among groups in the absolute length and weight of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. Similarly, the intestinal length index and weight index showed no significant changes compared with the CON group. Intestinal morphological changes in the duodenum and jejunum are presented in Fig. 1. Compared with the CON group, broilers in the T1, T2, and T3 groups showed significantly increased duodenal villus height, whereas T4 showed a significantly lower value (P < 0.05). No significant difference in duodenal crypt depth was observed among treatments (P > 0.05). In the jejunum, T4 showed a significantly lower villus height than the other treatment groups (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed among the CON, T1, T2, and T3 groups (P > 0.05). Compared with the CON group, jejunal crypt depth was significantly reduced in the T1, T2, and T3 groups, but significantly increased in the T4 group (P < 0.05). Accordingly, the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio in both the duodenum and jejunum was significantly increased in the T2 and T3 groups and decreased in the T4 group relative to the CON group (P < 0.05).

Cecal microbiota

16S rRNA sequencing analysis indicated that dietary supplementation with FPKC did not significantly affect the α diversity of the cecal microbial community. No significant differences were observed among treatments in the Sobs, Chao, or Ace indices (P > 0.05). In contrast, β diversity analysis revealed that FPKC markedly reshaped the overall structure of the cecal microbiota (P < 0.05). Both NMDS and PCoA ordination plots demonstrated a clear separation between the FPKC-treated groups and the control group in the ordination space (Fig. 3D–E). With respect to microbial composition, relative abundance analysis showed that FPKC altered the cecal microbiota at both the phylum and family levels (Fig. 4A–B). Differential abundance analysis further identified several significantly altered genera among treatments (Fig. 4C). Specifically, compared with the control group, the T2 and T3 groups showed significantly higher relative abundances of Faecalibacterium, Erysipelatoclostridium, and Shuttleworthia, but significantly lower relative abundances of Lactobacillus and Bacillus at the genus level (P < 0.05; Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 6, LEfSe analysis revealed that the CON group was mainly enriched in Lactobacillus. The T2 group was characterized by enrichment of Bacillus and Shuttleworthia (at genus level), whereas the T3 group was dominated by Faecalibacterium (at genus level),Erysipelotrichaceae (at family level) and Erysipelatoclostridium (at genus level). The T4 group exhibited distinct microbial biomarkers compared with the CON group.

Discussion

Growth performance

Liu et al. (2025) found that inclusion of 12.51% FPKC (synergistic fermentation with xylanase, mannanase, Lactobacillus plantarum and Saccharomyces boulardii) to replace corn significantly improved ADG, ADFI, and feed conversion ratio in Cobb broilers from 1 to 42 days of age. In addition, it increased DM and CP digestibility during days 19–21 and enhanced DM, CP, and acid detergent fiber digestibility during days 40–42, while also improving antioxidant capacity and immune function. The results of the present study indicated no significant differences in ADG, ADFI, or FCR among the CON, T1, T2, and T3 groups, suggesting that the inclusion of FPKC at levels up to 12% had no adverse effects on the growth performance, feed intake, and feed utilization efficiency of broiler chickens. This may be attributed to the improved nutritional composition of PKC following enzyme-microbe synergistic fermentation. After this treatment, the β-mannan content of FPKC was reduced by half from approximately 30% to 15%, and crude fiber content decreased from 18.01% to 11.45%; additionally, the FPKC retained approximately 2.5% lauric acid. These modifications may alleviate potential antinutritional effects, improve the utilization of digestible dietary nutrients, and exert a positive impact on the intestinal environment of broilers.

However, when the inclusion level of FPKC reached 16%, the ADG of broilers declined and the F/G increased significantly, suggesting that excessive inclusion suppressed growth performance. Although fermentation treatment reduced the contents of crude fiber and antinutritional factors in PKC to a certain extent, at high inclusion levels, the absolute intake of residual non-starch polysaccharides and structural fiber may exceed the digestive capacity of broilers. This can limit nutrient digestion and absorption, lower feed utilization efficiency, and ultimately lead to impaired growth performance (Alshelmani et al., 2021).

Intestinal morphology

No significant differences were observed in the length, weight, and relative indices of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum in the present study, indicating that dietary inclusion of FPKC at levels up to 16% did not affect the overall intestinal development of broiler chickens. Our results were consistent with those of Alshelmani et al. (2016), who reported that dietary inclusion of FPKC at levels up to 30% did not induce significant differences in liver weight or the relative weights of other major digestive organs in Cobb broilers.

Although there were no significant differences in intestinal length or weight, notable morphological changes were observed in the intestine. Compared with the control group, the T2 and T3 groups increased villus height and V/C in the duodenum and jejunum. Increased villus height results in a larger absorptive surface area, while shallower crypts are generally linked to a higher epithelial cell turnover rate(Wilson et al., 2018). Accordingly, the morphological improvements in the T2 and T3 groups indicate an enhanced absorptive function of the small intestine. These effects may be attributed to the production of mannan oligosaccharides (MOS) during PKC fermentation and the presence of Bacillus subtilis, both of which contribute to the maintenance of intestinal health (Jayaraman et al., 2013; Teng et al., 2021). MOS can inhibit pathogen adhesion by competitively binding to bacterial receptor sites, while simultaneously enhancing beneficial bacterial metabolism and improving intestinal microecology (Naveen et al., 2022; Rachel et al., 2022). Soumeh et al. (2019) reported that dietary supplementation with MOS increased the V/C in both the duodenum and jejunum of Ross 308 broilers, thereby improving nutrient utilization efficiency and feed conversion ratio. In addition, dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis in Ross 308 broilers has been shown to improve intestinal morphology, as evidenced by increased villus height and a higher V/C in the duodenum and jejunum (Chen et al., 2025). By contrast, the T4 group exhibited significantly lower villus height and V/C in both the duodenum and jejunum than the CON group. This may be attributed to residual antinutritional factors and a relatively high fiber content, which increased mucus secretion and energy expenditure, thereby adversely affecting intestinal development (Kim et al., 2024). Shastak et al. (2015) demonstrated that β-mannans increase intestinal digesta viscosity and trigger feed-induced immune responses, thereby exerting detrimental effects on intestinal structure and function in poultry.

Digestive enzyme activity

Improvements in villus height and crypt structure imply an enlarged intestinal absorptive surface area. Such structural advantages are often accompanied by changes in digestive enzyme secretion. In the present study, broilers in the T2 and T3 groups exhibited significantly higher amylase, lipase, and protease activities compared with the CON group. These elevated digestive enzyme activities were consistent with the concurrent improvements in intestinal morphology observed in the same groups, indicating an enhanced capacity for the digestion of carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins. The observed increases in digestive enzyme activities may also be attributed to the presence of Bacillus subtilis in FPKC. Bacillus subtilis is known to secrete a broad spectrum of extracellular hydrolytic enzymes and stimulate the synthesis of endogenous digestive enzymes, thereby enhancing the degradation and utilization of nutrients in the small intestine (Hesham et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Chen et al. (2025) reported that dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis in low-protein diets significantly increased α-amylase and protease activities in the jejunum and pancreas of Ross 308 broilers.

Gut microflora

Previous studies have demonstrated that the gut microbiota of chickens plays a crucial role in maintaining host health by participating in metabolic regulation and immune defense, and its composition highly sensitive to modulation by multiple factors (Aruwa et al., 2021; Richard et al., 2023). Among these factors, variations in dietary composition and nutrient levels are considered key drivers shaping the structure and functional potential of the intestinal microbial community (Ross et al., 2024). In the present study, although partial replacement of corn and soybean meal with FPKC did not significantly affect the α-diversity of the cecal microbiota in broilers, the Sobs, Ace, and Chao indices were numerically higher in the FPKC-treated groups than those in the CON group. This numerical tendency suggests a potential positive effect of FPKC on microbial richness and may be related to the excellent acid and bile salt tolerance of Bacillus subtilis in FPKC, as well as the prebiotic effect of MOS in selectively stimulates the growth of beneficial microbes.

At the genus level, microbial composition analysis revealed that the relative abundances of Faecalibacterium, Erysipelatoclostridium, Flavonifractor, and Shuttleworthia were significantly higher in the T2 and T3 groups than those in the CON group. As a major butyrate-producing genus, an increased abundance of Faecalibacterium is widely regarded as an indicator of improved intestinal health (López et al., 2017; Martín et al., 2023). Butyrate, a key short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), not only acts as the primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells but also enhances tight junction protein expression, alleviates inflammatory responses, and exerts beneficial effects on host immune function(Ney et al., 2023; Mann et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that Erysipelatoclostridium is involved in the further degradation of polysaccharides, protein-derived compounds, and fermentation intermediates, and is closely linked to SCFAs production (Tiffany et al., 2021). An increased abundance of Erysipelatoclostridium may therefore enhance substrate conversion efficiency in the gut, thereby providing metabolic precursors for downstream butyrate-producing bacteria and reinforcing overall fermentative activity(Karim et al., 2024). Flavonifractor is capable of degrading flavonoids and polyphenolic compounds, producing low-molecular-weight organic acids and phenolic derivatives that may contribute to an improved intestinal metabolic milieu (Yang et al., 2021). Meanwhile, Shuttleworthia, an obligate anaerobe closely linked to carbohydrate fermentation, is usually enriched in association with improved utilization efficiency of fermentable substrates and increased SCFAs production (Piero et al., 2022). The increase in the aforementioned bacterial genera may be closely linked not only to the secretion of various hydrolytic enzymes by Bacillus subtilis during the fermentation of FPKC, but also to the concurrent production of prebiotic components such as MOS (Ong et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2025). These processes significantly improved the substrate profile in the intestine, enhanced the accessibility of fermentable carbon sources, provided favorable conditions for the growth of functional genera such as Erysipelatoclostridium, Flavonifractor, and Shuttleworthia, and further promoted the enrichment of butyrate-producing bacteria represented by Faecalibacterium (Wang et al., 2024). Collectively, these effects synergistically enhanced SCFAs production and intestinal fermentation capacity, thereby driving the structure and function of the gut microecosystem toward a profile beneficial to host health.

In contrast, the relative abundances of Lactobacillus and Bacillus at the genus level were significantly reduced in the T2 and T3 groups compared with the CON group. Although these genera are commonly classified as probiotics, their excessive enrichment under specific physiological or nutritional conditions has been associated with gut microbial dysbiosis and excessive lactate accumulation (Taylor et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that overdominance of Lactobacillus can simplify intestinal metabolic pathways and consequently inhibit the growth of beneficial strictly anaerobic bacteria that rely on complex substrates (Heczko et al., 2024). Therefore, the marked decrease in Lactobacillus and Bacillus observed in the present study may reflect a transition of the gut microbiota from a structure dominated by facultative aerobes and lactic acid fermentation toward a more stable state characterized by strict anaerobic fermentation and SCFAs production. This shift reduced the activity of metabolic pathways centered on lactic acid and protein fermentation, which may represent one of the mechanisms underlying the reduced fecal ammonia emissions observed in the FPKC-treated groups. Meanwhile, although the changes in Eubacterium and Bifidobacterium were not statistically significant, both genera showed an overall decreasing trend in the T2 and T3 groups relative to the CON group. Certain Eubacterium species have been reported to be closely involved in intestinal protein fermentation, during which they can produce amines, ammonia, and other potentially harmful metabolites, thereby increasing intestinal nitrogen load and impairing the maintenance of gut microbial homeostasis (Alberto et al., 2019). Thus, the reduction in the abundance of Eubacterium observed in the present study may also represent one of the factors contributing to the decreased fecal ammonia emissions in the FPKC-treated groups. Bifidobacterium may exacerbate rapid fermentation of simple carbohydrates and disrupt intestinal metabolic balance (Karina et al., 2011). In the present study, the decreased abundance of Bifidobacterium was accompanied by improved intestinal morphology and enhanced digestive enzyme activities in the T2 and T3 groups. This suggests that suppressing excessive, rapid glycolysis driven by Bifidobacterium may alleviate metabolic pressure on intestinal epithelial cells, thereby promoting enterocyte maturation and more efficient nutrient digestion. A more balanced carbohydrate fermentation process also facilitates metabolic cross-feeding among microbial communities and contributes to the establishment of a functionally stable intestinal ecosystem(Culp and Goodman, 2023). In addition, LEfSe analysis revealed that Faecalibacterium (at genus level), Erysipelotrichaceae (at family level), and Erysipelatoclostridium (at genus level) were significantly enriched in the T3 group, whereas Shuttleworthia (at genus level) predominated in the T2 group. The results of the LEfSe analysis were consistent with those of the genus-level differential abundance analysis.

Intestinal permeability

Elevated serum levels of d-lactate, DAO, and endotoxin typically indicate impaired intestinal barrier integrity accompanied by increased intestinal mucosal permeability (Tan et al., 2025). Increased intestinal mucosal permeability can promote the translocation of macromolecules, bacteria, and toxins across the intestinal epithelial barrier via the paracellular pathway into surrounding tissues or the systemic circulation, thereby inducing inflammatory responses and elevating the risk of intestinal disorders and even systemic diseases (Di et al., 2023). In this study, serum levels of d-lactate, DAO, and endotoxinwere significantly lower in the T2 and T3 groups than those in the CON group, indicating an enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity. This may be attributed to the enrichment of butyrate-producing beneficial bacteria following FPKC supplementation, as well as the formation of MOS and lauric acid during PKC fermentation. Butyrate serves as a major energy source for intestinal epithelial cells and promotes tight junction protein expression as well as epithelial cell renewal (Peng et al., 2009). By inhibiting pathogen adhesion to the intestinal mucosal surface, promoting the colonization of beneficial bacteria, and enhancing the production of SCFAs, MOS improves the intestinal microecological environment and synergistically reduces intestinal mucosal permeability (Naveen et al., 2022). In addition, lauric acid exhibits pronounced antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive pathogens, thereby indirectly alleviating intestinal inflammatory burden and preserving mucosal integrity (Liu et al., 2021).

Serum nitrogen metabolism

Nitrogen metabolism is an important indicator of protein utilization and metabolic balance in poultry. Elevated UA and XOD levels typically reflect excessive protein catabolism, whereas higher TP levels suggest improved amino acid absorption and enhanced tissue deposition (Chen et al., 2025; Nath et al., 2025). In the present study, serum UA, XOD, and TP levels in broilers did not differ significantly between the T1, T2, and T3 groups and the CON group, indicating that dietary FPKC supplementation up to 12% exerted no effect on nitrogen metabolism in broilers under the current experimental conditions. However, compared with the CON group, T4 group exhibited significantly elevated serum UA and XOD levels, accompanied by a significant reduction in TP concentration, indicating that excessive dietary inclusion of FPKC may adversely affect protein utilization efficiency and nitrogen metabolic balance. This phenomenon may be associated with an insufficient supply of bioavailable nutrients and reduced protein utilization efficiency at high dietary inclusion levels of FPKC. When the inclusion level reached 16%, the viscous effects of non-starch polysaccharides and structural fiber, together with impaired intestinal morphology, may reduce digestive enzyme activity and amino acid transport efficiency, thereby diverting amino acids from anabolic processes toward energy metabolism(Oscar and Woo, 2021; Rajesh and Pravin, 2021). This shift may enhance amino acid catabolism, as reflected by elevated serum UA and XOD levels, ultimately resulting in reduced serum TP level.

Environmental parameters

A lower fecal pH generally indicates increased SCFAs production from carbohydrate fermentation and a more favorable gut environment, whereas a higher pH is often associated with excessive protein fermentation and ammonia accumulation (Oliver et al., 2024; Brinck et al., 2025). The results of the present study demonstrated that T3 group exhibited a significantly lower fecal pH compared with the control group. This finding suggests that a dietary inclusion level of 12% FPKC may provide a more favorable substrate environment for carbohydrate-degrading bacteria, thereby promoting SCFAs production and enhancing luminal acidification. The increased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria observed in the T3 group further supports this interpretation, as butyrate generation contributes to a reduction in intestinal luminal pH and plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal epithelial health (Liu et al., 2021; Karim et al., 2024).

Ammonia, a toxic byproduct of nitrogen metabolism, poses a significant threat to the welfare and production efficiency of broilers (Zhou et al., 2020). In the present study, fecal ammonia emissions were significantly lower in the T3 and T4 groups than those in the CON group at 24-48 h of fermentation, whereas those in the T2 group were significantly reduced at 36-48 h of fermentation. The underlying mechanisms for this phenomenon may involve the following two aspects. First, the decreased relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Bacillus, and Eubacterium in the T2 and T3 groups may suppress metabolic pathways dominated by protein fermentation in the gut, thereby reducing ammonia production (Alexandria and Manuel, 2022; Wu et al., 2022). Second, the reduced fecal pH in the T2 and T3 groups may also inhibit ammonia volatilization to a certain extent (Liu et al., 2020). A lower fecal pH favors the existence of ammonia in the form of NH₄⁺, thereby reducing the production of NH₃.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that FPKC could be effectively utilized in broiler diets as a partial substitute for conventional feed ingredients. Moderate inclusion levels (8%–12%) improved intestinal health and digestive function, decreased fecal ammonia emissions, and maintained growth performance. Conversely, excessive supplementation (16%) negatively influenced feed efficiency and nitrogen metabolism. Collectively, FPKC serves as a sustainable alternative feed resource for broilers, with 8%–12% recommended as the optimal inclusion range.
