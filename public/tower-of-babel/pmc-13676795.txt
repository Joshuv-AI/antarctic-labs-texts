Radioanatomical analysis for precise harvesting of the pedicled nasoseptal flap in anterior skull base reconstruction during craniofacial surgery
Authors: Dongsheng Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the role of the vascularized nasoseptal flap (VNSF) and septal flip flap (SFF) in anterior skull base (ASB) reconstruction of craniofacial therapy by studying the radioanatomy of VNSF, SFF and ASB reconstruction data on adult Computed Tomography (CT) multiplanar reconstruction.MethodsThe length, width and area of VNSF, SFF, and ASB defects were measured by Computed Tomography CT in 40 adults, and then the above date were statistically analyzed to confirm whether VNSF and SFF can be used to cover the ASB defect region produced after surgery.ResultsThe area of VNSF far exceeded that of ASB defect (10.21 ± 1.97 cm2), and the anterior and posterior widths, as well as the upper and lower lengths of VNSF were at least 8.4 mm longer than the corresponding width and length of ASB defect to be reconstructed. The VNSF containing vascular pedicle had a total length exceed that required for ASB reconstruction via the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus (SS) by at least 17.6 mm, but a mean length that was 7.02 ± 8.71 mm less than that required for transsphenoidal reconstruction of ASB. The area of SFF far exceeded that of the ASB defect (8.93 ± 1.49 cm2). The anterior and posterior widths, as well as the lower length of SFF all exceeded the corresponding width and length of the ASB defect to be reconstructed by at least 8.4 mm, and the upper length exceeded the ASB defect length by at least 1.5 mm. The independent sample t test showed no difference between sexes. Pearson correlation analysis showed no age difference except for the difference between SFF anterior width and ASB anterior width (P = 0.016). VNSF can reconstruct the defect of ASB through the anterior wall of the SS.ConclusionWith the assistance of SS packing and other methods, VNSF can transsphenoidally reconstruct the defect of the ASB, and SFF can reconstruct the defect of the ASB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02606-7.

Introduction

After craniofacial resection, the standard reconstruction technique is to directly close the dural defect with a fascia graft and rotate the pericranial flap based on the anterior skull to cover the dural defect without reconstructing the anterior skull base (ASB) bone defect [1, 2]. Overcoming the hurdles in SB reconstruction is crucial for advancing the broader adoption of transnasal endoscopic SB surgery [3]. Early reconstruction, usually using free grafts to repair SB defects, has been reported to result in more than 30% of CSF leaks [4]. With the introduction of vascularized nasoseptal flap (VNSF) and septal flip flap (SFF), the postoperative CSF leakage rate decreased significantly, with the rate ranging from 3.1% to 8.3% reported in each group [5–7]. Consistent repair efficacy of vascularized septal flaps has also been validated in pediatric endoscopic middle skull base reconstruction practice [8]. Recently, the combination of NSF and dural graft has emerged as a standard approach to transnasal endoscopic SB reconstruction [9–12].

This research attempted to investigate the clinical implications of VNSF and SFF in transnasal endoscopic ASB reconstruction by means of radioanatomy, with the view to verifying whether the NSF is adequate to cover the ASB defect after endoscopic SB surgery.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

This was a retrospective observational radioanatomical study. Thin-layer axial CT images of the SB (complete SB and paranasal sinus) of 40 adult patients, with a slice thickness of 0.3 mm, a tube voltage of 110 kV, and an exposure time of 3.6 s, were obtained. The sample size (n = 40) was determined based on previously published radioanatomic studies of similar skull base and flap measurement design. A post hoc power analysis was performed, and the achieved power was greater than 0.80 (α = 0.05), indicating that the sample size was sufficient for the statistical comparisons performed in this study. Later, multi-planar reformatting (MRP) was performed by software. Exclusion criteria were the presence of SB or sinus fractures, intracranial space-occupying lesions, or cephalo-facial deformities. All subjects enrolled in the study were aged from 21 to 78, including 24 females and 16 males (see Table 1 for details). All images were obtained from clinical radiological records at our institution.

Anatomical characteristics and imaging measurement of sphenopalatine foramen (SPF)

SPF is situated on the lateral wall of the nasal cavity near the base of the sphenoid sinus (SS) behind the middle nasal meatus or the caudal end of the middle turbinate. Its superior border is part of the sphenoid body, its anterior border is the orbital process of the palatine bone, its inferior and medial borders are the vertical plate of the palatine bone, and its posterior border is the sphenoid process of the palatine bone. The base outwardly and inferiorly connects the pterygopalatine fossa (PPF) that is communicated with the orbit anteriorly and superiorly through the inferior orbital fissure (IOF). Therefore, we choose these anatomical landmarks to locate the SPF on CT images. The images were reconstructed by software. After MRP, coronal CT images that showed IOF, PPF and SPF were chosen, and the axial and midsagittal images were located at the position of SPF (Fig. 1). In this study, three image parameters related to SPF were assessed: (1) the length from the projection point of SPF to the ipsilateral sphenoethmoidal suture through the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus was measured on the midsagittal MRP image (Figure S1A); (2) the length from the projection point of SPF to the ipsilateral sphenoethmoidal suture through the bottom of the sphenoid sinus and the posterior wall to the dorsal sella edge was measured on the midsagittal MRP image (Figure S1B); (3) the length from the SPF to the posterior edge of the nasal septum passing through the lower edge of the SS anterior wall was measured on the coronal MRP image (Figure S1C).

Imaging measurements of VNSF

There are five VNSF-related image measurement parameters: 1) VNSF anterior width (VNSF_AW), measuring the length from the spina nasalis anterior to the rhinion (free edge under the nasal bone) on the midsagittal plane (MSP) (Fig. 2A) VNSF posterior width (VNSF_PW); after locating the sphenoethmoidal suture on the MSP, the length from the ethmoidal crest to the nasal base in the coronal position of the same MPR image was measured, and 10 mm was subtracted from the result (Fig. 2B) the VNSF upper length (VNSF_UL), which is the distance from the rhinion (free edge under the nasal bone) 10 mm below the ethmoidal crest in the median sagittal position to the SS anterior wall, was measured (Fig. 2C); 4) the VNSF lower length (VNSF_LL), which is the length from the lowest end of the nasal septum to the foremost end of the maxillary palatine process in the median sagittal position, was measured (Fig. 2D) the VNSF area was measured (VNSF_S). The measurement was made 10 mm below the top of the ethmoidal crest from the SS anterior wall horizontally forward to the posterior wall of the frontal sinus, anteriorly downward along the nasal septum mucosal junction to the nasal floor, horizontally backward to the posterior edge of the nasal septum and upward along the SS anterior wall back to the starting point (Fig. 2E).

There are five measurement parameters related to SFF: 1) SFF anterior width (SFF_AW): after positioning the posterior wall of the frontal sinus on the MSP, the length from the ethmoidal crest to the nasal floor was assessed on the coronal plane of the same MPR image (Fig. 3A) SFF posterior width (SFF_PW). The measurement was consistent with the measurement method of VNSF_PW (Fig. 2B), but does not need to be reduced by 10 mm; 3) SFF upper length (SFF_UL), namely, the length from the rhinion (free edge under the nasal bone) 10 mm below the ethmoidal crest in the midsagittal position to the SS anterior wall was measured (Fig. 3B) SFF lower length (SFF_LL) was measured, with the same measurement method as VNSF_LL (Fig. 2D) the SFF area (SFF_S) was measured in the same way as VNSF_S (Fig. 3C).

The width of VNSF and SFF can be extended from the nasal floor to the ipsilateral inferior nasal meatus, which was measured by positioning the posterior wall of the frontal sinus in the MSP, and measuring the linear length between the lateral wall of the nasal cavity and the nasal septum below the head-end of the inferior turbinate on the coronal plane of the same MPR image (Fig. 3A).

Imaging measurements of ASB

Four parameters related to the ASB were measured: 1) anterior width of the ASB defect: after the anterior ethmoidal artery foramen was positioned in the midsagittal view, the linear distance between the ethmoidal-orbital plate on both sides of the anterior ethmoid foramen on the coronal plane of the same MPR image was measured (Fig. 4A); posterior width of the ASB defect: after locating the sphenoethmoidal suture in the MSP, the linear length between the ethmoidal-orbital plates at the sphenoethmoidal junction on the coronal plane of the same MPR image was measured (Fig. 4B); the ASB defect length was calculated as the straight line distance between the posterior wall of the frontal sinus and the ethmoidal crest of the SS anterior wall on the MSP (Fig. 4C); ASB defect area: after identifying the ethmoidal crest in the MSP, the rectangular area between the posterior wall of the frontal sinus and the anterior wall of the SS on both sides of the ethmoidal-orbital plate was measured (Fig. 4D).

Anatomical measurement software and statistical methods

CT imaging measurements were performed by IMAGE VIEWER software (standalone version) of CANONA (Japan). All measurements were performed by two independent senior radiologists in a blinded manner. To ensure reliability, intraobserver reproducibility was tested in 10 randomly selected cases, with an intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.90, indicating excellent measurement consistency. Additionally, interobserver reproducibility was assessed by comparing measurements from the two independent radiologists in 10 randomly selected cases. The interclass correlation coefficient was greater than 0.90, indicating excellent interobserver agreement. The data was statistically analyzed by SPSS software of IBM (Armonk, NY, USA). The independent sample t-test was utilized to identify gender differences, and the bivariate Pearson correlation analysis was adopted to determine age differences. All assumptions for Pearson correlation analysis, like normality and homoscedasticity, are satisfied in the study. All means were in the form of “mean±standard deviation”. 95% confidence intervals were calculated and statistical significance was indicated by P < 0.05, otherwise the difference had no statistical significance.

Results

Comparison of radioanatomical measurement data related to VNSF, SFF, and ASB

See Figure S2 and Table S1 for the means of the following parameters: the anterior and posterior widths, upper and lower lengths, VNSF area, and the total flap length (including vascular pedicle) of VNSF; the AW and PW, UL and LL of SFF, and SFF area; the AW, PW, and length of the ASB defect; the average length of SPF from the anterior SS and through SS to the anterior edge of the ASB defect. Based on linear and area measurements obtained from multiplanar reconstructed CT images, we quantitatively assessed whether VNSF and SFF could fully cover anterior skull base defects formed after transnasal endoscopic resection. Two unified quantitative cut-off values were adopted to judge reconstruction feasibility according to existing anatomical research [13]. First, each linear dimension of the flap should surpass the corresponding defect margin by at least 6 mm, to reserve tolerance for intraoperative mucosal stretching and postoperative slight flap contraction. Second, the total length of vascular pedicle flap needs to exceed the reconstruction pathway length by at least 3 mm to avoid pedicle tension during transposition. If any single index fails to reach the above standard, the flap is defined as anatomically insufficient for defect coverage. All mean differences, minimum values and standard deviations of flap-defect dimensional gaps are summarized in Table 2. Independent sample t-test results comparing male and female subgroups are fully listed in Table 3 to ensure transparent statistical presentation. It can be seen that the VNSF_S far exceeded that of the ASB defect (10.21 ± 1.97 cm2), and VNSF_AW, VNSF_PW, VNSF_UL, VNSF_LL all exceeded the corresponding width and length of the ASB defect to be reconstructed by at least 8.4 mm, so VNSF could completely cover the ASB defect. The total length of VNSF (containing vascular pedicle) was at least 17.6 mm longer than the length required to reconstruct the ASB via the SS anterior wall. But the mean total length of VNSF (containing vascular pedicle) was 7.02 ± 8.71 mm less than that required for transsphenoidal reconstruction of ASB (at least 20.7 mm); most cases did not meet the length threshold for transsphenoidal reconstruction. The area of SFF far exceeded that of the ASB defect (8.93 ± 1.49 cm2). The SFF_AW, SFF_PW, and SFF_LL exceeded the corresponding width and length of the ASB defect to be reconstructed by at least 8.4 mm, and the SFF_UL was at least 1.5 mm longer than the length required for ASB defect reconstruction (mean: 9.88 ± 3.83 mm). Since SFF_UL did not meet the 6 mm standard we set, we conducted a scatterplot analysis on this set of relevant data (see Figure S3 for details). On the basis of scatterplot analysis, SFF remains competent for anterior skull base defect reconstruction.

Comparison of VNSF and SFF related radioanatomical measurement data between the sexes

In this study, we analyzed whether the length, width and area of VNSF and SFF were different from the corresponding data required for ASB defect reconstruction between male and female patients (see Table 3 and Figure S4 for specific results), and no markedly difference were found between sexes. Pearson correlation analysis was used to analyze whether there were age differences in these parameters (see Table S2 for specific results); except for the difference between SFF_AW and SKB_AW (P = 0.016), the P-values of all other differences were greater than 0.05, suggesting the presence of a significant age difference between SFF_AW and the anterior width of the ASB, and the absence of significant age difference in other related parameters.

Discussion

With the continuous development of endoscopic skull base surgery in recent decades, endoscopic resection of ventral skull base lesions has become widely applied. Improved anatomical understanding and surgical techniques have extended the indications for removing larger and more complex tumors [14]. However, the need for reliable reconstruction of skull base defects remains a key challenge limiting the broader application of these procedures [15].

For small skull base defects, reconstruction outcomes are less dependent on the repair material, so vascularized flaps are not always necessary [16]. For large defects, multiple techniques have been used, including synthetic materials, free grafts, and vascularized flaps. Youngerman et al. [17] reported repair of large anterior skull base defects using acellular dermal matrix, with a 5% cerebrospinal fluid leak rate. Vascularized flaps—including pericranial, temporoparietal, palatal, buccal myocutaneous, inferior turbinate, middle turbinate, lateral nasal wall, and nasoseptal flaps—have been widely adopted for endoscopic reconstruction, as they provide reliable separation of intracranial and nasal compartments and promote faster healing [3, 12, 15, 18‑20]. The VNSF is the most commonly used due to its wide arc of rotation and ability to cover large anterior skull base defects [21]. Auxiliary intraoperative techniques can reduce mucosal injury during flap harvesting, and 2-µm thulium laser-assisted dissection has been proven to simplify nasoseptal flap preparation while protecting vascular pedicle integrity and improving intraoperative hemostasis [22]. Despite its widespread use, the VNSF may be associated with donor‑site morbidity, including nasal crusting, dryness, crust formation, and reduced nasal quality of life. In addition, vascular pedicle compromise or intraoperative injury to the pedicle or nasal mucosa may preclude reliable flap harvest and transfer. To address these concerns, the SFF has been introduced and successfully used for anterior skull base reconstruction [23, 24].

Recent radioanatomic studies have yielded conflicting conclusions regarding the reach of the VNSF. Some authors reported that the VNSF can cover all anterior skull base defects [25, 26], while others found it insufficient for reconstruction extending to the sellar region [27]. An Asian radioanatomic study noted that the smaller nasal septum in Korean patients may increase the difficulty of anterior skull base reconstruction, especially in women, and suggested widening the flap to improve coverage [28].

The present study focused on Chinese adults and evaluated the coverage ability of VNSF and SFF based on length, width, and area measurements. Both flaps were sufficiently large to cover anterior skull base defects, even after accounting for flap contraction and olfactory mucosa preservation. The VNSF reliably reached defects via the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus without sinus opening, with no significant sex or age differences. However, when the sphenoid sinus is opened—a common scenario in clinical practice [29]—the longer transsphenoidal pathway exceeded the pedicled length of the VNSF in most cases. Only 6 of 40 cases met the length requirement for transsphenoidal reconstruction. Preoperative radioanatomic measurement and case selection can improve feasibility. Sphenoid sinus packing with autologous tissue can also shorten the required path and rescue VNSF reconstruction [30, 31]. Dead‑space obliteration with fat or other grafts may further reduce the required flap dimensions.

In contrast, the SFF does not require transsphenoidal transposition and is unaffected by sphenoid sinus opening. The SFF provided reliable coverage in all cases. Although the upper length of the SFF exceeded the defect length by a mean of only 9.88 ± 3.83 mm (minimum 1.5 mm), which fell below our 6‑mm threshold in 6 cases, the flap remained clinically sufficient due to its gradual widening toward the nasal floor and ample overall width. No significant sex differences were observed, and the only age‑related difference involved anterior width.

Preoperative assessment is essential in endoscopic skull base reconstruction to match flap size to defect dimensions and avoid mismatch [32‑36]. A major limitation of endoscopic surgery is the lack of three‑dimensional volumetric feedback, making intraoperative size estimation difficult. Few studies have reported quantitative endoscopic skull base dimensions or flap‑to‑defect matching data [37‑39]. Individual variation in flap and defect dimensions did not alter the study conclusions but warrants further investigation.

Systematic preoperative radioanatomic analysis helps surgeons evaluate VNSF and SFF dimensions, defect size, septal quality, tumor involvement, and pedicle safety. This approach supports optimal flap design and reduces reconstructive complications [10, 28].

For consistency and reproducibility, linear measurements used straight lines, and defect areas were calculated using rectangular templates. Irregular outlines were avoided to minimize measurement variability. The reliability of radioanatomic modeling for skull base and flap sizing has been previously validated [40].

This study has several limitations. First, this is a purely radioanatomic investigation without correlation to real surgical procedures or postoperative clinical outcomes. All measurements are derived from static CT imaging data, and we cannot verify whether the dimensional gap between flap and defect directly predicts intraoperative coverage effect, postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leak risk, or long-term nasal function recovery. Further prospective clinical research is required to validate the clinical predictive value of these radiological measurement indicators. Second, the anterior skull base defect was defined as a standardized rectangular template, which may not accurately represent the variable morphology and size of actual surgical defects, particularly following oncologic resection. Tumor invasion and bone destruction often produce irregular, asymmetric skull base wounds that cannot be fully simulated by unified rectangular measurement models. Third, the 6 mm margin threshold and 3 mm pedicle length requirement were adopted from prior literature and remain somewhat arbitrary. These unified cut-off values do not take individual variations into account, including nasal septum thickness, mucosal elasticity and intraoperative tissue stretch degree of different patients. Fourth, although intraobserver reliability was confirmed, formal interobserver variability analysis was not performed. Fifth, individual differences in skull base anatomy were noted but not fully integrated into a practical clinical decision algorithm for individualized flap selection before surgery. Finally, adjunctive techniques such as fat grafting, multilayer reconstruction, and sphenoid sinus obliteration were not incorporated into the current radiological model. Autologous fat packing and multi-layer repair can shorten the required flap length during surgery, a variable that cannot be quantified in our current CT measurement system.

Conclusions

Preoperative radioanatomic analysis enables accurate assessment of VNSF and SFF dimensions relative to anterior skull base defects. The VNSF reliably covers defects via the anterior wall of the sphenoid sinus, while transsphenoidal reconstruction is limited by pedicle length in most cases. The SFF provides consistent and reliable coverage independent of sphenoid sinus opening. Sex and age do not significantly affect reconstructive suitability. Systematic preoperative CT measurements can guide flap selection, design, and harvest to improve the safety and reliability of anterior skull base reconstruction.
