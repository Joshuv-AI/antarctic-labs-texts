Multidimensional Phenotyping of the OB-DIGITHUM Cohort of 1,014 Adults with Obesity across Four Sex- and Age-Stratified Profiles
Authors: Clotilde Vázquez, Teresa Montoya-Alvarez, Jersy Cárdenas-Salas, Bogdana Luiza Luca, Amalia Paniagua, Yvonne Fernández, Víctor Pérez de Arenaza, Clara Marijuán, Francisco Arrieta, Claudia Causso, Leopoldo García-Valdecasas, Roberto Sierra, Irene Hoyas, Sebastián Mas-Fontao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity heterogeneity by sex and age is crucial for precise risk stratification. Limited phenotyping obscures sex-specific risks and contributes to diagnostic and treatment disparities.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study of 1,014 adults with obesity (68% women; median age 50) from four Madrid Obesity Centers using a digital pathway (OB-DigiThum) including anthropometry, bioimpedance, biomarkers, and comorbidity screening. Fat mass (FM) and skeletal muscle mass (SMM) were normalized to body surface area (FM/BSA; SMM/BSA). Participants were stratified into four predefined sex and age-groups (female <50, female ≥50, male <50, male ≥50). Multidimensional phenotyping was performed using discriminant analysis and hierarchical clustering applied to standardized group-level mean profiles.ResultsWomen had higher FM/BSA and lower SMM/BSA than men (p < 0.001), highlighting BMI’s limitations. With age, women’s BMI fell despite stable/increasing fat due to muscle loss (sarcopenic risk). Men showed more cardiometabolic disease overall, but post-menopausal women experienced sharp rises in type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Psychological distress was more frequent in women; bulimic behaviors appeared in younger men. Physical inactivity was common; Mediterranean diet adherence was relatively strong, especially in participants aged ≥50 years. The four sex- and age-stratified profiles showed distinct patterns in body composition, metabolic risk, and psychological burden, supporting differential risk stratification across groups.ConclusionSex and age strongly shape obesity phenotypes. Post-menopausal women face accelerated metabolic deterioration and sarcopenia. FM/BSA and SMM/BSA enhance risk profiling. Sex- and age-tailored metabolic, physical, and mental health screening should guide personalized management.

Introduction

Obesity is a multifactorial disease characterized by excess or dysfunctional adiposity, leading to adverse outcomes. Despite well-documented biological differences, the sex perspective has been overlooked in obesity research and management [1]. Sex hormones influence fat storage and distribution. Men accumulate more visceral adipose tissue, which is closely linked to insulin resistance, inflammation, and cardiometabolic complications. In contrast, women preferentially store subcutaneous fat, which is less metabolically active. Although menopause-related estrogen decline shifts fat distribution toward increased visceral accumulation, heightening metabolic risk, sex-specific differences in fat distribution have critical implications for obesity-related morbidity [2–4].

Clinical observations further underscore these disparities. Women are more likely than men to seek medical care for obesity yet tend to experience greater lifetime weight fluctuations and achieve poorer long-term outcomes despite similar exposure to lifestyle interventions. This paradox may reflect not only differential access to care but also underlying sex-specific differences in adipose tissue biology, lean mass composition, and the complex interplay of psycho-emotional, functional, and social determinants of obesity [5, 6]. Furthermore, postmenopausal women often exhibit a more limited response to caloric restriction alone, suggesting distinct physiological mechanisms that may reduce the effectiveness of conventional approaches.

Traditional diagnostic tools, primarily reliant on body mass index (BMI), frequently fail to capture these complex sex- and age-related variations [7, 8]. As a result, obesity in women, especially after menopause, may be underestimated, while associated metabolic and sarcopenic risks remain insufficiently addressed. Few studies have systematically analyzed how sex and age shape obesity-related clinical, phenotypic, and risk profiles, beyond cardiometabolic comorbidities. This limits the development of tailored interventions. We hypothesized that sex defines distinct obesity phenotypes and that age, especially in women, further modulates these patterns. To explore this, we performed multidimensional phenotyping across four predefined sex- and age-stratified groups within a large cohort referred for specialized obesity care.

Methods

Design and Participants

This observational study analyzed a cohort of 1,014 (690 women) adults living with obesity (PwO) referred from primary care to one of four specialized Obesity Management and Comorbidities Centers within the Quirónsalud Hospital Network in Madrid, Spain (online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552187). All participants were managed through the standardized, digitally supported OB-DigiThum clinical pathway, approved by the local Research Ethics Committee (CEIC), which enabled structured pre-visit data integration and systematic phenotyping in a real-world clinical setting [9]. All data collected through the OB-DigitHum platform were anonymized and stored securely, complying with European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Spanish data protection laws (LOPDGDD 3/2018). Data collection systematically followed the OBEDIS (OBEsity Data Integration Standardization) recommendations for obesity phenotyping [10]. All participants provided written informed consent. Obesity was classified according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria based on BMI.

Inclusion criteria were age >18 years and BMI >30 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included a current indication for bariatric surgery, insufficient digital literacy to actively participate in a clinical pathway such as OB-DigitHum, and any other condition that, in the researchers’ judgment, could significantly interfere with study assessments.

Clinical Pathway and Data Collection

The clinical pathway integrated phenotypic assessment, comorbidity screening, and personalized therapeutic planning [11]. During the initial diagnostic phase, PwO actively engaged in their evaluation by completing standardized forms and validated questionnaires through the Quirónsalud Patient Portal, a digital platform designed to ensure privacy and minimize stigma. Participants also completed a questionnaire on self-reported history of obesity, previously developed and applied by our group in the PROBesci strategy [12], which included age at onset, triggering and maintaining factors, weight fluctuation patterns, previous dietary interventions, and other behavioral and environmental aspects. The validated questionnaires completed by PwO prior to their first clinical visit were (online suppl. Table S2) Goldberg Health Questionnaire (GHQ-28) [13]; Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI) [14]; 14-Item Mediterranean Diet Assessment Tool [15]; Rapid Assessment of Physical Activity (RAPA) [16]; Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [17]. All patient-reported data were automatically integrated into the Casiopea electronic medical record system, ensuring a comprehensive and standardized dataset for each participant.

Anthropometric and Body Composition Assessment

Anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, waist circumference, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) [18], were obtained by trained personnel following standardized protocols. Body composition was assessed using a multifrequency bioimpedance analyzer (InBody 270, Microcaya, Spain), which provided estimates of BMI, fat mass (FM), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), fat-free mass (FFM), and basal metabolic rate (BMR). BMR was calculated using the validated formula: BMR (kcal/day) = 370 + (21.6 × FFM [kg]).

Given the heterogeneity in body size among individuals with obesity, absolute values of FM and SMM were normalized to body surface area (BSA) to allow for more meaningful interindividual comparisons. Accordingly, we applied specific indices proposed by our group to reflect relative fat and muscle burden: fat mass index, FM-to-BSA ratio (FM/BSA), and SMM-to-BSA ratio (SMM/BSA) [19] (online suppl. Table S3).

Biochemical and Metabolic Assessment

Fasting blood samples were analyzed centrally for biochemical and metabolic variables, and comorbidity risk scores were also calculated (online suppl. Table S4).

Assessment of Comorbidities

The comprehensive dataset integrated into Casiopea enabled the identification of associated risks and comorbidities during the first medical visit, in accordance with established clinical guidelines and phenotyping recommendations (online suppl. Table S5) [19]. All data were validated through cross-verification with confirmed diagnoses from other specialists, imaging reports, and/or the prescription of specific pharmacological agents.

Statistical Analysis

Participants were stratified a priori by sex and into two age-groups: <50 years and ≥50 years. This threshold was selected according to the average age of menopause in Spain [20], a key factor influencing body composition and metabolic health in women. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%). Differences between sexes and age-groups were assessed using the Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For comparisons involving more than two groups, ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used, with Tukey or Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner tests, respectively, for pairwise comparisons. Correlation analyses (Pearson or Spearman as appropriate) assessed associations between key variables.

To characterize the distinct phenotypic profiles of the four predefined sex and age-groups, Orthogonal Partial Least Squared-Discriminant Analysis (orthoPLS-DA) was applied to standardized continuous variables. The contribution of each variable to group discrimination was assessed using variable importance in projection (VIP) scores, derived from the orthoPLS-DA model. Subsequently, a heatmap was generated to visualize the influential variables for each of the four groups as standardized (Z-scored) group-level mean values. Hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance was applied to both variables and the group-level profiles (as mean Z scores) to reveal phenotypic patterns and inter-group similarities. This visualization and clustering of group-level profiles was used to summarize multidimensional differences between the four sex- and age-stratified profiles in a large cohort, complementing BMI-based classification.

Missing data were addressed through multiple imputations, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of findings. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [21].

Results

Body Composition according to Sex and Age

Table 1 summarizes body composition results by sex and age; overall population data are presented in online supplementary Table S6. The cohort included 1,014 adults with obesity, predominantly women (68.0%). Median (interquartile range) age was 50 (41–59) years, and median BMI was 37.0 (33.5–41.1) kg/m2, with most participants classified as having moderate level of obesity (35.0–39.9 kg/m2). Body fat percentage was consistently elevated (46.9 [41.3–50.9]%), as were waist circumference (113 [103–124] cm) and waist-to-height ratio (0.7 [0.6–0.7]), indicating marked adiposity and central fat accumulation.

Men exhibited significantly higher body weight, BMI, SMM, and waist circumference, consistent with greater lean mass and visceral adiposity. In contrast, women showed a significantly higher total body fat percentage and FM indexed to BSA (FM/BSA), reflecting greater relative adiposity. Conversely, SMM indexed to BSA (SMM/BSA) was markedly lower in women, underscoring a true muscle deficit not attributable to body size alone.

Post hoc comparisons by age-group are shown in online supplementary Table S7. Progressive deterioration in body composition was observed with aging in both sexes. In men, aging was associated with declines in weight, BMI, SMM, and SMM/BSA. Women exhibited a paradoxical decline in BMI with age, likely reflecting progressive muscle mass loss that masked fat gain, in parallel with a marked reduction in SMM/BSA and stable FM levels, alongside increasing waist circumference and WHtR, findings that collectively evidence a high risk of sarcopenic obesity in aging women, characterized by muscle depletion and central fat redistribution.

Weight History and Obesity Determinants

Table 2 shows weight history and circumstances that may have influenced weight gain. While the age of obesity onset did not differ by sex, it occurred significantly earlier in younger generations, reflecting increased exposure to obesogenic environments. Maximum weight was also reached at an earlier age in younger individuals. Despite generational shifts in timing, obesity triggers remained sex specific and consistent across age-groups. Women reported greater lifetime weight fluctuations and were more likely to seek medical attention during periods of weight gain. Men typically reached their maximum weight earlier and tended to seek care once weight plateaued. At 20 years, men were significantly heavier than women. At the time of evaluation, a higher proportion of women reported ongoing weight gain, while intentional weight loss was more frequently reported by men.

Men identified discontinuation of physical activity and smoking cessation as primary obesity contributors. In women, obesity is most often precipitated or aggravated during periods of hormonal change – such as pregnancy, lactation, or menopause – and in phases of emotional distress.

Table 3 summarizes the results of the self-reported questionnaires. The GHQ-28 questionnaire showed that, although psychological discomfort was common in the cohort, scores were significantly higher in women. A greater proportion of these met the clinical cutoff, especially at younger ages. Over half of the cohort scored in the pathological range in EDI-3, thus reflecting frequent disordered eating behaviors, bulimic features, or body image distortion. Prevalence was particularly high in individuals under 50, where differences between sexes were not observed, unlike the pattern in the older age-group. On the other hand, according to RAPA questionnaires, the cohort exhibited an extremely high level of sedentary behavior, with less than 20% of participants meeting the minimum recommended levels of physical activity, with no sex differences. Conversely, more than 90% of participants demonstrated moderate to high adherence to the Mediterranean diet, suggesting overall favorable nutritional behaviors. While adherence did not differ significantly by sex, it was somewhat greater among participants aged over 50, particularly women.

Occurrence and Risk of Comorbidities

Comorbidity occurrence, comorbidity-related biomarkers, risk factors, and risk indexes are summarized in Table 4. Cardiometabolic comorbidities were highly prevalent and exhibited marked sex- and age-related patterns. In women, the prevalence of several comorbidities (including hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2DM]) rose sharply after the age of 50, reflecting the profound impact of hormonal changes and aging. In particular, hypertension prevalence nearly tripled among postmenopausal women, while metabolic syndrome rates doubled compared to younger women. Notably, T2DM prevalence in women increased tenfold after the age of 50, although it remained lower in absolute terms than in men over 50, where the prevalence approximately doubled with age. Dyslipidemia in women also more than doubled after age 50, reaching similar levels to men, with a prevalence close to 50% in both sexes.

Similarly, while men had higher rates of hepatic steatosis and liver fibrosis risk, as indicated by the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) and the non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) scores, women also experienced a notable rise in hepatic steatosis prevalence after menopause, though the progression to advanced liver disease remained less pronounced than in men. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was more common in men, but its prevalence in women increased significantly with age Osteoarthritis showed a clear female predominance, especially among older women, while its prevalence remained stable in men regardless of age. Other biochemical and clinical parameters also reflected sex and age differences. Fasting glucose and insulin resistance mirrored these trends, with men exhibiting steeper age-related elevations.

The large sample size and the breadth of phenotypic variables assessed enabled a robust multidimensional characterization of obesity across four predefined sex- and age-stratified groups. Discussion

Sex and age are well-established determinants of obesity phenotypes [22, 23], yet comprehensive clinical portraits integrating body composition, metabolic risk, and psychosocial factors remain limited. Our study, based on a large, clinically diverse cohort referred for specialized obesity care, provides new insights by highlighting distinct sex- and age-related patterns in obesity presentation, progression, and associated risks.

Women in our cohort presented with specific physiological and metabolic challenges, particularly during the menopausal transition – a period in which women are more likely than men to seek medical treatment for obesity [22, 24]. The decline in estrogen was associated with a redistribution of fat toward central depots, accelerated muscle loss, and a deterioration in metabolic health, including increased prevalence of hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes (T2DM) after age 50 [25, 26]. These changes coincided with greater functional limitations and an increased risk of sarcopenic obesity, which may go undetected when relying solely on BMI [27, 28].

Although it is well known that women have less SMM and more FM than men, our use of body composition indices normalized by BSA (FM/BSA and SMM/BSA) enabled direct quantitative comparison between sexes, overcoming differences in body size. To our knowledge, this is the first time these indices have been applied to characterize obesity phenotypes in a large clinical cohort. While formal diagnosis of sarcopenic obesity was not possible due to the absence of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, the data clearly demonstrate a marked reduction in relative muscle mass among women, particularly postmenopausally. This deficit, alongside a higher prevalence of osteoarthritis, suggests early decline in functional capacity, likely underrecognized in routine clinical care.

Importantly, hormonal changes alone do not fully account for the observed disparities. Psychological distress was more prevalent in women, who also reported greater use of medications associated with weight gain, such as anxiolytics, antidepressants, and corticosteroids. These findings align with epidemiological data showing women are nearly twice as likely as men to develop mood disorders [29–31], as echoed in WHO reports [32]. Emotional vulnerability may contribute to poorer weight control and higher treatment complexity in this subgroup.

Comorbidity profiles further diverged by sex and age. Although younger women had lower T2DM prevalence, FINDRISC scores increased sharply after menopause, linked to rising fasting glucose and blood pressure levels. While T2DM remained less frequent in older women than in men, CVD – particularly stroke – is the leading cause of death in postmenopausal women in Spain, a fact often underappreciated in management of persons with obesity. These data emphasize the need for routine hypertension screening and refined cardiovascular risk stratification in women with obesity, especially beyond age 50.

NAFLD prevalence increased with age in women, but remained lower than in men, consistent with prior data [33, 34]. Age-related frailty in women was further evidenced by the increased occurrence of osteoarthritis and lower prevalence of sleep apnea compared to men [35, 36]. Osteoarthritis, in particular, may compound sarcopenic features, contributing to physical limitations and loss of independence in later life.

Men, in contrast, tended to develop obesity earlier (median onset ∼20 years), reported greater long-term weight stability and were more likely to seek care after reaching a weight plateau, suggesting a more reactive engagement with obesity treatment. At baseline, more men were in an active weight loss phase, whereas women more frequently continued to gain weight. In younger men, behavioral triggers such as physical inactivity and smoking cessation emerged as key drivers of obesity [37, 38].

Disordered eating behaviors also revealed unexpected patterns. Bulimic behaviors, as measured by EDI, were highly prevalent among younger men, with more than 60% scoring in the pathological range, substantially higher than previously reported in general obesity populations [39, 40]. These behaviors are often overlooked in clinical settings, underlining the importance of incorporating systematic screening tools into obesity care across all sexes and age-groups.

Despite a relatively preserved adherence to a Mediterranean dietary pattern – particularly among participants aged ≥50 years – physical inactivity was highly prevalent across all sex- and age-stratified profiles. This discordance suggests that dietary habits alone may be insufficient to counterbalance low energy expenditure and adverse body-composition trajectories in a clinically referred obesity population. From a mechanistic standpoint, sustained sedentarism may contribute to the observed reduction in relative SMM with aging, especially in women, thereby amplifying the risk of sarcopenic obesity even when BMI remains stable or declines [41]. Clinically, these findings support embedding structured physical-activity assessment and exercise prescription within phenotype-driven pathways, with a particular emphasis on progressive resistance training and functional capacity, complemented by tailored aerobic activity for cardiometabolic benefit [42]. The digital nature of the OB-DigiThum pathway provides an opportunity to monitor lifestyle behaviors longitudinally and to identify barriers – such as psychological distress or disordered eating traits – that may undermine adherence to activity programs.

Finally, age-related changes reflected the broader impact of modern obesogenic environments [43]. Younger individuals showed earlier obesity onset, often with fewer immediate complications. However, early-onset obesity is associated with higher lifetime cardiometabolic risk, reinforcing the importance of preventive interventions tailored to life stage.

Our study has limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference; however, ongoing longitudinal follow-up is expected to strengthen these findings. Body composition was assessed via multifrequency bioimpedance, which may be less precise than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or MRI. Socioeconomic data were not collected, limiting analysis of social determinants. Nevertheless, the cohort was geographically and demographically diverse. Self-reported data may be prone to recall or desirability bias. Finally, our group-level heatmap/clustering approach was exploratory and hypothesis generating; external validation and longitudinal follow-up are needed.

Clinical Implications

This study reinforces the clinical utility of comprehensive, multidimensional phenotyping – such as the OB-DigiThum model – for identifying sex- and age-specific patterns in body composition, obesity history, behavioral drivers, and comorbidities. Such integrative frameworks are essential for improving diagnostic precision, refining risk stratification, and informing individualized treatment pathways.

Our multidimensional phenotyping highlights clinically meaningful obesity profiles that differ by sex, age, body composition, and comorbidity burden, providing support for the growing shift toward phenotype-driven management of obesity. Importantly, the application of FM/BSA and SMM/BSA indices offers a valuable tool for early identification of relative muscle depletion and excess fat, especially in women, and may be particularly useful when gold-standard techniques are unavailable.

Given that CVD is the leading cause of death among postmenopausal women, with stroke as the most frequent vascular event, integrating routine blood pressure monitoring and vascular risk stratification into obesity care is imperative. Standard reliance on BMI alone may obscure elevated cardiovascular risk in this subgroup.

Women, particularly after menopause, experience greater psycho-emotional distress – including higher rates of anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem – together with unfavorable anthropometric and functional changes. These findings highlight the priority of integrating personalized psychological support and structured resistance training from the outset of their obesity management.

In men, especially younger individuals, obesity trajectories were closely linked to behavioral factors such as inactivity and smoking cessation, suggesting the need for proactive screening and early management of cardiometabolic complications such as OSA, NAFLD/ metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and T2DM. Lastly, bulimic-type eating behaviors were frequent across the cohort, but markedly more prevalent in men under 50, underscoring the need to systematically include eating disorder screening within obesity management programs.

Conclusions

Obesity phenotypes diverge by sex and life stage. Men carry a higher baseline cardiometabolic burden (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease/NAFLD and OSA), whereas women experience a sharp post-menopausal metabolic deterioration – hypertension nearly tripling, dyslipidemia more than doubling, and type 2 diabetes rising up to tenfold – despite lower absolute diabetes prevalence than men. Normalizing fat and muscle to BSA (FM/BSA; SMM/BSA) exposes sarcopenic obesity risk in aging women, often masked by stable or falling BMI, underscoring BMI’s limitations for risk appraisal.

These findings support phenotype-driven care: in women ≥50 years routine blood-pressure surveillance, vascular risk stratification, and resistance-training-centered programs; in men early screening for OSA, liver disease, and diabetes; and mental-health assessment across groups. Although cross-sectional, these data support embedding body-composition indices within digital, phenotype-driven pathways to enhance individualized management and potentially improve risk stratification and targeted follow-up.
