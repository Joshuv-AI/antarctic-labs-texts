On the principal eigenvectors of random Markov matrices
Jacob Calvert, Frank den Hollander, Dana Randall

Abstract
. We analyze the invariant distributions of continuous-time and discrete-time random walks on randomly weighted complete digraphs. These distributions correspond to the principal left eigenvectors of the associated random Markov generators and kernels, viewed as random matrices. While much is known about the spectra of these matrices, relatively little is known about the principal left eigenvectors, which are delicate random objects for which no explicit form is known. We consider a broad class of such matrices obtained by associating random weights to the vertices and edges of the complete digraph. Our main result concerns the total variation distance between the invariant distribution of the continuous-time random walk and the distribution that is inversely proportional to the vertex weights. It states that, if the edge weights are i.i.d. with a finite p p -th moment for some p > 4 p>4 , then this distance a.s. converges to zero as the number of vertices grows large, even when the vertex weights are heavy-tailed. We further answer a question of Bordenave, Caputo, and Chafaï by showing that, despite the dependence of the entries in the corresponding Markov kernel, its invariant distribution is asymptotically uniform a.s., so long as the edge weights have a finite second moment.

Introduction and main result

Background.
Diverse physical and computational processes motivate the study of random Markov matrices, including both the infinitesimal generators of continuous-time Markov chains and the stochastic kernels of discrete-time Markov chains. A natural model of a random Markov generator
Q
is

(1)

Q=A-D,

where

random matrix of non-negative weights that are i.i.d. (except possibly for the symmetry

), and

is the diagonal matrix of the row sums of

]
.

A closely related model is the random Markov kernel that results from normalizing the rows of

The Markov matrices in

Most results about random Markov matrices concern their spectra

By definition, the invariant distributions solve the matrix equations

When the matrix

(3)

\|\pi_{P}-u\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1)\quad\mathrm{a.s.},

where

In general, however, the principal left eigenvector is a “delicate random object with no explicit expression”

]

]

Goal.
The main purpose of this paper is to show that, although
\pi_{Q}
is generally a complicated function of the transition rates, for a broad class of random Markov generators it is closely approximated by a simple function of the exit rates

q_{i}=|Q_{i,i}|=\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}Q_{i,j}.

Regarding

, we extend

3

Main results.
Our first result states that
\pi_{Q}
is asymptotically inversely proportional to the exit rates and vertex weights whenever the edge weights have a finite
p
-th moment for some
p>4
. For
x\in(0,\infty)^{n}
, we denote by
\nu_{x}
the distribution that satisfies
\nu_{x}(i)\propto x_{i}^{-1}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\mathcal{L}
has a finite
p
-th moment for some
p>4
, then, for every
a<\min\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{4}{p}\}
,

\left\|\pi_{Q}-\nu\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=O(n^{-a})\quad\mathrm{a.s.},\quad\quad\nu\in\{\nu_{q},\,\nu_{\theta}\}.

Because
Theorem

1.2
places no restriction on the vertex weights, which can be deterministic (
Fig.

1
a) or random (
Fig.

1
b), the distributions
\pi_{Q}
,
\nu_{q}
, and
\nu_{\theta}
can all be far from uniform. For example, when all vertex and edge weights are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
random variables, these distributions grow closer in total variation distance—and farther from uniform—as
n
increases (
Fig.

1
c). Moreover, when the entries of
\theta
are i.i.d. with a heavy-tailed distribution,
Theorem

1.2
implies that
\pi_{Q}
localizes on “trap” states with extremely low exit rates or vertex weights
[
BAv06
]
. For comparison, in the reversible case when
\theta\equiv 1
and the edges are undirected,
\pi_{P}(i)
is known to be exactly proportional to
\sum_{j}X_{i,j}
, in which case i.i.d. heavy-tailed edge weights instead produce traps along the edges of highest weight
[
BCC11
, Theorem 1.8]
.

The intuition behind
Theorem

1.2
is that, although the generator
Q
is random, the transition probabilities
\widehat{Q}_{i,j}=Q_{i,j}/q_{i}
with which the embedded discrete-time Markov chain jumps to neighboring states are comparable. As a consequence, the latter Markov chain rapidly converges to a stationary distribution
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}
that is close to uniform. Since
\pi_{Q}(i)
is proportional to
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)/q_{i}
,
\pi_{Q}
is essentially determined by the reciprocal exit rates. The exit rates
q_{i}
, in turn, are approximately
\theta_{i}n\,\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1})
, due to the concentration of sums of edge weights.

Remark 1.3
.

In
Theorem

1.2
, we opt for a simple moment assumption on
\mathcal{L}
, but it is possible to prove qualitatively similar results under a technically sharper tail condition of the form

\sum_{k\geq 1}n_{k}^{2}\,\bar{F}\big(\sqrt{n_{k}}/g(n_{k})\big)<\infty,

where
\bar{F}(x)=\mathcal{L}([x,\infty))
is the complementary cumulative distribution function,

is a function satisfying
g(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{\log n}\,)
, and

is an increasing sequence. For example, taking
n_{k}=2^{k}
and

results in a condition that is stronger than
\mathcal{L}
having a finite fourth moment, but weaker than the finiteness of a
p
-th moment for some
p>4
. In this case, the total variation distance in
Theorem

1.2
is
O((\log n)^{-1})
instead.

The key input to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
is an

estimate of the

distance of

to the uniform distribution

, which uses the finiteness of a

. However, our second result states that it suffices for the edge weights to have a finite second moment to guarantee the asymptotic uniformity of
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}

sense. The same is true of
\pi_{P}
.

.

If

2.
Estimates of transition probabilities

We will repeatedly use a maximal version of the strong law of large numbers due to Bai and Yin
[
BY93
, Lemma 2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(X_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}
be an array of i.i.d. random variables. For any real numbers
\alpha\in(1/2,1]
,
\beta\geq 0
, and
C>0
, if
\mathbf{E}(|X_{1,1}|^{(1+\beta)/\alpha})<\infty
, then

\max_{i\in[Cn^{\beta}]}\left|\sum_{j\in[n]}(X_{i,j}-\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1}))\right|=o(n^{\alpha})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

For the proof of
Theorem

1.4
, we will also need to control the minimum among
n^{2}
sums of elements from
X
. Under the assumption of finite second moment alone,
Lemma

2.1
is not enough. However, because the entries of
X
are non-negative, the lower tail of their sums is exponential.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(Y_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d. non-negative random variables with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}\in(0,\infty)
. For every integer
n\geq 1
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the sum
S_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}Y_{i}
satisfies

(5)

\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\leq(1-\epsilon)\mu n)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\mu^{2}n}{2(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})}\right).

Proof.

For every
t>0
, the application of Markov’s inequality to
e^{-tS_{n}}\geq 0
implies that

(6)

\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\leq(1-\epsilon)\mu n)=\mathbf{P}\left(e^{-tS_{n}}\geq e^{-t(1-\epsilon)\mu n}\right)\leq e^{t(1-\epsilon)\mu n}\,\mathbf{E}(e^{-tS_{n}}).

By the independence of the
Y_{i}
and the basic inequalities
e^{-x}\leq 1-x+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}
and
1+y\leq e^{y}
, which hold for all
x\geq 0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
, the expectation satisfies

\mathbf{E}(e^{-tS_{n}})=\big(\mathbf{E}(e^{-tY_{1}})\big)^{n}\leq\big(1-t\mu+\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}m_{2}\big)^{n}\leq\exp\left(-(t\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}m_{2})n\right)

with
m_{2}=\mathbf{E}(Y_{1}^{2})=\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2}
. Substitution into
Eq.

6
yields the bound

\mathbf{P}(S_{n}\leq(1-\epsilon)\mu n)\leq\exp\left(-(t\epsilon\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}m_{2})n\right),

which is minimized at
t=\epsilon\mu/m_{2}
, and gives
Eq.

5
.
∎

Lemma

2.1
implies the following estimate of sums of squared entries of the kernels
\widehat{Q}
and
P
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K
denote either
\widehat{Q}
or
P
. If
\mathcal{L}
has finite fourth moment, then

\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}K_{i,j}^{\,2}=O(n^{-1})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

Denote the
i
-th off-diagonal row sum of
X
by
R_{i}=\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}X_{i,j}
. By
Lemma

2.1
, because
X_{1,1}
has finite second moment, these row sums satisfy

\min_{i\in[n]}R_{i}=(1-o(1))\,\mu n\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

with
\mu=\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1})
. We further apply
Lemma

2.1
to the array
(Y_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}
of squared entries
Y_{i,j}=X_{i,j}^{2}
. Because
\mathbf{E}(Y_{1,1}^{2})=\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1}^{4})<\infty
,
Lemma

2.1
implies that

\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}Y_{i,j}=(1+o(1))\,\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1}^{2})\,n\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

On these two a.s. events, we have that

\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}K_{i,j}^{\,2}\leq\frac{\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}Y_{i,j}}{(\min_{i\in[n]}R_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{(1+o(1))\,\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1}^{2})\,n}{((1-o(1))\mu n)^{2}}=O(n^{-1}).

Note that the first inequality holds because the numerator of the upper bound includes diagonal entries of
X
that
\widehat{Q}
excludes, while the row sum
R_{i}
in the denominator excludes the diagonal entries of
X
that
K=P
includes.
∎

We use the finiteness of a
p
-th moment for some
p>4
to control the largest entries of the kernels.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
K
denote
\widehat{Q}
or
P
. If
\mathcal{L}
has finite
p
-th moment for some
p>4
, then, for every
a<1-\frac{2}{p}
,

\max_{i,j\in[n]}K_{i,j}=O(n^{-a})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.1
, since
X_{1,1}
has finite second moment, the largest row sums of
\widehat{Q}
and
P
are both
\Omega(n)
a.s. On this event, it suffices to show that

W_{n}=\max_{i,j\in[n]}X_{i,j}=O(n^{1-a})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

In fact, since
W_{n}
is increasing in
n
, it is enough for this event to hold along the dyadic subsequence
n_{k}=2^{k}
. Let
\delta=1-\frac{2}{p}-a>0
. A union bound over the
n_{k}^{2}
possible values of
i
and
j
and the application of Markov’s inequality to
X_{1,1}^{p}
together imply that

\mathbf{P}(W_{n_{k}}>n_{k}^{1-a})=O\big(n_{k}^{2}n_{k}^{-p(1-a)}\big)=O\big(n_{k}^{-p\delta}\big)=O\big(2^{-p\delta k}\big).

Because this bound is summable over
k\geq 1
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies that
W_{n_{k}}=O(n_{k}^{1-a})
a.s.
∎

3.
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

We next address the key input to the proof of
Theorem

1.2
: an
o(n^{-1})
estimate of the
\ell^{\infty}
distance between
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}
and the uniform distribution
u
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\mathcal{L}
has finite
p
-th moment for some
p>4
, then, for every
a<\min\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{4}{p}\}
,

(7)

\|\pi_{\widehat{Q}}-u\|_{\infty}=O(n^{-(1+a)})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

The idea of the proof is to use Bernstein’s inequality to obtain exponential concentration of the two-step transition kernel
(\widehat{Q}^{2})_{i,k}
given the
i
-th row of
\widehat{Q}
. The exponential rate of concentration is determined by a balance of the
\ell^{2}
and
\ell^{\infty}
norms of the
i
-th row of
\widehat{Q}^{2}
, for which
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.4
provide a.s. estimates.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Fix
p>4
and
a<\min\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{4}{p}\}
. According to
Remark

1.1
,
\widehat{Q}
is irreducible and aperiodic, and hence
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}=\pi_{\widehat{Q}^{2}}
for all sufficiently large
n
a.s. It therefore suffices to show that

(8)

\max_{i,k\in[n]}\left|(\widehat{Q}^{2})_{i,k}-\frac{1}{n}\right|=O\big(n^{-(1+a)}\big)\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Indeed, because
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}
is stationary, on the event in
Eq.

8
, it satisfies

\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(j)(\widehat{Q}^{2})_{j,i}=\frac{1}{n}+O(n^{-(1+a)}),

which implies
Eq.

7
.

To prove
Eq.

8
, we begin by noting that, conditionally on the
i
-th row
\widehat{Q}_{i,\cdot}
of
\widehat{Q}
, the random variables
Y_{j}=\widehat{Q}_{i,j}(\widehat{Q}_{j,k}-\frac{1}{n-1})
indexed by
j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}
are independent, centered, and satisfy

(9)

S_{n}(i,k)=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}Y_{j}=(\widehat{Q}^{2})_{i,k}-\frac{1-\widehat{Q}_{i,k}}{n-1}.

Bernstein’s inequality states that there is a universal constant
C>0
such that, for every
t>0
,

(10)

\mathbf{P}\left(|S_{n}(i,k)|>t\mid\widehat{Q}_{i,\cdot}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-C\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{v_{n}},\frac{t}{b_{n}}\right\}\right),

with

v_{n}=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}\mathbf{E}(Y_{j}^{2}\mid\widehat{Q}_{i,\cdot}),\qquad b_{n}=\max_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}|Y_{j}|.

Regarding
v_{n}
,
Lemma

2.1
implies that, because
\mathcal{L}
has finite second moment, the off-diagonal row sums
R_{i}=\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}X_{i,j}
satisfy

\min_{i\in[n]}R_{i}=(1-o(1))\,\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1})n\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

On this event, as well as the a.s. event of
Lemma

2.3
, the fact that
X_{j,k}
is independent of
\widehat{Q}_{i,\cdot}
and has finite second moment implies that

v_{n}=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}\widehat{Q}^{2}_{i,j}\,\mathbf{E}(\widehat{Q}^{2}_{j,k}\mid\widehat{Q}_{i,\cdot})=O(n^{-2})\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}\widehat{Q}^{2}_{i,j}=O(n^{-3}).

Furthermore, on the a.s. event of
Lemma

2.4
, for every
s<2(1-\frac{2}{p})
,

b_{n}\leq\max_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}\widehat{Q}_{i,j}\Big(\widehat{Q}_{j,k}+\frac{1}{n-1}\Big)=O(n^{-s}).

In particular, we choose
s>1+a
.

When
t_{n}=\Theta(n^{-(1+a)})
the rate in
Eq.

10
satisfies

\min\left\{\frac{t_{n}^{2}}{v_{n}},\frac{t_{n}}{b_{n}}\right\}=\Omega\left(\min\left\{n^{1-2a},n^{s-1-a}\right\}\right)=\Omega(n^{\delta})\quad\mathrm{a.s.},

where
\delta=\min\{1-2a,s-1-a\}>0
. A union bound over the
n^{2}
possible values of
i
and
k
, together with
Eq.

10
, imply that

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{i,k\in[n]}|S_{n}(i,k)|>t_{n}\right)\leq 2n^{2}\exp\big(-\Omega(n^{\delta})\big)\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Because this upper bound is summable, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies that

\max_{i,k\in[n]}|S_{n}(i,k)|=O(n^{-(1+a)})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

To relate this to
Eq.

8
, we combine the preceding fact with
Eq.

9
. The triangle inequality and
Lemma

2.4
imply that, for every
c<2(1-\frac{1}{p})
,

\max_{i,k\in[n]}\left|(\widehat{Q}^{2})_{i,k}-\frac{1}{n}\right|\leq\max_{i,k\in[n]}\left(\left|S_{n}(i,k)\right|+\left|\frac{1-\widehat{Q}_{i,k}}{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}\right|\right)=O(n^{-(1+a)})+O(n^{-c})\quad\mathrm{a.s.},

In particular, we can take
c=\frac{3}{2}>1+a
because
p>4
, in which case
O(n^{-c})=o(n^{-(1+a)})
. This proves
Eq.

8
, and completes the proof.
∎

Theorem

1.2
follows readily from the fact that
\pi_{Q}(i)\propto\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)/q_{i}
and
Lemma

3.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Put
\delta_{i}=n\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)-1
. Since
\pi_{Q}(i)
is proportional to
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)/q_{i}
, it satisfies

\pi_{Q}(i)=\frac{\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)/q_{i}}{\sum_{k\in[n]}\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(k)/q_{k}}=\frac{(1+\delta_{i})/q_{i}}{\sum_{k\in[n]}(1+\delta_{k})/q_{k}}.

In terms of
\delta=\max_{i\in[n]}|\delta_{i}|
, this implies that

\left(\frac{1-\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\nu_{q}(i)\leq\pi_{Q}(i)\leq\left(\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}\right)\nu_{q}(i).

Since
\delta=n\|\pi_{\widehat{Q}}-u\|_{\infty}
,
Lemma

3.1
implies that, for every
a<\min\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{4}{p}\}
,
\delta=O(n^{-a})
a.s. On this event, the preceding bound implies that

\|\pi_{Q}-\nu_{q}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\max\left\{\frac{\delta}{1+\delta},\frac{\delta}{|1-\delta|}\right\}=O(n^{-a}).

The fact that we obtain the same estimate with
\nu_{\theta}
in place of
\nu_{q}
is a consequence of the triangle inequality and the following lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\mathcal{L}
has a finite
p
-th moment for some
p>4
, then, for every
a<\min\{\tfrac{1}{2},1-\tfrac{3}{p}\}
,

\|\nu_{q}-\nu_{\theta}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=O(n^{-a})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Proof.

Let
\mu=\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1})
. Because the
Y_{i,j}=X_{i,j}-\mu
are independent mean-zero random variables with finite
p
-th absolute moment for
p>2
, the Fuk–Nagaev inequality (see, e.g.,
[
Rio17
, Eq. 1.8]
) states that there are constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
depending on
\mathcal{L}
such that, for any
z>1
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\Big|\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}Y_{i,j}\Big|\geq C_{1}\sqrt{n\log z}+C_{2}(nz)^{1/p}\right)\leq 4/z.

Take
z=(n\log n)^{2}
. Combining a union bound over
i\in[n]
with the preceding inequality, yields, for all sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbf{P}\left(\max_{i\in[n]}\,\Big|\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}Y_{i,j}\Big|\geq C_{1}^{\prime}\sqrt{n\log n}+C_{2}n^{3/p}(\log n)^{2/p}\right)\leq\frac{4}{n(\log n)^{2}}

with
C_{1}^{\prime}
a further constant. Because this upper bound is summable, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies that

\max_{i\in[n]}\Big|\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}X_{i,j}-(n-1)\mu\Big|=\max_{i\in[n]}\,\Big|\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}Y_{i,j}\Big|=O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}+n^{3/p}(\log n)^{2/p}\right)\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

In particular, for every
a<\min\{\tfrac{1}{2},1-\tfrac{3}{p}\}
,

\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{n\mu}\Big|\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}X_{i,j}-n\mu\Big|=O(n^{-a})\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Recall that
q_{i}=\theta_{i}\sum_{j:\,j\neq i}X_{i,j}
. Hence, on the preceding event,
\nu_{q}
satisfies

\nu_{q}(i)=\frac{1/q_{i}}{\sum_{k\in[n]}1/q_{k}}=\frac{1/(\theta_{i}n\mu)}{\sum_{k\in[n]}1/(\theta_{k}n\mu)}\left(1+O(n^{-a})\right)=\nu_{\theta}(i)\left(1+O(n^{-a})\right),

which implies that

\|\nu_{q}-\nu_{\theta}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[n]}\nu_{\theta}(i)\,O(n^{-a})=O(n^{-a}).

∎

4.
Proof of
Theorem

1.4

As in the proof of
Lemma

3.1
, we establish
Theorem

1.4
by considering the two-step transition matrices. However, under the assumption of finite second moment alone, we cannot obtain a two-sided estimate like
Eq.

8
for their entries. Instead, we merely bound from below their smallest entries by using the exponential concentration of the lower tail of sums of
X
(
Lemma

2.2
). This is enough to bound the
\ell^{1}
norms in
Eq.

4
, because their row sums are normalized.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Let
K
denote either
P
or
\widehat{Q}
. Following
Remark

1.1
,
\pi_{K}=\pi_{K^{2}}
for all sufficiently large
n
a.s. It therefore suffices to show that

(11)

\min_{i,k\in[n]}(K^{2})_{i,k}\geq\frac{1-o(1)}{n}\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Indeed, because
\pi_{M}
is stationary,
Eq.

11
implies that

\pi_{K}(i)=\sum_{j\in[n]}\pi_{K}(j)(K^{2})_{i,j}\geq\frac{1-o(1)}{n}\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

On this event, defining
\mathcal{A}=\{i\in[n]:\pi_{K}(i)\leq 1/n\}
, we have that

\left\|\pi_{K}-u\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{n}-\pi_{K}(\mathcal{A})+\pi_{K}(\mathcal{A}^{c})-\frac{|\mathcal{A}^{c}|}{n}\right)=\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{n}-\pi_{K}(\mathcal{A})=o\left(\frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{n}\right)=o(1).

To prove
Eq.

11
, note that

(12)

(K^{2})_{i,k}\geq\frac{S_{n}(i,k)}{(\max_{i\in[n]}R_{i})^{2}},

where
R_{i}=\sum_{j\in[n]}X_{i,j}
and
S_{n}(i,k)=\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,k\}}X_{i,j}X_{j,k}
. By
Lemma

2.1
, since
X_{1,1}
has finite second moment, the row sums satisfy

(13)

\max_{i\in[n]}R_{i}=(1+o(1))\,\mu n\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

with
\mu=\mathbf{E}(X_{1,1})
. Additionally, because the summands of
S_{n}(i,k)
are i.i.d. random variables with finite variance,
Lemma

2.2
implies that there is a constant
c>0
such that, if
\epsilon_{n}=c((\log n)/n)^{1/2}
, then

\mathbf{P}(S_{n}(i,k)\geq(1-\epsilon_{n})\,\mu^{2}n)\leq n^{-4}.

As the union bound over the
n^{2}
possible values of
i
and
k
is summable, the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies that

(14)

\min_{i,k\in[n]}S_{n}(i,k)\geq(1-o(1))\,\mu^{2}n\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

By
Eq.

12
, on the combined events of
Eqs.

13
and
14
,
K^{2}
satisfies

\min_{i,k\in[n]}(K^{2})_{i,k}\geq\frac{(1-o(1))\,\mu^{2}n}{((1+o(1))\,\mu n)^{2}}=\frac{1-o(1)}{n},

which establishes
Eq.

11
, and completes the proof of
Eq.

4
for
\pi_{P}
and
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}
.

Regarding
\pi_{Q}
, when
\theta\equiv c\in(0,\infty)
, the row sums of
Q
are equal to those of
X
. Because
\mathcal{L}
has finite second moment,
Lemma

2.1
implies that the largest
\delta_{i}=\mu n/q_{i}-1
satisfies

\max_{i\in[n]}|\delta_{i}|=o(1)\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

Because
\pi_{Q}(i)
is proportional to
\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)/q_{i}
, on the preceding event, we have that

\pi_{Q}=\frac{\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i)\,(1+\delta_{i})}{\sum_{k\in[n]}\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(k)\,(1+\delta_{k})}=(1+o(1))\,\pi_{\widehat{Q}}(i).

Since
\|\pi_{\widehat{Q}}-u\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=o(1)
a.s., we find that

\|\pi_{Q}-u\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\|\pi_{\widehat{Q}}-u\|_{\mathrm{TV}}+o(1)=o(1)\quad\mathrm{a.s.}

∎
