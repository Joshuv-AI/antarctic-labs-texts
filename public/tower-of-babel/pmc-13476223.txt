DentalEdu-AI graph: mapping evidence-grounded and knowledge-structured AI for dental education, assessment, and accuracy
Authors: Pradeep Kumar Yadalam, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-2022 increase of large language models (LLMs) in dental education has generated a expansive heterogeneous literature spanning MCQ benchmarking, OSCE support, curriculum mapping. Despite significant increase, there is no evidence map showing where knowledge is robust or where methodological investment is needed. The function of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and knowledge graphs (KGs) in minimizing hallucinations is unexplored. The DentalEdu-AI Graph was created from 667 records: PubMed (549), Scopus (20), and web sources (98). After screening, 447 studies were chosen. Multi-label extraction identified AI type, dental specialty, assessment format, study design, and four quality flags was identified. A 37-node/49-edge knowledge graph of the dental AI ecosystem was constructed and validated with TF-IDF RAG-lite retrieval across 10 curriculum queries. Publication output risen considerably from 2 in 2022 to 203 in 2025 (+ 10,050%). ChatGPT/GPT-3.5 tops with 77.6%, followed by Gemini/Bard at 42.1% and Claude at 16.3%. Accuracy accounts for 80.5% of studies, but hallucination is only focused in 7.8%, revealing a major critical gap. RAG appears in 2.9%, and KG in 0.4%, despite being key hallucination-suppression strategies. The RAG-lite demo accomplished 80% top-1 accuracy, 90% top-3 accuracy, and Cohen’s κ = 0.640. Dentistry LLM education literature is obsessed with precision and lacks infrastructure. Quality-first system design using RAG pipelines and KG limitations must replace accuracy-focused benchmarks. A KG (37 nodes, 49 edges, 21 typed relations) and TF-IDF demo are publically available in DentalEdu-AI Graph, the first open evidence map and infrastructure blueprint.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44445-026-00230-3.

Introduction

November 2022 marks a fault line in dental education research. Before ChatGPT, querying conversational AI for dental knowledge was speculative; after, the literature exploded—measuring, benchmarking, praising, and cautioning—at a pace that was overwhelming to synthesize. By 2025, more than 200 dental AI studies will be published each year (Ali et al. 2024, Kirkwood et al. 2025). The field is now extensive, fast-growing, and urgently needs mapping.

Three technological paradigms now compete and converge in this space. Vanilla LLMs—queried directly via chat interfaces—offer transformative linguistic capabilities but carry an underappreciated clinical risk: hallucination —the confident generation of plausible but incorrect information (Elnagar et al. 2024). An LLM who invents contraindications, misquotes doses, or fabricates recommendations in dental instruction may harm patients through clinical decisions. RAG separates factual correctness from model memory by basing responses on authoritative passages (Herdiantoputri et al. 2024, Refaee et al. 2022). Knowledge graphs (KG) grow expert knowledge as typed entity-relation structures, limiting generation through integrity checks(Ali et al. 2024, Nguyen et al. 2025).

Despite three years of publication, no comprehensive quantitative evidence map exists; reviews are niche, model-specific, or outcome-focused (Hassanein et al. 2026, Fujimoto et al. 2024, Chatzopoulos et al. 2025). Corpus-level questions like which AI kinds, specialties, and applications prevail and where systematic gaps exist remain unsolved. Resource allocation, research priorities, and financing decisions are difficult without this map(Tussie and Starosta 2024).

We present DentalEdu-AI Graph: a CPU-only, fully reproducible, open-evidence map synthesizing 447 primary records across all major dental specialties (Rajabi and Etminani 2024, Soman et al. 2024), AI types, assessment formats, and quality-concern dimensions. We present the first validated RAG-lite dental curriculum retrieval pipeline, demonstrating practical infrastructure at low computational cost; a 37-node/49-edge didactic knowledge graph of the dental AI ecosystem, serving as a structural analysis substrate and a deployable open-source ontology; and a multi-dimensional methodological framework, the first in dentistry. First, the survey includes 447 dental specializations in 12 fields. The RAG/KG infrastructure gap in dental AI education is quantified for the first time.

Materials and methods

Research question framing (PCC framework)

Population: dental students, trainees, educators, and practitioners engaging with AI-generated educational content. Concept: LLMs (ChatGPT, GPT-4, Gemini/Bard, Claude, LLaMA, and equivalents), generative AI, RAG, and KGs applied to dental knowledge tasks. Context: Curriculum mapping, examination creation, MCQ/SAQ/OSCE support, clinical reasoning instruction, answer accuracy benchmarking, and hallucination characterisation in dentistry education and assessment.

Search strategy and source coverage

Three databases were searched simultaneously using code (May 2026). PubMed via E-utilities of NCBI (esearch + efetch; sort=relevance) by using a structured Boolean search with combination of dental domain specific terms (dental[Title/Abstract], dentistry[Title/Abstract], “dental education“[Title/Abstract], “dental student“[Title/Abstract], “Education, Dental“[MeSH]) AND LLM/AI specific terms (“large language model”, ChatGPT, “GPT-4”, “GPT-3”, Gemini, Bard, Claude, “generative AI”, “retrieval-augmented”, “knowledge graph”) resulted in 549 documents. Scopus was searched by using web search limited to scopus.com and produced 20 results; Sc Scholarly web search using category="scholarly” across six topic-specific queries (LLM × dental education MCQ, LLM × dental assessment OSCE, GPT-4 × dental board accuracy hallucination, RAG × dental curriculum, KG × dental education, generative AI × dentistry clinical reasoning) returned 120 raw hits, deduplicated to 98 unique records. Combined identification pool: 667 records (Fig. 1).

Eligibility screening and PRISMA compliance

Eligibility was regulated by a rule-based pipeline investigating concatenated title and abstract text. Inclusion required concurrent satisfaction of three criteria: (i) dental domain signal (dental, dent, odont, tooth, oral health, or named specialty); (ii) LLM/AI signal (ChatGPT, GPT-3/4, Gemini, Bard, Claude, LLaMA, LLM, large language model, generative AI, RAG, retrieval-augmented, knowledge graph); (iii) education/assessment signal (educat, student, curricul, exam, assess, MCQ, OSCE, board, licens, teach, train, question generation, SAQ). Records matching radiology/diagnostic-imaging patterns (radiograph, panoramic, CBCT, cephalo) without an education/assessment cue were excluded as diagnostic-only. Two off-topic 1992/1993 PubMed records (PMID:8406949, PMID:1286928) were removed post-hoc. Final corpus: n = 447 (220 excluded: 218 rule-based, 2 manual). Screening reliability was assessed using a stratified 100-record spot check (50 INCLUDED/50 EXCLUDED) against an independent regex heuristic, yielding a Cohen’s κ of 0.640 (substantial agreement).

Data extraction

For each record selected, the following were extracted using rules from titles and abstracts: (i) type of study (eight types: comparative/benchmark study, empirical study, cross-sectional/survey study, RCT, review – narrative/systematic/scoping, commentary/editorial, case report/series); (ii) type of AI (multi-label, nine types); (iii) specialty or domain of dental education (multi-label, 12 specialties); (iv) type of assessment (multi-label, seven types); (v) four quality criteria (accuracy, hallucination, retrieval/RAG, knowledge-graph); (vi) main use-case; (vii) key finding, limitation, and future direction stubs. The full extraction Table (447 × 18) is provided in Supplementary File S2.

Descriptive analysis

All analyses were deterministic and CPU-only. Frequencies were computed per axis (year, AI type, dental area, assessment type, focus flag, study type, application) and as cross-tabulations (AI × dental area; AI × application; year × top-5 AI types). Unigram and bigram term frequencies were extracted from all 447 included abstracts after English stop-word removal. No inferential statistics were applied; this is a frequency map, not a hypothesis test. Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 were generated via matplotlib/seaborn; all code is provided in Supplementary File S10.

RAG-lite retrieval demonstration and validation

To benchmark a practical, zero-cost retrieval pipeline, a TF-IDF index (scikit-learn; ngram_range=(1,2); English stop words; vocabulary size n = 1,312) was built over 18 hand-authored dental curriculum chunks spanning all major specialties (Supplementary File S7). Each query received cosine similarity-ranked chunks, and a deterministic template generated MCQ, OSCE, and concept-explanation outputs without an LLM call to avoid unverified clinical content. Retrieval was validated by defining a gold-standard top-1 chunk for each of 10 held-out queries (Supplementary Table S7) and scoring top-1 and top-3 retrieval accuracy alongside cosine similarity distributions.

Knowledge graph construction and validation

The DentalEdu-AI Graph KG was hand-authored, encoding 37 nodes across eight typed categories (Discipline, Concept, AI, Assessment, Quality, Skill, Process, Resource) and 49 directed edges across 21 typed relation labels. Structural validation confirmed: (i) single weakly connected component (no orphan nodes); (ii) right-skewed degree distribution with identifiable hubs; (iii) all 447 study IDs in the extraction table map to screening table rows. The full edge file is in Supplementary File S8.

Results

The chronological pattern of 447 records (n = 425 with a year; 22 NaN included) reveals an unprecedented speed of development. In 2022 when ChatGPT was introduced into the market, only 2 publications related to dental LLM education literature were found. This number grew up to 17 in 2023 and 70 in 2024. A year later, in 2025, 203 papers have been released and accounted for 45.4% of all publications accumulated during 4 years of research. Early 2026 brought in 133 additional publications. The Compound Annual Growth Rate for 2023–2025 was 246%, which proves the rapidity of development of this topic among other subfields in health professions education. There were 168 comparative benchmarks, 124 empirical researches, 76 cross-sectional surveys, and 9 RCTs in 2025.

AI type, dental specialty, and assessment format: the shape of evidence (Fig. 3)

ChatGPT/GPT-3.5 was the first publicly available, widely accessible LLM capable of dentistry knowledge tasks, with 347 mentions (39.4% of all mentions; 77.6% of studies) across AI type (multi-label; 881 total AI-type mentions across 447 research). Gemini/Bard followed at 188 (21.3%), Other LLM at 123 (14.0%), GPT-4 at 76 (8.6%), and Claude at 73 (8.3%), a statistic that had developed from near-zero pre-2024 to a large evidence basis by 2026 Only 13 trials (2.9%) had RAG and 1 (0.1%) had knowledge graphs, which is problematic given the hallucinatory dangers in the parallel accuracy literature.

Across dental specialties (multi-label), General dental education led with 164 studies, followed by Prosthodontics/Implants (66), Endodontics (56), Oral surgery (48), Pediatric dentistry (48), Periodontics (44), Curriculum general (33), Orthodontics (26), Oral medicine/pathology (22), and Cariology/Restorative (21). The concentration in high-board-exam-density specialties (Prosthodontics, Endodontics, Oral Surgery) is explicable by the availability of structured MCQ banks; the relative scarcity in Orthodontics (26), Oral Medicine (22), Cariology (21), Dental Hygiene (6), and Dental Public Health (3) represents a systematic blind spot for AI-augmented dental education.

Across assessment format (multi-label), 290 studies (64.9%) were not assessment-specific—reflecting the dominance of generic accuracy benchmarking over targeted assessment tool development. MCQ generation/evaluation was the leading specific format (105 studies; 23.5%), confirming the path-of-least-resistance effect: MCQ evaluation is binary, automatable, and publishable. OSCE/case-based (17; 3.8%), SAQ (9; 2.0%), and question generation activities (4; 0.9%) are underrepresented despite their importance in dental competency assessment.

The accuracy trap: quality focus flags and study design distribution (Fig. 4)

With 80.5% of dental LLM education studies focusing on accuracy, just 7.8% address hallucinations, providing a 10:1 ratio that could mislead students. Minimal coverage of retrieval (2.9%) and knowledge graphs (0.4%). This focus gap reverses the optimal therapeutic approach of KG grounding and RAG retrieval guiding and limiting LLM outputs, with hallucination monitoring and accuracy evaluation as quality controls.

Comparative/benchmark designs dominate (168; 37.6%), followed by empirical research (124; 27.7%) and cross-sectional surveys (76; 17.0%) in the late benchmark phase (Fig. 4b). The top 9 RCTs (2.0%) indicate that the field is beginning to investigate not only ‘how accurate is LLM X?’ but also ‘what effect does LLM X have on learning outcomes?’ for educational policy.

Validation

RAG-lite retrieval performance: What minimal infrastructure achieves

The TF-IDF retrieval was tested on 10 queries covering the 18-chunk corpus (Supplementary Table S7). Top-1 accuracy was 80% (44%–97% CI), top-3 was 90%. Mean top-1 cosine similarity was 0.239, with a 0.044 top-2 similarity, creating a 0.195 cosine gap. The system reliably identifies correct chunks for confidence thresholding. Two failures showed expanding the corpus is essential: for ‘Class II caries restoration,’ the top match was orthodontics_basics instead of caries_management, which lacked restoration terms. Because biomechanics was missing from orthodontics, oral_surgery_extractions was the top hit for ‘Orthodontic biomechanics,’ while the right chunk was third. Improving this requires growing the corpus, proving completeness trumps algorithmic complexity at modest scales. (Table 1).

Screening reliability: interpreting κ = 0.640

The screening reliability spot-check (100-record stratified random sample; 50 INCLUDED/50 EXCLUDED; Supplementary Table S9) yielded INCLUDED agreement of 48/50 (96%), EXCLUDED agreement of 34/50 (68%), and Cohen’s κ = 0.640—classified as ‘substantial agreement’ on the Landis and Koch scale (Kirkwood et al. 2025). The asymmetry between INCLUDED (96%) and EXCLUDED (68%) agreement shows the rule set’s calibration: focused on precision, it sacrifices some recall. Of 16 EXCLUDED-to-INCLUDED disagreements, ~ 10 were real edge cases, and ~ 6 were diagnostic-only. This was deliberate: false inclusions that add non-educational records distort more than false exclusions missing edge cases. True recall likely exceeds the reported by 5–10%, so 22–45 more studies might qualify with fewer criteria.

Knowledge graph structural validation

Three formal checks confirmed KG integrity: (i) Connectivity: all 37 nodes form one weakly connected component, covering every dental discipline, AI system, assessment, and quality concept, validating completeness. (ii) Degree distribution: a right-skewed hub-and-spoke topology with Dental Education (degree = 14) and LLM (degree = 11), indicating a purposeful ontology. (iii) Relation vocabulary includes 21 labels over 49 edges, density 0.43, with semantic precision, using terms like includes, studies, performs, assesses. Data is accurate; all 447 study IDs in Supplementary File S2 match the screening table, and evidence-map labels (1,041 cells, mean 2.33 per study) are consistent with independent labels.

Discussion

The accuracy trap: why 80.5% is the field’s most dangerous number

The finding that 80.5% of dental LLM education studies focus primarily on accuracy—while only 7.8% address hallucination—is the single most consequential result in this evidence map(Geduk et al. 2026, Tricco et al. 2018, Aura-Tormos et al. 2026). This isn’t a methodological preference but a failure of risk framing. The assumption that a 75% score on a dental exam means 75% safe is false. A model with high accuracy may confidently give erroneous answers on key questions like drug contraindications, emergency protocols, and dose, which students need AI aid with and are less likely to verify (Fuchs et al. 2023, Doubleday et al. 2026).

The infrastructure deficit: a 2.9%/0.4% evidence base for the field’s most powerful tools

Quantifying RAG at 2.9% and KG at 0.4% highlights the gap between evidence and clinical importance. Limited adoption of RAG and KGs likely stems from technical and governance barriers, such as lack of interdisciplinary retrieval and graph-engineering expertise, absence of standardized dental ontologies, and scarce open dental corpora. These challenges demand resources for corpus curation, expert validation, and maintenance. Although not directly studied here, addressing these issues should focus on educator–engineer collaboration, shared ontology standards, open benchmark corpora, and low-resource pipelines. Both techniques have strong evidence from biomedical AI for hallucination suppression: The RAG-lite demo counters the idea that RAG is complex or inaccessible. Using TF-IDF—an algorithm from 1972—on 18 corpus chunks, we achieved 80% top-1 and 90% top-3 accuracy with a vocabulary of 1,312 n-grams, running on a CPU in under 100 ms. If this old algorithm gets 80% accuracy on dental queries, modern dense retrieval methods like BM25 or ColBERT could likely exceed 95%. This offers an accessible, immediate improvement over current LLMs, which the field has largely overlooked (Claman and Sezgin 2024, Kim et al. 2025, Keles 2026).

Specialty coverage gaps: Who is left behind by the evidence gravity well

The AI × dental area heatmap reveals that the evidence gravity well centered on ChatGPT × General dental education (n = 125) has created a coverage topology in which some specialties are heavily mapped, and others are effectively invisible. Dental public health (3 studies), Dental hygiene, Oral medicine/pathology, and Cariology/Restorative are the most underrepresented relative to their clinical and educational importance(Keles 2026, Kavadella et al. 2024). These domains are characterized by reasoning processes that are complex, situational, and multifactorial, as opposed to straightforward factual memorization suitable for multiple-choice tests. This makes them relevant in the field of artificial intelligence, since it is harder to train a large language model to handle ambiguous situations and social factors than to recall procedures like working length in endodontics (Al-Khalifa et al. 2025, Kim and Kim 2025, Patil et al. 2026).

The data indicates that the severity of this issue will lead to a cycle whereby the best-known, most tested subjects will attract more resources in the form of AI tools, while the more complex, preventive, and public health specialties will receive none. This can be addressed through investing in the right way, through research grants, just like the NIH requires from clinical trial studies, except in AI education (Künzle and Paris 2024, Landis and Koch 1977).

The multi-model transition and its implications for quality standards

The shift from Phase 1 (ChatGPT-centric) to Phase 2 (multi-model), shown in Fig. 6, has significant quality implications. Phase 1 had low heterogeneity: most studies used ChatGPT 3.5 for dental MCQs and reported percent-correct scores. Phase 2 includes multiple models, such as Gemini 1.0/1.5/Advanced, Claude 2/3/Sonnet/Opus, GPT-4o, LLaMA 2/3, Mistral, Phi, Qwen, and fine-tuned local models.[18,27,28]. Comparing these needs standardized protocols—prompt formats, test sets, and hallucination rubrics—that the dental AI community has yet to develop. The DentalEdu-AI Graph provides the first corpus-level evidence base for creating such standards.[7,29].

A five-point methodological manifesto for the field

Based on the evidence map, we propose five priority standards for dental LLM education: (1) dual-mandate reporting with accuracy and hallucination evaluation; (2) benchmarking LLMs against a RAG baseline with public dental guidelines; (3) aligning entity definitions with SNOMED CT and reporting KG coverage for AI tools; (4) establishing funding for LLM-OSCE research; (5) incentivizing AI research in underrepresented dental fields to prevent coverage gaps.[7,30].

Since classification used only titles and abstracts, studies on RAG, knowledge graphs, or hallucination within the full text might have been overlooked. The reported proportions (2.9%, 0.4%, and 7.8%) should be seen as the prevalence of explicitly identifiable reporting, not definitive corpus-wide estimates.

The study has methodological limitations. Screening was rule-based with κ = 0.640; a full dual-reviewer process might identify 22–45 more studies. Web searches on limited Scopus returned no results, probably due to missing conference proceedings. Data extraction focused on titles and abstracts; full-text access could improve study type and application classification. The 37-node KG and 18-chunk curricular corpus are demonstrations of concept, not production tools. The RAG binomial CI (44%–97% for n = 10) is vast, proving the notion rather than estimating accuracy. Year data is missing for 22 of 447 records. In its scoping review, DentalEdu-AI Graph maps evidence but does not synthesize impact magnitude or pool accuracy and hallucination rates.

Conclusions

The DentalEdu-AI Graph provides the first reproducible evidence map of research on LLMs, RAG, and knowledge graphs in dental education, based on 447 studies. It highlights issues such as a focus on accuracy but poor infrastructure, with a 10:1 accuracy-to-hallucination ratio indicating methodological flaws. RAG and knowledge graphs are underused at 2.9% and 0.4%. Our RAG-lite demo shows minimal investment can improve quality, making large-scale RAG feasible. The field is moving from ChatGPT to multi-model approaches, necessitating standardized evaluation and advanced assessments to prevent errors. The RAG-lite framework demonstrates that a transparent, CPU-only retrieval system is feasible for low-resource settings, though initial testing of 10 samples was informal. The map, validated knowledge graph, RAG process, and reporting tools of the open DentalEdu-AI Graph enable robust, high-quality systems beyond accuracy.
