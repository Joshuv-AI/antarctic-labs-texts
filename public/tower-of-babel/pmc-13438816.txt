Sleep duration and efficiency predict heart rate variability during work hours in intensive care unit nurses
Authors: Aoi Kono, Natsuki Nakayama, Megumi Yokoyama, Kazuya Goto, Teruhiko Suzuki, Yoshimi Moriwaki, Momoka Kataoka, Koji Tamakoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of this study was to examine the associations between components of subjective sleep quality and parasympathetic nervous activity, as assessed by heart rate variability, in intensive care unit nurses during work hours. Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, which comprises seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medications, and daytime dysfunction. Heart rate variability was measured using a Holter monitor, with high-frequency power used as an index of parasympathetic activity. Age, energy expenditure, and step count were included as covariates. Associations between each Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index component and both raw and adjusted high-frequency power data were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons. Twenty-four full-time intensive care unit nurses participated. Significant differences in high-frequency adjusted for age and step count were found for sleep duration, with post hoc comparisons revealing significantly lower high-frequency in those with short sleep durations than in those with moderate sleep durations. Significant differences in both raw and all high-frequency residuals were observed for habitual sleep efficiency, with lower high-frequency power in participants with moderate sleep efficiency than in those with optimal efficiency. Poor sleep efficiency and short sleep duration were associated with reduced parasympathetic nervous activity during work hours among intensive care unit nurses.Key Words: heart rate variability, subjective sleep quality, nurse, intensive care unit

INTRODUCTION

Poor sleep quality is a prevalent concern among nurses and has been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, emotional dysregulation, and impaired cognitive function.1-3 In clinical environments, nurses with poor sleep quality also exhibit reduced work productivity, which contributes to compromised patient safety.4,5 Accordingly, poor sleep quality undermines both nurses’ health and care quality.

Irregular rotating shifts, often involving night shifts and extended work hours, disrupt circadian rhythms and physiological regulation, thereby diminishing sleep quality.6 Nurses working night shifts have reported significantly lower sleep quality than do those working only day shifts.7,8 In addition to shift-related disruptions, profession-specific demands also influence sleep. Nurses are frequently exposed to considerable emotional and cognitive demands, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), where they provide continuous care to critically ill patients.9 Collectively, these occupational characteristics place ICU nurses at increased risk for impaired sleep quality.

Sleep quality is frequently evaluated through subjective measures. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is one of the most widely used instruments and comprises seven components: sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, daytime dysfunction, use of sleep medication, and subjective sleep quality.10 Many previous studies have tended to use a composite PSQI score to represent sleep quality rather than examining the roles of individual components.

Heart rate variability (HRV) is a noninvasive and widely accepted physiological indicator of autonomic nervous system activity. High-frequency (HF) power is regarded as a reliable index of parasympathetic nervous activity and has been used in research.11,12 In healthy adults, significant correlations were observed between HF power during a stress test and both the sleep latency component and the global PSQI score.13 Compared with day shift nurses, night shift nurses consistently exhibited lower HF power and higher global PSQI scores.14 Previous findings suggest that subjective sleep quality, as measured by the PSQI, is most closely associated with parasympathetic activity during work hours, as assessed by the HRV.

However, the extent to which individual components of the PSQI are linked to physiological function, particularly parasympathetic nervous activity, in nurses during working hours remains poorly understood. Investigating the PSQI components individually may provide valuable insights into how specific aspects of sleep influence physiological function in demanding clinical settings. The purpose of this study was to elucidate the relationships between components of subjective sleep quality and HRV in ICU nurses during working hours.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

This study was conducted between November 2022 and December 2023 and included full-time nurses working in the ICU of a university hospital. Individuals with a history of arrhythmia or diabetes, which can affect HRV, were excluded.

Measures

Sleep quality. Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI over a one-month period.10 This self-report questionnaire consists of nineteen items, including bedtime, sleep duration, wake-up time, and other sleep-related items rated on a 4-point Likert scale. It includes seven components: C1 (subjective sleep quality), C2 (sleep latency), C3 (sleep duration), C4 (habitual sleep efficiency), C5 (sleep disturbances), C6 (use of sleeping medications), and C7 (daytime dysfunction). Each component is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, and the global score ranges from 0 to 21 points, with a higher score indicating poor sleep quality. In this study, participants rated overall sleep satisfaction based on their general experience, whereas items related to sleep duration and timing were answered with reference to periods without night shifts. Although this procedure differs from the original administration of the PSQI, it was adopted to obtain more accurate estimates of typical sleep duration that were not confounded by irregular shift schedules. The PSQI was used for two primary purposes: first, to examine the associations between the global score and its individual components; second, to investigate the associations between individual component scores and autonomic nervous system activity.

HRV. A Holter monitor (FM960; Fukuda Denshi Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) was used to obtain electrocardiographic waveforms throughout a scheduled long day shift. Each participant wore the device from before the start of work and continued throughout the waking period, and HRV was analyzed across the entire waking period including working hours. After excluding artifacts, normal RR intervals were extracted. A frequency analysis at 5-minute intervals was performed using frequency analysis software (MemCalc/Win; GMS Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) to separate the HF band (0.15–0.40 Hz). The HF is used as an index of parasympathetic nervous activity. To adjust for potential confounders such as age and physical activity, HF residuals derived from regression models including these variables were used for subsequent analyses.11,15

Physical activity. Physical activity was concurrently assessed to account for its potential confounding effects on HRV.15 A waist-worn triaxial accelerometer (HJA-705C; OMRON Corp, Kyoto, Japan) was used to collect data, with measurements beginning before the start of work and continuing throughout the waking period. Energy expenditure and step count were calculated using a proprietary algorithm on the basis of demographic data.

Data analysis

Quantitative variables are presented as medians and ranges (minimum to maximum), and categorical variables are presented as counts and proportions (no. [%]). Descriptive statistics were applied to demographic characteristics and questionnaire response variables.

The relationships among the global PSQI score and its seven component scores (C1–C7) were assessed by calculating the Spearman rank correlation coefficient.

Differences in HF according to PSQI component scores were examined using the following stepwise procedure. First, participants were grouped by response categories for each PSQI component. Second, unstandardized residual HF data were calculated using linear regression, with HF as the dependent variable; age, energy expenditure, step count, and the interaction terms between age and energy expenditure, and between age and step count were used as independent variables. Third, both raw and residual HF data were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post hoc comparisons using the Bonferroni correction were conducted for variables showing significant differences.

A P value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by Ethical Review Committee of Nagoya University Graduate School of Medicine (approval no. 2020-354) and conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants provided informed consent before enrollment in the study.

RESULTS

Characteristics of participants

The participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. Twenty-four nurses, 6 (25%) males and 18 (75%) females, participated in this study. The median age was 26.5 (23–42) years, and the BMI was 20.0 (17.1–24.0) kg/m2. Four (16.7%) participants reported their medical history, such as atopic dermatitis and gastrointestinal diseases. One participant (4.2%) was taking allergy medications as needed. Sixteen participants (66.7%) were unmarried, whereas 8 (33.3%) were married. The household size was 1 (0–5) person. Five (20.8%) participants did not drink alcohol, 13 (54.2%) reported drinking alcohol only when the opportunity arose, and 6 (25%) reported drinking alcohol three times weekly. Almost all the participants (23, 95.8%) in this study had no smoking habit, but 1 (4.2%) smoked. The number of years of nurse experience was 5 (1–20) years, and the number of years of ICU experience was 3.5 (1–10) years. The participants worked 6 (5–6) night shifts per month and participated in this study 3 (1–8) days after the last night shift.

No statistically significant differences were found for HR or any of its residuals across all PSQI components (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

A significant difference in HF power was observed for C3 (p = 0.046); however, post hoc pairwise comparisons did not reveal any statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). In contrast, a significant difference in HF power was found for C4 (p = 0.025), with a post hoc comparison identifying a significant difference between scores of 0 (190, 33–471) and 1 (52, 27–114) (p = 0.021). When the HF residuals adjusted for age were analyzed, significant differences were found for both C3 (p = 0.031) and C4 (p = 0.038). Post hoc analyses revealed a significant difference in C3 between scores of 1 (28.4, –147.5 to 304.1) and 2 (–81.5, –156.6 to –43.8) (p = 0.037). For C4, a significant difference was observed between scores of 0 (16.4, –110.5 to 304.1) and 1 (–115.3, –156.6 to –52.3) (p = 0.033). HF residuals adjusted for energy expenditure also significantly differed for C3 (p = 0.046), although no post hoc pairwise comparisons reached statistical significance (p > 0.05). In contrast, C4 demonstrated a significant group difference (p = 0.023), with a significant difference between scores of 0 (14.7, –115.0 to 284.0) and 1 (–103.9, –135.8 to –72.7) (p = 0.018). Similarly, an analysis of HF residuals adjusted for step count revealed a significant difference for C3 (p = 0.046); however, no significant differences were found in the post hoc tests. On the other hand, a significant difference was observed for C4 (p = 0.025), with a significant post hoc comparison between scores of 0 (16.8, –139.6 to 299.1) and 1 (–120.8, –144.8 to –57.7) (p = 0.021). When the HF residuals were adjusted for both age and energy expenditure, a significant group difference was observed in C1 (p = 0.046); however, no pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p > 0.05). A significant difference was again found in C4 (p = 0.024), with a post hoc analysis showing a significant difference between scores of 0 (15.5, –121.4 to 278.9) and 1 (–102.5, –131.0 to –79.5) (p = 0.019). Finally, the analysis of the HF residuals adjusted for both age and step count revealed significant group differences in C3 (p = 0.026) and C4 (p = 0.038). A significant difference was observed in C3 between scores of 1 (37.9, –152.9 to 284.1) and 2 (–65.8, –156.9 to –43.0) (p = 0.030). For C4, pairwise comparison identified a significant difference between scores of 0 (32.0, –107.2 to 284.1) and 1 (–112.9, –156.9 to –58.4) (p = 0.034). No other comparisons reached statistical significance.

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the relationships between various aspects of sleep quality and HRV during work hours among 24 ICU nurses. Significant associations were identified between specific PSQI components and HF, a reliable indicator of parasympathetic nervous system activity. The associations remained significant after controlling for confounding factors, including age and physical activity. The results underscore the relevance of specific aspects of sleep quality in shaping autonomic nervous system regulation among ICU nurses working in cognitively and emotionally demanding environments.

Associations between sleep efficiency and HF power

The most important finding of this study was the robust association between habitual sleep efficiency (C4) and HF observed during actual clinical work hours. Nurses with optimal sleep efficiency (scores of 0) consistently demonstrated significantly greater HF power than did those with moderate sleep efficiency (scores of 1). The relationship between sleep efficiency and HF power remained statistically significant after adjustment for age, energy expenditure, and step count. The results indicate that sleep efficiency was independently associated with parasympathetic nervous activity during clinical work, even after accounting for confounding factors. Additionally, the observed correlation between sleep efficiency and sleep latency (C2) suggests that prolonged sleep latency may partially account for reduced sleep efficiency. Several studies have demonstrated that reduced HF power is associated with elevated stress levels.16 ICU nurses are routinely exposed to high levels of cognitive and emotional stress in their work environments.17 It is plausible that occupational stress specific to ICU nurses may contribute to reduced HF power during work hours. The present findings suggest that occupational factors unique to ICU nurses may be associated with both reduced HF power during work hours and impaired sleep efficiency characterized by prolonged sleep latency. Furthermore, individuals with heightened vulnerability to stress exhibit prolonged sleep latency and impaired sleep efficiency.18 Moreover, days with elevated pre-sleep stress have been linked to reduced sleep efficiency and prolonged sleep latency compared with low-stress days.19 These previous studies support the role of physiological hyperarousal in sleep initiation. Although individual stress vulnerability was not directly assessed in this study, the observed reductions in HF and impaired sleep efficiency may reflect a physiological response to occupational stress.

Associations between sleep duration and HF power

Interestingly, sleep duration (C3) was not significantly correlated with any other PSQI component, suggesting that it may represent a distinct aspect of subjective sleep quality. This result further suggested that reductions in sleep duration did not necessarily co-occur with impairments in other components, such as sleep latency or disturbances. Among shift-working nurses, sleep duration may be constrained primarily by work schedules and lifestyle.20 Therefore, sleep duration in the study participants was more strongly influenced by external factors than intrinsic sleep characteristics.

Moreover, sleep duration was significantly associated with HF, especially after controlling for age and for age and step count. HF power was greater in nurses with moderate sleep duration (scores of 1; 6–7 hours) than in those with short sleep duration (scores of 2; <6 hours). However, no significant differences were observed between those with scores of 1 or 2 and those with the optimal sleep duration (scores of 0; >7 hours). According to the PSQI scoring criteria, a sleep duration exceeding 7 hours is considered optimal.10 Nevertheless, in the present study, HF power peaked among nurses reporting 6–7 hours of sleep, indicating a potential inverse U-shaped relationship between HF during work hours and sleep duration. Previous epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and health outcomes, with both short and long durations associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events.21,22 Reduced HF power is an established risk factor for cardiovascular disease.11,23 Thus, the present findings provide partial physiological insight into the associations between sleep duration and adverse health outcomes, particularly from the perspective of autonomic nervous system regulation.

Autonomic impairment associated with inadequate sleep

Poor sleep efficiency and short sleep duration were both associated with reduced HF in ICU nurses during their work hours. Adequate sleep is vital for maintaining critical restorative functions, including neural recovery, metabolic waste clearance, and the regulation of autonomic nervous activity.24,25 These physiological processes can be disrupted by poor sleep efficiency or short total sleep time, leading to sustained reductions in parasympathetic activity.26,27 Accordingly, the results of this study suggest that inadequate sleep, as characterized by poor sleep efficiency and short sleep duration, may impair autonomic recovery, potentially contributing to reduced parasympathetic nervous system activity in ICU nurses during work hours. In this context, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia offers a practical intervention, as it improves sleep efficiency by aligning sleep opportunity with actual sleep time, with a realistic wake-up time collaboratively determined with the participant.28 Brief and simplified cognitive behavioral therapy programs designed for healthcare workers have further been shown to improve insomnia symptoms and increase HF.29 These features indicate that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is a feasible intervention for shift-working nurses, as it can be adapted to different duty schedules while still supporting sleep efficiency and parasympathetic activity.

Dissociation between subjective daytime dysfunction and physiological impairment

Although HF power during work hours was lower among individuals with poor sleep efficiency and short sleep duration, daytime dysfunction (C7) was not significantly associated with HF across any of the analyses. Given that the vagus nerve mediates bidirectional communication between the brain and the heart, HF power is considered a physiological marker of work capacity, and its reduction may reflect diminished functional performance.30 Subjective alertness inadequately reflects behavioral performance deficits under sleep time restrictions.31 The results of this study may similarly reflect a dissociation, in which reduced HF power reflects physiological impairment even in the absence of self-reported daytime dysfunction. In high-pressure ICU environments, such a dissociation between perceived and actual performance may increase the risk of medical errors by compromising nurses’ ability to recognize declines in their own work capacity. Overall, this study highlights the potential for unnoticed performance deterioration in clinical settings, particularly in relation to reduced HF during work hours. From an occupational health perspective, strategic napping has been shown to improve cognitive and behavioral performance.32 Daytime naps taken more than seven hours before nocturnal sleep have been shown not to interfere with total sleep time and sleep latency compared with naps taken within seven hours of bedtime.33 Moreover, a study in older adults with cardiovascular risk factors demonstrated that regular nappers exhibited significantly higher parasympathetic activity across wakefulness and sleep compared with non-nappers.34 These findings suggest that strategic napping may enhance autonomic recovery as well as cognitive performance, providing a feasible and valuable occupational health strategy for ICU nurses.

The present study was conducted in a single ICU at one institution, and therefore two limitations related to the study population should be mentioned: the limited sample size and the sex distribution. First, the small number of participants in some PSQI component categories may have reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses. This reflects the inherent difficulty of recruiting ICU nurses for physiological monitoring studies. We adjusted HF values for relevant covariates to enhance robustness despite the limited sample size. Second, the proportion of male participants in this study exceeded that of the general nursing population in Japan, which is approximately 10%.35 Thus, caution is still warranted when generalizing to the entire nursing workforce. A further consideration is the difference in assessment periods: the PSQI reflects average sleep habits over the past month, while HRV was measured on a single day. Importantly, the PSQI responses included the day when HRV was recorded, ensuring partial overlap between the two measures. In addition, while we adjusted for physical activity to account for the quantity of workload exposure, we did not record specific in-shift work events or subjective stress (ie, workload quality), which could still have influenced parasympathetic activity.

Previous occupational studies have mainly examined sleep quality using the global PSQI score and have reported associations with outcomes such as cardiovascular risk and burnout.4,21 However, these approaches have not clarified which specific components of sleep contribute to physiological regulation during work. In the present study, poor sleep efficiency and short sleep duration were significantly associated with reduced HF power in ICU nurses during work hours, even after controlling for age and physical activity. These findings suggest that specific aspects of sleep quality may play critical roles in maintaining autonomic nervous system function during demanding clinical work. From an occupational health perspective, these results extend previous findings by demonstrating that impaired sleep quality compromises physiological recovery during ICU duties, underscoring the importance of workplace-level interventions to protect nurses’ health and performance.
