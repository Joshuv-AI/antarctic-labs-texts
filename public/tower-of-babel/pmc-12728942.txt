IUGR remodels glycogen synthesis in CG-IUGR rats
Authors: Bingshu Yuan, Binzhi Liu, Lamei Hou, Yaohui Wang, Lijuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigates glucose metabolism changes in CG-IUGR rats and analyzes the potential underlying mechanisms. It identifies a critical warning period for glucose metabolism changes in CG-IUGR individuals from birth to adulthood, providing key intervention targets for the prevention and treatment of diabetes.MethodsThe CG-IUGR rat model was established by a low-calorie diet during pregnancy. Rats were measured weekly after birth for body length, weight, and BMI. Rats were assayed at 8-week-old for the following indicators. Serum fasting insulin (FINS) and insulin levels at 15 min after glucose load were detected. Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were carried out. Skeletal muscle/liver glycogen content, glycogen synthase (GS) expression, and GS activity were assessed before and after glucose load.ResultsBMI and perirenal fat weight were significantly increased in CG-IUGR rats, showing catch-up growth. Serum FINS was decreased, whereas insulin at 15 min after glucose load was increased in CG-IUGR rats. GTT and ITT showed that CG-IUGR rats had significantly higher blood glucose levels at each time point after 15 min administration, suggesting their glucose intolerance and reduced insulin sensitivity. CG-IUGR rats manifested diminished skeletal muscle and liver glycogen content under basal conditions, accompanied by reduced GS expression and activity in these tissues. However, following a 15 min glucose challenge, a contrary trend was observed for all three parameters.ConclusionsCG-IUGR rats have an increased susceptibility to DM. IUGR remodels glycogen synthesis in CG-IUGR rats, as demonstrated by the preferential storage of energy as glycogen.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a prevalent clinical disease characterized by a disorder of glucose metabolism (1). The pathogenesis of diabetes and its progression are highly complex, involving interactions among multiple factors such as genetics, environment, and lifestyle (2). Clinically, DM is primarily classified into type Ⅰ diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type II diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with T2DM being predominant. Both T1DM and T2DM are characterized by hyperglycemia, the mechanism of which is mainly related to impaired glycogen synthesis. The onset of DM can be traced back to embryonic development (3). Many studies have confirmed that intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) individuals, particularly those with catch-up growth IUGR (CG-IUGR), face a markedly higher risk of developing DM (especially T2DM) (4). Our previous study found that CG-IUGR rats have potential glucose metabolism dysfunction (5). However, whether IUGR individuals, especially CG-IUGR individuals, have impaired glycogen synthesis and its onset and progression are unknown. Glycogenesis is the process of synthesizing glycogen from glucose, and its primary synthesizing tissues are the skeletal muscle and liver. Glycogen synthase (GS) is the rate-limiting enzyme for glycogen synthesis. Altered GS expression and activity are closely related to the development of DM. It has been found that GS activity is reduced in skeletal muscle of patients with T2DM, and an additional study has discovered decreased GS activity in the liver of T2DM rats (6, 7). We have observed changes in GS expression and activity in the skeletal muscle and liver of CG-IUGR rats. However, it remains unclear how glycogen synthesis function evolves during the development of DM in CG-IUGR rats, as well as how changes in GS expression and activity in the skeletal muscle and liver contribute to this process.

This study established a CG-IUGR rat model using a low-calorie diet, assessed its glucose metabolism and the expression and activity of GS in relevant tissues, investigated how IUGR alters glycogen synthesis function, and explored the potential role of these alterations in increasing the susceptibility of CG-IUGR rats to DM.

Materials and methods

Animal models and experimental design

All animal procedures were approved by the Zunyi Medical University Institutional Animal Experiment Ethics Committee (Approval number: ZMU11-2203-467). Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (body weight 180–200 g, animals were provided by Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd, China) were used as experimental animals. The experiment was divided into control group and CG-IUGR group. The CG-IUGR rat model was established by a low-calorie diet (5). The diet used in this research contained 66.0% carbohydrates, 11.1% lipids, and 22.9% protein. After confirming pregnancy in female rats via vaginal smear, they were randomly divided into a normal group and a malnourished group. Pregnant rats in the normal group were fed (diet: 30 g/d, 116.58 kcal) until the birth of their offspring, then the male offspring were fed routinely until 8 weeks of age (lactating female rat and post-weaning offspring) and were selected into the control group. Pregnant rats in the malnourished group were fed 40% of the normal feeding amount (diet: 12 g/d, 46.63 kcal) throughout pregnancy, and their male offspring were fed using the above method, in which the growth-catching offspring rats were selected into the CG-IUGR group. We successfully bred 16 normal rats and 16 CG-IUGR rats at 8-week-old.

Growth and development

Body length (from nose to tail base) and weight were measured weekly in control and CG-IUGR rats after birth. The BMI was calculated as follows: BMI = body weight (kg)/body length squared (m2). The rats were anesthetized after 12 h of fasting, and the perirenal fat of 8-week-old rats was separated and weighed.

Glucose metabolism

FINS and insulin levels at 15 min after glucose load were detected by radioimmunoassay (gamma radioimmunoassay counter, XH6080, Xi’an Instrument Factory). On the first and second days of the 8 weeks of age, all rats were subjected to a glucose tolerance test (GTT) and an insulin tolerance test (ITT). The blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg) and insulin (0.4 U/kg), and GTT and ITT curves were plotted (Johnson & Johnson MedTech, China).

Function of glycogen synthesis

After 12 h of fasting, eight rats were selected from each of the two groups by the random numerical table method, and their skeletal muscles and livers were extracted after intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g/kg) for 15 min. Similarly, skeletal muscles and livers were also extracted from the non-glucose-loaded group rats. After collection, the samples were stored in a −80°C ultra-low temperature freezer. Then, glycogen synthesis function was assayed in the basal state and after glucose loading, respectively.

Glycogen content in skeletal muscle and liver

0.1 g of skeletal muscle or liver tissue was placed in a 10 mL tube, followed by the addition of 0.75 mL of extraction buffer. The tubes were then boiled at 100°C for 20 min with a vigorous shake every 5 min to ensure adequate mixing. When the tissue was completely dissolved, the tubes were removed and cooled. The volume was then increased to 5 mL with distilled water, mixed well, and centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at 25°C, and the supernatant was collected. Following the glycogen assay kit (Solarbio, China) instructions, the absorbance was measured at 620 nm on a microplate reader, and the glycogen content was calculated.

Expression of GS in skeletal muscle and liver

The protein expression levels of glycogen synthase 1 (GYS1) in skeletal muscle and glycogen synthase 2 (GYS2) in liver were detected by Western blot (Bio-Rad, USA). Proteins were extracted from skeletal muscle and liver using lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay kit (Solarbio, China) and adjusted to be consistent. 20 μg of protein from each sample was separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were detected using super-sensitive electrochemiluminescence. ImageJ software was used to calculate the relative expression of target protein and normalize it to β-actin or GAPDH. The volume ratio of each antibody to BSA was as follows: 1:1,000 (anti-GYS1 rabbit monoclonal antibody, CST, USA), 1:500 (anti-GYS2 rabbit polyclonal antibody, Santa Cruz, USA), 1:5,000 (Recombinant Anti-beta Actin antibody, Servicebio, China), 1:5,000 (Recombinant anti-GAPDH antibody, Servicebio, China).

Activity of GS in skeletal muscle and liver

0.1 g of skeletal muscle or liver tissue was homogenized on ice in 1 mL of extraction solution, followed by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. According to the Glycogen Synthase Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio, China) instructions, the absorbance at 340 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the GS activity was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 19.0 (SPSS, Inc., USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x¯±s). The Student’s t test was used to compare the data of the two groups. A value of P < 0.05 indicated that the results were statistically significant.

Results

Growth and development of CG-IUGR rats

Compared with the control group, CG-IUGR rats showed a significant increase in BMI during growth and development (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A), and a similar increase in perirenal fat weight (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B), which suggests that CG-IUGR rats exhibit growth catch-up.

The changes of BMI and perirenal fat weight in CG-IUGR rats. (A) The changes of BMI in rats (n = 16). (B) The weight of perirenal fat in 8-week-old rats (n = 8). Note: *compared with the control group, P < 0.05.

IUGR attenuated glucose metabolism function in CG-IUGR rats

Compared with the control group, the serum FINS levels in CG-IUGR rats were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Insulin levels in CG-IUGR rats were significantly increased 15 min after glucose loading (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Blood glucose levels in these rats were significantly increased at each time point 15 min after glucose or insulin loading (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C and D). These results suggest that CG-IUGR rats have significantly attenuated glucose metabolism function and insulin resistance.

The changes of glucose metabolism status in CG-IUGR rats (n = 8). (A) The serum FINS levels in 8-week-old rats. (B) The serum insulin levels at 15 min after glucose load in 8-week-old rats. (C) The blood glucose levels of GTT in 8-week-old rats. (D) The blood glucose levels of ITT in 8-week-old rats. Note: *compared with the control group, P < 0.05.

IUGR remodeled glycogen synthesis in CG-IUGR rats

Glycogen content is reversed between the basal and glucose-loaded states

After a 12 h fast, glycogen content in the skeletal muscle and liver of CG-IUGR rats was significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). However, after 15 min of glucose loading, glycogen content in these tissues was significantly increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). Combined with glucose metabolism indices, these suggest that IUGR reduces glycogen content in the basal state but prioritizes glycogen storage after glucose challenge.

The changes of glycogen content in CG-IUGR rats (n = 8). (A) The glycogen content in skeletal muscle and liver under basal state. (B) The glycogen content in skeletal muscle and liver at 15 min after glucose load. Note: *compared with the control group, P < 0.05.

Expression of GS is reversed between the basal and glucose-loaded states

In CG-IUGR rats, the protein expression of skeletal muscle GYS1 and liver GYS2 was significantly downregulated under basal state (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A), but after 15 min of glucose loading, these expression levels showed opposite changes (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that IUGR altered the glycogen synthase expression pattern in CG-IUGR rats.

The changes of expression of GS in CG-IUGR rats (n = 8). (A) The relative protein expression of GYS1 in skeletal muscle and GYS2 in liver under basal state. (B) The relative protein expression of GYS1 in skeletal muscle and GYS2 in liver at 15 min after glucose load. Note: *compared with the control group, P < 0.05.

Activity of GS is reversed between the basal and glucose-loaded states

A significant reduction in GS activity was observed in the basal state of CG-IUGR rats (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A), whereas an opposite change emerged 15 min after glucose loading (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Based on the results of GTT and glycogen content, IUGR impairs glycogen synthesis and triggers a compensatory change following glucose intake, leading to increased GS activity.

The changes of activity of GS in CG-IUGR rats (n = 8). (A) The GS activity in skeletal muscle and liver under basal state. (B) The GS activity in skeletal muscle and liver at 15 min after glucose load. Note: *compared with the control group, P < 0.05.

Discussion

Our study found that IUGR resulted in catch-up growth and impaired glucose metabolism in rats, as exhibited by impaired glucose tolerance and insulin tolerance. IUGR is an abnormally sized fetus that has not reached its genetic growth potential in utero. It is usually defined as a birth weight below two standard deviations from the mean weight of fetuses of the same gestational age and gender, or below the 10th percentile for normal weight at the same gestational age (8). IUGR individuals are exposed to a nutrient-deficient environment in utero, exhibiting a thrifty phenotype. When exposed to a nutrient-rich environment after birth, they often experience catch-up growth, which increases the risk of developing T2DM in adulthood (9). Long et al. established an IUGR rat model through maternal dietary restriction and observed catch-up growth at 21 days postnatally (10). Wang et al. created a mouse model of IUGR by exposing pregnant mice to hypoxia, and their offspring showed catch-up growth at 2 weeks of age (11). Our findings also suggest that IUGR rats exhibited a significant catch-up growth. Numerous studies have shown that IUGR is associated with an increased susceptibility to DM, especially T2DM. An epidemiological study in Ukraine revealed that individuals born during periods of famine exhibit a significantly higher incidence of T2DM in adulthood (12). According to a study by Martin et al., female mice fed a low-protein diet during gestation had offspring with impaired glucose tolerance and an increased risk of T2DM in adulthood (13). Long et al. found that 8-week-old male CG-IUGR rats had glucose intolerance and reduced insulin sensitivity (10). Our results similarly show that CG-IUGR rats exhibit impaired glucose metabolism in the basal state, characterized by reduced glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. The above content indicates that CG-IUGR rats exhibit attenuated glucose metabolism in the basal state, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

In the basal state, we found that CG-IUGR rats exhibited reduced glycogen content, accompanied by decreased glycogen synthase expression and activity in the skeletal muscle and liver. T2DM is a clinically prevalent metabolic disorder characterized primarily by hyperglycemia. The maintenance of blood glucose levels is closely linked to the process of glycogen synthesis in organs, especially the skeletal muscle and liver. GS is a pivotal enzyme in glycogen synthesis, and its expression and activity directly affect glycogen synthesis. In the pathological progression of T2DM, significant alterations occur in both the expression and activity of GS. These molecular abnormalities are closely associated with impaired glycogen synthesis and the disruption of glucose metabolic homeostasis. Oyenihi et al. established a T2DM rat model using a high-fructose diet and intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ), finding a significant decrease in skeletal muscle glycogen content and GS activity (14). Yu et al. also observed that in a T2DM rat model established through a high-fat diet and intraperitoneal injection of STZ, liver glycogen content was markedly reduced (15). Chen et al. established a T2DM mouse model using the same method and observed that liver glycogen content was decreased, and liver GYS2 protein expression was also reduced (16). Gothandam et al. similarly noted that liver glycogen content was decreased alongside a reduction in GS activity in T2DM rats (17). Furthermore, Xirouchaki et al. discovered that skeletal muscle-specific GYS1 knockout mice exhibited impaired glucose tolerance and reduced skeletal muscle glycogen content (18). The above content suggests that IUGR disrupts glycogen synthesis in the basal state of CG-IUGR rats, manifested by decreased glycogen content and reduced GS expression and activity.

It is noteworthy that the glycogen synthesis function following glucose loading is entirely opposite to the changes observed under basal state. 15 min after glucose loading, blood glucose levels increased in CG-IUGR rats, while glycogen content significantly increased, and both the expression and activity of GS showed synchronized upregulation. Ailanen et al. analyzed a prediabetic phenotype mouse model with neuropeptide Y overexpression in noradrenergic neurons and found that the ratio of the labile to stabile glycogen fractions in hepatocytes was reduced, which suggested enhanced glycogen synthesis (19). Nkosi et al. also found that glycogen content in the skeletal muscle and liver of prediabetic rats was significantly increased (20). Di Sarli et al. used a high-fructose diet-induced model of prediabetic rats and found a significant increase in liver glycogen content (21). The above data suggest an increase in glycogen synthesis during the pathogenesis of pre-diabetes. In addition, Ali et al. further confirmed increased liver glycogen content and upregulated GYS1 expression in IUGR fetal sheep established by chorionic somatomammotropin deficiency (22). Based on the above literature combined with our findings, it is suggested that IUGR may enhance the function of glycogen synthesis in the skeletal muscle and liver following glucose loading in CG-IUGR rats. This change may be linked to the thrifty phenotype of CG-IUGR rats, where embryos exposed to poor nutritional conditions undergo adaptive alterations to improve survival, particularly in metabolic processes. This intrauterine thrifty phenotype may persist after birth and into adulthood. In response to nutritional supplementation, IUGR individuals preferentially store energy as glycogen, which can be rapidly mobilized as glucose to maintain blood glucose stability and meet potential energy demands (23). Therefore, following glucose loading, when rats are exposed to a nutrient-rich environment, they prioritize glycogen synthesis and energy storage. In addition, we found that blood glucose levels in CG-IUGR rats were higher than those in control rats at each time point following glucose loading, which appears to contradict the increased glycogen synthesis in the skeletal muscle and liver of these rats after glucose loading. Based on the above findings, this contradictory phenomenon is associated with the thrifty phenotype hypothesis, and we hypothesize that glycogen synthesis function may undergo a compensatory increase. In addition, a study has shown that the gluconeogenesis pathway is strongly activated in IUGR individuals (24). Therefore, the increased glycogen synthesis function in CG-IUGR rats following glucose loading, with elevated peripheral blood glucose levels, may also be related to mechanisms such as enhanced gluconeogenesis. However, the reasons for the increased glycogen synthesis function in CG-IUGR rats, the role of enhanced glycogen synthesis in the progression of DM in CG-IUGR rats, and why blood glucose levels remain elevated despite enhanced glycogen synthesis function following glucose loading still require confirmation. Moreover, considering the potential influence of estrogen fluctuations on metabolic regulation, only male rats were used in this study, which may limit the generalizability of our conclusions. Future studies will include female rats to provide a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic phenotypes.

In addition, our results also showed that fasting serum insulin levels were reduced in CG-IUGR rats. This may be associated with pancreatic dysplasia (25).

Conclusions

CG-IUGR rats have an increased susceptibility to DM. IUGR remodels glycogen synthesis in CG-IUGR rats, as demonstrated by the preferential storage of energy as glycogen.
