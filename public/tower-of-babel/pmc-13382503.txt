Current State of Play: Arteriovenous Fistulas

Arteriovenous fistulas (AVF) are widely used for hemodialysis and have lower infection rates and greater longevity than other modalities of hemodialysis access, however, complications with maturation and maintaining patency are common.1 Approximately 40% to 50% of fistulas fail to mature without intervention, and primary patency rates range from 34% without intervention to 83% with intervention.2 Dysfunctional vascular access impacts on patients quality of life, increases procedural burden and health care costs; approximately $3 billion is spent annually for vascular access creation and maintenance.1,3

Maintaining patency of the AVF dialysis circuit is a significant challenge and often requires multiple interventions, with a mean intervention rate of 0.37 procedures/patient year.4 The Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative suggests that < 3 interventions per year to maintain patency is a reasonable target goal.5 Interventions are recommended for clinical dysfunction, such as reduced flow and elevated venous pressures associated with > 50% stenosis.4 Pre-emptive stenting of lesions in the absence of clinical indicators was not beneficial.6 Older age, poor nutritional status, high dose erythropoietin, female sex, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, and vessel anatomy have been associated with AVF failure, stenosis and thrombosis.7 In this regard, the study by Tan et al.8 provides important insights into potential new therapies for treatment of AVF stenosis.

Neointimal Hyperplasia and AVF Stenosis

The mechanism of AVF stenosis formation is well described and highlights potential targets for intervention (Figure 1).6 After AVF creation the venous endothelium is exposed to arterial pressures, leading to a change in vascular smooth muscle cell phenotype. Cells adapt by reducing expression of contractile markers, extracellular matrix deposition resulting in outward venous remodelling.9 This process can become maladaptive if there is inward remodeling, known as neointimal hyperplasia (NIH) and contributes to over 60% of dysfunctional fistulas.1,9 Physical stressors such as repeated needle stick from cannulation, shear wall stress from anatomical variations, and trauma from angioplasty have been implicated in the development of NIH.4 The inflammatory milieu of kidney failure with uremia and oxidative stress leads to gene dysregulation where vascular smooth muscle cell alter into a fibrotic phenotype; with macrophage recruitment, endothelial dysfunction, and proinflammatory cytokines predisposing to a maladaptive response within the vessel wall and resultant NIH.4,6,S10) Histologically NIH is characterized by smooth muscle proliferation within the internal elastic lamina and extracellular matrix production within the intima, leading to poor compliance and reduced vessel diameter.1,3,S11Figure 1Upstream and downstream events leading to arteriovenous access stenosis. vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), extracellular matrix (ECM). Percutaneous Intervention

Percutaneous transluminal angiography (PTA) was first developed in the treatment of arteriovenous stenosis in 1982 and is still considered first line for management of dysfunctional AVFs.5,6 It involves expanding a stenotic vessel lumen through forceful dilatation to disrupt the neointimal and tunica media layer leading to outward remodelling.6 PTA is highly effective with over 90% intervention success rates at achieving the required < 30% residual stenosis for an adequate angiographic result.6 However, the durability of this intervention to maintain AVF primary circuit patency ranges from 41% to 76% at 6 months and 31% to 50% at 12 months post procedure.1,4 Venous stenoses form at sites of turbulence such as swing veins, anastomoses, cephalic arch, and frequent needling sites— they are the most common reason for fistula failure.3,6 Given the high recurrence rates at previous angioplasty sites1 further developments into high pressure balloons, cutting balloons, drug coated balloons, and medical optimization have been explored.

Drug Coated Balloons

Drug coated balloons (DCB) are designed to be complimentary to percutaneous transluminal angioplasty, and were first approved by the FDA for use in fistuloplasty in 2017.1 The initial PTA procedure dilates the stenoses. Subsequently the DCB is applied with varying medications, doses, lengths, durations, and excipients in order to provide a uniform local effect of drug to the vessel, without systemic side effects.S11 Paclitaxel is the most common DCB and interferes with microtubule formation, leading to cell cytotoxicity subsequently reducing rates of NIH and restenosis4; it has been studied extensively in coronary artery disease and peripheral vascular disease.S13 There are at least 7 different paclitaxel impregnated balloons available with varying lengths, excipients and doses between 2 mg/mm2 to 3.5 mg/mm2.S11 Recent meta-analyses comparing paclitaxel coated balloons (PCB) to PTA have not revealed a statistically significant difference in target lesion primary patency rates, however there are some signals for benefit to PCB in subgroup or time to event analysis (Table 1).

New interventions for coronary artery disease often lead to new treatments for AVF stenosis. The majority of drug eluting stents used in coronary stents are from the -limus family and have been found to have better target lesion revascularization rates than paclitaxel stents.S14 Sirolimus is an mTOR inhibitor and functions as an antiproliferative agent, preventing smooth muscle cell proliferation after vascular injury and reduces NIH post angioplasty; this has been shown in animal models and in clinical studies.4,S15

Recently, Tan et al.8 conducted the first multi-center randomized controlled trial comparing the effect of sirolimus coated balloons (n = 83) to placebo balloons (n = 87) on access circuit primary patency, defined as freedom from clinically driven reintervention or thrombosis of the AVF during the 6 months after the index procedure. At 6 months the access circuit primary patency was higher in the sirolimus coated balloons group compared with placebo with 70.1% patency versus 56.7% patency.S16 Although patients and managing physicians were blinded to the assigned intervention in this trial, proceduralists and trial coordinators were not blinded because of visible differences in the study balloons. This has important implications for the interpretation of the results because the assessment of access circuit primary patency is partially subjective. This is also a small trial, with limited capacity to identify differences in adverse events. Drug delivery of sirolimus to the vessel wall was not demonstrated in this study, which may be an important consideration for future trials. Nonetheless, this is an important trial and does open a new avenue to advance interventions to maintain AVF patency. Future trials may evaluate the longer-term outcomes beyond 6 months, cost effectiveness of sirolimus coated balloons, and direct comparison with other angioplasty technologies. In future trials assessment of drug delivery to the vessel wall will be necessary as sirolimus is less lipophilic with reduced tissue retention compared with paclitaxel and longevity of suppression of NIH beyond 6 months has not yet been determined.S15

Stenting and Medical Therapy

Stenting has had some success when treating stenoses of arteriovenous grafts and proximal stenoses within the cephalic arch, however there is limited randomized controlled trial data to suggest that stenting is superior to PTA within the native fistula.3 Retrospective studies (Table 1) have examined the role of antiplatelets and other medications that may improve fistula patency, albeit with higher risk of bias.S17

Future Directions and Other Treatment Approach

Ongoing investigation into the basic science and pathophysiology of NIH and AVF stenosis will be integral to identifying new translational targets for intervention. Metabolic responses and gene regulation are both strongly implicated in the dysfunction of AVF patency and transcriptomics have revealed significant gene regulation changes to inflammatory pathways, cellular adhesion, metabolic dysregulation, and coagulation cascade activation.9, S18 On the back of advances in discovery science and technology, other novel device therapies are in development.1, S19–S21

With new technological advances, it is important to delineate the best future therapies for maintaining AVF patency through clinical trials. Comparative effectiveness trials are required to examine and identify the optimal treatment, or combination of treatments, to maintain AVF patency for individual patients. Testing different vessel preconditioning, DCB dose, contact time and excipient used will likely reveal important insights into the best use of DCB technology. Embedding such trials in national clinical registries, S22 and using more efficient trial designs, such as coordinated multicenter adaptive platform trials, would be well suited to these future studies by reducing data collection costs, simplifying recruitment, and increasing availability of long term follow up. Future trials should also include cost-effectiveness analysis into trial design and standardize outcome measures that are important to healthcare providers and patients.
