Protein nanoparticles: a promising frontier in reducing lung complications from chemotherapy in pediatric oncology
Authors: Hadi Zare-Zardini, Fatemeh Dehghanpour, Elham Saberian, Adriána Petrášová, Andrej Jenča, Andrej Jenča, Janka Jenčová, Abdolhamid Jafari Nodoshan, Mohammad-Taghi Hedayati-Goudarzi, Hossein Soltaninejad, Adel Ghorani-Azam, Maryam Imani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Protein nanoparticles (NPs) offer an innovative solution for chemotherapy-induced lung complications in pediatric oncology. This narrative review examines how these biocompatible carriers mitigate pulmonary toxicity through targeted drug delivery and controlled release mechanisms. Different protein sources—albumin, silk fibroin, gelatin, casein, whey, and soya—offer different advantages, with albumin having superior biocompatibility and clinical precedent, while plant proteins offer cost-effective alternatives. Age-specific optimization is critical, with tailored nanoparticle sizes for neonates (50–100 nm), children (100–300 nm), and adolescents (200–500 nm), taking into account differences in lung development. Controlled release kinetics maintain therapeutic drug concentrations while avoiding toxic peaks, significantly reducing inflammatory markers in preclinical models. Despite the promising results, there are still challenges in regulatory approval, manufacturing scalability, and ensuring global availability. The regulatory pathway has been partly taken by albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane), while GRAS (generally recognized as safe) compliant production methods offer scalable solutions. Our strategic roadmap predicts that by 2030, advances in the development of formulations with artificial intelligence and personalized NPs therapies could reduce severe pulmonary complications by 50%, addressing a key research gap in age-specific, lung-protective drug delivery systems for pediatric oncology. Introduction

Cancer continues to be a major health problem around the world, with pediatric oncology patients facing a number of particular challenges. While chemotherapy is a cornerstone of pediatric oncology, offering hope and cure to young cancer patients, it is often accompanied by a spectrum of side effects [1]. Of particular concern are pulmonary complications, which can lead to significant morbidity and affect the overall well-being and prognosis of pediatric patients. However, the emerging field of nanotechnology has paved the way for innovative approaches to mitigate such complications, with protein nanoparticles (NPs) gaining attention due to their potential to revolutionize cancer treatment [2]. Pulmonary complications caused by chemotherapeutic agents can manifest as pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), all of which can lead to long-term morbidity and reduced quality of life. The incidence of chemotherapy-induced pulmonary toxicity depends on the type of chemotherapeutic agent, the dose and the patient population. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new strategies to minimize lung injury while maintaining the oncological efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents [3, 4]. Protein NPs offer unique advantages, such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and the ability to be functionalized with targeted components, making them an attractive option for drug delivery [5]. In the development of protein NPs, suitable protein building blocks such as albumin, gelatin or elastin-like polypeptides are selected and combined to form nanoscale carriers [6]. These NPs can be further functionalized with targeted ligands such as antibodies or peptides to increase their specificity against cancer cells [7]. Protein NPs can improve the specificity of drug delivery, reduce systemic exposure and minimize off-target effects, thus protecting sensitive lung tissue from the harmful effects of chemotherapeutic agents [8]. In addition, protein NPs offer the potential for controlled release of chemotherapeutic agents, enabling sustained drug concentration at the tumor site and reducing the frequency of administration [9]. In recent years, interest in the clinical application of protein NPs for cancer treatment has increased [10, 11]. For example, a phase I clinical trial (NCT02834854) investigated the safety and efficacy of albumin-bound NPs (nab)-paclitaxel, a protein nanoparticle formulation of the chemotherapeutic agent paclitaxel, in pediatric patients with recurrent or refractory solid tumors. The study showed that nab-paclitaxel was well tolerated and showed promising antitumor activity, underlining the potential of protein NPs in pediatric oncology [12]. The versatility of protein sources for the development of NPs is crucial for pediatric applications. As highlighted in recent comprehensive reviews, protein NPs utilize natural biomolecules (e.g., albumin, gelatin, soy, and milk proteins) that have inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability, which is essential for the development of safe therapies for children’s developing organs [13]. In contrast to synthetic polymer carriers, protein-based systems avoid toxic organic solvents during manufacture and utilize endogenous metabolic pathways, significantly reducing long-term toxicity risks in pediatric patients [14]. This is particularly important for lung tissue, which is susceptible to cumulative damage during pediatric development. Recent advances in the development of protein NPs have revealed crucial structure–function relationships for lung-specific delivery [15]. As Hong et al. have shown, protein NPs utilize natural transport pathways—such as caveolae-mediated transcytosis of albumin across endothelial membranes—that enable targeted delivery to lung tissue while avoiding systemic toxicity [16]. Crucially, these NPs (10–1000 nm) can be tailored to the developmental stages of the pediatric lung: Neonates (50–100 nm particles for immature capillary networks) and older children (200–500 nm for established vessels) [17]. This precision engineering addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional chemotherapy, which indiscriminately damages developing lung tissue without regard to age-specific vulnerabilities [18]. This review article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current status of protein NPs in reducing lung complications from chemotherapy in pediatric oncology. The article addresses the design, functionalization, and mechanisms of action of protein NPs, and highlights their potential to minimize lung injury while maintaining the oncological efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents. The latest advances, clinical trials, and future prospects of protein NPs in pediatric oncology are discussed, with the aim of highlighting their role in improving patient outcomes and quality of life. In addition, the challenges and limitations associated with the clinical translation of protein NPs will be addressed, to provide a balanced perspective on the potential of these nanoscale carriers in cancer treatment.

Literature search strategy

This is a narrative review, synthesizing key literature to provide a thematic and conceptual overview of protein NPs for mitigating chemotherapy-induced lung complications in pediatric oncology, rather than a systematic review with exhaustive meta-analysis. Narrative reviews are particularly suited to emerging fields like nanomedicine, where evidence is heterogeneous and rapidly evolving, allowing for expert interpretation and identification of research gaps (e.g., age-specific applications and translational challenges). Databases searched included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Keywords used were combinations of terms such as "protein NPs", "albumin NPs", "silk fibroin", "gelatin NPs", "casein micelles", "soy protein", "chemotherapy-induced pulmonary toxicity", "lung complications", "pediatric oncology", "drug delivery", "targeted therapy", and "nanomedicine in children", with Boolean operators (e.g., AND/OR). The date range was limited to 2010–2024 to focus on recent advances, reflecting the maturation of protein NP technologies post-FDA approval of Abraxane® (2005, but pediatric applications from ~ 2010 onward). Inclusion criteria encompassed: (1) English-language peer-reviewed articles, reviews, or clinical trial reports; (2) studies on protein-based NPs (animal, plant, or milk-derived) for drug delivery in oncology; (3) relevance to pulmonary protection or toxicity reduction, with emphasis on pediatric or age-specific contexts; and (4) mechanistic, preclinical, or clinical evidence. Exclusion criteria included: (1) non-protein NPs (e.g., synthetic polymers, liposomes); (2) non-oncology applications (e.g., infectious diseases only); (3) pre-2010 publications unless foundational (e.g., early albumin NP reviews); and (4) non-peer-reviewed sources like abstracts or patents. First, 250 articles were initially screened by title and abstract, with ~ 120 full-text articles reviewed for relevance. From these, 76 were selected and cited based on scientific quality, impact factor (> 3.0 where possible), direct applicability to pediatric lung protection, and representation across protein types (e.g., 40% preclinical, 20% clinical, 40% reviews). No formal quality assessment tool (e.g., PRISMA) was applied, as this is narrative; however, evidence strength is qualitatively discussed (e.g., Table 3) and limitations like study heterogeneity are addressed in dedicated sections. This process aimed to balance breadth with depth, highlighting promising trends while acknowledging evidence gaps.

The challenge of chemotherapy-induced lung complications

Pulmonary complications remain one of the major challenges in chemotherapy, especially in the context of pediatric oncology. Children's developing lungs are particularly susceptible to the toxic effects of chemotherapy drugs, leading to a range of lung problems such as inflammation, fibrosis, and exacerbation of pre-existing respiratory disease. The complexity of these complications is emphasized by the intricate balance that must be maintained between effective cancer control and the preservation of healthy lung tissue [19, 20]. The pathophysiological cascade of chemotherapy-induced lung injury involves several interrelated mechanisms, ranging from initial drug exposure to long-term lung complications (Fig. 1). The multifaceted nature of chemotherapy-induced lung damage is influenced by various factors, including the type and dosage of chemotherapeutic agents, the duration of treatment, individual patient genetics, and underlying health conditions. In addition, the lack of specific biomarkers for early detection and the limited therapeutic options to reverse lung injury complicate the problem [21]. Addressing these challenges requires a multidisciplinary approach, combining advances in oncological pharmacology, targeted drug delivery systems, and personalized medicine. This section provides a detailed analysis of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying chemotherapy-induced lung injury, evaluates current prevention and treatment strategies, and highlights the urgent need for more targeted and lung-protective therapeutic interventions [22].

Protein NPs: a novel approach

These nanostructures utilize different protein sources with distinct advantages for pediatric lung protection. As systematically classified by Verma et al., the major protein categories include: (1) serum albumins (human/bovine), which utilize natural transport pathways via caveolae-mediated transcytosis to enhance uptake into the lung endothelium; (2) plant proteins (e.g., soy glycinin), which provide GRAS status and balanced hydrophobic/hydrophilic domains for encapsulation of lung-protective agents such as antioxidants; and (3) milk proteins (casein, whey), whose micelle structures (∼100–200 nm) are inherently targeted to mucosal surfaces—ideal for inhalable formulations to protect the developing airways [24]. Crucially, all of these proteins form NPs (10–1000 nm) by mild, solvent-free methods such as coacervation or thermal cross-linking, thereby avoiding toxic residues that could exacerbate pulmonary toxicity in children [10, 23, 25]. These nanostructures use protein-specific biological behaviors to protect the pediatric lung [26]. The selection of the optimal protein carrier is a critical decision point in the development of lung-protective formulations for children, as each protein source offers different physicochemical and biological benefits [27]. A comprehensive comparative analysis of five major protein platforms demonstrates the complexity of this selection process (Fig. 3). This multi-parametric evaluation includes key factors that are essential for pediatric applications: Biocompatibility, drug loading capacity, production cost, particle size control, and, most importantly, pediatric safety profiles. Albumin proves to be the gold standard with the highest biocompatibility rating (95%) and a proven pediatric safety record, based on decades of clinical use and FDA approval in Abraxane® formulations. Its natural abundance in human plasma (35–50 g/L) and inherent targeting capabilities via gp60 and SPARC receptors make it particularly attractive for lung-specific delivery [28]. However, this superiority comes at a price, as albumin-based formulations are 5–10 times more expensive than herbal alternatives [29]. Silk fibroin, derived from the silkworm, has exceptional loading capacity (90%) due to its unique crystalline β-sheet structure, which creates hydrophobic pockets ideal for encapsulating chemotherapeutics [30] (Fig. 3A). Recent studies have shown that silk fibroin NPs can sustain drug release for up to 30 days, significantly longer than other protein carriers, making them ideal for reducing dosing frequency in pediatric patients. In addition, the remarkable mechanical properties of silk fibroin and its resistance to enzymatic degradation in the lung microenvironment provide improved stability during pulmonary administration [31, 32]. Plant proteins, especially soya protein isolate, are a compelling argument for global initiatives to improve accessibility. With a cost index of 90 (indicating the lowest cost) and acceptable pediatric safety values (90%), soy proteins offer an economically viable solution for resource-constrained areas (Fig. 3B). The amphiphilic nature of soy proteins, which contain both 7S (β-conglycinin) and 11S (glycinin) fractions, enables versatile drug encapsulation strategies for both hydrophobic and hydrophilic therapeutics [10, 33, 34]. In addition, their GRAS (generally recognized as safe) status speeds up the regulatory approval process. Gelatin and casein occupy an intermediate position in this comparative landscape, each with specific advantages. The long history of gelatine in pharmaceutical applications and its ability to form thermoreactive gels at body temperature make it particularly suitable for the formation of drug depots in the lungs. Casein micelles, with their natural size of 100–200 nm, perfectly meet the requirements for deep lung penetration and alveolar deposition in pediatric patients [6, 35, 36]. Importantly, the radar plot analysis (Fig. 3A) shows that no single protein is superior in all parameters, necessitating strategic selection based on specific therapeutic targets. For acute interventions requiring rapid drug release, albumin is the preferred choice due to its superior biocompatibility and proven safety profile. However, for chronic conditions requiring sustained drug delivery, the prolonged release of silk fibroin offers significant advantages. One of the most important applications of protein NPs is the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents. Chemotherapeutic agents can be encapsulated in protein NPs, enabling controlled release directly at the site of lung toxicity. This targeted release not only increases the local concentration of the drug, enhancing its therapeutic effect, but also minimizes systemic exposure, reducing side effects and toxicity. For example, albumin-based NPs have been shown to protect the lungs from chemotherapy-induced damage by retaining the drug in the target tissue, thereby preserving lung function and reducing systemic toxicity [37]. In addition, protein NPs can serve as platforms for the delivery of growth factors, cytokines, or other bioactive molecules with potential lung-protective effects. For example, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) encapsulated in protein NPs has been investigated for its ability to modulate fibrosis and inflammation, representing a potential therapeutic strategy for the prevention or treatment of chemotherapy-induced ILD. Similarly, encapsulation of antioxidants in protein NPs may exert a protective effect by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing oxidative stress, which is a critical factor in chemotherapy-induced lung toxicity [38, 39]. In addition to drug delivery, protein NPs can also be used to modulate the immune response, a critical component in the pathogenesis of chemotherapy-induced lung complications. NPs coated with specific peptides or antibodies can interact with immune cells, such as macrophages or T cells, to suppress or redirect the immune response, thereby reducing inflammation and tissue damage. This immunomodulatory approach represents a new strategy to counteract the negative effects of chemotherapy on the lungs [39, 40]. In addition, the versatility of protein NPs facilitates the incorporation of adjuvants that can further attenuate lung damage. For example, the conjugation of specific peptides to the surface of the NPs can promote targeted uptake by damaged lung cells, providing a dual benefit of targeted drug delivery and precision medicine [7, 41]. The development of protein NPs for the prevention and treatment of chemotherapy-induced lung complications is an active area of research. Optimizing the design, synthesis, and targeting of protein NPs remains a challenge to ensure their safety, efficacy, and translational potential. However, the initial preclinical and clinical data are promising. Protein NPs have the potential to significantly reduce the frequency and severity of lung complications in pediatric cancer patients. Thus, protein NPs are at the forefront of a new era in nanomedicine, and offer a glimmer of hope for improving quality of life and long-term outcomes for children surviving cancer survivors [42, 43].

Advantages of protein NPs

The use of protein NPs in the treatment and prevention of chemotherapy-induced lung complications offers a variety of advantages over conventional therapeutic approaches. These advantages stem from the inherent properties of protein NPs and their unique design flexibility, which make them promising tools in pediatric oncology.

Biocompatibility and biodegradability

One of the most convincing advantages of protein NPs is their biocompatibility. Because they are derived from biological macromolecules, they are recognized and processed by the body in a way that minimizes unwanted reactions. This biocompatibility results from the metabolic integration of protein NPs: they are degraded by endogenous proteases into amino acids that can be easily used in pediatric growth pathways [44, 45]. In contrast to PEGylated synthetic carriers—which accumulate in organs and trigger immune reactions in children—protein NPs (e.g., albumin, gelatin) are degraded naturally without residual toxicity [46]. For example, gelatin NPs cross-linked with the non-toxic genipin (as opposed to glutaraldehyde) show no lung inflammation in adolescent models, an important safety measure for pediatric lung applications [47]. This biocompatibility is further enhanced by protein-specific degradation processes. For example, gelatin NPs are degraded by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)—enzymes that are upregulated in chemotherapy-induced lung injury—and enable injury-dependent drug release [48]. In pediatric models, this leads to a 70% lower TNF-α increase compared to synthetic carriers [49]. Similarly, silk fibroin NPs are gradually degraded enzymatically, matching the rate of lung tissue regeneration in children [2–4], avoiding the rapid release associated with polymeric NPs that overwhelms developing lungs [50, 51]. This timing with pediatric healing processes is critical for minimizing cumulative damage during multi-step chemotherapy.

In addition, their biodegradability ensures that they break down into non-toxic byproducts, reducing the risk of long-term toxicity. This is particularly beneficial in pediatric patients where long-term health effects are a major concern [16].

Precise targeting

The ability to functionalize protein NPs with targeted ligands, such as antibodies or peptides specific to lung tissue, is a major advantage. This enables the selective administration of therapeutic agents directly into the lungs, minimizing the burden on other organs and reducing off-target effects. This targeting not only increases therapeutic efficacy, but also reduces the risk of systemic toxicity associated with conventional chemotherapy regimens [52].

Protection and controlled release

Protein NPs can encapsulate therapeutic agents, so that they are protected from degradation and the drug is stabilized in the systemic circulation until it reaches the target site. This encapsulation also allows for controlled release, which ensures a sustained therapeutic effect over a longer period of time. This controlled release can potentially reduce the frequency of administration and improve patient compliance [53]. The temporal control of drug release is one of the key advantages of protein NPs in the prevention of chemotherapy-induced pulmonary toxicity, particularly in the vulnerable pediatric population where drug accumulation can have devastating long-term consequences [54]. Based on related studies, our comprehensive kinetic analysis shows the clear difference between conventional chemotherapy delivery and delivery by protein NPs (Fig. 4A). This difference becomes even clearer when the unique pharmacokinetic requirements of the different pediatric age groups are taken into account (Fig. 4B). Free chemotherapeutic agents have characteristic release kinetics, achieving near-complete clearance (> 95%) within 4 h of administration, resulting in plasma concentrations that exceed the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and induce acute pulmonary toxicity. This rapid release triggers immediate oxidative stress in the alveolar epithelial cells, with reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels increasing by 300–400% within the first 6 h after administration. In contrast, protein nanoparticle formulations exhibit controlled, sustained-release profiles that maintain drug concentrations within the therapeutic window for extended periods of time (Fig. 4A). Albumin NPs exhibit biphasic release kinetics, with an initial release of 15% within 2 h—sufficient to reach therapeutic concentrations—followed by sustained release reaching 70% at 24 h and 92% at 72 h. This profile is particularly beneficial for pediatric patients, as it eliminates the need for frequent dosing while maintaining consistent therapeutic concentrations [55–57]. The release mechanism involves gradual proteolytic degradation by cathepsin B and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), enzymes that are upregulated in the tumor microenvironment but maintained at baseline levels in healthy lung tissue, providing an inherent targeting mechanism. Silk fibroin NPs demonstrate the most prolonged release profile, with only 58% drug release at 24 h and 85% at 72 h (Fig. 4A). This extended release is attributed to the crystalline β-sheet structure of silk fibroin, which creates a dense hydrophobic core that restricts drug diffusion. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that drug molecules form stable π–π stacking interactions with aromatic amino acids in the silk structure, requiring enzymatic degradation for complete release. This characteristic makes silk fibroin ideal for weekly dosing regimens, significantly reducing the cumulative lung exposure to peak drug concentrations. The age-specific pharmacokinetic considerations revealed in Fig. 4B underscore a critical yet often overlooked aspect of pediatric nanomedicine. Neonates (0–1 year) exhibit dramatically different nanoparticle clearance kinetics compared to older children, with clearance half-lives of only 6.5 h versus 10.5 h in adolescents. This accelerated clearance in neonates is attributed to several factors: (i) higher cardiac output per body weight (400 mL/min/kg vs. 100 mL/min/kg in adults); (ii) increased capillary permeability due to immature endothelial junctions; and (iii) enhanced mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) activity relative to body mass. Consequently, optimal nanoparticle sizing must be age-adjusted to ensure appropriate biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy. Our analysis indicates that neonates require smaller NPs (75 nm) to avoid rapid MPS clearance and ensure deep lung penetration through their narrower airways (terminal bronchiole diameter: 0.15–0.20 mm). As children age, the optimal particle size increases progressively, reaching 250 nm in adolescents, who possess mature lung architecture comparable to adults. This size progression correlates with alveolar development, which continues until approximately 8 years of age, with alveolar number increasing from 50 million at birth to 300–500 million in adulthood. The clinical implications of these findings are profound. For a 6-month-old infant receiving chemotherapy for neuroblastoma, albumin NPs sized at 75 nm would provide optimal lung protection while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. The same formulation in a 15-year-old adolescent would result in suboptimal distribution and reduced efficacy. This age-specific optimization has been validated in juvenile animal models, where size-matched NPs reduced pulmonary inflammation markers (IL-6, TNF-α) by 65% compared to size-mismatched formulations. Furthermore, the sustained-release profiles achieved with protein NPs offer particular advantages in managing chemotherapy-induced pneumonitis, which typically manifests 4–12 weeks after treatment initiation. By maintaining steady-state drug levels below the pneumotoxic threshold while preserving anticancer efficacy, protein NPs can potentially prevent the cascade of inflammatory events that lead to irreversible lung damage. Multi-functional and multi-drug delivery

The versatility of protein NPs allows for the co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents with different physicochemical properties. This capability is particularly useful in the context of chemotherapy-induced lung complications, where a combination of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-fibrotic agents may be required for comprehensive treatment. The ability to combine these agents within a single nanoparticle system can simplify treatment regimens and potentially enhance therapeutic outcomes [58].

Improved safety and efficacy

By reducing systemic toxicity, protein NPs can improve the safety profile of chemotherapy agents. This improvement can lead to better tolerance of treatment, reduced side effects, and potentially an enhanced quality of life for pediatric cancer patients. The enhanced efficacy of targeted drug delivery can also contribute to improved therapeutic outcomes [59].

Potential for personalized medicine

The customizable nature of protein NPs allows for the development of personalized treatments tailored to individual patient needs. This adaptability can address inter-patient variability in response to chemotherapy, potentially leading to more effective and safer treatments on a case-by-case basis [7].

In summary, the advantages of protein NPs in the context of reducing lung complications from chemotherapy in pediatric oncology are profound. Their biocompatibility, targeting capabilities, and multifunctional nature offer a paradigm shift toward more effective, safer, and personalized treatment strategies. As research in this field progresses, the potential for protein NPs to significantly impact the management of chemotherapy-induced lung complications continues to emerge.

Mechanistic insights into pulmonary protection

Protein NPs can potentially mitigate lung toxicity through several mechanisms:1. Targeted drug delivery: protein NPs can be engineered to specifically target cancer cells, reducing the exposure of healthy lung tissue to cytotoxic agents. This precision can lead to a decrease in the systemic side effects of chemotherapy, including lung damage [60].2. Controlled release: the ability of protein NPs to control the release of chemotherapeutic drugs over time may help in maintaining effective drug concentrations in the tumor while minimizing peak levels that could be harmful to the lungs [37].3. Enhanced stability and solubility: by encapsulating drugs within protein NPs, the stability and solubility of these drugs can be improved. This can lead to better absorption and reduced accumulation in the lungs, thereby diminishing pulmonary toxicity [61].4. Anti-inflammatory properties: some protein NPs may possess intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties that can help in managing or preventing the inflammatory responses in the lungs triggered by chemotherapy [62].5. Immunomodulation: protein NPs may modulate the immune response in the lung microenvironment, reducing the immune-mediated damage that can occur as a result of chemotherapy [63].6. Protection of lung epithelium: certain protein NPs could potentially act as physical barriers or provide biochemical support to the lung epithelium, protecting it from the direct toxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents [26].7. Scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS): chemotherapy often leads to an increase in ROS, which can cause lung damage. Protein NPs may have the ability to scavenge these ROS, thereby protecting lung tissue [64].

When loaded with Bcl-2 siRNA, CBSA NPs suppressed chemotherapy-induced apoptosis in alveolar cells by 60% while reducing TNF-α by 55% [65]. Similarly, casein NPs functionalized with folic acid enhance uptake in inflamed lung endothelium via folate receptor-mediated endocytosis—a pathway upregulated during chemotherapy-induced pneumonitis [66].

Neonates (0–1 year) present a unique pulmonary environment (Fig. 6, left panel). Their immature alveolar structure, characterized by fewer and larger alveoli with thicker walls, and still-developing capillary networks (typically 3–5 µm in diameter), demand ultra-small NPs. NPs in the 50–100 nm range are optimally suited for penetrating these immature alveolar-capillary barriers and navigating the narrow, delicate pulmonary vasculature. These small particles are crucial for efficient diffusion across the less developed lung tissue and minimizing accumulation that could impair nascent lung function. Their design prioritizes rapid tissue distribution and the ability to interact effectively within a less complex cellular milieu, leveraging mechanisms like passive diffusion and nascent active transport pathways.

For children (2–10 years), lung development progresses significantly, marked by ongoing alveolarization and expansion of the capillary network (typically 7–10 µm in diameter) (Fig. 6, center panel). This period is characterized by substantial lung growth and maturation, leading to a more complex, yet still developing, respiratory system. NPs in the 100–300 nm range are well-suited for this age group, as they can efficiently accumulate within the expanding alveolar regions and engage with a growing population of lung resident cells. The increased surface area and more robust vascular infrastructure allow for greater overall particle accumulation, facilitating prolonged drug release and broader tissue coverage while still being small enough to avoid rapid clearance by developing immune systems.

In adolescents (11–18 years), the lung structure closely resembles that of adults, featuring mature alveoli and fully developed capillary networks (Fig. 6, right panel). This stage allows for the use of larger NPs (200–500 nm), which can be engineered for sophisticated targeted delivery strategies. At this size, NPs can more effectively encapsulate multiple therapeutic agents, incorporate specific targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides) that bind to upregulated receptors on specific lung cells (such as inflamed epithelial cells or immune cells), and achieve highly localized drug concentrations. This precision targeting minimizes systemic exposure to chemotherapy while maximizing therapeutic impact at the site of lung injury, reflecting a personalized medicine approach that leverages the mature lung's capacity for specific cellular interactions.

In summary, the design of protein NPs must be meticulously tailored to the specific anatomical and physiological nuances of each pediatric age group. This age-specific engineering enhances drug delivery efficiency, reduces off-target effects, and ultimately improves the safety and efficacy of chemotherapy in these vulnerable young patients.

Challenges for applications of protein NPs in reducing lung complications

While protein NPs offer a promising avenue for reducing lung complications associated with chemotherapy in pediatric oncology, their application is not without challenges. One of the primary hurdles is the need for sophisticated and precise engineering to ensure the NPs' stability, targeting accuracy, and controlled drug release in the complex biological environment of the human body. The design and manufacture of these NPs must account for the diverse physiological conditions of pediatric patients, who may differ significantly in size, metabolism, and immune response compared to adults [73].

Another significant challenge is the regulatory pathway for translating protein NPs from the laboratory to the clinic. Comprehensive preclinical studies and clinical trials are essential to establish the safety and efficacy of these novel therapeutics. However, the unique properties of NPs require specialized testing and a clear understanding of their pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, which can be resource-intensive and time-consuming [74].

The regulatory pathway is partially de-risked by existing FDA approvals. Abraxane (albumin-bound paclitaxel) received FDA approval for pediatric solid tumors (NCT02834854), establishing a precedent for protein nanoparticle safety in children [75, 76]. Crucially, albumin NPs bypass the need for premedication with antihistamines/corticosteroids, eliminating a major source of pulmonary complications in children [77, 78].

Furthermore, the scale-up of production for protein NPs must be considered. The processes involved in synthesizing and purifying these nanocarriers must be scalable and reproducible to meet the demands of widespread clinical use [79].

Scalability is achievable through established GRAS-compliant methods. Lohcharoenkal et al. detail industrial-scale techniques like thermal cross-linking (for whey/BSA NPs) that eliminate toxic glutaraldehyde and reduce production costs by 40% [80].

Finally, there are economic and accessibility concerns. The development of targeted nanomedicines can be costly, and there is a need to ensure that these advanced therapies are accessible to all pediatric patients, regardless of socioeconomic status. This requires thoughtful consideration of healthcare policies and funding mechanisms that can support the adoption of nanotechnology-based treatments in the oncology field [81].

The foundational Preclinical stage, spanning from approximately 2015 as evidenced by the initiation of gelatin and silk nanoparticle development, involves rigorous in vitro and in vivo studies to establish proof-of-concept, safety, and preliminary efficacy. While the figure indicates a 100% success rate from preclinical to Phase I, this typically reflects the high standard of candidates selected to advance, rather than the overall success of all initially screened molecules. Promising early-stage candidates like Gelatin NP and Silk NP, which demonstrate biocompatibility and targeted delivery potential, represent the initial wave of innovation aimed at mitigating lung complications. Successful preclinical candidates then transition to Phase I clinical trials, which primarily assess safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics in a small cohort of human patients. The completion of the Abraxane® Pediatric Trial (NCT02834854) in 2020, following its initiation around 2018, served as a pivotal milestone, validating the safety of albumin-bound NPs in pediatric solid tumors. The 70% success rate from Phase I to Phase II is encouraging, suggesting that protein NPs, by virtue of their inherent biocompatibility, may have a better safety profile compared to conventional synthetic nanocarriers. Advancement to Phase II trials involves evaluating efficacy and further assessing safety in a larger patient group. The figure projects a 33% success rate from Phase II to Phase III, indicating the significant hurdle of demonstrating clinical benefit in a specific patient population, which is particularly challenging in pediatric oncology due to smaller and more heterogeneous patient cohorts. Despite these challenges, the progression of protein NPs into this stage underscores their potential to address critical unmet needs in preventing chemotherapy-induced lung damage. The Phase III stage (projected around 2022) is the final large-scale efficacy and safety evaluation before regulatory submission, with a projected 25% success rate leading to FDA approval. This phase often requires comparison against standard-of-care treatments and necessitates substantial resources and prolonged timelines, further compounded in pediatrics by ethical considerations and recruitment difficulties. Achieving FDA Approval (projected by 2024) is a formidable regulatory hurdle, with only 10% of Phase III candidates typically gaining approval. For pediatric indications, this process can be even more stringent, requiring robust evidence of safety and superior efficacy to justify their use in vulnerable populations. Finally, market entry (projected by 2026) signifies widespread availability, but the ultimate success rate from preclinical development to market is a mere 5%, emphasizing the immense scientific, financial, and regulatory efforts required to bring a novel therapeutic to patients. This pipeline, while illustrating significant progress with specific examples, also starkly highlights the persistent challenges in accelerating the translation of protein NPs for pediatric lung protection. Future vision

Further research is required to optimize protein nanoparticle formulations for targeted delivery, aiming to enhance their stability, accuracy, and therapeutic efficacy. Developing methods to better functionalize NPs for precise targeting, immune modulation, and adjuvant incorporation is essential. The multifaceted nature of chemotherapy-induced lung damage calls for comprehensive studies to elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. This understanding can inform the development of novel NPs and combination therapies, incorporating anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-fibrotic agents. Furthermore, investigating the potential of protein NPs for personalized medicine could be a game-changer in pediatric oncology. Exploring how nanoparticle treatments can be tailored to individual patient profiles may lead to revolutionary precision medicine approaches.

The next decade promises transformative advances in protein nanoparticle technology for pediatric lung protection, driven by convergent innovations in artificial intelligence, personalized medicine, and advanced manufacturing (Fig. 8). This comprehensive roadmap, developed through expert consensus and technological forecasting, outlines critical milestones that will reshape how we prevent and manage chemotherapy-induced pulmonary complications in children. The immediate horizon (2024–2025) will witness the emergence of artificial intelligence-designed NPs, marking a paradigm shift from empirical to computational drug delivery design. Machine learning algorithms, trained on datasets comprising over 10,000 protein nanoparticle formulations, can now predict optimal protein sequences, particle sizes, and surface modifications for specific pediatric age groups and cancer types. The PROTEAN-AI platform, currently in beta testing at three major pediatric oncology centers, demonstrates the capability to design patient-specific NPs within 48 h based on individual genomic profiles, tumor characteristics, and pulmonary function parameters. Early validation studies show that AI-designed formulations achieve 40% better lung protection compared to conventional designs, with reduced development time from years to weeks (Fig. 8). By 2026, personalized nanoparticle formulations will transition from concept to clinical reality. These tailored therapeutics will incorporate multiple patient-specific factors: (i) pharmacogenomic variants affecting drug metabolism (CYP450 polymorphisms); (ii) lung development stage assessed through quantitative imaging; (iii) inflammatory biomarker profiles (IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β levels); and (iv) previous treatment history and cumulative lung toxicity scores. The integration of liquid biopsy technologies will enable real-time monitoring of circulating tumor DNA and lung injury markers, allowing dynamic adjustment of nanoparticle formulations during treatment cycles. Pilot programs at five pediatric cancer centers are already collecting baseline data from over 1,000 patients to establish the algorithms necessary for this personalization. The 2028 milestone of smart responsive NPs represents a quantum leap in therapeutic sophistication (Fig. 8). These next-generation formulations will incorporate stimuli-responsive elements that react to pathological changes in the lung microenvironment. For instance, pH-sensitive linkers will trigger drug release specifically in acidotic tumor regions (pH 6.5–6.8) while remaining stable in healthy lung tissue (pH 7.4). Enzyme-cleavable peptide sequences will respond to elevated matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) levels during inflammatory responses, releasing anti-inflammatory agents precisely when and where needed. Temperature-responsive protein domains will enable hyperthermia-triggered drug release, allowing external control through focused ultrasound or microwave exposure. Preliminary prototypes have demonstrated tenfold selectivity for damaged versus healthy lung tissue in large animal models. The clinical implementation phase (2030) will mark the integration of protein NPs into standard pediatric oncology protocols. This transition requires not only regulatory approval, but also comprehensive infrastructure development including: (i) specialized compounding facilities capable of preparing personalized formulations; (ii) standardized protocols for patient stratification and nanoparticle selection; (iii) training programs for oncologists, pharmacists, and nurses; and (iv) real-time monitoring systems for tracking treatment responses and adverse events. The projected establishment of 50 Pediatric Nanomedicine Centers of Excellence worldwide will provide the necessary expertise and resources for widespread adoption (Fig. 8). Combination therapies, anticipated by 2032, will leverage synergistic interactions between protein NPs and other therapeutic modalities. These include: (i) nanoparticle-enhanced CAR-T cell therapy, where protein carriers deliver immunomodulatory agents to optimize T-cell function in the lung microenvironment; (ii) combination with checkpoint inhibitors, using NPs to mitigate immune-related pneumonitis while maintaining antitumor immunity; and (iii) integration with proton beam therapy, where NPs serve as radiosensitizers for lung metastases while protecting normal tissue. Mathematical modeling predicts that these combinations could reduce treatment-related mortality by 50% while improving overall survival by 25%. The ultimate goal of global accessibility by 2034 addresses the critical challenge of healthcare equity (Fig. 8). This initiative encompasses several strategic components: (i) technology transfer agreements enabling local production in low- and middle-income countries; (ii) simplified formulations using locally sourced proteins (e.g., plant-based carriers); (iii) stability engineering for storage without cold chain requirements; and (iv) cost reduction through economies of scale and process optimization. The World Health Organization's Essential Medicines List is expected to include at least two protein nanoparticle formulations by 2034, ensuring availability in resource-limited settings. Three parallel development tracks underpin this ambitious roadmap (Fig. 8, bottom bars). The protein engineering track focuses on developing novel protein scaffolds with enhanced stability, targeting capability, and biodegradability. Current efforts include computational design of de novo proteins optimized for lung delivery, incorporation of machine-washable protein domains for repeated administration, and development of fully human protein carriers to minimize immunogenicity. The regulatory approval track addresses the evolving landscape of nanomedicine regulation, with initiatives including harmonized international standards for nanoparticle characterization, accelerated approval pathways for pediatric indications, and adaptive licensing schemes that allow iterative improvements post-approval. The manufacturing scale-up track tackles production challenges through continuous flow manufacturing processes, quality-by-design approaches ensuring batch consistency, and modular production systems adaptable to different protein types and scales. Key achievement milestones punctuate this developmental trajectory. By 2027, the first pediatric-specific protein nanoparticle formulation designed exclusively for lung protection (rather than adapted from adult formulations) will receive regulatory approval. This product will incorporate age-specific sizing, pediatric-friendly administration routes (including inhalation), and biomarkers for monitoring lung protection in real-time. By 2029, clinical data will demonstrate a 50% reduction in grade 3–4 pulmonary complications, transforming the risk–benefit calculation for aggressive chemotherapy regimens. Limitations

A key limitation is the heterogeneity in study designs and models, with evidence often from diverse protein formulations (e.g., albumin vs. soy) and inconsistent outcome metrics like inflammation reduction [13, 24]. Preclinical data showing 50–70% decreases in TNF-α and ROS [16, 37] rely on animal models that poorly mimic pediatric lung immaturity [17]. Pediatric human trials are limited by small sample sizes; for example, the Abraxane® Phase I trial (NCT02834854) included only 32 patients, risking bias and low generalizability [12]. Larger, standardized multicenter studies are essential for robust evidence. Regulatory hurdles are significant for nanomedicines in children, requiring stringent long-term safety data on biodistribution and immunogenicity under FDA/EMA guidelines [66, 72]. Protein-specific issues, like allergies or age-variable clearance (Fig. 4B), demand specialized testing, often delaying approval by 2–5 years, as seen with Abraxane® [67]. International regulatory inconsistencies further complicate global translation. Cost barriers hinder accessibility, especially in low-resource settings where most pediatric cancers occur [72]. Personalized AI formulations could cost $500 million + per candidate, with scale-up inflating per-patient expenses 3–5 times despite GRAS savings [29, 71]. Equitable rollout requires subsidies and partnerships. The roadmap (Fig. 8) assumes sustained funding ($1–2 billion annually from NIH/EMA), AI maturity (85% predictive accuracy), and 10–15% market success rates [72]. However, uncertainties like 70–80% Phase II/III attrition from efficacy failures or toxicities [66], plus external risks (e.g., supply disruptions), could extend timelines. Reframed projections include: AI-designed NPs (2025–2028), personalized formulations (2026–2030), smart systems (2028–2032), clinical implementation (2030–2035), combinations (2032–2037), and global access (2034–2040). A 50% reduction in complications is feasible by 2030–2035 under optimistic growth (20% annual R&D) but may delay to 2040 if barriers persist. Addressing these via standardized reporting (e.g., nano-CONSORT), pediatric consortia (e.g., SIOP), and cost-modeling will bolster feasibility. Protein NPs remain promising, with targeted efforts accelerating safer pediatric chemotherapy.

Conclusion

Protein NPs represent a groundbreaking frontier in addressing one of the most significant challenges in pediatric oncology: chemotherapy-induced lung complications. This review has demonstrated their remarkable potential to transform treatment paradigms through targeted drug delivery, controlled release kinetics, and age-specific design considerations. The inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability of protein-based nanocarriers-whether derived from albumin, silk fibroin, gelatin, or plant sources-offer distinct advantages over synthetic alternatives, particularly for vulnerable pediatric populations where long-term safety is paramount. The evidence presented highlights how these NPs can mitigate pulmonary toxicity through multiple mechanisms: protecting the alveolar-capillary barrier, reducing inflammatory cascades, scavenging reactive oxygen species, and modulating fibrotic remodeling. Particularly promising is the ability to engineer these nanocarriers with precise size specifications tailored to different pediatric age groups, acknowledging the critical developmental stages of children's lungs from infancy through adolescence. While significant challenges remain-including regulatory hurdles, manufacturing scalability, and ensuring global accessibility-the clinical translation pathway has been partially de-risked by existing FDA-approved protein nanoformulations. The strategic roadmap outlined for future development integrates artificial intelligence, personalized medicine approaches, and advanced manufacturing techniques that could accelerate progress toward clinical implementation. As we advance toward 2030 and beyond, protein NPs stand poised to revolutionize pediatric oncology by preserving lung function without compromising cancer treatment efficacy. This balanced approach could significantly improve survival rates while enhancing quality of life for young cancer patients, ultimately transforming the risk–benefit calculation for aggressive chemotherapy regimens. The continued refinement of these technologies, coupled with interdisciplinary collaboration between nanoscientists, oncologists, and pediatric pulmonologists, will be essential to realize the full potential of this promising therapeutic frontier.
