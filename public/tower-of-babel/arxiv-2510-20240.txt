Li-Yorke chaos on fuzzy dynamical systems


Abstract
Given a dynamical system ( X , f ) (X,f) we investigate how several variants of Li-Yorke chaos behave with respect to the extended systems ( 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) , f ¯ ) (\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f}) and ( ℱ ⁡ ( X ) , f ^ ) (\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f}) , where f ¯ \overline{f} is the hyperextension of f f acting on the space 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{K}(X) of non-empty compact subsets of X X , and where f ^ \hat{f} denotes the Zadeh extension of f f acting on the space ℱ ⁡ ( X ) \mathcal{F}(X) of normal fuzzy subsets of X X . We first prove that the main variants of Li-Yorke chaos transfer from ( X , f ) (X,f) to ( 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) , f ¯ ) (\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f}) and from ( 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) , f ¯ ) (\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f}) to ( ℱ ⁡ ( X ) , f ^ ) (\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f}) , but that the converse implications do not hold in general. However, combining the notions of proximality and sensitivity we introduce Cantor-dense Li-Yorke chaos, and we prove that this strengthened variant of chaos does transfer from ( ℱ ⁡ ( X ) , f ^ ) (\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f}) to ( 𝒦 ⁡ ( X ) , f ¯ ) (\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f}) under natural assumptions.

The initial concept of chaos for dynamical systems was introduced by Li and Yorke in

–

A pair

\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))\geq\varepsilon\quad\text{ while }\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))=0.

In this case one also says that
(x,y)
is a
Li-Yorke
\varepsilon
-pair
(
LY
\varepsilon
-pair
for short).

–

A subset

is said to be
LY scrambled
(resp.
LY
\varepsilon
-scrambled

f
and the metric
d
(and resp. for some
\varepsilon>0
), if for every pair of distinct points

with
x,y\in S

(x,y)
is a LY pair (resp. LY
\varepsilon
-pair) for the map
f
and the metric

(and resp. for
\varepsilon>0
).

–

A dynamical system

is said to be (resp.

]

]

\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,n])}{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#(A\cap[1,n])}{n}.

Lemma 2.1

Let

(b)

In particular, we have that

Proof.

Let us start noticing that for each
\delta>0
and each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\delta\cdot\frac{1}{n}\#\{1\leq j\leq n\ ;\ d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\geq\delta\}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y)).

(2.1)

Moreover, using the bound
r>0
, for each
\delta>0
and each
n\in\mathbb{N}
it can be easily checked that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\leq\delta+r\cdot\frac{1}{n}\#\{1\leq j\leq n\ ;\ d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\geq\delta\}.

(2.2)

From now on we only have to use that
\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=1-\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus A)
for every subset
A\subset\mathbb{N}
. Actually:

(a)

If
(x,y)
is a MLY
\varepsilon
-pair, taking superior limits in (
2.2
) for
\delta=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r+1}
implies
\Phi_{(x,y)}(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r+1})\leq 1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{r+1}
, while taking inferior limits in (
2.1
) for each
\delta>0
shows that
\Phi^{*}_{(x,y)}(\delta)=1
for all
\delta>0
.

(b)

If
(x,y)
is a D2
\varepsilon
-pair, taking superior limits in (
2.1
) for
\delta=\varepsilon
implies that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\geq\varepsilon(1-\Phi_{(x,y)}(\varepsilon))\geq\varepsilon^{2},

while taking inferior limits in (
2.2
) shows that
0\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\leq\delta
for each positive
\delta>0
, and hence that this inferior limit is equal to
0
.∎

By Lemma
2.1
, the notions of U-MLYC and U-DC2 are equivalent for every dynamical system fulfilling the suitable boundedness conditions. This fact will be repeatedly used in Section
3
.

2.2
The hyperspaces of compact and fuzzy sets

Given a metric space
(X,d)
we will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{d}(x,\varepsilon)\subset X
the open
d
-ball centred at
x\in X
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, we will consider the spaces
\mathcal{C}(X):=\left\{C\subset X\ ;\ C\text{ is a non-empty closed set}\right\}
and
\mathcal{K}(X):=\left\{K\subset X\ ;\ K\text{ is a non-empty compact set}\right\}
. Given two sets
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
, the value

\textstyle d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2}):=\max\left\{\sup_{x_{1}\in C_{1}}\inf_{x_{2}\in C_{2}}d(x_{1},x_{2}),\sup_{x_{2}\in C_{2}}\inf_{x_{1}\in C_{1}}d(x_{2},x_{1})\right\}

is the
Hausdorff distance
between
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. In
\mathcal{K}(X)
, the map
d_{H}:\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
defined as above is a metric, called the
Hausdorff metric
, and every continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
induces a
d_{H}
-continuos map
\overline{f}:\mathcal{K}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(X)
, defined as
\overline{f}(K):=f(K)=\{f(x)\ ;\ x\in K\}
for each
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. We will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{H}(K,\varepsilon)\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
the open
d_{H}
-ball centred at
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
. Given
Y\subset X
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
we write
Y+\varepsilon:=\{x\in X\ ;\ d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon\text{ for some }y\in Y\}
. The next fact is then well-known, and we refer the reader to
[
18
]
for more on
\mathcal{C}(X)
and
\mathcal{K}(X)
:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and let
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}\in\mathcal{C}(X)
. Then:

(a)

We have that
d_{H}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq\varepsilon
if and only if
C_{1}\subset C_{2}+\varepsilon
and
C_{2}\subset C_{1}+\varepsilon
.

(b)

We always have that
d_{H}(C_{1}\cup C_{2},C_{3}\cup C_{4})\leq\max\{d_{H}(C_{1},C_{3}),d_{H}(C_{2},C_{4})\}
.

A
fuzzy set
on the metric space
(X,d)
will be a function
u:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
, where the value
u(x)\in\mathbb{I}
denotes the degree of membership of
x
in
u
. For such a set
u
, its
\alpha
-level
is the set

u_{\alpha}:=\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\}\ \text{ for each }\alpha\in\ ]0,1]\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{0}:=\overline{\{x\in X\ ;\ u(x)>0\}}.

The symbol
\mathcal{F}(X)
will stand for the space of normal fuzzy sets of
(X,d)
, i.e. that formed by the fuzzy sets
u
that are upper-semicontinuous functions and such that
u_{0}
is compact and
u_{1}
is non-empty. For each
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
, its characteristic function
\chi_{K}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}(X)
. The piecewise-constant function
u:=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}\alpha_{l}\chi_{K_{l}}
also belongs to
\mathcal{F}(X)
whenever
0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
and we choose
K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Moreover, in
[
21
,
22
,
34
]
it was proved (or used) the next:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Given any normal fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exist numbers
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
fulfilling that
d_{H}(u_{0},u_{\alpha_{1}})<\varepsilon
and that
d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{l+1}})<\varepsilon
for every level
\alpha\in\ ]\alpha_{l},\alpha_{l+1}]
with
0\leq l\leq N-1
.

Given a dynamical system
(X,f)
, it follows from
[
21
, Propositions 3.1 and 4.9]
that there exists a well-defined map
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, called the
Zadeh extension
of
f
, which maps each normal fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
to the normal fuzzy set
\hat{f}(u):X\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
with

\left[\hat{f}(u)\right](x):=\sup\{u(y)\ ;\ y\in f^{-1}(\{x\})\},\quad\text{ if }f^{-1}(\{x\})\neq\varnothing,

and
\left[\hat{f}(u)\right](x):=0
, if
f^{-1}(\{x\})=\varnothing
. Moreover, in
[
21
,
22
,
35
]
it was proved (or used) the next:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
,
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Then:

(a)

\left[\hat{f}(u)\right]_{\alpha}=f(u_{\alpha})=\overline{f}(u_{\alpha})
, i.e. the
\alpha
-level of the
\hat{f}
-image coincides with the
\overline{f}
-image of the
\alpha
-level;

(b)

\left(\hat{f}\right)^{n}=\widehat{f^{n}}
, i.e. the composition of
\hat{f}
with itself
n
times coincides with the fuzzification of
f^{n}
;

(c)

\hat{f}\left(\chi_{K}\right)=\chi_{f(K)}=\chi_{\overline{f}(K)}
, i.e. the fuzzification
\hat{f}
is an extension of the hyperextension
\overline{f}
.

To talk about the continuity of the Zadeh extension
\hat{f}:\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}(X)
, we will endow the space of normal fuzzy sets
\mathcal{F}(X)
with four different metrics. In particular, given a metric space
(X,d)
, the so-called
supremum metric

d_{\infty}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
is defined as

d_{\infty}(u,v):=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}d_{H}(u_{\alpha},v_{\alpha})\quad\text{ for each pair }u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X),

where
d_{H}
is the Hausdorff metric on
\mathcal{K}(X)
. The
Skorokhod metric

d_{0}:\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
is defined for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as

d_{0}(u,v):=\inf\left\{\varepsilon>0\ ;\ \text{there is }\xi\in\mathcal{T}\text{ such that }\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}|\xi(\alpha)-\alpha|\leq\varepsilon\text{ and }d_{\infty}(u,\xi\circ v)\leq\varepsilon\right\},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the set of strictly increasing homeomorphisms of the unit interval
\xi:\mathbb{I}\longrightarrow\mathbb{I}
. To talk about the
endograph
and
sendograph metrics
we need the auxiliary metric
\overline{d}
on the product
X\times\mathbb{I}
with

\overline{d}\left((x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\right):=\max\left\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\beta|\right\}\quad\text{ for each pair }(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in X\times\mathbb{I}.

Given now any fuzzy set
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, the
endograph of
u
is defined as the set

\operatorname{end}(u):=\left\{(x,\alpha)\in X\times\mathbb{I}\ ;\ u(x)\geq\alpha\right\},

and the
sendograph of
u
is defined as
\operatorname{send}(u):=\operatorname{end}(u)\cap(u_{0}\times\mathbb{I})
. Then, for each pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
the
sendograph metric

d_{S}(u,v)
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff metric
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{send}(u),\operatorname{send}(v))
on
\mathcal{K}(X\times\mathbb{I})
, and the
endograph metric

d_{E}(u,v)
on
\mathcal{F}(X)
is the Hausdorff distance
\overline{d}_{H}(\operatorname{end}(u),\operatorname{end}(u))
on
\mathcal{C}(X\times\mathbb{I})
. Although
d_{E}
is defined as a distance of closed but not necessarily compact sets on
X\times\mathbb{I}
, the fact that the supports of the fuzzy sets
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
are compact implies that
d_{E}(u,v)
is well-defined.

Following
[
32
,
33
]
we will denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(u,\varepsilon)
,
\mathcal{B}_{0}(u,\varepsilon)
,
\mathcal{B}_{S}(u,\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{B}_{E}(u,\varepsilon)
the open balls centred at
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and of radius
\varepsilon>0
for each of the metrics
d_{\infty}
,
d_{0}
,
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
. Moreover, for each metric
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
we will denote the metric space
(\mathcal{F}(X),\rho)
by
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
,
\mathcal{F}_{S}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{E}(X)
respectively. These metrics come from the general theory of Spaces of Fuzzy Sets and each of them has its own role and importance (see
[
32
, Section 2]
). In this paper we will need some of the main relations among these metrics (see
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
32
]
and the references therein for more details):

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
,
K,L\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
x\in X
. Then:

(a)

d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
and
d_{E}(u,v)\leq 1
.

(b)

d_{S}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{0}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{H}(\{x\},u_{0})=\max\{d(x,y)\ ;\ y\in u_{0}\}
.

(c)

d_{0}(\chi_{K},u)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{K},u)=\max\{d_{H}(K,u_{0}),d_{H}(K,u_{1})\}
.

(d)

d_{E}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=\min\{d_{H}(K,L),1\}
while
d_{S}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{0}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{\infty}(\chi_{K},\chi_{L})=d_{H}(K,L)
.

(e)

d_{H}(u_{0},v_{0})\leq d_{S}(u,v)
and also
\max\{d_{H}(u_{0},v_{0}),d_{H}(u_{1},v_{1})\}\leq d_{0}(u,v)
.

The next lemma was the key fact used in
[
32
]
to get most of the results obtained there, but we will also use it here in Section
4
:

Lemma 2.6

(
[
32
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, and assume that the sets
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfill that
\delta:=d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)<\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
for every
\alpha\in\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
.

For the Li-Yorke chaotic-type properties we need to bound from above and from below the value of the underlying metric. Thus, the following extension of Lemma
2.6
will be very useful:

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space,
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
,
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, and let
\delta,\varepsilon>0
. Hence:

(a)

If
\delta=d_{S}(\chi_{K},u)<1
, then we have that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
for all
\alpha\in[0,1-\delta]
.

(b)

If
\varepsilon<\min\{d_{S}(\chi_{K},u),1\}
, then we have that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})>\varepsilon
for some
\alpha\in[0,1-\varepsilon]
.

(c)

If
\varepsilon<\min\{d_{E}(\chi_{K},u),\tfrac{1}{2}\}
, then we have that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})>\varepsilon
for some
\alpha\in\ ]\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Statement (a) is a “sendograph version” of Lemma
2.6
. Moreover, statements (c) and (b) can be interpreted, respectively, as a reciprocal for statement (a) and Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

(a): Given any
\alpha\in[0,1-\delta]
we must show that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\delta
or, equivalently, that we have the inclusions
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
and
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\delta
(see Proposition
2.2
).

To check
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
, since
d_{S}(\chi_{K},u)=\delta
we have that
\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{send}(u)+\delta
, so that given
x\in K
we have that
(x,1)\in\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})
and that there exists
(y,\beta)\in\operatorname{send}(u)
for which

\overline{d}\left((x,1),(y,\beta)\right)=\max\{d(x,y),|1-\beta|\}\leq\delta.

This implies that
u(y)\geq\beta\geq 1-\delta\geq\alpha
so that
y\in u_{\alpha}
. We deduce that
x\in\{y\}+\delta\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
, and hence that
K\subset u_{\alpha}+\delta
. To check that
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\delta
we only need to use that
d_{H}(v_{0},w_{0})\leq d_{S}(v,w)
for every pair of fuzzy sets
v,w\in\mathcal{F}(X)
as stated in part (e) of Proposition
2.5
. This implies that
d_{H}(K,u_{0})\leq d_{S}(\chi_{K},u)=\delta
, and by part (a) of Proposition
2.2
we have that
u_{\alpha}\subset u_{0}\subset K+\delta
.

(b): Assume that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\varepsilon<1
for all
\alpha\in[0,1-\varepsilon]
and let us prove that then
d_{S}(\chi_{K},u)\leq\varepsilon
or, equivalently, that the inclusions
\operatorname{send}(u)\subset\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})+\varepsilon
and
\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{send}(u)+\varepsilon
hold.

The inclusion
\operatorname{send}(u)\subset\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})+\varepsilon
follows from the assumption
d_{H}(K,u_{0})\leq\varepsilon
as it implies that
u_{\alpha}\subset u_{0}\subset K+\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
. To check that
\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{send}(u)+\varepsilon
pick any point
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})
and note that, since
x\in K
and we assumed that
d_{H}(K,u_{1-\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
, then
K\subset u_{1-\varepsilon}+\varepsilon
and there exists a point
y\in u_{1-\varepsilon}
such that
d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon
. As
y\in u_{1-\varepsilon}\subset u_{\beta}
for all
\beta\in[0,1-\varepsilon]
, we have that

(y,\alpha)\in\operatorname{send}(u)\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{d}((x,\alpha),(y,\alpha))=\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\alpha|\}=d(x,y)\leq\varepsilon,

if
\alpha\in[0,1-\varepsilon]
, or we have that

(y,1-\varepsilon)\in\operatorname{send}(u)\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{d}((x,\alpha),(y,1-\varepsilon))=\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-(1-\varepsilon)|\}\leq\varepsilon,

if
\alpha\in[1-\varepsilon,1]
. Thus, we have that
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{send}(u)+\varepsilon
, and hence that
\operatorname{send}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{send}(u)+\varepsilon
.

(c): Assume that
d_{H}(K,u_{\alpha})\leq\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
for all
\alpha\in\ ]\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
and let us prove that then
d_{E}(\chi_{K},u)\leq\varepsilon
or, equivalently, that we have
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})+\varepsilon
and
\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{end}(u)+\varepsilon
.

We check that
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})+\varepsilon
. Indeed, our assumption implies that
u_{\alpha}\subset K+\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in\ ]\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, and then that
u_{\alpha}\subset u_{1-\varepsilon}\subset K+\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\in[1-\varepsilon,1]
. Moreover, for
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u)
with
\alpha\in[0,\varepsilon]
it follows that

(x,0)\in\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{d}((x,\alpha),(x,0))=\max\{d(x,x),|\alpha|\}\leq\varepsilon.

Altogether we get that
\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})+\varepsilon
. To prove that
\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})\subset\operatorname{end}(u)+\varepsilon
one can argue similarly to the respective part of statement (b), where only
d_{H}(K,u_{1-\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
is needed. In fact, the only difference with the proof of statement (b) is the case in which we pick
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\chi_{K})
for the value
\alpha=0
, but in this situation we trivially have that
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(u)\subset\operatorname{end}(u)+\varepsilon
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The statement and proof of Theorem
3.2
admit several comments:

(1)

The idea behind Theorem
3.2
is showing how easily the Li-Yorke chaotic-type notions transfer from the systems
(X,f)
or
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
to
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
. See Subsection
3.2
for some applications.

(2)

For
d_{\infty}
,
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
we could have considered the set
\hat{S}
as defined in (
3.1
) together with the two extra sets
u^{1}:=\chi_{L}
and
u^{0}:=\chi_{K}
, obtaining the same estimates (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). However, for
d_{0}
, although adding these two fuzzy sets to
\hat{S}
does not change its scrambled behaviour and the result remains valid in this case, the estimates obtained in (
3.5
) for such a metric fail.

(3)

The U-LYC and U-DC2 parts of statement (b) in Theorem
3.2
together with the LYC and DC2 parts of statement (c) in Theorem
3.2
also follow when the sets of the pair
(K,L)
are not ordered by inclusion. Actually, if we consider any arbitrary but fixed LY or D2 pair
(K,L)\in\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)
for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
, then one can check that at least one of the pairs
(K,K\cup L)
or
(K\cup L,L)
is again a LY or a D2 pair for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
, respectively, by using the readily verified formula

d_{H}(K_{1},K_{2})=\max\{d_{H}(K_{1}\cup K_{2},K_{2}),d_{H}(K_{1},K_{1}\cup K_{2})\}\quad\text{ for each pair }K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{K}(X).

(3.7)

Let us explain the argument:

–

If
(K,L)
is a LY
\varepsilon
-pair for some
\varepsilon>0
, then by (
3.7
) there must exist an increasing sequence of positive integers
(n_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
fulfilling either that

d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K\cup L))=d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K)\cup\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L))=d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L))\geq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, or that

d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K\cup L),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L))=d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K)\cup\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L))=d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(K),\overline{f}^{n_{k}}(L))\geq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In both cases, note that by (
3.7
) we also have the inequalities

\max\{d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(K\cup L)),d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K\cup L),\overline{f}^{j}(L))\}\leq d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(L))\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus, either
(K,K\cup L)
or
(K\cup L,L)
is a LY
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
-pair for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
. From there, Theorem
3.2
shows that there exists an uncountable subset of
\mathcal{F}(X)
that is LY
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
-scrambled for
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
but also LY scrambled for
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
.

–

If
(K,L)
is a D2
\varepsilon
-pair for some
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, then by (
3.7
) and the well-known properties of the lower and upper densities we have that

1-\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus(A\cap B))=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A\cap B)=\Phi_{(K,L)}(\varepsilon)\leq 1-\varepsilon,

where
\Phi_{(K,L)}(\varepsilon)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(L))<\varepsilon\})
and we are taking
A,B\subset\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(K)\cup\overline{f}^{j}(L))<\varepsilon\}=\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(K\cup L))<\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K)\cup\overline{f}^{j}(L),\overline{f}^{j}(L))<\varepsilon\}=\{j\in\mathbb{N}\ ;\ d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K\cup L),\overline{f}^{j}(L))<\varepsilon\}.

Using again the properties of the lower and upper densities it follows that

\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus A)+\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus B)\geq\overline{\operatorname{dens}}\left((\mathbb{N}\setminus A)\cup(\mathbb{N}\setminus B)\right)=\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus(A\cap B))\geq\varepsilon>0,

so that there exists some
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon\leq 1
fulfilling that

\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus A)\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}\quad\text{ or that }\quad\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(\mathbb{N}\setminus B)\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}.

(3.8)

In particular, for such
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 1
we have that

\Phi_{(K,K\cup L)}(\varepsilon)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)\leq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}\quad\text{ or that }\quad\Phi_{(K\cup L,L)}(\varepsilon)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(B)\leq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime},

which easily implies that

\Phi_{(K,K\cup L)}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\leq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}\quad\text{ or that }\quad\Phi_{(K\cup L,L)}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\leq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}.

In both cases, given any
\delta>0
it follows from (
3.7
) that

1=\Phi^{*}_{(K,L)}(\delta)=\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A\cap B)\leq\min\{\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A),\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(B)\}=\min\{\Phi^{*}_{(K,K\cup L)},\Phi^{*}_{(K\cup L,L)}\}\leq 1.

Thus, either
(K,K\cup L)
or
(K\cup L,L)
is a D2
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-pair for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
. From there, Theorem
3.2
shows that there exists an uncountable subset of
\mathcal{F}(X)
that is D2
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-scrambled for
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
but also D2 scrambled for
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
.

(4)

The previous reasoning does not seem to work for MLYC, DC1,
DC
1
\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{2}}
,
DC
2
\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{2}}
, DC3, and their uniform variants. In fact, for mean Li-Yorke chaos and these types of distributional chaos it is not enough to have infinitely many times the sets
\hat{f}^{j}(K)
and
\hat{f}^{j}(K\cup L)
or
\hat{f}^{j}(K\cup L)
and
\hat{f}^{j}(L)
far apart, nor is it enough to obtain some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
satisfying the condition in (
3.8
); one also needs to control the frequency with which these situations occur. Actually, for MLYC one has to control the means of such distances, for DC1 one needs to achieve
\varepsilon^{\prime}=1
in (
3.8
), for
DC
1
\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{2}}
one has to ensure that
\Phi_{(K,K\cup L)}(\delta)
or
\Phi_{(K\cup L,L)}(\delta)
converge to
0
as
\delta\to 0
, and for
DC
2
\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{2}}
and DC3 one has to control how
\Phi_{(K,K\cup L)}
or
\Phi_{(K\cup L,L)}
evolve compared to
\Phi^{*}_{(K,K\cup L)}
or
\Phi^{*}_{(K\cup L,L)}
respectively.

(5)

In Theorem
3.2
we have not considered DC3 for
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
. This will not be a problem in our counterexamples because
DC
2
\tfrac{\text{1}}{\text{2}}
(and hence DC1) implies DC3.

3.2
Some counterexamples

In this subsection we present three counterexamples. With Example

are false. This example provides a strong positive solution to Question

. In their turn, with Examples

and

can not be weakened.

Example 3.4
.

There exists a continuous function
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
such that:

(1)

Every
S\subset X
that is LY or D3 scrambled for
f
and
d
has at most two points.

(2)

Every
\overline{S}\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
that is LY or D3 scrambled for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
is finite.

(3)

The dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is U-DC1 and U-MLYC.

(4)

The systems
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
are DC1 and MLYC.

Proof.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that
\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=0
but that
\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=1
. These sets are well-known to exist and not difficult to be constructed. Consider
X:=\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\{0,1\}
and let
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
be defined for each pair
(n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})\in X
as

d((n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }(n_{1},n_{2})=(m_{1},m_{2}),\\[2.0pt]
|n_{1}-m_{1}|&\text{ if }n_{1}\neq m_{1},\\[2.0pt]
1&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2},\\[2.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{n_{1}}&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2}.\end{cases}

We leave to the reader checking that
(X,d)
is a metric space and to verify that the topology generated by
d
is discrete. Hence, every map on
(X,d)
is continuous and
\mathcal{K}(X)
is formed by the finite subsets of
X
. Consider now the map
f:X\longrightarrow X
defined as

f((n_{1},n_{2})):=(n_{1}+1,n_{2})\quad\text{ for each }(n_{1},n_{2})\in X.

To check that the dynamical system
(X,f)
fulfills the required conditions, let us prove that
((0,0),(0,1))
is a D1
\varepsilon
-pair for all
0<\varepsilon<1
. In fact, applying
j
-times the map
f
we have that

d(f^{j}((0,0)),f^{j}((0,1)))=d((j,0),(j,1))=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A,\\[2.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{j}&\text{ if }j\in A.\end{cases}

An easy computation and the properties of the lower density imply, for each
0<\varepsilon<1
, that

\Phi_{((0,0),(0,1))}(\varepsilon)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}\left(A\ \cap\ ]\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon},\infty[\right)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=0.

Moreover, the properties of the upper density implies, for all
\delta>0
, that

\Phi^{*}_{((0,0),(0,1))}(\delta)=\overline{\operatorname{dens}}\left(A\ \cap\ ]\tfrac{1}{\delta},\infty[\right)=\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=1.

Since
((0,0),(0,1))
is also a D2
\varepsilon
-pair for all
0<\varepsilon<1
, and since
d(f^{j}((0,0)),f^{j}((0,1)))\leq 1
for every integer
j\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows from Lemma
2.1
that
((0,0),(0,1))
is a MLY
\varepsilon
-pair for all
0<\varepsilon<1
. Using now statements (a) and (b) of Theorem
3.2
we have that
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is U-DC1 and U-MLYC, and using statements (a), (c) and (d) of Theorem
3.2
it follows that
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
are DC1 and MLYC. We have checked properties (3) and (4).

To check (1) and (2) we will use the projections
P_{1}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
P_{2}:X\longrightarrow[0,1]
on the first and second coordinates, defined as

P_{1}((n_{1},n_{2})):=n_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad P_{2}((n_{1},n_{2})):=n_{2}\quad\text{ for each point }(n_{1},n_{2})\in X.

(3.9)

Note that
P_{1}(f^{j}(x))=P_{1}(x)+j
and that
P_{2}(f^{j}(x))=P_{2}(x)
for all
x\in X
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In particular, if given
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we consider the set
P_{1}(K)+j=\{n+j\ ;\ n\in P_{1}(x)\text{ for some }x\in K\}
, the equality
P_{1}(f^{j}(x))=P_{1}(x)+j
implies that

P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(K))=P_{1}(K)+j\quad\text{ for all }K\in\mathcal{K}(X)\text{ and }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.10)

In addition, for every pair of distinct points
x\neq y
in
X
and each
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have that

d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))=\begin{cases}|P_{1}(x)-P_{1}(y)|&\text{ if }P_{1}(x)\neq P_{1}(y),\\[2.0pt]
1&\text{ if }P_{1}(x)=P_{1}(y)\text{ and }P_{1}(x)+j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A,\\[2.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{P_{1}(x)+j}&\text{ if }P_{1}(x)=P_{1}(y)\text{ and }P_{1}(x)+j\in A,\end{cases}

(3.11)

where we used that
|P_{1}(x)-P_{1}(y)|=|(P_{1}(x)+j)-(P_{1}(y)+j)|
. We are ready to check (1) and (2):

(1)

We claim that, if
(x,y)\in X\times X
is a LY or D3 pair for
f
and
d
, then
P_{1}(x)=P_{1}(y)
. In fact, if we had that
P_{1}(x)\neq P_{1}(y)
, using (
3.11
) we would have that

d(x,y)=|P_{1}(x)-P_{2}(y)|=d(f^{j}(x),f^{j}(y))\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

This condition contradicts that
(x,y)
is a LY or a D3 pair. As a consequence of the claim, the only possible LY and D3 pairs for
f
and
d
are those of the type
((n,0),(n,1))\in X\times X
. It follows that every LY or D3 scrambled set for the map
f
and the metric
d
has at most two points.

(2)

We claim that, if
(K,L)\in\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)
is a LY or D3 pair for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
, then
P_{1}(K)=P_{1}(L)
. In fact, if
P_{1}(K)\neq P_{1}(L)
, using (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) together with the fact that
|P_{1}(x)-P_{1}(y)|\geq 1
when
P_{1}(x)\neq P_{1}(y)
, it is not hard to check that

d_{H}(K,L)=\max\left\{|P_{1}(x)-P_{1}(y)|\ ;\ (x,y)\in K\times L\right\}=d_{H}(\overline{f}^{j}(K),\overline{f}^{j}(L))\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

This condition contradicts that
(K,L)
is a LY or a D3 pair. As a consequence of the claim, if a subset
\overline{S}\subset\mathcal{K}(X)
is LY or D3 scrambled for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
, and if we fix any
K\in\overline{S}
, then the only possible elements of
\overline{S}
are those
L\in\mathcal{K}(X)
fulfilling that
P_{1}(K)=P_{1}(L)
and hence that

L\subset\left\{(n,m)\in X\ ;\ n\in P_{1}(K)\text{ and }m\in\{0,1\}\right\}.

Since
K
is a finite set, we have that
P_{1}(K)
is finite and hence
\overline{S}
is also finite. Actually, one can even check that if
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is the cardinal of
P_{1}(K)
then the cardinal of
\overline{S}
is at most

. We deduce that every LY or D3 scrambled set for
\overline{f}
and
d_{H}
is finite, as we had to check.∎

Example 3.5
.

There exists a continuous function
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
such that:

(1)

The systems
(X,f)
,
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
are U-MLYC.

(2)

The dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
admits no MLY pair.

Proof.

Let
A:=\{2^{k^{2}}\ ;\ k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
, consider
X:=\mathbb{N}_{0}\times[0,1]
, and let
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
be defined for each pair
(n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})\in X
as

d((n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }(n_{1},n_{2})=(m_{1},m_{2}),\\[2.0pt]
|2^{n_{1}}-2^{m_{1}}|&\text{ if }n_{1}\neq m_{1},\\[2.0pt]
\tfrac{1}{2^{n_{1}}}&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2},\\[2.0pt]
n_{1}&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2}.\end{cases}

We leave to the reader checking that
(X,d)
is a metric space. The topology generated by
d
is discrete so that every map on
(X,d)
is continuous and
\mathcal{K}(X)
is formed by the finite subsets of
X
. Consider

f((n_{1},n_{2})):=(n_{1}+1,n_{2})\quad\text{ for each }(n_{1},n_{2})\in X.

We claim that the uncountable set
S:=\{0\}\times[0,1]
is MLY
1
-scrambled. Indeed, note that

d(f^{j}((0,n_{2})),f^{j}((0,m_{2})))=d((j,n_{2}),(j,m_{2}))=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2^{j}}&\text{ if }j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A,\\[2.0pt]
j&\text{ if }j\in A,\end{cases}

for every pair of distinct points
(0,n_{2})\neq(0,m_{2})
in
S
. It follows that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}((0,n_{2})),f^{j}((0,m_{2})))\geq\limsup_{A\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d((j,n_{2}),(j,m_{2}))

\displaystyle=\limsup_{A\ni n\to\infty}\left(\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{d((j,n_{2}),(j,m_{2}))}{n}\right)+1\right)\geq 1.

Moreover, if for each
n\in A
we use the notation
[n]:=\sqrt{\log_{2}(n)}
, we also have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(f^{j}((0,n_{2})),f^{j}((0,m_{2})))\leq\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}d((j,n_{2}),(j,m_{2}))

\displaystyle=\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n(n-1)}\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}^{j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A}\frac{1}{2^{j}}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}^{j\in A}j\right)\leq\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+1}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}^{j\in A}\frac{j}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n-1}^{j\in A}\frac{j}{n}=\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq k<[n]}\frac{2^{k^{2}}}{2^{[n]^{2}}}=\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\sum_{1\leq k<[n]}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{[n]^{2}-k^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{[n]^{2}-([n]-1)^{2}+1}=\liminf_{A\ni n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2[n]}=\lim_{A\ni n\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2[n]}=0.

We have obtained that
(X,f)
is U-MLYC for
d
. Applying now statement (f) of Theorem
3.1
we have that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is U-MLYC for
d_{H}
, and applying statement (g) of Theorem
3.1
we also obtain that the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
are U-MLYC. We have checked (1).

To check (2) we use the projections
P_{1}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
P_{2}:X\longrightarrow[0,1]
defined as in (
3.9
). Since the equalities
P_{1}(f^{j}(x))=P_{1}(x)+j
and
P_{2}(f^{j}(x))=P_{2}(x)
hold for all
x\in X
and all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
as in Example
3.4
, we can also use (
3.10
) here. In addition, since the map
f
is injective we have that

u(x)=[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](f^{j}(x))\quad\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{F}(X)\text{, }x\in X\text{ and }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3.12)

A direct consequence of (
3.12
) is that, given any
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any pair of integers
n,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then

\max\{u(x)\ ;\ x\in P_{1}^{-1}(\{n\})\}=\max\{[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](y)\ ;\ y\in P_{1}^{-1}(\{n+j\})\}.

(3.13)

Note that this value can be computed because the support of
u
, namely
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
, is compact and hence finite. Let now
u\neq v
be two distinct sets in
\mathcal{F}(X)
and let us prove that the pair
(u,v)
can not be a MLY pair for the map
\hat{f}
and the metric
d_{E}
. We distinguish two possible cases:

–

Case 1
:
There exists some level
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
such that
P_{1}(u_{\alpha})\neq P_{1}(v_{\alpha})
. In this case, renaming
u
as
v
and
v
as
u
if necessary, there exists some
n_{1}\in P_{1}(v_{\alpha})\setminus P_{1}(u_{\alpha})
. That is, there exists
z\in X
fulfilling that
P_{1}(z)=n_{1}
and that
v(z)\geq\alpha
, while the value
\beta:=\max\{u(x)\ ;\ x\in P_{1}^{-1}(\{n_{1}\})\}
fulfills that
0\leq\beta<\alpha
. Given any
j\in\mathbb{N}
and any value
0\leq\delta<\alpha-\beta
we are going to check that
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))\not\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))+\delta
. Actually, since
(z,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(v)
we have that the point
(f^{j}(z),\alpha)
belongs to
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))
, and by (
3.13
) we have that
\beta=\max\{[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](y)\ ;\ y\in P_{1}^{-1}(\{n_{1}+j\})\}
. Since

\inf_{(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))}\overline{d}((f^{j}(z),\alpha),(y,\gamma))=\inf_{(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))}\max\{d(f^{j}(z),y),|\alpha-\gamma|\},

and since for each
(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
we have that

\displaystyle\max\{d(f^{j}(z),y),|\alpha-\gamma|\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha&\text{ if }\gamma=0\text{ and }y\in X,\\[5.0pt]
d(f^{j}(z),y)&\text{ if }0<\gamma\leq 1\text{ and }P_{1}(y)\neq n_{1}+j,\\[5.0pt]
|\alpha-\beta|&\text{ if }0<\gamma\leq\beta\text{ and }P_{1}(y)=n_{1}+j,\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha&\text{ if }\gamma=0\text{ and }y\in X,\\[5.0pt]
|2^{n_{1}+j}-2^{P_{1}(y)}|&\text{ if }0<\gamma\leq 1\text{ and }P_{1}(y)\neq n_{1}+j,\\[5.0pt]
\alpha-\beta&\text{ if }0<\gamma\leq\beta\text{ and }P_{1}(y)=n_{1}+j,\end{array}\right\}>\delta,

the arbitrariness of “
j
” and “
\delta
” together with statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
show that

d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))\geq\alpha-\beta>0\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

It trivially follows that
(u,v)
can not be a MLY pair, not even a LY pair, for
\hat{f}
and
d_{E}
.

–

Case 2
:
We have that
P_{1}(u_{\alpha})=P_{1}(v_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
. In this case, given any
j\in\mathbb{N}
we claim that

\text{if }P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A=\varnothing,\quad\text{ then }\quad d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\min(P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0})))}.

(3.14)

Actually, assume that
P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A=\varnothing
and let
(x,\alpha)
be any arbitrary but fixed point of the endograph
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
fulfilling that
\alpha>0
. Since
P_{1}(u_{\alpha})=P_{1}(v_{\alpha})
, using (
3.10
) it follows that

P_{1}([\hat{f}^{j}(u)]_{\alpha})=P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{\alpha}))=P_{1}(u_{\alpha})+j=P_{1}(v_{\alpha})+j=P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(v_{\alpha}))=P_{1}([\hat{f}^{j}(v)]_{\alpha}).

In particular, there exists some
y\in X
with
P_{1}(x)=P_{1}(y)
and such that
(y,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))
. By assumption we have that
P_{1}(x)=P_{1}(y)\in P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\subset\mathbb{N}\setminus A
, so that

\overline{d}((x,\alpha),(y,\alpha))=\max\{d(x,y),|\alpha-\alpha|\}=d(x,y)=\frac{1}{2^{P_{1}(x)}}.

This implies, by the arbitrariness of
(x,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
with
\alpha>0
, that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\min(P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0})))}.

Arguing symmetrically (i.e. replacing
u
with
v
and
v
with
u
), and by statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
, we obtain (
3.14
). Now, note that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
one has the inequality

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}^{P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A=\varnothing}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\min(P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0})))}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}^{P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A\neq\varnothing}1\right),

(3.15)

where we used (
3.14
) and part (a) of Proposition
2.5
. Looking at the first part of the last expression in (
3.15
), and since
\min(P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))=\min(P_{1}(u_{0})+j)\geq j
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
by (
3.10
), we have that

0\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}^{P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A=\varnothing}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\min(P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0})))}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}=0.

(3.16)

For the second part of the last expression in (
3.15
) note that, since
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
is a finite set, there exists some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
P_{1}(u_{0})\subset[0,N]
. Thus, using (
3.10
) again we have that

P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))=P_{1}(u_{0})+j\subset[0,N]+j=[j,j+N]\quad\text{ for each }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Fix some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(2^{k^{2}}+1)+N<2^{(k+1)^{2}}
and, for each
n>2^{k^{2}}
, let us denote by
\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
the unique integer fulfilling that
n\in[2^{(k+\ell_{n}-1)^{2}}+1,2^{(k+\ell_{n})^{2}}]
. We claim that

\sum_{2^{k^{2}}<j\leq n}^{P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A\neq\varnothing}1\ \leq\sum_{2^{k^{2}}<j\leq n}^{[j,j+N]\cap A\neq\varnothing}1\ =\ \#\left\{2^{k^{2}}<j\leq n\ ;\ [j,j+N]\cap A\neq\varnothing\right\}\ =\ \ell_{n}\cdot(N+1).

(3.17)

Indeed, note that given
n\in[2^{(k+\ell_{n}-1)^{2}}+1,2^{(k+\ell_{n})^{2}}]
and
2^{k^{2}}<j\leq n
one has that

[j,j+N]\cap A\neq\varnothing\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad 2^{(k+m)^{2}}-N\leq j\leq 2^{(k+m)^{2}}\text{ for some }1\leq m\leq\ell_{n}.

Thus, using (
3.17
) we obtain that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}^{P_{1}(\overline{f}^{j}(u_{0}))\cap A\neq\varnothing}1\ \leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(2^{k^{2}}+\sum_{2^{k^{2}}<j\leq n}^{[j,j+N]\cap A\neq\varnothing}1\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left(2^{k^{2}}+\ell_{n}\cdot(N+1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ell_{n}\cdot(N+1)+2^{k^{2}}}{2^{(k+\ell_{n}-1)^{2}}}=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\ell\cdot(N+1)+2^{k^{2}}}{2^{(k+\ell-1)^{2}}}\leq\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\ell\cdot(N+1)+2^{k^{2}}}{2^{\ell^{2}}}=0.

This computation together with (
3.16
) show that, taking limits in (
3.15
), we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))=0.

We deduce that
(u,v)
can not be a MLY pair for
\hat{f}
and
d_{E}
.∎

The boundedness assumption in statement (j) of Theorem
3.1
can not be weakened:

Example 3.6
.

There exists a continuous function
f:X\longrightarrow X
on a metric space
(X,d)
such that:

(1)

The systems
(X,f)
,
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
are U-DC3.

(2)

The dynamical system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
admits no D3 pair.

Proof.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)<\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)
. As in Example
3.5
, consider
X:=\mathbb{N}_{0}\times[0,1]
, but this time let
d:X\times X\longrightarrow[0,+\infty[
be defined for each pair
(n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})\in X
as

d((n_{1},n_{2}),(m_{1},m_{2})):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }(n_{1},n_{2})=(m_{1},m_{2}),\\[2.0pt]
|n_{1}-m_{1}|&\text{ if }n_{1}\neq m_{1},\\[2.0pt]
2&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2},\\[2.0pt]
1&\text{ if }n_{1}=m_{1}\in A\text{ and }n_{2}\neq m_{2}.\end{cases}

We leave to the reader checking that
(X,d)
is a metric space. The topology generated by
d
is discrete so that every map on
(X,d)
is continuous and
\mathcal{K}(X)
is formed by the finite subsets of
X
. Consider

f((n_{1},n_{2})):=(n_{1}+1,n_{2})\quad\text{ for each }(n_{1},n_{2})\in X.

We claim that the uncountable set
S:=\{0\}\times[0,1]
is D3
(1,2)
-scrambled for
f
and
d
. Actually, given any pair of distinct points
(0,n_{2})\neq(0,m_{2})
in
S
, applying
j
-times the map
f
we have that

d(f^{j}((0,n_{2})),f^{j}((0,m_{2})))=d((j,n_{2}),(j,m_{2}))=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus A,\\[2.0pt]
1&\text{ if }j\in A.\end{cases}

Thus,
((0,n_{2}),(0,m_{2}))
is a D3
(1,2)
-pair for
f
and
d
because given any
1<\delta<2
we have that

\Phi_{((0,n_{2}),(0,m_{2}))}(\delta)=\underline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)<\overline{\operatorname{dens}}(A)=\Phi^{*}_{(0,n_{2}),(0,m_{2})}.

We have obtained that
(X,f)
is U-DC3 for
d
. Applying now statement (f) of Theorem
3.1
we have that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is U-DC3 for
d_{H}
, and applying statement (g) of Theorem
3.1
we also obtain that the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
are U-DC3. We have checked (1).

To check (2) we will prove that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is an isometry. We start noticing that, as in Example
3.5
, since the map
f
is injective we can also use (
3.12
) here. Let
u\neq v
be two distinct normal fuzzy sets in
\mathcal{F}(X)
. Since their supports
u_{0},v_{0}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
are compact and hence finite, we can compute the value
\beta:=\max\left\{|u(x)-v(x)|\ ;\ x\in X\right\}
, which is a number fulfilling that
0<\beta<1
. Renaming
u
as
v
and
v
as
u
if necessary, there exists some
z\in X
such that
u(z)-v(z)=\beta
, and by (
3.12
) we have that

\beta=\max\left\{\left|[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](f^{j}(x))-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](f^{j}(x))\right|\ ;\ x\in X\right\}\quad\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

To complete the example it is enough to check the following two inequalities:

–

Inequality 1
:
We have that
d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))\geq\beta
for
j\in\{0,1\}
. Fix
j\in\{0,1\}
and consider any
0\leq\delta<\beta
. By (
3.12
) we have that
(f^{j}(z),u(z))\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
and that
[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](f^{j}(z))=v(z)
. Thus,

\inf_{(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))}\overline{d}((f^{j}(z),u(z)),(y,\gamma))=\inf_{(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))}\max\{d(f^{j}(z),y),|u(z)-\gamma|\},

and for each
(y,\gamma)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))
we have that

\displaystyle\max\{d(f^{j}(z),y),|u(z)-\gamma|\}

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d(f^{j}(z),y)\geq 1&\text{ if }f^{j}(z)\neq y,\\[5.0pt]
|u(z)-v(z)|=\beta&\text{ if }f^{j}(z)=y,\end{array}\right\}>\delta.

The arbitrariness of “
\delta
” together with statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
implies
Inequality 1
.

–

Inequality 2
:
We have that
d_{E}(\hat{f}^{j}(u),\hat{f}^{j}(v))\leq\beta
for
j\in\{0,1\}
. Fix
j\in\{0,1\}
, let
(y,\alpha)
be a point in
\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
, and note that
(y,[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y))\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))
. There are two possibilities:

–

Case 1
:
We have that
[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)\geq\alpha
. In this case
(y,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))+\beta
.

–

Case 2
:
We have that
[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)<\alpha
. In this case, since
[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](y)\geq\alpha>0
and
f
is injective, there exists a unique point
y_{j}\in X
fulfilling that
f^{j}(y_{j})=y
. Thus, using (
3.12
) we have that

\displaystyle|\alpha-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)|

\displaystyle=\alpha-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)=\alpha-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](f^{j}(y_{j}))\leq[\hat{f}^{j}(u)](f^{j}(y_{j}))-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](f^{j}(y_{j}))

\displaystyle=u(y_{j})-v(y_{j})\leq\max\left\{|u(x)-v(x)|\ ;\ x\in X\right\}=\beta,

so that
\overline{d}((y,\alpha),(y,[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)))=\max\{d(y,y),|\alpha-[\hat{f}^{j}(v)](y)|\}\leq\beta
and
(y,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))+\beta
.

Since
(y,\alpha)\in\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))
was arbitrary, it follows that

\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(u))\subset\operatorname{end}(\hat{f}^{j}(v))+\beta.

Arguing symmetrically and using statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
we obtain
Inequality 2
.

We have checked that
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is an isometry. Note that an isometry admits no D3 pairs.
∎

4
Interlude: proximality and sensitivity

In Section
3
we have showed, via Example
3.4
, that Question
1.1
admits a positive answer. However, a natural question arises: under which conditions can one guarantee that a Li-Yorke chaotic-type property transfers from
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
? In order to obtain a general result in this direction, and following
[
26
]
, we will decompose Li-Yorke chaos into two fundamental ingredients: proximality and sensitivity. In this paper, this section serves as an
interlude
in which we establish some auxiliary results concerning proximality and sensitivity. These results will later be used to provide a partial negative answer to Question
1.1
, under certain natural completeness assumptions (see Section
5
).

This section is organized as follows. In Subsection
4.1
we discuss proximality, showing that it transfers from
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
, but that strong assumptions on
(X,f)
or on
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
are needed to ensure having a dense proximal relation in
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
; see Theorem
4.1
. In Subsection
4.2
we focus on sensitivity, which was originally considered for
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
in
[
20
]
; see Theorem
4.2
.

Throughout this section we will use of Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
in a crucial way. In addition, given any dynamical system
(X,f)
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
and any positive integer
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote by
f_{(N)}:X^{N}\longrightarrow X^{N}
the
N
-fold direct product
of
f
with itself. That is, the pair
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
will be the dynamical system

f_{(N)}:=\underbrace{f\times\cdots\times f}_{N}:\underbrace{X\times\cdots\times X}_{N}\longrightarrow\underbrace{X\times\cdots\times X}_{N},

where
X^{N}:=X\times\cdots\times X
is the
N
-fold direct product
of
X
, and where the respective
N
-fold direct map
is defined as
f_{(N)}\left((x_{1},...,x_{N})\right):=(f(x_{1}),...,f(x_{N}))
for each
N
-tuple
(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in X^{N}
. In addition, the space
X^{N}
will be endowed with the metric
d_{(N)}:X^{N}\times X^{N}\longrightarrow[0,\infty[
defined as

d_{(N)}\left((x_{1},...,x_{N}),(y_{1},...,y_{N})\right):=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}d(x_{l},y_{l}),

for each pair of
N
-tuples
(x_{1},...,x_{N}),(y_{1},...,y_{N})\in X^{N}
.

4.1
Proximality

Given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
:

–

A pair
(x,y)\in X\times X
is said to be
proximal
, for the map
f
and the metric
d
, if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))=0.

–

We will denote the
proximal relation
, for the map
f
and the metric
d
, by

\operatorname{Prox}(f,d):=\left\{(x,y)\in X\times X\ ;\ (x,y)\text{ is proximal for $f$ and $d$}\right\}.

–

We say that
(X,f)

has a dense proximal relation
, for
d
, if
\operatorname{Prox}(f,d)
is a dense set in
(X^{2},d_{(2)})
.

As far as we know, the property of having a dense proximal relation has not been studied in the context of dynamics on hyperspaces, so that the next result seems to be entirely new in the literature:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

If the system
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
has a dense proximal relation for the metric
d_{(N)}
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then so does
(\mathcal{K}(X)^{N},\overline{f}_{(N)})
for the metric
(d_{H})_{(N)}
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

(b)

If the system
(\mathcal{K}(X)^{N},\overline{f}_{(N)})
has a dense proximal relation for the metric
(d_{H})_{(N)}
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then so do the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
.

(c)

If any of the dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
has a dense proximal relation, then so does the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
for the metric
d_{H}
.

Proof.

(a): We will use the fact:
for any
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
M_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for each
M\geq M_{0}
one can find (possible repeated) points
x_{1},...,x_{M}\in K
for which every finite set of the type
K^{\prime}:=\{x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{M}^{\prime}\}
with
d(x_{l},x_{l}^{\prime})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
1\leq l\leq M
fulfills that
d_{H}(K,K^{\prime})<\varepsilon
. This fact is probably folklore, but the reader can easily check it by using the compactness condition of
K
together with statement (a) of Proposition
2.2
. Fix now
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}),(L_{1},L_{2},...,L_{N})\in\mathcal{K}(X)^{N}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. To conclude we will find
(K_{1}^{\prime},K_{2}^{\prime},...,K_{N}^{\prime})
and
(L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2}^{\prime},...,L_{N}^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{K}(X)^{N}
fulfilling that

\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\{d_{H}(K_{j},K_{j}^{\prime}),d_{H}(L_{j},L_{j}^{\prime})\}<\varepsilon,

(4.1)

and that

\left((K_{1}^{\prime},K_{2}^{\prime},...,K_{N}^{\prime}),(L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2}^{\prime},...,L_{N}^{\prime})\right)\in\operatorname{Prox}(\overline{f}_{(N)},(d_{H})_{(N)}).

(4.2)

Using the previously stated fact, on every
K_{j}
and
L_{j}
, we can find a positive integer
M\in\mathbb{N}
big enough to fulfill that: for each
1\leq j\leq N
there exist (possibly repeated) points
x_{j,1},x_{j,2},...,x_{j,M}\in K_{j}
but also
y_{j,1},y_{j,2},...,y_{j,M}\in L_{j}
for which every pair of finite sets of the type

K_{j}^{\prime}:=\{x_{j,1}^{\prime},x_{j,2}^{\prime},...,x_{j,M}^{\prime}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad L_{j}^{\prime}:=\{y_{j,1}^{\prime},y_{j,2}^{\prime},...,y_{j,M}^{\prime}\},

(4.3)

with
\max\{d(x_{j,l},x_{j,l}^{\prime}),d(y_{j,l},y_{j,l}^{\prime})\}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
for
1\leq l\leq M
, fulfill
\max\{d_{H}(K_{j},K_{j}^{\prime}),d_{H}(L_{j},L_{j}^{\prime})\}<\varepsilon
. Thus, for the
NM
-tuples

\displaystyle x:=(x_{1,1},x_{1,2},...,x_{1,M},x_{2,1},x_{2,2},...,x_{2,M},...,x_{j,1},x_{j,2},...,x_{j,M},...,x_{N,1},x_{N,2},...,x_{N,M})\in X^{NM},

\displaystyle y:=(y_{1,1},y_{1,2},...,y_{1,M},y_{2,1},y_{2,2},...,y_{2,M},...,y_{j,1},y_{j,2},...,y_{j,M},...,y_{N,1},y_{N,2},...,y_{N,M})\in X^{NM},

using that the system
(X^{NM},f_{(NM)})
has a dense proximal relation for
d_{(NM)}
, we can find

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=(x_{1,1}^{\prime},x_{1,2}^{\prime},...,x_{1,M}^{\prime},x_{2,1}^{\prime},x_{2,2}^{\prime},...,x_{2,M}^{\prime},...,x_{j,1}^{\prime},x_{j,2}^{\prime},...,x_{j,M}^{\prime},...,x_{N,1}^{\prime},x_{N,2}^{\prime},...,x_{N,M}^{\prime})\in X^{NM},

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=(y_{1,1}^{\prime},y_{1,2}^{\prime},...,y_{1,M}^{\prime},y_{2,1}^{\prime},y_{2,2}^{\prime},...,y_{2,M}^{\prime},...,y_{j,1}^{\prime},y_{j,2}^{\prime},...,y_{j,M}^{\prime},...,y_{N,1}^{\prime},y_{N,2}^{\prime},...,y_{N,M}^{\prime})\in X^{NM},

fulfilling that
d_{(NM)}(x,x^{\prime})<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
, that
d_{(NM)}(y,y^{\prime})<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
, and that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Prox}(f_{(NM)},d_{(NM)})
. If now we choose the sets
K_{j}^{\prime}
and
L_{j}^{\prime}
as described in (
4.3
), it is routine to check that (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) hold.

(b): Since
d_{E}(u,v)\leq d_{S}(u,v)\leq d_{0}(u,v)\leq d_{\infty}(u,v)
for every pair
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, it is enough proving that
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
has a dense proximal relation. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
, fix
\varepsilon>0
, and apply Lemma
2.3
to find values
0=\alpha_{0}<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<...<\alpha_{N}=1
fulfilling that
\max\{d_{H}(u_{0},u_{\alpha_{1}}),d_{H}(v_{0},v_{\alpha_{1}})\}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
and that
\max\{d_{H}(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{l+1}}),d_{H}(v_{\alpha},v_{\alpha_{l+1}})\}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
\alpha\in\ ]\alpha_{l},\alpha_{l+1}]
and
0\leq l\leq N-1
. Thus, for the
N
-tuples of compact sets
(u_{\alpha_{1}},u_{\alpha_{2}},...,u_{\alpha_{N}}),(v_{\alpha_{1}},v_{\alpha_{2}},...,v_{\alpha_{N}})\in\mathcal{K}(X)^{N}
, using our assumption that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X)^{N},\overline{f}_{(N)})
has a dense proximal relation for
(d_{H})_{(N)}
we can find

(K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}),(L_{1},L_{2},...,L_{N})\in\mathcal{K}(X)^{N},

fulfilling that
\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\{d_{H}(u_{\alpha_{j}},K_{j}),d_{H}(v_{\alpha_{j}},L_{j})\}<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
, and that

\left((K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}),(L_{1},L_{2},...,L_{N})\right)\in\operatorname{Prox}(\overline{f}_{(N)},(d_{H})_{(N)}).

Considering the fuzzy sets

u^{\prime}:=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}\alpha_{l}\chi_{K_{l}}\quad\text{ and }\quad v^{\prime}:=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}\alpha_{l}\chi_{L_{l}},

we can check that
\max\{d_{\infty}(u,u^{\prime}),d_{\infty}(v,v^{\prime})\}<\varepsilon
. Actually, by (b) of Proposition
2.2
we have that

d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha_{l}},u^{\prime}_{\alpha_{l}}\right)=d_{H}\left(\bigcup_{j\geq l}u_{\alpha_{j}},\bigcup_{j\geq l}K_{j}\right)\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq N}\left\{d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha_{j}},K_{j}\right)\right\}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\quad\text{ for all }1\leq l\leq N,

so that given any
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have the bound

\displaystyle d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha},u^{\prime}_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha},u^{\prime}_{\alpha_{1}}\right),&\text{ if }\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{1}]\\[5.0pt]
d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha},u^{\prime}_{\alpha_{l}}\right),&\text{ if }\alpha\in\ ]\alpha_{l},\alpha_{l+1}],1\leq l\leq N-1\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{1}}\right)+d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha_{1}},u_{\alpha_{1}}^{\prime}\right),&\text{ if }\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{1}]\\[5.0pt]
d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha},u_{\alpha_{l+1}}\right)+d_{H}\left(u_{\alpha_{l+1}},u_{\alpha_{l+1}}^{\prime}\right),&\text{ if }\alpha\in]\alpha_{l},\alpha_{l+1}],1\leq l\leq N-1\end{array}\right\}<\varepsilon.

This only shows that
d_{\infty}(u,u^{\prime})<\varepsilon
but, symmetrically, one can also check that
d_{\infty}(v,v^{\prime})<\varepsilon
. We claim that
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Prox}(\hat{f},d_{\infty})
. Indeed, again by statement (b) of Proposition
2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\infty}(\hat{f}^{n}(u^{\prime}),\hat{f}^{n}(v^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{I}}d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n}(u^{\prime}_{\alpha}),\overline{f}^{n}(v^{\prime}_{\alpha}))=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}d_{H}\left(\overline{f}^{n}\left(\bigcup_{j\geq l}K_{j}\right),\overline{f}^{n}\left(\bigcup_{j\geq l}L_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq l\leq N}d_{H}\left(\bigcup_{j\geq l}\overline{f}^{n}(K_{j}),\bigcup_{j\geq l}\overline{f}^{n}(L_{j})\right)\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq N}d_{H}\left(\overline{f}^{n}(K_{j}),\overline{f}^{n}(L_{j})\right).

The last expression equals
(d_{H})_{(N)}\left(\overline{f}_{(N)}^{n}(K_{1},K_{2},...,K_{N}),\overline{f}_{(N)}^{n}(L_{1},L_{2},...,L_{N})\right)
and the result follows.

(c): Fix
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S}\}
and assume that
\operatorname{Prox}(\hat{f},\rho)
is dense in
(\mathcal{F}(X)^{2},\rho_{(2)})
. We must prove that the set
\operatorname{Prox}(\overline{f},d_{H})
is dense in
(\mathcal{K}(X)^{2},(d_{H})_{(2)})
. To do so let
K,L\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary but fixed, and let us find
K^{\prime},L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
fulfilling the properties

\max\{d_{H}(K,K^{\prime}),d_{H}(L,L^{\prime})\}<\varepsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})\text{ is proximal for $\overline{f}$ and $d_{H}$}.

(4.4)

Indeed, by assumption there exists a pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)
fulfilling that

(u,v)\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(\chi_{K},\varepsilon)\times\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(\chi_{L},\varepsilon)\cap\operatorname{Prox}(\hat{f},\rho).

Let
K^{\prime}:=u_{0}
and
L^{\prime}:=v_{0}
. Since
d_{H}(w_{0},w_{0}^{\prime})\leq\min\{d_{S}(w,w^{\prime}),d_{0}(w,w^{\prime}),d_{\infty}(w,w^{\prime})\}\leq\rho(w,w^{\prime})
for every pair of normal fuzzy sets
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(X)
by Proposition
2.5
, we have that

\max\{d_{H}(K,K^{\prime}),d_{H}(L,L^{\prime})\}\leq\max\{\rho(\chi_{K},u),\rho(\chi_{L},v)\}<\varepsilon,

but also that

d_{H}\left(\overline{f}^{n}(K^{\prime}),\overline{f}^{n}(L^{\prime})\right)=d_{H}\left(\overline{f}^{n}(u_{0}),\overline{f}^{n}(v_{0})\right)\leq\rho\left(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(v)\right)\quad\text{ for every }n\in\mathbb{N}.

As
(u,v)\in\operatorname{Prox}(\hat{f},\rho)
, it follows that
(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})\in\operatorname{Prox}(\overline{f},d_{H})
and (
4.4
) is fulfilled.
∎

4.2
Sensitivity and strong sensitivity

Following
[
1
,
20
,
38
,
40
]
, given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a metric space
(X,d)
:

–

We say that the system
(X,f)
is
sensitive
(usually called
sensitive on initial conditions
), for the metric
d
, if there exists some constant
\varepsilon>0
(called
sensitivity constant
) such that for each
x\in X
and
\delta>0
there are
y\in X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that
d(x,y)<\delta
and that
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y))>\varepsilon
.

–

We say that the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
collectively sensitive
, for the metric
d
, if there exists some constant
\varepsilon>0
(called
collective sensitivity constant
) such that given any finite sequence of distinct points
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N}\in X
and any
\delta>0
there are
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N}\in X
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which:

(CS1)

we have that
d(x_{j},y_{j})<\delta
for all
1\leq j\leq N
;

(CS2)

there exists some
j_{0}\in\{1,2,...,N\}
such that either
d(f^{n}(x_{j_{0}}),f^{n}(y_{j}))>\varepsilon
for all
1\leq j\leq N
or
d(f^{n}(x_{j}),f^{n}(y_{j_{0}}))>\varepsilon
for all
1\leq j\leq N
.

–

We say that the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
strongly sensitive
, for the metric
d
, if there exists some constant
\varepsilon>0
(called
strong sensitivity constant
) such that for each
x\in X
and
\delta>0
there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that given any
n\geq n_{0}
we can find
y_{n}\in X
for which
d(x,y_{n})<\delta
and
d(f^{n}(x),f^{n}(y_{n}))>\varepsilon
.

The notion of sensitivity was included in the original definition of Devaney chaos and it is sometimes referred to as the “butterfly effect”, meaning that small changes in initial conditions may produce large variations after some time. The concept of strong sensitivity was introduced in
[
1
]
and has been called
cofinite sensitivity
in other references (see
[
41
]
). In its turn, collective sensitivity was considered for the first time in
[
40
]
. The reader should note that we have the implications

\textit{strong sensitivity $\Rightarrow$ collective sensitivity $\Rightarrow$ sensitivity}.

Actually, that collective sensitivity implies sensitivity is direct, while the fact that the “strong” version implies the “collective” one can be deduced from
[
40
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
38
, Proposition 2.8]
, or from Theorem
4.2
below. The converse implications are false (see
[
38
, Example 2.11]
and
[
41
, Example 1]
).

In the context of dynamics on hyperspaces, these properties were studied for the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
in
[
38
,
40
]
, and later for system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
in
[
20
]
. The next theorem was essentially proved in such references, if one combines properly their results. However, since one of the proofs given in
[
20
]
is not completely clear, and because we include an additional result concerning the endograph metric, we find it convenient to restate the theorem in full and to re-prove the unclear part:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the system
(X,f)
is collectively sensitive for the metric
d
;

(ii)

the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is sensitive for the metric
d_{H}
;

(iii)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is sensitive;

(iv)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is sensitive;

(v)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is sensitive.

(b)

If the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is (resp. strongly) sensitive for
d_{H}
, then so is
(X,f)
for the metric
d
.

(c)

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the system
(X,f)
is strongly sensitive for the metric
d
;

(ii)

the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is strongly sensitive for the metric
d_{H}
;

(iii)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
is strongly sensitive;

(iv)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
is strongly sensitive;

(v)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is strongly sensitive.

(d)

If the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is (resp. strongly) sensitive, then so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
for the metric
d_{H}
.

Proof.

(a): The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is
[
40
, Theorem 2.3]
. The implications (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii), (iv), (v) were proved in
[
20
, Theorem 3.10]
with a correct proof. However, for the implications (iii), (iv), (v)
\Rightarrow
(ii), the authors in
[
20
, Theorem 3.11]
said that such implications are a consequence of the equalities

d_{S}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{0}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{\{x\}},u)=d_{H}(\{x\},u_{0})\quad\text{ for all }x\in X\text{ and }u\in\mathcal{F}(X).

Although these equalities are true (see Proposition
2.5
), the fact that (iii), (iv), (v)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follow from them is not clear. Indeed, recall that for the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
to be sensitive, it is not enough that the system
(X,f)
is sensitive (see
[
38
, Example 2.11]
). Thus, we consider convenient to include a proof of the following
fact
(which uses both Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
in an essential way):

–

If any of the extended dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
o
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
is sensitive, then so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
for the metric
d_{H}
.

Proof of the fact
: Fix
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
, assume that
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
is sensitive for
\rho
, and let
\varepsilon>0
be a sensitivity constant, i.e. we are assuming that for any
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and any
\delta>0
there are
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that
\rho(u,v)<\delta
and that
\rho(\hat{f}^{n}(u),\hat{f}^{n}(v))>\varepsilon
. Without loss of generality assume that
0<\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
. We claim that
\varepsilon
is a sensitivity constant for
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and
d_{H}
. Indeed, given any compact set
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and any positive value
0<\delta<\varepsilon
, if we set
u:=\chi_{K}
, by assumption there exist a normal fuzzy set
v\in\mathcal{F}(X)
and a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that

\rho(\chi_{K},v)<\delta\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho(\chi_{\overline{f}^{n}(K)},\hat{f}^{n}(v))=\rho(\hat{f}^{n}(\chi_{K}),\hat{f}^{n}(v))>\varepsilon.

Recalling that
\overline{f}^{n}(v_{\alpha})=f^{n}(v_{\alpha})=[\hat{f}^{n}(v)]_{\alpha}
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
, we have three cases:

–

Case 1
:
We have that
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0}\}
. In this case, by statement (c) of Proposition
2.5
we have that
d_{0}(\chi_{K},v)=d_{\infty}(\chi_{K},v)<\delta
and that
d_{0}(\chi_{\overline{f}^{n}(K)},\hat{f}^{n}(v))=d_{\infty}(\chi_{\overline{f}^{n}(K)},\hat{f}^{n}(v))>\varepsilon
. It follows that

d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n}(K),\overline{f}^{n}(v_{\alpha}))>\varepsilon\quad\text{ for some }\alpha\in\mathbb{I},

and that
d_{H}(K,v_{\alpha})<\delta
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{I}
by the definition of
d_{\infty}
. The result follows since
v_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
.

–

Case 2
:
We have that
\rho=d_{S}
. In this case, part (b) of Lemma
2.7
shows that there exists some level
\alpha\in[0,1-\varepsilon]\subset[0,1-\delta]
such that
d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n}(K),\overline{f}^{n}(v_{\alpha}))>\varepsilon
. In its turn, part (a) of Lemma
2.7
shows that
d_{H}(K,v_{\alpha})<\delta
. The result follows since
v_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
.

–

Case 3
:
We have that
\rho=d_{E}
. In this case, part (c) of Lemma
2.7
shows that there exists some level
\alpha\in\ ]\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\subset\ ]\delta,1-\delta]
such that
d_{H}(\overline{f}^{n}(K),\overline{f}^{n}(v_{\alpha}))>\varepsilon
. An application of Lemma
2.6
shows now that
d_{H}(K,v_{\alpha})<\delta
. The result follows since
v_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
.

We deduce that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
admits
\varepsilon
as a sensitivity constant for the metric
d_{H}
, as required. With these arguments, the equivalences stated in (a) are now proved in full generality.

(b): A proof for this statement can be found in
[
38
, Propositions 2.1 and 2.8]
.

(c): The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is
[
38
, Propositions 2.3 and 2.8]
. That (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii), (iv), (v) was proved in
[
20
, Theorem 3.7]
, and the implications (iii), (iv), (v)
\Rightarrow
(i) were proved in
[
20
, Proposition 3.3]
.

(d): We have already proved the sensitivity part in statement (a). For strong sensitivity one can fix a general integer
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, and repeat the arguments above (with the three
Cases
) for each
n\geq n_{0}
.

4.1
and

at hand, we are now ready to proceed to Section

.

5
Cantor-dense Li-Yorke chaos

In this section we show that, although Question
1.1
admits a positive answer by Example
3.4
, under certain conditions on the system
(X,f)
one can guarantee that some Li-Yorke chaotic-type properties transfer from
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
. In particular, following the theory from
[
26
]
, we introduce the concept of
Cantor-dense Li-Yorke chaos
and we prove that it transfers from
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
whenever
(X,d)
is a complete metric space; see Theorem
5.2
. By adding further assumptions, such as the linearity of the underlying dynamical system, we even obtain certain equivalences with the original system; see Theorem
5.5
. Throughout this section, Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
will play a crucial role.

5.1
Another strengthened variant of Li-Yorke chaos

We will use a dynamical notion stronger than U-LYC but that has been recently considered in the context of dynamical systems on completely metrizable spaces (see
[
26
]
). From now on, a
Cantor set
will be a set homeomorphic to the classical Cantor ternary set of the unit interval.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
. We say that the dynamical system
(X,f)
is
Cantor-densely Li-Yorke chaotic
, for the metric
d
, if there exists some positive value
\varepsilon>0
such that for any sequence
(U_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of non-empty open subsets of
X
there is a sequence of Cantor sets
(K_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
fulfilling the properties

K_{j}\subset U_{j}\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}K_{j}\text{ is a Li-Yorke $\varepsilon$-scrambled set for $f$\penalty\ and\penalty\ $d$.}

For short, we will write that
(X,f)
is
CD-LYC
.

Since every Cantor set is uncountable, it is clear that CD-LYC
\Rightarrow
U-LYC
\Rightarrow
LYC. Moreover, it has been recently proved, using the celebrated
Mycielski theorem
(see
[
26
, Theorem 2.2]
), that

–

[
26
, Proposition 3.5]
:
Given a continuous map
f:X\longrightarrow X
acting on a
completely metrizable
space
(X,d)
, then the dynamical system
(X,f)
is CD-LYC for the metric
d
if and only if
(X,f)
has a dense proximal relation and it is a sensitive dynamical system for the metric
d
.

Using the results obtained in Section
4
on proximality and sensitivity we obtain that:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map on a complete metric space
(X,d)
. If any of the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{f})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{f})
or
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{f})
is CD-LYC, then so is
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
for the metric
d_{H}
.

Proof.

It is almost trivial to check that, if a dynamical system is CD-LYC, then it has a dense proximal relation and it is sensitive. Thus, the hypothesis of this result together with statement (c) of Theorem
4.1
and statement (a) of Theorem
4.2
imply that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
has a dense proximal relation and that it is sensitive for the metric
d_{H}
. Finally, since
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
is complete when
(X,d)
is complete (see for instance
[
18
, Exercise 2.15]
), the result follows from
[
26
, Proposition 3.5]
.
∎

From Theorem
5.2
we deduce that Question
1.1
may have a general negative answer for certain strengthened versions of LYC. Let us show that similar arguments apply to linear dynamical systems.

5.2
The case of infinite-dimensional linear operators

As a subfield of Topological Dynamics one finds the so-called
Linear Dynamics
, where the dynamical systems under consideration are usually denoted by
(X,T)
and consist of a
Fréchet space

(X,d)
and a
continuous linear operator
, or just
operator
for short,
T:X\longrightarrow X
. Recall that a Fréchet space is a locally convex topological vector space
X
, admitting a translation-invariant metric
d
for which
(X,d)
is complete. Throughout this section, given a Fréchet space
(X,d)
we denote by
0_{X}
the zero-vector in
X
, and given any subset
E\subset X
we denote by
\operatorname{span}(E)
its linear span. In addition, given any operator
T:X\longrightarrow X
and any vector
x\in X
we will denote its
T
-orbit
by
\mathcal{O}_{T}(x):=\{T^{n}(x)\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, and we will denote the
set of vectors with a Null Sub-
T
-orbit
by

NS(T):=\{x\in X\ ;\ (T^{n}(x))_{n}\text{ admits a $0_{X}$-convergent subsequence}\}.

The linear system
(X,T)
is called
equicontinuous
if for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
\delta>0
fulfilling that
d(x,y)<\delta
implies
d(T^{n}(x),T^{n}(y))<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the metric
d
is translation-invariant, the previous condition is equivalent to the fact that for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
\delta>0
fulfilling that
d(x,0_{X})<\delta
implies
d(T^{n}(x),0_{X})<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In Functional Analysis, this property is usually called
power-boundedness
, but we will keep the word “
equicontinuous
” for the rest of the paper.

When the Fréchet space
X
is finite-dimensional, then the dynamics of any operator
T:X\longrightarrow X
are completely determined by the Jordan canonical form of
T
and, in particular, Li-Yorke chaos can not occur. However, when
X
is infinite-dimensional the situation becomes substantially richer, and there exist several works (such as
[
5
,
26
,
31
]
) in which chaos has been studied for infinite-dimensional linear systems. In the context of dynamics on hyperspaces, various Li-Yorke chaotic-type properties for the respective extensions
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{T})
were explored in
[
6
,
34
]
. In this subsection we extend
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
34
, Theorem 3]
by considering the metrics
d_{S}
and
d_{E}
, but also including the notion of CD-LYC in the obtained equivalences; see Theorem
5.5
. To do so we strongly rely on the theory developed so far in this paper, together with the next two auxiliary (possibly folklore) lemmas:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
T:X\longrightarrow X
be an operator on a Fréchet space
(X,d)
. Assume that
\operatorname{span}(NS(T))
is dense in
X
and that
(X,T)
is equicontinuous. Then, for all
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and all
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{H}\left(\overline{T}^{n}(K),\{0_{X}\}\right)=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\infty}\left(\hat{T}^{n}(u),\chi_{\{0_{X}\}}\right)=0.

Proof.

When
(X,T)
is equicontinuous, using the arguments in
[
26
, Lemma 3.2]
it almost trivial to check that every orbit with a
0_{X}
-convergent subsequence is indeed full
0_{X}
-convergent, that is

NS(T)=\left\{x\in X\ ;\ \lim_{n\to\infty}d(T^{n}(x),0_{X})=0\right\}.

Such a set is a linear subspace, so that
NS(T)=\operatorname{span}(NS(T))
and
NS(T)
is dense in
X
. We claim that
NS(T)=X
: actually, note that given any
x\in X
and any
y\in NS(T)
we have that

0\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(T^{n}(x),0_{X})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(d(T^{n}(x),T^{n}(y))+d(T^{n}(y),0_{X})\right)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}d(T^{n}(x),T^{n}(y)),

which is arbitrarily small by the equicontinuity of
(X,T)
and the density of
NS(T)
. Now, let us fix any
K\in\mathcal{K}(X)
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Using that
(X,T)
is equicontinuous, that
K\subset NS(T)
, and that
K
is compact, it is not hard to find
n_{K,\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d(T^{n}(x),0_{X})<\varepsilon
for all
x\in K
and
n\geq n_{K,\varepsilon}
. It follows that
d_{H}(\overline{T}^{n}(K),\{0_{X}\})<\varepsilon
for all
n\geq n_{K,\varepsilon}
, so that
d_{H}(\overline{T}^{n}(K),\{0_{X}\})
converges to
0
. In its turn, given any
u\in\mathcal{F}(X)
we have that
u_{0}\in\mathcal{K}(X)
. Therefore, repeating the argument for the compact set
K=u_{0}
together with statement (b) of Proposition
2.5
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
T:X\longrightarrow X
be an operator acting on a Fréchet space
(X,d)
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the system
(X,T)
is not equicontinuous;

(ii)

the system
(X,T)
is sensitive for the metric
d
;

(iii)

the system
(X,T)
is collectively sensitive for the metric
d
.

Proof.

It is well-known that (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii); see for instance
[
26
, Theorem 3.11]
. In addition, by the definition of sensitivity and collective sensitivity, it is clear that (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii). To complete the proof we only need to check that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Assume (ii), let
\varepsilon>0
be a sensitivity constant for
(X,T)
, and let us check that
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
is a collective sensitivity constant for
(X,T)
. Actually, given any finite sequence of distinct points
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N}\in X
and any
\delta>0
, by the sensitivity of
(X,T)
we can find
\tilde{x}\in X
and some
n\in\mathbb{N}
fulfilling that
d(\tilde{x},0_{X})<\delta
and that
d(T^{n}(\tilde{x}),0_{X})>\varepsilon
. Let

y_{j}:=\begin{cases}x_{j}+\tilde{x}&\text{ if }d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\\[5.0pt]
x_{j}&\text{ if }d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\end{cases}\quad\text{ for each }1\leq j\leq N.

For the points
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N}\in X
, note that
d(x_{j},y_{j})\leq\max\{d(x_{j},x_{j}),d(x_{j},x_{j}+\tilde{x})\}=d(\tilde{x},0_{X})<\delta
for all
1\leq j\leq N
. Moreover, for each
1\leq j\leq N
we have that

d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(y_{j}))=\begin{cases}d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}+\tilde{x}))&\text{ if }d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\\[5.0pt]
d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))&\text{ if }d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.\end{cases}

Since
d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}+\tilde{x}))\geq d(T^{n}(\tilde{x}),0_{X})-d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(x_{j}))
by translation-invariance and the triangle inequality, we deduce that
d(T^{n}(x_{1}),T^{n}(y_{j}))>\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq N
.
∎

We finally obtain the previously announced generalization of
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
34
, Theorem 3]
:

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
T:X\longrightarrow X
be an operator acting on a Fréchet space
(X,d)
. Hence:

(a)

If
\operatorname{span}(NS(T))
is dense in
X
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the system
(X,T)
is not equicontinuous;

(ii)

the system
(X,T)
is U-LYC for the metric
d
;

(iii)

the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
is U-LYC for the metric
d_{H}
;

(iv)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
is U-LYC;

(v)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
is U-LYC;

(vi)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{T})
is U-LYC;

(vii)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{T})
is U-LYC.

(b)

If the set
X_{0}:=\{x\in X\ ;\ T^{n_{k}}x\to 0_{X}\text{ as }k\to\infty\}
is dense in
X
for some increasing sequence of positive integers
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

the system
(X,T)
is not equicontinuous;

(ii)

the system
(X,T)
is CD-LYC for the metric
d
;

(iii)

the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
CD-LYC for the metric
d_{H}
;

(iv)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
CD-LYC;

(v)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
CD-LYC;

(vi)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{T})
U-LYC;

(vii)

the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{T})
U-LYC.

Proof.

(a): Assume (i). Using
[
5
, Theorem 15]
we have that
(X,T)
is LYC. Thus, by
[
5
, Theorem 9]
we have that
(X,T)
admits an irregular vector, i.e. there exists some
x\in X
fulfilling that

(T^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is Fr\'{e}chet-unbounded}\quad\text{ while }\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(T^{n}(x),0_{X})=0.

It follows that
S:=\{\lambda x\ ;\ \lambda\in\ ]0,1[\}
is an uncountable LY
\varepsilon
-scrambled set for
f
and
d
, whenever we choose a small enough
\varepsilon>0
to verify that
d
is not bounded by
\varepsilon
. We have obtained that (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). The implications (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii), (iv), (v), (vi), (vii) follow from parts (f), (g) and (h) of Theorem
3.1
. Finally, the implications (iii), (iv), (v), (vi), (vii)
\Rightarrow
(i) follow from Lemma
5.3
. Actually, assume that either the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
or the system
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{T})
admits a LY pair for
d_{H}
or for some
\rho\in\{d_{\infty},d_{0},d_{S},d_{E}\}
, and denote such LY pair by
(K,L)\in\mathcal{K}(X)\times\mathcal{K}(X)
or by
(u,v)\in\mathcal{F}(X)\times\mathcal{F}(X)
respectively. Thus, if the system
(X,T)
was equicontinuous, an application of Lemma
5.3
would imply the contradiction

0<\limsup_{n\to\infty}d_{H}(\overline{T}^{n}(K),\overline{T}^{n}(L))\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(d_{H}(\overline{T}^{n}(K),\{0_{X}\})+d_{H}(\overline{T}^{n}(L),\{0_{X}\})\right)=0,

or, by statement (a) of Proposition
2.5
, the contradiction

0<\limsup_{n\to\infty}\rho(\hat{T}^{n}(u),\hat{T}^{n}(v))\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(d_{\infty}\left(\hat{T}^{n}(u),\chi_{\{0_{X}\}}\right)+d_{\infty}\left(\hat{T}^{n}(v),\chi_{\{0_{X}\}}\right)\right)=0.

(b): Since we have that
X_{0}\subset NS(T)
, the set
\operatorname{span}(NS(T))
is also dense in this part (b). Thus, the equivalences (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(vi)
\Leftrightarrow
(vii) follow from the already proved part (a) of this Theorem
5.5
, but also the implications (iii), (iv), (v)
\Rightarrow
(i) because the notion of CD-LYC is stronger than U-LYC.

In its turn, since it can be easily checked that
X_{0}^{2}\subset\operatorname{Prox}(f,d)
, the system
(X,T)
has a dense proximal relation and (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) holds by Lemma
5.4
and
[
26
, Proposition 3.5]
. To conclude we will prove the implications (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii), (iv), (v). Actually, if (i) holds:

(1)

It can be checked that
X_{0}^{2N}\subset\operatorname{Prox}(f_{(N)},d_{(N)})
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
(X^{N},f_{(N)})
has a dense proximal relation for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Using statements (a) and (b) of Theorem
4.1
we deduce that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
for
d_{H}
, and the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
, have a dense proximal relation.

(2)

We have that
(X,T)
is collectively sensitive for
d
by Lemma
5.4
so that
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
for
d_{H}
, and the systems
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
, are sensitive by statement (a) of Theorem
4.2
.

Statements (iii), (iv) and (vi) follow now, because the metric spaces
(\mathcal{K}(X),d_{H})
,
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X)
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}(X)
are completely metrizable (see
[
18
, Exercise 2.15]
,
[
25
, Theorem 3.8]
and
[
15
, Proposition 4.6]
), by applying
[
26
, Proposition 3.5]
to each of the systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
.
∎

From the proof of Theorem
5.5
one should note that, in both parts (a) and (b), we could have added the equivalent condition “
the system
(X,T)
is LYC for the metric
d
”. Thus, Theorem
5.5
is a generalization of
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
34
, Theorem 3]
. In particular, Theorem
5.5
applies to the examples of weighted shifts considered in
[
6
, Section 3]
. As a new application, let us recall that an operator
T:X\longrightarrow X
is called
hypercyclic
when it admits a dense
T
-orbit. We have the next:

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
T:X\longrightarrow X
be an operator acting on a Fréchet space
(X,d)
. If the operator
T
is hypercyclic, then the dynamical systems
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{T})
for
d_{H}
,
(\mathcal{F}_{\infty}(X),\hat{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{0}(X),\hat{T})
, are CD-LYC, and the dynamical systems
(\mathcal{F}_{S}(X),\hat{T})
and
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{T})
are U-LYC.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be a vector with dense
T
-orbit. Let
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
T^{n_{k}}(x_{0})\to 0_{X}
as
k\to\infty
and note that, by the continuity of
T
, we have that
X_{0}:=\{T^{n}(x_{0})\ ;\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a dense set in
X
fulfilling that
T^{n_{k}}(x)\to 0_{X}
as
k\to\infty
for every
x\in X_{0}
. Since hypercyclicity implies that
(X,T)
is not equicontinuous, this corollary follows automatically from statement (b) of Theorem
5.5
.
∎

Using the theory developed in
[
26
]
, similar results may be obtained when
(X,T)
is only assumed to be an endomorphism of a completely metrizable group. We leave the details to the reader.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have carried out a detailed study of the main variants of Li-Yorke chaos for the fuzzy extension
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
. Along the paper we have shown that these chaotic-type properties transfer naturally from
(X,f)
to
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
and from
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
to
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
, while the converse directions are either false or require particular conditions (such as completeness or linearity). Combining the results obtained here with those in
[
32
,
33
]
, we have now achieved a systematic description of the dynamical behaviour of the fuzzy extension
(\mathcal{F}(X),\hat{f})
as defined in this framework, that is, with
\mathcal{F}(X)
denoting the
space of normal fuzzy sets
. These three works together offer a coherent theory unifying most of the dynamical properties that have been considered for these fuzzy systems in the literature. As for possible directions for future research, several natural possibilities arise:

–

One could investigate other dynamical properties beyond those considered in
[
32
,
33
]
or here (such as the existence of invariant measures or other notions, see for instance
[
2
,
30
]
).

–

One could study alternative metrics on
\mathcal{F}(X)
, as suggested in
[
32
, Section 5]
and
[
33
, Section 6]
, and to analyse whether the results obtained here remain valid or need to be reformulated.

–

One could extend the setting to uniform (and not necessarily metrizable) spaces; see
[
23
,
24
]
.

–

One could explore other spaces of fuzzy sets, in which the elements included are not necessarily normal, as discussed in
[
32
, Section 5]
.

In addition, we leave here a list of open problems coming from
[
20
, Pages 74, 75 and 77]
and from the results that we have obtained in Section
4
:

Problem 6.1
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow X
be a continuous map acting on a metric space
(X,d)
:

(a)

Suppose that the system
(X,f)
is strongly sensitive for
d
. Is
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
strongly sensitive?

(b)

Suppose that the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
is sensitive for
d_{H}
. Is
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
sensitive?

(c)

Suppose that the system
(\mathcal{F}_{E}(X),\hat{f})
has a dense proximal relation. Has the system
(\mathcal{K}(X),\overline{f})
a dense proximal relation for the metric
d_{H}
?
