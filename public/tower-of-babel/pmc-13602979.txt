Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine Special Statement: Checklists for persons with sickle cell disease in pregnancy
Authors: Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine (SMFM), Nicolina Smith, Rebecca F Hamm, Jessica Peterson, Alexandra DeRusso Forrest, Nicole A Smith, Jourdan E Triebwasser, SMFM Patient Safety and Quality Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents checklists designed to guide clinicians in the comprehensive management of pregnant individuals with sickle cell disease, including prepregnancy, antepartum, delivery, and postpartum care, as well as the specific management of vaso‐occlusive crises. The checklists aim to standardize care, enhance documentation and communication, and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Implementation strategies are discussed, emphasizing the need for integration of checklists into clinical workflows and continuous evaluation of quality indicators to address disparities and optimize patient care.Keywords: antenatal surveillance, disparities, fetal growth restriction, hemoglobinopathy, hydroxyurea, maternal morbidity, maternal mortality, pain management, perinatal outcomes, prepregnancy counseling, transfusion, vaso‐occlusive crisis

INTRODUCTION

Pregnant individuals with sickle cell disease (SCD) face increased risks of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The checklists in this article aim to guide healthcare providers in managing pregnant patients with SCD in accordance with recommendations made by the Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine (SMFM) and the World Health Organization, ensuring thorough risk assessment, vigilant monitoring, and collaborative care to optimize maternal and neonatal health [7, 8]. By following these checklists, healthcare teams can better navigate the complexities of SCD in pregnancy, with the ultimate goal of improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPLEMENTATION

These checklists (Figures 1 and 2) are intended for use in both the inpatient and outpatient settings to increase the likelihood that obstetrical providers will perform all the unique tasks recommended for managing pregnancies complicated by SCD. Implementation of these checklists should start with the assembly of a motivated and qualified team. In this case, the team should include at least a physician (a maternal‐fetal medicine subspecialist or a general obstetrician), an inpatient nurse, an outpatient nurse, and a practice manager. Institutions may also consider involving a hematologist, given the close collaboration with this specialty in the management of pregnancies affected by SCD. The team should consider how the checklist components can be implemented effectively across the outpatient and inpatient settings. General recommendations for implementing checklists can be found in the Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine Special Report: The Development and Implementation of Checklists in Obstetrics [9].

QUALITY INDICATORS FOR SCD IN PREGNANCY

Once the checklists have been implemented, it is essential to follow up to evaluate outcomes, identify areas for future improvement and checklist revisions, and consider optimizing clinical processes. Clinical quality indicators can be used to assess whether checklists are being used effectively. As the majority of patients with SCD identify as Black, optimal management of SCD is inherently an issue of health equity [10, 11]. Sites can consider evaluating the implementation of these checklists using quality improvement study designs as an intervention to reduce the impact of implicit biases and structural racism on care decisions around SCD. For guidance on health equity in quality improvement, refer to SMFM Special Statement: How to Incorporate Health Equity Into Quality Improvement and Patient Safety Efforts [12].
