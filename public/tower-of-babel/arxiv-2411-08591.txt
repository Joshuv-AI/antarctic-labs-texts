Non-affine fractal hypersurfaces: construction and dimensions
A. Hossain 1, J. Buescu 2

Abstract
. This article presents the construction of a non-affine hypersurface on an n n -simplex in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Additionally, fractal dimension of the graph of a non-affine multivariate real-valued fractal function is estimated under certain conditions. Furthermore, the upper bound of the Hausdorff dimension of the invariant probability measure supported on the graph of such fractal function is estimated.



Keywords:
Iterated function system, Fractal hypersurface, Regular
n
-simplex, Non-affine fractal hypersurface, Fractal dimension, Invariant probability measure.

Primary; 28A80, Secondary; 41A30, 37A50

1.
Introduction

Fractal geometry is a field of study that primarily focuses on understanding and describing the complex patterns and structures found in natural phenomena and objects, such as clouds, trees, mountains, coastlines, and even the cells of the human body
[
24
,
17
,
13
]
. The world of fractals is home to many iconic and well-known examples, including the Koch curve, Cantor set, Cantor dust, Sierpiński gasket, and many more. These fractals have become synonymous with the field of fractal geometry and continue to inspire research and fascination. One key tool in fractal geometry is the theory of iterated function systems (IFSs), which offers powerful methods for generating and modeling fractals, allowing researchers to simulate and analyze these intricate patterns
[
4
,
21
,
36
]
. The attractors generated by IFSs are typically fractal sets, characterized by their unique and intricate geometric structures
[
4
,
17
,
8
,
1
,
15
]
. Building on this concept, Barnsley
[
3
]
introduced in 1986 the idea of fractal interpolation functions (FIFs), which are specifically generated by IFSs. This innovation enabled the creation of functions that can accurately model and reproduce the complex patterns found in fractal geometry
[
14
,
25
,
11
,
12
,
20
]
. Those functions are used to compress images by exploiting the self-similarity properties of fractals
[
18
]
, utilized in computer graphics to generate and simulate natural landscapes, such as mountains, rivers, and clouds, which have inherent fractal characteristics
[
4
,
5
,
37
]
. Navascués
[
28
]
introduced the concept of non-affine fractal functions, expanding the field of fractal geometry and opening up new avenues for research and applications. Non-affine fractal functions do not exhibit affine self-similarity, meaning their scaling properties are not uniform in all directions. These functions have been used to model and analyze complex phenomena in various fields, used to approximate trigonometric polynomials, to analyze and process signals with complex, non-stationary behavior, to generate realistic models of natural objects and environments
[
32
]
, or to model the growth and branching patterns of biological systems, such as blood vessels and trees. Many authors generalized these concepts by defining various fractal interpolation surfaces (FISs) on different types of regions, e.g. FISs on rectangular grids
[
14
]
, FIS on a triangular region without edge condition
[
25
]
, recurrent FISs on rectangular grids
[
23
]
, non-affine FIS on a rectangle
[
30
]
, etc.
In
[
27
]
, Massopust pioneered the development of multivariate, real-valued affine fractal functions defined on a regular
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, constructing an affine fractal basis for these functions which enables the representation of complex fractal structures. The graphical representation of these fractal functions is termed as affine fractal hypersurfaces.

Motivated by these results, in this article we introduce a new class of multivariate, real-valued non-affine fractal functions defined on an
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we term the graph of such a function a
non-affine fractal hypersurface
. In addition, we have included a graphical representation of this type of function, providing concrete examples that facilitate a deeper understanding of their characteristics and functionality (see Figures
2
and
3
).

Fractal dimensions are mathematical concepts used to describe the complexity and scaling properties of fractals. Many authors have studied the fractal dimension of graphs of different fractal functions in the literature
[
31
,
32
,
34
,
2
,
19
,
22
]
.
In
[
9
]
, Buescu et al. explored systems of non-affine iterative functional equations, deriving bounds for the Hausdorff dimension of the solution’s graph. Additionally, they elegantly connected these findings to related concepts in the literature, including Girgensohn functions, fractal interpolation functions, and Weierstrass functions, revealing a rich web of relationships between these mathematical objects. Verma at al.
[
38
]
constructed more general non-affine FIFs on the Sierpiński gasket by taking variable scaling factors. Liang at al.
[
23
]
also provided bounds for box dimensions of the graph of recurrent FISs for equally-spaced data sets. In this article in a more general setting we estimate the bound of fractal dimension of a non-affine fractal hypersurface on a regular
n
-simplex. We also study the Hausdorff dimension of the invariant probability measure supported on the graph.

2.1.
Iterated function system

Let
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
be a Banach space and
d
be the metric induced by this norm. Consider

\mathscr{H}(X)=\left\{K\subset X:~K\neq\emptyset~\mbox{and}~K~\mbox{is compact}\right\}

endowed with the Hausdorff metric
H_{d}
, defined by

H_{d}(A,B)=\max\left\{d(A,B),~d(B,A)\right\}

for all
A,B\in\mathscr{H}(X),
where
d(A,B)=\sup_{x\in A}\inf_{y\in B}d(x,y)
. The space
\left(\mathscr{H}(X),~H_{d}\right)
is complete if
(X,d)
is complete
[
4
]
. Let
W_{n}:X\rightarrow X
, for
n=1,2,\ldots,N
, be continuous functions; then
\mathscr{W}=\left\{\big(X;W_{n}\big):~n=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
is called an IFS
[
4
,
17
]
. If, for each
n=1,2,\ldots,N
, the
W_{n}
are contractive maps, that is, if there exist
s_{n}\in[0,1(
such that

d\left(W_{n}(x),W_{n}(y)\right)\leq s_{n}d(x,y)

for all
x,y\in X
, then the corresponding IFS
\mathscr{W}=\left\{\big(X;W_{n}\big):~n=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
is known as a hyperbolic IFS. In these conditions, the set-valued Hutchinson operator
W:\mathscr{H}(X)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}(X)
, given by
W(B)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}W_{n}(B)~\mbox{for all}~B\in\mathscr{H}(X)
, is also a contraction map with contractivity factor
s=\max\{s_{n}:~n=1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
Define
W^{0}(B)=B
and let
W^{k}(B)
denote the
k
-fold composition of
W
applied to
B
.

Definition 2.1.1
.

(See
[
6
]
)

A compact subset
F
of
(X,d)
is called an
attractor
of an IFS
\mathscr{W}=\left\{\big(X;W_{n}\big):~n=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
if

(1)

W(F)=F
and

(2)

there exists an open subset
U
of
X
such that
F\subset U
and

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}W^{k}(B)=F\quad\mbox{for all}~B\in\mathscr{H}(U),

where convergence is with respect to the Hausdorff metric
H_{d}
on
\mathscr{H}(X).

Note 2.1
.

The largest open set
U
in Definition
2.1.1
is known as the
basin
of attraction for the attractor
F
of the IFS
\mathscr{W}
and is denoted by
B(F)
.

Definition 2.1.2

(Hausdorff dimension)
.

Let
\big(X,~d\big)
be metric space. Then the Hausdorff dimension of a set
F\subset X
is given by

\dim_{H}F=\inf\left\{s>0:~\forall\delta>0,~\mbox{there is countable cover}~\left\{U_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}~\mbox{of}~F~\mbox{such that}~\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\lvert U_{i}\rvert^{s}<\delta\right\},

where
\lvert U_{i}\rvert
denotes the diameter of
U_{i}
.

Definition 2.1.3

(Box-counting dimension)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
F
be a compact subset of
X
. Let
N_{r}(F,d)
be the minimum number of balls of radius
r
that cover
F
. Then the upper and lower box dimensions of
F
are defined by

\displaystyle\overline{\dim}_{B}(F,d)=

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(F,d)}{-\log r}

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}_{B}(F,d)=

\displaystyle\liminf\limits_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log N_{r}(F,d)}{-\log r}.

If both exist and are equal, the common value is called the box dimension of
F
and is denoted by
\dim_{B}(F,d)

[
17
]
.

Definition 2.1.4
.

If
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
X
, then the Hausdorff dimension of
\mu
is given by

(1)

\dim_{H}(\mu)=\inf\big\{\dim_{H}F:~F~\mbox{is a Borel subset such that}~\mu(F)>0\big\}.

2.2.
Fractal Surfaces

Let
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
and
(Y,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y})
be two Banach spaces. In this section, a class of special attractors of IFSs, namely attractors that are the graphs of bounded functions
f:\Delta\subset X\to Y
, where
\Delta\in\mathscr{H}(X)
, is provided (see
[
27
]
). Suppose there exists a collection of injective maps
\{L_{i}:\Delta\to\Delta,~i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
such that

\displaystyle\{L_{i}(\Delta):~i=1,2,\ldots,N\}~\mbox{ is a set-theoretic partition of $\Delta$, that is}

\displaystyle\Delta=\bigcup_{i}^{N}L_{i}(\Delta)~\mbox{and}~\big(L_{i}(\Delta)\big)^{\circ}\cap\big(L_{j}(\Delta)\big)^{\circ}=\emptyset,~\mbox{for all}~i\neq j,

where
(A)^{\circ}
denotes the interior of the set
A
.
Let
\mathcal{B}(\Delta)=\left\{f:\Delta\to Y:~f~\mbox{is bounded}\right\}
and for all
f\in\mathcal{B}(\Delta)
, define a norm
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}:=\sup_{x\in\Delta}\lVert f(x)\lVert_{Y}
.
It is straightforward to show that
\big(\mathcal{B}(\Delta),\lVert f\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}\big)
is a Banach space.
For
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, let
F_{i}:\Delta\times Y\to Y
be a mapping which is contractive with respect to the second variable, i.e., there exists
0\leq c<1
such that

\lVert F_{i}(x,y_{1})-F_{i}(x,y_{2})\rVert_{Y}\leq c\lVert y_{1}-y_{2}\rVert_{Y},\quad\forall x\in\Delta~\mbox{and}~\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in Y.

Define a Read-Bajactarević (RB)-operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{B}(\Delta)\to\mathcal{B}(\Delta)
by

(2)

\mathcal{T}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}\big(L_{i}^{-1}(x),f\circ L_{i}^{-1}(x)\big)\cdot\chi_{\Delta_{i}},

where
\chi_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of
A
, which takes the value one on
A
and zero outside
A
. Then
\mathcal{T}
is well defined and is also a contraction map on the Banach space
\mathcal{B}(\Delta)
, thus having a unique fixed point
f
in
\mathcal{B}(\Delta)
. This unique fixed point is called the multivariate fractal function and its graph is a fractal surface on
X\times Y
; sometimes it is called
fractal hypersurface
on
X\times Y
. The graph of this function
f
is an attractor of the IFS
\left\{\big(\Delta\times Y;W_{i}\big):~i=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
, where the
W_{i}
’s are given by

W_{i}(x,y)=\big(L_{i}(x),F_{i}(x,y)\big).

For more details see Massopust
[
27
]
.

2.3.
Affinely generated fractal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

In this section, we deal mainly with the connections between IFS and multivariate real-valued affine FIF.

Definition 2.3.1
.

Let
\{e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be a set of affinely independent points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A regular n-simplex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as the point set

\Delta:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x=\sum_{k=0}^{n}t_{k}e_{k};0\leq t_{k}\leq 1;\sum_{k=0}^{n}t_{k}=1\right\}.

.

Over the
n
-simplex
\Delta
, consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\Delta)=\left\{f:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}~\mbox{such that $f$ is continuous on $\Delta$}\right\}.

Then the space
\left(\mathcal{C}(\Delta),d_{\infty,\Delta}\right)
forms a complete metric space, where the metric
d_{\infty,\Delta}
is induced by the sup norm, defined as
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}:=\sup_{x\in\Delta}\lvert f(x)\lvert
for
f\in\mathcal{C}(\Delta)
.

Now, let
\{\Delta_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,N\}
be a collection of non-empty compact subsets of
\Delta
with the properties:

\displaystyle(A1)\quad\Delta=\bigcup_{i}^{N}\Delta_{i};

\displaystyle(A2)\quad\Delta_{i}~\mbox{is similar to}~\Delta,~i=1,2,\ldots,N;

\displaystyle(A3)\quad\Delta_{i}~\mbox{is congruent to}~\Delta_{j}~\mbox{with}~(\Delta_{i})^{\circ}\cap(\Delta_{j})^{\circ}=\emptyset~\mbox{for all}~i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}.

Then there exist
N
contractive similarity maps
L_{i}:\Delta\to\Delta_{i}
given by

(3)

L_{i}=c_{i}O_{i}+t_{i},

where
c_{i}<1
is the similarity constant or the similarity ratio for
\Delta_{i}
with respect to

\Delta
,
O_{i}
is an orthogonal transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
t_{i}
is a translation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
V
be the set of vertices of
\Delta
and
V_{i}
be the set of vertices of
\Delta_{i}
. Let
l:\bigcup V_{i}\to V
be a
labeling map
, defined in such a way that the condition

L_{i}(l(v))=v

is satisfied for all
i=1,2,\ldots,N
and for all
v\in\bigcup V_{i}
. Consider the interpolation set

\{(v,z_{v})\in\Delta\times\mathbb{R}:v\in\bigcup V_{i}\}.

For
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, define the continuous maps
F_{i}:\Delta\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

(4)

F_{i}(x,y)=\lambda_{i}(x)+\alpha_{i}y,

where
\lambda_{i}:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
are affine maps and
\alpha_{i}\in(-1,1)
. The affine map
\lambda_{i}
is uniquely determined by the interpolation conditions

\lambda_{i}(l(v))+\alpha_{i}z_{l(v)}=z_{v}.

Impose the following join-up conditions:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\circ L_{i}^{-1}(s,t)+\alpha_{i}f\circ L_{i}^{-1}(s,t)=\lambda_{j}\circ L_{j}^{-1}(s,t)+\alpha_{j}f\circ L_{j}^{-1}(s,t)

for all
(s,t)\in E_{ij}:=\Delta_{i}\cap\Delta_{j}
,
i\neq j
and for all continuous functions
f:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
(see
[
27
,
35
,
10
,
7
]
). The set
E_{ij}
is called a common edge of
\Delta_{i}
and
\Delta_{j}
.

Finally, the IFS is generated by the mappings
L_{i}
and
F_{i}
, having an attractor which is a graph of the continuous map
f:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
. The map
f
is called a
multivariate
real-valued affine fractal function
and its graph is known as
affinely generated fractal
hypersurface
or an
affine fractal hypersurface

[
27
]
.

3.
Construction of non-affine fractal hypersurface.

In this section, we present the construction of a non-affine multivariate fractal function on an
n
-simplex.

Let
\Delta
be an
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\left\{\Delta_{i}:~i=1,2,\ldots,N\right\}
be the set-theoretic partition of
\Delta
satisfying the conditions
(A1)
,
(A2)
and
(A3)
, and
V_{0}
be the vertex set of
\Delta
. Let
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a vector such that
\lvert\alpha_{i}\rvert<1
, which will act as a scaling vector. Let
L_{i}:\Delta\to\Delta_{i}
be the contractive similarity maps given in (
3
). For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\bf i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, let
\Delta_{{\bf i}}:=L_{{\bf i}}(\Delta)=L_{i_{1}}\circ L_{i_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ L_{i_{k}}(\Delta)
and
V_{\bf i}
be the corresponding vertex set of
\Delta_{{\bf i}}
. Let us define
Z_{k}:=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}V_{\bf i}
and
Z_{0}=V_{0}
.

For a fixed
g\in\mathcal{C}(\Delta)
, let
g(v_{k})=z_{v_{k}}
for all
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
. Then the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)=\left\{f\in\mathcal{C}(\Delta):f(v_{k})=g(v_{k})=z_{v_{k}},~v_{k}\in Z_{k}\right\}

is a closed subset of
\left(\mathcal{C}(\Delta),\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}\right)
, being therefore complete. Consider the interpolation set

(5)

\mathscr{Z}:=\big\{(v_{k},z_{v_{k}})\in\Delta\times\mathbb{R}:v_{k}\in Z_{k}\big\}.

Let us define a labeling map
l_{k}:Z_{k}\to Z_{k-1}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
that satisfies the condition

(6)

L_{\bf i}(l_{k}(v_{k}))=v_{k}

for all
{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, and for all
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
. For
{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, consider the contraction homeomorphism
L_{\bf i}:\Delta\to\Delta_{{\bf i}}
given by

(7)

L_{{\bf i}}(x)=L_{i_{1}}\circ L_{i_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ L_{i_{k}}(x)

and the continuous maps
F_{\bf i}:\Delta\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(8)

F_{\bf i}(x,y)=\lambda_{\bf i}(x)+\alpha_{\bf i}y,

where
\alpha_{\bf i}=\alpha_{i_{1}}\cdot\alpha_{i_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{i_{k}}
,
i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
, and
\lambda_{\bf i}:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
are defined by

(9)

\lambda_{\bf i}(x)=g\circ L_{\bf i}(x)-\alpha_{\bf i}b(x),

where the function
b:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the condition
b(v_{k})=g(v_{k})
for all
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
and
b\neq g
. For all
{\bf i},{\bf j}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
and for all
x\in E_{\bf ij}=\Delta_{\bf i}\cap\Delta_{\bf j}
, we impose the following join-up condition

(10)

\alpha_{\bf i}(f-b)\circ L_{\bf i}^{-1}(x)=\alpha_{\bf j}(f-b)\circ L_{\bf j}^{-1}(x)

for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
. Since
\lvert\alpha_{i}\rvert<1
, for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
, therefore,
\lvert\alpha_{\bf i}\rvert<1
for all
{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, hence it follows that the map
F_{\bf i}
is contractive with respect to the second variable and for all
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
,

(11)

F_{\bf i}\big(l_{k}(v_{k}),z_{l_{k}(v_{k})}\big)=\lambda_{\bf i}(l_{k}(v_{k}))+\alpha_{\bf i}z_{l_{k}(v_{k})}.

Now,

(12)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\bf i}(l_{k}(v_{k}))

\displaystyle=g\big(L_{\bf i}(l_{k}(v_{k}))\big)-\alpha_{\bf i}b(l_{k}(v_{k}))

\displaystyle=g(v_{k})-\alpha_{\bf i}g(l_{k}(v_{k}))

\displaystyle=z_{v_{k}}-\alpha_{\bf i}z_{l_{k}(v_{k})}.

From (
11
) and (
12
), we get

(13)

F_{\bf i}\big(l_{k}(v_{k}),z_{l_{k}(v_{k})}\big)=z_{v_{k}}.

Recalling the RB-operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)\to\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
defined in equation (
2
) such that

(14)

\mathcal{T}(f)=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}g\cdot\chi_{\Delta_{\bf i}}+\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\alpha_{\bf i}\big(f-b\big)\circ L_{\bf i}^{-1}\cdot\chi_{\Delta_{\bf i}},

we then obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The operator
\mathcal{T}
is well defined and contractive on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that for
{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, on each partition
\Delta_{{\bf i}}
,
\mathcal{T}(f)
is continuous for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
. Let
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
. Then
v_{k}\in V_{{\bf i}}\subset\Delta_{{\bf i}}
for some
{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
. Therefore, from (
6
), we get

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}f)(v_{k})

\displaystyle=g(v_{k})+\alpha_{\bf i}\big(f-b\big)(L_{\bf i}^{-1}(v_{k}))

\displaystyle=g(v_{k})+\alpha_{\bf i}\big(f(l_{k}(v_{k}))-b(l(v_{k}))\big)=z_{v_{k}}.

Also for
x\in E_{\bf ij}=\Delta_{\bf i}\cap\Delta_{\bf j}
, from (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}f)(x)=g(x).

This shows that
\mathcal{T}
is well defined and
(\mathcal{T}f)(v_{k})=z_{v_{k}}
for all
v_{k}\in Z_{k}
. Also, from (
14
), for all
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
and all
x\in\Delta_{{\bf i}}
we get

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{T}(f_{1})(x)-\mathcal{T}(f_{2})(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\alpha_{\bf i}\rvert\lvert f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{k}_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\infty,\Delta},

where,
\alpha_{\infty}=\max_{{1\leq i\leq N}}\{|\alpha_{i}|\}<1
. The above inequality is true for all
x\in\Delta=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\Delta_{{\bf i}}
, hence taking the supremum over all
x\in\Delta
, we get

(15)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{T}(f_{1})-\mathcal{T}(f_{2})\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}\leq\alpha^{k}_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}.

Since
\alpha^{k}_{\infty}<1
,
\mathcal{T}
is contractive on
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
.
∎

The Banach fixed point theorem thus ensures that
\mathcal{T}
has a unique fixed point
f^{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)
. The function
f^{\alpha}
is said to be a
multivariate real-valued non-affine fractal function
and its graph a
non-affine fractal hypersurface
. Now, from (
14
), the function
f^{\alpha}
satisfies the functional equation

(16)

f^{\alpha}=\mathcal{T}(f^{\alpha})=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}g\cdot\chi_{\Delta_{\bf i}}+\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\alpha_{\bf i}\big(f^{\alpha}-b\big)\circ L_{\bf i}^{-1}\cdot\chi_{\Delta_{\bf i}}.

Consider the IFS
\mathscr{W}=\left\{\big(\Delta\times\mathbb{R};~W_{\bf i}\big):~{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}\right\}
, where the maps

W_{\bf i}:\Delta\times\mathbb{R}\to\Delta_{\bf i}\times\mathbb{R}
are given by

(17)

W_{\bf i}(x,y)=(L_{\bf i}(x),F_{\bf i}(x,y)).

It thus follows that this IFS is hyperbolic, hence it has a unique attractor
G
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The attractor of the above IFS
\left\{\big(\Delta\times\mathbb{R};W_{\bf i}\big):~{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}\right\}
is the graph of the fractal function
f^{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
G(f^{\alpha}):=\{\big(x,f^{\alpha}(x)\big):~x\in\Delta\}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}W_{{\bf i}}\left(G(f^{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\left\{W_{{\bf i}}\big(x,f^{\alpha}(x)\big):~x\in\Delta\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\left\{\left(L_{\bf i}(x),F_{\bf i}\big(x,f^{\alpha}(x)\big)\right):~x\in\Delta\right\}.

Now, from (

9

\displaystyle=g\big(L_{\bf i}(x)\big)+\alpha_{\bf i}\big(f^{\alpha}-b\big)(x)=f^{\alpha}\big(L_{\bf i}(x)\big).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}W_{{\bf i}}\left(G(f^{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\left\{\left(L_{\bf i}(x),f^{\alpha}\big(L_{\bf i}(x)\big)\right):~x\in\Delta\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\left\{\big(x,f^{\alpha}(x)\big):~x\in\Delta_{\bf i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\{\big(x,f^{\alpha}(x)\big):~x\in\Delta\}=G(f^{\alpha}).

This shows that
G(f^{\alpha})
is the attractor of the IFS, and hence by uniqueness
G=G(f^{\alpha})
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Dimension results

In this section, we explore the
\beta
-oscillation space (see
[
38
]
) and derive bounds for the fractal dimension of the graph of a non-affine multivariate fractal function. Additionally, we provide an upper bound for the Hausdorff dimension of the invariant probability measure supported on the graph.

For
N\geq 2
and
{\bf i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}
, recall the definition of the sets
\Delta_{{\bf i}}:=L_{{\bf i}}(\Delta)=L_{i_{1}}\circ L_{i_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ L_{i_{k}}(\Delta)
. The maximal range of a function
g:\Delta_{\bf i}\to\mathbb{R}
over the set
\Delta_{\bf i}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}]=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\Delta_{\bf i}}|g(x_{1})-g(x_{2})|

and the total oscillation of order
k
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(k,g)=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}].

For
0\leq\beta\leq 2
, define the function space

(21)

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta):=\left\{g:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}:~g~\mbox{is continuous and}~\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mathcal{R}(k,g)}{N^{k(2-\beta)}}<\infty\right\}.

\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)
is called the
\beta
-
oscillation space
. Here, we obtain the following results.

Proposition 4.1
.

For all
g\in\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)
, the following holds:

(1)

If
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle 2\leq\dim_{H}G(g)\leq\dim_{B}G(g)\leq 3-\beta.

(2)

If
1<\beta\leq 2
, then

\displaystyle\dim_{H}G(g)=\dim_{B}G(g)=2.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)
. Then by definition of
\beta
-oscillation space, there exists a real
M>0
such that

(22)

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mathcal{R}(k,g)}{N^{k(2-\beta)}}\leq M.

For a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\delta=\frac{1}{N^{k}}
. Then by the continuity of
g
, the number of mesh-prism of side lengths
\delta
in the column above the set
\Delta_{\bf i}
that intersects
G(g)
is at least
\frac{\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}]}{\delta}
and at most
2+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}]}{\delta}
. Summing over all such set
\Delta_{\bf i}
, we get

(23)

\displaystyle N^{k}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}]\leq N_{\delta}\big(G(g)\big)\leq 2N^{k}+N^{k}\sum_{{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}}\mathcal{R}_{g}[{\Delta_{\bf i}}].

Now since
N\geq 2
, using (
22
) and (
23
), we get

\displaystyle N_{\delta}\big(G(g)\big)

\displaystyle\leq N^{2k}+N^{k}MN^{k(2-\beta)}

\displaystyle=N^{k(3-\beta)}\big(M+N^{k(\beta-1)}\big).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\dim_{B}G(g)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log N_{\delta}\big(G(g)\big)}{\log N^{k}}\leq 3-\beta+\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\big(M+N^{k(\beta-1)}\big)}{\log N^{k}}.

Case 1.
If
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, then
N^{k(\beta-1)}\to 0
, which implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\big(M+N^{k(\beta-1)}\big)}{\log N^{k}}=0.

Also, since
\dim_{H}\Delta=2
and
G(g)
is the graph of the continuous function
g:\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
, it follows that
\dim_{H}G(g)\geq 2
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle 2\leq\dim_{H}G(g)\leq\dim_{B}G(g)\leq 3-\beta.

Case 2.
If
1<\beta\leq 2
, then
\frac{1}{N^{k(\beta-1)}}\to 0
, which implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log\big(M+N^{k(\beta-1)}\big)}{\log N^{k}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\log N^{k(\beta-1)}\left(\frac{M}{N^{k(\beta-1)}}+1\right)}{\log N^{k}}=\beta-1.

This shows that

\displaystyle 2\leq\dim_{H}G(g)\leq\dim_{B}G(g)\leq 3-\beta+\beta-1=2.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\dim_{H}G(g)=\dim_{B}G(g)=2.

This completes the proof.
∎

\mathcal{C}_{0}(\Delta)

Theorem 4.1

Suppose, for a fixed

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{T}f_{1}-\mathcal{T}f_{2}\rVert_{\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{k}_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}+(N\alpha_{\infty})^{k}\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mathcal{R}(m-k,f_{1}-f_{2})}{N^{m(2-\beta)}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k}_{\infty}\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}+\frac{(N\alpha_{\infty})^{k}}{N^{k(2-\beta)}}\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mathcal{R}(m-k,f_{1}-f_{2})}{N^{(m-k)(2-\beta)}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\alpha^{k}_{\infty},\frac{\alpha^{k}_{\infty}}{N^{k(1-\beta)}}\right\}\left(\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\infty,\Delta}+\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N},m>k}\frac{\mathcal{R}(m,f_{1}-f_{2})}{N^{m(2-\beta)}}\right)

\displaystyle=c~\lVert f_{1}-f_{2}\rVert_{\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)},

where
c=\max\left\{\alpha^{k}_{\infty},\frac{\alpha^{k}_{\infty}}{N^{k(1-\beta)}}\right\}<1
. This shows that
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction map on
\big(\mathscr{R}^{\beta}_{f^{\alpha}}(\Delta),\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)}\big)
. Hence, by the Banach fixed point theorem,
\mathcal{T}
has a fixed point
f^{*}\in\mathscr{R}^{\beta}_{f^{\alpha}}(\Delta)
. Also
\mathcal{T}(f^{*})\rvert_{Z_{k}}=f^{\alpha}\rvert_{Z_{k}}
, and it satisfies the functional equation (
16
). Hence by uniqueness we conclude that
f^{\alpha}=f^{*}\in\mathscr{R}^{\beta}(\Delta)
.

Now, using Proposition
4.1
, we get

\displaystyle 2\leq\dim_{H}G(f^{\alpha})\leq\dim_{B}G(f^{\alpha})\leq 3-\beta,~\mbox{if}~0\leq\beta\leq 1

and

\displaystyle\dim_{H}G(f^{\alpha})=\dim_{B}G(f^{\alpha})=2,~\mbox{if}~1<\beta\leq 2.

Also, since
\mu_{\alpha}
is the probability measure with support
G(f^{\alpha})
generated by the IFS

\left\{\big(\Delta\times\mathbb{R};W_{\bf i}\big):~{\bf i}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}^{k}\right\}
, it follows that
\mu_{\alpha}\big(G(f^{\alpha})\big)>0
. Hence by (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\dim_{H}\mu_{\alpha}\leq\dim_{H}G(f^{\alpha}).

Therefore,

(1)

if
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, then
\dim_{H}\mu_{\alpha}\leq 3-\beta
, and

(2)

if
1<\beta\leq 2
, then
\dim_{H}\mu_{\alpha}\leq 2
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Conclusion

In this article, we presented the construction of a non-affine hypersurface on an
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We also estimated the fractal dimension of the graph of this non-affine multivariate real-valued fractal function under certain conditions. Moreover, we estimated the upper bound of the Hausdorff dimension of the invariant probability measure supported on the graph of this fractal function. This investigation was conducted under the assumption of constant scale factors, within the context of Euclidean space. Future research directions may include considering variable scale factors and generalizing the results to
L^{p}
spaces, thereby broadening the scope of applicability.
