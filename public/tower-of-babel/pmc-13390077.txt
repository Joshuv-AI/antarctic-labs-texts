Time to rethink transvenous systems in younger patients?
Authors: Nayani Makkar, Yong-Mei Cha
Published: 2026

The growing necessity for the management of failing, abandoned, and infected transvenous leads borne out of an expanding number of patients living with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs)1 and a growing mismatch between patient longevity and lead service life2 have led to the need for safe and effective tools and techniques for transvenous lead extraction (TLE). 4 decades of this technological evolution and operator experience have transformed TLE from a procedure considered prohibitively risky to one considered standard of care with expanding indications3 and an overall risk comparable with that of complex ablation procedures.4 Nevertheless, a large part of the persisting challenge with appropriately timed5 and safe TLE remains risk stratification, multispecialty collaboration, and patient shared decision making.

In this issue of Heart Rhythm O2, Lin et al (unpublished data) deliver a compelling report on the outcomes of TLE in young adult patients enrolled in the University of California San Diego Lead Extraction Registry. They studied the procedural characteristics and acute outcomes of 1025 patients, 17% of whom were 50 years or younger at the time of extraction compared with those who were older than 50 years (83%). The study suggests that (1) the rate of major complications (defined as the combined incidence of procedural death, cardiac avulsion, pericardial effusion requiring intervention, vascular laceration, respiratory arrest, or a cerebrovascular accident3) was not meaningfully different in the cohort of younger patients; (2) however, the extraction procedure was significantly more complex in younger patients, typified by a lower complete procedural success rate, increased fluoroscopy time, and a greater need for powered extraction tools (upsizing laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath) in this population.

The literature on TLE has provided additional evidence on the risk profile and outcomes in elderly patients with prolonged lead dwell time and multiple comorbidities. The risk and outcome assessment of TLE in the younger patient group is limited. The authors uniquely studied 173 patients in the younger age group. These patients had a mean age of 37.3 ± 9.3 years at the time of lead extraction (28.2 ± 11.0 years at the time of CIED implantation) and a longer mean lead dwell time (108.5 ± 72.3 months) than patients in the older age group (90.5 ± 61.1 months). The reported rate of major procedure-related complications (2.0%) was comparable with contemporary multi-institutional registry data.6,7 Single- or dual-chamber defibrillators were the most commonly extracted devices, and lead malfunction was the most common indication for lead extraction in the younger population. Younger age continued to be significantly associated with metrics of procedural complexity (lower rate of complete procedural success, increased fluoroscopy time, a trend toward increased total procedural duration, and an increased need for upsizing the laser sheath or switching to a rotating mechanical sheath) after multivariable adjustment. The article succeeds in providing valuable insights into the safety, efficacy, and procedural complexity of TLE in high-risk patients.

However, certain limitations must be taken into account when interpreting these results. First, as the authors remind us, this was an observational analysis of retrospective data from a high-volume, referral TLE center, limiting its generalizability for risk stratification. Furthermore, the cohort of younger patients had a significantly younger age at device implant, a significantly longer lead dwell time, and a higher proportion of women, as well as more procedures performed for malfunctioning defibrillator leads, all factors that may have affected the reported procedural outcomes. In addition, the presented data does not provide details on the type and design of the extracted systems, a notable concern when risk-stratifying patients for lead extraction. Finally, it is vital for the audience to remember that although TLE can increasingly be performed in a safe and effective manner even in the highest risk patients, the importance of a multidisciplinary team approach, operator preparedness, and adequate preprocedural risk assessment and planning cannot be overstated. The University of California San Diego group (Lin et al, unpublished data) reports a meticulous risk assessment and procedural protocol designed to minimize procedural risk that included preprocedural computed tomography in all patients to identify high-risk features, utilization of a hybrid operating room with continuous availability of transesophageal echocardiographic guidance and a cardiac surgeon joining the extraction procedure, and a graded approach to the use of powered extraction tools.

Age seems to adversely affect procedural outcomes in patients undergoing TLE in several important ways. Although lead longevity is a significant limiting factor for many adult patients with CIEDs and improved survival (contemporary studies estimate the cumulative incidence of lead failure at 15%–25%1,8 for high-voltage leads), this is particularly significant in patients undergoing device implantation at a young age. Furthermore, the additional stressors imposed by higher activity levels and somatic growth–related lead stress in young patients seem to accelerate mechanical lead failure.9 These patients also seem to have a higher predisposition to an intense postsurgical inflammatory response, resulting in robust fibrous encapsulation frequently associated with calcification, a higher risk of vascular insult during extraction, and an increased risk of lead-to-lead binding, thus complicating the extraction procedure.10,11 Thus, the overall risk in this population is nonlinear rather than additive, compounded also by the very nature of the risk-benefit discussion for these patients. Notably, the long-term risk of abandoning and observing a malfunctioning lead carries significantly different connotations in a 20-year-old vs a 90-year-old patient, particularly when one considers the consequences of subsequent lead infection or vascular access issues that may lead to an eventual (and higher-risk) extraction procedure. It is unsurprising that, in most, if not all, such cases, both the physician and the patient would tend to favor early lead management.

As such, even with the acceptable procedural risks presented in this study, it seems decidedly more prudent to avoid transvenous systems in this population. It is these considerations that have been the drivers of the shift in our practice, among others, toward nontransvenous implantable devices, especially implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, in young patients, a technique that is likely to expand significantly with technological advancements in the future years, yet unknowns remain in the younger patients who require multiple intracardiac leadless pacemaker implants in their lifetime. In this context, this report by Lin et al (unpublished data) provides timely evidence and a reminder of the importance of patient-centered shared decision-making protocols at the time of CIED implantation, extraction, and reimplantation, particularly in younger patients.
