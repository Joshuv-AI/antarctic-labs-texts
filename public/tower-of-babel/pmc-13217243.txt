Ectopic ACTH syndrome in an adult with metastatic Ewing sarcoma
Authors: Deborah A Osafehinti, Jennifer M Kaplan, Elise F Nassif Haddad, Steven G Waguespack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEctopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome (EAS) accounts for 8-18% of Cushing syndrome cases and is typically due to neuroendocrine tumors such as small cell lung carcinoma and bronchopulmonary neuroendocrine tumors. EAS arising from Ewing sarcoma (EwS) is rare, with only 9 pediatric and 5 adult cases reported to date. We present the case of a 40-year-old woman with refractory, metastatic EwS who developed progressive swelling, fatigue, hypokalemia, profound proximal muscle weakness, and intermittent confusion over 2 months. Physical examination revealed facial plethora, supraclavicular fat pads, hirsutism, ecchymoses, and bilateral lower extremity weakness. Laboratory evaluation was consistent with severe ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, confirmed to be secondary to EAS after further evaluation, including the radiographic finding of rapid-onset bilateral adrenal hyperplasia. She started metyrapone, an 11-β hydroxylase inhibitor, which resulted in rapid biochemical improvement and allowed for discontinuation of insulin and potassium therapies. Due to the refractory, progressive EwS and the patient's poor performance status, she transitioned to palliative care at home, continuing metyrapone for hypercortisolism management until her death 4 months later. Herein, we summarize the existing literature of EAS in EwS, emphasize the importance of prompt recognition of the condition, and highlight the therapeutic challenges of managing paraneoplastic EAS.

Introduction

Endogenous hypercortisolism arising from the ectopic secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), termed ectopic ACTH syndrome (EAS), accounts for approximately 8% to 18% of Cushing syndrome (CS) [1-3]. Neuroendocrine tumors such as bronchopulmonary and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors are the most frequent culprits, sometimes secreting ACTH insidiously, resulting in gradual and progressive CS in an otherwise healthy patient. Occasionally, the underlying tumor remains occult despite a thorough evaluation. In contrast, EAS can develop rapidly as a paraneoplastic process in various malignancies such as small-cell lung and medullary thyroid carcinomas, resulting in a precipitous onset of severe CS. Primary therapy targeting the underlying tumor, ie, surgery or chemotherapy, is preferred. However, in advanced malignancies, treatment options may be limited, and the consequences of CS, including myopathy, infections, venous thromboembolism, and hyperglycemia, negatively impact performance status, quality of life, and survival [1, 3-5].

Ewing sarcoma (EwS), a malignant small round cell tumor of neuroectodermal origin, is primarily a disease of children and adolescents with a peak incidence at age 15 years [6]. While the majority of EwS cases arise from the bone, approximately 20% originate from an extraosseous location, also known as extraskeletal EwS [6]. It is uncommon in adults and is very rarely associated with EAS in any population. To date, only 14 cases of EwS with EAS have been published [7-20].

Herein, we report a case of EAS in a 40-year-old woman with recurrent metastatic EwS. Her presentation highlights the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic decisions involved in managing EAS in patients with aggressive malignancies, especially those not typically associated with hypercortisolism.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old woman with recurrent, metastatic EwS was admitted to the hospital for hypokalemia and pancytopenia. Two years prior, she had been diagnosed with metastatic EwS of bone with the primary lesion likely to be from the left humerus and involving the bilateral ovaries, abdominal wall, and multiple osseous sites. After presenting with abdominal pain, she underwent laparoscopic resection of the abdominal disease, hysterectomy, and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Postoperatively, histologic and somatic molecular studies confirmed the diagnosis with a characteristic EWSR1::FLI1 fusion. Systemic treatment consisted of cytotoxic chemotherapy with 6 cycles of vincristine, doxorubicin, and ifosfamide. After achieving complete remission on fluorine-18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-PET/CT), she received 4 additional cycles of ifosfamide and etoposide and completed all therapy 11 months after diagnosis. Five months later, her disease relapsed and she received an additional 5 cycles of topotecan and cyclophosphamide, followed by high-dose ifosfamide.

Eight months after the relapse of her cancer, she began developing easy bruising, facial plethora, bilateral lower extremity edema, facial hirsutism, intermittent confusion, progressive fatigue, and muscular weakness to the extent that she required a wheelchair. At the same time, she was also noted to have hypokalemia, and 60 mEq of oral potassium chloride supplements were started thrice daily. Amiloride was added for suspected ifosfamide-induced Fanconi syndrome, but her hypokalemia persisted. After 2 months of outpatient management, she was admitted for a potassium level of 2.0 mmol/L (reference range 3.5-5.1 mmol/L) and then transferred to our institution after aggressive replacement failed to normalize her potassium level.

On physical examination, she had facial plethora, supraclavicular fat pads, increased facial hair, scattered ecchymoses and petechiae, bilateral lower extremity edema, and significant weakness of the bilateral hip flexors (Fig. 1). Her appearance raised a strong suspicion for hypercortisolism.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial laboratory testing revealed markedly elevated serum cortisol, ACTH, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, and testosterone levels along with hypokalemia (Table 1). She had hyperglycemia with a hemoglobin A1c of 6.6% (49 mmol/mol) (reference range 4.3-5.6% [23-38 mmol/mol]), consistent with new-onset diabetes mellitus. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no evidence of a pituitary adenoma. Review of recent abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans revealed progressive bilateral adrenal hyperplasia over the course of 4 months (Fig. 2). A high-dose dexamethasone suppression test failed to suppress ACTH and cortisol levels (Table 1). A bone marrow biopsy demonstrated >90% tumor infiltration of the marrow space, indicating widespread dissemination of her cancer; immunohistochemistry (IHC) for ACTH was not done.

Treatment

Metyrapone was initiated at 1000 mg thrice daily. Within 24 hours, serum cortisol levels dropped significantly (Fig. 3). As a result, the dose was reduced to 750 mg thrice daily, and dexamethasone 1 mg daily was added in a block-and-replace fashion to avoid symptomatic adrenal insufficiency and the need to titrate the metyrapone dose frequently. Amiloride was discontinued, and oral potassium chloride was decreased to 40 mEq twice daily. A trial of spironolactone was initiated for the management of androgen excess and hypokalemia but was discontinued due to hypotension. She became normoglycemic, and insulin therapy was ultimately discontinued.

Outcome and follow-up

Given her overall disease burden, poor performance status, and lack of treatment options, the patient elected to forego further cancer-directed therapy and transitioned to palliative care at home. At discharge, the cortisol level remained excellent (Fig. 3), so the metyrapone dose was further reduced to 500 mg thrice daily and dexamethasone was decreased to 0.75 mg daily, which she continued for ongoing management of her EAS in a block-and-replace approach until her death 4 months later.

Discussion

This case represents a rare presentation of paraneoplastic EAS secondary to metastatic EwS in an adult patient. While EAS is a well-recognized complication of a multitude of benign and malignant neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), including medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, and various NETs arising from the thymus, lungs, and gastroenteropancreatic system [1, 21], its occurrence in EwS is extremely rare. To our knowledge, and as summarized in Table 2, only 15 cases, including ours, have been reported in the English literature [7-20], and at least one case of a corticotropin-releasing hormone-secreting EwS has been reported [22]. The median age of EAS diagnosis was 12 years, and all but 3 cases occurred within the first 2 decades of life, which reflects the typical age at which EwS is diagnosed. Notably, extraosseous primary tumors accounted for 9 of the 15 cases (60%), a proportion higher than expected, given that extraskeletal EwS accounts for approximately 20% of all EwS cases [6]. This observation suggests that extraosseous EwS may disproportionately be associated with EAS. IHC for ACTH was negative in all but one of the cases for which results were available [9, 13, 15, 17, 19, 20], suggesting the limited utility of IHC staining in this setting.

The exact mechanism by which EwS secretes ACTH ectopically remains unknown. EwS is a primitive neuroectodermal tumor, which can show features of neuroendocrine differentiation and express neuroendocrine markers [23]. In particular, the EWS::FLI1 fusion, which drives aberrant gene transcription in EwS, may contribute to neuroendocrine differentiation, although this mechanism remains speculative [24].

In malignancies, EAS often presents rapidly with severe hypercortisolism leading to marked hypokalemia, myopathy, hyperglycemia, and hypertension [14, 25]. This was evident in our patient, who quickly developed most of these classic signs of EAS. Nonetheless, her diagnosis was delayed by the initial diagnosis of ifosfamide-induced Fanconi syndrome, an established side effect of that medication [26], and the overall rarity of EAS in EwS patients. Indeed, in prior publications from our institution and others [1, 21, 27], EwS was not among the diagnoses identified as a cause of EAS. This case demonstrates the need for a high degree of suspicion in all patients presenting with signs of hypercortisolism, regardless of the primary cancer diagnosis. As seen in our case, delays in diagnosis are common, and the mean time to diagnosis of EAS is 14 months [28].

The diagnosis of EAS requires a nuanced interpretation of biochemical testing in the context of the patient's overarching clinical condition. After diagnosing ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, our patient was assessed for a culprit pituitary tumor. While a visible pituitary lesion on MRI is suggestive of a corticotroph adenoma in the appropriate clinical setting, it is not diagnostic; conversely, the lack of a visible tumor does not exclude the presence of a microadenoma [29]. The small size of these tumors limits the sensitivity of MRIs. Rather, the lack of response to a high-dose dexamethasone suppression test is the hallmark of EAS and, in the context of rapid onset of symptoms and adrenal hyperplasia in a patient with widely metastatic cancer, was diagnostic of EAS in our patient.

The preferred treatment for EAS is surgical resection and/or chemotherapy directed against the culprit tumor. However, medical therapy with adrenal steroidogenesis inhibitors can be used to control hypercortisolism as a bridge to definitive therapy or as long-term therapy in cases in which the tumor is unresectable or treatment-resistant. These agents include etomidate, ketoconazole, levoketoconazole, metyrapone, mitotane, and osilodrostat [25, 29]. Metyrapone, an 11-β hydroxylase inhibitor known to be effective in treating Cushing syndrome [30], was selected for this patient due to its rapid onset of action and availability for inpatient use, and this provided rapid normalization of cortisol. Given the risk of adrenal insufficiency, dexamethasone was co-administered as part of a block-and-replace strategy [29]. Bilateral adrenalectomy is an important treatment option that can provide durable control of severe hypercortisolism, especially in patients who have a life expectancy greater than 6 months [25, 27]. However, surgical intervention was not aligned with our patient's goals of care and life expectancy.

Ultimately, our patient's prognosis was driven by her underlying cancer. While cortisol blockade helped relieve symptoms, her extensive bone marrow involvement and prior treatment limited further oncologic options. Endocrine-directed therapy allowed for a more comfortable hospice transition and possibly a longer period of survival.

In summary, EAS is an uncommon paraneoplastic complication of EwS that can affect all age groups. A high index of suspicion for EAS is warranted, and the diagnosis may be prompted by the finding of progressive adrenal hyperplasia on imaging obtained as part of the patient's cancer care. Early recognition, prompt endocrine testing, and timely initiation of steroidogenesis inhibitors can successfully treat the co-morbidities of EAS and improve the quality and possibly quantity of life, even in the setting of advanced malignant disease.
