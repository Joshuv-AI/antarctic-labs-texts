QED vacuum polarization in the Coulomb field of a nucleus: a method of high-order calculation
Sergey Volkov

Abstract
A calculation of the QED vacuum polarization potential in the Coulomb field of a pointlike nucleus was presented in an earlier publication by the author and his collaborators. Corrections up to order α 2 ​ ( Z ​ α ) 7 \alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{7} were evaluated, where Z Z is the nuclear charge number and Z ​ α Z\alpha is treated as an independent variable. These corrections correspond to two-loop Feynman graphs with proper propagators of fermions in the external field. The calculation employed a reduction to free QED, leading to free QED Feynman graphs with up to eight independent loops. The method of calculation is described here in detail.

II.3
Divergence elimination and renormalization via a forest formula

We work with an unfolded graph and its subgraphs, considering only those that are one-particle irreducible and contain all lines connecting the vertices of the given subgraph.

Two subgraphs are said to
overlap
if neither is contained in the other and their sets of internal lines sets have a nonempty intersection.

A set of subgraphs of a graph is called a
forest
if no two of its elements overlap.

For an unfolded graph
G
, we denote by
\mathfrak{F}[G]
the set of all forests
F
consisting of self-energy, vertexlike, photon-photon scattering, or potential subgraphs of
G
and satisfying the condition
G\notin F
. For example, for
G
from Fig.
4

\mathfrak{F}[G]
is exactly the set of all subsets of
\{\langle 1,2,3,4\rangle,\ \langle 6,7,8,9\rangle,\ \langle 6,7,8,9,11,12,13\rangle\}
(8 subsets); here subgraphs are denoted by the enumeration of internal lines.

To obtain a divergence-free, properly renormalized Feynman amplitude for each unfolded graph
G
, we apply Zimmermann’s forest formula
Zavialov and Stepanov 1965
;
Zimmermann 1969

\sum_{F=\{G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}\}\in\mathfrak{F}[G]}(-1)^{n}S_{G_{1}}S_{G_{2}}\ldots S_{G_{n}}

(3)

to it.
Each term in this expression should be interpreted as a sequential transformation of the subgraph
G_{j}
Feynman amplitudes with the linear operators
S_{G_{j}}
. The subgraphs are ordered from smaller to larger (with respect to inclusion), and the transformed amplitudes of smaller subgraphs are taken into account in the transformations of the larger ones.

The linear operators
S_{G^{\prime}}
are defined differently for different types of subgraphs
G^{\prime}
:

(i).

If
G^{\prime}
is potential, its Feynman amplitude is
\Pi_{\mu}(p)
, where
p
is its external momentum entering the nonfictitious vertex, and
\mu
is the corresponding tensor index. In this case, we put

[S\Pi]_{\mu}(p)=\Pi_{\mu}(0).

(ii).

If
G^{\prime}
is a photon-photon scattering subgraph, its Feynman amplitude is
\Pi_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4})
. Analogously, we put

[S\Pi]_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4})=\Pi_{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\mu_{3}\mu_{4}}(0,0,0,0).

(iii).

If
G^{\prime}
is a photon self-energy subgraph, its Feynman amplitude is
\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)
. We can take the quadratic Taylor expansion

[S\Pi]_{\mu\nu}=\Pi_{\mu\nu}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)}{\partial p_{\xi}\,\partial p_{\eta}}\right|_{p=0}p_{\xi}p_{\eta}.

However, it is more efficient to utilize the substitution

\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)\rightarrow p^{2}g_{\mu\nu}[h_{2}(0)-h_{2}(p^{2})]

instead of
(1-S)\Pi
in the forest formula, where
\Pi_{\mu\nu}(p)=h_{1}(p^{2})g_{\mu\nu}+h_{2}(p^{2})p_{\mu}p_{\nu}
. This trick, used in our calculation, shortens the expressions and is common in the literature; see, for example,
Cvitanović and Kinoshita 1974b
.

(iv).

If
G^{\prime}
is vertexlike, its Feynman amplitude is
\Gamma_{\mu}(p,q)

p-\frac{q}{2}
and
p+\frac{q}{2}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mu}(p,0)

\displaystyle=a(p^{2})\gamma_{\mu}+b(p^{2})p_{\mu}+c(p^{2})\not{p}p_{\mu}+d(p^{2})(\not{p}\gamma_{\mu}-\gamma_{\mu}\not{p}).

In this case, we put

[S\Gamma]_{\mu}(p,q)=a((m_{0})^{2})\gamma_{\mu},

(4)

(v).

If
G^{\prime}
is a fermion self-energy subgraph, its Feynman amplitude is

\Sigma(p)=u(p^{2})+v(p^{2})\not{p}.

In this case, we put

[S\Sigma](p)=u(m^{2})+v(m^{2})m+v((m_{0})^{2})(\not{p}-m),

(5)

where
(m_{0})^{2}

It is well known that the photon-photon scattering amplitude

•

For arbitrary values of the Schwinger parameters

and an infrared regulator
\varepsilon>0

(\not{\mathit{q}}+m)e^{iz_{j}(\mathit{q}^{2}-m^{2}+i\varepsilon)},\quad ig_{\mu\nu}e^{iz_{j}(\mathit{q}^{2}+i\varepsilon)},

We obtain

\displaystyle F^{\text{Schw}}(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}R_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z)\right)e^{iA(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+iB(z)-\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}}

\displaystyle+\text{counterterms},

where

•

Pass to Feynman parameters via

F(\mathbf{p},z)=\frac{1}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow+0}

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{n-1}\left(F^{\text{Schw}}(\mathbf{p},\lambda z,\varepsilon)-F^{\text{Schw}}(0,\lambda z,\varepsilon)\right)d\lambda.

Here, division by

This integral and the limit can be evaluated analytically with the help of the following formulas:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{a\lambda}\lambda^{n}d\lambda=\frac{n!}{(-a)^{n+1}},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}(e^{a\lambda}-e^{b\lambda})\lambda^{-1}d\lambda=\log(-b)-\log(-a).

\begin{array}[]{c}F(\mathbf{p},z)=\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{3/2}}\\
\times\left[\frac{W_{0}(z)}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\log\left(1+\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}\mathbf{p}^{2}\right)\right.\\
+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\left(\frac{W_{l}(z)}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l}}-\frac{1}{B(z)^{l}}\right)\right)\\
\left.+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\frac{Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z)}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l}}\right]+\text{counterterms},\end{array}

(6)

Divergence at
\mathbf{p}=0

We can define

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p}=0,z)=\lim_{\mathbf{p}\rightarrow 0}F(\mathbf{p},z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{3/2}}\times\left(\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}\times\left[W_{0}(z)-\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\frac{lW_{l}(z)}{B(z)^{l}}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\frac{Y_{l}(0,z)}{B(z)^{l}}\right)+\text{counterterms}.

7

as

. If

Consider the unfolded graph of

in Fig.
5

\delta

is of asymptotic order

, one can use the analogy with electric circuits

5

See

This divergence disappears after summing over Feynman graphs. This can be showed by a more careful consideration of 4-photon and 6-photon Ward identities, but the details are beyond the scope of the present paper. We do not calculate the potential exactly at

II.6
Correctness of the regularization

The final value of
\widetilde{V}_{ij}(\mathbf{p})
is obtained as

\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{V}_{ij}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{r}\\
\int_{z>0}F_{r}(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z+\\
\text{products of lower-order terms},\end{array}

(8)

where the summation is taken over the Feynman graphs of
\widetilde{V}_{ij}
(excluding products of lower-order terms), and
F_{r}
is defined by (
6
) for the
r
-th graph. Since we use a special procedure to remove divergences, its correctness must be justified.

There is no formal definition of correct regularization in quantum field theory. However, dimensional regularization can be used as a specimen, since it has repeatedly been shown to be equivalent to other formulations, produce reliable results and preserve essential properties. Suppose we work in
D=(3-\varepsilon)+1
dimensions. We define the dimensionally regularized integrand for one graph analogously to the
D=4
case:

\begin{array}[]{c}F(\mathbf{p},z,\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{D_{0}(z)^{1/2}D_{1}(z)^{(3-\varepsilon)/2}}\\
\times\left[\sum_{l=0}^{L/2}\left(\frac{W_{l}(z,\varepsilon)}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l+k\varepsilon}}-\frac{1}{B(z)^{l+k\varepsilon}}\right)\right)\right.\\
\left.+\sum_{l=1}^{L/2}\frac{Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z,\varepsilon)}{(A(z)\mathbf{p}^{2}+B(z))^{l+k\varepsilon}}\right]+\text{counterterms};\end{array}

(9)

here
k=L_{1}/2
, where
L_{1}
is the number of independent loops in the unfolded graph; the functions
W_{l}(z,\varepsilon)
and
Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z,\varepsilon)
satisfy the same properties with respect to
\mathbf{p}^{2}
and
z
as in the
D=4
case. It is straightforward to check that

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}W_{l}(z,\varepsilon)=W_{l}(z),\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z,\varepsilon)=Y_{l}(\mathbf{p}^{2},z)

for
l\geq 1
, and

-k\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon W_{0}(z,\varepsilon)=W_{0}(z).

Now fix
\mathbf{p}\neq 0
. The integral (
7
), applied to
F(\mathbf{p},z,\varepsilon)
, exists for all
\varepsilon
such that
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{z>0}F(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\varepsilon)\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z

\displaystyle=\int_{z>0}F(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z.

By definition, set

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,1}}_{ij}(\mathbf{p},\varepsilon)=\sum_{r}

\displaystyle\int_{z>0}F_{r}(\mathbf{p},z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},\varepsilon)\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z

\displaystyle+\text{products of lower-order terms},

where
F_{r}
is defined by (
9
) for the
r
-th Feynman graph.

Let
\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,2}}_{ij}(\mathbf{p},\varepsilon)
denote the value defined without counterterm subtractions in the integrands, but with explicit perturbative renormalization of the constants. This definition may require an additional auxiliary regularization and analytic continuation of intermediate functions (we will not consider it in detail, since formulations of this kind are commonly used in calculations). We will use
\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,2}}_{ij}
as a specimen. One can prove that

\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,1}}_{ij}(\mathbf{p},\varepsilon)=\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,2}}_{ij}(\mathbf{p},\varepsilon)

for all
\varepsilon
with
0<|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
. To do this, all power of dimensional regularization can be used: the ability to change the order of momentum integrations, perform a linear change of integration variables, the preservation of Ward identities, independence of auxiliary regularization and so forth. As a consequence,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{V}^{\text{DR,2}}_{ij}(\mathbf{p},\varepsilon)=\widetilde{V}_{ij}(\mathbf{p}),

where
\widetilde{V}_{ij}(\mathbf{p})
is defined by (
8
).

General idea

To integrate (

•

III.3
Improving the stability

To make Monte Carlo convergence as fast as possible, one should minimize

S=\int_{z>0}\frac{F(\mathbf{p},z)^{2}}{g(z,\mathbf{p})}\delta(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{n}-1)d^{n}z,

where
F
is the integrand, and
g
is the probability density function. The following situations should be avoided:

(i)

S
is infinite (an underestimation of the asymptotic behavior of
|F(\mathbf{p},z)|
in even a single Hepp sector may lead to this, even if the approximation works well in all other sectors; for example, for
\widetilde{V}_{27}
there are
2^{18}=262144
sets and
18!=6402373705728000
sectors for each Feynman graph, all of which must be controlled);

(ii)

a region of the integration domain contributes significantly, but is visited only rarely during the integration;

(iii)

extremely large values of
|F(\mathbf{p},z)/g(z,\mathbf{p})|
occur too early in the integration process and spoil the result (this is possible because
F(\mathbf{p},z)/g(z,\mathbf{p})
is generally unbounded);

(iv)

the integration error is underestimated due to unvisited regions with large
|F(\mathbf{p},z)/g(z,\mathbf{p})|
.

To handle (
i
) and (
ii
), we introduce stabilization terms into the probability density function:

\displaystyle g(z,\mathbf{p})=C_{\text{main}}\times g_{0}(z,\mathbf{p})+C_{\text{modify}}\times g_{1}(z,\mathbf{p})+

\displaystyle C_{\text{sector}}\times g_{\text{sector}}(z,\mathbf{p})+C_{\text{uniform}}\times g_{\text{uniform}}(z)+

\displaystyle C_{\text{min}}\times g_{\text{min}}(z);

here
g_{0}
is constructed by the rules of Sec.
III.2
with (
12
);
g_{1}
is constructed with the same rules but with

C^{0}_{\text{mul}}=C^{\infty}_{\text{mul}}=1;

g_{\text{sector}}
is defined by the rules of Sec.
III.2
with (
12
), but normalized differently in different sectors so that the integral of
g_{\text{sector}}
over each Hepp’s sector is the same;

g_{\text{min}}
is constructed with
\mathrm{Deg}(s,\mathbf{p})=R
, where
R>0
is a constant;
g_{\text{uniform}}
is a constant function normalized so that (
10
) holds for it instead of
g_{0}
; The coefficients
C_{\text{main}}
,
C_{\text{modify}}
,
C_{\text{sector}}
,
C_{\text{uniform}}
, and
C_{\text{min}}
are arbitrary nonnegative constants satisfying

C_{\text{main}}+C_{\text{modify}}+C_{\text{sector}}+C_{\text{uniform}}+C_{\text{min}}=1.

In our calculations we use

\displaystyle C_{\text{sector}}=0.05,\quad C_{\text{uniform}}=0.06,

\displaystyle C_{\text{min}}=0.04,\quad R=0.75,

\displaystyle C_{\text{modify}}=0.005\text{ for }V_{23},\quad C_{\text{modify}}=0.03\text{ for }V_{25},V_{27}.

In many cases,
g_{1}
approximates the integrand behavior better than
g_{0}
as
\mathbf{p}\rightarrow 0,\infty
; however, using
g_{1}
as a probability density function slows down computations due to high-precision arithmetic (see
III.4
). This effect is compensated by the smallness of
C_{\text{modify}}
.

To mitigate the situation (
iii
), we use the techniques described in Section III.D of
Volkov 2017
, adapted for GPUs. To address (
iv
), we employ the heuristic from Section IV.F of
Volkov 2018
.

The most resource-intensive part of the computation is the Monte Carlo integration. We employed NVidia P100 GPUs (each of them was accompanied by one core of the CPU Intel Xeon E5-2698). The integration required 15 GPU-days for each of
V_{23},V_{25}
and 42 GPU-days for
V_{27}
. To obtain the results presented in
S. Volkov and V. A. Yerokhin and Z. Harman and C. H.
Keitel 2025
, two random number generators from the NVidia CURAND library were used:
MRG32k3a
and
Philox_4x32_10
. The results from different generators were compared and combined; no statistically significant difference between them was detected.

The integration algorithm is nonadaptive within a single integral. However, the program evaluated integrals for all Feynman graphs simultaneously, choosing the graph number randomly after each block of samples. This graph selection algorithm is adaptive and explained in
S. Volkov 2019
.

In our approach, all divergences are cancelled numerically at the integrand level. This cancellation introduces round-off errors. To control them, integrand values are evaluated using interval arithmetic. Samples producing overly large intervals are recomputed with higher precision. The acceptance-rejection algorithm for intervals is described in detail in Section IV.A of
S. Volkov 2019
. It ensures that the round-off error remains small relative to the Monte Carlo statistical error even in the worst case and accounts for the fact that round-off errors may have a nonzero mean and thus cannot simply be added as statistical errors.

The compiled integrand code sizes are 30 MB for
V_{23}
, 450 MB for
V_{25}
, and 11 GB for
V_{27}
. Special techniques are required to evaluate such huge functions on GPUs. The general scheme of the implementation, as well as the interval arithmetic, are described in
S. Volkov 2019
. We generated the integrand code separately for different precisions:

•

“Eliminated Interval Arithmetic” (EIA) is an approach that uses machine
double
precision, but is significantly faster than standard interval arithmetic (at the cost of larger intervals). It is described in Section IV.C of
Volkov 2018
.

•

Standard machine
double
-precision interval arithmetic.

•

Arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic with a variable mantissa size (128-bit, 256-bit, and 384-bit mantissas were used in our calculations).

V
Conclusion

Momentum-space values for the expansion of the vacuum polarization potential in the Coulomb field of a pointlike source up to order
\alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{7}
were presented in
S. Volkov and V. A. Yerokhin and Z. Harman and C. H.
Keitel 2025
in tabular form. Their application to high-precison calculations of energy levels of atomic systems was also discussed there. These values agree well with the result of order
\alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{3}
at
\mathbf{p}=0
from
Krachkov and Lee 2023
. The values of orders
\alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{5}
and
\alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{7}
, as well as those of order
\alpha^{2}(Z\alpha)^{3}
at
\mathbf{p}\neq 0
were presented for the first time. The methodology of the calculation is explained in this work.

The method relies on a reduction to free QED Feynman graphs (with up to 8 loops), the author’s approach to removing divergences and reducing to finite integrals, and Monte Carlo integration.

The reduction to free QED (unfolding) is effective for evaluating the vacuum polarization potential in a Coulomb field, but its extension to general bound-state problems is not feasible. The reduction to finite integrals is based on an almost direct application of BPHZ renormalization in Feynman parametric space. A slight modification of BPHZ addresses specific features of free QED Feynman graphs arising in bound-state QED and incorporates ideas from the author’s previous work on the free electron
g-2
calculation.

A nonadaptive Monte Carlo algorithm was employed. It constructs probability density functions from the combinatorial structure of the Feynman graphs. The algorithm has 10 parameters that were preadjusted prior to integration. A wide range of external momenta was taken into account. GPUs were used to carry out the integrations. The integration algorithm itself, along with its computer implementation, was adapted from the author’s earlier work on the free-electron
g-2
calculation. Technical details are provided.

The contributions of individual Feynman graphs are reported together with Monte Carlo integration statistics. This information can be used for verification. In addition, it shows how efficient the method is and how large the cancellations are (between integrals and within an integral) across the full range of external momenta and also how it depends on the intermediate renormalization.
