CsWRKY29, a key transcription factor in tea plant for freezing tolerance, ABA sensitivity, and sugar metabolism
Authors: Chengjin Xue, Xiaozhen Huang, Yichen Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tea plants (Camellia sinensis L.) are prone to spring frosts, leading to substantial economic damage. WRKY transcription factors are key in plant abiotic stress responses, yet the role of CsWRKY29 in freezing tolerance is unclear. In this study, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and transient green fluorescent protein assay revealed that CsWRKY29 localizes to the nucleus and its expression is induced by cold and abscisic acid (ABA). CsWRKY29 overexpression in Arabidopsis enhanced freezing tolerance, reduced electrolyte leakage, increased soluble sugars, and boosted superoxide dismutase activity, with upregulated COR genes. These lines also showed heightened ABA and glucose sensitivity. Cold treatment of CsWRKY29-overexpressing lines upregulated AtABI5, AtHXK1, and AtSUS4 compared to wild type, and yeast one-hybrid assays confirmed CsWRKY29 binding to the W-box in the CsABI5 promoter. Furthermore, the application of virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology to reduce CsWRKY29 expression in tea plants revealed a significant decrease in the transcript levels of CsCBFs, CsABI5, CsHXK1, and CsSUS4 in the silenced plants. In summary, our findings indicate that CsWRKY29 may serve as a critical transcription factor that contributes to freezing tolerance, ABA responsiveness, and sugar metabolism within tea plants.

Introduction

Cold stress restricts the geographic range of plant populations, disrupting growth and development, and causing production declines. The tea plant (Camellia sinensis) is a commercially significant warm-season crop, primarily cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions with favorable climates1,2. However, it is susceptible to unexpected spring frosts during the critical stages of bud break and leaf harvest3,4. Cold stress can impact cell membrane rigidity and enzymatic activity, suppressing protein synthesis and, consequently, impair photosynthesis in tea plants. This leads to a reduction in the biochemical composition of new shoots and a decrease in commercial yield5–7. To counteract the detrimental effects of cold-induced oxidative stress on metabolic functions, plants have developed complex regulatory mechanisms. During cold acclimation, thousands of genes operate in a highly coordinated network, regulating gene expression at transcriptional, posttranscriptional, and posttranslational levels7–9. The most thoroughly understood cold acclimation pathway in plants is the C-repeat/dehydration responsive element-binding factor (CBF)-dependent signaling pathway. CBFs are swiftly induced by cold and activate a suite of downstream Cold-Regulated genes (COR), initiating the "ICE1-CBF-COR" regulatory cascade10. Studies have shown that CBFsmodulate a wide array of genes involved in phosphoinositide metabolism, membrane lipid biosynthesis, osmolyte accumulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification, and hormonal dynamics11–13. As key regulators of cold acclimation, CBFs enhance their functions by integrating complex phytohormonal signals, including abscisic acid (ABA).

The hormone ABA plays a predominant role in seed dormancy and germination, as well as in adaption to various abiotic stresses, especially in drought and cold stress responses14. Emerging research indicates that cold stress can modulate ABA biosynthesis and signaling in plants. For instance, ABA levels can double transiently following cold exposure in Arabidopsis15. Similarly, 9-CIS-EPOXYCAROTENOID DIOXYGENASE2 (NCED2), an enzyme crucial for ABA biosynthesis, is rapidly induced by cold treatment in tomatoes, and its silencing confers increased cold sensitivity to plants16. The transcription factor ABSCISIC ACID-INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5) participates in ABA signaling during seed germination and dormancy, seedling growth, and stress adaptation14. In bananas, MaABI5 can regulate ABA-induced cold tolerance through the ubiquitination pathway17. Ectopic overexpression of a MaABI5-like gene in tomato plants also improved cold tolerance18. In rice, OsABI5 can collaborate with the transcription factor OsNAC5 to protect plants against cold stress19. Besides, ABA-deficient mutants, such as los5/aba3, exhibited a loss of cold acclimation ability by abolishing the cold induction of COR47 and RD29a20. Previous studies have shown that the CBF regulatory factor plays a main role in ABA-independent pathways21. However, more and more evidence revealed that many important genes participating in CBF-mediated freezing tolerance can depend on ABA22. For instance, the transcription factor MYB96 can integrate the ABA and CBF signaling pathways to activate plant freezing tolerance12. Both ABA-dependent and independent pathways are involved in cold acclimation23–25.

WRKY transcription factors, characterized by a plant-specific heptapeptide sequence (WRKYGQK) and a zinc finger-like motif, are deeply engaged in regulating plant senescence, pathogen defense, and abiotic stress tolerance26,27. These proteins modulate transcription by directly binding to the W-box core sequence within the promoters of target genes, either activating or repressing gene expression28–30. Over the past decade, research has uncovered a strong link between WRKY transcriptional control and ABA signaling pathways. For instance, dysfunction of AtWRKY40 and AtWRKY63 can affect the expression of AtABI5, leading to an ABA-hypersensitive phenotype in Arabidopsis31. In addition, the WRKY family has emerged as a key player in plant cold tolerance. The overexpression of PoWRKY1 from Polygonatum odoratum, for instance, enhances cold tolerance by altering osmolyte content32. VbWRKY32 from Verbena bonariensis exerts a promoting role in freezing tolerance by modulating transcription levels of cold-responsive genes33. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that WRKY proteins act as transcriptional regulators to integrate cold and ABA signaling pathways. In bananas, several WRKY genes including MaWRKY31, MaWRKY33, MaWRKY60, and MaWRKY71 have been found to directly activate the expression of MaNCED1 and MaNCED2, thereby regulating ABA-induced cold tolerance34. CsWRKY46 from cucumber plays a positive role in the ABA-dependent cold signaling pathway30. In tea plants, CsWRKY21 improves plant cold resistance by regulating the expression of a key ABA catabolic enzyme 8’-Hydroxylase35.

Previously, CsWRKY29 has been reported to function in the cold tolerance of tea plants36. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying how CsWRKY29 mediates the overlap between the cold and ABA response remain unknown. In this study, we found that the transcript of CsWRKY29 was upregulated by both cold and ABA treatments in tea plants. The overexpression of CsWRKY29 in Arabidopsis enhanced freezing tolerance by elevating sugar contents and antioxidant enzyme activity. Additionally, the overexpression of CsWRKY29 also enhanced sensitivity to ABA and glucose during germination and seedling growth. Furthermore, the suppression of CsWRKY29 in tea plants led to a decrease in the expression of CBFs, ABA-responsive, and sugar-related genes. Notably, CsWRKY29 was also found to interact with the W-box element in the promoter of CsABI5. These findings offer a more detailed understanding of the molecular pathways involved in cold adaptation mediated by CsWRKY29 in tea plants.

Results

Characterization of CsWRKY29 which is induced by both cold and ABA stimuli

To identify important components involved in the plant response to cold and ABA, CSS0030511 was isolated as a cold- and ABA-inducible gene from the transcriptome library of the Guizhou tea cultivar Qiancha 1 (Camellia sinensis, cv. Qiancha 1, QC1). The coding region sequence of CSS0030511 was amplified from QC1 (Fig. S1). According to the previous study and the BLAST result in the IPTA database (http://tpia.teaplants.cn), CSS0030511 encodes a plant-specific WRKY transcription factor named CsWRKY29 36. There are 86 putative WRKY genes in tea plants, which can be clustered into three groups based on the number of WRKY domains and the characteristic of zinc-finger motifs (Fig. S2). CsWRKY29 contained a WRKY heptapeptide domain and a complete C2H2 zinc finger motif at the and therefore belongs to Group II (Fig. 1a). Promoter analysis revealed that CsWRKY29 harbors the ABA-responsive elements (ABRE) and several stress-responsive elements (Fig. 1b). Molecular expression patterns and protein localization of CsWRKY29

To elucidate the molecular function of CsWRKY29, organ-specific expression of CsWRKY29 was examined in QC1. The research findings revealed that CsWRKY29 was highly expressed in leaves, especially in mature and old leaves, while its expression is relatively lower in roots, stems, and flowers (Fig. S3). In addition, to determine the subcellular localization of CsWRKY29, Green fluorescent protein (GFP) transformation in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves with the pSuper1300-CsWRKY29-GFP construct was conducted. The result of the transient assay indicated that CsWRKY29 is mainly localized in the nucleus (Fig. 2a). Moreover, pSuper1300-CsWRKY29-GFP construct was overexpressed in Arabidopsis and two independent T3 hygromycin-resistant homozygous overexpression lines (CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2) were used in next analyses (Fig. S4). Furthermore, to determine whether ABA or cold treatment can influence the CsWRKY29 protein localization or signal intensity, CsWRKY29-OE1 seedlings were treated with 50 μM ABA or cold, then examined the fusion protein by confocal microscopy. As the results show, the CsWRKY29-GFP signal was strongly enhanced in the root after 6 h of ABA treatment or cold treatment after 24 h in CsWRKY29-OE1 plants without affecting nucleus localization (Fig. 2b). Overexpression of CsWRKY29 gene improved plant freezing tolerance

To investigate the role of CsWRKY29 in cold stress response, the freezing tolerance of wild-type (WT) and CsWRKY29-OE lines was assessed. Two-week-old seedlings of the overexpression lines and WT, grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium plates at 22 °C, were subjected to freezing treatment following acclimation. The acclimated CsWRKY29-overexpressing lines, OE1 and OE2, demonstrated enhanced tolerance to freezing at − 10 °C compared to the WT (Fig. 3a, b). Statistical analysis revealed that the survival rate of the CsWRKY29-OE transgenic plants was significantly higher than that of the WT plants (Fig. 3c). It has been reported that superoxide dismutases (SOD) can affect ROS homeostasis to regulate plant cold tolerance37. Therefore, the SOD activities in the OE lines were determined. As the result show, the SOD activities were increased in CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants compared to the WT after cold treatment (Fig. 3d). In addition, since the production of O2- and H2O2 molecules in plants can be regulated by SOD, the accumulation of superoxide (O2-) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the OE lines was then further examined. Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining showed high levels of O2- and H2O2 molecules in the OE lines after cold treatment at 4 °C (Fig. 3e and 3f). The ABA response during germination and seedling growth in CsWRKY29 overexpressing plants

As the transcript of CsWRKY29 is induced by ABA, we sought to delve into its role within the ABA signaling pathway by assessing the response of plants overexpressing CsWRKY29 to ABA. Notably, the CsWRKY29-OE lines demonstrated enhanced sensitivity to ABA during the germination phase and subsequent seedling growth (Fig. 5). In comparison to the WT, the CsWRKY29-OE lines exhibited pronounced delays in greening when grown on an ABA-containing medium (Fig. 5a-c). The germination rates of CsWRKY29-OE1 and CsWRKY29-OE2 were reduced to 55% and 32%, respectively, whereas the WT maintained approximately 85% germination after 10 days of ABA treatment. Furthermore, when seedlings were initially grown on MS medium for 4 days and then transferred to MS medium supplemented with 50 μM ABA for an additional 10 days, the overexpression lines developed a more pronounced yellowing of the leaves (Fig. 5d,e). The total chlorophyll content in the CsWRKY29-OE lines was significantly lower compared to that of the WT (Fig. 5f). The expression of ABA associated genes was affected in the OE lines

As the CsWRKY29 overexpressing plants alter freezing tolerance and ABA sensitivity, we wondered whether the transcript of ABA-related genes is affected in the CsWRKY29 overexpressing plants under ABA and cold treatment. The transcript levels of ABA biosynthesis and signaling genes were therefore evaluated, including AtNCED3, AtABA1, AtABI1, AtABI2, and AtABI5 in the WT and CsWRKY29-OE seedlings under ABA treatment. qRT-PCR analysis showed that the levels of AtNCED3 and AtABA1 in the CsWRKY29-OE were similar to those in wild-type plants before or after ABA treatment (Fig. S5). However, the expression of AtABI1 and AtABI2 in CsWRKY29-OE was lower than in the wild-type after ABA treatment (Fig. 6). Intriguingly, the expression of AtABI5 in CsWRKY29-OE was higher than in the WT with or without ABA treatment (Fig. 6). Additionally, we assessed the impact of cold treatment on the expression profiles of AtNCED3, AtABI1, and AtABI5 in the overexpression lines. The findings showed no significant changes in the transcriptional levels of AtABI1 and AtNCED3 between the OE lines and the wild-type controls after a 24-h treatment at 4 °C. However, the expression of AtABI5 was significantly increased under cold treatment (Fig. 6). This suggests that CsWRKY29 may act as a positive regulator of AtABI5 gene expression under ABA and cold stress conditions.Fig. 6Expression of ABA-related genes in the transgenic CsWRKY29 overexpression plants. CsWRKY29 binds to the CsABI5 promoter

Based on the above observations, we postulate that CsWRKY29 may directly bind to the promoter region of CsABI5, thereby facilitating the cold- and ABA-mediated transcription of the CsABI5 gene. The promoter sequence of CsABI5 was successfully amplified from QC1 (Fig. S6), and subsequent bioinformatics analysis identified the presence of five W-box motifs within the promoter region, which are known cis-regulatory elements recognized by WRKY transcription factors (Fig. 7a). To further analyze whether CsWRKY29 interacts with the CsABI5 promoter and regulates its expression, yeast one-hybrid system was conducted. Two domains of the CsABI5 promoter were investigated: domain 1 (pCsABI5−1) covering the first W-box, and domain 2 (pCsABI5-2) covering the third, fourth, and fifth W-box. The EGY48 yeast strain was co-transformed with the empty pB42AD and pLacZi vectors or with pB42AD-CsWRKY29 in conjunction with pLacZi constructs containing various domains of the CsABI5 promoter. The resulting transformant grew on the tryptophan and uracil-deficient SD plate in the presence of X-gal (Fig. 7b). The yeast color development indicated that CsWRKY29 binds specifically to domain 1 (pCsABI5−1) but not to domain 2 (pCsABI5−2) (Fig. 7B). These findings imply that CsWRKY29 can bind to a specific region of the CsABI5 promoter, thereby influencing its activation in response to cold and ABA stimuli.Fig. 7Yeast one-hybrid assay. Reducing CsWRKY29 expression attenuates cold and ABA-responsive gene expression

To determine the impact of CsWRKY29 knockdown on signal transduction, the virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) assay was conducted in QC1 cuttings. Cuttings that had been transformed with the TRV2 vector harboring the CsPDS gene fragment (TRV-CsPDS) served as the positive control, whereas those transformed with the empty TRV2 vector (EV) were used as the negative control. qRT-PCR was used to examine the expression of CsWRKY29 in TRV-CsWRKY29 plants when the leaves of TRV-CsPDS cuttings exhibited a photobleaching phenotype. As anticipated, the transcript levels of CsWRKY29 were significantly reduced in the TRV-CsWRKY29 plants, varying in degree (Fig. 8a, b). Subsequent analyses revealed that the transcript levels of ABA-responsive genes, including CsNCED2, CsNCED3, and CsABI5, were markedly diminished in the CsWRKY29-silenced cuttings (Fig. 8c). Additionally, the cold-induced expression of CsCBF1, CsCBF2, and CsCBF5 was also significantly downregulated in the CsWRKY29-silenced cuttings (Fig. 8d). Changes in CsWRKY29 expression affect sugar accumulation and response

Soluble sugar plays an important role in tea quality and cold adaptation. High sugar levels are often the indicator of freezing resistance38. Interestingly, we found that the concentration of total soluble sugars in the CsWRKY29-OE lines was much higher compared to the WT under normal conditions and cold treatment (Fig. 9a). Moreover, the expression of two sucrose biosynthetic enzymes, AtHXK1 and AtSUS4 was significantly upregulated in the OE lines after cold treatment (Fig. 9b, c). By contrast, the concentration of total soluble sugars in TRV-CsWRKY29 cuttings was much lower compared to the TRV-EV controls under normal conditions and cold treatment (Fig. 9d). Consistently, the transcript of CsHXK1 and CsSUS4 was significantly downregulated in TRV-CsWRKY29 cuttings (Fig. 9e, f). Next, we investigated whether CsWRKY29-OE lines altered sugar response. Interestingly, CsWRKY29-OE lines exhibited increased sugar sensitivity phenotypes after glucose treatment (Fig. 9g-i). Compared with the WT, the relative primary root lengths were much shorter in CsWRKY29-OE lines when germinated in a 7% glucose medium (Fig. 9g, h). Besides, when seedlings were grown on MS medium for 3 d and subsequently transferred to MS medium containing 5% glucose for 7 d, OE lines showed a significantly enhanced anthocyanin accumulation phenotype (Fig. 9i, j). The content of anthocyanin was much higher in the CsWRKY29-OE lines compared to the WT (Fig. 9k). Taken together, these results suggest that CsWRKY29 may associated with sugar metabolism in tea plants.

Discussion

In the plant kingdom, WRKY proteins serve as pivotal signal transducers that exercise precise transcriptional regulation by directly binding to the W-box core sequence found in the promoters of numerous key genes39,40. Collaboratively, these target genes and WRKY proteins effectively modulate a spectrum of biological processes, encompassing seed germination, root development, leaf senescence, disease resistance, and stress adaptation41,42. Previously, CsWRKY29 was reported to participate in cold tolerance of tea plants36. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the essential functions of CsWRKY29 in tea plants remains to be delineated. In this study, we characterized CsWRKY29 from the Guizhou tea cultivar ‘QC1’ and uncovered its significant role in freezing tolerance, ABA response, and sugar metabolism.

Analysis of transcript patterns indicated that CsWRKY29 is predominantly expressed in mature leaves (Fig. S3). Notably, plants overexpressing CsWRKY29 exhibited elevated concentrations of soluble sugars compared to WT plants under both normal conditions and cold stress (Fig. 9a). In agreement, qRT-PCR analyses suggested that the expression of AtHXK1 and AtSUS4 which are involved in sucrose synthesis were upregulated in Arabidopsis overexpressing lines. Consistently, the tea leaves which suppress the CsWRKY29 gene through VIGS technology, reduced the sugar content and inhibited the expression of CsHXK1 and CsSUS4 in tea plants (Fig. 9). These findings suggest that CsWRKY29 may regulate sugar metabolism in response to cold stress. Additionally, SOD enzyme activity was enhanced in the overexpressing lines following cold stress exposure. Consequently, the improved freezing tolerance of transgenic plants may be associated with the increased accumulation of osmolytes and antioxidant enzymes, as these cold-induced metabolites can protect cells against stress43,44. Furthermore, it is well-established that the CBF module is a critical regulatory pathway in cold adaptation45. In this study, we observed that the expression of AtCBF1, AtCBF2, and AtCBF3 was slightly upregulated in the CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants. Additionally, the expression levels of downstream target genes regulated by CBFs, including AtRD29a, AtKIN1, AtCOR47, and AtZAT12, were all significantly upregulated in the CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants. Conversely, tea leaves in which CsWRKY29 was silenced via VIGS showed decreased expression of CsCBF1, CsCBF2, and CsCBF5 under cold stress. These results imply that CsWRKY29 may primarily influence the expression of CBF downstream genes through a non-classical regulatory pathway.

Analysis of transcript expression patterns revealed that CsWRKY29 is induced not only by cold treatment but also by the ABA signal. In addition, we found that CsWRKY29 was mainly localized in the nucleus (Fig. 2). ABA treatment may enhance CsWRKY29 accumulation without changing the nucleus distribution of CsWRKY29. We figured CsWRKY29 mediates freezing tolerance and might connected with ABA. It is not surprising to suggest such a hypothesis, as many reports have shown that WRKY are key nodes in ABA-responsive signaling networks and cold resistance of plants46. This hypothesis is supported by the hypersensitive response of CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants to ABA during seed germination and seedling growth (Fig. 5). Intriguingly, the expression of ABI5 was tightly correlated with the expression of CsWRKY29, especially after ABA and cold treatment (Fig. 6). ABI5 is an important transcription factor which can regulate ABA-induced cold tolerance through gene transcription regulation and protein modification17. Previously, overexpression of ABI5 in tomato plants improved cold tolerance47. Consistent with this, we found that the expression of ABI5 was dramatically increased in CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants (Fig. 6). It is noteworthy that ABI5 can positively regulate COR gene transcription in rice. Consistently, the transcription of COR genes, including AtRD29a, AtCOR47, and AtZAT12, were all upregulated and positively correlated with the expression of AtABI5 and CsWRKY29 in CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants. The high transcript level of AtABI5 and COR genes may partially account for the freezing tolerance of OE lines. By contrast, the tea leaves which suppress the CsWRKY29 gene through VIGS technology, significantly reduced the transcript level of CsABI5. These results raised the question of whether CsWRKY29 can regulate CsABI5 expression through binding the W-box sequences in the CsABI5 promoter. Sequence analysis and yeast one-hybrid system confirmed that CsWRKY29 can directly bind the promoter of CsABI5, which might lead to the activation of CsABI5 during tea cold adaption. Based above results, we propose that CsWRKY29 may function as a positive regulator in the crosstalk between the freezing tolerance and the ABA signaling pathway in tea plants.

Studies have suggested that alterations in ABA synthesis and signaling can result in fluctuations of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anion48. In the present study, we noted an accumulation of H2O2 and superoxide anion in CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants. Arabidopsis mutants such as chs2 and chs3 have been shown to accumulate excessive ROS and exhibit increased cold sensitivity49,50. In contrast, mutants like amp1 overproduce superoxide anion but less H2O2 and demonstrate a freezing-tolerant phenotype51. Establishing a quantitative relationship between the level of ROS accumulation and freezing resistance remains challenging. This complexity may arise because cellular responses are the cumulative result of multiple signaling pathways52. In plants, ABA plays a role in regulating ROS homeostasis; for instance, the ABA-hypersensitive mutant abo6 activates ABA-induced ROS accumulation in mitochondria in Arabidopsis53. Additionally, high levels of ABA and ROS are observed in mung bean seedlings as an adaptive response to environmental stress54. However, the precise relationship between the activation of the ABA pathway and ROS accumulation remains an intricate puzzle. In CsWRKY29-OE1 and OE2 plants, the observed ROS imbalance is likely due to the enhanced activation of the ABA signaling pathway resulting from overexpression.

Interestingly, we found changes in CsWRKY29 expression also affect sugar accumulation and response. Sugars not only act as energy providers but are also committed to signaling molecules55. The identification of many glucose-insensitivity mutants, which are allelic to ABA-deficient or ABA-sensitive mutants, implies the close connection between ABA signaling and sugar signaling56. For example, ABA-signaling mutants abi5 is insensitive to sugar, this result indicates that activation of ABI5 may lead to high glucose sensitivity57. In this study, our results suggest that CsWRKY29 can directly bind the promoter of ABI5 and attribute to its cold-induced activation, which in turn may result in high glucose sensitivity with overaccumulation of anthocyanin phenotype (Fig. 7 and 9). The above results indicated that CsWRKY29 may play a central role in freezing tolerance linking ABA and sugar signaling.

In summary, we proposed a working model depicting the role of CsWRKY29 in tea plants (Fig. 10). During cold stress, the CsWRKY29 gene is induced rapidly by cold stress which leads to the activation transcription of cold-responsive genes, ABA-associated genes, and sucrose synthesis-related genes, then result in the accumulation of the osmolyte and the ROS system, at last attributed to the freezing tolerance of tea plants. Conclusion

The transcript level of CsWRKY29 was induced by both cold and ABA signals in the tea plant. Overexpression of CsWRKY29 improved freezing tolerance by increasing sugar contents and antioxidant enzyme activity in Arabidopsis. Besides, overexpression of CsWRKY29 also enhanced sensitivity to ABA and glucose during germination and seedling growth. A combination of phenotype observations, protein localization, physiological analysis, gene expression, promoter binding assay, and virus-induced gene silencing technology suggested that CsWRKY29 acts as a positive regulator of sugar metabolism under cold stress, activating the expression of CsABI5 and COR cascade genes and thereby affected freezing tolerance of plants.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth condition

Qiancha 1 tea plants and Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia (Col-0) plants were employed in this study. Tea plants were cultivated in the soil in the germplasm resources repositories located at Guizhou University or grown in a greenhouse at 25 °C (16-h light/8-h dark)58. The Arabidopsis wild type and overexpression transgenic lines were grown in soil in a greenhouse at 22 °C (16-h light) and 22 °C (8-h dark) or on MS medium (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 2% sucrose and 0.8% agar at 22 °C.

RNA extraction and quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tea plants or Arabidopsis plants using an RNA Extraction Kit (Omega). qRT-PCR was performed using a 2 × SYBR Green Power PCR Master Mix kit (Genestar) as previously described59. Values are the means ± standard deviations (SD) of three repeats, with Student’s t-test used for statistical analysis60. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Plasmid construction and subcellular localization

To overexpress CsWRKY29 in Arabidopsis, the coding sequences of CsWRKY29 were amplified by PCR from QC1 tea plants and cloned in-frame into the binary vector pSuper1300-GFP61. The resulting construct was then introduced into Arabidopsis Col-0 by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The independent transgenic lines were selected on MS medium containing 50 g·L−1 hygromycin, and the T3 homozygous lines were used for the phenotypic analysis. GFP fluorescence was imaged as previously described, using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany)62. For yeast one-hybrid analysis, the coding sequences of CsWRKY29 were amplified by PCR, and cloned in-frame into the vector pB42AD. The different promoter regions of CsABI5 were cloned into the pLacZi reporter vector separately to generate pLacZi-CsABI5−1 and pLacZi-CsABI5-constructs. For VIGS analysis, the pTRV2-CsPDS and pTRV2-CsWRKY29 vectors were constructed individually. A 278 bp specific fragment of CsPDS and a 292 bp specific fragment of CsWRKY29 were cloned into the pTRV2 vector respectively with EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Freezing and ABA tolerance assays

The freezing and ABA tolerance assay was carried out as described51. For the freezing assay, 2-week-old cold acclimated Arabidopsis plants were exposed to a freezing temperature of − 10 °C for 2 h, then placed at 4 °C in the dark for 12 h before being transferred to 22 °C. The survival rates were calculated after a 2-day recovery period. In the ABA germination assay, Arabidopsis seeds were directly germinated on 1/2 MS media containing 0 or 0.5 μM ABA. For the seedling ABA tolerance assay, Arabidopsis seedlings were initially grown on 1/2 MS medium for 4 d and then transferred to MS medium containing 50 μM ABA for 10 d.

Physiological analyses and measurement of ROS in plants

The total soluble sugar content was measured as described63. The chlorophyll content of the sample was determined as described64. The anthocyanin content was determined as described65. NBT and DAB staining with seedlings were performed to detect superoxide as described previously66.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

Yeast one-hybrid analysis was performed as described in a previous report67. The pB42AD-CsWRKY29 construct was co-transformed with pLacZi-CsABI5−1 and pLacZi-CsABI5−2 constructs into yeast strain EGY48, respectively. Transformants were cultured on SD-Trp/-Ura plates at 30 °C for 3 d, followed by transfer into 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-b-d-galactopyranoside plates for blue color reaction. The empty pB42AD combination with the pLacZi reporter constructs was used as negative controls.

Virus-Induced gene silencing

VIGS was conducted as previously described with minor modifications68. Using a solution containing 4.74 g·L−1 of MS, 2 mol·L−1 of 6-BA, 2 mol·L−1 of acetosyringone (AS), 100 µmol·L−1 of 1-naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), and 16 g·L−1 of MgCl2 to suspend Agrobacterium GV3101 cells. VIGS experiment in this using the vacuum infiltration method for Agrobacterium infection with tea plant shoot. Agrobacterium solution containing pTRV1/pTRV2 empty vector as the control, an agrobacterium mixture containing pTRV1/pTRV2 CsPDS as the positive control, Agrobacterium solution containing pTRV1/pTRV2 CsWRKY29 as the treatment group. Cuttings were incubated in the greenhouse with a 16/8 light cycle at 25 °C. Samples were collected after 4–6 weeks of hydroponic growth, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent gene analysis.
