YouTube as a source of patient education on scaphoid fractures: A quality, transparency, and educational content analysis
Authors: Ryan Nishi, Kirk Terada-Herzer, Tyler Shimabukuro, Sean Chan, Kyle Obana, Kyle Ishikawa, Matthew Nishimura, Christina Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScaphoid fractures are the most common carpal fractures and often affect young, active individuals. Patients frequently turn to online media for medical guidance, where quality and transparency vary widely.PurposeTo evaluate the transparency, comprehensibility, and educational value of YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures using established and condition-specific assessment tools.MethodsThe fifty most-viewed English-language YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures were analyzed for content source, engagement metrics, and quality using the JAMA benchmark criteria, a 4-point Likert comprehensibility scale, and a Scaphoid Fracture-Specific Score (SFS-SS).ResultsPhysicians produced over half of the videos (50%), followed by non-physician health professionals (24%) and trainers/therapists (12%). The median JAMA transparency score was 2, the median comprehensibility score was 2, and the median SFS-SS was 8 out of 16. Physician-created videos had higher JAMA scores (p < 0.001) but lower comprehensibility than non-physician sources (p = 0.016).ConclusionYouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures demonstrated limited transparency, modest comprehensibility, and incomplete educational coverage. Physician-created videos were common but did not consistently provide more comprehensive patient-oriented information. Improved online educational content may require clearer authorship, evidence citation, and patient-centered communication.Level of evidenceLevel V

Introduction

Scaphoid fractures are the most common carpal fractures and frequently affect young, active individuals.1 Their retrograde blood supply increases the risk that missed or delayed diagnoses result in nonunion and avascular necrosis.2,3 Although many nondisplaced fractures heal with immobilization, displaced or unstable injuries may require surgery.4,5 Because management decisions often involve imaging, immobilization, operative indications, and return-to-activity timelines, patients frequently seek supplemental educational resources outside the clinic.6

With the increase in access to online information, patients, particularly younger patients, often turn to the internet as a convenient and immediate source of medical information. Online platforms offer accessibility unmatched by traditional healthcare encounters, but patients often place undue trust in published content regardless of its transparency or authorship.7 Because most digital media lack peer review or editorial oversight, particularly on social platforms where influencers can freely share medical advice, the quality of information available to the public is highly variable.8 YouTube, with billions of monthly logged-in users, represents one of the most influential repositories of online health information.9

Prior analyses of orthopedic topics have raised concerns regarding the transparency and comprehensibility of online resources.10, 11, 12, 13 Studies across multiple subspecialties, including posterior cruciate ligament injuries,14 acromioclavicular joint pathology,15 reverse shoulder arthroplasty,16 and femoroacetabular impingement,17 consistently demonstrate that patients often rely on low-quality online material. Despite the prevalence of wrist injuries and the clinical importance of scaphoid fractures, no prior study has systematically evaluated the educational quality of YouTube videos addressing this condition.

The purpose of this study was therefore to evaluate the transparency, comprehensibility, and educational value of YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures using established and condition-specific assessment tools.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was not required because the study did not involve human or animal subjects.10 On August 11, 2025, the YouTube platform was searched using the terms “scaphoid fracture,” “scaphoid injury,” “carpal navicular fracture,” and “snuffbox injury.” Searches were conducted in a logged-out browser session using incognito mode in Google Chrome, United States region settings, English-language results, and default relevance sorting to reduce personalization bias. Exclusion criteria included duplicate uploads, unrelated or non-English videos, YouTube Shorts, and videos shorter than 30 seconds.18

After screening, 104 unique videos were identified (Fig. 1). Consistent with prior orthopedic YouTube methodology, the 50 most-viewed eligible videos were selected for detailed analysis to reflect the content most likely to be encountered by patients and to permit comparison with prior studies in the literature.10,12 No a priori power calculation was performed because this study was designed as a cross-sectional descriptive content analysis using a predefined sampling frame rather than population-level prevalence estimation. Two evaluators (RN and KTH) independently reviewed each video using standardized scoring criteria. Any scoring discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third author (SC).Fig. 1Flow diagram demonstrating the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final inclusion of YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures.

Flow diagram demonstrating the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final inclusion of YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures.

Video characteristics (duration, upload date, views, likes, dislikes, and source) were recorded. Engagement was quantified using the Video Power Index (VPI) = (like ratio × view ratio)/100.13 Video source categories were mutually exclusive and assigned based on uploader identity: physician, non-physician health professional, academic institution, trainer/therapist (considered a subset of non-physician health professionals but analyzed as a distinct category due to differences in content focus and presentation), or patient. Each video was assigned one primary content category based on the dominant topic occupying the greatest proportion of runtime and emphasized in the title or description. Content type was defined as general scaphoid fracture education, rehabilitation exercises, surgical technique, patient experience, or non-surgical management. General scaphoid fracture education included content covering epidemiology, anatomy, diagnosis, and management rather than procedural or experiential material. Rehabilitation exercise videos focused on postoperative recovery, mobility, strengthening, or return-to-function exercises. Surgical technique videos primarily demonstrated operative approaches, fixation methods, intraoperative footage, or surgeon-directed procedural instruction. Patient experience videos consisted of first-person accounts from individuals describing their injury, treatment course, recovery process, or personal outcomes. Nonoperative management videos emphasized cast treatment, immobilization, conservative care strategies, or observation without surgery. Quality was assessed using three systems:1.JAMA Benchmark Criteria (0–4) for transparency which evaluate four domains of information transparency: authorship, attribution, currency, and disclosure19 (Table 1). Each video received one point per domain met, yielding a total score from 0 to 4. Authorship credit was awarded only when a presenter's full name and professional credentials (e.g., MD, DO, PT) were clearly displayed in the video or description. Attribution required citation of references or sources of medical information. Currency was fulfilled when an upload date or update date was visible. Disclosure was credited only if financial conflicts, sponsorships, or institutional affiliations were explicitly mentioned. The normality of each review score was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk's normality test. Since no score was normally distributed, Kruskal-Wallis' rank sum test was used to detect overall differences and Wilcoxon's rank sum test was used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. P-values of the post-hoc analyses were adjusted using Holm's method to reduce the false discovery rate. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p value < 0.05. Inter-rater reliability between the two independent evaluators was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0.

Discussion

This study evaluated the transparency, comprehensibility, and educational completeness of YouTube videos related to scaphoid fractures. Although physicians produced more than half of the videos, one of the higher proportions reported among orthopedic YouTube analyses, their educational quality did not significantly exceed that of non-physician sources. The median JAMA score (2) indicated modest transparency, and the median comprehensibility score (2) reflected limited accessibility for general audiences. The median SFS-SS (8 of 16) suggested incomplete coverage of essential topics. Additionally, good to excellent inter-rater reliability across all scoring systems supports the reproducibility of the study methodology.

The 50% physician share observed here sits toward the upper–middle of previously reported ranges. For instance, physicians comprised 42% of uploaders in patellar dislocation videos,12 22% in carpal tunnel syndrome,21 and 88% in thoracic outlet syndrome.11 The relatively high representation in scaphoid fracture content may reflect the nature of the condition, a single-bone injury managed primarily by surgeons rather than multidisciplinary teams. Unlike ACL or UCL injuries that involve extended rehabilitation and multiple provider types, scaphoid fractures typically follow a streamlined care pathway.22, 23, 24 Consequently, non-physician contributors such as trainers or physical therapists may produce fewer videos in this domain.

The median view count (40 k) lies in the moderate range compared with other orthopedic YouTube topics. Rotator cuff videos, for example, average 317 k views,20 and cartilage restoration content 87 k.24 However, engagement on YouTube is highly skewed: in one carpal tunnel study, a single video accounted for 66% of total views25. The present distribution suggests sustained but specialized interest rather than viral reach, likely reflecting the niche and transient nature of scaphoid injuries.

Although physicians produced the majority of scaphoid-related videos, their educational performance did not substantially exceed that of other creators. Only 4% of all videos fulfilled all four JAMA criteria, and nearly half met only two (Table 7). Among physician-created videos, missing JAMA points most commonly reflected absent citation of sources and lack of financial or sponsorship disclosure, whereas authorship and currency were usually satisfied. This distribution mirrors prior orthopedic YouTube analyses,10,13 emphasizing persistent gaps in authorship transparency, citation, and disclosure. The median JAMA score (2) and the predominance of mid-tier transparency reinforce the platform's limited quality control. Taken together, these patterns indicate that shortcomings in transparency, rather than lack of expertise, are a primary limitation of physician-generated scaphoid fracture content on YouTube. Clinician-created content may target peers or trainees rather than lay audiences, leading to greater use of jargon and less emphasis on stepwise patient education.

As shown in Fig. 2, the educational focus of most videos centered on anatomy (90%) and etiology (72%), whereas far fewer discussed outcomes (38%), patient population (24%), physical therapy (14%), or pharmacotherapy (2%). This imbalance likely reflects the relative consensus surrounding foundational topics such as anatomy and injury mechanism, whereas recovery timelines, rehabilitation strategies, and outcome expectations remain more variable and less standardized across practice. Physician-authored content often highlights surgical fixation or intraoperative footage, material valuable to peers but less informative for patients seeking guidance on healing timelines or activity restrictions. Similar topic imbalances have been reported in other orthopedic YouTube analyses, where foundational concepts such as anatomy, symptoms, and mechanisms of injury are more frequently discussed than recovery expectations, rehabilitation protocols, or long-term outcomes. Studies evaluating rotator cuff disease, patellar dislocation, and other musculoskeletal conditions have likewise demonstrated inconsistent coverage of postoperative care and return-to-function counseling12, 20, 25. These recurring patterns suggest that highly viewed orthopedic videos often prioritize introductory or procedural information over the longitudinal concerns most relevant to patients.

The combination of modest JAMA performance and incomplete topic coverage illustrates a broader trend: orthopedic content on YouTube remains procedurally oriented, lacking comprehensive, evidence-based explanation of diagnosis, management, and rehabilitation. Non-physician educators occasionally achieve higher comprehensiveness by presenting structured, stepwise summaries, but their videos less frequently meet transparency-based quality criteria, particularly with respect to citation of sources and disclosure of credentials or financial relationships. Collectively, these patterns reinforce the need for collaboration between surgeons, educators, and communication specialists to develop understandable and accurate online resources.

The findings of this study highlight a critical issue for both clinicians and patients: while YouTube is among the most accessible and popular health information platforms, it is not necessarily a consistently accurate comprehensive source for medical education about scaphoid fractures. Unlike peer-reviewed journals or professional society websites, YouTube lacks editorial oversight, peer review, and standardized quality control. As a result, misinformation, omission of key clinical details, and inconsistent terminology are common.10,13,19 Patients may encounter conflicting advice regarding immobilization duration, imaging recommendations, or surgical indications, potentially leading to delayed diagnosis, inappropriate self-management, or unrealistic recovery expectations. This concern is particularly relevant for scaphoid fractures, which are prone to nonunion and avascular necrosis when inadequately diagnosed or treated.2,3 A patient who relies on an oversimplified or inaccurate video may underestimate the injury's severity or the need for follow-up imaging, increasing the risk of poor outcomes. While the accessibility of online video content can enhance public awareness, it may also foster a false sense of medical understanding if the information is incomplete or biased.

From a clinical perspective, orthopedic surgeons and healthcare providers should recognize that patients are increasingly exposed to online information before their first encounter.14,17 Actively discussing common misconceptions, recommending vetted educational resources, and encouraging open dialogue about online content can help mitigate these risks. At the same time, orthopedic societies and academic institutions have an opportunity to produce accurate, high-quality, and patient-centered content to fill this educational gap. Videos produced in collaboration with medical professionals, health communicators, and patient educators could help bridge the transparency gap while maintaining accessibility and engagement.

Ultimately, YouTube's reach makes it an invaluable platform for disseminating orthopedic education, but the current landscape prioritizes visibility over veracity. A shift toward evidence-based video production, emphasizing transparency, educational quality, and accessibility, could significantly improve the quality of online musculoskeletal health information. Until such improvements are achieved, clinicians should caution patients that YouTube, while informative, should not substitute for professional evaluation or personalized treatment planning.

This study has several limitations. First, the JAMA benchmark criteria were developed for evaluation of online health information broadly and were not specifically designed for social media or video-based platforms such as YouTube. As a result, these criteria may incompletely capture platform-specific features of information quality and should be interpreted as measures of transparency rather than factual accuracy or overall educational merit. Importantly, higher transparency scores do not necessarily indicate factual accuracy, as videos may still present incomplete or incorrect information despite clearly identifying authorship or sources. Second, the study evaluated only the 50 most-viewed English-language videos at a single time point. Although this sampling strategy reflects content most likely to be encountered by patients and is consistent with prior orthopedic YouTube studies, it may not represent the full range of available videos and may introduce selection bias. Third, comprehensibility was assessed using a 4-point Likert scale by medically trained reviewers, which may not fully reflect how lay patients interpret or understand video content. Fourth, the Scaphoid Fracture-Specific Score was adapted from prior orthopedic YouTube frameworks but has not yet undergone external validation. Finally, this study did not directly assess factual accuracy, guideline concordance, or patient outcomes associated with video viewing. Future work incorporating validated patient-facing readability or comprehensibility tools, platform-specific quality metrics, and formal accuracy-based assessments may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of online scaphoid fracture education.

Conclusion

Among commonly viewed YouTube videos on scaphoid fractures, overall transparency, comprehensibility, and educational completeness were limited. Physician-created videos were common but did not consistently provide more patient-centered or comprehensive educational content. Improved online resources should emphasize transparent authorship, evidence citation, plain-language communication, and balanced discussion of diagnosis, treatment, and recovery.

Patient consent

This study analyzed publicly available online content and did not involve human participants, patient data, or identifiable personal information. Therefore, patient or guardian consent was not required.
