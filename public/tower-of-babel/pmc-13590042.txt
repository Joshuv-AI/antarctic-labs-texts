Salvage therapy for radiorecurrent prostate cancer: beyond equipoise – a call for biomarker-driven stratification
Authors: Qing-Hua Ke, Shi-Qiong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Salvage focal therapy and radical prostatectomy show similar cancer-specific survival but differ in complications.•Lack of PSMA PET/CT and genomic risk assessment limits generalizability of current comparisons.•A biomarker-driven, patient-centered approach (including reirradiation) is needed for personalized management.Keywords: Radiorecurrent prostate cancer, Salvage therapy, Biomarker stratification, PSMA PET/CT, Decipher classifier, Reirradiation

The management of localized radiorecurrent prostate cancer is an increasing clinical challenge, driven by more frequent use of primary radiotherapy and improved post-treatment surveillance. Light et al [1] provide a timely and methodologically sound matched comparison of salvage focal therapy (sFT) versus salvage radical prostatectomy (sRP), concluding that both offer comparable 10-year cancer-specific survival, while sFT leads to significantly fewer perioperative complications. Nikitas and Vapiwala [2] rightly highlight lingering concerns about unmeasured confounders and the omission of reirradiation as a comparator. In our view, the central question should shift from which salvage modality is better to which patient is best suited for which approach — and what biological or technical factors might tip the balance.

The study by Light et al. is commendable for its large, international, matched design. From a clinical oncologist‘s perspective, however, several unaddressed variables could alter the risk-benefit calculus. First, the cohort was treated largely before the widespread availability of PSMA PET/CT. Contemporary restaging with PSMA PET/CT would likely downstage a proportion of patients presumed to have localized recurrence, excluding those with occult metastases from local salvage altogether. That selection shift would probably improve survival outcomes in both arms but might also unmask a differential benefit: sRP, as a whole-gland therapy, could theoretically offer better control of multifocal radiorecurrent disease that escapes even PSMA PET/CT detection [3], whereas sFT might miss ipsilateral or contralateral low-volume recurrence. Nevertheless, PSMA PET/CT has limited sensitivity for microscopic or small-volume lesions (<5 mm) and false-positive findings can occur due to inflammation or benign prostatic hyperplasia, which may lead to unnecessary treatment or overtargeting [4]. Without histopathological mapping validation post-PSMA PET/CT, the claim of equivalent cancer-specific survival remains uncertain.

Second, the study does not incorporate genomic risk stratification. Radiorecurrent tumors are not biologically homogeneous. Emerging data indicate that the Decipher genomic classifier can independently predict metastasis and prostate cancer-specific mortality after biochemical failure. In the salvage setting, a high Decipher score might favor a more radical approach (sRP or whole-gland reirradiation) over focal ablation, regardless of apparent lesion confinement. However, no validated threshold for “high risk” currently exists specifically for radiorecurrent disease. Most available studies use a cutoff of 0.6 derived from primary radiation cohorts, and extrapolating this threshold requires caution. We therefore emphasize that recommending a Decipher score ≥0.6 to lean toward whole-gland therapy is a research proposal, not a clinical standard. Prospective validation of an optimal threshold in the salvage setting is urgently needed. The absence of such biomarker integration in Light et al.‘s matching limits the generalizability of their “therapeutic ratio” conclusion to genomically high-risk radiorecurrent disease. We argue that the next critical step is not a randomized trial of sFT versus sRP per se, but a prospective registry or trial stratifying by both imaging (PSMA PET/CT) and genomic risk (e.g., Decipher with predefined thresholds to be validated), with coprimary endpoints of metastasis-free survival and patient-reported functional outcomes.

Third, the time-varying effect on overall survival — where sRP was associated with worse survival within 5 years but better survival after 5 years — is intriguing but under-explored. The authors suggest treatment year effects may not explain this, but they did not adjust for cumulative comorbidity burden (e.g., Charlson score) or prior androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) duration. Light et al. did include treatment year (2000–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2024) in their extended Cox model and found no statistically significant interaction. Nevertheless, the absence of an interaction with treatment year does not rule out other time-varying factors, such as improvements in perioperative care, patient selection, or surgical experience over time. Patients undergoing sRP in earlier eras (2000–2009) had higher perioperative morbidity, which could drive early mortality, whereas contemporary robotic sRP series report lower complication rates. The time-varying hazard might still reflect surgical learning curves and better perioperative care rather than an intrinsic oncologic advantage of sRP after 5 years. A sensitivity analysis restricted to patients treated after 2015, when robotic sRP dominated, would be revealing.

A further unconsidered factor is the cumulative radiation dose to organs at risk. Primary radiotherapy doses vary widely (74–80 Gy for EBRT, 110–145 Gy for brachytherapy), and this variation could influence both the severity of radiation-induced fibrosis and the subsequent risk of salvage complications. Light et al. only categorize primary treatment type (EBRT, brachytherapy, or both), not dose or fractionation. Higher biologically effective doses are associated with more dense periprostatic fibrosis, potentially increasing sRP morbidity and complicating HIFU/cryotherapy energy delivery. Future comparative effectiveness research should capture radiation dosimetry to refine patient selection for salvage modalities.

Finally, where does reirradiation fit? Nikitas and Vapiwala correctly raise this omission. Emerging data from prospective series show that salvage stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) or high-dose-rate (HDR) brachytherapy for locally recurrent prostate cancer yields 5-year biochemical control rates of 60–70% with predominantly grade 1–2 genitourinary toxicity and rare grade 3+ events [5,6]. Notably, reirradiation can be delivered focally (hemigland) or to the whole gland, offering a third pathway that may combine the lower toxicity of sFT with the whole-gland coverage of sRP. For consistency, we use the term “salvage reirradiation” throughout to encompass both SBRT and HDR brachytherapy approaches. A three-arm comparison (sFT vs. sRP vs. salvage reirradiation) using modern image guidance and patient-reported outcomes would be far more clinically informative than the current binary framing.

To move the field forward, we need more than a list of unanswered questions. A practical next step would be a multicenter prospective registry or a phase II/III trial design with the following elements: biomarker stratification (PSMA PET/CT status plus Decipher score using a prespecified cutoff — to be prospectively validated), 3 p.m. (sFT, sRP, and salvage reirradiation), coprimary endpoints (metastasis-free survival at 5 years and patient-reported genitourinary/bowel function at 1 year), and sample size estimation assuming a non-inferiority margin for metastasis-free survival between sFT and sRP (hazard ratio ≤1.25). Based on published salvage series, we anticipate a 5-year metastasis-free survival of approximately 75–80% in the sRP arm. With a one-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, a non-inferiority margin of HR ≤1.25 would require approximately 300 events, translating to roughly 600–700 patients total across the three arms, assuming equal allocation and 5 years of accrual with 2 years of additional follow-up. These estimates are conservative and should be refined as more precise data from contemporary salvage cohorts become available. A formal sample size calculation with interim monitoring would be essential in the final protocol. Such a design would directly address whether genomic risk and imaging volume should dictate modality selection, and would also capture comparative cost-effectiveness over 10 years, including quality-adjusted life-years.

In conclusion, Light et al. have provided an invaluable benchmark, but the field must now move beyond modality-centric comparisons toward biomarker- and imaging-stratified, patient-centered decision-making. For the practicing oncologist seeing a patient with radiorecurrent prostate cancer tomorrow, we would recommend:

Obtain PSMA PET/CT to exclude extraprostatic disease, while being aware of its limited sensitivity for small-volume lesions and the possibility of false positives.

Consider genomic testing of the recurrent biopsy — recognizing that no validated threshold exists for radiorecurrent disease; based on primary radiation data, a Decipher score ≥0.6 may lean toward whole-gland therapy (sRP or reirradiation), whereas lower scores make sFT reasonable, but prospective validation is needed.

Assess baseline functional status — patients with moderate-to-severe urinary symptoms or poor baseline erectile function may tolerate sRP poorly; sFT or reirradiation is preferable.
