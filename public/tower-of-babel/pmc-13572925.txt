Percutaneous Transhepatic Biliary Drainage Tract Embolization Using Contrast Labelled Gelfoam Plug in Malignant Biliary Obstruction: An Observational Study
Authors: Soumil Singhal, Anurag Mehndiratta, Apoorva Batra, Pallav Bhatter, Vrishit Saraswat, Anubhav Khandelwal, Sanjay Saran Baijal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To evaluate the feasibility, safety, and clinical efficacy of percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage tract embolization using a contrast-labeled Gelfoam plug in patients with malignant biliary obstruction following stenting.Materials and Methods: Between February 2021 and August 2025, 54 patients with malignant biliary obstruction underwent 84 percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage procedures with tract embolization using a Gelfoam plug. The plug was prepared as a cylindrical roll preloaded into a vascular sheath, with radio-opaque contrast medium (Lipiodol) applied at both ends for fluoroscopic visualization. Fluoroscopy, occasionally supplemented with ultrasound guidance, was used to position the plug so that it extended to the liver capsule. Technical success was defined as complete occlusion of the percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage tract, assessed by fluoroscopic imaging performed immediately after plug deployment. Clinical success was defined as the absence of bile leakage, hemorrhage, or other complications throughout the hospital stay. Minor and major complications were recorded and classified according to Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society of Europe standards.Results: Technical success was achieved in all 84 procedures (100%). Clinical success was observed in 87% (47/54) of patients. Two patients experienced minor self-limiting tract bleeding requiring no intervention. Five patients developed pleural effusion or ascites (1 and 4, respectively) requiring drainage and prolonged hospitalization by a mean of 4.5 ± 0.9 days, attributed to plug displacement on follow-up.Conclusion: Gelfoam plug embolization of percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage tracts is a safe, effective, and straightforward technique to prevent bile leakage and hemorrhage after biliary stenting in malignant obstruction. This method offers an efficient alternative to agent-based embolic techniques such as N-butyl cyanoacrylate glue or metallic coils with minimal morbidity.

Introduction

Malignant biliary obstruction often requires decompression of the biliary system using stents or drains to relieve jaundice and prevent cholangitis. In cases where percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) is performed, an iatrogenic tract is created from the biliary tree to the skin surface. Tract embolization refers to the deliberate occlusion of this iatrogenic access tract, with the goal of preventing complications related to the open tract. These complications can include bleeding, bile leakage, infection, and even tumor cell seeding along the tract. Bile leakage from the PTBD tract after catheter removal may cause biliary peritonitis or subcapsular biloma, potentially necessitating additional interventions or prolonging hospital stay.

Various embolic materials have been reported, including NBCA glue, coils, fibrin sealant, and gelatin sponge (gelfoam) [1-11]. Gelfoam, an absorbable gelatin sponge, offers hemostatic and occlusive properties, is widely available and inexpensive, and is resorbed over several weeks, making it an attractive option.

Although gelatin sponge embolization of PTBD tracts has been previously described, prior techniques have relied on uncontrolled pledget deployment without real-time fluoroscopic documentation of plug extent. The present study describes a standardized technique using a precisely fashioned cylindrical plug with contrast marking and sheath-based delivery, designed to address the limitations of conventional approaches. In this observational study, we report our experience using a Gelfoam plug preloaded into a vascular sheath with radiopaque contrast markings at both ends for enhanced fluoroscopic visualization, combined with occasional ultrasound guidance to ensure accurate deployment extending to the hepatic capsule. We aimed to assess the feasibility, technical and clinical success rates, and complications associated with the contrast-labeled Gelfoam plug technique in patients with malignant biliary obstruction undergoing PTBD and stenting.

Materials and Methods

This single-center observational study was conducted from February 2021 to August 2025. Inclusion criteria comprised adult patients (>18 years) with malignant biliary obstruction requiring PTBD and biliary stenting. Exclusion criteria included coagulopathy uncorrectable before intervention (INR >1.5, platelet count <80,000/μL), severe ascites precluding safe percutaneous access, or patient refusal.

A total of 54 patients (32 males, 22 females; mean age 63.7 ± 9.4 years) underwent PTBD with biliary stenting followed by tract embolization. Malignancy distribution was as follows: cholangiocarcinoma (n = 22), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (n = 18), gallbladder carcinoma (n = 8), and metastatic carcinoma to the liver (n = 6). Some patients required multiple PTBD catheters, resulting in 84 embolized tracts (Table 1).

PTBD and stenting procedure

Under ultrasound guidance, the biliary duct was punctured, and the stenotic segment was negotiated using a 4F or 5F catheter (Cobra or KMP, respectively). An appropriately sized vascular sheath was placed over an Amplatzer wire, and a transpapillary self-expanding metal stents (E-Luminexx, Bard Peripheral Vascular Inc., Tempe, AZ, USA; WALLSTENT Endoprosthesis, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) was subsequently deployed. Abdominal radiograph and ultrasound assessment were performed successively to assess adequate stent expansion and biliary decompression. After confirming adequate biliary decompression of the drained segments, tract embolization was performed during catheter removal.

Criteria for external drain removal

The criteria for PTBD tube removal and tract embolization were: (1) technically adequate stent placement with biliary decompression confirmed on ultrasonography; (2) clinical improvement with resolution of jaundice and no evidence of cholangitis; (3) normalization or a significant downward trend in serum bilirubin; and (4) a negative clamping trial, defined as the absence of cholangitis following catheter clamping. The decision to remove the tube was made by the interventional radiology team in conjunction with the primary referring team.

Gelfoam plug preparation and embolization technique

On the day of track embolization, absorbable gelatin sponge sheets (AbGel, SGK Labs Pvt. Ltd., Vadodara, India) were first cut into rectangular blocks measuring approximately 5 × 5 × 40 mm using sterile scissors, and then rolled and compressed into a cylindrical configuration. The cylindrical plugs were tailored to match the PTBD tract diameter. Each plug was prepared and preloaded into a 6F vascular sheath, and a small drop of radiopaque contrast (Lipiodol) was applied to both the proximal and distal ends by piercing the sheath using a 22G hypodermic needle to provide radiopacity (Video 1, Supplementary file). We used Lipiodol (Guerbet, Villepinte, France) in our group for better fluoroscopic visualization of the ends of the plug. The plug was then transferred from the 6F sheath to the indwelling 8F sheath using a pusher. The pusher was fashioned from a 6F dilator with the hub cut off to create a blunt pushing device (Video 2, Supplementary file). A two-sheath system was used to (1) maintain structural integrity and prevent breakage of the Gelfoam column, (2) contrast tagging of the ends, and (3) allow controlled transfer of the preloaded embolic, preventing premature contact with tissue fluids. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the plug was advanced into the PTBD tract and positioned precisely from the biliary access site to the hepatic capsule, ensuring complete tract occlusion. Correct placement was confirmed by fluoroscopic visualization of the marked plug (Figure 1). One or more plugs were used depending on the length of the tract. Ultrasound was used in selected cases to confirm adequate plug positioning, serving as an adjunct rather than as primary real-time guidance. Lipiodol produces a highly echogenic interface owing to its lipid composition, rendering the plug ends visible as hyperechoic foci on greyscale sonography. Manual compression was applied following deployment, and patients were monitored with USG for any evidence of bile leakage, hemorrhage, or other procedure-related complications.

Outcome measures

Technical success was defined as complete occlusion of the PTBD tract, assessed by fluoroscopic imaging performed immediately after plug deployment with the 8F sheath fully withdrawn to the skin surface, together with the absence of bile extravasation from the access site on ultrasonography performed immediately afterward. Clinical success was defined as the absence of bile leakage, hemorrhage, biliary peritonitis, or abscess formation on serial daily ultrasonography throughout the hospital stay. Procedure-related complications were categorized as major or minor according to the Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society of Europe (CIRSE) classification [12]. The duration of hospital stay was recorded from the time of PTBD placement to patient discharge.

Follow-up

Patients were monitored clinically during hospitalization with daily assessments for pain, fever, drainage output, and laboratory markers. Imaging (ultrasound or CT) was performed if complications were suspected. Follow-up continued until hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26 (IBM Corp.). No comparative statistics were performed due to the lack of a control group.

Results

Fifty-four patients underwent 84 PTBD procedures followed by tract embolization. PTBD access was obtained via the right hepatic lobe in 39 patients (72.2%) and via the left hepatic lobe in 15 patients (27.8%). The mean duration between PTBD catheter placement and embolization was 4.2 ± 1.1 days. Technical success was achieved in all 84 embolization procedures (100%). The duration from PTBD placement to tube removal did not differ significantly between patients who developed complications (mean 4.5 ± 0.9 days) and those who did not (mean 4.1 ± 1.1 days), suggesting that timing of catheter removal was not a significant contributor to complication risk. The contrast-marked Gelfoam plug allowed clear visualization during deployment (Figure 2), enabling precise placement up to the hepatic capsule under fluoroscopy with adjunct ultrasound guidance. Clinical success, defined as no bile leakage or bleeding complications during the hospital stay, was achieved in 47 of 54 patients (87%). Minor complications occurred in two patients (3.7%), who developed self-limiting bleeding at the catheter tract. Both cases were managed conservatively without the need for blood transfusion or surgical intervention. Follow-up USG confirmed no intra-abdominal hematoma or collection.

Fluid collections were observed in five patients (9.3%), presenting as pleural effusion (n = 1) or ascites (n = 4) within 2-4 days following embolization. Regarding access route and complications: of the five patients with fluid collections, 4 (80%) had right-sided access and 1 (20%) had a transpleural approach via the right lobe. No complications were observed in patients with left-sided access alone, though the sample size precludes definitive conclusions. These were attributed to partial dislodgment of the Gelfoam plug and required percutaneous drainage, resulting in a mean prolongation of hospital stay by 4.5 days (range, 3-7 days). No CIRSE grade 4 and above complication or procedure-related mortality were observed (Table 2).

Discussion

PTBD is an indispensable intervention for biliary decompression in malignant obstruction. However, the iatrogenic transhepatic tract represents a potential route for bile leakage and hemorrhage after catheter removal. Effective tract embolization can reduce these complications, improve patient comfort, and shorten hospitalization. Parenchymal tract embolization is a technique that has previously been used for various transhepatic procedures, including portal and hepatic venous interventions [13]. No direct comparative studies exist between biliary and portal tract embolization using Gelfoam.

Our study demonstrates that Gelfoam plug embolization is a feasible, safe, and effective technique. The use of a Lipiodol-marked cylindrical plug, advanced under fluoroscopic and occasional ultrasound guidance, enabled accurate plug placement extending up to the hepatic capsule, reducing the risk of bile leak and hemorrhage. It is important to clarify that the novelty of this study does not lie in the use of gelatin sponge per se—which has been previously reported—but rather in three specific technical modifications: (1) the cylindrical plug configuration, which preserves structural integrity and allows controlled, predictable deployment; (2) Contrast marking of both plug ends, enabling real-time fluoroscopic visualization and accurate placement up to the hepatic capsule; and (3) sheath-based delivery using a coaxial 6F-within-8F sheath system, which standardizes plug transfer and deployment. Ultrasound was used as an adjunct to fluoroscopy for final plug positioning and not as a primary guidance modality.

Deployment of a cylinder-shaped plug is more controlled compared to that of other liquid embolics such as Gelfoam slurry or NBCA glue injected via a syringe, because the cylindrical form maintains a defined shape and length corresponding to the tract diameter, resists proximal migration during deployment, and allows the operator to predict the volume of gelatin sponge placed. In contrast, a liquid embolic like Gelfoam slurry (a suspension of fragmented gelatin pieces in saline or contrast medium) or glue can be challenging to quantify, may disperse unpredictably within the tract, and may not maintain continuous column occlusion.

Importantly, the use of the 6F delivery sheath was essential to this technique: manual rolling and direct insertion of the plug without a sheath would risk premature expansion, shape loss, or incomplete loading—particularly in long tracts requiring multiple plugs. The 6F sheath acts as a mold and delivery conduit, maintaining the cylindrical geometry until the plug is transferred into the 8F PTBD sheath for final deployment. In vitro testing done at our center to observe the structural integrity of the Gelfoam cylinder in varying environments of blood and bile demonstrated that the cylindrical structure of the Gelfoam plug maintained its integrity in bile for several hours, confirming adequate shape retention. However, we did not test other brands of gelatin sponge, and further research is needed to define any differences between them.

Lipiodol was preferred over iodinated contrast because Lipiodol-labeled plugs were more radio-opaque. Being oil-based, Lipiodol did not diffuse away as quickly as iodinated contrast, allowing better visualization and deployment. The plug was not soaked in contrast before insertion into the sheath, as this could break up the Gelfoam column and predispose to premature dislodgment. Iodinated contrast can, however, be applied to the plug ends as a relatively more cost-effective alternative.

Lipiodol also produces a highly echogenic interface on ultrasound owing to its lipid composition, rendering the plug ends visible as hyperechoic foci on greyscale sonography. This facilitated sonographic confirmation of plug position, which was particularly useful in patients in whom the biliary access site could not be clearly delineated fluoroscopically. Although ultrasound allowed more precise deployment, it was not used uniformly, and we have therefore described its role as occasional.

When positioned against the broader literature on preventive transhepatic tract embolization, our technique addresses a specific and previously articulated gap (Table 4). In the most comprehensive synthesis to date — a systematic review of seven studies comprising 314 patients reported by Schmitz et al. [10] reported uniformly high technical success (96%-100%) across cyanoacrylate, gelatin sponge, and coil-based methods, yet concluded that the optimal embolic agent remains undefined and explicitly called for a standardized gelatin sponge delivery system available in defined diameters and lengths. Notably, slurry-based gelatin delivery is scarcely represented in the published literature; the dominant gelatin techniques are manually prepared pledgets [11] and torpedoes [1], shaped and deployed in an operator-dependent manner. Of all prior gelatin series, only one incorporated radiopacity using a commercially available device, and then only as a single marker on one pledget [11]; our dual-end contrast marking permits real-time fluoroscopic confirmation of plug position from the biliary access site to the hepatic capsule in every case. With 54 patients and 84 tracts treated in a uniformly malignant cohort, our experience is among the larger gelatin-specific series reported, alongside those of Dale et al. [11] (n = 92) and Augustin et al. [1] (n = 98), most of which comprised mixed benign and malignant indications.

Our incidence of fluid collections requiring drainage (9.3%) appears higher than the near-zero embolization-related event rates of comparator gelatin series (1,11). The likely causes were ascertained to be due to 1) Transpleural access: One patient had a transpleural right-sided PTBD tract and developed pleural effusion, consistent with the known risk of transpleural access. 2) Pre-existing ascites: Two patients who developed ascites had previously been drained dry before PTBD; however, ascites was considered the cause of plug dislodgment, and 3) longer tracts (>6 cm) require multiple sequential plugs, and the inter-plug interfaces represent potential points of partial displacement. We did not identify a statistically significant association between access route (right vs. left) and complication rate in our limited series, although all fluid collection cases occurred in right-sided or transpleural procedures. Importantly, no CIRSE grade 4 or higher complication or procedure-related mortality occurred, aligning with the safety outcomes reported by Lyon et al. [4] and Schmitz-Rode et al. [3]. Minor bleeding complications in our cohort were self-limited, consistent with the literature emphasizing Gelfoam's hemostatic properties.

The mean interval of 4.2 ± 1.1 days in our series is consistent with the published literature. Lyon et al. [4] reported a median interval of 4 days (range 1-21 days) between drain placement and tract embolization. When 6-9 F external drains were placed, an average indwelling time was found to be 3 days (3-13 days) [14, 15].

Each alternative embolic agent has drawbacks that favor gelatin sponge. Cyanoacrylate (NBCA) polymerizes unpredictably and demands precise glue-to-oil preparation, with a recognized risk of glue migration, catheter entrapment, and nontarget embolization; glue migration was the only embolization-related adverse event in the systematic review by Schmitz et al. [10], with one case requiring reopening of an occluded stent. Metallic coils occlude by thrombosis and suit vascular bleeding but are poorly adapted to sealing a bile-filled tract, may migrate months later, and add cost and follow-up imaging artefact [11]. Gelatin sponge, by contrast, is inexpensive, widely available, needs no specialized preparation, and is resorbed over 4 to 6 weeks while occlusion persists through an inflammatory reaction—with no embolization-related adverse events in either gelatin series of the review [1, 11]. Our cylindrical, Lipiodol-marked plug retains these advantages while adding the controlled, radiopaque, reproducible delivery that earlier gelatin techniques lacked.

The study’s real-time fluoroscopic deployment technique strengthens its clinical relevance, but several limitations apply. There was no control or comparison group, and follow-up was relatively short, limited to the hospital stay; asymptomatic or minor post-embolization fluid collections occurring after discharge may therefore have gone undetected, potentially underestimating the true complication rate. Ultrasound images were also not systematically archived as a protocol step. We did not test other brands of gelatin sponge, and further research is needed to define any differences between them. Longer-term outcomes, quality-of-life measures, and cost-effectiveness analyses would provide valuable adjunct data.

Conclusion

PTBD tract embolization with a Lipiodol-marked Gelfoam plug is a safe, simple, and effective method to achieve tract closure and prevent bile leakage and hemorrhage following biliary stenting in malignant obstruction. This technique offers an effective alternative to other embolic materials, with excellent technical success and low complication rates. Wider adoption and further comparative studies are warranted to establish this approach as a standard of care.
