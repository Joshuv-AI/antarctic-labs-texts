Maximal Equidistant Spacings
Michael Puthawala

Abstract
We call a family { Y 1 , … , Y I } \left\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{I}\right\} in Euclidean space an equidistance spacing if ‖ y i − y j ‖ = 1 \left\lVert y_{i}-y_{j}\right\rVert=1 whenever y i ∈ Y i , y j ∈ Y j y_{i}\in Y_{i},y_{j}\in Y_{j} and i ≠ j i\neq j . In other words, choosing a representative from each set produces a complete distance graph (i.e. equilateral set). We say such a spacing is maximal if each Y i Y_{i} is maximal under inclusion. In this work we characterize maximal equidistant spacings in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . For each equidistant spacing there is an associated center (a point in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} ) and radius (a non-negative scalar) so that the centers form an orthocentric system. Using arguments from classical geometry we find that the moduli space of maximal equidistant spacings is described in terms of the movement of its center and radii. Using tools from geometric combinatorics, we develop a discrete combinatorial object called the signature . Our classification theorem shows that maximal equidistant spacings are isometric if and only if they have the same signature. We also construct all maximal equidistant spacings in ℝ 1 , ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{2} and ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , outline a procedure for constructing all maximal equidistant spacings in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , and give an algorithm for checking if a locus of points is equidistantly spaced that is linear in the number of points, an improvement over the naive direct quadratic algorithm.

1.1
Comparison to Prior Work

Equidistant spacing is related to other classical problems in geometric combinatorics. For example,
[
3
,
9
]
characterized equilateral sets which are equidistant spacings where each set is a single element.

There are many coloring problems in geometric combinatorics that consider a geometric graph (where edges are determined by a geometric rule) and ask what the minimal number of colors is so that the graph may be colored without coloring two nodes that share an edge the same color. See e.g.
[
10
]
and
[
2
, Chapt. 5]
. One example is the famous Hadwiger–Nelson problem
[
7
]
where the vertices of the graph are the points on the plane, and nodes are connected if they are unit distance. Equidistant spacings are related these coloring problems through a sort of duality relationship. In coloring problems the nodes of the graph are fixed, and the goal is to minimize the number of colors needed. In the equidistant spacing setting, the number of colors is fixed and the number of points is maximized.

The geometry of equidistant spacings is also related to the applied field of contrastive learning in machine learning and statistics

1

\mathcal{X}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=I}
, each of which is associated with some label

. The task is to find a function

that embeds

1.2
Manuscript Organization

In Section
2.2
we establish the basic properties of equidistant spacings. We find that each class of a equidistant spacing may be summarized by two quantities, the class’ center (a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) and its radius (a scalar), Definition
2.2.8
. The general strategy for the rest of the manuscript is to understand equidistant spacings by understanding their centers and radii.
The most important result from this section is Corollary
2.2.13
which says that
\mathcal{Y}
being a equidistant spacing may be deduced from its centers and radii (plus some trivial orthogonality conditions). In particular, the classes and radii of equidistant spacings satisfy a system of compatibility equations, Equation
22
.

In Section
2.3
we consider a trick whereby a equidistant spacing may be expanded via a orthogonal projection and reflection, Lemma
2.3.1
. Maximal equidistant spacings are invariant under this operation. By exploiting this invariance, we obtain two remarkable results. The first, Corollary
2.3.7
, says that the centers of maximal equidistant spacings are either distinct points, or else all coincide. The second, Proposition
2.3.13
, says that if the centers of equidistant spacings do not coincide, then any center is the orthocenter of the simplex formed by the complimentary centers. Combined, these two results heavily constrain maximal equidistant spacings.

In Section
2.4
we consider when multiple equidistant spacings may be ‘glued’ together in a natural way to form a larger equidistant spacing. We find that we may associate a scaler, called the extent, to each equidistant spacing, Definition
2.4.4
, and that two equidistant spacings glue together if and only if their extents satisfy an inequality, Proposition
2.4.6
. Moreover, we may easily compute the extent of such gluings, Proposition
2.4.8
.

In Section
2.5
we consider what the natural isometries are on equidistant spacings. We find that there are three natural isometries. Two (Euclidean isometries and recolorings) are trivial. There is, however, one other isometry of equidistant spacings which we call a squash and stretch, Definition
2.5.2
. These isometries are less trivial to describe and characterize, and typically do not extend to an isometry of the ambient space.

In Section
2.6
we characterize the squash and stretch isometries of maximal equidistant spacings. We do this by considering a simple squash and stretch called an outer-inner squash and stretch, Proposition
2.6.1
. Then, by deriving a canonical form, Definition
2.6.8
, we show that squash and stretches are generated by outer-inner squash and stretches for maximal equidistant spacings, Theorem
2.6.10
and Proposition
2.6.11
. Importantly, the canonical forms is concrete and may be easily constructed.

In Section
2.7
we associate to each maximal equidistant spacing an object called its signature, Definition
2.7.1
. We then show that maximal equidistant spacings are isometric if and only if they have the same signature, Theorem
2.7.6
. We then compute all signatures in
\mathbb{R}^{0}

\mathbb{R}^{1},\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and thereby compute all maximal equidistant spacings in those spaces. We conclude by counting the number of maximal equidistant spacings in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and find it has a simple expression, Proposition
2.7.16
.

In Section
2.8
we give an algorithm, Algorithm
3
, to verify if a finite collection of points form a equidistant spacing. By analyzing the run time of this algorithm, Theorem
2.8.1
, we find that its runtime grows linearly with the number of points.

Remark 2.2.14

(Counting Compatibility Equations)
.

In Corollary
2.2.13
, we can see that the problem of generating equidistant spacings reduces to making a choice of compatible
r_{i}
’s,
c_{i}
’s and choosing subsets

that satisfy all of the necessary orthogonality conditions. Making the choice of compatible
r_{i}
’s and

’s, however, is non-trivial. One idea is to choose a

, and then let

be given by solving the linear (in

) system of equations

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{1}-c_{2}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-r_{1}^{2}-r^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{1}-c_{3}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-r_{2}^{2}-r^{2}_{3},

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{I-1}-c_{I}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-r^{2}_{I-1}-r^{2}_{I}.

There are, however,
I
unknowns and
\binom{I}{2}
equations. When
I>2
,
\binom{I}{2}>I
and so it seems natural, therefore, to expect that for most choices of
c_{1},\dots,c_{I}
, there are no choices of

that satisfy Eqn.
22
when

. This is indeed the case, and ruins the plan to choose

’s first and then choose the

’s.

To make Remark
2.2.14
more concrete, we present the following example.

Example 2.2.15

(Non-triviality of choosing
\left\{c_{i}\right\}
)
.

Let
I=3
and
\left\{c_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{I}
be given by

\displaystyle c_{1}\coloneqq(0,-\epsilon),\quad c_{2}\coloneqq(0,\epsilon)\quad,c_{3}\coloneqq(\alpha,0)

(24)

where
\epsilon>0
is given. For which values of

are there choices of
\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{I}
so that Eqn.

From symmetry, it is clear that

. From Eqn.

, we then have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{1}-c_{2}\right\rVert=2\epsilon=\sqrt{1-2r_{1}^{2}}\implies r_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon^{2}}.

(25)

Applying Eqn.

\displaystyle\left\lVert c_{1}-c_{3}\right\rVert^{2}=\epsilon^{2}+\alpha^{2}=1-r^{2}_{1}-r^{2}_{3}\implies\alpha^{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon^{2}-r_{3}^{2}.

So, if

there is no

.

Next, we introduce a construction that enables deeper geometric results. The idea, broadly speaking, is that we may make a equidistant spacing larger in one class by projecting the class’ center orthogonally onto the subspace spanned by the other centers, and doing a reflection.

Lemma 2.3.1

(Ortho-Reflection Lemma)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
with centers
\left\{c_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{I}
.

Then, there is a equidistant spacing
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{I}Y_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
so that

\displaystyle Y_{i}^{\prime}=Y_{i},\quad\text{for each }i\leq I-1

(31)

and

\displaystyle Y_{I}^{\prime}=Y_{I}\cup\operatornamewithlimits{refl}_{c^{*}_{I}}Y_{I}\quad\text{where}\quad c^{*}_{I}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{I-1})}c_{I},

(32)

where
\operatornamewithlimits{refl}
denotes the reflection operator, see Def.
B.0.4
.

Proof.

Let
y_{I},y_{I}^{*}\in Y_{I}^{\prime}
be reflections of each other, and
y_{j}\in Y_{j}
for some
j\leq I-1
. Consider the triangles
\triangle y_{I}c^{*}_{I}y_{j}
and
\triangle y_{I}^{*}c^{*}_{I}y_{j}
. The triangles share the sides
c^{*}_{I}y_{j}
, and the length of sides
y_{I}c^{*}_{I}
and
y_{I}^{*}c^{*}_{I}
are the same. Further, the angles
\angle y_{I}c^{*}_{I}y_{j}
and
\angle y_{I}c^{*}_{I}y_{j}
are the same (namely, right angles), as follows from the following computation.

\displaystyle\left\langle c_{j}-c^{*}_{I},c^{*}_{I}-y^{*}_{I}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle c_{j}-c^{*}_{I},c^{*}_{I}-y_{I}-2(y-c_{I}^{*})\right\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=\left\langle c_{j}-c^{*}_{I},y_{I}-c^{*}_{I}\right\rangle

(34)

\displaystyle=\left\langle c_{j}-c^{*}_{I},y_{I}-c_{I}\right\rangle+\left\langle c_{j}-c^{*}_{I},c_{I}-c^{*}_{I}\right\rangle=0.

(35)

Hence, the two triangles are equivalent by side angle side and so
\left\lVert y^{*}_{I}-y_{j}\right\rVert=\left\lVert y_{I}-y_{j}\right\rVert=1
which proves that
\mathcal{Y}_{I}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
.
∎

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\left\{\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon\text{$\mathcal{Y}$ is maximal}\right\}.

(36)

Proposition 2.3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle c_{I}\in\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{I-1}).

\operatornamewithlimits{refl}_{c^{*}_{I}}Y_{I}\subset Y_{I}

By using the trick of reflecting across affine spaces, we may prove the following theorem that relates the condition of being a

Theorem 2.3.6

(Leave-two-out Affine Rank implies Maximization by Coinciding Centers)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a equidistant spacing with centers
\left\{c_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{I}

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{j-1},c_{j+1},\dots,c_{I})=\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{I}).

\hat{\mathcal{Y}}
that maximizes
\mathcal{Y}
, so that
\hat{c}_{1}=\dots=\hat{c}_{I}

Proof.

To ease notation, w.l.o.g. let

and

. By Proposition
2.3.4
, we have that

\displaystyle c_{I}=\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{I-2})}c_{I}=\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{I-2})}c_{I-1}=c_{I-1}.

From Corollary

, we have that

Consider
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
so that
\hat{\mathcal{Y}}=\left\{\hat{Y}_{k}\right\}^{I}_{k=1}

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{k}=Y_{k}\cup\operatornamewithlimits{refl}_{c_{I}}Y_{k}\quad\text{ for each }k=1,\dots,I-2,

(51)

\hat{Y}_{I}=Y_{I}

y_{k}\in Y_{k}

y_{I-1}\in Y_{I-1}

We now prove that all centers of

c_{k}=\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(Y_{k}\cup\operatornamewithlimits{refl}_{c_{I}}Y_{k})}y_{I}=c_{I}
for any

.

Definition 2.3.11

(Orthocentric Simplex)

Let
\sigma

n

All triangles have an orthocenter. In higher dimensions, this is not the case. A simplex is generically not an orthocentric simplex.

The points

There are

\displaystyle A\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}p(x_{0}-x_{i})&\dots&p(x_{i-1}-x_{i})&p(x_{i+1}-x_{i})&\dots&p(x_{n+1}-x_{i})\end{pmatrix}

Now we present a powerful geometric result that gives a geometric criterion for how centers in a maximal embedding lie.

Proposition 2.3.13

(Centers are Orthocentric Systems)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}_{\neq}
. Then
c_{1},\dots,\dots,c_{I}
are an orthocentric system.

Proof.

Let us define
\bigtriangleup_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{conv}(c_{1},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{I})
. The claim follows if we can show that
\bigtriangleup_{i}
is a simplex, and
c_{i}
is its orthocenter.
\bigtriangleup_{i}
being a simplex is equivalent to showing that
c_{1},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{I}
are affinely independent. This follows from Corollary
2.3.7
.

Now we show that
c_{i}
is the orthocenter of
\bigtriangleup_{i}
. Note, we know that
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
when
i\neq j
from Corollary
2.3.8
. Hence, the line
\overline{c_{j}c_{i}}
is well-defined. For ease of notation, let us define

\displaystyle A_{(i,j)}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{j-1},c_{j+1},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{I}\right).

(59)

and
c^{*}_{j}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{A_{(i,j)}}c_{j}
. From Proposition
2.3.4
, we have that

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{A_{(i,j)}}c_{i}=c^{*}_{j}

Thus,
c_{i},c_{j}
, and
c^{*}_{j}
all lie on a line. Because
c^{*}_{j}
is defined by the orthogonal projection, this line is orthogonal to
A_{(i,j)}
. Hence,
c_{i}
is contained on the line containing
c_{j}
orthogonal to
A_{(i,j)}
, that is,
c_{i}
lies on the line connecting
c_{j}
to its orthogonal projection on its opposite face in
\bigtriangleup_{i}
. This holds for each
j
, and so
c_{i}
is the orthocenter of
\bigtriangleup_{i}
.

(The Ortho-Reflection Lemma Produces Orthocentric Systems)

.

The goal is to show that
\overline{c_{i}c^{*}_{I}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i+1},\dots,c_{I-1})^{\perp}
for each

. By the ortho-reflection trick (applied to
I
), we may define

where
c^{\prime}_{i}=c_{i}
when

, and
c_{I}^{\prime}=c^{*}_{I}
. Then, Corollary
2.3.5
applied to
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
implies that

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{i-1}^{\prime},c_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{I-1}^{\prime})}c_{i}^{\prime}=\operatornamewithlimits{proj}_{\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}(c_{1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{i-1}^{\prime},c_{i+1}^{\prime},\dots,c_{I-1}^{\prime})}c_{I}^{\prime}.

Theorem 2.3.17

(Small Orthocentric Systems Correspond to Centers in

Let

be an orthocentric system. If

\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}_{\neq}(n+1,\mathbb{R}^{m})
so that
\alpha c_{0},\dots,c\alpha_{n+1}

Let
c_{0},\dots,c_{n+1}
be given. Then, by Eqn.
57

\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n+1}
so that
\left\lVert c_{i}-c_{j}\right\rVert^{2}=\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}
for each

. If we choose

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}\leq\min(\inf_{i}\frac{1}{2\lambda_{i}},\inf_{i,j}\left\lVert c_{i}-c_{j}\right\rVert)

(66)

then
\left\lVert\alpha c_{i}-\alpha c_{j}\right\rVert\leq 1
and
\frac{1}{2}-\alpha^{2}\lambda_{i}\geq 0

\left\lVert\alpha c_{i}-\alpha c_{j}\right\rVert^{2}=\alpha^{2}\lambda_{i}+\alpha^{2}\lambda_{j}

\left\lVert\alpha c_{i}-\alpha c_{j}\right\rVert^{2}=1-r_{i}^{2}-r_{j}^{2}
. Thus, this choice of
\alpha c_{0},\dots,\alpha c_{n+1}
and
r_{0},\dots,r_{n+1}
satisfy Eqn.

,
r_{i}^{2}+r_{j}^{2}\leq 1
.

By Corollary
2.3.14
,
\dim\operatornamewithlimits{Aff}{\alpha c_{0},\dots,\alpha c_{n+1}}
. Hence, by Corollary
2.2.16
if

∎

Propositions

Definition 2.3.18

(Inner Class)
.

Let

. We say

Lemma 2.3.19

(Existence of Inner Center)
.

When

has an inner class. If
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}_{\neq}(I)

Proof.

The proof is obvious when

as the centers coincide.

By Proposition
2.3.13
, the centers of

2.4
Gluing equidistant spacings together

Given some number of equidistant spacings, it may be possible to embed them into some common space so that they form a larger equidistant spacing. In this section, we determine when this is possible.

Definition 2.4.1

(Glue Site, Gluing)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}f^{(n)}(\mathcal{Y}^{(n)})\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(\sum^{N}_{n=1}I^{(n)},\mathbb{R}^{m}).

(68)

We call the tuple

are clear from context.

We say that

and

\mathcal{Y}^{(1)},\dots,\mathcal{Y}^{(N)}

\mathbb{R}^{m}

Definition 2.4.4

(Extent)
.

For each

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\infty&\text{if }\text{ $\mathcal{Y}$ has no glue site}\\
\left\lVert y-c^{*}\right\rVert&\text{if }\text{where $c^{*}$ is the glue site of $\mathcal{Y}$ and }y\in\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}\right\rvert\end{cases}

(73)

If the set in the r.h.s. of Eqn.
73
is empty, then we define
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}=\infty
.

The concept of extent is so called because it measures both the size of a equidistant spacings, and also the degree to which it is glueable as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 2.4.5

(Elementary Properties of Extent)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then there is a
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
so that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
glue if and only if
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\leq 1
.
\mathcal{Y}
has a glue site
c
. Then, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\coloneqq\left\{(\bm{0}^{n},\pm\sqrt{1-r^{2}})\right\}

(74)

where
r\coloneqq\left\lVert y-c^{*}\right\rVert
. Clearly,
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(1)
, and
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
glue via the mapping
(x\mapsto(x-c,0),Id_{n+1})
. Hence,
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\leq 1
implies that there is a
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
so that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
glue.

Suppose that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
glue. Let
y\in\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}\right\rvert
,
y^{\prime}\in\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right\rvert
,
c
denote the glue site of
\mathcal{Y}
and
c^{\prime}
denote the glue site of
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Then, by Proposition
2.2.10
, Remark
2.4.2
, and pythagoreans theorem

\displaystyle 1=\left\lVert y-y^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert y-c\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert c-c^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert c^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\geq\left\lVert y-c\right\rVert^{2}=\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}.

(75)

Hence,
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\leq 1
.

The extent also has a concrete use. It gives simple conditions under which two equidistant spacings may be glued together, as shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4.6

(Extent Characterizes Gluablity)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}(I^{\prime},\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}})

\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}

\displaystyle f(x)\coloneqq(x-c^{*},\bm{0}^{n^{\prime}},0)

and

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}+(1-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2})+\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}

\displaystyle=1.

(82)

(Gluing Spacings in the Same Space)

The following proposition gives a convenient expression for

in terms of
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}
and

Proposition 2.4.8

(Extent of Glueable Equidistant Spacings)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
glue together. If
\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\left(\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{1-4\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}}}{2\sqrt{1-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}}}.

(85)

Proof.

Let us denote by
c^{\vee*}
the glue site of
\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. By orthogonality, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right)^{2}=\left\lVert y-c^{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\vee*}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert y^{\prime}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert c^{\prime}{}^{*}-c^{\vee*}\right\rVert^{2}

(86)

By the recoloring trick,
c^{\vee*}
lies in the line connecting
c^{*}
and
c^{\prime}{}^{*}
. Hence there is a
\lambda\in[0,1]
so that

\displaystyle c^{\vee*}=(1-\lambda)c^{*}+\lambda c^{\prime}{}^{*}.

(87)

By calculation,
\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\vee*}\right\rVert=\lambda\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert c^{\prime}{}^{*}-c^{\vee*}\right\rVert=(1-\lambda)\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert
. Substituting this into Eqn.
86
shows that
\lambda
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert y-c^{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\lambda^{2}\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert y^{\prime}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}+(1-\lambda)^{2}\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}

(88)

\displaystyle\alpha+\lambda^{2}\gamma

\displaystyle=\beta+(1-\lambda)^{2}\gamma

(89)

where we have denoted
\alpha\coloneqq\left\lVert y-c^{*}\right\rVert^{2},\beta\coloneqq\left\lVert y^{\prime}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
, and
\gamma\coloneqq\left\lVert c^{*}-c^{\prime}{}^{*}\right\rVert^{2}
.

Solving for
\lambda
yields

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\beta-\alpha+\gamma}{2\gamma}.

(90)

Now we show Eqn.
85

86
,
89
and
90

\displaystyle\left(\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right)^{2}=\alpha+\lambda^{2}\gamma=\alpha+\frac{\left(\beta-\alpha+\gamma\right)^{2}}{4\gamma}.

(91)

Observing that
1=\alpha+\gamma+\beta
and simplifying Eqn.
91
with this identity, we have that

\displaystyle\left(\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\alpha(1-\alpha-\beta)+\left(1-2\alpha\right)^{2}}{4\left(1-\alpha-\beta\right)}

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-4\alpha\beta}{4\left(1-\alpha-\beta\right)}.

(93)

Finally, by noting that
\alpha=\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2},\beta=\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}
we have that

\displaystyle\left(\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}\vee\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\right)^{2}=\frac{1-4\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}}{4\left(1-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{2}-\operatornamewithlimits{ext}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}{}^{2}\right)}

(94)

2.6
Canonical Form

The main difficulty in classifying maximal equidistant spacings is understanding when two equidistant spacings in
\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}_{\neq}
are squash and stretch isomorphic. To solve this problem we develop a canonical form of maximal equidistant spacings in this section. It is an invariant of isometries, Proposition
2.6.11

The big idea behind the canonical form is that the centers of

are
not
geometrically homogeneous. There is one distinguished center that lies inside the simplex generated by the other centers, as in Def.

The following proposition is very useful, but requires some motivation. The idea is to explore the simplest possible squash and stretch, one that is the identity on all classes except the inner class and one outer class. These simple squash and stretches are the building blocks of many results to come.

lie on an equilateral simplex. Hence,
\mathcal{Y}^{(2)}
is in equilateral normal form.

∎

Theorem
2.6.10
says that maximal equidistant spacings always have equilateral normal forms. It turns out, such forms are invariant under isometries, as shown by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6.11

(Invariance of Equilateral Normal Form)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
be such that there is an isometry
f
so that
f\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Then the equilateral normal forms of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
are the same.

Proof.

Clearly, all the isometries preserve the number of classes of a equidistant spacing. Also clearly, classes with radius
>0
are mapped to classes with radius
>0
via isometries. Therefore, two maximal equidistant spacings must have the same number of classes total and the same number of classes of radius 0 and radius
>0
. Hence the
k
and
k^{\prime}
in the notation of Proposition
2.6.9
are the same. So the centers of their equilateral normal forms are the same.
∎

2.7
Signatures of Equidistant Spacings

Proposition
2.6.11
shows that normal forms are invariant under isometries. A natural question, is if this invariant characterizes isometry classes. That is, are two maximal equidistant spacings isometric if the centers of their equilateral normal forms coincide? This is true, subject to a natural dimension counting condition. To make the statement of the resulting theorem as clean as possible, we introduce the following definition. It is a combinatorial description of equidistant spacings which have a equilateral normal.

Definition 2.7.1

(Signature of Equidistant Spacing)
.

We call a pair of a natural number and list of natural numbers
(m,(d_{1},\dots,d_{k}))
where
m,d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
a
signature
if
d_{i}\geq d_{i+1}
for each
2\leq i\leq k-1
. We denote the set of signatures by
\mathcal{S}
. We call
m
the
zero-radius term
of the signature,
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
the
positive-radius terms
of the signature, and
d_{1}
the
inner term
.

If
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
is in equilateral normal form, we say that the
signature of
\mathcal{Y}
denoted by
\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}
is given by

\displaystyle(\#(r=0),\dim A_{1},\dim{A_{2}},\dots\dim A_{k})

(152)

where
\#(r=0)
is the number of zero-radii centers of
\mathcal{Y}
and

1.

if
\mathcal{Y}
has centers that coincide,
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
are ordered so that
\dim{A_{1}}\geq\dots\geq\dim A_{k}
,

2.

if
\mathcal{Y}
has centers that do not coincide, then
1
is the inner class of
\mathcal{Y}
and
A_{2},\dots,A_{k}
are ordered so that
\dim{A_{2}}\geq\dots\geq\dim A_{k}
.

If
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
is isometric to a
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}
where
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
is in equilateral normal form, then we declare
\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.

We define the map
\mathrm{sig}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{S}
by
\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}
as the map that associates equidistant spacings to signatures.

We call
m+\sum^{k}_{i=1}d_{i}
the
sum
of signature
s
.

Note that Definition
2.7.1
only makes sense if signatures are invariant under isometries of equidistant spacings. This is the case for maximal equidistant spacings, which the following lemma shows.

Lemma 2.7.2

(Signatures are Invariant Under Isometries)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}
and
f
be an isometry. Then,
\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}=\operatornamewithlimits{sig}f\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

First, we prove a helper result that if
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}
are isometric and in equilateral normal form, then
\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}=\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. This, combined with Theorem
2.6.10
, implies the lemma.

Let
\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{Y}\cong\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Then
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
have the same centers of their equilateral normal forms by Proposition
2.6.11
. By the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
2.6.11
, the length and radius 0 terms of their signatures are also the same.

Note that if
f
is a rotation or squash and stretch of
Y_{i}
, then
\dim Y_{i}=\dim fY_{i}
. Further, if
f
is a recoloring of
\mathcal{Y}
, then the total number of classes
\mathcal{Y}
and
f\mathcal{Y}
are the same. Thus, after sorting the dimensions of the non-zero-radii classes of
\mathcal{Y}
, it must agree with
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. Hence, their signatures agree in the non-zero component as well. So
\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}=\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
.

∎

Proposition 2.7.3

(Determination of Coinciding Centers from Signature)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
s=\operatornamewithlimits{sig}\mathcal{Y}

\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}_{=}
if and only if

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By Corollary

\displaystyle n=\dim\tilde{A}+\sum^{I}_{i=1}\dim A_{i}=\dim\tilde{A}+\sum^{k}_{i=1}d_{i}.

(153)

If
\mathcal{Y}
has centers that coincide, then

, so

If
\mathcal{Y}\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

implies that

, and so

.
∎

(Maximal Equidistant Spacings are Isometric if and only if they have the same Signature)

∎

Remark 2.7.7

(Geometric Interpretation of Signatures)
.

In light of Theorem

, it is clear that the signature of a maximal equidistant spacing is a combinatorial descriptor of its isometry class. It also has a straight forward geometric meaning. If

The geometric interpretation is especially useful, because it applies even if
