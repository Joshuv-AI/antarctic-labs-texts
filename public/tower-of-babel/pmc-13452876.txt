Vitamin K in photosynthetic organisms
Authors: Jerzy Kruk, Beatrycze Nowicka, Renata Szymańska, Michał Gabruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMain conclusionRecently, great progress in vitamin K research in plants was made, especially on vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) homolog, new functions of VK in plant plasma membrane and VK1 biosynthesis.AbstractAlthough vitamin K (VK) was discovered in the 1930s, only recently has significant progress in the research on this lipid-soluble vitamin been made both in mammals and in photosynthetic organisms. For example, the molecular structure and catalytic mechanisms of the cyanobacterial vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) homolog have been resolved. It has been recently shown that all organisms from bacteria to higher plants share common, phylogenetically related VKOR-type enzymes, engaged in the formation of disulfide bridges in various target proteins. This activity is related to the phylloquinone (VK1)-dependent regulation of the STN7 kinase, which is responsible for optimal energy distribution between two photosystems. Moreover, new functions of VK1 and menaquinone-4 (VK2) in plant plasma membrane electron transport chain were identified. Recently, the plastid-localized biosynthetic pathway of VK1 has been nearly completely recognized. However, there are also indications of an alternative, plasma membrane-targeted VK1 biosynthesis pathway.Keywords: Biosynthesis, Chloroplasts, Cyanobacteria, Phylloquinone, Plants, Vitamin K

Introduction

Vitamin K (VK) was discovered in the 1930 s as a factor required for blood coagulation (Almquist and Stokstad 1935; Dam 1935) and named vitamin K from the word ‘Koagulation’. By the end of the decade, purification, structure determination and chemical synthesis was completed (Binkley et al. 1939; Fieser et al. 1939).

All forms of vitamin K share a common 2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone nucleus, but differ in the length and degree of saturation of the aliphatic side-chain (Fig. 1). Phylloquinone (VK1) has a monounsaturated, phytyl side-chain of four isoprenyl residues, while the side-chain of VK2 (menaquinone-n, MK-n) is composed of various numbers of usually unsaturated units. Besides, vitamin K3 (VK3, menadione) is used, which is a water-soluble homolog without a side-chain.

The chemical structures of phylloquinone (VK1), menaquinone (MK-n, VK2) and vitamin K3 (VK3, menadione)

VK, which belongs to lipid-soluble vitamins, occurs in nearly all organisms from bacteria to mammals and higher plants; however, it performs different functions in these organisms. In humans, VK1 or VK2 is mainly engaged in blood clotting (Suttie 2013). In bacteria, VK2 functions in anaerobic respiration (Nowicka and Kruk 2010), while in plants VK1 is mainly known from its function in the photosynthetic electron transport (Oostende et al. 2011). In recent years, significant progress has been made in VK research, both in mammals where the molecular structure of enzymes involved in the VK epoxide cycle has been determined (Wang et al. 2025) and in bacteria with the discovery of a new VK2 biosynthetic pathway, the so-called futalosine pathway, as well as in plants where new functions of VK1 have been identified. Interestingly, it has been recently shown that all the organisms share common, phylogenetically related enzymes with VK as a cofactor, engaged in the formation of disulfide bridges in various proteins.

In this review, we have focused on recent progress in research on the occurrence, function and biosynthesis of VK1 and menaquinone-4 (MK-4) in photosynthetic organisms.

Occurrence and function

Phylloquinone is best known for its role as a component of photosystem I (PSI) in the photosynthetic electron transport chain. Accordingly, the highest levels of this vitamin are found in leaves, including green vegetables, and it is also present in significant amounts in green fruits. Although at considerably lower levels, VK1 is also detected in non-photosynthetic plant organs such as roots, fruits, seeds, and flowers (Table 1), which may indicate additional, non-photosynthetic functions of VK1.

It has been known that within each PSI complex, there are two VK1 molecules, operating as one-electron carriers between oxidized and semiquinone forms. These molecules, named A1, are arranged in two electron transfer branches, A and B (Fig. 2), corresponding to reaction center polypeptides (PsaA and PsaB). However, it was not known if both branches are in fact active in the electron transport. Based on studies of Chlamydomonas mutants, where selected amino acids close to both VK1 molecules were substituted (Guergova-Kuras et al. 2001), it was concluded that both branches are active in electron transfer; however, the transfer rate through branch-B is over ten times slower than in branch-A (Fig. 2).

The VK1 reoxidation half-times in A and B branches are shown, as well as oxygen reduction by VK1 (Mehler reaction), leading to hydrogen peroxide (Guergova-Kuras et al. 2001, modified)

VK1 in PSI, due to its low redox potential has been found to be the main site of O2 reduction in thylakoid membranes (Kruk et al. 2003; Kozuleva et al. 2014, 2021), leading to enhanced production of hydrogen peroxide. This is so-called Mehler reaction, especially pronounced when the terminal PSI electron acceptors are saturated (Kozuleva et al. 2021).

In plants and cyanobacteria, the organisms performing oxygenic photosynthesis, VK1 occurs ubiquitously as a component of PSI with a few exceptions where it is replaced by MK-4. These are cyanobacteria Gloeobacter violaceus (Mimuro et al. 2005) and Synechococcus sp. PCC 7002 (Sakuragi et al. 2005), red algae Cyanidium caldarium (Yoshida et al. 2003) and diatom Chaetoceros gracilis (Ikeda et al. 2008), which do not synthesize VK1 at all. Moreover, in the cyanobacterium Anacystis nidulans and an euglenoid Euglena gracilis strain Z, 5’-hydroxyphylloquinone (5’-OH-VK1) with the hydroxyl group in the phytyl side-chain (Fig. 1) was identified as dominant quinone in PSI (Ziegler et al. 1989). A stoichiometry of two 5’-OH-VK1 per P700, reaction center of PSI, was determined for A. nidulans, while Euglena contained only one such molecule per P700.

The product of isochorismate synthase (ICS) is a branching point for salicylic acid and VK1 synthesis in plants. The ics1 mutant of one of two ics genes in Arabidopsis, showed 58% of VK1 levels of the wild-type (WT) in relation to chlorophyll, and was affected in several photosynthetic parameters (Gawroński et al. 2013). The mutant showed impaired state transition, similar to that of the stn7 mutant and altered thylakoid structure with an increased number of stacked thylakoids per granum. Moreover, the plastoquinone-pool was more reduced than in the WT and the mutant showed increased yield of cyclic electron flow around PSI. All these effects seem to be independent of the VK1 function in PSI and of salicylic acid. These data indicate that VK1 is essential for optimal energy distribution between photosystems in Arabidopsis; however, the molecular mechanism of the observed effects remained unexplained until recently. As LTO1 (Lumen Thiol Oxidase 1) thylakoid membrane protein is engaged in the formation of disulfide bonds in lumenal proteins and requires specifically VK1 as a cofactor (Furt et al. 2010), indirect VK1-dependent regulation of STN7 kinase activity, containing a critical disulfide bridge at C68-C73 (Singh et al. 2016) is possible. In line with this hypothesis, it has been recently shown that the TRX-like domain of LTO1 oxidized the conserved lumenal cysteines of STN7 in vitro (Wu et al. 2021). Moreover, loss of LTO1 affected the STN7 activity in Arabidopsis. It was concluded that LTO1 helps to maintain STN7 in an oxidized active state through redox interactions between the lumenal cysteines of STN7 and LTO1.

A number of reports, including those concerning the presence of VK1 in non-photosynthetic organs (Table 1), suggest that this prenyl lipid occurs and functions outside chloroplasts. Especially interesting in this respect is the identification of VK1 in plasma membranes of maize roots (Lüthje et al. 1998) and soybean seedlings (Biniek et al. 2017), where VK1 was suggested to take part in the plasma membrane electron transport chain. Unexpectedly, soybean plasma membranes from hypocotyls and roots were also found to contain MK-4 or its isomer in the amounts exceeding that of VK1, 4 and 12-fold, respectively (Fig. 3). This is the first identification of VK2 in higher plants. MK-4 probably contributes to the VK pool in the plasma membrane of soybean. The electron transport chain includes NADPH quinone oxidoreductase NQR, which is a two-electron-transferring flavoenzyme, reducing VK on the cytosol site of the plasma membrane (Fig. 4). The reduced VK undergoes autoxidation producing superoxide anions which reduce AIR12, an apoplast-exposed, ascorbate-reducible cytochrome b protein, anchored in the membrane by glycosylphospatidylinositol (Preger et al. 2009). AIR12 can be also directly reduced by VK semiquinone and slowly reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide (Fig. 4). Therefore, this enzyme plays an antioxidant role in its oxidized state and a pro-oxidant role in its reduced state. Thus, the whole membrane electron transport chain plays an important role in controlling production of reactive oxygen species, which are signaling molecules and control many growth processes. In line with this, the double mutant nqr-air12 of Arabidopsis, showed increased superoxide level which is responsible for stimulation of mutant seed germination (Biniek et al. 2017). Additionally, there are recent indications that VK1 biosynthesis is targeted to plasma membrane in non-photosynthetic parasitic plants (Gu et al. 2021a), where it is suggested to participate in membrane electron transport for transmembrane redox signaling associated with parasitism.

In contrast to mammals, neither Gla-proteins nor vitamin K-dependent carboxylase genes were identified in plant genomes (Furt et al. 2010). Therefore, the function of VK in plants as a cofactor of VKGC can be excluded. On the other hand, mammalian and bacterial VKOR homologs were found to be widespread in cyanobacteria and plants (Singh et al. 2008; Li et al. 2010) which raised the question regarding the function of this enzyme. These enzymes are bifunctional, bimodular fusion proteins composed of a catalytic subunit similar to the mammalian vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1) and a thioredoxin-like moiety. A cyanobacterial enzyme from JA-2-3B’a(2–13) strain of Synechocystis sp. has been cloned into Escherichia coli, expressed and purified for crystal structure determination (Li et al. 2010). The structure shows the arrangement of the components engaged in the catalytic reaction (Fig. 5). The electrons are shuttled from thiol groups of the substrate protein, through pairs of cysteines in the enzyme to a quinone molecule (Li et al. 2010) which was ubiquinone-8 or demethyl-MK-8 in the cloned enzyme, but is most probably VK1 in the cyanobacterium. The ortholog cyanobacterial protein SynDsbAB from Synechocystis 6803, containing 325 residues with a molecular weight of 35 kDa, was found to be required for disulfide bond formation in the extracytoplasmic space of the cyanobacterium (Singh et al. 2008). SynDsbAB shows structural similarities to proteins involved in disulfide bond formation in non-photosynthetic prokaryotes and eukaryotes where disulfide bridges in the substrate proteins are formed by electron transfer from two target cysteines in these proteins through cysteine pairs in SynDsbA, SynDsbB to a quinone acceptor (Inaba and Ito 2008). Although the prenylquinone terminal electron acceptor was not investigated in this study, based on the homology of the cyanobacterial enzyme with that of Arabidopsis (Furt et al. 2010), it can be predicted that the quinone cofactor of SynDsbAB is VK1. The Arabidopsis VKOR homolog, LTO1 is a membrane protein composed of 375 residues, molecular weight of 40.3 kDa and is targeted to chloroplasts (Furt et al. 2010). It is supposed to be engaged in disulfide bond formation in thylakoid lumenal proteins (Karamoko et al. 2011; Lu et al. 2013) by an analogous mechanism to that of SynDsbAB in cyanobacteria. When the Arabidopsis LTO1 was cloned to yeasts, it specifically reduced VK1 and MK-4 in vitro but not plastoquinone-9 or ubiquinone-10 (Furt et al. 2010). In contrast to the mammalian VKOR, the Arabidopsis enzyme showed no activity with VK1 epoxide and was resistant to warfarin (Furt et al. 2010), an inhibitor of the mammalian VKOR. These data, along with the absence of naphthoquinone epoxides in bacteria and plants, suggest that the enzyme in these organisms functions only as a VK reductase and not also as VK epoxide reductase. Thylakoid targeting of LTO1 implies the presence of a free VK1 pool in the membrane and raises the question how VK1 is reoxidized after reduction by LTO1. This issue deserves further study.

Biosynthesis

Biosynthesis of VK1 in plants follows similar enzymatic steps to those of E. coli (Fig. 6), however with some unexpected differences. The most striking was that the four enzymes (menF, MenD, MenH and MenC in E. coli), catalyzing subsequent reactions are encoded not by separated genes, as in the case of E. coli, but by a fusion gene called PHYLLO, encoding the corresponding enzymes in the form of a fusion protein (Gross et al. 2006) consisting of 1715 residues. The MenF domain is catalytically inactive and replaced by two other isoenzymes ICS1/ICS2 in Arabidopsis. ICS1 has the main contribution to the isochorismate synthesis and some plant species have only a single copy of the ics gene. Isochorismate is a branching point for the synthesis of salicylic acid, therefore ICS activity might have other physiological effects beyond those dependent on VK1. The most recently identified enzyme in the pathway of VK1 biosynthesis is DHNA-CoA thioesterase, belonging to the Hotdog fold family of proteins (Widhalm et al. 2009). Prior to its discovery, the requirement for a dedicated DHNA-CoA thioesterase remained ambiguous since the cognate hydrolysis reaction was known to occur non-enzymatically (albeit at a very low level in vivo). Similarly, the reaction catalyzed by MenH proceeds spontaneously to some extent (Chen et al. 2013) (Fig. 6).

Another peculiar feature of VK1 biosynthesis is its compartmentation between chloroplasts and peroxisomes (Fig. 6). DHNA-CoA synthase (DHNS) and DHNA-CoA thioesterase (DHNAT1/2) are localized exclusively in peroxisomes (Widhalm et al. 2012), while OSB-CoA ligase (AEE14) is dual targeted to both organelles. The compartmentation requires specific transporters that are to be identified. The enzymes of the final steps of the biosynthetic pathway are localized in different parts of chloroplasts: phytyltransferase (ABC4) in the inner envelope membrane, NDC1 in plastoglobules (Piller et al. 2011), while MENG in thylakoids (Fig. 6).

Besides the described compartmentation of VK1 biosynthesis, it has been suggested that the late biosynthetic steps can be also targeted to plasma membrane in parasitic plants (Gu et al. 2021a, 2021b), as well as in Arabidopsis. In the latter case, alternative splicing of both AtMenA and AtMenG transcripts was found and the products are predicted to lack chloroplast targeting (Li et al. 2016). As NDC1 is confined to plastids (Piller et al. 2011), the corresponding step in VK1 biosynthesis outside plastids must be catalyzed by a different enzyme or non-enzymatically. These data are in line with previous identification of VK in plasma membranes of several plant species (Lüthje et al. 1998; Lochner et al. 2003; Biniek et al. 2017; Kruk unpublished).

It is not quite clear why in the biosynthetic pathway of VK1, the oxidized and not the reduced forms of some intermediates (DHNA-CoA, DHNA) are depicted in the literature (Oostende et al. 2011; Basset et al. 2017), even though their names suggest that the naphthoquinone rings are in the reduced state. This is in contrast to the pathway of MK in E. coli where only the reduced forms are indicated (Nowicka and Kruk 2010; Chen et al. 2013). Biosynthesis of MKs in bacteria takes place predominantly under anaerobic conditions which prevents oxidation of the reduced forms, while in plants the reduced forms might undergo oxidation under oxygen atmosphere due to instability of the reduced naphthoquinones (low redox potential). Therefore, the question is if the enzymes, such as DHNAT and ABC4 accept both substrate redox forms or not. It has been shown that Synechocystis DHNAT is active on oxidized DHNA-CoA in vitro (Widhalm et al. 2009). In contrast, DHNAT activity was measured on Arabidopsis extracts and subcellular fractions in presence of DTT, which likely reduced the DHNA-CoA substrate (Widhalm et al. 2012). It therefore appears that DHNAT is active on both oxidized and reduced DHNA-CoA at least in vitro. In the case of MENG methyl transferase, it was shown that the enzyme shows strict preference for the reduced form, therefore the action of the additional reducing enzyme NDC1 in plants and its NdbB ortholog in cyanobacteria (Fatihi et al. 2015), preceding methylation reaction, is required. In contrast to MenG/UbiE methyltransferase of E. coli, MENG and its cyanobacterial orthologs are strictly monofunctional naphthoquinol C-methyltransferase in most cyanobacteria and plastids (Stutts et al. 2023; Dickinson et al. 2025) with two so far identified exceptions in Gloeobacter violaceus PCC 7421 and Synechococcus sp. JA-2-3B’a, where the enzyme is bifunctional and shows activity in both MQ-8 and ubiquinone-8 synthesis when cloned in E. coli. This indicates evolutionary ancient nature of the transferase in these species of cyanobacteria (Dickinson et al. 2025).

The presence of MQ-4 in some algae and soybean raises the question about its biosynthesis. One possibility is that the prenyltransferase in these organisms shows different specificity regarding the side-chain transferred. The other, suggested in the case of soybean, is the action of enzyme homologs to mammalian UBIAD1, which replace the phytyl to geranylgeranyl side chains in VK1. A sequence analysis on mammalian UBIAD1 showed that some homologue sequences are present in cyanobacteria, green algae and higher plants (Biniek et al. 2017). In soybean, a noncharacterized protein with a sequence identity of 22% and similarity of 40% was found. Certainly, the function of UBIAD1 in the photosynthetic organisms deserves further studies.

Conclusions

Although it has been known for years that VK plays various functions in vertebrates, bacteria and photosynthetic organisms, further progress has been done in the recognition of VK function in these groups of organisms only recently. For example, in photosynthetic organisms, the structure, function and the catalytic mechanism of the enzyme responsible for disulfide bond formation in target proteins was elucidated (LTO1, SynDsbAB).

Nevertheless, several questions deserve to be answered. Is there any biological function of UBIAD1 prenyltransferase homologue in plants? It is also to be explained why only the mammalian VKOR is able to reduce VK epoxide, in contrast to enzymes from bacteria and photosynthetic organisms. The compartmentation of some of VK1 biosynthetic enzymes and their substrate redox state specificity (DHNAT1/2, ABC4) are to be elucidated. In some species of photosynthetic organisms, MK-4 origin is to be explored. Moreover, the function of plasma membrane VK-mediated electron transport and that of LTO1 in plants are to be examined more thoroughly.
