Stemness plasticity in hepatocellular carcinoma: spatial niches and therapeutic implications
Authors: Hye Ri Ahn, Sujin Kim, Hyo Jung Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major global health burden, with therapeutic resistance limiting the long-term efficacy of current treatments. Although cancer stem cell (CSC) models have been proposed to explain tumor initiation and relapse, strategies targeting marker-defined CSC populations have shown limited clinical benefit. Increasing evidence indicates that stemness in HCC is not a fixed cellular trait but a dynamic and reversible state that can be induced, maintained, and re-established under therapeutic pressure. This stemness plasticity is regulated by tumor-intrinsic signaling pathways, including transforming growth factor beta, Yes-associated protein/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif, and Wnt/β-catenin, and stabilized by epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms such as N6-methyladenosine modification. In parallel, spatially organized tumor microenvironmental niches composed of cancer-associated fibroblasts, macrophages, and endothelial cells provide critical signals that support stem-like phenotypes and immune evasion. These interconnected mechanisms collectively contribute to therapeutic resistance and tumor progression. This review synthesizes current evidence linking stemness plasticity to spatial niche regulation and treatment failure in HCC, and outlines a therapeutic framework that integrates targeting of intracellular regulatory programs with disruption of microenvironmental support systems. We further discuss the implications of this framework for the development of combination strategies and dynamic biomarkers that better capture the context-dependent nature of stemness. Together, these insights support a shift from targeting static cell populations toward targeting the regulatory processes that govern tumor cell plasticity in HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and despite advances in systemic therapies, durable clinical benefit remains limited.1,2 Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations have improved outcomes, sustained responses are observed in only a subset of patients, with most cases eventually developing therapeutic resistance.3-5 This clinical pattern suggests the presence of adaptive tumor cell states that enable survival under treatment pressure.6,7 Although pharmacokinetic factors, genetic clonal selection, and pre-existing intratumoral heterogeneity contribute to therapeutic resistance, these mechanisms do not fully account for the dynamic and reversible patterns of treatment response observed in HCC.8 Single-cell analyses have identified resistant phenotypes in the absence of corresponding genetic alterations, highlighting the contribution of non-genetic adaptive cell states to treatment failure. These observations position stemness plasticity as a complementary mechanism of therapeutic resistance that operates alongside genetic and clonal evolutionary processes.9-11

Tumor cell stemness has long been considered a central mechanism underlying therapeutic resistance and disease progression.7 Traditionally, cancer stem cells have been defined as a rare, hierarchically organized population characterized by specific surface markers.12-14 However, emerging evidence challenges this static model, demonstrating that stem-like phenotypes can be dynamically acquired and lost. These findings indicate that stemness represents a reversible functional state rather than a fixed cellular identity.15-17

This conceptual shift raises a critical question: under what conditions do tumor cells transition into stem-like states? Accumulating evidence suggests that stemness is not an intrinsic property of a predefined cell population, but rather emerges from the interaction between tumor-intrinsic regulatory programs and microenvironmental signals.18-20 Advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics further reveal that stem-like tumor cells are not randomly distributed, but instead localized within specific microenvironmental niches.21,22

In this review, we examine stemness in HCC as a dynamic state-transition process operating within a spatially organized tumor microenvironment (TME). HCC provides a particularly relevant context for this discussion because it arises in a chronically injured and regenerating organ characterized by substantial cellular plasticity, recurrent activation of developmental programs, and an intrinsically tolerogenic immune environment.23,24 We therefore focus on how niche-derived signals and tumor-intrinsic regulatory programs interact to govern stem-like state transitions, and how these processes contribute to therapeutic resistance and disease progression.

Theoretical clarity requires explicit definitions for the central constructs employed in this analysis. “Stemness” denotes the functional capacity for self-renewal and tumor initiation. “Stemlike state” refers to a transient cellular configuration exhibiting stemness. “Plasticity” describes the capacity for state interconversion. “State transition” denotes the directional switch between configurations. The regulatory pathways implicated in stemness plasticity, including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), Wnt/β-catenin, and Yes-associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) signaling, overlap in multiple respects with canonical drivers of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). However, stemness plasticity is conceptually broader, encompassing the reacquisition of self-renewal and tumor-initiating capacity rather than the change in migratory or morphological phenotype that defines EMT. Accordingly, EMT and dedifferentiation are positioned as potential mechanisms through which stem-like states may be acquired, rather than as comprehensive descriptions of stemness plasticity.

The liver represents a unique setting in which extensive regenerative capacity is coupled to cellular plasticity, enabling mature hepatocytes and biliary epithelial cells to undergo lineage reprogramming and cell-state transitions during injury and repair.25,26 This physiological plasticity, together with the recurrent injury–regeneration cycles that characterize chronic liver disease and the ductular reaction, provides a permissive background in which stemness-associated and oncofetal programs can be reactivated. 24,26 The liver is also an inherently tolerogenic organ, with a microenvironment characterized by mechanisms that suppress immune responses.27 These features suggest that the interplay between tissue regeneration, oncofetal reprogramming, and immune tolerance may render HCC especially prone to the dynamic, niche-coupled stemness behavior that is the focus of this review.

FROM CANCER STEM CELLS TO DYNAMIC, CONTEXT-DEPENDENT STEMNESS STATES

Tumor cell stemness in HCC has traditionally been conceptualized within the framework of the cancer stem cell (CSC) model, in which a rare subpopulation of tumor cells possesses self-renewal capacity and tumor-initiating potential.15,28 These cells have been operationally defined by the expression of surface markers such as CD133, epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), CD90, and CD44, and have been associated with tumor aggressiveness, metastatic potential, and resistance to therapy.13,29-31

However, accumulating evidence indicates that this static and hierarchical model is insufficient to fully explain the biological behavior of HCC.22,32,33 Multiple studies have demonstrated that HCC cells can dynamically alter their stemness-associated marker states and reacquire tumor-initiating properties in response to intrinsic programs and microenvironmental cues, suggesting that stemness is not a fixed property restricted to a predefined subset of cells.7,34,35 Consistent with this view, lineage-tracing and lineage-ablation studies in HCC models have provided insights into liver CSC plasticity, renewal, and therapeutic response, while functional studies further indicate that stem-like capacity can be dynamically regulated by microenvironmental cues.35,36

Advances in single-cell transcriptomic analyses have further challenged the discrete CSC paradigm by revealing substantial heterogeneity in stemness-associated transcriptional programs. 21,37 Rather than being confined to a clearly defined subpopulation, stemness appears to be distributed along a continuum of cellular states.21 Computational approaches, including pseudotime inference and RNA velocity, support the existence of dynamic trajectories linking stem-like and more differentiated tumor cell states, although direct evidence for the kinetics and reversibility of these transitions in HCC remains limited.38,39

Collectively, these observations support a conceptual shift in which stemness is best understood not as a fixed cellular identity, but as a dynamic and reversible cell state. In this framework, tumor cells exist along a continuous state spectrum, with the potential to transition between stem-like and differentiated phenotypes depending on regulatory inputs.40,41 Importantly, this perspective implies that therapeutic resistance is driven not only by the persistence of pre-existing stem-like cells, but also by the ability of tumor cells to acquire or re-enter stem-like states through intrinsic signaling pathways and extrinsic microenvironmental cues under therapeutic stress.42,43

Having established stemness as a dynamic and reversible state, we next consider how its contribution varies across the heterogeneous landscape of HCC. The marked molecular and biological heterogeneity of HCC suggests that the contribution of stemness plasticity to tumor progression, as well as the organization of stemness-supportive niches, is likely to vary according to molecular subtype and disease etiology. At the molecular level, transcriptomic classification studies have distinguished a proliferative class, enriched for progenitor- and stemness-associated features such as EpCAM and Keratin 19 expression and associated with more aggressive behavior, from a non-proliferative class with a more differentiated, hepatocyte-like phenotype.44,45 Distinct molecular and histological subclasses, including CTNNB1/β-catenin-activated tumors, are further characterized by unique oncogenic programs and immune-microenvironmental features; notably, β-catenin-activated HCC is associated with an immune-excluded phenotype and reduced responsiveness to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 therapy.46,47 Stemness programs, their dominant regulatory drivers, and the composition of supportive niches are therefore likely to differ across subtypes, for example, between the progenitor- and stemness-enriched proliferative class and the Wnt/β-catenin-activated, immune-excluded class, so that the relative contribution of stemness plasticity to progression and treatment resistance differs accordingly across these subtypes.

In addition to molecular subtype, disease etiology is also an important determinant of the cellular and immune context in which stemness-associated programs emerge, broadly distinguishing virus-related HCC, including hepatitis B virus (HBV)- and hepatitis C virus-associated disease, from non-viral HCC associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) or alcohol-related liver disease. Chronic viral hepatitis drives immune-mediated injury and an immunosuppressive, macrophage-rich microenvironment marked by T cell exhaustion; in HBV-associated tumors, which are the best characterized at single-cell resolution, macrophage–tumor cell interactions have been shown to enhance cancer stemness. By contrast, MASLD-related HCC is characterized by metabolic inflammation and dysfunctional CD8+ T cell responses that may reduce responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade. Alcohol-related HCC is commonly associated with chronic inflammatory and fibrotic injury, but direct evidence linking alcohol etiology to specific stemness programs remains limited. Therefore, stemness plasticity should be interpreted as a context-dependent process rather than a uniform feature of all HCCs.48-51

INTRACELLULAR MECHANISMS GOVERNING STEMNESS STATE TRANSITIONS

The transition of tumor cells into and out of stem-like states is regulated by tumor-intrinsic mechanisms that interpret and respond to microenvironmental signals.7,35 These intracellular programs provide a mechanistic basis through which external cues are translated into dynamic and reversible changes in cell state.33

A key component of this process is the activation of state-switching programs that drive transitions toward stem-like phenotypes. Among these, EMT, particularly partial or hybrid EMT states, has been closely associated with enhanced cellular plasticity and stemness.52,53 TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling is a representative inducer of partial EMT in HCC, promoting intermediate epithelial-mesenchymal states that are linked to stem-like phenotypes and therapeutic resistance.54,55 Rather than a binary process, EMT represents a continuum of intermediate states that confer both migratory capacity and stem-like properties. In parallel, signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, YAP/TAZ, and neurogenic locus notch homolog protein (NOTCH) act as central regulators that integrate extracellular cues and activate transcriptional programs associated with self-renewal and undifferentiated states.52,56 Recent studies further highlight the YAP axis as a central node linking mechanotransduction, stemness plasticity, and immune evasion in HCC.57,58

Beyond state switching, the maintenance of stem-like states requires stabilization mechanisms that reinforce these phenotypes. Epigenetic and epitranscriptomic regulation, including chromatin remodeling and RNA modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), have been implicated in sustaining stemness-associated gene expression programs.59-61 In particular, m6A regulators such as methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) and YTH N6-methyladenosine RNA-binding protein 1 (YTHDF1) have been reported to modulate liver cancer stem cell properties and therapeutic resistance through multiple downstream pathways, including frizzled class receptor 10 (FZD10)/Wnt-YAP, suppressor of cytokine signaling 3/Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), epidermal growth factor receptor, and NOTCH1 signaling.62-64 For example, METTL3-mediated m6A modification of FZD10 has been linked to activation of Wnt/β-catenin and YAP-related signaling, thereby promoting liver cancer stem cell properties and lenvatinib resistance.65 In addition, metabolic reprogramming, including enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation, contributes to the stabilization of stem-like states through epigenetic mechanisms such as histone lactylation. Histone lactylation has been shown to transcriptionally activate stemness-associated genes, including MCM7, thereby promoting cancer stem cell maintenance and therapeutic resistance in HCC. Hypoxic conditions may further reinforce stemness plasticity by promoting metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic adaptation within the TME.66-69

Importantly, these intrinsic regulatory programs do not operate in isolation but are tightly coupled with microenvironmental signals. The same external cues may induce different cellular outcomes depending on the intrinsic state of tumor cells, suggesting that stemness emerges from the dynamic interplay between intracellular regulatory networks and the TME.7,35

Collectively, these observations suggest that stemness in HCC is shaped by tumor-intrinsic mechanisms that promote both the transition into stem-like states and the stabilization of these phenotypes, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for tumor cell plasticity and therapeutic resistance.7,14

SPATIAL NICHE REGULATION OF STEMNESS

While tumor-intrinsic mechanisms govern the execution of stemness state transitions, these processes are strongly influenced by the spatial organization of the TME. Spatial transcriptomic and single-cell studies in HCC have identified structured oncofetal-like niches in which stromal, immune, and vascular components are spatially coordinated, with regions enriched for stemlike tumor cell states.70,71

Within these niches, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and endothelial cells co-localize to form a multicellular network that shapes the TME. This coordinated cellular organization is associated with enhanced EMT, regulatory T cell enrichment, and immunosuppressive conditions, and has been linked to early relapse and reduced response to immunotherapy.70,72 This spatial organization indicates that stemness is preferentially supported within defined microenvironmental contexts rather than uniformly distributed across tumors.71

CAFs play a central role in shaping the stromal landscape through extracellular matrix remodeling and paracrine signaling. Collagen deposition and matrix stiffening activate mechanotransduction pathways such as integrin-FAK-YAP signaling, while CAF-derived factors including TGF-β, CXCL12, and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) promote stemness-associated programs in tumor cells.35,73-76 Among CAF subsets, periostin-positive (POSTN+) CAFs are enriched within oncofetal niches and are associated with stemness-supportive tumor regions and poor immunotherapy response.70,77 Emerging evidence further suggests that POSTN+ CAFs may contribute to spatial immune barriers that limit effective T cell infiltration, linking CAF-rich niches to impaired antitumor immunity.77 In addition, spatial multiomics analyses have identified follistatin-like protein 1-positive (FSTL1+) CAF populations, characterized by COL1A2, COL4A1, CTGF, and FSTL1 expression, which localize within or around tumor nests and co-localize with cancer cells exhibiting high stemness features, suggesting a role for specific CAF subsets in establishing stemness-supportive microenvironments.71

TAMs provide an additional immune-regulatory layer within these niches. Spatial analyses have shown that secreted phosphoprotein 1-positive (SPP1+) macrophages co-localize with stemness-enriched hypoxic regions, where SPP1-related signaling is associated with tumor cell communication, poor prognosis, and unfavorable immunotherapy response.78 These macrophage-rich regions may contribute to stemness-supportive and immunosuppressive environments. Recent studies in liver TMEs further link SPP1+ macrophage interactions to tumor-specific T cell exhaustion, supporting their role in immune evasion within these niches.78,79 Although SPP1 is predominantly discussed in the context of macrophage-rich niches, CAF-derived SPP1 has also been implicated in promoting EMT programs in HCC, suggesting that SPP1-mediated signaling may represent a convergent mechanism across multiple stromal compartments.80,81

Beyond CAF- and macrophage-associated niches, other immune populations, exhausted CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and tumor-associated neutrophils, contribute to the immunosuppressive, immune-excluded environment associated with stem-like states, although their direct roles in driving stemness remain less well defined in HCC than those of CAFs and macrophages.82,83

Endothelial cells integrate vascular and immune regulation within the niche. Through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)- and NOTCH-dependent signaling, endothelial cells regulate angiogenesis and immune modulation; in this context, NOTCH ligands such as DLL4 and JAG1 expressed by endothelial cells may contribute to niche signaling that influences tumor cell plasticity.72 Oncofetal reprogramming of endothelial cells has been associated with immunoregulatory macrophage populations and sustain an immunosuppressive TME in HCC.72

Importantly, these niche components function as an integrated system in which stromal, immune, and vascular signals converge to shape tumor cell behavior. Rather than being solely determined by tumor-intrinsic programs, stemness emerges from the dynamic interaction between intracellular regulatory networks and spatially organized microenvironmental niches.70,71,78

The frequent co-occurrence of stemness-associated tumor states and immunosuppressive niche features suggests that these processes are closely interconnected within the HCC microenvironment. Several pathways implicated in stemness regulation have also been linked to immune evasion. For example, YAP activation has been associated with both stemness-associated transcriptional programs and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-mediated immune suppression,57 whereas hypoxic niche regions have been associated with the co-localization of stem-like tumor cells and immunosuppressive SPP1+ macrophages. 78 These observations support a model in which stemness-associated and immune-regulatory programs are coordinated within common microenvironmental contexts, and in some cases by shared upstream regulators such as YAP, rather than operating as independent processes. From a clinical perspective, this coupling suggests that resistance to immunotherapy may not be attributable to stemness or the immunosuppressive niche in isolation, and that targeting shared upstream regulators, or combining stemness- and immune-directed approaches, may be more effective than addressing either alone.

These spatial associations should, however, be interpreted within the scope of the methodologies used. Spatial transcriptomic and single-cell approaches identify patterns of cellular organization and potential intercellular communication, but do not by themselves establish direct functional interaction or causal dependency, as they are based primarily on spatial co-localization and computationally inferred signaling relationships.84-86 Nevertheless, the recurrent identification of stemness-associated tumor cells in proximity to specific immune, stromal, and vascular populations across independent studies supports the spatial organization of stemness-supportive niches in HCC. Further integration of spatially resolved molecular profiling with perturbation-based and lineage-resolved approaches will help clarify the mechanistic relationships underlying these cellular associations and their contribution to stemness regulation and tumor progression.87

STEMNESS PLASTICITY AS A DRIVER OF THERAPEUTIC RESISTANCE

Therapeutic resistance remains a major challenge in HCC, and accumulating evidence indicates that stemness plasticity is a key mechanism underlying treatment failure.5,14 Stem-like tumor cell states are not static but can be dynamically induced, maintained, and re-established under therapeutic pressure.

One mechanism contributing to resistance is the selective survival and enrichment of pre-existing stem-like tumor cell populations. 6,13 These cells exhibit enhanced self-renewal capacity, resistance to apoptosis, and increased drug efflux, enabling them to persist following treatment and drive tumor recurrence.

In parallel, non-stem tumor cells can acquire stem-like properties through reprogramming processes.19,88 Treatment-induced stress, including hypoxia and microenvironmental signaling, activates pathways such as EMT, YAP/TAZ, and Wnt/β-catenin, promoting the transition of differentiated tumor cells into stemlike states. Notably, YAP-dependent signaling has been implicated in linking stemness plasticity to immune evasion, as activation of the dysadherin/YAP axis enhances cancer stem-like traits and PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression in HCC.57 Locoregional therapy-induced hypoxia, particularly following transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), has also been associated with increased expression of stemness markers and stromal remodeling.68

The TME further reinforces these processes by providing protective niches that sustain stem-like states. Stromal and immune components, including POSTN+ cancer-associated fibroblasts and SPP1+ TAMs, contribute to immunosuppressive conditions and limit effective antitumor immune responses.70,78 These niche-mediated effects, including T cell exclusion and exhaustion, enhance the survival of stem-like tumor cells under therapeutic pressure. In addition, coordinated interactions between POSTN+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages may establish spatial immune barriers that restrict T cell infiltration and reduce responsiveness to immunotherapy.

Key microenvironmental signaling pathways further amplify these effects. VEGF-dependent signaling contributes to vascular remodeling, immune exclusion, and the formation of stemness-supportive niches, while TGF-β signaling promotes epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity and facilitates the acquisition of transitional cell states associated with stem-like phenotypes. Together, these pathways link microenvironmental regulation to both tumor cell plasticity and immune suppression.2,54,89,90

Importantly, these mechanisms operate in a coordinated manner rather than independently. Stem-like tumor cells are not only selected but also continuously regenerated within supportive microenvironments, creating a dynamic system that sustains resistance. This integrated model highlights that therapeutic resistance in HCC arises from the interplay between tumor-intrinsic plasticity, therapy-induced stress, and spatially organized niche signals.

THERAPEUTIC TARGET LANDSCAPE OF STEMNESS PLASTICITY IN HCC

Current first-line systemic therapy for advanced HCC is built on regimens that remodel the TME-immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations such as atezolizumab-bevacizumab, durvalumab-tremelimumab, and nivolumab-ipilimumab, and multikinase inhibitors such as sorafenib and lenvatinib.2,91,92 Although none was designed to target stemness directly, each acts on pathways intertwined with the stemness-supportive niche, VEGF- and immune-checkpoint-directed agents modulating the vascular and immune compartments that maintain stem-like states, while stemness-associated programs (Wnt/β-catenin, Hippo/YAP, HIF-1α, m6A) have been implicated as determinants of resistance to lenvatinib.62,64,74,93 These therapies may therefore influence stemness plasticity indirectly, through niche regulation, even though their survival benefit has not been shown to be stemness-mediated-motivating a closer look at stemness plasticity as a therapeutic target.

The therapeutic landscape targeting stemness plasticity in HCC spans multiple regulatory layers, including tumor-intrinsic signaling pathways and microenvironmental niche components (Table 1).

Challenges in targeting cancer stem cells in HCC

Therapeutic strategies targeting cancer stem cell populations in HCC largely rely on a static hierarchical view, focusing on the elimination of marker-defined subsets such as EpCAM+ or CD133+ cells.76 While these strategies have demonstrated preclinical promise, their clinical impact has been limited due to the dynamic nature of stemness. Approaches including antibody–drug conjugates, bispecific T cell engagers, and CAR-T therapies targeting glypican-3 or CD133 show preclinical efficacy, but translation into durable clinical benefit remains limited.94-96

This limitation arises from several interrelated challenges. First, marker-positive populations are dynamically reconstituted from marker-negative precursors.97 Tumor cells lacking canonical stemness markers can regenerate marker-positive subpopulations, supporting the view that these populations are plastic rather than fixed.97,98

Second, conventional therapies may inadvertently promote stemness-associated states. Sorafenib exposure has been reported to be associated with enrichment of CD133+/EpCAM+ HCC cells and linked to PI3K/AKT signaling.99,100 Locoregional therapies such as TACE are also associated with increased expression of stemness-related markers, including CK19 and, in some studies, CD133 and EpCAM.101,102 Taken together, these findings underscore that therapeutic stress can favor expansion of stemness-associated phenotypes.99,101

Third, pathway-directed strategies face comparable limitations. Inhibitors of Wnt/β-catenin, NOTCH, and Hedgehog pathways show preclinical activity, but their clinical translation remains limited.15,103,104 This may reflect the parallel activity of other stemness-associated pathways, including JAK/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT, which can reduce the impact of single-pathway inhibition. 15,105

Taken together, these findings indicate that the limited efficacy of current CSC-targeting strategies reflects the dynamic and adaptive nature of stemness. Rather than eliminating a fixed cell population, effective therapies must address the regulatory processes that enable continuous regeneration and maintenance of stem-like states.

Reframing intracellular targets as regulators of stemness state transitions

Because stem-like tumor cell states are dynamically regulated, effective therapeutic strategies should not only eliminate cells expressing stemness markers but also interfere with intracellular programs that enable state transitions and stabilize stem-like phenotypes.14,15 In this context, therapeutic targeting can be broadly conceptualized along two axes: inhibition of state-switching pathways and disruption of stabilization mechanisms.

State-switching pathways represent key drivers of stemness acquisition under therapeutic pressure. Among these, TGF-β signaling has one of the strongest translational rationales in HCC. Although TGF-β is often produced by stromal and immune components within the TME, its downstream SMAD-dependent signaling in tumor cells induces epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, promoting migratory phenotypes and stemness-associated transcriptional programs.54 Pharmacologic inhibition of TGF-β signaling has been evaluated in HCC, including clinical studies of TGF-β receptor I kinase inhibitors and dual TGF-β/PD-L1 blockade.106,107 In parallel, YAP/TAZ and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways contribute to tumor cell plasticity, self-renewal, and resistance to therapy.108,109 While these pathways have demonstrated strong preclinical activity in HCC models, their clinical translation remains investigational.

In addition to state-switching pathways, post-transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms play a critical role in stabilizing stemlike states once they are induced. The m6A regulatory network is a representative upstream control node: METTL3-mediated m6A modification of FZD10 promotes stem-like phenotypes and lenvatinib resistance via Wnt/β-catenin and Hippo/YAP signaling, while the m6A reader YTHDF1 enhances NOTCH1 expression and contributes to resistance to lenvatinib and sorafenib.62,64 These findings suggest that targeting m6A regulators may disrupt multiple stemness-associated pathways simultaneously.

Epigenetic modulation provides an additional approach to destabilize stem-like phenotypes. Histone deacetylase inhibition with resminostat has been reported to alter epithelial–mesenchymal gene expression and sensitize HCC cells to sorafenib in preclinical models, while the resminostat–sorafenib combination showed acceptable safety and early signs of efficacy in a phase I/II study.110,111 These findings indicate that chromatin remodeling can influence plasticity-associated drug resistance.

Metabolic-epigenetic coupling represents an emerging regulatory layer. Histone lactylation, driven by glycolysis-derived lactate, has been linked to stemness-associated gene expression and therapy resistance in preclinical HCC models.66 However, compared with TGF-β, YAP/Wnt signaling, and m6A-dependent regulation, its therapeutic relevance in HCC remains to be fully defined.

Although these intracellular pathways and regulatory mechanisms represent biologically compelling therapeutic targets, their clinical translation is constrained by a fundamental limitation: many of the networks implicated in stemness regulation, including TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin, YAP/TAZ, and NOTCH signaling, are also indispensable for normal stem cell function, tissue homeostasis, and physiological liver regeneration. On-target toxicity arising from this dual role has been a recurring contributor to the limited therapeutic window of agents directed against these pathways. 112-115 This challenge is particularly relevant in the liver, where regenerative capacity is central to organ function and recovery from injury, and where systemic pathway inhibition may compromise hepatocyte renewal and tissue repair.24,116,117 These considerations underscore the need for approaches that selectively disrupt tumor-associated state-transition programs while preserving physiological regenerative processes, including tumor-selective delivery, context-dependent pathway modulation, and the exploitation of tumor-specific downstream effectors.

Disrupting the niche: targeting oncofetal architecture

Beyond tumor-intrinsic programs, spatially organized microenvironmental niches provide critical support for the maintenance of stem-like states.70,72 Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have identified oncofetal-like niches in HCC, characterized by coordinated interactions among POSTN+ CAFs, FOLR2+ macrophages, and PLVAP+ endothelial cells.70,72,77 These multicellular architectures are associated with immune exclusion, early relapse, and reduced responsiveness to immunotherapy, indicating that niche disruption is a rational therapeutic strategy.70

CAF-derived factors, including TGF-β, CXCL12, HGF, and FSTL1, promote epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, tumor-initiating capacity, and therapeutic resistance.35,54,73,76,118 Targeting CAF-tumor interactions or CAF-driven signaling may therefore weaken niche-derived support for stem-like states.

Macrophage-associated signals further reinforce these effects. SPP1+ and FOLR2+ macrophage populations contribute to immunosuppressive microenvironments and may cooperate with CAFs and endothelial cells to restrict T cell infiltration and sustain stemness-associated tumor states.70,72,78 The SPP1-CD44 signaling axis has emerged as a biologically plausible mechanism linking macrophage activity to stemness and immune evasion; however, direct clinical targeting of SPP1 in HCC remains undeveloped. Current therapeutic strategies are more likely to rely on indirect modulation of macrophage recruitment, polarization, or downstream signaling pathways.

The vascular component of the niche provides a clinically validated intervention point. VEGF-NOTCH signaling contributes to endothelial reprogramming, vascular remodeling, and immune exclusion in HCC, and VEGF-directed therapy is already established in first-line HCC treatment.72

Collectively, these observations indicate that the oncofetal niche represents a potentially important therapeutic target. While some components, such as VEGF-dependent signaling, are already clinically actionable, others, including CAF- and macrophage-associated signals, remain under investigation.70,72,78 Because stemness-supportive niches are maintained through coordinated interactions among stromal, immune, vascular, and tumor-intrinsic programs, effective therapeutic disruption may ultimately require combination approaches.119 Potential strategies could include combining microenvironment-directed therapies with immune checkpoint blockade to counteract immune exclusion, or integrating niche-directed interventions with approaches targeting stemness-associated plasticity.9,10,91,93 Such strategies may help limit both microenvironmental support and the emergence of adaptive stem-like tumor states, although their clinical utility remains to be established.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Stemness in HCC is best understood not as a fixed cellular hierarchy, but as a dynamic and reversible state shaped by intracellular programs and spatially organized microenvironments. This perspective explains why therapies targeting marker-defined cancer stem cell populations have shown limited clinical benefit, as stem-like states can be continuously regenerated under therapeutic pressure. Future therapeutic strategies should therefore focus on simultaneously disrupting tumor cell plasticity and the niche signals that sustain it, supported by the development of dynamic, state-based biomarkers to guide precision treatment (Fig. 1).
