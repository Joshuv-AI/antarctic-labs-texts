In vivo shoulder kinematic changes and rotator cuff healing after surgical repair of large-to-massive rotator cuff tears
Authors: Eiji Tashiro, Naoya Kozono, Hidehiko Higaki, Takeshi Shimoto, Yasuharu Nakashima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew studies have investigated the correlation between shoulder kinematics and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing rotator cuff repair using dynamic analysis. This study assessed shoulder kinematics before and after surgical repair in patients with rotator cuff tears (RCTs) and determined the relationship among shoulder kinematics and between shoulder kinematics and clinical outcomes.MethodsTen patients with large-to-massive RCTs and 10 control participants were included. In vivo shoulder kinematics during scapular plane abduction were measured preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively using validated image-registration techniques and compared among the control, preoperative, and postoperative groups. Mixed models were used to compare the effects of the groups on shoulder kinematics, followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to identify the correlations among shoulder kinematics and between each kinematic and clinical outcome.ResultsThe scapula, tilted more anteriorly preoperatively, was not different from the control group postoperatively. Additionally, the change in scapular posterior tilt (PT) throughout dynamic abduction was 18.17° ± 3.59° in the postoperative group, greater than that in the control group (11.54° ± 2.29°; p = 0.0037). The postoperative change in PT significantly correlated with acromiohumeral distance (AHD) and rotator cuff integrity (Sugaya classification) (AHD: r = 0.71, p = 0.023; Sugaya classification: r = − 0.75, p = 0.013), but not preoperative change in PT. Functional score improved from preoperative to postoperative (p < 0.0001). Abduction angle and functional score significantly correlated with Sugaya classification (abduction angle: r = − 0.67, p = 0.034; functional score: r = − 0.70, p = 0.025) but not with shoulder kinematics. Mean superior translation of the humeral head and AHD throughout abduction changed from 1.77 ± 1.34 to 0.61 ± 1.37 and 1.44 ± 1.59 to 2.71 ± 2.27 mm, respectively, from preoperative to postoperative (both p < 0.0001).ConclusionsAfter the surgical repair of large-to-massive RCTs, glenohumeral stability normalized, and the more anteriorly tilted orientation of the scapula improved. Additionally, the preoperative increased scapular motion throughout dynamic abduction was further enhanced postoperatively. Interestingly, in postoperative patients, scapular motion toward PT during dynamic abduction correlated with minimum AHD and cuff healing.Keywords: Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, Shoulder kinematics, Scapular posterior tilt, Rotator cuff healing, Rotator cuff retear, Acromiohumeral distance

Background

Structural changes in the rotator cuff tendon increase with age [1, 2], while imaging findings do not always correlate with clinical symptoms [3, 4]. However, the risk of enlargement of the tear size and the appearance of symptoms increases over time [5, 6]. Most patients with rotator cuff tears (RCTs) can be successfully treated conservatively; however, approximately 25% of patients may require surgical intervention [7]. Surgical approaches for RCTs have evolved from open repair to mini-open and eventually to fully arthroscopic shoulder rotator cuff repair [8, 9]. Additionally, repair techniques have evolved from single-row to double-row and, more recently, to the widely adopted transosseous equivalent or suture-bridge technique [10]. Various techniques have also been reported for massive cuff or irreparable tears [11–17].

The rotator cuff acts as a dynamic stabilizer, maintaining glenohumeral stability and the concentricity of the humeral head on the glenoid during shoulder joint motion [18–20]. Dynamic stabilization depends on the balance of two force couples [21–23]. However, RCTs can disrupt force couples and cause glenohumeral instability and humeral head translations [24–26]. The main direction of the translation is superior, and the acromiohumeral distance (AHD) has been proposed to be a reliable measure of superior translation of the humeral head [27]. Furthermore, translations and AHD are generally considered to worsen as the tear size increases [28–30].

In addition to glenohumeral instability, patients with RCTs usually exhibit scapular dyskinesis with increased scapular upward rotation (UR) and decreased scapular posterior tilt (PT) during arm abduction [31, 32]. These changes in scapular orientation and dynamic motion have also been shown to correlate with tear size [33, 34]. However, previous studies have not clarified whether changes in scapular kinematics are the cause or result of RCTs or compensatory mechanisms to maintain elevation.

Although some studies have reported postoperative changes in humeral head translation and scapular motions [35–38], most of them used static measurement techniques or analyzed patients with relatively small tears. Furthermore, few studies have investigated the correlation between shoulder kinematics and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing rotator cuff repair using dynamic analysis.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) assess the glenohumeral and scapular kinematics during scapular plane abduction before and after rotator cuff repair in patients with large-to-massive RCTs using a dynamic measurement technique and (2) determine the relationships between glenohumeral and scapular kinematics and between these kinematics and clinical outcomes.

We hypothesized that (1) after rotator cuff repair, glenohumeral instability (i.e., increased translation and decreased AHD) and scapular dyskinesis (i.e., decreased PT and increased UR) would improve and (2) PT and UR would correlate with AHD, and the integrity of the repaired rotator cuff would correlate with improvement in shoulder kinematics.

Methods

Patient selection

This study protocol was approved by the institutional review board. All patients provided informed consent to participate in the study. Patients with large-to-massive RCTs scheduled for arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) between July 2015 and May 2021 were prospectively recruited. Large or massive RCTs were first diagnosed by preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and finally confirmed intraoperatively according to the DeOrio and Cofield classification [39]. Tear size was measured in the anterior–posterior direction. Tear patterns of multiple tendon tears were classified according to the Collin classification [40]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ARCR using the suture-bridge repair technique (including cases of medialization within 1 cm), (2) no previous surgery of the ipsilateral shoulder joint, and (3) maximum active abduction angle > 90°. In contrast, the exclusion criteria were (1) irreparable tear, (2) concomitant labral repair, (3) conversion to reverse shoulder arthroplasty, (4) postoperative complications, and (5) follow-up periods < 1 year. An asymptomatic control group with no history of shoulder injury or surgery was recruited for comparison, the results of which have been previously published [41]. Therefore, this study included 10 patients (2 males, 8 females; age: 71 ± 6 years; Table 1) and 10 healthy participants (10 males; age: 32 ± 2 years).

Surgical procedure

All operations were performed using an arthroscopic surgical approach with a knotless suture-bridge repair technique. Medialization of less than 1 cm was allowed when the rotator cuff tendon could not be pulled to the lateral edge of the footprint, even after sufficient mobilization. Two cases resulted in medialized repairs of less than 1 cm. Additionally, biceps tenotomy was performed in two cases. Arthroscopic subacromial decompression was performed in all eligible patients. Acromioplasty was limited to minimal removal of the subacromial spur to preserve the coracoacromial ligament.

An abduction brace was applied immediately after the operation and removed 6 weeks later. Passive abduction in the scapular plane was started 1 week postoperatively; passive external and internal rotations were allowed 6 weeks postoperatively; and active range-of-motion (ROM) exercises were permitted 8 weeks postoperatively. Patients underwent supervised physiotherapy 2–3 times a week for at least 6 months postoperatively.

Clinical data

Clinical ROM was measured by the surgeon using a goniometer preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively. Internal rotation was reported on a 10-point scale based on the anatomic level reached [42]. The University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) shoulder score and MRI scans were also obtained preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively. Fatty infiltration was evaluated using the Goutallier classification modified by Fuchs et al. [43]. Postoperative rotator cuff integrity was evaluated using the Sugaya classification [44]. These assessments were conducted by a single board-certified orthopedic surgeon.

Kinematic analysis

In vivo shoulder kinematics were measured preoperatively and 1 year postoperatively.

Continuous radiographic images were collected during dynamic scapular plane abduction (30° anterior to the coronal plane) at a sampling rate of 10 frames per second (Fig. 1A). Each participant stood in front of a flat-panel detector (Ultimax-I; Toshiba, Tochigi, Japan) with an image area of 420 × 420 mm and resolution of 0.274 × 0.274 mm/pixel and was positioned with the coronal plane perpendicular to the X-ray beam. The participants were instructed to elevate the arm from the side to maximum abduction, with the elbow at full extension and the thumb pointing toward the ceiling. Additionally, they were instructed to avoid trunk movement. Radiographic images were analyzed using a previously published image-matching technique [41, 45, 46], which was performed with custom software in a virtual space on a computer.

Protocol for image-matching techniques. A Continuous radiographic images were collected during dynamic scapular plane abduction using a flat-panel detector. B Computed tomography (CT) slices were converted to voxels to reconstruct the three-dimensional (3D) bone model, and digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) were generated by computer simulation of the radiographic process with the 3D bone model. C The position and orientation of the humerus and scapula were determined using the image correlations between the continuous radiographic images and DRRs

Computed tomography (CT) (Aquilion, Toshiba, Tochigi, Japan) images of the entire humerus and scapula of each participant were obtained using the following settings: image matrix, 512 × 512; 0.35 × 0.35 mm/pixel; and thickness, 1 mm. CT data were converted into voxels to reconstruct a three-dimensional (3D) grayscale digital bone model. The 3D bone model was placed in a virtual 3D space on a computer, and a computer simulation of the radiographic process was performed under the same imaging conditions as actual dynamic radiography in terms of the light source and projection plane to create virtual digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) (Fig. 1B). DRRs display bone contours and contain pixel values reflecting X-ray absorption values (i.e., tissue density), which consist of gradation images, as in continuous radiographic images. The density-based DRRs were overlaid on continuous radiographic images to match each contour, and the positions and orientations of the DRRs were fine-tuned using the correlations between the pixel values of the DRRs and real radiographic images (Fig. 1C).

A previous study estimated that the root-mean-square errors of this matching method were 0.16 mm for in-plane translation, 0.12 mm for out-of-plane translation, and 0.22° for rotations [47]. The anatomical coordinate systems of the humerus and scapula, as recommended by the International Society of Biomechanics, were embedded in each 3D bone model (Fig. 2) [41, 48]. The origin of the humerus was set at the center of the humeral head, which was defined by fitting a sphere to the articular surface. The Z-axis was defined as the line connecting the origin of the humerus and the midpoint of the medial and lateral epicondyles; the Y-axis was the line perpendicular to the plane formed by the origin of the humerus, medial epicondyle, and lateral epicondyle; and the X-axis was the line perpendicular to the Z- and Y-axes. Furthermore, the origin of the scapula was the angulus acromialis. The X-axis was defined as the line connecting the origin of the scapula and the trigonum spinae scapulae; the Y-axis was the line perpendicular to the plane formed by the origin, trigonum spinae scapulae, and angulus inferior; and the Z-axis was the line perpendicular to the X- and Y-axes.

Anatomical coordinate systems of the humerus and scapula. Humeral abduction was defined as rotation around the Y-axis of the humerus. Scapular motion was defined as rotation around the X-axis (positive, posterior tilt), Y-axis (positive, upward rotation), or Z-axis (positive, external rotation)

Humeral abduction was defined as rotation around the Y-axis of the humerus. The humeral head translation was defined as the movement of the origin of the humerus relative to the origin of the scapula in the superior/inferior (S/I), anterior/posterior (A/P), and lateral/medial (L/M) directions. Humeral head translations were normalized relative to the starting position of abduction. AHD was defined as the shortest 3D distance between the acromion and humeral head, which was measured using commercial software (CATIA V5; Dassault Systemes, Velizy-Villacoublay, France) [49]. Humeral head translations and AHD were averaged across participants at 15° intervals from 15 to 135° humeral abduction. Additionally, the mean humeral head translation and AHD across the ROM were calculated.

Scapular motion was defined as rotation around the X-axis (positive, PT; negative, anterior tilt), Y-axis (positive, UR; negative, downward rotation), or Z-axis (positive, external rotation; negative, internal rotation). Scapulohumeral rhythm (SHR) was defined as the ratio of the difference between humeral abduction and UR against the UR in each 15°increment of humeral abduction [50]. Scapular orientation and SHR were averaged across all participants at 15° intervals from 15 to 135° humeral abduction. Furthermore, the mean changes in PT and UR from 15 to 90° humeral abduction were calculated. The humeral abduction angle was selected from 15 to 90° because AHD was minimized by 90° [49, 51], which could enable a more accurate assessment of the relationship between AHD and scapular motion.

Data were compared among the control, preoperative, and postoperative groups. Additionally, the relationships among shoulder kinematics and between the kinematic and clinical data were investigated.

Statistical analyses

JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. A mixed model analysis of variance was used to compare the effects of the groups (control, preoperative, and postoperative groups) on shoulder kinematics. The fixed-effect factors were the between-subject factor of the group and a within-subject factor of the humeral abduction angle, and the random-effect factor was the subject. Pairwise comparisons at each angle were performed using Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test. Additionally, a mixed model with a fixed-effect factor of the group and random-effect factor of the subject was used to examine the effect of the group on the mean humeral head translation and AHD across the ROM and the mean change in PT and UR from 15 to 90° humeral abduction, followed by Tukey’s HSD test. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to identify the correlations among the kinematic outcomes and between each kinematic and clinical outcome. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Effect size was calculated for all significant pairwise comparisons using Cohen’s d.

In the postoperative group, the change in PT during dynamic abduction was significantly correlated with the minimum AHD (r = 0.71, p = 0.023, Fig. 7A), while that in UR was not significantly associated with the minimum AHD (r = 0.25, p = 0.49). Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that patients with large-to-massive rotator cuff tears who presented with greater scapular anterior tilt showed increased posterior tilt after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, and the mean change in posterior tilt during dynamic abduction correlated with the minimum acromiohumeral distance and repaired rotator cuff integrity. Contrary to our hypothesis, the UR increased further postoperatively and did not correlate significantly with the AHD or rotator cuff integrity.

Increased UR in scapular motion has usually been highlighted as a compensatory or adaptive mechanism for the loss of glenohumeral joint function [34, 52–55], although the kinematic change in PT was particularly remarkable in this study. Compared with the control participants, the scapula of the patients, which was more anteriorly tilted preoperatively, was identical postoperatively, and the mean change in PT during dynamic abduction was significantly greater postoperatively. Kijima et al. [31] indicated that the PT was significantly lower in patients with symptomatic RCTs than in healthy controls. They found similar kinematics in patients with asymptomatic RCTs and healthy participants, suggesting that the differences in PT are associated with the occurrence of symptoms in RCTs. Additionally, Baumer et al. [56] demonstrated that in comparison with asymptomatic control participants, PT and glenohumeral joint elevation were smaller in patients with symptomatic RCTs and that after physical therapy, clinical outcomes improved; however, in patients showing abnormal kinematics, only PT improved to the same level as in the control participants. They also mentioned that kinematic changes in the PT could play an important role in the etiology and treatment of RCTs. Similarly, several studies have reported that physical therapy focused on the scapula may improve PT and symptoms [57–59]. However, the present study was not designed to investigate the impact of postoperative rehabilitation and could not account for its effect.

RCTs result from extrinsic and intrinsic mechanisms; however, subacromial impingement, an extrinsic factor, is caused by the narrowing of the subacromial space and is a known risk factor for RCTs and re-tears [60–64]. Patients with subacromial impingement generally show lower scapular PT, lower UR, and greater internal rotation than healthy individuals [65]. However, no previous reports have investigated the relationship between scapular kinematics and the subacromial space in the postoperative shoulder. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to show a correlation between the change in PT and the minimum AHD in postoperative shoulders with RCTs. To date, cadaveric and static studies have not provided a consistent view of the relationship between scapular kinematics and the subacromial space [66–70].

In an in vivo dynamic analysis, Lawrence et al. [71] reported that UR affects the subacromial space depending on the angle of humerothoracic elevation, and in another study [72], they indicated that scapular tilt appears to have the strongest effect on the subacromial space. However, in their study, scapular anterior tilt increased the subacromial space. The primary factor contributing to the discrepancy between previous studies and this study appears to be the difference in participants. A previous study included patients with impingement syndrome and scapular dyskinesis, none of whom were treated. In contrast, the patients in the present study underwent ARCR and showed improved scapular motion. Furthermore, the absence of a correlation between PT and AHD in the control participants and the preoperative assessments supports this speculation. Several studies have also reported on the impact of physical therapy on AHD [56, 73]. Miller et al. found that AHD increased after physical therapy in patients with RCTs; however, the AHD was 2–3 mm smaller than that of those who underwent rotator cuff repair [73].

More importantly, in clinical practice, the change in PT was correlated with postoperative rotator cuff integrity. Similar to the lack of clarity to explain whether scapular dyskinesis is a result, cause, or compensatory movement, we could not determine whether persistent postoperative anterior tilt caused the re-tear or the re-tear caused the anterior tilt again. Nevertheless, the fact that PT correlated with AHD as a potential factor for extrinsic compression and rotator cuff integrity suggests that persistent scapular dyskinesis could have been the cause of the re-tear. Our results are supported by the findings of Song et al. [74], showing that improvement in scapular dyskinesis correlated with rotator cuff healing and that most patients with improved scapular dyskinesis had type 1 scapular dyskinesis (i.e., decreased PT). Furthermore, they reported that patients with improved scapular dyskinesis showed a larger postoperative forward flexion angle and higher postoperative functional score than those with persistent scapular dyskinesis. However, the present study found no association between shoulder kinematics, postoperative ROM, and functional score. Our findings are similar to those of previous reports [35, 75], which suggested that glenohumeral kinematics may not be the primary factor in improving postoperative clinical outcomes.

Rotator cuff integrity was associated with postoperative change in PT, abduction angle, and functional scores. However, PT did not directly correlate with these parameters. A separate analysis of functional scores, taking into consideration muscle strength, pain, and activities of daily living, may help clarify the influence of shoulder kinematics.

UR is generally increased in shoulders with RCTs, as observed in this study and previous reports [31, 36–38, 55]. Kolk et al. [37] reported that increased UR normalized to asymptomatic contralateral scapular motion 1 year postoperatively, whereas in this study, compared with the control group, the mean change in UR during dynamic abduction did not normalize but further increased 1 year postoperatively. This discrepancy may reflect differences in tear size. The previous study included participants with small- and medium-sized tears, which were smaller than those in our patients. Ueda et al. [38] demonstrated that the increased UR in patients with small RCTs improved 5 months postoperatively, whereas UR did not improve in those with massive tears until 1 year postoperatively. These findings support the results of the present study. Paletta et al. [36] mainly analyzed patients with large tears and reported that abnormal scapular motion normalized 2 years postoperatively. Their findings suggested that normalization of abnormal scapular motion in patients with larger tears might take more than 1 year.

The humeral heads with RCTs showed significantly superior translation preoperatively; however, they showed kinematics similar to those of the control shoulders postoperatively. This finding is consistent with our hypotheses. Several cadaveric studies have shown that rotator cuff repair prevents superior translation of the humeral head [76, 77]. However, Bey et al. [35] reported that the humerus of the repaired shoulder was positioned more superiorly on the glenoid than on the contralateral and healthy shoulders. While their results are inconsistent with those of our study, this discrepancy could be attributed to methodological differences, such as the use of a 3-lb hand weight and the observation of the glenohumeral joint contact center rather than the humeral head center. Additionally, they did not evaluate the preoperative glenohumeral kinematics and the change in humeral position associated with rotator cuff repair was not assessed. Similarly, Kane et al. [75] demonstrated that the humeral head was positioned more superiorly 1 year postoperatively than preoperatively, although their different results from those obtained in our study could be attributed to the inclusion criteria of patients who underwent superior capsule reconstruction (SCR) for irreparable RCTs. In contrast, Paletta et al. [36] found that 86% of patients who indicated superior translation of the humeral head preoperatively demonstrated that the humeral head remained at or below the center of the glenoid postoperatively and that glenohumeral kinematics were normalized to those of controls. Of the 15 patients in their study, 11 had large-to-massive RCTs similar to those in this study.

Regarding dynamic AHD, Bey et al. [35] found that AHD was greater in repaired shoulders than in the contralateral shoulders. They mentioned that this difference probably reflected the acromioplasty. In this study, all image-matching analyses were performed using preoperative CT; therefore, postoperative AHD did not reflect the effect of acromioplasty. Since SCR showed different tendencies of postoperative glenohumeral superior translation and AHD according to graft size [78–80], the thickness of the repaired rotator cuff may have acted as a spacer [75, 81] to decrease superior translation and increase AHD in this study. However, a more probable reason is that the rotator cuff reacquired its concavity-compression mechanism after ARCR since humeral head translations were reduced in all directions in this study.

Limitations

One limitation of this study was that the sex and age distributions of the patients and control participants were not similar. Specifically, older patients have been reported to exhibit increased thoracic kyphosis with more extension and internal rotation of the scapula, limiting abduction [82–84]. A control group with similar ages and postures would have been desirable. These differences may have influenced the findings for shoulder kinematics. Additionally, rotator cuff status in the control group was not directly evaluated, as only X-ray and CT scans were performed without MRI or ultrasound. Scapulothoracic kinematics could also not be evaluated due to the difficulties in defining the thoracic spine and sternum using image-matching techniques. Furthermore, the number of participants included in the study was small. In this study, AHD was measured as a 3D distance, making it difficult to compare with measurements obtained from plain radiographs. Finally, the imaging evaluations were conducted by a single surgeon, which limited the reliability of the data. Therefore, these limitations should be accounted for when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

After the surgical repair of large-to-massive RCTs, glenohumeral stability normalized, the more anteriorly tilted orientation of the scapula improved, and the preoperative increased scapular motion throughout dynamic abduction was further enhanced. In particular, increased motion toward the PT correlated with minimum AHD and rotator cuff healing.
