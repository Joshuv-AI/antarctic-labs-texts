Impact of the Simultaneous Loss of the Plastid Envelope‐Localized Homologous Pair Ycf10 and DLDG1 in Plant Growth and Photosynthetic Performance
Authors: Elham Esmailpourmoghadam, Mai Duy Luu Trinh, Ryoichi Sato, Masaru Kono, Issei Nakazato, Shin‐ichi Arimura, Michael Palmgren, Shinji Masuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYcf10 and DLDG1 are homologous proteins embedded in the chloroplast envelope membranes (EM) and encoded in the plastidial and nuclear genomes, respectively. We previously characterized Arabidopsis dldg1 and tobacco ycf10 mutants, and showed that these proteins mediate proton transport across the EM, thereby influencing ATP synthesis and the non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ) induction. However, the consequences of the simultaneous loss of Ycf10/DLDG1 homologs have remained unclear. Here, we generated and analyzed Arabidopsis ycf10 and ycf10‐dldg1 double mutants. These mutants exhibited a pale‐green phenotype under continuous light conditions, which was complemented by the exogenous addition of NaCl, as previously observed in mutants lacking the plastid EM‐localized K+/H+ antiporters KEA1 and KEA2. Both single mutants (ycf10 and dldg1), as well as the ycf10‐dldg1 double mutant, showed enhanced NPQ induction compared with wild‐type. Furthermore, the ycf10‐dldg1 double mutant showed stronger NPQ induction than either single mutant upon transition from dark to light, suggesting that Ycf10 and DLDG1 function independently while playing redundant roles. These mutants also showed reduced stomatal conductance and sugar accumulation compared with the wild‐type. Together, these findings indicate that EM‐localized DLDG1 and Ycf10 act in concert to maintain chloroplast proton/ion homeostasis, contributing to chloroplast pH homeostasis for sustaining plant growth and efficient photosynthesis.Keywords: arabidopsis, chloroplast, DLDG1, photosynthesis, Ycf10

Introduction

Chloroplasts originated from an ancient cyanobacterial endosymbiont over 1.5 billion years ago (Archibald 2015). Throughout their coevolution with the nuclear genome, the chloroplast genome has retained a degree of independence, despite undergoing significant reduction (Zhang et al. 2024). The chloroplast proteome comprises approximately 3000 proteins, the majority of which are encoded in the nucleus, as many genes from the ancestral chloroplast genome were transferred to the nucleus during evolution (Dobrogojski et al. 2020). To date, complete nucleotide sequences of chloroplast genomes from various land plants and algae have been determined (Asano et al. 2004; Chunwongse et al. 2016; Hirao et al. 2008; Reith and Munholland 1995; Wakasugi et al. 1997; Wu et al. 2009). Most genes retained in plastid genomes are involved in gene expression and photosynthesis. In addition, several hypothetical chloroplast open reading frames (ycfs) are highly conserved and likely play important functional roles (Boudreau et al. 1997). The Arabidopsis chloroplast retains the ycf10 gene, along with many other key genes for photosynthesis, fatty acid synthesis, and subunits of electron transport chain complexes (Allen 2015). Ycf10 homologs are highly and specifically conserved among oxygenic phototrophs, including cyanobacteria (Harada et al. 2019), underscoring their important functional role.

Pea Ycf10 was first studied and shown to localize in the chloroplast envelope membrane (EM), where it was annotated as chloroplast EM protein A (CemA; Sasaki et al. 1993). Moreover, the Ycf10 homolog localized in the cytoplasmic membrane of the cyanobacterium Synechocystis sp. PCC6803 (PxcA/slr1596) was found to be crucial for H+ extrusion from the cytoplasm to the outside of the cells upon transition from dark to light (Katoh et al. 1996; Sonoda et al. 1998; Inago et al. 2020), suggesting that Ycf10 homologs are involved in light‐regulated H+ translocation across the chloroplast EM in plants and the cytoplasmic membrane in cyanobacteria.

In natural environments, light intensity fluctuates substantially. Under excess light conditions, plants dissipate surplus excitation energy through non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ) to prevent overexcitation of chlorophylls, reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, and photoinhibition (Tikkanen et al. 2015). In land plants, the major component of NPQ is energy‐dependent quenching (qE), which is triggered by acidification of the thylakoid lumen (Niyogi et al. 1998; Li et al. 2000; Nicol and Croce 2021). A low lumenal pH induces protonation of the PsbS protein and promotes the conversion of violaxanthin to zeaxanthin via violaxanthin de‐epoxidase activity (Berne et al. 2018; Demmig‐Adams et al. 2020; Hieber et al. 2002; Blommaert et al. 2021). These processes facilitate thermal dissipation of excess absorbed light energy likely through PsbS‐dependent aggregation of LHCII complexes (Johnson et al. 2011; Li et al. 2000). Because both induction and relaxation of NPQ strongly depend on lumenal proton dynamics, fine regulation of thylakoid proton homeostasis is essential for optimizing photosynthetic efficiency under fluctuating light conditions (Ruban and Wilson 2021; Zuo 2025).

Ycf10 shares homology with the nuclear‐encoded Day‐Length‐dependent Delayed Greening1 (DLDG1) in plants (Figure S1; Harada et al. 2019). We previously showed that tobacco Ycf10 mutants exhibit a high NPQ phenotype during the induction stages of photosynthesis, similar to that observed in Arabidopsis dldg1 mutants (Harada et al. 2019; Trinh et al. 2021; Suzuki and Masuda 2023). Notably, Arabidopsis dldg1 mutants displayed a slower photosystem II (PSII) antenna state transition from quenched (State II) to unquenched (State I) upon a shift from high to low light, perhaps due to slower ΔpH relaxation (Harada et al. 2019), as well as reduced survival under high‐light and heat stress conditions (Balfagón et al. 2024). These findings suggest that EM‐localized DLDG1 and Ycf10 are key regulators of H+ and ion homeostasis, thereby fine‐tuning photosynthesis and NPQ (Trinh and Masuda 2022).

In addition to DLDG1 and Ycf10, the chloroplast EM contains several proteins involved in proton transport. Among them are the K+/H+ antiporters KEA1 and KEA2 (Kunz et al. 2014; Tsujii et al. 2019). Previous studies have suggested that DLDG1 and Ycf10 function primarily under illuminated conditions, whereas KEA1 and KEA2, together with the thylakoid membrane‐localized K+/H+ antiporter KEA3, function predominantly during transitions from high to low light or from light to darkness (Armbruster et al. 2014; Aranda Sicilia et al. 2021; Trinh and Masuda 2022). Indeed, the phenotypes of the dldg1 and ycf10 mutants are observed under continuous light (CL) conditions (Harada et al. 2019), whereas the phenotype of the kea3 mutant is most pronounced under fluctuating light conditions (Armbruster et al. 2016). These observations suggest that proton and ion fluxes across the chloroplast EM are tightly regulated through the coordinated actions of multiple membrane transporters.

A recent study suggested that Arabidopsis DLDG1 is involved in iron transport into the chloroplast, and the protein was named Fe‐deficiency‐induced chlorosis1 (FIC1; Zhang et al. 2025), although we had already named the protein DLDG1 in 2019 (Harada et al. 2019). However, the impact of losing both Ycf10 and DLDG1/FIC1 homologs on the photosynthetic regulation of land plants remains unclear.

Recently, a new technology for editing the chloroplast genome was established using transcription activator‐like effector nuclease (TALEN; Nakazato et al. 2021; Nakazato and Arimura 2025). In this technique, the nuclease domain of TALEN was replaced with a cytidine deaminase that converts C:G pairs to T:A pairs (Mok et al. 2020), generating a system designated as TALE‐CD (Nakazato et al. 2021; Hosoda et al. 2024). Using the base‐editing technique, we successfully isolated Arabidopsis ycf10 loss‐of‐function mutants and subsequently generated ycf10‐dldg1 double mutants by crossing the respective single mutants. This enabled us to characterize the consequences of losing both Ycf10 and DLDG1 homologs. Phenotypic analysis of the mutants revealed that the EM‐localized DLDG1 and Ycf10 functionally interact to maintain chloroplast proton/ion homeostasis, which is essential for chloroplast metabolism and efficient photosynthesis.

Material and Methods

Plant Materials and Mutant Construction

Arabidopsis thaliana WT (Columbia‐0 ecotype), and the T‐DNA insertion mutant of DLDG1 (At4g31040), dldg1‐2 (SAIL‐253‐F06; Harada et al. 2019), were used in this study. Two TALE‐CD toolkits constructed as described below were employed to knock out the ycf10 gene. The TALE‐CD toolkit was designed to substitute G/C pairs with A/T pairs within the target windows, thereby introducing a premature stop codon early in the ycf10 coding region (Figure S2; Nakazato and Arimura 2024). Specifically, the cytidine deaminase was split after Gly 1397 to reduce its toxicity (Mok et al. 2020), with the C‐ and N‐terminal halves fused to the left and right TALE domains, respectively. TALE‐CD molecules generated in this manner are referred to as 1397CN. The TALE‐binding DNA sequences listed in Table S1 were designed such that the targeted C:G pair was positioned at sites within the target window that, in our previous studies, were preferentially edited by TALE‐CD 1397CN (Nakazato et al. 2021; Nakazato et al. 2022; Nakazato et al. 2023; Hosoda et al. 2024; Nakazato et al. 2025). In the present study, the target guanines (62G and 63G in ycf10) were located at the fourth and fifth positions within the 14‐bp target window of construct 1, whereas the target cytosines (244C and 247C in ycf10) were located at the 10th and 13th positions within the 14‐bp target window of construct 2 (Figure S2A). Binary vectors that encode plastid‐targeted TALE‐CD molecules were subsequently constructed following the previously described protocol (Nakazato and Arimura 2025).

The two chloroplast‐targeting TALE‐CD constructs were independently introduced into Arabidopsis WT using the standard Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation method. After obtaining T1 seeds, a four‐step selection strategy was implemented to isolate homoplasmic ycf10 mutants (Figure S2; Nakazato et al. 2021). First, T1 seeds were screened for GFP fluorescence, which indicated the presence of the T‐DNA marker in the nuclear genome and suggested potential editing of the chloroplast ycf10 locus. Second, total DNA was extracted from leaves of T1 plants, and the ycf10 locus was amplified by PCR. The PCR fragments were directly sequenced using the SuperDye Cycle Sequencing Kit v3.1 (Advances). A complete list of the primers used in these analyses is provided in Table S2. This step enabled the isolation of homoplasmic ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2 mutants harboring site‐specific mutations at two target windows. Third, transgene‐free offspring retaining the ycf10 mutation in the chloroplast genome were isolated based on genetic segregation and the uniparental maternal inheritance of plastids (Daniell 2007; He et al. 2022). Accordingly, T2 seeds lacking GFP fluorescence were selected, indicating the segregation and removal of the inserted T‐DNA. The ycf10 mutation was verified by direct sequencing of PCR products from the ycf10 locus. Finally, the sensitivity of the transgenic seeds to 50 μg ml−1 kanamycin was tested on medium to further confirm the absence of T‐DNA markers. To verify the stability and heritability of the introduced mutations, the edited lines were propagated through an additional backcrossing, ensuring both chloroplast genome homoplasmy and the absence of nuclear transgenes. The ycf10‐1 was used as ycf10 mutant throughout this study.

Subsequently, the ycf10‐dldg1 double mutant was generated by crossing the isolated ycf10‐1 line with dldg1‐2 (Harada et al. 2019). In the F2 progeny, homoplasmic ycf10 and homozygous dldg1 double mutants were obtained. The dldg1 homozygous mutation was verified by PCR genotyping as reported previously (Harada et al. 2019); primers used are shown in Table S2.

The DLDG1 overexpression line was constructed as follows: the region of DLDG1 was amplified by PCR with isolated genomic DNA as a template, and the primer pair, Fw‐DLDG1 and Rv‐DLDG1‐FLAG (Table S2). The amplified fragment was cloned into the pRI201‐AN vector (TaKaRa) using the In‐fusion cloning system (TaKaRa). The plasmid construct was then introduced into the dldg1 mutant by the standard Agrobacterium‐mediated flower infiltration method.

Growth Conditions

Seeds were surface sterilized by treatment with a solution containing 16.6% NaClO and 0.027% Tween‐20 for 3 min, followed by three rinses with distilled water. Seeds obtained after floral dip transformation underwent an additional sterilizing step with 70% ethanol for 1 min. After sterilization, seeds were spread out on half‐strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) plates solidified with 0.8% agar. Stratification was carried out by storing the plates at 4°C in the dark for 3 days to break seed dormancy and promote uniform germination. Plants were then grown under three distinct light regimes: continuous light (CL; 24‐h light, 23°C, 150–200 μmol m−2 s−1), short day (SD; 8‐h light/16‐h dark, 150–200 μmol m−2 s−1), and long‐day (16‐h light/8‐h dark, 150–200 μmol m−2 s−1). Temperature was maintained at 23°C during the light period and 16°C during the dark period. When necessary, 2‐week‐old seedlings were transferred to soil containing a 1:1 mixture of Promix and vermiculite. D(−)‐Mannitol (Nacalai Tesque), NaCl (Nacalai Tesque), or FeSO4·7H2O (Fuji Film) were added to the MS medium when necessary.

Chlorophyll Fluorescence Measurements

The photosynthetic parameters of PSII were calculated from chlorophyll fluorescence (Krause and Weis 1984), which were analyzed using an IMAGING‐PAM Maxi chlorophyll fluorometer (Walz). The fifth and sixth leaves of 3–4 week‐old plants were used in all fluorescence measurements. Prior to measurements, plants were dark‐adapted for 30 min. The maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (F v /F m ) was calculated by comparing variable fluorescence (F m − F 0 ) to maximal fluorescence in the dark‐adapted leaves (F m ), [(F m − F 0 )/F m ] (Butler and Kitajima 1975; Schreiber and Klughammer 2008). The effective quantum yield of PSII [Y(II)] was calculated as (F m ′ − F′)/F m ′ (Genty et al. 1989; Schreiber and Klughammer 2008). NPQ was calculated as (F m − F m ′)/F m ′ (Schreiber and Klughammer 2008; Tietz et al. 2017). where 0.5 represents the proportion of absorbed light directed to PSII, 0.84 corresponds to the leaf absorption efficiency, and I is the incoming light intensity. These values (0.5 and 0.84) are commonly used assumptions in the literature and were not experimentally determined in this study.

Rapid light response curves were obtained under stepwise increments of actinic light as described (White and Critchley 1999; Trinh et al. 2021; Trinh et al. 2025). In separate experiments, NPQ dynamics under fluctuating light were assessed by subjecting leaves to transitions between low light (35 μmol m−2 s−1) and high light (610 μmol m−2 s−1). For NPQ induction and relaxation kinetics, leaves dark‐adapted for 30 min were illuminated with three actinic light intensities (35, 145, and 800 μmol m−2 s−1) for 5 min, followed by a 10‐min dark period. Prior to conducting rapid light curves, chlorophyll fluorescence induction kinetics, and fluctuating light experiments, plants were kept in the dark for 30 min to ensure full relaxation of PSII. The measuring light was applied in consecutive 10‐s intervals (Schreiber and Klughammer 2008). Fluorescence images were continuously recorded and analyzed to derive quantitative parameters using the PAM integrated software (ImagingWin, Walz).

Gas Exchange Measurements Under Normal CO2 Conditions

Plants grown under SD conditions with light intensity of 150–200 μmol m−2 s−1 for 7–8 weeks were used. Gas exchange was measured in intact leaves aged 6–7 weeks in a measuring chamber with the LED‐Array/PAM‐Fluorometer 3057‐FL as the light source using a portable gas exchange system (LI‐6400XT, Li‐COR). The chamber conditions were as follows: CO2 and O2 partial pressures, 40 Pa and 20 kPa. Vapor pressure deficit ranged from 0.6 to 0.9 kPa, leaf temperature was 23°C; humidity = 65%–75%. Light responses of the CO2 assimilation rate (Pn; μmol CO2 m−2 s−1), stomatal conductance to water vapor (gs; mol H2O m−2 s−1) and internal CO2 concentration (Ci; ppm) in the leaves to continuous light at various irradiances of the red and blue actinic light (ratio = 9:1). The leaf was illuminated at the photosynthetic flux density (PPFD) of 110 μmol m−2 s−1 for at least 60 min and then the PPFD was gradually increased to 1200 μmol m−2 s−1 to open stomata. After the stabilization of Pn and gs, the measurement was started and the PPFD was decreased from 1200 to 850, 500, 250, 135, 60, 30, and 0 μmol m−2 s−1. At each light step, the parameters were obtained in the steady‐state attained at 300–600 s after the change in the PPFD. Intrinsic water‐use efficiency (iWUE) was calculated as the ratio of Pn to gs.

Sucrose and Glucose Quantification

Reducing and non‐reducing soluble sugars, including glucose, fructose, sucrose, and others, were extracted using 95% ethanol. For extraction, powdered dry whole‐shoot samples were mixed with solvent at a ratio of 7 g dry matter to 15 mL 95% ethanol and boiled for 20 min. Then centrifuged at 10000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was separated and heated at 80°C to evaporate ethanol. The resulting residue was dissolved in 300 μL distilled water and shaken for 5 min on a microtube shaker (Maness 2010). Subsequently, half of the extract was stored at 4°C, while the remaining portion was incubated with yeast invertase (2 U ml−1 in 50% Glycerin solution; Wako) for 2 h at 65°C. Sucrose content was determined by measuring glucose concentration before and after invertase treatment using the LabAssay Glucose kit (Wako), based on the mutarotase‐glucose oxidase enzymatic method.

Thermal Imaging Experiments

Plants were grown under SD conditions for 4 weeks. Photographs were taken with an infrared camera (FLIR A35 sc, FLIR Systems AB) inside the climate chamber when all growth conditions were maintained and then analyzed using FLIR ResearchIR software. The experiment was repeated five times with similar results.

Western Blotting

Leaves of plants grown under CL conditions were weighed and homogenized in five volumes (vol/wt) of SDS sample buffer containing 125 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 4% (wt/vol) SDS, 20% (vol/vol) glycerol, 0.1 mg ml−1 bromophenol blue, and 10% (vol/vol) 2‐mercaptoethanol. Each homogenate was then divided into two aliquots: one was left at room temperature, and the other was boiled for 2 min. Both samples were centrifuged at 16000 g, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to SDS‐PAGE. The separated proteins were electroblotted onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (GE Healthcare). Immunodetection was carried out using an anti‐FLAG antibody (Anti‐FLAG M2, Merck), and an ECL Plus Western Blotting Detection System (GE Healthcare).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (agricola and tidyverse packages) using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons at a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Isolation and Characterization of Arabidopsis ycf10 Mutants

We designed two different TALE‐CD target windows within the ycf10 locus, located 40–90 bp and 215–270 bp downstream of the start codon, to introduce premature stop codons into the ycf10 gene (Figure S2A). The two TALE‐CD constructs, having the plastid targeting signal peptide at their N‐terminus, were independently introduced into WT Arabidopsis. Because the T‐DNA constructs carried two marker genes, encoding green‐fluorescence protein (GFP) and kanamycin‐resistant gene, T‐DNA‐inserted lines could be identified by screening T1 seeds that exhibited green fluorescence (Nakazato et al. 2021). Approximately 10 fluorescent seeds were isolated from each T1 seed pool. The ycf10 region was amplified by PCR with total DNA extracted from the leaves of individual T1 plants as template, and the DNA products were directly sequenced to examine homoplasmic nucleotide substitutions within ycf10 target windows. We identified recombinant T1 plants harboring homoplasmic nucleotide substitutions at the two target windows: the first window contained mutations 62, 63, and 69 nucleotides downstream of the start codon, and the second window contained a mutation 247 nucleotides downstream. These substitutions included G62A and G63A, and C69T in the first window, and G247A in the second window (Figure S2A,B). These changes generated premature stop codon(s) in the ycf10 gene; the two resulting mutants were designated ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2. At the T2 and T3 generations, we selected non‐fluorescent seeds and kanamycin‐sensitive plants to confirm the absence of the inserted T‐DNA construct (Figure S2C). Following backcrossing with WT, stable homoplasmic mutations were verified in the progeny, demonstrating both the genetic purity and heritability of the engineered lines.

The ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2 mutants grown under CL exhibited pale‐green coloration at the base of the leaf lamina, which gradually disappeared as the leaves expanded (Figure 1A). This phenotype was not observed under short‐day (SD) conditions (Figure 1B). Similar pale‐green phenotypes restricted to CL conditions have also been reported in Arabidopsis dldg1 mutants and tobacco ycf10 mutants (Harada et al. 2019; Trinh et al. 2021), suggesting functional similarity between Ycf10 and DLDG1. Under CL conditions, the pale‐green phenotype was associated with a reduction in the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (F v /F m ; Figure 1A), whereas no such reduction was detected in plants grown under SD conditions (Figure 1B).

We further analyzed the photosynthetic activities of ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2 by rapid light curve measurements. In this method, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were monitored during short illumination periods (10 s) at each light intensity step, allowing assessment of photosynthetic activity during changes in light intensity (White and Critchley 1999). For this analysis, plants were grown under SD conditions, in which the pale‐green phenotype was not observed. As shown in Figure 1C, NPQ values in ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2 were higher than those in WT. In addition, effective quantum yield of PSII [Y(II)] and electron transfer rate at PSII [ETR(II)] were lower in the two mutants compared with WT. These phenotypes resembled those of tobacco ycf10 mutants (Trinh et al. 2021), suggesting that the functions of Ycf10 are conserved among land plants. It should be noted that the phenotypes of ycf10‐1 and ycf10‐2 were essentially identical, indicating that they result from the mutational loss of ycf10 rather than other unrelated mutations. Therefore, ycf10‐1 was used as the representative ycf10 mutant in subsequent experiments.

Isolation of the ycf10‐dldg1 Double Mutant

Next, we generated the ycf10‐dldg1 double mutant by crossing ycf10‐1, generated in this study, with the previously isolated dldg1 mutant (Harada et al. 2019). The resulting ycf10‐dldg1 mutant exhibited pale‐green sectors at the base of developing leaves under CL conditions, but not under SD conditions, similar to the single ycf10 and dldg1 mutants (Figure 2A,B). To evaluate the overall photosynthetic responses of the mutants, the rapid light curve measurement was conducted with plants grown under SD conditions. All mutants exhibited higher NPQ values than WT across the entire range of light intensities (Figure 2C). The elevated NPQ values were accompanied by reductions in Y(II) and ETR(II). NPQ induction kinetics were also measured at low, moderate, and high‐light intensities (35, 145, and 800 μmol m−2 s−1, respectively; Figure 2D). Although all mutants showed higher NPQ values than WT under low‐ and moderate‐light intensities, the subsequent relaxation of NPQ is slower in the double mutant than in the single mutants. These results suggest that Ycf10 and DLDG1 act independently to optimize NPQ induction, at least during the induction phase.

Quantitative chlorophyll fluorescence imaging analysis revealed that F v /F m values were comparable between WT and the ycf10‐dldg1 mutant grown under SD conditions (Figure 3A, left). In contrast, NPQ enhancement in the ycf10‐dldg1 mutant was most pronounced in developing young leaves, particularly at the basal region (Figure 3, red arrows), whereas only minor differences were observed in matured leaves (Figure 3A, white arrows). The lamina near the petiole junction is developmentally younger due to the proximodistal pattern of leaf development in Arabidopsis (Li et al. 2024). Moreover, the basal zone of the lamina corresponds to cell proliferation, while the apical region is associated with cellular differentiation (Richardson 2020). These results suggest that Ycf10 and DLDG1 functions are particularly important during chloroplast proliferation and development. However, the fresh weight of ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants grown under both CL and SD conditions did not differ significantly from that of WT (Figure S3), indicating that Ycf10 and DLDG1 are not required for determining plant size, at least under the laboratory conditions tested. Interestingly, at the reproductive growth stage in soil under CL conditions, inflorescence stem length of ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 is significantly greater, while their rosette leaf number was significantly lower, compared with the WT (Figure 3B). These distinct phenotypes suggest that Ycf10 and DLDG1 contribute to the regulation of plant reproductive growth.

We next examined the responses of the mutants to fluctuating light conditions. Photosynthetic activities were assessed in 28‐day‐old plants grown under SD conditions using an actinic light regime that alternated between 35 μmol m−2 s−1 for 4.5 min and 610 μmol m−2 s−1 for 1 min. In the initial cycles, NPQ was strongly induced in all mutants compared with WT, although it diminished during the subsequent low‐light phases (Figure 4A). The differences in NPQ between WT and the single mutants decreased in later high‐light phases, whereas the difference between WT and the double mutant increased. However, Y(II) gradually recovered during successive illumination (Figure 4B). These findings suggest that Ycf10 and DLDG1 independently contribute to fine‐tuning photosynthetic regulation. The absence of either gene can be partly compensated by the other under sustained fluctuating high‐light exposure; however, in the double mutant, NPQ was progressively enhanced during each high‐light cycle.

We previously showed that the pale‐green phenotype of dldg1 was reverted to a green color in the presence of high concentrations of NaCl (75 mM), as observed in the envelope‐localized K+/H+ antiporter mutant kea1‐kea2 (Kunz et al. 2014; Trinh et al. 2025), suggesting that mutations in DLDG1 and its homolog Ycf10 affect the transmembrane transport of ions, including H+. To further test this hypothesis, we next grew ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 under CL conditions in the presence and absence of high concentrations of NaCl (75 mM). For the analysis, plants initially grown on standard MS medium under CL conditions were transferred either to fresh MS medium as a control or to MS medium supplemented with 75 mM NaCl. As shown in Figure 5, the pale‐green phenotype and reduced F v /F m values of ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 were recovered to WT levels within 5 days after transfer to NaCl‐containing medium (Figure 5A–D), whereas the mutants maintained markedly reduced F v /F m on normal MS plates (Figure 5E). Notably, although F v /F m values were recovered under high‐NaCl conditions (Figure 5E), NPQ levels in the mutants remained higher than those of the WT (Figure 5F). These findings suggest that exogenous NaCl only partially alleviates the negative effect of Ycf10 and DLDG1 loss‐of‐function on the regulation of photosynthetic activities. It should be noted that under NaCl‐containing conditions, NPQ induction in the ycf10‐dldg1 mutant showed no significant differences from that of the single mutants (Figure 5F), unlike in plants grown in soil under SD conditions (Figure 2D), suggesting that Ycf10 and DLDG1 do not functionally compensate for each other under the conditions.

We also grew ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 under CL conditions in the presence and absence of high concentrations of FeSO4 (400 mM), where the pale‐green phenotype of the dldg1/fic1 mutant was previously reported to be alleviated even under CL conditions (Zhang et al. 2025). After transfer to FeSO4‐containing plates, the pale‐green color of the mutants was alleviated (Figure S4). However, plant growth itself was almost completely inhibited, suggesting that the disappearance of the pale‐green color may be due to the inhibition of leaf expansion. The reduced F v /F m values observed in the dldg1 and/or ycf10 mutants were slightly recovered by FeSO4 supplementation (Figure S4); however, this effect was much less pronounced than that observed following NaCl supplementation (Figure 5). These results indicate that NaCl supplementation is more effective than FeSO4 supplementation in compensating for the loss of Ycf10 and DLDG1 function.

Influence of the ycf10 and dldg1 Mutation on Gas Exchange Activity

Next, to investigate the importance of Ycf10/DLDG1‐mediated H+/ion homeostasis in chloroplasts for the regulation of photosynthesis, we measured the gas‐exchange activities of the various mutants. At low light intensity (60 μmol m−2 s−1) under normal CO2 conditions (400 ppm), no significant differences were observed in the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) between WT and the mutants, although the Pn value of dldg1 was significantly lower than that of ycf10‐dldg1 (Figure 6). This suggests that Ycf10 and DLDG1 are not substantially involved in CO2 assimilation under the conditions tested. At moderate to high‐light intensities (> 150 μmol m−2 s−1), the intracellular CO2 concentration (Ci) in ycf10, and stomatal conductance (g s ) in ycf10 were significantly lower than those in WT (Figure 6), suggesting that Ycf10 is required for proper CO2 assimilation under these conditions. At light intensities of 500 and 800 μmol m−2 s−1, the intrinsic water‐use efficiency (iWUE) of ycf10 was significantly higher than that of WT (Figure 6), suggesting that loss of Ycf10 function positively affects the suppression of water transpiration through stomata during CO2 uptake. The presence of functional chloroplasts in guard cells is directly linked to stomatal conductance through the regulated supply of ATP (Wang et al. 2014). Thus, both guard cell chloroplasts and mesophyll chloroplasts likely contribute to guard‐cell ATP pools, which are critical for optimizing CO2 assimilation via stomatal regulation.

Water transpiration through stomata was also assessed by thermal imaging, in which higher leaf temperature indicates reduced stomatal opening. The ycf10‐dldg1 double mutant grown under SD conditions exhibited significantly higher leaf temperature compared with the WT (Figure 7A,B). The ycf10 and dldg1 single mutants also tended to show higher leaf temperature than WT, although the differences were not significant. These results suggest that both Ycf10 and DLDG1 are required for proper regulation of stomatal movements.

To gain further insights into the physiological importance of Ycf10/DLDG1‐dependent stomatal movement, we tested the drought‐stress tolerance of the mutants. WT and mutant plants were grown under CL conditions on agar‐solidified medium with or without a high concentration of mannitol (250 mM) to reduce water potential (Figure 7C). Previous studies have shown that high concentrations of mannitol such as 250 mM inhibit primary root elongation through ABA‐ and ethylene‐dependent signaling pathways, while low concentrations can influence or even promote root growth (Rathor et al. 2020; Yuan et al. 2021). As described above, ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants showed a pale‐green phenotype on mannitol‐free control plates. The growth of both WT and the mutants was drastically inhibited on mannitol‐containing plates due to the reduced water potential and osmotic stress. Under these conditions, the leaves of ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants retained a green coloration, whereas WT leaves showed chlorosis and senescence (Figure 7C). It should be noted that the root length of the three mutants grown on mannitol‐free plates was significantly shorter than those of WT, with dldg1 and ycf10‐dldg1 roots being shorter than those of ycf10 (Figure 7D, left). By contrast, under mannitol‐containing conditions, the root lengths of the mutants were longer than those of the WT (Figure 7D, right).

To investigate the status of photosynthetic product utilization, we next analyzed glucose and sucrose contents in the shoots of plants grown on normal MS medium under CL conditions. The results revealed that sucrose accumulation was significantly lower in all three mutants than in the WT (Figure 7E). In addition, the free glucose level in ycf10 was reduced relative to the WT. These reductions in sucrose and glucose levels in the mutants may explain their shortened root length (Figure 7C), as sugars synthesized in the shoot through photosynthesis act as long‐distance signals regulating root elongation (Tong et al. 2022; Kircher and Schopfer 2023). Interestingly, the shorter primary root lengths of the ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants compared with the WT were still observed on MS medium supplemented with 3% (w/v) sucrose (Figure S5), suggesting that the mutations affect not only carbon availability but also sugar sensing, sugar transport, or downstream signaling processes that cannot be fully compensated for by exogenous sucrose.

Next, we examined drought‐stress tolerance in soil‐grown plants. WT, ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 plants were grown in soil under SD conditions for 46 days, after which watering was withheld for 26 days. The plants were then rewatered and grown for an additional 5 days. All mutant lines exhibited wilting after the 26‐day drought treatment, similar to WT, and none of the plants recovered after 5 days of rewatering (Figure S6). These results suggest that ycf10, dldg1, and/or ycf10‐dldg1 mutants have neither a positive nor a negative effect on drought tolerance in soil, at least under the present experimental conditions.

Impact of DLDG1 Overexpression on Plant Growth

To determine whether DLDG1 functions independently of Ycf10 in Arabidopsis, we next analyzed the effects of DLDG1 overexpression. The 35S‐promoter was placed upstream of the FLAG epitope‐tagged DLDG1 gene, and the construct was introduced into the dldg1 mutant. Western blot analysis using an anti‐FLAG antibody revealed that one of the obtained transgenic lines, OX‐#7, markedly accumulated FLAG‐tagged DLDG1 (Figure S7A). Interestingly, the OX‐#7 line exhibited a pale‐green phenotype even at the reproductive stage, at which dldg1 had recovered its normal green color (Figure S7B), suggesting that overexpression of DLDG1 can negatively affect chloroplast ion homeostasis even in mature leaves. The OX‐#7 line exhibited a sterile phenotype, and no T2 seeds could be obtained.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully isolated ycf10 knockout mutants using plastid‐targeted TALE‐CD constructs (Figure S2). The obtained ycf10 and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants exhibited a stage‐specific day‐length‐dependent pale‐green phenotype under CL but not SD conditions (Figure 2A,B), similar to that observed in dldg1 mutants (Harada et al. 2019). Furthermore, Ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants showed similar photosynthetic parameters [NPQ, Y(II), and ETR(II)] in response to increasing light intensity (Figure 2C), suggesting that Ycf10 cannot complement the mutation of DLDG1, and vice versa. However, during the induction phase of photosynthesis, NPQ induction upon the transition from dark to low light occurred at higher levels in the ycf10‐dldg1 mutant than in WT and the single mutants (Figure 2D). Moreover, under fluctuating light conditions, NPQ induction during high‐light periods became progressively stronger across successive cycles in the double mutant compared with the single mutants (Figure 4). These findings suggest that Ycf10 and DLDG1 act independently, yet redundantly, at least in part. The fact that overexpression of DLDG1 resulted in a stronger pale‐green phenotype than that of dldg1 (Figure S7) further suggests the abundance of DLDG1, and possibly that of Ycf10 as well, is important for their normal functions.

The exact biochemical activities of Ycf10 and DLDG1 remain elusive. Supplementation with high concentrations of NaCl restored the chlorotic phenotypes of the ycf10 and/or dldg1 mutants (Figure 5), similar to the chloroplast EM‐localized K+/H+ antiporter mutant kea1‐kea2 (Kunz et al. 2014), suggesting that they have similar functions. By contrast, DLDG1/FIC1 alone, when heterologous expressed in yeast and Xenopus oocytes, showed Fe2+ import activity (Zhang et al. 2025). Given that the addition of FeSO4 only partially rescued the mutant phenotypes (Figure S4), unlike NaCl supplementation (Figure 5), it is plausible that DLDG1/FIC1 possesses a distinct activity independent of Ycf10. Clearly, further biochemical and structural studies are required to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which Ycf10 and DLDG1 regulate ion transport across the chloroplast EM membranes.

Since our previous study demonstrated that the increase in NPQ caused by the dldg1 mutation is dependent on PsbS (Suzuki and Masuda 2023), the elevated NPQ values observed in ycf10 and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants (Figure 2D) are likely attributable to excessive lumen acidification and the resulting enhanced protonation of PsbS. Previous studies have suggested that Ycf10 and DLDG1 are involved in proton export or import across the chloroplast EM (Sasaki et al. 1993; Rolland et al. 1997; Harada et al. 2019; Trinh et al. 2021). Interestingly, the ΔpH across the thylakoid membrane in the dldg1 mutant is similar to that in the WT (Harada et al. 2019), suggesting that the stromal pH is also likely to be acidified in dldg1 and possibly in the ycf10 and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants. Insufficient stromal alkalization would directly inhibit ATP generation by lowering ΔpH, reduce the activity of pH‐sensitive enzymes required for carbon fixation such as ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), fructose‐1,6‐bisphosphatase (FBPase), and sedoheptulose‐1,7‐bisphosphatase (SBPase), and disrupt the delicate ion balance necessary for optimal photosynthetic performance (Werdan et al. 1975; Elsässer et al. 2020). This hypothesis is consistent with the mutant phenotypes of reduced sucrose content (Figure 7E).

It was reported that the gene expression level of DLDG1, along with other cation transporters (AKT2‐like membrane protein, ZIFL1, CHX24, and CHX14), the H+ pump (AHA2), and the sucrose transporter (ATSUC8) is significantly higher in bundle sheath cells than in mesophyll cells of Arabidopsis (Wigoda et al. 2017). These data imply that DLDG1, and perhaps its chloroplast‐encoded homolog Ycf10, are profoundly involved in ion homeostasis on the whole‐plant level. The diverse phenotypes displayed by the mutants, including altered root length (Figure 7C) and flowering time (Figure 3B), further support this notion. Moreover, ycf10 and ycf10‐dldg1 grown on medium containing high concentrations of NaCl, the pale‐green phenotype disappeared, and the lowered F v /F m values also recovered to the WT level (Figure 5). Notably, even with the recovery of F v /F m under high NaCl conditions, the NPQ levels in the mutants remained the higher values (Figure 5F). The findings imply that disruption of chloroplast ion homeostasis due to the loss of Ycf10 and DLDG1 constitutes one limiting factor for photosynthesis, which can be partially compensated by the addition of NaCl. Interestingly, whereas the stromal pH has been shown to be elevated in the kea1‐kea2 mutant (Aranda Sicilia et al. 2021), stromal acidification has been proposed for the dldg1 mutant as discussed above (Harada et al. 2019). These observations further suggest that the pale‐green phenotype of ycf10, dldg1, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants is unlikely to result directly from changes in stromal pH, but rather from disruption of chloroplast ion homeostasis. As proposed by Aranda Sicilia et al. (2021), NaCl supplementation may alter the competition among ions transported across the chloroplast envelope membrane, thereby partially restoring the ion homeostasis disrupted in the kea1‐kea2, dldg1, ycf10, and ycf10‐dldg1 mutants.

Taken together, these results and data from previous studies strengthen the idea that Ycf10 and DLDG1 affect pH and ion homeostasis in chloroplasts, which are required for proper NPQ induction and for balancing carbon assimilation and water loss, although their exact activities remain elusive. The mechanisms by which Ycf10 and DLDG1 regulate stomatal conductance, thereby affecting intrinsic iWUE, must be clarified in the future, as such knowledge will be valuable for improving plant biomass production and drought tolerance.
