Association of gut microbiome with mobility impairment in the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos
Authors: Brandilyn A. Peters, Qibin Qi, Xiaonan Xue, Jee-Young Moon, Bing Yu, Stefani N. Thomas, Christina Cordero, Martha L. Daviglus, Robert D. Burk, Robert C. Kaplan, Carmen R. Isasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging-related declines in mobility are more common in women than men. The gut microbiome may play a role in physical function, but sex-specific roles are unknown. In adults ≥50 years old (n = 1187 women, 585 men), we examined associations of self-reported mobility impairment with gut microbiome assessed by stool shotgun sequencing, and examined heterogeneity by sex. Gut microbiome α-diversity was lower and overall composition (β-diversity) altered in women with mobility impairment compared to without, but this was not the case in men. Fifteen microbiome species were associated with mobility impairment in both women and men, including enrichment of Streptococcus and Lactobacillus and depletion of Eubacterium species. An additional 84 species were associated with mobility impairment in women only, including enrichment of Gammaproteobacteria species, but none were associated with mobility impairment in men only. Correlations of impaired mobility-related microbiome scores, derived from universal and women-specific microbiome species, with serum metabolites (n = 385) suggested that impaired mobility-related species may be involved in synthesis of imidazole propionate and deoxycholic acid metabolites, while species depleted with mobility impairment may be involved in sex hormone metabolism and guanidinoacetate production, the latter in women only. Gut microbiota may play a role in physical function and sex differences therein.

Introduction

Declines in physical function (e.g., strength and mobility) with aging, often referred to as physical frailty, are associated with substantially reduced quality of life and increased risk of falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality in older adults1,2. Physical frailty and mobility impairment are consistently more common in women than men in the U.S. and globally3–7, contributing to the ‘male-female health-survival paradox’, in which women have longer lifespans than men but spend a greater proportion of life with disability and poor health8. Sex differences in aging-related physical health are likely due to a combination of biological (e.g., sex hormones, inflammation) as well as behavioral and psychosocial factors9, but are not completely understood.

The gut microbiome, the community of microorganisms residing in the human gut, may also contribute to aging-related physical health and sex differences therein. The composition of the gut microbiome differs by sex and could contribute to sexual dimorphism in chronic diseases10. Gut microbiota also play key roles in modulating the immune system, gut permeability, and inflammation, suggesting their influence on the chronic “inflammaging” associated with physical frailty11–14. Gut microbiome structure is known to shift with age15–18, and specific taxa have been related to healthy or unhealthy aging outcomes, including frailty, across multiple populations11,19,20. A recent meta-analysis reported enrichment of Eggerthella lenta, Eubacterium cylindroides, and E. dolichum and depletion of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Alistipes shahii, and Roseburia inulinivorans in frail compared to non-frail older adults21. However, despite clear sex differences in physical frailty, studies to date have not considered sex-specific associations of the gut microbiome with physical health. This lack of research leaves a gap in our understanding of whether the gut microbiome may contribute to sex differences in aging-related physical health, which could be an important consideration in future development of microbiome-targeted therapies19.

Here, we examined associations of the gut microbiome with self-reported mobility impairment in women and men ≥50 years old from the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) (Fig. 1a). U.S. Hispanics/Latinos may have higher prevalence of physical frailty than non-Hispanic Whites22, and also have longer lifespans, implying a greater proportion of life spent with physical impairment23,24. Results

Participant characteristics

Among 1,187 women and 585 men, 34% and 20% reported any mobility impairment, respectively (Table 1). Higher prevalence of mobility impairment in women vs. men persisted across age groups (Fig. 1b). Mobility impairment and gut microbiome diversity

In women, mobility impairment was significantly associated with lower Shannon diversity index in multivariable linear regression models adjusted for demographic and microbiome-related factors (Model 1), behavioral and socioeconomic factors (Model 2), and comorbidity factors (Model 3) (Fig. 1c). In contrast, mobility impairment was not associated with Shannon diversity in men, and the effect differed significantly by sex (P interaction < 0.005 for Models 1–3; Fig. 1c). Similarly, in women mobility impairment was significantly associated with overall gut microbiome composition, measured by the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD), in multivariable permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) models (p < 0.05 for Models 1–3), but this was not observed in men, and the effect differed significantly by sex (P interaction ≤ 0.02 for Models 1–3; Supplementary Table 1).

Mobility impairment and gut microbiome species

Of 1,160 species tested in Analysis of Composition of Microbiomes with bias correction (ANCOM-BC) with Model 1 covariate adjustment, 15 species were associated with mobility impairment in both women and men (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). These “universal” species included 11 enriched with mobility impairment (e.g., species from Lactobacillus and Streptococcus) and 4 depleted (e.g., species from Eubacterium) (Fig. 2a). Most of these species remained significantly associated with mobility impairment after further adjustment for Model 2 (behavioral and socioeconomic) and Model 3 (comorbidity) covariates, with some exceptions (e.g., attenuation of Lactobacillus salivarius in men, and Streptococcus mitis and Subdoligranulum sp. Associations of mobility impairment with universal and women-specific species were highly similar when restricting to participants not reporting any antibiotics use in the last 6 months, or when excluding pre-menopausal women (Supplementary Fig. 3). In a sensitivity analysis with more lenient cut-offs to identify universal and sex-specific impaired mobility-related species, 12 more species were considered “universal” species in women and men (Supplementary Fig. 4a), while 2 less species were considered specific to women (i.e., became universal species; Supplementary Fig. 4b). However, there remained no species associated with mobility impairment that were specific to men. In a sensitivity analysis with 500 times repeated sub-sampling of women to match the sample size of men, the median (IQR) number of species identified as “women-specific” was 27 (11, 56) (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In contrast, the median (IQR) number of species identified as “men-specific” when comparing to each sub-sample of women was 0 (0,0). Among the species identified as “women-specific” across ≥20% of repetitions (51 species), 50 of them overlapped with the 84 species identified as “women-specific” in the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

Impaired mobility-related microbiome scores

Impaired mobility microbiome scores were derived from 15 species associated with mobility impairment in both women and men (universal score) or 84 species associated with mobility impairment in women only (women-specific score), based on results from ANCOM-BC with Model 1 covariate adjustment. Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study of older U.S. Hispanic/Latino adults, we observed universal and sex-specific associations of the gut microbiome with self-reported mobility impairment. Specifically, gut microbiome diversity was lower and overall composition altered in women with mobility impairment compared to those without, but this was not the case in men. Low gut microbiome diversity is generally considered a marker of dysbiosis and associated with many disease states25, suggesting a detrimental association of mobility impairment and the gut microbiome in women, though the causal direction cannot be ascertained. Fifteen gut microbiome species were associated with mobility impairment in both women and men, including enrichment of species from Streptococcus and Lactobacillus and depletion of Eubacterium sp. with mobility impairment. Streptococcus species are typically oral commensals, but some (e.g., S. parasanguinis) colonize in the gut with potential pathogenicity26,27, and have been previously associated with unhealthy aging11,27; while Lactobacillus species (e.g., L. gasseri, L. fermentum) are used in probiotics and generally considered beneficial28, making this an unexpected result. Eubacterium species generally produce short-chain fatty acids and suppress inflammation29, in line with a potential protective role in physical function, though the specific roles of the Eubacterium spp. identified here (Eubacterium sp. CAG:115 and CAG:786) in human health are not yet known. An additional 84 species were associated with mobility impairment in women only, including enrichment of many potential pathogens from Gammaproteobacteria (e.g., E. coli, Shigella flexneri). Correlations of impaired mobility-related gut microbiota with serum metabolites such as imidazole propionate, secondary bile acids, sex steroid hormone metabolites, and guanidinoacetate may indicate potential mechanisms for their role in physical frailty.

A number of prior studies have examined differences in the gut microbiome for physically frail and non-frail individuals, defined in whole or part by objective measures of strength and mobility such as gait speed and handgrip strength (e.g., Fried frailty phenotype1, short physical performance battery)19,21. However, most of these studies were small (n < 100), many were conducted in hospitalized or nursing home populations, which may not be generalizable to community-dwelling adults, and sex-specific associations were not explored. Consistent with this prior literature, we observed enrichment of Lactobacillus species with mobility impairment, and inconsistently, we observed depletion of Eubacterium species with mobility impairment (some Eubacterium species were enriched with frailty in prior studies21). A recent large study (n = 740) of older men from the MrOS cohort found that faster walking speed was associated with higher gut microbiome diversity and 86 microbial genera, including Alistipes, Paraprevotella, and Fusicatenibacter (associated with faster walking speed) and Escherichia/Shigella (associated with slower walking speed)30. Inconsistent with the MrOS cohort, in our study, mobility impairment was not associated with gut microbiome diversity nor many species in men; however, the men in MrOS (mean age 84) were older than men in our study (mean age 60). Given the relative paucity of studies examining the gut microbiome and mobility impairment, particularly with stratification by sex, more research is needed in this area to identify gut microbiome species robustly related to mobility in women and men.

Since mobility impairment may be influenced by many factors, including socioeconomic position, physical activity, diet, cardiometabolic health, and lung function, we explored how adjusting for these factors in multivariable models influenced the associations of gut microbiota with mobility impairment. Most associations of gut microbiome diversity and species with mobility impairment, including impaired mobility microbiome scores representing the composite association of mobility impairment with the gut microbiome, persisted after adjustment for these factors, suggesting an independent association of the gut microbiome with mobility impairment. Higher BMI and lower physical activity are well-known risk factors for mobility impairment31,32, while mobility impairment may also lead to increased BMI and limited physical activity, suggesting these factors may serve as confounders or mediators in the association of mobility impairment with the gut microbiome. Higher BMI and lower physical activity were significantly correlated with the universal and women-specific impaired mobility microbiome scores among women but not men, which may indicate that gut microbiome-mobility impairment associations in women may be partly explained by BMI and physical activity, though the directionality remains unclear. In both women and men, the universal impaired mobility microbiome score was correlated with worse lung function, which is a consistent predictor of frailty33–35, and poorer diet quality, underscoring the key role that diet plays in shaping the gut microbiome and health36. Women and men with mobility impairment had worse cardiometabolic health and lung function than those without, and women with mobility impairment also had greater antibiotic use than those without, suggesting greater prevalence of infection or greater healthcare utilization among women. Though we accounted for behavioral and health-related factors through careful covariate adjustment and sensitivity analysis, we cannot rule out residual confounding by these and other factors in the associations of the gut microbiome with mobility impairment.

The globally observed phenomenon of greater mobility impairment in aging women compared to men3–7 suggests that underlying biological sex-related factors may play a role in the etiology of mobility impairment3,9. Additionally, the menopausal transition, accompanied by sharp declines in ovarian sex hormones, may be a risk factor for physical frailty specific to women37–39. Interestingly, we observed several sex steroid hormone metabolites (i.e., metabolites of progesterone and androgens) that were inversely correlated with the universally impaired mobility microbiome score. The gut microbiome exhibits bi-directional relationships with sex hormones, and can modify sex hormones and influence their retention through conjugation reactions40–42. We have previously reported associations of menopause and sex hormones with the gut microbiome in HCHS/SOL43 and others44. Our results suggest that gut microbiome modification of sex hormones may be a protective mechanism against mobility impairment in older adults, though additional research is needed with targeted measurement of sex hormones (e.g., estradiol, testosterone).

The impaired mobility-related gut microbiome was also correlated with higher levels of serum imidazole propionate and secondary bile acid metabolites (i.e., deoxycholic acid metabolites). These are well-known microbial metabolites that are widely associated with risk of various health outcomes: imidazole propionate with diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease in HCHS/SOL and other cohorts45–49, and secondary bile acids with fatty liver disease, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and some cancers50,51. Our findings indicate that gut microbial production of these metabolites may also play a role in physical health.

The association of decreased gut microbiome diversity and 84 additional species with mobility impairment in women, beyond the associations observed in both women and men, may suggest that the gut microbiome contributes in a unique way to mobility impairment in women. The women-specific impaired mobility-related microbiome score was inversely correlated with serum guanidinoacetate, the precursor to endogenous synthesis of creatine. Creatine is a required source of energy for skeletal muscle in humans, which can be obtained from dietary sources (e.g., meat, fish) or endogenous synthesis from guanidinoacetate52. In line with our results, guanidinoacetate has been previously identified as a key metabolite associated with a lower risk of physical frailty53, and supplementation with guanidinoacetate and creatine improves functional performance in the elderly54. The gut microbiome may contribute to guanidinoacetate synthesis55, suggesting a plausible mechanism by which certain gut microbiota may protect against mobility impairment. Taken together, our results suggest that improved mobility in older women may be related to microbial synthesis of guanidinoacetate.

This study was limited by several factors. First, due to the observational and cross-sectional design, we are not able to identify gut microbiota that precede and may be causal factors in mobility impairment (i.e., potential for reverse causation). Indeed, mobility impairment is correlated with many other health factors, and though we implemented a robust adjustment strategy, residual confounding may remain, and some results could reflect broader health more generally rather than specificity to mobility impairment. Second, mobility impairment was defined by self-report, raising the possibility of misclassification and reporting bias (including sex-specific reporting bias); whereas objective measures would be more reliable indicators of impairment. Third, the sample size for the men in this study was ~50% smaller than that of the women, which may have limited power to observe associations in men; indeed, sensitivity analysis with more lenient cut-offs suggested that more species may be universally associated with mobility impairment in both women and men, but power may have been limited in men to detect them. Further sensitivity analysis involving repeated sub-sampling of women to match sample size in men indicated that a unique gut microbiome signature associated with mobility impairment in women only persisted even under equivalent power conditions for both women and men. Fourth, several underlying conditions that contribute to mobility impairment and may be more prevalent in older women than men, such as arthritis and osteoporosis9, were not measured in this study and could not be accounted for in the analysis. Finally, results in the Hispanic/Latino study population may not be generalizable to other ethnic groups.

In conclusion, our results suggest that the gut microbiome related to mobility impairment in women and men is characterized by higher abundance of Streptococcus and Lactobacillus species and lower abundance of Eubacterium species, while mobility impairment in women specifically is additionally associated with lower gut microbiome diversity and enrichment of Gammaproteobacteria species. Integration with serum metabolomics data suggested that impaired mobility-related species may be involved in synthesis of imidazole propionate and secondary bile acids, while species depleted with mobility impairment may be involved in sex steroid hormone metabolism and production of guanidinoacetate, the latter observed in women only. Large prospective studies of the gut microbiome, microbial metabolites, and objectively measured physical function are warranted to validate these results and to support a role of the gut microbiome in mobility impairment. Given the modifiable nature of the gut microbiome, there is promising therapeutic potential in altering the gut microbiome to promote healthy aging.

Methods

Study cohort

The Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) is a prospective, population-based cohort study of 16,415 Hispanic/Latino adults (ages 18-74 years at the time of recruitment during visit 1 [2008–2011]) who were selected using a multi-stage probability sampling design from randomly sampled census block areas within four U.S. communities (Chicago, IL; Miami, FL; Bronx, NY; San Diego, CA)56,57. The HCHS/SOL Gut Origins of Latino Diabetes ancillary study58 was conducted to examine the role of gut microbiome composition on diabetes and other outcomes, enrolling ~3000 participants from the HCHS/SOL approximately concurrent with the second in-person HCHS/SOL visit cycle (visit 2, 2014–2017). For this analysis, we included participants ≥50 years old at visit 2, without prevalent cancer or cardiovascular disease, not on kidney dialysis, and with ≥100 K sequence reads in their microbiome sample and available data on self-reported mobility (total n = 1772). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the field centers (Albert Einstein School of Medicine, University of Miami, San Diego State University, University of Illinois at Chicago) and the coordinating center (University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill) of the HCHS/SOL. Written informed consent was provided by all study participants.

Definition of self-reported mobility impairment

At HCHS/SOL visit 2, participants were asked in a yes or no format whether they had difficulty walking, difficulty walking half a mile, or difficulty climbing 10 steps. Mobility impairment was defined as answering “yes” to any of these questions, while those answering “no” to all the questions were considered as not having mobility impairment.

Covariate data

Participant characteristics were included for statistical adjustment in our analysis, based on known or suspected relationships with mobility impairment and/or the gut microbiome. These variables were age (continuous), sex (male, female), field center (Chicago, Miami, Bronx, San Diego), Hispanic/Latino background (Dominican, Central American, South American, Cuban, Mexican, Puerto Rican, more than one heritage/other/missing), U.S. nativity (born in 50 U.S. states/DC or a U.S. territory, foreign born), antibiotic use in last 6 months (yes, no), probiotics use (yes, no), Bristol stool type (8 categories), income (<$30,000, ≥30,000, missing), educational attainment (less than high school, high school or equivalent, greater than high school, missing), cigarette smoking (never, former, current), alcohol use (non-drinker, low-level use, high-level use), the Alternative Healthy Eating Index 2010 (AHEI2010; continuous), total physical activity based on the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ; continuous), BMI (continuous), systolic blood pressure (continuous), diastolic blood pressure (continuous), triglycerides (continuous), HDL cholesterol (continuous), fasting glucose (continuous), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; continuous), anti-hypertensive medication (yes, no), anti-diabetic medication (yes, no), lipid-lowering medication (yes, no), asthma (yes, no), joint pain (yes, no), and a composite score of lung function (continuous). For the lung function score, z-score standardized values of forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and peak expiratory flow (PEF) were averaged, based on the method of Buchman et al. 59. All covariates were based on visit 2 data, except AHEI2010, lung function, and joint pain which were based on visit 1 data (since these data were only collected at visit 1). Missing covariate data were imputed at the median and mode for continuous and categorical variables, respectively, with the exception of categorical variables with >1% missing, for which a missing category was created. Menopause status (defined previously in this cohort43) was not included in main analyses due to high levels of missingness, but was incorporated in sensitivity analyses.

Microbiome measurement

Stool samples were collected by participants at home using stool collection kits, as described previously58. Shotgun sequencing was conducted in the Knight laboratory at the University of California San Diego60. Briefly, DNA is extracted from fecal samples following the Earth Microbiome Project protocol61. Input DNA is quantified in a 384-well plate using a PicoGreen fluorescence assay (ThermoFisher, Inc), and normalized to 1 ng using an Echo 550 acoustic liquid-handling robot (Labcyte, Inc). Enzyme mixes for fragmentation, end repair and A-tailing, ligation, and PCR are added using a Mosquito HV micropipetting robot (TTP Labtech). Fragmentation is performed at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by end-repair and A-tailing at 65 °C for 30 min. Sequencing adapters and barcode indices are added in two steps, following the iTru adapter protocol62. Universal “stub” adapter molecules and ligase mix are first added to the end-repaired DNA using the Mosquito HV robot and ligation performed at 20 °C for 1 h. Unligated adapters and adapter dimers are removed using AMPure XP magnetic beads and a BlueCat purification robot (BlueCat Bio). Next, individual i7 and i5 are added to the adapter-ligated samples using the Echo 550 robot. Then, eluted bead-washed ligated samples are added to PCR master mix and PCR-amplified for 15 cycles. The amplified and indexed libraries are purified again using magnetic beads and the BlueCat robot, resuspended in water, and transferred to a 384-well plate using the Mosquito HTS liquid-handling robot for library quantitation, sequencing, and storage. Samples are then normalized based on a PicoGreen fluorescence assay for sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq.

Microbiome bioinformatics processing

FASTQ sequence reads were processed using the standard shotgun sequencing pipeline in Qiita. Briefly, per sample sequence adapters were removed via fastp, and sequence reads mapping to the human genome were filtered via minimap2. The sequence reads were then aligned against the WolR1 reference database of bacterial and archaeal genomes using Woltka with the Bowtie2 aligner63, to generate an operational genomic unit (OGU) table. Woltka assigns reads to taxa based on their alignments to reference sequences, resolving ambiguity by assigning reads to the lowest common ancestor (LCA) of all matching taxa or proportionally distributing counts when multiple assignments are retained64. The sequence alignments were classified at the species taxonomic rank. Samples with <100,000 sequence reads after bioinformatics processing were excluded. The samples included in this analysis had 807,823 average reads per sample (median [IQR]: 731,460 [487,856, 1,020,377]). Indices of α-diversity (Shannon diversity index) and β-diversity (Jensen-Shannon Divergence) were calculated from the OGU table using “vegan” and “phyloseq” packages in R65–67.

Metabolomics measurement

A subset of 825 participants was selected for metabolomics profiling of visit 2 fasting serum samples, based on availability of gut microbiome samples collected within 30 days of blood sampling at visit 2. Quantification of serum metabolites was achieved using the discoveryHD4 platform at Metabolon Inc., an untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomic quantification protocol, as previously described68. After further restricting by our inclusion criteria, 385 participants remained for cross-sectional metabolomics analysis at visit 2. We imputed values below detection as half the minimum value per metabolite.

Statistical analysis

An overview of the analytical process is shown in Fig. 1a. For the association of mobility impairment with the gut microbiome, we considered nested models to serially adjust for potential confounders or mediators, based on suspected factors related to mobility impairment, and to the gut microbiome in this cohort58. Model 1 (demographic and microbiome-related factor model) adjusted for age, sex (only in model with both sexes), field center, Hispanic/Latino background, U.S. nativity, antibiotics use, Bristol stool type, and probiotics use. Model 2 (socioeconomic and behavioral model) adjusted for Model 1 covariates plus income, educational attainment, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, AHEI2010, and total physical activity. Model 3 (comorbidity model) adjusted for Model 2 covariates plus BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, fasting glucose, eGFR, hypertension medication, diabetes medication, lipid-lowering medication, joint pain, asthma, and lung function score. We used Model 1 (the minimally adjusted model) as the primary discovery model to identify mobility impairment-related gut microbiome signatures while controlling for key a priori confounders and avoiding overadjustment, which can reduce power or mask true associations when additional variables may be mediators or highly correlated covariates. We then applied more extensively adjusted models (Models 2 and 3) to test whether the identified associations were robust to further adjustment. Models were stratified by sex unless otherwise indicated. Interactions between mobility impairment and sex on microbiome features were tested with cross-product terms in the model.

Multivariable linear regression was used to examine the association of mobility impairment (predictor) with the Shannon diversity index (outcome), adjusting for covariates in Models 1, 2, and 3. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to assess the association of mobility impairment with overall microbiome composition, as measured by the Jensen-Shannon Divergence, adjusting for covariates in Models 1, 2, and 3. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant in diversity analyses.

Analysis of Composition of Microbiomes with bias correction (ANCOM-BC)69 was used to examine associations of mobility impairment with gut microbiome species abundance, adjusting for covariates in Models 1, 2, and 3. ANCOM-BC identifies differentially abundant taxa by modeling log-transformed abundances while correcting for compositional bias and sampling fractions, taking raw count data as input. Species present in at least 20% of women and men were included, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 5%. “Universal” species (i.e., associated with mobility impairment in both women and men) were defined as those with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 in women and men combined and passed the default pseudo-count sensitivity analysis in ANCOM-BC in Model 1, and nominal P < 0.05 in both women and men separately with same direction. “Sex-specific” species (i.e., associated with mobility impairment in only women or men) were defined as those with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 in one sex and passed the pseudo-count sensitivity analysis in ANCOM-BC in Model 1, and with nominal P ≥ 0.05 in the other sex (any direction) or nominal P < 0.05 in the other sex but opposite direction. Associations of mobility impairment with universal and sex-specific species were examined upon further adjustment for Model 2 and 3 covariates using ANCOM-BC.

Since sample size was lower in men than women which could influence power to observe associations in men, we conducted two sensitivity analyses: (1) using a more lenient nominal P cut-off of 0.10 in the definitions of “universal” vs. “sex-specific” species as described above; and (2) we repeatedly (500 times) conducted random sub-sampling of the women (within strata of mobility impairment) in order to match the sample size of men (n = 585), and identified species associated with mobility impairment in each sub-sampled dataset using ANCOM-BC with Model 1 covariate adjustment. For the latter analysis, we then examined the number of species identified as “women-specific” in each sub-sampled dataset according to the same criteria as the main analysis. As further sensitivity analyses, we examined ANCOM-BC models excluding participants who reported antibiotic use in the last 6 months, or were pre-menopausal (among women only).

Universal and sex-specific impaired mobility-related microbiome scores were constructed by adding (or subtracting) centered log ratio (CLR)-transformed standardized abundance for species positively (or inversely) associated with mobility impairment in Model 1, followed by standardization of the score.

Spearman correlations were used to examine associations of the universal and sex-specific impaired mobility microbiome scores with cardiometabolic risk factors (AHEI2010, GPAQ total physical activity, BMI, eGFR, triglycerides, HDL, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, lung function score) in women and men separately. Correlations with blood lipids (triglycerides, HDL), blood pressure, and fasting glucose were determined after excluding those taking lipid-lowering, anti-hypertensive, or anti-diabetic medication, respectively (n = 1,073 women and n = 525 men not taking lipid-lowering medication; n = 698 women and n = 359 men not taking anti-hypertensive medication; and n = 911 women and n = 446 men not taking anti-diabetic medication). Correlations were also examined for accelerometer-derived moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA, average min/day) and sedentary time70,71, for a subset with available data and at least 3 days of ≥10 h/day accelerometer wear time at visit 2 (women n = 782, men n = 345). Since sedentary time is highly correlated with wear time, sedentary time was standardized to 16 h of wear time following the method in Qi et al.71.

For correlations of impaired mobility microbiome scores with serum metabolites, metabolite concentrations were inverse-normal transformed. Only named metabolites with <20% missing were considered, totaling 773 metabolites. Correlation of the universal impaired mobility microbiome score with serum metabolites was examined in women and men combined, via sex-adjusted partial Spearman correlations. Spearman correlations of sex-specific impaired mobility microbiome scores with serum metabolites were examined separately by sex. Metabolites with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 and Spearman r ≥ 0.20 were considered significant. For metabolites significantly associated with impaired mobility microbiome scores, we also examined: (a) their Spearman correlations with individual impaired mobility-related gut microbiome species, and (b) their association with mobility impairment using multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, Hispanic/Latino background, field center, and U.S. nativity.
