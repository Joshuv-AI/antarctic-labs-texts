Machine-learning-model based tool for screening bone metastases from lung cancer patients in primary care practice
Authors: Yang Zhou, Tianpan Cai, Chunyu Lan, Shuang Feng, Zhili Liu, Jiaming Liu, Lei Cao, Jiangbo Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to use the machine-learning methods to predict bone metastasis (BM) in patients with lung cancer.MethodsThis study included 8,612 patients with lung cancer, from whom we collected baseline characteristics and hematological data. BM was diagnosed based on imaging results. We normalized metric data using Z-scores and divided the patients into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. After under-sampling of the training set to relieve the interlocking imbalance, the key variables were then selected using the LASSO method. Nine distinct machine-learning models were developed on the training set, and their performance was evaluated on the validation set. Finally, we created an online tool to support real-time analysis of BM in lung cancer patients.ResultsAmong 8,612 lung cancer patients, 947 (11%) were diagnosed with BM. Nine key indicators associated with BM were identified, including ALP, LYM%, HCT, FBG, TT, TBIL, smoking status, DBIL, and D-dimer. Among the evaluated models, the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model showed consistently strong performance, achieving AUCs of 0.8423 and 0.7871; accuracies of 0.7655 and 0.7309; balanced accuracies of 0.76645 and 0.72385; specificities of 0.7652 and 0.7329; and sensitivities of 0.7677 and 0.7148 in the training and validation sets, respectively, along with higher Youden index and F1 score. Consequently, we identified XGBOOST as the optimal predictive model and developed an online platform based on this model to facilitate prediction of BM in lung cancer patients.ConclusionA machine learning model was developed to diagnose BM in lung cancer based on hematological indicators, along with a real-time website. This tool cloud guide further diagnostic evaluations and intervention for high-risk patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06979-x.Keywords: Lung cancer, Bone metastasis, Serum indicators, Machine-learning models

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. Pathologically, lung cancer is primarily classified into two types: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of cases [1]. The five-year survival rate of patients with lung cancer is less than 26.4% due to the distant metastasis [2]. And patients with bone metastases (BM) suffered from pain, pathological fractures, nerve compression syndromes and hypercalcemia, further compromising patient survival [3]. Early identification and stratification of the high-risk patients with BM is necessary to improve outcomes.

PET-CT and radionuclide bone scanning are important diagnostic methods for patients suspected of BM. However, the most recent National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend that routine PET-CT or ECT scans are unnecessary for early-stage lung cancer patients during post-treatment follow-up [4]. Meanwhile, limited availability of medical equipment restricts BM diagnosis in some community hospitals. These limitations underscore the need for simpler, more accessible indicators to stratify patients and effectively assess their BM risk. BM is a dynamic, multi-systemic process accompanied by alterations in various blood-related markers [5]. Some peripheral blood indicators, such as CX3CL1, CCL28 and bone metabolism-related markers, might serve as biomarkers for diagnosing of BM of lung cancer [6, 7]. Therefore, hematological indicators reflecting systemic conditions might provide new insights into the prediction of BM in lung cancer patients.

In this study, 52 physical examination characteristics, including baseline and hematology test data, were collected from 8,612 patients for further analysis. Based on the machine-learning models, a serum molecular model for diagnosing BM was constructed, providing an experimental basis for the early diagnosis and progression monitoring of BM in lung cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (IITS2024738). Since this study was a retrospective study and no intervention was performed on the patients, the written informed consent was waived. The research process was shown in Fig. 1. A total of 10,144 patients, pathologically confirmed with lung cancer in the Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University form January 1, 2020 to August 31, 2024, were involved in this study. Patients were excluded according to the following exclusion criteria: (1) with other malignant tumors; (2) with severe heart, lung, liver, kidney or blood system diseases; (3) received surgery or chemotherapy in the past three months; (4) the pregnant and breastfeeding women; (5) lacked of PET-CT, MRI, CT, or X-ray for evaluating bone metastases; (6) with incomplete medical records.

The flow chart of the study

Data collection

All datasets contained 52 physical examination characteristics, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLO), albumin/globulin (A/G), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (AST), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), D-dimer, direct bilirubin (DBIL), eosinophil (EOS), percentage of eosinophils (EOS%), basophils (BAS), percentage of basophils (BAS%), fibrinogen beta chain (FGB), hematocrit (HCT), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), hemoglobin (HGB), international normalized ratio (INR), lymphocyte (LYM), percentage of lymphocyte (LYM%), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), monocyte (MONO), mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophil (NEU), percentage of neutrophil (NUE%), serum prealbumin (PA), procalcitonin (PCT), platelet distribution width (PDW), platelet count (PLT), prothrombin time (PT), Prothrombin Time Ratio (PTO), red blood cell (RBC), total bilirubin (TBIL), triglyceride (TG), total protein (TP), thrombin time (TT), uric acid (UA), UREA, white blood cell (WBC), total serum cholesterol (TCHO), smoke, drink, hypertension and diabetes. Patients with missing data had been excluded according to the workflow. All variables were the examination indicators when the patients were first admitted to the hospital, and were collected from the electronic medical record system of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.4.1) and descriptive statistics were calculated using the “moonBook” R package. The distribution normality of continuous variables was assessed through the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables following a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test. For non-normally distributed data, values were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), with comparisons between groups performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data was summarized as frequency and percentage, and analyzed with either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the expected counts.

Model construction and evaluation

The data was normalized by the Z-score method and patients were divided into the training and validation sets in a ratio of 7:3 using the “createDataPartition” function by “caret” package. Due to the severe class-imbalance of all datasets, under-sampling of the negative samples was performed on the training set. Furthermore, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was utilized on the training dataset to identify key variables, with those having a variance inflation factor (VIF) greater than 5 excluded, for constructing machine learning models [8]. To achieve state-of-the-art diagnostic performance, machine-learning models, including the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBOOST) algorithm, Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), K-Nearest Neighbor machine (KNN), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), GaussianNB models (GBM), Bayesian Model (Bayse) were constructed using “caret” package. And Deep Neural Networks (DNN) model was developed using the “h2o” package. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed on the training dataset for hyperparameters selection. The performances of the machine-learning models were evaluated by multiple well-established metrics, such as, Specificity, Sensitivity, Accuracy, Youden Index, F1 Score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). After selecting the best performing model, the predictive ability and clinical utility of the model were evaluated using calibration curves and clinical decision curve analysis (DCA) using “rmda” package. And the relationship and importance between each factor was showed using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method by using “SHAPforxgboost” package. Ultimately, an online computing platform based on the optimal model was developed for the diagnosis of bone metastasis in lung cancer patients.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 8,612 patients included in this study, 947 patients (10.996%) had bone metastasis (Table 1). We then conducted an initial analysis on the baseline data of patients between BM and NBM groups (Table 1). In the BM group, the proportion of male patients was higher (P < 0.001), the average age was older (P < 0.001), and the BMI was lower (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Meanwhile, the levels of serum ALP, D-dimer, FGB, GGT, GLO, MONO, NEU, NEU% and PLT were significantly higher in the BM group (P < 0.001). Conversely, the concentrations of serum A/G, ALB, BAS%, HCT, HDLC, HGB, LYM, LYM%, MCV, MCHC, MCH, PA, RBC, TBIL, TG, TT, and TCHO were lower in the BM group compared with the NBM groups (P < 0.05). These results indicated that serum indicators were closely related to the development of BM in lung cancer patients.

Key variables

To eliminate redundant or irrelevant variables and prevent model overfitting, we applied the LASSO method to identify and select the most appropriate variables. In the LASSO algorithm, ten-fold cross-validation were performed and the lambda.1se was selected as the suitable cut-off lambda value (Fig. 2A–B and Sup Table 1). A total of 9 factors with nonzero coefficients, including ALP, LYM%, HCT, FBG, TT, TBIL, Smoke, DBIL, D-dimer were identified as the key variables for further analysis (Fig. 2C).

Model performance

Then, parameters were used to constructed 9 machine-learning models for predicting BM of lung cancer. AUC is one the most important indicators for evaluating model performance [9].The ROC curves of 9 models were visualized in the Fig. 3A and B. Compared with other models, the XGBOOST model achieved higher AUC in both the training set (0.8423) and the validation set (0.7871), highlighting the exceptional performance of the XGBOOST model (Fig. 3A–B). The comparison results of different models were showed in the Tables 2 and 3. We used 10-fold cross validation to evaluate the stability of the model. The standard deviations (SD) of AUC of XGBOOST model were 0.0076 and 0.0134 in the training and validation sets respectively, that significantly smaller than in other models. And the XGBOOST model achieved an accuracy of 0.7655 (training set) and 0.7309 (validation set), sensitivity of 0.7677 and 0.7148, specificity of 0.7652 and 0.7329, and F1 score of 0.4186 and 0.3688, demonstrating its strong performance (Tables 2 and 3). In addition, the calibration curve of XGBOOST model closely aligned with the ideal diagonal line (Fig. 4A), and the decision curve demonstrated that the model provided a substantial net benefit (Fig. 4B). In conclusion, XGBOOST model was regarded as the best model for evaluating BM of lung cancer. Instead of under-sampling of the negative samples, we employed inverse frequency weighting with the full features for model construction, which is generally advised in low-N settings; no significant difference in performance was observed (Sup Tables 2 and 3).

Model interpretation

Subsequently, we used the SHAP package to analyze the XGBOOST model. The impact of each variable on the predictive power of the model and the importance rank of each variable were showed in the Fig. 5A and B. We calculated the optimal cutoff value for the model using the Youden index, which was determined to be 12.72%. This suggested that if the risk value calculated by XGBOOST model exceeded 12.72%, the patient was at high risk of BM and should undergo imaging examinations.

Construction of web tool and case demonstrations

To enhance the model’s usability, we developed a web application platform using the Shiny package. Two case studies were conducted to demonstrate the use of the web tool. Case 1, with ALP at 68 U/L, D-dimer at 0.95 mg/L, DBIL at 3.6 µmol/L, FGB at 2.44 mmol/L, HCT at 43%, LYM% at 22.4%, TBIL at 13.5 µmol/L, TT at 16 s, and no history of smoking, was entered into the tool. The model predicted a risk score of 0.1107 (11.07%), which was below the optimal cutoff value, indicating a low-risk status for BM (Fig. 6A). Case 2, with ALP at 331.468 U/L, D-dimer at 2.18 mg/L, DBIL at 7.6 µmol/L, FGB at 4.12 mmol/L, HCT at 32.9%, LYM% at 10.3%, TBIL at 16.2 µmol/L, TT at 13.2 s, and a history of smoking, was also analyzed. The predicted risk score was 0.6349, suggesting a high-risk status for BM (Fig. 6B). Notably, the predicted risk scores for both cases aligned closely with the patients’ actual bone metastasis statuses, demonstrating the tool’s accuracy and reliability. The web tool was available at https://bmprediction.shinyapps.io/shiny/.

Disscussion

In this study, we found that the BM rate of lung cancer was approximately 11%, which was lower than the 20 and 50% reported in other studies [10, 11]. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in sample size and the number of institutions involved in the respective studies. Nonetheless, the prevention and treatment of BM in lung cancer patients remains critical clinical priorities. Previous studies found that BM of lung cancer presented insidiously and lacked distinct clinical features, leading to significant challenges for early diagnosis [12]. Imaging for BM in lung cancer patients is costly and often limited to symptomatic cases, leading to missed diagnoses [13]. And the lack of advanced imaging equipment, such as CT or PET-CT, in some community hospitals further hampers early detection. Thus, a simpler, cost-effective, and accessible screening strategy is essential for the preliminary evaluation of BM in lung cancer.

Currently, many studies have focused on the risk predictors of BM in lung cancer patients. Su et al. analyzed the clinical and imaging features of 501 patients and identified five independent predictors of BM, such as cytokeratin 19 fragment (CYFRA21-1), disease stage, Ki-67 expression level, tumor margin, and lobulation [14]. In our study, we tried to screen the potential serum factors associated with the development of BM in patients. By LASSO analysis, we finally identified ALP, LYM%, HCT, FBG, TT, TBIL, Smoke, DBIL and D-dimer as the critical factors. ALP is a phosphomonoesterase present in high concentrations in both bone and liver [15]. During bone destruction, ALP levels increase significantly [15]. We found that serum ALP level had the highest importance score in the model for diagnosing BM in lung cancer, and it was identified as an independent risk factor for BM, consistent with findings from other studies [16–18]. These results highlight the significant role of ALP in monitoring BM in lung cancer.

Lymphocytes had been reported to be the dynamic marker reflecting the inflammatory and immune status in the serum and could be used to assess the dynamics of host-tumor interactions [19, 20]. Recent studies have demonstrated the significant prognostic value of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in predicting BM and prognosis in lung cancer [20–23]. A higher NLR is associated with an increased risk rate of distant metastasis and poorer prognosis [22]. This study confirmed that the LYM% independently predicted BM, offering a valuable alternative to the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). In addition, we found that patients with BM had lower hematocrit (HCT) and higher TT and D-dimer levels, which might be attributed to the phagocytosis of red-blood cells by malignant tumors and the tumor-induced abnormalities in coagulation function [24–27].

Machine-learning algorithms are particularly effective at handling large datasets, identifying intricate patterns and associations that conventional statistical techniques may overlook. By automatically optimizing model parameters and weights, these algorithms enhance predictive accuracy, adapt to varying data distributions, and account for the interactions between features [28]. This study used machine-learning methods to build models for predicting the risk of BM in lung cancer patients, and a total of 9 variables were included into the models. Overall, the XGBOOST model demonstrated the best predictive performance, achieving AUCs of 0.8423 and 0.7871 in the training and test datasets, respectively. The calibration curve indicated that the model’s predictions were in good agreement with the actual observed outcomes. When the calculated risk value exceeded the optimal cutoff of 12.72%, the patient was at higher risk for bone metastasis. Additionally, we developed a web-based tool for clinicians to facilitate its use in clinical practice. Users should ensure input values were within reasonable ranges, and future development or deployment of the web tool should follow appropriate patient data protection and regulatory guidelines.

In this study, we developed an excellent BM diagnostic model for lung cancer patients. However, there are several limitations. First, the study is retrospective and lacks prospective validation. Second, all data were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, making it a single-center study without multicenter data. Therefore, re-standardization might be necessary when applying this model in other medical institutions.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed nine predictive models for BM in lung cancer patients using hematological indicators and assessed their performance with various statistical metrics. Among these, the XGBOOST model demonstrated the best performance and was effective in identifying BM patients. We believed that this model could play a crucial role in early screening, risk stratification, and preventive interventions, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes.
