The potential of lymphocyte–C-reactive protein ratio upon admission to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active ulcerative colitis
Authors: Chen Liu, Yan Zhang, Ya-Jun Wang, Yu-Jia Tang, Yue Yang, Chun-Hui Gu, Yi-Lu Lin, Yao Li, Jia-Le Deng, Jia-Xi Xu, Jia-Ning Zhang, Na Zhan, Bo-Wen Liu, Kai Kang, Yang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the potential of lymphocyte-to-C-reactive protein ratio (LCR) upon admission to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active ulcerative colitis (UC).MethodsThis single-center cohort study retrospectively recruited adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC who received mesalazine monotherapy as first-line treatment and underwent the first lower digestive endoscopy within 7 days after admission. Multiple demographic characteristics and clinical indicators were extracted, collated, and calculated from the medical records. Based on their therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine, the enrolled patients were classified into the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group, respectively. All the aforementioned data were compared between different groups.ResultsA total of 244 adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC were recruited and subsequently stratified into the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group (n = 165) and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group (n = 79). The overall therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC was 67.62%. LCR upon admission exhibited a significant positive correlation with disease duration and remarkable negative correlations with disease severity as well as the first ulcerative colitis endoscopic index of severity (UCEIS) and Mayo endoscopic score (MES) within 7 days after admission. Disease duration, neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), and LCR upon admission were determined as protective factors for therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine, whereas disease severity, the first UCEIS within 7 days after admission, and length of hospital stay were identified as risk factors. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUCs) of disease duration, disease severity, NEUT%, LCR, UCEIS, length of hospital stay, and combination of disease duration, disease severity, NEUT%, LCR, UCEIS, and length of hospital stay were 0.614, 0.818, 0.637, 0.907, 0.800, 0.738, and 0.938, respectively.ConclusionLCR upon admission holds significant potential to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, intermittent inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that primarily involves the mucosa and submucosa of the colon and rectum to varying degrees throughout life, and can further be associated with various frequent comorbidities, long-term disability, and serious decline in the quality of life [1, 2]. With the in-depth understanding and more attention to UC, its incidence and prevalence have been on the rise in recent years, with millions of confirmed cases reported globally [3, 4]. Numerous investigations have revealed that genetic predisposition, exposure to environmental toxins, dysregulated immune responses, inflammatory cascade reaction following intestinal epithelial barrier disruption, gut microbial dysbiosis, and luminal factors are strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of UC [5–11]. In daily clinical practice, adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC typically receive mesalazine monotherapy (pH- or time-dependent mesalazine sustained-release granules or tablets or their combination with mesalazine enema or suppository) as first-line treatment, with considerations for escalation to corticosteroids, biologics (e.g., anti-cytokines and anti-integrins), azathioprine, small molecules, or even proctocolectomy in cases of therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine, severe comorbidities, or rapid progression [12, 13]. Early and accurate identification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine will be instrumental in the implementation of precision therapy and personalized medicine, thereby avoiding unnecessary sufferings from patients and alleviating the economic burden on the healthcare system. However, there is currently a dearth of simple and high-reliable clinical objective parameters, combinations, or existing scoring systems to provide a satisfactory solution on this pressing practical issue [14].

Lymphocyte-C-reactive protein ratio (LCR), defined as the ratio of lymphocyte count (LYMPH) to C-reactive protein (CRP), indicates the crosstalk between protective immune responses and systemic inflammatory cascade reaction within the body at that time, aligning with some above-mentioned underlying pathogenesis of UC known so far [15, 16]. Mesalazine exerts therapeutic effects in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC by regulating the gut immune system and alleviating intestinal inflammation [17, 18]. Therefore, immunity and inflammation, as shared effector targets, tightly link LCR with mesalazine. Moreover, previous studies performed by our research team have demonstrated the substantial predictive and prognostic potentials of LCR in adult patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), acute pancreatitis (AP), diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), and pediatric patients with Kawasaki disease (KD), irrespective of whether LCR is measured upon admission or dynamically evaluated [15, 16, 19–21]. All pertinent research findings, including the contributions from our research team, have broadened the clinical application of LCR from the initial field of malignancies to many other disorders with immuno-inflammatory crosstalk within the body as the primary pathogenesis, particularly infectious and auto-immune diseases [22–24]. Nevertheless, to date, no clinical studies have attempted to apply LCR to adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC for early and accurate identification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine and tailored treatment regimens.

To address this current issue, we aimed to investigate for the first time in this study whether LCR upon admission could function as a stratified tool to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine and accordingly optimize interventions in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC. Our findings will offer a novel, simple, and high-reliable clinical objective parameter for early and accurate identification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, thus avoiding this ‘trial and error’ approach and unnecessary sufferings from patients, facilitating the implementation of precision therapy and personalized medicine, and alleviating the economic burden on the healthcare system, all of which demonstrate significant clinical practical value.

Methods

Study design

This cohort study retrospectively recruited adult patients with active UC who were admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2024. Disease severity was identified by the modified Truelove and Witts criteria (Table 1), and then adult patients with severe active UC were excluded. All enrolled adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC received mesalazine monotherapy (pH- or time-dependent mesalazine sustained-release granules or tablets or their combination with mesalazine enema or suppository) as first-line treatment and underwent the first lower digestive endoscopy within 7 days after admission. Disease extent was divided into three categories: E1 (rectum), E2 (left colon and rectum), and E3 (whole colon and even terminal ileum) through the first Montreal classification within 7 days after admission. After that, adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC in the first two categories and the last category were respectively included in the local lesion group and the extensive lesion group for subsequent statistical analysis. A total dose of 4 g/day of mesalazine was provided to adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC in the local lesion group and the extensive lesion group through oral administration union enema and complete oral administration, respectively [12]. Demographic characteristics, such as gender, age, and active smoking history, as well as clinical indicators, involving disease duration, fever history before admission, disease severity (mild/moderate), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil percentage (NEUT%), neutrophil count (NEUT), lymphocyte percentage (LYM%), LYMPH, hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), platelet count (PLT), CRP, LCR, prothrombin activity (PTA), fibrinogen (FIB), D-Dimer, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (SCr) upon admission, the first ulcerative colitis endoscopic index of severity (UCEIS), Mayo endoscopic score (MES), and Montreal classification (E1 + E2/E3) within 7 days after admission, and length of hospital stay were extracted, collated, and calculated from the medical records of the enrolled patients. Therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine was typically determined 5–7 days after its initiation. Based on their therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine, the enrolled patients were stratified into three groups: complete remission, partial remission, and ineffectiveness, in which the patients in the first two groups and the last group were incorporated in the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group, respectively [25] (Fig. 1). Subsequently, all the aforementioned data were compared between different groups. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (IRB number: KY2025-010).

Study population

The inclusion criteria of this study were adult patients (aged 18 years and older) with mild-to-moderate active UC who were admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2024. They received mesalazine monotherapy (pH- or time-dependent mesalazine sustained-release granules or tablets or their combination with mesalazine enema or suppository) as first-line treatment and underwent the first lower digestive endoscopy within 7 days after admission. The exclusion criteria encompassed co-existing infectious, allergic or other auto-immune diseases, malignancies, severe malnutrition, acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), chronic organ failure, immunotherapy or organ transplantation within the past 6 months, pregnant or breastfeeding women, re-admission, and incomplete medical records (Fig. 2).

Identification of active smoking history

Active smoking history was identified by smoking at least 100 cigarettes before admission [26].

Diagnosis and treatment of mild-to-moderate active UC

In daily clinical practice, a combination of typical clinical manifestations and digestive endoscopy features was necessary for the definitive diagnosis of active UC on the basis of excluding infectious and other non-infectious colitis [12, 27]. Further biopsy or pathological examination of the excised specimen tissue was needed to confirm the presence of active UC for some patients with difficult diagnosis [12, 27]. On this basis, mild-to-moderate active UC was identified according to the modified Truelove and Witts criteria. All enrolled adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC received mesalazine monotherapy (pH- or time-dependent mesalazine sustained-release granules or tablets or their combination with mesalazine enema or suppository) as first-line treatment, with considerations for escalation to corticosteroids, biologics, or azathioprine in cases of therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine in this study [12, 13].

Determination and classification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine

Therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine was typically determined 5–7 days after its initiation. Based on their therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine, the enrolled adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC were stratified into three groups: complete remission, partial remission, and ineffectiveness, in which the patients in the first two groups and the last group were incorporated in the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group, respectively [25]. Among them, complete remission, defined as complete improvement in clinical symptoms, involving defecation frequency ≤ 2 times/day without bloody stool, abdominal pain, fever, weight loss, or extraintestinal comorbidities, is the desired therapeutic goal in daily clinical practice; partial remission referred to partial improvement in clinical symptoms, including defecation frequency ≤ 4 times/day, bloody stool, abdominal pain, or a decrease of defecation frequency by 4 times/day compared with that before mesalazine administration, but without systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or extraintestinal comorbidities; while ineffectiveness indicated no improvement in clinical symptoms [25].

Measurement of LYMPH and CRP

Semiconductor laser flow cytometry and sheath flow direct current impedance method was adopted for the measurement of LYMPH (0.70 ~ 4.00 × 109/L), with reagents from Sysmex Corporation and testing instrument of dimension, whereas immunological method was employed for the measurement of CRP (0.00 ~ 6.00 mg/L), with reagents from Siemens Corporation and testing instrument of dimension.

Calculation of LCR upon admission

LCR upon admission was calculated from LYMPH and CRP within 24 h of admission according to the following formula: LCR = LYMPH (× 109/L) × 10,000/CRP (mg/L).

Data collection

The medical data required for this study, obtained by the dedicated personnel within our research team from the Medical Record Department of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University, had been removed the personal information of the enrolled patients. Therefore, written informed consent from the study participants was waived in compliance with institutional requirements.

Statistical analyses

SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was utilized for statistical analyses. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those without a normal distribution were presented as median (P25, P75). Counting variables was represented by frequency. Independent sample t-test was conducted for inter-group comparisons of continuous variables with a normal distribution, whereas Mann-Whitney U test was employed for those of continuous variables without a normal distribution. Chi-square (χ2) test was adopted for inter-group comparisons of counting variables. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to analyze the correlations between LCR upon admission and disease duration, disease severity as well as the first UCEIS, MES, and Montreal classification within 7 days after admission. On the basis of inter-group comparisons, logistic regression was applied for univariate logistic regression analysis. The variables with significance less than 0.1 in both inter-group comparisons and univariate logistic regression analysis were included in the multiple logistic regression analysis model and then the independent variables were screened by Wald backward method. Finally, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were analyzed, and the areas under the ROC curves (AUCs), the corresponding sensitivity and specificity, the cut-off values, and the positive and negative predictive values were calculated. A p-value < 0.05 suggested a significant difference.

Results

Comparison of demographic characteristics and clinical indicators between the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group

A total of 244 adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC were recruited in this single-center retrospective cohort study and subsequently categorized into the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group (n = 165) and the therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine group (n = 79) based on their therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine. The overall therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine was 67.62% in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC. Significant differences in multiple clinical indicators were noted between different groups (Table 2).

The correlations between LCR upon admission and disease duration, disease severity as well as the first UCEIS, MES, and Montreal classification within 7 days after admission

LCR upon admission exhibited a significant positive correlation with disease duration and remarkable negative correlations with disease severity as well as the first UCEIS and MES within 7 days after admission, with correlation coefficients of 0.190, −0.729, −0.597, −0.484, respectively (Table 3). However, there was no significant correlation between LCR upon admission and the first Montreal classification within 7 days after admission (p = 0.090).

Analysis of the factors related to therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine

Disease duration, NEUT%, and LCR upon admission were determined as protective factors for therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine, whereas disease severity, the first UCEIS within 7 days after admission, and length of hospital stay were identified as risk factors. The possibility of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with moderate active UC was only 0.310 times of that in adult patients with mild active UC (Table 4). Discussion

UC, characterized by relapsing and remitting immune dysregulation-mediated inflammation, usually necessitates lifelong medication to induce and maintain stable and sustained clinical remission, achieve endoscopic and histological mucosal healing, mitigate or even avoid recurrence, and therefore enhance the quality of life [28, 29]. Although mesalazine, an anti-inflammatory agent, is recommended as first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate active UC due to favorable risk-benefit profile, its performance in inducing an effective therapeutic responsiveness still falls short of being satisfactory [30–32]. In the present study, the overall therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine was only 67.62% in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC. Considering the millions of confirmed cases around the world, mild-to-moderate active UC adult patients with therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine will be a very large patient population, posing a rigorous challenge to effective clinical management and imposing a heavy economic burden on the healthcare system. The continued advancements in pharmacotherapeutics have enabled the individualized choice of the most appropriate agents suitable for the unique profile of each patient in daily clinical practice [33–35]. However, the current practical challenge lies in how to stratify therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine by simple and high-reliable clinical objective parameters or combinations in the early stage of the disease and accordingly optimize interventions in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC.

UCEIS and MES have been proposed as well-established endoscope-based scoring systems to support diagnosis, assess disease activity and severity, formulate and adjust interventions, monitor therapeutic responsiveness, and predict clinical outcomes in UC, although their invasive profile and limitation induced by operator subjectivity [36–39]. Our results demonstrated that LCR upon admission exhibited remarkable negative correlations with the first UCEIS and MES within 7 days after admission, suggesting that LCR upon admission may perform well in the above clinical scenarios. Non-significant correlation between LCR upon admission and the first Montreal classification within 7 days after admission suggests that disease extent (E1 + E2 vs. E3) has little impact on the predictive ability of LCR upon admission on therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC. Its potential reason lies in that there was no statistical difference in disease severity (mild/moderate) between the local lesion group and the extensive lesion group (95/58 vs. 47/44, p = 0.110), which means that the expansion of disease extent does not equal to the deterioration of disease severity, and therefore does not lead to significant changes in LCR upon admission [40]. This innovative attempt revealed the superior performance of LCR upon admission to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, with AUC of 0.907, sensitivity of 80.00%, specificity of 91.10%, cut-off value of 1321.20, positive predictive value of 94.96%, and negative predictive value of 68.57%. Specifically, LCR upon admission below 1321.20 indicates a high possibility of therapeutic non-responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, and this possibility will become greater and greater with the continuous decrease in LCR upon admission. From the results of the stratified AUCs by disease extent, LCR upon admission showed statistical differences in both the local lesion group and the extensive lesion group, suggesting that it had the ability to classify adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC in both groups into the therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine group, although their AUC was different (0.764 vs. 0.701). From the ROC curves in our results, the AUC of LCR upon admission was the largest among all single variables. Although the AUC of combination of disease duration, disease severity, NEUT%, LCR, UCEIS, and length of hospital stay is slightly larger than that of LCR alone (0.938 vs. 0.907), the invasive and lagging properties of lower digestive endoscopy and the unobtainable feature of length of hospital stay are obviously not suitable for predicting therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in daily clinical practice.

The concrete embodiment of “one size does not fit all” in the clinical management of adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC is the implementation of precision therapy and personalized medicine, and more specifically, the right medication with the right dosage and duration should be selected for the right patient at the right time (5R principle). Apparently, for all adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, it is a huge bug to adopt standard-dose mesalazine monotherapy as first-line treatment in daily clinical practice. Our novel findings lay a preliminary foundation for the diversity of initial empirical therapy in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, and provide theoretical support for prompt medication switching, escalation, or combination, thereby avoiding unnecessary sufferings from patients and alleviating the economic burden on the healthcare system.

As a preliminary innovative attempt, there are many limitations in this study. Firstly, the nature of retrospective study with a relatively small sample size in a single center weakens the reliability and generalizability of our conclusion due to potential selection and temporal biases. Secondly, our novel findings are only applicable to adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC who received mesalazine monotherapy as first-line treatment, and can not be directly generalized to other similar patient populations. Moreover, the different ethnic or regional features of adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC may also have an impact on our results and hinder their extension and application in daily clinical practice. Thirdly, the determination and classification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine depend on the degree of improvement in clinical symptoms rather than objective criteria, which may affect the reliability of our conclusion. Fourthly, potential challenges to clinical implementation, including different detection methods and testing instruments as well as reagents from different corporations, will obstruct extension and application of our results. Fifthly, this study is the absence of external validation in an independent cohort, which may exaggerate our results and further affect their clinical utility and reproducibility. Lastly, our study opens new vista in early and accurate identification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC by simple and high-reliable clinical objective parameter. Therefore, the results still have room for improvement, and the conclusion warrants further verification through more high-quality, large-sample trials in real-world clinical settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, LCR upon admission holds significant potential to predict therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in adult patients with mild-to-moderate active UC, which paves the way for early and accurate identification of therapeutic responsiveness to mesalazine in this patient population, therefore avoiding this 'trial and error' approach and unnecessary sufferings from patients, facilitating the implementation of precision therapy and personalized medicine, and alleviating the economic burden on the healthcare system. As a preliminary innovative attempt, our novel findings warrant further verification and refinement through more high-quality, large-sample trials in real-world clinical settings.
