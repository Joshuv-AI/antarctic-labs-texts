Gut microbiota-derived metabolite isovalerylcarnitine modulates salt sensitivity of blood pressure and incident hypertension: a multicenter dietary salt intervention trial
Authors: Zhennan Lin, Shujie Li, Mengyao Liu, Jianxin Li, Fangchao Liu, Jie Cao, Shufeng Chen, Keyong Huang, Yaqin Wang, Hongfan Li, Yan Wang, Bin Yang, Dongmei Xing, Qing Wang, Xu Ji, Xingqiang Bai, Dongsheng Hu, Ming Zhang, Dongshuang Guo, Jianfeng Huang, Bin Geng, Dongfeng Gu, Xiangfeng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the roles of gut microbiota and plasma metabolites in salt sensitivity (SS) of blood pressure (SSBP) and hypertension. A 23-day, multicenter, dietary salt intervention trial (the MetaSalt study) recruited 528 participants who underwent a baseline observation, low-salt, and high-salt interventions. SSBP was assessed and used as the primary outcome, and fecal shotgun metagenome and plasma targeted metabolome were measured. We found that high salt significantly altered 85 gut-microbial species (p < 9.42 × 10−5) and 70 metabolites (p < 2.26 × 10−4). Among them, the changes in 22 species and 8 metabolites were associated with SSBP (p < 0.05), and a gut microbiota-acylcarnitine network implicated in SSBP was identified, with a gut microbiota-derived metabolite, isovalerylcarnitine, as the core metabolite. Isovalerylcarnitine was also inversely associated with SSBP in the GenSalt study (p = 0.0102). Importantly, increased isovalerylcarnitine attenuated SS hypertension and improved endothelial function in rats, and was associated with reduced risk (ranging from 13% to 19%) of BP progression and incident hypertension in a prospective cohort (n = 3907, median follow-up = 5.5 years). This study demonstrated that the gut-acylcarnitine axis may play roles in the development of SS hypertension. Trial number: ChiCTR1900025171.

Introduction

High-salt diet (HSD) has been ranked as the first dietary cause contributing to the global burden of disease in 20191, especially increasing the incidence and mortality of cardiovascular disease (CVD)2,3. The deleterious impact of HSD on the cardiovascular system predominantly derives from high salt-induced blood pressure (BP) elevation4. However, BP response to salt varies among individuals, with some people’s BP dramatically changing in response to salt intake, which is defined as salt sensitivity (SS) of BP (SSBP)5, and CVD risk also varies across individuals with different degrees of SSBP6. The mechanisms of high salt-induced BP elevation and the development of SS hypertension involve dysfunction in the sympathetic nervous and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone systems5,7. Additionally, the Genetic Epidemiology Network of Salt Sensitivity (GenSalt) study, led by our team, has provided insights into the genetic susceptibility and other risk determinants of SSBP8–10. More recently, multi-omics studies have expanded our understanding of the relationships between dietary salt, BP regulation, and underlying biological processes based on animal experiments11–13. In particular, the roles of gut microbiota and metabolites have been highlighted as key factors in these processes. For example, a study in rats found that HSD increased the level of corticosterone by reducing the levels of Bacteroides fragilis and arachidonic acid, ultimately leading to elevated BP11. This finding underscores the potential interplay between gut microbiota and metabolites in the regulation of SS hypertension. Despite these advances, the specific microbiota-metabolite pathways that influence SSBP in humans remain poorly understood, and key metabolic biomarkers critical for the precision prevention and treatment of SS hypertension have yet to be identified.

To address this gap, we conducted this study to reveal the roles of microbiota and metabolites in SSBP. First, we utilized the Metabolome, Microbiome, and Dietary Salt Intervention (MetaSalt) study, a multicenter trial, to evaluate the impacts of dietary salt on gut microbiota and plasma metabolites and identify potential microbiota-metabolite pathways associated with SSBP. Second, we replicated these findings in another dietary salt intervention trial (the GenSalt study). Third, given that metabolites are more readily quantifiable and may serve as reliable biomarkers or therapeutic targets compared to gut microbiota, we verified the influence of key SSBP-related metabolites on BP in rats. Finally, we evaluated the effect of key metabolites on BP status progression and incident hypertension in a real-world setting based on a prospective cohort (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Overall flow chart of the study.BP blood pressure; SSBP salt sensitivity of blood pressure.

Results

General characteristics and gut-microbial diversity of the MetaSalt participants

The MetaSalt study was conducted among 528 participants over 23 days, including a 3-day baseline observation (usual diet), a 10-day low-salt intervention (3 g/d of salt), and a 10-day high-salt intervention (18 g/d of salt). A total of 503 participants completed the entire trial (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Participants’ BP levels were measured on each day of the baseline stage, and on days 2, 8, 9, and 10 of the low-salt and high-salt stages. Mean BP levels were calculated by averaging the readings during the three-day baseline and the last 3 days of each intervention stage. The baseline characteristics of these participants are presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Data 1. Among them, the average age was 48.11 ± 9.26 years, and 63.02% of them were female. The mean systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) levels were 129.32 ± 13.64 and 80.83 ± 9.75 mmHg, respectively (Table 1). We applied linear mixed models (LMMs) to compare the differences in BP, 24-h urinary sodium (UNa), and 24-h urinary potassium (UK) across study phases. All of them were significantly decreased from the baseline to the low-salt intervention, and increased after the high-salt intervention, indicating effective intervention and good compliance. For instance, SBP was decreased by 4.99 [95% confidence interval (CI): 4.28, 5.70] mmHg when switching to the low-salt stage, and increased by 4.84 (95% CI: 4.14, 5.55) mmHg after the high-salt intervention. Notably, BP, 24-h UNa, and 24-h UK at baseline were close to those during the high-salt period (Supplementary Fig. 2). The degrees of SSBP were determined based on the responses of mean arterial pressure (MAP) to the low-salt (the averaged MAP on the last 3 days of the low-salt intervention minus the average MAP at baseline) and high-salt interventions (the averaged MAP on the last 3 days of the high-salt intervention minus the average MAP on the last 3 days of the low-salt intervention), categorizing 503 participants into 247 salt-resistance (SR), 168 moderate SS, and 88 extreme SS participants (Table 1). We evaluated α-diversity, β-diversity, and median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of gut microbiota for each participant, and compared differences across study phases and SSBP groups using permutational analysis of variance (ANOVA) or LMMs. No significant difference in gut-microbial β-diversity was observed across the three study phases (p = 0.3660) (Supplementary Fig. 4). However, both gut-microbial α-diversity and median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity increased from the baseline to the low-salt phase (p = 0.0080 and 4.35 × 10−6, respectively), and decreased from the low-salt to the high-salt stage (p = 0.0156 and 0.0380, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, higher SSBP was consistently associated with increased gut-microbial α-diversity and median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity in all study phases (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Discovery of salt-related biomarkers in the MetaSalt study

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and plasma targeted metabolomics profiling were performed on samples collected from the MetaSalt participants at each study phase. After quality control, a total of 531 gut-microbial species, 323 gut-microbial functional pathways [including 190 Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways and 133 Carbohydrate-Active EnZymes (CAZy) families], and 221 metabolites were kept. We used the rank-based inverse normalization method to standardize the relative abundance of metagenome data and absolute concentration of metabolites before analysis. LMMs were used to compare the differences in gut-microbial species, functional pathways, and plasma metabolites between the low-salt and high-salt interventions. Biomarkers with Bonferroni-corrected significant differences between study phases were defined as salt-related biomarkers.

From 531 species, we observed significantly differences in 85 (16.00%) gut-microbial species between the low-salt and high-salt periods (Bonferroni correction: p < 0.05/531 = 9.42 × 10−5). Among them, 79 were depleted after the high-salt intervention, with 60 from Bacillota (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 2). For example, the relative abundance of Anaerotruncus colihominis was decreased in the high-salt stage compared to the low-salt stage (p = 6.48 × 10−5). In addition, 44 gut-microbial functions, including 36 (18.95%) KEGG pathways (p < 0.05/190 = 2.63 × 10−4) and 8 (6.02%) CAZy families (p < 0.05/133 = 3.76 × 10−4), were significantly influenced by dietary salt intake (Fig. 2c, d; Supplementary Data 3 and 4).

From 221 metabolites, we identified 70 (31.67%) salt-related metabolites after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05/221 = 2.26 × 10−4), involving 13 classes, with 65 metabolites decreased in the high-salt period (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 5). For example, the plasma level of isovalerylcarnitine decreased from the low-salt to the high-salt intervention (p = 7.97 × 10−9). The 70 salt-related metabolites were enriched in 34 KEGG pathways (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Among them, nine pathways overlapped with the salt-related KEGG pathways identified from metagenome data, of which the top two pathways were fatty acid biosynthesis and valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis (Fig. 2f). A total of 68 salt-related metabolites showed nominally significant associations with various salt-related gut-microbial species and functional pathways (p < 0.05), indicating that the changes in gut microbiota may contribute to the changes in metabolite (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). Based on the annotation of “MetOrigin”14, 52 out of 68 metabolites were identified as potentially microbiota-derived (Supplementary Data 6).

In sensitivity analyses, most results were replicated after further adjusting for several covariates or excluding dyslipidemia patients (Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, the effects of dietary salt on gut microbiota and metabolites were robust to other nutritional components (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Discovery of SSBP-related biomarkers in the MetaSalt study

We further used LMMs to assess the associations of SSBP with the changes in salt-related gut-microbial species, functional pathways, and metabolites from the low-salt to the high-salt intervention (ΔbiomarkerH-L), and identified those biomarkers that may account for the disparity of SSBP. Of those salt-related biomarkers, we observed that the changes in 24 gut-microbial species, 3 KEGG pathways, and 9 metabolites were associated with SSBP (all ptrend < 0.05). Among them, we further focused on those biomarkers showing consistent directions between SSBP-associations and high-salt-induced changes, including 22 species, 1 KEGG pathway, and 8 metabolites. All of the 22 SSBP-related species were negatively related to SSBP, and 21 belonged to Bacillota (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 7). Of the 8 SSBP-related metabolites, 7 showed negative associations, and one showed positive, and these metabolites belonged to 5 classes (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 8). To establish the SSBP-related microbiota-metabolites network and identify key metabolites, we carried out association analysis for SSBP-related species and SSBP-related metabolites by using LMMs. A total of 31 associations between them with p < 0.05 were kept, including 4 metabolites and 17 species (Supplementary Data 9). After visualizing with Cytoscape software, a gut microbiota-metabolite network was identified, in which an acylcarnitine (i.e., isovalerylcarnitine) and 3-hydroxyisovaleric acid (an acylcarnitine-related precursor) were the core metabolites (Fig. 3c). Nearly all microbes in this network belonged to Bacillota, of which some were known short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producers, such as Pseudoflavonifractor capillosus, Intestinimonas butyriciproducens, Anaerotruncus colihominis, and microbes in the genus Clostridium15,16. Furthermore, we extracted microbial genes related to the biosynthesis and metabolism of carnitine/acylcarnitine from metagenome data, and assessed their associations with isovalerylcarnitine and 16 isovalerylcarnitine-related species. We observed that three genes, including caiB (carnitine CoA-transferase), caiC (carnitine-CoA ligase), and TC.BCT (betaine/carnitine transporter, BCCT family) were positively associated with isovalerylcarnitine (p < 0.05). Among them, caiB and TC.BCT showed consistent positive associations with 15 and 13 isovalerylcarnitine-related species, respectively (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 10).

In sensitivity analyses, most results were replicated after further adjusting for several covariates, excluding dyslipidemia patients (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11), or using SBP or DBP to determine SSBP groups (Supplementary Data 11), demonstrating that the results were robust.

Replication of salt-related and SSBP-related metabolites in the GenSalt study

From the 1879 participants of the GenSalt study, we 1:1 matched 113 extreme SS individuals and 113 SR individuals according to age, sex, field centers, and MAP (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 12). Of the 226 GenSalt participants, the average age was 40.26 ± 7.16 years, and 46.90% of participants were female. As expected, there was no significant difference in general characteristics between the extreme SS and SR individuals (Supplementary Data 12). We applied LMMs to validate salt-related and SSBP-related metabolites. Among the 70 salt-related metabolites, 67 were detected and passed quality control in the GenSalt participants, and 52 (77.61%) salt-related metabolites were replicated (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 13). Particularly, 5 short-chain acylcarnitines were also found to be significantly decreased after the high-salt intervention in these participants (Fig. 4b). We further evaluated the effects of isovalerylcarnitine on HSD-induced hypertension in Dahl SS rats (n = 7, 11, and 11 in the control, HSD, and HSD + isovalerylcarnitine groups, respectively) (Fig. 5d). After 28 days, the administration of isovalerylcarnitine significantly increased its plasma concentrations, reaching 0.05 ± 0.01, 0.03 ± 0.01, and 0.05 ± 0.02 μmol/L in the control, HSD, and HSD + isovalerylcarnitine groups, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13). Baseline SBP and DBP did not differ among groups. Following an HSD treatment, SBP and DBP were gradually elevated, whereas isovalerylcarnitine supplementation significantly lowered the HSD-induced BP elevation. On average, SBP and DBP in the HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group were 14.20 mmHg (p for t-test = 0.0009) and 8.81 mmHg (p for t-test = 0.0059) lower than those in the HSD group at the end of the experiment, respectively (Fig. 5e). Body weight and feed intake were comparable across groups. While HSD significantly increased natriuresis and albumin/creatinine ratio compared to controls (p < 0.05), and these parameters did not differ significantly between the HSD and HSD + isovalerylcarnitine groups (Supplementary Data 15). An additional experiment demonstrated that oral administration of isovalerylcarnitine via drinking water (7.5 μg/ml) similarly reduced SBP by 10.51 mmHg in the HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group versus HSD alone (p for t-test = 0.0132) (Supplementary Fig. 14).

We further examined whether isovalerylcarnitine modulates relaxation in mesenteric resistance arteries of Dahl SS rats. Compared with controls, the arteries of rats in the HSD group displayed impaired endothelium-dependent vasorelaxation in response to acetylcholine (ACh). Isovalerylcarnitine supplementation significantly improved the response to ACh in arteries compared with the HSD group (p for ANOVA = 0.0132) (Fig. 5f). The endothelium-independent vasorelaxation to sodium nitroprusside (SNP) was unaffected. Immunofluorescence staining of the aorta showed that the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) decreased in the HSD group (p for t-test = 0.0014), and restored in the HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group (p for t-test = 0.9039), compared with the control group (Fig. 5g, h), indicating a likely endothelial protective effect. Masson’s staining revealed a significant increase in collagen deposition within the tunica media of the thoracic aorta in the HSD group compared with the control group (p for Wilcoxon-test = 6.29 × 10−5). In the HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group, a modest reduction in collagen fraction was observed (Fig. 5i, j). Sirius Red staining showed a significantly reduced renal interstitial fibrosis in the HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group compared with the HSD group (p for Wilcoxon-test = 0.0192) (Fig. 5i, k). These findings indicated that isovalerylcarnitine exerts protective effects on salt-induced tissue remodeling.

To ascertain whether isovalerylcarnitine is produced by gut microbiota, we performed an antibiotic experiment. After a 28-day antibiotic experiment, both gut microbiota and isovalerylcarnitine were significantly decreased in rats with antibiotic cocktail treatment (ACT) and increased in rats with ACT and subsequent fecal microbiota transplantation (p for t-test <0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results indicate that isovalerylcarnitine is partially derived from gut microbiota.

Discussion

This study unveiled that HSD significantly influences the gut microbiota and plasma metabolite profiles, and the alterations in microbes and metabolites were implicated in SSBP. Notably, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite, isovalerylcarnitine, appeared to play a crucial role and hold promise as a potential target for the precision prevention of hypertension. Our study provides several insights into understanding the complex relationships between dietary salt and BP.

First, based on dietary salt intervention trials in humans, we systematically identified a salt-responsive gut-microbial signature. We observed that the high-salt intervention influenced the relative abundance of a lot of gut-microbial species and functional pathways. Some species in various taxonomic levels, such as Lactobacillales, Alistipes, Anaerotruncus, Parabacteroides, etc., have been reported to be affected by HSD in previous animal experiments and cross-sectional studies11,17,18. We suppose that HSD potentially disturbs the intestinal microenvironment, in which a more hypertonic gut is not conducive to microbial survival, thereby causing gut microbiota dysbiosis. This suggests that salt reduction may help restore or maintain microbial balance. In addition, the relationships between gut microbiota and SS hypertension have been reported by previous studies in animals11,19, but evidence in humans remains limited. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale dietary intervention trial in humans to identify SSBP-related microbes. We observed that the alterations in 22 species were negatively associated with SSBP. Notably, several of these species were known SCFA-producing bacteria, which were shown to promote cardio-metabolic health by anti-inflammation, regulating the immune system, improving lipid metabolism, and reducing BP15,20,21, such as Pseudoflavonifractor capillosus, Intestinimonas butyriciproducens, and Anaerotruncus colihominis15,16. We also identified some SSBP-related microbes that may act as protective factors. For example, the microbes in the genus Alistipes, Eubacterium, Clostridium, etc. have previously been found to be negatively associated with CVD and stroke22–24. Hence, these SSBP-related microbes are expected to be potential probiotics for preventing SS hypertension, which is worth exploring in depth in the future.

Second, we discovered a salt-responsive metabolite signature by performing plasma targeted metabolomics profiling in the MetaSalt study, with most findings well-replicated in our GenSalt study. Apart from previously reported metabolites, such as phenyllactic acid, indolelactic acid, indoleacetic acid, etc.12,25, we identified a lot of novel salt-related metabolites. Interestingly, the majority of these metabolites were decreased by the high-salt intervention, possibly due to the antimicrobial properties of salt. A large proportion of them were associated with salt-related gut microbiota and could be annotated to microbial origins using the “MetOrigin” tool. In addition to microbiota-derived pathways, dietary components also contribute to the plasma metabolome. High-salt intake may impair digestion and absorption by altering enzyme activity and pancreatic function26, thereby affecting protein and lipid metabolism. Most of the salt-related metabolites identified in our study were amino acids or fatty acids, and enrichment of relevant KEGG pathways, such as fatty acid biosynthesis, supports this explanation. In addition, this study not only validated previously reported SSBP-related metabolites, such as histidine27, but also identified some novel metabolites. For example, methylcysteine was the only one whose change was positively associated with SSBP; it has been reported to induce cardiac injury and oxidative stress28. The change in 2-hydroxybutyric acid was negatively related to SSBP. An isomer of this metabolite, β-hydroxybutyric acid, has been shown to decrease under high-salt conditions, and supplementation with this metabolite attenuated hypertension via anti-inflammation29. Our findings demonstrate that SS hypertension may also be partly ascribed to high salt-induced metabolite changes.

Third, by integrating metagenome and metabolome data, we speculated that the HSD-gut microbiota-acylcarnitine axis may be a key pathway accounting for the variability of SSBP. Previous animal experiments have discovered the roles of some gut microbiota-derived metabolites in SS hypertension11–13. For example, a mouse study found that HSD could deplete Lactobacillus and decrease indole metabolites, thereby leading to SS hypertension through promoting the proliferation of T helper 17 cells12. In our study, we highlighted the gut-acylcarnitine axis, specifically evaluating the potential of acylcarnitines as the target for SS hypertension. We observed that HSD decreased the plasma levels of five acylcarnitines, and the change in isovalerylcarnitine was inversely associated with SSBP. Both the levels and changes in isovalerylcarnitine were related to various gut-microbial species. Furthermore, our antibiotic experiment confirmed that isovalerylcarnitine is at least partially derived from gut microbiota. Acylcarnitines are esters composed of fatty acids and carnitine, primarily generated through fatty acid metabolism and branched-amino acid degradation30. We speculate that high salt-induced acylcarnitine decrement may be owing to the following reasons: (1) High salt influences the absorption of exogenous carnitine, thereby inhibiting acylcarnitine synthesis. (2) High salt decreases acylcarnitines by influencing amino acid metabolism, as we observed a reduction in lysine, methionine, and leucine, which are precursors of endogenous carnitine or acyl coenzyme A. (3) The lack of SCFAs may also contribute to the reduction in acylcarnitines. Although we did not observe high salt decreasing SCFAs directly, some SCFA producers associated with isovalerylcarnitine were significantly depleted by the high-salt intervention, suggesting a potential effect of SCFA-producing bacteria in modulating acylcarnitine levels. Previous studies have linked the imbalance of acylcarnitine homeostasis to various diseases, such as diabetes mellitus30. In our study, isovalerylcarnitine emerged as a potential driver metabolite of SSBP, with both oral and subcutaneous supplementation of isovalerylcarnitine attenuating SS hypertension. This protective effect may be due to the property of improving endothelial function and against salt-induced tissue remodeling, as evidenced by enhanced acetylcholine-induced vasodilation in the mesenteric arteries of rats.

Finally, we revealed the potential benefits of isovalerylcarnitine in preventing BP status progression and incident hypertension in a prospective cohort. Compared to individuals with the lowest level of isovalerylcarnitine (first tertile), those with the highest levels (third tertile) had a 33% and 31% lower risk of transitioning from normotension to hypertension and BP status increasing ≥ 1 step, respectively. Currently, there is a lack of targeted medications or supplements specifically designed for SS individuals. Since the responses of isovalerylcarnitine to salt are more prominent in SS individuals, isovalerylcarnitine may serve as a promising candidate for precision prevention in this population. Furthermore, supplementation with isovalerylcarnitine may show an important public health significance, particularly in China, where the average salt intake is high (17 g/d), and the prevalence of prehypertension (41.3%) and hypertension (23.2%) remains substantial31,32. Some acylcarnitines, such as acetylcarnitine and propinylcarnitine30, have been used as dietary supplements to treat or prevent chronic conditions, such as type II diabetes mellitus and nervous system diseases. Therefore, we believe that it is also worth conducting randomized clinical trials to evaluate the potential of isovalerylcarnitine as an intervention target and develop dietary supplement strategies.

Our study has several strengths. First, it was a multicenter dietary salt intervention trial with controlled salt intake and standardized recipes, ensuring that the observed changes in gut microbes and metabolites were primarily attributed to salt. The repeated-measured and self-control design further minimized confounding and enhanced statistical power. Second, we leveraged advanced omics approaches, i.e., metagenomic sequencing and targeted metabolomics profiling, to identify key etiological pathways, providing more comprehensive and reliable results. Third, our findings were validated in independent populations and animal models, strengthening the credibility of the evidence. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, owing to the absence of fecal samples in the GenSalt participants, we were unable to replicate the findings about gut microbiota. Second, the absence of a washout period in the dietary salt intervention trials might have introduced carryover effects, which may lead to underestimated effects; however, less likely to result in false positive results. Additionally, the duration of each intervention stage was long enough to allow the potential bias to disappear or diminish33. Third, the long storage time (approximately 20 years) of blood samples in the GenSalt and cohort participants may have influenced some metabolites to be validated. Nevertheless, the results for isovalerylcarnitine remain robust in these two populations. Fourth, this study was conducted exclusively with Chinese participants, and the findings need to be validated in other ethnic populations.

In conclusion, through multicenter dietary salt intervention trials, we found that high-salt intake could alter a list of gut-microbial species and plasma metabolites, and that the changes in gut microbes and metabolites were parallel to SSBP. In particular, the HSD-gut microbiota-acylcarnitine axis may be the key pathway, with a gut microbiota-derived metabolite, isovalerylcarnitine, substantially attenuating BP status progression and incident hypertension. Our study provides new insights into understanding the biological mechanism of SS hypertension, and isovalerylcarnitine holds promise to be a potential target for hypertension.

Methods

Study approval

This study involved two dietary salt intervention trials and one prospective cohort, which were conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from participants before the investigations. Moreover, rats were used to validate our findings. All animal experiments were conducted following the recommendations, and the protocol was approved by the institutional animal care and use committee, Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Science.

The MetaSalt study

The MetaSalt study was a multicenter, dietary salt intervention trial, registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry database on August 15, 2019 (registration number: ChiCTR1900025171), aiming at identifying the key gut microbes and metabolites related to the SSBP. Its detailed information was depicted in the study protocol34, and summarized in Supplementary Methods. Briefly, 528 participants were recruited from four Chinese communities in 2019, consisting of probands and their family members. All eligible participants should meet with SBP < 160 mmHg and DBP < 100 mmHg and without taking antihypertensive medications within 1 month before screening. Participants underwent a 3-day baseline observation (usual diet), a 10-day low-salt intervention (3 g/d of salt), and a 10-day high-salt intervention (18 g/d of salt) (Fig. 1). Eventually, 503 participants completed the entire trial. General characteristics and risk factors were collected during the baseline stage. Blood, fecal, and urinary specimens were collected during each study phase.

An electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron® HBP1300, Japan) was used to measure participants’ SBP and DBP three times on each day of the baseline stage, and on days 2, 8, 9, and 10 of the low-salt and high-salt stages. Participants’ BP levels were measured three times in a fasting state in the morning of each day. Before each measurement, participants were requested to avoid alcohol, smoking, exercising, and drinking caffeine-containing beverages for at least 30 min, and to remain seated for at least 5 min. MAP levels were calculated using the formula: MAP = 1/3 × SBP + 2/3 × DBP. To minimize day-to-day fluctuation in BP and avoid the carryover effect from the previous study phase, mean BP levels were calculated by averaging the nine readings measured during the 3-day baseline period (BPB) and the nine readings from the last 3 days of both the low-salt (BPL) and high-salt intervention periods (BPH).

SSBP degrees were determined according to the changes of MAP from the baseline to the low-salt intervention (ΔMAPL-B = MAPL–MAPB) and from the low-salt to the high-salt intervention (ΔMAPH-L = MAPH–MAPL), since MAP combined the information of SBP and DBP, and it was a well-used index to measure SSBP5. All eligible participants were grouped into extreme SS individuals (ΔMAPL-B ≤ −10 mmHg or ΔMAPH-L ≥ 10 mmHg), moderate SS individuals (ΔMAPL-B ≤ −5 mmHg and > −10 mmHg or ΔMAPH-L ≥ 5 mmHg and <10 mmHg), or SR individuals (the remaining participants).

The GenSalt study

The GenSalt study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT00721721, and conducted during 2003–2005. Details were described before8, and summarized in Supplementary Methods. In brief, 1906 participants took part in the dietary salt intervention trial, and 1879 participants completed the entire trial. The study design of the GenSalt study was similar to the MetaSalt study. To replicate the key metabolites identified from the MetaSalt study, we applied a 1:1 matching method to select eligible pairs as many as possible from 1879 participants in the GenSalt study, according to age (±5 years), sex, field centers, and MAP (±5 mmHg). Eventually, 113 extreme SS individuals and 113 SR individuals were selected (Fig. 1).

The prospective cohort

To ascertain whether the key SSBP-related metabolite (isovalerylcarnitine) could serve as an intervention target for hypertension in a real-world setting. We used an independent population (n = 3907) with a median follow-up of 5.5 years to analyze the associations of isovalerylcarnitine with prevalent hypertension, BP status progression, and incident hypertension (Fig. 1). Detailed information was presented in Supplementary Methods. Participants’ BP information was collected during the baseline and 2012–2015 visit. For each participant, BP levels were measured three times after a 5-min quiet rest in a seated position using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron® HBP1300, Japan). Participants were asked to avoid alcohol, cigarette smoking, coffee or tea, and exercise for at least 30 min before BP measurement. The average SBP and DBP were calculated using three BP readings. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg and/or taking antihypertensive medication in the past two weeks. Ideal BP status was defined as SBP < 120 mmHg and DBP < 90 mmHg and without taking antihypertensive medication. Prehypertension was defined as SBP of 120–139 mmHg and/or DBP of 80–89 mmHg and without taking antihypertensive medication. Normotension included ideal BP status and prehypertension. The progression of BP status was defined as BP status increasing ≥ 1 step.

We used all participants to assess the association between isovalerylcarnitine and the prevalence of hypertension. After further excluding participants without BP information during the 2012–2015 visit (n = 1181), 2726 participants remained to evaluate the association between isovalerylcarnitine and the risk of BP status increasing ≥ 1 step. After further excluding participants with hypertension during the 2007–2008 visit (n = 1404), 1322 participants were kept to estimate the predictive value of isovalerylcarnitine on the incidence of prehypertension or hypertension (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Animal experiments

To verify the findings from human studies, we conducted several experiments in rats. All rats were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions in a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted following the recommendations of the Ministry of Science and Technology of China, and the protocol was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (License no.: FW-2022-0016).

Effects of dietary salt on isovalerylcarnitine

Four-week-old male Wistar rats were randomly allocated to the HSD group [8% sodium chloride (NaCl), n = 15] and the normal-salt control group (0.45% NaCl, n = 15). After adaptive training, rats were intervened for 4 weeks. Blood samples were collected at the end of the fourth week, and the plasma concentration of isovalerylcarnitine was measured using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). We conducted this experiment in Wistar rats, given that this strain has balanced organ functions and is not particularly susceptible to diseases. Fundamental effects rather than disease-oriented shifts can be observed in these rats. Detailed information is available in Supplementary Methods.

Effects of isovalerylcarnitine on SS hypertension

Seven-week-old male Dahl SS rats, a strain commonly used to investigate SS hypertension, were randomly allocated to three groups: group A (normal-salt control, 0.45% NaCl, n = 7), group B (HSD, 8% NaCl, n = 11), and group C (HSD + isovalerylcarnitine, 8% NaCl + 200 μ/kg/d of isovalerylcarnitine, n = 11). Rats in group C received a subcutaneous infusion of isovalerylcarnitine (MedChemExpress, Cat#139144-12-0) dissolved in saline at a dose of 200 μg/kg/d, administered via an osmotic mini-pump for 28 days. Rats in groups A and B were implanted with an osmotic mini-pump containing saline. To monitor BP, rats were surgically implanted with radiotelemetry (HD-X10, Data Sciences International, New Brighton, Minnesota). SBP and DBP were continuously recorded from 10 pm to 1 am on the monitoring day using the Ponemah v6.50 software, and average BP was calculated for analysis. Endothelium-dependent and endothelium-independent relaxation of mesenteric arteries was assessed using increasing concentrations of acetylcholine or sodium nitroprusside. The response to each concentration of the drug was recorded35. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s, Sirius red, and immunofluorescence staining were used to assess pathological changes of the aorta and kidney. Blood samples were collected at the end of the experiment, and the plasma concentration of isovalerylcarnitine was measured using UPLC-MS/MS. In addition, another intervention trial with replacing subcutaneous administration with oral administration (7.5 μg/ml added to drinking water, determined by pharmacokinetic analysis) was conducted among 20 Dahl SS rats [control group (n = 4), HSD group (n = 8), and HSD + isovalerylcarnitine group (n = 8)]. All rats were intervened for 28 days, and BP levels were measured using a tail-cuff system. Detailed information is available in Supplementary Methods.

Validation of microbiota source for isovalerylcarnitine

To confirm that isovalerylcarnitine could be produced by gut microbiota, we also conducted an antibiotic experiment in Sprague-Dawley rats, including a control group (without any intervention, n = 7), an ACT group (n = 7), and an ACT and subsequent fecal microbiota transplantation group (n = 6). Detailed information is available in Supplementary Methods.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed for each MetaSalt participant’s three fecal samples. Microbial DNA concentration was measured using Qubit® dsDNA Assay Kit in Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA), and above 1 μg per sample was used to construct library. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA). Briefly, the DNA sample was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp, which was end-polished, A-tailed, and ligated with the full-length adapter for Illumina sequencing with further PCR amplification. At last, PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system), and libraries were analyzed for size distribution by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified using real-time PCR. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System. After cluster generation, the library preparation was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform, and paired-end reads were generated.

Raw data obtained from the Illumina HiSeq platform using Readfq (version 8, https://github.com/cjfields.readfq) were processed into clean data for subsequent analysis. The specific steps were as follows: (1) removing reads containing low-quality bases (quality threshold value ≤ 38) above 40 bp; (2) removing reads in which the N base has reached 10 bp; (3) removing reads that shared an overlap above 15 bp with the Adapter. Subsequently, clean data were blasted to the host database by using Bowtie2 software (version 2.2.4, https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml) to filter the reads that were of host origin. After that, clean data were assembled by SOAPdenovo software (version 2.04, https://github.com/aquaskyline/SOAPdenovo2). The Scaftigs assembled predicted the open reading frame by MetaGeneMark software (version 2.10, http://topaz.gatech.edu/GeneMarK), and filtered the length information shorter than 100 nt from the predicted result. CD-HIT software (version 4.5.8, http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit) was adopted to reduce redundancy and obtain the unique initial gene catalog. The clean data of each sample were mapped to the initial gene catalog using Bowtie2, yielding the absolute abundance of genes mapped in each sample. DIAMOND software (version 0.9.9, https://github.com/bbuchfink.diamond/) was used to blast the Unigenes to the sequencing of microbes, which were extracted from the NR database (version 2018-01-02, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) of NCBI. For the finally aligned results of each sequence, as each one may have multiple aligned results, the result of which the e value ≤ the smallest e value × 10 was chosen to take the lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm, which was applied to system classification of MEGAN software to ensure the species annotation information of sequences36. The table containing the number of genes and the abundance information of each sample in each taxonomy hierarchy (kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) was obtained based on the LCA annotation result and the gene abundance table.

Eventually, 14,533 gut-microbial species, 400 KEGG pathways, and 313 CAZy families were detected in the MetaSalt participants. We included species and functions with a minimum detective relative abundance of 0.01% in at least 10% of the samples, which yielded 531 species, 190 KEGG pathways, and 133 CAZy families. Zero relative abundance was replaced with a very minimum value (1 × 10−11). Before analysis, the relative abundances of species were rank-based inverse normalized, since many of them were skewed in distribution.

Metabolomics profiling

Targeted metabolome kits

Using the UPLC-MS/MS system (ACQUITY UPLCXevo TQ-S, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, 570 USA), we performed plasma targeted metabolomics profiling for the participants of the MetaSalt, GenSalt, and cohort participants. Two metabolomics kits provided by Metabo-Profile Corp. (Shanghai, China) were used for profiling. (1) The Q300 Kit, containing 310 metabolites from 12 classes, such as amino acids, fatty acids, bile acids, carnitines, indoles, etc., which was a well-used kit by previous researchers37,38. (2) The trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) Kits, containing seven TMAO-related metabolites, i.e., TMAO, choline, carnitine, betaine, creatinine, imidazolepropionic acid, and trimethylamine. The detailed methods were described elsewhere39. In brief, plasma samples were thawed on ice, and ice-cold methanol and internal standards were added to 20 µl of each sample. Subsequently, the supernatant was used for analysis. UPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed, with ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μM) and UPLC HILIC analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μM) used for chromatographic separation for the Q300 and TMAO kits, respectively. The raw data files generated by UPLC-MS/MS were processed to perform peak integration, calibration, and quantitation for each metabolite using the TMBQ software (version 1.0, Metabo-Profile, Shanghai, China). The absolute plasma concentration of each metabolite was obtained by comparing the sample with an unknown metabolite concentration to a set of standard samples with known metabolite concentrations. We removed those metabolites detected in less than 80% of samples, leaving 221, 218, and 213 metabolites in the MetaSalt, GenSalt, and cohort samples, respectively. We further used K-nearest neighbor algorithm to impute missing values with five nearest neighbors. When performing discovery and replication analysis in the MetaSalt and GenSalt samples, we applied rank-based inverse normalization to scale the concentrations of metabolites.

Targeted isovalerylcarnitine measurement in rats

We used the following steps to measure the absolute plasma concentration of isovalerylcarnitine in rats: Plasma samples were thawed on ice, and 50 μL of each sample was homogenized with 450 μL of ice-cold extraction solution. The extraction solution consisted of methanol, acetonitrile, and deionized water in a ratio of 4:4:2 (v:v:v). After mixing, the solution was placed in a −20 °C refrigerator overnight to precipitate the protein. The mixture was then centrifuged at 18,000 × g and 4 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, pipet 300 μL of the supernatant was pipetted into a new centrifuge tube and evaporated to dryness. The precipitate was reconstituted with 100 μL of the extraction solution to reconstitute the precipitate. The clear supernatant was transferred to glass vials with microinserts for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) was used for chromatographic separation. Solvent A comprised 5% acetonitrile and 10 mM ammonium formate in water, and solvent B contained 95% acetonitrile and 10 mM ammonium formate in water. The gradient conditions were set as follows: 0–0.5 min (5% B), 0.5–5 min (5–95% B), 5–6 min (95% B), 6–6.1 min (95–5% B), and 6.1–8 min (5% B). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min with a 2 μL injection volume, and the column temperature was set at 40 °C. The multiple reaction monitoring mode was employed with the following transition: isovalerylcarnitine at m/z 249.1 → 85.1. Prior to each analysis, the instrument was calibrated using a series of isovalerylcarnitine standards with known concentrations. The standard curve was then utilized to quantify isovalerylcarnitine in the samples.

Statistical analysis

We used the MetaSalt study to discover key biomarkers. First, we estimated the intervention effects of dietary salt on BP, 24-h UNa, and 24-h UK, and examined the differences in gut-microbial diversity among study phases and SSBP groups. Gut-microbial α-diversity and β-diversity were assessed based on the Simpson index and species-level Bray–Curtis distance, respectively. We also calculated the median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity for each individual against other individuals in each stage, which could represent inter-individual compositional variability40. LMMs with the random intercepts of individuals and families were applied to test the differences in BP, 24-h UNa, 24-h UK, α-diversity, and median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, and permutational ANOVA (999 permutations) was used to examine the disparity in β-diversity. Second, to assess whether HSD influences gut microbiota and metabolites, we compared the differences in biomarkers between the low-salt and high-salt interventions using LMMs with the random intercepts of individuals and families. Those with a Bonferroni significance (p < 9.42 × 10−5 = 0.05/531 for species, p < 2.63 × 10−4 = 0.05/190 KEGG pathways, p < 3.76 × 10−4 = 0.05/133 for CAZy families, and p < 2.26 × 10−4 = 0.05/221 for metabolites) were defined as salt-related biomarkers. KEGG pathways over-representation analysis was used for salt-related metabolites to identify enriched pathways using ConsensusPathDB41. “MetOrigin”14, a tool that could discriminate the origins of metabolites, was used to annotate the potential sources of salt-related metabolites based on known databases. Third, to examine whether the changes in salt-related biomarkers were parallel to the changes in BP, we assessed the associations of ordinal SSBP degree with the changes in salt-related biomarkers from the low-salt to the high-salt intervention (ΔbiomarkerH-L) using LMMs with the random intercepts of families, adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), field centers, hypertension, total cholesterol, and smoking. Those significantly associated with SSBP (p < 0.05) were defined as SSBP-related biomarkers. We also included SSBP as a categorical variable in models with the SR group as the reference, and obtained the adjusted average changes (least-squares means) of these SSBP-related biomarkers in each SSBP group. Furthermore, the associations of ΔmetabolitesH-L with ΔspeciesH-L and ΔfunctionH-L were assessed using LMMs, and co-occurrence networks were visualized based on significant associations (p < 0.05) using “Cytoscape” software (version 3.8.2). Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our results. (1) For the identification of salt-related biomarkers, we included the levels of BMI, MAP, total cholesterol, and glucose in the low-salt and high-salt interventions as covariates to examine the impacts of cardiometabolic risk factors on our findings. (2) For the identification of SSBP-related biomarkers, we further adjusted for dietary score, work-related physical activity, alcohol drinking, education, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. (3) We excluded patients with dyslipidemia to examine the influence of individuals with cardiometabolic conditions on the results. (4) We replaced MAP with SBP or DBP to evaluate how robust the key findings were to different BP indices. We used the changes of SBP (thresholds of 15 and 10 mmHg) or DBP (thresholds of 8 and 3 mmHg) to determine participants’ SSBP groups, and replicated the SSBP-related biomarkers identified when using MAP to classify participants. (5) We also further adjusted for each nutrient in the low-salt and high-salt interventions to test whether the results were robust to other nutritional components beyond salt.

We then replicated the salt-related and SSBP-related metabolites in GenSalt participants using LMMs, with the main covariates (the variables used for matching were not included) similar to those used in the MetaSalt study. Those metabolites with p < 0.05 were considered as being replicated.

Moreover, we verified the findings of an SSBP-related metabolite (isovalerylcarnitine) among rats. The differences in BP, metabolites, endothelial function, and histological assessment among rats in different groups were examined using repeated ANOVA, t-test, or Wilcoxon rank sum test.

We further evaluated the associations of isovalerylcarnitine with prevalent hypertension, BP status progression, and incident hypertension using logistic regression models or Cox proportional hazards models among the prospective cohort, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, work-related physical activity, area, region, smoking status, current drinking, education, dietary score, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and carnitine.

All analyses are two-sided, and p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance unless otherwise stated. All analyses were conducted using R software (R version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
