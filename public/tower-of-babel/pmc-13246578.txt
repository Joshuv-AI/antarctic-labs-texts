Coronal hamate body fractures with dorsal fourth and fifth carpometacarpal joint instability: fracture patterns, injury mechanisms, surgical strategies, and outcomes
Authors: Seung Hoo Lee, Hyung-Jin Chung, Soo Min Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCoronal hamate body fractures are rare injuries, and studies regarding their morphology and management are limited. This study analyzed fracture patterns, injury mechanisms, associated injuries, surgical strategy, and clinical outcomes in patients with coronal hamate body fractures involving more than one-third of the articular surface and concomitant carpometacarpal (CMC) joint instability.MethodsFifty-eight male patients were retrospectively reviewed. Fractures were classified as dorsal oblique or coronal splitting types based on computed tomography findings. Surgical treatment involved open reduction and multiple screw fixation of the hamate using a K-wire-guided technique, with supplementary Kirschner wire stabilization for associated metacarpal base fractures or CMC joint instability. Radiographic and functional outcomes were evaluated after a minimum follow-up of 6 months.ResultsDorsal oblique fractures (63.8%) occurred more frequently than coronal splitting fractures (36.2%) and were most commonly associated with punching (OR, 4.9; p = 0.01), whereas falls from height were more common in coronal splitting types. Concomitant fractures occurred in 82.8% of cases, most frequently involving the fourth metacarpal base. Among 35 patients with a minimum 6-month follow-up, 34 achieved successful healing. In the 33 patients with functional outcome data, the mean DASH score was 3.0, and mean grip strength was 94.1% of the contralateral side. One patient treated 45 days post-injury showed recurrent subluxation and poorer outcome. No differences in functional outcomes were observed between fracture types.ConclusionsCoronal hamate body fractures with CMC instability frequently presented as dorsal oblique patterns following punching injuries and were commonly associated with fourth metacarpal base fractures. Open reduction with multiple small-diameter screw fixation through K-wire tracts, combined with supplementary K-wire stabilization, was feasible and provided favorable clinical outcomes. Favorable outcomes were generally observed when anatomical reduction was achieved within 3 weeks after injury, and fracture morphology was not associated with significant differences in clinical outcomes.Level of evidenceIV.Keywords: Carpometacarpal fracture-dislocation, Coronal hamate fracture, Hamate body fracture, Hamate fracture, Hamatometacarpal fracture-dislocation

Introduction

Hamate fractures are uncommon injuries, accounting for < 5% of all carpal fractures [1]. Fractures of the hook of hamate have been considered more common [1]; however, a recent epidemiologic study suggests that hamate body fractures may occur more frequently than previously reported and are often associated with instability of the fourth and fifth carpometacarpal (CMC) joints [2]. Non-displaced and stable coronal hamate body fractures can be successfully treated non-operatively [3, 4]. However, because these fractures involve the articular surface and are often associated with displacement or CMC joint instability, surgical intervention is frequently required [5, 6]. Delayed or inadequate anatomical reduction may result in poor functional outcomes, sometimes necessitating arthrodesis [3, 4, 7]. Accordingly, coronal hamate body fractures should be regarded not simply as isolated fractures, but as complex injuries involving the articular surface and joint stability.

Despite their clinical relevance, the existing literature on coronal hamate body fractures remains limited. Most reports consist of case reports or small case series [6, 8], and a recent review identified only 123 cases including six cohort studies and 33 case reports, highlighting the limited clinical evidence available for this injury [4]. A recent review of carpal fractures also noted that because of the limited available data, general treatment recommendations remain difficult to establish for non-scaphoid carpal fractures, including hamate fractures [9]. These injuries are only briefly discussed in standard orthopaedic fracture textbooks, and detailed descriptions of surgical techniques and their clinical outcomes are scarce [1].

Several classification systems and treatment methods have been proposed; however, they have also largely been described as part of fourth and fifth CMC fracture-dislocations rather than focusing on coronal hamate body fractures [5, 10, 11]. Accordingly, further investigation is required to better characterize the relationships among fracture morphology, injury mechanisms, and associated injuries, as well as surgical strategies and their clinical outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze fracture morphology, injury mechanisms, and associated injuries in patients with coronal hamate body fractures accompanied by fourth and fifth CMC joint instability. We hypothesized that fracture morphology may be associated with specific injury mechanisms and patterns of concomitant injuries. In addition, we evaluated whether fracture morphology is associated with clinical outcomes and described a stepwise surgical approach and assessed the corresponding radiographic and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This multicenter retrospective study included patients treated at two university hospitals for coronal fractures of the hamate body with concomitant fourth and fifth CMC joint instability between January 2010 and January 2023. The institutional review boards of both participating institutions approved the study protocol and waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design of the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [12].

The inclusion criteria were (1) radiologically confirmed coronal fractures of the hamate body involving more than one-third of the articular surface, and (2) dorsal instability of the fourth and/or fifth CMC joints, defined as subluxation or dislocation on plain radiographs or three-dimensional computed tomography (3D CT).

Patients with hook-of-hamate fractures, open fractures, severe soft-tissue injuries, or major concomitant injuries of the ipsilateral upper extremity (such as distal radius fracture or perilunate dislocation) were excluded.

Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, hand dominance, mechanism of injury, interval from injury to surgery, and presence of associated fractures, were collected from electronic medical records and imaging studies.

Radiologic evaluation

At presentation, all patients underwent standard hand radiography (anteroposterior, oblique, and lateral views) (Fig. 1A–C) and 3D CT. Fracture morphology was classified according to the Hirano classification (dorsal oblique and coronal splitting types) based on CT findings. A dorsal oblique fracture was defined as one in which the fracture line exited dorsally from the hamate body without extending into the hamate–triquetral joint (Fig. 1D). A coronal splitting fracture was defined as a coronal fracture pattern in which the proximal fracture line extended toward the hamate–triquetral joint (Fig. 2A).

The relative size of the involved dorsal hamate articular fragment was measured on axial CT images at a level approximately 3 mm proximal to the hamate–fourth metacarpal articular surface. It was calculated as the percentage of the dorsal fractured fragment relative to the total articular width at that level using the following formula: (fractured fragment length / [fractured fragment length + remnant articular fragment length]) × 100 (Fig. 2B).

Associated osseous injuries involving the adjacent carpal bones or metacarpal bases were assessed using CT. Based on their distribution, the associated fractures were categorized as: (1) isolated fourth metacarpal base fractures, (2) fourth metacarpal base fractures with additional adjacent fractures, (3) associated fractures without fourth metacarpal base involvement, or (4) no associated fractures.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by three surgeons under general or regional anesthesia. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a longitudinal dorsal incision was made centered over the fourth and fifth CMC joints. The dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve was identified and protected. After irrigation and hematoma evacuation, gentle traction of the ring and little fingers allowed visualization of the joint and fracture site.

The intra-articular fracture of the hamate was reduced under direct vision with gentle traction. Reduction was first directed toward restoration of the hamate articular surface against the bases of the fourth and fifth metacarpals, with meticulous reconstruction in cases of a depressed or comminuted articular surface, followed by reduction of the dorsal cortical fragment. Provisional fixation was achieved using multiple 1.1 mm Kirschner wires (K-wires), which were advanced toward the palmar cortex without penetrating the far cortex to avoid injury to the ulnar neurovascular structures (Fig. 3A–C). Fracture reduction and wire placement were confirmed by direct visualization and intraoperative fluoroscopy, ensuring adequate wire length and avoidance of the articular space. If the wire was deemed short, or articular penetration was suspected, its position was adjusted or replaced. In cases with metaphyseal or subarticular bone defects after reduction, the defects were supplemented with demineralized bone matrix.

After provisional fixation, the screw length was determined using an identical K-wire as a depth gauge. Without additional drilling, the selected wires were exchanged for 1.4-mm (Arix, Jeil) or 1.5-mm (Synthes) cortical screws. Because of the limited size of the hamate, the wires were placed with sufficient spacing to avoid screw head crowding; if interference occurred, the wire with optimal positioning was selected for screw fixation. Each screw was tightened until it was firmly seated against the dorsal cortex, without penetrating the far cortex. When concomitant capitate fractures required fixation, the incision was extended proximally or distally to expose the capitate, and screw fixation was performed using the same technique.

Following fracture fixation, the stability of the fourth and fifth CMC joints and any associated metacarpal base fractures were assessed manually under direct visualization and fluoroscopic guidance. If the CMC joints and associated metacarpal base fractures were stable without residual subluxation under manual stress testing, no additional fixation was performed. Supplementary percutaneous K-wire fixation was performed in cases of persistent instability. When the third metacarpal base was intact, transverse pinning was performed across the third, fourth, and fifth metacarpal bases to stabilize the CMC joints (Fig. 3D, E). If the third metacarpal base was fractured or unsuitable for pin passage, stabilization was achieved by pinning the fourth and fifth metacarpal bases to the capitate and/or hamate. During pin insertion, longitudinal traction was maintained to facilitate reduction, and rotational alignment of the involved digits was carefully monitored.

Postoperatively, a short-arm splint was applied for 2 weeks, followed by a removable short-arm brace for an additional 4 weeks. Active finger motion was initiated on postoperative day 2. K-wires were typically removed 6 weeks postoperatively. After removal of the pins and brace, progressive wrist and finger range-of-motion exercises were initiated. Light activities of daily living were permitted at 8 weeks, and unrestricted hand use was allowed after 3 months, provided that no complications were observed.

Radiologic and functional outcome assessment

Radiological and clinical outcomes were assessed in patients with a minimum follow-up period of 6 months. Patients who were followed up for < 6 months were excluded from functional outcome analysis.

Satisfactory healing was defined as radiological evidence of bony union of fractures and maintenance of stable CMC joint alignment confirmed at a minimum of 1 month after brace and/or K-wire removal.

Functional outcomes were evaluated at a minimum of 6 months postoperatively. Upper extremity function was assessed using the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) Questionnaire [13]. Pain was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS) during daily activities. Grip strength was measured bilaterally using a Jamar dynamometer according to the guidelines of the American Society of Hand Therapists and expressed as a percentage of the contralateral uninjured side [14]. Wrist range of motion (ROM), including flexion, extension, radial deviation, and ulnar deviation, was measured using a goniometer and expressed as a percentage of the contralateral side. Finger motion was assessed based on the ability to achieve full grip and full extension (Fig. 3J, K). Postoperative complications, such as pin-site infection, implant-related complications, and rotational deformities, were recorded.

Patients were encouraged to return for annual follow-up for radiologic surveillance after functional outcome assessment. Radiological outcomes were evaluated at the final follow-up, including recurrence of CMC joint subluxation or dislocation and the presence of osteoarthritic changes (Fig. 3F–I). Osteoarthritis was graded radiographically using a modified Kellgren–Lawrence classification system based on standard radiographic findings: grade 0, no osteoarthritis; grade 1, small osteophytes and/or mild joint space narrowing, slight sclerosis (mild osteoarthritis); grade 2, moderate osteophytes and/or moderate joint space narrowing, sclerosis, and erosion (moderate osteoarthritis); and grade 3, large osteophytes and severe joint space narrowing, sclerosis, and erosion (severe osteoarthritis) [15].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation with ranges, and categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test due to the non-normal distribution of the data. The association between the fracture morphology (dorsal oblique vs. coronal splitting) and injury mechanism was analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Because punching injuries and falls from a height accounted for the majority of injury mechanisms, these two mechanisms were further compared. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify the strength of the association between injury mechanism and fracture morphology. Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the patterns of associated fractures, surgical treatment methods, and clinical outcomes. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics and injury characteristics

A total of 58 male patients with coronal hamate body fractures associated with fourth and fifth CMC joint instability were evaluated. The mean age was 33.1 ± 10.1 years, with dominant hand involvement in 87.9%. The primary injury mechanisms were punching injuries and falls from height (84.5%). According to the Hirano classification, dorsal oblique fractures (63.8%) occurred more frequently than coronal splitting fractures (36.2%). The mean relative size of the involved dorsal hamate articular fragment was significantly greater in coronal splitting fractures (51.5 ± 7.5%; range, 40.2–64.7%) than in dorsal oblique fractures (36.6 ± 4.2%; range, 33.3–46.9%) (p < 0.001). Surgical management and fixation strategy

Three patients underwent primary arthrodesis after presenting > 3 months post-injury. Ten patients were lost to follow-up after evaluation and counseling regarding surgical treatment. Consequently, operative fixation was performed in 45 patients (Fig. 4).

The mean time to treatment was 8.6 ± 7.8 days (range, 2–45 days), and 41 patients (91.1%) underwent surgery within 3 weeks. Six patients (13.3%) underwent screw fixation without additional K-wire fixation (five with hamate screw fixation alone and one with combined hamate and capitate screw fixation), as the CMC joints were stable without residual subluxation on intraoperative manual stress testing. The remaining 39 patients underwent additional K-wire fixation for stabilization of the metacarpal base fractures and/or CMC joints due to persistent instability after screw fixation (Figs. 3A–E and 5A–D).

Clinical and radiologic outcomes

Ten patients followed up for < 6 months were excluded from the functional outcome analysis. Consequently, 35 patients with a minimum 6-month follow-up were included. The mean follow-up duration was 20.5 months (range, 6–98 months).

Satisfactory healing occurred in 34 patients. One patient treated 45 days post-injury due to delayed diagnosis showed residual subluxation of the fourth and fifth CMC joints (Fig. 5).

At final follow-up, 32 patients had congruent CMC joints without radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis. Mild osteoarthritic changes were observed in two patients, while one showed moderate changes with residual CMC joint subluxation, indicating joint space narrowing with erosion (Fig. 5E, F).

Functional outcomes were assessed between 6 and 12 months postoperatively. Of 35 follow-up patients, data were unavailable for two; therefore, evaluation was performed in 33 patients. The mean grip strength was 94.1 ± 9.4% (range, 66.7–112.0%) of the contralateral side, and the mean DASH score was 3.0 ± 3.7 (range, 0–13.9). The mean VAS score was 0.9 ± 0.9 (range, 0–3), and wrist ROM was 97.9% ± 3.6% (range, 85.6–100%) compared with the contralateral side. Finger motion was preserved with full flexion and extension achieved in all cases. Comparative analysis of functional outcomes between the dorsal oblique (n = 21) and coronal splitting (n = 12) types showed no significant differences. The mean DASH scores were 2.6 ± 3.9 and 3.7 ± 3.6 (p = 0.242), grip strength was 93.6 ± 10.2% and 95.1 ± 8.0% (p = 0.897), VAS scores were 0.76 ± 1.00 and 1.17 ± 0.72 (p = 0.131), and wrist ROM was 97.8 ± 3.9% and 98.0 ± 3.2% (p = 0.956), respectively. The patient with residual CMC joint subluxation demonstrated the poorest functional outcome, with a DASH score of 13.9, grip strength of 66.7%, wrist ROM of 85.6%, and a VAS score of 3.

One patient developed mild rotational deformity of the small finger toward the thumb side and reported a subjective difference compared with the contralateral hand; however, no functional impairment was noted, and no additional intervention was required.

No surgical site infections occurred. No implant loosening, breakage, or migration was observed. Routine screw removal was not performed in asymptomatic patients; however, screw removal was performed in one patient at their request due to implant-related aversion, despite no symptoms.

Discussion

This study evaluated coronal fractures of the hamate body with fourth and fifth CMC joint instability and analyzed their epidemiologic characteristics, injury mechanisms, fracture morphology, associated injuries, as well as clinical and radiographic outcomes. We demonstrated stable fixation through anatomic reduction and screw fixation using multiple small screws inserted through pre-established K-wire tracts, with K-wire stabilization of the fourth and fifth CMC joints for residual instability.

Milch [16] categorized hamate fractures into hook and body fractures based on plain radiographs. Cain et al. [10] proposed a classification system for hamatometacarpal fracture-dislocations, in which hamate involvement was subdivided into dorsal, dorsal comminution, and coronal splitting fractures. Hirano et al. [17] simplified coronal fractures into dorsal oblique and coronal splitting patterns. Recent classification systems have incorporated CT-based parameters such as articular involvement and metacarpal base fractures [5, 11]; however, the increasing number of subtypes limits practical applicability in routine clinical practice. Accordingly, in this study, coronal fractures of the hamate were classified using the two morphologic patterns described by Hirano et al. [17]. Prior descriptions of these patterns were largely illustrative; however, we applied explicit radiologic criteria based on the fracture line orientation relative to the hamate–triquetral joint on CT for consistent classification.

Consistent with previous studies [18, 19], these injuries predominantly affect young male patients from axial loading mechanisms, such as punching injuries or falls. Dorsal oblique fractures occurred more frequently than coronal splitting fractures, especially following punching injuries. This difference may be due to punching mechanics, in which axial loading occurs with the hand in slight flexion rather than in a purely neutral alignment [20], resulting in preferential force transmission toward the dorsal part of the hamate. In contrast, falls involve variable force directions, explaining the similar frequencies of both fracture patterns in these cases. No significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed between fracture types, suggesting that fracture morphology may not substantially influence functional recovery.

Here, the most common associated injury was a fourth metacarpal base fracture, identified in more than two-thirds of patients with concomitant fractures. This finding is consistent with previous reports, in which fourth metacarpal base fractures were observed in 40–67% of hamatometacarpal fracture–dislocations [5, 11, 19]. Kim and Shin [5] highlighted that fourth metacarpal base fractures, particularly intra-articular fractures, affect injury pathomechanics, as dorsal subluxation of the fourth and fifth CMC joints frequently persists after closed reduction. This instability stems from the functional linkage between the fourth and fifth metacarpal bases, as ligamentous attachments of the fifth metacarpal originate from the fourth metacarpal base and may be influenced by the extensor carpi ulnaris tendon traction [5, 21, 22]. The high prevalence of fourth metacarpal base fractures supports the proposed pathomechanical mechanism in coronal hamate body fractures with CMC joint instability.

Surgical techniques for treating these injuries include closed reduction with immobilization; percutaneous K-wire fixation; and open reduction with internal fixation using K-wires, screws, and plates [3, 5, 23, 24]. Satisfactory reduction may be achieved with closed methods [18]; however, accurate assessment of the hamate articular congruity and CMC joint stability based solely on fluoroscopic imaging can be challenging, and conversion to an open procedure has been reported in the literature [19]. Therefore, we prefer an open approach for direct visualization of the hamate articular surface and assessment of CMC joint stability.

The fixation strategy has several advantages. Supplementary K-wires can be removed in an outpatient setting, and small unicortical screws rarely cause implant-related symptoms, thereby minimizing the need for secondary implant removal. Additionally, the screw trajectories were guided using thin (1.1-mm) K-wires without using a drill bit. This approach is well-suited for hamate body fractures, in which the fracture fragments are often small and fragile. Conventional drilling may generate drill-related vibrations that contribute to reduction loss and increase the risk of fragment comminution or compromised fixation [25], particularly when re-drilling is required. In contrast, K-wire-guided screw insertion allows the screw trajectory to be defined and fluoroscopically confirmed before placement, facilitating adjustments when necessary. Furthermore, provisional fixation with multiple K-wires permits stepwise exchange of screws while maintaining fracture reduction, thereby minimizing reduction loss during definitive fixation. In the present study, stable screw fixation was achieved without intraoperative hamate fragmentation or technical complications related to screw advancement (Fig. 6).

The clinical outcomes were favorable and comparable to those reported previously (Table 5) [3–7, 11, 17, 25]. This may be attributed to the predominance of young, active patients, frequent involvement of the dominant hand, and preservation of the radial digits, which facilitate early functional use and active rehabilitation. Most patients underwent surgical treatment within 3 weeks of injury, and even among the three patients treated slightly beyond this period, favorable outcomes were achieved. These findings suggest that satisfactory clinical results can be expected when anatomical reduction is achieved within 3 weeks after injury. In contrast, one patient who underwent surgery 45 days after the injury experienced recurrent CMC joint subluxation and inferior functional outcomes. Definitive conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case; however, this observation suggests the potential challenges associated with delayed presentation. When open reduction is considered beyond 6 weeks after injury, the possibility of persistent instability and suboptimal outcomes should be carefully considered, and a thorough preoperative discussion with the patient may be warranted.

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective study design may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Second, all patients were treated using open reduction and internal fixation, precluding direct comparison with alternative treatment strategies and limiting the ability to evaluate relative treatment efficacy. Third, the relationship between surgical timing and clinical outcomes should be interpreted cautiously, as delayed presentation beyond 6 weeks occurred in only one patient, preventing definitive conclusions. Fourth, postoperative radiological assessment was primarily based on plain radiographs, which may have limited the detection of subtle joint incongruity or early degenerative changes. Finally, although this represents a large cohort for this uncommon injury, functional outcome analysis was limited by loss to follow-up in a subset of patients, and the relatively short follow-up duration may not be sufficient to assess the development of posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Future studies may benefit from longer-term clinical outcome assessment and advanced imaging follow-up to better characterize joint congruity, posttraumatic degenerative changes, and their clinical relevance, as suggested by a recent long-term study of another uncommon non-scaphoid carpal fracture, capitate fracture [26].

In conclusion, coronal hamate body fractures with fourth and fifth CMC joint instability showed an association between fracture morphology and injury mechanism: dorsal oblique–type fractures occurred more frequently after punching-related injuries and were commonly associated with fourth metacarpal base fractures. Open reduction with multiple small-diameter screw fixation through K-wire tracts, combined with supplementary K-wire stabilization, was feasible and provided favorable clinical outcomes. Favorable outcomes were generally observed when anatomical reduction was achieved within 3 weeks after injury, and fracture morphology was not associated with significant differences in clinical outcomes.
