Digital analysis of age-related dentoalveolar changes: a 50-year longitudinal study
Authors: Anas Basim, Julia Naoumova, Klara Leffler, Kristina Johansson, Maurits Persson, Nameer Al-Taai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the growing demand for orthodontic treatment among adults, a clear understanding of the age-related dentoalveolar changes has become increasingly important for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and long-term retention strategies. This study aims to characterize dentoalveolar changes in individuals with normal occlusion from early adolescence (13 years) to late adulthood (62 years) across four time points, using digitally derived measurements and strict inclusion and exclusion criteria.MethodologyThirty orthodontically untreated subjects (11 males, 19 females) recruited through the Public Dental Health Care system in, were examined at mean ages of 13 (T1), 16 (T2), 31 (T3), and 62 years (T4). Stone casts of the maxillary and mandibular arches were digitized using a high-resolution 3D scanner. Eleven variables were digitally assessed: overjet, overbite, intercanine width, intermolar width, arch depth, palatal height, curve of Spee, clinical crown height, arch length, space deficiency, and irregularity index. Changes across time points were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.ResultsSagittally, overjet remained stable, whereas arch depth decreased significantly in both arches. Vertically, overbite decreased significantly, while clinical crown height increased significantly in both arches. Palatal height increased significantly up to age 31, and the curve of Spee decreased significantly by age 16. Transversely, maxillary and mandibular intermolar widths, as well as maxillary intercanine width, showed no significant changes, whereas mandibular intercanine width decreased significantly. In terms of space, maxillary and mandibular arch lengths decreased significantly at all time points, accompanied by significant increases in space deficiency and in the irregularity of the mandibular incisors throughout the observation period.ConclusionsAge-related dentoalveolar changes continue from adolescence into late adulthood, supporting the concept that occlusion remains dynamic throughout life. These longitudinal findings provide clinically relevant reference data for orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning, and the development of effective long-term retention strategies.

Background

Craniofacial changes persist into late adulthood, with more pronounced alterations occurring in the soft tissues than in the skeletal structures [1–4]. Age-related changes also affect dentoalveolar morphology [5–8]. Numerous longitudinal studies have demonstrated that dental arch dimensions and occlusal relationships continue to change from adolescence through adulthood [5, 7, 8]. Specifically, reductions in inter-canine width, particularly in the mandibular arch, have been consistently reported in individuals with normal occlusion followed into adulthood [6, 8–10]. Decreases in dental arch length and depth have also been observed [5, 9, 10], often accompanied by increased anterior crowding, especially in the lower arch [5, 10, 11].

Although these changes are gradual, they are unpredictable at the individual level and may influence dentofacial aesthetics and the long-term stability of orthodontic and prosthodontic outcomes [5, 8, 9]. With the growing demand for orthodontic treatment among adults [12], a clear understanding of the age-related dentoalveolar changes has become increasingly important for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and long-term retention strategies.

Additionally, continuous tooth eruption over many years has been documented [8, 13, 14], however, its magnitude and individual variability remain poorly understood. Existing evidence suggests only a minor average eruption over time [13, 14]. Larger longitudinal cohort studies are still needed to clarify the changes associated with eruption.

While changes occurring during childhood and early adulthood are relatively well documented [5–8], research focusing on dentoalveolar changes during late adulthood remains limited [9, 10]. Methodological challenges, including small sample sizes, high dropout rates, and insufficient assessment of measurement reliability, often constrain existing longitudinal studies. Moreover, broad inclusion criteria such as the enrolment of individuals with diverse malocclusions, heavily restored or extracted teeth, or previous orthodontic treatment, introduce heterogeneity and limit clinical applicability [9, 10]. More uniform and stringent inclusion criteria are therefore essential to reduce bias and strengthen the validity of research in this area [15]. Furthermore, a standardized, common method for assessing occlusal variables would facilitate comparisons across studies.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to investigate dentoalveolar changes in individuals with normal occlusion from early adolescence (13 years) to late adulthood (62 years) across four longitudinal time points. By employing digital measurement techniques and well-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, this study aims to provide a comprehensive three-dimensional assessment of dentoalveolar changes, offering more reliable insights into their trajectory into the sixth decade of life.

Materials and methods

Materials

A 50-year longitudinal analysis of age-related dentoalveolar changes was conducted from early adolescence to late adulthood. The cohort consisted of orthodontically untreated subjects recruited through the Public Dental Health Care system in Umeå, Sweden, from 1965 to 2014. The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Board at Umeå University, Sweden (registration no. 2012–410 − 31 M). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria were Angle Class I normal occlusion, normal overjet and overbite, normal transversal relation, no more than 1 mm of space deficiency in each jaw, and a harmonious soft tissue profile.

A total of 30 subjects (11 males, 19 females) were included at baseline at a mean age of 13 years (T1). Follow-up examinations were performed at 16 years (T2), 31 years (T3), and 62 years (T4) (Fig. 1). All examinations were made at the Department of Odontology, Umeå University.

Exclusion criteria included any malocclusion, a history of previous orthodontic treatment or retention therapy, use of a snoring device, history of jaw surgery, extracted or missing teeth, or prosthodontic replacement involving teeth anterior to the permanent second molars during the observation period. By the final time point (T4), one subject had deceased, three had moved, and five had undergone extraction of a first permanent molar; therefore, these were also excluded. A total of 21 (9 males and 12 females) remained eligible for inclusion at the age of 62 (T4) (Fig. 1).

Methods

Stone cast models of the maxillary and mandibular arches were obtained for each subject at all four time points. The dental casts were digitized using a 3Shape R700 scanner (3Shape A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark), which has a reported baseline accuracy of 50 μm or less. The digital scan files were processed using DDP-Ortho® (version 2.16.0_2024, DDP Dental Software, Poland), where they were converted to a format compatible with the software for subsequent digital analysis.

Eleven variables were measured digitally, including overjet, overbite, intercanine width, intermolar width, arch depth, arch length [8] (Fig. 2), palatal height, curve of Spee, clinical crown height [9], space deficiency, and irregularity index. Palatal height was measured between the deepest point of the palate and a tangent to the maxillary occlusal surface [8, 9]. In addition, tooth width was measured to calculate the tooth size-arch length discrepancy (TSALD) as described by Bishara et al. [6]. TSALD was calculated for the incisors (ant TSALD), for the posterior teeth, premolars, and canines (post TSALD), and for the whole arch mesial to the first molars (total TSALD). A negative value for TSALD indicates a space deficiency. Irregularity of the mandibular incisors was assessed according to Little’s Irregularity Index [16]. Tooth widths and clinical crown heights were measured at each time point throughout the study. However, at T1, three subjects presented with partially erupted maxillary canines and one subject with a partially erupted mandibular canine. For these teeth, mesiodistal crown widths and clinical crown heights were acquired from the subsequent examination after eruption.

Error of method

To assess intra-observer reliability of the measurements, the digital measurements of 10 randomly selected models were repeated by the first observer (AB), on two occasions, with a 2-month interval. The inter-observer reliability of the measurements was also evaluated. A second observer (NA) repeated the digital measurements of the same 10 selected models. The reliability level was estimated using Dahlberg’s formula to quantify the method error and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals to assess agreement. Paired t-tests were additionally performed to evaluate potential systematic differences between repeated measurements.

Statistical analysis

Differences in dentoalveolar variables between time points were analyzed using paired comparisons (T1–T2, T2–T3, T3–T4, and T1–T4). Normality of the within-subject differences was assessed for each pairwise comparison by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Because several variables showed deviations from normality, paired comparisons (T1–T2, T2–T3, T3–T4, and T1–T4) were performed using the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each comparison. To reduce the likelihood of false-positive findings due to multiple testing, p-values were adjusted within each group of variables using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Adjusted p-values are reported in the tables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Results

The longitudinal follow-up showed no dropouts at the first three time points; however, nine subjects were excluded at T4 for various reasons.

The intra-observer method error ranged from 0.07 to 0.30 mm, and the inter-observer method error from 0.07 to 0.35 mm. The ICC values indicated excellent agreement, ranging from 0.94 to 0.99 for intra-observer reliability and from 0.90 to 0.97 for inter-observer reliability.

Sagittally, overjet decreased significantly from T1 to T2 and remained relatively stable thereafter until T4. Arch depth decreased significantly at all time points in both arches, with mean total reductions of 2.1 mm in the maxilla and 2.3 mm in the mandible (Fig. 3) (Table 1).

Vertically, overbite showed a progressive and statistically significant reduction from T1 to T2 and from T3 to T4 (Fig. 3) (Table 2). Palatal height increased significantly by 1.5 mm from T1 to T3 and subsequently showed a non-significant decrease toward T4 (Fig. 3). The curve of Spee showed a borderline significant decrease between T1 and T2 and remained relatively stable between consecutive time points (T2–T3, and T3–T4) (Table 2). No statistically significant differences in crown height were observed between the right and left sides for any tooth; therefore, the mean crown height of both sides was used for the maxillary and mandibular arches. Clinical crown height of all teeth increased in both arches throughout the study period. In both maxilla and mandible, canines, premolars, and molars showed a greater increase in clinical crown height than central and lateral incisors. The largest increase was observed in the maxillary canines, whereas the smallest increase was observed in the mandibular central incisors, with mean total increases of 2.6 mm and 0.4 mm, respectively (Table 2).

Transversally, maxillary and mandibular intermolar widths showed a small, non-significant increase over the study period; however, mandibular intermolar width increased significantly between T2 and T3. Maxillary intercanine width remained relatively stable throughout the observation period. In contrast, mandibular intercanine width showed a continuous, statistically significant reduction, with a mean total decrease of 1.3 mm (Fig. 3) (Table 1).

Regarding the space analysis, the posterior arch length reported in the tables represents the mean of the right and left sides (measured from the mesial of the first molar to the distal surface of the lateral incisor). The anterior and posterior arch lengths in both arches decreased significantly across the observation period, with mean total reductions of 3.7 mm in the maxilla and 4.3 mm in the mandible. The posterior TSALD values reported in the tables represent the sum of the right and left sides in the premolar and canine regions. Maxillary TSALD showed a progressive increase in space deficiency throughout the observation period. Anterior TSALD showed no significant change from T1 to T2, followed by a reduction of 0.2 mm from T2 to T3 (p = 0.035) and a further decrease of 0.8 mm from T3 to T4 (p < 0.001), resulting in a total reduction of 1.1 mm from T1 to T4 (p < 0.001). Posterior TSALD decreased by 0.4 mm from T1 to T2 (p = 0.017), 0.3 mm from T2 to T3 (p = 0.003), and 0.2 mm from T3 to T4 (p = 0.045), resulting in a total reduction of 0.9 mm (p = 0.010). Consequently, maxillary total TSALD decreased significantly at all intervals, with reductions of 0.5 mm from T1 to T2 (p = 0.028), 0.7 mm from T2 to T3 (p < 0.001), and 0.9 mm from T3 to T4 (p = 0.001), amounting to a total reduction of 1.9 mm (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Mandibular TSALD showed a more pronounced and earlier increase in space deficiency. Anterior TSALD decreased by 0.3 mm from T1 to T2 (p = 0.030), 0.6 mm from T2 to T3 (p < 0.001), and 0.9 mm from T3 to T4 (p < 0.001), resulting in a total reduction of 2.0 mm (p < 0.001). Posterior TSALD decreased by 0.4 mm from T1 to T2 (p = 0.023), 0.7 mm from T2 to T3 (p < 0.001), and 0.3 mm from T3 to T4 (p = 0.007), resulting in a total reduction of 1.1 mm (p < 0.001). Accordingly, mandibular total TSALD decreased significantly across all intervals, with reductions of 0.7 mm from T1 to T2 (p = 0.012), 1.3 mm from T2 to T3 (p < 0.001), and 1.2 mm from T3 to T4 (p < 0.001), amounting to a total reduction of 3.1 mm (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Mandibular incisor irregularity also increased significantly during the observation period, with a mean total increase of 2.9 mm.

Discussion

Longitudinal studies provide the most reliable framework for evaluating age-related dentoalveolar changes; however, such datasets are rare because of the limited availability of untreated individuals with normal occlusion. Unlike previous studies that have examined selected dentoalveolar variables, the present study provides a comprehensive three-dimensional longitudinal assessment integrating sagittal, vertical, transverse, and space-related dentoalveolar changes within a single untreated cohort followed from adolescence to late adulthood over five decades, demonstrating that dentoalveolar adaptation continues throughout life.

Sagittally, overjet decreased slightly from early to late adolescence, consistent with Thilander [8] and likely reflects growth-related adaptations. Thereafter, overjet remained stable in adulthood, in agreement with previous studies [6, 9, 10, 17]. This suggests that overjet reaches a functional equilibrium in adulthood, maintained by a balance between occlusal forces, lip pressure, and incisor inclination [18]. Clinically, overjet appears to be a relatively stable parameter in adulthood, although its early changes highlight the importance of growth assessment in treatment planning. Greater changes were observed in arch depth, which decreased gradually in both arches from early adolescence to late adulthood, consistent with previous longitudinal studies and supporting the concept of continuous mesial migration of the dentition [6, 9–11, 19]. The persistence of this reduction into late adulthood confirms that physiological mesial drift is a lifelong process that directly contributes to reduced arch space and increased crowding (Fig. 5).

Vertically, the overbite reduced progressively during the observation period, consistent with earlier reports [8, 9, 17, 20]. This decrease may be explained by continuous tooth eruption [14, 21], mandibular growth [22], and by age-related incisal wear [23, 24]. However, other studies have reported either stable [3, 10] or increased overbite [6, 7, 19]. These differences may reflect differences in age ranges and sample characteristics. Overall, overbite appears to be influenced by multiple interacting factors, including craniofacial growth, tooth eruption, occlusal function, parafunctional habits, and age-related incisal wear [10, 22–24]. Palatal height increased significantly from early adolescence to early adulthood, consistent with Thilander’s findings [8]. Palatal height would be expected to decrease with age due to occlusal wear [23] and possible palatal remodeling with bone deposition orally [25]. However, the continued increase of palatal height observed during adolescence and early adulthood has been interpreted as evidence of slow, continuous tooth eruption and ongoing vertical dentoalveolar adaptation [8]. Although Massaro et al. observed increases only between ages 13 and 17, this discrepancy may be due to the lack of follow-up between 17 and 60 years of age and to the fact that 55% of their subjects had more than three teeth extracted [9]. Between 31 and 62 years of age, palatal height decreased slightly, but not significantly, suggesting increasing influence of age-related occlusal wear rather than tooth eruption and vertical alveolar development [3, 13, 14, 23]. The curve of Spee decreased from early to late adolescence, possibly associated with tooth eruption during pubertal growth [21, 22], and remained relatively stable thereafter, due to slight, continuous tooth eruption and mandibular incisor wear [23, 24] consistent with previous studies [9, 26]. Clinically, this suggests that leveling during orthodontic treatment might be necessary, as spontaneous improvement in adulthood is unlikely.

Clinical crown height increased in both arches throughout the observation period, particularly in canines, premolars, and molars, in agreement with previous studies [9, 13, 14]. These changes likely reflect a combination of continuous eruption and gingival recession [13, 14]. Continuous eruption occurs at variable rates depending on age, whether anterior or posterior teeth are involved, and, potentially, on the presence and intensity of occlusal contacts [13, 14, 27]. Depending on tooth type, it has been suggested that continuous eruption appears to play a greater role in increasing crown height of anterior teeth, whereas gingival recession contributes more to increasing crown height in premolars and molars [13]. Gingival recession has been proposed to represent not only an inflammatory process but also a physiologic component of aging [14]. Incisal wear may explain the smaller increase in clinical crown height observed in mandibular central incisors [9, 23, 24].

Transversally, intermolar width increased slightly in both arches during the observation period, with a greater increase in the maxilla, consistent with earlier reports [10, 17]. In contrast, other investigations have reported no change in intermolar width [6, 9], which may be explained by differences in sample characteristics, follow-up duration, and muscle function [9, 28]. Maxillary intercanine width decreased slightly from late adolescence to early adulthood and then remained stable, consistent with previous findings [8, 9], although other studies have reported a continuous reduction [7, 10, 19]. On the other hand, mandibular intercanine width decreased significantly from early adolescence to late adulthood, supporting the widely reported age-related reduction in mandibular intercanine width [6–10, 19, 20, 26].

More marked changes were observed in arch depth and arch length, which, together with intercanine width, decreased progressively throughout the observation period, resulting in progressive maxillary and mandibular space deficiency, as reflected by worsening TSALD and Little’s Irregularity Index (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Notably, these changes continued into late adulthood and have been reported in both treated and untreated individuals, making it difficult to distinguish physiological aging from orthodontic relapse [9, 29, 30]. Therefore, it is important to inform orthodontic patients that age-related lower incisor crowding may result from a combination of physiologic dentoalveolar changes and treatment relapse [9, 29, 30]. These findings support the concept that occlusion is dynamic rather than static [8] and likely reflect a gradual anterior migration of the dentition, as observed in both the present study and previous longitudinal investigations [3, 7–10, 19, 31]. The progressive reductions in arch length and arch depth are consistent with continuous mesial drift of the permanent dentition, particularly the first permanent molars, possibly driven by the anterior components of occlusal force [8, 32] arising from the mesial inclination of tooth crowns [33], which provides a plausible biological explanation for these changes. This gradual migration decreases the available arch parameters and contributes to the development of space deficiency and anterior crowding [8, 10, 34]. The concomitant reduction in intercanine width may also be related, at least in part, to this mesial migration of the dentition [10]. In addition, reductions in arch length may reflect age-related changes in the equilibrium of intrinsic forces exerted by the tongue, lips, and cheeks [35], further influencing dental arch form and stability. Mandibular incisor crowding has previously been attributed to age-related lingual inclination of the mandibular incisors [36, 37]. However, a longitudinal analysis of craniofacial changes in the same sample revealed that the apparent retroclination of the mandibular incisors from early to late adulthood is mainly related to the posterior mandibular rotation rather than true retroclinations of the lower incisors [1]. Furthermore, age-related mandibular incisor crowding appears to have only a weak association with the presence of third molars [38, 39]. Instead, the observed increases in TSLAD and mandibular incisor irregularity are more likely the result of cumulative dentoalveolar adaptations involving mesial drift, reductions in arch dimensions, and intrinsic occlusal and physiologic forces acting within the dental arches. Consequently, the long-term prognosis for anterior alignment may be unfavorable regardless of treatment and may deteriorate over time in the absence of retention [7, 20, 40].

Ultimately, occlusal relationships result from continuous interactions among facial growth, dental development, and function [8]. Influenced by both genetic and environmental factors, these processes remain active throughout life and contribute to the ongoing adaptation of the dentoalveolar complex [8]. Accordingly, differences among longitudinal studies may partly reflect considerable individual variability in dentoalveolar aging, including variability in tooth eruption, occlusal wear, crowding development, muscle function, and craniofacial growth patterns.

Strengths and limitations

In contrast to previous studies focusing on selected variables, the present study provides a comprehensive three-dimensional assessment of dentoalveolar changes in a longitudinal sample followed from 13 to 62 years, restricted to subjects with normal occlusion, and no extractions mesial to the second molars, and no dropouts during the first three follow-ups. Therefore, the findings may be applicable to comparable cohorts of untreated Caucasian individuals with normal occlusion. Although limiting the sample to subjects with normal occlusion reduces the generalizability of the results to broader populations, it also reduces heterogeneity. This approach reduced confounding factors associated with different malocclusions and orthodontic treatments. Accordingly, the study provides longitudinal reference data for physiologic dentoalveolar aging under relatively normal occlusal conditions.

In addition, all models were analyzed by a single investigator to minimize methodological variability. Digital dental models were used because they provide reliable linear measurements [41, 42], and the intra-examiner analysis demonstrated excellent agreement. Furthermore, mandibular incisor crowding has most frequently been assessed using Little’s Irregularity Index [16], although dental crowding may also be described as a space deficiency within the dental arch [43]. In the present study, both the Irregularity Index and tooth size-arch length discrepancy (TSALD) were used because they represent different aspects of crowding. TSALD quantifies the space required to align the dentition, but does not describe the degree of irregular alignment [31].

A limitation of the study is that nine subjects were excluded at the final follow-up, although the overall attrition rate remained relatively low. To assess potential attrition bias, baseline characteristics of the 21 completers were compared with those of the 9 excluded subjects using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. No statistically significant differences were found in any of the measured variables (p-values ranged from 0.10 to 0.97), suggesting that attrition was unlikely to have introduced substantial baseline selection bias. For variables that did not reach statistical significance at the final follow-up, the possibility of a type II error due to the reduced sample size cannot be ruled out.

In addition, sex-specific age-related dentoalveolar changes could not be evaluated because of the relatively small and unequal numbers of male and female participants (11 males and 19 females at ages 13, 16, and 31 years; 9 males and 12 females at age 62 years), which limited the statistical power for sex-stratified analyses. Furthermore, although previous studies have reported inconsistent sex-related differences in dental arch dimensions and occlusal changes [8, 44, 45], little is known about the influence of sex on dentoalveolar changes extending into late adulthood. Therefore, the predominance of female subjects may have influenced some of the observed longitudinal changes.

Because direct periodontal measurements were not included, the relative contributions of continuous eruption and gingival recession to the observed increases in clinical crown height remain speculative. Nevertheless, the cohort represented subjects with relatively stable periodontal conditions, as individuals with tooth loss mesial to the second molars due to periodontitis, caries, or defective restorations were excluded. Moreover, future investigations, including detailed periodontal examinations, may provide additional insight into the biological mechanisms underlying age-related changes in clinical crown height.

Clinical implications

The findings indicate that continuous dentoalveolar changes occur from early adolescence to late adulthood in individuals with normal occlusion and reflect physiologic processes rather than the effects of extraction or malocclusion. These changes include reductions in mandibular intercanine width, arch depth, and arch length, as well as increasing mandibular incisor crowding. Recognition of these long-term patterns is important for orthodontic treatment planning and for the design of retention protocols to minimize post-retention changes. Age-related increases in clinical crown height should be interpreted cautiously during long-term follow-up, as they may reflect physiologic eruption rather than periodontal attachment loss. Clinicians should also be cautious in recommending surgical crown lengthening, as clinical crown height increases naturally with age. In addition, when planning implant placement in the anterior maxilla, clinicians should consider the potential effects of continued tooth eruption on long-term aesthetic stability. Nevertheless, the risk of age-related infraocclusion of posterior implants after the age of 30 may be limited, given the relative stability of palatal height and the fact that increases in posterior clinical crown height are primarily attributable to gingival recession rather than continued tooth eruption.

Conclusions

In orthodontically untreated individuals with normal occlusion, dentoalveolar changes occur slowly and continuously from early adolescence into late adulthood, supporting the concept that occlusion is dynamic rather than static throughout life. Progressive reductions in arch depth, arch length, mandibular intercanine width, and available arch space contribute to increasing mandibular incisor crowding, whereas changes in clinical crown height reflect ongoing dentoalveolar adaptation. Recognition of these long-term physiologic changes provides valuable longitudinal reference data for orthodontic diagnosis, treatment planning, implant therapy, and the development of effective long-term retention protocols.
