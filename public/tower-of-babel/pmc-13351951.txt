Early clinical outcomes of simultaneous biportal endoscopic decompression for tandem spinal stenosis: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Tran Vu Hoang Duong, Pham Anh Tuan, Phan Quang Son, Huynh Van Vu, Le Tan Bao, Luc Dinh Phuong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTandem spinal stenosis (TSS), defined as simultaneous spinal canal narrowing at noncontiguous levels, most commonly involves the cervical and thoracolumbar regions and presents substantial challenges in surgical planning. Although staged decompression has traditionally been favored to reduce perioperative risk, simultaneous decompression may shorten overall treatment duration and accelerate neurological recovery. However, evidence regarding the safety and effectiveness of simultaneous minimally invasive decompression remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and early clinical outcomes of simultaneous biportal endoscopic spine surgery (BESS) for TSS.MethodsThis retrospective single-center case series included 11 patients with TSS who underwent simultaneous BESS between January 2024 and June 2025. All patients presented with cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) accompanied by clinically significant lower-extremity neurological symptoms. Nine patients had concurrent lumbar radiculopathy, while two had additional thoracic spinal cord compression. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for leg pain. Perioperative parameters and complications were recorded. Neurological recovery was assessed using the Hirabayashi recovery rate.ResultsSimultaneous multiregional BESS decompression was successfully completed in all patients without conversion to open surgery. The mean follow-up duration was 10.2±2.7 months. Mean operative time and estimated blood loss were 307.7±48.5 minutes and 286.4±86.9 mL, respectively. No major perioperative complications, including dural tears, neurological deterioration, or surgical site infection, were observed. Significant clinical improvement was achieved, with mJOA scores improving from 9.9±1.1 preoperatively to 14.2±0.9 at final follow-up and VAS leg pain scores decreasing from 7.3±0.7 to 2.4±0.5 (both P<0.001). The mean Hirabayashi recovery rate was 52.9%±7.5%.ConclusionsSimultaneous BESS decompression appears to be a feasible minimally invasive approach for TSS, with encouraging short-term neurological improvement, highlighting its potential for broader application in complex spinal pathology.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This preliminary study suggests that simultaneous biportal endoscopic spine surgery (BESS) decompression for tandem spinal stenosis (TSS) is feasible, with all procedures successfully completed without major perioperative complications.

• Encouraging neurological improvement was observed, with mean modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association scores improving from 9.9 to 14.2 and Visual Analog Scale leg pain decreasing from 7.3 to 2.4 (P<0.001); the mean Hirabayashi recovery rate was 52.9%.

What is known and what is new?

• TSS is a complex multiregional pathology for which both staged and simultaneous decompression strategies have been reported. Although neurological outcomes are generally comparable, simultaneous surgery using traditional open techniques may increase perioperative risk due to longer operative time and greater tissue trauma.

• This study provides early clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of endoscopic simultaneous decompression using BESS across multiple spinal regions in TSS.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that BESS may be a feasible minimally invasive option for managing selected TSS cases, highlighting its potential applicability in more complex multiregional spinal pathologies.

• Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and comparative designs are needed to better define its role, safety, and long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Tandem spinal stenosis (TSS) is defined as concurrent narrowing of the spinal canal in two or more noncontiguous regions, most commonly involving the cervical and lumbar segments (1-3). The condition was first noted by Brain et al. (4) and formally termed by Teng and Papatheodorou (5), who described simultaneous cervical and lumbar stenosis. TSS encompasses several subtypes, among which the cervico-lumbar form is the most prevalent due to the greater mobility and mechanical stress of these regions, whereas cervico-thoracic involvement occurs less frequently (1,2,6-8).

Clinically, TSS presents with complex manifestations combining features of cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) and lower-extremity neurogenic claudication, which often obscure the primary pathology and complicate diagnosis and surgical planning (3,9,10). Although decompressive surgery remains the mainstay of treatment, the optimal management strategy, whether simultaneous or staged decompression, remains controversial (2,8,9,11). Simultaneous surgery enables complete neural decompression in a single setting but poses challenges related to prolonged operative and anesthesia time, greater invasiveness, and higher perioperative risk (1,3,7,12).

In recent years, minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) has gained widespread use as surgical instruments and imaging systems continue to advance. Within these developments, biportal endoscopic spine surgery (BESS) has emerged as a prominent technique, using two separate portals that allow simultaneous endoscopic visualization and instrument manipulation (13-15). This design offers an expanded, high-magnification field of view and greater technical flexibility during decompression (16). BESS has been increasingly applied across the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine for various degenerative conditions, providing advantages such as reduced soft-tissue disruption, shorter operative time, and faster recovery while maintaining effective neural decompression (16-22). However, TSS presents unique challenges due to the coexistence of multilevel stenosis in different regions of the spine, often requiring comprehensive decompression strategies. Although the technical strengths of BESS may theoretically offer benefits for managing such complex, multiregional pathology, clinical evidence supporting its application in TSS remains limited (23).

This study reports a single-center case series of patients with TSS who underwent simultaneous BESS decompression, including cervical laminotomy with bilateral decompression (C-LBD) and thoracic or lumbar decompression with or without fusion. The objective is to describe the surgical technique, present preliminary clinical outcomes, and assess the feasibility of BESS as an option for simultaneous management of TSS. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0059/rc).

Methods

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent simultaneous BESS decompression, including C-LBD and thoracic or lumbar laminotomy decompression with or without interbody fusion for the treatment of TSS between January 2024 and June 2025. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Xuyen A General Hospital (IRB No. NCKH-40/2025/QD-BVXA). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria and patient selection

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) consecutive patients diagnosed with TSS during the study period who were eligible for general anesthesia; (II) patients with a confirmed diagnosis of TSS presenting with CSM and lower-extremity neurological symptoms caused by thoracic or lumbar canal compression; (III) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence of cervical stenosis accompanied by thoracic or lumbar stenosis consistent with clinical findings; and (IV) those who underwent simultaneous BESS decompression.

Exclusion criteria included patients with spinal infection, trauma, neoplasm, or a history of prior spinal surgery.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by the same neurosurgeon, who is right-handed and has 3 years of experience in BESS. The surgical instruments included the standard UBE set (BONSS Medical Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China); a high-speed surgical drill (NSK Primado2, Nakanishi Inc., Kanuma, Japan) equipped with 2-mm and 4-mm diamond burrs; an ArthroCare bipolar and radiofrequency (RF) system; and standard microsurgical spine instruments for fine dissection and neural decompression.

Operating room setup and instrumentation

All patients underwent general endotracheal anesthesia and were positioned prone on a radiolucent spine table with a Wilson frame (Figure 1). The head was securely fixed in a neutral position using adhesive tape, while both shoulders were firmly pulled caudally to provide a clear lateral fluoroscopic view of the lower cervical vertebrae (Figure 1A). Two operative regions were prepared and draped separately using dual waterproof endoscopic drape systems to prevent fluid leakage and cross-contamination (Figure 1B).

The table height was carefully adjusted to allow convenient anteroposterior (AP) and lateral fluoroscopic imaging of both the cervical and thoracolumbar regions (Figure 1C). The primary surgeon stood on the patient’s left side, with the endoscopic monitor placed contralaterally (Figure 1D). The positions of the assistant surgeon, anesthesiologist, scrub nurse, and the arrangement of the irrigation system, RF coagulation generator, and instrument table were organized as illustrated in Figure 1E.

C-LBD

To facilitate simultaneous decompression, C-LBD was performed first in most cases (Figure 1C), followed by thoracic or lumbar decompression (Figure 1D). Under fluoroscopic guidance, the scope and working portals were created through two small paramedian incisions approximately 2 cm from the midline to achieve proper triangulation (Figure 2, A1,A2). After serial dilation and sheath insertion, the operative field was established under continuous saline irrigation (Figure 2, A3).

The initial landmark “V-point” formed by the junction of the cranial and caudal lamina and the medial border of the facet joint, was identified endoscopically. This point served as the lateral boundary of bone resection. Using a 4-mm diamond burr, the cranial lamina was drilled until the free margin of the ligamentum flavum (LF) was exposed, followed by thinning of the cranial edge of the caudal lamina to fully expose both ligamentous attachments.

To accomplish contralateral decompression, drilling was continued medially along the base of the spinous process, allowing partial thinning and floating of its tip. This maneuver enabled visualization across the midline and access to the contralateral lamina and facet junction. The same drilling technique was applied contralaterally until the dorsal surface of the LF was completely visualized.

The LF was then detached from its cranial and caudal attachments using a fine hook and removed en bloc with pituitary forceps (Figure 2, A4) to expose the dural sac and confirm adequate neural decompression (Figure 2, A5). Hemostasis was meticulously achieved using RF coagulation and hemostatic matrix (Floseal, Baxter International Inc., USA), followed by thorough saline irrigation to confirm dural pulsation and ensure complete decompression. A drainage catheter was then inserted before closing the portals in a standard layered fashion (Figure 2, A6).

Thoracic or lumbar laminotomy with bilateral decompression (Th-LBD or L-LBD)

After completion of the cervical procedure, thoracic or lumbar decompression was performed in the same stage. The patient remained in the prone position, and the target level was localized under fluoroscopic guidance. On a true AP view, two transverse skin incisions were made along the medial pedicle line: the working portal at the disc level and the scope portal approximately 2.5–3.0 cm cranial to it (Figure 2, B1,B2). The spino-laminar junction served as the reference point for initial entry and creation of the operative corridor.

Decompression was performed using a five-step “Z-technique” (18). First, ipsilateral laminotomy was initiated by drilling the lower edge of the cranial lamina until the superior attachment of the LF was visualized. Second, contralateral decompression was achieved via an “over-the-top” approach, drilling the contralateral lamina in an undercutting manner while keeping the deep LF layer intact as a protective barrier. Third, the cranial edge of the caudal lamina was drilled to release the lower LF attachment. Fourth, the inferior contralateral LF insertions were detached with a Kerrison punch or osteotome. Finally, the remaining LF was removed en bloc to complete decompression of the central canal and bilateral recesses (Figure 2, B3).

When segmental instability was identified, biportal endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion (BE-LIF) was performed using a multilayer bone grafting technique (20) (Figure 2, B4-B6). This was subsequently followed by percutaneous pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) to achieve stable segmental fixation.

Measurement of outcomes

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score to evaluate functional disability in all patients. In cases of cervico-lumbar type TSS, lower-extremity symptoms were additionally evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for leg pain. All clinical scores were recorded at three time points: preoperatively, at hospital discharge, and at the last follow-up. Operative parameters including operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and perioperative complications were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate clinical outcomes. Continuous variables, including mJOA and VAS scores, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test due to the small sample size. Preoperative and postoperative clinical variables were compared using paired-sample t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, depending on data distribution. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 11 patients TSS underwent simultaneous BESS decompression. The mean follow-up duration was 10.2±2.7 months (range, 6–15 months). The mean patient age was 61.0±9.3 years (range, 49–75 years), and 8 of the 11 patients (72.7%) were male. Comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, or prior cerebral infarction were present in 54.5%. Osteoporosis was identified in 36.3% of cases. All 11 patients presented with CSM. Among them, 2 patients exhibited additional thoracic cord compression manifested by segmental sensory disturbances, while the remaining 9 patients demonstrated lower extremity radiculopathy and neurogenic claudication attributable to lumbar canal stenosis (Table 1).

Perioperative finding

Regarding the biportal endoscopic procedures performed, all patients underwent C-LBD. Additional Th-LBD was performed in 2 patients, L-LBD in 2 patients, and BE-LIF in 8 patients. The mean operative time was 307.7±48.5 minutes (range, 225–400 minutes), and the mean estimated blood loss was 286.4±86.9 mL (range, 150–400 mL). No major intraoperative or postoperative complications such as dural tears, neurological deterioration, surgical site infection, or implant-related issues were observed. All patients had their drains removed and were mobilized on postoperative day 2, in accordance with the hospital’s standard protocol. Patients were discharged after satisfactory wound healing, with a mean hospital stay of 8.7±2.0 days (range, 5–12 days).

Clinical outcomes

Preoperatively, all 11 patients exhibited moderate to severe CSM with a mean mJOA score of 9.9±1.1. Nine patients were classified as having the cervico-lumbar type of TSS and presented with lower-extremity radicular symptoms, reflected by a mean preoperative VAS for leg pain of 7.3±0.7. Clinical scores improved at discharge and continued to improve at the last follow-up (Table 2). Because both mJOA and VAS for leg pain met normality criteria on the Shapiro-Wilk test (P>0.05), paired t-tests were used for pre-post comparisons. The analyses demonstrated a statistically significant postoperative improvement (P<0.001).

Notably, 8 of the 11 patients achieved a recovery rate of ≥50%, and none fell into the poor recovery category (<25%), reflecting the consistent neurological improvement obtained after simultaneous BESS decompression.

Discussion

TSS is defined as concomitant narrowing of the spinal canal in at least two noncontiguous regions of the cervical, thoracic, or lumbar spine, typically presenting with mixed upper and lower motor neural deficits and neurogenic claudication (1,2,24). The condition was first described by Brain in a patient with both cervical and lumbar stenosis, and later Teng and Papatheodorou formally introduced the term after reporting seven similar cases (4,5). Dagi subsequently refined the concept as a clinical triad of intermittent claudication, gait disturbance, and combined neurological symptoms, while Koizumi later expanded it to include thoracic involvement (10,25). TSS is generally classified into four subtypes according to the affected regions: cervico-thoracic, thoraco-lumbar, cervico-thoraco-lumbar, and cervico-lumbar (1,2,7). The reported prevalence of TSS ranges from 7.6% to 60% among patients with spinal stenosis, with slightly higher rates in women (1,2,6,24). Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, primarily involving degenerative spondylosis and heterotopic ossification (1,2,7,10). Age-related degeneration typically causes cervico-lumbar TSS, whereas ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) and ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) are more common in cervico-thoracic, thoraco-lumbar, and cervico-thoraco-lumbar types. These ossified lesions frequently coexist, up to 65% of cervical OPLL cases also have OLF, and over 50% of thoracic OPLL cases involve the cervical spine (1,2,6,8-10,24). Developmental spinal stenosis is another important risk factor. A Torg-Pavlov ratio below 0.75–0.78 predicts a higher likelihood of TSS, with cervical and lumbar stenosis coexisting in approximately 15–17% of patients (24,26).

The clinical manifestations of TSS are highly variable and often difficult to diagnose due to overlapping symptoms arising from multiple levels of neural compression (1,2,24). In our series, all 11 patients presented with signs of CSM, while lower-extremity radiculopathy associated with concurrent lumbar compression was observed in nine cases. In two patients, thoracic cord compression was identified based on band-like sensory disturbances across the trunk that could not be explained by cervical pathology alone; additional thoracic MRI confirmed thoracic stenosis. These findings highlight the diagnostic challenges of TSS, in which upper and lower motor neuron signs may coexist or obscure one another, leading to delayed or missed diagnosis (10,24). Previous studies have also shown that hyperreflexia resulting from cervical or thoracic lesions can be masked by cauda equina involvement, and thoracic stenosis is frequently overlooked because of its subtle and nonspecific presentation (6,10,11,24). Therefore, TSS should always be considered in patients presenting with cervical or lumbar stenosis, particularly when symptoms cannot be fully explained by a single lesion (6,8,9). Radiological evaluation is essential for confirming the diagnosis of TSS. Among the available imaging modalities, MRI has become the gold standard, as it allows simultaneous assessment of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions (2,24,26). Diagnosis is typically based on regional stenosis criteria, with the Lee grading system widely used to evaluate the degree of compression on T2-weighted axial and sagittal images (26). Whole-spine MRI is strongly recommended for patients whose symptoms are inconsistent with a single-level lesion, as early identification of multilevel stenosis is critical to prevent underdiagnosis (1,2,24,26).

The surgical management of TSS remains controversial, particularly regarding whether decompression should be performed simultaneously or in staged procedures (1,6,7,11,12,24). The primary objective is to achieve adequate neural decompression while minimizing invasiveness, operative time, and perioperative risks. Staged surgery has traditionally been preferred because it carries a lower anesthetic risk, involves shorter operative time for each stage, and allows evaluation of symptom improvement after the initial decompression (6,7,9). However, determining the optimal sequence whether cervical or lumbar first can be challenging. Several studies have shown that cervical-first decompression may relieve or even resolve lumbar symptoms by improving corticospinal conduction, thus reducing the need for subsequent lumbar surgery (7,8). Conversely, lumbar-first surgery may be appropriate for patients with predominant neurogenic claudication or radiculopathy without myelopathy but often requires secondary cervical decompression due to the progression of cervical pathology (7,11,24). From another perspective, simultaneous decompression offers several advantages, including single anesthesia exposure, reduced total operative time and hospitalization, and faster overall recovery (10,11,24,27). Early studies demonstrated that simultaneous decompression can be performed safely with satisfactory outcomes (10,27). More recent series have reported comparable neurological improvement between simultaneous and staged procedures, without significant differences in blood loss or complication rates, while demonstrating a shorter overall hospital stay (1,11). These benefits are most pronounced in younger patients with fewer comorbidities. However, despite these advantages, recent evidence suggests that while both strategies achieve similar neurological outcomes, simultaneous decompression using traditional open techniques may increase perioperative complications due to longer anesthesia duration and greater tissue invasiveness (7,28). Therefore, patient selection, anesthetic tolerance, and surgical invasiveness remain key considerations.

The development of minimally invasive surgery (MIS) techniques, particularly BESS, has introduced several important advantages (16,19,21,22,29). Beyond providing a clear, magnified operative field that enhances surgical safety and reduces the risk of complications, BESS minimizes paraspinal soft-tissue trauma, leading to secondary benefits, particularly faster recovery (14,17,20,22,29). In the context of TSS, our experience is illustrated by three representative patterns of combined pathology, including single-level C-LBD combined with single-level Th-LBD (Case 1, Figure 3), multilevel C-LBD combined with single-level BE-LIF (Case 2, Figure 4), and multilevel C-LBD combined with L-LBD and BE-LIF (Case 3, Figure 5). Favorable clinical improvement was observed across cases and was further supported by imaging findings, with preliminary whole-spine MRI at 6 months demonstrating effective decompression at all involved levels (Figures 3E,4F,5F). BESS also demonstrated a favorable safety profile in this series, with no major intraoperative or postoperative complications observed, an important finding given concerns related to prolonged anesthesia exposure. However, operative time and blood loss were higher in TSS cases, particularly in patients who required interbody fusion, as observed in 8 of 11 cases (Table 1). In addition, the need for meticulous two-region setup, including draping and fluoroscopic positioning, may have contributed to increased overall operative time. Despite this, all patients were mobilized early on postoperative day 2, regardless of the regions decompressed, suggesting that early functional recovery can still be achieved following BESS even in complex TSS cases. The relatively longer hospital stay reflects our institutional protocol, in which patients are discharged only after complete wound healing and suture removal.

As BESS is performed under continuous water irrigation, intraoperative pressure control is critical to minimize the risk of increased epidural and intracranial pressure. Maintaining a balanced inflow and outflow is essential to stabilize the surgical chamber and prevent fluid extravasation into the surrounding soft tissues (30). During decompression of the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar spine, the LF is preserved until completion of bony work, serving as a protective barrier that reduces direct irrigation impact on the dura (17,18,30,31). The LF is then removed en bloc, followed by assessment of adequate decompression, hemostasis, and drainage placement, thereby minimizing the duration of direct irrigation exposure to the dura. A similar staged LF opening strategy is applied during BE-LIF procedures (20), in which only a limited window of the LF is initially opened to access the disc space for graft placement. Complete LF removal is performed after cage insertion to achieve final decompression, further reducing pressure-related risks.

Our findings are consistent with the existing literature supporting the effectiveness and safety of BESS when applied to isolated cervical, thoracic, or lumbar lesions (17,31-35). Kim (17) reported favorable outcomes in 26 patients undergoing C-LBD using the spinous-process floating technique, demonstrating safety and efficacy even in multilevel decompression. Improvements were evident not only clinically but also radiographically, with preservation of cervical alignment with the minimal change in Cobb angle, sustained expansion of the dural sac cross-sectional area (CSA) and minimal reduction in the CSA of the posterior cervical muscles at 1 year postoperative. For thoracic stenosis associated with TSS most commonly caused by OLF (6,8,25,26), current evidence supports the use of biportal endoscopic en bloc flavectomy, which we also adopted (31,32). This technique provides effective decompression while reducing repeated instrument contact with the dura and spinal cord that are already compressed, thereby minimizing the risk of iatrogenic cord injury (32,35). Regarding lumbar stenosis, a recent meta-analysis showed that biportal L-LBD significantly reduced operative time and resulted in superior postoperative dural sac expansion compared with uniportal endoscopy, while clinical outcomes, blood loss, hospital stay, complication rates, and ipsilateral facet joint resection angles remained comparable (14). The use of the “Z” technique to en bloc LF removal has likewise been reported to improve safety and shorten operative time compared with piecemeal flavectomy (18,33). In our series, 8 patients required BE-LIF due to lumbar stenosis with segmental instability (Table 1). We applied a multilayer bone grafting strategy using hydroxyapatite (HA), an en bloc inferior articular process (IAP) autograft, and a single cage placed sequentially (20). This technique has been shown to be safe and to achieve reliable fusion even in low-grade spondylolisthesis. Recent meta-analysis evidence also indicates that BE-LIF offers shorter operative times compared with uniportal endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion (34).

Although the initial results of this study are encouraging, demonstrating the potential applicability of BESS in the management of TSS, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size and relatively short follow-up duration, which limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the lack of quantitative radiological parameters, such as canal diameter or dural sac CSA, may limit objective evaluation of the adequacy of decompression. Larger studies with longer follow-up and comprehensive radiological measurements are required to better assess long-term neurological recovery, confirm the adequacy of decompression, and identify potential late complications, such as progression of stenosis at adjacent segments or implant-related issues. Second, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon with extensive experience in individual BESS techniques, which may introduce bias, particularly regarding operative time and the low rate of complications. The study also did not formally evaluate the learning curve for each component technique, making it unclear how much experience is required to safely apply BESS in the more complex setting of TSS without increasing operative time. Although BESS is generally considered to have a relatively short learning curve due to its biportal design, which facilitates instrument handling and visualization, decompression in the cervical and thoracic spine requires a more advanced level of technical proficiency and a thorough understanding of endoscopic anatomy. In our experience, a stepwise progression is essential, with sufficient experience in lumbar BESS serving as a prerequisite before extending the technique to more complex regions. In this study, TSS cases were performed after approximately 5 years of BESS practice, with a cumulative experience of over 1,500 cases. Third, TSS involves multiregional pathology, and the optimal management strategy, simultaneous vs. staged decompression remains controversial. The absence of a comparison group, such as staged BESS procedures or other MIS/open approaches, further limits the ability to clearly delineate the advantages of simultaneous BESS decompression.

Conclusions

This study suggests that simultaneous biportal endoscopic decompression may be a feasible option for TSS, with encouraging short-term neurological improvement, highlighting the potential for expanding the indications of BESS. However, this study has several limitations, including a small sample size, procedural heterogeneity, and the lack of comparative data. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are warranted to better clarify the safety and effectiveness of this approach.
