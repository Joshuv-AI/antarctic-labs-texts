Clinical Significance of Optical Coherence Tomography–Guided Percutaneous Coronary Intervention for In‐Stent Restenosis Within Drug‐Eluting Stents: Impact on Patient Outcomes
Authors: Yan Han, Xiaohang Yuan, Wei Wang, Ningyuan Wang, Yingqian Zhang, Jing Jing, Yundai Chen, Lei Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe evidence for optical coherence tomography (OCT)–guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in improving the prognosis of individuals with in‐stent restenosis (ISR) is lacking.Methods and ResultsThis retrospective study enrolled 588 consecutive individuals with drug‐eluting stent ISR undergoing PCI from March 2010 to March 2022. Two hundred seven (35.2%) underwent OCT guidance, and 381 (64.8%) underwent angiography guidance. Clinical outcomes were analyzed using survival curves. The primary clinical endpoint was 2‐year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), a composite of all‐cause death, myocardial infarction, and target‐vessel revascularization. Compared with angiography guidance, OCT guidance demonstrated a higher frequency of drug‐coated balloon use and adjunctive therapeutic modalities, including predilation, postdilation, nonslip element balloons, and noncompliant balloons (P<0.05). Following PCI, the OCT‐guided group achieved a significantly larger minimum lumen diameter (2.36 versus 2.15 mm, P<0.001) and a lower percentage diameter stenosis (17% versus 20%, P<0.001) than the angiography‐guided group. Survival analysis revealed significantly lower 2‐year MACEs in the OCT‐guided group compared with the angiography‐guided group (7% versus 15%, P=0.007), validated in the propensity matching analysis (7% versus 15%, P=0.001). Multiple sensitivity analyses showed that OCT‐guided PCI treatment was an independent protective factor for 2‐year MACEs in individuals with drug‐eluting stent ISR.ConclusionsCompared with angiography guidance, OCT guidance is associated with a lower 2‐year MACE risk among individuals with drug‐eluting stent ISR. Therefore, OCT should be actively considered for guiding PCI treatment in individuals with drug‐eluting stent ISR.RegistrationUrl: clinicaltrials.gov. Identifier: NCT03809754.

Drug‐eluting stent (DES) technology has significantly enhanced the effectiveness and safety of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). 1 However, the occurrence rate of in‐stent restenosis (ISR) and the need for repeat revascularization persist at an annual rate of 1% to 2%, affecting ≈5% to 10% of individuals undergoing PCI. 2 Previous studies demonstrated that ISR‐related major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) pose a higher risk than PCI for de novo coronary lesions. 3 , 4 Considering the substantial population affected by coronary heart disease and the increasing complexity of lesions requiring PCI, personalized treatment for ISR remains a significant challenge.

DES‐ISR has a complex mechanism, with numerous risk factors and poor prognosis. Advanced intracoronary imaging techniques are helpful in identifying the mechanisms and guiding the intervention for target lesions. 5 The 2018 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio‐Thoracic Surgery guidelines and the expert consensus of the European Association of PCI recommend the use of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) as important tools for guiding the diagnosis and treatment of ISR. 6 , 7 Currently, limited research has explored whether optimizing treatment with different intracoronary imaging techniques can provide tangible clinical benefits for individuals with DES‐ISR. Comparative studies between IVUS‐guided and angiography‐guided PCI treatment for individuals with DES‐ISR showed that IVUS guidance can significantly improve clinical outcomes. 8 , 9 However, the evidence for OCT‐guided PCI treatment in improving the prognosis of individuals with DES‐ISR remains insufficient. In this study, we compared the clinical outcomes of OCT‐guided PCI with those of angiography‐guided PCI for DES‐ISR, with the goal of assessing clinical benefits associated with OCT guidance for individuals with ISR.

METHODS

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of the study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This retrospective, single‐center observational study included 588 consecutive individuals with DES‐ISR who underwent PCI at the Chinese PLA General Hospital from March 2010 to March 2022. ISR was defined as a ≥50% reduction in luminal diameter within 5 mm of the proximal or distal edge of the stent. 10 The exclusion criteria were (1) bare‐metal stent ISR, (2) incomplete clinical and angiographic data, and (3) poor image quality of OCT or angiographic images. The enrolled individuals were divided into 2 groups according to the guidance strategy: the OCT‐guided group (n=207) and the angiography‐guided group (n=381). This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration, and all procedures were reviewed in advance and approved by the institutional review board at the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2018‐049) without requiring explicit consent from participants.

Clinical Baseline and Angiographic Characteristics

The baseline data for this study were retrospectively collected by cardiology physician trainees using the medical record management system. The collected data included age; sex; status of certain diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney dysfunction, and chronic cardiac dysfunction; coronary artery bypass graft; smoking history; and blood‐derived values such as low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, serum creatinine, and brain natriuretic peptide. The definitions of hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney dysfunction, and chronic cardiac dysfunction are shown in Table S1. The coronary angiographic details of all individuals were analyzed by independent expert at the Imaging Core Laboratory of the PLA General Hospital. Quantitative coronary angiographic analysis was using QAngio XA software (Medis Medical Imaging Systems, Leiden, Netherlands) in a blinded manner.

PCI Procedure and OCT Examination

PCI treatment of DES‐ISR was performed according to the standard guidelines. 11 , 12 With the exception of those who had previously undergone antiplatelet therapy, all individuals received aspirin (300 mg) and a P2Y12 receptor antagonist (clopidogrel 300–600 mg, ticagrelor 180 mg) before PCI. The revascularization strategy, choice of DES, duration of dual antiplatelet therapy, and any adjunctive pharmacotherapy after PCI were based on physician preference. In the OCT‐guided PCI group, the timing of OCT use (before DES implantation, after DES implantation, or both) was also determined at the discretion of the treating physician. OCT examinations were performed using frequency‐domain OCT systems (Model M3, LightLab Imaging, Westford, MA; C7‐XR, St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN; and Ilumien Optis, Abbott Laboratories, Green Oaks, IL). 13 During PCI, the OCT catheter was passed through the ISR lesion, contrast agent was injected at a rate of 3 to 4 mL/s, and simultaneous pullback was performed. All OCT images were analyzed by 2 trained investigators who were blinded to the clinical and angiographic data using proprietary offline software (St. Jude Medical). In case of disagreement, a third independent expert made the final assessment.

Patient Follow‐Up

All enrolled individuals with DES‐ISR underwent clinical follow‐up within 2 years postoperatively (via telephone or outpatient visits). The primary clinical end points were 2‐year history of MACEs, a composition of all‐cause death, myocardial infarction (MI), and target‐vessel revascularization (TVR). The secondary end points included all‐cause death, MI, and TVR. MI was defined as the presence of ischemic symptoms or electrocardiographic evidence of ischemia, 14 along with elevated creatine kinase–myocardial band or troponin levels. Perioperative MIs were not included in the clinical events. TVR is defined as any repeat percutaneous intervention or bypass grafting occurring in any segment of the target vessel (the main coronary artery proximal or distal to the target lesion, including the side branches proximal or distal to the target lesion itself).

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous data are presented as the mean±SD, and an independent sample t test was used for intergroup comparison. Skewed continuous data are presented as the median (interquartile range), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for intergroup comparison. Count data are presented as rates (percentages), and intergroup comparison was performed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test. Survival curve analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests. Multiple sensitivity analyses including multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, inverse‐probability‐weighted, and propensity score–matched analyses, all consistently adjusting for the same set of confounding factors, were performed to adjust for baseline differences. Propensity score matching was performed on the basis of nearest‐neighbor matching with a 1:1 ratio, including variables such as age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney dysfunction, chronic cardiac dysfunction, smoking history, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, serum creatinine, brain natriuretic peptide, multiple lesions, pre‐PCI reference lumen diameter, lesion length, and the use of drug‐coated balloons (DCBs). An additional sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the 2‐year results to unmeasured confounding using the E‐value methodology of VanderWeele and Ding. 15 A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Clinical Baseline Characteristics

A total of 588 individuals with DES‐ISR (682 lesions) were included (mean age, 63±10 years; 482 men) in this retrospective study. Among them, 381 individuals underwent angiography‐guided PCI (444 lesions), whereas 207 individuals underwent OCT‐guided PCI (238 lesions). Regarding baseline clinical characteristics, mean imputation was performed in 67 (11%) patients with missing data for at least 1 of low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, creatinine, and brain natriuretic peptide. The OCT‐guided group exhibited a greater proportion of individuals with chronic kidney dysfunction than the angiography‐guided group (14% versus 6%, P<0.001). Conversely, the percentage of individuals with hypertension was lower in the OCT guidance group than in the angiography‐guided group (62% versus 71%, P=0.025), while the low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were lower in the former group (1.98 [interquartile range, 1.52–2.29] mmol/L versus 2.04 [interquartile range, 1.57–2.64] mmol/L; P=0.023). The distribution of individuals with multiple DES‐ISR lesions was similar between the 2 groups (15% versus 14%, P=0.578). In addition, there were no significant intergroup differences in the usage rates of aspirin (90% versus 94%, P=0.080), clopidogrel (51% versus 50%, P=0.939), ticagrelor (39% versus 44%, P=0.245), and statins (91% versus 94%, P=0.179) following PCI (Table 1).

Intervention and Imaging Indicators

The distribution location of DES‐ISR did not significantly differ between the OCT‐guided and angiography‐guided groups (P=0.350). However, compared with the angiography‐guided group, the OCT‐guided group exhibited greater use rates of predilation (99% versus 96%, P=0.025), postdilation (39% versus 30%, P=0.016), nonslip element balloons (7% versus 2%, P<0.001), and noncompliant balloons (54% versus 29%, P<0.001). Moreover, a greater proportion of individuals in the OCT‐guided group received DCBs for DES‐ISR than those in the angiography‐guided group (54% versus 41%, P<0.001). The OCT‐guided group also differed from the other group in terms of DCB diameter (3.00 [interquartile range, 2.75–3.00] mm versus 3.00 [interquartile range, 2.50–3.00] mm, P=0.009), length (25 [interquartile range, 17–30] mm versus 20 [interquartile range, 17–26] mm, P=0.025), maximum dilation time (60 [interquartile range, 60–70] seconds versus 60 [interquartile range, 50–60] seconds, P<0.001), and maximum dilation pressure (10 [interquartile range, 10–12] atm versus 12 [interquartile range, 10–12] atm, P<0.001). Although the proportion of individuals receiving DES reimplantation was similar between the OCT‐guided and angiography‐guided groups (40% versus 35%, P=0.200), the former had a larger DES diameter (3.00 [interquartile range, 3.00–3.50] mm versus 3.00 [interquartile range, 2.75–3.50] mm, P=0.020) and shorter length (22 [interquartile range, 16–29] mm versus 26 [interquartile range, 18–36] mm, P=0.001; Table 2).

Before PCI, the reference lumen diameter (2.72±0.45 mm versus 2.62±0.46 mm, P=0.007) was larger and the lesion length (11.04 [interquartile range, 7.91–14.92] mm versus 12.64 [interquartile range, 9.22–16.73] mm, P=0.004) was slightly smaller in the OCT‐guided group than in the angiography‐guided group. However, there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of minimum lumen diameter (0.57 [interquartile range, 0.36–0.80] mm versus 0.57 [interquartile range, 0.34–0.79] mm, P=0.300) and percentage diameter stenosis (77.20%±12.46% versus 77.83%±13.02%, P=0.539). After PCI, the reference lumen diameter (2.83±0.44 mm versus 2.70±0.42 mm, P<0.001) and minimum lumen diameter (2.36±0.41 mm versus 2.15±0.40 mm, P<0.001) were larger in the OCT‐guided group, whereas the percentage diameter stenosis (16.91% [11.54%–21.28%] versus 19.46% [14.84%–26.49%], P<0.001) was lower. Accordingly, the OCT‐guided group obtained larger luminal gains than the angiography‐guided group, with median luminal gain of 1.71 (interquartile range, 1.44–1.99) mm versus 1.58 (1.25–1.88) mm, respectively (P<0.001; Table 2 and Table S2).

The OCT‐guided group revealed that DES‐ISR associated with stent underexpansion and stent malapposition were present in 20% and 6% of the lesions. Stent undersizing and fracture were not found. Neointimal hyperplasia characterized as homogeneous, layered, and heterogeneous was observed in 23%, 20%, and 57%, respectively. Furthermore, neoatherosclerosis, lipid neointima, calcified neointima, and spotty calcifications were documented in ≈78%, 76%, 10%, and 6%, respectively (Table S3).

Clinical Outcomes

The clinical outcomes of 566 individuals (96%) who completed follow‐up were obtained, with an estimated median follow‐up time of 58 months (95% CI, 56–62). Among them, 126 (22%) experienced MACE, with 95 (26%) in the angiography‐guided group and 31 (15%) in the OCT‐guided group. Survival analysis revealed that OCT guidance was associated with a significantly lower cumulative incidence of MACE in individuals with DES‐ISR compared with angiography guidance (P=0.025). Among the individuals who completed follow‐up, 530 (90.1%) had a follow‐up duration of >2 years or experienced a MACE within 2 years. Individuals with OCT guidance exhibited a significantly lower 2‐year cumulative incidence of MACEs compared with those with angiography guidance (7% versus 15%, P=0.007). Additionally, a lower 2‐year cumulative incidence of TVR was observed in the OCT‐guided group (6% versus 12%, P=0.018). However, there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of all‐cause death (1% versus 1%, P=0.700) or MI (1% versus 4%, P=0.076; Table 3 and Figure 1).

Based on 1:1 nearest neighbor propensity score matching, the 2 groups achieved a high degree of overlap in propensity scores, with the vast majority of standardized mean differences within 10%, except for lesion length (Figures S1 and S2). The OCT‐guided and the angiography‐guided groups each enrolled 177 individuals, with no significant differences in baseline data between the 2 groups (Table 5). The cumulative incidence rates of 2‐year MACE (7% versus 15%, P=0.001) and TVR (3% versus 14%, P=0.007) in the OCT‐guided group remained significantly lower than those in the angiography‐guided group, whereas there were no significant differences in the cumulative incidence rates of 2‐year all‐cause death (1% versus 2%, P=0.200) or MI (1% versus 3%, P=0.200) between the 2 groups (Table 3 and Figure 2). Multivariable analysis after matching, including propensity scores, and lesion length (standardized mean difference >10%) as confounding factors, demonstrated that OCT guidance is still independently associated with a lower incidence of 2‐year MACEs (HR, 0.40 [95% CI, 0.20–0.79]).

DISCUSSION

According to the clinical and procedural characteristics and follow‐up data from 588 individuals with DES‐ISR at a single center, we compared the clinical benefits of angiography‐guided and OCT‐guided PCI. The results can be summarized as follows: (1) OCT‐guided PCI showed a higher use of adjunctive therapeutic modalities, larger maximum balloon diameter of DCB, and larger diameter of DES compared with angiography‐guided PCI for DES‐ISR; (2) OCT‐guided PCI resulted in a larger minimum lumen diameter and lower percentage diameter stenosis in individuals with DES‐ISR compared with angiography‐guided PCI; and (3) OCT guidance demonstrated greater benefits in terms of 2‐year MACEs and TVR for individuals with DES‐ISR compared with angiography guidance.

The guidelines in Europe and the United States recommend using OCT for intracoronary imaging to understand the mechanism of ISR (Class IIa). 6 , 7 OCT, with its high axial resolution and anatomic imaging advantages comparable to ex vivo pathology, provides more reliable evidence on the potential causes and mechanisms of ISR than angiography. 16 This is crucial for optimizing the management of individuals with DES‐ISR. 17 Using OCT to identify mechanical factors, locate and evaluate lipid plaques and calcifications, and diagnose neointimal hyperplasia, cardiologists can develop more targeted intervention strategies for DES‐ISR before and after PCI. 18 As demonstrated by our findings, OCT guidance for DES‐ISR involves using more adjunctive therapeutic modalities, such as predilation, postdilation, nonslip element balloons, and noncompliant balloons, compared with angiography guidance. Furthermore, the precise qualitative and quantitative evaluation of lesion morphology by OCT can enhance confidence in selecting appropriate DCB and DES size specifications, thereby enabling a higher degree of precision in treatment planning. Our results revealed that DES‐ISR lesions guided by OCT received larger diameter DCBs and DESs, potentially leading to more complete expansion and apposition of DCBs and DESs against the vessel wall, as well as comprehensive lesion coverage. Consequently, this may result in a larger minimum lumen diameter and lower percentage diameter stenosis of the target lesion after PCI.

Coronary angiography is a classic imaging technique used to guide PCI for coronary artery disease. However, it has inherent limitations in plaque characterization, lumen diameter measurement, and assessment of stent deployment, and suboptimal PCI can increase the risk of early and long‐term adverse events. 19 Modern intracoronary imaging techniques, including IVUS and OCT, provide reliable technical support for optimizing PCI strategies and improving treatment outcomes, as evidenced by previous randomized controlled trials and observational studies. A network meta‐analysis of follow‐up data from 17 clinical trials and 14 observational studies involving 17 882 individuals with coronary artery disease demonstrated that compared with angiography‐guided procedures, IVUS or OCT‐guided interventions were associated with a sustained significant reduction in MACEs and cardiac death (odds ratios, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.67–0.91] and 0.68 [95% CI, 0.49–0.97], respectively), with no significant differences observed between IVUS and OCT guidance in any clinical outcomes. 20 Similar results were obtained in subsequent meta‐analyses specifically comparing OCT guidance with other techniques. 21 , 22 Nevertheless, whether advanced intracoronary imaging techniques can provide additional clinical benefits for ISR, particularly for individuals with DES‐ISR, remains to be confirmed. Given the increased complexity, greater procedural difficulty, and worse prognosis associated with DES‐ISR compared with de novo lesions, 23 it is crucial to conduct a focused analysis on the potential impact of modern intracoronary imaging techniques in enhancing clinical outcomes for individuals with DES‐ISR.

Few studies have investigated the clinical benefits of IVUS‐guided PCI in individuals with DES‐ISR. 8 , 9 A subgroup analysis of the iOPEN Complex (Intravascular Ultrasound on Outcomes Following Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Complex Lesions) study, which aimed to explore the impact of IVUS on outcomes in individuals with complex coronary lesions, including 1522 individuals with ISR found that IVUS‐guided PCI for ISR significantly reduced the occurrence of 1‐year MACE, a composite event of all‐cause death, Q‐wave MI, and TVR at 1‐year follow‐up (18.0% versus 24.5%). 8 Another IVUS study that specifically focused on complex lesions, including 553 individuals with DES‐ISR, demonstrated that IVUS‐guided PCI had a lower incidence of MACE (cardiac death, MI, ischemia‐driven target lesion revascularization, and stent thrombosis) during a median follow‐up of 64 months compared with angiography‐guided PCI (18.5% versus 28.1%, P<0.001). 9 Both studies showed that IVUS‐guided interventions resulted in more frequent predilation and larger stent diameters, which may be the main reasons for the improved prognosis of individuals undergoing IVUS‐guided PCI. To the best of our knowledge, no previous comparative studies have investigated the clinical benefits of OCT‐guided PCI in individuals with DES‐ISR. This study, however, demonstrated that OCT, when guided by the improved luminal gain from aggressive lesion preparation, led to a significantly lower 2‐year MACE incidence compared with angiography (7% versus 15%, P=0.007).

To validate the independent impact of OCT on 2‐year MACEs in individuals with DES‐ISR, multivariable Cox regression analysis and propensity score matching considering potential confounding clinical risk factors was conducted. Findings consistently showed a significant association between OCT guidance and 2‐year MACEs, both before and after propensity score matching, confirming the independent protective effect of OCT guidance on the clinical prognosis of individuals with DES‐ISR. In addition to PCI guidance, multiple ISR lesions also were identified as the independent predictors of 2‐year MACEs, which have been widely discussed for their prognostic value in individuals with coronary artery disease. 24 Validation with larger sample sizes is necessary to ensure the reproducibility and generalizability of the model.

Study Limitations

First, given the nonrandomized nature of the study, there were important baseline differences between OCT and angiography guidance cohorts that may have influenced the statistical analyses. While using both multivariable Cox regression and propensity score matching to minimize the impact of potential confounders, and calculating the E‐value to evaluate the robustness of the results, it is crucial to recognize that unmeasured variables could not be entirely accounted for. Second, the enrolled individuals received specific PCI strategies that did not strictly adhere to the optimal stent implantation guidelines. The selection of PCI strategy, vascular access, stent type, and postoperative medication was based on individual physician preferences, particularly regarding the use of OCT, timing of OCT usage, and standardization of OCT guidance. Third, the treatment duration for different lesions varied because of the presence of multiple DES‐ISR lesions in some individuals. Moreover, limited by patient recall bias, accurate data on specific lesion‐related events could not be obtained during clinical follow‐up. Therefore, for individuals with multiple lesions, the lesion with the highest percentage diameter stenosis as the target lesion for survival analysis was selected, which may have impacted the accuracy of the follow‐up data. Finally, all included individuals were from a single large tertiary referral center, where the complexity of DES‐ISR lesions in the patient population might be higher, thus affecting the generalizability of the study findings. Further validation with multicenter and large‐sample follow‐up data would provide additional evidence regarding the clinical benefits of OCT‐guided PCI in individuals with DES‐ISR.

CONCLUSIONS

With the support of high‐precision OCT imaging, DES‐ISR can receive more comprehensive pretreatment measures and appropriate strategies. Compared with angiography alone, OCT guidance is independently associated with a lower incidence of 2‐year MACEs. For DES‐ISR, especially in cases of complex lesions, OCT should be actively considered to assess the underlying mechanisms and guide PCI.
