Reducing Cartilage Warp in Nasal Reconstruction: The Role of Collagen and Proteoglycans
Authors: Saba Rafieian, Cari M. Whyne, Margarete K. Akens, Wilfred W. Lam, Jeffrey A. Fialkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Costal cartilage is commonly used in nasal reconstruction due to its strength and biocompatibility; however, warping of carved struts remains a challenge. Although proteoglycans (PGs) are known to generate internal swelling pressures contributing to warping, the role of collagen fiber orientation in modulating these stresses and the spatial interplay between PGs and collagen across costal cartilage remains poorly understood. This study compared regional variations in collagen alignment and PG distribution from 2 anatomical sites: one that when transversely cut demonstrates no warping, and one that has higher tendency to warp.Methods:Human costal cartilage samples were collected from the sixth/seventh rib costal bridge (autograft) and long rib (allograft). Collagen fiber orientation was assessed using diffusion tensor imaging/tractography and polarized light microscopy. Matrix composition was evaluated via histology. Fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity maps were used to correlate structural and compositional patterns.Results:In the costal bridge and long rib cartilage, collagen fibers were predominantly aligned anterior–posterior in the mid and deep zones, with a tangential circumferential alignment near the surface. PG content was concentrated in the mid and deep zones and depleted superficially.Conclusions:These results suggest that cartilage warping arises from internal stress imbalances linked to regional variations in PGs and collagen. The elucidated microstructural geometry of struts sliced transversely from the costal bridge preserves matrix architecture and the circumferential tension band, preventing warping due to unresisted PG expansion. In contrast, disruption of the collagen fibers and unbalanced PG distribution in long cylindrical ribs leaves nonperfectly concentric carved struts, prone to deformation.

Takeaways

Question: Does collagen and proteoglycan (PG) architecture of costal cartilage explain graft warping and guide graft selection for nasal reconstruction?

Findings: Human costal cartilage from the sixth/seventh costal bridge and long rib was analyzed using diffusion tensor imaging/tractography, polarized light microscopy, and histology. Mid/deep zones showed anterior-posterior collagen with higher PG content, whereas superficial zones formed a circumferential collagen “tension band” with lower PG content. Disruption of this band and the PG symmetry in long rib struts promoted warping, whereas transversely sliced costal bridge struts preserved matrix integrity and remained stable.

Meaning: Preserving native architecture at the costal bridge reduces warping and improves graft predictability.

INTRODUCTION

Costal cartilage remains a preferred graft material in nasal reconstruction due to its favorable mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and low incidence of postoperative complications such as inflammation and extrusion.1–3 However, a persistent clinical challenge is the warping of cartilage struts following carving—an unpredictable deformation attributed to intrinsic residual stresses within the tissue.3,4

Concentric carving, oblique split,5 oppositional suturing,6 and other balancing techniques6–10 have been introduced to try to reduce the warping of costal cartilage grafts. The transverse slicing technique11 offers promising results by harvesting autologous cartilage at the intercostal junction of ribs 6 and 7, yielding multiple long (2.3–4.6 cm), thin (1.5–3 mm) nonwarping struts (Fig. 1). The sixth/seventh rib junction (costal bridge) enables the preparation of multiple struts that demonstrate no or negligible warping. In contrast, struts carved from long rib segments are prone to deformation.10,12

Transverse slicing technique. Cartilage struts are harvested from the sixth/seventh costal bridge, with a computed tomography image highlighting the harvest site (A) and the excised costal bridge shown sliced transversely ex vivo for clinical use (B).

Early pioneers in the field, including Gibson, Fry, and Robertson, demonstrated that warping of costal cartilage results from the release of intrinsic internal stresses during surgical carving or manipulation.4,10,13 Fry and Robertson13 attributed these stresses primarily to interlocked tensile forces generated by proteoglycans (PGs), which act in opposition to the swelling pressure created by the tissue’s high water content. In this early model, collagen was considered a passive framework, anchoring the PGs without directly contributing to stress alignment. Enzymatic degradation of glycosaminoglycans effectively eliminated warping, whereas collagen degradation only altered structural integrity. Gibson and Davis10 proposed that differences in cellular shape and microstructural organization between the peripheral and deeper zones of cartilage contribute to variations in tensile stress upon slicing. More recent studies4 have recognized that the collagen fiber network plays an active role, with its regionally aligned architecture establishing a prestressed configuration that contributes to asymmetric force distribution and warping during graft preparation. Internal stresses, originating from both the collagen matrix and the hydrated PG network, contribute to the cartilage’s tendency to deform when structural continuity is disturbed.

A recently described technique to create reliably nonwarping strut grafts for nasal reconstruction harvests transversely sliced grafts from the sixth/seventh costochondral costal bridge at the intercostal junction.11 This unique donor site offers insight into the effect of microstructural organization on the tendency of rib cartilage to warp. Comparing regional variations in collagen fiber orientation and PG distribution between long ribs (with a tendency to warp) and the transverse sections of the triangular costal bridge (nonwarping) may provide insight into how internal forces are distributed and released during graft preparation.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and polarized light microscopy (PLM) can be used to assess collagen fiber orientation and anisotropy; PG gradients can be evaluated through histological analysis. To this end, this study investigated and compared the regional collagen fiber architecture and PG distribution within costal cartilage from long ribs and the costal bridge using complementary imaging techniques.

By correlating structural features with the known patterns of warping, we aimed to elucidate how compositional heterogeneity contributes to residual stress imbalance, offering new insights for improving graft predictability in reconstructive surgery. Importantly, these findings extend clinical knowledge by explaining why cartilage warps beyond the cut plane alone. It is hypothesized that the interplay between collagen and PG distributions is what impacts costal cartilage warp, and that there is a balance of forces generated by this microstructure that is unique to the transverse slicing technique that prevents graft warping. Recognizing these material behaviors will provide surgeons with mechanistic insight that can guide graft selection and carving strategies to minimize deformation.

METHODS

Sample Preparation

Human costal cartilage specimens left over following nasal reconstruction at the Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre were used for the study (Research Ethic Board no. 5466). Autograft tissue was acquired from the costal cartilage caudal protrusion of rib 6, known as the costal bridge (Fig. 1). The perichondrium was removed during the nasal reconstruction procedure. Excess long rib cartilage allograft (without perichondrium, unknown rib number) was also used in the study. Following collection, specimens were placed in sterile saline and transported to the laboratory within 12 hours, where they were stored at 4°C for up to 3 days. A total of 5 samples were included in this ex vivo study: 3 costal bridge samples (from 1 man aged 22 y and 2 women aged 25 and 26 y) and 2 long rib samples (from male donors aged 19 and 23 y). The scanned sample volumes ranged from 1.5 × 2.0 × 3.0 to 2.8 × 2.5 × 3.0 cm3 (height × depth × width) (Fig. 1).

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI)

Costal bridge and long rib samples were imaged using an optimized protocol to visualize collagen fiber tracks. To optimize the DTI protocol, various parameters were systematically tested, including gadoteridol (ProHance; Bracco Diagnostics Inc., Princeton, NJ) concentrations, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radiofrequency (RF) coils, diffusion directions, and spatial resolutions. The best performance was obtained using a 7T vertical-bore MRI scanner (Ascend 300WB; Bruker BioSpin, Billerica, MA) with Paravision 6.0.1 software. Cartilage anisotropy was characterized by calculating the relative diffusion coefficients of water molecules in each voxel of the MRI scan.14–16 A 30-mm surface RF coil was selected for a high signal-to-noise ratio, which decreases with distance from the coil. A custom cylindrical sample holder with a conical cavity maintained the samples within the region of the highest coil sensitivity throughout the imaging process. Samples were immersed for 24 hours before imaging in saline containing 0.5% ProHance, reducing the T1 to approximately 110 ms.17 Imaging parameters included 150-μm isotropic resolution, a field of view of 0.5 × 0.5 × 2 cm, TE of 7 ms, TR of 2000 ms, a b value of 1000 s/mm2 with unique diffusion gradients in 42 directions to ensure high angular resolution, and 6 images with b value 0. Two averages were acquired to provide a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio, resulting in a scan time of approximately 20 hours. DTI data were processed using Synaptive Medical’s tractography pipeline (ON, Canada). Specimens were selectively chosen from known regions of the costal bridge and long rib due to the limited sensitive volume of the RF coil (Figs. 1–3). Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps were generated and scaled to physiologically relevant ranges18 to enable visualization of subtle microstructural variations.

Histology

Samples adjacent to those used in DTI were subjected to histological examination, which is a widely established, standard method for the assessment of PG and collagen distribution. Samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 6-μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for general morphology, safranin O for glycosaminoglycan evaluation (indicative of PG content), and picrosirius red for collagen fiber identification. A rapid decalcifier (RDO, Aurora, IL) was applied to ensure that the paraffin block surfaces were free of calcifications (which may be present in human costal cartilage postpuberty).

Polarized Light Microscopy

The validity of DTI of the 3-dimensional (3D) collagen orientation was supported by PLM in 2D. PLM was used to observe the orientation and spatial distribution of collagen fibers (Axio Imager Z1 upright stand microscope equipped with an X-Cite 120 LED fluorescence lamp and a 4-MP sCMOS camera; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Histological slices stained with picrosirius red were imaged using a 10× objective, resulting in an optical resolution of 0.185 µm/pixel for the captured section of the slice. The staining enhanced image contrast, making it easier to visualize anisotropic subcellular structures. The camera output generated images highlighting birefringent structures (ie, collagen fibers).

PG and Collagen Distribution

The spatial distribution of PGs and collagen was evaluated across the anterior-to-posterior surface of costal cartilage specimens, specifically comparing the surface layers to mid and deep zones (ImageJ19). Color thresholding was applied to isolate the stain corresponding to either PGs or collagen. The images were converted to 32-bit grayscale, and pixel intensity was evaluated across the tissue thickness. The intensity values were plotted as microstructure concentration profiles along the anterior–posterior direction, adjacent to each corresponding stained histological image, to illustrate regional differences in matrix composition.

RESULTS

Tractography of Collagen Fibers

Tractography results, shown in Figures 2–4, illustrate the 3D orientation of collagen fibers within the costal cartilage. The accompanying color map encodes the principal direction of water diffusion at each voxel, allowing voxel-wise estimation of fiber orientation in 3D space. Streamlines were generated from user-defined seed points, tracing the path of highest diffusion directionality to reconstruct fiber tracts. This technique enabled visualization of structural pathways, reflecting regions where water preferentially diffuses, primarily along collagen fibers and water channels. The FA and MD maps derived from the MRI analysis are represented in the same image sets.

Fiber Orientation and Matrix Composition at the Costal Bridge

The tractography results reveal a dominant collagen fiber orientation in the mid and deep zones of the costal bridge, where fibers are primarily aligned anterior–posterior (dorsal–ventral) relative to anatomical position (perpendicular to the cartilage surface). These fibers are visualized in green in the first images in both Figure 2 (transverse section) and Figure 3 (sagittal section). In contrast, the superficial zone exhibits a predominant craniocaudal orientation (visualized in red), with fibers arranged nearly orthogonal (at ~90 degrees) to those in the deeper layers. This organization results in a distinct radial zone of collagen surrounding the mid and deep zones of the tissue.

PLM images in Figures 2 and 3 confirm the presence of tangentially aligned fibers near the surface, consistent with a circumferential fiber layer. These tangential fibers likely contribute to the generation of circumferential stress around the costal bridge, adding to the tissue’s structural integrity.

Histological analysis (safranin O and picrosirius red) in Figures 2 and 3 further highlights the matrix composition at the costal bridge. Collagen is primarily concentrated in the superficial zones, particularly surrounding ossification centers, and intracartilaginous water canals. In contrast, PGs are predominantly located in the mid and deep zones of the cartilage. These spatial differences are quantified in the adjacent grayscale intensity plots, which show pixel-based concentration profiles across the anterior–posterior thickness of the tissue. In the costal bridge samples (thickness ranging from 4.5 to 7.0 mm), PG content was lower within the first 1.0–1.3 mm from the surface. A plateau of higher PG levels was observed in the mid-depth region (~2.5–4.4 mm), as illustrated in Figures 2B and 3B.

FA maps in Figures 2 and 3 show higher anisotropy in the mid and deep zones (green-red regions), corresponding to the anterior–posterior fiber alignment observed in tractography and PLM. In contrast, the surface zones exhibit lower FA values (blue), which aligns with the tangential fiber orientation that may be less coherently aligned across voxels. MD maps in Figures 2 and 3 display higher diffusivity near the cartilage surface, where the matrix is less dense and water mobility is greater. By comparison, the mid and deep zones exhibit lower MD values, consistent with denser collagen networks and higher PG content that restrict water diffusion. Localized regions of elevated MD, likely corresponding to intracartilaginous water canals, can also be observed as areas of reduced structural resistance to diffusion.

Fiber Orientation and Matrix Composition in the Long Rib

Similar to the costal bridge, tractography of the long rib cartilage reveals predominantly anterior–posterior (dorsal–ventral) collagen alignment in the mid and deep zones, with superficial fibers gradually reorienting tangentially, especially at the anterior and posterior edges (Fig. 4). Occasional deviations (30–45 degrees) were observed (which may be influenced by intercostal muscle attachment). The PLM in Figure 4 confirms well-aligned fibers in both surface and deep zones. Histological staining with safranin O and picrosirius red shows collagen enrichment near the surface and ossification zones and PG concentration peaking in the mid-depth region (2.6–3.2 mm), with lower levels in the superficial region 1.2–1.9 mm. FA maps in Figure 4 align with collagen orientation patterns, showing high anisotropy centrally and reduced values superficially. MD maps in Figure 4 indicate higher water mobility near the surface and localized elevations corresponding to intracartilaginous canals.

DISCUSSION

There exists an incomplete understanding of the internal microstructural organization and the biomechanical factors inherent in costal cartilage tissue that impact force imbalance, leading to cartilage warp during graft preparation.10 Although PGs have been identified as the primary contributors to warping due to their osmotic swelling capacity,10,13,20,21 collagen fibers play a structural role in constraining or directing this deformation. Understanding the interplay between the collagen fibers and PG distribution is essential for predicting and managing warping in graft design.13 By demonstrating the balanced cross-sectional pattern of this microstructure, our results explain the observation that transverse slicing of the unique anatomical triangular costal bridge can yield multiple nonwarping grafts.

Our tractography results revealed a constricting circumferential zone of tangential fibers surrounding the surface of the rib. Histological analysis showed that PGs, which are expansile by binding water and generating internal swelling pressure, are concentrated in the mid and deep zones. During slicing of long rib cartilage, when the circumferential collagen (acting as a tension band) is cut, central expansive forces from PG-associated swelling lead to asymmetric warping of slices toward the superficial surface. Theoretically, in a perfectly concentric cut, warping will not occur along the central axis due to balanced forces.12 However, achieving a perfectly centered slice is technically challenging. The transverse costal bridge slicing technique effectively balances internal forces, producing multiple, long, straight, nonwarping grafts.

FA and MD maps consistently reflected the microstructural composition across the costal bridge and long rib samples. The collagen network in cartilage guides the directional motion of water, inducing diffusion anisotropy that is captured through FA.18,22,23 Higher FA values were found corresponding to regions with well-aligned collagen fibers, typically in the mid and deep zones of the costal cartilage. In contrast, lower MD values were found in regions with high PG content, as PGs lack directional organization but restrict water diffusion uniformly. The interplay of these 2 matrix components, collagen influencing FA and PGs modulating MD, explains the spatial distribution observed in our maps and reflects patterns seen in hyaline cartilage.24

Interestingly, struts harvested from the costal bridge11 were found to be more dimensionally stable, likely due to the balanced internal stress distribution maintained by the collagen fibers along the long axis and the symmetrical PG content on both cranial and caudal surfaces. To further illustrate the relationship between matrix composition and cartilage warping, Figure 5 presents an overlaid visualization of PG distribution and collagen fiber orientation on a representative transverse slice of the junctional costal bridge. On the left side, the intact cartilage structure is shown, whereas on the right, the strut graft has been sectioned in the coronal plane, creating 2 mirrored halves. The PG distribution map highlights a higher concentration in the midzones, with reduced levels toward the surface. Upon sectioning, the side containing a higher PG concentration tends to expand more, driven by increased water uptake, resulting in differential swelling across the slice. The collagen orientation map shows circumferentially aligned surface fibers that provide tensile resistance. When sectioned, the tension band is released, and the collagen network is no longer able to resist the swelling pressure from the PG-rich midzone, leading to warping of the cartilage toward its surface. These findings explain the efficacy of the transverse costal bridge slicing method, which maintains the symmetry of the microstructural architecture, in preventing graft warp. Furthermore, they emphasize the critical importance of matrix composition and collagen architecture in maintaining the structural stability of costal cartilage and highlight how subtle asymmetries in these features can drive significant shape change when the tissue is manipulated (Fig. 5).

The schematic illustrates microstructure-driven deformation of a straight strut harvested from the costochondral costal bridge, showing transverse strut geometry before and after coronal slicing (A), simulated PG distribution (B), and corresponding collagen fiber organization associated with directional warping (C).

The costal cartilage of long ribs exists in a mechanically active environment, surrounded by several layers of intercostal muscles,25,26 which may contribute to its internal organization. The external intercostal muscle fibers extend from back to front from the lower edge of 1 rib to the upper edge of the rib below, with the internal intercostal muscle fibers running in a perpendicular direction. The innermost intercostal muscle fibers run in a cranial–caudal direction. As such, it is plausible that tensile loading from muscle attachments influences collagen fiber alignment during cartilage growth and remodeling. In imaging of the long rib, we observed localized deviations from the dominant anterior–posterior collagen orientation. In certain regions, fiber bundles appeared angled, potentially reflecting adaptive alignment in response to nearby intercostal muscle fibers. This implies that the mechanical tension exerted by muscle contractions may guide collagen organization through adaptive remodeling processes.

Previous research on costal cartilage grafts demonstrates that tissue handling and graft origin can modify the matrix components implicated in warping. Allografts undergo gamma irradiation, ethylene oxide, and chemical decellularization processing,27,28 which may reduce warping tendency by reducing PG content and collagen crosslinking. However, this processing may consequently increase brittleness and the risk of resorption.9,10,27,29 The perichondrium, although biologically important in cartilage growth and nutrient supply, has minimal influence on graft warping because it is routinely removed during autograft harvesting and is absent in processed allografts.10,30

Although septal cartilage remains the preferred graft source for nasal reconstruction, costal cartilage provides a dependable alternative when septal supply is limited. Its straightness and stiffness make it superior to auricular cartilage for structural support.31 Recognizing microstructural differences between anatomical sites and the influence of collagen and PG distribution enables informed graft selection. Our findings support using transversely sliced costal bridge grafts for reliable, nonwarping struts in nasal reconstruction.

Some limitations of this study include the small donor cohort (n = 5, ages 19–26 y), which restricts statistical power and generalizability. This was primarily due to the long DTI acquisition time (~20 h) and limited scanner access. Larger and more age-diverse cohorts would validate the relevance of the microstructural patterns observed across various demographics. Although all donors were relatively young, older patients often experience less graft deformation with aging due to diminished PG content and increased calcification,32,33 changes that render costochondral grafts more brittle and less pliable, and thus less clinically suitable.3 Importantly, validation of our imaging findings was supported by histological correlation, as PLM confirmed the collagen fiber orientations identified through DTI. Such multimodal agreement adds strength to our results.

CONCLUSIONS

This study links the internal microstructural organization of costal cartilage to its warping behavior during graft preparation. The microstructural symmetry of the costal bridge enables transverse slicing to preserve balanced internal forces, yielding multiple nonwarping grafts suited for nasal reconstruction. These findings offer a scientific rationale for graft selection and slicing strategies that enhance shape predictability in reconstructive surgery. Future work should incorporate biomechanical validation to determine whether these microstructural features correspond to functional stability under physiological loading.
