Abstract

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be the algebra of all bounded linear operators on a complex Banach space
\mathcal{X}
of dimension at least three. For an arbitrary nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we determine the form of mappings
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with sufficiently large range such that
\lambda(AB+BA)
is idempotent if and only if
\lambda(\phi(A)\phi(B)+\phi(B)\phi(A))
is idempotent, for all
A
,
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Note that
\phi
is not assumed to be linear or additive.

1
Introduction

In the last few decades, there has been a lot of research interest in studying mappings on algebras, which leave invariant certain sets, relations and/or functions, often referred to as preservers. In this context, consider a map
\phi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
such that for every pair
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
we have
\phi(A)\phi(B)=0
whenever
AB=0
. These maps are typically called
zero-product preserving maps
. The study of such maps has been extensive, mainly under the assumption that

\phi
is linear (see
[
1
]
for a historical account). It is clear that characterizing these maps becomes significantly more challenging when neither linearity nor even additivity of
\phi
is assumed. In this case, a stronger preserving property is required, leading to the condition
\phi(A)\phi(B)=0
if and only if
AB=0
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Maps satisfying this property are called
preservers of zero-product in both directions
. In
[
3
]
, the authors provide a detailed structure of all bijective maps on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
that preserve zero-product in both directions without assuming linearity. This result is relatively new, but a similar result concerning rank-one idempotents has been known for a longer time. Namely, in 2003, Molnár characterized all bijective maps on rank-one idempotents that preserve the zero-product in both directions, which was a key step in his proof of Uhlhorn’s Theorem,
[
13
]
.

Beyond zero-product-preserving maps, idempotent-preserving maps appear across multiple branches of mathematics. For instance, in mathematical morphology, many image-processing operations rely on idempotent transformations. Likewise, in operator theory and the study of
C^{\ast}
-algebras, preservers of idempotents under different types of products play a crucial role in analyzing morphisms and invariant subspaces. These contexts provide strong motivation for studying maps that preserve nonzero-idempotency. Specifically, a mapping
\phi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is said to
preserve the (nonzero) idempotency of a product
\ast
when
\phi(A)\ast\phi(B)
is (nonzero) idempotent whenever
A\ast B
is (nonzero) idempotent, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. If the converse also holds – the (nonzero) idempotency of
\phi(A)\ast\phi(B)
implies the (nonzero) idempotency of
A\ast B
– then
\phi
preserves (nonzero) idempotency of the product
\ast
in both directions
. Restricting our attention to the standard product
A\ast B=AB
, the structure of surjective nonzero-idempotency preservers in both directions has been described in
[
7
,
8
]
. A more general treatment, covering various types of products - including the usual product of
k
operators,
k\geq 2
, and the Jordan triple product
A\ast B=ABA
- can be found in
[
14
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the Jordan product of
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
defined as
A\ast B=AB+BA
. Additive zero-Jordan product preservers have been studied in
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
19
,
20
]
, while linear preservers of nonzero idempotency of the Jordan product were explored in
[
6
]
. Let us note that the Jordan product of operators
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is sometimes in the literature also defined as
A\circ B=\frac{1}{2}\left(AB+BA\right)
,
which has the desirable property that
I\circ I=I
. In the sequel, whenever we refer to Jordan product, we have the product
\circ
in mind.
Results concerning additive maps preserving nonzero idempotency of the Jordan product
\circ
can be found in
[
18
]
.

A natural question arises: can meaningful results still be obtained if the assumptions of linearity and additivity are removed? In this work, we address an even more challenging problem by setting the preserver property in a more general form: the Jordan product of
A
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is idempotent if and only if the Jordan product of
\phi(A)
and
\phi(B)
is idempotent. Notably, we allow the possibility that a nonzero Jordan product between
A
and
B
corresponds to a zero-Jordan product between
\phi(A)
and
\phi(B)
, and vice-versa. As far as we know, such a preserving property has not yet been studied in the literature.

Throughout this paper, if not stated otherwise,
\mathcal{X}
will denote a complex Banach space of dimension at least three, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
will stand for the algebra of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{X}
. By
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
we denote the topological dual of
\mathcal{X}
. We adopt standard notation and terminology concerning idempotents. Specifically, an operator
P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is idempotent if
P^{2}=P
and anti-idempotent if
P^{2}=-P
and
P\neq 0
. We denote by
{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}
and
-\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
the set of all idempotent and anti-idempotent operators, respectively. Let

\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})=(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})\backslash\{0\})\cup-\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. The symbols
\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
,
{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
will represent the subsets of nonzero idempotents, rank-one idempotents and the union of rank-one idempotents and rank-one anti-idempotents, respectively.

We say that an idempotent
P
is nontrivial if it is neither equal to zero nor to the identity operator on
\mathcal{X}
, denoted throughout by
I
. Analogously, an anti-idempotent
Q
is nontrivial if
Q\neq-I
(by definition
Q\neq 0
).

In the following, we will refer to a natural relation on idempotents called orthogonality. We say that
P,Q\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
are orthogonal, denoted by
P\bot Q
, if
PQ=QP=0
. We will also use the notation
{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}={\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{0\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
to denote the set of all operators of rank at most
k
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 1

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-dimensional complex Banach space and let
\phi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be a map whose range contains the set
\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
. Suppose that

A\circ B\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi(A)\circ\phi(B)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X}),

for every
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then either there exists a bounded invertible linear or conjugate-linear operator
T:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
such that

\phi(X)=\lambda TXT^{-1},\qquad\text{ for every }X\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}),

or, only if
\mathcal{X}
is reflexive, there exists a bounded invertible linear or conjugate-linear operator
T:{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
such that

\phi(X)=\lambda TX^{\prime}T^{-1},\qquad\text{ for every }X\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}),

where
X^{\prime}
denotes the adjoint operator of the operator
X
.

When
\mathcal{X}
is a finite-dimensional complex vector space, then it will be regarded as
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and its members are seen as column vectors. Linear endomorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
will be identified with
n\times n
complex matrices, denoted by
M_{n}
.
By
{\mathcal{I}}_{n}
,
{\mathcal{I}}^{*}_{n}
,
{\mathcal{I}}_{n,1}
,
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\pm}\subseteq M_{n}
we denote the set of idempotent, the set of nonzero idempotent, the set of rank-one idempotent, and the union of rank-one idempotent and rank-one anti-idempotent matrices, respectively.

Theorem 2

Let
\phi:M_{n}\rightarrow M_{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be a map whose range contains the set
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\pm}
. Suppose that

A\circ B\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi(A)\circ\phi(B)\in{\mathcal{I}}_{n},

for every
A,B\in M_{n}
. Then there exists a field automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}
, a non-singular matrix
T\in M_{n}
and
\lambda\in\{1,-1\}
such that

\phi([x_{ij}])=\lambda T[\sigma(x_{ij})]^{\diamond}T^{-1},\qquad\text{for every }[x_{ij}]\in M_{n},

where
\diamond
denotes one of the maps on
M_{n}
: identity or transposition.

Remark 3

By
[
12
]
there exist many non-continuous automorphisms of the complex field.

Applying Theorems
1
and
2
, we obtain the following characterizations as a simple consequence. Here, for a scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
the notations
\alpha\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
and
\alpha\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\pm}
refer to the sets of all operators of the form
A=\alpha P
, where
P\in\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
or
P\in\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\pm}
, respectively.

Theorem 4

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-dimensional complex Banach space and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
be nonzero. Suppose that
\phi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a map whose range contains the set
\sqrt{(2\alpha)^{-1}}\;\mathcal{I}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
and that

\alpha(AB+BA)\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(\phi(A)\phi(B)+\phi(B)\phi(A))\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X}),

for every
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then either there exists a bounded invertible linear or conjugate-linear operator
T:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
such that

\phi(X)=\lambda TXT^{-1},\qquad\text{ for every }X\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}),

or, only if
\mathcal{X}
is reflexive, there exists a bounded invertible linear or conjugate-linear operator
T:{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
\lambda\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
such that

\phi(X)=\lambda TX^{\prime}T^{-1},\qquad\text{ for every }X\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}),

where
X^{\prime}
denotes the adjoint operator of the operator
X
.

Theorem 5

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
be nonzero, and let
\phi:M_{n}\rightarrow M_{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be a map whose range contains
\sqrt{(2\alpha)^{-1}}\;\mathcal{I}_{n}^{\pm}
. Suppose that

\alpha(AB+BA)\in{\mathcal{I}_{n}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha(\phi(A)\phi(B)+\phi(B)\phi(A))\in\mathcal{I}_{n},

for every
A,B\in M_{n}
. Then there exists a field automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathbb{C}
, a non-singular matrix
T\in M_{n}
and
c_{\alpha}
,
c_{\alpha}^{2}=\sigma(\alpha)/\alpha
, such that

\phi([x_{ij}])=c_{\alpha}T[\sigma(x_{ij})]^{\diamond}T^{-1},\qquad\text{for every }[x_{ij}]\in M_{n},

where
\diamond
denotes one of the maps on
M_{n}
: identity or transposition.

In the next section, we establish some preliminary results, followed by the proofs of the above theorems in the subsequent section.

2
Preliminaries

Recall that every rank-one operator on
\mathcal{X}
can be written as
x\otimes f
for some nonzero vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
and some nonzero functional
f\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. It is defined by
(x\otimes f)z=f(z)x
for every
z\in\mathcal{X}
, and it holds
A(x\otimes f)=Ax\otimes f
and
(x\otimes f)A=x\otimes A^{\prime}f
for every
A\in{\mathcal{B}\left(X\right)}
, where
A^{\prime}
stands for the adjoint operator of
A
. The rank-one operator
x\otimes f
is idempotent (anti-idempotent) if and only if
f(x)=1
(
f(x)=-1
, resp.). Operator
x\otimes f
is nilpotent if and only if
f(x)=0
. It is clear that
\lambda x\otimes f=x\otimes\lambda f
for every scalar
\lambda
.

First, we will introduce some elementary results that will be the cornerstone of the subsequent investigation. Let us start with two simple observations stated without proofs.

Observation 6

Let
A
and
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be nonzero operators. Then the following statements hold true.

(i)

A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
and
-A\circ X\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if
A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}^{\ast}(\mathcal{X})
.

(ii)

A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
and
-A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if
A\circ X=0
.

We now proceed with several auxiliary lemmas that will be instrumental in the sequel.

Lemma 7

[
6
, Lemma 2.4]

Let
A
and
x\otimes f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be nonzero operators. If
f(x)\neq 0
, then
A\circ x\otimes f
is a nonzero idempotent if and only if
Ax=\frac{1}{f(x)}x
or
A^{\prime}f=\frac{1}{f(x)}f
.

Lemma 8

Let
A
and
x\otimes f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be nonzero operators. If
f(x)\neq 0
, then
A\circ x\otimes f=0
if and only if
Ax=0
and
A^{\prime}f=0
.

Proof.

Let us prove only the nontrivial part. Suppose that
A\circ x\otimes f=0
for some
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
f\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
f(x)\neq 0
. Then,
Ax=\lambda x
and
A^{\prime}f=-\lambda f
for some scalar
\lambda
. As
f(x)\neq 0
, from
f(Ax)=\lambda f(x)
and
f(Ax)=-\lambda f(x)
it follows that
\lambda=0
.

\Box

Lemma 9

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
and
x\otimes f\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be nonzero operators. If
f(x)=0
, then
A\circ x\otimes f
is a nonzero idempotent if and only if
f(Ax)=2
and
f(A^{2}x)=0
.

Proof.

Again, the ‘if’ statement is obvious, so let us prove the ‘only if’. Direct calculation of
A\circ x\otimes f\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
gives

2Ax\otimes f+2x\otimes A^{\prime}f=f(Ax)Ax\otimes f+f(x)Ax\otimes A^{\prime}f+f(A^{2}x)x\otimes f+f(Ax)x\otimes A^{\prime}f,

which is the same as

Ax\otimes\left(\left(2-f(Ax)\right)f-f(x)A^{\prime}f\right)+x\otimes\left(\left(2-f(Ax)\right)A^{\prime}f-f(A^{2}x)f\right)=0.

Then apply
f(x)=0
to see that

\left(2-f(Ax)\right)Ax\otimes f+x\otimes\left((2-f(Ax))A^{\prime}f-f(A^{2}x)f\right)=0.

(1)

If
x
and
Ax
are linearly independent, we have
2-f(Ax)=0
and
f(A^{2}x)=0
. If this is not the case,
Ax=\lambda x
for some scalar
\lambda
and so,
f(Ax)=0
and
f(A^{2}x)=0
. But then we can rewrite the identity (
1
) to
x\otimes(2\lambda f+2A^{\prime}f)=0
. This gives
A\circ(x\otimes f)=0
, a contradiction.

\Box

Lemma 10

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
be a nonzero operator. Suppose that for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and some nonzero
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have
A\circ x\otimes f\in\mathcal{I}^{\ast}(\mathcal{X})
, for all
f\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}
. Then
Ax=\lambda x
.

Proof.

Assuming
A\circ x\otimes f\in\mathcal{I}^{\ast}(\mathcal{X})
and applying Lemma
7
gives that
Ax=\lambda x
or
A^{\prime}f=\lambda f
. If
Ax\neq\lambda x
, then
A^{\prime}f=\lambda f
for every
f
with
f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}
. Replacing
f
by
f+g
, where
g\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
satisfies
g(x)=0
, one obtains that
g(Ax)=0
yielding that
x
and
Ax
are linearly dependent, say
Ax=\mu x
for some
\mu\neq\lambda
. From
A^{\prime}f=\lambda f
it follows that
\mu=\lambda
, a contradiction. Therefore,
Ax=\lambda x
.

\Box

We continue with some characterizations of trivial idempotent operators in terms of the idempotency of the Jordan product
\circ
.

Lemma 11

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

A=0
.

(ii)

A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
for every
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
.

(iii)

A\circ R\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
for every
R\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})\cup(-\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X}))
.

Proof.

Clearly (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Assume that (iii) holds true. By Observation
6
,
A\circ x\otimes f=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all bounded functionals
f
with
f(x)=1
. Then, by Lemma
8
,
Ax=0
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

\Box

Lemma 12

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

A=I
.

(ii)

A\circ R\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
for every
R\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

Proof.

While (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is clear, the reverse implication is a simple consequence of Lemma
10
.

\Box

If
P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a nontrivial idempotent, the underlying Banach space
\mathcal{X}
can be decomposed into the direct sum
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathrm{Im}\,P\oplus\mathrm{Ker}\,P
, where
\mathrm{Im}\,P
and
\mathrm{Ker}\,P
denote the image and the kernel of
P
, respectively.

The next Lemma is then technical.

Lemma 13

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Banach space with
\dim\mathcal{X}\geq 2
and let
A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}})\backslash\{0,I,-I\}
. If
A^{2}=0
or
A^{2}=I
, then there exists an operator
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
of rank at most two such that
A\circ B\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
and
B\circ B\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
.

Proof.

Let us firstly assume that
A^{2}=0
and
A\neq 0
. Then there exists an
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
x
and
Ax
are linearly independent. Hence, we can take an
f\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
such that
f(x)=0
and
f(Ax)=2
. By setting
B:=x\otimes A^{\prime}f-Ax\otimes f+x\otimes f
, we compute that
A\circ B=\frac{1}{2}Ax\otimes f+\frac{1}{2}x\otimes A^{\prime}f
, which is a sum of two orthogonal rank-one idempotents, and thus an idempotent. Moreover, since
Bx=2x
,
B^{2}
cannot be an idempotent.

It remains to consider
A^{2}=I
. The operator
A
is a nonscalar involution, so
A=I-2Q
for some nontrivial idempotent
Q
. As
Q
is nontrivial, we can choose a nonzero
y\in\mathrm{Ker}\,Q
and a nonzero
z\in\mathrm{Im}\,Q
to have
Ay=y
and
Az=-z
. Then we pick a functional
g\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
such that
g(\mathrm{Ker}\,Q)=0
and
g(z)=2
and take a
h\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
with
h(y)=1
and
h(\mathrm{Im}\,Q)=0
. It is not difficult to verify that
A^{\prime}g=-g
and
A^{\prime}h=h
. Set
B:=y\otimes g+z\otimes h
, then a simple computation shows that
A\circ B=0
. Furthermore, it is easy to see that
B^{2}z=2z
and so,
B^{2}=B\circ B
is not idempotent.

\Box

Up to now, we have considered those operators
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
for which
A
or
A^{2}
is a trivial idempotent. We now turn our attention to the case of nontrivial idempotents.

Lemma 14

Let
A,P\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
with
A
nonzero and
P
nontrivial idempotent. If
A\circ(I-P)=0
, then
\mathrm{Im}\,P
is a nontrivial
A
-invariant subspace, and
Ax=0
for every
x\in\mathrm{Ker}\,P
.

In particular, if
\mathrm{rank}\,P=1
, then the assumption
A\circ(I-P)=0
gives that
A=\lambda P
, for some nonzero scalar
\lambda
, and additionally, when
A
is idempotent, we have
A=P
.

Proof.

With respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathrm{Im}\,P\oplus\mathrm{Ker}\,P
, operators
A
and
P
have the following matrix representations:

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

As
A\circ(I-P)=0
it is clear that
A_{12}=0
,
A_{21}=0
and
A_{22}=0
. The second claim is now obvious.

\Box

Lemma 15

Let
P,Q\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. If
P\neq Q
and
Q\circ(I-P)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
, then there exists a rank-one idempotent
R\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
such that
Q\circ R=0
and
P\circ R\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
.

Proof.

Let
P:=x_{1}\otimes f_{1}
and
Q:=x_{2}\otimes f_{2}
, for some
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
and
f_{1},f_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
with
f_{1}(x_{1})=1=f_{2}(x_{2})
. The condition
Q\circ(I-P)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
forces us to assume that
Q\circ(I-P)\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
, since otherwise,
Q=P
by virtue of Lemma
14
. Note that the Jordan product is a commutative operation, thus,
Q\circ(I-P)=(I-P)\circ(x_{2}\otimes f_{2})\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
. Clearly,
(I-P)x_{2}=x_{2}
or
(I-P)^{\prime}f_{2}=f_{2}
by Lemma
7
, which is equivalent to
Px_{2}=0
or
P^{\prime}f_{2}=0
. In particular,
f_{1}(x_{2})=0
or
f_{2}(x_{1})=0
.

Suppose
f_{1}(x_{2})=0
. Vectors
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are obviously linearly independent, so we can choose a nonzero functional
g\in{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}
with
g(x_{1})=0=g(x_{2})
. It is easy to see that
f_{1},f_{2}
and
g
are now linearly independent, and we can pick a vector
y\in\mathcal{X}
such that
f_{1}(y)=0=f_{2}(y)
and
g(y)=1
. Set
R:=\frac{1}{2}(y+x_{1}-f_{2}(x_{1})x_{2})\otimes(g+f_{1})
and check that
R\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and that
Q\circ R=0
. On the other hand,
P\circ R
is not idempotent since its trace equals
\frac{1}{2}
.

The case
f_{2}(x_{1})=0
can be treated in a very similar manner; there exists a vector
y\in\mathcal{X}
such that
f_{1}(y)=0=f_{2}(y)
and a functional
g\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with
g(x_{1})=0=g(x_{2})
and
g(y)=1
. Define

\displaystyle A=A^{2}+BC,

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt4A=-4A^{2}-BC

(2)

Lemma 17

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a Banach space with

0=(\gamma_{1}-\gamma)e_{1}+\alpha_{1}e_{3}+(\alpha_{1}+\gamma_{1}\lambda^{2})z
. As

are linearly independent, it follows that

, a contradiction. Therefore, the spectrum of

equals
\{0\}
, and since

is algebraic,
A
is nilpotent of nilindex

. Hence, there exists a linearly independent four-tuple

with
A^{4}w=0
. We can take

, for an existing linearly independent set
\{u,Au,\dots,A^{r-1}u\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then, by (iii) of Proposition

Bw=Aw,\qquad BAw=A^{2}w\qquad\text{and}\qquad BA^{2}w=A^{3}w.

(16)

Suppose that for some

for some
\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

\displaystyle Ay

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}Aw+\beta_{1}A^{2}w+\beta_{2}A^{3}w,

\displaystyle By

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}Bw+\beta_{1}BAw+\beta_{2}BA^{2}w+\beta_{3}BA^{3}w.

Inserting properties from (
16
) into linear combination of

By=Ay+\beta_{3}BA^{3}w
. But, by Corollary
19

\mathrm{Ker}\,A=\mathrm{Ker}\,B
and, since
A(A^{3}w)=A^{4}w=0

A^{3}w\in\mathrm{Ker}\,A
. Consequently,
A^{3}w\in\mathrm{Ker}\,B
and hence,
By=Ay
. However, as
\gamma\neq 0
, it is evident that
Ae_{3}\neq Be_{3}
and so,
{\s}\{w,Aw,A^{2}w,A^{3}w\}\cap\mathcal{Y}=\{0\}
. Now, consider the vector

. Clearly,
A^{4}(e_{3}+w)=0
and
e_{3}+w,A(e_{3}+w),A^{2}(e_{3}+w),A^{4}(e_{3}+w)
are linearly independent vectors. Thus, by (iii) of Proposition

, it follows that
A(e_{3}+w)=B(e_{3}+w)
, a contradiction since

and
Ae_{3}\neq Be_{3}

Therefore,

. If

D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{X}),

so that

A_{12}
introduced in (

for some bounded functionals

(the latter identity multiplied by

K=\begin{bmatrix}D&X\\

As a result we get that

We close this section with a characterization of when two operators coincide in terms of the Jordan product
\circ
. Let

{\mathcal{T}}=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{X})\cup\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{X})\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{T}(3;\lambda)&\text{if }\dim{\mathcal{X}}\geq 4\\
\mathcal{T}(2;\lambda)&\text{if }\dim\mathcal{X}=3\end{array}\right.,

and note that

is a similarity-invariant set, i.e. for every invertible

we have that

for every

.

Lemma 25

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

A=B

(ii)

A\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}\Leftrightarrow B\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}
, for every
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
.

(iii)

A\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}\Leftrightarrow B\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})}
, for every
X\in\mathcal{T}
.

Proof.

The implications (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) are straightforward. So, assume that (iii) holds true and proceed to show that this implies
A=B
. By Observation
6
note that

A\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})}\Leftrightarrow B\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})}\quad\text{and}\quad A\circ X=0\Leftrightarrow B\circ X=0,

(17)

for every
X\in\mathcal{T}
. In the following, we will distinguish two cases, depending on whether
A
and
B
are algebraic.

At first, we suppose that
A
is not algebraic. Choose an arbitrary
x\in\mathcal{X}
. If the set
\{x,Ax,A^{2}x,A^{3}x,A^{4}x\}
is linearly independent, then
Ax=Bx
by (iii) of Proposition
23
. If this is not the case, let
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{span}\{x,Ax,A^{2}x,A^{3}x,A^{4}x\}\oplus\mathcal{Z}
and write

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
0&A_{22}\end{bmatrix}

with respect to this decomposition. Note that
A_{22}
is not algebraic. So, there exists a
z\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
z,A_{22}z,A_{22}^{2}z,A_{22}^{3}z,A_{22}^{4}z
are linearly independent. It is then easy to see that both
\{z,Az,A^{2}z,A^{3}z,A^{4}z\}
and
\{x+z,A(x+z),A^{2}(x+z),A^{3}(x+z),A^{4}(x+z)\}
are linearly independent as well. By (iii) of Proposition
23
it follows that
Az=Bz
and
A(x+z)=B(x+z)
providing
Ax=Bx
.

The same conclusion follows if
B
is not algebraic.

Suppose further that both
A
and
B
are algebraic operators. To reach a contradiction, assume that
A\neq B
. Then there exists a vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
Ax\neq Bx
. As at least one of
Ax
and
Bx
is nonzero, we may and we do suppose that
Ax\neq 0
.

If
x
,
Ax,\dots
,
A^{4}x
are linearly independent or,
x
,
Ax
,
A^{2}x
,
A^{3}x
are linearly independent and
A^{4}x=0
, we arrive at a contradiction by (iii) of Proposition
23
. Hence, for

E_{A}(x)={\s}\left\{x,Ax,A^{2}x,A^{3}x,A^{4}x\right\}

we have
1\leq m:=\dim E_{A}(x)\leq 4
. It is easy to see that
\left\{x,Ax,\ldots,A^{m-1}x\right\}
is a basis of
E_{A}(x)
and
E_{A}(x)
is a
m
-dimensional
A
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{X}
. Let us split
\mathcal{X}=E_{A}(x)\oplus\mathcal{Z}
. With respect to this decomposition, the operator
A
can be represented by

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}&\ast\\
0&\ast\end{bmatrix},

where
A_{11}
is a
m\times m
complex matrix (recall that
m=\dim E_{A}(x)
). There is no loss of generality in assuming that
A_{11}
is in Jordan normal form. Because the vectors
x,Ax,\dots,A^{m-1}x
are linearly independent, the minimal polynomial of

is equal to its characteristic polynomial. Thus,
A_{11}
is nonderogatory, i.e. it has exactly one Jordan block per eigenvalue, and all eigenvalues are distinct. Now, since
A_{11}
is a direct sum of distinct Jordan blocks

, where

, apply Lemma
22

and Lemma

for every

. But then also

, a contradiction. Therefore,

as desired.

\Box

3
Proofs of the main results

We are now ready to start simultaneously proving Theorems
1
and
2
.

Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
.
Let us assume that
\phi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
is a map such that
{\mathcal{I}}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
is a subset of the range of
\phi
. Further suppose that
A\circ B
is idempotent if and only if
\phi(A)\circ\phi(B)
is idempotent for any
A
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. The proof will be set up through several steps.

Step 1.

\phi(0)=0
.

The claim is a direct application of Lemma
11
and the fact that the range of
\phi
contains
\mathcal{I}_{1}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
.

Step 2
.
\phi
is injective.

Let
\phi(A)=\phi(B)
. Obviously,
\phi(A)\circ Y\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if
\phi(B)\circ Y\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
for every
Y
in the range of
\phi
. It follows that
A\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if
B\circ X\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
for every
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. By Lemma
25
,
A=B
.

Step 3.

\phi(I)=I
or
\phi(-I)=I
.

As
I
is in the range of
\phi
, there exists an
A
such that
\phi(A)=I
. Clearly,
A^{2}=A\circ A
is idempotent and by
Step 1
and
Step 2
,
A\neq 0
. It follows that either
A^{2}=I
or
A^{2}
has a nontrivial kernel. Accordingly, we separate two cases. In each we find an operator
B
such that
A\circ B\in\mathcal{I}(X)
and
B\circ B=B^{2}\notin\mathcal{I}(X)
. Then
I\circ\phi(B)=\phi(B)\in\mathcal{I}(X)
and, in turn, also
\phi(B)^{2}=\phi(B)\circ\phi(B)
should be idempotent. But this contradicts the fact that
B^{2}
is not idempotent.

Case 1.

A^{2}=I
. If
A
is a scalar operator, we are done. Otherwise,
A
is a nonscalar involution. Applying Lemma
13
provides a
B
with the required properties to reach a contradiction.

Case 2.

A^{2}=P
, where
P
is an idempotent with a nontrivial kernel (possibly
P=0
). It is easy to verify that both
\mathrm{Ker}\,P
and
\mathrm{Im}\,P
are
A
-invariant subspaces. Define the operator
N:\mathrm{Ker}\,P\to\mathrm{Ker}\,P
as the restriction of
A
to
\mathrm{Ker}\,P
by
Nx:=Ax
for all
x\in\mathrm{Ker}\,P
. Clearly,
N^{2}=0
. If
N
is nonzero, then
\dim\mathrm{Ker}\,P>1
and, we apply Lemma
13
to find a
B_{0}\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathrm{Ker}\,P)
such that
N\circ B_{0}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathrm{Ker}\,P)
and
B_{0}\circ B_{0}\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathrm{Ker}\,P)
. Then
B=B_{0}\oplus 0_{\mathrm{Im}\,P}
will do the job. Finally, if the restriction of
A
to
\mathrm{Ker}\,P
is equal to zero, then take any nonzero
x\in\mathrm{Ker}\,P
, split
\mathrm{Ker}\,P=\mathbb{C}x\oplus\mathcal{Y}
and choose an
f\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that
f(x)=2
and
f(\mathcal{Y}\oplus\mathrm{Im}\,P)=0
. Now the operator
B=x\otimes f
has the required property.

From now on, without loss of generality, we assume that
\phi(I)=I
. The map
X\mapsto-\phi(X)
has the same properties as
\phi
, so by
Step 3
, we get that
\phi(-I)=-I
. Consequently,
\phi({\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
and
\phi(-{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})})\subseteq-\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. Furthermore, observe that for every idempotent (anti-idempotent)
P
in the range of
\phi
, its pre-image
\phi^{-1}(P)
is an idempotent (anti-idempotent, resp.).

Step 4.

For every
P\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
we have
\phi(P-I)=\phi(P)-I
.

As
\phi(-{\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})})\subseteq-\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
, for an arbitrary rank-one idempotent
P
, we conclude that
\phi(P-I)=Q-I
for some nontrivial
Q\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. Since
Q
is in the range of
\phi
,
\phi^{-1}(Q)
is idempotent by the above observation. Hence, from
Q\circ(Q-I)=0
we know that
\phi^{-1}(Q)\circ(P-I)
is idempotent. More precisely,
\phi^{-1}(Q)\circ(P-I)=0
by Lemma
16
. Now, as
\mathrm{rank}\,P=1
and
\phi^{-1}(Q)
is a nonzero idempotent, applying Lemma
14
gives that
\phi^{-1}(Q)=P
and so,
\phi(P-I)=\phi(P)-I
.

Step 5.

P\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
if and only if
\phi(P)\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

Let us take any
P\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(X)
and note that
\phi(P)
is a nontrivial idempotent. As in the range of
\phi
there are all idempotents of rank one, we can choose an
R\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
such that
R\circ(\phi(P)-I)=0
. The previous step gives us
R\circ\phi(P-I)=0
and in turn, by Lemma
16
, we obtain that
\phi^{-1}(R)\circ(P-I)=0
. Hence, as
\mathrm{rank}\,P=1
and
\phi^{-1}(R)
is a nonzero idempotent, Lemma
14
provides that
\phi^{-1}(R)=P
and therefore,
\mathrm{rank}\,\phi(P)=\mathrm{rank}\,R=1
.

For the reverse implication, let
\phi(P)\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. By the observation made immediately prior to
Step 4
, it follows that
P
is an idempotent; moreover, due to the injectivity of
\phi
, this preimage is unique and nontrivial. To show that
\mathrm{rank}\,P=1
, suppose to the contrary that
\mathrm{rank}\,P>1
. In that case, there exists a rank-one idempotent
S\neq P
such that
S\circ(P-I)=0
. Applying Step 4, we conclude that
\phi(S)\circ\phi(P-I)=\phi(S)\circ(\phi(P)-I)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. Then, by Lemma
16
, it follows that
\phi(S)\circ(\phi(P)-I)=0
. Since
\phi(P)
is a rank-one idempotent and
\phi(S)
is a nonzero idempotent, Lemma
14
implies that
\phi(S)=\phi(P)
, contradicting the injectivity of
\phi
. Therefore,
\mathrm{rank}\,P=1
, and hence
P\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

Step 6.

For every
P\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
we have
\phi(-P)=-\phi(P)
.

The map
X\mapsto-\phi(-X)
,
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
, inherits the same preserving properties as
\phi
. So, applying
Step 5
for this particular map,
-\phi(-P)
is a rank-one idempotent for every
P\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

Let us write
\phi(-P)=-Q
for some
Q\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and we want to show that
Q=\phi(P)
. Using
Step 4
and the identity
-P\circ(P-I)=0
we obtain
\phi(-P)\circ\phi(P-I)=-Q\circ\left(\phi(P)-I\right)=Q\circ\left(I-\phi\left(P\right)\right)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. If
Q\neq\phi(P)
, by Lemma
15
there exists an
R\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
(and hence in the range of
\phi
) such that
Q\circ R=0
and
\phi(P)\circ R\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. From
Q\circ R=0
it follows that also
-Q\circ R=0
and therefore,
-P\circ\phi^{-1}(R)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. Since
\phi^{-1}(R)
is an idempotent (by the Observation preceding
Step 4
), Lemma
16
provides that
-P\circ\phi^{-1}(R)=0
. So,
P\circ\phi^{-1}(R)=0
while
\phi(P)\circ R\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
, a contradiction. Therefore,
Q=\phi(P)
as desired.

Let
\psi:\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
with
\psi(P)=\phi(P)
denote the restriction of
\phi
to the set of rank-one idempotents. Note that the assertions established in
Steps 4
,
5
and
6

\psi
.

Step 7.

The map
\psi
is bijective and preserves orthogonality in both directions, i.e.
P\bot Q
if and only if
\psi(P)\bot\psi(Q)
,
P,Q\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.

It is clear that
\psi
is surjective by

\phi

with

-P\circ Q=0
. Applying

we obtain
\phi(-P)\circ\phi(Q)=-\phi(P)\circ\phi(Q)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})

\phi(P)\circ\phi(Q)=0

\phi(P)\bot\phi(Q)
.

The proof of
Step 7

.

Now, if
\mathcal{X}
is infinite-dimensional the conditions of
[
16

such that

for all

. Similarly, if

is finite-dimensional, properties of

are fulfilled for both

.

Having this information at hand, consider the map

, possessing the same preserving properties as

. At first, we observe that it fixes all rank-one idempotents; this is due to

. To sum up,
\phi(P)=P
, for every

Step 8.

For every

and every

.

The claim is a direct consequence of Observation
6
. Indeed, let us suppose that there exists an

and

such that

and
\phi(A)\circ P=0
. By Observation

and so,
\phi(A)\circ(-P)\notin\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
, leading to a contradiction with
\phi(A)\circ P=0
.

Step 9.

\phi(K)=K
for every
K\in{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{X})
. Consequently, for every
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
and
K\in{\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{X})
we have
A\circ K\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})\Leftrightarrow\phi(A)\circ K\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
and

.

Applying

, the first part of the claim is an immediate consequence of Corollary

if and only if

Step 10.

\phi(N)=N

. Consequently, for every

and

and

.

For an arbitrary

, we can take a nonzero

and

. Then, take an

with

and, since

and

are linearly independent, we can choose a functional

such that

and

. The operators

and

are now orthogonal rank-one idempotents, and hence,

is a nontrivial idempotent. From

\phi(N)\circ(I-R)=0
. Furthermore, Lemma

\phi(N)x=0

\phi(N)

Hence, according to the decomposition

have the following matrix representations:

Now, for every

Since

\phi(N)\circ\phi(K_{\alpha})=\phi(N)\circ K_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}a+\frac{c}{2}\alpha&\frac{(a+d)}{2}\alpha\\
0&-d+\frac{c}{2}\alpha\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0=0

and

\phi(N)\circ\phi(H_{2})=\phi(N)\circ H_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}a+b&0\\
a+d&b-d\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0\in{\mathcal{I}}^{*}(\mathcal{X}),

for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. From the first equation, we obtain
a=c=d=0
, and from the second property, we conclude that
b=1
. Thus,
\phi(N)=N
as desired.

As
\phi(N)=N
and
\phi(-N)=-N
for every
N\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
, the consequence follows immediately from Observation
6
.

Step 11.

\phi(\lambda(P-Q))=\lambda(P-Q)
for every orthogonal
P,Q\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and every nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

For arbitrary orthogonal
P,Q\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
set
K:=\lambda(P-Q)
and
R:=P+Q\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{X})
. Since
K\circ(I-R)=0
, it follows that
\phi(K)\circ(I-R)=0
by
Step 9
. Applying Lemma
14
we conclude that
Kx=0=\phi(K)x
for every
x\in\mathrm{Ker}\,R
and that
\mathrm{Im}\,R
is
\phi(K)
-invariant subspace. With respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathrm{Im}\,R\oplus\mathrm{Ker}\,R
and choosing a suitable basis in
\mathrm{Im}\,R
, we can write
K
and
\phi(K)
in the following matrix forms:

K=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&-\lambda\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(K)=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0.

Next, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, consider the families of rank-one nilpotents

N_{\alpha}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
\beta&0\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0.

As
K\circ N_{\alpha}=0
and
K\circ M_{\beta}=0
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, using
Step 10
gives that

\phi(K)\circ\phi(N_{\alpha})=\phi(K)\circ N_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}c\alpha&(a+d)\alpha\\
0&c\alpha\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0=0

and

\phi(K)\circ\phi(M_{\beta})=\phi(K)\circ M_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}b\beta&0\\
(a+d)\beta&b\beta\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0=0,

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. This implies that
c=b=0
and
d=-a
. In turn,
\phi(K)=\frac{a}{\lambda}K
. Let us then introduce a rank-one nilpotent

N_{\lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{-1}&-1\\
\lambda^{-2}&-\lambda^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\oplus 0.

Then
K\circ N_{\lambda}=I\oplus 0
, while
\frac{a}{\lambda}K\circ N_{\lambda}=\frac{a}{\lambda}I\oplus 0
, obviously forcing
a=\lambda
since
a\neq 0
by
Step 1
. So,
\phi(K)=K
.

Step 12.

\phi(\lambda P)=\lambda P
for every
P\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

As
\lambda P
is now of rank one, Lemma
14
ensures that
\phi(\lambda P)=\mu P
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. Then, set
K:=\lambda^{-1}(P-Q)
, where
Q\in\mathcal{I}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
is orthogonal on
P
. Since
\lambda P\circ K=P\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
, we can conclude that
\phi(\lambda P)\circ\phi(K)=\mu P\circ K=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}P\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
by
Step 11
. Therefore,
\mu=\lambda
and so,
\phi(\lambda P)=\lambda P
.

Step 13.

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
be an arbitrary. Then
\phi(T)=T
for every
T\in{\mathcal{T}}(k;\lambda)
, where
k<\dim\mathcal{X}
.

We apply induction on
k
. Since the operator
T
can be written as
T=J_{k}(\lambda)\oplus 0
with respect to some basis of
\mathcal{X}
, Lemma
16
provides that
\phi(T)=B_{11}\oplus 0
with respect to the same basis of
\mathcal{X}
. The aim is to see that
B_{11}=J_{k}(\lambda)
.

For
k=1
the claim holds true by Corollary
18
. Then, assuming the claim holds for some
k
, applying Lemma
22
at the induction step completes the proof.

Step 14.

\phi(A)=A
for every
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
.

At first, let
A=\lambda I
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. Hence, from
A\circ\lambda^{-1}P=P\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
for every
P\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
, it follows that
\phi(A)\circ\lambda^{-1}P=\lambda^{-1}\phi(A)\circ P\in\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})
for every
P\in{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Lemma
12
then provides that
\phi(A)=\lambda I
.

Let us now assume that
A
is not scalar operator and denote
B:=\phi(A)
. So far, we have shown that

A\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})}\Leftrightarrow B\circ X\in{\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathcal{X})}

and

A\circ X=0\Leftrightarrow B\circ X=0
,
for every
X\in\mathcal{T}
. Then Lemma
25
finally closes the proof.

\Box

Proof of Theorems
4
and
5
.
Define the map
\psi:{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
by
\psi(X)=\sqrt{2\alpha}\;\phi\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}X\right)
, for every
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X})
. The map
\psi
is well-defined and satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
4
in the infinite-dimensional setting. For the finite-dimensional case, we can assume without loss of any generality that the map
\phi
is of the form
\psi([x_{ij}])=[\sigma(x_{ij})]
by Theorem
5
. Then

\phi([x_{ij}])=1/\sqrt{2\alpha}\,\psi([\sqrt{2\alpha}x_{ij}])=\sigma(\sqrt{2\alpha})/\sqrt{2\alpha}\,[\sigma(x_{ij})]=c_{\alpha}[\sigma(x_{ij})].

Thus, the desired result follows.

\Box
