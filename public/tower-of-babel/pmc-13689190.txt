Risk of mycoplasma sepsis after cesarean section: a case-control study
Authors: Wanying Zhao, Yu Shen, Huifen Xiang, Dan Gao, Xianyi Yu, Liling Zhong, Jianning Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCesarean section (CS), a widely utilized mode of delivery globally, is associated with elevated risks of bloodstream infections (BSIs). Atypical organisms such as Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum are often overlooked in clinical practice. Despite their potential to cause severe postpartum sepsis, risk factors and clinical course for mycoplasma BSIs remain poorly defined.MethodsData were collected from 14,454 women who underwent cesarean delivery at Foshan Maternal and Child Health Hospital. Among them, 27 cases developed sepsis caused by mycoplasma infection. For each case, a control group was established using 1:4 matching on age and admission time. A conditional logistic regression model was employed for statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 135 participants between January 2024 and August 2025 were included. Multivariate analysis identified preoperative leukocytosis as an independent risk factor for postpartum bloodstream infection, with a significant dose-response relationship (OR = 1.93 per 1 × 10⁹/L increase; 95% CI: 1.31–2.82). A clinically relevant WBC cutoff of 10.5 × 10⁹/L was established, above which the infection risk increased more than eightfold (OR = 8.19; 95% CI: 2.31–29.04). subgroup analysis further indicated that preoperative leukocytosis was strongly associated with Ureaplasma urealyticum infection (OR = 33.66; P = 0.011), but not with Mycoplasma hominis. Several procedural factors also showed elevated though non-significant associations with infection risk.DiscussionPreoperative leukocytosis was significantly associated with Ureaplasma urealyticum bloodstream infection. This readily available marker could aid in identifying high-risk patients prior to cesarean delivery. Background

Cesarean section (CS) has evolved into a predominant mode of delivery globally, accounting for 21.1% of world live births, with averages ranging from 5% in sub-Saharan Africa to 42.8% in Latin America [1]. This trend is particularly pronounced in Turkey, which has the highest CS rate among OECD nations, and similarly, rates as high as 58% have been observed in a southern state of India [2, 3].

Mycoplasma species, as atypical pathogens, are often overlooked during empirical antimicrobial therapy in clinical practice. Mycoplasma hominis (Mh) is a commensal organism colonizing the human oral cavity, respiratory tract, and urogenital tract [4, 5]. It is primarily associated with genitourinary and respiratory infections, though rare cases of extrapulmonary manifestations—such as brain abscesses, osteomyelitis, wound infections, and joint infections—have been reported [6–9]. Ureaplasma urealyticum (Uu) has been reclassified by the International Committee on Systematics of Prokaryotes into two distinct species: Ureaplasma parvum (Up) and Ureaplasma urealyticum (Uu). Among these, Up is more frequently identified as a commensal organism, whereas Uu exhibits a stronger association with pathogenic conditions, such as male urethritis and male infertility, as well as vaginitis, cervicitis, and pelvic inflammatory disease in women [10–12]. It colonizes the lower genital mucosa of healthy adults, often without symptoms. Although the majority of published studies on mycoplasma infections have focused on urogenital manifestations, research regarding bloodstream infections remains limited and consists predominantly of case reports, largely due to the scarcity of documented cases. To date, no well-established risk factors have been clearly delineated. Most reported BSIs cases occur in patients with a recent history of surgical procedures.

Despite their established role in urogenital pathology, mycoplasmal bloodstream infections remain significantly underdiagnosed due to their fastidious growth requirements, lack of routine culture in standard laboratories, and frequent oversight in empirical antibiotic regimens—which often lack coverage against these resistant pathogens. Clinically, mycoplasma infections in post-cesarean patients may present with nonspecific signs such as persistent fever, leukocytosis, or unexplained inflammatory response, often leading to delayed or misdirected treatment due to their absence in conventional bacterial culture protocols [13]. The diagnostic challenge compounded by their intrinsic resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics, which are frequently used as first-line prophylactic agents. Without timely species-specific detection—often requiring Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) or specialized serological testing—these infections can progress to severe sepsis, organ dysfunction, or even septic shock. Mycoplasma hominis infections may be important preventable causes of adverse pregnancy outcomes in identifiable subgroups of women [14]. Current understanding relies heavily on isolated case reports, leaving critical knowledge gaps in risk stratification, diagnostic timing, optimized antimicrobial prophylaxis, and targeted treatment strategies.

This study specifically focuses on postpartum women following cesarean delivery—a population with elevated susceptibility to invasive mycoplasmal infections due to the combination of surgical tissue injury, potential bacterial translocation from the lower genital tract, and postpartum immune modulation. By analyzing clinical, microbiological, and epidemiological data from confirmed cases, we seek to identify specific risk factors—such as operative duration, premature rupture of membranes, and timing of antibiotic prophylaxis—that may predispose patients to mycoplasma bacteremia. Ultimately, our findings are expected to contribute to improved surveillance protocols and evidence-based management approaches for mycoplasma-related sepsis in obstetric populations, reducing associated morbidity and healthcare burdens.

Materials and methods

Study population

All obstetric inpatients who underwent cesarean delivery in Foshan Maternal and Child Health Care Center between January 2024 and August 20, 2025 were included in the study(N = 15,030). We further excluded individuals who met one or more of the following criteria: (1) not having been discharged from the hospital and lacking important data(N = 12), (2) patients combine with positive results from other bacterial cultures(N = 564). A total of 14,454 patients remained in the current study. Among the 27 patients with bloodstream infections related to mycoplasma, a 1:4 matching was conducted with non-infected women (confirmed by blood culture) based on age and the month of admission. Our control group design followed the guidelines of case-control studies, using stratified random sampling to ensure comparability of the two groups in major clinical characteristics.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the Infectious Disease Real-time Monitoring System, which is fully interoperable with the Laboratory Information System (LIS) and Hospital Information System (HIS), ensuring high data integrity. Variables collected included: demographic characteristics (e.g., age, BMI), obstetric history (e.g., parity, gestational age), intraoperative details (e.g., duration of surgery, estimated blood loss), postoperative outcomes, and laboratory findings (e.g., white blood cell count, C-reactive protein). Intraoperative blood loss was determined by summing the blood volume calculated using the Volume Method and the Gravimetric Method. For more details, please refer to the Supplementary methods, Table S9-11.

Case definition

A confirmed case of mycoplasma bloodstream infection was defined as the presence of clinical evidence of systemic infection, characterized by a postoperative fever ≥ 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) and at least one positive blood culture specifically identifying Uu or Mh.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) depending on distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) for categorical variables.

To identify independent risk factors for mycoplasma sepsis, conditional logistic regression analyses were employed. Clinically relevant covariates and variables demonstrating univariate associations with a significance level of P < 0.05 were considered for inclusion to mitigate the risk of omitting potentially important predictors. Variable selection was performed using a bidirectional stepwise elimination approach based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to ensure a parsimonious yet robust final model. This data-driven approach balanced model fit with complexity.The final model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, with a non-significant result (P > 0.05) indicating satisfactory calibration. The results of the regression analysis are presented as adjusted odds ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To identify the optimal preoperative white blood cell (WBC) threshold for predicting postoperative sepsis, we utilized the ‘cutpointr’ package in R, employing Youden’s J statistic as the optimization criterion [15]. The resulting cutoff (10.5 × 10⁹/L) was converted into a binary variable and incorporated into the multivariate model for adjusted analysis.

To further explore the relationship between preoperative leukocytosis and infection, the population was stratified by pathogen species (Mh vs. Uu). The matching and regression procedures were repeated within each subgroup to assess consistency.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. Initially, the matching ratio was adjusted by performing comparative analyses using different ratios, such as 1:3 versus 1:5, to evaluate the stability and consistency of the results. Secondly, outliers were excluded by removing extreme cases, such as patients with excessively prolonged hospital stays, followed by a re-analysis to assess the influence of these outliers on the overall findings. Lastly, statistical approaches were modified by exploring alternative methods to validate the consistency and reliability of the results, thereby ensuring methodological rigor and accuracy.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.0.3, R Core Team, https://www.r-project.org). A 2-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Characteristic of postpartum women

Cases and controls were matched at a 1:4 ratio based on age and admission time, a total of 135 individuals were included in the analysis, with 27 assigned to the case group and 108 to the control group (Table 1, Table S1). As expected due to matching, the mean age was identical between the two groups (28.96 ± 3.42 years for cases vs. 28.96 ± 3.47 years for controls). The case group exhibited higher WBC (mean 11.57, SD 2.20 × 10⁹/L) compared to controls (mean 9.18, SD 1.92 × 10⁹/L), along with a greater number of invasive procedures (mean 1.33, SD 0.62 vs. mean 1.09, SD 0.29) and higher intraoperative blood loss (mean 494.44, SD 261.04 mL vs. mean 373.98, SD 141.86 mL). Notably, cases showed a significantly higher prevalence of obesity (11.1% vs. 0.9%) and a markedly different profile of intraoperative antibacterial therapy, with predominant use of mycoplasma-related agents (63.0% vs. 6.5%) compared to cephalosporins in controls (37.0% vs. 89.8%). Emergency surgeries were more frequent among cases (96.3% vs. 70.4%), and both preoperative antibiotic duration and urinary catheterization time were longer in the case group. No significant differences were observed in preoperative hospital stay, comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension), obstetric history, or preoperative prophylactic antibiotic selection between groups.

Risk factors for bloodstream infection after cesarean delivery

The analysis identified WBC as a statistically significant independent predictor (Fig. 1), with each unit increase associated with a 93% increase in the odds of the outcome (95% CI: 1.31–2.82, P < 0.001). Several other factors demonstrated strong but statistically non-significant associations with increased odds of the outcome, including the number of surgeries during the current hospitalization (OR = 5.72, P = 0.067), duration of urinary catheterization (OR = 8.94, P = 0.101), and duration of preoperative prophylactic antibiotic use (OR = 5.42, P = 0.128). Intraoperative blood loss also showed a trend towards significance (OR = 1.01 per unit, P = 0.087). Factors such as obesity, surgical type, preoperative hospital stay, and surgical duration were not found to be significantly associated with the outcome in this model.

Clinical risk threshold verification for WBC

To further explore the clinical relevance of WBC, an optimal cutoff value of 10.5 × 10⁹/L was established using rigorous statistical methods. Patients were stratified into two clinically meaningful groups: those with normal or mildly elevated WBC (≤ 10.5 × 10⁹/L) and those with markedly elevated WBC (> 10.5 × 10⁹/L). This secondary analysis reinforced the critical prognostic importance of preoperative leukocytosis (Fig. 2). A preoperative WBC count exceeding 10.5 × 10⁹/L emerged as a potent and statistically independent risk factor (OR = 8.19, 95% CI: 2.31–29.04, P = 0.001). Specifically, patients with WBC levels above this threshold exhibited more than an eight-fold increase in the odds of the outcome compared to those with values at or below the cutoff.

Infection type-specific associations of preoperative leukocytosis with postoperative mycoplasma infections

Our stratified analysis revealed distinct patterns of association between preoperative leukocytosis and different mycoplasma infection subgroups following cesarean section (Table 2, Table S5-7). For Mh infections (13 cases VS. 52 controls), elevated preoperative WBC levels demonstrated no statistically significant association with postoperative infection risk. In contrast, for Uu infections (12 cases VS. 48 controls), preoperative leukocytosis emerged as a strong and statistically significant risk factor. After adjusting for potential confounders, patients with elevated WBC levels preoperatively had 33.66 times higher odds of developing postoperative Uu infections compared to those with normal levels (95% CI: 2.26–500.50, P = 0.011).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using alternative matching ratios (1:5 and 1:3) and median-based imputation for missing white blood cell data yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Table S2-S4). Preoperative white blood cell count remained significantly associated with the outcome across all models (P < 0.01). The stability of these associations underscores the robustness of the primary findings.

Discussions

In the present study, a key finding is that preoperative leukocytosis exhibited a particularly strong and statistically significant association with postoperative Uu bloodstream infection, whereas no such association was observed for Mh infection. Furthermore, a clinically actionable leukocyte threshold of 10.5 × 10⁹/L was identified, beyond which infection risk increased more than eightfold. Notably, while other factors—such as number of invasive procedures and intraoperative blood loss—did not reach statistical significance, they showed trends suggesting potential clinical relevance.

The significant association between preoperative leukocytosis and postoperative ureaplasma sepsis suggests that subclinical inflammation or pre-existing immune activation may play a role in increasing the susceptibility to postoperative infections. Preoperative leukocytosis is an established predictor of adverse outcomes in various malignancies and sepsis [16–20]. While physiological WBC counts during pregnancy may extend to 15.0 × 10⁹/L [21], our study identified a threshold exceeding 10.5 × 10⁹/L as significantly associated with a higher risk of Ureaplasma bloodstream infection. It should be noted that our study identified this risk even within this extended ‘normal’ range. This observation is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that using a 15 × 10⁹/L cutoff offers limited diagnostic sensitivity and specificity (53% and 73%, respectively) [21]. Mechanistically, this threshold effect may be explained by the transition from bacterial colonization to invasive infection. This threshold effect may be understood through the lens of host-pathogen interactions: even a moderate increase in leukocytes can reflect an underlying, dysregulated immune response where pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) have already primed the host immune system [22]. Host responses to Ureaplasma infection exhibit marked heterogeneity, driven by genetic predispositions (e.g., PRR and TNF-α polymorphisms), sepsis-induced immunosuppression, and specific infection compartmentalization, which collectively explain the clinical spectrum from asymptomatic colonization to severe sepsis [23–26]. Consequently, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for underlying infection when WBC levels approach the upper quartile, particularly for atypical pathogens like Ureaplasma that may lack overt acute inflammatory signs.

Stratified analyses revealed significant differences in the association between leukocytosis and sepsis caused by different Mycoplasma species. While we observed a positive trend, the analysis did not identify preoperative leukocytosis as a statistically significant predictor for Mh sepsis, which may be partially attributed to the limited sample size within this subgroup and insufficient statistical power to detect a significant association. In contrast, a very strong association was found between leukocytosis and Uu infection (OR = 33.66; P = 0.011). Although the odds ratio should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of cases, this statistically significant trend remains clinically indicative. The divergence in predictive value between these two species may also reflect genuine differences in virulence factors, host immune recognition, or tissue tropism.

The management of mycoplasma infections in obstetrics is constrained by both diagnostic limitations and treatment challenges. Conventional culture is slow and insensitive for fastidious organisms like Ureaplasma, but molecular techniques such as multiplex PCR address this gap: one study found multiplex PCR detected significantly more pathogens in sepsis than standard culture (98 vs. 21, p < 0.0001) and accelerated antimicrobial decision-making by a median of 50 h [27]. Nevertheless, the implementation of PCR warrants caution. Universal screening carries substantial cost implications, and clinical judgment is still required to rigorously distinguish asymptomatic colonization from active infection. Applying this rapid screening to our identified high-risk group — parturients with preoperative leukocytosis exceeding 10.5 × 10⁹/L — would enable early targeted intervention. From a treatment perspective, while multidrug resistance in Mh often limits effective therapy to contraindicated tetracyclines, Uu remains susceptible to azithromycin, which is safe in pregnancy and lactation [28, 29]. This supports the strategy of preoperative azithromycin prophylaxis for high-risk women, which is consistent with ACOG guidelines for non-elective cesarean delivery [30]. Although intraoperative blood loss did not reach formal statistical significance in the multivariable model, it demonstrated strong trends toward association with infection risk (P = 0.087). Excessive intraoperative bleeding not only leads to insufficient tissue perfusion and ischemia-hypoxia, but also may cause immune dysfunction, thereby significantly increasing the risk of postoperative infection. Furthermore, resultant anemia and hypoproteinemia impair both cellular and humoral immunity, reducing the parturient’s capacity to clear pathogens [31, 32]. Hematomas or accumulated blood within the surgical site and uterine cavity provide an ideal medium for bacterial proliferation, particularly facilitating the growth of facultative anaerobes such as Mycoplasma hominis, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Enterobacteriaceae [33]. These pathogens can enter the bloodstream through disrupted endometrial vessels, leading to bacteremia or even sepsis. In clinical practice, significant intraoperative hemorrhage is often associated with more frequent intrauterine manipulations, prolonged surgical exposure, and a higher likelihood of re-intervention—all of which increase the risk of exogenous pathogen inoculation and mucosal barrier compromise.

The strengths of this study include its status as the first case-control investigation of risk factors for maternal mycoplasma sepsis, utilizing rigorous 1:4 matching between cases and controls to enhance intergroup comparability and control for potential confounders. Additionally, it identified preoperative WBC as a novel prenatal predictive marker. However, several limitations must be considered. The most notable limitation is the small sample size, with only 27 cases included. Although a 1:4 matching approach was utilized to improve statistical efficiency, the limited number of cases may have inflated the odds ratio (e.g., OR = 33.66) due to the instability of small sample estimates. Despite this potential influence on effect size, the statistically significant trend identified remains clinically indicative and provides valuable preliminary evidence for the association. Additionally, as a retrospective single-center study, the findings may be limited in generalizability. Notably, the absence of a control group for bloodstream infections caused by other pathogens is a limitation; however, since our primary aim was to compare predictive differences between Mycoplasma species rather than versus other bacteria, this omission does not diminish the internal validity of our results. Additionally, as a retrospective study relying on standard culture techniques, the true incidence of infection might be underestimated compared to molecular methods like PCR. Finally, residual confounding factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that preoperative leukocytosis is a clinically valuable predictor of postoperative Uu sepsis following cesarean delivery. Integration of this readily accessible parameter into preoperative evaluations may improve risk stratification, facilitate targeted prophylactic interventions, support antibiotic stewardship efforts, and contribute to improved postoperative outcomes. Further validation in larger multi-center cohorts and investigation into the efficacy of targeted prophylaxis in high-risk populations are warranted.
