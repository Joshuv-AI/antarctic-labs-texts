Summability of Elliptic Functions Via Residues
Matthew William Babbitt

Abstract
Summability has been a central object of study in difference algebra over the past half-century. It serves as a cornerstone of algebraic methods to study linear recurrences over various fields of coefficients and with respect to various kinds of difference operators. Recently, Dreyfus, Hardouin, Roques, and Singer introduced a notion of elliptic orbital residues, which altogether serve as a partial obstruction to summability for elliptic functions with respect to the shift by a non-torsion point over an elliptic curve. We explain how to refine this into a complete obstruction, which promises to be useful in applications of difference equations over elliptic curves, such as elliptic hypergeometric functions and the combinatorics of walks in the quarter plane.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Given a field
\mathcal{K}
and an automorphism
\tau\in\Aut(\mathcal{K})
, so-called
first-order linear difference equations
of the form

\tau(y)-y=b

(1.1)

for
b\in\mathcal{K}
and formal variable
y
, are of interest in the field of difference algebra. Should there exist a solution
y=m
to the above equation (
1.1
) where
m\in\mathcal{K}
, we say that
b
is
summable
. This leads naturally to the question of which elements of
\mathcal{K}
are summable: the dissertation before you lays a theoretical framework for answering this question in the case that
\mathcal{K}
is the field of meromorphic functions over an elliptic curve
E
, and
\tau
is a shift automorphism on
\mathcal{K}
by a non-torsion point of
E
.

This work is positioned inside a larger program of calculating
differential Galois groups of linear difference equations
over elliptic curves. Deciding the summability of an elliptic function is a key subproblem. This serves in particular as a part of the computation of such differential Galois groups for second-order linear difference equations over elliptic curves. The goal is to perform this computation in an algorithmic manner whereas current methods are either ad-hoc or partial in nature. While those methods serve their applications well, a systematic algorithm for the computation can facilitate further, more demanding results.

1.1
Difference Equations over Elliptic Curves

Elliptic curves first arose in connection with the problem of calculating arc lengths of ellipses. This quickly evolved into the study of doubly-periodic functions on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Since then, and particularly in more recent decades, they have seen more number theoretic applications, such as in the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem and in the construction of cryptographic schemes. In the past decade there have been some additional and surprising applications in more disparate fields: in the combinatorial study of analyzing walks confined to the first quadrant of the plane, and in an application towards mathematical physics via analyzing elliptic hypergeometric functions.

Elliptic curves can be defined in several different ways: for example, see
[
Sil09
,
Sil94
]
. Most familiar is a complex-analytic construction of the quotient space of
\mathbb{C}
modulo a
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
\Lambda
. Less familiar and more general is the multiplicative version
K^{\times}/q^{\mathbb{Z}}
, often called a Tate curve, where
K
is a complete normed field and
|q|<1
, covered in
[
Sil94
, Ch. V]
. In algebraic geometry, an elliptic curve can be defined over any field
K
as a curve of genus one with a distinguished
K
-rational point. In all contexts elliptic curves have the prominent feature of a
group law

\oplus
on the points of the curve, and the difference equations of interest are defined in terms of this group operation.

Many important phenomena concerning an elliptic curve
E
can be seen at the level of the field
\mathcal{K}
of meromorphic functions on
E
. The group law on
E
can be used to endow
\mathcal{K}
with the structure of a
difference field
as follows. Given a point
1
1

1

This point is usually assumed for technical reasons to be
non-torsion
, i.e. have infinite order under the group law.

s\in E
, the shift
z\mapsto z\oplus s
on
E
induces a shift automorphism

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle:f(z)\mapsto f(z\oplus s).

A
linear difference equation
over the elliptic curve
E
is an equation of the form

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}(z)\cdot\tau^{i}(y)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}(z)\cdot f(z\oplus is)=b(z),

(1.2)

where
a_{i}(z)\in\mathcal{K}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, where
a_{0}\cdot a_{n}\neq 0
, and
y=f(z)
is an unknown “function” (or perhaps a more general entity like a formal power series). If
b(z)=0
then the equation is said to be homogeneous.

A second application of linear difference equations over elliptic curves is to elliptic hypergeometric functions. These objects were studied in
[
Spi08
]
as a generalization of classical Euler–Gauss hypergeometric functions
\phantom{.}{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c;z)
. For
p,q\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that
p^{\mathbb{Z}}\cap q^{\mathbb{Z}}=\{1\}
and
|p|,|q|<1

\underline{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{8})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{8}
satisfying
\prod_{j=1}^{8}t_{j}=p^{2}q^{2}
, the
elliptic hypergeometric function
is

V(\underline{t}\ ;p,q):=\int_{S^{1}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{8}\Gamma(t_{j}z;p,q)\Gamma(t_{j}z^{-1};p,q)}{\Gamma(z^{2};p,q)\Gamma(z^{-2};p,q)}\cdot\frac{dz}{z};

where the
elliptic Gamma functions

\displaystyle\Gamma(z;p,q):=(pq/z;p,q)_{\infty}/(z;p,q)_{\infty};\quad\text{where}\quad(z;p,q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{j,k\geq 0}(1-p^{j}q^{k}z).

V(\underline{t};p,q)=f_{\varepsilon}(z)

E=\mathbb{C}^{*}/q^{\mathbb{Z}}
with respect to the (multiplicative) shift automorphism
\tau(f(z))=f(pz)
, whose coefficients are elements of
\mathcal{K}
that depend on some parameters
\underline{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{8})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{8}
. Recently it was shown in
[
ADR21

are hypertranscendental over
\mathcal{K}
.

1.2.1
Commonalities: Galois Theory

The key to both of the above results is differential Galois theory for difference equations. This theory, developed in
[
HS08
]
, associates with equation (
1.2
) a geometric object called the differential Galois group which encodes in its algebraic structure the differential properties of the solutions to equation (
1.2
). In particular, if the Galois group is “sufficiently large” then the Galois correspondence implies that there are “few” differential equations satisfied by the solutions to equation (
1.2
). Indeed, the work of
[
DHRS18
]
and
[
ADR21
]
in the applications above show that, for the specific difference equations over elliptic curves relevant to their respective applications, the associated differential Galois groups are so large that the solutions must be hypertranscendental.

Remarkably, these authors were successful in deducing the largeness of these differential Galois groups in the absence of a complete algorithm to compute them. However, such methods are inherently unable to yield
positive
results: should the solutions of some difference equation over an elliptic curve actually satisfy some nontrivial differential equations, there are no systematic methods to discover them. This dissertation is part of a larger program to enable this discovery.

A future aim of the author is to develop a suite of theoretical and algorithmic results to compute differential Galois groups associated to second-order linear difference equations over elliptic curves. This has been done in the contexts of linear difference equations over rational functions for various difference automorphisms: the shift automorphism in
[
Arr17
]
, and the
q
-difference automorphism in
[
AZ22
]
. A common subproblem in both algorithms is determining the summability of a rational function with those automorphisms. The recent work of
[
CS12
]
develops new technologies called
discrete residues
and
q
-discrete residues
, numbers that can be extracted from a rational function, that detect whether that rational function is summable. The goal of this thesis is to extend this technique to the elliptic case.

Problem
.

Given a meromorphic function over an elliptic curve, how can we extract constants from the function that let us decide whether this function is summable? In other words, can we find constants that form an
obstruction
to summability?

Chapter 2
Background on Difference Equations

The study of differential Galois theory of linear difference equations is one in a long line of Galois theories that extend past the classical Galois theory of polynomial equations. The initial study is of the Galois theory of differential equations, also named
Picard-Vessiot Theory
. The development of these theories is not only interesting, but it informs the approaches we will see in the coming chapters.

2.1
A History of Picard-Vessiot Theory

In order to study equations more involved than polynomial, we require algebras with more equipped machinery than a field and its operations. These constructs have their own subfields of algebra:

•

Differential algebra
, which lets us analyze a field
K_{1}
equipped with a
derivation

\delta:K_{1}\to K_{1}
satisfying the addition and Leibniz rules

\delta(a+b)=\delta(a)+\delta(b),\qquad\delta(ab)=\delta(a)b+a\delta(b)

for all
a,b\in K_{1}
. The data
(K_{1},\delta)
is referred to as a
differential field
.

•

Difference algebra
, which lets us analyze a field
K_{2}
equipped with an automorphism
\tau
. The data
(K_{2},\tau)
is referred to as a
difference field
.

The study of these structures lets us systematically from an algebraic viewpoint analyze
linear differential equations
and
linear difference equations
, respectively of the forms

\displaystyle\delta^{n}(y)+a_{n-1}\delta^{n-1}(y)+\cdots+a_{1}\delta(y)+a_{0}y=0,

\displaystyle\tau^{n}(y)+b_{n-1}\tau^{n-1}(y)+\cdots+b_{1}\tau(y)+b_{0}y=0,

for
a_{i}\in K_{1}
,
b_{j}\in K_{2}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Algebraic developments in the 19th and 20th century led to the birth and actualization of a Galois theory for each of these two types of equations, both of which were endowed with the name
Picard-Vessiot theory
. These fields of study are more commonly referred to as the Galois Theories of Linear Differential and Difference equations.

The usual study of Galois theory examines polynomial equations: to a polynomial with coefficients in a field
K
is associated a splitting field
L
containing its roots, and Galois theory studies the
K
-automorphisms of
L
. In 1948, Ellis R. Kolchin developed in
[
Kol48
]
such a theory for linear differential equations that uses a
Picard-Vessiot extension
in the place of a splitting field. The Galois theory of difference equations, however, requires the use of rings instead of fields, and was only formulated in 1997 by Marius van der Put and Michael F. Singer in
[
vdPS97
]
. There is little room in the thesis for a comprehensive treatment, but the interested reader can find a history of the Galois theory of linear differential equations in
[
Bor01
, Chapter VIII]
, and the preface of
[
vdPS97
]
gives a precise timeline of the development of the Galois theory of linear difference equations.

Remark 2.1.1
.

Borel’s essay
[
Bor01
]
on the Galois theory of linear differential equations is quite illuminating as it contextualizes its development alongside that of Lie theory and the theory of linear algebraic groups. It also explains the contributions of Picard and Vessiot to their namesake theory, whereas the name and the primary development of the theory are due to Kolchin!

The Galois theories above are each focused on finding and analyzing a Galois group. In the case of the Galois theory of linear differential equations, one computes a
differential Galois group
of their linear differential equation, and this encodes the “differential symmetries” satisfied by the solutions to such an equation. An analogous viewpoint holds for the Galois theory of linear difference equations.

A natural question then is how one can find the
differential
symmetries of a linear
difference
equation. Hardouin and Singer established this new Galois theory in 2008 in
[
HS08
]
, naming the paper and the theory the
differential Galois theory of linear difference equations
. This dissertation ties directly into problems in this particular Galois theory.

2.1.1
Second-Order Linear Difference Equations

Each time a Galois theory of linear difference equations is formulated, mathematicians begin by applying it to second-order linear difference equations: those of the form

\tau^{2}(y)+a\tau(y)+b=0

(2.1)

where

Remark 2.1.2

It is a result of the general theory that the differential Galois group of equation (

In 2015 Dreyfus and Roques created an algorithm in

The algorithms in

2.2
Residues in the Case of Rational Functions

Here we motivate and summarize the work of
[
CS12
]
on the development of residues as an obstruction to summability. We will be drawing parallels between this section and future chapters.

In this section,
C
refers to an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. We work with rational functions: elements of the field
\mathcal{K}=C(x)
. Recall, the elements of
\mathcal{K}
are ratios of polynomials with coefficients in
C
. Finally, as started in the previous section
\tau
is the rational shift automorphism on
\mathcal{K}
given by
\tau(f(x))=f(x+1)
for all
f(x)\in C(x)
.

The fact that
C
is algebraically closed gives us the crucial tool of partial fraction decompositions, and the theory of residues is implemented through the lens of such expressions of rational functions. The following result is a formalization of the concept of partial fraction decompositions.

Proposition 2.2.1
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathcal{K}
be expressed as
a(x)/b(x)
, where the polynomials
a(x),b(x)\in C[x]
share no linear factors. Let
\mathcal{R}\subset C
be the set of zeros of
b(x)
. Then there exist constants
c_{j}(f,\alpha)
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{R}
, and a polynomial
g(x)\in C[x]
, such that

f(x)=g(x)+\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\alpha)}{(x-\alpha)^{j}}.

The fact that
C
is algebraically closed is necessary for
b(x)
to split into linear factors of the form
(x-\alpha)
. The sum is of finite support: if
j
is greater than the multiplicity of
\alpha
as a root of
b(x)
then
c_{j}(f,\alpha)=0
. For instance, the following is a partial fraction decomposition of a rational function in
\mathbb{C}(x)
:

\frac{3x^{6}-12x^{5}+11x^{4}+9x^{3}-28x^{2}+39x-12}{(x-2)^{2}(x^{2}+1)}=3x^{2}-4+\frac{3}{x-i}+\frac{3}{x+i}-\frac{1}{x-2}+\frac{2}{(x-2)^{2}}.

Remark 2.2.2
.

There is motivation to viewing the partial fraction decomposition of a rational function as an aggregation of its local expansions at its poles. In the above example the local expansions of the above function at the poles
i
,
-i
, and
2
are respectively

\displaystyle\frac{3}{(x-i)^{1}}+

\displaystyle\left[3x^{2}-4+\frac{3}{x+i}-\frac{1}{x-2}+\frac{2}{(x-2)^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{3}{(x+i)^{1}}+

\displaystyle\left[3x^{2}-4+\frac{3}{x-i}-\frac{1}{x-2}+\frac{2}{(x-2)^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(x-2)^{2}}+\frac{-1}{(x-2)^{1}}+

\displaystyle\left[3x^{2}-4+\frac{3}{x-i}+\frac{3}{x+i}\right],

with the bracketed expressions having no poles at
i
,
-i
, and
2
respectively. Each unbracketed expression is called the
principal part
of the local expansion near that particular pole. In the rational case we have the luxury of this aggregation forming a
global
expression for the rational function. Partial fraction decompositions do not exist in the elliptic context. They will be replaced with more technical tools in Chapters
3
and
4
.

The residues defined in
[
CS12
]
are expressed in terms of such partial fraction decompositions. We first analyze this in the case of the shift automorphism.

2.2.1
Discrete Residues

Define the shift automorphism
\tau:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
by
\tau(f(x))=f(x+1)
. We are interested in for which rational functions
f(x)\in\mathcal{K}
there exist
g(x)\in\mathcal{K}
for which

\tau(g(x))-g(x)=f(x).

We call such
f(x)

summable
.

An immediate observation is that all polynomials in
C(x)
are summable. Indeed, for
n\geq 1
we have

\tau(x^{n})-x^{n}=(x+1)^{n}-x^{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}x^{k},

and this collection of polynomials forms a basis of
C[x]
. These polynomials are summable by construction, and because
\tau
is a field automorphism it follows that all elements of their span
C[x]
are summable.

The question of whether rational functions are summable is slightly harder. But with sufficient numerical experiments we can see patterns that are suggestive to proper summability criteria. Suppose we construct the following rational function in
\mathbb{C}(x)
from its partial fraction decomposition:

g(x)=\frac{3}{x-1}-\frac{4}{x-2}+\frac{7}{x^{2}}+\frac{1}{x-\pi}.

Then acting
\tau-\Id
on
g(x)
yields

\displaystyle\tau(g(x))-g(x)=\

\displaystyle\frac{3}{x}-\frac{7}{x-1}+\frac{4}{x-2}

\displaystyle+\frac{7}{(x+1)^{2}}-\frac{7}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{x-\pi+1}-\frac{1}{x-\pi}.

The right-hand side is summable by definition. We have sorted the terms so the poles are grouped by:

•

Orbits of the underling shift action of
x\mapsto x+1
on
\mathbb{C}
,

•

Orders of the poles in each degree.

Within each group the numerators sum to zero. This will hold in all experiments, and in fact is exactly the summability criterion in
[
CS12
]
. These sums are what they refer to as discrete residues, as defined in
[
CS12
, Definition 2.3]
.

Definition 2.2.3
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathcal{K}
have partial fraction decomposition given by

f(x)=g(x)+\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\alpha)}{(x-\alpha)^{j}},

where
\mathcal{R}
is the set of poles of
f(x)
, the constants
c_{j}(f,\alpha)\in C
are the coefficients of the principal parts of the local expansion of
f(x)
near
\alpha
, and
g(x)\in C[x]
is a polynomial. For a
\beta\in C
, let
[\beta]
be the
\mathbb{Z}
-orbit
\{\beta+n\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Then the
discrete residue
of
f
at the orbit
[\beta]
of order
j
is

\text{dres}(f,[\beta],j)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{j}(f,\beta+n).

These discrete residues then form an obstruction to summability of rational functions, as shown in
[
CS12
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Theorem 2.2.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}
. Then
f
is summable with respect to the shift automorphism if and only if the discrete residues of
f
at all orbits and all orders vanish.

2.2.2
q
-Discrete Residues

In this section we still consider the field of rational functions
\mathcal{K}=C(x)
, but now we consider the
q
-difference automorphism
\tau:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}
defined by
\tau(f(x))=f(qx)
, where
q
is not a root of unity in
C

\tau\left(\frac{x+1}{x^{2}+3x+2}\right)=\frac{qx+1}{q^{2}x^{2}+3qx+2}.

This new automorphism produces complications to the formulation of residues in terms of a partial fraction decomposition. In the previous subsection, the shift difference automorphism shifted every pole by
1
. Now, the
q
-difference automorphism does not shift any pole at
0

\mathcal{K}
sends

to
qx
. In all, one must use the following form of the partial fraction decomposition:

f(x)=c+xp_{1}(x)+\frac{p_{2}(x)}{x}+\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{c_{j}(f,q^{\ell}\cdot\alpha)}{(x-q^{\ell}\cdot\alpha)^{j}}

(2.2)

where
c\in C
is constant,
p_{1}(x),p_{2}(x)\in C[x]
are polynomials, and
\mathcal{R}
is a maximal set of poles of
f(x)
that are in distinct
q^{\mathbb{Z}}
-orbits. The definition of
q
-discrete residues is then the following, from
[
CS12
, Definition 2.7]

Definition 2.2.5
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}
have a partial fraction decomposition as in Equation (
2.2
). Then the
q
-discrete residue of
f
at the
q^{\mathbb{Z}}
-orbit
[\beta]_{q}
of multiplicity

is defined to be

\text{qres}(f,[\beta]_{q},j)=\sum\limits_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{-\ell j}c_{j}(f,q^{\ell}\cdot\beta).

In addition, the constant

q
-discrete residue of

at infinity, denoted by

These

-discrete residues form a complete obstruction to summability for the

-difference automorphism, as shown in
[

Theorem 2.2.6

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}
. Then
f
is summable with respect to the
q
-difference automorphism if and only if the

-discrete residues of

at all orbits and all orders vanish, as well as the
q
-discrete residue at

This shows that even in a case as simple as rational functions, the summability obstruction for a difference automorphism can become quite involved very quickly. In future chapters we will see the technology of residues ramping up in complexity, but this technology proves to be an effective obstruction to summability in the elliptic context as well as that of rational functions.

3.1

The initial formulation of elliptic curves came from the study of doubly-periodic meromorphic functions on the complex plane. Jacobi proved in 1835 in

.

Each strategy presents its own difficulties, but a commonality is that we no longer have easily accessible prototypes of functions with a small number of poles in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
.

3.1.1
The Weierstrass Elliptic Functions

The first literary example of a nonconstant elliptic function is quite commonly the Weierstrass
\wp
function. This function is manually constructed to have poles of order two at precisely the lattice points in
\Lambda
, and the value is given via a series carefully constructed to satisfy convergence wherever it is defined.

Definition 3.1.4
.

The
Weierstrass elliptic function
The function
\wp_{\Lambda}(z):\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
associated with
\Lambda
is defined by the following series:

\wp_{\Lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Lambda\\
\lambda\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{(z-\lambda)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

It is a classical result that
\wp_{\Lambda}(z)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. Furthermore the series definition of
\wp_{\Lambda}(z)
can be differentiated to obtain a series expression for
\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(z)
, another elliptic function. These two functions in fact satisfy a polynomial relationship:

Proposition 3.1.5
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\Lambda
,

\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(z)^{2}=4\wp_{\Lambda}(z)^{3}-g_{2}(\Lambda)\wp_{\Lambda}(z)-g_{3}(\Lambda)

where the constants
g_{2}(\Lambda)
and
g_{3}(\Lambda)
are given by the following Eisenstein series:

g_{2}(\Lambda)=60\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Lambda\\[1.63885pt]
\lambda\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},\qquad g_{3}(\Lambda)=140\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Lambda\\[1.63885pt]
\lambda\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}.

This polynomial relationship leads to the
Weierstrass equation

y^{2}=4x^{3}-g_{2}(\Lambda)x-g_{3}(\Lambda)
. We will see this sort of cubic equation again in the algebraic perspective. Indeed, Weierstrass equations are the
starting point
of that direction of inquiry!

An incredible fact is that the two examples discussed thus far are all that is required to formulate the entire field
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
of elliptic functions. That is, all elliptic functions in
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
can be characterized simply using
\wp_{\Lambda}(z)
and
\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(z)
as building blocks. Precisely stated:

Proposition 3.1.6
.

Any
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
can be expressed uniquely as

A(\wp_{\Lambda}(z))+\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}(z)\cdot B(\wp_{\Lambda}(z)),

where
A
and
B
are rational functions over
\mathbb{C}
. Conversely, all such expressions are elliptic functions.

Consequently
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
can be expressed as
\mathbb{C}(\wp_{\Lambda})[\wp_{\Lambda}^{\prime}]
.

For our purposes we require a function that is not actually elliptic. This function is still related to
\wp_{\Lambda}(z)
in a way that facilitates a method of expression for all elliptic functions. Once again one can show that the series definition in fact converges on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\Lambda
.

Definition 3.1.7
.

The
Weierstrass
\zeta
-function

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z):\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
associated with
\Lambda
is defined by the following series:

\zeta_{\Lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{z}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\Lambda\\[1.63885pt]
\lambda\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{z-\lambda}+\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{z}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

Remark 3.1.8
.

Because
\Lambda
is taken to be fixed and arbitrary throughout this dissertation, we make the practice of dropping the subscript
\Lambda
from both
\wp(z)
and
\zeta(z)
.

From the construction of
\zeta(z)
there are several immediate observations.

Proposition 3.1.9
.

The function
\zeta(z)
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

\zeta^{\prime}(z)=-\wp(z)
.

(b)

\zeta(z)
has only poles of order
1
located at the lattice points in
\Lambda
.

(c)

There exists an additive group homomorphism
\eta:\Lambda\to\mathbb{C}
associated to
\Lambda
called the
quasi-period map
that satisfies

\zeta(z+\lambda)=\zeta(z)+\eta(\lambda)

for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

The property (a) suggests that the Weierstrass
\zeta
-function can be used to construct all elliptic functions as well. Property (b) and Corollary
3.1.3
confirm that
\zeta(z)
is indeed not an elliptic function. Property (c) will be useful for computations that follow. It also implies the following fact that is crucial for later steps in our reduction process.

Corollary 3.1.10
.

For any constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the function
\zeta(z+\alpha)-\zeta(z)
is an elliptic function.

Proof.

The function is
\lambda
-periodic for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
:

\displaystyle\zeta(z+\lambda+\alpha)-\zeta(z+\lambda)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\big[\zeta(z+\alpha)-\zeta(z)\big]+\eta(\lambda)-\eta(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\zeta(z+\alpha)-\zeta(z).

∎

3.1.2
\zeta
-Expansions of Elliptic Functions

We recall that our immediate goal is to find an analogue for the partial fraction decompositions used in Chapter
2
that enables us to determine summability. Those partial fraction decompositions were expressed in terms of functions with poles of order
1
, so there should also exist expressions for elliptic functions using the Weierstrass
\zeta
-function.

One view of a partial fraction decomposition over
\mathbb{C}
is that it is an aggregation of all local expansions of a rational function. An expression for an arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
in terms of
\zeta(z)
could probably be constructed by using the series expansion of
\zeta(z)
to mimic local expansions at all poles of
f
, and then aggregating them in some way to produce the function
f
in a way that guarantees the expression to be elliptic.

Because
\zeta(z)
follows quasiperiodic behavior as per Proposition
3.1.9
, the expressions in terms of
\zeta(z)
are highly nonunique. This will be a mainstay of computations to follow.

Proposition 3.1.11
.

Let
f(z)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be any elliptic function, and let
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
be a set of poles of
f(z)
such that no two are equivalent modulo
\Lambda
, but any pole of
f(z)
is equivalent to
\alpha_{k}
modulo
\Lambda
for some
1\leq k\leq n
. Then there exist unique constants
c_{0}(f)
and
c_{j}(f,\alpha_{k})
for
1\leq k\leq n
and
j\geq 1
such that
f(z)
can be expressed as

f(z)=c_{0}(f)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}c_{j}(f,\alpha_{k})}{(j-1)!}\zeta^{(j-1)}(z-\alpha_{k}).

(3.1)

We call this a
\zeta
-expansion
of
f(z)
.

This is a classical result whose proof, while seldom recorded, can be found in
[
WW27
, pp. 449-450]
. We recall the proof here.

Proof.

We show that such a
\zeta
-expansion can be constructed if we let
c_{j}(f,\alpha_{k})
be the coefficient of the order
j
term of the principal part of
f(z)
near
\alpha_{k}
, for
j\geq 1
and
1\leq k\leq n
.

For the terms on the right-hand side, if
j\geq 2
then
\zeta^{(j-1)}(z-\alpha_{k})
is in fact elliptic with principal part having a single term that has a pole of order
j
. Thus if
c_{j}(f,\alpha_{k})
agrees with the coefficients of the principal part of
f
near
\alpha_{k}
for poles of order at least
2
, then the higher-order terms cancel and it suffices to assume that
f
only has simple poles. In this case, we wish to show that there is a constant
c_{0}(f)
such that

f(z)=c_{0}(f)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\zeta(z-\alpha_{k}).

If
c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})
agrees with the principal part of
f(z)
near
\alpha_{k}
, then they are the classical residues of
f(z)
from complex analysis, and from Theorem
3.1.2
these sum to zero. Then the right-hand side is indeed elliptic: for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\zeta(z+\lambda-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\cdot\eta(\lambda)+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\zeta(z-\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle=0+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\zeta(z-\alpha_{k}).

Therefore the difference

f(z)-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}c_{1}(f,\alpha_{k})\zeta(z-\alpha_{k})

is elliptic and entire, and therefore constant from Corollary
3.1.3
. Thus such a constant
c_{0}(f)
exists, finishing the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.1.11
shows that the coefficients of all the local expansions of an elliptic function
f(z)
can in fact be aggregated into a global expression for
f(z)
, mirroring Remark
2.2.2
. This will be the sort of construct we will reduce to find a summability criterion for elliptic functions.

Remark 3.1.12
.

Expressions of elliptic functions in terms of the Weierstrass
\zeta
-function exist sparsely in literature relative to the more favored expressions in terms of
\wp(z)
, or even as products of the Weierstrass
\sigma
-function (cf.
[
Sil94
, Proposition I.5.5]
). These expressions focus more on algebraic data, whereas the analytic nature of Proposition
3.1.11
has the advantage of the direct connection to local expansions of elliptic functions.

3.2
Finding a Summability Criterion

We have laid down all the necessary background on elliptic functions to discover a summability criterion in the analytic setting. Now we can formulate and solve this problem. After we place a difference algebra structure on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
and define preliminary terms, we describe a process of reducing the
\zeta
-expansion of an arbitrary
f(z)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
by summable elements. As a result,
f(z)
will be summable if and only if this reduction is zero.

Yet there is little benefit from a summability criterion that demands the computation of a reduction: it would be of much greater utility to have a criterion computable from
f(z)
without needing to calculate any reductions. Thus from the reduction we extract particular coefficients that we will declare to be the analytic panorbital residues of
f(z)
. These coefficients will be the key ingredient in our criterion for summability.

3.2.1
A Difference Structure on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda

When formulating the terminology in this subsection we borrow heavily from the work of
[
DHRS18
, Appendix B]
which formulates orbital residues of elliptic functions in the algebraic setting. What follows is an analytic reinterpretation.

We reaffirm that we are working with the elliptic functions
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
that are periodic with respect to the lattice

\Lambda=\Span_{\mathbb{Z}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

for a choice of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
with
\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{2}\not\in\mathbb{R}
. The difference algebra structure of interest is induced by the
shift automorphism

\tau
on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
defined in the following way: for a fixed
s\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{Q}\Lambda
, the automorphism
\tau
acts on a function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
by
3
3

3

It is easy to conflate the function
f
being a function on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
and being a function on
\mathbb{C}
. By declaring
s\in\mathbb{C}
, we are implicitly making the choice to view all functions in the following sections as being meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
, and all elements of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
are considered to be
\Lambda
-periodic meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}
.

\tau:f(z)\mapsto f(z+s).

The automorphism
\tau
places a difference field structure on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. We denote the corresponding shift on the points of
\mathbb{C}
by

\displaystyle\tau^{*}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\tau^{*}

\displaystyle:a\mapsto a-s

for all
a\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus
\tau^{-1}(f(a))=f(\tau^{*}(a))
for all
a\in\mathbb{C}
and
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. This implies that
\tau
shifts the poles of an elliptic function in accordance with
\tau^{*}
shifting those poles on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Put precisely:

Lemma 3.2.1
.

A point
p\in\mathbb{C}
is a pole of order
k
of
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
if and only if
\tau^{*}(p)
is a pole of order
k
of
\tau(f)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
.

Notice the condition placed on
s
that it not be an element of
\mathbb{Q}\Lambda
: this is equivalent to the automorphism
\tau
on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
having infinite order. This can also be phrased as
s
being a non-torsion point of the torus
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
.

Furthermore, the shift automorphism
\tau
commutes with differentiation because of the chain rule: this implies that given an elliptic function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
, a
\zeta
-expansion of
\tau(f)
is obtained by applying
\tau
to the individual terms of the
\zeta
-expansion of
f
.

Finally, the elements of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
that are fixed by
\tau
are those functions that are periodic with respect to
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and the non-torsion point
s
, and again by a classical result of Jacobi in
[
Jac35
]
this implies that those functions are constant. Thus if
g\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
satisfies
\tau(g)=g
then
g\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, for any
g\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}\backslash\mathbb{C}
there does not exist a positive integer
n
for which
\tau^{n}(g)=g
.

With this difference structure on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
we may now define summability.

Definition 3.2.2
.

An element
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
is called
summable
if there exists
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that
f=\tau(a)-a
.

Example 3.2.3
.

Because
\tau
is a field automorphism of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
the uniqueness of such a
a
is up to a term fixed by
\tau
, and as discussed before those terms are constants:

\displaystyle\tau(a_{1})-a_{1}

\displaystyle=\tau(a_{2})-a_{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\tau(a_{1}-a_{2})

\displaystyle=a_{1}-a_{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle a_{1}-a_{2}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{C}.

Example 3.2.4
.

Summability is equivalent to being in the image of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
under the operator
\tau-\Id
. The following telescoping series computation shows that the image of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
under
\tau^{n}-\Id
consists of summable functions:

\tau^{n}(a)-a=\tau\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\tau^{k}(a)\right]-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\tau^{k}(a).

This technique will feature in the reduction process. It also does not require the analytic structure of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
, so it is applicable to general difference fields.

Our reduction process will focus on cancelling poles of
f
by adding summable elements of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
to
f
. The poles of a summable elliptic function
\tau(a)-a
for
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
are related by
\tau^{*}
orbits, so we require language to analyze the poles on these orbits.

Because
s
is a non-torsion point of
\Lambda
, the elements of an individual orbit of
\tau^{*}
are all distinct modulo
\Lambda
. Any elliptic function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
has only finitely many poles in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
, so there exist only finitely many poles of
f
in a given orbit of
\tau^{*}
. Therefore it is possible for us to quantify the spread of the poles in a given orbit.

Definition 3.2.5
.

Let
0\neq f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
.

(a)

The polar dispersion of
f
is the maximum integer
n
such that there exist poles
p
and
p+ns
of
f
, and is denoted
\pdisp(f)
.

(b)

The weak polar dispersion of
f
is the maximum integer
n
such that there exist poles
p
and
p+ns
of
f
with orders both at least
2
, and is denoted by
\wpdisp(f)
.

Remark 3.2.6
.

The reasons for introducing the notation
\tau^{*}
in place of the simpler “subtract
s
” are twofold. First, the computations to follow will be cast in terms of orbits of the action of
\tau^{*}
on the torus
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
. Second, this setup is more natural in the more technical algebraic setting in Chapter
4
, and introducing it now sets up the analogy.

The measure of polar dispersion is crucial to the study of summability in view of the observation that the
\tau-\Id
operator increments the polar dispersion and weak polar dispersion of a function.

Lemma 3.2.7
.

If
f=\tau(a)-a
for some nonconstant
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
then

\pdisp(f)-\pdisp(a)=\wpdisp(f)-\wpdisp(a)=1.

Proof.

The poles of
\tau(a)
are
\tau^{*}(p)=p-s
for all poles
p
of
a
, so
\pdisp(f)-\pdisp(a)\leq 1
. If
p
and
p+ns
are poles of
a
where
n=\pdisp(a)
, then
p-s
and
p+ns
are poles of
\tau(a)-a
, so
\pdisp(f)-\pdisp(a)\geq 1
. The statement for weak polar dispersion is shown analogously.
∎

Example 3.2.8
.

Lemma
3.2.7
immediately implies that particular functions are not summable. If
a\in\mathbb{C}
then
\tau(a)-a=0
, and if
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
is nonconstant then
\tau(a)-a
has more poles than
a
. As a consequence, no elliptic function with fewer than two
distinct
poles in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
is summable. Thus nonzero constant functions are not summable, and neither are the Weierstrass elliptic function
\wp(z)
or its derivatives.

3.2.2
Analytic Pinnings

The mechanics of the rational case as in Chapter
2
are extremely accessible because the (function) field of interest is the field of meromorphic functions on the projective line, which has genus zero. To contrast, the elliptic functions
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
are meromorphic functions over the torus
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
, which is a curve of genus
1
. This introduces such algebraic-geometric computations as Corollary
3.1.3
: any nonconstant elliptic function has at least two poles, counting multiplicity. This will complicate the later parts of the reduction process where we work to cancel poles of order
1
.

In Subsection
3.2.4
we will compute a reduction of an elliptic function
f
: we construct
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that the polar dispersion of

f-[\tau(a)-a]

is minimized. After this is done, summability will be equivalent to this reduction vanishing. The reduction will be performed with respect to the following choice of points on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
:

Definition 3.2.9
.

An
analytic pinning
is a collection
\mathcal{R}
of the following choices: for each
(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
-orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
, a representative
q_{\omega}\in\omega
, with the condition that
0\in\mathcal{R}
. We denote the orbit containing
0
by
\widehat{\omega}
.

Analytic pinnings will be used throughout to express summations over
\tau^{*}
-orbits so they are anchored at a specific location, that is, a summation will be taken over all
\hat{q}+ns
where
\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The choice of an analytic pinning can in fact be encoded within a
\zeta
-expansion of a elliptic function. Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic, and let
\mathcal{R}
be an analytic pinning. By virtue of a
\zeta
-expansion of
f
being guaranteed to exist by Proposition
3.1.11
, the analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
lets us organize such a
\zeta
-expansion by orbits of
\tau^{*}
.

Corollary 3.2.10
.

Given a choice of analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
of
f
, there exist constants
c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and a constant
C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)\in\mathbb{C}
depending only on
\mathcal{R}
, such that
f(z)
has global expansion

f(z)=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns).

(3.2)

Indeed, this summation has finitely many terms because we are summing over orbits of the shift
\tau^{*}
, namely

\{q_{\omega}+ns\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}s)

for each
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
. Effectively the summation is over a selection of representatives of the orbits in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
, so it will have finite support because the elliptic function
f(z)
has finitely many poles in
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
.

3.2.3
Orbital Residues

Analogous to the work of
[
DHRS18
,
HS21
]
, we define analytic orbital residues and demonstrate how they form a partial obstruction to summability.

Definition 3.2.11
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic. For an orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
, choose a representative
q_{\omega}
. The
analytic orbital residue
of
f
at the orbit
\omega
of order
j\in\mathbb{N}
is

\ores(f,\omega,j):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{j}(f,q_{\omega}+ns).

With this definition the orbital residues of
f
in equation (
3.2
) are visible from the
\zeta
-expansion of
f
.

Remark 3.2.12
.

Recall that the values of
c_{j}(f,\alpha)
are determined from the principal parts of
f
at its local expansion near
z=\alpha
, so the definition of orbital residues is not tied to a particular
\zeta
-expansion. Furthermore, they are independent of the choice of representative of
\omega
: the principal parts of
f
are not affected when moving by lattice periods, and the orbital residues are defined as sums over all shifts by integer multiples of
s
.

Each orbital residue is invariant under the action of
\tau
, implying the following criterion for summability:

Lemma 3.2.13
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. If there exists
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that
f=\tau(a)-a
, then the orbital residues of
f
all vanish.

Proof.

Let
a
have
\zeta
-expansion with coefficients
c_{j}(a,q_{\omega}+ns)
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\tau(a)
has a
\zeta
-expansion with coefficients

c_{j}(\tau(a),q_{\omega}+ns)=c_{j}(a,q_{\omega}+(n-1)s).

Consequently the orbital residues of
\tau(a)
are

\ores(\tau(a),\omega,j)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{j}(\tau(a),q_{\omega}+ns)=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{j}(a,q_{\omega}+(n-1)s)=\ores(a,\omega,j).

Orbital residues are additive, so
\ores(f,\omega,j)=0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
and
j\geq 1
.
∎

This result mirrors
[
CS12
, Proposition 2.5]
, and in fact an algebraic analogue exists as
[
DHRS18
, Proposition B.8]
. However, this result is only unidirectional. The converse is not true as the following lemma demonstrates. The lemma concerns a small example, but it is by no means a toy–it becomes an important edge case in the summability of elliptic functions!

Lemma 3.2.14
.

Consider the elliptic function

f(z)=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}[\zeta(z+s)-\zeta(z)]

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
are not both zero.

(i)

All the orbital residues of
f
vanish, and yet
f
is not summable.

(ii)

f^{\prime}(z)
is summable.

Proof.

(i)

First note that the function is indeed elliptic by Corollary
3.1.10
. all the orbital residues vanish by construction. Now suppose there did exist
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that
f=\tau(a)-a
. Then
a
must be a nonconstant elliptic function, because
f
is nonzero. Because
f
has at most simple poles, Lemma
3.2.7
implies that
a
has at most simple poles. It also implies that
\pdisp(a)=0
. Therefore the elliptic function
a
has a single simple pole, contradicting Corollary
3.1.3
.

(ii)

The derivative of
f
is

f^{\prime}(z)=-\alpha_{2}\wp(z+s)+\alpha_{2}\wp(z)=(\tau-\Id)\big(-\alpha_{2}\wp(z)\big),

which is summable.

∎

This shows that orbital residues are not enough to determine summability. We need additional measurements associated with elliptic functions that can somehow detect functions as in Lemma
3.2.14
.

3.2.4
Searching for a Reduction

We now show the complete reduction process for an elliptic function to minimize polar dispersion. This reduction shall be done in three steps:

1.

Canceling poles of order higher than
1
,

2.

Canceling order
1
poles away
\widehat{\omega}
,

3.

Canceling order
1
poles on
\widehat{\omega}
.

The first step mimics the rational case so closely that the orbital residues fall out. The fact that we are working with meromorphic functions over a genus
1
curve means that the second and third steps have complications where interesting additional terms appear in the orbit containing
0
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic and let
\mathcal{R}
be an analytic pinning. Recall the
\zeta
-expansion for
f
that is guaranteed from Corollary
3.2.10
:

f(z)=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns).

(3.3)

For
j>1
the function
\dfrac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)
is elliptic: indeed it is
-\dfrac{d^{j-2}}{dz^{j-2}}\wp(z-\hat{q}-ns)
. Now consider

\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q})-\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)=\tau^{n}\left[\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)\right]-\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns).

(3.4)

This is summable by Example
3.2.4
. Thus all the higher-order poles can be grouped at the elements of the analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
by adding a summable elliptic function to
f
as follows:

\displaystyle f(z)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)}{(j-1)!}\left[\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q})-\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)}\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}\frac{\ores(f,\omega,j)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}).

(3.5)

This new function has at most one pole of higher order on each orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
, and therefore by logic similar to Example
3.2.8
this function can only be summable if these orbital residues at higher orders all vanish. This completes the first step of the reduction.

For the second step of the reduction assume now that
f(z)
only has simple poles. Thus it has a
\zeta
-expansion of the form

f(z)=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns).

We show a reduction of
f(z)
so it has at most one pole on each orbit besides
\widehat{\omega}
. Consider the elliptic function

\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)-\zeta(z-ns).

Applying
\tau^{n}-\Id
yields the summable elliptic function

\zeta(z-\hat{q})-\zeta(z)-\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)+\zeta(z-ns).

(3.6)

We cancel order
1
poles away from the orbit
\widehat{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
by adding a summable elliptic function to
f(z)
as follows:

\displaystyle f(z)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\hat{q}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\big[\zeta(z-\hat{q})-\zeta(z)-\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)+\zeta(z-ns)\big]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,ns)\zeta(z-ns)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\hat{q}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-\hat{q})

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\hat{q}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\hat{q}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,ns)\zeta(z-ns)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z-q_{\omega})

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}\\
\hat{q}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z-q_{\omega})-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z)

(3.7)

In this second reduction step we once again have orbital residues congregating at representatives in
\mathcal{R}
, but these orbital residues also congregate at
0
by virtue of being coefficients of
\zeta(z-0)
. Furthermore, we witness the coefficients from other orbits falling onto the elements of the set
\mathbb{Z}s
.

We move to the third and final reduction step. Here we assume that
f
only has simple poles and they are all in
\mathbb{Z}s
. We suppose that
f(z)
has
\zeta
-expansion

f(z)=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns),

to continue our reduction from Equation (
3.7
). Once again we must handle differences of
\zeta
functions like in the previous step, but now we must adhere to all poles being in
\mathbb{Z}s
to not undo previous work.

The goal for this third step is not to congregate poles at one particular representative in
\mathcal{R}
. Instead, we congregate poles at
0
and at
s
. There are two analogous methods that we must use at each potential pole
ns
, depending on whether
n\geq 2
or
n\leq-1
. For
n\geq 2
the following function is elliptic and summable:

\zeta(z-(n-1)s)-\zeta(z-ns)-\zeta(z)+\zeta(z-s)=(\tau-\Id)[\zeta(z-ns)-\zeta(z-s)]

This gives rise to the following telescoping series for all
n\geq 2
:

\sum\limits_{k=2}^{n}\left[\zeta(z-(k-1)s)-\zeta(z-ks)-\zeta(z)+\zeta(z-s)\right]\\
=-(n-1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z-ns)

(3.8)

Therefore we have the following partial reduction:

\displaystyle f(z)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\big[-(n-1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z-ns)\big]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\bigg\{\sum\limits_{n\leq 1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)-\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-s)\bigg\}

(3.9)

Now for
n\leq-1
the following function is elliptic and summable:

\zeta(z-(n+1)s)-\zeta(z-ns)+\zeta(z)-\zeta(z-s)\\
=(\tau-\Id)[\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z-(n+1)s)]\quad\text{ for }n\leq-1.

(3.10)

The following series telescopes for
n\leq-1
(be wary of signs):

\sum\limits_{k=n}^{-1}[\zeta(z-(k+1)s)-\zeta(z-ks)+\zeta(z)-\zeta(z-s)]\\
=-\zeta(z-ns)+(-n+1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)

(3.11)

We now complete the reduction, importing the previous expressions from Equation (
3.9
).

\displaystyle f(z)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\big[-(n-1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z-ns)\big]

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\leq-1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\big[-\zeta(z-ns)+(-n+1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)\big]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\bigg\{\sum\limits_{n\leq 1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)-\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{n\geq 2}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-s)\bigg\}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\bigg\{-\sum\limits_{n\leq-1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)-\sum\limits_{n\leq-1}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\leq-1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{n\leq-1}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-s)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\bigg\{\sum\limits_{n=0}^{1}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-ns)-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\not\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\not\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\not\in\{0,1\}\end{subarray}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-s)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\bigg\{-\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)\zeta(z-s)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z)).

(3.12)

We are at the end of our reduction of
f(z)
. Now recall Lemma
3.2.14
: any nonzero function of the form
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}[\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z)]
for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
is not summable. Thus if the orbital residues of
f
all vanish, then
f
is summable if and only if the remaining expression

C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z))

vanishes. These coefficients are precisely what we must extract to complete our obstruction.

Definition 3.2.15
.

The
analytic panorbital residues
of
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
relative to
\mathcal{R}
of orders
0
and
1
are, respectively,

\displaystyle\vphantom{\frac{a}{b}}\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,0):=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,1):=\smash{\sum_{\hat{q}\,\in\,\mathcal{R}}\ \sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)}

.

Combining the reductions (
3.5
), (
3.7
), and (
3.12
) of equation (
3.3
) gives us the following complete reduction of
f(z)
.

Theorem 3.2.16
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic and let
\mathcal{R}
be an analytic pinning, and let the following be the
\zeta
-expansion for
f
guaranteed from Corollary
3.2.10
:

f(z)=C_{\mathcal{R}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns).

(3.13)

Then there exists
g\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that

\displaystyle f-[\tau(g)-g]=\

\displaystyle\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,0)+\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,1)(\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z))

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\frac{\ores(f,\omega,j)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega})

We are now able to state and prove the full analytic analogue of
[
DHRS18
, Proposition B.8]
.

Corollary 3.2.17
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic, and let
\mathcal{R}
be an analytic pinning. The following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists elliptic
g\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
and constants
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
such that

f-(\tau(g)-g)=a+b(\zeta(z+s)-\zeta(z)).

(ii)

For all orbits
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
and all
j\geq 1
,

\ores(f,\omega,j)=0.

Proof.

In fact
a
and
b
are respectively the
0
th and
1
st panorbital residues with respect to the analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
by which
f
is reduced.
∎

3.2.5
Statement of the Criterion Via Panorbital Residues

Theorem
3.2.16
, in combination with Lemma
3.2.13
and Lemma
3.2.14
, show the following obstruction for summability.

Corollary 3.2.18
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic and let
\mathcal{R}
be an analytic pinning. Then
f
is summable if and only if all the analytic orbital residues of
f
and both the analytic panorbital residues of
f
relative to
\mathcal{R}
vanish.

3.3
Discussion of the Results

We have found a complete obstruction to summability of elliptic functions. Our methods required the use of an analytic pinning relative to which the obstruction is calculated. An immediate question is how choices of the analytic pinning affect the obstruction of Corollary
3.2.18
and the reduction in Theorem
3.2.16
. We discuss this in more detail in Subsection
3.3.1
.

Knowing the obstruction a priori creates the possibility for proofs that circumvent the need for deriving the reduction. We provide such a proof in Subsection
3.3.2
.

3.3.1
Effects of the Choice of Analytic Pinning

While the orbital residues of an elliptic function are not associated with an analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
, the panorbital residues require such a choice of
\mathcal{R}
to even be defined. The effects of this choice can be made concrete by analyzing when two analytic pinnings differ on a single orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
. This suffices. Even if two analytic pinnings differ on infinitely many orbits, the panorbital residues of any given elliptic function are computed as sums of finite support.

Proposition 3.3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
be two analytic pinnings with the same representatives, except for exactly one orbit
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
for which
q_{\omega}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
has been replaced with
q_{\omega}+ks+\lambda\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be elliptic. Then:

1.

\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f,0)=\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f,0)+\eta(\lambda)\cdot\ores(f,\omega,1)
,

2.

\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f,1)=\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f,1)-k\cdot\ores(f,\omega,1)
.

Proof.

Take the difference of the two
\zeta
-expansions of
f
relative to
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
that are guaranteed from Corollary
3.2.10
.

\displaystyle f(z)-f(z)=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)

\displaystyle-C_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f)+\sum\limits_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,\hat{q}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f)-C_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,q_{\omega}+ks+\lambda+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-ks-\lambda-ns)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-ns).

Because
f
is elliptic, the coefficients
c_{j}(f,\alpha)
of its principal parts are invariant under translates of
\alpha
by elements of
\Lambda
. Furthermore, the counter variable
n
in the first summation can be translated by
-k
to obtain

\displaystyle 0=\

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f)-C_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-\lambda-ns)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{c_{j}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-ns).

Recall that
\dfrac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z)
is elliptic for all
j\geq 2
. Therefore the two summations cancel for
j\geq 2
and we are left with

0=\ C_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f)-C_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f)+\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)\cdot\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-\lambda-ns)-\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)\cdot\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-ns).

The
\zeta
expressions are related precisely by the quasi-period map
\eta
, implying that

C_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f)-C_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f)=\eta(\lambda)\cdot\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)=\eta(\lambda)\cdot\ores(f,\omega,1).

This shows item 1. Item 2 can be shown directly:

\displaystyle\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}(f,1)-\pano_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}(f,1)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{q}\,\in\,\mathcal{R}_{2}}\ \sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)-\sum_{\hat{q}\,\in\,\mathcal{R}_{1}}\ \sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,\hat{q}+ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ks+\lambda+ns)-\sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ (n-k)\cdot c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)-\sum_{n\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}}\ n\cdot c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-k\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{1}(f,q_{\omega}+ns)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-k\cdot\ores(f,\omega,1).

∎

This gives us a precise statement of how the panorbital residues are affected by choice of analytic pinning. An immediate corollary gives us information on when they are
independent
of the analytic pinning.

Corollary 3.3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
be an elliptic function such that all the orbital residues of
f
vanish. Then the panorbital residues
\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,0)
and
\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,1)
are unaffected by the choice of
\mathcal{R}
.

3.3.2
A Tidier Proof of Theorem
3.2.16

Now that we have found the required definition of analytic panorbital residues, we can work to show Theorem
3.2.16
with their knowledge a priori. We restate the reduction here for convenience before any simplifications: given elliptic
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
and analytic pinning
\mathcal{R}
, there exists
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
such that

\displaystyle f-[\tau(a)-a]=\

\displaystyle\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,0)+\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,1)(\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z))

\displaystyle+\ores(f,\widehat{\omega},1)\zeta(z)+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z-q_{\omega})

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)}\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}\frac{\ores(f,\omega,j)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega})

(3.14)

The proof method will involve analyzing the reduction in the following way:

1.

Show that all poles of the above reduction are contained in
\mathcal{R}\cup\{s\}
,

2.

Use the fact that the orbital
and
panorbital residues are invariant under the reduction process to conclude that the above reduction
must
be correct.

Proof.

First, note that under the reduction of Subsection
3.2.2
the constant term of the
\zeta
-expansion is never changed, so
\pano_{\mathcal{R}}(f,0)
is indeed the correct constant coefficient. Furthermore, the reduction is done by adding summable functions, which have vanishing orbital residues from Lemma
3.2.13
, so the orbital residues remain invariant through every step of the process.

1.

In the first step of the reduction, we cancelled poles of order
j\geq 2
by using the summable elliptic functions

\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega})-\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}-ns)

from Equation (
3.4
). The orbital residues of these functions are
0
due to summability and the panorbital residues are
0
because these functions have
\zeta
-expansions with no terms with lower-order poles. Thus the orbital and panorbital residues of
f
are not changed in this reduction. Once all higher-order poles of
f
are accumulated at
f
the resulting part of the
\zeta
-expansion must indeed be

\sum\limits_{\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)}\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}\frac{\ores(f,\omega,j)(-1)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\cdot\frac{d^{j-1}}{dz^{j-1}}\zeta(z-q_{\omega}),

which does indeed match the higher order terms of Equation (
3.14
).

In the second step of the reduction, we cancelled poles of order
1
on orbits
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
excluding
\widehat{\omega}
by using

\zeta(z-\hat{q})-\zeta(z)-\zeta(z-\hat{q}-ns)+\zeta(z-ns)

from Equation (
3.6

0\cdot 1+0\cdot(-1)+n\cdot(-1)+n\cdot 1=0,

so once again the orbital and panorbital residues of

are congregated at

, the resulting part of the
\zeta
-expansion must be

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)\\
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}\end{subarray}}\ores(f,\omega,1)\zeta(z-q_{\omega}),

which does indeed match the reduction in Equation (
3.14
).

The third step cancels poles on
\widehat{\omega}
so all the remaining poles are at
0
and
s
. It remains to be shown that the coefficients of
\zeta(z)
and
\zeta(z-s)
are correct. The functions used in the reduction are

\displaystyle-(n-1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)-\zeta(z-ns)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ for }n\geq 2,

\displaystyle-\zeta(z-ns)+(-n+1)\zeta(z)+n\zeta(z-s)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ for }n\leq-1,

from Equations (
3.8
) and (

). These have vanishing orbital residues because they are summable, and their panorbital residues are respectively

\displaystyle 0\cdot(-n+1)+1\cdot n+n\cdot(-1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle n\cdot(-1)+0\cdot(-n+1)+1\cdot n

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore the orbital and panorbital residues of

are invariant through the entire reduction process. At the end of the reduction, the only possible pole away from
\mathcal{R}
is contributed by

must be

∎

4.1.2

4.2
Previous Results on Summability

We now have a sufficient collection of algebraic geometric technology to handle the question of summability. We are also now able to precisely state the previous results of

An additional difficulty in the algebraic setting is that we no longer have access to a partial fraction decomposition, or even a global expression that simulates such a decomposition. g
has at most one simple pole, which is impossible from the discussion of Example
4.1.11
. We will relate this result to orbital residues in the following discussion.

4.2.2
Orbital Residues

Orbital residues of elliptic functions were first developed in
[
DHRS18
, Appendix B]
in precisely this algebraic setting. The authors circumvented the issue of no global decompositions by simply writing the orbital residues in terms of the local expansions. These expansions are in terms of
local uniformizers

u_{Q}
at each point
Q\in E
, where
u_{Q}
is an elliptic function with a zero at
Q
of multiplicity
1
(cf.
[
Har77
, p. 137]
). But in order to aggregate the data along the orbitals of the shift action
\tau^{*}
, one must ensure some compatability of the local uniformizers along this orbit. Dreyfus et al. accomplish this with
[
DHRS18
, Definition B.6]
:

Definition 4.2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{Q}\mid Q\in E\}
be a set of local uniformizers at the points of
E
. We say
\mathcal{U}
is a
coherent set of local uniformizers
if for any
Q\in E
,

u_{\tau^{*}(Q)}=\tau(u_{Q}).

This is
not
to say that
u_{Q_{1}\ominus Q_{2}}(Q_{3})=u_{Q_{1}}(Q_{3}\oplus Q_{2})
for all
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\in E
: the uniformizers are not required to respect general shifts. The uniformizers chosen on different orbits of
\tau^{*}
do not interact with each other, so coherent sets of local uniformizers can be created from one another by altering those uniformizers on a single orbit of
\tau^{*}
.

Definition 4.2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a set of local uniformizers at the points of
E
. If
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
has a pole at
Q
of order
n
, we may write

f=\frac{c_{Q,n}}{u_{Q}^{n}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{Q,2}}{u_{Q}^{2}}+\frac{c_{Q,1}}{u_{Q}}+g

where
g
is elliptic and holomorphic at
Q
, and
c_{Q,j}\in K
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. We shall refer to
c_{Q,j}
as the
algebraic residue
of
f
of order
j
at
Q
, and denote it by
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q,j)
.

Remark 4.2.4
.

To reiterate previous remarks, the terminology overloads the term “residue”, which are attributes of differentials. The terminology here is chosen to remain congruous with the previous work in
[
CS12
]
and
[
DHRS18
]
.

The aggregation of these algebraic residues via the orbits of
\tau^{*}
forms the notion of orbital residues, as in
[
DHRS18
, Definition B.7]
.

Definition 4.2.5
.

Let
f
be an elliptic function and
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{Q}\mid Q\in E\}
be a coherent set of local uniformizers. Denote the bidirectional orbit of
Q
through
\tau^{*}
by

\omega_{Q}:=\{Q\oplus iS\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\{(\tau^{*})^{i}(Q)\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
we define the
algebraic orbital residue
of
f
of order
j
at the orbit
\omega
to be

\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega,j)=\sum\limits_{Q\in\omega}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q,j).

In contrast to Chapter
3
we retain the subscript
\mathcal{U}
in the notation to indicate that the orbital residue is highly dependent on the choice of the set
\mathcal{U}
of local uniformizers.

Notice that
\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega_{Q},r)
vanishes if there do not exist poles in the orbit
\omega_{Q}
that have order at least
r
. These residues can then be used to formulate, up to a term with two or fewer simple poles, a criterion for elliptic functions, shown in
[
DHRS18
, Proposition B.8]
.

Proposition 4.2.6
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{Q}\mid Q\in E\}
be a coherent set of local uniformizers. The following are equivalent.

(1)

There exist
Q\in E
,
g\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
, and
e\in\mathcal{L}([Q]+[\tau^{*}(Q)])
such that
f=\tau(g)-g+e
.

(2)

For any
Q\in E
and
j\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
we have
\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega_{Q},j)=0
.

That is to say, for any elliptic function
f
for which its orbital residues vanish, there exists a reduction by a summable function
\tau(g)-g
to a function
e
with at most two simple poles related to each other by
\tau^{*}
. We can see this result when we look closely at the reduction in the analytic case in Theorem
3.2.16
: when the analytic orbital residues vanish, all terms in the reduction disappear besides those including the analytic panorbital residues, namely a term in
\mathcal{L}([0]+[s])
. In fact, our derivation of the terms Theorem
3.2.16
that
don’t
involve the panorbital residues mimics the proof of
[
DHRS18
, Proposition B.8]
while giving an explicit reduction process.

This result shows that the problem of summability reduces to determining where
g\in\mathcal{L}([Q]+[\tau^{*}(Q)])
is summable. From Lemma
4.2.1
this occurs precisely when
g
vanishes. It would be computationally convenient of the orbital residue could detect this case. Unfortunately, the worst-case scenario occurs:

Lemma 4.2.7
.

If
g\in\mathcal{L}([Q]+[\tau^{*}(Q)])
then
\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(g,\omega_{Q},r)=0
for all
r\geq 1
.

Because
g
has at most simple poles the result is clear for
r>1
. The result when
r=1
is a special case of
[
DHRS18
, Lemma B.15]
in which the function has two simple poles in the
\tau^{*}
-orbit of
Q
. It is a technical lemma that requires the fact that the residues of a differential form on a compact Riemann surface must sum to zero. In the interest of space, we merely cite the proof and do not replicate it here.

Proposition
4.2.6
and Lemma
4.2.1
show that algebraic orbital residues fail as an oracle of determining summability, just as in the analytic case. Our goal is the same in the algebraic setting: define panorbital residues that let us define a reduction of elliptic functions similar to Theorem
3.2.16
that lets us see summability from knowing the values of a set of constants.

4.3
Reduction of Elliptic Functions

Just as in Chapter
3
, a reduction of an elliptic function
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
will be highly nonunique. In the analytic setting we used an analytic pinning to make a choice of to where the poles of
f
will be gathered in the reduction. Here we must make even more choices.

Definition 4.3.1
.

An
algebraic pinning

\Xi
is a collection of the following choices:

•

A coherent set
\mathcal{U}_{\Xi}
of local uniformizers
u_{Q}
for each point
Q
on the curve,

•

A set
\mathcal{R}_{\Xi}
of representatives of
\tau^{*}
orbits of
E
.

•

An anchor
\widehat{Q}\in\mathcal{R}_{\Xi}
.

In all following discussions where
\Xi
is considered to be fixed, we make the convention of dropping the subscript
\Xi
from
\mathcal{U}_{\Xi}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\Xi}
.

We wish to reduce an elliptic function
f
, by way of subtracting summable elements, to an elliptic function
\overline{f}
with poles only at
\widehat{Q}
,
\widehat{Q}\oplus S
, and the orbit representatives in
\mathcal{R}
. Depending on the choices made above the clerical work involved will vary in difficulty. The easiest case is when the poles of
f
are all to one side of all the elements of
\mathcal{R}
. Denoting the orbit of
\widehat{Q}
by
\widehat{\omega}=\{\widehat{Q}\oplus nS\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, we also wish to specify that
\widehat{\omega}
is free of any poles of
f
: this is to ensure that any poles that “rain down” from other orbits will not be interacting with any preexisting poles on
\widehat{\omega}
. With this in mind we make the following definition.

Definition 4.3.2
.

For an elliptic function
f
, let
\sing(f)
denote the set of poles of
f
. We say an algebraic pinning
\Xi
is
admissible
if the following are true:

\displaystyle\sing(f)\cap\widehat{\omega}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\sing(f)\cap\{Q\ominus nS\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}

\displaystyle=\emptyset\ \text{ for all }Q\in\mathcal{R}_{\Xi}.

We must now define the functions with which we reduce. These functions will be defined in terms of their divisors and principal parts that we may utilize in the reduction. First, fix an admissible pinning
\Xi
. For each
\tau^{*}
-orbit
\omega
, denote by
Q_{\omega}
the unique point in the intersection
\omega\cap\mathcal{R}
.

Lemma 4.3.3
.

For each
\widehat{\omega}\neq\omega\in\Omega
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist unique

\varphi_{\omega,j}\in\mathcal{L}\left(j[Q_{\omega}]+\Bigl[\widehat{Q}\Bigr]\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{j}(\omega)\in K\qquad\text{such that}

•

\varphi_{\omega,j}-u_{Q_{\omega}}^{-j}
is nonsingular at
Q_{\omega}
; and

•

\varphi_{\omega,j}-d_{j}(\omega)\cdot u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}
has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
.

Proof.

We proceed by strong induction on
j
. Consider any choice of
\varphi_{\omega,1}\in\mathcal{L}\left([Q_{\omega}]+[\widehat{Q}]\right)
and scale by a constant in
K
so that
\varphi_{\omega,1}-u_{Q_{\omega}}^{-1}
is nonsingular at
Q_{\omega}
. There clearly exists
d_{1}(\omega)
for which
\varphi_{\omega,1}-d_{1}(\omega)\cdot u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}
is nonsingular at
\widehat{Q}
. Add the proper constant in
K
so it in fact has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
. This proves the base case
j=1
.

For the inductive step assume that for all
j<M
the result holds, for some
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
. A quick inductive proof shows the existence of an element
f
in the Riemann–Roch space with divisor
j[Q_{\omega}]+[\widehat{Q}]
from Theorem
4.1.10
. Suppose the local expansion of
f
at
Q_{\omega}
is

f=\frac{c_{j}}{u_{Q_{\omega}}^{j}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{1}}{u_{Q_{\omega}}}+g,

where
g
is holomorphic at
Q_{\omega}
and
c_{1},\cdots,c_{j}\in K
and
c_{j}\neq 0
. Then

\frac{1}{c_{j}}f-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}\frac{c_{k}}{c_{j}}\varphi_{\omega,k}

is nonsingular at
Q_{\omega}
from the inductive hypothesis. Denote this function by
\varphi_{\omega,j}
. Then there exists
d_{j}(\omega)
such that
\varphi_{\omega,j}-d_{j}(\omega)\cdot u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}
is nonsingular at
\widehat{Q}
. Again correct by adding a constant in
K
to ensure that it in fact has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
. This proves the inductive step, which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.3.4
.

For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
there exists a unique

\psi_{j}\in\mathcal{L}\left(\Bigl[\widehat{Q}\oplus(j-1)S\Bigr]+\Bigl[\widehat{Q}\Bigr]\right)

such that
\psi_{j}+u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}
has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
.

Proof.

Consider any choice of nonsingular
\psi_{j}
and scale by a constant in
K
so that
\psi_{j}+u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}
is nonsingular at
\widehat{Q}
. Add the proper constant in
K
so it in fact has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
.
∎

Using these functions we reduce
f
in two steps.

1.

Partially reduce
f
to
\tilde{f}
such that for all orbits
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
the only pole of
f
on
\omega
is
Q_{\omega}
, creating order
1
poles of
\tilde{f}
on
\widehat{\omega}
in the process.

2.

Reduce
\tilde{f}
to
\overline{f}
by collecting all poles on
\widehat{\omega}
to
\widehat{Q}
and
\widehat{Q}\oplus S
.

Throughout the following computations, we will primarily use the difference operator
\Delta^{(n)}:=\Id-\tau^{-n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. That is,

\Delta^{(n)}(f)=f-\tau^{-n}(f)=\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{1}\tau(\tau^{k}(f))-\tau^{k}(f).

Because
\tau
is an automorphism on
\mathcal{K}_{1}
it follows that
\Delta^{(n)}(f)
is summable for all
f\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Notice furthermore that
\Delta^{(n)}
is
not
defined to be the
n
-fold composition of
\Delta^{(1)}
.

4.3.1
The First Reduction

Proposition 4.3.5
.

Let
\Xi
be an admissible algebraic pinning of
f
. The following reduction
\tilde{f}
of
f
only has order-
1
poles on
\widehat{\omega}
, and all other poles are elements of
\mathcal{R}
.

\tilde{f}=f+\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}).

Proof.

First confirm that the above is indeed a reduction:
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
is summable, so
f-\tilde{f}
is a linear combination of summable functions and therefore summable.

Now let us consider the local expansions of
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
. By definition the nonzero residues of
\varphi_{\omega,j}
are

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\varphi_{\omega,j},Q_{\omega},j)=1,\quad\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\varphi_{\omega,j},\widehat{Q},1)=d_{j}(\omega).

So the nonzero residues of
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
are

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),Q_{\omega},j)=1,\quad\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)=-1,

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),\widehat{Q},1)=d_{j}(\omega),\quad\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=-d_{j}(\omega).

Therefore the only terms that affect the residue of
\tilde{f}-f
at
Q_{\omega}\oplus mS
of order
j
for fixed
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
and fixed
m,j\in\mathbb{N}
are
f
itself and the
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
term. Therefore, for
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)=\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)+\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot(-1)=0.

The terms
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
have no poles at
Q_{\omega}\ominus mS
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, so all residues of
\tilde{f}
on orbits
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
vanish except at the elements of
\mathcal{R}
. Because
f
has no poles on
\widehat{\omega}
and
\varphi_{\omega,j}
only has poles of order
1
on
\widehat{\omega}
, this proves the result.
∎

To collect our bearings we now recompute all other residues of
\tilde{f}
with respect to the algebraic pinning after this first reduction.

Lemma 4.3.6
.

Let
\Xi
be an admissible algebraic pinning of
f
. For
\tilde{f}
as defined in Proposition
4.3.5
, the remaining residues of
\tilde{f}
are as follows:

•

For
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},Q_{\omega},j)=\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega,j).

•

For
n>0
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega).

•

For
n=0
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q},1)=\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega).

All other residues of
\tilde{f}
with respect to the algebraic pinning are zero.

Proof.

For
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
, as stated in the proof of Proposition
4.3.5
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),Q_{\omega},j)=1.

Therefore

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},Q_{\omega},j)=\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega},j)+\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot 1.

Because we chose an admissible algebraic pinning,
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega},j)=0
. Similarly, the sum is precisely the orbital residue
\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega,j)
. This proves that
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},Q_{\omega},j)=\ores_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\omega,j)
for all
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
.

For
n>0
, note that
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=0
due to the pinning being admissible, so only
\Delta^{(n)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
will contribute to
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)
for each orbit
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
and order
j\geq 1
, and as in the proof Proposition
4.3.5
,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(n)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=-d_{j}(\omega).

Therefore

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot(-d_{j}(\omega))

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega).

For the final residue
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q},1)
, note that
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\widehat{Q},1)=0
due to
f
being admissible, so only the terms
\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})
will contribute to this residue for all
\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}
, all
m\geq 1
, and all orders
j\geq 1
, and

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),\widehat{Q},1)=d_{j}(\omega).

Therefore

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q},1)=\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega).

It remains to confirm that all uncomputed residues of
\tilde{f}
actually vanish. From Proposition
4.3.5
the only residues we have left to analyze are
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)
for
n<0
. All functions involved in the reduction, including
f
itself, are by definition regular at
\widehat{Q}\oplus nS
for
n<0
. This proves the lemma.
∎

4.3.2
The Second Reduction

Proposition 4.3.7
.

Let
\Xi
be an admissible algebraic pinning of
f
. Let
\tilde{f}
be defined as in Proposition
4.3.5
. The following reduction
\overline{f}
of
f
only has order
1
poles on
\widehat{\omega}
at
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{Q}\oplus S
in particular, and all other poles are elements of
\mathcal{R}
.

\overline{f}=\tilde{f}-\sum\limits_{m\geq 2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{m}\left[\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k})\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\right].

Proof.

Each
\psi_{k}
has its two poles on
\widehat{\omega}
, so all poles of
\overline{f}
off of
\widehat{\omega}
are elements of
\mathcal{R}
by virtue of Proposition
4.3.5
. Furthermore,
\tilde{f}
and
\psi_{k}
only have order
1
poles on
\widehat{\omega}
, so
\overline{f}
does as well. In fact all these poles are at
\widehat{Q}\oplus nS
for
n\geq 0
. It therefore only remains to verify that

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\overline{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=0

for all integers
n\geq 2
.

By definition, the poles of

\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k})=\psi_{k}-\tau^{-1}(\psi_{k})

are at
\widehat{Q}
and
\widehat{Q}\oplus(k-1)S
contributed by
\psi_{k}
, and at
\widehat{Q}\oplus S
and
\widehat{Q}\oplus kS
contributed by
\tau^{-1}(\psi_{k})
. In particular,

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k}),\widehat{Q}\oplus kS,1)=-\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tau^{-1}(\psi_{k}),\widehat{Q}\oplus kS,1)=-1.

Therefore the order
1
residue of
\overline{f}
at
\widehat{Q}\oplus nS
for
n\geq 2
is

\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\overline{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)=\ \res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)\\
-\sum\limits_{m\geq n+1}\sum_{k=n}^{n+1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k}),Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,1)\cdot\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\\
-\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{n}),Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,1)\cdot\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\tilde{f},\widehat{Q}\oplus nS,1)-\sum\limits_{m\geq n+1}(1-1)\cdot\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)+\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus nS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 0.

This proves that the above reduction has its poles at the prescribed locations.
∎

Proposition 4.3.8
.

Let
\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
be the zero function, and let
\Psi_{m}:=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\psi_{k}
. The local expansion of
\overline{f}
at
\widehat{Q}
is given by

\displaystyle\Bigg(

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\cdot m\Bigg)\cdot u_{\widehat{Q}}^{-1}

\displaystyle+\Bigg(

\displaystyle f(\widehat{Q})-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\varphi_{\omega,j}(\widehat{Q}\ominus mS)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\cdot\Psi_{m}(\widehat{Q}\ominus S)\Bigg)\cdot u_{\widehat{Q}}^{0}

\displaystyle+g,

where
g\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
has a zero at
\widehat{Q}
.

Proof.

We find the coefficients by analyzing the contributions of the first and second reductions to the order
1
and
0
residues at
\widehat{Q}
. For order
1
residues: recall the first reduction

\tilde{f}=f+\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j})

where
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(m)}(\varphi_{\omega,j}),\widehat{Q},1)=d_{j}(\omega)
, and the second reduction

\overline{f}=\tilde{f}-\sum\limits_{m\geq 2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{m}\left[\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k})\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\right].

where
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\Delta^{(1)}(\psi_{k}),\widehat{Q},1)=-1
. Because we chose an admissible algebraic pinning we have
\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,\widehat{Q},1)=0
, therefore

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\overline{f},\widehat{Q},1)=\

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{m\geq 2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{m}\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega),

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\cdot m

as desired.

Because

is regular at

f
to the order
0

at

\displaystyle\res_{\mathcal{U}}(\overline{f},\widehat{Q},0)=\

\displaystyle f(\widehat{Q})-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\varphi_{\omega,j}(\widehat{Q}\ominus mS)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\geq 2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{m}\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\psi_{k}(\widehat{Q}\ominus S)

\displaystyle f(\widehat{Q})-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\varphi_{\omega,j}(\widehat{Q}\ominus mS)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m\geq 1}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\psi_{k}(\widehat{Q}\ominus S)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle f(\widehat{Q})-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\varphi_{\omega,j}(\widehat{Q}\ominus mS)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\Psi_{k}(\widehat{Q}\ominus S)

∎

This finishes the reduction process. We now have the proper definition of the panorbital residues of an elliptic function.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot d_{j}(\omega)\cdot m,

\displaystyle\pano_{\Xi}(f,0)=\

\displaystyle f(\widehat{Q})-\sum\limits_{\omega\neq\widehat{\omega}}\sum\limits_{j,m\geq 1}\res_{\mathcal{U}}(f,Q_{\omega}\oplus mS,j)\cdot\varphi_{\omega,j}(\widehat{Q}\ominus mS)

Future Directions: Differential Dependence

As alluded to in Chapters
1
and
2
, criteria for summability play a basic role in calculating the differential Galois groups of linear difference equations. While this has not been explicitly shown in the elliptic case, it is the case that summability is an integral facet of the current algorithms, developed in
[

]
and
[
AZ22
]
, for these calculations in the shift and
q
-difference cases discussed in Section

. In particular there exist results that concern differential dependence of a particular family of solutions

,

5.1
Previous Results on Differential Dependence and Summability

Let us first review the requisite concepts from differential algebra. Consider a
\delta
-algebra

R
: an algebra
R
equipped with a derivation
\delta
. A
differential polynomial

L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
is an element of the polynomial ring over
R
with infinitely many variables
\delta^{(i)}(Y_{j})
for
i\geq 1
and
j=1,\dots,n
. If furthermore all the terms of
L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
belong to the
R
-span of the variables
\delta^{(i)}(Y_{j})
, we call the differential polynomial a
homogeneous linear differential polynomial
. If
S
is a
\delta
-algebra containing
R
, then
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
in
S
are
differentially dependent
over
R
if there exists a nonzero differential polynomial
L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
with coefficients in
R
such that
L(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=0
.

We have the following lemma on differential dependence of variables. This result is also proved in
[
Kol73
]
, but the statement that we need is elementary so we give the proof here.

Lemma 5.1.1
.

Let
R
be a
\delta
-field, and
S\supset R
be a
\delta
-field containing
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
. Then
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
are differentially dependent over
R
if and only if the logarithmic derivatives

\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\quad\dots,\quad\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}

are differentially dependent over
R
.

Proof.

If
\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}}
through
\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}
are differentially dependent over
R
then so are
z_{1}
through
z_{n}
, because any relation among
\frac{\delta(z_{k})}{z_{k}}
are relations among
z_{k}
. We show the other direction. We have the following diagram of inclusions of
\delta
-algebras:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right>}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\supset}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cup}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cup}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}\right>}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\supset}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>}}

The extension
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>\Big/R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>
has finite
algebraic
transcendence degree, in fact at most
n
, in view of

R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right>.

Because
z_{1}
through
z_{n}
are differentially dependent there exists a suitable relabeling of the variables such that
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right>/R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>
has finite algebraic transcendence degree: given a differential relationship between
z_{1}
through
z_{n}
from which
z_{n}
or its derivatives cannot be eliminated, adjoining sufficiently many terms of the form
\delta^{(j)}(z_{n})
to
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
will produce an algebraic relationship between some
\delta^{(j)}(z_{n})
, the lower-order derivatives of
z_{n}
, and the elements of
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>
. Differentiating that particular relationship shows that
\delta^{(\ell)}(z_{n})
are already expressed algebraically in terms of lower-order derivatives of
z_{n}
for
\ell>j
, implying that the extension
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right>/R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}\right>
has finite algebraic transcendence degree.

This shows that the extension
R\left<z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right>\Big/R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>
has finite algebraic transcendence degree, due to algebraic transcendence degree being additive in towers. Therefore the intermediate extension

R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}\right>\bigg/R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>

has finite algebraic transcendence degree. This implies that
\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}}
,
\dots
,
\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}
are differentially dependent over
R
: if they were not, then by adjoining all the derivatives of
\frac{\delta(z_{n})}{z_{n}}
to
R\left<\frac{\delta(z_{1})}{z_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(z_{n-1})}{z_{n-1}}\right>
one would obtain an extension of
infinite
algebraic transcendence degree. This proves the result.
∎

The pioneering work of
[
HS08
]
establishing the differential Galois theory of linear difference equations includes a result that links differential dependence to summability. For the formulation of this result, define a
\tau\delta
-algebra
to be an algebra, either a field of a ring, equipped with an automorphism
\tau
and a derivation
\delta
satisfying
\tau\circ\delta=\delta\circ\tau
. The following result is
[
HS08
, Proposition 3.1]
:

Proposition 5.1.2
.

Let
K
be a
\tau\delta
-field with the subfield of
\tau
constants
K^{\tau}
differentially closed and let
S\supset K
be a
\tau\delta
-ring such that the subring of
\tau
constants
S^{\tau}
is precisely
K^{\tau}
. Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in K
and
z_{1}\dots z_{n}\in S
satisfy

\tau(z_{i})-z_{i}=b_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

(5.1)

Then
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
are differentially dependent over
K
if and only if there exists a nonzero homogeneous linear differential polynomial
L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
with coefficients in
K^{\tau}
, and an element
f\in K
, such that

L(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})=\tau(f)-f.

Deeper than just a connection between differential dependence and summability, this result gives a test for when there exist differerential symmetries amongst a set of solutions
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
to the nonhomogeneous system (
5.1
) of linear difference equations. If there does not exist
L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
as described above, then no differential symmetries exist between
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
. If there does exist
L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
as above, there
do
exist differential symmetries between
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
, and searching for them is worthwhile. In the broader Galois theoretic context, this result gives us a gauge of the size of the differential Galois group associated with system (
5.1
): the existence of differential symmetries implies a smaller Galois group, and the lack of existence implies the Galois group is as large as possible.

It would certainly be daunting to search for all homogeneous linear differential polynomials
L
and elements
f\in K
satisfying
L(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})=\tau(f)-f
. The prevailing strategy for computing differential Galois groups is to show that in particular cases it suffices to search for differential polynomials
L
of particular forms. The rational shift context of
[
Arr17
, Proposition 2.1]
and the rational
q
-difference context of
[
AZ22
, Corollary 3.5]
concern systems of
homogeneous
linear difference equations

\tau(z_{i})=a_{i}z_{i}.

(5.2)

Taking the logarithmic derivative shows that this system gives rise to a nonhomogeneous system as in (
5.1
):

\displaystyle\frac{\delta(\tau(z_{i}))}{\tau(z_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(a_{i}z_{i})}{a_{i}z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}+\frac{\delta(z_{i})}{z_{i}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\tau\left(\frac{\delta(z_{i})}{z_{i}}\right)-\frac{\delta(z_{i})}{z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}},

(5.3)

using the fact that
\tau
and
\delta
commute. The authors of
[
Arr17
]
and
[
AZ22
]
apply
[
HS08
, Proposition 3.1]
to show that for determining the differential dependence of
z_{1}
,
\dots
,
z_{n}
, it suffices to search for differential polynomials

of the form

L(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=\ell_{1}Y_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}Y_{n},

where
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. These are in fact polynomials in the
algebraic
sense. Linear polynomials with integer coefficients form a much smaller search space and create much more manageable algorithms for determining differential dependence of solutions to system (
5.2
).

Here we give the precise wording of the result
[
AZ22
, Corollary 3.5]
. While it rests on an application of
[
HS08
, Proposition 3.1]
it twists the condition of summability in a manner that is mirrored in the elliptic case. For this proposition,
K=C(x)
where
C
is a differentially closed field with derivation
\delta
of characteristic
0
, and
\tau
is the automorphism on
C(x)
defined by
x\mapsto qx
where

is a nonzero
\delta

Proposition 5.1.3
.

Let
K
be a
\tau\delta
-field, and let

be a
\tau\delta
-algebra containing

such that the subalgebras of
\tau

and

are the same field

be rational functions with coefficients in the

satisfy

Then

,
\dots

if and only if there exist

not all zero and with

This result is shown in

by analyzing the

5.2
Differential Dependence in the Elliptic Case

With this context we are equipped to start analyzing the connection of summability to differential dependence in the elliptic case. There are results that we can formulate immediately using the technology of orbital residues, but it is currently unknown if there exists a result as strong as
[
AZ22
, Corollary 3.5]
in the elliptic case.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
and
\tau
be as in Chapter
3
, and let
\delta
be the derivation
\frac{d}{dz}
on
\mathbb{C}
. Note that
\tau
and
\delta
commute due to the chain rule, and this places a
\tau\delta
-field structure on
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. We will need the following computational lemma:

Lemma 5.2.1
.

Suppose
a\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}^{\times}
,
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
, and
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then

\ores\left(\delta^{r}\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a}\right),\omega,r+1\right)=(-1)^{r}\cdot r!\cdot\ores\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a},\omega,1\right)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Recall that the orbital residues are computed with respect to an analytic pinning: the pinning is still assumed to be fixed a priori.

Proof.

Recall from the discussion of Section
3.4
that the logarithmic derivative
\frac{\delta(a)}{a}
has a
\zeta
-expression of the form

\frac{\delta(a)}{a}=b+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}\zeta(z-\alpha_{k}),

where
b\in\mathbb{C}
,
m_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
are representatives of a complete set of zeros and poles of
a
modulo
\Lambda
. It follows that

\ores\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a},\omega,1\right)\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{ for all }\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s).

For
r\geq 1
the
r
th derivative of
\frac{\delta(a)}{a}
is

\delta^{r}\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a}\right)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}\zeta^{(r)}(z-\alpha_{k}),

implying that the analytic orbital residue of
\delta^{r}(\delta(a)/a)
of order
r+1
is

\ores\left(\delta^{r}\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a}\right),\omega,r+1\right)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}\cdot r!\cdot(-1)^{r}=(-1)^{r}\cdot r!\cdot\ores\left(\frac{\delta(a)}{a},\omega,1\right).

These numbers are integers for all
\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s)
, which proves the result.
∎

The following result can be shown now in the analytic setting, and it only relies on the technology of orbital residues developed in
[
DHRS18
]
and reformulated analytically in Chapter
3
:

Proposition 5.2.2
.

Let
R
be a
\tau\delta
-
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
-algebra such that the elements of
R
invariant under
\tau
are precisely the elements of
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
invariant under
\tau
: denote these subalgebras

and
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}^{\tau}
respectively. Suppose
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}^{\times}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in R^{\times}
satisfy

\tau(y_{i})=a_{i}y_{i};\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Then
y_{1}
,
\dots
,
y_{n}
are differentially dependent over
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
if and only if there exists
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
not all zero and an element

such that

\ell_{1}\delta\left(\dfrac{\delta(a_{1})}{a_{1}}\right)+\cdots+\ell_{n}\delta\left(\dfrac{\delta(a_{n})}{a_{n}}\right)=\tau(f)-f.

The proof of this result shall mimic that of
[
AZ22
, Corollary 3.5]
.

Proof.

For the if direction, assume that for some
f\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
and some integers
\ell_{1}
,
\dots
,
\ell_{n}
not all zero,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}\right)=\tau(f)-f

holds. Because
\delta
and
\tau
commute,

\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}=\tau\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)-\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}

for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Therefore

\displaystyle\tau\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)\right)-\sum\limits_{y=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau(f)-f

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad\tau\left(-f+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-f+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right).

It follows that

-f+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\delta\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)\in R^{\tau}.

From the assumptions of the proposition this implies that the above expression is also in
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}^{\tau}
, which is precisely the field
\mathbb{C}
because the shift was chosen to be a non-torsion point with respect to
\Lambda
. Therefore
y_{1}
,
\dots
,

are indeed differentially dependent over
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
.

For the only if direction, assume that
y_{1}
,
\dots
,
y_{m}
are differentially dependent over
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. Then from Lemma
5.1.1
we also have that
\frac{\delta(y_{1})}{y_{1}}
,
\dots
,
\frac{\delta(y_{n})}{y_{n}}
are differentially dependent over
\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
. From the derivation of equation (

), they satisfy the nonhomogeneous linear difference equations

\tau\left(\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}\right)-\frac{\delta(y_{i})}{y_{i}}=\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Then
[
HS08
, Proposition 3.1]
implies that there exists a nonzero homogeneous linear differential polynomial
L
and an elliptic

such that

g:=L\left(\frac{\delta(a_{1})}{a_{1}},\dots,\frac{\delta(a_{n})}{a_{n}}\right)=\tau(f_{1})-f_{1}.

We can express
g
on the level of individual terms as

g=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{r_{i}}c_{i,j}\delta^{j}\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}\right)

(5.4)

for

\delta(a_{i})/a_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{\Lambda}
only has poles of order

\delta^{j}(\delta(a_{i})/a_{i})

\ores\left(\delta^{j}\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}\right),\omega,j^{\prime}\right)=0\quad\text{ for all }j^{\prime}\geq j+1\text{ and }\omega\in\mathbb{C}/(\Lambda\oplus\mathbb{Z}s).

Let

that appears in equation (

\displaystyle\ores(g,\omega,r+1)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r_{i}}c_{i,j}\cdot\ores\left(\delta^{j}\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}\right),\omega,r+1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i,r}\cdot\ores\left(\delta^{r}\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}}\right),\omega,r+1\right)

From Lemma

\ores(g,\omega,r+1)=(-1)^{r}\cdot r!\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}c_{i,r}\cdot\ores\left(\frac{\delta(a_{i})}{a_{i}},\omega,1\right).

Because

implies that all the orbital residues of

. That is, the

with entries corresponding to

are
\mathbb{C}
-linearly dependent. Recall from Section

that the orbital residues of the

are all integers, so these vectors are in fact
