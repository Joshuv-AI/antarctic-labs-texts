Research on the prediction of slow blood flow in pPCI of STEMI patients based on CatBoost
Authors: Chao Huang, Yuekun Wei, Lianxiang Deng, Wen Li, Weilong Lin, Maochang Qin, Xiaocong Zeng, Daizheng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, the incidence of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) has been on the rise, leading to an increase in the number of patients undergoing direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI). However, some patients experience slow blood flow after pPCI, significantly raising the risk of death. To help doctors intervene early in cases of slow blood flow in pPCI patients, we employ appropriate methods for data preprocessing, model training, and prediction. This approach enables doctors to diagnose, develop, and implement treatment plans earlier. The study also demonstrates the practical application of machine learning and deep learning algorithms in analyzing slow blood flow, providing valuable references and guidance for other researchers.Methods and resultsData of patients undergoing direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) were collected from four public tertiary hospitals in Nanning, City, China. The paper employed 4 methods for imputing missing data, 9 methods for addressing data imbalance, and 8 integrated machine learning methods for prediction. By pairing each of the 4 imputation methods with each of the 9 balancing methods, 36 combined CatBoost methods were created. These 36 combinations were then each paired with one of the 8 integrated machine learning methods, resulting in a total of 288 prediction combination methods. ROC curve, AUC, and F1 score were primarily used as evaluation indicators to compare the results. In the end, the Bayesian-based Optuna method was used to optimize the hyperparameters of the CatBoost. The results showed that the AUC value obtained with GAN data imputation was the highest, and the F1 score was also elevated. After hyperparameter optimization, the AUC value of the CatBoost model improved significantly. The CatBoost prediction model identified creatine kinase isozyme, hypersensitive C-reactive protein, time to first medical contact, and stent length as the most important factors affecting slow blood flow.ConclusionsThe prediction of slow blood flow in STEMI patients undergoing direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) using CatBoost combined with GAN can achieve promising results. This approach provides a theoretical basis for doctors to intervene in advance.Keywords: Slow flow predication, Generative adversarial networks, Catboost, Data imputation, Data balancing

Acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), also known as non-Q-wave myocardial infarction, is a common acute coronary syndrome characterized by the presence of ST-segment elevation on the electrocardiogram (ECG) without significant Q-waves. There are notable differences in the clinical presentation and treatment of STEMI compared to conventional Q-wave myocardial infarction. STEMI typically involves a smaller area of the myocardium and often affects the middle portion of the coronary arteries, resulting in a smaller infarct area. Consequently, patients may not immediately recognize their symptoms. According to the Global Burden of Disease report, STEMI is a severe form of coronary heart disease and one of the leading causes of death worldwide [1].

In patients with STEMI, direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) or thrombolysis is the standard reperfusion treatment option, both of which are effective in improving patient prognosis [2]. After the infarct-related artery is opened, if myocardial tissue still cannot achieve complete reperfusion, the patient may be experiencing the Slow Flow (SF) phenomenon. Slow Flow, also known as No-Reflow (NR), is characterized by a significant slowing of blood flow within a coronary artery due to stenosis or obstruction, though complete occlusion has not yet occurred. This condition can result in inadequate blood supply to the heart muscle, leading to myocardial ischemia or infarction. When the Slow Flow phenomenon occurs, therapeutic agents often struggle to be effective due to difficulties in accessing the distal microvasculature. This reduces the benefits of reperfusion therapy and leads to serious adverse clinical outcomes, including increased in-hospital mortality and long-term adverse cardiovascular events. Therefore, prevention of slow blood flow is more crucial than treatment. Predicting slow blood flow is essential for the treatment and management of STEMI patients, as it helps clinicians develop intraoperative prevention strategies and intervention plans in advance, thereby improving surgical outcomes and mitigating the impact of the Slow Flow phenomenon.

Currently, the prediction of slow blood flow and the survival prediction of STEMI mainly relies on traditional statistical methods or traditional clinical scoring models, such as TIMI risk score, GRACE score, etc. The TIMI risk score for STEMI was created and validated in samples of STEMI patients with a specific variable clinical status [3]. The GRACE score was created and validated in samples of ACS patients and it was widely used in STEMI diagnosis [4]. Wang et al. developed a simple scoring system based on the traditional methods to predict the risk of slow blood flow in patients with STEMI treated by PCI [5].

Luis et al. collected data on 152 cases from a local hospital in Salvador, Brazil, to validate and compare the performance of two traditional clinical scoring models in the prediction of STEMI survival [6].

These traditional models perform well in some cases, but they also have limitations, such as the inability to fully reflect individual patient differences and the difficulty of handling large amounts of complex clinical data.

As a result, researchers have begun to explore the use of machine learning algorithms to predict slow blood flow with the aim of improving the accuracy and efficiency of prediction.

Frolovd et al. used Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to construct a prediction model to predict whether patients with myocardial infarction treated with PCI would experience slow blood flow [7]. The ROC curve of the model corresponds to an AUC of 0.69, and the accuracy is not high enough to meet the needs of most of the practical applications. The dataset collected in this paper has only 4% of the patients with slow flow, and there is a serious imbalance in the proportion of patients with slow flow and those with non slow flow, but the study does not have the data were balanced.

Li selected several learning models for comparison in terms of model prediction performance for in-hospital mortality in STEMI patients, including logistic regression, Cox regression, random forest, plain Bayes, and Bayesian networks [8]. The study only used the traditional mean and median to fill in the missing data, and did not use advanced methods, such as multiple interpolation, K-nearest neighbor, and deep neural network. The study has not yet selected the emerging prediction methods, such as gradient boosting decision tree and deep neural network, to participate in the comparison.

Aiming at the current status and shortcomings of research, this paper adopts CatBoost as the core method of the prediction model by combining CatBoost with its characteristics of high prediction performance and interpretability. In addition, other machine learning methods will be used in this paper to preprocess the data, improve the quality of the dataset, and enhance the performance of the model.

CatBoost

CatBoost is an improved algorithm for gradient boosting decision tree [11] proposed by Liudmila et al. in recent years [12]. The algorithm adopts a fully symmetric tree as the basic model, and uses the method of sort boosting to combat the noise points in the training set, so as to reduce the bias of the gradient estimation, solve the problem of prediction shift (prediction shift), and improve the prediction effect. At the same time, the algorithm uses combined category features, which can effectively utilize the connection between features, greatly enriching the feature dimension and improving the classification effect.

The most important aspect for the processing of category-based features is to encode all the categories of a single category-based feature and represent the corresponding categories in the corresponding numeric format, so that the model can be trained and analyzed. One traditional encoding method is One-Hot encoding [13]. One-Hot encoding converts each category into a binary vector of only 0s and 1s. Each element in the vector corresponds to a category, and the element corresponding to the current category will be assigned a value of 1, while the rest of the elements will be assigned a value of 0. However, if the number of categories of a certain category-type feature is large, One-Hot coding will produce too many features, which affects the training effect of the model. One solution is to obtain the encoding by calculating target statistics (TS), such as calculating probability values. Compared to the traditional greedy target statistics (Greedy TS) [14] and holdout target statistics (Holdout TS), CatBoost proposes a new target statistic called ordered target statistics (Ordered TS). The calculated Ordered TS is computed by randomly ordering the data. For each sample, the TS value for the category value of that sample is calculated using the previous data of that sample. This method avoids the problem of label leakage caused by the direct use of label information in the Greedy TS method, and also avoids the problem of decreasing the number of samples available in the training set in the Holdout TS method.

The core method of CatBoost is shown in Fig. 2. Multiple numerical and categorical features are first obtained from the training set, and the corresponding target statistic method is used to encode the categorical features. Then, several features with strong correlation are combined to form new category-type features to enhance the classification effect of the model. Then, category classification is continuously performed in each prediction and the weights of the corresponding categories are enhanced. Meanwhile, the previous prediction results will be input to the next prediction as the basis of the next prediction. Finally, the model will take the average weight of all the predictions to serve as the final prediction result of the model.

The core method of a CatBoost model

CatBoost, to further address the overfitting problem, proposes a new method for gradient boosting (Boosting) sessions called Ordered Boosting. In the Ordered Boosting method, the samples are first randomly disrupted, and then, for each sample, the model is trained using only the samples that are ordered ahead of it. Such a model is used to estimate the first-order and second-order gradients of the predictions of this sample. These gradients are then used to construct the structure of a tree, and the value of each leaf node of the final tree is computed using the whole sample. Compared to traditional unordered boosting (Plain Boosting), Ordered Boosting reduces the prediction bias caused by gradient bias and reduces the likelihood of model overfitting.

In this paper, to verify and compare the effect of CatBoost, other methods are used to process the data, respectively. The classification effects of CatBoost and other methods are compared through relevant indicators. Since Catboost is a method based on decision tree model, to ensure the scientific nature of the comparison process, some similar methods based on decision tree model need to be used as reference methods. These related methods are briefly described below:

LightGBM: LightGBM is an efficient, distributed, and scalable Gradient Boosting method [15]. LightGBM uses Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) to deal with instances with large gradients (i.e., instances that are harder to predict), which improves the efficiency of the model. LightGBM includes the Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB) method, which bundles mutually exclusive features (i.e., features that are rarely non-zero at the same time) to reduce the number of features, thereby reducing computation time without significantly affecting accuracy.

XGBoost: XGBoost is an efficient and versatile Gradient Boosting method. XGBoost contains several algorithmic optimizations that improve model performance and accuracy [16]. It introduces a novel tree learning algorithm that can handle sparse data and a formalization of the regularized model to prevent model overfitting, resulting in more robust model performance. XGBoost handles missing data through a sparse-aware algorithm that efficiently handles sparse data due to missing values or categorical variable coding.

Gradient Boosting: Gradient Boosting is a machine learning method for regression and classification problems [17]. It generates predictive models in the form of an ensemble of weak predictive models (usually decision trees). Similar to other Boosting methods, Gradient Boosting builds models in a staged manner, but it extends and enhances these methods by optimizing an arbitrarily variable loss function. Because Gradient Boosting can be used with any differentiable loss function, it is suitable for a wide variety of machine learning tasks, including two-class classification, multi-class classification, and regression.

AdaBoost: AdaBoost is a machine learning method. As an ensemble method, AdaBoost is used to improve the performance of decision trees on binary classification problems [18]. It creates a strong classifier by combining multiple weak learners (usually decision trees.) AdaBoost can achieve high accuracy on binary classification problems, often outperforming the underlying classification model.

Bagging: Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) is an integrated machine learning method. It aims to reduce variance in model learning and avoid overfitting of the model, thus improving the stability and accuracy of machine learning algorithms [19]. It can be applied to a variety of methods, especially for high variance methods like decision trees.

Random Forest: Random Forest is an integrated machine learning method. Random Forest works by constructing multiple decision trees at training time and outputting either the majority vote category of the tree as a result of the classification task, or the average predicted value of the tree as a result of the regression task [20]. Random Forest references Bagging's method of samples drawn from the training set with replacement (i.e., bootstrap samples) to build each tree, thus introducing more randomness and ensuring diversity in each tree.

ExtraTrees: ExtraTrees (Extremely Randomized Trees) is an integrated machine learning method. On different subsamples of the dataset, ExtraTrees improves prediction accuracy and controls overfitting by fitting many randomized decision trees (also known as extra-trees) and using averaging [21]. While Random Forest selects the optimal split point for each feature considered, ExtraTrees makes the tree construction process more random using random thresholds for each feature, rather than finding the best possible thresholds, so that features can be randomly split.

Imputation method for missing clinical data

With the development of machine learning techniques in recent years, some researchers have tried to use emerging techniques to realize missing data filling.

GAN: Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN, Generative Adversarial Networks) is a deep learning model [22], which is one of the most promising approaches for unsupervised learning on complex distributions in recent years. The model learns by letting the two modularizers in the framework, i.e., generative model and discriminative model, play with each other to produce better-performing output models, which better satisfy specific tasks, such as data augmentation and data classification. In the original GAN theory, the generator and discriminator can be a neural network or a function that can fit the corresponding generator and discriminator. However, in practical applications, deep neural networks are generally used as generators and discriminators. A good GAN application requires good training methods; otherwise, the output may be undesirable due to the free nature of the neural network model.

MissForest: MissForest is an estimation method for dealing with missing data and is particularly suitable for dealing with complex datasets with mixed numerical and categorical data, non-linear relationships of variables, and interactions between variables [23].MissForest works by using the random forest algorithm for better prediction and estimation of the missing values in the dataset.

MICEForest: MICEForest is an extension of the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method [24].MICE is a well-recognized method for dealing with missing data. MICE can create multiple imputations based on a series of regression models to predict and estimate missing values in datasets, and estimation to predict and estimate the treatment of missing data.

KNN: K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) is a simple but powerful algorithm in machine learning that can be used for classification and regression tasks [25]. KNN does not learn the model explicitly but makes predictions by storing the training dataset and analyzing the most similar instances in the stored data. KNN can also be used to estimate the missing values in a dataset using its similarity principle to estimate missing data points.

Balancing method for imbalancing clinical data

To balance the training data, we compared various methods for dealing with imbalanced data. The methods used are as follows:

Random oversampling: Random Over-sampling (ROS) is a method used to solve the problem of class imbalance in machine learning datasets [26]. It replicates the minority class data by randomly taking samples from the minority class data, so that the number of samples in the minority class data is similar to the number of samples in the majority class data.

SMOTE: Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) is an innovative oversampling method for solving the class imbalance problem of datasets in machine learning [27]. It is based on the K-nearest neighbor method, which selects a few adjacent minority class samples to synthesize new minority class samples, so that the proportion of minority class data is similar to the proportion of majority class data. SMOTE can effectively reduce the overfitting phenomenon, and it can be better applied to multiclassification problems.

ADASYN: ADASYN (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling) is an oversampling method for solving unbalanced dataset problems in machine learning [28]. It introduces a key innovation based on SMOTE. It can adaptively generate new samples based on the learning difficulty of different minority samples.

Random Under-sampling: Random Undersampling (RUS) is a method used to solve the class imbalance problem in machine learning datasets [29]. It removes samples from the majority class data by randomly taking samples from the majority class data, so that the number of samples from the majority class data is similar to the number of samples from the minority class data. This method is simpler compared to more advanced sampling methods.

Cluster centroids: Cluster Centroids is an undersampling method used to address class imbalance in machine learning datasets [30]. Unlike Random Undersampling, Cluster Centroids aim to retain information from the majority class while reducing its size. This approach effectively reduces the number of instances and attempts to maintain the structural integrity and distribution of the original data by forming corresponding clusters in the majority class and representing the majority class with the centroids of these clusters.

NearMiss: NearMiss is a class of undersampling methods used to deal with unbalanced datasets in the field of machine learning [31]. It is used to balance the distribution of classes by reducing the size of the majority class. However, it has a specific strategy to ensure that the resulting dataset improves the effectiveness of the machine learning process. NearMiss-3 (NearMiss version 3) is one of the variants, which is characterized by undersampling based on the distance of the majority class instances from the minority class instances.

SMOTEENN: SMOTEENN is a hybrid sampling method [32]. It combines two powerful methods, SMOTE and Edited Nearest Neighbors (ENN), to solve the class imbalance problem in machine learning. This method first creates synthetic samples by SMOTE to oversample a few classes. It then uses ENN to remove any instances that may cause class overlap or noise, thus cleaning up the dataset and solving the dataset imbalance problem.

SMOTETomek: SMOTETomek is a hybrid sampling method that combines the SMOTE oversampling method and the Tomek Links undersampling method for dealing with unbalanced datasets in machine learning [33]. This method aims to address class imbalance by oversampling a minority group using SMOTE. The dataset is then cleaned by removing Tomek Links (i.e., pairs of instances that are very close but of opposite classes) to resolve the imbalance and improve the quality of the dataset.

Discussion

Model discussion

Traditional scoring models like GRACE and TIMI are widely used in clinical practice to predict the risk and manage patients with myocardial infarction. However, these models primarily rely on static clinical parameters and have limitations, such as the inability to process dynamic physiological information and the exclusion of certain relevant factors.

In the preface section, this paper mentions that the risk scoring model proposed by Wang et al. [5] has several shortcomings. These include the limited number of variables, which may not always provide accurate predictions based on individual patient differences; the model's reliance on traditional methods, making it unsuitable for large-scale clinical datasets; and the lack of validation using independent data. In contrast, Luis et al.'s study of TIMI scores and GRACE scores [6] highlights several issues that scoring models may encounter. These include the subjective influence of researchers on the quantification criteria of variables and the scoring criteria of the scales, as well as the potential impact of the small sample size on the generalization performance of the scoring model.

In a study by Ke et al. on the prediction of major cardiovascular events in patients with acute chest pain in the emergency department [34], TIMI scores, HEART scores, and GRACE scores were compared, revealing several shortcomings. These methods struggled with sample heterogeneity, which could not be sufficiently addressed by sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Additionally, the characteristics of the sample's follow-up duration could affect the prediction results.

While traditional scoring models have unique advantages in specific clinical scenarios, their limitations in addressing complex situations should not be overlooked. This is especially true for predicting slow blood flow in STEMI patients, where these models often lack sufficient accuracy and sensitivity.

In contrast, machine learning models, particularly the CatBoost algorithm, have demonstrated significant value and advantages in predicting slow blood flow in STEMI patients. Unlike traditional models, machine learning models can process vast amounts of clinical data and identify complex, non-linear relationships, and interactions, resulting in more accurate risk assessments.

Machine learning models are particularly well suited for handling high-dimensional and unbalanced datasets, which often contain complex interactions between variables. Efficient algorithms can effectively extract useful information from these complex data for risk assessment [35]. Given that myocardial infarction patient samples are typically unbalanced, with a lower incidence of disease events compared to non-disease events, machine learning models can address this imbalance through appropriate strategies, thereby improving predictive performance.

Machine learning models exhibit strong generalization capabilities. By learning from extensive patient data, these models can capture general patterns of disease progression and make accurate predictions for new, unseen patients. This is particularly valuable for clinicians, as machine learning models can help assess disease risk at an early stage, enabling timely interventions [36].

Despite the common perception of deep learning models as "black boxes," machine learning models can achieve good interpretability. By incorporating interpretable tools and techniques, such as feature importance scores, researchers can identify which clinical features significantly impact the model's predictions [37]. This enhances the model's transparency and allows physicians to better understand the decision-making process, enabling them to adjust treatment plans based on the model's feedback.

The implementation of machine learning models fosters multidisciplinary collaboration. Developing and validating these models require close cooperation among medical experts, data scientists, biostatisticians, and other specialists [38]. This interdisciplinary effort not only enhances the models' accuracy and utility but also provides a platform for mutual learning and progress among researchers.

To address the limitations of traditional scoring models and leverage the advantages of machine learning, this paper adopts a CatBoost-based approach. The goal is to improve the accuracy of slow blood flow prediction by integrating more clinical and physiological parameters. Additionally, the paper compares the effects of different imputation and balancing methods on model performance and validates the proposed model by comparing it with other classification methods.

Compared to other studies, the model in this paper addresses several shortcomings. Frolovd et al. proposed an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model for predicting slow blood flow in myocardial infarction patients treated with PCI, which achieved an AUC of 0.69, indicating inadequate prediction accuracy [7]. In contrast, the model in this paper achieves an AUC of 0.77 to 0.815, demonstrating better predictive performance.

Li et al. compared various learning models for predicting in-hospital mortality in STEMI patients, including logistic regression, Cox regression, random forest, naive Bayes, and Bayesian network. They found that random forest achieved the highest AUC (0.842–0.849), outperforming other methods. However, their study had limitations, such as using only traditional mean and median imputation for missing data and not including emerging prediction methods like gradient boosting decision trees and deep neural networks [8]. In contrast, this paper employs Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for advanced data imputation, improving dataset quality. The study also compares the CatBoost model with various decision tree models, finding that CatBoost achieves a higher AUC, verifying its superior predictive performance.

In summary, machine learning models, particularly those based on CatBoost, show great potential and advantages in predicting slow blood flow in STEMI patients. Accurate risk assessment through these models can enhance patient survival quality and reduce medical resource wastage, crucial for advancing cardiovascular disease prevention and treatment. This paper aims to provide a more accurate and comprehensive solution for slow blood flow prediction in STEMI patients, offering better decision support for clinicians.

Discussion of slow flow

In this paper, we identified several key factors strongly associated with the risk of slow blood flow in STEMI patients by analyzing predictors in depth and applying machine learning methods to clinical data. These factors include creatine kinase isoenzyme, ultrasensitive C-reactive protein, and troponin I.

Creatine kinase isoenzyme emerged as a significant predictor. A previous study found that levels of creatine kinase isoenzyme in patients with slow blood flow were at least twice the upper limit of normal [39]. Creatine kinase isoenzyme is a recognized biomarker in the diagnosis of AMI [40]. According to a study about the usefulness of cardiac biomarkers, the researcher suggested that creatine kinase isoenzyme was released into the bloodstream by necrotic cardiomyocytes during myocardial infarction, which leaded to a poor prognosis [41]. This indicates that STEMI patients with creatine kinase isoenzyme levels substantially above this threshold have a significantly increased risk of developing slow blood flow.

Ultrasensitive C-reactive protein is also an important predictor. According to a study, ultrasensitive C-reactive protein levels were significantly higher in patients with slow flow (3.4 ± 0.8 mg/L) compared to those without slow flow (2.0 ± 0.9 mg/L) [42]. In some studies about CRP–albumin ratio as predictor markers of disease activity, these articles indicated that ultrasensitive C-reactive protein was a novel inflammatory marker [43] and it had strong predictive power for 30-day mortality in patients undergoing pPCI treatment [44]. This suggests that significantly elevated levels of ultrasensitive C-reactive protein are among the most critical predictors of slow blood flow.

Troponin I is another crucial factor influencing prediction. In one study, troponin I levels in patients with slow blood flow were found to be 100 times higher than the upper reference limit [45]. Troponin I is also a recognized biomarker in the diagnosis of AMI [40]. According to an article about the kinetics of troponin I, the researcher found that the onset of acute ischemia initiates cardiomyocyte necrosis, which causes the breakdown of cell membranes and organelles, leading to the subsequent release of cellular proteins into the bloodstream [46]. This indicates that significantly elevated troponin I levels are also a key predictor of slow blood flow.

In summary, this paper presents a multidimensional model for predicting slow blood flow. By integrating data from various aspects—including basic patient information, laboratory test results, and medical history—the model can more accurately predict the risk of slow blood flow in STEMI patients.

This finding has significant implications for clinicians, as it aids in early identification of high-risk patients and provides a foundation for developing individualized treatment plans. For instance, patients can be administered coronary vasoprotective drugs before pPCI treatment to effectively reduce the risk of slow blood flow during the procedure [47]. Additionally, emerging technologies, such as dual-guided catheterization techniques, can be employed to prevent slow blood flow in patients [48].

However, it is important to recognize that predicting slow blood flow remains challenging. Future studies should continue to explore new predictors and methods to enhance prediction models, aiming to improve their accuracy and reliability.

Conclusion

In this paper, we used CatBoost to construct a model for analyzing and predicting the onset of slow blood flow in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) who underwent direct percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI). The goal is to enable doctors to intervene early.

We collected data from STEMI patients undergoing pPCI from four public tertiary hospitals in Nanning City, China. During data preprocessing, four methods, including Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and MissForest, were used to fill in missing data. Nine methods, such as Random Undersampling, Random Oversampling, and SMOTE, were applied to balance the data. AUC, F1 score, and other metrics were used to evaluate the results.

For model training, we applied eight methods, including CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost, to predict slow blood flow. The performance of each model was compared using AUC, F1 score, and other evaluation metrics to verify the CatBoost model's performance. In the model optimization session, the Optuna method based on Bayesian optimization was used to optimize hyperparameters such as iterations, depth, and learning rate.

The results showed that the highest AUC values and higher F1 scores were achieved after data filling using GAN. The AUC values of the CatBoost model improved after hyperparameter optimization. According to the CatBoost prediction model, key factors affecting slow blood flow included creatine kinase isoenzyme, ultrasensitive C-reactive protein, time to the first medical contact, and stent length.

One limitation of this paper is the geographical restriction of the dataset. Due to the scarcity of publicly available slow blood flow datasets, finding other suitable datasets for further validation is challenging, affecting the model's generalizability. To enhance the model's applicability in other regions, it would be necessary to obtain and integrate data from those areas. Other shortcomings include discrepancies between the model's factor importance judgments and clinical experience, as well as the inability to analyze the specifics of the slow blood flow condition in detail.

In summary, this study proposes that a CatBoost-based model for predicting slow blood flow in STEMI patients undergoing pPCI can achieve promising results. This model provides a theoretical basis for doctors to intervene in advance for these patients.
