Application of percutaneous cryoablation for pulmonary nodules and influence in pulmonary function
Authors: Huohuan Tian, Yue Cheng, Dan Li, Rao Du, Kaige Wang, Hongli Bai, Wanxin Liang, Jue Li, Weimin Li, Dan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCryoablation has been reported as a non-surgical option for early-stage lung cancer and a palliative cytoreductive therapy for advanced disease and pulmonary metastases; however, evidence remains relatively limited.PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of cryoablation in patients with different types of pulmonary nodules and to identify appropriate clinical scenarios for its application.MethodThis retrospective study included 94 patients with 107 pulmonary nodules who underwent CT-guided percutaneous cryoablation between May 2022 and May 2024. Patients were classified into curative-intent (early-stage lung cancer) and palliative-intent (advanced/recurrent lung cancer or pulmonary metastases) groups. The curative group was further subdivided into solitary pulmonary nodules, synchronous multiple primary nodules (sMPN), and second primary lung cancer (SPLC). Primary outcomes were local recurrence-free survival (LRFS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in the curative group, and progression-free survival (PFS) in the palliative group. Secondary outcomes included residual nodule progression in sMPN patients, pulmonary function changes and adverse events.ResultSeventy-three patients with 86 nodules received curative treatment, and 21 patients received palliative treatment. Cryoablation achieved 100% local control in pure ground-glass nodules (pGGNs), while local recurrence occurred in one part-solid and two solid nodules. Among 22 patients with sMPN, all the patients showed 100% LRFS and RFS, regardless of whether they received combined surgery and cryoablation or cryoablation alone. In 15 patients with SPLC, local control was 100%, but three developed recurrence or metastasis outside the ablation zone. The median PFS in the palliative group was 409 days. Pulmonary function did not show significant deterioration after cryoablation. Pneumothorax and hemoptysis were the most common adverse events (21.4% each), with a fatal complication rate of 0.9%.ConclusionCryoablation preserves pulmonary function and may benefit patients ineligible for surgery. It may serve as a treatment option for patients with unresectable pure ground-glass nodules or GGN-dominant MPN. Careful avoidance of vascular injury during the procedure is critical to minimizing the risk of potentially fatal complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15929-z.Keywords: percutaneous cryoablation, local control, recurrence free survival, pulmonary function

Introduction

Currently, surgical resection remains the standard of care for early-stage lung cancer. Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients are deemed ineligible for surgery because of compromised cardiopulmonary function, poor performance status, or personal refusal of surgical treatment. For these patients, non-surgical approaches represent an important therapeutic alternative, among which radiotherapy and thermal ablation are the most widely applied modalities in clinical practice [1].

Previous study has demonstrated that SBRT may achieve long-term outcomes comparable to those of surgical resection for early-stage lung cancer smaller than 2 cm [2]. It has become alternative treatment to surgery. However, SBRT may be associated with treatment-related complications, including radiation pneumonitis, rib fractures, fistula, and other adverse events. Pre-existing interstitial lung disease, severe pulmonary dysfunction, combined immunotherapy or targeted therapy are known risk factors for radiation pneumonitis [3]. Meanwhile, the feasibility and safety of SBRT are strongly influenced by tumor location, particularly for centrally located tumors [4]. Finally, a definitive pathological diagnosis of malignancy is a prerequisite for SBRT. However, biopsy of ground-glass nodules yields false-negative results in considerable cases. For patients in whom SBRT is contraindicated or associated with an unacceptable risk of toxicity, ablation may serve as an alternative treatment [5]. Thermal ablation encompasses various modalities, including radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), and cryoablation. For tumors with a diameter less than 3 cm, all three ablation modalities can achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes. A recent meta-analysis suggested that cryoablation provides superior local tumor control compared with radiofrequency ablation [6]. Moreover, a comprehensive review summarizing multiple studies reported that cryoablation was associated with the lowest recurrence rate and better patient tolerance in the treatment of early-stage lung cancer [7]. Specifically, patients with pacemakers may undergo cryoablation more safely, as pacemaker interference is minimal [8]. Notably, cryoablation offers distinct advantages for patients with tumors located subpleural region, airway, diaphragm [9, 10]. Its ability to create a well-defined ice ball enhances the monitoring of therapeutic efficacy [11]. A 2012 report from Japan indicated that the local control rates for stage I non-small cell lung cancer after cryoablation at 2 and 3 years were 78% and 67%, respectively [12], underscoring its effectiveness in treating early-stage lung cancer.

Ablation therapies are primarily indicated for patients with stage IA peripheral NSCLC who are ineligible for surgery, particularly those with compromised cardiopulmonary function. Nevertheless, the optimal patient population for these minimally invasive treatments remains undefined. Ablation is expected to have minimal impact on pulmonary function, yet robust data to support this assumption is limited. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the efficacy of cryoablation for pulmonary nodules and its effects on pulmonary function.

Methods

Study population and outcomes

This was a retrospective study including patients who underwent CT-guided percutaneous cryoablation for pulmonary nodules or lesions between May 2022 and May 2024. Pulmonary nodules were either pathologically confirmed as lung cancer or radiologically assessed as malignant by both respiratory medicine specialists and thoracic surgeons. For curative intent population, patients fulfilling one of the following criteria were included: impaired pulmonary function (FEV1 or DCLO ≤ 50%); ≥75 years associated with increased surgical risk; pulmonary hypertension; resting or exercise PaO2 < 55 mmHg, and pCO2 > 45 mmHg; left ventricular ejection fraction ≤ 40%; patients with prior lung resection who were intolerant to repeat surgery; multiple pulmonary nodules (MPN) located in different lung lobes following multidisciplinary team discussion (MDT); unsuitable for stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) due to existing interstitial lung abnormality, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or lack of definitive pathological confirmation; patients with numerous or diffusely distributed pulmonary nodules; patient refusal of surgery or SBRT after shared decision-making. For palliative intent population, the inclusion criteria were: patients experienced local disease progression after systemic therapies; patients with large pulmonary tumors underwent cytoreductive or palliative treatment; extrapulmonary primary maligancy with pulmonary oligometastases. The exclusion criteria included: the platelet count is lower than 500,000/µL; coagulation disorders; inability to place the ablation probe due to proximity to major vessels, airways, or mediastinal structures.

All the patients received pre-ablation examinations including cranial and abdominal CT, and bone SPECT, as well as bronchoscopy, or PET–CT. No history of lung cancer, no evidence of hilar or mediastinal lymph node involvement, and no distant organ involvement were classified as having early-stage primary lung cancer. These patients were further divided into solitary pulmonary nodule and synchronous multiple primary nodule (sMPN), which was defined as the presence of two or more distinct pulmonary lesions detected simultaneously and considered to be independent primary tumors based on Martini-Melamed’s criteria. Patients with suspected metastases on PET–CT or those with metastatic disease confirmed by tissue biopsy were classified as having advanced lung cancer. In patients with a history of malignancy, pulmonary nodules presenting as ground-glass nodules (GGNs) were considered secondary primary lung lesions, whereas solid nodules were regarded as suspected recurrent or metastatic lesions.

CT-guided percutaneous cryoablation procedure and lung puncture

The treatment position was determined based on a comprehensive analysis of imaging data and the patient’s clinical condition. A grid was affixed to the patient’s body to aid in accurate targeting. Real-time CT scanning provided continuous imaging of the tumor’s size, morphology, and its relationship with surrounding organs, which facilitated the determination of critical parameters such as puncture position, insertion depth, angle, and the number of ablation probes required. The corresponding puncture points were then marked accordingly. The cryoablation system was activated and tested for three minutes to ensure proper functioning. The formation of an ice ball and the absence of continuous bubbles underwater near the probe confirmed that the abaltion device was operating as intended.

Under local anesthesia, the ablation probe(s) were percutaneously inserted into the target lesion according to the pre-procedural plan. Briefly, for lesions smaller than 2 cm, 1 cryoprobe was usually inserted, and for 2- to 3-cm lesions, 2 cryoprobes were used to secure a freezing margin. For a successful ablation, it was essential that the tip of the probe extends approximately 5 mm beyond the margins of the lesion. The cryoablation process commenced after confirming the probe’s position via CT scan. Cryo-thermal multi-modal ablation utilized liquid nitrogen as the cryogen, achieving a minimum temperature of -150℃~ -170 °C for a duration of 5 to 15 min. Following cryoablation, rewarming was typically conducted for 3 to 8 min. One complete cycle of treatment consists of a freeze phase followed by a thaw phase, known as a freeze-thaw cycle. The number of freeze-thaw cycles, which ranged from 1 to 3 (usually 3), was determined based on the specific characteristics of the lesion. It was ensured that the ablation zone extended at least 1 cm beyond the edges of the target lesion. During the ablation, CT scans were conducted at 5- to 10-minute intervals to monitor the advancement of the ice ball and its proximity to adjacent normal tissues and organs. Treatment parameters were adjusted promptly based on the distance between the lesion’s boundary and surrounding structures. During the rewarming phase, the probe(s) were gradually or stepwise retracted. A CT scan was performed immediately following the procedure to evaluate any potential complications, such as bleeding or pneumothorax, which would be addressed as necessary.

Data collection

Information gathered included age, gender, smoking history, smoking cessation, comorbidities, tumor history and treatment strategy, previous lung cancer surgery, current cancer therapeutic regimen, density, location, and maximum diameter of the target lung nodules, histology results of the nodule prior to ablation, and the status of hilar or mediastinal lymph nodes as well as any suspicious metastasis. The imaging data mentioned above were concurrently assessed and collected by both a pulmonologist and a radiologist.

Follow-up and outcomes

Post-ablation CT imaging was performed at 48 h, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years to assess the outcomes of cryoablation. The ice ball formed during cryoablation, which typically exhibits well-defined margins on CT imaging, was used as a surrogate marker of ablation adequacy. At the follow-up time points described above, the maximal cross-sectional area of the ice ball and the number of contiguous axial CT slices (slice thickness, 1 mm) encompassing the ice ball were measured. Ice ball volume was estimated by multiplying the maximal cross-sectional area by the number of contiguous slices.

Local control was defined radiologically as a reduction in size or stability of the ablation zone (ice ball) on serial follow-up imaging, without the appearance of new lesions within the ablation area. In contrast, local recurrence was defined as continuous focal or diffuse enlargement of the ablated lesion on follow-up imaging, or the development of new lesions within the ablation zone. Local recurrence-free survival (LRFS) was defined as the interval from the date of cryoablation to the first radiologically confirmed local recurrence. Distant tumor progression was defined as the appearance of new solid pulmonary nodules outside the ablation region or evidence of lymph node or organ involvement consistent with metastatic disease. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from cryoablation to the first occurrence of local recurrence, distant progression, whichever occurred first. For patients receiving palliative therapy, progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the date of palliative ablation to the first documented disease progression at any site or death from any cause. The final follow-up date for survival analysis was January 16, 2026. Imaging assessments were independently performed by one radiologist and one respiratory physician. Inter-observer reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC(A,1)). In cases of discrepant interpretations, a consensus was reached after further review by senior respiratory physicians and radiologists.

Adverse events resulting from cryoablation were documented. Instances of pneumothorax requiring thoracentesis or chest tube drainage, hemoptysis, or thoracic hemorrhage necessitating hemostatic agents and/or blood transfusions were recorded. Additionally, some rare complications were noted, including cardiac arrest, fever, chest pain, arrhythmia, acute exacerbation of COPD, and nerve injury.

Regarding pulmonary function, we focused on parameters indicative of ventilation and gas exchange capabilities. Pre- and post-cryoablation assessments included forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FEV1/FVC ratio (FEV1%), total lung capacity (TLC), diffusion lung capacity for CO (DLCO SB) at pre and post cryoablation of individuals were collected and paired compared. Patients who underwent lung surgery between the pre-ablation and post-ablation pulmonary function assessments were excluded.

The predicted reduction in pulmonary function was calculated as follows: Predicted reduction volume= (preoperative pulmonary function) × (Ablation volume / total lung volume). The ablation volume was estimated based on the ice ball volume measured on CT scanning within 48 h after the procedure, calculated by multiplying the maximal cross-sectional area by the number of contiguous slices.

The primary outcome measured were local recurrence free survival and recurrence free survival for curative intent population, and PFS for palliative intent population. Secondary outcomes included adverse events occurring within one week after cryoablation, and changes in pulmonary function over a two-year period post-procedure. Particularly, in patients with sMPN, progression of residual untreated nodules defined as an increase in maximal diameter (≥ 2 mm), the emergence of a solid component, or an increase in the size of the solid component (≥ 2 mm) was also observed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as either means or medians, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Parametric tests, including t-tests and ANOVA, were employed for group comparisons when the data followed a normal distribution. Non-parametric tests, such as the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test, were applied when the normality assumption was violated. For self-comparisons, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were utilized. Recurrence-free survival was illustrated using the Kaplan-Meier curve, and log-rank test was used to compare groups. A significant difference was defined as a P value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.5.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 9).

Cryoablation procedure characteristic

The cryoablation procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2, with detailed parameters documented in Table 3 and Fig. 3. Most patients undergoing radical ablation completed their procedure within 30 min, utilizing a single ablation needle for three cycles. In contrast, tumor debulking typically required the use of two needles and extended duration. Regarding nodule types, the majority of GGNs were treated with a single cryoablation needle, whereas mGGN and SN typically involved the use of two ablation needles. Notably, one patient with a relatively larger mGGN, which exhibited cystic changes, was treated with three ablation needles. Ground-glass nodule ablations tended to be completed in less than 35 min, whereas solid nodules typically required around 40 min. Nearly all nodules underwent three cycles of ablation, and the needles were withdrawn after a 5-minute rewarming period, regardless of nodule type.

Treatment efficacy of cryoablation for lung nodule

Inter-observer agreement for ablation volume measurements on post-ablation CT scans acquired within 48 h was excellent, with an ICC of 0.913 (95% CI: 0.865–0.944). This finding demonstrates excellent inter-observer reliability and supports the reproducibility of serial follow-up measurements. In the curative-intent group, tumor progression occurred in 6 patients. The local control rates at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years were 100% (82/82), 100% (82/82), 100% (82/82), and 95% (55/58), respectively. Sensitivity analyses assuming worst- and best-case scenarios for nodules with incomplete or short follow-up yielded 1-year local control rate of 93.0-100% and a 2-year local control rate range of 62.8–96.5%. Local recurrence was observed only in the solitary pulmonary nodule (SPN) group, including one part-solid nodule and two solid nodules (Fig. 4A and B). No distant progression was observed in the SPN group. Representative imaging of local control is shown in Figure S1, and images of the three local recurrence cases are presented in Fig. 5. Patients with synchronous multiple primary lung nodule were associated with the favorable prognosis (Fig. 4A). In the sMPN group, patients were further categorized into four subgroups according to treatment strategy: cryoablation followed by surgery (n = 10), surgery followed by cryoablation (n = 5), multiple-session cryoablation (n = 4), and single-session cryoablation (n = 3) (Table 4). Detailed treatment information is provided in Table S2. In all the patients receiving combined treatment, ablated and surgically resected lesions were distributed bilaterally in the lungs. The interval between ablation and surgery ranged from 3 days to 6 months in ablation-first treatment, whereas 7 days to 9 months in surgery-first treatment (Table S2). Nearly all nodules treated with multiple ablation sessions were ipsilateral. Lesion-level local control was 100% across all treatment strategies (Table 4). However, residual untreated nodules existed in some patients, particularly in those who underwent single-session cryoablation (Table 4). In the second primary lung cancer group, previous surgical approaches and timing are summarized in Table S3. The time intervals from lung surgery history to ablation were 2–9 years. No local recurrence or progression of residual nodules was observed. However, three patients developed newly detected distant lesions. Recurrence cases in secondary primary lung cancer

Because pathological confirmation was not available for all treated nodules, we compared recurrence-free survival (RFS) and local recurrence-free survival (LRFS) between pathologically confirmed nodules and radiologically presumed malignant nodules. As shown in Fig. 4, RFS did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 4C). Similarly, LRFS showed no statistically significant difference, although a trend toward better local control was observed in radiologically presumed nodules (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that the inclusion of radiologically presumed malignant nodules did not substantially bias the survival outcomes, although the possibility of confounding by benign nodules cannot be completely excluded.

As for palliative group, 87.5% patients (14/16) experienced tumor progression with the median PFS 409 days (Figure S2).

The influence in pulmonary function of cryoablation and its adverse events

The analysis revealed that the cryoablation procedure did not adversely affect pulmonary ventilation or diffusion function (Table 6). Meanwhile, the predicted reduction based on ablation volume and actual observed reduction pulmonary function were also calculated and compared (Table 6). Furthermore, we stratified patients according to nodule size and the time interval between pre- and post-ablation assessments. In this study, a total of 103 cryoablation sessions were carried out on the 94 enrolled patients. The adverse events related to ablation are detailed in Table 7. Pneumothorax and hemoptysis emerged as the most prevalent complications, each with an incidence rate of 21.4%. Fortunately, all patients who experienced adverse events successfully resolved their condition following routine medical management. One patient (Pre-ablation platelet count: 88 × 10⁹/L) developed a severe complication shortly after cryoablation. Approximately 5 min after the procedure, the patient experienced sudden bradycardia (heart rate decreased to 30 beats/min), hypotension (70/50mmHg), and impaired consciousness. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was immediately initiated, and intravenous epinephrine was administered. The patient was intubated and mechanically ventilated, and fluid resuscitation was performed. No ventricular fibrillation was detected on electrocardiographic monitoring. Hemodynamic parameters recovered within 5 min. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed hypoxemia (PaO₂ 61.6 mmHg) and elevated lactate levels (5 mmol/L). Laboratory tests showed a decreased hemoglobin level (96 g/L, compared with 134 g/L pre-ablation) and elevated high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (459.8 ng/L). A right-sided chest tube was inserted, which drained bloody pleural effusion. The patient was subsequently transferred to the thoracic surgery department for emergency single-port video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Intraoperative findings revealed approximately 1000 mL of hemothorax with active bleeding from the intercostal vessels of the posterior chest wall. Bronchial artery angiography demonstrated a common trunk of bilateral bronchial arteries arising from the descending aorta, predominantly supplying the right lower lung. Bronchoscopy showed massive bleeding from the right lower lobe, and balloon occlusion was performed for hemostasis. After receiving blood transfusion, plasma infusion, fluid resuscitation, and anti-infective therapy, the patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation and discharged after recovery. Discussion

This study demonstrates the clinical value of cryoablation in selected patients with lung cancer. Cryoablation is a minimally invasive, image-guided local therapy that preserves the collagen extracellular matrix, offering potential advantages for tumors adjacent to vital structures such as the diaphragm, airways, and pleura [10]. Previous studies have shown favorable outcomes of cryoablation in early-stage NSCLC, with reported two-year survival rates of 78% in medically inoperable patients [12], and three-year recurrence-free survival exceeding 90% in tumors smaller than 1.8 cm [13]. In addition, a study of 50 ground-glass nodules (GGNs) reported a 100% local control rate after cryoablation with long-term follow-up [14]. Consistent with these findings, our study achieved a 100% local control rate in pure GGNs, whereas local recurrence was observed in part-solid and solid nodules. These results suggest that cryoablation may be most suitable for pure GGNs, while careful patient selection is required for part-solid or solid lesions. The number of solid nodules included in this study was limited; therefore, the therapeutic value of cryoablation for solid nodules remains inconclusive. In addition, local recurrence in solid nodules may be related to procedural factors, such as potential needle-track seeding during puncture. Notably, the recurrent part-solid nodules in our cohort exhibited a higher solid component ratio, and all pure GGNs were smaller than 2 cm, limiting extrapolation to larger lesions. Further studies are needed to clarify the optimal radiologic characteristics of subsolid nodules for cryoablation. Importantly, lesions adjacent to blood vessels may be at risk of incomplete ablation due to the heat-sink effect and should be approached with caution. The relatively short follow-up in this study also warrants validation in cohorts with longer observation periods.

Multiple primary lung nodules represent another clinical scenario in which surgery may not be the sole treatment option. In such patients, preservation of pulmonary function is critical, and pneumonectomy should be avoided whenever possible. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of cryoablation for treating pulmonary nodules in the residual lung after prior resection, with durable local control reported [15]. In our cohort, all 15 patients with second primary lung cancer achieved local control after cryoablation; however, distant recurrence occurred in three patients. As reported in prior studies, the risk of tumor recurrence within postoperative 5 years is accompanied by the emergence of SPLC [16]. These findings highlight the need for ongoing surveillance for distant recurrence after cryoablation, despite excellent local control. Notably, all second primary tumors in this study were located outside the previously resected lobe, most commonly in the contralateral lung. Whether cryoablation can be safely and effectively applied to residual lung tissue after surgical resection warrants further investigation. Although prior studies suggest comparable cancer-specific survival between SBRT and surgery for second primary lung cancer [17], comparative evidence evaluating ablation versus radiotherapy or surgery remains limited, highlighting the need for further prospective studies.

For synchronous multiple primary lung nodules (sMPNs), surgical resection remains the preferred treatment, with synchronous resection for ipsilateral disease and staged intervention for bilateral nodules, prioritizing the dominant lesion [18]. SBRT has been reported to be less effective for synchronous than for metachronous multiple primary lung cancer (MPLC) [19]. Accumulating evidence suggests that surgery combined with ablation can achieve favorable tumor control while preserving pulmonary function, although most published data involve RFA or MWA rather than cryoablation [20, 21]. In our cohort of 22 patients with sMPNs, no local recurrence, lymph node involvement, or distant metastasis was observed, regardless of treatment modality. These results support the application of cryoablation in GGN-dominant MPLC, whether combined with surgery or delivered as cryoablation alone. Of note, sMPNs receiving combined treatment in this study were bilaterally distributed, with the dominant lesion in one lung and minor lesions in the contralateral lung, allowing resection and ablation to be completed during a single hospitalization. Transient post-ablation changes, such as limited pulmonary exudation or pneumothorax on the ablated side, were generally self-limiting, thereby preserving adequate pulmonary reserve to meet the requirements of single-lung ventilation during contralateral surgery. Of course, a surgery-first approach followed by ablation of contralateral lesions is also a feasible option. A subtle distinction is that surgery-first was associated with a longer treatment interval. Given that a 3–6 month interval is considered optimal for staged treatment, an ablation-first strategy may facilitate a more efficient treatment course, particularly when rapid control of multiple lesions is desired [22]. When the dominant and secondary lesions are located within the same lung but cannot be completely resected surgically, we consider an approach of resecting the dominant lesion first, followed by ablation of the secondary lesions after postoperative recovery to be a safe strategy. Compared with surgery, ablation offers the advantage of repeatability. Thus, given the favorable local control achieved by cryoablation for subsolid nodules, repeated ablation may represent a feasible surrogate when high-risk lesions show a numerous, highly dispersed, multilobar distribution. In addition, each nodule in patients with MPN exhibits heterogeneous natural growth patterns. When residual nodules demonstrate progression and are assessed as high risk, further intervention may be required. In this context, repeated ablation tailored to disease progression may achieve adequate tumor control while preserving pulmonary function [23, 24]. Indeed, a prior study also reported that repeated ablation may be a viable treatment strategy for selected patients with MPN [25]. Due to the small sample size in each subgroup, formal statistical comparisons were not feasible. Anyway, as the complexity of MPN, these findings also highlight the importance of individualized treatment strategies determined through MDT discussion.

Cryoablation may induce tumor necrosis and apoptosis, potentially promoting tumor antigen release and anti-tumor immune responses [26, 27], which has supported its use in recurrent or advanced lung cancer and metastatic disease [28, 29]. In our study, 21 patients with advanced or metastatic lesions received cytoreductive cryoablation, with a median PFS of 409 days. Some patients underwent cryoablation in combination with systemic therapy; however, due to the limited sample size, formal subgroup analyses were not feasible. Notably, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the timing of ablation relative to systemic treatment, underscoring the need for standardized strategies and further studies to define optimal combination regimens and treatment sequencing.

Consistent with previous reports, cryoablation did not result in significant deterioration of pulmonary function regardless of lesion size, supporting its use in patients with limited pulmonary reserve [12]. However, only a subset of patients had paired pulmonary function data. The missing data occurred due to multiple factors such as limited compliance, followed-up in other departments or external hospitals, which may introduce potential selection bias. The incidence of pneumothorax in our study was comparable to prior reports [13], and no patient required emergency intervention. Known risk factors for pneumothorax include patient-related factors and procedural characteristics, such as increased length of traversing aerated lung or interlobar fissures [30]. In addition to pulmonary vessels visible on CT, ablation near the intercostal arteries located in the mid-intercostal space adjacent to the spine, the azygos and hemiazygos veins, or the internal thoracic arteries may also result in significant hemorrhage. Enhanced CT and careful planning of the needle trajectory are essential to minimize the risk of injury to these vessels. One patient experienced a fatal complication following cryoablation. Although hemorrhagic shock was identified, air embolism related to pulmonary vein injury could not be excluded, given the lesion location and puncture trajectory. Previous studies have identified pulmonary vein injury, cavitary lesion, procedural cough or hemoptysis, and proximity of the lesion to pulmonary veins as risk factors for air embolism [31–34]. Actually, the biopsy needle in our patient may have damaged the right inferior pulmonary vein (Fig. 4B); however, chest CT performed after resuscitation did not reveal definitive signs of air embolism (Fig. 4C). Only potential intercostal artery injury was identified during intraoperative exploration. These findings underscore the importance of thorough preprocedural evaluation of coagulation status, careful planning of puncture paths to avoid pulmonary veins, and appropriate patient instruction during the procedure to minimize the risk of severe complications.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, selection bias may be present, as all patients included were either unable or unwilling to undergo surgery or radiotherapy, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study lacked a comparison group, precluding direct assessment of relative efficacy or safety compared with standard treatments. Furthermore, the follow-up period was relatively short, particularly for indolent ground-glass nodules, and may not capture late recurrences, potentially leading to an overestimation of long-term local control and survival outcomes. Finally, mediastinal staging was primarily based on imaging findings, and routine invasive mediastinal lymph node evaluation was not performed. This may have resulted in potential understaging, particularly in patients with solid nodules, which carry a higher risk of occult nodal metastasis. Therefore, while early results suggest that cryoablation is feasible, safe, and capable of achieving good short-term local control, further studies with longer follow-up and comparative cohorts are warranted.

Conclusion

Cryoablation preserves pulmonary function and may benefit patients ineligible for surgery. It may serve as a treatment option for patients with unresectable pure ground-glass nodules or GGN-dominant MPN. Careful avoidance of vascular injury during the procedure is critical to minimizing the risk of potentially fatal complications.
