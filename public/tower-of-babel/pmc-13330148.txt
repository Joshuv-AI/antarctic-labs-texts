TAPSE/PAi, a practical echocardiographic functional–structural index, better reflects functional severity than TAPSE/sPAP in pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yunus Emre Yavuz, Yakup Alsancak, Sefa Tatar, Cem Korucu, Hasan Kan, Hakan Akilli, Abdullah İçli̇
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe evaluated TAPSE/PAi, defined as the ratio of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to the indexed main pulmonary artery diameter, as a pragmatic functional-structural index and compared it with TAPSE/sPAP.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 53 patients with PAH were analyzed. Associations of TAPSE/PAi and TAPSE/sPAP with clinical, biomarker, and invasive hemodynamic variables were assessed. Discrimination for WHO-FC ≥ 3, 6MWD < 165 m, and PVR > 5 WU was evaluated by direction-aligned ROC analysis with Youden-optimal thresholds. Age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression models reported odds ratios (ORs) per 1-SD increase.ResultsTAPSE/PAi correlated more strongly than TAPSE/sPAP with WHO-FC (ρ=-0.592 vs. -0.384) and 6MWD (ρ = 0.493 vs. 0.378), whereas TAPSE/sPAP correlated more strongly with NT-proBNP/proBNP (ρ=-0.619 vs. -0.484) and with mPAP/PVR. TAPSE/PAi showed better discrimination for WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m while TAPSE/sPAP performed modestly better for PVR > 5 WU The Youden-optimal TAPSE/PAi threshold was ≤ 1.296 for both WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m. The higher TAPSE/PAi remained associated with lower odds of WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m after age- and sex-adjustment.ConclusionsTAPSE/PAi better reflects functional severity and exercise limitation, whereas TAPSE/sPAP is more closely related to biomarker/hemodynamic burden. The indices appear complementary, and TAPSE/PAi may be a practical TR-independent severity marker in PAH. Graphical abstract

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive form of pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular (RV) dysfunction, and premature mortality. This is due to increased pulmonary vascular resistance, or higher pressure within the blood vessels of the lungs. RV function is considered the most critical factor in determining disease prognosis under current management strategies [1–3]. RV function depends not only on intrinsic ventricular contractility but also on right ventricle-pulmonary artery (RV–PA) coupling. This coupling is the dynamic interaction between the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery, which carries blood from the heart to the lungs [4]. In gold-standard invasive pressure-volume loop analyses, RV-PA coupling is expressed as the ratio of ventricular elastance (the stiffness or contractile strength of the ventricle) to arterial elastance (the resistance the artery offers to blood flow). However, the invasive nature and technical challenges of this method have led to the development of non-invasive, practical alternatives [5].

The most commonly used echocardiographic surrogate (imaging-based substitute) of right ventricular-to-pulmonary artery (RV-PA) coupling in clinical practice is the tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion-to-systolic pulmonary artery pressure (TAPSE/sPAP) ratio. This index is among the key criteria recommended for assessing right ventricular (RV) function and risk stratification in the 2022 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society (ESC/ERS) pulmonary hypertension (PH) Guidelines [1, 6]. Many studies have shown that TAPSE/sPAP strongly predicts mortality and hospitalizations [7]. However, estimating sPAP to calculate this ratio depends closely on the quality of the tricuspid regurgitation (TR) jet [8]. The literature reports that many patients with suspected pulmonary disease lack an adequate continuous-wave (CW) Doppler jet. Also, echocardiography-based sPAP estimates may not match invasive measurements [9, 10].

On the other hand, an enlarged pulmonary artery (PA) diameter signals chronic structural remodeling and loss of vascular compliance in the pulmonary vascular bed [11, 12]. The PA index (PAi) is the PA diameter indexed to body surface area. It is easily measured, independent of the TR jet, and reflects both anatomical expansion and the disease’s chronic burden. Indexing TAPSE, a measure of longitudinal right ventricular function, with PAi (TAPSE/PAi) could help evaluate RV-PA interaction from both functional and structural perspectives. However, the clinical severity and hemodynamic performance of this new composite index remain inadequately studied.

This study aimed to determine the relationship between TAPSE/PAi, a novel functional-structural index, and clinical severity (WHO-FC), exercise capacity (6MWD), and invasive pulmonary arterial measurements (mPAP, PVR) in patients with PAH. It also compared this performance to the traditional TAPSE/sPAP ratio.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a single-center, retrospective observational study of consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years) with pulmonary arterial hypertension evaluated at our institution. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethics committee of Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine (reference number 2025/6229). Patients admitted to our clinic between 2020 and 2025 were examined.

PAH was defined based on right heart catheterization according to contemporary haemodynamic criteria as mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg at rest, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 2 Wood units. Patients were eligible if they had a confirmed diagnosis of PAH based on right heart catheterization, a clinically interpretable transthoracic echocardiographic examination, and available clinical and hemodynamic data for analysis. All included patients were managed as a PAH cohort in routine clinical practice and subsequently received PAH-specific therapy at our center.

Patients were excluded if TAPSE or pulmonary artery diameter could not be measured reliably on echocardiography, if they had complex congenital heart disease or a history of major congenital cardiac surgery, if they had significant left-to-right shunt lesions (e.g., ASD, VSD) leading to an uninterpretable hemodynamic assessment, or if essential demographic or hemodynamic variables were missing from the medical records.

Clinical outcomes and laboratory variables

Functional status was assessed using the World Health Organization functional class (WHO-FC), exercise capacity using the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), and biomarker burden using NT-proBNP/proBNP values. In our cohort, 6MWD testing, NT-proBNP/proBNP measurement, transthoracic echocardiography, and right heart catheterization were obtained on the same day. WHO-FC was recorded during the same clinical evaluation period.

Echocardiography

Standard transthoracic echocardiography parameters were collected, including TAPSE, RV fractional area change (RV-FAC), RV S′, TR grade, IVC parameters, RVSP/PASP estimates, and RVOT indices. The main pulmonary artery (MPA) diameter was measured in the parasternal short-axis view at the level of the great arteries/main pulmonary artery, from inner edge to inner edge, at end-diastole. The value was then indexed to body surface area to derive the pulmonary artery index (PAi).

Index definitions.

Invasive hemodynamics

Right heart catheterization data included mPAP, PCWP, RAP, CO, and calculated PVR (WU), where available. Analyses used available-case data per variable.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using appropriate standard statistical software. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed and as median (minimum–maximum) when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as number and percentage, n (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous or ordinal variables, and the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, for categorical variables. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Exploratory associations between echocardiographic indices and WHO-FC rank, as well as other clinical, biomarker, and hemodynamic variables, were assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis. In particular, associations with NT-proBNP/proBNP were evaluated using Spearman correlation rather than linear regression because of the non-normal distribution of biomarker values.

For clinically oriented discrimination analyses, adverse phenotypes were defined as WHO-FC ≥ 3, 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) < 165 m, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 5 Wood units (WU). The discriminative performance of TAPSE/PAi and TAPSE/sPAP for these outcomes was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Because both indices increase with better right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling, ROC direction was aligned for interpretability, and the direction yielding an AUC ≥ 0.50 was reported. For each ROC model, the optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1), and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were recorded. In addition, because the TAPSE/PAi threshold of ≤ 1.296 was identified for both WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m, this same cut-off was secondarily applied to PVR > 5 WU to report its sensitivity and specificity for that endpoint.

To examine associations after limited adjustment, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for WHO-FC ≥ 3, 6MWD < 165 m, and PVR > 5 WU. Given the modest sample size and the limited number of events for some outcomes, particularly 6MWD < 165 m, these models were considered exploratory and were intentionally kept parsimonious to reduce the risk of overfitting. Therefore, adjustment was limited to age and sex, and the resulting estimates should be interpreted as associations after limited adjustment rather than as fully adjusted explanatory models. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in the predictor. Bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations) was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

A total of 53 patients were included in the analysis. For descriptive group comparisons, patients were separated into two groups based on the median TAPSE/PAi value of 1.271. Those with a TAPSE/PAi below 1.271 were classified as the Low TAPSE/PAi group (n = 26), and those with a value equal to or above 1.271 were classified as the High TAPSE/PAi group (n = 27). Baseline age and sex distribution were comparable between groups (age: 59.65 ± 17.60 vs. 51.74 ± 17.67 years, p = 0.109; female sex: 76.9% vs. 66.7%, p = 0.601). As expected, the High TAPSE/PAi group had significantly higher TAPSE/PAi values (0.90 ± 0.22 vs. 1.70 ± 0.39, p < 0.001) and higher TAPSE/sPAP values (0.29 ± 0.15 vs. 0.63 ± 0.30, p < 0.001) than the Low TAPSE/PAi group (Table 1).

Clinical severity and functional capacity differed across TAPSE/PAi groups. The High TAPSE/PAi group had a lower WHO functional class (median1.00(1.00–4.00) vs. 3.00(1.00–4.00), p < 0.001), a longer 6-minute walk distance (214.65 ± 147.67 vs. 348.04 ± 143.77 m, p = 0.002), and lower NT-proBNP/proBNP levels (1455.5 [5.0–16192.0] vs. 362.0 [33.2–15058.0], p = 0.002) than the Low TAPSE/PAi group. Invasive hemodynamic parameters, such as mPAP (43.42 ± 18.92 vs. 44.85 ± 16.89 mmHg, p = 0.773), PVR (5.7 [2.2–19.5] vs. 6.3 [2.2–20.5] WU, p = 0.580), and cardiac output (4.70 ± 1.21 vs. 4.63 ± 1.32 L/min, p = 0.861), were broadly similar between groups. Building on these correlation findings, direction-aligned ROC analyses showed that TAPSE/PAi demonstrated better discrimination for clinically severe phenotypes than TAPSE/sPAP. For WHO-FC ≥ 3 (22/53 events), the AUC of TAPSE/PAi was 0.838 (95% CI 0.722–0.934) compared with 0.672 (95% CI 0.526–0.810) for TAPSE/sPAP. The Youden-optimal threshold for TAPSE/PAi was ≤ 1.296, yielding 90.9% sensitivity and 67.7% specificity (Youden J = 0.587), whereas the corresponding threshold for TAPSE/sPAP was ≤ 0.438, yielding 77.3% sensitivity and 58.1% specificity (Youden J = 0.353). For 6MWD < 165 m (14/53 events), the AUC of TAPSE/PAi was 0.755 (95% CI 0.623–0.875) versus 0.681 (95% CI 0.527–0.829) for TAPSE/sPAP. The Youden-optimal threshold for TAPSE/PAi was again ≤ 1.296, yielding 92.9% sensitivity and 56.4% specificity (Youden J = 0.493), while the threshold for TAPSE/sPAP was ≤ 0.286, yielding 57.1% sensitivity and 76.9% specificity (Youden J = 0.341). For PVR > 5 WU (32/50 events), discrimination was limited for both indices; TAPSE/PAi had an AUC of 0.528 (95% CI 0.503–0.708) with a Youden-optimal threshold of ≥ 1.390 (46.9% sensitivity, 72.2% specificity; Youden J = 0.191), whereas TAPSE/sPAP performed modestly better with an AUC of 0.618 (95% CI 0.510–0.768) and a Youden-optimal threshold of ≤ 0.212 (28.1% sensitivity, 100.0% specificity; Youden J = 0.281) (Table 2). For completeness, we also evaluated the TAPSE/PAi threshold of ≤ 1.296, which was identified for WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m, for the endpoint of PVR > 5 WU. At this threshold, the sensitivity was 50.0% and the specificity was 33.3%, indicating limited discriminatory performance for elevated PVR.

Discussion

In this retrospective PAH cohort, TAPSE/PAi—a functional-structural echocardiographic index combining RV longitudinal systolic function (TAPSE) and PA remodeling (MPA diameter with index)—was more closely associated with functional severity (WHO-FC) and exercise capacity (6MWD) than TAPSE/sPAP. TAPSE/PAi also better differentiated clinically severe phenotypes (WHO-FC ≥ 3 and 6MWD < 165 m) and was independently associated with these outcomes after age- and sex-adjustment. In contrast, TAPSE/sPAP showed stronger associations with NT-proBNP and moderately stronger associations with invasive hemodynamic metrics (mPAP and PVR), suggesting that these metrics may capture complementary dimensions of the RV-pulmonary circulation interaction.

In current clinical practice, the TAPSE/sPAP ratio has become one of the key non-invasive parameters recommended in the 2022 ESC/ERS Pulmonary Hypertension Guidelines for assessing right ventricular (RV) function and risk stratification of patients [1]. The main importance of this ratio lies in the fact that the invasive gold standard for RV–PA matching offers a simple alternative by proportioning the right ventricle’s longitudinal shortening capacity (contractility) to the afterload it encounters [10, 13]. However, despite the widespread use of TAPSE/sPAP, some important limitations are increasingly discussed in the literature. The most fundamental problem is the high degree of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) jet dependence of this index; clinical studies show that 10% to 30% of PH patients do not obtain a sufficient quality of TR jets for sPAP estimation, or the values obtained may be incompatible with invasive measurements [14–16]. Furthermore, because TAPSE reflects only longitudinal function, it can ignore the radial and circumferential shortening components in the complex geometry of the RV [4]. Recent studies emphasize that functional loss in the RV is associated not only with transient increases in pressure but also with chronic remodeling and reduced compliance in the pulmonary vascular bed [11, 17]. In this context, it is considered essential to develop additional parameters that incorporate structural and chronic load markers, such as pulmonary artery diameter, and that do not rely on a TR jet, instead of “instantaneous” pressure load, such as sPAP, for more accurate phenotyping, especially in patients with limited echo window or complex vascular remodeling [18].

In our study, the correlation of TAPSE/PAi with WHO-FC and 6MWD was more pronounced than that of TAPSE/sPAP. This suggests that common pulmonary artery dilation may better reflect chronic vascular remodeling and long-term disease burden than the immediate pressure load [11, 18, 19]. Other studies in the literature highlight that functional class is one of the strongest predictors of prognosis in PAH patients [1].

As in the literature, TAPSE/sPAP showed a stronger association with NT-proBNP levels and invasive hemodynamic parameters (mPAP and PVR) in our study. This finding is consistent with physiopathological evidence indicating that pressure-derived afterload is more closely associated with RV wall stress and neurohormonal activation. A study by Chen et al. found that a lower TAPSE/sPAP ratio was associated with a worse hemodynamic profile and right ventricular remodeling [7]. Similarly, in our study, TAPSE/sPAP was more effective than TAPSE/PAi (AUC: 0.618 vs. 0.528) in distinguishing patients with PVR > 5 WU, indicating that these two indices capture complementary aspects of the RV-pulmonary circulation interaction. A recent study also found significantly lower TAPSE/sPAP in HFpEF patients compared with controls, and lower ratios were associated with higher NT-proBNP levels and greater clinical burden [20]. These results align with our observations in PH, where TAPSE/sPAP has a stronger correlation with NT-proBNP and invasive indicators of load.

One of the key clinical advantages of TAPSE/PAi is that it is independent of TR jet quality. In the literature, it is reported that the sPAP value cannot be estimated correctly in 10–15% of patients due to insufficient TR jet or technical difficulties [8, 14, 15]. In addition, in the presence of severe TR, the sPAP value may be underestimated relative to the actual Doppler value, leading to a misleadingly high TAPSE/sPAP ratio. The biggest advantage of TAPSE/PAi is that it is completely independent of the TR jet. MPA diameter is a standardized anatomical measurement in routine echocardiography guidelines and is less affected by momentary respiratory or hemodynamic fluctuations, such as sPAP. In our study, indexing the MPA diameter to body surface area (PAi) improved index comparability by minimizing individual anatomical variation. According to these results, it can function as a pragmatic “clinical severity index”.

Recent studies have argued that RV global longitudinal strain (RVGLS) and RV free-wall longitudinal strain (RVFWLS) based match indices (e.g., RVFWLS/sPAP) are more sensitive than conventional parameters [21, 22]. Alturaif et al. showed that RVGLS is superior to TAPSE in predicting mortality and hospitalization. Hădăreanu et al. found that the RVFWLS/sPAP ratio is a very strong prognostic marker in patients with heart failure. However, while these sophisticated methods require specialized software and high-quality images, the TAPSE/PAi proposed in our study is a simpler, cost-effective alternative that can be performed on any device during routine echocardiography.

Although the median value used in our study was determined as an intra-cohort cutoff, the high AUC values obtained in the ROC analyses indicated that TAPSE/PAi demonstrates strong discriminative performance for detecting severe clinical phenotypes. The “multimodality imaging” approach is at the forefront of current PD management, and parameters such as TAPSE/PAi can serve as an additional tool for risk stratification, especially in “gray zone” patients where sPAP is unreliable [23, 24].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis, which may limit generalizability and introduce potential selection bias. Second, the sample size was modest; however, this reflects real-world constraints of studying PAH cohorts with invasively derived hemodynamic endpoints. In routine practice, a substantial proportion of screened patients cannot be included because invasive data are unavailable or incomplete, and because key echocardiographic measurements required for TAPSE/PAi (particularly main pulmonary artery diameter) may not be reliably obtainable in some cases due to imaging window limitations. The multivariable models were limited by the modest sample size and the relatively small number of events for some outcomes, particularly 6MWD < 165 m. As a result, only parsimonious adjustment for age and sex was performed, and residual confounding as well as model overfitting cannot be excluded. TAPSE/PAi is a novel composite echocardiographic index, the absence of a formal intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility analysis represents an important limitation of the present study. Finally, we did not assess longitudinal change or hard clinical endpoints (such as hospitalization or mortality); the prognostic value and responsiveness of TAPSE/PAi to therapy should be evaluated in larger, prospective, multicenter studies.

In summary, the TAPSE/PAi index performs better than TAPSE/sPAP in determining functional severity and exercise limitation in PAH patients. It can serve as a pragmatic indicator of the clinical situation, especially when the TR jet is unreliable. These findings need to be confirmed by larger, prospective, and longitudinal studies.

Conclusion

TAPSE/PAi demonstrated superior association and discrimination for functional severity and exercise limitation compared with TAPSE/sPAP in this PAH cohort, while TAPSE/sPAP related more closely to NT-proBNP and invasive afterload signals. TAPSE/PAi may be a pragmatic clinical severity index complementary to TAPSE/sPAP.
