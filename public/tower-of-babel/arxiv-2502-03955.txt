Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

In this paper we will study the existence of solutions in the complex domain of first-order difference equations of the form

(1.1)

y(z+1)=F(z,y(z)),

where
F(z,y)
is a rational function of
y
with coefficients that are meromorphic functions of
z
.
The existence of meromorphic solutions of the constant coefficient first-order nonlinear difference equation

(1.2)

y(z+1)=R(y(z)),

where
R
is a rational function with constant coefficients, has a long history going back to Koenigs
[
10
]
, Poincaré
[
12
]
, Fatou
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
and Julia
[
7
]
. Clearly if
y(z)
is a meromorphic solution of equation (
1.1
) then so is

(1.3)

y(z+p(z)),

where
p
is any entire period one function.
It is natural to try to eliminate some of this freedom by restricting the class of solutions of meromorphic solutions of equation (
1.1
), for example

(1.4)

y(z)\longrightarrow\gamma\quad\textrm{as}\quad\Re(z)\longrightarrow-\infty.

This is only possible if
\gamma
is a fixed point of
R
, i.e.
R(\gamma)=\gamma
.

The main idea to prove global existence is first to show that for any
\sigma>0
, there exists
\rho
such that there is a one-parameter family of solutions
y
of equation (
1.2
) subject to (
1.4
) that is analytic on

{D}(\rho,\sigma)=\left\{z:\Re(z)<-\rho,\ |\Im(z)|<\sigma\right\}.

The equation itself can then be used to continue the solutions as meromorphic functions to the infinite strip
-\sigma<\Im(z)<\sigma
. Finally, since
\sigma
is arbitrary, it follows that these solutions are meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
. Similarly, it is possible to find solutions analytic as
\Re(z)\to+\infty
. In this case, the equation can be used to continue the solution over all
\mathbb{C}
, but generically the function so obtained will have infinitely many branch points and will only be defined on an infinitely sheeted cover of
\mathbb{C}
.

The following lemma is due to G. Julia
[
7
]
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
R
be a rational function of degree greater than one. Then
R
has a fixed point
\gamma
such that
\lambda:=R^{\prime}(\gamma)
satisfies either

(a)

|\lambda|>1
or

(b)

\lambda=1
.

This divides the analysis into two essentially different cases.

In the case (a), the transformation
y(z)=w(\zeta)
,
\zeta=\lambda^{z}
, maps equation (
1.2
)
to the inverse Schröder equation

w(\lambda z)=R(w(z)).

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the existence of a unique meromorphic solution satisfying
w(0)=\gamma
and
w^{\prime}(0)=\alpha
goes back to Poincaré
[
12
]
and it is implicit in earlier work of Koenigs
[
10
]
. The existence of a meromorphic solution in a neighbourhood of
0
can be proved in a straightforward manner and once again the equation itself provides the meromorphic extension ot
\mathbb{C}
.
This case has been studied by S. Shimomura in the case when
R
is a polynomial
[
13
]
and by N. Yanagihara in the case of rational
R

[
15
]
. We have the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
R
be a rational function with a fixed point
\gamma
such that
\lambda:=R^{\prime}(\gamma)
satisfies
|\lambda|>1
. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, equation (
1.2
) has a unique meromorphic solution in the complex plane such that for all
\sigma>0
,

(1.5)

(y(z)-\gamma)\lambda^{-z}\longrightarrow\alpha,\quad\mbox{for}\ -\sigma<\Im(z)<\sigma\quad\textrm{as}\ \ \Re(z)\longrightarrow-\infty.

Observe that if there is a fixed point of
R
such that
0<|R^{\prime}(\gamma)|<1
, then the same reasoning produces the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
R
be a rational function with a fixed point
\gamma
such that
\lambda:=R^{\prime}(\gamma)
satisfies
|\lambda|<1
. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and any
\sigma>0
, there is a
\rho>0
such that equation (
1.2
) has a unique analytic solution in the set

\widehat{D}(\rho,\sigma)=\left\{z:\Re(z)>\rho,\quad|\Im(z)|<\sigma\right\}

satisfying

(1.6)

(y(z)-\gamma)\lambda^{-z}\longrightarrow\alpha\quad\textrm{as}\quad\Re(z)\longrightarrow+\infty,\quad z\in\widehat{D}(\rho,\sigma).

Proving the existence of a meromorphic solution of equation (
1.2
) corresponding to case (b) of Lemma
1.1
is more subtle. Existence was proved by Fatou
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
and by T. Kimura
[
8
,
9
]
.
Baker and Liverpool
[
2
]
and Azarina
[
1
]
later showed that all meromorphic solutions of equation (
1.2
) can be obtained from the solutions constructed in Shimomura, Yanagihara and Kimura using a transformation of the form (
1.3
).

When the degree of
R
is one, then either equation (
1.2
) is linear
y(z+1)=a_{0}y(z)+a_{1}
or it is the discrete Riccati equation
y(z+1)=\{a_{0}(y(z)+a_{1}\}/\{y(z)+b_{1}\}
, which is linearised by the transformation
y(z)=a_{0}w(z-1)/w(z)
. So in either case the general solution can be given explicitly in terms of an exponential and a period one function.

The purpose of the present paper is threefold. First, we will provide a more direct proof than that given by Fatou and Kimura of the existence of a meromorphic solution of the autonomous difference equation (
1.2
) subject to the asymptotic condition (
1.4
), where
R(\gamma)=\gamma
and
R^{\prime}(\gamma)=1
. To this end in section
3
, we will give a proof of Theorem
2.1
, building on ideas outlined in Halburd and Korhonen
[
6
]
. Our proof is more direct than those by Fatou and Kimura, who first studied solutions to the Abel equation. The second purpose is to present a detailed analysis of global solutions of the equation

y(z+1)=\lambda y(z)+y(z)^{2},\qquad 0<\lambda<1.

This equation does not admit any non-constant meromorphic solutions, however, from Theorem
1.3
we see that it has a solution that is meromorphic for large positive
\Re(z)
. In section
4
we will describe the analytic extension of this solution to an infinitely-sheeted cover of the complex plane.
Finally, we will consider meromorphic solutions of the non-autonomous difference equation

(1.7)

y(z+1)=\frac{\lambda y(z)+a_{2}(z)y(z)^{2}+\cdots+a_{p}(z)y(z)^{p}}{1+b_{1}(z)y(z)+\cdots+b_{q}(z)y(z)^{q}}=:F(z,y(z)),

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and for some
\nu<|\lambda|
,

a_{j}(z)
and
b_{k}(z)
are meromorphic functions such that for all
\sigma>0
there exists a
\rho>0
such that

|a_{j}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
and
|b_{k}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
for all

z\in D(\rho,\sigma)=\{z:\Re(z)<-\rho,\ |\Im(z)|<\sigma\}.

In general, the only fixed point that
F(z,y)
has a function of
y
is
y=0
(i.e.,
F(z,0)=0
). Note that
\lambda=F_{y}(z,0)
. We will have to consider the cases
|\lambda|<1
and
|\lambda|>1
separately.

2.
Main results

For
\rho>0
, let

D(\rho)=\left\{z:\Re(z)<-\rho\right\}.

The first theorem that we will prove is the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
R
be a rational function with a fixed point
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
such that
R^{\prime}(\gamma)=1
. Let
m
be the smallest positive integer such that
R^{(m+1)}(\gamma)\neq 0.
Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
, there exist constants
\rho>0
and
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and exactly
m
solutions
y(z)
of (
1.2
), meromorphic in the complex plane, such that

(2.1)

\frac{2}{R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma-y(z)}=z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)

if
m=1,
and

(2.2)

-\frac{(m+1)!}{mR^{(m+1)}(\gamma)}\cdot\frac{1}{(y(z)-\gamma)^{m}}=z+\alpha+\beta z^{\frac{m-1}{m}}+W(z)

if
m\geq 2
and for all
z
such that
\Re(z)<-\rho
and
-\sigma<\Im(z)<\sigma
we have

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}.

The next two theorems are generalisations of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
to non-autonomous equations.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
|\lambda|>1
,
0<\nu<|\lambda|
and let
a_{2},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
be meromorphic functions such that,
given
\sigma>0
there is a
\rho>0
such that
|a_{j}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
and
|b_{k}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
for

z\in D(\rho,\sigma)=\{z:\Re(z)<-\rho,\ |\Im(z)|<\sigma\}.

Then given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, equation (
1.7
) has a unique meromorphic solution in the complex plane such that

(2.3)

y(z)\lambda^{-z}\longrightarrow\alpha,\quad\textrm{as}\quad\Re(z)\longrightarrow-\infty,\quad z\in D(\rho,\sigma).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
0<\nu<|\lambda|<1
and let
a_{2},\ldots,a_{p};b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}
be meromorphic functions such that,
given
\sigma>0
there is a
\rho>0
such that
|a_{j}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
and
|b_{k}(z)|\leq\nu^{|z|}
for

z\in D(\rho,\sigma)=\{z:\Re(z)<-\rho,\ |\Im(z)|<\sigma\}.

Then given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, equation (
1.7
) has a unique solution analytic in
D(\rho,\sigma)
such that

(2.4)

y(z)\lambda^{-z}\longrightarrow\alpha,\quad\textrm{as}\quad\Re(z)\longrightarrow+\infty,\quad z\in D(\rho,\sigma).

3.
The proof of Theorem
2.1

We will prove Theorem
2.1
using Banach’s fixed point theorem, including more detail than was provided in the earlier proof outlined by two of us
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
.
The advantage of this approach is that the basic idea is very direct and simple. We do not need to transform the difference equation into the Abel’s functional equation, which simplifies the procedure significantly. Banach’s theorem also immediately guarantees the uniqueness of the solution, which is not the case in the reasoning based on normal families.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Banach fixed point theorem). If
T
is a contraction defined on a complete metric space
X
, then
T
has a unique fixed point in
X
.

We begin by expanding the right hand side of (
1.2
) as a Taylor series around the fixed point
\gamma
. We obtain

(3.1)

y(z+1)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}R^{(j)}(\gamma)\left(y(z)-\gamma\right)^{j},

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
such that
R(\gamma)\not=\infty
. Substituting
y(z)=w(z)+\gamma
, we then have

(3.2)

w(z+1)=w(z)+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}R^{(j)}(\gamma)w(z)^{j}.

By applying the transformation
w(z)=1/g(z)
to equation (
3.2
), we obtain

(3.3)

g(z+1)=\frac{g(z)}{1+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j!}R^{(j)}(\gamma)g(z)^{-j+1}}=g(z)\left(1-\frac{1}{2}R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)g(z)^{-1}+\cdots\right).

Assume first that
R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\not=0
. Then

\tilde{y}(z)=-\frac{2g(z)}{R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)}

satisfies

(3.4)

\tilde{y}(z+1)=F(\tilde{y}(z)),

where

(3.5)

F(\zeta)=\zeta+1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}\zeta^{-j},

for some constants
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
.
So
\widetilde{y}(z)
is a solution of (
3.4
) if and only if

y(z)=-\frac{2}{R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)}\frac{1}{\widetilde{y}(z)}+\gamma

is a solution of the equation (
1.2
).

We will next prove that equation (
3.4
) has an analytic solution in the set
D(\rho)
. It will be done by showing that there exists a function
W(z)
such that the following three conditions are satisfied:

(1)

W(z)
is analytic in the domain
D(\rho)
, where
\rho
is sufficiently large number.

(2)

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}
, where
\delta\in(0,1)
is a fixed constant.

(3)

Y(z)=z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, is a solution of (
3.4
).

We will use Banach’s fixed point theorem to find such a function. To apply the theorem, we need a contraction mapping in a Banach space. For this purpose we define a family
X
of analytic functions
W
such that

(3.6)

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}

holds in
D(\rho)
. Then
X
is a metric space under the
\sup
-norm

\|W\|:=\sup_{z\in D(\rho)}\left|W(z)\right|.

Moreover, if a sequence
\{W_{n}\}
of
X
is Cauchy, then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}W_{n}(z)=W(z)
exists uniformly in
D(\rho)
. Furthermore,
W
is analytic in
D(\rho)
by Weierstrass’ theorem on uniformly convergent series of analytic functions. Let finally
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(3.7)

|W(z)|\leq|W(z)-W_{N}(z)|+|W_{N}(z)|\leq\|W-W_{N}\|+|W_{N}(z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}+\varepsilon.

Since (
3.7
) holds for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(3.8)

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
. Therefore
W\in X
, and so
X
is a complete metric space.

We will now find a suitable operator in the Banach space
X
. Condition
(3)
yields

\begin{split}W(z+1)-W(z)&=-\beta\log\left(1+\frac{1}{z}\right)+\frac{c_{1}}{z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z))^{j}}.\\
\end{split}

By expanding out the first two terms on the right hand side, we get

(3.9)

\begin{split}&W(z+1)-W(z)=\frac{c_{1}}{z}-\frac{\beta}{z}+\beta\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{jz^{j}}\\
&\quad+\frac{c_{1}}{z}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)}{z}\right)^{n}+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z))^{j}}.\\
\end{split}

We want our operator to satisfy condition (
3.8
). For this purpose we need the right hand side of (
3.9
) to tend to zero sufficiently fast as
|z|
tends to infinity. Therefore we now
fix
\beta=c_{1}
to cancel out the
z^{-1}
term, and obtain

\begin{split}W(z+1)-W(z)&=\beta\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{jz^{j}}+\frac{c_{1}}{z}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)}{z}\right)^{n}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z))^{j}}.\\
\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(W(z+1-k)-W(z-k)\right)&=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{c_{1}}{z-k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k+\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k))^{j}}\\
&\quad+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{j(z-k)^{j}}.\end{split}

Letting
m\longrightarrow\infty
and using (
3.8
), we then have

\begin{split}W(z)&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{1}}{z-k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k+\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k))^{j}}\\
&\quad+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{j(z-k)^{j}}.\end{split}

This suggests that we define the operator

(3.10)

\begin{split}T[W](z)&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{1}}{z-k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k+\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k))^{j}}\\
&\quad+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{j(z-k)^{j}}\\
&=:T_{1}\left(W(z)\right)+T_{2}\left(W(z)\right)+T_{3}\left(z\right).\end{split}

We have already shown that
X
is a complete metric space. We will prove next that
T
is a mapping from
X
into
X
. For this it is sufficient to show that

(3.11)

|T[W](z)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
, where
\delta
is the constant introduced in the requirement
(2)
.
We will prove our claim in three parts. First, we look at the function
T_{1}
. By choosing
\rho
large enough,

|\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)|\leq|z-k|^{\frac{\delta}{2}}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
. Therefore,

(3.12)

\begin{split}\left|T_{1}\left(W(z)\right)\right|&=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{1}}{z-k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{c_{1}}{z-k}\right|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n(1-\frac{\delta}{2})}\\
&=|c_{1}|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{2-\frac{\delta}{2}}\frac{|z-k|^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}{|z-k|^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}-1}\\
&\leq\frac{2|c_{1}|}{|z|^{1-\frac{3\delta}{4}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{1+\frac{\delta}{4}},\end{split}

when
\rho
is large enough. Furthermore,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{1+\frac{\delta}{4}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1+\frac{\delta}{4}}}=:K,

where the constant
K
depends only on
\delta
. So we have a majorising series for
T_{1}[W]
, so the series converges absolutely and uniformly to an analytic function on
D(\rho)
.
Finally we also have by (
3.12
)

(3.13)

\left|T_{1}\left(W(z)\right)\right|\leq\frac{2|c_{1}|K}{|z|^{1-\frac{3\delta}{4}}}\leq\frac{1}{3}|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho_{1})
, where
\rho_{1}
is sufficiently large.

We will now find a majorising series for function
T_{2}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We note that

|z-k+\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k)|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|z-k|

for all
z\in D(\rho)
when
\rho
is large enough. Moreover, since the series (
3.5
) is convergent, there is a constant
M>0
such that
|c_{j}|\leq M
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,

(3.14)

\begin{split}\left|T_{2}\left(W(z)\right)\right|&=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k+\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W(z-k))^{j}}\right|\\
&\leq M\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{(1-\varepsilon)(z-k)}\right|^{j}\\
&\leq\frac{M}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{(z-k)}\right|^{2}\frac{(1-\varepsilon)|z-k|}{(1-\varepsilon)|z-k|-1}\\
&\leq\frac{2M}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{(z-k)}\right|^{2},\\
\end{split}

when
\rho
is large enough. Since

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{(z-k)}\right|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|z|^{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}},

we have a majorising series for
T-[W]
and by (
3.14
)

(3.15)

\left|T_{2}\left(W(z)\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{3}|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho_{2})
, where
\rho_{2}
is sufficiently large.

Similarly,

(3.16)

\left|T_{3}\left(z\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{3}|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho_{3})
, where
\rho_{3}
is sufficiently large. Combining (
3.10
), (
3.13
), (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) we conclude that

|T[W](z)|\leq|T_{1}\left(W(z)\right)|+|T_{2}\left(W(z)\right)|+|T_{3}\left(z\right)|\leq|z|^{-1+\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
, where
\rho:=\max\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}\}
. Therefore
T
maps
X
into itself.

We will now show that
T
is a contraction. Assume that
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
belong to
X
. Then

(3.17)

\begin{split}&T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W_{1}(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\right)^{j}\\
&\quad-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)+W_{2}(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\right)^{j}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{z-k}\right)^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right)^{n-i}W_{1}(z-k)^{i}\right)^{j}\\
&\quad-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{z-k}\right)^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right)^{n-i}W_{2}(z-k)^{i}\right)^{j}.\\
\end{split}

Denoting

\mathcal{S}(k,z,W)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{z-k}\right)^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right)^{n-i}W(z-k)^{i},

equation (
3.17
) takes the form

(3.18)

\begin{split}&T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(\left(1+\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{1})\right)^{j}-\left(1+\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{2})^{j}\right)\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\sum_{l=1}^{j}\binom{j}{l}\left(\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{1})^{l}-\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{2})^{l}\right)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{(z-k)^{j}}\left(\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{1})-\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{2})\right)\sum_{l=1}^{j}\binom{j}{l}\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{1})^{p}\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{2})^{l-1-p}.\\
\end{split}

We will now estimate the modulus of
\mathcal{S}
. For this purpose we note that by Stirling’s formula there is a constant
C>0
not depending on
n
such that

(3.19)

\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\binom{n}{i}\leq C\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Also, by choosing sufficiently large
\rho
,

(3.20)

|\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)|\leq|z-k|^{\delta}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
, where
\delta
is the fixed constant in (
3.6
). Since
\frac{n+1}{\sqrt{n}}2^{n}\leq 4^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(3.21)

\begin{split}|\mathcal{S}(k,z,W)|&=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{z-k}\right)^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right)^{n-i}W(z-k)^{i}\right|\\
&\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\left|z-k\right|^{\delta(n-i)}\left|z-k\right|^{(-1+\delta)i}\\
&\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{4^{n}}{n+1}\left|z-k\right|^{\delta n-i}\\
&=C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n(1-\delta)}\frac{4^{n}}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{i}.\end{split}

By
\left|z-k\right|>1,
we have

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{i}\leq 1+\frac{n}{\left|z-k\right|}.

By choosing
n
suitably large

1\leq\frac{n}{\left|z-k\right|}

for all
z\in D(\rho),
and then we can get

(3.22)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{i}\leq\frac{2n}{\left|z-k\right|}.

Substituting (
3.22
) into (
3.21
), we can get

\begin{split}|\mathcal{S}(k,z,W)|&\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n(1-\delta)}\frac{4^{n}}{n+1}\cdot\frac{2n}{\left|z-k\right|}\\
&\leq 2C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}4^{n}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n(1-\delta)+1}\\
&=2C\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{4}{(z-k)^{(1-\delta)}}\right|^{n}\\
&\leq 5C\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{2-\delta}\end{split}

for all
W\in X
. By choosing
\rho
suitably large

(3.23)

|\mathcal{S}(k,z,W)|\leq\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|

for all
z\in D(\rho)
and for all
W\in X
. Moreover, since

\begin{split}\left|W_{1}(z-k)^{i}-W_{2}(z-k)^{i}\right|&=\left|\left(W_{1}(z-k)-W_{2}(z-k)\right)\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}W_{1}(z-k)^{i-1-j}W_{2}(z-k)^{j}\right|\\
&\leq n\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\end{split}

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have, by (
3.19
), (
3.20
) and using the fact that
n\sqrt{n}2^{n}\leq 4^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.24)

\begin{split}|&\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{1})-\mathcal{S}(k,z,W_{2})|\\
&=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{z-k}\right)^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right)^{n-i}\left(W_{1}(z-k)^{i}-W_{2}(z-k)^{i}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left|\alpha+\beta\log(z-k)\right|^{n-i}\left|W_{1}(z-k)^{i}-W_{2}(z-k)^{i}\right|\\
&\leq C\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{n(1-\delta)+\delta}n^{\frac{3}{2}}2^{n}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|.\\
&\leq C\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{\delta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{4}{(z-k)^{(1-\delta)}}\right|^{n}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
&\leq 5C\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
\end{split}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
.

We will now turn our attention back to the operator
T
. By (
3.18
), (
3.19
), (
3.23
) and (
3.24
)

\big|T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\big|\leq 5C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|c_{j}|j^{\frac{3}{2}}2^{j}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{j+1}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|.

Then, there is a constant
B
such that

5C|c_{j}|j^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq B2^{j}

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore

\begin{split}\big|T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\big|&\leq B\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{4}{z-k}\right|^{j}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
&=4B\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{2}\frac{|z-k|}{|z-k|-4}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
&\leq 5B\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{z-k}\right|^{2}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
\end{split}

for all
z\in D(\rho)
when
R
is sufficiently large. Hence

(3.25)

\begin{split}\big|T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\big|&\leq\frac{5B}{|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|\\
&=\frac{K}{|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|,\\
\end{split}

where
K
is a constant. Let
k<1
. Then, by (
3.25
),

\big|T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\big|\leq k\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|

for all
z\in D(\rho)
when
R
is large enough. Hence

\big\|T[W_{1}](z)-T[W_{2}](z)\big\|\leq k\left\|W_{1}-W_{2}\right\|,

and so
T:X\longrightarrow X
is a contraction in
z\in D(\rho)
as asserted.

By Theorem
3.1
the mapping
T:X\longrightarrow X
has a unique fixed point. Hence the existence of a function
W(z)
satisfying
(1),(2)
and
(3)
is proved. In particular,

Y(z)=z+\alpha+\beta\log z+W(z)

is a solution of (
3.4
). The final step is to continue the analytic solution
Y(z)
into a meromorphic solution in the whole complex plane by using equation (
1.7
).

We will finally outline the proof in the case
R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)=0
. Full details are not presented, since the reasoning is very similar to that of the case
R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\not=0
. Assume now that

R^{(j)}(\gamma)=0

for all
j=2,\ldots,m
, and that
R^{(m+1)}(\gamma)\not=0
. The transformation

\tilde{y}(z)=-\frac{(m+1)!g(z)^{m}}{mR^{(m+1)}(\gamma)}

maps equation (
3.3
) into

(3.26)

\tilde{y}(z+1)=\widetilde{F}(\tilde{y}(z)),

where

(3.27)

\widetilde{F}(z)=z+1+\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}c_{j}z^{1-\frac{j+1}{m}}.

Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{m})
. The required Banach space
\widetilde{X}
is the family of analytic functions
W
such that

(3.28)

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-\frac{1}{m}+\delta},

holds in
D(\rho)
. By applying Banach’s fixed point theorem with a similar reasoning as in the case
R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\not=0
, but instead of (
3.10
), using the operator

\begin{split}\widetilde{T}[W](z)&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{m}}{(z-k)^{\frac{1}{m}}}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{l}(m)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{\alpha+\beta(z-k)^{1-\frac{1}{m}}+W(z-k)}{z-k}\right)^{n}\right)^{l}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}}{\left(z-k+\alpha+\beta(z-k)^{1-\frac{1}{m}}+W(z-k)\right)^{\frac{j+1}{m}-1}}\\
&\quad+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(z-k)^{1-\frac{1}{m}}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\xi_{j}(m)\frac{1}{(z-k)^{j}},\\
\end{split}

where
\kappa_{l}(m)
and
\xi_{j}(m)
are constants depending only on
m
. Showing that
\widetilde{T}
is indeed a contraction mapping from
\widetilde{X}
into
\widetilde{X}
is straightforward task by using similar reasoning as before in the case
R^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\not=0
for the operator
T
. Therefore, we have that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
there exists a constant
R>0
, and a unique solution
\widetilde{y}(z)
of (
3.26
), meromorphic in the complex plane, such that

\widetilde{y}(z)=z+\alpha+\beta z^{\frac{m-1}{m}}+W(z),

where

|W(z)|\leq|z|^{-\frac{1}{m}+\delta}

for all

, and
\beta=\frac{mc_{m}}{m-1}.

4.
Examples of branched solutions of Theorem
1.3

Theorem
1.3
guarantees the existence of a unique solution of equation (
1.2
) which is meromorphic for large
\Re(z)
, satisfying the asymptotic condition

(y(z)-\gamma)\lambda^{-z}\to\alpha,\qquad\Re(z)\to+\infty.

In this section, we will consider this solution in the special case

(4.1)

y(z+1)=\lambda y(z)+y(z)^{2},\qquad 0<\lambda<1,

subject to the asymptotic condition

(4.2)

y(z)\lambda^{-z}\to\alpha,\qquad\Re(z)\to+\infty

and show that its analytic continuation results in a function defined on a Riemann surface with an infinite number of sheets over
\mathbb{C}
, which we describe in detail.

Before we describe this solution, we remark that equation (
4.1
) admits a variety of interesting solutions that can be obtained by analytic continuation of series solutions.
Under the transformation

y(z)=Y(u)-\frac{\lambda}{2},\qquad u=\exp\{-2^{z}\},

equation (
4.1
)
becomes

(4.3)

Y(u^{2})=Y(u)^{2}+c,

where

c=\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}.

This equation was studied by Wedderburn
[
14
]
, where he observed that a solution in the case
c=2
is a generating function for a combinatorial problem arising in the theory of groups.
Equation (
4.3
) belongs to a class of functional equations studied by
Poincaré and Picard (see references in
Fatou
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
).
Independently, Mahler
[
11
]
gave a very detailed analysis of solutions of equation (
4.3
) that have a simple pole at
u=0
. Notice that this corresponds to a different class of solutions to equation (
4.1
) than that described by Theorem
1.3
.
Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, a simple consequence of Mahler’s work
[
11
]
is the existence of a solution of equation (
4.1
) satisfying the asymptotic condition

\exp\{2^{-z}\}y(z)\to\alpha,\qquad\Re(z)\to+\infty,\quad\beta<\Im(z)<\gamma,

for some
\beta<\gamma
.

In
[
11
]
, Mahler showed that when
c>0
, equation (
4.3
) admits a solution having a simple pole at
u=0
with the following properties.

(1)

When
c\in(0,1/4]
, the solution is analytic on the unit disc
\{u:|u|<1\}
and continuous on the unit circle
|z|=1
, which is a natural boundary for
Y

[
11
, Th. 7,8]
.

(2)

When
c>1/4
, the solution can be analytically continued to the unit disc
\{u:|u|<1\}
with infinitely many quadratic branches where the unit circle is still a natural boundary
[
11
, Th.3-6]
. In this case the solution has infinitely many sheets.

Since
\lambda\in(0,2)
, then
c\in(0,1/4]
. Thus, the solution
y(z)
of equation (
4.1
) that corresponds to case 1 is defined on the horizontal strips

\log_{2}\left(\frac{4n-1}{2}\pi\right)<\Im(z)<\log_{2}\left(\frac{4n+1}{2}\pi\right),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The boundaries of these strips, i.e. the lines
\Im(z)=\log_{2}\left([2m+1]\pi/2\right)
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, are natural boundaries, so the solution cannot be analytically extended.

Equations (
4.1
) and (
4.3
) can be mapped to the
q
-difference equation

(4.4)

g(qw)=\lambda g(w)+g^{2}(w),

where
w=q^{z}=1/\{\log(1/u)\}
and

y(z)=g(w)=Y(u)-(\lambda/2)
. Here if
q=1/2
, then the solution
g(w)
that corresponds to Mahler’s solution is defined on
\Re(w)>1
where
\Re(w)=1
is a natural boundary.
For other choices of
q
,
g(w)
can be branched and still have natural boundary.

We now return to the solution described in Theorem
1.3
.
In the constant coefficient case, it is slightly easier to study equation (
4.4
) than the equivalent form (
4.1
).
The solution of equation (
4.1
) characterised by
Theorem
1.3
, i.e., subject to the asymptotic condition (
4.2
), corresponds to the solution
g
of equation (
4.4
) of the form

(4.5)

g(w)=g_{1}w+\sum_{n\geq 2}g_{n}w^{n},

for small
w
.

By substituting (
4.5
) into (
4.4
), we note that the leading order term yields
q=\lambda
and
g_{1}
is a free parameter, reflecting the symmetry

w\mapsto g_{1}w
of equation (
4.4
). When
g_{1}\neq 0
, we use the symmetry to set
g_{1}=-1
, without loss of generality.
Assuming that
g
admits a solution of the form (
4.5
), then we have
q\in(0,1)
and
g(w)w^{-1}\to g_{1}
as
w\to 0
, which corresponds to
g(q^{z})q^{-z}\to g_{1}=-1
as
q^{z}\to 0
. This is equivalent to
y(z)\lambda^{-z}\to g_{1}=-1
as
\Re(z)\to+\infty
confirming that condition (
4.2
) holds. In section
4

we will use (
4.5
) to show that
y(z)
is defined on a Riemann surface with infinitely many sheets over
\mathbb{C}
with only quadratic branches.
Moreover, with
g_{1}=-1
, we will show that all branches on each Riemann sheet belong to certain sequences on the positive real axis. We apply arguments similar to those used by Mahler in obtaining the Mahler solution case 2.

We will show that the solutions of
equation (
4.1
)
from Theorem
1.3
are indeed branched. By substituting (
4.5
) into (
4.4
), we obtain the following relations

(4.6)

b=\lambda,~g_{j}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{j}-\lambda}\sum_{s=1}^{j-1}g_{s}g_{j-s},

where
g_{1}
is arbitrary.

Now we will show that (
4.5
) has a non-zero finite radius of convergence by exhibiting a majorising series.
Observe that for the appropriate branch of the square root function,

(4.7)

\tilde{g}(x)=\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\tilde{g}_{1}x}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\tilde{g}_{n}x^{n},

where for all
n>1
,
\tilde{g}_{n}=\sum_{s=1}^{n-1}\tilde{g}_{s}\tilde{g}_{n-s}
.
Since this series has a non-zero radius of convergence and
K|g_{n}|\leq\tilde{g}_{n}
, where
K=\sup_{j\geq 2}(\lambda-\lambda^{j})^{-1}
, then
g(w)
also has a non-zero radius of convergence about
w=0
.

Next we show that
g
in not entire. Assume that the radius of convergence of (
4.5
) is infinite, then
g(w)
is an entire solution of (
4.4
). If
g
has a zero at some point
w_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, then from equation (
4.4
),
g(\lambda^{n}w_{1})=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\lambda^{n}w_{1}\to 0
and
g
is analytic at
w=0
, it follows that
g(w)\equiv 0
. If
g(w)\not\equiv 0
, then
g
is a non-constant entire function, so by Picard’s Theorem there exists a point
w_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
g(w_{2})=1-\lambda\neq 0
. It follows from equation
(
4.4
) that
g(\lambda^{n}w_{2})=1-\lambda\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but
\lambda^{n}w_{2}\to 0
, which contradicts the fact that
g
is analytic and has a zero at
w=0
.

Hence, (
4.5
) has a finite radius of convergence, which we denote by
\hat{r}
. This implies that on the circle
|w|=\hat{r}
, there must be a singular point, which we will call
w_{0}
. Here,
w_{0}
cannot be a pole; otherwise (
4.4
) shows that
\{\lambda^{n}w_{0}\}
forms a sequence of poles of
g
converging to
0
, which contradicts the fact that (
4.5
) converges for all
w
in a neighbourhood of
0
. Similarly,
w_{0}
cannot be an algebraic pole of
g
. Therefore,
g
has a regular branch at
w_{0}
and (
4.4
) shows that
g(w_{0})=-\lambda/2
and
g(\lambda w_{0})=-\lambda^{2}/4
.

We will show that
w_{0}=\hat{r}
is the unique singularity of
g(w)
defined by equation (
4.5
) on the circle
|w|=\hat{r}
. Furthermore, we will show that
g
can be analytically continued to a function with only square root-type branch points. The resulting Riemann surface
X
has an infinite number of sheets over
\mathbb{C}
. All branch points lie over points of the form
\lambda^{1-n}\hat{r}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will describe
X
in terms of a collection of sheets, each of which is of the form
\mathbb{C}\setminus(\lambda^{1-n}\hat{r},+\infty)
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, together with the two boundary lines, each lying over
(\lambda^{1-n}\hat{r},+\infty)\subset\mathbb{R}
. Each of these boundary lines contains an infinite number of square root branch points and different segments over
((\lambda^{1-m}\hat{r},\lambda^{-m}\hat{r})
,
m\geq n
, will be common to two sheets. The restriction of the analytic continuation of
g
to each such sheet will be called a branch. We will now prove these claims and provide a more detailed description of how the different branches fit together.

Let
h_{0}
be the analytic continuation of
g
(where defined) along all straight lines in
\mathbb{C}
through the origin.

Claim 1:
The function
g(w)
defined by equation (
4.5
) can be continued as an analytic function to every point of the circle
|w|=\hat{r}
, except
w=\hat{r}
, where it has a quadratic branch point, i.e.
w_{0}=\hat{r}
.

Proof of Claim 1.

Since
g_{1}=-1
and
q=\lambda\in(0,1)
, then (
4.6
) shows that
g_{n}<0
. Also,
g^{\prime}(0)=g_{1}=-1<0
shows that
g
is locally injective in a domain
D
containing
0
, and
g(w)
is decreasing from the value
0
on
(0,\epsilon)\subset D
for some small
\epsilon>0
. Assume that
w_{0}=\hat{r}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\nu}\neq\hat{r}
for some
\nu\in(0,2\pi)
. Then
g(w_{0})=-\lambda/2
and (
4.4
) shows that

\{g(\lambda^{n}w_{0}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a sequence of negative numbers converging to
g(0)=0
.
We can choose
N
sufficiently large such that
g(\lambda^{N}w_{0})=g(v_{0})
for some point
v_{0}\in(0,\epsilon)\subset D
(the point
v_{0}
exists by the intermediate value theorem).
Using the injectivity of
g
in
D
, we find that
\lambda^{N}w_{0}=v_{0}
, which shows that
w_{0}
is real and greater than zero, so
w_{0}=\hat{r}
.
∎

Claim 2:
The function
g(w)
can be continued as an analytic function to
\hat{X}_{0}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus[\hat{r},+\infty)
. Further continuation to the two boundary lines
[\hat{r},\infty)^{+}
and
[\hat{r},\infty)^{+}
results in a function
h_{0}(w)
analytic at all points of
X_{0}:=\hat{X}_{0}\cup[\hat{r},+\infty)^{+}\cup[\hat{r},+\infty)^{-}
, except at points lying above

B:=\{\lambda^{1-n}\hat{r}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, each of which is a square root branch point.

Proof of Claim 2.

Since
w_{0}=\hat{r}
is a branch point of
h_{0}
, then (
4.4
) shows that all points lying above
B
are also branch points.
Assume that there exists a point
\tilde{w}_{0}\notin B
where
h_{0}
is branched. Then, using (
4.4
), we find that
\lambda^{n}\tilde{w}_{0}
for all sufficiently large

are regular points of
g
. Thus, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g(\lambda^{N}\tilde{w}_{0})=-\lambda/2,g(\lambda^{N+1}\tilde{w}_{0})=-\lambda^{2}/4
.

Let
\tilde{w}_{1}=\lambda^{N}\tilde{w}_{0}
. Then
g(\lambda\tilde{w}_{1})=-\lambda^{2}/4
.
Therefore,
g(\lambda^{n}\tilde{w}_{1})=g(\lambda^{n}\hat{r})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For all
n>0

c_{4}(z)&=a_{4}(z)-a_{3}(z)b_{1}(z)+a_{2}(z)b_{1}(z)^{2}-a_{2}(z)b_{2}(z)-a_{1}(z)b_{1}(z)^{3}\\
&\quad+a_{1}(z)b_{1}(z)b_{2}(z)+a_{1}(z)b_{1}(z)b_{2}(z)-a_{1}(z)b_{3}(z).\\
&\,\,\vdots\\
\end{array}\right.

From (
5.3
), we obtain that
c_{2}(z)
is composed of
2=2^{1}
terms,
a_{2}(z)
and
-a_{1}(z)b_{1}(z),

c_{3}(z)
is composed of
4=2^{2}
terms, and
c_{4}(z)
is composed of
8=2^{3}
terms. By an induction argument, it follows that
c_{j}(z)
is composed of
2=2^{j-1}
terms. Moreover, the only term in
c_{j}(z)
that has degree
j
is
\pm a_{1}(z)b_{1}(z)^{j-1}
.
Hence, we obtain

(5.4)

|c_{j}(z)|\leq\sum_{k\leq j-1}\max_{i=1,\dots,p}|a_{i}(z)|\max_{i=1,\dots,p}|b_{i}(z)|^{k-1}+|a_{1}||b_{1}|^{j-1}\leq|\lambda|2^{j-1}\nu^{(j-1)|z|}.

Assume that
y(z)
satisfies (
2.3
). Denoting
w(z)=y(z)\lambda^{-z}
, equation (
5.2
) becomes

(5.5)

w(z+1)-w(z)=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}c_{j}(z)\lambda^{(j-1)z-1}w(z)^{j}.

Therefore,

(5.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w(z)-w(z-1)&=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}}c_{j}(z-1)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-1)-1}w(z-1)^{j}\\
w(z-1)-w(z-2)&=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}}c_{j}(z-2)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-2)-1}w(z-2)^{j}\\
&\,\,\vdots\\
w(z-m+1)-w(z-m)&=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}}c_{j}(z-m)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-m)-1}w(z-m)^{j}.\\
\end{array}\right.

By adding up (
5.6
), we obtain

(5.7)

w(z)-w(z-m)=\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}.

By letting
m\longrightarrow\infty
in (
5.7
), assumption (
2.3
) yields

(5.8)

w(z)=\alpha+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}.

We have now transformed equation (
1.7
) into the desired form. Now, define an operator
T
by

(5.9)

T[w](z)=\alpha+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}.

We will define a particular Banach space and show that
T
is a contraction mapping in this space.

Note that

(5.10)

\left|w(z)\right|<L

and

(5.11)

\left|u(z)^{j}-v(z)^{j}\right|<jK^{j-1}\left|u(z)-v(z)\right|

for suitable constants
L
and
K
and for all
(z,w)
,
(z,u)
,
(z,v)
in the set

(5.12)

\begin{split}\widetilde{D}(\rho,\sigma)=\{(z,w):\Re(z)<-\rho,~|\Im(z)|<\sigma,~|w-\alpha|\leq b\},\end{split}

where
\rho>0
,
\sigma>0
and
b
are fixed constants. Let
X
be the set of all functions
z\longrightarrow g(z)
, analytic and bounded in

(5.13)

D(\rho,\sigma)=\left\{(z:\Re(z)<-\rho,,~|\Im(z)|<\sigma,\right\},

for which
g(z)\longrightarrow\alpha
as
\Re(z)\longrightarrow-\infty
and
\|g-\alpha\|\leq b
,
where

(5.14)

\left\|g-\alpha\right\|=\sup_{z\in D(\rho,\sigma)}\left|g(z)-\alpha\right|.

Clearly
X
is a metric space under the
\sup
-norm. Moreover, if a sequence
\{g_{n}\}
of
X
is Cauchy, then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}(z)=g(z)
exists uniformly in
D(\rho,\sigma)
. Furthermore,
g
is analytic and bounded in
D(\rho,\sigma)
, and

(5.15)

\lim_{\Re(z)\rightarrow-\infty}g(z)=\lim_{\Re(z)\rightarrow-\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}(z)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\Re(z)\rightarrow-\infty}g_{n}(z)=\alpha

by uniform convergence. Let finally
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(5.16)

\|g-\alpha\|\leq\|g-g_{N}\|+\|g_{N}-\alpha\|<b+\varepsilon.

Since (
5.16
) holds for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.17)

\|g-\alpha\|\leq b.

Therefore
g\in X
, and so
X
is a complete metric space.

Next we prove that
T
is a contraction which maps the space
X
into itself. For this purpose we need some more information about the set
D(\rho,\sigma)
. Choosing the constant
\rho>0
in (
5.13
) sufficiently large, the following four assertions are valid for all
z\in D(\rho,\sigma)
:

(i)

None of the coefficients
c_{j}
of (
1.7
) have poles in
D(\rho,\sigma)
.

(ii)

Using (
5.4
), we have for all
j\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
,

(5.18)

\begin{split}|c_{j}(z)|L^{j}\leq|\lambda|2^{j-1}L^{j}\nu^{(j-1)|z|}&\leq|\lambda|^{\epsilon(j-1)(|\Re(z)|+|\Im(z)|)}\\
&=|\lambda|^{\epsilon(j-1)(-\Re(z)+|\Im(z)|)},\end{split}

where
0<\epsilon<1
can be chosen to be fixed and independent of
\rho,

\sigma
. This is possible since
\nu<|\lambda|.

(iii)

Similarly,

(5.19)

|c_{j}(z)|jK^{j-1}\leq|\lambda|j2^{j-1}\nu^{(j-1)|z|}\leq|\lambda|^{\epsilon(j-1)(-\Re(z)+|\Im(z)|)}.

(iv)

Let
b
be the constant in (
5.12
). Then

(5.20)

\frac{1}{|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)}\frac{M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}{1-M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}<\min\left(b,\tilde{k}\right),

where
\tilde{k}<1
and
M_{S}=\sup_{z\in D(\rho,\sigma)}\{|\lambda|^{\epsilon|\Im(z)|-\theta\Im(z)/\ln|\lambda|}\}
.

We continue by showing that
T[g]
is analytic in
D(\rho,\sigma)
whenever
g
is. Since by (
5.10
)

(5.21)

\begin{split}|T[g](z)-\alpha|&=\left|\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|c_{j}(z-k)\right|\left|\lambda\right|^{(j-1)(\Re(z)-\theta\Im(z)/\ln|\lambda|-k)-1}\|w^{j}\|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|c_{j}(z-k)\right|L^{j}\left|\lambda\right|^{(j-1)(\Re(z)-\theta\Im(z)/\ln|\lambda|-k)-1}.\end{split}

By using
(ii)
, (
5.21
) becomes

(5.22)

\begin{split}|T[g](z)-\alpha|&\leq\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda|^{\epsilon(j-1)(-\Re(z)+|\Im(z)|)}|\lambda|^{(j-1)(\Re(z)-\theta\Im(z)/\ln|\lambda|-k)-1}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\lambda|}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}|\lambda|^{(j-1)(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}^{j-1}\sum_{k=1}\left(|\lambda|^{(j-1)}\right)^{-k}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\lambda|}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\left(|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}\right)^{j-1}\frac{1}{|\lambda|^{j-1}-1}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\left(|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}\right)^{j-1}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)}\frac{M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}{1-M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}.\\
\end{split}

Therefore the sum
T[g](z)-\alpha
converges absolutely and uniformly in
D(\rho,\sigma)
, and so
T[g](z)
is analytic in
D(\rho,\sigma)
. Moreover, by (
5.22
) and property
(iv)
,
T[g](z)\longrightarrow\alpha
as
\Re(z)\longrightarrow-\infty
, and

(5.23)

\|T[g]-\alpha\|\leq b.

We conclude that
T
maps
X
into itself.

We still need to show that
T
is a contraction. Let
g,h\in X
. Then by (
5.11
) and
(iii)

(5.24)

\begin{split}|T[g](z)-T[h](z)|&=\left|\sum_{j=2}\sum_{k=1}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}\left(g(z-k)^{j}-h(z-k)^{j}\right)\right|\\
&\leq\sum_{j=2}\sum_{k=1}\left|c_{j}(z-k)\right|jK^{j-1}\left|\lambda\right|^{(j-1)(\Re(z)-\theta\Im(z)/\ln|\lambda|-k)-1}\|g-h\|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|(|\lambda|-1)}\frac{M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}{1-M_{S}|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}}\|g-h\|,\\
\end{split}

and so, by property
(iv),

(5.25)

\|T[g]-T[h]\|\leq\tilde{k}\|g-h\|,

where
\tilde{k}<1
. Hence
T
is a contraction as asserted.

Since by (
5.25
)
T
is a contraction defined on a complete metric space
X
, we conclude by Theorem
2.2
that
X
has a unique fixed point under
T
. In other words, there is an analytic function
w\in X
such that

(5.26)

w(z)=T[w](z)=\alpha+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}.

Hence,
w
satisfies equation (
5.5
), and so
y(z)=\lambda^{z}w(z)
is a solution of (
1.7
) in
D(\rho,\sigma)
. Finally, using (
1.7
), we may continue the solution
y(z)
into a meromorphic function in the set

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\Im(z)|<\sigma,\right\}.

Since
\sigma>0
was chosen arbitrarily, the solution is meromorphic in the whole complex plane.

6.
The proof of Theorem
2.3

The proof of Theorem
2.3
follows by repeating the reasoning in Section
5
, but, instead of the set
D(\rho,\sigma)
, we look at

\widehat{D}(\rho,\sigma)=\left\{z:\Re(z)>\rho,\quad|\Im(z)|<\sigma\right\},

where
R
is large enough. Since now
|\lambda|<1
, the convergence of

\alpha+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{j}(z-k)\lambda^{(j-1)(z-k)-1}w(z-k)^{j}.

is assured in
\widehat{D}(\rho,\sigma)
. In the following proof, it is only necessary to make the following changes to equations (
5.20
), (
5.22
) and (
5.24
) in Section
5
:

(1)

Replace (
5.20
) with

\frac{1}{|\lambda|(1-|\lambda|)}\frac{|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}{1-|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}<\min\left(b,\tilde{k}\right),

(2)

Replace (
5.22
) with

|T[g](z)-\alpha|\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|(1-|\lambda|)}\frac{|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}{1-|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}.

(2)

Replace (
5.24
) with

|T[g](z)-T[h](z)|\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda|(1-|\lambda|)}\frac{|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}{1-|\lambda|^{(1-\epsilon)\Re(z)}M_{S}}\|g-h\|.

Then the rest of the proof of the local existence and uniqueness of the solution in
\widehat{D}(\rho,\sigma)
is identical to what was done in Section
5
, and we will not repeat it here.

7.
Discussion

In this paper we have studied the existence of solutions of first-order difference equations in the complex domain. We proved the existence of meromorphic solutions for a class of non-autonomous equations with growth conditions on the coefficients as
\Re(z)\to-\infty
. We also provided details of a direct proof of a known result on the existence of meromorphic solutions in the constant coefficient case in which the multiplier at a fixed point of
R
is one. Additionally, we conducted a detailed analysis of the infinite-sheeted Riemann surface on which the analytic continuation of a solution to a simple constant coefficient equation is defined.
