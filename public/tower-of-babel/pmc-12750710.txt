Hierarchical clustering-based coarse-to-fine classification framework for microbial protein function prediction
Authors: Shengyang Chen, Xinyue Gao, Congmin Zhu, Honglei Liu, Yuqing Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate prediction of microbial protein functions is essential for understanding microbial physiology, discovering novel probiotics, and driving biotechnological innovation. However, protein function prediction remains challenging due to the hierarchical and class-imbalanced nature of functional labels, particularly in large-scale annotations such as Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers and Gene Ontology (GO) terms. Most existing deep learning approaches fail to adequately address the long-tail distribution problem.MethodsWe propose a Hierarchical Cascaded Context Network (HCCN) that explicitly models functional hierarchies and emphasizes prediction of low-frequency (long-tail) labels. For EC classification, we design a coarse-to-fine network that captures parent–child dependencies among hierarchical labels. For GO prediction, we construct a semantically grounded hierarchical structure using ontology embedding and clustering, and develop an attention-based multi-level cascade predictor to exploit structured dependencies across Biological Process (BPO), Molecular Function (MFO), and Cellular Component (CCO). To mitigate label imbalance, we introduce a dynamic resampling strategy and a hierarchical loss weighting mechanism, which enforce inter-level regularization and enhance sensitivity to rare functions.ResultsExperimental results show that HCCN consistently outperforms traditional sequence-alignment methods (e.g., DIAMOND, BLAST) and baseline neural networks (MLP and DeepGOPlus) across all major functional categories. On the full test set, HCCN achieves AUPR gains of up to 5.5% (EC), 6.5% (BPO), 4.9% (MFO), and 5.3% (CCO) over the best baseline. For low-frequency labels, HCCN demonstrates strong few-shot generalization, with improvements of + 11.2% (EC low) , + 6.7% (BPO low) , + 9.2% (MFO low) and + 4.6% (CCO low) in mAUPR.ConclusionsThe proposed HCCN framework provides an effective solution to hierarchical and imbalanced protein function prediction, significantly improving performance on long-tail functional labels. Code and data are publicly available at: https://github.com/YangLab-BUPT/HCCN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-025-06326-7.

Introduction

Proteins are central to life processes, performing diverse critical functions in biological systems. They act as enzymes catalyzing over 5000 biochemical reactions (classified under the EC system) and their functional diversity reflects the complex relationship between sequence, structure, and function. Deciphering these functions in the post-genomic era remains a significant challenge.

To standardize functional annotation, the bioinformatics community has developed a dual system: Gene Ontology (GO) and Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. GO describes protein functions in three dimensions—Biological Process (BPO), Molecular Function (MFO), and Cellular Component (CCO)—while EC numbers provide a four-level coding system for enzyme-catalyzed reactions. These frameworks, implemented in databases like UniProtKB, support functional genomics research.

Protein function prediction is one of the core tasks in bioinformatics, playing a crucial role in understanding biological roles of proteins, uncovering disease mechanisms, and advancing drug discovery. Currently, tools based on sequence alignment, such as BLAST and DIAMOND, are widely used in the field of protein function annotation. However, despite their generally strong performance, sequence-alignment-based tools exhibit significant limitations when handling low-homology sequences. Their core algorithms rely heavily on sequence similarity to known sequence databases, which can lead to challenges in accurately identifying matches for long-tail sequences (novel proteins or rapidly evolving sequences) and inaccurate functional predictions.

Recently, the rapid advancement of deep learning technologies has brought revolutionary breakthroughs to the field of bioinformatics [1, 2]. Deep learning models, especially Transformer-based methods, leverage their powerful feature extraction capabilities and self-attention mechanisms to simultaneously capture both local and global features within protein sequences, enabling them to outperform traditional alignment-based tools in many protein-related tasks [3, 4]. Representative approaches, such as TAPE [5] and ProteinBert [6], have demonstrated strong performance across a variety of downstream tasks. Unlike traditional sequence alignment tools, deep learning methods can directly learn functional patterns of protein sequences from large-scale labeled datasets and achieve end-to-end protein function prediction (e.g., GO annotations and EC numbers).

Despite the success of current pre-trained models in extracting rich semantic features from protein sequences, significant challenges remain in handling the complex ontological structures inherent in protein functional annotations. Specifically, the EC numbering system is organized hierarchically, while GO terms are structured as a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Such structural heterogeneity requires that predictive models effectively capture the topological associations between labels.

Recent works have attempted to address this challenge by incorporating prior knowledge of ontology structures. For instance, DeepGO-SE [7] utilizes ELEmbedding [8] to map GO terms into embedding vectors, learning hierarchical relationships through a loss function defined between embeddings. PANDA2 [9] combines protein sequence features with GO term embeddings using graph neural networks (GNNs), while TALE [10] leverages a cross-modal attention mechanism to integrate sequence and ontology descriptions. GORetriever [11] integrates protein textual descriptions with GO semantic ontologies, creating a unified embedding space to improve biological interpretability.

However, existing deep learning methods for protein function prediction still suffer from several limitations. For example, TALE focus primarily on sequence-label associations but overlook the complex semantic relationships among function labels. DeepGO-SE introduces ontology embeddings to encode hierarchical semantics, yet it fails to explicitly model the structural heterogeneity of different annotation systems, which may lead to violations of the true-path rule and degraded label consistency.

In reality, protein annotation systems are structurally heterogeneous and semantically rich. The GO follows a DAG architecture involving multiple edge types like “is_a” and “part_of”, while EC numbers are organized as a strictly hierarchical tree. Prior models rarely account for this heterogeneity in a unified framework, limiting their ability to generalize across annotation systems. On the data side, the challenges are equally pronounced. Microbial protein datasets from UniProt contain only a limited set of experimentally verified labels, where many EC and GO terms appear fewer than 100 times—making them difficult to learn via standard training. Most existing methods focus on high-frequency label performance and lack explicit mechanisms to boost low-frequency label prediction.

To address the challenges posed by the structural heterogeneity of functional annotations and the long-tail distribution of protein functions, we propose a unified hierarchical prediction framework named HCCN. This framework is specifically designed for the dual annotation systems of EC numbers and GO terms, each exhibiting distinct hierarchical properties. For EC numbers, we design a model that performs coarse-grained classification before refining predictions at a fine-grained level. For GO terms, we extend the AttentionXML architecture [12] by incorporating hierarchical clustering to create virtual labels, enabling similar hierarchical predictions. Datasets and materials

Datasets acquisition

The data for this study were sourced from the UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot database (Release 2024_03), published in March 2024. This authoritative database is renowned for its expert-curated protein annotation records. All included data have undergone rigorous bioinformatics validation and continuous maintenance, providing comprehensive annotations such as protein functional characterization, domain information, GO terms, EC classifications, and disease associations. To address the needs of microbial proteomics research, we employed a systematic filtering strategy to extract protein sequences derived from prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) and fungi. The data were classified into multiple dimensions based on functional annotation systems: a functional annotation dataset was constructed using the three main ontologies of Gene Ontology (MFO, BPO, and CCO), while an enzyme catalytic activity classification dataset was established based on EC numbers.

Data preprocessing

We first analyzed the sequence length distribution, as shown in Figure S1, which reveals that the data lengths are concentrated around 700. Consequently, we selected a maximum sequence length of 512 and applied a sliding window approach to truncate sequences that exceed this length. On one hand, using an appropriate sliding window for truncation reduces the input size for our ProtBert model, facilitating the standardization of feature representations in the sequence embedding vectors. On the other hand, extracting different segments of the sequences enhances the model’s generalization ability by exposing it to diverse subsequences.

We analyzed the number and frequency of labels for MFO, BPO, and CCO, as shown in Figure S2. The figure reveals a significant long-tail effect among ontology labels. Therefore, we calculated the proportion of samples for different labels within each dataset. Additionally, we took annotation propagation into account, which means that if a GO term is annotated to a protein, all ancestor GO terms are also annotated to that protein. Consequently, we considered only those GO term labels that have at least one positive sample present in our dataset.Fig. 2t-SNE visualization results of K-Means clustering by SapBert embedding. a–c cluster-annotated visualizations for BPO, MFO, and CCO respectively; d joint embedding space of MFO, BPO, and CCO features

Moreover, to enhance the efficiency of model training, we allocated the dataset as follows: sequences with label frequencies less than 100 were split into training and test sets at a ratio of 1:1, while data with label frequencies greater than 100 were divided into training and test sets at a ratio of 7:3. The statistical results of the dataset partitioning are summarized in Table S1.

Additionally, given the severe imbalance in data labels, we optimized the sampling process for the training set to better address long-tail labels. During training, we performed oversampling of low-frequency samples. Specifically, the ratio of sequences containing only high-frequency labels to those containing low-frequency labels was adjusted from 1:1 to 8:2 as the epoch progressed. This approach allows the model to first adapt to a larger proportion of challenging (low-frequency) samples before adjusting to the real-world scenario where both simple (high-frequency) and challenging samples coexist.

Methods

In this section, we will describe the design of the model structure. Our approach involves three main components: ontology embedding and hierarchical clustering of ontologies, designing an appropriate model architecture to accommodate long-tail labels, and the design of the loss function.

Label clustering

We employed the OWL2Vec*[13] method proposed by Chen et al. to extract attributes such as “def,” “synonym,” “is_a,” and “relationship” from the go-basic.obo file, constructing triple statements that capture the ontological relationships. These triples were then processed using SapBert to generate embedding vectors for protein functions. The resulting clusters are visualized using t-SNE, as shown in Fig. 2, to qualitatively assess the embedding space.

Subsequently, we applied both K-means and spectral clustering algorithms to the resulting embedding vectors. By appropriately setting the clustering threshold, we obtained a “short and wide” hierarchical clustering structure, which is conducive to optimizing predictions in our hierarchical prediction model. The number of clustering layers was set to 2. For MFO, BPO, and CCO, during the construction of the hierarchical clustering process, we utilized the elbow method to determine and fine-tune the clustering hyperparameters, as detailed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis of the virtual labels after clustering reveals the frequency distribution presented in Fig. S3. The results demonstrate that clustering alleviates the long-tailed label problem to some extent, thereby facilitating model learning.

Li [16] introduced an unsupervised model combining clustering and contrastive learning on label embeddings, dynamically updating models during training to improve prediction accuracy.

Loss function

Traditional loss functions, such as Focal Loss, were originally designed to tackle foreground–background class imbalance in multi-label image classification. Lin [19] introduced Focal Loss, which assigns higher losses to negative samples, thereby improving the model’s ability to focus on harder examples. While effective, Focal Loss does not fully address the issue of multi-label imbalance. Ridnik [20] extended this idea with Asymmetric Loss, which improves prediction performance in large-scale, long-tail label scenarios by reducing the loss contribution from negative samples. To further refine this, Li [21] proposed Equalized Focal Loss, which assigns label-specific weights based on the label’s distribution, helping to balance the impact of frequently and infrequently occurring labels.

In our approach, we adopt a hierarchical constraint loss specifically designed for the virtual labels obtained from clustering. This loss function optimizes the coarse-grained classification and ensures that the hierarchical structure of labels is respected during training. The loss function is formally defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ {\mathbf{loss}}_{{layer_{i} }} = BCE\left( {{\mathbf{y}}_{{layer_{i} }} ,\widetilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{{layer_{i} }} } \right) $$\end{document}

In the selection of the loss function for fine-grained classification, the number of ontology labels associated with a single amino acid sequence is significantly smaller than the total number of ontology labels. This imbalance results in a much higher proportion of negative labels compared to positive labels during loss computation, which hinders the prediction of positive labels. Additionally, the presence of a large number of long-tail labels further complicates the identification of rare samples.

To address these challenges, we adopted the Asymmetric Loss function to mitigate the impact of data distribution. ASLoss reduces the contribution of negative labels to the loss by applying a probability threshold. In addition to reporting overall macro-averaged scores over all labels, we also conduct a stratified analysis based on label frequency. Specifically, labels with ≥ 100 positive training instances are considered high-frequency, and those with < 100 are treated as low-frequency. AUPRC and AUC are computed separately within each group to assess model robustness under label imbalance.

Experimental settings

A two-layer feedforward network having hidden dimensions of [2048, 2048] was used, compared to [1024, 1024] for EC classification. Training stability and convergence were improved with a batch size of 512, a learning rate of 1e-4, and a linear warm-up strategy over the first 10% of training steps. The model was implemented in PyTorch and trained on two NVIDIA A6000 GPUs with bfloat16 precision. For GO prediction, the batch size was 2048 and learning rate was 3e-4, while the EC module employed a [1024, 1024] architecture with a batch size of 1024 and learning rate of 3e-5. DIAMOND and BLAST were configured with an E-value threshold of 1e-4, a minimum identity of 50%, and a maximum of 10 target sequences per query. To address label imbalance, a dynamic sampling strategy was implemented, progressively adjusting the ratio of rare-to-common label instances from 1:1 to 8:2 throughout training. This curriculum-based adjustment enabled more effective learning of underrepresented functional labels.

HCCN, our proposed hierarchical deep learning framework for microbial protein function prediction, captures multi-level label dependencies by leveraging the hierarchical structure of EC and GO ontologies. This allows for context-aware, semantically consistent predictions. Compared to alignment-based methods like DIAMOND and BLAST, HCCN improves both accuracy and efficiency, especially in low-frequency label prediction.

To evaluate generalization across frequency spectra, high-frequency labels were defined as those with ≥ 100 samples, and low-frequency labels as those with < 100 samples. Experimental results showed that while HCCN performed similarly to DIAMOND and BLAST on high-frequency labels, it significantly outperformed them on low-frequency labels. This performance gap arises from two key limitations of alignment-based methods: sparse representation of rare functional sequences in reference databases and domain-centric matching biases, which miss subtle or context-specific functional patterns. In contrast, HCCN captures these nuanced patterns by exploiting ontology-based hierarchical supervision, leading to better generalization in data-scarce conditions.

Discussion

As shown in Table 1, alignment-based methods (DIAMOND, BLAST) and learning-based models generally perform better on EC prediction than on GO annotation. This can be attributed to the simpler tree-structured hierarchy of EC numbers, compared to the more complex DAG topology of GO ontologies.

The proposed HCCN consistently surpasses all baselines across tasks. Its clear improvements over DeepGOPlus and the MLP baseline confirm the effectiveness of hierarchical contrastive clustering and cross-frequency attention, which enhance representation learning under label imbalance.

Notably, HCCN exhibits strong robustness to low-frequency labels, as evidenced by cross-frequency AUPR comparisons. This robustness arises from adaptive sampling and loss reweighting strategies that alleviate the dominance of frequent labels.

However, a minor trade-off is observed in some high-frequency categories, where alignment-based methods retain slightly higher precision. This may reflect HCCN’s optimization bias toward low-frequency generalization. Nonetheless, the overall balance achieved across the label spectrum highlights the framework’s suitability for long-tail protein function prediction.

Finally, Table 5 and Table 6 presents results from a tenfold cross-validation. HCCN achieves higher mean test AUPR and AUC than DeepGOPlus across all functional categories, with statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05). This demonstrates that HCCN not only yields superior predictive accuracy but also maintains stable generalization performance across different data splits.

Limitation

Although our method demonstrates improved performance over existing baselines, several limitations remain. While adaptive sampling and loss reweighting alleviate some imbalance issues, predictive performance on ultra-rare labels remains suboptimal. The extreme label imbalance, particularly for GO terms with very low label frequencies, should be improved further. Second, the current approach didn’t fully capture complex semantic dependencies among functional terms. Incorporating dynamic label representations, graph neural networks, or contrastive learning in the label space may further enhance label relationship modeling. Lastly, the external biological knowledge sources (e.g., protein–protein interaction networks or metabolic pathways) can be integrated to provide complementary information for function prediction.

Conclusion

This paper presents HCCN, a hierarchical deep learning framework designed for microbial protein function prediction. The proposed model integrates cross-view learning, hierarchical label modeling, and adaptive strategies to address label imbalance and semantic diversity across different functional ontologies. Experimental results demonstrate that HCCN consistently outperforms traditional alignment-based methods. Notably, HCCN achieves substantial gains in predicting low-frequency labels across EC numbers and GO categories, highlighting its effectiveness in handling long-tail distributions. HCCN can serve as a valuable tool for microbial genomics research and downstream biological discovery.
