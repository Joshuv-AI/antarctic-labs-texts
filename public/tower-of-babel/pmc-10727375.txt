Patient Awareness of Heart Failure Diagnosis: A Community Study
Authors: Stacie J. Shropshire, Matteo Fabbri, Sheila M. Manemann, Véronique L. Roger, Jill M. Killian, Susan A. Weston, Alanna M. Chamberlain
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a complex disease that contributes to a high number of hospitalizations, deaths, and economic health care costs each year. However, among patients with HF, there is a lack of awareness of their HF diagnosis that has not been fully examined.Methods and ResultsResidents from 3 counties of southeast Minnesota with a first‐ever International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) code 428 or Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code I50 between January 1, 2013 and March 31, 2016 (N=2461) were prospectively surveyed to measure HF self‐awareness. A total of 1114 patients returned the survey (response rate, 45%), and 787 had validated HF upon medical record review. Among these 787 patients with HF (mean age, 76 years; 53% men), 37% (n=293) were aware of their HF diagnosis. After adjustment, being a woman (odds ratio [OR], 1.56 [95% CI, 1.10–2.22]), having HF with reduced ejection fraction (OR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.13–2.22]), attending the HF clinic (OR, 4.07 [95% CI, 2.25–7.36]), and having coronary artery disease (OR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.16–2.37]) were all associated with increased awareness of an HF diagnosis. Conversely, having diabetes was associated with decreased awareness of an HF diagnosis (adjusted OR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.50–0.95]).ConclusionsAwareness of an HF diagnosis is low in a community population of patients with HF. Strategies to improve patient awareness of their diagnosis should be implemented to improve self‐care behaviors and outcomes in patients with HF.

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome in which a structural or functional disorder of the heart impairs ventricular filling or systemic blood flow. 1 HF affects >6 million adults in the United States and 64 million people throughout the world. 2 , 3 , 4 In 2014, HF was the main cause of 1 million hospitalizations and >80 000 deaths, and the total costs of HF were estimated to be >$30 billion in 2012. 5 By 2030, the total cost of HF is projected to be around $70 billion. 4 For patients with low health literacy or a decreased understanding of HF, self‐care management may be inadequate, which can increase mortality. 6 , 7 Self‐care management is vitally important in prolonging life, but to practice adequate self‐care, individuals must first be informed about and understand their diagnoses. 8 Among patients with HF, there is a considerable lack of understanding of the development, definition, and trajectory of the syndrome which can affect their ability to correctly identify their HF diagnosis. 9 Previous studies reported the prevalence of being aware of an HF diagnosis to be between 17% and 40%. 10 , 11 , 12 A cross‐sectional community study of 1402 participants conducted by Halling and Berglund comparing disease awareness in patients diagnosed with HF and patients diagnosed with diabetes found that only 39.8% of patients with HF were aware of their diagnosis compared with 97.1% of patients with diabetes. 11 HF is often difficult to diagnose, which can lead to issues in communication between providers and patients regarding diagnosis and prognosis of HF and can leave patients with an inadequate understanding of HF. 9 There is a need to increase self‐awareness of HF diagnosis among HF patients and identify influencing factors that promote or inhibit HF diagnosis awareness in order to reduce morbidity and death. Few of the aforementioned studies were population based or took into account the varying characteristics of patients with HF, such as sex, education, ejection fraction (EF), and HF clinic attendance. Thus, the aim of this study was to identify characteristics and predictors of being aware of an HF diagnosis in a population‐based cohort of patients with validated HF.

Methods

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to the corresponding author. This study was conducted in southeast Minnesota, in the 3 counties of Olmsted, Dodge, and Fillmore, using the resources of the Rochester Epidemiology Project. 13 The Rochester Epidemiology Project is a medical records linkage system that covers a 27‐county region in southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin and includes data from the local health care providers including Mayo Clinic, Mayo Clinic Health System clinics and hospitals, and Olmsted Medical Center and its affiliated clinics. It covers a population of ≈700 000 patients and includes information from all health care encounters, both inpatient and outpatient, including diagnoses, labs, procedures, and medications.

Study participants were identified using a first‐ever International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) code 428 or Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code I50 between January 1, 2013 and March 31, 2016. The cases were manually validated for clinical HF, defined as a physician's diagnosis in the medical record. EF was collected from the medical record (±90 days of HF diagnosis) and categorized into HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (EF ≥50%) and HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (EF <50%).

Details of this study have been described elsewhere; however, in brief, patients with HF were asked to complete a survey of sociobehavioral measures. 14 A mixed‐mode design was used to maximize the response rate. Patients were mailed a survey packet containing the survey, an introductory cover letter, and a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act form. Patients who did not want to complete the survey through the mail were given the opportunity to complete the survey over the phone by requesting a telephone call. The survey packet was resent to nonresponders ≈1 month after the first mailing. A telephone interview was attempted ≈1 month after the second mailing for the remaining nonresponders. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center institutional review boards. For this minimal risk study, the institutional review boards granted a waiver of written informed consent. All elements of informed consent were presented to participants in the cover letter that accompanied the survey packet in the mail. By completing and returning the survey, the institutional review boards considered this implied consent. The signed Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act form allowed linkage of survey data to electronic health record data.

For the current analysis, patients were required to complete the survey by December 31, 2016. To assess awareness of an HF diagnosis, the following question was asked: Do you have, or in the past have you experienced, any of the following conditions? (Mark all that apply) High blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, heart failure (heart not pumping enough blood), heart attack, stroke. Patients who indicated that they had experienced HF were classified as being aware of their HF diagnosis.

Education level and marital status were obtained from the patient survey. Chronic conditions were ascertained by electronically retrieving ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes (from both inpatient and outpatient visits), using code sets recommended by the US Department of Health and Human Services. 15 Two occurrences of a code (either the same code or 2 different codes within the code set for a given disease) separated by >30 days and occurring within 3 years before the incident HF date were required to be classified as having the condition. Since all patients had HF, this condition was not included as a predictor. In addition, few individuals in our cohort had autism, hepatitis, and HIV; thus, these were also not included as predictors. However, we added anxiety as a predictor because this is a common chronic condition, resulting in 17 chronic conditions included as predictors (hypertension, coronary artery disease, arrhythmia, hyperlipidemia, stroke, arthritis, asthma, cancer, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, osteoporosis, dementia, depression, schizophrenia, substance abuse disorder, and anxiety). Attendance at an HF specific clinic or a cardiac rehabilitation program, defined as at least 1 appointment, was abstracted from the medical records.

In accordance with the guidelines of the American Association for Public Opinion Research, the response rate was calculated using formula 2: dividing the number of complete and partial surveys by the total number of complete and partial surveys, refusals, noncontacts, others, and cases of unknown eligibility. 14 Baseline characteristics overall and by awareness of HF diagnosis are presented as median (interquartile range) for age and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Difference in age between HF diagnosis awareness status was tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test; differences in the categorical variables were tested with Pearson's chi‐square test.

Logistic regression was used to determine the association between patient characteristics and HF awareness. A multivariable model was run including age, sex, marital status (married versus not married), educational attainment (more than high school education versus high school graduate or less), inpatient HF diagnosis, EF (HFrEF versus HFpEF), HF clinic attendance, cardiac rehabilitation attendance, hypertension, coronary artery disease, and diabetes. In secondary analyses, we stratified the adjusted models by sex, and differences in associations between each predictor and awareness of HF diagnosis by sex were tested by including an interaction term (predictor×sex) in the models. A level of significance of <0.05 was used for all analyses. All analyses were performed using SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Checklist was used to guide the reporting of this study. 16

Results

The survey was mailed to 2461 patients, and 1114 patients completed the survey (American Association for Public Opinion Research formula 2 response rate: 45%). Of those who responded, 71% had validated HF (n=787) and were included in the analysis. Among the 787 patients with validated HF (mean age, 76 years; 53% men), 37% were aware of their HF diagnosis (n=293) (Table 1). Patients who were aware of their HF diagnosis were more likely to attend the HF clinic (18% versus 5%) and have HFrEF (52% versus 35%) and coronary artery disease (59% versus 49%), and were less likely to have hypertension (78% versus 84%) and diabetes (47% versus 54%) compared with those who were not aware. After adjustment, being a woman (odds ratio [OR], 1.56 [95% CI, 1.10–2.22]), having HFrEF (OR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.13–2.22]), attending the HF clinic (OR, 4.07 [95% CI, 2.25–7.36]) and having coronary artery disease (OR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.16–2.37]) were associated with increased awareness of having an HF diagnosis. Conversely, having diabetes was associated with decreased awareness of an HF diagnosis (adjusted OR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.50–0.95]) (Figure 1).

Discussion

In this study of community patients with HF, 37% of patients were aware of their HF diagnosis. After adjustment, we found that being female, having HFrEF, attending the HF clinic, and having coronary artery disease were all associated with increased awareness of an HF diagnosis. Conversely, having diabetes was associated with decreased awareness of HF.

There are limited studies comparing self‐reporting of an HF diagnosis with a confirmed medical record diagnosis. In addition, very few studies identify influencing factors of a patient's awareness of an HF diagnosis. Our findings are consistent with what has been previously reported. 17 Gure et al conducted a study using data from the HRS (Health and Retirement Study), a national representative survey. The analysis was restricted to 5753 adults aged ≥67 years. The following questions were used to determine self‐report of HF diagnosis: “Has a doctor ever told you that you had a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” For participants who responded “Yes,” the following question was then asked: “Has a doctor told you that you have congestive heart failure?” Of the participants with an HF diagnosis Medicare claim, only 25% self‐reported a diagnosis of HF. Characteristics of those participants who correctly self‐reported an HF diagnosis included increased encounters with a health care provider, increased mean number of chronic conditions, and increased mean HF claims.

In our study, patients who previously attended the HF clinic were more likely to be aware of their HF diagnosis, which suggests that attending an HF clinic may provide patients with more information regarding their diagnosis. Cardiac rehabilitation attendance may also increase encounters with health care providers which, as previously mentioned, is a characteristic of patients who correctly self‐reported their HF diagnosis. 17 Increasing the opportunities to meet with health care providers allows for patients to ask questions and increase their understanding of HF and their diagnosis.

Patients with HFrEF were also more aware of their HF diagnosis than those with HFpEF. This may be explained by the challenges presented in diagnosing and treating patients with HFpEF due to nonspecific symptoms and multiple etiologies of HFpEF that are not completely understood. 18 As HF, especially HFrEF, can be a direct result of coronary artery disease, 19 it is possible that patients who experience a myocardial infarction with subsequent HF may be more aware of an HF diagnosis due to an identifiable cause of the disease and a more predictable disease progression based on the cardiac event. Emphasizing the possibility of nonspecific symptoms or lack of symptoms to patients with HFpEF may be important in adapting the patient's understanding of the existence of the disease diagnosis.

Being a woman was another characteristic of participants with increased awareness of having an HF diagnosis. This may be due to multiple factors; however, this association requires further study. Our results indicate that having a greater than high school education was associated with increased HF diagnosis awareness in women and not in men. Having a previous diabetes diagnosis was also associated with a decreased awareness of HF diagnosis in men. However, we did not adjust our interaction results for multiple comparisons; therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution. Additional studies should be conducted to determine whether similar associations are found in other populations.

Diabetes is a contributing factor in developing HF; however, in our study, patients with diabetes were more likely to be unaware of their HF diagnosis. This is especially concerning because up to 47% of patients with HF have diabetes, and the risk of recurrent hospitalizations and death significantly increases when both diseases are present. 20 As patients with diabetes and HF can have complex and challenging medical regimens with an influx of information from multiple providers, it is possible for patients to be unaware of an HF diagnosis. 20 Collaboration between providers may be necessary for patients with diabetes and HF to understand the diagnosis of both diseases and how to self‐manage care for both conditions simultaneously.

Clinical Implications

The considerable lack of patient awareness of having an HF diagnosis is concerning because HF continues to be associated with poor outcomes and increased economic burden. 5 As HF is a complex disease with many challenges in diagnosing and prognosing, patients may experience difficulty understanding the disease and its implications, especially in regard to medication, terminology, self‐care behavior, and outlook. 6 , 8 , 9 , 21 Strategies to increase patient knowledge and self‐care adherence are recommended to reduce rehospitalization and death. 22 Suggested strategies include encouraging patients to ask questions, enrolling patients in a self‐regulation program, and person‐centered interventions. 22 However, additional strategies may be needed to ensure that patients understand their diagnosis of HF. Cardiac rehabilitation attendance should be prioritized and highly encouraged for patients with an HF diagnosis, including those with an HFpEF diagnosis. Patients with diabetes and HF and patients with HFpEF may need increased explanations or follow‐up appointments to allow for understanding of their HF diagnosis. Further research is needed to identify specific strategies that will increase patient awareness of HF.

Limitations

Our study was conducted in a population of mostly non‐Hispanic White race and ethnicity with higher‐than‐average education; thus, the generalizability to other populations may be limited. However, this region has similar age, sex, and racial and ethnic characteristics as the state of Minnesota and the Upper Midwest region of the United States. 13 In addition, we did not adjust our testing of interactions for multiple comparisons; thus, future studies may be warranted to confirm our results. Furthermore, nonresponder bias could have affected our results; however, we cannot provide an assessment of the possible extent of bias because it is an unobservable error. Nevertheless, our response rate (45%) is relatively high and is similar to other national surveys. 23 Finally, as in any observational study, we cannot rule out the effect of residual confounding due to unmeasured variables.

Strengths

Our study had several strengths. We identified a large community‐based cohort of patients with HF, and through use of the Rochester Epidemiology Project, we had comprehensive ascertainment of comorbidities in a large area of southeastern Minnesota. We were able to validate the HF diagnosis and investigated several clinical characteristics as predictors of being aware of an HF diagnosis.

Conclusions

In community patients with HF, approximately one‐third of patients are aware of their HF diagnosis. As having an awareness of an HF diagnosis may play a role in patients' activation and their active collaboration with the health care provider, strategies that increase awareness of their HF diagnosis should be used to improve patient education, self‐care behavior, and outcomes.
