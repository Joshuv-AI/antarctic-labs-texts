On linear evolutionary equations with skew symmetric spatial operators
Evgeny Yu. Panov

Abstract
We study generalized solutions of an evolutionary equation related to a densely defined skew-symmetric operator in a real Hilbert space. We establish existence of a contractive semigroup, which provides generalized solutions, and find criteria of uniqueness of generalized solutions. Some applications are given including the transport equations and the linearised Euler equations with solenoidal (and generally discontinuous) coefficients. Under some additional regularity assumption on the coefficients we prove that the corresponding spatial operators are skew-adjoint, which implies existence and uniqueness of generalized solutions for both the forward and the backward Cauchy problem.

Introduction

Let
H
be a real Hilbert space,
A_{0}
be a skew-symmetric linear operator in
H
with a dense domain
X_{0}=D(A_{0})\subset H
. Skew-symmetricity of the operator
A_{0}
means that
(A_{0}u,v)=-(u,A_{0}v)

\forall u,v\in X_{0}
, where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the scalar multiplication in
H
. The skew-symmetricity condition is equivalent to the relation

-A_{0}\subset(A_{0})^{*}
, where
(A_{0})^{*}
is the adjoint operator. Since an adjoint operator is closed, we conclude that there exists the closure
A
of the operator
A_{0}
. This operator
A
is a closed skew-symmetric operator, and
A^{*}=(A_{0})^{*}
.
We underline that in view of the identity
2((A_{0}u,v)+(u,A_{0}v))=(A_{0}(u+v),u+v)-(A_{0}(u-v),u-v)
the skew-symmetricity of
A_{0}
is equivalent to the condition
(A_{0}u,u)=0

\forall u\in X_{0}
.

The operator
A^{*}
is an extension of the operator
-A
, and may be not skew-symmetric. Its skew-symmericity holds only in the case
A^{*}=-A
, that is, when the operator
A
is skew-adjoint.

We consider the evolutionary equation

u^{\prime}-A^{*}u=0,\quad u=u(t),\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

(1.1)

where
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,+\infty)
, with the initial condition

u(0)=u_{0}\in H.

(1.2)

Definition 1.1
.

A function
u=u(t)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
is called a generalized solutions (g.s.) of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) if

\forall f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
, where the space
X_{0}
equipped with the graph norm
\|u\|^{2}=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|A_{0}u\|_{H}^{2}
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0.

(1.3)

Remark 1.1
.

(1) It follows from relation (
1.3
) with
f(t)=v\varphi(t)
, where
\varphi(t)\in C_{0}^{1}((0,+\infty))
,
v\in X_{0}
, that

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),v)=(u(t),Av)
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}((0,+\infty))
, where we denote by
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(I)
the space of distributions on an open set
I
. The latter relation directly implies weak continuity of
u(t)
(after possible correction on a set of null measure). As is easy to see, relation (
1.3
) also implies initial condition
(
1.2
) understood in the sense of weak convergence
u(t)\rightharpoonup u_{0}
as
t\to 0+
;

(2) Let
X=D(A)
be the domain of operator
A
, equipped with the graph norm
\|u\|^{2}=\|u\|_{H}^{2}+\|A(u)\|_{H}^{2}
, then
X
is a Banach space in view of closedness of
A
. Since the operator
A
is the closure of
A_{0}
, the space
X_{0}
is dense both in
H
and in
X
, which implies that the space
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
is dense in
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
. Therefore, every test function
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
can be approximated by a sequence

f_{r}(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
,
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so that
f_{r}(t)\to f(t)
,
f_{r}^{\prime}(t)\to f^{\prime}(t)
,
A_{0}f_{r}(t)\to Af(t)
as
r\to\infty
in
H
, uniformly with respect to
t
. Passing to the limit as
r\to\infty
in relations (
1.3
) with the test functions
f=f_{r}
, we arrive at the relation

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+Af(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0

for all
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
. In particular, since
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)\supset C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X)
, we can replace
A_{0},X_{0}
in Definition
1.1
by
A
and
X
, respectively. Thus, we may initially suppose that
A_{0}=A
is a closed skew-symmetric operator.

2
Contractive semigroups of g.s.

We consider a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T_{t}=e^{tB}
of bounded linear operators in
H
with an infinitesimal generator
B
. It is known that
B
is a densely defined closed operator.
We are interesting when this semigroup provides g.s. of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
). The following theorem gives the answer.

Theorem 2.1
.

Functions
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
are g.s. of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) for every
u_{0}\in H
if and only if
B\subset A^{*}
.

Proof.

First, we assume that
B\subset A^{*}
and that
u_{0}\in D(B)
. Then the function
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+},D(B))\cap C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
and
u^{\prime}(t)=T_{t}Bu_{0}=BT_{t}u_{0}=Bu(t)
. Therefore, for each
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})
there exists a continuous derivative

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))=(u^{\prime}(t),f(t))+(u(t),f^{\prime}(t))=(Bu(t),f(t))+(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)).

(2.1)

By our assumption
B\subset A^{*}
and therefore

(Bu(t),f(t))=(A^{*}u(t),f(t))=(u(t),Af(t))=(u(t),A_{0}f(t)).

Now it follows from (
2.1
) that

\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))=(u(t),A_{0}f(t)+f^{\prime}(t)).

After integration this relation implies

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{d}{dt}(u(t),f(t))dt=-(u_{0},f(0)).

Hence, identity (
1.3
) holds, and
u(t)
is a g.s. of problem
1.1
), (
1.2
).

In the case of arbitrary
u_{0}\in H
we consider a sequence

u_{0k}\in D(B)
, which converges to
u_{0}
in
H
as
k\to\infty
. As was already established, the functions
u_{k}(t)=T_{t}u_{0k}
are g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with initial data
u_{0k}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

\|u_{k}(t)-u(t)\|_{H}\leq\|T_{t}\|\|u_{0k}-u_{0}\|_{H}\mathop{\to}_{k\to\infty}0

uniformly on any segment
[0,T]
, where we are taking into account the known for
C_{0}
-semigroups bound
\|T_{t}\|\leq Ce^{\alpha t}
with some constants
C>0
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
in the identity (
1.3
)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u_{k}(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0k},f(0))=0,

we conclude that for all test functions
f(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},X_{0})

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),f^{\prime}(t)+A_{0}f(t))dt+(u_{0},f(0))=0.

Hence,
u(t)
is a g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
), as was to be proved.

Conversely, assume that the function
u(t)=T_{t}u_{0}
is a g.s. of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) for each
u_{0}\in H
. If
u_{0}\in D(B)
, then
u(t)=T_{t}u_{0}\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)
and
u^{\prime}(0)=Bu_{0}
. Therefore, for all
v\in D(A_{0})

I(t)=(u(t),v)\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}),\ I^{\prime}(0)=(Bu_{0},v).

On the other hand for all
h(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}I(t)h^{\prime}(t)dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),vh^{\prime}(t))dt=-h(0)(u_{0},v)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),A_{0}v)h(t)dt

by identity (
1.3
) with
f=h(t)v
. Integrating by parts, we arrive at

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}I^{\prime}(t)h(t)dt=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}(u(t),A_{0}v)h(t)dt.

Since
h(t)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
is arbitrary, we obtain the equality
I^{\prime}(t)=(u(t),A_{0}v)
. In particular,

(u_{0},A_{0}v)=I^{\prime}(0+)=(Bu_{0},v)
. The identity
(u_{0},A_{0}v)=(Bu_{0},v)
holds for all
v\in D(A_{0})
, and by the definition of the adjoint operator
A^{*}=(A_{0})^{*}
we obtain the inclusion
u_{0}\in D(A^{*})
and the equality
A^{*}u_{0}=Bu_{0}
. Thus,
B\subset A^{*}
, which completes the proof.
∎

By the Lumer-Phillips theorem (see, for instance,
[
14
, Theorem 1.1.3]
) a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T_{t}=e^{tB}
is contractive (i.e.,
\|T_{t}\|\leq 1

\forall t>0
) if and only if its generator
B
is an
m
-dissipative operator. Remind that an operator
A
in a Banach space
H
is called dissipative if
\|u-hAu\|\geq\|u\|

\forall u\in D(A)
,
h>0
. A dissipative operator
A
is called
m
-dissipative if
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-hA)=X
for all
h>0
(it is sufficient that this property is satisfied only for one value
h>0
). It is known that an
m
-dissipative operator is a maximal dissipative operator, in the case of a Hilbert space
H
the inverse statement is also true. Notice also that in the case of Hilbert space
H
dissipativity of an operator
A
reduces to the condition
(Au,u)\leq 0

\forall u\in D(A)
. In particular, skew-symmetric operators are dissipative. The following property means that the requirement
B\subset A^{*}
of Theorem
2.1
for an

m
-dissipative operator is equivalent to the condition that
B
is an extension of the operator
-A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
B
be an
m
-dissipative operator in
H
. Then
B\subset A^{*}\Leftrightarrow-A\subset B
.

Proof.

Let
B\subset A^{*}
. Since also
-A\subset A^{*}
, then
-Au=Bu=A^{*}u
on
D(A)\cap D(B)
. This allows to construct an extension
\tilde{B}

of both the operators
-A
and
B
defined on
D(A)+D(B)
by the equality

\tilde{B}u=-Au_{1}+Bu_{2},\quad u=u_{1}+u_{2},\ u_{1}\in D(A),u_{2}\in D(B).

Since the operators
-A
and
B
coincide on their common domain
D(A)\cap D(B)
, the above definition of the operator
\tilde{B}
is correct and does not depend on the representation
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
. By the construction
-A\subset\tilde{B}
,
B\subset\tilde{B}
. Let us show that the operator
\tilde{B}
is dissipative. We take an arbitrary
u\in D(A)+D(B)
. Then
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
for some
u_{1}\in D(A)
,
u_{2}\in D(B)
, and

\displaystyle(\tilde{B}u,u)=(-Au_{1}+Bu_{2},u_{1}+u_{2})=

\displaystyle-(Au_{1},u_{1})+(Bu_{2},u_{2})+((u_{1},Bu_{2})-(Au_{1},u_{2}))=

\displaystyle(Bu_{2},u_{2})+((u_{1},A^{*}u_{2})-(Au_{1},u_{2}))=(Bu_{2},u_{2})\leq 0

(2.2)

by dissipativity of the operator
B
. We take also into account that
(Au_{1},u_{1})=0
by skew-symmetricity of
A
and that

B\subset A^{*}
. By (
2
) the operator
\tilde{B}
is dissipative. But
B
is a maximal dissipative operator and we conclude that
\tilde{B}=B
. In particular,
D(A)+D(B)=D(B)
, that is,
D(A)\subset D(B)
. This implies that
-A\subset B
.

Conversely, suppose that
-A\subset B
. Let
u\in D(A)
,
v\in D(B)
. Then
u+sv\in D(B)
for all
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and by dissipativity of
B
we have
f(s)\doteq(B(u+sv),u+sv)\leq 0
. The function
f(s)
is quadratic and can be written as

\displaystyle f(s)=(Bu,u)+s((Bu,v)+(Bv,u))+s^{2}(Bv,v)=

\displaystyle s((Bu,v)+(Bv,u))+s^{2}(Bv,v),

(2.3)

because
(Bu,u)=-(Au,u)=0
by the condition
-A\subset B
. Notice also that
(Bv,v)\leq 0
. It now follows from representation
(
2
) and the condition
f(s)\leq 0

\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}
that
(Bu,v)+(Bv,u)=0
. Thus, for all
v\in D(B)
and
u\in D(A)

(Au,v)=-(Bu,v)=(Bv,u)=(u,Bv).

This identity implies that
v\in D(A^{*})
and
A^{*}v=Bv
for all
v\in D(B)
. This means that
B\subset A^{*}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We underline that the implication
-A\subset B\Rightarrow B\subset A^{*}
was also established in
[
14
, Lemma 1.1.5]
.

3
Main results

By Theorem
2.1
and Lemma
2.1
generators of contractive semigroups of g.s. to equation (
1.1
) are exactly
m
-dissipative extensions of the operator
-A
. Existence of such extensions is well-known, see for example

[
14
]
. We want to construct such an extension that is “maximally close” to a skew-symmetric operator. First, we need to choose a skew-symmetric extension of
-A
. Such extensions reduces to symmetric extensions of the symmetric operator
iA
, which are based on the Cayley transform. In the framework of skew-symmetric operators the Cayley transform
Q=(E+A)(E-A)^{-1}
is even more natural. The operator
Q
is an isometry between the spaces
H_{-}=\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A)
and
H_{+}=\mathop{\rm Im}(E+A)
(notice that the spaces
H_{\pm}
are closed due to the closedness of operator
A
). The Cayley transform is invertible, the inverse transform is defined by the formula
A=(Q-E)(Q+E)^{-1}
(under the assumption that the domain

D(A)=\mathop{\rm Im}(Q+E)
is dense, the operator
Q+E
is always invertible). Hence, skew-symmetric extensions of the operator
A
corresponds to isometric extensions of the operator
Q
, which are reduced to construction of isometric maps from
(H_{-})^{\perp}
into
(H_{+})^{\perp}
. Remind that Hilbert dimensions of these spaces (i.e., Hilbert codimensions of
H_{\pm}
) are called the deficiency indexes of
A
. We denote them
d_{+}
,
d_{-}
, respectively, so that
d_{\pm}=d_{\pm}(A)=\mathop{\rm codim}H_{\pm}
. Maximal skew-symmetric operators
A
are characterized by the condition that at least one of the deficiency indexes
d_{\pm}(A)
is null. The condition
d_{+}=d_{-}=0
describes skew-adjoint operators, their Cayley transforms
Q
are orthogonal operators. It is known that any skew-symmetric operator can be extended to a maximal skew-symmetric operator. Let
\tilde{A}
be a maximal skew-symmetric extension of
A
, and
d_{\pm}=d_{\pm}(\tilde{A})
be the deficiency indexes of this extension. Then one of these indexes is null. If
d_{+}=0
then
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+\tilde{A})=H
and the operator
B=-\tilde{A}
is
m
-dissipative.
Similarly, if
d_{-}=0
the operator
\tilde{A}
is
m
-dissipative. It is known (see, for instance,
[
14
, Theorem 1.1.2]
) that the operator
(\tilde{A})^{*}
is also
m
-dissipative, and that

-A\subset-\tilde{A}\subset(\tilde{A})^{*}
by skew-symmetricity of
\tilde{A}
. Taking

B=(\tilde{A})^{*}
, we obtain an
m
-dissipative extension of
-A
in the case
d_{-}=0
. We established the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a contractive semigroup of g.s.
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
of equation (
1.1
) such that the following two cases are possible:

(i) The generator
B
is skew-symmetric. In this case the operators
T_{t}=e^{tB}
are isometric embeddings and the conservation of energy
\|u(t)\|=\|u_{0}\|
holds for all
t>0
;

(ii) the adjoint operator
B^{*}
is skew-symmetric. Then the operators
(T_{t})^{*}
are isometric embeddings.

Concerning the uniqueness of a semigroup of g.s., the following statement holds.

Theorem 3.2
.

A contractive semigroup of g.s. of equations (
1.1
) is unique if and only if the skew-symmetric operator
A
is maximal.

Proof.

Suppose that the skew-symmetric operator
A
is maximal and that
d_{\pm}
are its deficiency indexes. If
u(t)=e^{tB}u_{0}
is a contractive semigroup of g.s. then its generator
B
is
m
-dissipative and
-A\subset B\subset A^{*}
by Theorem
2.1
and Lemma
2.1
. As has been already demonstrated above, one of the operators
-A
,
A^{*}
is
m
-dissipative,
-A
in the case
d_{+}=0
and
A^{*}
if
d_{-}=0
. Since
B
is an
m
-dissipative operator as well while such operators are maximal dissipative operators, we conclude that
B=-A
if
d_{+}=0
, and
B=A^{*}
if
d_{-}=0
(notice that
B=-A=A^{*}
in the case
d_{+}=d_{-}=0
). Thus, the operator
B
is uniquely defined and the corresponding semigroup is also unique.

Conversely, if the operator
A
is not maximal then both its deficiency indexes are not zero. As is easy to realize, then there exist two different maximal skew-symmetric extensions
\tilde{A}_{1}
,
\tilde{A}_{2}
of the operator
A
with the same deficiency indexes
d_{\pm}
. The corresponding
m
-dissipative operators

B_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}-\tilde{A}_{1}&,&d_{+}=0,\\
(\tilde{A}_{1})^{*}&,&d_{-}=0,\end{array}\right.\quad B_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}-\tilde{A}_{2}&,&d_{+}=0,\\
(\tilde{A}_{2})^{*}&,&d_{-}=0\end{array}\right.

are different and generate different contractive semigroups of g.s.
∎

Now we are going to prove uniqueness of g.s. to problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
).

Theorem 3.3
.

A g.s. of the Cauchy problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) is unique if and only if the deficiency index
d_{-}
of skew-symmetric operator
A
equals zero.

Proof.

Suppose that a g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) is unique. Let us show that the deficiency index
d_{-}
of the operator

is null. Assuming the contrary,
d_{-}>0
, we find that the closed subspace
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A)
is a proper subspaces of
H
. Then
\ker(E-A^{*})=(\mathop{\rm Im}(E-A))^{\perp}\not=\{0\}
and therefore there exists a nonzero vector
u_{0}\in D(A^{*})
such that

. As is easy to see, the function
u=e^{t}u_{0}
is a g.s. of problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
), different from the semigroup g.s.

constructed in Theorem

, because the latter is bounded. We also notice that for all

the functions

\tilde{u}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}e^{t}u_{0},&,&0\leq t\leq t_{0},\\

are pairwise different bounded g.s. of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
), and we come to a contradiction even with the condition of uniqueness of a bounded (!) g.s. Hence,

.

Conversely, assume that
d_{-}=0
. Let
u(t)
be a g.s. of (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with null initial data. By the linearity,
it is sufficient to prove that
u(t)\equiv 0
. Since
d_{+}(-A)=d_{-}=0
then, according to the proof of Theorem
3.1
the operator

generates the semigroup

of g.s. to the equation

. Let

and
v(t)=e^{(t_{0}-t)A}v_{0}
,

. Then

and
v^{\prime}(t)=-Av(t)
.
We choose such a function
\beta(s)\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
that
\mathop{\rm supp}\beta(s)\subset[-1,0]
,
\beta(s)\geq 0
,
\displaystyle\int\beta(s)ds=1
, and set for
{\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\beta_{\nu}(s)=\nu\beta(\nu s)
,

\displaystyle\theta_{\nu}(t)=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\beta_{\nu}(s)ds
. It is clear that

\beta_{\nu}(s)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}),\ \mathop{\rm supp}\beta_{\nu}(s)\subset[-1/\nu,0],\ \beta_{\nu}(s)\geq 0,\ \int\beta_{\nu}(s)ds=1.

Therefore, the sequence
\beta_{\nu}(s)
converges as
\nu\to\infty
to the Dirac

\delta
-function in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
while
\theta_{\nu}(t)\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
are decreasing functions,
\theta_{\nu}(t)=1
for
t\leq-1/\nu
,
\theta_{\nu}(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
and the sequence
\theta_{\nu}(t)
converges point-wise as
\nu\to\infty
to the function
\theta(-t)
, where
\displaystyle\theta(s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&s\leq 0,\\
1,&s>0.\end{array}\right.
is the Heaviside function. The function

lies in the space
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},H)\cap C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},D(A))
(we agree that

for

), and by Remark

(2) it is a proper test function in relation (
1.3
) for the g.s.

. Revealing this relation and taking into account the equality

, we arrive at the equality

-\int(u(t),v(t))\beta_{\nu}(t-t_{0})dt=0.

Since the function
(u(t),v(t))
is continuous (this follows from strong continuity of

and weak continuity of

, see Remark

(1)), this equality in the limit as

implies that
(u(t_{0}),v_{0})=(u(t_{0}),v(t_{0}))=0
for each

. By the density of

in

, we conclude that
u(t_{0})=0
. Since

is arbitrary,
u(t)\equiv 0
, as was to be proved.
∎

We can also study the backward Cauchy problem for equation (

We have proved the following statement.

Corollary 3.1
.

Uniqueness of g.s. to both the forward and the backward Cauchy problems for equation (

.

We observe that for g.s.

\forall t>0\quad E(t)\doteq\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq E(0)=\frac{1}{2}\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Theorem 3.4

4.1
A transport equation

Let
a(x)=(a_{1}(x),\ldots,a_{n}(x))\in L^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a solenoidal vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, that is,

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits a(x)=0\ \mbox{ in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

(4.1)

We consider the transport equation (also called the continuity equation)

u_{t}+\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{x}(au)=0,

(4.2)

u=u(t,x)
,
(t,x)\in\Pi=[0,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The notion of g.s.
u(t,x)\in L^{2}_{loc}([0,+\infty),L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))
to the Cauchy problem for equation (
4.2
) with the initial condition

u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

(4.3)

is defined by the standard integral identity (
4.4
) below.

Definition 4.1
.

A function

is called a g.s. of problem (
4.2
), (
4.3
) if for all
f=f(t,x)\in C_{0}^{1}(\Pi)

\int_{\Pi}u[f_{t}+a\cdot\nabla_{x}f]dtdx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u_{0}(x)f(0,x)dx=0.

(4.4)

In (
4.4
) we use the notation
v\cdot w
for the scalar product of vectors
v,w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We introduce the Hilbert space
H=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and unbounded linear operator
A_{0}u=a(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)
on
H
with the domain
D(A_{0})=C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset H
. This operator is skew-symmetric. In fact, for each

\displaystyle(A_{0}u,v)_{H}+(u,A_{0}v)_{H}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a(x)\cdot(v(x)\nabla u(x)+u(x)\nabla v(x))dx=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}a(x)\cdot\nabla(u(x)v(x))dx=0

by solenoidality condition (
4.1
). Analyzing Definitions
1.1
,
4.1
, we find that the notion of g.s. of the problem (
4.2
), (
4.3
) is consistent with the theory of g.s. to the abstract problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
).

We are going to demonstrate that in the case of Lipschitz coefficients
a_{i}(x)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, the closure
A
of the operator
A_{0}
is a skew-adjoint operator. Thus, we assume that for some constant
m>0

|a(x)-a(y)|\leq m|x-y|\ \forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(4.5)

Here and everywhere below we use the notation
|z|
for the
Euclidean norm of a finite-dimensional vector
z
(including the modulus of a number).
By the Lipschitz condition (
4.5
) the generalized derivatives

(a_{i})_{x_{j}}(x)\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.
In this case we can solve the problem (
4.2
), (
4.3
) by the classical method of characteristics.
Recall that characteristics of equation (
4.2
) are integral curves
(t,x(t))
of the characteristic system of ODE

\dot{x}=a(x).

(4.6)

In view of Lipschitz condition there exists a unique solution to a Cauchy problem for system (
4.6
). Besides, it also follows from the Lipschitz condition that the vector
a(x)
may have at most linear growth at infinity:

|a(x)|\leq c(1+|x|),\quad c={\rm const}

(4.7)

Therefore, solutions
x(t)
of system (
4.6
) cannot reach infinity for finite time and therefore they are defined for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote by
x(t;t_{0},x_{0})
the unique solution of system (

) satisfying the condition

, and set
y(t_{0},x_{0})=x(0;t_{0},x_{0})
. From the relation

it follows the equality
u(t,x)=u_{0}(y(t,x))
. By the Liouville theorem the diffeomorphisms

keep the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which implies that the linear operators
T_{t}(u_{0})=u_{0}(y(t,x))
are orthogonal in

. It is also clear that these operators satisfy the group property
T_{t+s}=T_{t}T_{s}
,

, and the continuity condition
T_{t}u_{0}\to u_{0}
in

as

. Hence,
\{T_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is a
C_{0}
-group of orthogonal operators, and by the Stone theorem, (cf.
[
8
, Chapter IX]
,
[
9
, Theorem 4.7]
)

, where the infinitesimal generator

is a skew-adjoint operator. We will show that actually

.

We will need some estimates of solutions to the stationary equation

\phi+ha\cdot\nabla\phi=\psi

(4.8)

with
h>0
. Let
M(x)=\|Da(x)\|
be the operator norm of the
n\times n
matrix
Da(x)=\left(\frac{\partial a_{i}(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
. It readily follows from condition (
4.5
) that
M(x)\leq m
. The following statements hold.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\psi(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
0<h<1/m
. Then there exists a solution
\phi(x)\in W_{\infty}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
of equation (
4.8
) such that

|\phi(x)|\leq c_{1}(1+|x|)^{-\alpha/h},\quad|\nabla\phi(x)|\leq c_{2}(1+|x|)^{-\alpha(1/h-m)},

(4.9)

c_{1},c_{2},\alpha
are positive constants, herewith
\alpha=1/c
, where
c>0
is the constant from condition (
4.7
).

Proof.

On a characteristic
x=x(t)
equation (
4.8
) turns to ODE
\phi+h\phi^{\prime}=\psi
where
\phi=\phi(x(t))
,
\psi=\psi(x(t))
. A particular solution of this equation is given by the expression

\phi(t)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{(s-t)/h}\psi(x(s))ds.

Taking
x(s)=x(s;t,y)
, we arrive at the representation

\phi(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{(s-t)/h}\psi(x(s;t,y))ds=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{(s-t)/h}\psi(x(s-t;0,y))ds.

Making the change
s-t\to s
, we obtain the equality

\phi(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}\psi(x(s;0,y))ds.

(4.10)

Since the function
\psi(x)
is bounded and continuous, the integral in (
4.10
) converges uniformly with respect to
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, consequently,
\phi(y)
is continuous. We choose such
r>0
that the support
\mathop{\rm supp}\psi(x)
lies in the ball
|x|\leq r
. Let
|y|>r
and
x(s)=x(s;0,y)
. Since
x^{\prime}(s)=a(x(s))
then, with the help of (
4.7
), we obtain that

\frac{d}{ds}|x(s)|\leq|a(x(s))|\leq c(1+|x(s)|).

Therefore,
\frac{d}{ds}\ln(1+|x(s)|)\leq c
and after integration on
[s,0]
we obtain the inequality

\ln\left(\frac{1+|y|}{1+|x(s)|}\right)\leq c|s|=-cs.

It follows from this inequality that
|x(s)|>r
for
s>s(y)\doteq-\frac{1}{c}\ln((1+|y|)/(1+r))
and therefore
\psi(x(s))=0
. Then, by representation (
4.10
)

\displaystyle|\phi(y)|=\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{s(y)}e^{s/h}\psi(x(s;0,y))ds\right|\leq\frac{\|\psi\|_{\infty}}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{s(y)}e^{s/h}ds=

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\infty}e^{s(y)/h}=c_{1}(1+|y|)^{-\alpha/h},

where
\alpha=1/c
,
c_{1}=\|\psi\|_{\infty}(1+r)^{\alpha/h}
. The obtained inequality remains true for
|y|\leq r
as well, because for such
y

|\phi(y)|\leq\frac{\|\psi\|_{\infty}}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}ds=\\
\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq c_{1}(1+|y|)^{-\alpha/h}.

The first of estimates (
4.9
) is proved.

Then we notice that for
y_{1},y_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
s<0

\displaystyle|x(s;0,y_{2})-x(s;0,y_{1})|\leq|y_{2}-y_{1}|+\int_{s}^{0}|x^{\prime}(t;0,y_{2})-x^{\prime}(t;0,y_{1})|dt=

\displaystyle|y_{2}-y_{1}|+\int_{s}^{0}|a(x(t;0,y_{2}))-a(x(t;0,y_{1}))|dt\leq

\displaystyle|y_{2}-y_{1}|+m\int_{s}^{0}|x(t;0,y_{2})-x(t;0,y_{1})|dt.

By Grönwall’s lemma we derive from this relation the estimate
|x(s;0,y_{2})-x(s;0,y_{1})|\leq|y_{2}-y_{1}|e^{m|s|}
.
We find that the map
y\to x(s;0,y)
is Lipschitz with the constant
e^{m|s|}
. Therefore, its generalized derivatives are bounded and the operator norm of the Jacobian matrix

X(s)=D_{y}x(s;0,y)
is bounded by
e^{m|s|}
:
\|X(s)\|\leq e^{m|s|}
. It follows from the equality
\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))=X(s)^{\top}\nabla_{x}\psi(x(s;0,y))
(valid for almost each
(s,y)
) that

|\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))|\leq\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}e^{m|s|}.

(4.11)

Representation (
4.10
) implies that for a.e.
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\nabla\phi(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))ds.

(4.12)

By estimate (
4.11
) for
0<h<1/m
the integral in the right-hand side of this equality converges uniformly with respect to
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. As we have already established, for
0>s>s(y)=-\frac{1}{c}\ln((1+|y|)/(1+r))
(if
|y|\leq r
we set

s(y)=0
) the function
\psi(x(s;0,y))
equals zero, consequently its gradient
\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))
is zero as well. Therefore,

\nabla\phi(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{s(y)}e^{s/h}\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))ds,

which implies the estimate

\displaystyle|\nabla\phi(y)|\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{s(y)}e^{s/h}|\nabla_{y}\psi(x(s;0,y))|ds\leq\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}h^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{s(y)}e^{(1/h-m)s}ds=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}}{1-mh}e^{(1/h-m)s(y)}=c_{2}(1+|y|)^{-\alpha(1/h-m)},

where, as before,
\alpha=1/c
, and
c_{2}=\frac{\|\nabla\psi\|_{\infty}}{1-mh}(1+r)^{\alpha(1/h-m)}
. Hence, we derive the second estimate in (

) (after replacing

by

).
The proof is complete
∎

We need also the fact that finite functions from the space

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
u(x)\in W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be a function with compact support. Then

and
(Au)(x)=a(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)
.

Proof.

We take a sequence
u_{k}(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that

as

in
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and that

are contained in some common ball

. For instance we can take the sequence of averaged functions

with a kernel
\rho(z)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
\rho(z)\geq 0
,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\rho(z)dz=1
. Then

and as

u_{k}\to u,\quad A_{0}u_{k}=a(x)\cdot\nabla u_{k}(x)\to a(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)\ \mbox{ in }H,

where we take into account that

in the ball

). Since

is a closure of the operator

we conclude that

and

. The proof is complete.

Now we are ready to prove that

is a skew-adjoint operator.

Proposition 4.1
.

The operator
A
is skew-adjoint.

Proof.

Let
h>0
be so small that
\alpha(1/h-m)>1+n/2
and
\psi(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. By Lemma
4.1
there exists a function
\phi(x)\in W_{\infty}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, which solves equation (
4.8
) and satisfies estimates (
4.9
).
Let us show that
\phi(x)\in D(A)
and that
\phi+hA\phi=\psi
.
First of all we remark that
\alpha/h>1+n/2
and it follows from (
4.9
) and (
4.7
) that
\phi(x),|a(x)|\phi(x)\in H=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. We choose such a function
\rho(y)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
that
\rho(y)\geq 0
and
\rho(0)=1
. By Lemma
4.2
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
the functions
u_{k}\doteq\phi(x)\rho(x/k)\in D(A)
and
Au_{k}=a(x)\cdot\nabla u_{k}(x)
. Obviously,
u_{k}\to\phi
in
H
as
k\to\infty
.
Further, the functions

v_{k}\doteq Au_{k}=(a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x))\rho(x/k)+k^{-1}\phi(x)a(x)\nabla_{y}\rho(x/k).

(4.13)

Since
a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)=(\psi(x)-\phi(x))/h\in H
then

(a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x))\rho(x/k)\mathop{\to}_{k\to\infty}a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)

a.e. in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and therefore in
H
. Using that

|\phi(x)a(x)\nabla_{y}\rho(x/k)|\leq\|\nabla\rho\|_{\infty}|a(x)||\phi(x)|\in H,

we find that second term in (
4.13
) converges to zero in
H
as
k\to\infty
. Thus, we established that in the limit as
k\to\infty

u_{k}\to\phi
,
v_{k}\to a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)
in
H
. Since the operator
A
is closed, we conclude that

\phi\in D(A)
and
A\phi(x)=a(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)=(\psi(x)-\phi(x))/h
. In particular,
\phi+hA\phi=\psi
and therefore
\psi\in\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)
. But
\psi(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is arbitrary and we see that
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\subset\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)
(notice also that the constant
\alpha=1/c
and the above choice of the parameter

does not depend on
\psi
). Remind that the subspace
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)
is closed in
H
and since
C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is dense in
H
we obtain that
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)=H
. By skew-symmetricity of
A
the property
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)=H
holds for all
h>0
. Similarly we prove that
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-hA)=H
(for that we can just repeat our arguments replacing the field
a(x)
by
-a(x)
).
Thus, the deficiency indexes of the operator
A
are both zero, that is, this operator is skew-adjoint. The proof is complete.
∎

), (
4.3
) (both forward and backward) was also established in the case when the coefficients have Sobolev regularity in the paper by R.J. DiPerna and P.L. Lions
[
7
]
, later these results were extended to more general case of BV coefficients, see paper
[
2
]
. According to Corollary
3.1
in these cases the operator
A
is skew-adjoint. But, in general case there are numerous examples of non-uniqueness of g.s. to problem (
4.2
), (
4.3
), see for instance
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
11
]
, so that the operator
A
may fail to be skew-adjoint.

Notice that for a bounded vector of coefficients
a(x)
the statement of Corollary
3.1
, applied to problem (
4.2
), (
4.3
), follows from results of
[
3
]
, see also
[
12
,
13
]
.

4.2
Linearized Euler system

We consider the linearized Euler system with the same solenoidal vector of coefficients
a(x)\in L^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
as in the previous section
4.1
:

u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}(x)u)_{x_{j}}+\nabla p=0,\quad\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{x}u=0,

(4.14)

where
(t,x)\in\Pi
,
u=(u^{1}(t,x),\ldots,u^{n}(t,x))\in L^{\infty}_{loc}([0,+\infty),H)
,
H\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a Hilbert space of solenoidal vector fields in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
p=p(t,x)
is a pressure. We study the Cauchy problem with the initial condition

u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in H.

(4.15)

Definition 4.2
.

A vector
u=u(t,x)\in L^{\infty}_{loc}([0,+\infty),H)
is called a g.s. of problem (
4.14
), (
4.15
) if for each test
vector-function
f=f(t,x)\in C_{0}^{1}(\Pi,{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits_{x}f=0

\int_{\Pi}[u\cdot f_{t}+u\cdot(a\cdot\nabla_{x})f]dtdx+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u_{0}(x)\cdot f(0,x)dx=0.

The choice of solenoidal test vectors allows to exclude the pressure
p=p(t,x)
from the system.
Definition
4.2
is consistent with Definition
1.1
if we define the operator
A_{0}
by the equality
A_{0}u=Pv
, where
u\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H=D(A_{0})
,

\displaystyle v=v(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)u_{x_{j}}(x)
while
P:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\to H
is an orthogonal projector of the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
onto its closed linear subspace
H
. If
u_{1}=(u_{1}^{k})_{k=1}^{n},u_{2}=(u_{2}^{k})_{k=1}^{n}\in D(A_{0})
then

\displaystyle(A_{0}u_{1},u_{2})+(u_{1},A_{0}u_{2})=(Pv_{1},u_{2})+(u_{1},Pv_{2})=(v_{1},u_{2})+(u_{1},v_{2})=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)((u_{1}^{k})_{x_{j}}u_{2}^{k}+u_{1}^{k}(u_{2}^{k})_{x_{j}})(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)(u_{1}^{k}u_{2}^{k})_{x_{j}}dx=0

by condition (
4.1
). Thus, the operator
A_{0}
is skew-symmetric.

We are going to show that in the case of Lipschitz coefficients
a_{j}(x)
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, the operator
A_{0}
admits a skew-adjoint extension. For that we need to restore the term
\nabla p
from the equality

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}u)_{x_{j}}+\nabla p\right)=0.

Taking into account that
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u=0
for
u=(u^{1},\ldots,u^{n})\in H
, we obtain the relation

\sum_{k,j=1}^{n}((a_{j})_{x_{k}}(x)u^{k})_{x_{j}}+\Delta p=0,

understood in the sense of distributions. Applying the Fourier transform, we arrive at the equality

\tilde{p}(\xi)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}\right)(\xi)\frac{i\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{2}}.

(4.16)

Here
i
is the imaginary unit,
\tilde{u}(\xi)={\mathcal{F}}(u)(\xi)
is the Fourier transform of a function (more generally, a distribution)
u(x)
, which is defined for
u\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by the standard equality

\tilde{u}(\xi)={\mathcal{F}}(u)(\xi)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}u(x)dx.

Then we find
{\mathcal{F}}(\nabla p)(\xi)=i\xi\tilde{p}(\xi)
and it follows from (
4.16
) that

{\mathcal{F}}(\nabla p)^{l}(\xi)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}\right)(\xi)\frac{\xi_{l}\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{2}}.

(4.17)

Since the functions
(a_{j})_{x_{k}}(x)
,
\xi_{l}\xi_{j}/|\xi|^{2}
are bounded while the Fourier transform is an unitary linear operator in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we conclude that
\nabla p=Tu
is a bounded linear operator in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. By the construction
A_{0}u=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(a_{j}(x)u)_{x_{j}}+Tu
for
u(x)\in D(A_{0})\subset H
. Let us define the operator
B_{0}
in

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
according to the equalities
(B_{0}u)^{l}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)u^{l}_{x_{j}}
,
l=1,\ldots,n
, with the domain
D(B_{0})=C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The operator
B_{0}
is a direct sum of
n
scalar transport operators considered in the previous section. By Proposition
4.1
the closure
B
of operator
B_{0}
is a skew-adjoint operator. The operator
\tilde{A}_{0}=B_{0}+T
is defined on
D(B_{0})
and it is an extension of the operator
A_{0}
. Since
T
is a bounded operator, the closure
\tilde{A}
of the operator
\tilde{A}_{0}
coincides with
B+T
.
Recall that the operator
B
is skew-adjoint, and therefore
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-hB)=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
\tilde{A}=B+T
is a bounded perturbation of
B
, then this property remains valid for the operator
\tilde{A}
,
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-h\tilde{A})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
if
|h|
is sufficiency small.

Our next step is to demonstrate that the space
H
is invariant for the resolvent
(E-h\tilde{A})^{-1}
, where
|h|<h_{0}
is small enough.
Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
h>0
. The case of a negative
h
is treated using the change

a(x)
by
-a(x)
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
u\in D(\tilde{A})
,
u-h\tilde{A}u=v\in H
, and
h>0
be small enough. Then
u\in H
, i.e.,
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u=0
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
\psi(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and
\phi(x)\in W_{\infty}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be the function constructed in Lemma
4.1
. We chose
h>0
so small that
\alpha(1/h-m)>1+n/2
. Then it follows from estimates (
4.7
), (
4.9
) that
|\nabla\phi(x)|\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
|a(x)||\nabla\phi(x)|\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Multiplying the equality
u-h\tilde{A}u=v
by the potential vector
\nabla\phi
, we obtain the equality

(u,\nabla\phi)-h(\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=(v,\nabla\phi)=0.

Remark that, by Parseval’s identity,

\displaystyle(Tu,\nabla\phi)=({\mathcal{F}}(Tu),{\mathcal{F}}(\nabla\phi))=

\displaystyle-\int\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}\right)(\xi)\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\xi_{l}\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{2}}\overline{i\xi_{l}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi)(\xi)}d\xi=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}\right)(\xi)\overline{i\xi_{j}{\mathcal{F}}(\phi)(\xi)}d\xi=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}(x)u^{k}\phi_{x_{j}}(x)dx.

Then, for
u\in D(\tilde{A}_{0})=D(B_{0})

\displaystyle(Bu,\nabla\phi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)u^{k}_{x_{j}}(x)\phi_{x_{k}}(x)dx=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(a_{j}(x)u^{k})_{x_{j}}(x)\phi_{x_{k}}(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}(a_{j}(x)u^{k})_{x_{k}}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x)dx.

By the above relations we find the equality

\displaystyle(\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=(Bu,\nabla\phi)+(Tu,\nabla\phi)=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)(u^{k})_{x_{k}}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x)dx.

(4.18)

Thus, for all
u\in D(\tilde{A}_{0})

\displaystyle(u-h\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u(x)\cdot\nabla\phi(x)dx-h(\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)(\phi+h\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x))dx=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)\psi(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u(x)\cdot\nabla\psi(x)dx=(u,\nabla\psi).

Since
D(\tilde{A}_{0})
is dense in
D(\tilde{A})
in the graph norm of
\tilde{A}
then the relation

(u-h\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=(u,\nabla\psi)

can be extended by continuity to the case
u\in D(\tilde{A})
. By the assumption
u-h\tilde{A}u=v\in H
and therefore

(u-h\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=0
. Then
(u,\nabla\psi)=0
. But
\psi(x)\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is arbitrary, and we conclude that
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)=0
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, that is,
u\in H
. The proof is complete.
∎

It follows from (
4.2
) that for
u\in D(A_{0})=D(B_{0})
,
\phi\in C_{0}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

\displaystyle(\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x)dx=

\displaystyle-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}u(x)\cdot\nabla\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)\phi_{x_{j}}(x)\right)dx

(4.19)

and since
D(\tilde{A}_{0})
is dense in
D(\tilde{A})
in the graph norm of
\tilde{A}
then equality (
4.2
) remains fulfilled for
u\in D(\tilde{A})
. If
u\in H\cap D(\tilde{A})
then
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits u(x)=0
and it follows from (
4.2
) that
(\tilde{A}u,\nabla\phi)=0
for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, that is,
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\tilde{A}u=0
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Hence, the subspace
H
is invariant for
\tilde{A}
. By the construction vectors
Tu
are potential, this implies that
Tu\perp H
and
PTu=0
. Therefore,
\tilde{A}u=PBu+PTu=PBu
for
u\in D(\tilde{A})\cap H
. This and skew-symmetricity of the operator
B
easily imply that the restriction
\tilde{A}|_{H}
is a skew-symmetric operator in
H
.
Since
\mathop{\rm Im}(E-h\tilde{A})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
for sufficiently small
h>0
then for each
v\in H
there exists such
u\in D(\tilde{A})
that
u-h\tilde{A}u=v
. By Lemma
4.3
we find that
u\in H\cap D(\tilde{A})
. Hence,

\mathop{\rm Im}(E-h\tilde{A}|_{H})=H
. The similar statement holds after replacement
h
by
-h
.
Thus, the skew-symmetric operator
\tilde{A}|_{H}
has null deficiency indexes and therefore it is skew-adjoint.
Since
\tilde{A}_{0}|_{H}=A_{0}
then
\tilde{A}|_{H}
is a skew-adjoint extension of the operator
A
because it is the closure of
A_{0}
. We have proven the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

The operator
A
admits a skew-adjoint extension. In particular, deficiency indexes
d_{+}
and
d_{-}
of
A
coincide.

To prove skew-adjointness of the operator
A
itself, we need additional assumptions on the coefficients

a_{j}(x)
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. Namely, we suppose that
a_{j}(x)\in W_{\infty}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, i.e.,
a_{j}(x)
are bounded together with all generalized derivatives up to the second order. In other words,
a_{j}(x)
are bounded Lipschitz functions with Lipschitz first derivatives.

Proposition 4.3
.

Under the assumption
a_{j}(x)\in W_{\infty}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, the operator
A
is skew-adjoint.

Proof.

If
u(x)+hBu(x)=v(x)\in W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
0<h<1/m
(we remind that
m
is the Lipschitz constant of the vector
a(x)
) then due to representations (
4.10
), (
4.12
) we obtain the equalities

u(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}v(x(s;0,y))ds,\quad\nabla_{y}u(y)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}\nabla_{y}v(x(s;0,y))ds,

which imply the estimates (recall that
|z|
denotes the Euclidean norm of a vector or a matrix)

\displaystyle|u(y)|\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}|v(x(s;0,y))|ds,

(4.20)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{y}u(y)|=\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}|\nabla_{y}v(x(s;0,y))|ds\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1/h-m)s}|\nabla_{x}v|(x(s;0,y))ds,

(4.21)

because

\displaystyle|\nabla_{y}v(x(s;0,y))|=|(\nabla_{x}v)(x(s;0,y))D_{y}x(s;0,y)|\leq

\displaystyle\|D_{y}x(s;0,y)\||(\nabla_{x}v)(x(s;0,y))|\leq e^{m|s|}|(\nabla_{x}v)(x(s;0,y))|

due to the estimate
\|D_{y}x(s;0,y)\|\leq e^{m|s|}
for the operator norm of the Jacobian matrix

D_{y}x(s;0,y)
, derived in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Considering that the map
y\to x(s;0,y)
keeps the Lebesgue measure for each fixed
s<0
, we obtain the equalities

\|v(x(s;0,y))\|_{2}=\|v(x)\|_{2}
,
\|(\nabla_{x}v)(x(s;0,y))\|_{L^{2}(dy)}=\|\nabla_{x}v(x)\|_{2}
, and it follows from (
4.20
), (
4.21
) that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}\|v(x(s;0,y))\|_{2}ds=\frac{\|v\|_{2}}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{s/h}ds=\|v\|_{2},

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1/h-m)s}\|(\nabla_{x}v)(x(s;0,y))\|_{2}ds=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla v\|_{2}}{h}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(1/h-m)s}ds=\frac{1}{1-mh}\|\nabla v\|_{2}.

Thus,
u=(E+hB)^{-1}v\in W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and the norm of the resolvent
(E+hB)^{-1}
, as an operator in
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
does not exceed
(1-mh)^{-1}
. Further, as follows from the condition
a_{j}\in W_{\infty}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the operators
u\to\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}
are bounded in
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. This implies boundedness of the operator
Tu
in
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, because
{\mathcal{F}}(Tu)(\xi)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{j})_{x_{k}}u^{k}\right)(\xi)\frac{\xi_{l}\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{2}}
.
Therefore, for small enough
h>0
the operator
E+h\tilde{A}=E+hB+hT=(E+hT(E+hB)^{-1})(E+hB)
has a bounded inverse operator and, in particular, it is surjective. If
v(x)\in W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H
then, using Lemma
4.3
, we have
u(x)=(E+h\tilde{A})^{-1}v\in W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H
. Let us show that
u\in D(A)
. It is known, see for example
[
10
]
, that the space of smooth solenoidal vectors
D(A_{0})=C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H
is dense in
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H
. Hence, there exists a sequence
u_{m}\in D(A_{0})
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that the sequences
u_{m}
,
(u_{m})_{x_{j}}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, converge as
m\to\infty
to
u(x)
and respectively to
u_{x_{j}}
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. By the assumption
a_{j}(x)\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
we find that
A(u_{m})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(x)(u_{m})_{x_{j}}(x)+Tu_{m}\to Bu+Tu=\tilde{A}u
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Since the operator
A
is closed, we have
u\in D(A)
and
Au=\tilde{A}u
. We conclude that
u+hAu=v
and therefore the range of the operator
E+hA
contains the space
W_{2}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cap H
dense in
H
. By the closedness of this range we find that
\mathop{\rm Im}(E+hA)=H
. The similar statement holds for
h<0
as well (in particular, this follows from the equality
d_{-}=d_{+}
, stated in Proposition
4.2
). Hence, the operator,
A
is skew-adjoint, as was to be proved.
∎
