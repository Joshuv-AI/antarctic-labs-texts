Shielding the Hägg carbide by a graphene layer for ultrahigh carbon efficiency during syngas conversion
Authors: Xueqing Zhang, Zhe Li, Wei Sun, Yuhua Zhang, Jinlin Li, Li Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe advocacy for green ecology has sparked keen interest in converting nonpetroleum carbon resources into value-added products with high carbon efficiency. We have introduced a practical approach by utilizing graphene as a robust stabilizer to preserve the active phase of Hägg carbide while simultaneously reducing the production of unwanted CO2 and CH4. This has led to a substantial increase in carbon efficiency, from 50% to around 90%. The controllable Hägg carbide, shielded by a graphene layer, offers a promising avenue for achieving highly efficient regulation of carbon transfer pathways.

The depletion of crude oil and increasing environmental concerns have sparked a worldwide interest in converting nonpetroleum carbon resources, such as natural gas, coal, and biomass, into clean fuels and high-value chemicals. Fischer-Tropsch (FT) synthesis serves as a vital technique for transforming these resources into various products, including olefins, gasoline, and diesel (1, 2). Iron-based FT synthesis catalysts are widely used in industrial coal-to-liquids, gas-to-liquid, and biomass-to-liquid plants. These catalysts are employed in diverse reactor configurations, including circulating fluidized-bed reactor, fluidized-bed reactor, fixed-bed reactor, and slurry-phase reactor (1). Notably, extensively studies and industrialized iron-based catalysts continue to garner significant attention due to their cost-effectiveness, adaptable operational conditions, and versatile product outputs (3–5).

However, the iron-based catalysts exhibit imperfect selectivity of C1 products, as observed in the water gas shift (WGS) reaction and methanation reaction, respectively (6–8). This leads to increased CO2 emissions and inefficient utilization of carbon/hydrogen resources. Therefore, a primary challenge associated with iron-based FT catalysts lies in effectively suppressing the formation of excessive C1 by-products, namely CO2 and CH4.

Numerous fundamental research endeavors have focused on diminishing the production of CO2 by-products through the hydrophobic medication of catalysts (Fig. 1). Ding et al. (9) devised a core-shell FeMn@Si catalyst, exhibiting exceptional hydrophobic properties to curtail the generation of CO2 and CH4 (C1 by-products selectivity ~21.2% at CO conversion of 31%). Meanwhile, Xiao et al. (10) shifted the water-sorption equilibrium on the catalyst surface by a hydrophobic additive of polydivinylbenzene. The result was a significant improvement in catalytic performance and concurrently reducing CO2 (CO2 selectivity of 23.7% and CH4 selectivity of 16.7% at a CO conversion of 61.7%). Common strategies generally involve the hydrophobic modification of the active site while keeping the active site structure unchanged. Additionally, some studies have identified adjusting the active phase of Fe and preventing the WGS reaction as key strategies for reducing CH4 (11, 12) and CO2 (13, 14), respectively. Iron catalysts typically reduce methane selectivity through the incorporation of alkali metal promoters (e.g., potassium) (15, 16) and transition metal promoters (e.g., manganese, zinc) (17–19).

However, the active sites of iron-based FT catalysts undergo surface reconstruction, which can lead to reduced activity and increased selectivity toward C1 by-products. Additionally, during the activation process or under FT synthesis reaction conditions, an amorphous carbon layer has been observed to form on iron carbides (25, 26). Unfortunately, this carbon layer may continue to grow, and the phase of Fe evolves during prolonged reaction periods, ultimately leading to catalyst deactivation (3).

Compared to SiO2 (9, 27) and polydivinylbenzene (10), graphene, identified as an ideal hydrophobic shielding domain (28), may also serve as the perfect candidate to stabilize active sites and minimize selectivity toward CO2. A graphene layer and stabilized iron species could be fabricated via catalyst synthesis, by employing a graphene-layer-enveloped Hägg carbide (χ-Fe5C2). The graphene layer could serve to protect the iron species from oxidation or phase transition and to limit the extent of WGS reaction, thereby minimizing CH4 and CO2 formation. Given the demonstrated hydrophobic properties (29–31) and the protective effect (32, 33), a controllable Hägg carbide with a graphene layer could offer an effective approach to achieving ultrahigh carbon efficiency by minimizing CH4 and CO2 formation during syngas conversion.

Herein, we successfully synthesized the χ-Fe5C2@Graphene catalyst via a pyrolysis method employing iron phthalocyanine as the precursor. When the graphene layer completely enveloped the Hägg carbide, the selectivities of CO2 and CH4 were significantly limited to 4.6% and 5.9%, respectively, resulting in low CO2 production and an increased carbon efficiency (Fig. 1). Remarkably, the graphene layer serves as an effective stabilizer for the Hägg carbide active phase, maintaining its active phase for long period of reaction. Additionally, the layer impeded WGS reaction to reduce CO2 and CH4 formation.

Results

Following the synthesis of Fe@Graphene as we have demonstrated previously (20), three catalysts (5Fe@-I, 5Fe@-II, and 5Fe@-III) were fabricated with 5 wt.% loading of Fe from different temperature of calcination (1,023 K, 1,373 K, and 1,573 K, respectively) (34, 35). The structure of Fe was verified by XRD (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and 57Fe Mössbauer characterizations to confirm the formation of χ-Fe5C2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) (21, 36–38). As measured by XRD, these 5Fe@C catalysts displayed a diffraction peak around ~43°, indicating the presence of χ-Fe5C2 (PDF#20-0508) phase, which is further supported by the 57Fe Mössbauer characterizations. 57Fe Mössbauer spectra (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Table S1) of 5Fe@C-I and 5Fe@C-II catalysts provided further evidence for the formation of χ-Fe5C2. Thus, the designed Fe@Graphene catalysts were successfully fabricated via the pyrolysis method and the active phase, χ-Fe5C2, were formed.

Then, the catalytic performance of the Fe@Graphene catalysts was investigated under operating conditions of 543 K and 1 MPa, utilizing a gas composition of 50% H2 and 50% CO. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, decent conversions and low selectivity of CH4 (<10%) were observed from the 5Fe@C-II and 5Fe@C-III catalysts. Notably, the 5Fe@C-II catalyst, which was synthesized from medium temperature (1,373 K), demonstrates higher activity and lower selectivity of CO2 compared to the other two catalysts. The selectivity is measured at 6.3%, significantly lower compared to 5Fe@C-I (20.7%) and 5Fe@C-III (14.3%), please see details in SI Appendix, Table S2.

For comparison, we also synthesized a conventional Fe catalyst, 5Fe/AC (activated carbon), by employing activated carbon through the incipient wetness impregnation method with similar metal loading. We proceeded to assess its performance in FT synthesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 and Table S2). At 563 K, the catalyst achieved a comparable CO conversion (6.7%) while exhibiting CO2 selectivity of 20.7% and CH4 selectivity of 10.1%, which is similar to 5Fe@C-I but much higher than 5Fe@C-II and -III catalysts even at lower conversion of CO. These findings demonstrate a significantly superior catalytic performance of 5Fe@C-II catalyst compared to the reported iron-based catalysts (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Table S3), showcasing improvements in the selectivity toward reducing CO2.

For 5Fe@C-II catalyst, as the reaction temperatures rise, both the CO conversion and CO2 selectivity exhibit a simultaneous increase. As depicted in Fig. 2B, the CO conversion increased to 15.3% at 533 K, accompanied by a 5.3% CO2 selectivity. Over the 180-h stability test (Fig. 2C), the CO conversion remained consistent. Throughout this duration, the selectivities toward CO2 and CH4 was notably limited to approximately 5 ~ 6%, respectively, while the CO conversion stabilized around 17%.

Following the reaction, we collected 3.64 g of water, 1.50 g of oil, and 0.75 g of wax from both the cold trap and hot trap. As depicted in Fig. 2D, for carbon numbers exceeding 3, the logarithm of Wn/n against the carbon number (n) exhibited a linear correlation in the case of the 5Fe@C-II catalyst. The α value was calculated within carbon number ranging from 3 to 42, resulting in a substantial α value of 0.81, indicating a high probability toward long-chain hydrocarbon formation. It is noteworthy that this catalyst also displayed a significant selectivity toward alcohols, reaching approximately 15%. Alcohols are considered high value–added products in FT synthesis, formed through the insertion of associated CO into the growing hydrocarbon chain (39).

To understand the super selectivity toward CO2 on the 5Fe@C-II catalyst, comprehensive analysis has been carried out. Although the XRD and 57Fe Mössbauer spectra confirmed the formation of χ-Fe5C2 in the catalysts, the diffraction peak observed at 26.2° from XRD corresponds to the (002) crystal plane of graphitic carbon (PDF#75-1621), showing increased definition with higher calcination temperatures, indicating enhanced graphitization (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Additionally, the diffraction peak at 35.8° for the 5Fe@C-III catalyst corresponds to the Fe3O4 (PDF#75-0449) phase, known to facilitate the WGS reaction. The unfinished graphitization process and the formation of Fe3O4 might explain the relatively poorer performance of 5Fe@C-I and -III catalysts, respectively.

The transmission electron microscope (TEM/HRTEM) images and elemental mappings provide detailed insights for the catalyst structure (Fig. 3 A–C and SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S6). Before calcination, the fresh materials exhibit a high dispersion of N, O, C, and Fe elements without the presence of iron nanoparticles. The surface nitrogen is derived from urea, the precursor employed, and is incorporated into the fresh materials. After calcination at 1,023 K, the 5Fe@C-I catalyst notably lacks graphitic carbon lattice fringes, indicating an absence of a formed graphite layer around the iron nanoparticles. Conversely, the 5Fe@C-II catalyst displays a core–shell structure. Here, the graphene carbon layer tightly enveloped χ-Fe5C2 nanoparticles, exhibiting high dispersion without any observable agglomeration. Additionally, the crystal lattice fringes of the graphene carbon are clearly visible. The wrapping effect of the graphene layer contributes to its hydrophobic nature, thereby impeding the WGS reaction and consequently reducing the selectivities toward CO2 and methane. The images from the 5Fe@C-III catalyst which was synthesized from higher temperature showed numerous hollow structures in its carbon matrix. The N2 physical adsorption and Raman analysis further supported the graphene and hollow structure formation of the catalysts (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8 and Table S4). This occurrence stems from the iron nanoparticles disassociating from the graphene layer and aggregating during high-temperature calcination. This leads to the formation of iron particles adopting a semisurrounding structure within the carbon matrix.

The differences in the formation and the covering effect of graphene layer were further confirmed by XPS analysis (Fig. 3 D and E and SI Appendix, Table S5). The disappearance of N supported the graphitization process during the temperature increment (40, 41). Remarkably, the surface of the 5Fe@C-II catalyst exhibits no detectable Fe, potentially due to the graphene layer enveloping iron carbide or iron nanoparticles, forming a complete core-shell structure that conceals Fe-related peaks on the surface. As the calcination temperature increases to a certain level, the carbon layer of 5Fe@C-III catalyst undergoes damage, and Fe particles are re-exposed on the catalyst’s surface, consistent with the TEM findings.

Comparing to the optimal catalyst, 5Fe@C-II, the one from lower temperature of calcination was generated with less graphene layer and lower proportion of χ-Fe5C2, and the one from higher temperature would be formed with broken graphene layers. It was hypothesized that the well-enveloped χ-Fe5C2 structure could consistently convert syngas into C2+ hydrocarbons with high selectivity and good efficiency, while the imperfect or broken graphene layer would lead to the oxidation of χ-Fe5C2 under reaction condition to form Fe3O4. The Fe3O4 would serve as the major phase for WGS reaction to generate CO2.

Thus, the spent catalysts were carefully investigated. The XRD (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) and 57Fe Mössbauer (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Table S6) results suggest a gradual disappearance of the iron carbide phase within the 5Fe@C-I catalyst and a dramatic formation of Fe3O4 within the 5Fe@C-I catalyst. This transformation indicates that the χ-Fe5C2 active phase likely converted into the iron oxides during the FT synthesis without the protection of the graphene layer (33, 42). This phase transformation adversely affects the FT synthesis reaction, resulting in significantly lower activity and higher CO2 emission. Interestingly, the 5Fe@C-II catalyst effectively hinders the oxidation process by the protective nature of the graphene layer surrounding the χ-Fe5C2 active phase. This encapsulation endows the χ-Fe5C2@C catalyst with exceptional stability under reaction conditions and exposure to air, eliminating the need for reduction or activation prior to the FTS reaction. The confinement effect of the few graphene layers at the interfaces facilitates CO adsorption/dissociation and hydrocarbon chain growth polymerization (20).

Moreover, due to the presence of N-containing functional groups, the 5Fe@C-II catalyst exhibits a strong CO dissociation capability (43), which effectively suppresses methane formation and enhances C–C coupling, leading to a high C5+ selectivity of 87.1% (SI Appendix, Table S2).

Since the naked Hägg carbide phase for FT synthesis is prone to oxidation upon exposure to air especially (21, 44–46), the fresh catalysts were tested by H2-TPR and WGS reactions to confirm the protection effect from the graphene layer and verify the key point on CO2 selectivity. From the reduction of H2 (Fig. 4A), it can be observed that the 5Fe@C-I catalyst, due to incomplete carbonization, produced the highest amount of water and completely different phenomenon from the others. The water generated here is a result of the reduction of iron oxide by hydrogen. The 5Fe@C-II and -III catalysts showed a similar pattern of water generation during H2-TPR process, but the 5Fe@C-III catalyst produced significantly more water. This is consistent with the previous understanding that incomplete encapsulation of the graphene layer leads to the oxidation of Fe. Thus, the residual FeOx from incomplete carbonization or the in situ formed one during FT synthesis could both accelerate the CO2 generation.

The performance of WGS reaction (Fig. 4B), which was treated as the major contributor for the CO2 formation, further supported the hypothesis. Since the 5Fe@C-III catalyst was exposed to air and the χ-Fe5C2 was not fully protected by the graphene layer, the FeOx species showed the highest WGS reactivity. It would be not only generated by air but also produced by reacting with H2O under the reaction condition of FT synthesis (47, 48). In contrast, the 5Fe@C-II catalyst, where the χ-Fe5C2 was enveloped by graphene layers, barely showed the reactivity of WGS, leading to the formation of CO2.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to investigate the favorable adsorption sites of OHx (x = 0-2), CO, and CO/H species on the χ-Fe5C2 surface, with and without a graphene layer. On both χ-Fe5C2 and χ-Fe5C2@Graphene, OHx, CO, and H preferentially adsorb on the χ-Fe5C2 surface (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). The adsorption energies (Ead) on χ-Fe5C2@Graphene are higher than on the bare χ-Fe5C2 surface (Fig. 4C), indicating weaker adsorption due to graphene confinement. However, with a carbon vacancy (VC) in the graphene layer, the preferred adsorption sites change to the VC, except for OH, which binds to Fe beneath the graphene-VC. While OHx adsorbs weakly on χ-Fe5C2@Graphene-VC, CO and CO/H adsorptions remain strong. Additionally, the preferred adsorption of H2O shifts from the inner confinement space of χ-Fe5C2@Graphene to the outer region of χ-Fe5C2@Graphene-VC. This shift inhibits the WGS reaction, thereby reducing CO2 emissions during Fischer–Tropsch synthesis.

Discussion

In conclusion, the χ-Fe5C2@Graphene catalyst fabricated for FT synthesis with the graphene layer completely enveloping the Hägg carbide showed extremely low selectivities of CO2 (4.6%) and CH4 (5.9%) at decent conversion of CO. The intimate contact between the graphene layer and the core χ-Fe5C2 active phase prevents the oxidation of the Hägg carbide by water generated during FT synthesis. The hydrophobic graphene layer not only ensures sustained stable reactivity and selectivity over extended reaction periods but also effectively suppresses the WGS reaction, leading to the minimal formation of CO2. This controllable Hägg carbide with a graphene layer offers a promising avenue for minimizing CH4 and CO2 during FT synthesis.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

The chemicals were offered by commercial companies and directly used without purification.

Catalyst Preparation.

Preparation of 5Fe@C catalysts. 0.76 g of iron phthalocyanine (FePc) was combined with 5.00 g urea and 3.00 g glucose at 433 K, resulting in the formation of a transparent solution. The resulting molten mixture was then heated at 453 K in an oven for 24 h. The solid obtained was subjected to heat treatment in flowing nitrogen (20 mL min−1) at 673 K for 30 min, followed by additional heat treatment at the final temperatures of 1,023 K, 1,373 K, and 1,573 K for another 2 h each. The catalysts obtained were denoted as 5Fe@C-I, 5Fe@C-II, and 5Fe@C-III.

Preparation of 5Fe/AC catalyst. The preparation of the 5Fe/AC catalyst involved the following steps using the incipient wetness impregnation (IWI) method: 0.51 g of iron phthalocyanine (FePc) was dissolved into 12.00 mL of ethanol. The resulting solution was then added dropwise to 1.00 g of activated carbon support. The resulting slurry was dried in a rotary evaporator at 343 K for 90 min, followed by calcination under nitrogen at 723 K for 2 h. The as-synthesized sample was denoted as 5Fe/AC.

Structural Characterization.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a Bruker D8 powder diffractometer equipped with a Cu-Kα source operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, along with a Vantec-1 detector.

57Fe Mössbauer spectrum analysis was performed at room temperature using a Topologic 500A spectrometer with a 57Co/Rh irradiation source in constant acceleration transmission mode. Isomer shifts (IS) were reported relative to the α-Fe foil at room temperature.

The size and morphology of samples were determined utilizing a FEI Tecnai G20 TEM operated at 200 kV and a Hitachi SU8000 field emission scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted on a VG Multilab 2000 photoelectron spectrometer using Al Kα radiation under vacuum (2 × 10−6 Pa). The binding energy (BE) was calibrated using the C 1 s peak at 284.6 eV.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were obtained at 77 K using a Quanta-chrome Autosorb-1-C instrument. Prior to the experiment, samples were evacuated and dried at 473 K for 6 h. Specific surface areas were calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller model, with pore size distributions estimated using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda method.

H2-temperature program reduction mass spectrometry (H2-TPR-MS) was carried out on the Altamira instrument (AMI-300). Specifically, 20 mg of fresh samples were pretreated under Ar flow at 723 K for 1 h and then heated from RT to 873 K in the 10% H2/Ar atmosphere. Signals for H2O were monitored by online MS.

Raman spectra were collected using a DXR2xi microscopic Raman spectrometer with laser excitation (λ = 532 nm).

Catalytic Evaluation.

Fischer–Tropsch synthesis was conducted in a stainless steel fixed-bed reactor with an inner diameter of 12 mm. Prior to testing, catalysts were diluted with inert SiC particles in a mass ratio of 1:10. The diluted catalysts (4.30 g) were introduced to a syngas mixture with an H2/CO ratio of 1. The reaction temperature was gradually increased to 533 K. In the effluent of the reactor, the concentrations of permanent gases (H2, CO, CO2) and light alkanes (CH4, C2H6, etc.) were monitored using an online Agilent Micro GC3000A gas chromatograph (GC). This GC was equipped with molecular sieve, Plot-Q and Al2O3 capillary columns, as well as a TCD detector. The oil and wax products were separated using a cold trap set at 271 K and a hot trap set at 373 K, respectively. Aqueous products were obtained through phase separation in these traps. The oil products were analyzed using an Agilent 6890N GC with an FID detector and a HP-5 column. The wax products were dissolved in CS2 and analyzed using an Agilent 7890B GC equipped with an FID detector and a HT-5 column. The aqueous fraction was analyzed using an Agilent 4890D GC equipped with an FID detector and a HP-Innowax column. Product selectivity was calculated based on the carbon balance.

Computational Methods.

DFT calculations were performed using generalized gradient approximation Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional (49) and projector-augmented wave method (50) as implemented in Vienna ab initio simulation package (51). Second-order Methefessel–Paxton electron smearing scheme (sigma = 0.2 eV) was used because of the metallic properties of χ-Fe5C2. Grimme’s dispersion (D3) correction (52) was included for modeling the confinement of graphene. Plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV was applied for the spin polarization calculations with the energy and force convergence criteria of 10−4 eV and 0.03 eV/Å, respectively. The carbon-rich χ-Fe5C2 (111) surface was modeled by a 4-layered slab containing 34 Fe and 16 C atoms, which is the most abundant facet from Wulff construction (53). The bottom two layers were kept fixed and the top ones were fully relaxed as well as the graphene and adsorbates. A thick vacuum of ca. 15 Å was added to the vertical direction for screening the lateral interaction. Structures were optimized with Monkhorst–Pack k-points of 5 × 5 × 1. The adsorption energy of OHx was calculated by Ead = EOHx/Cat – ECat – EH2O + (1 – x/2)EH2. The adsorption energy (Ead) of CO and CO/H was calculated by Ead = ECO/Cat – ECat – ECO and Ead = ECO/H/Cat – EH/Cat – ECO, respectively. The most stable adsorption structures were screened by considering both the Fe and C sites on the χ-Fe5C2 as well as that of the perfect or C-vacant graphene. The interaction energy between χ-Fe5C2 and graphene(−VC) was evaluated by Eint = Eχ-Fe5C2@Graphene(-Vc) – Eχ-Fe5C2 – Egraphene(-Vc), i.e., −0.92 and −3.16 eV for perfect and C-vacant graphene, respectively. The confinement distance increases from 3.1 to 5.9 Å (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) with the adsorptions of OHx, CO, and CO/H on χ-Fe5C2@Graphene, indicating weakened confinement interactions. In contrast, for χ-Fe5C2@Graphene-VC, the confinement distance remains relatively unchanged (2.2 ~ 2.6 Å) except in the case of CO/H coadsorption (3.3 Å).
