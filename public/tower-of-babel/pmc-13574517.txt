‘Waiting poorly’ for hip and knee arthroplasty – unmet needs and lack of support while waiting for surgery
Authors: Conor J McCann, Nick D. Clement, Christopher Gee, Jon Clarke, Phil Walmsley, Nicholas E. Ohly, David Deehan, Deiary F. Kader, Ciara Stevenson, Andrew J. Hall, WAIT-DATA Collaborative
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsIn socialized health systems, prolonged waiting times for hip and knee arthroplasty lead to progressive decline in patients’ health-related quality of life. This multicentre study aimed to evaluate patients’ experience of the information, advice, and support received during the waiting period, and to identify which groups feel least supported.MethodsThis study was a multicentre prospective observational audit including 851 patients undergoing elective hip or knee arthroplasty across 14 hospitals in the UK. Participants completed a standardized questionnaire assessing satisfaction with nine domains of preoperative information and support, each rated on a five-point Likert scale. Composite scores for information and advice and support were generated, and associations with demographic, clinical, and psychosocial variables were examined using multivariable linear regression.ResultsOverall satisfaction was moderate. Patients were generally well informed about their condition (mean 4.14 (SD 0.92)) and operation (mean 4.34 (SD 0.88)), but less satisfied with support for weight management (mean 3.49 (SD 0.98)), general health (mean 3.54 (SD 1.01)), and mental wellbeing (mean 3.46 (SD 1.02), p < 0.001). Older age, obesity, socioeconomic deprivation, and new physical or mental health diagnoses were associated with lower satisfaction, though most effects were small. Larger effects were seen between self-reported deterioration in health and quality of life and dissatisfaction with waiting time. Almost half of the patients reported opioid use, and most described worsening function and activity while waiting.ConclusionPatients awaiting hip or knee arthroplasty experience substantial physical and psychological decline, compounded by limited holistic support. While procedural information is generally adequate, guidance on lifestyle, wellbeing, and self-management is lacking. The waiting period should be reframed as an active phase of care, with coordinated, multidisciplinary input and clearer responsibility among care providers to ensure patients receive appropriate support.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(9):1215–1225.

Introduction

Hip and knee arthroplasty are among the most effective procedures in modern medicine for improving pain, function, and quality of life (QoL) in patients with advanced osteoarthritis.1 Increasing demand, workforce shortages, and constrained theatre capacity across the UK have led to unprecedented waiting times for surgery.2,3 Despite being highly cost-effective,4 and providing safe and lasting resolution of symptoms, hip and knee arthroplasty are often deprioritized and subject to delay when financial, capacity, or service pressures arise elsewhere in the NHS.3,5 For many patients, these delays are characterized by ongoing pain, progressive disability, loss of independence, and deteriorating mental and physical health.6-8

The waiting period represents a critical but under-studied aspect of the arthroplasty pathway.9 Patients incapacitated by end-stage joint disease often become deconditioned and increasingly frail,7 although the deterioration observed during extended waits is not solely physical.10 Negative emotional consequences of delayed surgery are common, with over two-thirds reporting feelings of frustration, anxiety, worry, or helplessness during the waiting period.11 These psychological stresses compound the burden of pain and immobility, undermining wellbeing and functional capacity. Declining physical and mental health are frequently accompanied by the initiation of new opioid prescriptions,12 which are associated with poorer postoperative outcomes, prolonged recovery, and persistent pain.13,14 Patients who feel unsupported while waiting may be more likely to seek pharmacological rather than behavioural strategies, reinforcing a cycle of inactivity and dependence.

Many patients experience uncertainty regarding their prognosis and report limited access to advice on pain management, physical activity, or weight optimization.15,16 The perception of inadequate support may compound feelings of isolation and helplessness, further reducing motivation to remain engaged with health-promoting behaviours while awaiting surgery. While initiatives such as prehabilitation programmes and self-management resources have been introduced in some areas,17,18 national data demonstrate that provision across the NHS is heterogeneous, inconsistently delivered, and frequently constrained by barriers including staffing, funding, and unclear responsibility across care pathways.15,19 Consequently, access to these interventions remains variable and often limited for those most in need. In this context, understanding the patient experience of support is essential in order to identify gaps in care delivery and inform more consistent, patient-centred pathways, a priority recently reinforced by work from the James Lind Alliance, which highlights the need to optimize wellbeing, access to care, and engagement while awaiting arthroplasty.20

The What Are the Implications To Delayed Access to Arthroplasty? (WAIT-DATA) study was established as a UK-wide collaborative audit to quantify the patient- and service-level consequences of waiting for hip and knee arthroplasty. This study focuses on patients’ perceptions of the information, advice, and support received while waiting for surgery. The primary aim of this study is to identify which groups of patients feel least supported, and to explore how these perceptions reflect wider patterns of functional and psychological deterioration during the preoperative period.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilizes data from the WAIT-DATA national multicentre project, a UK-wide multicentre prospective observational study which employed an established collaborative clinical audit model to evaluate the patient- and service-related effects of waiting for elective hip or knee arthroplasty.21 Participation was open to all NHS hospitals delivering arthroplasty in the UK, with prospective data collection conducted from May to June 2024. The study was designed to capture patient-reported experience through multiple pathways, but did not include detailed data on specific care pathways or service configuration.

Study population

In all, 14 different hospitals across the UK participated in the study (Supplementary Table 2). All adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing primary elective total hip arthroplasty, total knee arthroplasty, or unicompartmental knee arthroplasty during the data collection period were eligible for inclusion. Patients undergoing revision procedures were excluded. Each participating site collected data for at least ten complete operating days. Patients completed a standardized questionnaire during admission, either independently or with assistance from an audit facilitator where required (e.g. visual impairment or difficulty writing) to ensure completeness (Supplementary Figure 1). The questionnaire was developed with patient and public involvement to ensure clarity, relevance, and acceptability to the target population.

Data collection and variables

Collected variables included age, sex, BMI, Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation (SIMD),22 procedure, carer status, and wait time. Self-reported change in health and QoL was assessed using five-point Likert scales (1 = “much worse” to 5 = “much better”). Functional status was assessed using ordinal measures of outdoor activity and need for assistance with activities of daily living, with change scores calculated between recalled ‘time of listing’ and preoperative states. These measures have demonstrated convergent validity and reliability with established instruments, including EuroQol five-dimension five-level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L)23 and Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale24 in a prospective validation study of the WAIT-DATA Health Assessment Tool and patient-reported frailty measures. Nine Likert scale items (1 = ‘very dissatisfied’ to 5 = ‘very satisfied’) assessed satisfaction with information/support regarding the following: 1) wait time; 2) condition; 3) surgery; 4) pain relief; 5) physical activity; 6) weight management; 7) living with the condition; 8) general health; and 9) mental health. Higher values indicated greater satisfaction. Secondary variables included opioid use and new physical or mental health diagnoses.

‘Weak opioids’ included codeine or dihydrocodeine; ‘strong opioids’ included tramadol, morphine, oxycodone, buprenorphine, and tapentadol. IMD or SIMD is a measure of socioeconomic deprivation that ranks people based on their residential postcode according to seven domains: income; employment; education; housing; health; crime; and geographical access. Data zones are divided into population-weighted quintiles (1 = ‘most deprived’ and 5 = ‘least deprived’).

Ethical approval

The study was evaluated prospectively in accordance with NHS Health Research Authority guidelines and classified as a clinical audit, and therefore not subject to further research ethics committee consideration (Supplementary Figure 2). The study was conducted in each centre locally under the jurisdiction of the local hospital clinical governance team as part of their own service evaluation protocols, before anonymized pooled data were shared with the study lead group. All data were collected and stored in accordance with local information governance policies, and in accordance with the UK Caldicott Principles governing the use of data.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R (RStudio 2025.09.0+387; Posit, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean with SD or median with IQR as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequency (n, % of group). Perceived support scores were analyzed for each key subgroup. Comparisons between the satisfaction domains were made with a repeated-measures analysis of variance.

To summarize patient responses across related questionnaire items, two composite scores were created: an information and advice composite (comprising satisfaction with information about their condition, surgery, and pain relief, and physical activity advice) and a support composite (comprising satisfaction with support to optimize body weight, live with their condition, and improve general and mental health). Composite scores were calculated as the mean of constituent items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale, with higher values indicating greater satisfaction. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α, which demonstrated excellent reliability for both information and advice (α = 0.88; 95% CI 0.87 to 0.90) and support (α = 0.93; 95% CI 0.92 to 0.94).

Associations between satisfaction scores (information/advice, support, and waiting-time satisfaction) and continuous variables (age, waiting time, BMI, deprivation, and self-reported changes in health, QoL, outdoor activity, and assistance requirement) were examined using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlations. Between-group differences (sex, caring status, surgery type, new diagnoses, opioid use) were tested using Mann-Whitney U tests. Exploratory multivariable linear regression identified independent predictors of perceived support. Models included patient-reported change in health, QoL, and new diagnoses as pragmatic measures of overall health status. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In all, 851 patients from 14 different hospitals across the UK were included, representing a broad range of centre types from district general hospitals to specialist orthopaedic centres (Supplementary Table 2). The mean age was 69 years (range 27 to 93) and there were 491 females (57.7%) and 360 males (42.3%) (full characteristics in Table I).

Preoperative functional status varied: of 816 patients with completed functional level responses, 36.2% (295/816) of patients required help with heavy tasks and 28.9% (236/816) with housework, and 18.4% (150/816) were fully independent. The mean self-reported decline in functional level was –0.86 points (SD 1.00), and the mean reduction in activity level was –1.60 points (SD 1.46). Information/advice on physical activity was given to 392 patients (46.4%). During the waiting period, 164 (19.3%) reported a new physical health diagnosis, and 42 (4.9%) reported a new mental health diagnosis. Opioids were used by 383 (45.0%) patients, including 108 (12.7%) using strong opioids.

Health deteriorated while waiting; mean health change and QoL change scores were 1.85 (SD 0.74) and 1.89 (SD 0.87), respectively, representing a change in health between ‘worse’ and ‘much worse’.

Satisfaction with support

Responses were analyzed to determine patterns of perceived support and evaluation according to characteristics (Table I). Composite scores were created and analyzed. The mean information/advice composite satisfaction score (4.12 (SD 0.81)) was significantly higher than the mean support satisfaction score (3.50 (SD 0.92); mean difference = 0.62, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.67; p < 0.001).

A repeated-measures analysis of variance demonstrated significant variation in satisfaction across the nine domains assessing patients’ perceived information and support while awaiting arthroplasty (F(4, 3928) = 259.1; p < 0.001). Patients did not experience all aspects of preoperative information and support equally. Informational domains received the highest ratings (Figure 1), particularly information about the surgical procedure (mean 4.34 (SD 0.88)) and information about the condition itself (mean 4.14 (SD 0.92)), followed closely by information about pain relief options (mean 4.09 (SD 0.96)).

Patient satisfaction across support domains.

Pairwise comparisons confirmed that satisfaction with the length of waiting time (3.20 (SD 1.34)) was significantly lower than every other domain (all Holm-adjusted p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure 3). Domains related to ongoing or behavioural support, namely support to improve mental health (3.46 (SD 1.02)), optimize body weight (3.49 (SD 0.98)), live with the condition (3.53 (SD 1.03)), and improve general health (3.54 (SD 1.01)), were also consistently and significantly lower than informational domains (all p < 0.001). Differences among these support domains were generally small; most pairwise comparisons were non-significant (Holm-adjusted p = 0.054 to 0.544), although satisfaction with general health support was higher than mental health support (p = 0.001).

Lower perceived satisfaction with support was significantly associated with worse self-reported QoL, health, activity, and functional level (p < 0.05 for all, Table II). Older age and longer waiting times were correlated with lower information and advice satisfaction, and longer waits were also strongly associated with lower wait time satisfaction (r = −0.25; p < 0.001). Exploratory categorical analysis showed progressively lower wait time satisfaction with increasing duration, evident by six to 12 months (vs < 6 months; p = 0.024), with further reductions at longer durations but no significant differences beyond 12 to 18 months (Supplementary Table 3). Socioeconomic deprivation (lower IMD/SIMD quintile) was modestly associated with lower satisfaction across all domains. Male participants reported slightly higher satisfaction with the support they received than females (p = 0.003), but there was no difference in the satisfaction with information and advice received according to sex (p = 0.943). Patients who were diagnosed with a new physical health condition while awaiting surgery experienced poorer support (p = 0.006) and wait time (p < 0.001) satisfaction, and those with a new mental health condition diagnosed while waiting reported lower wait time satisfaction (p = 0.002). Opioid use was also associated with lower satisfaction with support (p = 0.048). No significant differences were observed according to surgery type or participants’ carer status.

Patient factors and perceived support

In the multivariable model for the composite support score, higher age (B = −0.007; p = 0.034), presence of obesity (B = −0.159; p = 0.016), higher deprivation (lower IMD/SIMD quintile; B = −0.048; p = 0.033), use of an opioid analgesic (B = 0.143; p = 0.027), and the presence of a new physical diagnosis (B = −0.182; p = 0.026) were independently associated with lower scores (Table III). Better self-reported QoL change (higher scores indicating milder deterioration) was associated with higher satisfaction (B = 0.105; p = 0.030). Male sex (B = 0.143; p = 0.028) was associated with higher support satisfaction. A new mental health diagnosis was associated with lower support satisfaction but missed statistical significance (p = 0.062). Other associations were non-significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This multicentre observational study evaluated patient perceptions of information, advice, and support while awaiting hip or knee arthroplasty across the UK. It represents one of the largest recent analyses of patient experience during prolonged surgical delays. Patients were generally satisfied with information about their condition and operation, but expressed considerably lower satisfaction with support to maintain physical and psychological wellbeing during the waiting period. These findings sit against a background of substantial deterioration in function, activity, and self-reported QoL, with almost one-quarter of patients developing new mental or physical health problems and almost half using opioid analgesia while waiting. Collectively, this depicts a population living through sustained physical decline and psychological distress that receives limited holistic support from the healthcare system.

Across the cohort, satisfaction with preoperative information was higher than with support, reflecting the relative strength of surgical communication pathways compared with ongoing patient engagement. Informational content about the surgical procedure and pain management was well received, suggesting that most patients felt technically informed. However, advice and support relating to physical activity, weight optimization, and mental wellbeing were rated far lower. This disparity illustrates that while clinical teams communicate surgical facts effectively, they are less able to offer the behavioural, motivational, and emotional support needed for patients to sustain health while waiting. Previous studies assessing patient experience while awaiting surgery have highlighted similar issues surrounding their difficulty coping with physical and psychosocial deterioration, as well as difficulties accessing adequate information and support from healthcare services.15,25 In essence, patients feel equipped to undergo an operation, but not to live well in the months preceding it.

Associations between patient characteristics and perceived support were generally small. Older age, obesity, deprivation, and new diagnoses were linked to slightly lower satisfaction, indicating greater unmet need in more vulnerable groups. These differences represent small effect sizes with limited standalone clinical significance, but are directionally consistent and highlight where inequities may accumulate. Poorer self-reported QoL was more clearly associated with lower perceived support, suggesting that declining health directly shapes patient experience. Opioid use was also associated with lower perceived support, consistent with reliance on pharmacological strategies in the absence of adequate non-pharmacological care.12,13,26

A similar gradient was observed for satisfaction with information and advice. Older adults, those in deprived areas, carers, and patients with new mental health diagnoses reported lower satisfaction, but again, the effect sizes were small. The association between new mental health diagnoses and reduced satisfaction was larger, consistent with the recognized challenges faced by those experiencing psychological distress in processing and engaging with health information.27,28 Barriers such as reduced digital access, lower health literacy, or competing caregiving demands likely contribute to these disparities.29 This highlights the importance of flexible, inclusive communication strategies that go beyond written leaflets or digital portals to ensure that all patients, regardless of circumstance, can access and use information meaningfully.

Dissatisfaction with waiting time showed stronger associations. Relationships between longer waiting duration, new diagnoses, and worsening health or QoL were of greater magnitude. These findings suggest that prolonged waiting time is a more dominant driver of patient experience than demographic characteristics. In practical terms, as patients’ health worsens, their perception of the waiting experience deteriorates accordingly. This relationship reinforces the urgency of minimizing waiting-related morbidity.

In the broader context of elective orthopaedic care, these findings resonate with existing evidence that prolonged waits exacerbate frailty, mental distress, and opioid use.7,12,13 These negative consequences were not shouldered equally between patient groups; individuals with greater physical or social burden felt less supported, reflecting unmet needs within these groups. Lower perceived satisfaction with support and information among socioeconomically deprived individuals mirrors existing inequalities in the provision of hip and knee arthroplasty, with more deprived areas experiencing inferior access to surgery.30 Excessively long wait times for surgery must be avoided, and in light of the disproportionate burden experienced by patients with greater physical/psychosocial health needs, prioritization of these groups may lead to fairer and more effective service delivery.31

These findings also reflect a wider sense of disconnection between patients and the healthcare system. Many experience the waiting period as a time of abandonment rather than preparation.10 Recent work suggests that patients often experience the arthroplasty pathway as fragmented and difficult to navigate, describing it as a “maze” rather than a coherent process.32 This fragmentation reflects a lack of continuity and clear ownership of care, with responsibility dispersed across primary, secondary, and community services, which may undermine both patient experience and engagement. Evidence from other specialities similarly identifies continuity of care as a key determinant of patient outcomes and satisfaction.33-36 While the concept of ‘waiting well’ has gained traction, for most individuals in this study, the reality was one of ‘waiting poorly’, with ongoing health deterioration and limited proactive support. Reframing the waiting period as an active phase of care, in which multidisciplinary teams help patients optimize physical function, manage pain, and sustain mental wellbeing, represents an opportunity to transform both patient experience and surgical outcomes.37

Medical preoptimization before hip or knee arthroplasty is known to improve outcomes and includes optimization of modifiable risk factors such as BMI, haemoglobin, albumin, smoking status, and vitamin D deficiency.38-40 Aside from medical optimization, prehabilitation programmes can improve pain, function, and length of stay.41,42 These are most commonly delivered across primary and secondary care, typically incorporating exercise therapy, weight optimization, analgesia review, and behavioural support, although specific regimes vary.17,18 Even simple interventions, such as preoperative education and rehabilitation advice, have been associated with improved patient satisfaction, shorter hospital stay, and subsequent cost savings of almost £600 per patient.43 However, national data demonstrate that provision remains inconsistent and fragmented across the NHS, with many centres lacking formal prehabilitation pathways, and facing barriers including funding, staffing, and unclear ownership of care delivery.19 This highlights that the challenge is not only what to deliver, but how care is coordinated and sustained across the patient journey. The preoperative period represents a key opportunity to mitigate deterioration while waiting,37 as reflected in recent James Lind Alliance priorities to improve access to and delivery of prehabilitation.20 Widespread implementation would require investment, consistent infrastructure, and clear responsibility for delivery.

This study has several strengths. It was large, multicentric, and representative, capturing patient voices across a diverse range of NHS hospitals. The use of validated, internally consistent composite measures adds methodological robustness. However, several limitations warrant consideration. Of all hospitals providing arthroplasty in the UK that were invited to participate, 14 centres contributed data, representing a relatively small proportion of eligible sites. This cohort only included patients who reached admission for surgery and excluded those who remained on the waiting list, were cancelled, dropped out, or sought private care, which may underestimate the true burden of waiting-related deterioration and introduce selection bias. The self-reported metrics used to assess satisfaction were pragmatic audit tools developed with patient and public involvement for the WAIT-DATA project and were not derived from previously validated instruments, which may limit comparability. In contrast, the measures used to assess change in health, function, and QoL have demonstrated convergent validity against established instruments within this programme. These were chosen ahead of validated patient-reported outcome measures as currently validated tools do not capture all relevant aspects of wait-related deterioration or satisfaction with support, and their simplicity allowed an excellent rate of completion without disrupting clinical workflows. Deterioration and questionnaire responses were retrospectively self-reported at the point of admission, introducing potential recall bias. However, data capture at the point of admission is common in large-scale service evaluations and offers a universal timepoint for consistent measurement. The cross-sectional design also precludes inference of causation, but was appropriate for describing patient experience across many sites.

The effect sizes of most associations were small, indicating that patient perceptions are influenced by a complex interplay of factors not captured in the current dataset. These may include interpersonal dynamics, expectations, local service culture, and prior healthcare experiences. Although trends were found, the clinical impact may be limited. Future research should adopt longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to capture evolving experiences over time, quantify the clinical impact of ‘waiting well’ interventions, and identify scalable strategies to deliver equitable, compassionate preoperative care.

Patients awaiting hip or knee arthroplasty frequently experience a decline in physical function, psychological wellbeing, and QoL, yet receive inconsistent and often inadequate support during this period. The waiting period for hip and knee arthroplasty should not be viewed as a passive delay but as a valuable opportunity for active optimization, led at a system level by healthcare services, managers, and budget holders, and supported by clear direction and investment at the government level. With coordinated input from primary care and community teams, this phase can be used to prioritize prehabilitation, risk factor modification, and patient education. Reframing waiting lists as preparation lists would not only enhance patient experience but also support better surgical outcomes, promote equity in access and readiness, and strengthen trust in an overstretched health service.

Take home message

- Prolonged waiting times for hip and knee arthroplasty are associated with worsening physical and psychological health.

- Patients report adequate procedural information but limited ongoing support for managing their condition during the waiting period, reflecting gaps in continuity of care.

- Preoperative optimization and community-based support are feasible and variably delivered, but wider and more consistent implementation requires coordinated, multidisciplinary care with clear responsibility for delivery.

- Delivering this level of care is likely to require additional resources and system-level investment.
