Stigma, discrimination-related events, and determinants among adult people living with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE): Systematic review and indicator-level meta-analysis
Authors: Hebatullah M Abdulazeem, Syrine Bellakhal, Rym Abida, Saeed Soliman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease with 0.4 million new cases diagnosed annually. With its wide variety of visible and invisible manifestations, people living with SLE report being exposed to stigmatization, which impacts their personal and professional lives. However, the current literature is unclear on whether healthcare management teams assess this concern during follow-up. This study aims to synthesize existing evidence on the prevalence and determinants of stigma among people living with SLE.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis gathered evidence from observational studies identified from three databases on 16 July 2025. Dual independent screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment (using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) were performed. Results were synthesized using descriptive statistics, narrative synthesis, and indicator-level meta-analyses.ResultsWithin the past two decades, 11 studies comprising 2254 people living with SLE reported and measured stigma- and discrimination-related events using various scales. Stigma was found to be prevalent across its three constructs: interpersonal, perceived, and intrapersonal stigma. This review demonstrated that people living with SLE reported a moderate overall burden of stigma (34.71 [95% CI 26.15, 43.27]), with average stigma scores indicating psychological impact. Additionally, nearly one in two persons (46% [95% CI 28–66%]) experienced at least one form of stigma or discrimination, most commonly social isolation and unfair treatment. Mental health associations were correlated with higher stigma burden.ConclusionThis review demonstrates that stigma and discrimination are not just social challenges but also critical determinants of health. With cautious interpretation, pooled evidence reveals a consistent high prevalence of stigma and discrimination, which act as “toxic” stressors, creating a vicious cycle with psychological stress and psychiatric manifestations and disease activity. There is an urgent clinical need to move beyond a mere biological approach to disease assessment and management and to begin screening for the “invisible” burden of invalidation and discrimination.Keywords: stigma, discrimination, systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune systemic disease of unknown origin.1,2 Its global prevalence varies widely between geographic regions, from 1 per 1000 in Europe to 7 per 100 individuals in some African countries. 3 This discrepancy not only reflects demographic heterogeneity across populations but also reflects reporting biases and differences in the definition of SLE across cohorts. 3 Hence, the real number of people living with SLE may not be accurately identified. However, a recent systematic modelling analysis of 112 epidemiological studies estimated that approximately 0.4 million people are newly diagnosed with SLE annually. 4

Clinically, SLE often follows a relapsing-remitting pattern, with phases of increased disease activity, also called flares, and periods of quiescence/remission that are challenging to predict. 5 When active, SLE can involve multiple organ systems, separately or simultaneously, with mucocutaneous and musculoskeletal involvement most commonly observed.1,2 SLE most commonly presents with malar rash, non-scarring alopecia, and peripheral joint swelling and tenderness. A more diverse presentation with extensive rashes associated with mouth ulcers was also reported. 2

Life-threatening complications of SLE also can occur, such as cytopenias, nephritis, myocarditis, enteritis, or myelitis. 1 Furthermore, overt manifestations of SLE can be observed outside a flare and are inherent to flare-induced damage. For instance, discoid lupus in the scalp can cause permanent scarring and hair loss, severe joint involvement can lead to joint deformities, also known as Jaccoud’s arthropathy, and neurological involvement can lead to sensory-motor sequelae. 6 The overt clinical phenotypes of SLE can be easily detected not only by clinical inspection but also by individuals’ families, friends, or colleagues.

Beyond the visible manifestations of SLE flares, people living with SLE experience daily struggles that might not be visible to their social and professional circles. In 2021, fatigue was reported by 85% of 4375 persons living with SLE across 35 countries as the most frequently experienced and most bothersome symptom. 7 Additionally, they expressed their need for effective management plans for fatigue, weakness, and pain, regardless of their disease activity status. 7 Accumulating literature suggests that fatigue, mood disorders and chronic pain in SLE are functional and non-inflammatory in nature and were recently grouped as Type 2 symptoms8,9. These hidden struggles, in addition to disease activity and unpredictability, increase the challenges that people living with SLE face every day, potentially compromising their physical and mental well-being, as identified through reviewing 1.4 million people living with SLE worldwide. 10

With the wide variety of visible and invisible manifestations of the disease, people living with SLE are vulnerable to stigmatization, which impacts their personal and professional lives. Experiences of 4260 people living with moderate to severe SLE burden indicated that psychological and social impacts were among the main contributing factors of lupus burden upon life. 11 The socio-cultural complexity of stigma enforces an additional burden in many aspects of the lives of people living with SLE.

‘Stigma’ was initially used as a word in the 16th century to describe “A mark made upon the skin by burning with a hot iron”. 12 A broader meaning was then attributed to this term to define “a distinguishing mark or characteristic of a bad or objectionable kind”. 12 In social and professional settings, stigma involves the discrediting of an individual based on a particular attribute. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines stigma as “a process whereby certain individuals and groups are unjustifiably rendered shameful, excluded and discriminated against”. 13 In the same context, WHO states that “discrimination means any distinction, exclusion or preference that has the effect of nullifying or impairing equal enjoyment of rights”. 13 Stigma can be seen in every domain of life, including healthcare, and can be experienced externally, internally, or mentally.

The concept of stigma in the context of SLE was repeatedly reported. In an analysis of online content of SLE forums, societal and self-invalidation, along with uncertainty, misunderstanding, and a lack of resilience and support, were the most frequently discussed themes. 14 A number of calls and initiatives have been launched, such as “Breaking stigmas: What lupus people living with wish you knew,” to increase awareness and societal support for SLE. 15

Most primary and secondary studies have explored the concept of stigma among people living with SLE and its associations with disease activity and outcomes.16,17 These studies focused primarily on the clinical perspectives, making it difficult to ascertain the prevalence of stigma among people living with SLE and its potential determinants. Understanding these factors helps identify psychological barriers that worsen health outcomes and guides the development of targeted interventions to improve overall well-being and care for people living with SLE. Therefore, a comprehensive systematic review is needed to synthesize existing evidence addressing stigma as a primary focus in the context of SLE.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted using a pre-designed protocol (PROSPERO CRD420251120706) following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, adapted for observational studies, and reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines to synthesize quantitative evidence on stigma experienced by people living with SLE.

Eligibility criteria

For our PECO-formatted research question, we included observational studies that addressed the adult population (+18 years) diagnosed with SLE through a validated method and exposure to stigma- or discrimination-related experiences, without limitations by region or ethnic group. The anticipated study design to be included was cross-sectional; however, studies including control groups were considered eligible whenever targeted populations were presented. Peer-reviewed publications and conference abstracts were eligible. Preprints, commentaries, and reviews were not considered in this review. Eligible outcomes for stigma or discrimination included quantitative measures of events, such as unfair treatment and social isolation, as well as frequencies or scores reported by the relevant instruments. As a secondary outcome, potential psychosocial determinants were also identified, whenever possible. Studies were excluded if they addressed exclusively cutaneous lupus erythematosus, did not present SLE-relevant data separately, particularly when other diseases were also addressed, or lacked quantitative data that could be extracted.

We conducted a comprehensive database search on 16 July 2025 across three databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Google Scholar was used as a source of gray literature up to the first 300 hits. Our search terms were tailored using keywords and MeSH terms across three main domains: identifying the population with SLE (e.g., Lupus, systemic lupus erythematosus), exposure to stigma or discriminatory events (e.g., stigma, prejudice, discrimination), and specifying the expected statistical outcomes (e.g., prevalence, epidemiology). No language or publication data restrictions were applied. Backward citation tracking of the relevant reference lists was performed. A detailed search strategy is presented in Supplemental File 1.

Screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment

Using Covidence, title and abstract screening, full-text screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment were performed independently by all investigators. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

The data extraction template was used to capture the following items:

• Study characteristics, including study design, settings, country, and sample size

• Study characteristics, including study design, settings, country, and sample size

• Population characteristics, including average age, gender, and the SLE diagnostic tool,

• Population characteristics, including average age, gender, and the SLE diagnostic tool,

• Outcomes, including the instrument used to identify stigma-related events, prevalence of stigma, discrimination, or relevant events, mean and standard deviation of the continuous scores of the instrument, if applicable.

• Outcomes, including the instrument used to identify stigma-related events, prevalence of stigma, discrimination, or relevant events, mean and standard deviation of the continuous scores of the instrument, if applicable.

Risk of bias assessment was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) for observational studies, adapted for cross-sectional studies as anticipated. This tool assesses three domains in each study: selection, comparability, and outcome, using a star system. The studies were categorized as low-, moderate-, or high-risk of bias.

Data analysis and synthesis

For a consistent and comprehensive investigation of stigma among people living with SLE, stigma was conceptualized to encompass three distinct yet interrelated constructs, which are as follows18–21:

(1) External (interpersonal/public/enacted/discriminatory) stigma: This construct refers to negative attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, or discriminatory actions (e.g., stereotyping, prejudice, dismissiveness) directed toward a person by others, including the public, institutions, and broader society.

(1) External (interpersonal/public/enacted/discriminatory) stigma: This construct refers to negative attitudes, beliefs, behaviors, or discriminatory actions (e.g., stereotyping, prejudice, dismissiveness) directed toward a person by others, including the public, institutions, and broader society.

(2) Perceived stigma: This construct refers to the person’s belief or expectations that others hold negative attitudes toward them or will behave negatively because of their condition (e.g., perceiving that others feel superior).

(3) Internal (intrapersonal/internalized/internalized/self-) stigma: This construct refers to the personal acceptance or applying negative stereotypes about themselves, independent of others’ attitude or behavior (e.g., self-shame, underestimated self-worth)

Although these constructs are theoretically distinct, some manifestations of stigma are challenging to classify. For example, social isolation may reflect internalized stigma when driven by self-directed negative thoughts, but may alternatively arise from anticipation of societal rejection, consistent with perceived stigma. To ensure consistent categorization of stigma across studies, we considered only the endpoint presentation of stigma and accordingly classified social isolation as internal stigma. Each extracted stigma item or scale was mapped to its corresponding stigma construct and the specific subdomain.

Despite the planned pooling of prevalence estimates for stigma-related events, a significant lack of consistent reporting across studies necessitated a deviation from the original protocol. Therefore, outcomes were analyzed at the indicator (assessment measure) level rather than participant or study level, reflecting the prevalence or mean score of specific stigma-related indicators reported within each study. As individual studies were anticipated to frequently contribute multiple stigma indicators, three-level random-effect model meta-analyses were applied to account for the statistical dependence of indicators nested within studies (i.e., sampling errors, within-study variability, and between-study variability). Analyses were conducted in R (version 2025.05.0 + 496) using the metafor package, and the results were presented as pooled estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To ensure comparability rather than proportional equivalence, the values and scores of the heterogeneous instruments and scales identified to assess stigma-related outcomes were standardized. Continuous scales with reported minimum and maximum possible scores, means, and standard deviations were transformed to a 0–100 metric using the percentage of maximum possible method. 22 The PROMIS T-Scores were treated as inherently standardized; however, when reported differently, scores were linearly rescaled to a 0–100 metric using established score transformation methods from classical test theory. 23 All outcomes were directionally harmonized, where higher values represent greater stigma-related burden.

A prevalence meta-analysis was conducted using a three-level generalized linear mixed-effects model with logit-transformed proportions, accounting for the dependence among multiple stigma-related indicators nested within studies. The analysis estimated the overall pooled indicator-level prevalence of any stigma-related outcome. For continuous outcomes, standardized mean estimates were similarly pooled using three-level random-effect models. A sensitivity analysis was conducted, excluding the psychological proxy measures (i.e., coping strategies, perceived stress, and emotional reactions) to assess the robustness of pooled estimates to more explicit stigma definitions. Heterogeneity was quantified using variance components Tau2 at the between-study and within-study (indicator-level) levels rather than I2, which is not well-defined for multilevel models.

Prespecified subgroup analyses by stigma constructs and domains were not feasible due to the small number of contributing studies per subgroup and substantial heterogeneity, which would preclude reliable inference.

Characteristics of included studies

The 11 included studies were cross-sectional, published between 2004 and 2025, and included a total of 2254 persons living with SLE recruited from hospital outpatient clinics or specialized rheumatology units.24–34 Eight studies24–26,29–32,34 specified the data-collection period, which collectively ranged from March 2011 to October 2023, with an average of 1 year of data collection. Five studies were conducted in the United States,24,25,27,29,34 while six were performed in Spain 26 , Australia, 28 Italy, 30 China, 31 Saudi Arabia, 32 and Turkey. 33 Apart from one study, 29 all studies reported using validated methods to include people living with SLE: the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria in eight studies,24–28,30,32,34 the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria in one study, 31 and the 2012 SLICC criteria in one study. 33

The study populations were overwhelmingly female (2017 females/2109, 95.63%, n = 10 studies), with three studies focusing exclusively on women30,32,34 and one study not reporting the gender ratio. 29 The age of participants ranged from 34.6 to 60.3 years. The duration of living with SLE was reported in nine studies25–28,30–34 and ranged from 5.3 to 32.3 years. Disease activity was measured using the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), ranging from 5.3 ± 4.7 to 7.1 ± 626,27,31,33 and the Brief Index of Lupus Damage (BILD), with an average of 2.4 ± 2.4.24,25,34 Glucocorticoid use was reported in four studies, ranging from 13.5% to 88% among participants.24,25,30,31 A few studies reported organ damage,24,30 with the highest rate of kidney damage (38%). 30

Stigma-related outcomes

(1) Forms of stigma-related events

(1) Forms of stigma-related events

Across the included studies, stigma and discrimination among people living with SLE were reported in both illness-related and socially mediated forms. Perceived illness stigma was directly documented in two studies, where stigma was reported to be linked to visible disease manifestations, which contributed to a feeling of social discreditation and anticipated discrimination.24–26,30–33

Social isolation and impaired social participation were also documented as stigma-related experiences. Limitations in engaging in social roles and activities alongside social withdrawal and reduced interpersonal satisfaction were reported.24,26–29,33 In addition, illness repercussions affecting relationships, associations, social activities, and appearance were reported as broader experiences of social disadvantage and perceived handicap associated with SLE. 28 Self-blame,24,30 and denial and behavioral disagreement24,30 were also reported.

Discrimination-related events were additionally documented as a recurrent social stressor through reports of unfair treatment among African American women with SLE, including being treated with less courtesy or respect or receiving poorer services, 34 threatened or harassed,25,27–29 and interpersonal mistreatment. 24 Such experiences encompassed both racially attributed discrimination and unfairness attributed to non-racial causes.

Quantitatively, the pooled standardized mean of stigma-related burden measures across 24 indicators reported in the included studies was 34.71 [95% CI 26.15, 43.27] (Figure 2). Individual measures demonstrated substantial variability, indicating heterogeneity in both the stigma construct/domain and the measuring instrument. More social isolation (n = 8 of 24 indicators) and perceived stigma (n = 5) measures were frequently reported, with a higher indication of stigma burden among the people living with SLE.

Standardized stigma-related burden measures in SLE.

While psychological proxy measures are not direct measures of stigma, rather potential coping mechanisms for the occurring or perceived burden, a sensitivity analysis of the pooled mean was performed. Figure 3 presents that the pooled standardized mean remained similar at 33.03 [95% CI 22.33, 43.74], indicating that the overall estimate of stigma-related burden was robust. However, heterogeneity was predominant within-study variance, suggesting that variability across stigma-related measures persisted even when restricting the analysis to more direct stigma measures.

Determinants of stigma and discrimination clustered across clinical, psychological, and sociodemographic domains. Disease manifestations, particularly visible and disfiguring symptoms, were linked to higher perceived stigma.31,32 Disease activity and pain severity were positively correlated with the stigma scores. 31

Depression showed a strong association, where significant correlations between stigma severity and depressive symptoms were reported, 32 in which SLEDAI and SLICC/ACR Damage Index scores were higher in people living with SLE and depression. Multivariable analyses further identified depression and mental health composite score as independent predictors of perceived stigma and predicted stigma levels, respectively. 31

Stress appraisal and maladaptive coping patterns, such as disengagement, self-blame, and emotional coping, were more frequent in people living with SLE with higher psychological burden, indicating psychological contexts in which stigma experience may occur. 30 Major life stressors and coping styles were similarly associated with psychological distress in a single population. 27

Income level and the broader socioeconomic indicators were correlated with perceived stigma. 31 Racial minority status intersected with discrimination exposure; African American women reported unfair treatment, which they attributed to the cumulative disease burden. 34 Discrimination experiences were also documented among Asian individuals with SLE, with frequencies of specific events of being followed in stores, others acting as a superior, and association of worse depression and anxiety with self-reported disease activity. 25

Social isolation, reduced social support, and impaired participation in social roles were documented to be associated with psychosocial functioning impairment. 26 Broader social risk factors, including financial strain, housing instability, and transportation barriers, were prevalent in people living with SLE, indicating contextual vulnerability relevant to stigma and discrimination exposure. 29 Additionally, social isolation was found to be more frequent among people living with SLE and joint pain compared to rheumatoid arthritis (RA), spondyloarthritis, vasculitis, and other connective tissue diseases. 29 Similarly, found in another study, people with SLE showed significantly greater social withdrawal, self-criticism, and life-threatening events than RA patients and healthy controls. 27 However, Cano-Gracia et al. reported similar social isolation scores among people living with SLE, RA, and spondyloarthritis. 26

Beyond exposure and determinants, several studies documented downstream psychosocial and functional repercussions linked to stigma-related experience. Stigma was associated with a worse quality of life and psychological well-being. 31 People’s perceived handicap affected multiple life domains, including activity, relationship, and social engagement, documenting functional consequences of stigmatization. 28 Chronic pain, social isolation, and stress were also described as interrelated psychological burdens in the sleep population. 24

(3) Assessment tools for stigma-related events

(3) Assessment tools for stigma-related events

Several validated instruments were used to quantify stigma and discrimination constructs in the SLE population. Illness stigma was assessed using the stigma impact scale (SIS), enabling quantification of perceived stigma severity. 32 Devaluation and discrimination were measured using the Perceived Devaluation and Discrimination (PDD) scale, which captures perceptions of societal attitudes and anticipated discriminatory responses. 31

Discrimination exposure was operationalized through a self-reported unfair treatment measure that examined frequency and attribution of racial versus nonracial unfair treatment, allowing discrimination to be examined as a social stressor. 34

While not stigma-specific, several instruments captured stigma-proximal or discrimination-relevant social impact. The disease percussion profile (DRP) assessed individual-perceived handicap across domains, including relationships, social activities, emotions, finances, appearance, and functioning. 28 Social participation constraints were measured using the PROMISE ability to participate in social roles and activities. 26 A few tools were identified as indirect tools by assessing stigma-related psychological coping mechanisms, such as the COPE 30 and PSS 30 scales. Other identified scales that assessed stigma-related constructs, such as social isolation, social withdrawal, and disengagement, using subscales from the PROMIS,24,26 NHP, 33 CSI, 27 PERi-M, 27 and SRF 29 inventories were identified.

Quantitatively, the pooled estimate of indicator-level prevalence of any stigma or discrimination events across five studies was 46% [95% CI 28–66%], with the largest indicator weights for unfair treatment in healthcare and social isolation (Figure 4). Substantial within- and between-study variance was observed, with the majority of the variance attributable to differences among stigma indicators.

Mean indicator-level prevalence of stigma in people living with SLE.

The risk of bias of the included studies is summarized in the Supplemental File 2. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Seven studies were classified as low risk of bias24–26,29,31,33,34 while four studies had a moderate risk of bias.27,28,30,32 The sample selection domain and simple size subdomain were the main sources of potential bias.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to synthesize evidence on the prevalence of stigma- and discrimination-related events among people living with SLE. Across 11 included studies, comprising 2254 participants, stigma and discrimination-related events were reported and measured using various scales. Stigma was found to be prevalent across its three constructs: interpersonal, perceived, and intrapersonal stigma.

Although the level and type of stigma varied across studies, our findings demonstrated that people living with SLE reported a moderate overall burden of stigma (34.71 [95% CI 26.15, 43.27]), with average stigma scores indicating psychological impact. Additionally, nearly one in two persons (46% [95% CI 28–66%]) experienced at least one form of stigma or discrimination, most commonly social isolation and unfair treatment. Mental health associations were correlated with higher stigma burden.

Comparison with existing literature

Stigma is present in every aspect of life; it is difficult to separate SLE from other causes. However, SLE can be a distinct source of stigmatization through not only its visible and/or invisible aspects but also through its physical and mental complications. Previous systematic reviews have established that stigma and discrimination are predictors of poor outcomes and reduced quality of life. For example, a landmark 2024 systematic review examining social determinants of health found 12-fold higher odds of poor clinical outcomes among people living with lupus nephritis who had adverse social determinants, including stigma and disease activity. 35 Another large review on 23.000 people living with SLE confirmed this structural toxicity and revealed that approximately 35% suffer from depression and 26% from anxiety, outcomes heavily mediated by stigma. 10

Relating stigma to SLE phenotypes and complications: The toxic cycle

Collectively, our findings and the broader research demonstrate that stigma and discrimination are consistent with a hypothesis that stigma and discrimination may contribute to a “toxic cycle” linking this psychological burden to disease activity and poor outcome.10,36 Exposure to everyday discrimination is a chronic external stressor that has been associated with pathophysiologic stress responses (abnormal cortisol secretion and systemic inflammation), which may exacerbate autoimmune activity. 37 This external pressure appears to be absorbed as “self-blame” and “perceived illness stigma”. These internalized and perceived stigmas were identified as a potent predictor of depression, suggesting a feedback loop where poor mental health likely amplifies pain perception and contribute to disease exacerbations.

The invisible paradox of the dual threat of stigma

Social isolation emerged as the most prominent dysfunction across the studies we included. This reflects what can be described as an invisible paradox: The invisible nature of SLE symptoms as pain and dysfunction can lead to symptom invalidation and disappreciation, resulting in persons withdrawing to avoid judgment or repeatedly needing to explain their condition. Furthermore, there is the dual threat of “visible” and “invisible” stigma, where people living with SLE can experience stigma for appearing too healthy or looking too ill. People living with SLE were found to experience appearance-related distress due to visible signs such as rash, alopecia, and appearance changes from steroids. Additionally, non-racial attribution to unfair treatment and the unpredictability of disease-related discrimination were also reported even when visible signs are absent.

Underreported determination of social and clinical perspectives

Despite the socio-cultural complexity of stigma, the included studies failed to capture determinants for stigma, such as how SLE-related stigma interacts with gender, race, and socio-economic status. Furthermore, the economic and workplace aspects of stigma were not addressed, such as job loss and subsequent financial constraints, which remain severely under-examined and under-reported.

The role of health literacy and improving access to information

Access to health care, particularly information access, is another unexposed determinant for SLE stigma. People with limited health literacy may not be able to recognize, articulate and challenge stigma experience, which may lead to undermining their self-advocacy and engagement with health care providers. This is mostly evident in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), where limited information access, compounded by cultural taboos around SLE, amplifies perceived stigma and delays health care seeking. 38 Evidence suggests that targeted health literacy interventions in people with chronic disease improve their capacity to navigate stigma, openly discussing symptoms and engage in shared health care decision making, signifying the need for SLE-specific patient education programs that explicitly address stigma as a modifiable barrier to care. 39

Social limitation in SLE patients

Qualitative research on the lives of patients with SLE has highlighted the negative impact of the disease on their social lives. K. McElhone et al., 40 based on semi-structured interviews with 30 women with SLE, found that patients frequently reported being unable to plan ahead to participate in events. Some compensated for this by not making any commitments and declining invitations, but they also feared becoming isolated and no longer being invited in the future.

S. Booth et al. 41 conducted an online survey of 393 patients with SLE. All participants reported that SLE had a negative impact on their ability to work: 40.45% of them believe they left their jobs because of the disease.

Important to highlight that, despite being a female-predominant disease, emerging evidence highlights the experience of men living with SLE. Karakikla-Mitsakou et al. 42 identified that 3 out of 4 men in a sample of 139 male SLE patients reported perceived psychosocial challenges, underscoring the importance of gender-inclusive assessment in stigma research.

Patient–physician discordance of stigma recognition

Chiche et al. 43 highlight significant differences between patients’ and physicians’ perceptions regarding the impact of SLE on patients’ lives. Doctors rarely reported that SLE had a significant impact on all aspects of their patients’ lives—whether physical, psychological, social, occupational, sexual, or emotional. Most patients reported a lack of medical guidance in areas such as sexual and emotional health, family life, work life, and anxiety.

Strengths and limitations

Despite the comprehensive evidence review, the findings should be interpreted with caution. The main limitations are the relatively small number of included studies (N = 11) and the significant heterogeneity across studies regarding sample size, settings, and definitions of stigma. Additionally, we were unable to directly pool prevalence rates using a pre-designed standard meta-analysis due to methodological heterogeneity and varying definitions of stigma constructs, which hindered the calculation of a single reliable overall stigma prevalence. Instead, we adapted our analyses to perform a narrative synthesis and an indicator-level statistical summary to best summarize the available data while avoiding misleading pooled estimates. Lastly, given the infeasibility of measuring publication bias, the possibility of unpublished negative or non-significant findings on stigma in SLE cannot be excluded.

Clinical and research implications

Our review findings underscore the need for future research to further examine possible differences in the effects of unfair treatment by attribution. Clinically, developing and validating an SLE-specific stigma measure or scale would enable clinicians to identify individuals at risk of invalidation and social withdrawal, thereby preventing poor outcomes. Potential health and social benefits may be expected from including such a scale as an essential part of the assessment and management of people living with SLE.

Conclusions

To conclude, this systematic review contributes to the body of knowledge, confirming that stigma and discrimination are not only social challenges but also critical determinants of health. Pooled evidence reveals a consistent high prevalence of stigma and discrimination, which act as “toxic” stressors, creating a vicious cycle with psychological stress and psychiatric manifestations and disease activity. There is an urgent clinical need to move beyond a mere biological approach to disease assessment and management and to begin screening for the “invisible” burden of invalidation and discrimination.
