Excitation of Confined Bulk Plasmons in metallic nanoparticles by penetrating electron beams within a non-local analytical approach Preprint: APS/123-QED
Mattin Urbieta, Eduardo Ogando, Alberto Rivacoba, Javier Aizpurua, Nerea Zabala

Abstract
Using a linear hydrodynamic model (HDM) we investigate theoretically the interaction between penetrating electron beams and sub-5 nm metallic spherical nanoparticles (NPs), and provide an analytical expression of the electron energy loss (EEL) probability including non-local effects in the response of the confined electron gas. We focus on the characterization of the longitudinal plasmon excitations, or confined bulk plasmons (CBPs), which cannot be addressed within local dielectric frameworks, and show that their excitation is highly sensitive to the impact parameter and kinetic energy of the incident electron beam, as well as to the NP’s size. In contrast to the local approach, our decription captures a blueshift of the bulk plasmon envelope (BPE) with decreasing NP size and a blueshift with increasing impact parameter. Moreover, it predicts a threshold impact parameter, or minimum electron path inside the NP, to efficiently activate a set of CBPs. Exploiting the multipolar description of the CBPs we identify the underlying symmetry rules governing their excitation by electron beams, and correlate the observed blueshift of the BPE for increasing impact parameters with the excitation of higher-order CBPs. Dispersion of the CBPs with decreasing NP sizes further increases this impact parameter dependent blueshift of the BPE and also explains the decrease in the impact parameter threshold.



I
Introduction

Our knowledge about the properties and dynamics of plasmons in nanoparticles (NP) is to large extent due to the development of electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS)
Browning et al. 1993
;
Batson 1993
;
Egerton 2011
;
Krivanek et al. 2014
in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)
Batson et al. 2002
;
Krivanek et al. 2010
and optical spectroscopies
Grillet et al. 2011
;
Sigle et al. 2015
.
Over the last two decades, sub-nanometer resolution
Batson et al. 2002
;
Nellist 2004
and sub-eV energy sensitivity
Hart et al. 2017
have been attained, which has pushed the potential of EELS and STEM to unprecedented levels and provided new pathways to research novel materials and nanostructures
Krivanek et al. 2014
;
Ke et al. 2015
;
Krivanek et al. 2010
;
Rez et al. 2016
;
Hachtel et al. 2019
;
Lagos et al. 2017
;
Govyadinov et al. 2017
;
Zeiger and Rusz 2020
;
Lourenço-Martins and Kociak 2017
.
The study of localized surface plasmon (LSP) resonances in metallic nanoparticles
Batson 1982a
;
Batson 1982b
achieved extraordinary resolution in EELS of single NPs of sizes below 10 nm
Jiang et al. 2009
;
Scholl et al. 2012
;
Raza et al. 2013
;
Raza et al. 2015
;
Hobbs et al. 2016
;
Campos et al. 2019
, making STEM a suitable tool for the study of new and complex phenomena at the nanometer scale.

Beyond LSPs, EELS is capable of detecting confined bulk plasmons (CBPs), longitudinal collective oscillations of the electron density confined to the volume of the NP, also referred to as volume plasmons in the literature
Hobbs et al. 2016
.
These modes are typically inaccessible with optical spectroscopy techniques due to their weak coupling to light, which is three orders of magnitude weaker as compared to that of the dipolar LSP
Ruppin 1973
;
Christensen et al. 2014
.
As a result, CBPs have received far less attention than their LSP counterparts.
Efficient excitation of CBP modes requires the electron probe to penetrate the NP and to induce longitudinal perturbations of the electron cloud.
Experimentally, CBPs have been measured in EELS for a wide range of materials, including Ag
Koh et al. 2009
;
Scholl et al. 2012
, Al
Batson 1985
, Bi
Wang et al. 2006
;
Borja-Urby et al. 2019
, and Si
Mitome et al. 1992
.

From a theoretical perspective, the excitation of plasmons by electron beams penetrating small NPs has been studied for a long time within dielectric local response approximation (LRA) frameworks, both analytically
Bausells et al. 1987
;
Rivacoba and Echenique 1990
;
Stamatopoulou et al. 2024
and numerically
García de Abajo and Howie 2002
;
Hohenester 2014
;
Das et al. 2012
;
Cao et al. 2015
.
In this approximation the presence of a surface produces a reduction of the bulk plasmon probability due to the excitation of LSPs, but such an approach does not account for CBPs, which are introduced
ad hoc
through an unbound bulk plasmon term centered at the plasma energy and proportional to the electron path inside the NP.
Thus, approaches that go beyond the LRA and consider the non-local response of the material are required to properly address the excitation of CBPs in sub 10-nm NPs
Ruppin 1978
.
Non-local effects emerge in nanoplasmonics at the few-nanometer scale which act as a pressure that prevents the pilling up of electrons at short distances.
This pressure exerted by the electron density, known as Thomas-Fermi pressure
Raza et al. 2011
, must be considered to address the CBPs and their excitation by penetrating electron beams.
Indeed, the experimental observation of CBPs has been considered a hallmark of the hydrodynamic behavior of the electron density at the sub-5 nm scale
Christensen et al. 2014
, underscoring the need for deeper theoretical insight into their excitation mechanisms.

Quantum
ab initio
frameworks offer
Urbieta et al. 2024
;
Candelas et al. 2025
, a suitable platform to address the atomic-scale regime and the quantum nature of the electrons, and their influence on CBPs, although the interpretation of the results may be hindered by the complexity of the model, and the computational requirements for such frameworks is prohibitive for large systems.
In a recent publication,
ab initio
calculations demonstrated that although the atomistic shape affects the excitations of LSPs in the EEL spectra, the energy of the CBPs is almost unaffected by the shape or relative orientation of NPs of the same size
Urbieta et al. 2024
.
Additionally, these studies also demonstrate that the energy of the bulk plasmon peaks depends on the electron beam’s impact parameter a behavior that is not captured by the models based on LRA, but is instead well described by a linear hydrodynamic model (HDM).
Compared to numerical approaches based on quantum
ab initio
frameworks, the HDM offers analytical tractability and more straightforward data analysis at the cost of their applicability to a handful of highly symmetric geometries.
Since the seminal work of Fujimoto and Komaki
Fujimoto and Komaki 1968
, in which they studied the excitation of plasmons in small spherical NPs with broad electron beams, the HDM has been employed in several theoretical works studying the EEL spectra of NPs excited by penetrating electron beams
Barberán and Bausells 1985
;
Tran
Thoai 1986
;
Tran Thoai and Zeitler 1988
;
v. Baltz et al. 1995
;
Gildenburg et al. 2016
.
Despite these advances, an explicit expression of the EEL probability for spherical NPs as a function of the impact parameter for penetrating trajectories within a HDM remains lacking, yet it would be of high interest to study CBPs and their excitation by electron beams at the nanometer scale within this analytical tool.

In this work, we derive an analytical expression of the EEL probability of spherical NPs within the HDM, valid both for penetrating and external electron beams.
We apply the derived expressions to Na NPs and analyze the dependence of the LSPs and, specially, CBPs on the impact parameter and velocity of the electron probe, as well as on the size of the NP.
We identify the symmetry rules for the excitation of such plasmons by electron beams.
This work thus unravels the rules governing the excitation of CBPs, and establishes a basis for a more exhaustive analysis of the EEL spectra of small NPs in the bulk plasmon energy range.

II
Theoretical framework

The electron beam is modeled as a point-like charged particle traveling with constant velocity
v
(non-recoil approximation) along a trajectory parallel to the
z
-axis at distance
b
(impact parameter) from the center of the NP [Fig.
1
].
Then the charge density associated to the traveling electron can be expressed in spherical coordinates,
(r,\theta,\varphi)
, and in frequency domain,
\omega
, as:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r},\omega)=-\frac{e}{v}\frac{\delta(r\sin\theta-b)\delta(\varphi)}{r\sin\theta}e^{i\omega r\cos\theta/v},

(1)

where
e
is the electron charge.
The electron probe, described by the external electron density
\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
, interacts through its electric potential,
\phi^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r}^{\prime})
, with the NP electron gas and induces a charge density in the NP,
\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)
.
The induced charge density produces an induced potential,
\phi^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
, that acts back on the electron probe decelerating it and making it lose certain energy [see
Fig.
1
].
Within the quasistatic approximation, the total energy loss experienced by the electron probe is related to the induced potential at the position of the traveling electron,
\textbf{r}^{\prime}=(b,0,vt)
, as:

\displaystyle W=\frac{e}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dz^{\prime}\frac{\partial\phi^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)}{\partial z}\bigg|_{\textbf{r}=\textbf{r}^{\prime}}e^{-i\omega z^{\prime}/v}.

(2)

The total energy loss is usually expressed as a function of the probability of losing energy
\hbar\omega
as:

\displaystyle W=\int^{\infty}_{0}d\omega\,\hbar\,\omega\,\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}(\omega),

(3)

where
\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}(\omega)
is the electron energy loss probability per energy unit, which is the magnitude directly comparable with the experimentally measured energy loss spectrum.
For finite targets, such as isolated NPs, the EEL probability,
\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}(\omega)
, can be written as
Note1
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\;\frac{e}{\pi\hbar v}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\textrm{Im}\bigg\{-\phi^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i\omega z^{\prime}/v}\bigg\}.

(4)

Thus, to obtain
\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}(\omega)
, we need to compute the induced scalar potential
\phi^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
along the trajectory of the electron beam,
which may be calculated assuming different approaches to address the probe-target interaction
Rivacoba et al. 1992
;
Zabala and Rivacoba 1993
;
Wang 1996
;
Rivacoba et al. 2000
;
García de
Abajo 2010
.
In the next subsection, we obtain the induced scalar potential from the induced charge density
\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
at the spherical metallic NP as a response to the electron beam using a linear HDM.
From these lines onward we use atomic units (
\hslash=e=m_{e}=1
) throughout this paper.

II.1
Dynamics of an electron gas confined to a spherical nanoparticle

We model the target as a spherical metallic NP with radius
a
and a homogeneous electron density
n=3/4\pi r_{s}^{3}
for
r<a
, where
r_{s}
is the Wigner-Seitz radius.
The plasma frequency of the NP medium is given by
\omega_{p}=\sqrt{4\pi n}
and the dispersion is described by a constant compressibility parameter
\beta=\sqrt{3/5}v_{\textrm{F}}
, where
v_{\textrm{F}}=(3\pi^{2}n)^{1/3}
is the Fermi velocity of the electron gas.
The assumption of such a constant electron density reduces the dynamics equation of the electron gas to the Helmholtz equation inside the metal
Rivacoba 2019
:

\displaystyle[\nabla^{2}+\mu^{2}(\omega)]\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r},\omega),

(5)

where
\mu
is a
\omega
-dependent parameter defined as,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\Big[\omega(\omega+i\gamma)-\omega_{p}^{2}\Big],

(6)

with
\gamma
a damping constant.
The imaginary part of
\mu(\omega)
is taken to be positive.
The solutions of Eq. (
5
) are required to satisfy the following boundary condition (BC) at the interface
r=a
:

\displaystyle\hskip-100.00015pt-\frac{1}{4\pi}\omega_{p}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int d\textbf{r}^{\prime}\frac{\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}{\mid\textbf{r}^{\prime}-\textbf{r}\mid}-\beta^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\omega_{p}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int d\textbf{r}^{\prime}\frac{\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}{\mid\textbf{r}^{\prime}-\textbf{r}\mid},

(7)

which accounts for the hydrodynamic condition of zero electron current normal to the surface
Crowell and Ritchie 1968
;
Villó-Pérez and Arista 2009
, i.e., radially moving electrons are confined by the hard wall boundary and reflected back into the NP.
For the sake of simplicity, in this formalism we neglect the influence of
d
-band electrons on the response of the free electrons (
\varepsilon_{\infty}=1
), although the model could be extended to consider their influence
v. Baltz et al. 1995
.

II.2
Electron energy loss probability for electron trajectories penetrating a spherical NP

Making use of the spherical symmetry of the target we expand the external,
\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
[see Eq. (
1
)], and the induced,
\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)
, charge densities in terms of the spherical harmonics,

The BC at

\displaystyle\hskip-50.00008pt\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2l+1}\frac{l+1}{a^{l+2}}\int_{0}^{a}dr\,r^{l+2}\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{lm}(r,\omega)-\beta^{2}\frac{\partial\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{lm}(r,\omega)}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=a}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2l+1}\bigg\{-\frac{l+1}{a^{l+2}}\int_{0}^{a}dr\,r^{l+2}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r,\omega)

\displaystyle+l\,a^{l-1}\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^{l-1}}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r,\omega)\bigg\},

(the probing electron inside the nanoparticle).
Moreover, penetrating trajectories require the solution of the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation (
5

For the sake of brevity we will omit writing the

\Gamma^{\textrm{ext}}
contributes to
\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}
in Eq. (
16
) [with
\mathcal{O}_{lm}(b,\infty)
instead of
\mathcal{O}_{lm}(a,\infty)
], which is then reduced to
Tran
Thoai 1986
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{4e^{2}a\omega_{p}^{2}}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{l}\frac{(2-\delta_{m0})}{(l-m)!(l+m)!}\bigg(\frac{\omega a}{v}\bigg)^{2l}

\displaystyle\times K_{m}^{2}\bigg(\frac{\omega b}{v}\bigg)\textrm{Im}\bigg\{\frac{l\,j_{l+1}(\mu a)}{(2l+1)M_{l}}\bigg\},

(19)

where
K_{m}
is the modified Bessel function of order
m
.
Moreover, in that case it is easily proven that within the LRA limit,
\mu a\rightarrow\infty
, this expression reduces to that given by Ferrell and Echenique
Ferrell and Echenique 1985
.
On the other hand, for penetrating trajectories, within the LRA limit we obtain:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\textrm{Begr,i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4e^{2}a}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{l}\chi_{lm}\;\textrm{Im}\Big\{\eta_{l}\;[\mathcal{I}_{lm}(b,a)]^{2}\Big\},

(20)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\textrm{Begr,o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4e^{2}a}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{l}\chi_{lm}\;

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\times\textrm{Im}\Big\{2\zeta_{l}\;\mathcal{I}_{lm}(b,a)\mathcal{O}_{lm}(a,\infty)\Big\},

(21)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\textrm{ext}}

\displaystyle=\frac{4e^{2}a}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{l}\chi_{lm}\;\textrm{Im}\Big\{\zeta_{l}\;[\mathcal{O}_{lm}(a,\infty)]^{2}\Big\},

(22)

with
\chi_{lm}
defined by Eq. (
18
), and,

\displaystyle\eta_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{(l+1)(\varepsilon-1)}{\varepsilon(l\varepsilon+l+1)},

(23)

\displaystyle\zeta_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{l(1-\varepsilon)}{(l+1+l\varepsilon)},

(24)

where we have considered
\varepsilon(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\omega(\omega+i\gamma)}
.
Thus, within the LRA limit, the sum of the Begrenzung and external terms is equivalent to the expression for the surface corrections to the EEL probability given in Refs.
61
;
47
.
Notice that, as opposed to the nonlocal Begrenzung terms [Eqs. (
17c
)-(
17d
)], there are no
\mathcal{J}_{lm}
terms in their local counterparts [Eqs. (
20
)-(
21
)] as the terms containing
\mathcal{J}_{lm}
tend to zero for
\mu a\rightarrow\infty
, which illustrates the fact that the LRA cannot describe CBPs.

It is worth noting that within LRA the contribution of the unbound bulk plasmon to the losses is proportional to
\textrm{Im}[-1/\varepsilon(\omega)]
and is divergent.
To overcome such divergency a momentum cutoff,
q_{c}
, is usually introduced, which leads to the following expression of the loss probability
García de
Abajo 2010
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\textrm{LRA}}^{\textrm{bulk}}=\frac{2e^{2}L}{\pi\hbar v^{2}}\textrm{Im}\bigg\{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigg\}\log(q_{c}v/\omega)

(25)

This momentum cutoff is usually chosen to align with the typical values for the collection angle in STEM setups.
Notice that within the HDM the bulk contribution is expanded in terms of spherical harmonics, i.e., in terms of
(l,m)
-tuples as given by Eq. (
17a
) and its contribution to the total EEL probability is reduced by the Begrenzung term, Eq. (
17b
).
In this regard, few modes are necessary to converge the total
\Gamma_{\textrm{EELS}}
, and thus the momentum cutoff is naturally introduced by the
l_{\textrm{max}}
for which convergence is reached.

Eigenmodes of the spherical NP

a) Charge density isosurfaces associated to the lowest order eigenmodes of a spherical NP within the HDM in the absence of an external excitation source, obtained as a solution of Eq. (

). The number
l

The eigenmodes of a free electron gas confined to a spherical NP within the HDM are obtained from the solution of the homogeneous problem described by Eqs. (
5
) and (
7
) with
\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r},\omega)=0
, which leads to the mode condition
M_{l}=0
[see Eq.(
10b
)], well known from the literature
Fujimoto and Komaki 1968
;
Tran
Thoai 1986
;
v. Baltz et al. 1995
:

\displaystyle\omega_{p}^{2}\frac{l+1}{2l+1}j_{l+1}(\mu a)-\beta^{2}\mu^{2}j^{\prime}_{l}(\mu a)=0.

(26)

This expressions includes nonlocal effects but does not include relativistic effects (quasistatic limit).

This condition shows that the dispersion does not break the degeneracy on the azimuthal number
m
, in analogy to the LRA.
Nevertheless, the solution of Eq. (
26
) gives multiple eigenenergies
\omega_{ln}
, labeled as
n=0,1,2,3,...
, and associated to the number of nodes of the charge density in the radial direction, for each
l
polar term.

These solutions are obtained numerically and provide all the possible plasma resonances excited at a spherical NP of radius
a
for a homogeneous electron gas with Wigner-Seitz radius
r_{s}
.
In the case of
l=0
, the energies are obtained analytically as:

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{0n}^{2}}{\omega_{p}^{2}}=1+\frac{x_{0,n}^{2}}{\omega_{p}^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\beta}{a}\bigg)^{2},

(27)

where
x_{0,n}
is the
n^{\textrm{th}}
positive root of
j^{\prime}_{0}(x)
, the derivative of the
0^{\textrm{th}}
-order spherical Bessel function (assuming
\gamma=0
)
.

It is instructive to make a distinction between the first solution for each
l
(i.e.,
n=0
) and the rest of the solutions (
n>0
), as the former represents the LSPs and the latter the CBPs [see Ref.
27
for details in this regard], except for the nonphysical
(l,n)=(0,0)
mode, which violates the charge conservation principle.
Such distinction is clearly visualized in Fig.
3
, where we show the charge density distributions associated to the lowest order eigenmodes (
l=0\mbox{ - }3
, m=l,
n=0\mbox{ - }3
). The radial components,
j_{l}(\mu r)
, of the charge density associated to the modes inside the NP for
l=0\mbox{ - }3
and
n=0\mbox{ - }3
(LSPs top row, CBPs bottom three rows) are also shown in Fig.
3
(b).
They clearly show the distinct nature of the modes: LSPs (
n=0
) have an exponential decay of the associated charge density from the surface into the NP, while the CBPs (
n>0
) present
n
oscillations of the charge density inside the NP.
As we will describe in the next section, the excitation of these modes by the penetrating electron probe can be understood in terms of the symmetry of these eigenmodes, characterized by the
(l,m,n)
-tuples.
Nevertheless, due to its degeneracy in energy we will omit the number
m
from these lines onward, and we will refer to the modes as
(l,n)
unless otherwise stated.

Results

The low-energy range of the spectra in Fig.

Contrary to what is obtained within the LRA for a metallic spherical NP, within the HDM the LSP modes are not bounded from above by the local surface plasmon energy,

The former one,

In contrast, the contribution of

Likewise, the external term,
\Gamma^{\textrm{ext}}

et al

Furthermore, the analytical expression of the EEL probability [Eq. (

In the following sections we proceed to calculate and analyze the dependence of the EEL probability on those parameters that are typically studied in experiments: the electron probe’s impact parameter and velocity, and the size of the NP.

III.3
Size effects in the excitation of CBPs

Once the symmetry rules governing the excitation of the LSP and CBP modes are established, we focus on the blueshift of the BPE with increasing impact parameters observed in Fig.

IV
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have presented a non-local analytical description of the EEL probability for electron beams probing sub-5 nm spherical NPs within a linear HDM.
The derived analytic expressions in Eqs. (
16
) and (
17a
)-(
17e
) describe the EEL spectra in terms of the multipoles for both penetrating and external electron trajectories, and allow for identifying the nature of the Begrenzung effect and its role in the excitation of CBPs for penetrating trajectories.
We have applied this approach to sodium NPs as an example of a free electron gas response and proved that, contrary to external electron beam trajectories, penetrating trajectories can efficiently excite both LSPs and CBPs.
Our expression unveils two main characteristics of the BPE, in agreement with
ab initio
calculations and experiments, which are not reproduced by the widely used local approaches.
First, we show that there is a threshold impact parameter
b<a
to efficiently activate the BPE.
Second, there is a blueshift of the BPE as the electron beam impact parameter moves from the center of the NP towards the surface,
b\rightarrow a
.
Indeed, we show that the EEL probability greatly depends on the electron beam’s impact parameter.
Moreover, we find that the excitation of CBPs obey subtler symmetry rules than those identified for the excitation LSPs, which are related to the path of the electron beam within the NP and the symmetry of the excited modes.
The sensitivity of CBPs to the impact parameter of the incoming electron beam trajectory explains this effective blueshift of the bulk peak observed experimentally in Al disks
Hobbs et al. 2016
and Bi NPs
Borja-Urby et al. 2019
for increasing impact parameters, which we relate to the excitation of higher-order CBPs for penetrating trajectories close to the NP’s surface.
Additionally, we show that the excitation of the CBPs in EEL spectra also depend on the electron beam kinetic energy.
In axial trajectories the relative intensity of each CBP mode is maximized when the
n
-number of the mode matches the reduced wavenumber
\widetilde{q}
of the electron beam.
We also explore the effect of NP size on EELS.
As the NP decreases, the increasing blueshift and energy-splitting of the modes produces a decrease in the threshold impact parameter
\widetilde{b}_{t}
and a greater relative blueshift for increasing impact parameters.
This study is a further step in understanding the dependence of the excitation of CBPs on the parameters of the electron beam, which will allow the characterization of more intricate EEL spectra obtained within sensitive experiments or
ab initio
atomistic TDDFT calculations
Urbieta et al. 2024
;
Candelas et al. 2025
.

Induced charge density

To obtain the charge density induced in the NP by the electron beam we solve Eq. (
5
) by applying the BC given by Eq. (
7
).
Exploiting the spherical symmetry of the problem, the solution is written in terms of spherical harmonics,
Y_{l}^{m}(\Omega)
. Omitting explicitly writing the
\omega
-dependence of the terms [
\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r},\omega)\Rightarrow\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r})
] we have:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{lm}(r)Y_{l}^{m}(\Omega).

(29)

where
\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{lm}(r)
is the radial component of the induced charge density.
On the other hand, the expansion of the
external
charge density (Eq. (
1
)) in terms of the spherical harmonics is given by:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r)Y_{l}^{m}(\Omega),

(30)

where,

\displaystyle\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\,\alpha_{lm}}{vr^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{b^{2}}{r^{2}}}}\;\Pi_{lm}(r),

(31)

\displaystyle\alpha_{lm}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}},

(32)

\displaystyle\Pi_{lm}(r)

\displaystyle=g_{lm}\bigg(\frac{\omega}{v}\sqrt{r^{2}-b^{2}}\bigg)P_{l}^{m}\bigg(\sqrt{1-\frac{b^{2}}{r^{2}}}\bigg),

(33)

and,

\displaystyle g_{lm}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\cos(x),&\textrm{ if }l+m\textrm{ is even},\\
i\sin(x),&\textrm{ if }l+m\textrm{ is odd}.\end{cases}

(34)

In Eq. (
33
),
P_{l}^{m}(x)
is the associated Legendre polynomial of degree
l
and order
m
.
Such decomposition allows us to write the BC at
r=a
as Eq. (
8
) of the main text, and the radial part of the solution now satisfies the spherical Bessel differential equation:

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}+\mu^{2}\bigg]\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{lm}(r)=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r).

(35)

Moreover, the induced charge density can be separated into two parts:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r})=\varrho^{h}(\textbf{r})+\varrho^{p}(\textbf{r}).

(36)

The
homogeneous
solution,
\varrho^{h}(\textbf{r})
, satisfies the homogeneous Helmholtz equation:

\displaystyle[\nabla^{2}+\mu^{2}]\varrho^{h}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=0

(37)

with the inhomogeneous BC for
l>0
, given by Eq. (
8
):

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2l+1}\frac{l+1}{a^{l+2}}\int_{0}^{a}dr\,r^{l+2}\varrho_{lm}^{h}(r)-\beta^{2}\frac{\partial\varrho_{lm}^{h}(r)}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=a}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2l+1}\bigg\{-\frac{l+1}{a^{l+2}}\int_{0}^{a}dr\,r^{l+2}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r)

\displaystyle+l\,a^{l-1}\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^{l-1}}\varrho_{lm}^{\textrm{ext}}(r)\bigg\}.

(38)

On the other hand, the
particular
solution,
\varrho_{lm}^{p}(\textbf{r})
, satisfies the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation:

\displaystyle[\nabla^{2}+\mu^{2}]\varrho^{p}(\textbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\varrho^{\textrm{ext}}(\textbf{r}),

(39)

with homogeneous BC for
l>0
:

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{2l+1}\frac{l+1}{a^{l+2}}\int_{0}^{a}dr\,r^{l+2}\varrho_{lm}^{p}(r)

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt-\beta^{2}\frac{\partial\varrho_{lm}^{p}(r)}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=a}=0.

(40)

Moreover, the general solution must also satisfy the
charge conservation principle
, i.e., the net induced charge in the volume of the NP,
V
, has to be zero:

\displaystyle\int_{V}d\textbf{r}\,\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}(\textbf{r})=0.

(41)

Due to the symmetry of the
l>0
terms of the spherical harmonics, the contribution of the
l>0
terms of the full solution to this integral is trivially zero, i.e., only the
\varrho_{00}(r)
has a non-zero contribution.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}dr\,\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{00}(r)\,r^{2}=0,

(42)

which leaves the BC for the

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varrho^{\textrm{ind}}_{00}(r)}{\partial r}\bigg|_{r=a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{a^{2}\beta^{2}}\int_{0}^{a}\varrho_{00}^{\textrm{ext}}(r)\,r^{2}dr.

(43)

The particular solution is obtained through the Green’s function that must satisfy,

\displaystyle[\nabla^{2}+\mu^{2}]\mathcal{G}(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime})=\delta(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime}).

(44)

Exploiting the spherical symmetry of the problem the Green’s function is decomposed as:

(45)

Kim and Jackson 1993
