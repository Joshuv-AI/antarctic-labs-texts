Clinical Outcomes for Patients who Received Both Trastuzumab Deruxtecan (T-DXd) and Trastuzumab Emtansine (T-DM1) for HER2-Mutant non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Daniel Reinhorn, Jonathan Kuten, Isabel Preeshagul, Juliana Eng, Kenneth Ng, Alexander Drilon, Jamie Chaft, Fernando C. Santini, Mark M. Awad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) and trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) are HER2-targeting antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) with activity in HER2-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Whether patients benefit from sequential HER2-directed ADCs therapy remains unknown.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study of patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC who received both T-DXd and T-DM1 in either order. Clinical characteristics, confirmed overall response rate, real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS), and overall survival (OS) were evaluated. All time-to-event endpoints were calculated from the start of the second ADC.ResultsTwenty-five patients were identified: 17 received T-DM1 post-T-DXd and 8 received T-DXd post-T-DM1. The median age for the entire cohort was 67 years; 56% were female. Most patients (21/25, 84%) harbored HER2 kinase-domain mutations, while 3 had extracellular and 1 had transmembrane domain alterations. The median number of prior systemic therapies before the second ADC was three. Seventeen patients (68%) received the two ADCs in direct sequence without other intervening therapies. In the T-DM1 post-T-DXd cohort, the median rwPFS was 3.3 months (95% CI, 1.9–5.6) and the median OS was 9.2 months (95% CI, 4.9–NR). No confirmed responses were observed; however, two patients (12%) achieved unconfirmed partial responses, and one had prolonged stable disease for 13.9 months. In the T-DXd post-T-DM1 cohort, the median rwPFS was 5.2 months (95% CI, 4.1–NR) and the median OS was 25.0 months (95% CI, 8.2–NR); three patients (37.5%) achieved confirmed partial responses.ConclusionsT-DXd retains clinical activity after prior T-DM1 use, whereas T-DM1 has limited efficacy after prior T-DXd in HER2-mutant NSCLC. Understanding the mechanisms of response and resistance to ADCs will hopefully help to inform treatment strategies using sequential ADCs in lung cancer and other malignancies.

Introduction

Mutations in the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2, ERBB2) gene occur in approximately 2–4% of lung adenocarcinomas1 and represent a distinct molecular subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The majority of HER2 mutations are in-frame insertions in exon 20 of the tyrosine kinase domain, with Y772_A775dup (A775_G776insYVMA) being the most frequently observed variant, accounting for 50–80% of all HER2 mutations. Less common alterations include missense mutations in exons 19 and 20 of the kinase domain, as well as extracellular domain mutations, such as S310F.2, 3, 4

HER2-targeting therapies have revolutionized treatment approaches in breast and gastric cancers5,6, but therapeutic options for HER2-mutant NSCLC were limited until recently. Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) linking an anti-HER2 antibody to a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor, received accelerated approval from the FDA for previously treated HER2-mutant NSCLC7 based on the DESTINY-Lung01 trial, which demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 55% with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 8.2 months and a median overall survival (mOS) of 17.8 months.8 The DESTINY-Lung02 trial showed comparable outcomes with T-DXd doses of 5.4 and 6.4 mg/kg, with lower toxicity at 5.4 mg/kg, establishing it as the FDA-approved dose.9 Trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) is a distinct HER2 ADC that links trastuzumab to the cytotoxic microtubule inhibitor MMAE. T-DM1 is FDA-approved for the treatment of metastatic and early stage HER2-amplified breast cancer10,11 but has not gained approval for lung cancer. T-DM1 has shown clinical activity in HER2-mutant NSCLC, although its efficacy appears to be more limited compared to T-DXd. A basket trial evaluating T-DM1 in HER2-mutant and HER2-amplified NSCLC reported an ORR of 51%, with similar response rates observed across HER2 amplification and mutations, including exon 20 insertions and point mutations in various HER2 domains. The median PFS in this trial was 5 months.12 A separate phase 2 trial further supported these findings, reporting an ORR of 38.1% and underscoring the role of T-DM1 in this patient population.13

Given that T-DXd and T-DM1 utilize distinct cytotoxic payloads, the sequential use of these agents may be considered in clinical practice, either following disease progression on one agent or after treatment discontinuation due to adverse events such as T-DXd-related pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease (ILD). However, the optimal sequencing and potential cross-resistance between these agents need to be defined. Recently, two new ADCs, datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd)14 and telisotuzumab vedotin15, have been approved for the treatment of advanced lung adenocarcinoma, targeting different antigens (TROP2 and cMET, respectively). The growing landscape of ADCs in lung cancer and specifically in HER2-mutant lung cancer introduces challenges in defining the optimal sequencing of these drugs. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective analysis aimed at evaluating clinical outcomes in patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC who received sequential therapy with T-DM1 and T-DXd in either treatment order.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This was a single-center retrospective study conducted at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center between January 2018 and December 2024 and included adult patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC who received treatment with T-DXd followed by T-DM1 or T-DM1 followed by T-DXd as part of routine clinical care or clinical trials; other intervening systemic treatments between the two ADCs were allowed. We queried our institutional clinical database to identify patients aged ≥18 years with NSCLC harboring an oncogenic HER2 mutation who received both T-DXd and T-DM1 in either sequence. Patients were included if they received ≥1 dose of each agent. Patients who did not receive both agents were excluded, as were patients with HER2 fusions or HER2 amplification without a concurrent HER2 mutation. This retrospective analysis was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, protocol # 24–286. The requirement for informed consent was waived, given the retrospective nature of the study and minimal risk to patients.

Data Collection, Assessments, and Statistical Analysis

Clinical and demographic data were collected, including patient age, sex, race, HER2 mutation type (kinase domain, extracellular domain, or transmembrane domain), presence of concurrent HER2 amplification (when available, as determined by next-generation sequencing), presence of HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC; when available), other genetic alterations, previous and subsequent treatments, start and end dates of first ADC (ADC1) and second ADC (ADC2), and reasons for discontinuation. Treatment-related clinical outcomes, including real-world progression-free survival (rwPFS), overall survival (OS), overall response rates, duration of response, and time on treatment (ToT), were abstracted from patient medical records. The rwPFS was defined as the time from initiation of ADC2 to clinical or radiographic progression or death from any cause. Disease progression was identified based on radiology reports and physician notes. Patients were censored at the start of subsequent treatments if no progression had occurred before a change in treatment. Overall response rate was defined as the proportion of patients achieving a partial or complete response based on retrospective review of imaging using RECIST criteria 1.1. In cases where imaging was not available for retrospective review, radiology reports and treating physician notes were used to assess response. A response was considered confirmed if persistent tumor shrinkage was observed on a follow-up scan performed more than 30 days after the initial response. Duration of response was defined as the time from documented radiological response to disease progression or death. ToT was defined as the difference between the date of the first dose and the date of the last dose of treatment. If a patient received only one dose, then ToT was calculated as 1 day. OS was defined as the time from initiation of ADC2 therapy to death from any cause, with patients censored at the date of last follow-up.6

Continuous variables were summarized using medians and ranges, and categorical variables were presented using counts and percentages. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to estimate rwPFS and OS; median survival times and survival rates at 6 and 12 months were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.2). All tests were two-sided, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Treatment Outcomes

T-DM1 Post-T-DXd Cohort

In the T-DM1 post-T-DXd cohort (n = 17), T-DXd was discontinued due to disease progression in 10 patients and toxicity in 7 patients, all of whom were suspected cases of ILD or pneumonitis. Regarding dosing, 9 patients (53%) initiated T-DXd at 6.4 mg/kg and 8 (47%) at 5.4 mg/kg; 12 of the 17 patients (71%) required at least one dose reduction during T-DXd therapy. The median time on T-DXd was 6.5 months (range, 0–24), and 12 patients (71%) achieved a partial or complete response (11 confirmed, 1 unconfirmed). Most patients (13/17) received the ADCs in direct succession; one patient received intervening platinum-based chemotherapy and pembrolizumab; one received intervening gemcitabine; one received atezolizumab; and one received afatinib (Supplement Table 1). Three patients received T-DM1 in combination with afatinib; all others (n = 14) received T-DM1 as monotherapy.

The median time on treatment with T-DM1 was 2.8 months (95% CI, 2.1–4.7; range, 0–13.7). The probability of remaining on treatment at 3 months was 47% (95% CI, 28–78%), declining to 12% (95% CI, 3.2–43%) at 6 months. All 17 patients received T-DM1 at the standard dose of 3.6 mg/kg, with 3 patients (18%) requiring dose reduction. Treatment was discontinued due to disease progression or death in 13 patients, toxicity in three (one due to hepatic and hematological toxicity, one due to diarrhea, and one due to a severe infusion reaction), and patient preference in one case. No patients remained on therapy at last follow-up. Treatment durations are illustrated in the swimmer plot (Figure 2). No confirmed responses were observed, although 2 patients (12%) had unconfirmed partial responses to T-DM1 after prior T-DXd (Figure 3A); in the first case, the patient received T-DM1 in combination with afatinib but discontinued both treatments after two cycles due to toxicity. This patient subsequently experienced disease progression and died within four months of treatment discontinuation. A second patient achieved a partial response but died from an unknown cause within 4 weeks of achieving a response. Among the remaining patients, 9 achieved stable disease as their best response, including one with prolonged stability for 13.9 months and another who demonstrated a metabolic response on PET-CT but had stable disease by RECIST for 6 months before progression. Five patients had primary progressive disease, and one was not evaluable, having discontinued therapy after a single dose due to toxicity and subsequently transitioned to a HER2 TKI through a clinical trial. The median rwPFS was 3.3 months (95% CI, 1.9–5.6), with a 6-month rwPFS rate of 13% (95% CI, 1–44%) (Figure 4). 9 of 17 patients (53%) had CNS metastases at the time of T-DM1 initiation, all of whom had been previously treated. The best intracranial response was stable disease in 5 patients and progressive disease in 4; 7 of 9 patients (78%) experienced intracranial progression during T-DM1 therapy.

7 patients (41%) experienced T-DXd-related pneumonitis or ILD prior to initiating T-DM1, which was the reason for T-DXd discontinuation in all seven cases: 2 events were grade 1–2 and 5 were grade 3–4. All were managed with corticosteroids, and all patients achieved clinical improvement; 3 had complete radiographic and clinical resolution, while 4 had mild residual radiological or clinical findings. No cases of pneumonitis were attributed to subsequent T-DM1 therapy in this cohort. In this subgroup, no objective responses were observed. In this group, the median time on treatment was 3.4 months, and the median PFS was 2.5 months (95% CI, 1.2–NE; data not shown).

T-DXd Post-T-DM1 Cohort

In the T-DXd post-T-DM1 cohort (n = 8), all patients had discontinued T-DM1 because of disease progression. All 8 patients initiated T-DM1 at 3.6 mg/kg, with 2 patients (25%) requiring a dose reduction. The median time on T-DM1 was 5.7 months (range, 1.0–12.8); confirmed responses were observed in 3 of 8 patients. Four patients (50%) received T-DXd directly after T-DM1, while one patient received intervening cisplatin-gemcitabine, one received intervening carboplatin-pemetrexed-bevacizumab, another received intervening carboplatin-pemetrexed-pembrolizumab, and another received intervening atezolizumab (Supplement Table 2).

The median time on treatment with T-DXd was 4.6 months (95% CI, 3.9–NE). The probability of remaining on treatment was 88% (95% CI, 67–100%) at 3 months and 38% (95% CI, 15–92%) at 6 months. T-DXd was initiated at 5.4 mg/kg in 6 patients (75%) and at 6.4 mg/kg in 2 patients (25%); 5 patients (63%) required at least one dose reduction, including both patients who initiated at 6.4 mg/kg and three who initiated at 5.4 mg/kg. Six patients discontinued treatment due to disease progression. One patient remains on therapy nearly four years after initiation, and one patient discontinued due to death secondary to pulmonary infection or inflammation without confirmed disease progression. Confirmed partial responses were achieved in 3 patients (37%) (Figure 3B); 3 had stable disease, including one patient with a metabolic response per PET-CT. One patient had progressive disease as the best response, and another was not evaluable due to early death, presumed to be related to pulmonary infection or inflammation. The median rwPFS was 5.2 months (95% CI, 4.1–NE), with a 6-month rwPFS rate of 38% (95% CI, 15–92%) (Figure 4). The median OS was 25.0 months (95% CI, 8.0–not reached), with 6-month and 12-month OS rates of 75% (95% CI, 50–100%) and 63% (95% CI, 37–100%), respectively (Figure 5).

4 of 8 patients (50%) had CNS metastases at the time of T-DXd initiation, all of which were previously treated. The best intracranial response was stable disease in 2 patients, progressive disease in 1, and not evaluable in 1; 2 of the patients with initial stable disease subsequently experienced intracranial progression during treatment.

No cases of pneumonitis or ILD were observed during T-DXd or during prior T-DM1 therapy in this cohort.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated clinical outcomes in patients with advanced HER2-mutant NSCLC who were treated sequentially with the ADCs T-DM1 and T-DXd in either order. T-DM1 appears to have marginal efficacy when administered after treatment with T-DXd, whereas T-DXd demonstrated more clinical activity after T-DM1, with outcomes on par with those in the DESTINY-Lung trials, which excluded patients with prior HER2 ADCs.

Physicians may consider the use of off-label T-DM1 after failure of T-DXd, whether following disease progression or after treatment discontinuation due to drug-related pneumonitis or interstitial lung disease.17 This study provides evidence that this approach may have limited efficacy, but only in a minority of cases. Notably, even among the 40% of patients who discontinued T-DXd due to adverse events before initiating T-DM1, the subsequent efficacy of T-DM1 remained limited. Nevertheless, two patients achieved unconfirmed partial responses to T-DM1, suggesting that distinct mechanisms of resistance to these ADCs may exist, given their different cytotoxic payloads. Reassuringly, no cases of pneumonitis were observed with T-DM1 following prior T-DXd-related pulmonary toxicity, suggesting non-overlapping pulmonary toxicity profiles between these two agents.

In contrast, the use of T-DXd following T-DM1 is uncommon in current clinical practice. T-DXd is FDA-approved and recommended in treatment guidelines for HER2-mutant NSCLC as standard second-line therapy after platinum-based chemotherapy and is commonly used earlier in the treatment sequence.18 However, global access to T-DXd remains inconsistent due to variations in reimbursement policies and drug pricing across different geographic regions. In regions where T-DXd is not readily available or reimbursed, clinicians may resort to off-label use of T-DM1, which may be more accessible for some patients.

The mechanisms underlying resistance to HER2 ADCs in NSCLC remain incompletely understood. Possible mechanisms include receptor-mediated resistance, likely overlapping between T-DXd and T-DM1, as well as intracellular resistance mechanisms that are potentially specific to the payload. Most insights into T-DXd resistance mechanisms originate from the breast cancer literature, where T-DXd is widely used.19,20 Limited data exist regarding resistance specifically in NSCLC. However, key differences between breast cancer (characterized predominantly by HER2 overexpression and/or amplification) and NSCLC (characterized predominantly by HER2 mutations) suggest that distinct resistance mechanisms may exist.

Several breast cancer studies with paired pre-T-DXd and post-T-DXd biopsies have demonstrated decreased HER2 expression in 49–65% of cases at progression, with approximately a quarter of cases in one study exhibiting complete HER2 loss21,22, a pattern consistent with similar reductions in HER2 expression and amplification observed in HER2-directed ADC-resistant breast cancer cell lines.23 However, in lung cancer, IHC) = HER2 overexpression does not correlate with T-DXd efficacy in HER2-mutated cases, and numerous responses occur even in HER2-mutant cases lacking HER2 expression by IHC.8 The increased binding and trafficking of the receptor-payload complex in HER2-mutated cases, as demonstrated in preclinical T-DM1 studies24, may explain the activity of ADCs even when HER2 protein levels on the surface are low. It remains unclear whether loss or downregulation of HER2 expression levels contributes significantly to resistance in NSCLC.

Additional receptor-mediated resistance mechanisms include epitope loss or masking, which reduces antibody binding efficacy. For instance, truncated HER2 variants (e.g., p95HER2), which lack the extracellular domain and are undetectable by HER2-directed antibodies, may confer resistance. These truncated forms have been implicated in both de novo and acquired resistance in breast cancer.25 Such truncated HER2 proteins have also been identified in resected NSCLC, although they are rarely associated with HER2 mutations,26 and their role in acquired resistance in NSCLC remains unknown. Other mechanisms described in breast cancer include rare point mutations in the ECD of ERBB2 that confer de novo and acquired resistance to T-DXd.22 To our knowledge, such mutations have not been reported in NSCLC. Other resistance mechanisms include mutations in the DNA-repair gene SLX4, which were identified in 14% of biopsies at progression and are associated with deruxtecan resistance in breast cancer cell lines.21 Conversely, activation of DNA damage response pathways has also been linked to ADC resistance.27 Further studies are needed to clarify the relevance of these mechanisms in NSCLC.

Some resistance mechanisms may be overcome with TKIs that target the intracellular kinase domain, which remains unaffected by extracellular changes. Next-generation HER2-directed therapies, such as zongertinib and sevabertinib, have demonstrated promising efficacy in HER2-mutant NSCLC previously treated with HER2-targeted ADCs; in these patients, these TKIs achieved ORRs ranging from 38% to 48%, suggesting limited cross-resistance between ADCs and HER2-directed TKIs.28,29 Therefore, TKIs might offer superior efficacy post-T-DXd compared with T-DM1. Another promising future treatment approach may involve combining ADCs with TKIs to enhance receptor internalization and trafficking, which has shown synergistic potential in preclinical models.24 This concept is supported by a response to T-DM1 post-T-DXd in our series, which involved combination therapy with afatinib; however, this combination was not well tolerated by the patient who received this treatment. Newer-generation TKIs, known for their reduced toxicity, may provide viable options for combination therapies that should be evaluated in the context of a clinical trial.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, small sample size, and heterogeneity in prior treatments. Radiographic responses were retrospectively assessed, and radiologic modalities and intervals were not standardized. Systematic intracranial imaging was not uniformly performed at defined intervals, limiting the completeness of CNS response data. Additionally, the two cohorts were not formally balanced in terms of baseline disease characteristics, including CNS and liver involvement, which may have influenced PFS and OS. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable real-world insights into the activity of sequential HER2-targeted ADC therapy in a patient population with limited treatment options. The limited efficacy of T-DM1 following T-DXd in HER2-mutant NSCLC underscores the need for novel therapeutic approaches. Conversely, T-DXd demonstrates greater effectiveness following T-DM1 in HER2-mutant NSCLC. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the efficacy of sequential ADC in lung cancer. With the growing landscape of ADCs in lung cancer, including some with overlapping payloads (such as T-DXd and Dato-DXd) or targets (such as T-DXd and T-DM1), we need to better define the resistance mechanisms and sequential efficacy to optimize outcomes.
