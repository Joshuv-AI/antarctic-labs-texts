Eigengap Sparsity for Covariance Parsimony
Tom Szwagier ( ✉ ) {}^{(\text{{\char 0\relax}})}, Guillaume Olikier, Xavier Pennec

Abstract
Covariance estimation is a central problem in statistics. An important issue is that there are rarely enough samples n n to accurately estimate the p ⁡ ( p + 1 ) / 2 p(p+1)/2 coefficients in dimension p p . Parsimonious covariance models are therefore preferred, but the discrete nature of model selection makes inference computationally challenging. In this paper, we propose a relaxation of covariance parsimony termed “eigengap sparsity” and motivated by the good accuracy–parsimony tradeoffs of eigenvalue-equalization in covariance matrices. This penalty can be included in a penalized-likelihood framework that we propose to solve with a projected gradient descent on a monotone cone. The algorithm turns out to resemble an isotonic regression of mutually-attracted sample eigenvalues, drawing an interesting link between covariance parsimony and shrinkage.



1
Introduction

The
principle of parsimony
, also known as Occam’s razor (“The simplest explanation is usually the best one.”), is central in statistics. It implies that for two competing statistical models with similar likelihoods, one should choose the model with fewer parameters. It can be quantitatively achieved via model selection criteria such as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
[
25
]
,
or the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)
[
1
]
.
Since the seminal LASSO paper
[
31
]
, it has been common to investigate parsimonious model estimation as a regularized optimization problem with a
sparsity-inducing penalty

[
2
]
.
Sparsity involves
\ell^{0}
-norms and leads to hard combinatorial problems, which is why it is classically
relaxed
with
\ell^{1}
-norms, enjoying convergence and sparsity guarantees
[
2
]
.

The principle of parsimony finds a particular interest in the fundamental problem of
covariance estimation

[
23
]
. Indeed, in dimension
p
, covariance matrices have
p(p+1)/2
independent parameters, which makes them often
overparameterized
with respect to the number
n
of available samples.
The
sample covariance matrix
—which is the maximum likelihood estimate of the population covariance matrix—then suffers from many
sampling errors
.
A typical high-dimensional phenomenon is the Marčenko–Pastur law
[
20
]
, which describes an important dispersion of the sample eigenvalues around their true value. This
eigenvalue-spreading
phenomenon
[
14
]
is illustrated in the seminal paper of Ledoit and Wolf
[
17
, Fig. 4]
and motivates the idea of
shrinking
eigenvalues towards a mean value. To that extent, many regularized covariance estimation methods have emerged, notably involving shrinkage
[
26
,
17
,
9
,
18
]
, sparsity
[
13
,
10
]
, Bayesian methods
[
36
,
21
]
and random matrix theory
[
15
]
.

In this paper, we propose to tackle the problem of covariance estimation with a
penalized-likelihood
optimization on
positive-definite
matrices:

\hat{\Sigma}\in\argmin_{\Sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)}\,\left(-2\ln\mathcal{L}(\Sigma)+\alpha\operatorname{dim}(\Sigma)\right),

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)\to\mathbb{R}
is a likelihood function (e.g. Gaussian
[
34
,
29
]
, elliptical
[
8
]
or Gaussian mixture
[
33
,
7
,
27
]
),
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a constant (like
~{\alpha_{n}=\ln n}
for the BIC,
\alpha=2
for the AIC or any tuning hyperparameter) and
\operatorname{dim}\colon\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is a penalty on the number of covariance parameters, i.e. the dimension of the submanifold of
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
to which
\Sigma
belongs, as properly defined later.
There are many ways to partition the space of covariance matrices into submanifolds (e.g. via coefficient-wise sparsity
[
10
]
or rank constraints
[
2
]
). The one that we defend in this paper is the stratification of covariance matrices by the
multiplicities of the eigenvalues

[
11
]
.
This original notion of parsimony is strongly motivated by the recent principal subspace analysis methodology
[
29
]
(equalizing close eigenvalues yields a
quadratic
decrease in the number of parameters without much decreasing the likelihood) which is a natural extension of the seminal probabilistic principal component analysis (PPCA) of Tipping and Bishop
[
34
]
.

This paper is organized as follows.
In section
2
, we study the aforementioned stratification of the optimization space
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
, which enables to formally define the penalty
\operatorname{dim}
in terms of eigenvalue multiplicities. We deduce that
\operatorname{dim}
is constant on fixed-multiplicities submanifolds of
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
, which drastically simplifies the optimization problem. The bad news, however, is that solving the latter would require solving
2^{p-1}
(unpenalized) optimization problems, which is computationally prohibitive, even in moderate dimensions. To that extent, we propose in section
3
a relaxation of the penalty.
This
\ell^{1}
-relaxation is based on the intriguing result that the number of covariance parameters can be
exactly
rewritten as an
\ell^{0}
-norm of eigenvalue gaps. We will subsequently refer to our parsimony-inducing penalty as
eigengap sparsity
.
We study the relaxed problem in the Gaussian setting in section
4
and propose a projected gradient descent algorithm on the monotone cone of eigenvalues to address it. The algorithm draws an interesting link between covariance parsimony and shrinkage methods
[
18
]
, and it shows promising results in synthetic experiments in section
5
.

2
Stratification of
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
by Eigenvalue Multiplicities

Let
\Sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
eigendecompose as
\Sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}v_{j}{v_{j}}^{\top}
with
~{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}
the eigenvalues (ordered-decreasing and positive) and
(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})\in\mathrm{O}(p)
some associated eigenvectors (
\mathrm{O}(p)
denotes the orthogonal group). If all the eigenvalues are
simple
, then we need
p
parameters to describe the eigenvalues and
\operatorname{dim}(\mathrm{O}(p))=p(p-1)/2
parameters to describe the eigenvectors, resulting in a total of
\operatorname{dim}(\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p))=p(p+1)/2
covariance parameters to estimate.
Otherwise, some eigenvalues are
multiple
, therefore we need fewer parameters not only for these eigenvalues, but also for the associated eigenspaces—since we quotient the rotational invariance of the eigenvectors within the eigenspace they span.

More precisely, let
\mathrm{C}(p)\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{d}\colon\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}=p,\,d\in[\![1,p]\!]\}
be the set of
compositions
of
p

[
4
]
.
Let
\gamma\coloneqq(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathrm{C}(p)
,
q_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\gamma_{l}
, and

\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma)\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\colon\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{q_{1}}>\lambda_{q_{1}+1}=\dots=\lambda_{q_{2}}>\dots>0\}

(2)

be the set of
ordered-decreasing

piecewise-constant

p
-tuples of positive reals, whose pieces have respective sizes
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{d}
.
By eigenvalue decomposition,

\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)\coloneqq\left\{V\mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda\right)V^{\top}\colon\lambda\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma),V\in\mathrm{O}(p)\right\}

(3)

is the set of symmetric positive-definite matrices with eigenvalue multiplicities
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}
. It is a
submanifold
of
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
of
dimension

d+(p^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}^{2})/2
.

Remark 1

The theory behind this result is deep and has been worked out in
[
11
]
. Let us still give a concise justification.
Let
\mathrm{Fl}(\gamma)
be the
flag manifold
of
type

\gamma
, i.e. the space of mutually-orthogonal linear subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{V}_{d}
of respective dimensions
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}

[
37
,
28
]
. Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}
denote the orthogonal projection matrix onto
\mathcal{V}_{k}
. Then the eigendecomposition map
\lambda,\mathcal{V}\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{q_{k}}\Pi_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}
induces a diffeomorphism between
\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma)\times\mathrm{Fl}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
. The submanifold and dimension properties then follow from those of
\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma)
and
\mathrm{Fl}(\gamma)
.

The previous result tells us that
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
can be
stratified
into submanifolds
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
of dimension
d+(p^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}^{2})/2
, as
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)=\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\mathrm{C}(p)}\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
. Therefore, for any
\Sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
, one can define
\operatorname{dim}(\Sigma)
as the dimension of the
unique
stratum
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
it belongs to.
This implies that the
penalized
covariance estimation problem (
1
) can be replaced with a set of
unpenalized
optimization problems on smooth matrix manifolds
[
37
]
.

In the Gaussian case, problem (
1
) even has an
explicit
solution, referred to as
principal subspace analysis

[
29
]
.
1
1

1

Principal subspace analysis was originally introduced to address a
curse of isotropy
in PCA, in which close sample eigenvalues yield unstable principal components.

On each stratum
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
, the maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{\Sigma}(\gamma)
consists in the eigenvalue decomposition of the sample covariance matrix followed by a
\gamma
-block-averaging of the eigenvalues. Therefore, problem (
1
) is equivalent to

\argmin_{\gamma\in\mathrm{C}(p)}\,\left(-2\ln\mathcal{L}\bigl(\hat{\Sigma}(\gamma)\bigr)+\alpha\,\left(d+\frac{p^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}^{2}}{2}\right)\right),

(4)

which can be solved
exactly
as
\mathrm{C}(p)
is finite.
Unfortunately,
\#\mathrm{C}(p)=2^{p-1}
, so that solving exactly the problem is computationally prohibitive, even in moderate dimensions.
And this is not to mention non-Gaussian distributions, which do not even have an explicit maximum likelihood estimate of the covariance.

3
Relaxation via Eigengap Sparsity

We want to relax problem (
1
) in order to make it computationally tractable. The first step consists in rewriting the parsimony-inducing penalty
\operatorname{dim}
as
\ell^{0}
-norms of spectral gaps.

Theorem 3.1

Let
\gamma\coloneqq(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathrm{C}(p)
and
\Sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma)
with eigenvalues
~{\lambda\coloneqq(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma)}
. Let
\delta\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
s.t.
\delta(a,b)=0
iff
a=b
. One has

\operatorname{dim}(\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(\gamma))=1+\left\lVert\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})_{1\leq s<p}\right\rVert_{0}+\left\lVert\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})_{1\leq s<t\leq p}\right\rVert_{0}.

(5)

Proof

By definition of
\lambda\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(\gamma)
, the eigenvalues can be divided into
d
blocks of equal eigenvalues, of respective sizes
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d}
.
Let
q_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\gamma_{l}
.
Then one has
\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})=0\iff\exists k\in[\![1,d]\!]\colon(s,t)\in{]\!]q_{k-1},q_{k}]\!]}^{2}
.
Consequently, one has

\left\lVert\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})_{1\leq s<p}\right\rVert_{0}\coloneqq\#\{s\colon\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})\neq 0\}=\#\{k\colon\lambda_{q_{k}}\neq\lambda_{q_{k}+1}\}=d-1.

Similarly, one has

\left\lVert\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})_{1\leq s<t\leq p}\right\rVert_{0}=\#\{(s,t)\colon s<t,\,\lambda_{s}\neq\lambda_{t}\}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}\sum_{l=k+1}^{d}\gamma_{l}.

The last quantity can be rewritten by double summation inversion as

\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq d}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}\left(p-\gamma_{k}-\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\gamma_{l}\right)=p^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}^{2}-\sum_{1\leq l<k\leq d}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}.

Hence,
\left\lVert\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})_{1\leq s<t\leq p}\right\rVert_{0}=(p^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\gamma_{k}^{2})/2
, which concludes the proof. ∎

Although relatively simple, Theorem
3.1
is quite remarkable.
While one may have thought of penalizing the successive gaps between eigenvalues (with total-variation-like penalties
[
32
,
12
]
), our theorem conveys a greater message: to account for the parsimony of the
eigenspaces
too, one should also penalize the gaps between non-adjacent eigenvalues. In other words, covariance parsimony tends to make
all
the eigenvalues attracted to each other, which is reminiscent of the recent findings of Ledoit and Wolf in shrinkage estimation
[
18
]
.
One can now naturally relax problem (
1
) by replacing the
\ell^{0}
-norm with the
\ell^{1}
-norm.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)=\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\colon\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}>0\}
denote the
positive monotone cone
. Then the
\ell^{1}
-relaxation of problem (
1
) is

\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathrm{O}(p)\\
\lambda\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)\end{subarray}}\biggl(-2\ln\mathcal{L}\left(V\mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda\right)V^{\top}\right)+\alpha\,\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}\biggl(\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})+\sum_{t=s+1}^{p}\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})\biggr)\biggr).

(6)

The relaxed problem (
6
) can be solved with classical constrained optimization algorithms, for instance alternating between the update of the eigenvectors in
V\in\mathrm{O}(p)
and the eigenvalues in
\lambda\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)
.
In the remainder of the paper, we focus on the fundamental case where
\mathcal{L}
is a Gaussian likelihood.

4
Projected Gradient Descent for Gaussian Densities

Under the Gaussian assumption, the maximum likelihood estimate of the eigenvectors is explicit. The relaxed optimization problem (
6
) can then be simplified.

Proposition 1

Let
X\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
. Let
S\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}XX^{\top}
be the sample covariance matrix and
\ell_{1}\geq\dots\geq\ell_{p}
be its eigenvalues. Then the relaxed problem (
6
) is equivalent to

\argmin_{\lambda\in\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)}\,\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\ln\lambda_{j}+\frac{\ell_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}\left(\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})+\sum_{t=s+1}^{p}\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})\right)\right).

(7)

Proof

Under the Gaussian assumption, the log-likelihood can be rewritten as
-2\ln\mathcal{L}\left(V\mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda\right)V^{\top}\right)=n(p\ln(2\pi)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\ln\lambda_{j}+\mathrm{tr}\left(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)^{-1}V^{\top}SV)\right)

[
29
]
.
An optimal
\hat{V}\in\mathrm{O}(p)
is any sequence of ordered eigenvectors of
S
(with decreasing eigenvalues)
[
29
]
. This implies that
\mathrm{tr}\left(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)^{-1}{\hat{V}}^{\top}S\hat{V}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\ell_{j}
. We conclude the proof by dividing by
n
and removing the constant
p\ln(2\pi)
. ∎

Remark 2

In the remainder of this paper, to ensure the compactness of the constraint set and the good conditioning of the covariance matrices
[
6
]
, the cone
\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)
is replaced with
{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}(p)\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\colon 1/\varepsilon\geq\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq\varepsilon\}}
with
~{\varepsilon\coloneqq 10^{-10}}
. It turns out to have little effect in practice, although it sometimes does in high dimensions or when some population eigenvalues are small.

We propose to solve problem (
7
) on
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}(p)
with a
projected gradient descent
(see e.g.
[
22
]
and references therein) summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Projected Gradient Descent for Gaussian Eigengap Sparsity

\ell\in\mathcal{K}_{0}(p),Q\in\mathrm{O}(p),\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\triangleright
sample eigenvalues/vectors, hyperparameter

\lambda\leftarrow\ell

\triangleright
initialization with sample eigenvalues

for

i
= 1, 2, …
do

g\leftarrow\frac{\lambda-{\ell}}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{\alpha}{n}\partial_{\lambda}\sum_{s=1}^{p-1}\left(\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{s+1})+\sum_{t=s+1}^{p}\delta(\lambda_{s},\lambda_{t})\right)

\triangleright
gradient (elementwise)

\lambda\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}(p)}(\lambda-\beta\,g)

\triangleright
backtracking projected line search
[
22
]

end

for

\hat{\Sigma}\coloneqq Q\mathrm{diag}\left(\lambda\right){Q}^{\top}

\triangleright
optimal covariance matrix

A central observation is that the orthogonal projection onto the constraint set
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}(p)
corresponds to the well-known problem of
isotonic regression
(cf.
[
24
]
for a pedagogical explanation and illustration).
This quadratic program can be
exactly
solved in
linear time
via the Pools Adjacent Violators Algorithm (PAVA)
[
5
]
, which roughly boils down to sequentially investigating the eigenvalues and block-averaging the ones that violate the ordering constraints. It is followed by an eigenvalue thresholding to ensure that the eigenvalues fit the bounds in
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon}(p)
.

Consequently, at each iteration, Algorithm
1
somewhat makes a tradeoff between increasing the Gaussian likelihood and decreasing the eigengaps via a gradient step, and subsequently equalizes the eigenvalues that “intersect” (i.e. that cross the boundary of the monotone cone) via an isotonic regression.
While isotonization has been historically employed by Stein as a post-shrinkage trick to avoid numerical issues caused by close eigenvalues (see e.g.
[
18
]
), it naturally arises in our Algorithm
1
as a projected gradient step.
Therefore, while close eigenvalues are a
curse
in the classical shrinkage literature, they are a
blessing
in our framework since they get equalized and consequently bring parsimony.

The choice of eigengap function
\delta
has a great impact on the results. While the most natural choice seems to be the
absolute eigengap

\delta(a,b)\coloneqq|a-b|
, we conjecture that it tends to make
all
the eigenvalues equal (in the Gaussian case), which is not always desired.
Consequently, we choose to consider the
relative eigengap

\delta(a,b)\coloneqq(a-b)/a
(for
a\geq b
), which is strongly justified by theory
[
29
]
and which also turns out to yield better experimental results.

6
Conclusion

We formulated the problem of parsimonious covariance estimation as a penalized-likelihood optimization with an eigengap-sparsity-inducing penalty. The latter was derived from the
\ell^{1}
-relaxation of intrinsic dimensionality in the stratification of symmetric positive-definite matrices by the multiplicities of the eigenvalues.
We proposed a projected gradient descent algorithm for the Gaussian case, which boils down to iteratively updating the ordered-eigenvalues via a penalized-likelihood gradient-step and equalizing the intersecting eigenvalues via an isotonic regression which automatically induces parsimony. We illustrated the good performance of our method in terms of speed (with respect to classical model selection
[
29
]
), parsimony and covariance estimation (with respect to the sample covariance matrix and shrinkage methods
[
17
]
).

The eigengap sparsity penalty is only at an early stage of research and many perspectives arise: evaluating the performance of our method on more diverse settings (eigenvalue profiles, non-Gaussian distributions) and choices of hyperparameters (regularization strength
\alpha
and eigengap function
\delta
); making the learning algorithm more robust and efficient in high dimensions using flag manifold optimization
[
37
,
30
]
; studying the theoretical guarantees of the algorithm; considering Riemannian metrics on
\mathrm{Sym}_{+}(p)
or
\mathcal{K}_{+}(p)
beyond the embedded one etc. One could also extend our penalized-likelihood estimation framework beyond covariance matrices, considering for instance graph Laplacians and Hessians, whose parsimony is very important
[
19
,
16
]
.

Our proposed approach draws some bridges with active areas of research.
First, the eigengap sparsity can be of interest to the sparse optimization community
[
2
]
.
Our algorithm moreover surprisingly induces sparsity via an isotonic regression instead of classical thresholding operators like in LASSO
[
31
]
.
Second, the eigengap penalty results from a relaxation of covariance parsimony, which addresses the eigenvalue-spreading phenomenon studied in pioneering shrinkage methods
[
17
]
. Interestingly, while most shrinkage methods focus on the eigenvalues, our framework for covariance estimation includes both the eigenvalues and the flag of eigenspaces. Moreover, while low-rank models like PPCA
[
34
]
only group the smallest eigenvalues (considered as noise), our models may also group some of the largest eigenvalues, therefore better taking into account the eigenvalue-spreading phenomenon.
Third, our work has interesting links with the
elasso
method from David E. Tyler and Mengxi Yi
[
35
]
and follow-up works
[
3
]
. Although the motivations, the penalties and the optimization algorithms are quite different from ours, these methods also result in an automatic grouping of the eigenvalues. We believe that they could be seamlessly integrated in our framework by modifying the eigengap function
\delta
and taking advantage of automatic differentiation.
