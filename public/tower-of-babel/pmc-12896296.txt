A mixed-method study on the screening practices and barriers to diabetic microvascular complications by general practitioners in community health centers of Xiamen, China
Authors: Zhang-Yan Chen, Cui-Ling Huang, Cheng-Dian Lan, Hua Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications are of crucial importance for preventing diabetes progression and improving patient outcomes. This study aimed to assess the current status of screening practices employed by general practitioners (GPs) in community health centers (CHCs) in Xiamen, China, and identify implementation barriers.MethodsA mixed quantitative and qualitative method was adopted between March 2023 and January 2024. The quantitative component involved a cross-sectional study using stratified sampling in nine CHCs from two Xiamen districts. The qualitative component employed semi-structured interviews with participants recruited through purposive sampling. Data analysis used a thematic step-by-step approach.ResultsA total of 93 GPs participated in the quantitative study, with a practice score of 45.9 ± 15.4/100. Higher attitude scores (β = 0.802, P < 0.001) and managing more diabetic patients (β = 0.032, P = 0.008) were associated with better practice scores. A total of 13 GPs participated in qualitative interviews. Current screening practices in CHCs were characterized by inadequate screening items, non-adherence to guideline-recommended screening frequencies, and lack of sustainable screening. Identified barriers included insufficient attention to screening of GPs and patients, knowledge and skill deficits of GPs, human resource shortages, lack of incentive mechanisms, inadequate screening instruments, and low adherence of patients. Strengthening the training of GPs, providing screening instruments, optimizing the screening process, and enhancing patient education are needed for effective improvement.ConclusionThe screening practices for diabetic microvascular complications among GPs in Xiamen were suboptimal, mainly due to factors associated with workforce constraints, variations in GP capabilities, and limitations in available screening instruments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03135-y.

Background

In 2021, there were approximately 537 million people with diabetes mellitus, and this number has been estimated to reach 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045 [1]. With the rising prevalence of diabetes mellitus, there is a corresponding increase in the incidence of diabetic microvascular complications. The “DISCOVER” observational study program revealed that between 2014 and 2019, 18.8% of individuals with type 2 diabetes had microvascular complications [2]. These complications, including diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic kidney disease (DKD), and diabetic neuropathy (DN), can significantly impact the patient’s quality of life, causing different health issues such as vision loss, end-stage renal disease, and amputation, and posing serious health and economic burdens on individuals, families, and health systems [3, 4].

Clinical practice guidelines recommend regular screening to detect diabetic microvascular complications [5, 6]. According to Guideline for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes Mellitus in China (2024 edition) and Expert Consensus on Screening and Prevention of Diabetic Microvascular Disease for Primary Care (2024 edition) [7, 8], it is recommended that patients with type 1 diabetes of at least five years’ duration, as well as all patients with type 2 diabetes, undergo annual screening for diabetic microvascular complications. For patients in whom complications are already present, more frequent screening is indicated, with the frequency tailored to the individual’s clinical status. Early screening and managemnt of diabetic microvascular complications are of crucial importance for preventing the progression of diabetes and improving patient outcomes [9, 10]. These early screening and management processes are continuous, ongoing, long-term work. It has been suggested that screening for diabetic microvascular complications should be carried out by community general practitioners (GPs) in community health centers (CHCs) [11, 12]. GPs are at the forefront of diabetes care, providing essential services for early detection, prevention, and treatment of microvascular complications in diabetic patients.

According to several studies, the current practices for screening and managing diabetic microvascular complications in CHCs were suboptimal. In 2016, Australia introduced two new Medicare Benefits Schedule items to facilitate DR screening; however, the outcomes have not met the expected level of success, with only 50–77% of non-Indigenous Australians and 20–44% of Indigenous Australians undergoing the recommended DR screening [13]. The main barriers hindering DR screening in general practice included GPs’ incompetency to accurately diagnose DR, costs of retinal cameras, lack of time within the practice, and uncertainty about GPs’ role in the retinal screening program. A cross-sectional study from Arabia revealed that only 64.5% of primary care providers performed foot inspection in high-risk diabetic foot patients at every visit [14]. In a cross-sectional observational study involving 191 primary care clinics in Korea, screening rates for diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy within the previous 12 months were 28.4%, 23.3%, and 13.3%, respectively [12]. Screening practices on diabetic microvascular complications vary across countries; however, there is a lack of research exploring the current status of screening diabetic microvascular complications by GPs in China.

The current study used a mixed-method approach, a quantitative component assessing the present status of screening diabetic microvascular complications by GPs in CHCs in Xiamen, China, alongside a qualitative component aiming at identifying barriers encountered in the implementation process to identify screening gaps in current practices and propose suggestions for improving screening diabetic microvascular complications in the CHCs.

Methods

The current study employed a explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. An initial quantitative phase identified key issues in the screening of diabetic microvascular complications. A subsequent qualitative phase, utilizing semi-structured interviews, was then conducted to explore the underlying causes behind these issues and to identify potential solutions. Following the qualitative analysis, the findings were systematically integrated with those from the quantitative study. The current study was conducted between March 2023 and January 2024. The research was granted approval by the Institutional Review Board at the Xiamen Branch of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2022-013).

Quantitative component

Study population

This cross-sectional study employed a stratified sampling procedure to recruit participants. Xiamen is administratively divided into six districts. Among them, Huli District and Siming District form the central urban, whereas Haicang, Jimei, Xiang’an, and Tong’an are suburban areas. The central urban and suburban areas exhibit distinct disparities in fiscal revenue and healthcare expenditure. Therefore, to ensure the representativeness of the entire city of Xiamen, two districts—Huli from the central urban and Haicang from the suburban areas—were purposefully selected from among its six administrative districts, and then GPs were recruited from all nine CHCs in two districts. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) registered GPs who worked in the CHCs for at least six months; (2) GPs who volunteered to participate in the survey and signed informed consent. There were 108 eligible GPs in the nine CHCs. They were all invited to participate in the study. A consent statement detailing the study’s objectives and procedures was provided for participants to review before determining whether to continue the survey. Participants could withdraw from the online questionnaire at any time without providing a reason.

Survey

The survey was conducted via the online platform WJX (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Limited company) between March 2023 and December 2023. The Quick Response (QR) code of the questionnaire and consent statement were sent to 108 eligible GPs of nine CHCs. The electronically signed informed consent was obtained from each participant at the beginning of the questionnaire. The survey questionnaire contained socio-demographic characteristics, the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) scale for screening and managing diabetic microvascular complications, training experience, and instruments for screening diabetic microvascular complications.

The KAP scale was adapted from a previous study validated for the Chinese population (Supplementary File 1) [15, 16].

The knowledge section contained 24 items across 6 dimensions related to the knowledge of general knowledge of diabetic microvascular complications, DR, DKD, DN, Diabetic foot (DF) and medication use. Each item of the scale was scored by the GPs as true, false or do not know, and 1 point was allocated for a correct response and 0 points for an incorrect or ‘do not know’ response. The total score was converted into a hundred-mark system from 0 to 100. The higher scores indicated a better knowledge of participants.

The attitude section contained 12 items, each scored by the GPs with 1–5 points indicating strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree and strongly agree. The total score was converted into a hundred-mark system from 0 to 100. The higher scores indicated a better attitude of participants.

The practice section contained 14 items across 3 dimensions related to self-reported screening suggestions, screening action and patient education. Four of the 14 items were open questions and were not scored. The remaining 10 items of the scale were scored by the GPs with 1–5 points indicating none, minority of diabetic patients, half of diabetic patients, majority of diabetic patients and all diabetic patients. The total score was converted into a hundred-mark system from 0 to 100. A score of 60 or higher indicated that the GP addressed practice with at least half of their diabetic patients. This threshold of 60 was established as the passing mark. The higher scores indicated better practices of participants.

To evaluate the validity and reliability of the KAP scale, an electronic questionnaire-based survey was administered to GPs from 55 CHCs across four districts in Shanghai [15]. A total of 693 questionnaires were collected, of which 661 were valid. The validity was assessed in terms of content validity, structural validity, and discriminant validity. Content validity was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). Structural validity was tested via exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). EFA was performed using principal component analysis, while CFA model fit was assessed using multiple indices. CFA indicated that the scale had a chi-square degree of freedom ratio (χ²/df) of 2.729, a comparative fit index (CFI) of 0.784, and a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) of 0.072. The CFI values of the knowledge, attitude, and practices sub-scales were 0.847, 0.983, and 0.989, respectively, while the RMSEA values were 0.036, 0.066, and 0.070, respectively. In terms of discriminant validity, the differences in total and subscale scores between high- and low-score groups were statistically significant (t values ranging from 10.82 to 44.49, all p-value < 0.001). The reliability was evaluated using internal consistency and test-retest reliability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the total scale was 0.891; the intraclass correlation coefficient for test-retest reliability was 0.76.

In the present study, the KAP scale still demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.901. Among the three subscales, the values for knowledge, attitude, and practice were 0.480, 0.889, and 0.934, respectively. The relatively low Cronbach’s α coefficient observed for the knowledge subscale may be attributed to its multidimensional structure—comprising six distinct domains (general knowledge of diabetic microvascular complications, DR, DKD, DN, DF, and medication use), which likely introduced heterogeneity in response patterns.

Statistical analysis

Data were coded and recorded on a spreadsheet. SPSS software version 25 was used for statistical analysis (SPSS Inc., IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as proportions (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. The participants’ KAP scores and socio-demographic characteristics were compared using a chi-square test and T-test. The association between practice score and demographic characteristics, training experience, screening instruments, knowledge score, and attitude score was assessed using multiple linear regression analysis. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative component

Study population

To ensure comprehensive interview outcomes, a purposive sampling technique, commonly employed in qualitative research [17], was adopted to recruit GPs representing variations in gender, age, years of service, and professional titles. All 93 participants included in the quantitative phase of the study were considered eligible for participation in the qualitative component. Invitation letters, participant information consent forms, and the QR code of the questionnaire were sent to GPs. The final sample size was determined according to the principle of thematic saturation [18]. Participant selection occurred iteratively during data analysis, and sampling was terminated when additional interviews no longer yielded new thematic information—as evidenced by the absence of new codes in the analysis of the last interviewee’s transcript. Ultimately, a total of 13 GPs were interviewed.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were used for data collection. The interview guide was developed by a study group, which included one associate chief physician, one attending physician, and two resident physicians. The interview centered on three open-ended questions about the interviewee’s prior screening experiences and their perceptions of barriers and facilitators in screening diabetic microvascular complications (Supplementary File 2). Two study group members conducted interviews between September 2023 and January 2024, adhering to an interview manual; each interview lasted approximately 15 to 30 min. In addition, the informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interview.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio recorded. The recorded content was transcribed verbatim after the interview was completed (within 24 h post-interview). To mitigate potential bias, the transcribed text subsequently returned to the respective interviewees for review. This process was implemented to ensure the transcribed text accurately reflected the participants’ intended meanings. Two members of the study group undertook a thematic step-by-step analysis. The procedure consisted of: (1) becoming familiar with transcriptional data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes and sub-themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming of topics, (6) reporting generation by all research members through discussion (with disagreements decided by study group) [19, 20].

Results

Quantitative component

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 108 eligible GPs across the nine CHCs were invited to participate in the study. Among these, 93 GPs completed the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 86.1%. Of the 93 participating GPs, the mean age was 38.1 ± 5.3 years. The overall mean duration of practice was 12.8 ± 6.4 years, while the duration of practice as a GP was 9.1 ± 9.8. Only 1.1% of the respondents did not have a bachelor’s degree or above. Most respondents (79.6%) held at least a middle professional title, while 61.3% received a GP residency program. Furthermore, 91.4% were contracted family doctors, with an average of 858.6 ± 508.0 community residents per doctor. Among the contracted family doctors, 79.6% managed patients with diabetes, with an average of 103.3 ± 109.2 diabetic patients per doctor. The investigation through interviews revealed the current status and deficiencies in screening at CHCs, thereby corroborating the quantitative findings that GPs achieved lower scores in screening practices.

Theme 2: barriers to screening

The interview data further elucidated the reasons for insufficient screening. Several findings from the interviews were consistent with quantitative results, such as the identified shortage of screening instruments in CHCs.

Lack of attention to screening

GPs tended to adopt a reactive approach to screening for diabetic microvascular complications, typically initiating assessment only following the onset of symptoms in patients.

In diabetes management, we placed more emphasis on glycemic control but less on screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications, except in patients who already had symptoms of complications (GP11, attending physician, 1 year).

In diabetes management, we placed more emphasis on glycemic control but less on screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications, except in patients who already had symptoms of complications (GP11, attending physician, 1 year).

When I communicated with patients with diabetes, I rarely asked about the complications of diabetes unless the patients had obvious symptoms and took the initiative to bring them up (GP13, attending physician, 6 years).

When I communicated with patients with diabetes, I rarely asked about the complications of diabetes unless the patients had obvious symptoms and took the initiative to bring them up (GP13, attending physician, 6 years).

Lack of knowledge and skills in screening

A significant proportion of GPs exhibited deficits in knowledge and skills regarding the screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications, which impeded their ability to effectively encourage patient participation in screening.

We were aware of the need for screenings, but we were not sure about the frequency of these screenings, and we lacked knowledge about the pathogenesis of diabetic microvascular complications, which made it challenging for us to persuade patients to undergo screening (GP7, resident physicians, 4 years).

We had received training, but mostly in diabetes, and there was very little training on diabetic microvascular complications (GP1, associate chief physician, 10 years).

Integration of quantitative component and qualitative component

We integrated the quantitative and qualitative research findings. On the one hand, the results demonstrated strong mutual corroboration between the two datasets. The quantitative results indicated that GPs achieved a mean score of only 45.9 ± 15.4 out of 100 in practice, while qualitative data concurrently highlighted suboptimal screening practices—reflected in incomplete screening items, non-adherence to guideline-recommended frequencies, and a lack of sustained screening implementation. Quantitative survey data revealed that only 4.3% of GPs reported having all eight essential screening instruments available in their CHCs. This finding was directly supported by qualitative interview responses, in which participants reported being unable to perform necessary screenings due to insufficient resources. On the other hand, qualitative research further complemented and expanded the reasons behind the quantitative findings, identified barriers to screening, and gathered suggestions for improvement from the perspective of GPs.

Discussion

In the current study, the total score for screening diabetic microvascular complications was low, especially for the screening action subscale. Only 4.3% of the GPs reported that all 8 basic screening tools, i.e., tonometer, ophthalmoscope, fundus camera, urine albumin creatinine ratio, serum creatinine, routine urine test, 128 Hz tuning fork, and 10 g monofilament, were available in their CHCs. Furthermore, Among these, the three least frequently available items were the tuning fork, 10 g monofilament, and tonometer. The main findings of the qualitative interview part of this study showed that most GPs did not carry out diabetic microvascular complications screening in practice. The results of quantitative and qualitative research demonstrated strong mutual corroboration between the two datasets. Moreover, the qualitative research showed that a lack of attention to diabetic microvascular complications, lack of knowledge and skills, lack of GPs and auxiliary specialists, lack of incentive mechanism for screening, lack of screening instruments, and poor patient compliance were the main factors affecting the screening action in CHCs.

The undesirable screening practice for diabetic microvascular complications was consistent with some studies. In a survey of DR Screening in Jakarta, Indonesia, only 4 out of 92 GPs (4.3%) engaged in good practice [11]. In a cross-sectional study conducted in Changsha, China, a KAP questionnaire on DF showed that the GPs achieved an average score of 61.53 ± 14.69 out of 100 on DF risk screening behaviors, and only 32.1% of GPs reported always or frequently performing foot risk screening for diabetic patients [21]. In a quantitative survey on recommended screening procedures in Norway, screening for albuminuria was recommended by 31.5% of GPs, monofilament by 27.5% of GPs, and eye examination by 60.0% of GPs [22]. Furthermore, our qualitative interviews revealed that, in addition to the inadequate rate of screening practice, the frequency of screening did not meet the recommendation, and the screening lacked sustainability.

The GPs were the main body of screening for diabetic microvascular complications. The negative attitude to screening in practice, insufficient knowledge and skills, and the low number of GPs were perceived as the most important human factors in screening barriers in the current study. Good practice was determined by correct knowledge and a positive attitude [23]. The quantitative study showed that screening practices were associated with attitude, not knowledge. Research on the interrelationship between knowledge, attitudes, and practice is inconsistent. A survey of GPs’ KAP on DR screening found a weak positive correlation between attitude and practice but no significant correlation between knowledge and practice [11]. Unlike our results, another survey on DF screening found that practice positively correlated with knowledge and attitude [21]. In the current study, the possible reasons why knowledge could not be translated into action were the critical shortage of screening instruments in CHCs, which frustrates the application of learned skills; the absence of such instruments also limits opportunities to develop and maintain operational proficiency, further obstructing practical application; and without incentive mechanisms to encourage screening, GPs lack the motivation to actively apply their knowledge, even when it is available. Meanwhile, in the quantitative study, only 58.1% of the surveyed GPs had training experience in addressing diabetic microvascular complications. Some studies have found that the more training the GPs participated in, the higher the level of screening understanding and the better screening practice they had [24, 25]. On the other hand, the insufficient number of GPs was also an important factor affecting the implementation of screening. In 2022, China had 463,000 qualified GPs, accounting for 10.4% of the total number of medical practitioners in China, which is still far from the average of 23.0% in Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development countries [26]. GPs had to rank all their work, and screening for diabetic microvascular complications was often second in line due to the large amount of work faced by underrepresented GPs.

There seemed to be a general lack of instruments in CHCs. A survey in China showed that the coverage rate of urine protein dipstick testing was only 40.9% in urban primary health care and 7.6% in rural primary health care [27]. In other studies, the limited instrument was also an important factor affecting GPs’ diabetic microvascular complications screening [21, 28].This phenomenon may be related to the inverted triangle of allocation of health resources in China [29]. Tertiary hospitals, which have more abundant resources than primary care, are representative of high-quality health in China. Although the national health financial annual report for 2012–2022 has shown that the proportion of medical insurance fund income of CHCs in public medical and health institutions has decreased on a yearly basis, ranging from 15.7% to 12.0%, the expert consensus in China suggested that screening of diabetic microvascular complications should be done in primary care institutions [30]. Using the non-mydriatic fundus camera and remote consultation system, GPs could perform fundus examinations and obtain reliable results through the entire consultation system, thus addressing the community’s lack of ophthalmic medical care [31]. Accordingly, equipping basic screening instruments should be included in the scope of medical institution evaluation of health administrative departments at all levels and CHCs.

GPs also mentioned a lack of motivation. Non-mandatory screening and few financial incentives were associated with lower screening rates for diabetic microvascular complications [22]. The lack of institutionalization of screening practices might have led to a low screening rate among GPs [32]. Although the guidelines recommended regular screening for diabetic microvascular complications, there was no national or large-scale screening program for routine diabetic microvascular complications promoted by the national or regional government in China [28]. Without national and regional routine screening, a well-developed system and appropriate incentives in CHCs may be beneficial to promote physician screening. In order to improve the screening process, GPs suggested that diabetic microvascular complications screening should be included in the annual free health examination. The consolidation of screening and health examination could have increased the work efficiency of GPs and the compliance of patients. Establishing a screening team and strengthening cooperation with superior hospitals have proven effective management models [33]. In Xiamen, there has always been cooperation between primary hospitals and specialized departments of higher-level hospitals, improving the capabilities of GPs and enhancing the satisfaction of patients seeking medical treatment in community hospitals [34]. During the interview, we found collaboration beneficial for the screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications for patients; however, maintaining such collaboration was difficult.

During the interview, we found that some screening programs had been interrupted for various reasons. The program’s sustainability was very important, regardless of whether the program was carried out in cooperation with higher hospitals or by the CHC itself. The sustainability was related to program burden, program familiarity, perceived competence in program skills, workplace support for the program, staff mobility and turnover, supervision and peer support, and ongoing technical assistance [35]. Most of these factors were mentioned by the GPs interviewed. The project’s sustainability required more rigorous planning, implementation, supervision, and support, which also required higher levels of support from the administrative departments and CHCs.

The interviewees believed that poor patient compliance was the hindering factor for screening diabetic microvascular complications. The lack of patient knowledge also affected their medical behavior [36]. Active health education was conducive to the improvement of patients’ compliance. A survey from South Korea found that after strengthening education for diabetic patients, the prescription compliance rate increased from 64.0% to 73.6% [37]. Distance education of patients using social media may be a cost-effective way. Compared to the traditional follow-up group, diabetic patients who were followed up through WeChat exhibited lower fasting blood glucose levels, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose, and glycosylated hemoglobin. Such an approach is rather convenient as the patient’s educational content can be updated and sent to the patient in real-time. There was also a significant improvement in their self-efficacy and a remarkable increase in satisfaction with the follow-up process [38].

Notably, the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings revealed that the interview data provided substantive explanatory insights into the quantitative outcomes. For instance, whereas quantitative analysis identified attitudes as a significant predictor of practice, qualitative interviews uncovered a generally passive stance toward screening among many GPs. This alignment suggests that inadequate screening practices within this group may be attributable to underlying negative perceptions—a conclusion strongly supported by the convergent evidence from this mixed-methods study.

Considering the findings above, a better framework for a successful model of diabetic microvascular complications screening in general practice should be developed. The following enabling strategies can be adopted: (1) increasing GPs’ access to training and comprehensive personnel development on screening and management of diabetic microvascular complications; (2) increasing the number of GPs of CHCs and equipping them with basic screening instruments; (3) ensuring the establishment of a screening team for diabetic microvascular complications, including GPs and auxiliary specialist. Once the screening test is positive, the referral can be facilitated, thus ensuring the continuity of screening services; (4) the screening process should be improved by integrating screening for diabetic microvascular complications into existing medical tasks, incorporating screening rates for diabetic microvascular complications into performance evaluations or incentive schemes for GPs, and strengthening cooperation with superior hospitals; (5) strengthening the education of patients to improve their compliance, e.g., providing distance education through WeChat.

Study strengths and study limitation

This was one of the few relatively complete mixed studies on screening diabetic microvascular complications among GPs. It did not only address individual diabetic microvascular complications but comprehensive diabetic microvascular complications, including DKD, DR, DN, and DF. The present study also has some limitations. Firstly, the assessment of practice performance was based on self-reported data provided by the GPs. As with any study relying on self-assessment, the results are subject to social desirability bias and recall bias, representing a known limitation of the methodological approach. Secondly, the CFA revealed a limitation in the structural validity of the KAP scale. While the CFI for the overall model was suboptimal, the three sub-scales each demonstrated a high CFI. This discrepancy indicates that the poor fit at the full-scale level likely stems from substantive heterogeneity across the distinct knowledge, attitude, and practice sub-scales. Thirdly, the current study exclusively focused on GPs, excluding administrative staff of CHCs and patients; thus, the issues reflected solely represent the perspective of GPs. Fourthly, the study was only conducted in Xiamen, which can only reflect the situation in Xiamen, not other cities in China, thus limiting the extrapolation of the study results. In future research, we plan to expand the survey to a broader range of GPs, administrative staff, and diabetic patients.

Conclusion

The screening practice of diabetic microvascular complications among GPs in Xiamen was sub-optimal and should be strengthened. It was influenced by multiple factors, including workforce constraints, variations in GP capabilities, and limitations in available instruments. To address these challenges, it is imperative for administrative authorities to extend both financial and policy support. Such support may include allocating funds for the procurement of screening instruments, incorporating screening into routine clinical protocols as a compulsory component, and establishing performance assessment and incentive systems. These findings can guide future intervention programs to improve screening practices for diabetic microvascular complications.
