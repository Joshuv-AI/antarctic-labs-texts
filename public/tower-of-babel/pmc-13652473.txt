Pregnancy after the Manchester–Fothergill procedure: a case report

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Cervical elongation is commonly associated with uterine prolapse. The Manchester–Fothergill procedure offers a fertility-preserving surgical option for its correction. Introduction

Uterine prolapse is frequently associated with cervical elongation[1]. For women desiring uterine preservation, several surgical options are available, including the Manchester repair, abdominal uterosacral hysteropexy, and vaginal sacrospinous hysteropexy[1,2]. The Manchester–Fothergill procedure, originally introduced by Archibald Donald in 1888 and later refined by W.E. Fothergill, was specifically designed to treat uterovaginal prolapse in the context of cervical elongation[2]. As a uterus-conserving intervention, it offers a viable alternative to hysterectomy, particularly for younger women seeking to maintain fertility[3]. Despite its advantages, pregnancy following this procedure is exceedingly rare, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 10 000 cases[4]. Reported obstetric complications include preterm labor, spontaneous miscarriage, fetal loss, abnormal uterine bleeding, maternal urinary dysfunction, and cervical neoplasia[4]. Given these risks, close antenatal surveillance is essential for managing pregnancies following this intervention. We present a rare case of a successful term pregnancy occurring 18 months after the patient underwent the Manchester–Fothergill procedure. Recent case reports and small series have documented the possibility of successful conception and term delivery following uterus-sparing surgeries, such as the Manchester–Fothergill procedure, although the literature remains sparse. In particular, a 2022 case by Baldota et al highlighted a favorable maternal outcome post-embolization for hemorrhage following a modified Manchester repair, underscoring both the complexity and potential of post-surgical fertility[3]. Ertandri and Ermawati[4] also reported a successful pregnancy outcome, emphasizing the importance of vigilant antenatal monitoring. Case presentation

Case history and examination

This case report is presented in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria [2]. A 35-year-old woman, G3P2L2, presented with a positive β-hCG test at 5 weeks and 4 days' gestation.

Her obstetric history included a spontaneous vaginal delivery during her first pregnancy, 14 years earlier. Methods (differential diagnosis, investigations, and treatment)

The patient was followed closely throughout gestation. Serial transvaginal ultrasonography was performed to monitor fetal growth and assess cervical length. Cervical length was consistently measured at 30 mm, with no evidence of funneling or shortening. Given the stable cervical integrity, neither cerclage placement nor progesterone therapy was indicated. No signs of prolapse recurrence were observed during physical examinations. The patient was advised to avoid excessive physical exertion. Regular antenatal visits and ultrasound evaluations were maintained throughout the pregnancy.

Conclusions and results (outcome and follow-up)

The pregnancy progressed uneventfully, with cervical length remaining stable. At 39 weeks, an elective cesarean section was performed, resulting in the delivery of a healthy male infant (3700 grams). The decision for elective cesarean delivery at 39 weeks was made based on the potential risk of cervical trauma and the theoretical risk of prolapse recurrence associated with vaginal delivery. Intraoperatively, no evidence of uterine adhesions, cervical abnormalities, or pelvic organ prolapse was observed. Postpartum follow-up at 6, 12, 18, and 22 months revealed no recurrence of pelvic organ prolapse or menstrual irregularities. The patient reported satisfaction with the outcome and expressed gratitude for the uterus-preserving surgical option that allowed for an unexpected but successful pregnancy. This case demonstrates that pregnancy after the Manchester–Fothergill procedure can have a favorable outcome with proper antenatal surveillance. Discussion

Uterine prolapse is often linked to cervical elongation and is typically treated through surgery, especially in women seeking to maintain their fertility. The Manchester–Fothergill method, initially outlined by Donald and subsequently revised by Fothergill, continues to be a suitable fertility-preserving alternative to hysterectomy for certain patients[5]. Nonetheless, conceiving after this procedure is extremely uncommon, with an estimated rate of 1 in 10 000 cases[4].

The main concern in pregnancies following a Manchester procedure is cervical insufficiency, which can result in negative obstetric outcomes, such as preterm labor and pregnancy loss during the second trimester[6]. Certain studies indicate that the rate of pregnancy loss in such cases may reach up to 50%[4]. Additional complications reported include cervical stenosis, hematometra, pyometra, and recurrence of postpartum prolapse[7]. Due to these associated risks, diligent monitoring is crucial to enhance both maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Management approaches for pregnancies following a Manchester procedure primarily aim to preserve cervical competence and prevent preterm labor. Regular sonographic monitoring of cervical length is essential for risk assessment, with a cervical length of <25 mm serving as a critical threshold for intervention[6]. In certain instances, cervical cerclage may be considered; however, it was not necessary in our patient due to a stable cervical length of 30 mm. Furthermore, progesterone supplementation has been proposed as a preventive strategy for women with cervical shortening, though it was not deemed appropriate in this case.

Delivery planning is a critical consideration in pregnancies following the Manchester procedure. Although vaginal delivery has been documented, the majority of studies advocate for elective cesarean section to mitigate the risks of cervical injury and recurrence of prolapse[8]. In the present case, an elective cesarean section performed at 39 weeks’ gestation led to a positive neonatal outcome, consistent with findings from previous studies[3].

When compared with the few reported cases in the literature, this case aligns with key observations: stable cervical length, absence of prolapse recurrence during pregnancy, and favorable neonatal outcomes with elective cesarean delivery. Jasonni et al[8] similarly documented a positive pregnancy outcome after Manchester repair with close follow-up. These findings collectively support the conclusion that, with appropriate surgical technique and diligent antenatal care, uterine conservation is compatible with future pregnancy.

Postpartum follow-up is essential for the evaluation of prolapse recurrence and menstrual irregularities. Long-term studies suggest that although prolapse recurrence remains a concern, successful pregnancies without recurrence have been documented[1]. In the case of our patient, no prolapse recurrence was observed during a 22-month follow-up period.

This case underscores that while pregnancy following the Manchester–Fothergill procedure is uncommon, it can result in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes when appropriate antenatal surveillance is conducted. A multidisciplinary approach, involving obstetricians and urogynecologists, is recommended to optimize the management of such pregnancies and ensure positive long-term outcomes for these patients.

Conclusion

The Manchester–Fothergill procedure is an effective uterus-sparing option for women with uterine prolapse who desire future fertility. Although rare, successful pregnancies are achievable with close monitoring, proper management, and a planned delivery. With multidisciplinary coordination, this surgical approach can result in excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed gratitude for the uterus-preserving surgery, stating that although she did not anticipate future fertility, the outcome was both unexpected and welcome. She reported no psychological distress during her pregnancy and appreciated the thorough antenatal care provided.
