Spatial Regulation of Preameloblast Development in Response to Distal Mouse Incisor Injury
Authors: Alexia Campbell, Kevin Lin, Jake Ngu, Cierra Rose Ahlstrand, Terumi Kohwi-Shigematsu, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enamel, the outermost mineralized tissue of the tooth, is produced by specialized dental epithelial cells called ameloblasts. Unlike human enamel, which lacks regenerative capacity, the mouse incisor grows throughout life, driven by adult stem cells residing in the labial cervical loop (LaCl). To maintain tissue homeostasis, dental epithelial stem cells produce transit-amplifying cells (TACs) that commit to preameloblasts (PABs), migrate distally, and differentiate into enamel-forming ameloblasts. The full dental epithelial differentiation trajectory coexists within a single mouse incisor, making it an accessible model for studying adult tissue repair and regeneration. We have shown that the genome organizer SATB1 is enriched in PABs and is required for their differentiation into ameloblasts. Here, we investigated the injury response of PABs following mouse incisor tip trimming. Injured wild-type (wt) incisors exhibited an expanded PAB zone with intensive proliferation, reduced SATB1 in the ameloblast lineage, associated with a spatial delay in the deposition of the dentin/enamel matrix compared to uninjured controls. Trimming of Satb1 cKO mouse incisor failed to elicit this response, highlighting SATB1’s role in PAB’s response to injury. Compared with wt controls, injured wt incisors and both Satb1 cKO groups showed increased Ki67 immunoreactivity in LaCl mesenchymal and epithelial compartments, along with reduced Col1a1 expression in PAB microenvironment. In vitro, SATB1-transduced ameloblast lineage cells (ALCs) cultured on increasing concentrations of type I collagen exhibited reduced Ki67 but elevated Amelx/Ambn expression. There findings indicate that SATB1 is required for epithelial TACs to exit the cell cycle and transition toward PABs. Introduction

Adult stem cells and their precursors maintain tissue homeostasis and repair injury throughout postnatal life, offering significant promise for regenerative medicine [1, 2]. These cells reside within specialized cell niches, where they integrate biochemical signals with biophysical inputs from the surrounding microenvironment. Together, these cues orchestrate the balance between stem cell quiescence and activation, regulate transit-amplifying cell (TAC) kinetics, direct lineage specification, and coordinate spatial migration, thereby ensuring robust tissue homeostasis and regenerative capacity [3]. Understanding how adult stem cells are activated and progress during injury responses—including endogenous repair mechanisms adapted to the physiological and pathological context of adult tissues—is fundamental to developing safe and effective regenerative therapies for degenerative diseases, trauma, and age-related tissue decline.

Tooth formation is initiated by the reciprocal signaling interactions between the dental epithelium and mesenchyme [4, 5]. In adult human and murine teeth, slow cycling dental mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) retain regenerative capacity, enabling repair of dentin, cementum, and pulp when needed [6–10]. In contrast, enamel—the hardest tissue in mammals produced by the dental epithelium-derived ameloblasts—lacks regenerative potential in humans because ameloblasts are lost after tooth eruption [11]. However, the mouse incisor grows continuously throughout life, sustained by active dental epithelial stem cells. These adult stem cells continually generate TAC progenitors, which then progressively differentiate into enamel-forming cells, including the highly specialized secretory and maturation ameloblasts [12, 13]. During the secretory stage, ameloblasts deposit an extracellular matrix composed primarily of amelogenin, ameloblastin, and enamelin; during enamel maturation, these cells regulate ion transport and matrix remodeling to drive mineral accretion, ultimately producing a highly organized hydroxyapatite-based structure containing ~ 96% mineral by weight [14–17]. Uniquely, the entire dental epithelial cell lineage hierarchy, from stem cells to fully differentiated ameloblasts, coexist spatially within a single adult mouse incisor. This developmental continuum makes the incisor a robust model for dissecting the molecular and microenvironmental determinants that govern the stage-specific differentiation of dental epithelial cells [4, 12, 18].

Adult mouse dental epithelial stem cells reside in the labial cervical loop (LaCL) at the proximal end of the incisor. Within the LaCL, the stellate reticulum (SR) forms the central core between the inner enamel epithelium (IEE) and the outer enamel epithelium (OEE) [8–10]. Previous studies have shown that stem cells localize to the OEE, adjacent SR, and stratum intermedium (SI) underlying IEE, where they either undergo slow cycling to maintain self-renewal or give rise to rapidly proliferating TAC progenitors in IEE that sustain lifelong incisor growth [12, 19–21]. Key signaling pathways—including Wnt, Notch, BMP-Smad4, and Hedgehog—regulate the dental epithelial stem cell-to-TAC transition [22–25]. These multilayered TACs interact with neighboring rapidly proliferating dental mesenchymal cells through the basement membrane [26–28]. An intact basement membrane is essential for dental mesenchymal differentiation into odontoblasts [29]. Consistently, laminin α5 knockout mice display small tooth germs and defective cusp formation [5]. As odontoblasts deposit dentin matrix, the underlying multilayered IEE TACs consolidate into a single layer of low columnar PABs, which elongate into tall secretory ameloblasts with basal nuclei, expanded cytoplasm, and apical picket-fence shaped Tomes’ processes [22, 30, 31].

However, the precise molecular mechanisms governing IEE TAC commitment to the ameloblast lineage remain poorly defined. Previous studies indicate that TAC differentiation is promoted by activation of FGF signaling, in which the cell-surface receptors FGFR1b and FGFR2b bind the paracrine ligands FGF3 and FGF10 secreted by adjacent dental mesenchymal cells [22]. We previously identified the genome organizer SATB1 (special AT-rich sequence–binding protein 1), a critical regulator of enamel formation, as being initially expressed in TACs, reaching peak levels in PABs, and gradually declining as cells progress into secretory and maturation-stage ameloblasts [32]. In this study, we used a murine incisor injury model and Satb1 cKO mice to investigate the molecular cues driving ameloblast lineage commitment from proliferative progenitors under disrupted homeostasis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that trimming the distal tip of the mouse incisor triggers the migration of various dental stem cell populations to the IEE within the LaCL [25]. In this study, we observed that incisor trimming exacerbated PAB proliferation in wt mice, resulting in delayed TAC differentiation and the initiation of dentin and enamel matrix deposition. This was accompanied by reduced SATB1 expression in TACs and PABs.

Notably, this injury response was absent in SATB1-deficient mice. We further detected reduced type I collagen in the proximal incisor region—the extracellular matrix (ECM) supporting PABs—following distal injury. Based on these observations, we propose that distal injury alters dental mesenchymal cell–mediated ECM stiffness, thereby modulating the dynamics of TAC differentiation into PABs. Consistent with our in vivo findings, in vitro experiments using SATB1-transduced ameloblast lineage cells (ALCs) showed reduced Ki67 expression and increased amelogenin and ameloblastin levels when cultured on type I collagen substrates at increasing concentrations. Elucidating the mechanisms that govern ameloblast lineage commitment will provide a foundation for advancing ameloblast and enamel bioregeneration.

Methods

Animal maintenance: Wild-type (wt) C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. The generation of Satb1fl/fl mouse has been previously reported [33]. Satb1 conditional knock out (K14Cre;Satb1fl/fl or Satb1 cKO) was generated by matting Satb1fl/fl mice with K14Cre mice [34] (a gift from Dr. Ophir Klein at University of California, San Francisco). All mice were housed in the UCSF animal care facility, a barrier facility accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC). No differences in enamel or ameloblast phenotypes were observed between male and female mice in these models; thus, both sexes were included in this study. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol AN207430-00 A.

Acute incisor injury model: Postnatal 50 days (P50) wt and Satb1 cKO were anesthetized with isoflurane until the toe pinch reflex was absent. Mice were then randomly assigned to either the treatment or control group. In the treatment group, oral cavity was opened, and left incisor was trimmed by 1/3 using a mouse bone cutter. Soft chow was provided in the housing cages for both control and injured mice. Four days post-injury, the mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. The experimental schedule is illustrated in the Fig. 1A. Hemimandibles were dissected free of surrounding soft tissue and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C. Samples were then decalcified in 8% EDTA at 4 °C for 2 weeks, with daily EDTA changes.

Histological analyses: The decalcified hemimandibles were dehydrated using a gradient ethanol series, cleared through xylene, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned sagittally at a thickness of 5 μm per section. Sections were subsequently deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with either routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Trichrome staining. For the latter staining, rehydrated sections were incubated in Bouin’s fluid at 56 °C for 60 min, followed by staining with modified Mayer’s hematoxylin for 2 min at room temperature. After thoroughly washing, sections were immersed in a one-step Trichrome blue and red staining solution (StatLab, TX, USA), then washed, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips. The sections were analyzed under a Nikon Eclipse E800 microscope and images were documented with an Olympus DP74-CU camera.

In the Trichrome stained sagittal sections of mouse incisors, dl was defined as the linear distance from the onset of dentin deposition to the proximal end of the third molar, and el as the distance from the onset of enamel deposition to the proximal end of the third molar. The el/dl ratio was used as a quantitative measure of PAB zone length for the subsequent statistical analyses. Variations in sectioning angle and image rotation in sagittal sections of mouse incisors can influence anatomical presentation. To minimize these effects, we used the el/dl ratio as a normalized metric to quantify the progression of preameloblast differentiation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and Microsoft Office 2016. Statistical significance was assessed using a paired Student’s t-test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Immunostaining assessment: Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Sections were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, blocked with Trident Universal Protein Blocking Reagent (GeneTex, Inc., GTX30963) for 1 h at RT. Sections were next incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against amelogenin [35], ameloblastin (a gift from Dr. Yong-Hee Chun), SATB1 (Abcam, 109122), or Ki67 (Abcam, ab15580). The following day, sections were washed with 1.6% NaCl in PBST (phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 0.04% Tween), followed by PBST, and then incubated with either anti-rabbit IgG Alex 594 conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc). For immunofluorescence staining, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Life Technologies). Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse E800 microscope equipped with an Olympus DP74-CU camera.

In situ hybridization mRNA level assessment: RNAscope in situ hybridization was performed using the RNAscope 2.5 HD Red Detection Kit and mouse Col1a1 probes (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, ACD Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following the procotol previously described [36], this assay was used to evaluate Col1a1 mRNA expression in odontoblast lineage cells on sagittal sections of mouse incisors.

Enamel mineralization status assessment by micro–computed tomography (microCT): Hemimandibles from three mice per group were collected as described above and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Samples were stored in PBS and shipped to Micro Photonics in Allentown, PA for scanning on a SkyScan 1275 microCT system. Briefly, scans were performed at 40 kV/250uA of voltage with an isotropic voxel size of 8 μm per slice. Following acquisition, raw data were uploaded into Amira software (ThermoFisher) for processing and three-dimensional reconstruction of mouse hemomanible images.

RNA collection and RT-qPCR analysis of expression of target genes: Ameloblast lineage cells (ALCs) [37, 38] were transduced by pLXNX-hSATB1 (human SATB1 mRNA). Culture plates were coated with either 0.02 N acetic acid (control) or 0.02 N acetic acid supplemented with varying concentrations of type I collagen (Millipore, MA, USA). The plates were then incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow for substrate attachment. Next morning, the coated plates were washed with PBS twice, 6 × 105 cells were seeded on each 3.5 cm culture plate. The cells were harvested 24 h later for RNA purification. Cell pellet was lysed in TRIzol™ Reagent (Ambion®, 15596018) by vortexing and passing it through a 23-gauge needle. Total RNA was purified by Direct-zol™ RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, R2052). RNA concentration was quantified with NanoDrop Spectrophotometer and cDNA libraries were generated using SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen™, 18080093) with 1500 ng of total RNA as input for each sample. Semi-quantitative PCR was performed using cDNA libraries and iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., 1725120) in an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio™ 6 Pro Real-Time PCR System. Gapdh and Rps9 were used as housekeeping genes, and the ΔΔCt method was used to calculate fold change of target genes in the type I collagen treated groups relative to controls. ΔCt values were used to calculate statistical significance by the multiple paired t-test with Bonferroni correction following one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism. P value < 0.05 was considered significant, adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Incisor tip injury results in an expanded preameloblast zone, and spatially delaying initiation of dentin and enamel matrix deposition. To visualize the preameloblast (PAB) zone in sagittal sections of the mouse incisor, we used trichrome staining to distinguish the type I collagen–rich organic dentin matrix (blue) from the protein-rich organic enamel matrix (pink) (Fig. 1 and supplement Fig. S1). We defined the PAB zone as the segment extending from the onset of dentin deposition to the initiation of enamel matrix deposition. During development, PABs reside along the organic dentin matrix. We found that in wt controls (Fig. 1B), dentin deposition initiated immediately distal to the cervical loop (Fig. 1B’), followed shortly thereafter by enamel matrix deposition (Fig. 1B’’). In wt injured incisors, the distance between the cervical loop and the onset of dentin matrix formation was increased (Fig. 1C). Compared to wt control (Fig. 1B’ and B’’), both dentin and enamel matrix deposition was shifted distally (Fig. 1C’), and their thicknesses were reduced following injury (Fig. 1C’’). The el/dl ratio of wt controls was 0.8044 ± 0.0382 (n = 8), which significantly decreased to 0.6041 ± 0.0855 (n = 11) following injury (P < 0.01 by student’s t-test) (Fig. 1D and supplement Fig. S1). The reduction in the el/dl ratio indicates a spatial expansion of PAB zone in response to the injury.

Injury to the incisor tip decreases SATB1 levels within dental epithelial cells and spatially delays the expression of amelogenin and ameloblastin in ameloblasts. Upon attachment to the dentin matrix, preameloblasts polarize and transition into a single layer of low columnar cells, characterized by a distinct nuclear shift toward the basal pole. This transformation coincides with the synthesis of primary enamel matrix proteins—initially amelogenin, followed by ameloblastin—which are then secreted by the ameloblasts into the developing enamel space, as demonstrated in the Fig. 2A, C, and supplement Fig. S2A). Following injury, both the onset of amelogenin and ameloblastin synthesis and their subsequent secretion were delayed (Fig. 2B and D, and supplement Fig. S2B). As previously demonstrated [32], SATB1 expression began in the inner enamel epithelium (IEE) of the cervical loop and peaked within the preameloblasts of wild-type control incisors (Fig. 2E and E’, and E’’). In the injured mice, however, the SATB1-positive signal only emerged as the dental epithelial cells exited the cervical loop, showing a marked reduction in overall abundance (Figs. 2F, F’, F’’, and supplement Fig. S2D).

Deletion of SATB1 in epithelial cells expands the preameloblast (PAB) zone; however, incisor tip injury does not further affect the PAB zone in the conditional knockout incisors. Satb1−/− mice rarely survive beyond two weeks after birth. To overcome this limitation, we generated a conditional knockout model to delete SATB1 in cytokeratin 14–positive epithelial cells, including all dental epithelial lineages, by crossing Satb1fl/fl mice with K14Cre transgenic mice (Fig. 3A). Histology analysis showed that secretory- stage ameloblasts (SABs) in Satb1cKO mouse incisors were shorter and lacked the characteristic picket fence appearance of Tomes’ processes (see Fig.S4), a phenotype similar to that observed previously in Satb1 global knockout mice. Immunostaining demonstrated robust SATB1 expression in PABs of Satb1fl/fl control mice (Fig. 3B), whereas SATB1 was undetectable in PABs from Satb1 cKO mouse incisors (Fig. 3C). MicroCT analysis revealed well-contrast enamel on both the incisors and first molars of Satb1fl/fl control mice (Fig. 3D). In contrast, enamel was worn out in the first molars and erupted incisors of Satb1 cKO mice (Fig. 3E). This mouse enamel phenotype resembles the hypoplastic and hypomineralized enamel defects observed in den Hoed–de Boer–Voisin syndrome (MIM 619229), which is caused by mutations in the human SATB1 gene [39]. SATB1 regulates the turnover of rapidly cycling cells within the cervical loop domain. Previous studies have identified a population of rapidly proliferating dental mesenchymal cells at the lingual aspect of the labial transit-amplifying (TA) dental epithelial precursors. Immunofluorescence staining for Ki67, a marker of cells in the active phases of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2, M) [40–42], revealed Ki67-positive cells in the IEE as well as in the adjacent pulp tissue (Fig. 4A). A portion of these pulp cells could differentiate into odontoblasts. Following distal incisor tip injury, Ki67-positive cells increased in both the inner enamel epithelial layer (IEE) and the dental mesenchymal (M) space, exhibiting a more widespread spatial distribution (Fig. 4B). In the Satb1 cKO mouse incisors, we observed an increased number of Ki67-positive cells in both the IEE and dental mesenchyme (M). Notably, the pool of Ki67-positive mesenchymal cells was shifted away from the cervical loop (Fig. 4C). After injury, this distribution pattern in Satb1 cKO incisors remained largely unchanged (Fig. 4D). This analysis demonstrates the important role of SATB1 in promoting precursor cell cycle progression and powering their differentiation into odontoblastic and ameloblastic lineages. Discussion

Adult stem cells sustain tissue replenishment and repair throughout life, thereby inspiring intensive research efforts in regenerative medicine. Prior to terminal differentiation, adult stem cells generate rapidly cycling transit-amplifying cells (TACs), which expand the progenitor pool to replenish cellular mass and restore tissue integrity following injury [47–49]. Understanding the mechanisms that regulate the balance between TAC proliferation and differentiation would greatly facilitate the clinical exploitation of the robust regenerative potential inherent to defined functional cell populations.

The continuous growth of mouse incisors is supported by an accessible pool of adult stem cells and their progeny, providing a valuable system to study tooth development and model coordinated cellular responses to injury. Using an incisor tip injury model, we observed a prolonged TAC zone and delayed differentiation of preameloblasts (PABs) and ameloblasts, accompanied by postponed onset and reduced expression of SATB1. Compared to wt controls, the epithelial-specific ablation of SATB1 resulted in an expansion of the IEE TAC compartment and a spatial delay in ameloblast commitment, with no statistically significant differences observed between the injured and uninjured groups. Given SATB1’s role in organizing higher-order chromatin architecture, these findings suggest that the transition from TACs to terminally differentiated ameloblast-lineage cells during development and repair necessitates extensive transcriptional reprogramming.

In this mouse incisor injury model, we removed approximately one-third of the erupted enamel, along with the underlying dentin and pulp tissue. Given that enamel is acellular and therefore incapable of directly sensing damage, we propose that pulp cells serve as the primary transducers of the injury response. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed expansion of the rapidly cycling dental mesenchymal cell population surrounding the cervical loop inner enamel epithelium (IEE), accompanied by distal migration—findings that align with reports from Paul Sharpe and colleagues [10]. In this context, injury-activated pulp cells likely initiate reciprocal signaling interactions with adjacent IEE cells, thereby promoting coordinated and enhanced proliferative activity within the epithelial compartment. This multiscale, cross-tissue coordination is critical for maintaining the anatomical integrity and functional restoration of regenerated dentin and enamel matrices.

The mechanisms by which dental mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) at the incisor apex sense distal tip injury and become reactivated to guide tissue repair remain incompletely defined. Although incisor clipping eliminates occlusal loading, this alteration in mechanical force has been shown not to drive proximal MSC activation [10], despite mechanical load being a primary regulator of periodontal tissue regeneration [50, 51].

Previous studies have demonstrated that Thy1-expressing, slow-cycling cells located within the dental mesenchyme between the lingual and labial cervical loops constitute a subpopulation of MSCs capable of differentiating into fast cycling dental pulp cells and odontoblasts during both homeostasis and following incisor tip injury [10]. Distal injury may stimulate the release of Sonic hedgehog from the neurovascular bundle, thereby activating MSCs at the proximal end of the incisor [52].

In the uninjured wt mouse incisor, the pool of rapidly cycling dental mesenchymal cells is smaller than the adjacent IEE TAC population. This distinctive pattern reflects physiological homeostasis, in which only the outer enamel layer of tooth undergoes continuous wear and requires replenishment. These findings further suggest that activated IEE TACs may not generate sufficient paracrine signals to robustly stimulate neighboring dental mesenchymal cells. While, in the Satb1 cKO mouse model, defective enamel fails to adequately protect the underlying dentin. Consequently, both rapidly cycling dental mesenchymal cells and IEE TACs become activated to compensate for accelerated enamel and dentin wear. As a result, the number of Ki67-positive cells increases in both compartments. Because LaCL precursor cells in the Satb1 cKO mouse model are already in a proliferative state, trimming fails to elicit an additional proliferative response.

When rapid cycling dental mesenchymal cells commit to odontoblasts and begin depositing dentin, adjacent IEE TACs differentiate to PABs. Once dentin deposition begins, reciprocal interactions between the ECM and cells play critical roles in driving ameloblast differentiation. Mouse model genetic studies have shown that dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP) and dentin matrix protein 1 (DMP1) are indispensable for normal enamel formation [53–55]. In vitro experiment demonstrate that type I collagen enhances ALC cell adhesion while suppressing PCNA expression [56]. In parallel, the organic dentin matrix, composed of approximately 90% type I collagen by weight [43, 57], may substantially modulate matrix stiffness, thereby influencing the chromatin architecture, transcriptional programs, and cellular behaviors of adjacent PABs.

Previous studies indicate that ECMs enriched in laminin or fibronectin—principal components of basement membranes—form relatively compliant tissues with elastic moduli in the kilopascal (kPa) range. In contrast, type I collagen assembles into thick, densely packed fibrils that markedly reduce tissue compliance, generating matrices with stiffness reaching hundreds of megapascals [45, 58–60]. In the incisors of injured wt and Satb1 cKO mice, we observed delayed dentin deposition and reduced dentin matrix thickness, suggesting altered matrix stiffness during the transition from TCA cells to preameloblasts under these conditions. We hypothesize that a less stiff microenvironment sustains continuous cell proliferation and impedes ameloblast differentiation. In vitro, we observed that ALCs exited the cell cycle and upregulated amelogenin and ameloblastin expression when cultured on increasing concentrations of type I collagen. These observations together indicate that modulation of ECM stiffness can influence ameloblast differentiation and regeneration. Supporting this concept, prior studies have demonstrated that mechanically softer microenvironments generally favor progenitor cell proliferation and migration during tissue repair, thereby facilitating functional restoration of the tissue [61].

YAP (Yes-associated protein) and TAZ (transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif) act as key mediators of mechanotransduction, converting mechanical cues from the cellular microenvironment into biochemical signals that influence cell fate, including stem cell differentiation and metabolic reprogramming. Tissue-specific deletion of Yap1 results in defective ameloblast differentiation [62]. Specifically, YAP/TAZ regulates proliferation and differentiation of dental epithelial progenitor TACs through the ITGA3-mediated FAK–YAP–mTOR signaling axis [34]. In the cervical loop, rapidly cycling dental mesenchymal cells and IEE TACs interact across the basement membrane. Major basement membrane components—including laminin-5, collagen type IV, and fibronectin—serve as established ECM ligands for ITGA3 [63–66]. ITGA3 is highly expressed in IEE TACs [34]. It is therefore plausible that basement membrane thickness and composition or mesenchymal derived paracrine factors may facilitate dental TAC differentiation by modulating ITGA3-dependent mechanic signaling.

SATB1’s roles in developmental ECM regulation remains largely unexplored. During odontogenesis, SATB1 expression is first detected in inner enamel epithelium (IEE) cells, increases in PABs, and subsequently declines as ameloblasts progress toward full differentiation. This temporal pattern coincides with dynamic changes in its extracellular microenvironment. As dental epithelial cells differentiate, they sequentially migrate across distinct ECM compartments—transitioning from the basement membrane to the dentin matrix, then to the organic enamel matrix, and ultimately to mineralized enamel. These transitions involve remarkable alterations in matrix composition and mechanical properties. In wt injury models exhibiting reduced SATB1 expression, as well as in Satb1 cKO mice lacking SATB1, we observed prolonged retention of the basement membrane and TAC zone, and a spatial dealy in the initiation of dentin and enamel matrix deposition. We assume that the dentin phenotype here is a consequence of dental epithelial–mesenchymal interactions. However, we have not detected SATB1 expression in the dental mesenchymal compartment. Furthermore, the secretory ameloblast phenotype observed in the Satb1 global knockout mice closely resembles that of the Satb1 cKO mice. Together, these findings suggest that dental mesenchymal cells exert minimal influence on the dental epithelial cell phenotype when SATB1 is deficient in the uninjured model.

These findings indicate that SATB1 regulates cell proliferation, which in turn affects cell differentiation and the extracellular matrix microenvironment, collectively influencing the progression of ameloblast differentiation. Consistent with this model, our in vitro experiments show that increasing the concentration of type I collagen in the culture substrate suppresses cell proliferation while enhancing enamel matrix protein expression. These results suggest that a suitably stiff matrix environment promotes differentiation over proliferation in SATB1-overexpressing ALCs. Together, these data support a model in which SATB1 coordinates cell cycle dynamics and ECM mechanical properties to balance self-renewal and differentiation, providing mechanistic insight into ameloblast lineage progression and enamel regeneration.
