Genomic Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) markers and mitochondrial haplotypes illuminate the origins of Crown-of-Thorns Starfish (Acanthaster solaris) outbreaks in the South China Sea
Authors: Peng Tian, Wei Wang, Site Luo, Xiao Du, Yinghui Zhong, Fucheng Sun, Ziqing Xu, Jiaguang Xiao, Shuangen Yu, Wentao Niu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOutbreaks of the coral predator Crown-of-Thorns Starfish (CoTS) pose a severe threat to coral reefs in the Indo-Pacific Ocean. In 2018, the South China Sea (SCS) experienced significant CoTS outbreaks, leading to extensive coral mortality across the Xisha, Zhongsha, Dongsha, and Nansha Islands, severely impacting the coral reef ecosystem.ResultsTo explore the origins of these outbreaks, we conducted a comprehensive genomic analysis using data from genomic single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs) and mitochondrial haplotypes. Our analysis reveals that CoTS populations in the SCS, which exhibit moderate genetic diversity and may have undergone positive selection or population expansion. There was limited genetic differentiation among CoTS populations from XS, ZS, and NS groups. Especially between the XS and ZS groups, there was almost no genetic differentiation. The populations from XS, ZS, and NS groups have strong genetic connections with populations in Vietnam and the Philippines. There was high gene flow from Vietnam to the Xisha Islands and from the Philippines to the Nansha Islands, suggesting that the CoTS populations in these regions primarily originate from these neighboring countries.ConclusionThe comprehensive analyses of SNP and mitochondrial genomes have provided valuable insights into the population genetics of CoTS. This research has generated significant genomic resources and facilitated important studies on the genetics of the CoTS species. By identifying potential source populations and understanding the genetic basis of their spread, managers can develop more effective conservation strategies to protect vulnerable coral reef ecosystems in the SCS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11011-9.

Background

Reef-building corals from the Scleractinia order are the backbone of shallow tropical reefs, providing crucial habitats and protection for a wide variety of marine species [1, 2]. Renowned for their remarkable biodiversity and essential ecological roles, these coral reefs are some of the most important marine ecosystems on the planet [3, 4]. However, they are increasingly under threat from a range of challenges, including coral bleaching, storms, and predator outbreaks [5, 6].

The Crown-of-Thorns Starfish (CoTS, Acanthaster solaris), a voracious coral predator, has emerged as a major factor driving the ongoing decline of coral reefs across the Indo-Pacific ocean [7]. Recurrent outbreaks of CoTS have been responsible for approximately 42% of coral loss on the Great Barrier Reef over a span of 27 years [8]. These outbreaks have also inflicted significant damage on the coral reefs of the Ryukyu Islands, Philippines, French Polynesia, and other regions [9, 10]. Preventing CoTS outbreaks is essential for reducing coral mortality. However, despite years of study, the triggers of these outbreaks are still not well understood, and finding effective countermeasures has been challenging [11]. A thorough understanding of CoTS outbreak dynamics is urgently needed to develop effective management strategies and to protect vulnerable coral reef ecosystems, ensuring the long-term health and survival of coral reefs.

Between 2005 and 2010, the South China Sea (SCS) experienced devastating outbreaks of the CoTS, leading to a reduction in coral cover by over 90% on some reefs, with no signs of recovery observed [12]. These outbreaks resurfaced in 2018, impacting the Xisha, Zhongsha, Dongsha, and Nansha Islands, and once again causing extensive coral death [12–15]. These recurrent outbreaks pose a significant threat to the coral reef ecosystems and their biodiversity within the SCS.

Similar to other benthic reef organisms, the dispersal of CoTS primarily occurs during their planktonic larval stage, when ocean currents can carry them over vast distances [4, 11, 16]. It has been suggested that ocean currents may transport larvae from the Xisha to the Nansha Islands, particularly between May and August [4]. Though genetic connectivity could be observed over distances up to 1000 km, the exact origins of CoTS outbreaks in the South China Sea remain unclear, including whether they originate from single or multiple source reefs [17, 18]. To better understand the dynamics of CoTS outbreaks, further investigation into larval dispersal patterns and genetic connectivity is crucial.

Previous studies on CoTS populations have utilized various genetic methods, including mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), microsatellites, and limited single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs), each contributing uniquely to our understanding of CoTS genetics. Benzie and Stoddart argued that allozyme data were insufficient for clearly identifying individual CoTS populations [19]. Vogler et al. [20] used the highly variable mitochondrial control region to analyze the phylogeography of CoTS across the Pacific Ocean, revealing the species’ high larval dispersal potential despite significant barriers to genetic exchange among Pacific populations. Harrison et al. [21] aimed to determine the specific origin of CoTS outbreaks on the Great Barrier Reef (GBR) by analyzing genetic diversity and structure using 17 microsatellite loci. However, the limited genetic structure observed made it challenging to pinpoint a source population for the outbreaks. Yasuda et al. [22] expanded the scope by analyzing complete mitochondrial genomes to investigate the sources of outbreaking CoTS larvae and their dispersal routes across the Western Pacific Ocean. Their research revealed that CoTS from various regions, including the Great Barrier Reef (GBR), Vietnam, the Philippines, Japan, and Micronesia, form a Pan-Pacific clade (PP-C) capable of spawning repeated outbreaks. Iguchi et al. [23] examined CoTS populations in the Ryukyu Archipelago and Miyazaki, Japan, using a limited set of biallelic SNPs, they concluded that there was limited genetic differentiation and strong connectivity and gene flow among these populations. Chen et al. [4] provided the only study specifically focusing on the population genetic structure of CoTS in the South China Sea. By employing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and microsatellite markers, their research highlighted historical population expansion and high genetic diversity within South China Sea CoTS populations. Collectively, these studies advance our understanding of CoTS population genetics, which is crucial for managing and mitigating CoTS outbreaks by informing conservation strategies and elucidating the genetic drivers behind these events.

Recent advancements in genetic technology, such as whole genome sequencing (WGS), have significantly enhanced the analysis of CoTS population structure and connectivity, single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs) have proven especially valuable for detecting fine-scale genetic variations within individuals and across populations [11, 24]. These variations offer a higher resolution and a more detailed genetic overview, enabling in-depth studies of CoTS population genetic diversity, structure, and dispersal patterns [21, 25–27]. In this research, we focused on investigating the genetic diversity and population structure of CoTS through SNPs, and validated the findings of population structure using the mitochondrial genome. These approaches aim to infer the origins of CoTS outbreaks in the South China Sea by combining historical gene flow data with current survey monitoring. This information helps managers develop effective strategies to protect vulnerable coral reef ecosystems, ensuring their long-term health and survival of coral reefs.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 257 CoTS individuals were used for population genetic analysis, including 140 newly sequenced individuals in this study from the South China Sea (SCS) and 117 individuals downloaded from NCBI (accession number: PRJDB10499) (Fig. 1 and Table 1). During the period of March to May 2022, a total of 140 adult CoTS with a diameter ranging from 250 to 350 mm (this size of CoTS have reached sexual maturity and have strong reproductive capabilities) were collected from the South China Sea including three stations located in areas rich in coral reef resources while these areas have also been severely affected by CoTS outbreaks. Of these, 89 specimens were collected from the Xisha Islands (XS), with another 32 specimens sourced from Meiji Jiao within the Nansha Islands (NS) and the other 19 from the Zhongsha Islands (ZS) (Table 1). To extend the analysis, we got the whole genome Sequencing data for Crown-of-Thorns Starfish (Acanthaster planci) PE150 from the NCBI project PRJDB10499 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJDB10499). DNA isolation and sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction from the tube feet samples was performed using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kits (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The quality of the gDNAs was assessed through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Scientific). Subsequently, the samples were sent to Novogene Company (Beijing, China) for next-generation sequencing (NGS) library preparation, this step utilized the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit from NEB, a process designed to prepare the DNA for sequencing by creating a library of fragments that can be efficiently sequenced. The concentrations of the libraries were then accurately determined through quantitative PCR, allowing for the effective pooling of libraries based on their measured concentrations. Pooled libraries underwent rolling circle amplification to generate DNA nanoballs (DNBs). These DNBs were loaded into the flow cell, and paired-end 150bp sequencing was conducted on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform, following standard procedures. Each of the 140 samples from three distinct populations (NS, XS, and ZS) produced approximately 12 gigabytes of sequencing reads.

Data analysis workflow

To integrate datasets from various studies, including those from non-Chinese populations, we implemented specific measures to minimize batch effects. We standardized processing protocols across all samples collected and sequenced in this study as well as those obtained from Yasuda et al. 2021. Specifically, we applied a uniform data quality control pipeline and sequence alignment protocol to ensure consistency. Additionally, we conducted a rigorous evaluation of potential batch effects, adhering to best practices for multi-study genomic analyses as recommended by previous reports [28, 29]. This meticulous approach ensures that the observed genetic patterns truly reflect biological attributes rather than discrepancies from varying data processing methods.

Initially, we assessed the quality of all data using FastQC (v0.11.5) [30], which provides a comprehensive overview of data integrity. To improve data quality, we applied Fastp (v0.23) with parameters "-q 15 -u 40 -w 16 -f 10 -F 10" for stringent filtering of low-quality reads [31]. The high-quality, filtered reads were then aligned to the Crown-of-Thorns Starfish (CoTS) reference genome (OKI-Apl_1.0, GCF_001949145.1) sourced from NCBI, using BWA-mem (v0.7.17) with default settings for precise alignment [32]. Following this, the SAMTools software (v1.11) [33] was used to efficiently sort and organize the alignments. To ensure data accuracy, PCR duplicates, which could potentially bias the results, were identified and removed using Picard (v1.92, http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/).

The identification of single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs) was conducted using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.2.5, https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk), following the protocol by Heldenbrand et al. [34]. The process began with initial variant calling using the HaplotypeCaller module. This was followed by the CombineGVCFs and GenotypeGVCFs modules for joint genotyping across the entire sample set. Following this, raw SNP sites were selected using the SelectVariants function and filtered with the VariantFiltration module, employing parameters recommended to ensure a high-quality SNP dataset [35]. The resulting curated SNP sites, in VCF format, were then prepared for downstream analyses. This marks a critical step in understanding the genetic diversity and structure within the CoTS populations.

Genetic diversity estimation

To analyze the genetic diversity both within and among CoTS populations, a series of genetic indices were computed, including observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), average pairwise nucleotide diversity (Pi), Tajima’s D, the inbreeding coefficient (Fis), and the pairwise genetic differentiation (Fst values). The measure of individual heterozygosity was determined by calculating the ratio of heterozygous SNPs to the total genome size, as detailed by Liu et al. [35]. The observed heterozygosity (Ho) for each population was then ascertained by averaging these individual heterozygosity values. The computations for Ho, He, Pi, and Tajima’s D were executed using VCFtools, applying a 100-kilobase sliding window technique along with a 10-kilobase step for analysis [36]. Furthermore, the calculations for the Fis and Fst values were also carried out in VCFtools, using its default parameters. These comprehensive indices provide critical insights into the levels of genetic diversity, structure, and variability present within and between CoTS populations, offering a robust framework for understanding their genetic dynamics and potential impacts on coral reef ecosystems.

Nucleotide variation in the CoTS mitogenomes was detected using sliding window analyses by DnaSP v.6.12. Mitogenome sequences excluding CR were aligned using Clustal W, as implemented in MEGA-X. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) among protein coding genes (PCGs) were estimated by sliding window analyses (a 100 bp window in 25 bp step size) across all the populations (Table 2). Population structure analysis

To analyze the population structure of CoTS, a comprehensive analytical method was used, integrating phylogenetic, ancestry, and principal component analyses. Central to this approach was the stringent filtering of SNPs, removing alleles that were rare, deviated from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, or showed significant linkage disequilibrium. This preliminary step was carried out using VCFtools (v0.1.16) and PLINK (v1.07), as per the methodology described by Purcell et al. [37]. Following the SNP filtration, the genetic relationships among individuals were visualized through the construction of a maximum likelihood tree. This tree, generated in Iqtree2 (v2.3.5) and reinforced by 1000 bootstrap replicates, illustrated the genetic distances connecting individuals. To further explore the genetic ancestry of the CoTS populations, the ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) was utilized, conducting 20 iterations across potential ancestral configurations (K = 2–5), each backed by 100 bootstraps. The coefficient of variation (CV) error was used to determine the best K value [38]. The exploration of population structure was rounded out with principal component analysis (PCA), a technique instrumental in unravelling the genetic composition and inter-population dynamics. This analysis was facilitated by GCTA (v 1.94.1), with an initial step of principal component filtering through dimensionality reduction to manage dimensionality, as advocated by Yang et al. [39]. Through these sophisticated genetic analyses, profound insights were gained into the underlying genetic makeup and the intricate web of relationships existing within and across CoTS populations, shedding light on their evolutionary dynamics and potential dispersal patterns.

Gene flow analysis

Patterson’s D-statistic, also known as the ABBA-BABA test, was applied to investigate signs of introgression among CoTS populations, revealing genomic markers indicative of gene flow post-speciation. This test is pivotal for identifying historical admixture events that blur the genetic boundaries between populations. Additionally, to map out the patterns of directional admixture across CoTS populations, TreeMix software was utilized. This tool constructs a graphical representation based on the covariance structure of allele frequencies, thus offering insights into the historical population splits and mixtures. The process involved conducting multiple TreeMix analyses under varied migration event scenarios to pinpoint the model that best explained the observed genetic covariance among the samples, the TreeMix run featuring nine migration events yielded the most accurate interpretation of sample covariance [40]. This approach not only underscored the complexity of CoTS population dynamics but also enhanced the understanding of how gene flow and introgression have shaped the current genetic landscape of these CoTS populations.

Mitogenome variations analysis

Mitochondrial maternal lineage analysis is a method of studying the maternal genetic relationships among individuals, focusing on the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) passed down exclusively through the maternal line. In this study, we employed the GetOrganelle pipeline (v1.7.7.0) to assemble all mitochondrial genomes [41]. The mitogenome assembly utilized 'animal_mt' as seed reads sourced from the standard database provided. Following assembly, the short-reads derived from WGS were aligned to the newly assembled mitochondrial genomes using BWA (v.0.7.17), ensuring accuracy and alignment quality. To refine the assembly and enhance its reliability, a polishing step was conducted using Pilon (v.1.24), the polished mitochondrial genomes were then annotated with the Mitoz toolkit (v3.4) [42], to identify and delineate the 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) critical for mitochondrial function by comparing them against homologous sequences with the reference mitogenome (Acanthaster planci, NC_007788). This comparison facilitated a deeper understanding of the mitochondrial genetic composition of the CoTS populations under study.

For the phylogenetic analysis, the 13 concatenated protein-coding genes (PCGs) from the mitochondrial genome were aligned using MAFFT with default parameters [43]. Subsequently, Maximum likelihood trees were constructed using Iqtree2 (v2.3.5), offering robust bootstrap support with 10,000 repeats, and visualized with FigTree (v1.4.4) to elucidate the maternal lineage relationships among the samples. Additionally, to capture the diversity and distribution of haplotypes across the sampled populations, DNASP (v6.12) was utilized for haplotype identification, and the resultant haplotype networks were visualized using the PopART (Population Analysis with Reticulate Trees) software, which employing the TCS network inference method to provide a visual representation of the haplotype connections [44]. This analysis offers valuable insights into the maternal genetic structure and variability within and among CoTS populations.

Results

Sequencing and mapping assessment

The quality assessment for the sequencing data of the comprehensive set of 140 sequenced CoTS samples revealed an impressively low error rate of less than 0.04%. This remarkable precision underscores the sequencing protocol’s efficacy, guaranteeing the reliability of the obtained genetic data. Additionally, the average GC content, a crucial metric in genomics, was determined to be 40.92%, further confirming the consistency and stability of the genome. By aligning the sequenced data to the CoTS reference genome, an average sequencing depth of 37 × was achieved, indicating the extent of coverage for each base in the genome by sequencing reads. This substantial sequencing depth enhances confidence in accurately capturing the genetic information within the CoTS samples.

The mapping efficiency, with an average success rate of 98%, underscores the high degree of alignment between the sequenced reads and the reference genome. Such high mapping percentages are crucial for reliable genomic analyses, as they ensure that nearly all the sequenced data can be matched with corresponding regions in the reference genome, minimizing the loss of potentially valuable genetic information. Using the GATK4 for variant calling, 6,243,808 SNPs were identified. This large number of SNPs indicates considerable genetic variation within the CoTS populations analyzed. This wealth of genetic information is pivotal for comprehensive studies on genetic diversity, population structure, and the evolutionary dynamics of CoTS.

Furthermore, nearly all these populations exhibited moderate diversity expect the Vietnam (VIET) population which displayed the lowest genetic diversity, with a Pi value of only 0.001 and Ho < He. The Fis values provide insights into the breeding dynamics within these populations: positive Fis values suggest inbreeding, while negative values indicate outbreeding. For instance, the XS population demonstrated outbreeding dynamics,while NS and ZS demonstrated inbreeding dynamics [46].

The Fst values, used to gauge genetic differentiation between populations, were below the threshold of 0.05 among the NS, ZS, and XS populations. This lack of significant genetic differentiation aligns with Wright’ criteria [47], suggesting a high degree of genetic flow among these populations within the SCS. Besides, genetic differentiation between the VIET and SCS populations was also noted as insignificant, similar to the lack of significant differentiation between the Philippines (PHILI) and SCS populations. However, clear genetic differentiation was observed between the VIET and PHILI populations, indicated by Fst values exceeding 0.05. The highest Fst was observed between the VIET and GBR population (Fst = 0.083), indicating great genetic differentiations between them.

Tajima’s D values being negative in the NS, ZS, and XS populations suggest potential scenarios of positive selection or population expansion within these groups. Especially for XS population, Ho > He and negative Tajima’s D indicate an abundance of low-frequency polymorphisms relative to expectation, which could be a consequence of recent population growth or selective sweeps, population expansion brings new gene flow, increasing the observed heterozygosity (Ho). For NS and ZS populations, Ho < He may be due to population inbreeding. These genetic diversity metrics provide insights into the evolutionary dynamics and population structure of CoTS, crucial for understanding their dispersion and the genetic basis of their outbreaks.

The overall nucleotide diversity based on mitogenome for all CoTS populations combined is 0.00289. Among the individual populations, GBR exhibits the highest nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.0061), indicating a relatively high level of genetic variation within this population which may be indicative of a larger effective population size or historical events that have introduced greater genetic variability. Conversely, the VIET population shows the lowest nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.00209), suggesting a lower genetic variability, which could be attributed to factors such as genetic bottlenecks, smaller population size, or limited gene flow. Other populations such as PHILI and JA also show considerable diversity with values of 0.0041 and 0.00331, respectively. The overall nucleotide diversity of 0.00289 is within the range typically observed in marine invertebrates, suggesting moderate genetic diversity.

The comparison between SNP and mitochondrial protein-coding gene diversity values across CoTS populations reveals significant insights into genetic variation. High diversity in both SNP and mitogenome data in populations like GBR suggests higher genetic variation maintained across both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, likely due to large effective population sizes and stable population histories. However, discrepancies are observed in populations such as VIET, which show lower SNP diversity but relatively higher mitogenome diversity. This may result from different evolutionary pressures on nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, as mitochondrial DNA, inherited maternally and without recombination, can retain genetic diversity through maternal lineages even when nuclear diversity is reduced by bottlenecks or founder effects. In population like PHILI, moderate SNP diversity contrasts with higher mitogenome diversity, possibly reflecting selective sweeps in mitochondrial genomes or differential impacts of genetic drift. Similarly, POH exhibits higher SNP diversity but lower mitogenome diversity, which might indicate recent population expansions where nuclear diversity has increased faster than mitochondrial diversity. These differences underscore the distinct evolutionary dynamics between nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, shaped by their unique inheritance patterns, selective pressures, and historical population dynamics. Understanding these patterns is crucial for a comprehensive view of genetic diversity in CoTS populations.

The differences between SNPs and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in genetic diversity metrics significantly impact their use as genetic markers. SNPs, despite being less informative individually compared to microsatellites, provide more consistent and narrower confidence intervals for population estimates, highlighting their robustness in capturing nuclear genetic diversity [48]. Additionally, SNPs are highly informative for inferring population structure when a sufficient number of loci are used, making them valuable for detailed genetic analysis [49]. In contrast, mtDNA evolves at varying rates across species, leading to discrepancies in genetic diversity compared to nuclear DNA. Its unique inheritance pattern—maternally inherited and non-recombining—means mtDNA can retain genetic diversity through maternal lineages even when nuclear diversity is reduced by bottlenecks or founder effects [50]. The genetic diversity within mitochondrial and nuclear genomes of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii underscores how mtDNA shows different patterns of diversity compared to nuclear DNA due to distinct evolutionary dynamics [51]. In this study, combining the diversity results from both SNPs and mtDNA is crucial for accurately assessing the current genetic background of COTS.

The cross-validation (CV) error was at its lowest at k = 2, indicating the presence of two ancestral cluster. Populations from both the Great Barrier Reef (GBR) and Vietnam (VIET) exhibited homogeneous compositions, suggesting limited differentiation. This indicates a relatively uniform genetic structure within each of these populations, which could be due to isolated breeding or similar environmental pressures leading to parallel evolutionary paths. Interestingly, the Micronesia (POH) population exhibited genetic similarities with the GBR population. This resemblance hints at potential historical connectivity or shared genetic ancestry between these distant populations, suggesting that gene flow might have occurred across vast oceanic distances, possibly facilitated by ocean currents or past migration events. In contrast, the populations from the South China Sea, including Nansha Islands (NS), Zhongsha Islands (ZS) and Xisha Islands (XS), as well as the Philippines (PHILI) populations, displayed greater resemblance to the VIET population, implying shared ancestry or possible gene flow among these populations, and indicating a complex web of genetic connectivity within the region. Population gene flow analysis

The application of TreeMix analysis to explore the population history and gene flow among CoTS populations has provided insightful revelations regarding migration patterns, particularly within the South China Sea (SCS) region (Fig. 5). The analysis uncovers significant migration edges that underscore a pronounced genetic exchange occurring from the Vietnam (VIET) and Philippines (PHILI) populations toward the populations located in the Nansha Islands (NS), Zhongsha Islands (ZS), and Xisha Islands (XS). The significant genetic flow from Vietnam and the Philippines into the South China Sea islands underscores a dynamic and interconnected genetic landscape in the region, suggesting continuous rather than occasional genetic exchange. The observed migration patterns suggest that the VIET and PHILI populations may serve as significant genetic reservoirs for the CoTS populations in the NS, ZS, and XS regions. Mitogenome analysis

The comprehensive analysis of mitochondrial genomes from 213 individual CoTS has yielded rich insights into their maternal lineage and genetic structure. Through meticulous extraction, assembly, and annotation processes, each individual’s mitochondrial DNA was detailed, revealing a composition that includes 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 23 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs, and a non-coding D-loop region. This thorough characterization achieved an average coverage depth of 300X, ensuring high reliability and accuracy in the mitogenomic data obtained. The construction of a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree based on concatenated coding sequences (CDS) of the 13 PCGs unveiled the intricate genetic relationships among the CoTS individuals (Supplementary Fig. S1). Notably, there is no distinct, independent branching pattern in these relationships. Instead, a more nuanced pattern of relationships is evident, with a clustering of individuals from Vietnam (VIET) alongside those from the Xisha Islands (XS) and Zhongsha Islands (ZS). This clustering suggests a shared maternal lineage or historical gene flow between these regions, highlighting the maternal genetic connectivity within the South China Sea.

In addition, analysis of the mitochondrial sequences from the 213 successfully extracted individuals identified a total of 163 haplotype sites (Supplementary Table S2). The distribution of specific haplotypes varies among populations, the XS population exhibits the highest diversity with the greatest number of distinct haplotypes. This high level of haplotype diversity within the XS population suggests a rich genetic pool, potentially indicative of a broad and diverse maternal lineage. Conversely, the Philippines (PHILI) population displays the lowest number of haplotypes, which could be attributed to the smaller sample size of PHILI individuals collected for this study.

The Tree of Clustered Singleton (TCS) haplotype network structure, generated based on the 163 identified haplotype sites, displays a widespread distribution of haplotypes across various populations (Fig. 6). Notably, VIET haplotypes cluster with XS haplotypes, indicating a strong genetic similarity and a close relationship between the VIET and XS populations. This clustering suggests shared genetic lineages or historical gene flow between these two groups. Discussion

This study represents a significant advancement in the understanding of the genetic diversity and structure of CoTS populations by utilizing genomic resequencing technology to analyze single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs). By focusing on gene flow into the Nansha Islands (NS), Xisha Islands (XS), and Zhongsha Islands (ZS) populations, this research aim to trace the origins of CoTS outbreaks in the South China Sea (SCS). Unlike previous studies that only relied on mtDNA [4, 20–22], microsatellites [4], or limited sets of SNPs [23], this application of single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs) provided a high-resolution perspective on genetic diversity, structure, and gene flow among these populations.

Genetic diversity and structure

The CoTS populations in the SCS displayed moderate genetic diversity, as indicated by heterozygosity (Ho, He) and nucleotide diversity (Pi) values. Additionally, a signature of population expansion was detected, evidenced by negative Tajima’s D values, which suggest recent population growth. Such expansions may be driven by environmental changes or shifts in predator–prey dynamics, as also seen in other marine species [52]. These findings align with earlier studies, such as those by Benzie et al. [53], who also observed population expansions and genetic connectivity across crown-of-thorns starfish populations in Australia. Similarly, our findings of genetic diversity are somewhat consistent with the results obtained by Chen et al. [4], who reported that the nucleotide diversity of all populations was lower than 0.01 in the SCS based on microsatellite markers analysis. And the evidence of population expansion in CoTS within the SCS, also consistented with the findings from Chen et al. [4], which underscores the dynamic nature of these populations.

Mitogenome haplotype analysis

Our mitochondrial haplotype analysis revealed significant variation between populations, with the XS population showing the highest haplotype diversity. This supports the idea that central populations, like those in the Xisha Islands, often exhibit higher genetic diversity due to their role as hubs for gene flow [54]. In contrast, the PHILI population exhibited lower haplotype diversity, which could be attributed to genetic bottlenecks or founder effects, where smaller populations experience reduced genetic diversity [55]. Additionally, the TCS haplotype network and phylogenetic analysis based on mitochondrial genes revealed the genetic affinity between the VIET and XS populations, which is consistent with the results of phylogenetic analysis based on nuclear genomic SNPs. Meanwhile, the SNP analysis can also reveal clearer clustering relationships among populations besides the South China Sea population.

Congruency and incongruency between genomic data

Interestingly, our comparison of mitochondrial and nuclear SNPs data highlighted both congruent and incongruent patterns. For example, while mitochondrial data revealed a strong relationship between the VIET and XS populations, SNPs data showed clearer differentiation between VIET and PHILI populations. This incongruence may arise from the different evolutionary forces acting on mitochondrial and nuclear genomes, as suggested by Ballard & Whitlock [56], where nuclear DNA reflects more recent gene flow and selective pressures, while mitochondrial DNA captures longer-term maternal lineage signals. These discrepancies have also been observed in other marine species, such as corals and sea urchins, which exhibit different evolutionary dynamics between their nuclear and mitochondrial genomes [57]. The complementary use of both mtDNA and SNPs can offer a more complete and in-depth understanding of the population relationships of CoTS.

Population connectivity and gene flow

The genetic structure analyses in this study were instrumental in delineating CoTS populations into groups based on genetic similarities and geographical separations. Our findings indicate that the NS population is primarily derived from the PHILI population, while the XS and ZS populations have closer genetic ties to the VIET population. This underscores the strong genetic connectivity across CoTS populations in the SCS, facilitated by stable ocean currents and other marine processes. This connectivity enables effective larval dispersal across habitats that are up to 800 km apart, highlighting the significant dispersal potential of CoTS larvae [58, 59]. The use of high-resolution SNPs not only supports earlier studies on the broad genetic connectivity of CoTS populations but also provides the specificity and resolution necessary to better understand the origins and dynamics of outbreaks more clearly [23].

Conservation implications

Massive outbreaks of CoTS pose a significant threat to the survival of coral reef ecosystems [7, 60]. By identifying potential source populations of CoTS and understanding the genetic basis of their spread, managers can develop more effective conservation strategies to protect vulnerable coral reef ecosystems in the SCS. The insights gained from this study have important conservation implications. Given the high connectivity and genetic diversity observed in populations such as those in the Xisha Islands, targeted conservation efforts should focus on monitoring these regions, as they serve as important reservoirs for genetic diversity and potential sources of larval dispersal. Effective management strategies will need to consider the genetic connectivity and dispersal potential of CoTS populations to mitigate the risk of future outbreaks. Similar conservation approaches have been recommended for other marine species, where source populations play a critical role in maintaining genetic diversity across regions [58, 61].

Future research directions

Although this study includes data from populations with smaller sample sizes (e.g., VIET, PHILI, POH), consistent patterns of gene flow and genetic structure were observed across different analytical methods. However, we recognize that small sample sizes can introduce uncertainty in genetic analyses. Future studies should prioritize increasing sample sizes and incorporating additional populations to refine our understanding of CoTS population dynamics. Furthermore, investigating environmental factors, such as ocean currents and habitat structure, will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors shaping genetic diversity and connectivity in CoTS populations.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive genomic analysis, utilizing both genomic single nucleotide polymorphism sites (SNPs) and mitogenome haplotypes datas, has significantly advanced our comprehension of CoTS population genetics in the South China Sea. Identifying over six million SNPs has been instrumental in revealing the complex genetic diversity and evolutionary dynamics within CoTS populations. Exploring genetic diversity indices and pairwise genetic differentiations revealed intriguing patterns, notably lower diversity in the Vietnam (VIET) population. All the CoTS populations in the South China Sea (primarily XS, ZS and NS) exhibited moderate diversity, and these populations may have undergone positive selection or population expansion. The GBR and VIET populations emerged as the two main ancestral groups, with the CoTS populations in the South China Sea showing a closer genetic relationship to the VIET population. There was limited genetic differentiation among COTS populations from XS, ZS, and NS groups. Especially between the XS and ZS groups, there is almost no genetic differentiation. The XS and ZS population mainly originated from the VIET population, while the NS population mainly originated from the PHILY population. Stable ocean currents may also assist COTS larvae to travel from XS to NS population from June to August. Mitogenome haplotype analysis highlighted the widespread distribution of haplotypes and emphasized the genetic affinity between the VIET and XS populations.

This study provides a valuable theoretical foundation for developing targeted management strategies to mitigate the impact of CoTS outbreaks, thereby contributing to the conservation and resilience of coral reef ecosystems in the South China Sea.
