Learning Large-Scale Competitive Team Behaviors with Mean-Field Interactions
Bhavini Jeloka, Yue Guan, Panagiotis Tsiotras

Abstract
While multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has shown strong empirical performance, existing methods struggle with a large number of agents, due to the combinatorial growth of joint interactions. Mean-field (MF) approximations address this challenge by replacing pairwise interactions with surrogate interactions against population distributions, yielding tractable formulations to solve for the large-population policies. However, existing MF formulations focus on fully cooperative or purely competitive settings and fail to capture the mixed cooperative–competitive structure of team-based games. We extend mean-field learning to zero-sum team games , where agents cooperate within each team and compete at the team level. We show that such games admit ϵ \epsilon -optimal decentralized policies that depend only on the local states of each agent and the population team distributions. Building on this structure, we propose MF-MAPPO, a scalable algorithm with a shared actor and a minimally informed critic per team. MF-MAPPO is trained directly in finite-population simulators rather than using mean-field oracles, thereby enabling deployment to realistic scenarios with thousands of agents. We further extend MF-MAPPO to partially observable settings via a simple gradient-regularized training scheme. Experiments on large-scale benchmarks in our simulation platform ( MFEnv ), including population games with analytical solutions and high-dimensional battlefield scenarios, demonstrate that MF-MAPPO outperforms existing MARL baselines and yields rich, heterogeneous behaviors.



1
Introduction

Large-scale multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) suffers from a scalability bottleneck:
the joint state–action space grows exponentially with the number of agents, rendering standard approaches such as MADDPG
(
Lowe et al., 2017
)
and MAPPO
(
Yu et al., 2022
)
intractable in large populations.
A principled remedy is provided by mean-field theory
(
Huang et al., 2006
)
, which enables tractable analysis and learning by approximating interactions among agents through an interaction between a representative agent and the population distribution (i.e., the mean-field).

Mean-field methods have led to a rich body of work in both non-cooperative settings, through mean-field games (MFGs)
(
Huang et al., 2006
;
Lasry and Lions, 2007
)
, and fully cooperative settings, through mean-field control (MFC)
(
Bensoussan et al., 2013
;
Gu et al., 2021
)
.
Recent learning-based methods include, MF-Q
(
Yang et al., 2020
)
, DDPG-based extensions for mean-field team games
(
Shao et al., 2024b
)
, and GAN-based approaches for continuous domains
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
.
However, existing methods often rely on access to a
mean-field oracle
for exact population distribution trajectories, which are typically available only for simple scenarios.
Additionally, these methods are primarily designed for either fully competitive or fully cooperative regimes.
Consequently, scenarios involving
mixed cooperative–competitive interactions
, where agents collaborate within teams while competing across teams, remain underexplored despite their prevalence in real-world systems such as team sports
(
Gaviria Alzate et al., 2025
)
and social dilemmas
(
Leibo et al., 2017
)
.

Recent work has begun to address this gap through team-based mean-field formulations.
Early single-team results appear in
Arabneydi and Mahajan (2014)
, while multi-population extensions have been studied in
Perolat et al. (2021)
, typically under restrictive independence assumptions.
More recently,
Guan et al. (2024b)
introduced
zero-sum mean-field team games
(ZS-MFTGs), later generalized to general-sum team settings by
Subramanian et al. (2023)
.
While these formulations provide a principled foundation, existing numerical solution approaches rely primarily on linear–quadratic structure or discretization.
Although such methods scale in the number of agents, they struggle
computationally
to handle complex environments with high-dimensional state spaces and nonlinear dynamics, limiting their applicability in modern settings.

Beyond computational challenges, introducing competition fundamentally alters the structure of mean-field interactions.
In standard mean-field settings, optimality is characterized through single-agent deviations
(
Cui and Koeppl, 2022
)
, whereas competitive team settings require reasoning about
team-level deviations
.
As a result, optimal policies must depend on feedback from the opponent’s distribution
(
Guan et al., 2024b
)
.
This introduces another key challenge: agents require access to the opponent population distribution during execution, which is difficult under
partial observability of population distributions
.
Most existing approaches assume perfect mean-field measurement or communication
(
Guan et al., 2024b
;
Subramanian et al., 2023
)
.
While partial-observation and estimation methods
(
Inoue et al., 2021
;
Perrin et al., 2021
;
Benjamin and Abate, 2025b
)
have been explored, they often struggle in discrete spaces or in competitive environments.
Moreover, addressing partial information requires not only accurate estimation of mean-field distributions, but also systematic adaptation of the learning framework to ensure robustness to estimation errors.

In this work, we address these challenges by developing a scalable learning framework for mixed cooperative–competitive mean-field systems.

Contributions.

We introduce
Mean-Field Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization
(MF-MAPPO), which achieves scalability for mixed cooperative–competitive settings through three key design principles with theoretical guarantees:
(i) a
minimally-informed critic
that depends only on team distributions, ensuring that the input dimension remains independent of the number of agents;
(ii) a
shared actor per team
, which reduces the joint strategy space to identical team policies and enables efficient learning (Theorem
3.1
); and
(iii) the ability to train with
N
-agent simulators and generalize to larger populations (Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
).

To address partial observability, we show that MF-MAPPO can be combined with gradient regularization and deployed using estimated mean-fields without requiring retraining, and we provide performance bounds depending on the estimation accuracy (Proposition
2
).

We validate our approach across several environments, demonstrating that MF-MAPPO produces stable learning dynamics and rich behaviors without reward engineering.

Overall, our contributions advance
scalable
reinforcement learning for realistic large-population systems by jointly addressing
mixed cooperative–competitive interactions
,
partial observability
, and
finite-population training
.

Problem Formulation and Mean-Field Approximation

A zero-sum mean-field team game (ZS-MFTG) models a discrete-time stochastic game between two large teams of agents (Red and Blue)
(
Guan et al., 2024b
)
.
The Blue and Red teams consist of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}

identical
agents for each team.
Let
X^{N_{1}}_{i,t}\in\mathcal{X}
and
U^{N_{1}}_{i,t}\in\mathcal{U}
represent the state and action of Blue agent
i\in[N_{1}]
at time
t
.
Here,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{U}
are the finite state and action spaces of the Blue team.
Similarly,
Y^{N_{2}}_{j,t}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
V^{N_{2}}_{j,t}\in\mathcal{V}
denote the state and action of Red agent
j\in[N_{2}]
at time
t
.
The joint state-action variables for the Blue and Red teams are denoted as
(\mathbf{X}^{N_{1}}_{t},\mathbf{U}^{N_{1}}_{t})
and
(\mathbf{Y}^{N_{2}}_{t},\mathbf{V}^{N_{2}}_{t})
, respectively.
We denote the space of probability measures over a set
E
as
\mathcal{P}({E})
.
Below,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\big)
represents the total variation between
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(E)
.

Definition 1
.

The
empirical distributions
(ED) for the two teams are respectively defined as
\mathcal{M}^{N_{1}}_{t}\!(x)\!=\!\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\!\mathds{1}_{x}\!(X^{N_{1}}_{i,t})
and
\mathcal{N}^{N_{2}}_{t}(y)\!=\!\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\!\mathds{1}_{y}\!(Y^{N_{2}}_{j,t})
, where
\mathds{1}_{\cdot}\big({\cdot}\big)
is the indicator.

Specifically,
\mathcal{M}^{N_{1}}_{t}(x)
gives the fraction of Blue agents at state
x
.
We use
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{N_{1}}=\mathrm{Emp}_{\mu}(\mathbf{X}^{N_{1}}_{t})
and

\mathcal{N}_{t}^{N_{2}}=\mathrm{Emp}_{\nu}(\mathbf{Y}^{N_{2}}_{t})

to denote the EDs computed from the given joint states.

Dynamics and Rewards.

We consider weakly-coupled dynamics where the dynamics of each individual agent are coupled with other agents through the EDs
(
Huang et al., 2006
)
.
For the Blue agent
i
, its stochastic transition is governed by the kernel

f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
:

\mathbb{P}(X_{i,t+1}^{N_{1}}=x_{i,t+1}^{N_{1}}|U_{i,t}^{N_{1}}=u_{i,t}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{X}_{t}^{N_{1}}=\mathbf{x}_{t}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{Y}_{t}^{N_{2}}\!=\!\mathbf{y}_{t}^{N_{2}})=f_{t}(x_{i,t+1}^{N_{1}}|x_{i,t}^{N_{1}},u_{i,t}^{N_{1}},\mu^{N_{1}}_{t},\nu^{N_{2}}_{t}),

(1)

where
\mu^{N_{1}}_{t}\!\!=\!\!\mathrm{Emp}_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{N_{1}})
and
\nu^{N_{2}}_{t}\!\!=\!\!\mathrm{Emp}_{\nu}(\mathbf{y}_{t}^{N_{2}})
. Similarly, the dynamics of a Red agent is governed by the transition kernel
g_{t}(y_{j,t+1}^{N_{2}}|y_{j,t}^{N_{2}},v_{j,t}^{N_{2}},\mu^{N_{1}}_{t},\nu^{N_{2}}_{t})
.
All agents in the Blue team receive an identical
weakly-coupled
team reward, i.e.,
r_{t}\triangleq r_{t}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}):\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{R}
.
The Red agents receive
-r_{t}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})
as their rewards (zero-sum).
We assume that the Blue team is
maximizing
while the Red team is
minimizing
and
r_{t}\in[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]
for all
t
.

Assumption 1

(Lipschitz Model)
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and all
t
,
there exist constants
L_{f},L_{r}>0
such that

\textstyle\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\lvert f_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u,\mu,\nu)-f_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u,\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq L_{f}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\big)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\nu,\nu^{\prime}\big)\big)
and
\textstyle\left\lvert r_{t}(\mu,\nu)-r_{t}(\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq L_{r}\big(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\big)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\nu,\nu^{\prime}\big)\big)
.

We similarly assume that
g_{t}
is
L_{g}
-Lipschitz.
The Lipschitz model is a standard assumption in the mean-field literature
(
Huang et al., 2006
;
Gu et al., 2021
)
, without which the mean-field approximation may fail as shown in the counterexamples
(
Cui et al., 2024
;
Guan et al., 2024b
)
.

Information Structure.

We consider a mean-field sharing information structure
(
Arabneydi and Mahajan, 2015
)
, where each agent’s decision depends on its own state and the two teams’ EDs.
We start by assuming full observation of EDs and later we relax this assumption in Section
4
.
Specifically, the Blue and Red teams seek to construct
mixed
Markov policies

\phi_{i,t}(u|x_{i,t}^{N_{1}},\mu^{N_{1}}_{t},\nu^{N_{2}}_{t})~~\text{and}~~\psi_{j,t}(v|y_{j,t}^{N_{2}},\mu^{N_{1}}_{t},\nu^{N_{2}}_{t}).

We use
\Phi_{t}
to denote the set of individual Blue policies at time
t
.
We define the Blue team policy
\phi^{N_{1}}_{t}\!=\!\{\phi_{i,t}\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}}
as the collection of the
N_{1}
Blue agent individual policies, and denote the set of Blue team policies as
\Phi^{N_{1}}_{t}\!=\!\times_{N_{1}}\Phi_{t}
.
Similarly, the Red team policy is denoted as
\psi_{t}^{N_{2}}\in\Psi_{t}^{N_{2}}\!=\!\times_{N_{2}}\Psi_{t}
.

Optimization Objective.

The performance of a team policy pair
\textstyle(\phi^{N_{1}}\!,\psi^{N_{2}})
is measured by

J^{N,\phi^{N_{1}},\psi^{N_{2}}}\big(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{0}^{N_{2}}\big)=\mathbb{E}_{{\phi^{N_{1}},\psi^{N_{2}}}}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T}r_{t}(\mathcal{M}^{N_{1}}_{t},\mathcal{N}^{N_{2}}_{t})\big|\mathbf{x}_{0}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}^{N_{2}}_{0}\!\Big].

When the maximizing Blue team considers its worst-case performance, we have the following max-min optimization:

\hskip-0.72229pt\underline{J}^{N*}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{0}^{N_{2}})=\max_{\mathbf{\phi}^{N_{1}}\in\Phi^{N_{1}}}\min_{\mathbf{\psi}^{N_{2}}\in\Psi^{N_{2}}}J^{N,\phi^{N_{1}},\psi^{N_{2}}}\hskip-1.4457pt(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{N_{1}}\hskip-3.61371pt,\mathbf{y}_{0}^{N_{2}}),\hskip-7.22743pt

(2)

where
\underline{J}^{N*}
is the lower game value for the
N
-agent finite-population game.
Similarly, the minimizing Red team considers a min-max optimization and the upper game value.
Note that the optimization in (
2
) allows agents within each team to follow different strategies.
Such
non-identical team policies
preserve maximum flexibility for the Blue team and, more crucially, avoid underestimating the adversary Red team’s strategy flexibility.
However, this generality leads to exponential growth in the joint policy space, rendering the problem intractable
(
Bernstein et al., 2002
)
.

Battlefield Scenario.

Figure 1:
Battlefield Scenario.

To motivate and ground the ZS-MFTG formulation, we consider a grid-based battlefield scenario in which Blue agents aim to reach designated targets (green cells), while Red agents attempt to block their access.
At each cell, an activation–deactivation mechanism governs interactions:
an agent’s probability of being deactivated is proportional to the opponent’s numerical advantage relative to its own team’s presence in that cell.
This induces strategic grouping and dispersion behaviors.
The Blue team seeks to maximize the fraction of its population that reaches the target cells, while the Red team aims to minimize this quantity, resulting in a zero-sum objective.
Applying the
\mathrm{Emp}
operator yields the empirical distributions of both teams.

Mean-Field Approximation.

To reduce the complexity of team policy optimization domains in (
2
), previous works
(
Guan et al., 2024b
;
Subramanian et al., 2023
)
propose to approximate complex team behaviors using
identical team policies
in the
infinite-population
limit.

Definition 2

(Identical Team Policy)
.

The Blue team policy
\phi^{N_{1}}_{t}=(\phi_{1,t},\ldots,\phi_{N_{1},t})
is
identical
, if
\phi_{i_{1},t}=\phi_{i_{2},t}
for all times
t
and all
i_{1},i_{2}\in[N_{1}]
.
The set of identical Blue team policies is denoted as
\Phi
.
Similarly,
\Psi
represents the set of identical Red team policies.

With an identical team policy, an infinite-population team’s joint states can be represented using the team population distribution, which
coincides with the state distribution of a typical agent and is commonly known as the
mean-field
(MF)
(
Huang et al., 2006
)
.
We use
\mu_{t}
and
\nu_{t}
to denote the Blue and Red MFs, respectively.

Guan et al. (2024b)
showed that MFs induced by identical team policies closely approximate finite-population EDs under non-identical policies.
This justifies the restriction of the optimization domain in (
2
) to identical policies, yielding the infinite-population optimization

\underline{J}_{\infty}^{*}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})=\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\;\min_{\psi\in\Psi}~J_{\infty}^{\phi,\psi}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),

(3)

where
\mu_{t}
and
\nu_{t}
follow
deterministic
dynamics, corresponding to the evolution of the state distributions of the Blue and Red representative agents.

While the lower (max–min) and upper (min–max) values may not coincide in general, i.e., a Nash value may not exist in the infinite-population game, we can show that a Nash equilibrium exists under a convex–concave reward structure and independent dynamics (where transitions depend only on an agent’s state and action).
See Appendix
C
for details.

3
Mean-Field Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization

Note that the optimization (
3
) is defined in the infinite-population limit and depends on the MF trajectories
(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})
.
To obtain such trajectories, existing learning approaches
(
Shao et al., 2024a
;
Perolat et al., 2021
)
typically rely on mean-field oracles that propagate the state distributions of representative agents.
However, these oracles are only available when the scenarios are simple enough to permit exact distributional propagation.
In realistic environments with complex dynamics and high-dimensional state spaces, one needs to leverage finite-population simulators to generate ED trajectories using samples of joint states and actions as approximations of the underlying MF trajectories.
Motivated by this need for generality, we design the MF-MAPPO algorithm to operate directly on finite-population simulators,
which introduces two key challenges:
(i) The learned policies are optimal only for finite-population games, whereas existing theoretical guarantees rely on optimality in the infinite-population limit; thus, new performance guarantees are required.
(ii) Policy gradients estimated from finite-population simulators are stochastic and often deviate from their mean-field counterparts, requiring additional convergence analysis.

Guan et al. (2024b)
show that the optimal policy
\phi^{*}
obtained from the
infinite-population
optimization in (
3
) is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\underline{N}})
-optimal in the finite-population game, even against non-identical exploiting Red team policies,
where
\underline{N}=\min\{N_{1},N_{2}\}
.
The following theorem presents a similar guarantee when the identical policy is obtained
as an
exact
solution to the finite-population optimization problem
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\tilde{\phi}^{*}
be a solution to the finite-population optimization restricted to identical policies, i.e.,

\max_{\phi\in\Phi}\min_{\psi\in\Psi}J^{N,\phi,\psi}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}_{0}^{N_{2}})
.
Then, the exploitability of
\tilde{\phi}^{*}
satisfies

\min_{\psi^{N_{2}}\in\Psi^{N_{2}}}J^{N,\tilde{\phi}^{*},\psi^{N_{2}}}(\mathbf{x}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}^{N_{2}})\geq\underline{J}^{N*}(\mathbf{x}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}^{N_{2}})-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\underline{N}}),\quad\forall\mathbf{x}^{N_{1}}\in\mathcal{X}^{N_{1}},\mathbf{y}^{N_{2}}\in\mathcal{Y}^{N_{2}}.

Theorem
3.1
provides a principled justification for learning
identical
team policies in finite-population games, even when the opponent may exploit using non-identical strategies (i.e.,
\psi^{N_{2}}\in\Psi^{N_{2}}
).
Moreover, the exploitability vanishes as
N_{1},N_{2}\to\infty
, recovering the classical infinite-population mean-field result
(
Huang et al., 2006
)
.

With identical policies justified, we introduce our main contribution: the Mean-Field Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MF-MAPPO) algorithm.
MF-MAPPO builds on the proximal policy optimization (PPO) framework with two key adaptations:

minimally-informed critics

and

shared team actors
.
These components exploit the underlying game structures to enable efficient learning while maintaining scalability.
We describe these design details below.

Minimally-Informed Critic.

The shared team reward and identical policy approximation imply that the value function depends only on the team distributions (See Proposition
5
in Appendix
D
).
Accordingly, we employ a
minimally-informed critic
per team, which takes
(\mu,\nu)
as input, independent of joint agent states and actions.
The Blue team critic evaluates the value function
V_{\text{Blue}}(\mu,\nu)
.
We use
\zeta_{\text{Blue}}
to parameterize the critic network and train it via minimizing the MSE loss as follows

L_{\text{critic}}(\zeta_{\text{Blue}})=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\Bigl(V_{\zeta_{\text{Blue}}}(\mu_{k},\nu_{k})-\hat{R}_{\text{Blue},k}\Bigr)^{2},

(4)

where
B
is the mini-batch size and
\hat{R}_{\text{Blue},k}
is the discounted reward-to-go for sample
k
.
The Red team critic is parameterized with
\zeta_{\text{Red}}
and updated similarly.
The following proposition justifies the usage of minimally-informed critics.

This result reduces the learning problem to a single critic per team.
Unlike standard centralized training/decentralized execution (CTDE),
which requires joint observations of all agents
(\mathbf{x}^{N_{1}}_{t},\mathbf{y}^{N_{2}}_{t})
, our critic depends only on the empirical distributions.
This design is critical for scalability, as the input dimension is independent of the number of agents.

Shared Team Actor.

The exploitability bound of identical team strategies in Theorem
3.1
allows us to employ a shared actor per team.
The actor optimizes a PPO-based objective with a decaying entropy bonus
(
Schulman et al., 2017
;
Huang et al., 2022
)
, which promotes exploration and stabilizes learning in mean-field settings
(
Cui and Koeppl, 2022
;
Guan et al., 2022
)
.
Identical team policies further allow a single replay buffer per team, reducing memory costs and simplifying experience collection.
The PPO-based objective function of the Blue team actor is given by:

L_{\text{actor}}(\theta_{\text{Blue}})=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\Big[\text{min}\Bigl(g_{k}(\theta_{\text{Blue}})A_{k},\operatorname{clip}_{[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]}(g_{k}(\theta_{\text{Blue}}))A_{k}\Bigr)+\omega S(\phi_{\theta_{\text{Blue}}}(x_{k},\mu_{k},\nu_{k}))\Big],

(5)

where,
\textstyle g(\theta)=\frac{\phi_{\theta}(u|x,\mu,\nu)}{\phi_{\theta^{\text{old}}}(u|x,\mu,\nu)}
,
A_{k}
is the generalized advantage function estimate
(
Schulman et al., 2018
)
and the tunable parameter
\omega
weighs the entropy term
S(\cdot)
and decays as training progresses.

3.1
Theoretical Guarantees for Finite-Population Training

As discussed earlier, the infinite-population limit remains of significant theoretical interest.
It is therefore desirable to ensure that policy gradients computed using finite-population simulators converge to their infinite-population counterparts as the population size grows, which motivates the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2

(
Informal
)
.

The approximate policy gradient of the infinite-population Blue (Red) team policy computed using identical local policies
(\phi_{\theta_{\mathrm{Blue}}},\psi_{\theta_{\mathrm{Red}}})
via MF-MAPPO from the
(N_{1},N_{2})
-agent simulator converges to the infinite-population policy gradient, i.e.,
\|\nabla_{\theta_{\mathrm{Blue}}}J_{\infty}(\phi_{\theta_{\mathrm{Blue}}},\psi)-\nabla_{\theta_{\mathrm{Blue}}}\hat{J}^{N_{1},N_{2}}(\phi_{\theta_{\mathrm{Blue}}},\psi)\|_{2}\to 0
as
(N_{1},N_{2})\to\infty
.

Theorem
3.2
relates policies learned using finite-population simulators to their infinite-population counterparts.
This connection allows us to use the infinite-population solution as a bridge to analyze the performance of the learned policy under different population sizes, leading to Theorem
3.3
.
In particular, Theorem
3.3
allows MF-MAPPO to be trained on smaller populations and deployed to larger teams without additional tuning, while maintaining performance guarantees.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{N}
denote the finite-population game in which agents use identical team policies
(\phi^{*}_{t},\psi^{*}_{t})
with an
(N_{1},N_{2})
-agent simulator.
Consider another game
\mathcal{G}_{\tilde{N}}
with the same setup but population sizes
(\tilde{N}_{1},\tilde{N}_{2})
satisfying
{\tilde{N}_{1}}/{\tilde{N}_{2}}={N_{1}}/{N_{2}}
and
\tilde{N}_{1}\geq N_{1}
.
If
(\phi^{*}_{t},\psi^{*}_{t})
is
\epsilon
-optimal in
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, then it remains
\epsilon
-optimal in
\mathcal{G}_{\tilde{N}}
.

4
MF-MAPPO for Partially Observable MFTGs

Unlike standard mean-field formulations based on single-agent deviations, ZS-MFTGs consider team-level deviations (
2
), requiring strategies that depend on feedback from both teams’ distributions.
Since access to the opponent distribution is often unrealistic, we extend MF-MAPPO to partially observable settings where opponent distributions must be estimated.

Figure 3:
PO-MFTG

Partially Observable Battlefield.

To motivate partial observability, consider a battlefield scenario with a state-dependent visibility graph
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{viz}}_{t}
and communication graph
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{comm}}_{t}
, which define the states an agent can observe and the agents it can communicate with.
In the example to the right, the cyan ego agent observes a
3\!\times\!3
neighborhood under
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{viz}}_{t}
.
Based on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{comm}}_{t}
, it can communicate with agents in four neighboring cells, leading to information exchange with the teammate to the left.

Given the inevitable noise in the estimates, it is first necessary to ensure that the learned policies are robust to errors in the estimated mean-field distributions
(\hat{\mu}_{t},\hat{\nu}_{t})
.
To this end, we introduce gradient regularization
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
in the actor objective (
5
) to enforce Lipschitz continuity with respect to the mean-field inputs.
This promotes robustness, so that small distribution estimation errors induce only minor changes in the action distributions.
Specifically,

L^{\text{GR}}_{\text{actor}}(\theta_{\text{Blue}})=L_{{\text{actor}}}(\theta_{\text{Blue}})+\frac{\lambda^{\text{GR}}}{|B|}\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\|\nabla_{\eta}\log\phi(u_{k}|x_{k},\eta)\big|_{\eta=(\mu_{k},\nu_{k})}\|^{2}_{2},

where

\eta=(\mu,\nu)
and

is a tunable hyperparameter to control the regularization.
The following proposition relates the gradient regularization to the Lipschitz constant
L_{\phi}
of the learned Blue team policy.

Proposition 1
.

If the log-probability of the Blue team policy is uniformly bounded such that
for all
x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\mathcal{U},\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
,
\textstyle\left\|\nabla_{\eta}\log\phi(u\mid x,\mu,\nu)\right\|_{2}\leq{L_{\phi}}/{2|\mathcal{U}|}

\eta\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})

\phi(u\mid x,\mu,\nu)
is
L_{\phi}
-Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,
\sum_{u}|\phi_{t}\left(u|x,\mu,\nu\right)-\phi_{t}\left(u|x,\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\right)|\leq L_{\phi}\left(\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\big)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\nu,\nu^{\prime}\big)\right)

Proposition 2
.

When a policy pair

Classical Consensus.

In consensus-based mean-field estimation
(
Benjamin and Abate, 2025a
)
, agents observe opponent counts based on their visibility graph
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{viz}}_{t}
and exchange the counts through the communication graph
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{com}}_{t}
over
R_{\mathrm{com}}
rounds.
Each agent then constructs a mean-field estimate from the aggregated counts, assigning the remaining probability mass uniformly to unobserved states.
The resulting estimator is
\big(1-\mathcal{O}(R_{\mathrm{com}}/|\mathcal{X}|)\big)
-accurate.

Dynamic-Projected Consensus.

Instead of communicating agent counts, we propose the dynamic-projected consensus (D-PC) algorithm that exchanges mean-field estimates directly.
The agent at state
x
first constructs an initial estimate
\hat{\nu}^{(0)}_{x,t}\in\mathcal{R}(x)
, where
\mathcal{R}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
denotes the set of distributions consistent with locally observed opponent counts.
During communication round
\tau
, agents exchange their previous estimates and compute the weighted consensus

\xi^{(\tau)}_{x,t}=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Neighbor}}w(x,x^{\prime})\hat{\nu}^{(\tau-1)}_{x^{\prime},t}
,
followed by the projection

\hat{\nu}^{(\tau)}_{x,t}=\Omega_{\mathcal{R}(x)}(\xi^{(\tau)}_{x,t})
, which preserves consistency with locally observed data.
Compared to classical consensus, D-PC is
\mathcal{O}\!\left(e^{-cR_{\mathrm{com}}}\right)
-accurate (Theorem
B.1
), i.e., exponentially decreasing error with
R_{\mathrm{com}}
, and is empirically more robust to noisy communication.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we studied zero-sum mean-field team games and introduced MF-MAPPO, a scalable MARL framework for mixed cooperative–competitive large-population systems.
MF-MAPPO combines a minimally informed critic with shared team policies, enabling efficient learning in finite-population simulators while scaling to thousands of agents.
We further extended the framework to partially observable settings through gradient regularization and decentralized mean-field estimation algorithms.
Experiments on large-scale benchmarks demonstrated that MF-MAPPO outperforms existing MARL and mean-field baselines while generating rich emergent team behaviors.
Future work includes improving scalability in high-dimensional state spaces and extending the framework to more general communication and interaction structures.

Appendix A
Related Work

Mean-Field Teams.
The single-team problem was explored in
Arabneydi and Mahajan [2014]
, where agents share a common team reward (MFC).
In contrast,
Mahajan and Nayyar [2015]
established optimality for finite-population games
only
in the LQG setting, while
Sanjari et al. [2022]
analyzed a two-team setting with continuous states and actions—unlike our finite state-space formulation that directly admits the familiar MDP-type structure.
Similarly,
multi-population
MFGs (MP-MFGs) have been studied in the past but often restrict agent dynamics and policies to be independent of both other agents and other population distributions, see
Perolat et al. [2021]
and references therein.
More recently,
Guan et al. [2024b]
introduced
zero-sum mean-field team games
(ZS-MFTGs), modeling large-population teams that compete while cooperating internally.
A common-information decomposition
[
Nayyar et al., 2013
]
enables team-size-independent learning with identical optimal team policies using MF feedback, unlike aforementioned MP-MFGs (open-loop MF policies).
However, computing such policies numerically, especially in large state-action spaces using dynamic programming, is costly.
By contrast, MF-MAPPO (Algorithm
A
) leverages shared actor–critic networks per team and uses only commonly accessible information (exact/estimated), ensuring tractability and scalability.

Mean-Field Theory and Learning.

Recent advances in mean-field learning span Q-function–based methods, such as MF-Q and MF-AC
[
Yang et al., 2020
]
and DDPG-MFTG
[
Shao et al., 2024b
]
, to value-function–based methods like Dec-POMFPPO
[
Cui et al., 2024
]
(MFC only).
While DDPG-MFTG incorporates team games, it is restricted to simple grid worlds, unlike our focus on tightly coupled collaborative–competitive domains (ZS-MFTGs).
We adopt it as a baseline and show that MF-MAPPO consistently outperforms it in stability and performance.
Other related works include PMD-TD for MFGs
[
Yardim and He, 2024
]
and GAN-based ECA-Net for continuous-space attack–defense games
[
Wang et al., 2022
]
, both differing in scope and structure.
Moreover,
Yang et al. [2020]
,
Subramanian et al. [2020]
,
Subramanian et al. [2022a]
,
Subramanian et al. [2022b]
define mean fields over (neighboring) agents’ actions rather than their state distribution, yielding a rich line of research that is orthogonal to our formulation.
MF-MAPPO extends PPO
[
Schulman et al., 2017
]
to competitive MFTGs, using team distributions as critic inputs for scalability, a shared actor–critic per team with a single buffer for efficiency, and simultaneous team training to avoid the inefficiencies of iterative best-response methods
[
Lanctot et al., 2017
,
Smith et al., 2021
]
.
Unlike prior MF methods that rely on infinite-population oracles
[
Shao et al., 2024a
,
Perolat et al., 2021
,
Carmona et al., 2021
]
, MF-MAPPO is trained directly in finite-population simulators, making it suitable for realistic deployment.
Finally, to standardize evaluation in large-scale MFTGs, we create novel MFTG benchmark environments (Constrained Rock–Paper–Scissors, Battlefield) going beyond existing ones that either focus only on MFGs
[
Guo et al., 2023
]
or omit MF coupling altogether
[
Terry et al., 2021
,
Zheng et al., 2018
]
.

Mean-Field Estimation and Opponent Modeling.

To enable reactive behavior to opponents’ actions, most existing MF approaches assume centralized or exact knowledge of the opponent’s MF, which is rarely practical.
While
Cui et al. [2024]
considers partial observability, it is limited to MFC.
Estimation methods such as kernel density estimation
[
Inoue et al., 2021
]
or normalizing flows
[
Perrin et al., 2021
]
struggle with discrete spaces and limited agent-visibility.
Communication-based methods
[
Benjamin and Abate, 2025b
,
Benjamin and Abate, 2025a
]
address these constraints but assume uniform estimates for unobserved states and rely on perfect multi-round communication while being restricted to fully cooperative or competitive regimes; noisy variants may even produce invalid distributions.
We instead propose Dynamic-Projected Consensus (D-PC), a constrained consensus algorithm that ensures validity, exponential convergence, and bounded deviations when paired with a
gradient-regularized
MF-MAPPO policy.
Gradient regularization naturally stabilizes MF-MAPPO in partially observable MFTGs.
Experiments show that D-PC matches baseline performance and even outperforms them under limited communication, especially critical in adversarial settings requiring rapid adaptation
[
Richards et al., 2012
]
, enhancing robustness and fault tolerance.
To our knowledge, this is the first MF estimator for opponent modeling in competitive team settings.

Remark 1
.

One could alternatively cast this problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (PO-MDP)
[
Bernstein et al., 2002
]
, where the environment provides arbitrary observations.
However, our focus is on policies that explicitly depend on the mean-field distributions of the two teams, for two key reasons: (a) Distinct opponent strategies induce different mean-field trajectories, and closed-loop feedback on these distributions enables an appropriate response to the strategies deployed by the opponent
[
Guan et al., 2024b
]
; and (b) Access to team-level distributions facilitates credit assignment
[
Pignatelli et al., 2024

in MARL by allowing agents to reason about which collective distributions are optimal.
Thus, having access to MF information—particularly that of the opponent—is desirable.
Yet, it is unrealistic to assume that the environment directly provides these distributions, which motivates the design of estimation algorithms proposed in this work.
Finally, unlike general PO-MDP formulations that often require maintaining long observation histories, our setting involves population sizes for which such history-based tracking is computationally infeasible in terms of memory.

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Big[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\pi_{t}(u|x)\Big]\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\Big]\mu_{t}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\Big[\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)>0}\;\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\Big[\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)=0}\;\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\underbrace{\Big[\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)\Big]}_{=0\,}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)>0}\;\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\frac{\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)}{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)}\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)=0}\;\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\underbrace{\Big[\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\Big]\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)}_{=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left[\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\bar{f}_{t}(x^{\prime}|x,u)\bar{\pi}_{t}(u|x)\right]\bar{\mu}_{t}(x),

where the “averaged" local policy
\bar{\pi}_{t}
is given by

\bar{\pi}_{t}(u|x)=\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)>0}\frac{\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)}{\lambda\mu_{t}(x)+(1-\lambda)\mu^{\prime}_{t}(x)}+\mathds{1}_{\bar{\mu}_{t}(x)=0}\Big(\lambda\pi_{t}(u|x)+(1-\lambda)\pi^{\prime}_{t}(u|x)\Big).

Consequently, we have

\bar{\mu}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mu,t}(\lambda\mu_{t}+(1-\lambda)\mu_{t}^{\prime},\nu_{t}).

Hence,

\mathcal{R}_{\mu,t}(\lambda\mu_{t}+(1-\lambda)\mu^{\prime}_{t},\nu_{t})\supseteq\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\mu,t}\big(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}\big)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{R}_{\mu,t}\big(\mu^{\prime}_{t},\nu_{t}\big).

∎

Proof.

We can now prove Theorem
C.1
. The game value at time
T
is directly the mean-field reward, which is assumed to be concave-convex.
Hence, one can provide an inductive proof leveraging Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
to show that both the lower and upper game values are concave-convex at each time step. Note that the lower game value in (

Finally, as the reachable sets are compact and convex, one can apply the minimax theorem

Remark 4
.

Theorem
3.3
applies to the
exact optimizers
of the finite-population optimization restricted to identical team policies.
The suboptimality bound comprises two components: the first captures the approximation error of the identical policy
\phi^{*}
trained in the
(N_{1},N_{2})
-population game (Theorem
3.1
); the second captures the stochasticity mismatch between training and deployment due to differing population sizes.
Thus, for a fixed deployment population, increasing the training population size can improve the guarantee by reducing the first component, while training beyond the target size offers diminishing returns.
For simplicity, we assume matching initial empirical distributions.
The result can be extended to mismatched initial distributions using a similar argument to that in the original mean-field paper
[
Huang et al., 2006
]
.

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\frac{L_{r}(L_{\phi}+L_{\psi})}{\kappa_{2}-1}\left(\kappa_{2}\frac{\kappa_{2}^{T}-1}{\kappa_{2}-1}-T\right)}_{K}\epsilon+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big)\frac{L_{r}}{\kappa_{2}-1}\left(\kappa_{2}\frac{\kappa_{2}^{T}-1}{\kappa_{2}-1}-T\right)

\displaystyle=K\epsilon+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\Big).

∎

J.1
State Space

We have three states in this representation of the game: rock, paper and scissors.
We denote this state space as
\mathcal{S}=\{\texttt{R,P,S}\}
.
The empirical distribution of the Blue team is denoted by
{\mu}\in\mathcal{P(S)}
and that of the Red team is denoted by
{\nu}\in\mathcal{P(S)}
.
Since we have three states for each team, both EDs lie in a three-dimensional simplex denoted by
\mathcal{P(S)}
.

J.2
Action Space

J.2.1
RPS

At each state, we define three actions denoted by
\mathcal{A}=\{\texttt{CW},\texttt{CCW},\texttt{Stay}\}
.
These actions represent the ability of the agents to move from one state to the other in the following fashion:

1.

CW
denotes a clockwise cyclic action from one state to the other, i.e., from
\texttt{R}\rightarrow\texttt{P}
,
\texttt{P}\rightarrow\texttt{S}
,
\texttt{S}\rightarrow\texttt{R}
.

2.

CCW
denotes a counterclockwise cyclic movement, i.e., from
\texttt{R}\rightarrow\texttt{S}
,
\texttt{S}\rightarrow\texttt{P}
,
\texttt{P}\rightarrow\texttt{R}
.

3.

Stay
denotes the idle action (remain in the same state as before).

J.2.2
cRPS

At each state we have two actions denoted by
\mathcal{A}=\{\texttt{CW},\texttt{Stay}\}
.
These actions represent the ability of the agents to move from one state to the other in the following fashion:

1.

CW
denotes a clockwise cyclic action from one state to the other, i.e., from
\texttt{R}\rightarrow\texttt{P}
,
\texttt{P}\rightarrow\texttt{S}
,
\texttt{S}\rightarrow\texttt{R}
.

2.

Stay
denotes the idle action (remain in the same state as before).

Thus, we cannot directly jump from
R
to
S
within a single step, but must go via
P
.
Mathematically,
S
does not lie in the reachable set of
R
.
The reachable set
\mathcal{R}(s)
for each state
s
at a given time step under this modified action space is as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\texttt{R})=\{\texttt{R},\texttt{P}\},\quad\mathcal{R}(\texttt{P})=\{\texttt{P},\texttt{S}\},\quad\mathcal{R}(\texttt{S})=\{\texttt{S},\texttt{R}\}.

.

J.3
Dynamics and Transition Probabilities

For both RPS and cRPS, we consider deterministic transitions
\mathcal{T}(s,a,s^{\prime})
, which implies that given a state-action pair
(s,a)
, the agent reaches a unique next state
s^{\prime}
with certainty (no distribution over the reachable states).
Thus, for state
R
and action

\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is given as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{Stay},\texttt{R})=1,\quad\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{Stay},\texttt{P})=0,\quad\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{Stay},\texttt{S})=0.

This implies that an agent in the state
R
upon taking the

R
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{CW},\texttt{R})=0,\quad\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{CW},\texttt{P})=1,\quad\mathcal{T}(\texttt{R},\texttt{CW},\texttt{S})=0.

represent the method to transition from
R
to
P
.

J.4
Reward Structure

In a two player RPS game, the reward matrix for Player 1 is defined as:

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&-1&1\\
1&0&-1\\
-1&1&0\end{array}\end{bmatrix}.

We extend this two-player framework to the multi-agent team game formulation.
Define the pairwise reward for an agent at state
x\in\mathcal{S}
within the Blue team and at state
y\in\mathcal{S}
from the Red team as

r(x,y)\triangleq A_{xy},

where
A_{xy}
represents the element from the reward matrix
A
corresponding to the states
x
(row player) and
y
(column player).
In lieu of the zero-sum structure, the reward for the agent at
y
with respect to
x
becomes
-r(x,y)
.
Thus, for each player
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\text{ and }i=1,2,\ldots,N_{1}
in the Blue team and
y_{j}\in\mathcal{S}\text{ and }j=1,2,\ldots,N_{2}
in the Red team, the reward for the Blue team can be defined as

R_{\mathrm{Blue}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\underbrace{\Big[\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}r(x_{i},y_{j})\Big]}_{\text{reward for agent $i$}}.

Rewriting the term inside the square brackets as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}r(x_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{2}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}r(x_{i},y)\mathbf{1}_{y_{j}=y}

\displaystyle=A_{x_{i}s_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\frac{1}{N_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{j}=s_{0}}+A_{x_{i}s_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\frac{1}{N_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{j}=s_{1}}

\displaystyle+A_{x_{i}s_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\frac{1}{N_{2}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{j}=s_{2}}

\displaystyle=A_{x_{i}s_{0}}{\nu}(s_{0})+A_{x_{i}s_{1}}{\nu}(s_{1})+A_{x_{i}s_{2}}{\nu}(s_{2})

\displaystyle=A(x_{i}){\nu},

(J.53)

where
A(x_{i})
is the row of the reward matrix corresponding to state
x_{i}
.
Using (
J.53
), the total Blue reward can be expressed as

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{Blue}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}A(x_{i}){\nu}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{1}{N_{1}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}A(x_{i})\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}=x}\Bigr){\nu}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(A(s_{0}){\mu}(s_{0})+A(s_{1}){\mu}(s_{1})+A(s_{2}){\mu}(s_{2})\Bigr){\nu}

\displaystyle={\mu}^{\mbox{\tiny\sf T}}A{\nu}.

\displaystyle 0\leq x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\leq 1,\quad x_{2}\leq 1-x_{1},\quad x_{3}\leq x_{1}.

J.5
Implementation Details and Hyperparameters

The state distributions are represented as arrays that are concatenated together to form the global observation.
This becomes the input to the critic network, which consists of a single hidden layer of 64 neurons and two
tanh
activation functions.
The output is a single value that is equal to the estimated value function.
On the other hand, the actor-network consists of a single MLP layer of 64 neurons that is concatenated with the local agent observation.
Additionally, the
logits
are converted to a probability distribution through a softmax layer.
The dimension scales with
|\mathcal{A}|
.
Both the actor and critic networks are initialized using orthogonal initialization
[
Huang et al., 2022
]
.

The single-stage RPS game is trained for 5,000 time steps with the actor and critic learning rates set to 0.0005 and 0.001, respectively, which remain constant throughout training.
The networks are updated using the ADAM optimizer
[
Kingma and Ba, 2017
]

every 50 time steps for 10 epochs and a PPO clip value of 0.1.
The entropy is decayed from 0.01 to 0.001 geometrically.
We use an episode length of 1 after which the rewards are bootstrapped.

Moreover, since we have a single “team” buffer and the input/output dimensions are small, we do not use a mini-batch based update.
For cRPS we use an episode length of 10 after which the rewards are bootstrapped.
cRPS
is trained using 200,000 time steps (=20,000 episodes) and is updated every 100 time steps.
The algorithm was trained on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU and the training times are given in Tables
M.1
and
2
.

Appendix K
Battlefield Setup

K.1
State and Action Space

We consider a large-scale two-team (Blue and Red) ZS-MFTG on an
n\times n
grid world.
The state of the
i^{th}
Blue agent is defined as the pair
x_{i}=(p_{i}^{x},s_{i}^{x})
where
p_{i}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}
denotes the position of the agent in the grid world and
s_{i}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}}=\{0,1\}
defines the status of the agent: 0 being inactive and 1 being active.
Similarly, we define the state of the Red agent as
y_{i}=(p_{i}^{y},s_{i}^{y})
.
The state spaces for the Blue and Red teams are denoted by
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}\times\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}}
, respectively.
The mean-fields of the Blue (
\mu
) and Red (
\nu
) teams are distributions over the joint position and status space, i.e.,
{\mu},{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}\times\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}})
.
The action spaces are given by
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}=\{\texttt{Up},\texttt{Down},\texttt{Left},\texttt{Right},\texttt{Stay}\}
for both teams, representing discrete movements in the grid world.
The learned identical team policy assigns actions based on an agent’s local position and status, as well as the observed mean-fields of both teams.
In the following subsections, we elaborate on the weakly coupled transition dynamics and reward structure introduced in the game, followed by a detailed discussion of the training procedure and network architecture for MF-MAPPO in this example.

K.2
Interaction Between Agents

The transitions between states for agents belonging to both teams are characterized by their dynamics.
These dynamics are probabilistic and depend on interactions among agents and are weakly coupled through their mean-field distributions.
The weak coupling dynamics is keeping in line with the assumption in
Guan et al. [2024b]
.

An agent at a given grid cell can be deactivated by the opponent team with a nonzero probability if the empirical mean-field of the opponent team at the grid cell supersedes that of the agent’s own team.
Similarly, a deactivated agent can be revived if the empirical mean-field of the agent’s team is greater than the opponent’s.
This is referred to as numerical advantage.
The total transition probability from state
(p,s)
to state
(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})
by taking an action
a
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big((p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\,|\,(p,s),a,\mu,\nu\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(p^{\prime}\,|\,(p^{\prime},s^{\prime}),a\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(s^{\prime}\,|\,(p,s),\mu,\nu\big),

where the first term on the right-hand side corresponds to the deterministic position transition when the agent is active.
The second term corresponding to the status transition is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(0\mid(p,1),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\big(\alpha_{x}(\nu(p)-\mu(p))\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(1\mid(p,1),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\big(0\mid(p,1),\mu,\nu\big),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(1\,|\,(p,0),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\big(\beta_{x}(\mu(p)-\nu(p))\big),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(0\,|\,(p,0),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\big(1\,|\,(p,0),\mu,\nu\big),

where
\nu(p)-\mu(p)
is the Red team’s numerical advantage over the Blue team at
p
.
Similarly, the Blue team’s numerical advantage over Red is given by
\mu(p)-\nu(p)
.

\alpha_{x}
and
\beta_{x}
are tuning parameters to control the Red team’s deactivation power and Blue team’s reactivation power respectively.
The Red team, being the defending team, is given a slight advantage in terms of higher deactivation power.
This enables the possibility of capturing Blue team agents.
However, to avoid degeneracy, the Red team agents are not allowed to enter the target.
For our experiments, we assume
\alpha_{x}=15
,
\alpha_{y}=5
, and
\beta_{x}=\beta_{y}=0.

K.3
Reward Structure

The team rewards only depend on the mean-fields of the two teams.
For the battlefield scenario, the Blue team agents receive a positive reward corresponding to the fraction of agents that reach the target alive.
This is a one-time reward that depends on the change in the fraction of the population of the agents at the target, i.e., if
{\mu}_{t}|_{\text{target}}={\mu}_{t+1}|_{\text{target}}
, then the team does not receive any positive reward.
Each agent in the team receives an identical “team reward.”
The reward function is mathematically formulated as

\displaystyle R_{\text{Blue},{t+1}}(\mu,\nu)=\kappa\,\Delta\mu_{t+1}|_{\text{target}},

where,

\displaystyle\Delta\mu_{t+1}|_{\text{target}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t+1}(p^{x}=\text{Target},s^{x}=1)-\mu_{t}(p^{x}=\text{Target},s^{x}=1).

We have chosen

\kappa=100
in our simulations (heavier emphasis on reaching the target).
The Red team’s reward is the negative of the Blue team since we have a zero-sum game.
Each team aims to maximize its own expected reward.

K.4
Implementation and Hyperparameters

The state distribution for a grid world of size
n\times n
is represented as a
three-dimensional array of size
(2,n,n)
for each team.
The first layer depicts the mean-field of the agents over an
n\times n
grid that are alive and active, while the second layer gives information about the team’s deactivated population.
Each team’s distribution is then concatenated together to form the global observation.
This becomes the common information that is the input to the critic network which in our case is of size
(4,n,n)
as we have two teams.
Both neural networks consist of two main parts: a convolutional block and a fully connected block.

For the critic, the first CNN layer is the input layer that takes the 4 channels and outputs 32 channels, with a kernel size of 3x3, stride of 1, and padding of 1.
Followed by
ReLU
activation, we have a hidden layer that takes 32 channels and outputs 64 channels, with the same kernel size, stride, and padding.
Lastly, after another
ReLU
activation, we have the output layer that takes 64 channels and outputs 64 channels, again with the same kernel size, stride, and padding.
After another
ReLU
layer, the output of the CNN is passed through an MLP.
Namely, a fully connected (dense) layer takes the flattened output of the convolutional block and reduces it to 128 units.
Between the input and the output layers, we have a single
tanh
activation function.

On the other hand, the input to the actor-network is split into two CNN blocks: one to process the common information and one to process the local information.
The local information channel is an array of size
(1,n,n)
that locates the position of the agent with value +1 if it is active and -1 if it has been deactivated.
This local information is passed through a single CNN layer that outputs 16 channels with a kernel size of 3x3, stride of 1, and padding of 1 while the common information is passed through two such layers with the output of 32 channels.
Both outputs are then followed by a
ReLU
activation function and the latent representation of the common information combined with the local agent observation is then passed through an MLP architecture.

A fully connected (dense) layer takes the flattened output of the convolutional block and reduces it to 512 units.
We have a single hidden layer that reduces the dimension further to 128 and then the output
logits
.
The layers are separated by the
tanh
activation functions.
Finally, the
logits
are converted to a probability distribution through a

softmax
layer.
Both the actor and critic networks are initialized using orthogonal initialization
[
Huang et al., 2022
]
.
The architectures of the shared-team actor and minimally-informed critic networks for this example are shown in Figures
K.2
and

Appendix L
Epidemiology Setup

L.1
State and Action Space

We consider a large-scale two-team (Blue and Red) ZS-MFTG on an
n\times n
grid world where the Blue team represents a population trying to prevent itself from a spreading virus, represented by the Red team.
The state of the
i^{th}
Blue agent is defined as the pair
x_{i}=(p_{i}^{x},s_{i}^{x})
where
p_{i}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}
denotes the position of the agent in the grid world and
s_{i}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}}=\{0,1\}
defines the status of the agent: 0 being unhealthy and 1 being healthy.
The state of the Red agent as
y_{i}=p_{i}^{y}
, denoting the position of the Red agent.
The state spaces for the Blue and Red teams are denoted by
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}\times\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}
, respectively.
The mean-fields of the Blue (
\mu
) and Red (
\nu
) teams are distributions over the joint position and status space, i.e.,
{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}}\times\mathcal{S}_{\text{status}})
while
{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}_{\text{position}})
.
The action spaces are given by
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}=\{\texttt{Up},\texttt{Down},\texttt{Left},\texttt{Right},\texttt{Stay}\}
for both teams, representing discrete movements in the grid world.
The learned identical team policy assigns actions based on an agent’s local position and status, as well as the observed mean-fields of both teams.

L.2
Interaction Between Agents and Reward Structure

We consider weakly coupled dynamics where the Blue team agent at a cell can be infected by a non-zero probability that is proportional to the Red team population as well as the infected Blue team population at that cell.
For the Blue agents, the total transition probability from state
(p,s)
to state
(p^{\prime},s^{\prime})
by taking an action
a
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big((p^{\prime},s^{\prime})\,|\,(p,s),a,\mu,\nu\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(p^{\prime}\,|\,(p^{\prime},s^{\prime}),a\big)\,\mathbb{P}\big(s^{\prime}\,|\,(p,s),\mu,\nu\big),

where the first term on the right-hand side corresponds to the deterministic position transition.
The second term corresponding to the status transition is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(0\mid(p,1),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\nu(p)+\mu(p,s=0))

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(1\,|\,(p,0),\mu,\nu\big)

\displaystyle=\beta_{x}(\mu(p)-\nu(p)),

where
\alpha
is a tuning parameter to control the rate of infection.
The Blue team agents can go to the hospital to heal and become healthy with probability
\beta
.
The Red team on the other hand moves around the grid trying to infect as much of the population.
However, to avoid degeneracy, the Red team agents are not allowed to enter the hospital.
The reward depends on the proportion of healthy Blue agents, i.e.,
\sum_{s=1}\mu(p,s)
, thereby incentivizing Blue agents to decongest and if infected, heal at the hospital and Red agents to surround the hospital, ready for contaminating the Blue team.
Thus, this example, unlike the battlefield, exhibits asymmetry in the dynamics between the Red and Blue teams.
For our experiments, we assume
\alpha=15
, and
\beta=5.

L.3
Implementation and Hyperparameters

The MF-MAPPO architecture resembles the Battlefield networks introduced in
K
.
However, the Red team mean-field has a size
(1,n,n)
, while Blue continues having
(2,n,n)
.
The policy was trained using a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU.
Due to asymmetry in the dynamics, we separately tune the hyperparameters for both teams.
For the Blue team the actor and critic learning rates are set to 0.0001 and 0.0005 with 3 epochs of training.
On the other hand the Red team network learns with 0.0005 for the actor and 0.001 for the critic, with updates over 10 epochs.
The entropy coefficient is initialized to 0.01 and both the entropy and the learning rates for both teams decay geometrically throughout the training period.
The total training period is about half a day.

In this section, we present additional analyses and simulation results.
In particular, we evaluate the exploitability of policies trained using MF-MAPPO and investigate the algorithm’s sample efficiency in the battlefield scenario.

M.1
Exploitability Evaluation

For cRPS, the Blue and Red policies exhibit very low exploitability (
0.002
and
0.004
, respectively), closely matching the equilibrium performance of
-0.331
.
Together with the learned mean-field trajectories matching the analytical equilibrium in Proposition
6
, this provides empirical evidence that MF-MAPPO converges toward the equilibrium.

For Battlefield, the Blue policy has relatively low exploitability (
4.735
), while the Red policy is substantially more exploitable (
26.114
), compared with an equilibrium performance of
76.26
.
Although our training losses indicate convergence of the MF-MAPPO updates, this does not necessarily imply convergence to an equilibrium.
One possible explanation is that, unlike cRPS, the Battlefield game may not admit a game value and therefore may not possess an equilibrium toward which MF-MAPPO can converge.
In addition, the asymmetric structure of the environment where Red is prohibited from entering the target region may make the Red policy more constrained and sensitive to best-response exploitation.
Approximation error, entropy regularization, and limited exploration may also contribute, and we therefore refrain from attributing the higher Red-team exploitability to any single cause, given the limited experiment we performed.
Nevertheless, the Red policy consistently outperforms the baselines in cross-play (Table
3
), providing complementary evidence that it is comparatively strong despite not being fully non-exploitable.

Establishing broader conditions (beyond independent-dynamics) for game-value existence in ZS-MFTGs and understanding practical learning convergence beyond the settings considered here are important directions for future work.

Guidelines:

•

The answer
[N/A]
means that the paper does not use existing assets.

•

The authors should cite the original paper that produced the code package or dataset.

•

The authors should state which version of the asset is used and, if possible, include a URL.

•

The name of the license (e.g., CC-BY 4.0) should be included for each asset.

•

For scraped data from a particular source (e.g., website), the copyright and terms of service of that source should be provided.

•

If assets are released, the license, copyright information, and terms of use in the package should be provided. For popular datasets,
paperswithcode.com/datasets
has curated licenses for some datasets. Their licensing guide can help determine the license of a dataset.

•

For existing datasets that are re-packaged, both the original license and the license of the derived asset (if it has changed) should be provided.

•

If this information is not available online, the authors are encouraged to reach out to the asset’s creators.
