Mycoplasma hominis infections in deep sternal wound infections post-cardiac surgery: insights from three case reports
Authors: Qimei Wei, Rong Cong, Tingting Liu, Kaili Liu, Gengsheng Zhao, Shanshan Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative deep sternal wound infections (DSWIs) following cardiac surgery are increasingly common due to various factors, including high tension at the sternotomy site and the presence of obesity. While Mycoplasma hominis primarily colonizes the urogenital tract, it can cause opportunistic infections, including surgical wound infections, particularly in patients with specific risk factors such as immunosuppression or surgical trauma.Case presentationIn this study, we reported three cases of post-cardiac surgery DSWIs attributable to M. hominis. The clinical symptoms manifested in these patients were fever accompanied by clear exudate from the wound, with elevated C-reactive protein levels evident in serological tests. It is noteworthy that all three patients achieved significant therapeutic outcomes following the application of active surgical debridement and the administration of quinolone antibiotics for a duration exceeding two weeks.ConclusionsThese cases underscore the importance of recognizing the clinical features and effective treatment strategies for DSWIs caused by M. hominis, thereby warranting increased clinical awareness and attention.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Postoperative deep sternal wound infections (DSWIs) following cardiac surgery are observed more frequently, attributed to the thin subcutaneous tissue and high tension at the sternotomy incision site [1]. Classical risk factors for deep surgical site infections (SSIs), specifically mediastinitis and DSWIs, include diabetes mellitus, obesity, prolonged ventilation, and the use of invasive devices [2]. Mycoplasma hominis is known to primarily colonize the urogenital tract and seldom initiates infections elsewhere [3]. Yet, under specific conditions such as immunosuppression, transplantation, malignant tumors, trauma, reproductive tract manipulation, or surgery, it can lead to opportunistic infections, including central nervous system infections and bloodstream infections [4, 5]. Recent studies have emphasized cases of bloodstream and mediastinal infections associated with M. hominis. In this report, we detail three cases of DSWIs resulting from cardiac surgery linked to M. hominis and explore their clinical characteristics. All three cases involved elective surgeries where sternotomy was performed.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 70-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Following a comprehensive evaluation, it was determined that the patient would undergo elective coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Intraoperatively, cefazolin was administered for infection prophylaxis. For coronary revascularization, the surgical team utilized the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) and the saphenous vein graft (SVG). The surgical procedure was uneventful; however, on the first postoperative day, the patient’s temperature rose to 38.2 °C. A bedside chest radiograph revealed decreased translucency in the left lower lung field and inadequate lung expansion. Meanwhile, laboratory tests revealed a procalcitonin (PCT) level of 1.39 ng/mL and a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 147 mg/L. These findings raise suspicion of a pulmonary infection. Subsequently, lower airway secretions were aspirated via the tracheostomy tube and sent for culture. The antibiotic regimen was adjusted to cefoperazone-sulbactam to provide more intensive anti-infective treatment. Following further evaluation, the tracheostomy tube was successfully removed, and the patient was transferred from the intensive care unit to the general ward on postoperative day 3. Although the final sputum culture results were negative, the patient developed a fever on postoperative day 6, with a maximum temperature reaching 39.1 °C. In response to this development, four blood culture samples were promptly sent for analysis, and the antibiotic regimen was modified to include meropenem and moxifloxacin. Following 106 h of culture, one of the blood culture anaerobic bottles yielded a positive result, yet no bacterial morphology could be observed in the Gram stain of the smear. The sample was then transferred to a blood agar plate, which was incubated at 35 °C in an atmosphere containing 7% CO2 for 48 h. Subsequent growth of pinpoint-like colonies led to identification by Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) as M. hominis (Fig. 1). Consequently, the antibiotics regimen was further adjusted to include moxifloxacin combined with azithromycin. Upon changing the wound dressing on the 14th day after the operation, it was discovered that the lower part of the wound exhibited poor healing, with approximately 50 mL of clear exudate being aspirated. Peripheral blood laboratory investigations revealed a white blood count (WBC) of 12.2 × 109/L, a PCT level of 2.62 ng/mL, and a CRP level of 207.38 mg/L. The wound secretions were sent for culture, and after 72 h, pinpoint-like colonies were observed on the blood agar plate, which were later confirmed as M. hominis by MALDI-TOF MS. A decision for surgical debridement was made on the 19th day after surgery. During the procedure, poor healing of the subcutaneous tissue was evident, although no obvious infection focus was identified in the mediastinum. The surgical site was then irrigated with active iodine and normal saline, followed by the placement of right chest and mediastinal drainage tubes. Subsequently, vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) was applied to promote healing at the surgical site. The excised wound tissue was sent for examination and culture, yielding identification of M. hominis after 72 h. Following a two-week treatment course of moxifloxacin (ivgtt, 0.4 g, qd) combined with azithromycin (po, 0.5 g, qd), the patient’s wound displayed significant healing, culminating in discharge from the hospital.

Case 2

A 56-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of descending thoracic aortic aneurysm. The treatment involved selective partial resection of the ascending aorta with artificial blood vessel replacement. After the operation, successful extubation allowed for the patient’s transfer from the intensive care unit to the general ward on the second day. To prevent infection, cefazolin was administered for 48 h after surgery. However, a subsequent CT scan revealed patchy solid shadows in the upper lobe of the left lung and the lower lobes of both lungs. Consequently, the patient was commenced on an intensive anti-infective regimen of piperacillin-tazobactam (ivgtt, 4.5 g, q8h). On the 6th day after the transfer to the ward, a small amount of exudate was observed from the lower sternum during the dressing change of the wound. The nature of the exudate was relatively clear and no suppuration or bleeding was observed; thus, a local dressing change was performed. On the 8th day after transfer, the patient’s incision showed signs of poor healing, and he intermittently complained of pain near the incision, raising concerns regarding a potential mediastinal infection. Subsequent laboratory tests revealed that WBC was 28.3 × 109/L, CRP was 190.56 mg/L, and PCT was 0.60 ng/mL. Following discussions with the patient and his family, exploratory thoracotomy and delayed chest closure were promptly performed. During this procedure, it was observed that the subcutaneous tissue had healed well, yet a moderate amount of purulent secretions were identified in the mediastinum. Post-clearance of these secretions, the mediastinum was thoroughly irrigated before placement of a drainage tube in the suprasternal fossa. To facilitate drainage and promote healing, VSD was applied to the surgical site. Subsequent culturing of mediastinal drainage fluid yielded the growth of M. hominis (Fig. 2), as identified by MALDI-TOF MS. Therefore, the antibiotic regimen was modified to azithromycin (po, 0.5 g, qd) combined with levofloxacin (ivgtt, 0.4 g, qd) for a duration of two weeks, after which the M. hominis culture turned negative. Following this, the patient developed a pulmonary infection caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae; however, he ultimately recovered and was discharged after receiving appropriate treatment.

Case 3

A 17-year-old female patient was diagnosed with congenital heart malformation and tetralogy of Fallot (TOF). Following a comprehensive evaluation, the decision was made to proceed with elective corrective surgery for TOF. Postoperatively, the patient exhibited low cardiac output, and auscultation revealed pronounced sputum sounds in the lungs. Additionally, the chest radiograph indicated severe pulmonary exudation. The patient’s body temperature peaked at 39.2 °C, and the PCT level was measured at 5.27 ng/ml, raising concern for postoperative infection. In response, the attending physician promptly ordered blood and sputum cultures. Given the patient’s preoperative hospital stay exceeding one week and her self-reported recent history of upper respiratory tract infection, the physician adjusted the antibiotic regimen from prophylactic cefazolin to meropenem to enhance anti-infective coverage. Sputum and blood cultures returned negative, and the patient was transferred from intensive care on postoperative day 5. However, due to the persistence of intermittent fever, the physician deemed the infection serious and subsequently modified the antibiotic regimen to include piperacillin-tazobactam in combination with vancomycin to provide broader coverage against potential resistant pathogens. By the 10th day after the transition to the ward, the drainage tube was removed owing to the patient’s minimal pericardial and mediastinal drainage volume. However, on the 12th day in the ward, the patient persistently experienced intermittent fever. Laboratory tests revealed that WBC was 18.1 × 109/L, CRP was 142.28 mg/L, and PCT was 0.21 ng/mL. In response, levofloxacin (ivgtt, 0.4 g, qd) was incorporated into the treatment regimen for intensive anti-infective treatment. Persisting, on the 15th day in the ward, the patient still exhibited fever, and clear exudate was noted in the upper part of the wound. A change of dressing was performed, and wound secretions were collected for culture, however, these cultures also returned negative. Given the poor wound healing, a decision was made for a thoracotomy to pursue further diagnostic clarity. During the procedure, a disruption was discovered between the patient’s sternal body and sternal manubrium, with the latter showing signs of ischemic necrosis yet devoid of obvious purulent secretions. All necrotic bone and surrounding tissue were excised and submitted for examination and culture. Ultimately, the tissue culture grew pinpoint colonies on the blood agar plate (Fig. 2), which were identified as M. hominis by MALDI-TOF MS, prompting the continuation of treatment with levofloxacin combined with vancomycin (ivgtt, 0.5 g, q6h). Following two weeks of aggressive treatment, the wound healed, culminating in the patient’s discharge.

Discussion and conclusion

We report three rare cases of post-cardiac surgery DSWIs attributed to M. hominis and provide a summary of the clinical characteristics of these infections (Table 1). The path of infection by M. hominis is notably surreptitious, rendering its detection and treatment challenging. In our case series, all three patients experienced infections within a six-month period, with intervals exceeding two months between each case. Although these cases did not occur within the same ward, they all shared the common factor of having been admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). Despite our hospital’s surveillance program not identifying any cases of M. hominis during this timeframe, and no additional cases documented in the subsequent six months, it is imperative that we further evaluate the potential sources or routes of infection. Some researchers have proposed that invasive medical procedures, notably catheterization, can facilitate the entry of M. hominis into the bloodstream and its subsequent colonization at the site of surgery [6]. Each of the patients in our series underwent invasive procedures, including urinary catheterization, which implicates this as a potential pathway for infection. While the culture of the urine sample from Case 1 confirmed the absence of M. hominis, this finding raises important considerations regarding the origin of the infection. It is plausible that M. hominis could have been introduced during the surgical procedure itself, or through other invasive interventions, rather than being detected in the urine. The patient’s prior antibiotic treatment may have suppressed the organism’s presence in the urine, complicating our ability to trace its origin definitively. Furthermore, M. hominis is part of the normal flora of the urogenital tract, and its pathogenicity often arises when the host’s defenses are compromised, such as after surgical interventions. This indicates that while catheterization may contribute to the introduction of the organism, the infection’s source is likely multifactorial, involving endogenous flora or procedural contamination. Further studies are needed to better understand these mechanisms.

At present, a significant delay exists in the clinical diagnosis of M. hominis infection. To further illustrate the diagnostic challenges and diverse clinical presentations associated with this condition, we have summarized previously reported cases of M. hominis mediastinitis following cardiac surgery in Table 2 [6–13]. This overview includes patient demographics, presenting symptoms, laboratory findings, interventions, and outcomes, providing valuable context for understanding our own case series. As highlighted by both our experience and the literature, this diagnostic lag can be attributed primarily to two factors. Firstly, the symptoms of infection are atypical, with high fever being a predominant manifestation. Additionally, patients may exhibit purulent secretions or pleural effusion, frequently involving the sternum and potentially leading to sternal pain [11, 14]. A review of previous studies, which included 17 cases of post-cardiac mediastinitis caused by M. hominis, revealed that the median onset of clinical infection symptoms occurred 14 days post-surgery [6]. The three patients we describe were diagnosed with DSWI after their respective surgeries: on the 6th day following coronary artery bypass grafting, the 10th day after ascending aorta replacement, and the 20th day post-TOF surgery. Notably, the clinical symptoms of infection in these cases manifested as fever accompanied by exudate from the wound. However, it is essential to highlight that the exudate was clear, distinguishing it from the purulent turbid effusion typically associated with bacterial infections. This distinct characteristic should alert clinicians to the possibility of M. hominis infection, prompting further microbiological investigation.

Secondly, another contributing factor to the diagnostic delay is the challenging identification of this microorganism. Although M. hominis can grow on plain blood agar petri dishes, it is characterized by slow growth and resistance to conventional Gram staining techniques, which complicates its identification. In the cases presented, the presence of M. hominis was confirmed 72 h after the clinical specimens were submitted, resulting in a consequential delay in diagnosis. Our clinical routine still predominantly relies on traditional culture methods; however, we acknowledge the limitations of these approaches, particularly in the identification of M. hominis. In our cases, the isolates were identified using MALDI-TOF MS, as traditional biochemical reaction identification methods were not applicable. Furthermore, we recognize the potential advantages of molecular diagnostic techniques, such as targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR). These methods could significantly expedite the identification process and improve diagnostic accuracy. Future studies that incorporate these molecular approaches may deepen our understanding of M. hominis and other challenging microorganisms, leading to more timely and effective patient management.

Emerging from research reports [15], patients with M. hominis infection may show a characteristic pattern of inflammatory markers. Specifically, PCT levels often do not increase significantly or exhibit only a marginal rise, whereas CRP levels frequently demonstrate a marked elevation. In our study, the measured PCT levels were 2.62 ng/mL, 0.60 ng/mL, and 0.21 ng/mL, while the corresponding CRP levels were 207.38 mg/L, 190.56 mg/L, and 142.28 mg/L, indicating a significant increase in CRP. Notably, the elevated PCT level in the first case might be associated with a concurrent bloodstream infection, while the pronounced increase in CRP across all three cases is consistent with findings from previous research. While this pattern of inflammatory markers may assist in the clinical evaluation of patients, it’s important to acknowledge that similar CRP elevations can occur in various infections, including DSWIs. Thus, while these observations could contribute to the diagnostic process, they should not be interpreted as definitive indicators of M. hominis infection alone.

Notably, M. hominis exhibits resistance to all beta-lactam antibiotics, owing to its unique lack of a cell wall. However, treatment options such as macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines demonstrate good antibacterial activity against it [16]. Macrolides, such as azithromycin, function by inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis through binding to the 50 S ribosomal subunit, making them effective against a range of atypical pathogens, including M. hominis. Furthermore, quinolones have been shown to have good tissue penetration, which is particularly important in treating deep-seated infections. In addition to pharmacotherapy, surgical debridement is often a necessary intervention [17]. Early surgical intervention, including debridement and drainage of pus, is often key to effectively controlling surgical site infections caused by pathogens such as M. hominis. Failure to consider this pathogen in empirical medication strategies can substantially increase the risk of prolonged disease course and treatment failure. Therefore, DSWIs caused by M. hominis require significant clinical attention. In the cases we documented, all three patients received quinolones as their treatment regimen. Two of them also received azithromycin, while one was treated with levofloxacin alone. Surgical debridement was performed on all patients, underscoring the importance of combining surgical and medical management in the treatment of M. hominis infections. Following a treatment course of two weeks or more, each patient demonstrated a favorable response, achieving a good therapeutic outcome.

In conclusion, DSWIs following cardiac surgery pose a significant threat to patient outcomes, often resulting in prolonged hospitalizations, increased healthcare costs, and higher mortality rates. To improve the management of these infections, clinicians and laboratory personnel must maintain a high index of suspicion, particularly when faced with poorly healing postoperative wounds that exhibit clear exudate, an elevated CRP level, and a lack of response to empirical antibiotic therapy. A comprehensive diagnostic approach is essential in these cases, which should include consideration of fastidious and atypical organisms such as M. hominis. As highlighted in our cases, M. hominis a pertinent example of a pathogen that is frequently overlooked due to its specific culture requirements. Therefore, we recommend that clinicians broaden their differential diagnoses and advocate for sensitive molecular techniques (e.g., 16 S rRNA PCR) for accurate pathogen identification and the implementation of effective, targeted therapy.​.
