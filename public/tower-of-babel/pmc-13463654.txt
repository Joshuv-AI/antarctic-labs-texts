Analysis of the effect of primaquine dose on efficacy, safety, and tolerability in patients with Plasmodium vivax malaria in Ethiopia: a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis
Authors: Tamiru Shibiru Degaga, Kamala Thriemer, Megha Rajasekhar, Tesfay Abreha, Ashenafi Assefa, Nicholas M Douglas, Endalamaw Gadisa, Asrat Hailu, Jimee Hwang, Gavin C K W Koh, Marcus V G Lacerda, Alejandro Llanos-Cuentas, Daniel Abebe Mekonnen, Julie A Simpson, Fitsum G Tadesse, Walter R J Taylor, Lorenz von Seidlein, Adugna Woyessa, Asnakew K Yeshiwondim, Daniel Yilma, Ric N Price, Gudissa Assefa Bayissa, Robert J Commons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 8-aminoquinoline antimalarials are used to prevent P. vivax relapses. Recent evidence suggests high total dose primaquine (7 mg/kg) provides optimal efficacy, however, many countries, including Ethiopia, use low total dose primaquine (3.5 mg/kg). To understand the risks and benefits of different primaquine dosing regimens for P. vivax malaria in Ethiopia, we undertook a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis.MethodsWe searched for antimalarial efficacy studies in patients with uncomplicated P. vivax mono-infections conducted in Ethiopia, published between January 1, 2000, and March 8, 2024. Studies were included if they had at least 28 days of follow-up and included a treatment arm with daily primaquine, commenced within seven days of starting antimalarial treatment. Study investigators were approached to share individual patient data, which were standardised and pooled. We performed a one-stage meta-analysis to estimate the cumulative incidence of P. vivax recurrence by day 180 with different primaquine total dose regimens. The number and proportion of gastrointestinal symptoms on days 5–7 and clinically significant haemolysis within 14 days were described and stratified by daily primaquine dose. PROSPERO CRD42023491851.ResultsOf 297 identified studies, 5 were eligible for inclusion. Data from 1,378 patients from all 5 studies were obtained. The cumulative incidence of P. vivax recurrence by day 180 was 61.0% (95%CI: 55.3–66.8) in patients who were not treated with primaquine, 16.6% (12.0–22.7) following low total dose and 8.9% (6.4–12.4) following high total dose primaquine. High total dose primaquine (7 mg/kg) was associated with a reduced rate of recurrence compared with low total dose (3.5 mg/kg) (Adjusted Hazard Ratio 0.40; 95% CI 0.24–0.68, p = 0.001). Gastrointestinal disturbance on days 5–7 was recorded in 20/104 (19.1%) patients without primaquine, 36/225 (16.0%) patients treated with 0.5 mg/kg primaquine daily and 54/224 (24.1%) patients treated with 1 mg/kg primaquine daily. In patients with normal G6PD activity (≥ 30%), one patient had a ≥ 25% fall in haemoglobin to < 7 g/dL following 0.5 mg/kg primaquine daily and two patients had a > 5 g/dL fall, one following 0.5 mg/kg daily and one following 1 mg/kg daily.ConclusionsLow and high total dose primaquine reduced the risk of recurrence of P. vivax malaria substantially in Ethiopia compared with no primaquine, with high total dose regimens conferring greater protection. All doses demonstrated acceptable tolerability and clinically significant haemolytic events were uncommon in patients with ≥ 30% G6PD activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05968-z.Keywords: P. vivax, Recurrence, Ethiopia, Tolerability, Safety, Primaquine, Efficacy, Malaria

Introduction

Historically, P. vivax caused approximately 30% of malaria in Ethiopia but this proportion is increasing [1–4]. Unlike P. falciparum, P. vivax has a dormant liver stage, the hypnozoite, which complicates effective treatment by causing relapsing episodes of malaria weeks or months after the resolution of the initial infection. The radical cure of P. vivax requires an acute blood stage antimalarial (schizontocidal) combined with a drug active against the dormant liver stages (hypnozoiticidal). Deployment of safe and effective radical cure is critical for the control and elimination of P. vivax and has the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality [3, 4].

In Ethiopia, primaquine is the only available antimalarial that can kill P. vivax hypnozoites [2]. Tafenoquine, another 8-aminoquinoline compound, can be administered as a single-dose treatment and although registered in the national drug list, it is not yet widely available for clinical use. Both primaquine and tafenoquine can cause severe drug-induced haemolysis in individuals with reduced glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity, and high daily primaquine doses can cause gastrointestinal disturbance and methaemoglobinaemia [5, 6]. Although nationally representative data are lacking, the prevalence of G6PD deficiency in Ethiopia is generally low, with 0% in the highlands [7]. However, in some areas it is much higher, with the prevalence of G6PD deficiency in the Nuer ethnic group almost 15% [7–9]. The Nuer are a major population in the ethnically diverse region of Gambella in southwest of Ethiopia, where the number of recorded malaria cases has recently doubled from 451,000 in 2023 to 871,000 in 2024 [10].

Since 2012, the Ethiopian national malarial guidelines have recommended a low total dose (3.5 mg/kg) of primaquine administered as 0.25 mg/kg/day over 14 days without G6PD deficiency testing. The national guidelines emphasise the careful monitoring of patients for early signs of drug-induced haemolysis after starting primaquine, so that primaquine can be discontinued and the patient can be referred to a health facility for further clinical review [2].

A global individual patient data pooled analysis found that a high total dose of primaquine (approximately 7 mg/kg) reduced the risk of P. vivax recurrences within 180 days by 55% compared with a low total dose of primaquine (approximately 3.5 mg/kg) across a range of countries that included Ethiopia [11]. We undertook a systematic review and individual patient data meta-analysis to investigate the anti-relapse efficacy of different primaquine regimens in Ethiopia and assess their safety and tolerability to inform decisions on the optimal national treatment protocols.

Methods

Setting

Malaria remains a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia, with malaria present across 75% of its land area and approximately 60% of the population at risk of infection. High transmission is present in 54 of 1084 woredas (a mid-level governmental administrative unit in the community), particularly in those below an altitude of 2,000 m [1]. However, malaria is also increasing in the highlands due to increased industrialisation and deforestation, influencing highland temperatures and facilitating parasite transmission [12–15]. Since 2019, Ethiopia has experienced a major resurgence of malaria; in 2024, reported cases more than doubled to 7.3 million compared with 4.1 million in 2023 [10]. The rise in malaria was particularly high in the Oromia region, Gambella region and Dire Dawa city administration [10].

Search strategy and selection criteria

MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus and Cochrane Central were searched for antimalarial efficacy studies of patients with uncomplicated P. vivax mono-infections conducted in Ethiopia, published in any language between January 1, 2000, and March 8, 2024. The search was an extension of an existing review [11] and was performed according to PRISMA guidelines (Supplementary Checklist S1) with search terms vivax AND (arterolane OR amodiaquine OR atovaquone OR artemisinin OR arteether OR artesunate OR artemether OR artemotil OR azithromycin OR artekin OR chloroquine OR chlorproguanil OR cycloguanil OR clindamycin OR coartem OR dapsone OR dihydroartemisinin OR duo-cotecxin OR doxycycline OR halofantrine OR lumefantrine OR lariam OR malarone OR mefloquine OR naphthoquine OR naphthoquinone OR piperaquine OR primaquine OR proguanil OR pyrimethamine OR pyronaridine OR proguanil OR quinidine OR quinine OR riamet OR sulphadoxine OR tetracycline OR tafenoquine).

Studies were included if they enrolled patients with P. vivax monoinfection and followed them for at least 28 days after commencement of treatment. Studies also had to have at least one treatment arm with daily primaquine administered over multiple days and in which primaquine was commenced within 7 days of schizontocidal treatment (Supplementary Table S1 & S2). Ethiopian data derived from multinational studies conducted at Ethiopian sites were included. Studies were excluded if they focused on prophylaxis, animal models or if they included patients with severe malaria or did not provide supervised antimalarial treatment. We also excluded reviews and studies that included retrospectively extracted data from medical records outside of a planned prospective study.

The review process was undertaken by two independent reviewers (TSD and RJC), with discrepancies resolved by discussion. The study was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023491851).

Data collation

All investigators of the eligible studies were invited to share individual patient data, including any eligible data from unpublished studies. Data were pseudo-anonymised and collated using standardised methodology in the WorldWide Antimalarial Resistance Network (WWARN) repository.

Individual patient data were excluded if age, sex, baseline parasite density, or total mg/kg dose of primaquine administered were missing. Patients with severe malaria or mixed species infection at enrolment, as well as patients who received adjunctive antimalarials within 14 days of enrolment or a weekly primaquine regimen were also excluded. The tolerability analysis was limited to studies that included symptom checklist data and provided primaquine treatment within 3 days of starting schizontocidal therapy. The safety analysis of haematological outcomes was restricted to patients who began primaquine treatment within 3 days of starting schizontocidal therapy and were known to have ≥ 30% G6PD activity.

The shared data were collected in accordance with ethical approvals from the original studies. As the data were pseudo-anonymised and could not be traced back to individuals, this analysis did not require additional ethical approval, following the guidelines of the Oxford Central University Research Ethics Committee.

Definitions

The total dose of primaquine (mg/kg) was considered as the exposure for the efficacy analyses, while the daily dose of primaquine (mg/kg/day) was used for the tolerability and safety analyses [16]. The total dose of primaquine was defined as a categorical variable to allow comparison between current recommended dosing regimens. The total dose was classified as very low total dose if < 2 mg/kg, low total dose if 2 to < 5 mg/kg, and high total dose if ≥ 5 mg/kg. The daily primaquine mg/kg dose was defined as low daily dose if < 0.375 mg/kg/day, intermediate daily dose if 0.375 to < 0.75 mg/kg/day, and high daily dose if ≥ 0.75 mg/kg/day [16].

Primaquine treatment supervision was defined as supervised if all doses were directly observed and partially supervised if > 1 dose but not all doses were observed.

Patients whose G6PD status was available at enrolment, were classified as deficient (< 30% activity) or normal (≥ 30% activity). Age was categorised into < 5 years, 5 to < 15 years, and 15 years and older.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the incidence risk of any P. vivax recurrence between day 7 and day 180. The secondary efficacy outcome was incidence risk of symptomatic P. vivax recurrence between day 7 and day 180.

The primary tolerability outcome was a composite endpoint defined as the presence of any of vomiting, anorexia, or diarrhoea reported on days 5–7. Nausea and abdominal pain were not included in the composite endpoint as they are unreliably reported by young children. The secondary tolerability outcomes were the presence of the composite outcome on days 1–2 (in the subset of patients having started primaquine treatment on day 0); and acute vomiting (within 1 h of primaquine administration).

The primary safety outcome was a composite endpoint of any of (i) an acute drop in haemoglobin > 5 g/dL within 14 days of starting primaquine treatment, (ii) a blood transfusion, (iii) an acute drop to a haemoglobin concentration < 5 g/dL within 14 days or (iv) an acute drop ≥ 25% from day 0 to a haemoglobin < 7 g/dL within 14 days. The secondary safety outcome was the absolute change in haemoglobin between day 0 and day 2/3.

Data analysis

A one stage meta-analysis was undertaken. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed to calculate the cumulative incidence of recurrent P. vivax parasitaemia between day 7 and 180. Patients were right censored on the last day of follow-up, the day of recurrent parasitaemia or the last day prior to a blood smear gap of > 60 days. Cox regression analyses compared the rate of recurrence (day 7 to 180) between primaquine total dose categories and adjusted for age, sex and baseline parasitaemia with shared frailty for study site. Separate sensitivity analyses restricted the dataset to patients treated with chloroquine, patients where the actual primaquine dose administered was known and to an analysis without shared frailty for study site. The tolerability and safety endpoints were described by primaquine daily dose category.

Within study bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and the Joanna Briggs Institute Case Series tool [17, 18] (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). Since data from all eligible studies were available for the analysis, an assessment of inclusion bias was not necessary. Study heterogeneity was investigated by repeat analysis following removal of one study at a time and calculation of the coefficient of variation.

Results

A total of 297 P. vivax efficacy studies were identified, of which 292 studies were excluded from the analysis: 66 studies were published before January 1, 2000, 209 studies were conducted outside Ethiopia, 14 did not include a daily primaquine treatment arm, and three administered weekly primaquine regimens. Five studies were included in the final analysis, comprising four randomised controlled trials and one prospective cohort study [16, 19–22]. Individual patient data were available for the five eligible studies, contributing a total of 1,443 patients, of which 65 patients were excluded due to patient-level criteria, and 1,378 patients were available for inclusion in the analyses (Fig. 1).

Study flow chart

Of the 1,378 patients included in the analysis, the median age was 18 years (interquartile range [IQR] 11.0–25.0, range 1–70 years). Overall, 807 (58.6%) of the patients were male, and the median weight was 50.0 kg (IQR 29.0–57.0, range 7–97 kg). The median parasite density was 8,043 parasites/μL (IQR 2,648–18,720) and chloroquine was used as the schizontocidal drug in 1,174 (85.2%) patients (Table 1). Risk of bias was low or unclear in all studies (Tables S3-S4).

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

Cq: chloroquine; Al: artemether-lumefantrine

Primaquine was not administered to 470 (34.1%) patients, 17 (1.2%) patients were prescribed very low total dose primaquine (< 2 mg/kg), 434 (31.5%) low total dose primaquine (2- < 5 mg/kg) and 457 (33.2%) high total dose primaquine (≥ 5 mg/kg) (Figure S1). Owing to the small number of patients administered the very low total dose, they were not included in subsequent efficacy analyses. Primaquine was fully supervised in 852 (61.8%) patients (two studies) and partially supervised in 526 (38.2%) patients (three studies; Supplementary Table S1). Study follow-up ranged from 42 to 365 days, with two studies (383 patients) following patients for less than 180 days.

Effect of primaquine total mg/kg dose on efficacy

The cumulative incidence of any (symptomatic or asymptomatic) P. vivax recurrence by day 180 was 61.0% (95% CI 55.3–66.8) in patients not treated with primaquine, 16.6% (95% CI 12.0–22.7) in patients who were treated with a low total dose of primaquine and 8.9% (95% CI 6.4–12.4) in those treated with high total dose primaquine (Fig. 2). In a sensitivity analysis where patients recruited into the non-randomised cohort study [22] were excluded, the cumulative incidence of recurrence remained consistent (Supplementary Figure S4).

Cumulative incidence of first Plasmodium vivax recurrence between day 7 and day 180 by treatment arm. PQ: primaquine

The cumulative incidence of symptomatic P. vivax recurrences by day 180 was 51.2% (95% CI 45.1–57.6) in patients not treated with primaquine, 13.7% (95% CI 9.4–19.8) in patients treated with low total dose primaquine and 7.5% (95% CI 5.2–10.7) in those treated with high total dose primaquine (Fig. 2).

In a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, the total dose of primaquine significantly reduced the rate of P. vivax recurrence. Compared to patients not treated with primaquine, those treated with low total dose primaquine had an adjusted hazard ratio (AHR) for recurrence of 0.20 (95% CI 0.14–0.28, p < 0.001), and those treated with high total dose primaquine had an AHR of 0.08 (95% CI 0.05–0.12, p < 0.001). The rate of P. vivax recurrence was significantly lower in patients treated with high total dose compared to low total dose primaquine (AHR 0.40, 95% CI 0.24–0.68; p = 0.001). In sensitivity analyses where inclusion was restricted to patients treated with chloroquine, shared frailty for study site was not undertaken and study sites were excluded one at a time, the association between primaquine mg/kg total dose and risk of recurrence remained relatively consistent (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7).

Tolerability analysis

Of the 1,378 patients included in the efficacy analysis, 295 patients were excluded from the tolerability analysis because of lack of data, resulting in 1,083 patients from 4 studies available for inclusion. Primaquine was not administered to 320 (29.6%) patients, 299 (27.6%) patients received low daily dose primaquine (< 0.375 mg/kg/day), 234 (21.6%) intermediate daily dose (0.375 to < 0.75 mg/kg/day) and 230 (21.2%) high daily dose (≥ 0.75 mg/kg/day; Figure S2).

Acute vomiting within one hour of primaquine was observed in 1.1% (1/91) of patients following low daily dose primaquine, 1.3% (3/229) of patients following intermediate daily dose and 0.4% (1/230) of patients following high daily dose primaquine. Reporting of tolerability symptoms varied between studies (Supplementary Table S5). To minimise bias, analyses of composite tolerability outcomes were restricted to studies that randomised patients to different daily primaquine dose regimens or randomised patients to treatment with or without primaquine. This approach was undertaken to reduce potential symptom reporting bias. In 554 patients from one study comparing no primaquine, intermediate and high daily dose primaquine, gastrointestinal disturbance on days 5–7 was recorded in 20 (19.1%), 36 (16.0%) and 54 (24.1%) patients respectively (Table 2). In 374 patients from a study comparing no primaquine to low daily dose primaquine, gastrointestinal disturbance on days 5–7 was not recorded in any patients, however, symptoms were similar between treatment groups on day 0 and days 1–2 (Table 2).

Safety analysis

Of the 1,378 available patient records, 957 (69.4%) patients from three studies were eligible for the haematology analyses. Patients were excluded because they started primaquine after day 2 (n = 146), had no haemoglobin measurements (n = 165), G6PD activity was < 30% (n = 1) or G6PD activity was unknown (n = 109). In total, 308 (32.2%) patients were not treated with primaquine, 193 (20.2%) patients were treated with a low daily dose, 231 (24.1%) with an intermediate daily dose, and 225 (23.5%) with a high daily dose (Table 3).

The absolute reduction in haemoglobin between day 0 and days 2–3 was 0.8 g/dl (SD: 1.1) in patients not treated with primaquine, 0.8 g/dl (SD: 1.0) in patients treated with a low daily dose, 0.9 g/dl (SD: 1.2) in patients treated with an intermediate daily dose, and 1.0 g/dl (SD: 1.2) in those treated with a high daily dose primaquine (Table 3, Supplementary Figure S3 & S5).

Only one male patient, who was treated with intermediate daily dose primaquine, experienced a ≥ 25% drop in haemoglobin to a concentration < 7 g/dL, starting from a haemoglobin of 10.2 g/dL and falling to 6.8 g/dL on day 3. Two male participants had a drop in haemoglobin > 5 g/dL; one treated with an intermediate daily dose fell from 16.8 g/dL to 11.3 g/dL, and the other treated with a high daily dose fell from 17.0 g/dL to 11.9 g/dL. No patients required a blood transfusion and no patients had a haemoglobin fall to < 5 g/dL.

The patient with G6PD activity < 30% received 0.5 mg/kg/day of primaquine and fell from a haemoglobin of 14.6 g/dL to 14.0 g/dL on day 3 (4.1% reduction). The haemogloblin then increased to 14.8 g/dL by day 7.

Of 109 patients with unknown G6PD activity, 9 were treated without primaquine and 100 received low daily dose primaquine for 14 days. Of the 100 receiving primaquine, 92 had haemoglobin measurements at day 3, but none had measurements between days 5–7. In a post hoc analysis, the mean absolute change in haemoglobin between day 0 and days 2–3 was an increase of 0.2 g/dL (SD 2.2; range − 6.6 to 5.6 g/dL). No patients had a ≥ 25% fall in haemoglobin to a concentration < 7 g/dL or required a blood transfusion, however, two patients (2.2%) fell > 5 g/dL.

Discussion

This pooled analysis of individual patient data from primaquine efficacy studies provides a comprehensive assessment of the efficacy and safety of different primaquine regimens for the radical cure of P. vivax in Ethiopia. Patients treated with either low (3.5 mg/kg) or high (7 mg/kg) total dose primaquine had substantially lower risks of recurrence compared to patients not treated with primaquine. Treatment with high total dose primaquine conferred greater protection and was not associated with a significantly greater risk of severe haemolysis in G6PD normal patients (≥ 30% activity) compared with no or lower daily dose primaquine.

A recent global meta-analysis found that high total dose primaquine reduced the risk of recurrence by more than 50% compared with low total-dose primaquine [11]. This finding was adopted by WHO Malaria Treatment Guidelines in 2024, with a recommendation to use high total dose primaquine in most endemic locations but included a stipulation for G6PD testing prior to treatment. High total dose primaquine can be given over 7 days as 1 mg/kg/day or 14 days as 0.5 mg/kg/day, with the higher daily dose being restricted to patients with G6PD activity ≥ 70%. However, these global recommendations have yet to be introduced into national policy in most endemic countries [23].

The Ethiopian national malaria control program has emphasised the importance of antimalarial policy being based on national data rather than global estimates alone. To address this need, the current analysis generated national estimates. These national estimates show that high total dose primaquine is associated with a 60% reduction in the risk of P. vivax recurrence compared with low total dose primaquine, which is similar to the 55% reduction reported in the global data [11]. The consistency of findings across study sites in the sensitivity analysis in this study further supports the robustness and generalisability of the observed dose–response relationship between primaquine and prevention of P. vivax recurrence.

Recurrent P. vivax infections during follow-up may arise due to recrudescences from failed schizontocidal treatment, relapses from residual dormant hypnozoites or reinfection from a new mosquito inoculation. The transmission intensity in Ethiopia is heterogeneous, hence whilst any of these mechanisms may contribute to recurrences, early recurrences are more likely to reflect relapse [24]. The inability to distinguish relapses from reinfection will inevitably result in an overestimation of treatment failure rates, potentially attenuating differences between treatment arms.

Gastrointestinal symptoms are well-recognised side effects of higher daily doses of primaquine; however, they also commonly occur in acute malaria and in association with some other antimalarial drugs. Our analysis of gastrointestinal symptoms after the acute malaria phase was restricted to a single trial. Results showed that primaquine was well-tolerated, with gastrointestinal adverse events at days 5–7 similar in patients treated with or without primaquine. At day 0, or day 1–2, in the setting of acute malaria and schizontocidal treatment, the presence of gastrointestinal symptoms was significantly greater in all dosing categories. Consistent with previous studies [11] acute vomiting within one hour of primaquine administration was rare, occurring in less than 1% of patients.

Although gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently reported with primaquine, symptoms are generally mild and self-limiting [16, 25, 26]. Taking primaquine with food improves gastrointestinal tolerability. Recent clinical trials [26, 27] have found that high daily dose primaquine (1 mg/kg/day) was generally well tolerated when given with food, with gastrointestinal symptoms occurring at similar rates to patients treated with low daily dose primaquine (0.25 mg/kg/day). However, our current analysis was not able to assess tolerability by food intake.

The haematological safety profile of primaquine observed in this pooled analysis was reassuring. Clinically significant haemolysis was rare, even at higher daily doses in patients with ≥ 30% G6PD activity. Among the 957 patients with ≥ 30% G6PD activity, only one patient treated with 0.5 mg/kg/day primaquine developed a ≥ 25% fall in haemoglobin to < 7 g/dL, and two participants (one receiving 0.5 mg/kg/day and one receiving 1 mg/kg/day) experienced > 5 g/dL reductions. No patients developed severe anaemia or required a blood transfusion. The absolute mean haemoglobin reduction by day 2–3 was modest and comparable between treatment groups, consistent with haemoglobin declines typically observed in acute malaria without additive drops related to primaquine in G6PD normal patients [28]. These findings reflect those in a recent individual patient data meta-analysis, which also found that the risk of haemolysis with high daily dose primaquine in G6PD-normal individuals was low [29]. The absence of severe haemolysis at higher daily doses (≥ 0.75 mg/kg/day) is consistent with existing evidence that whilst short-course high total dose primaquine regimens are generally well tolerated in G6PD-normal individuals [29], it is important that patients are screened for G6PD deficiency before primaquine is prescribed. Severe haemolysis was uncommon in patients with unknown G6PD activity, although numbers were small, low daily dose primaquine was used and there were no haemoglobin measurements available between days 5–7. The investigators from the study that enrolled these patients rationalised this approach due to the low G6PD prevalence in the study population [22].

Our study has important implications for malaria control programs in Ethiopia. The efficacy of high total dose primaquine in G6PD normal patients, combined with its favorable safety profile, supports its wider use for radical cure. However, the variability in treatment supervision (all the studies used supervised or at least partially supervised treatment) suggests that adherence may have been better than under routine programmatic conditions. In a recent trial, patients treated with an unsupervised, 7-day, high total dose primaquine regimen had a risk of recurrence at 6 months of 18.4% (95% CI 12.2–27.0), considerably higher than the estimates in our pooled analysis [27].

Ethiopia uses 7.5 mg tablets, which substantially increases the pill burden for patients in the high daily dose regimen. This may contribute to reduced adherence, a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of short course high total dose regimens. Simplifying dosing through the additional availability of higher-strength tablet formulations, would reduce pill burden in higher weight bands and could improve adherence and overall treatment outcomes. This potential gap between efficacy and effectiveness highlights the need for operational research into interventions that can enhance adherence, such as community follow-up [30], digital adherence tools, and patient education [31]. It also underscores the advantages of tafenoquine, as its single-dose regimen can overcome adherence challenges and serve as a more practical alternative in patients with G6PD activities ≥ 70%.

In this pooled analysis, one-third of patients in the efficacy studies had unknown G6PD status, a common situation in Ethiopia where routine G6PD testing is rarely available. The safe implementation of high total dose primaquine depends on identifying patients at risk of haemolysis, hence introducing point of care G6PD testing into routine malaria case management will be essential. Feasibility studies to inform its introduction are currently ongoing in Ethiopia [32] and a phased roll out similar to other settings is likely required [33] to ensure confidence is built among patients, health care workers and policy makers alike.

This pooled analysis has several limitations. Firstly, although all eligible studies were included, only a small number of studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria and there was heterogeneity in the study designs, which may impact on the generalisability of findings. Secondly, the included studies did not compare high total dose versus low total dose primaquine directly, potentially increasing the risk of bias from comparing data from different arms between studies. The comparisons were further constrained by non-standardised symptom reporting across studies, which limits consistency in safety assessment (Supplementary Table S5) [34]. The absence of ethnicity data from the included studies, prevented further exploration of haematological safety outcomes in ethnic groups with varied G6PD activity.

In conclusion, our analysis affirms the increased efficacy and acceptable safety profile of high total dose primaquine for the radical cure of patients with ≥ 30% G6PD activity and P. vivax malaria in Ethiopia, consistent with global evidence. Expanding the use of high total dose regimens, improving adherence, and ensuring universal G6PD screening will be critical priorities to enhance the impact of radical cure and facilitate the control and elimination of P. vivax malaria in Ethiopia.
