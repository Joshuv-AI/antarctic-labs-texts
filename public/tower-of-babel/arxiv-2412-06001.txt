M6: Multi-generator, Multi-domain, Multi-lingual and cultural, Multi-genres, Multi-instrument Machine-Generated Music Detection Databases
Yupei Li, Hanqian Li, Lucia Specia, Björn W. Schuller

Abstract
Machine-generated music (MGM) has emerged as a powerful tool with applications in music therapy, personalised editing, and creative inspiration for the music community. However, its unregulated use threatens the entertainment, education, and arts sectors by diminishing the value of high-quality human compositions. Detecting machine-generated music (MGMD) is, therefore, critical to safeguarding these domains, yet the field lacks comprehensive datasets to support meaningful progress. To address this gap, we introduce M6 , a large-scale benchmark dataset tailored for MGMD research. M6 is distinguished by its diversity, encompassing multiple generators, domains, languages, cultural contexts, genres, and instruments. We outline our methodology for data selection and collection, accompanied by detailed data analysis, providing all WAV form of music. Additionally, we provide baseline performance scores using foundational binary classification models, illustrating the complexity of MGMD and the significant room for improvement. By offering a robust and multifaceted resource, we aim to empower future research to develop more effective detection methods for MGM. We believe M6 will serve as a critical step toward addressing this societal challenge. The dataset and code will be freely available to support open collaboration and innovation in this field.

Machine-generated music (MGM) recently has seen rapid adoption with platforms like OpenAI’s MuseNet

Its pervasive influence risks stifling originality as artists may unconsciously replicate AI-driven patterns. For audiences, there are concerns that AI-generated styles could dominate the music scene, shifting public taste. Effective MGM detection (MGMD) is vital for preserving the creative integrity of the music community and preventing disruptions to creators and consumers.

Recent works

]
reviewed the current research of MGMD, together with other music detection tasks

The authors highlight critical gaps, noting that the only publicly available dataset, FakeMusicCaps

[
11

, has its own dataset generation strategy but lacks public access and does not sufficiently cover the diversity of music genres. Given that music is inherently subjective and an art form with diverse formats, providing a sufficiently varied dataset is essential for training effective MGMD models. Such a dataset would accelerate the development of MGMD, which is considerably more complex than a simple binary classification task
[
12
]
. In response to these challenges, we aim to create a comprehensive dataset encompassing various music types and scenarios to further advance the field.

Similar to the well-established field of machine-generated text detection, which has benefited from widely used datasets like M4
[
13
]
, we draw inspiration from these efforts and extend their ideas to the knowledge of music. This has led to the development of M6: a comprehensive database for Multi-Generator, Multi-Domain, Multi-Lingual and Cultural, Multi-Genre, and Multi-Instrument Machine-Generated Music Detection. We acknowledge that different generators produce distinct output distributions, resulting in various “styles” of music, supporting by research indicating that watermarking techniques can effectively identify the outputs of different generators
[
14
]
. Consequently, we incorporate multiple generators into our dataset to enhance its diversity. Li et al.
[
6
]
emphasise the two key stages of music production: the composition stage, where melody and harmony express the musical narrative, and the decoration stage, where sound design and mixing enhance the music’s aesthetic quality. To reflect the diversity of composition, we have curated a multi-domain music collection suitable for various scenarios, such as background music or songs with lyrics, as well as multi-lingual and culturally diverse pieces tailored to different audiences. Additionally, for the decoration stage, selecting appropriate genres to shape the sound and choosing instruments to complement melodies are crucial steps in the process
[
15
]
. These principles have guided the organisation of our dataset.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We construct, to the best of our knowledge, the first comprehensive music database specifically designed for MGMD. We also provide detailed data analysis on the database.

•

We evaluate the performance of foundational detectors from various perspectives: (a) comparing detectors across different generators, (b) assessing detectors across different domains, and (c) evaluating out-of-domain detector performance. From these experiments, we derive several key observations that can guide future research directions.

•

We will release our data and code publicly, and we plan to continuously update our repository by adding new generators, domains, and languages.

Music generators

While human-generated music is widely available, Music-Generated Music (MGM) needs to be produced, as there are often more valuable and diverse generators available to the research community than direct samples. Several types of music generation models exist, including Autoregressive Music Generators (e.g., Awesome-Music-Generation (AMG)

AMG

This model is based on autoregressive techniques, with music generation conditioned on melody inputs. It aligns the melody with corresponding text, utilizing a text-prompted generation approach. Notably, it achieves high-quality music generation with a relatively smaller amount of training data but it could only generate maximum 10-second audio background music.

MG

MusicGen is built on a single language model, conditioned on compressed discrete music representations. It also incorporates prompt engineering for music generation, offering enhanced flexibility. Additionally,
MGPT
is an extended interface for MusicGen, which facilitates the integration of additional music generators, broadening its capabilities. However, these could only generate maximum 30-second audio music.

II-C

Music detectors

All of the aforementioned datasets are employed in our MGMD task, which is framed as a binary classification problem. The existing models for this task are limited, with fewer than five detectors currently available and under development. Furthermore, these models lack a sufficient baseline, and their approaches are highly fragmented
[
6
]
. However, drawing inspiration from binary classification and deepfake audio detection tasks, we select three representative types of classification models for evaluation: Quadratic Support Vector Machine (Q-SVM)
[
32
]
as a traditional machine learning model, ResNet
[
33
]
as a deep neural network model, and ViT
[
18
]
as a Transformer-based model. These models are selected based on their frequent adoption in deepfake audio detection tasks and the inherent characteristics of music feature representation, such as mel-spectrograms, which are effectively processed by image-based models.

Q-SVM

Q-SVM is a variant of SVM that utilises a quadratic kernel function to map input data into a higher-dimensional space, which is a simple yet effective classifier.

ResNet

ResNetis a CNN that incorporates residual connections to prevent information loss and facilitate the training of deeper networks. This model excels at extracting latent image features.

ViT

ViT is a transformer-based model that treats images as sequences of patches, leveraging the self-attention mechanism to capture intricate patterns and dependencies. This allows ViT to effectively extract high-level features.

III
Database collection process

Our data collection process is divided into two stages: reassenble human-made music and generating our own MGMs, as outlined in “Fig.
1
”.

Fig. 1:
The process pipeline for collecting our database begins with determining the types of music to include. We aim to collect six key categories: music based on different instruments, different languages and cultures, with or without lyrics, various genres, differing music lengths, and general musics without any content restriction. Initially, we assess existing research datasets to see if they meet our requirements. If they do not, we obtain the necessary music from websites with appropriate licensing. Next, we generate our own set of MGM by utilising either research-based models or commercial models, conditioned on prompts generated by LLMs such as GPT-3.5.

For the creation of the M6 dataset, we focus on six types of music. We will utilise all the models reviewed in
II
to ensure diversity across multiple generators. The types of music to be collected are: (a) different instruments, (b) different languages and cultures, (c) with or without lyrics (including voiceable songs and background music for various domains), and (d) different genres. Furthermore, we recognise that current audio processing models have limitations when handling long audio sequences. Previous work has highlighted that models like Transformers and other deep learning architectures struggle with long-range dependencies in audio
[
34
]
. To address these limitations, we propose averaging the length of each type of music to 60 seconds, ensuring a manageable duration for existing models. Additionally, we will create a separate set (e) of long songs, with duration of each song ranging from 2 to 3 minutes, to represent typical song lengths and challenge the current limitations of these models to encourage their robustness. Besides, for the out of domain test, we construct some random set (f) for random general music collection. The resources are summarised in “Table

Human made songs sampling

Given the vast number of available human-made music resources, we do not cover the entire set. Instead, we randomly select a representative subset, ensuring that the quantity of samples is sufficient. All selected datasets are licensed for non-commercial use and redistribution, except for NetEase Cloud and Spotify. However, these resources could still be downloaded and used for personal research purposes. As such, we provide metadata only for these two datasets in the supplementary materials.

MGM prompts setting

We utilise simple instruction-akin prompts for either research tools or commercial tools, while we utilise the lyrics generated by GPT-3.5
[
35
]
with prompts
Give me 100 30-second-language song lyrics.
for type (c), and
Give 100 prompts to generate music.
for type (f) for Mureka. Guided by principles of prompt engineering
[
36
]
, we design all prompts to be straightforward, concise, and easy to interpret, aiming to reduce the complexity of prompt understanding that could compromise the quality of machine-generated music (MGM). For example, for AMG and MG, we use
Please generate a song with instrument / genres
. Additionally, we introduce intentional randomness by varying prompts. For example, we use
Give me instrument based / genres songs.
for MGT and
One 30-second language song.
for Mureka (generating type (b, c)).

Quality control

Music generation, unlike others, is a form of art production, which is very subjective and biased if we ask human annotators to mark. After simple sanity check with our melody sense, we find objective metrics to provide a relatively fair quality measurements for MGM to control the generated data quality. We selected Rhythmic Entropy
[
37
]
, which quantifies the complexity of rhythmic patterns in music, providing insights into rhythmic structure; Melodic Range
[
38
]
, which represents the span between the lowest and highest pitches, serving as an indicator of melodic consistency; and Harmonic-to-Noise Ratio (HNR)
[
39
]
, which measures the ratio of harmonic components to noise, reflecting the clarity and quality of harmonic content. Across different music types, there were no significant differences between MGM and human-composed music, except for certain cases of HNR. These discrepancies in HNR may be attributed to unavoidable noise in the recording process of some human-composed music, in other way showing high MGM quality. Overall, these findings indicate that the quality of MGM is comparable to that of human-composed music. The results are in “Table

It can be observed that ResNet18 achieves the best performance in standard cases, demonstrating a relatively high level of accuracy and F1 score for the detection task. However, for longer songs (e), the Transformer-based ViT model performs slightly better, which is consistent with the expectation that the attention mechanism excels at capturing long-term dependencies. While Q-SVM performs marginally worse, its results remain competitive. Furthermore, the similarity between the accuracy and F1-score, combined with insights from the AOC-ROC curves, provides evidence supporting the claim that our dataset is relatively balanced. Overall, the models exhibit fundamentally strong performance in the in-domain evaluation.

However, when evaluating the models trained on each type using (f) as an out-of-domain test set, the performance across all models and types is notably poor. The Q-SVM model, in particular, exhibits some level of randomness in its performance for the (e) out-of-domain test, likely due to the simplicity of the model. This outcome provides evidence that the dataset poses a considerable challenge for the task at hand, reinforcing the inherent difficulty of the problem. The results further suggest that the models lack robustness and have not sufficiently captured the underlying musicological characteristics. In practical applications of MGMD, it is common to encounter new, out-of-domain music as the music landscape continues to evolve. Consequently, there is an urgent need for more robust models that can generalise effectively across diverse musical contexts, as well as models with enhanced explainability.

VI
Conclusion

We have introduced the M6 database for the MGMD task, providing a detailed analysis of this database and comparing it with existing datasets, highlighting its comprehensive and abundant sources as well as its broad coverage. Additionally, we have established baseline performance using commonly used models. Building on these insights, we identify several challenges that should be addressed in future work. We advocate for the development of more robust models that can improve out-of-domain performance. Furthermore, we call for greater model explainability, moving beyond simple binary classification outputs to provide more meaningful interpretations of model decisions. Moreover, we propose further efforts to improve performance on this challenging dataset. Looking ahead, multimodal models that incorporate lyrics and hierarchical models for handling long-term music structure require great attention and research.
