Numerical Approaches for Identifying the Time-Dependent Potential Coefficient in the Diffusion Equation
Arshyn Altybay, Michael Ruzhansky

Abstract
. We address the inverse problem of identifying a time-dependent potential coefficient in a one-dimensional diffusion equation subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions and a nonlocal integral overdetermination constraint reflecting spatially averaged measurements. After establishing well-posedness for the forward problem and deriving an a priori estimate that ensures uniqueness and continuous dependence on the data, we prove existence and uniqueness for the inverse problem. To compute numerically the unknown coefficient, we propose and compare three numerical methods: an integration-based scheme, a Newton–Raphson iterative solver, and a physics-informed neural network (PINN). Numerical experiments on both exact and noisy data demonstrate the accuracy, robustness, and efficiency of each approach.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega:=(0,l)
be a bounded spatial domain, and let
T>0
denote a fixed final time. We consider the inverse problem of jointly identifying a time-dependent potential coefficient
p(t)
and the corresponding solution
u(x,t)
of the following initial-boundary value problem for the one-dimensional diffusion equation:

u_{t}(x,t)=u_{xx}(x,t)-p(t)u(x,t)+f(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in Q:=\Omega\times(0,T],

(1.1)

subject to the initial condition

u(x,0)=\varphi(x),\quad x\in\overline{\Omega},

(1.2)

and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions

u(0,t)=u(l,t)=0,\quad t\in[0,T].

(1.3)

Here:

u(x,t)
denotes the state variable (e.g. temperature or concentration),

f\in C^{2,1}(\overline{Q})
is a known source term,

\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is the known initial condition,

p\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
is the unknown reaction or potential coefficient.

To identify the unknown function
p(t)
, we are given an additional nonlocal integral overdetermination condition of the form

\int_{0}^{l}u(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx=g(t),\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.4)

where
\omega\in C^{2}([0,l])
is a given non-negative weight function representing spatial sensitivity of measurements, and
g\in C^{1}([0,T])
is a given measurement function.

Objective:
Given
f(x,t)
,
\varphi(x)
,
\omega(x)
, and
g(t)
, determine the pair
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}
that satisfies the PDE (
1.1
), initial condition (
1.2
), boundary conditions (
1.3
), and the overdetermination condition (
1.4
).

Such a problem arises in various contexts involving heat transfer, diffusion, or reaction processes in which a source parameter is present. In particular, when
u(x,t)
denotes the temperature distribution, the problem can be interpreted as a control problem featuring a source term that serves as the control variable
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

The integral overdetermination condition (
1.4
) represents a fundamental class of inverse problems where measurements correspond to spatially integrated quantities. This formulation was described in detail by Prilepko and his co-authors in their books published in 2000
[
4
]
; it models practical scenarios where sensors capture aggregate field values rather than point measurements, such as total pollutant mass in environmental monitoring or integrated heat flux in thermal systems. The weighting function
\omega(x)
encodes the measurement device’s spatial sensitivity, while

g(t)
provides the time-dependent global observation data essential for coefficient identification. Such integral overdetermination conditions have also been extensively investigated in the recent literature

In the study conducted by Cannon et al.

Kanca

The problem involves periodic boundary and integral overdetermination conditions, and the existence and uniqueness of classical solutions were demonstrated. Numerically, the author utilised a Crank-Nicolson finite-difference scheme combined with an iteration method; however, accuracy analyses were not included.

Hazanee et al.

The authors showed the existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence of classical solutions by employing the generalised Fourier method. Hazanee and Lesnic

Numerically, they used the Boundary Element Method with second-order Tikhonov regularization, demonstrating the accuracy and stability of their solutions through benchmark examples.

Durdiev et al.

u_{x}(0,t)=h(t)

While the aforementioned studies have made significant contributions to the theoretical understanding of inverse problems in parabolic equations, they exhibit several limitations from a numerical analysis perspective. First, these works primarily focused on either homogeneous integral overdetermination conditions or pointwise conditions as their additional constraints. Second, they did not provide a comprehensive numerical analysis of the complete inverse problem, particularly concerning convergence properties and error estimation.

Modern scientific computing increasingly adopts neural networks to solve PDEs. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) approximate solutions by optimising parameters through PDE-based loss functions
[
39

[

41

This study advances the field by developing a computational framework that overcomes existing limitations through two key innovations. First, we introduce a non-homogeneous integral overdetermination condition as a supplementary constraint, vastly broadening the method’s applicability to real-world scenarios. Second, we design enhanced numerical solvers that integrate the classical integration method, an optimized Newton–Raphson iteration, and the PINN approach. This hybrid strategy delivers three major benefits: rigorous convergence guarantees, improved computational efficiency, and enhanced numerical stability over long simulations. By combining these methodological advances with a solid mathematical formulation, our work not only surmounts previous challenges but also preserves the theoretical foundations of prior research. 2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some fundamental definitions, inequalities, lemmas and theorems used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Poincaré’s Inequality
[
42
, Prop. 8.13, p. 218]
)

\int_{0}^{l}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{l^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{l}|u_{x}(x)|^{2}\,dx,\quad\forall u\in H^{1}_{0}(0,l).

Lemma 2.2
.

(Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality
[
43
, p. 624]
)
Let
f
and
g
be measurable functions on a domain
\Omega
such that
f,g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then

\left|\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\;\leq\;\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/2}\;\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}|g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Schauder’s Fixed-Point Theorem,
[
44
]
)
Let
X
be a Banach space and
K\subset X
a nonempty, closed, bounded, and convex subset. Suppose that the operator
A:K\rightarrow K
is continuous and compact. Then the operator
A
has at least one fixed point, i.e., there exists
x\in K
such that

A(x)=x.

Remark 2.4
.

An operator
A:K\rightarrow K
is called compact if it maps bounded subsets of
K
into relatively compact subsets of
K
.

Theorem 2.5

(Arzelà–Ascoli)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset C(\overline{Q})
, where
\overline{Q}=\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]
. If
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on
\overline{Q}
, then every sequence in
\mathcal{F}
has a uniformly convergent subsequence in
C(\overline{Q})
.

Definition 2.6

(Newton–Raphson Method)
.

The Newton–Raphson method is an iterative root-finding algorithm for solving nonlinear equations of the form
F(p)=0
. Given an initial guess
p^{(0)}
, the method updates it according to

p^{(j+1)}=p^{(j)}-\frac{F(p^{(j)})}{F^{\prime}(p^{(j)})},

where
F^{\prime}(p)
is the derivative (or Jacobian in higher dimensions). In the context of inverse problems, it is used to iteratively adjust unknown parameters so that the numerical solution satisfies overdetermined conditions.

Definition 2.7

(Physics-Informed Neural Network
[
39
]
)
.

A Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) is a machine learning framework that embeds the structure of a differential equation into the training process of a neural network. Instead of relying solely on data, PINNs minimise a loss functional that includes the residuals of the governing PDE, initial/boundary conditions, and any additional constraints.

3.
Existence and Uniqueness Results

In this section, we demonstrate the well-posedness of the direct and inverse problems defined by (
1.1
)–(
1.4
).

We have the following assumptions for the problems (
1.1
)–(
1.4
):

(1)

p\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
.

(2)

\varphi\in C^{2}([0,l])
with
\varphi(0)=\varphi(l)=0
.

(3)

f\in C^{2,1}(\overline{Q})
satisfying
f(0,t)=f(l,t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

(4)

\omega\in C^{2}([0,l])
with
\omega(0)=\omega(l)=0
, and
g\in C^{1}([0,T])
with
g(t)\neq 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

3.1.
Existence and uniqueness of the direct problem

In this subsection, we present existence and uniqueness results as well as an a priori estimate of the direct problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Theorem 3.1

(Existence and Uniqueness)
.

Let the assumptions (1)-(3) be satisfied. Then the initial-boundary value problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) admits a unique solution
u\in C([0,T];H^{2}(0,l)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,l))
with
u_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(0,l))
.

Proof.

Existence:
The solution is constructed via Fourier series. Consider the eigenfunctions
\phi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{l}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{l}\right)
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}=\left(\frac{n\pi}{l}\right)^{2}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Expand:

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n}(t)\phi_{n}(x),\quad\varphi(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}\phi_{n}(x),\quad f(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}(t)\phi_{n}(x),

where
\varphi_{n}=\langle\varphi,\phi_{n}\rangle
,
f_{n}(t)=\langle f(\cdot,t),\phi_{n}\rangle
. Substitution into the PDE yields:

u_{n}^{\prime}(t)+(\lambda_{n}+p(t))u_{n}(t)=f_{n}(t),\quad u_{n}(0)=\varphi_{n}.

Solving this ODE gives:

u_{n}(t)=\varphi_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}t-\Lambda(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda_{n}(t-s)-(\Lambda(t)-\Lambda(s))}\,f_{n}(s)\,ds,\quad\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds.

(3.1)

Thus, the full solution is:

u(t,x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\varphi_{n}e^{-\lambda_{n}t-\Lambda(t)}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda_{n}(t-s)-(\Lambda(t)-\Lambda(s))}\,f_{n}(s)\,ds\right]\phi_{n}(x).

(3.2)

Since
p\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
, let
P:=\|p\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}
. Then, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,

|\Lambda(t)|=\left|\int_{0}^{t}p(s)ds\right|\leq Pt,\qquad|\Lambda(t)-\Lambda(s)|\leq P(t-s),

and hence

\big|e^{-\Lambda(t)}\big|\leq e^{Pt},\qquad\big|e^{-(\Lambda(t)-\Lambda(s))}\big|\leq e^{P(t-s)}.

The smoothness and boundary vanishing of
\varphi
and
f(\cdot,t)
imply the Fourier–sine
coefficient decay

|\varphi_{n}|=O(n^{-2}),\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|f_{n}(t)|=O(n^{-2}).

Consequently, with
\lambda_{n}\asymp n^{2}
, there exist
C,C>0
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

|u_{n}(t)|\;\leq\;C\!\left(\frac{e^{-cn^{2}t}}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right),\qquad\lambda_{n}|u_{n}(t)|\;\leq\;C\!\left(e^{-cn^{2}t}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

In particular, for every
\delta\in(0,T]
,

\sup_{t\in[\delta,T]}|u_{n}(t)|=O(n^{-4}),\qquad\sup_{t\in[\delta,T]}\lambda_{n}|u_{n}(t)|=O(n^{-2}).

Therefore, for each
\delta>0
, the Fourier series defining
u
,
u_{x}
, and
u_{xx}

converge
uniformly
on
[0,l]\times[\delta,T]
.
At
t=0
one has
u(\cdot,0)=\varphi
in
L^{2}(0,l)
.
Moreover,

u_{t}=u_{xx}-p(t)u+f\in L^{2}\!\big(0,T;L^{2}(0,l)\big).

Uniqueness:

Suppose
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are two solutions satisfying (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). Define
w(x,t)=u_{1}(x,t)-u_{2}(x,t)
. Then
w
satisfies:

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=w_{xx}-p(t)w,\quad(x,t)\in Q,

\displaystyle w(x,0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad x\in[0,l],

\displaystyle w(0,t)

\displaystyle=w(l,t)=0,\quad t\in[0,T].

Consider the energy functional:

E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx.

Differentiate with respect to
t
and substitute the PDE:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle=Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{w}w_{t}dx

\displaystyle=Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{w}[w_{xx}-p(t)w]dx

\displaystyle=Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{w}w_{xx}dx-p(t)\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx.

Integration by parts (using boundary conditions
w(0,t)=w(l,t)=0
) yields:

\int_{0}^{l}\bar{w}w_{xx}dx=\left[\bar{w}w_{x}\right]_{0}^{l}-\int_{0}^{l}\bar{w_{x}}w_{x}dx=-\int_{0}^{l}|w_{x}|^{2}dx.

So,

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-\int_{0}^{l}|w_{x}|^{2}dx-p(t)\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx

Since
p\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
there exists
P>0
such that
|p(t)|\leq P\ \text{for almost every $t\in[0,T]$,}
Then,

-p(t)\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx\leq P\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx

Hence,

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq-\int_{0}^{l}|w_{x}|^{2}dx+P\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx.

Apply Poincaré’s inequality for
H_{0}^{1}(0,l)
:

\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx\leq\frac{l^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{l}|w_{x}|^{2}dx\implies\int_{0}^{l}|w_{x}|^{2}dx\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{l^{2}}\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx.

Substitute into the inequality:

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq-\frac{\pi^{2}}{l^{2}}\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx+P\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx=(P-\frac{\pi^{2}}{l^{2}})\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx.

Since
\int_{0}^{l}|w|^{2}dx=2E(t)
, we get:

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq 2(P-\frac{\pi^{2}}{l^{2}})E(t).

Let
M=2(P-\frac{\pi^{2}}{l^{2}})
. Then

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq ME(t).

Since
E(0)=0
and
E(t)\geq 0
, Gronwall’s inequality implies:

E(t)\leq 0,\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

Therefore,
E(t)=0
for all t, so
w(x,t)=0
almost everywhere. Hence,

u_{1}(x,t)=u_{2}(x,t),

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
A priori estimate for the direct problem

Theorem 3.2

(A priori estimate)
.

Let
p\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
,
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,l)
,
and
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(0,l))
. Then the solution
u
of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) satisfies:

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq e^{(1+2P)t}\|\varphi(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(1+2P)(t-s)}\|f(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}ds.

(3.3)

where
P=\|Re\,p\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Proof.

Multiply (
1.1
) by
\bar{u}(x,t)
and integrate over
(0,l)
:

\int_{0}^{l}u_{t}\,\bar{u}\,\,dx=\int_{0}^{l}u_{xx}\,\bar{u}\,\,dx-p(t)\int_{0}^{l}|u|^{2}\,\,dx+\int_{0}^{l}f\,\,\bar{u}\,\,dx.

Integration by parts and boundary conditions yield:

\int_{0}^{l}u_{xx}\,\,\bar{u}\,dx=-\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\int_{0}^{l}u_{t}\,\ \bar{u}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Thus:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+Re\left(p(t)\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)=Re\left(\int_{0}^{l}f\,\,\bar{u}\,\,dx\right).

(3.4)

Using Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities:

\left|\int_{0}^{l}f\,\,\bar{u}\,\,dx\right|\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
p\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
, Let
P=\|Re\,p\|_{L^{\infty}}
. Then:

|Re\left(p(t)\right)|\geq-P\implies Re\left(p(t)\right)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\geq-P\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Substituting into (
3.4
) and multiply by 2 we can get

\frac{d}{dt}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2\|u_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2P\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(3.5)

Dropping the nonnegative gradient term and changing variable
t
by
s
in inequality (
3.5
) and integrating it over
s
from
0
to
t
gives:

\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+(1+2P)\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}ds.

Applying Grönwall’s lemma, we obtain our estimate (
3.3
)
∎

3.3.
Existence and uniqueness of the solution of the inverse problem

Theorem 3.3

(Existence for Inverse Problem)
.

Under assumptions (1)–(4), the inverse problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) admits at least one solution pair
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}
with

p\in C([0,T])\cap L^{\infty}([0,T]),\quad u\in C([0,T];H^{2}(0,l)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,l))\text{ and }u_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(0,l)).

Proof.

For any
\hat{p}\in L^{\infty}([0,T])
, Theorem
3.1
guarantees a unique solution
u_{\hat{p}}\in C([0,T];H^{2}\cap H_{0}^{1})\text{ and }(u_{\hat{p}})_{t}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})
to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).
We employ Schauder’s fixed-point theorem (Theorem
2.3
).
Define the operator
\mathcal{A}:L^{\infty}([0,T])\to C([0,T])
:

(\mathcal{A}\hat{p})(t):=\frac{\int_{0}^{l}f(x,t)\omega(x)dx+\int_{0}^{l}u_{\hat{p}}(x,t)\omega^{\prime\prime}(x)dx-g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}.

Boundary terms ([
u_{x}\omega]_{0}^{l}
and
-[u\omega^{\prime}]_{0}^{l}
) vanish due to
\omega(0)=\omega(l)=0
and
u(0,t)=u(l,t)=0
.

Continuity:
Let
\hat{p}_{n}\to\hat{p}
in
L^{\infty}([0,T])
(in particular in C). By continuous dependence of solutions (Theorem
3.1
),
u_{\hat{p}_{n}}\to u_{\hat{p}}
in
C([0,T];L^{2})
. Uniform convergence of all terms implies
\mathcal{A}\hat{p}_{n}\to\mathcal{A}\hat{p}
.

Compactness:
Fix
P>0
and let
B_{R}=\left\{\hat{p}\in L^{\infty}:\|\hat{p}\|_{\infty}\leq P\right\}
. By Theorem
3.2
, there exists
C_{0}
depending only on
\varphi
,
f
,
l
, and
T
(but independent of
\hat{p}\in B_{R}
) such that

\|u_{\hat{p}}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{0},\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],\quad\|(u_{\hat{p}})_{t}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2})}\leq C_{1}.

Therefore, for all
\hat{p}\in B_{R}
,

\|\mathcal{A}\hat{p}(t)\|_{C([0,T])}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{t\in[0,T]}|g(t)|}\left(\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{l}f\omega dx\right|+\|\omega^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\cdot C_{0}\right):=M,

and the family
\left\{\mathcal{A}\hat{p}:\hat{p}\in B_{R}\right\}
is equicontinuous and uniformly bounded in
C([0,T])
. So
\mathcal{A}(B_{R})
is relatively compact by Arzelà-Ascoli.

Invariance:
Define
K=\{p\in C([0,T]):\|p\|_{\infty}\leq P\}
.
Then
K
is nonempty, closed, bounded, convex in the Banach space

C([0,T])
, and by the bound above,
\mathcal{A}(K)\subseteq K
.

By Schauder’s fixed-point theorem,
\mathcal{A}
has a fixed point
p\in K\subset C([0,T])
. The pair
\{p,u_{p}\}
satisfies (
1.1
)–(
1.4
).
∎

Theorem 3.4

(Uniqueness of the Solution)
.

Under assumptions (1)–(4), the solution pair
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}
to (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) is unique.

Proof.

Suppose
\{p,u\}
and
\{\tilde{p},\tilde{u}\}
are solution pairs. Define

U(x,t):=u(x,t)-\tilde{u}(x,t),\quad P(t):=p(t)-\tilde{p}(t).

Subtracting the two PDEs gives:

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=U_{xx}-p(t)U-P(t)\tilde{u}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in Q,

(3.6)

\displaystyle U(x,0)

\displaystyle=0,\quad x\in[0,l],

\displaystyle U(0,t)

\displaystyle=U(l,t)=0,\quad t\in[0,T],

Differentiate (
1.4
) for each solution
(u,\tilde{u})
with respect to
t
:

\int_{0}^{l}u_{t}\,\,\omega\,\,dx=g^{\prime}(t),\quad\int_{0}^{l}\tilde{u}_{t}\,\,\omega\,\ dx=g^{\prime}(t),\,\,t\in[0,T],

with

from (

).

Substitute the PDE (

) into these expressions. Using integration by parts and the boundary conditions

\omega(0)=\omega(l)=0
, we obtain:

g^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{l}u\omega_{xx}dx-pg(t)+\int_{0}^{l}f\omega dx,\\

g^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{l}\tilde{u}\omega_{xx}dx-\tilde{p}g(t)+\int_{0}^{l}f\omega dx.\\

Subtracting the two identities yields

P(t)g(t)=\int_{0}^{l}U\omega_{xx}dx.

(3.7)

Since
g(t)\neq 0
on

, we can write

P(t)=\frac{\int_{0}^{l}U\omega_{xx}dx}{g(t)}.

(3.8)

Consider the energy functional
E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{l}|U|^{2}dx
. Differentiate and substitute (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle=Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}\,\,U_{t}dx

\displaystyle=Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}[U_{xx}-p(t)U-P(t)\tilde{u}]dx

\displaystyle=Re\underbrace{\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}U_{xx}dx}_{I_{1}}-p(t)\underbrace{\int_{0}^{l}|U|^{2}dx}_{I_{2}}-P(t)Re\underbrace{\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}\tilde{u}dx}_{I_{3}}.

For

Thus:

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-\|U_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-p(t)\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-P(t)Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}\tilde{u}dx.

Since
-\|U_{x}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 0

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq-p(t)\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-P(t)Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}\tilde{u}dx.

From (
3.8

|P(t)|\leq\frac{1}{|g(t)|}\left|\int_{0}^{l}U\omega_{xx}dx\right|\leq\frac{\|\omega_{xx}\|_{L^{2}}}{|g(t)|}\|U\|_{L^{2}}.

Define the following constants:

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\tilde{u}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}<\infty\quad\text{(since $\tilde{u}\in C([0,T];H^{2}(0,l)\cap H_{0}^{1}(0,l))$ by Theorem \ref{thm:direct-wellposed})}

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle:=\|\omega_{xx}\|_{L^{2}}<\infty\quad\text{(since $\omega\in C^{2}([0,l])$ by assumption (4))}

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle:=\min_{t\in[0,T]}|g(t)|>0\quad\text{(since $g\in C^{1}([0,T])$ and $g(t)\neq 0$ by assumption (4))}.

Then:

|P(t)|\leq\frac{M_{2}}{m}\|U\|_{L^{2}}.

(3.10)

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz to

\left|Re\int_{0}^{l}\bar{U}\tilde{u}dx\right|\leq\|U\|_{L^{2}}\|\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq M_{1}\|U\|_{L^{2}}.

(3.11)

Substitute (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle\leq-p(t)\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|P(t)|M_{1}\|U\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-p(t)\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left(\frac{M_{2}}{m}\|U\|_{L^{2}}\right)M_{1}\|U\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(-p(t)+\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m}\right)\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\|U\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=2E(t)
, we obtain:

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq 2\left(-p(t)+\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m}\right)E(t).

Let
K:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|-p(t)+\frac{M_{1}M_{2}}{m}\right|<\infty
(finite because

by assumption (1), and
M_{1},M_{2},m
are finite with

). Then:

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)\leq 2KE(t).

(3.12)

Since
E(0)=0
, Grönwall’s inequality applied to (

) implies:

E(t)=0\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Hence,
U=0
for all

, so

.
Substituting
U=0
into (

) gives:

P(t)g(t)=0\implies P(t)=0\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

Since
g(t)\neq 0
, thus,

, proving uniqueness.
∎

4.1.
Stability of the implicit difference schemes

To justify the proposed algorithm, we will derive estimates of the stability of schemes (
4.1
) and (

) concerning the initial data and the right-hand side.

Theorem 4.1
.

(Stability of the Finite Difference Scheme).
Suppose
\{u_{i}^{k}\mid 0\leq i\leq N,0\leq k\leq M\}
is the solution of the finite difference scheme (
4.2
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions (
4.3
). Assume the coefficient
p^{k+1}>0

for all
k.
Then, the scheme is
unconditionally stable
in the maximum norm (
L^{\infty}
), meaning for any time step
\tau>0
and mesh size
h>0
, the solution satisfies the stability estimate:

\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u^{k}\|_{\infty}+\tau\|f^{k+1/2}\|_{\infty},\quad 0\leq k\leq M-1,

where
\|u^{k}\|_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq i\leq N}|u_{i}^{k}|
and
\|f^{k+1/2}\|_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq i\leq N}\left|\frac{1}{2}(f_{i}^{k+1}+f_{i}^{k})\right|
.

Corollary
(Stability for Homogeneous Case). If
f\equiv 0
, the solution is non-increasing in time:

\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u^{k}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

At interior points
i=1,…,N-1
, the scheme is:

(1+\frac{\tau}{h^{2}}+\tau p^{k+1})u_{i}^{k+1}-\frac{\tau}{2h^{2}}(u_{i+1}^{k+1}+u_{i-1}^{k+1})=(1-\frac{\tau}{h^{2}})u_{i}^{k}+\frac{\tau}{2h^{2}}(u_{i+1}^{k}+u_{i-1}^{k})+\tau f_{i}^{k+1/2},

where

f_{i}^{k+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}(f_{i}^{k+1}+f_{i}^{k}).

By (
4.3
),
u_{0}^{k}=u_{N}^{k}=0
for all
k
. Thus, the maximum norm
\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}=\max_{0\leq i\leq N}|u_{i}^{k+1}|

is attained at an interior point
j

Let
\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}=|u_{j}^{k+1}|
. At
i=j
, take absolute values and apply the triangle inequality:

(1+\frac{\tau}{h^{2}}+\tau p^{k+1})|u_{j}^{k+1}|-\frac{\tau}{2h^{2}}(|u_{j+1}^{k+1}|+|u_{j-1}^{k+1}|)\leq(1-\frac{\tau}{h^{2}})|u_{j}^{k}|+\frac{\tau}{2h^{2}}(|u_{j+1}^{k}|+|u_{j-1}^{k}|)+\tau|f_{j}^{k+1/2}|.

Since
|u_{j\pm 1}^{k+1}|\leq\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}=|u_{j}^{k+1}|
and
|u_{j\pm 1}^{k}|\leq\|u^{k}\|_{\infty}=|u_{j}^{k}|
, we have:

(1+\frac{\tau}{h^{2}}+\tau p^{k+1})|u_{j}^{k+1}|-\frac{\tau}{h^{2}}|u_{j}^{k+1}|\leq(1-\frac{\tau}{h^{2}})|u_{j}^{k}|+\frac{\tau}{h^{2}}|u_{j}^{k}|+\tau|f_{j}^{k+1/2}|.

We can simplify this as

(1+\tau p^{k+1})|u_{j}^{k+1}|\leq|u_{j}^{k}|+\tau|f_{j}^{k+1/2}|.

Since
p^{k+1}>0
,
\frac{1}{(1+\tau p^{k+1})}\leq 1
and
\frac{\tau}{1+\tau p^{k+1}}\leq\tau
, we have:

|u_{j}^{k+1}|\leq|u_{j}^{k}|+\tau|f_{j}^{k+1/2}|.

Taking the maximum over all
i
:

\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u^{k}\|_{\infty}+\tau\|f^{k+1/2}\|_{\infty}.

For homogeneous cases
f\equiv 0
, the solution satisfies:

\|u^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u^{k}\|_{\infty},

which shows that the scheme is unconditionally stable.
∎

4.2.
Convergence Analysis

We are now ready to show the convergence of the difference scheme (
4.2
).

Theorem 4.2
.

(Convergence).
Let
u(x,t)
be the exact solution of the PDE with sufficient smoothness, and
\{u_{i}^{k}\}
be the numerical solution of the finite difference scheme (
4.2
). Assume the truncation error is
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}+h^{2})
. Then, under the stability result of Theorem
4.1
, the numerical solution converges to the exact solution with the error bound:

\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq C\left(\tau^{2}+h^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq k\leq M,

where
e_{i}^{k}=u(x_{i},t_{k})-u_{i}^{k}
, and
C>0
is a constant independent of
\tau
and
h
.

Proof.

Let define the error
e_{i}^{k}=u(x_{i},t_{k})-u_{i}^{k}
. Substituting
u(x_{i},t_{k})
into the scheme (
4.2
), the error satisfies:

\frac{e_{i}^{k+1}-e_{i}^{k}}{\tau}-\frac{e_{i+1}^{k+1}-2e_{i}^{k+1}+e_{i-1}^{k+1}+e_{i+1}^{k}-2e_{i}^{k}+e_{i-1}^{k}}{2h^{2}}+p^{k+1}e_{i}^{k+1}=T_{i}^{k+1/2},

where
T_{i}^{k+1/2}
is the truncation error at time
t_{k+1/2}=t_{k}+\tau/2
.

Using Taylor expansions, the Crank-Nicolson approximation:
\frac{u_{i}^{k+1}-u_{i}^{k}}{\tau}
has a truncation error of
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
. The central difference approximation

\frac{u_{i+1}^{k+1}-2u_{i}^{k+1}+u_{i-1}^{k+1}+u_{i+1}^{k}-2u_{i}^{k}+u_{i-1}^{k}}{2h^{2}}

has a truncation error of
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
.
And the reaction and source terms: The
p^{k+1}u_{i}^{k+1}
and
\frac{1}{2}(f_{i}^{k+1}+f_{i}^{k})
introduce no additional error beyond
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}+h^{2})
.
Thus, the truncation error satisfies:

\|T^{k+1/2}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{T}(\tau^{2}+h^{2}),

where
C_{T}>0
is a constant.

From Theorem
4.1
we know that the error equation inherits the stability of the original scheme:

\|e^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}+\tau\|T^{k+1/2}\|_{\infty}.

Substituting the truncation error bound, we get

\|e^{k+1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}+\tau C_{T}(\tau^{2}+h^{2}).

Iterating over time steps yields

\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\|e^{0}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{m=1}^{k}\tau C_{T}(\tau^{2}+h^{2}).

Since
\|e^{0}\|_{\infty}=0
(assuming exact initial conditions), and
\sum_{m=1}^{k}\tau=t_{k}\leq T
, we have

\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{T}T(\tau^{2}+h^{2}).

Combining results, we obtain

\|e^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq C(\tau^{2}+h^{2}),

where
C=C_{T}T
.
∎

5.
Numerical Solution of the Inverse Problem

This section presents and compares three numerical methods for simultaneously identifying the time-dependent coefficient
p(t)
and computing the solution
u(x,t)
of the inverse problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
). The first method, referred to as the integration-based approach, is a classical technique relying on analytical manipulation of the overdetermination condition. The second is a Newton–Raphson-based iterative method that refines
p(t)
by minimising the residual in the integral condition. Finally, we present a third approach based on the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework, which enables mesh-free learning of both
u(x,t)
and
p(t)
by embedding the governing PDE into the training process.

The three approaches are implemented independently and compared through numerical experiments. All numerical examples are conducted on the domain
[0,1]\times[0,T]
, and the results are benchmarked against an exact analytical solution to validate accuracy.

5.1.
Integration-based approach for identification of
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}

We first consider a classical approach that leverages the overdetermination condition (
1.4
) to derive an explicit formula for the unknown coefficient
p(t)
, assuming that the solution
u(x,t)
is available at each time step via numerical simulation of the direct problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).

Differentiating the integral condition (
1.4
) with respect to time yields:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{0}^{l}u(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx\right)=\int_{0}^{l}u_{t}(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx=g^{\prime}(t),

(5.1)

Substituting the PDE (
1.1
) into (
5.1
) gives:

g^{\prime}(t)=\int_{0}^{l}\left(u_{xx}(x,t)-p(t)\,u(x,t)+f(x,t)\right)\omega(x)\,dx.

We now integrate the
u_{xx}
term by parts twice:

\int_{0}^{l}u_{xx}(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx=\left[u_{x}\omega\right]_{0}^{l}-\left[u\omega^{\prime}\right]_{0}^{l}+\int_{0}^{l}u(x,t)\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx.

Since
u(0,t)=u(l,t)=0
and
\omega(0)=\omega(l)=0
, the second boundary term vanishes, and we obtain:

\int_{0}^{l}u_{xx}(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{l}u(x,t)\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx.

Substituting back, and isolating
p(t)
, we arrive at the reconstruction formula:

p(t)=\frac{\int_{0}^{l}u(x,t)\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{l}f(x,t)\,\omega(x)\,dx-g^{\prime}(t)}{g(t)}.

(5.2)

Here, we have assumed in assumption (4) that
g(t)\neq 0
.
This expression allows us to recover
p(t)
pointwise at each time level after computing the numerical solution
u(x,t)
of the direct problem.

The full computational procedure is summarised in Algorithm
1
, where we use the Crank–Nicolson scheme described in Section

to compute

.

5.2.
Newton–Raphson approach for identification of
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}

We now describe a more accurate and iterative method for identifying the time-dependent coefficient
p(t)
based on minimising the discrepancy in the overdetermination condition (
1.4
). At each time step
t^{k+1}
, the unknown value
p^{k+1}
is computed by solving the nonlinear equation

F(p^{k+1}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}u_{i}^{k+1}(p^{k+1})\,\omega(x_{i})\,h-g(t^{k+1})=0,

(5.3)

where
u_{i}^{k+1}(p^{k+1})
denotes the discrete numerical solution of the direct problem at time
t^{k+1}
computed using scheme (
4.2
) with current guess for
p^{k+1}
.

5.3

p^{k+1,(0)}
, the update rule is:

p^{k+1,(j+1)}=p^{k+1,(j)}-\frac{F(p^{k+1,(j)})}{F^{\prime}(p^{k+1,(j)})},

(5.4)

Remark 5.1
.

The Newton-Raphson algorithm benefits from several advantageous features. First, both the forward and sensitivity problems share the same tridiagonal matrix structure, allowing for the reuse of efficient solvers such as the Thomas algorithm. Each iteration therefore, has linear complexity
\mathcal{O}(N)
, where
N
is the number of spatial discretisation points, making the method computationally efficient.

Moreover, the implicit time discretisation contributes to the scheme’s numerical stability without requiring additional regularisation. The residual
\|F(p^{k+1})\|
, evaluated at each iteration, serves as both a stopping criterion and a diagnostic tool for monitoring convergence and solution quality.

5.3.
Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) approach for identification of
\{p(t),u(x,t)\}

As a modern alternative to classical numerical schemes, we propose a physics-informed neural network (PINN) approach for the simultaneous identification of the time-dependent coefficient
p(t)
and the solution
u(x,t)
to the inverse problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
). In this method, the unknowns are approximated by neural networks trained to satisfy the governing PDE, initial and boundary conditions, and the integral overdetermination condition.

Neural network representation.

We represent the solution
u(x,t)
by a trial function of the form:

u_{\theta}(x,t)=(1-t)\,\varphi(x)+x(1-x)\,t\,N_{\theta}(x,t),

(5.7)

where
N_{\theta}(x,t)
is a feedforward neural network with parameters
\theta
. This construction ensures that the initial and boundary conditions,

u_{\theta}(x,0)=\varphi(x),\quad u_{\theta}(0,t)=u_{\theta}(1,t)=0,

are exactly satisfied for all
(x,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,T]
.

The unknown coefficient
p(t)
is approximated by a second neural network
p_{\eta}(t)
, parameterised by weights
\eta
. The two networks are trained simultaneously using a composite loss functional based on the residuals of the PDE and the integral constraint.

Loss formulation.

Let
\{(x_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\subset(0,1)\times(0,T)
be interior collocation points used to enforce the PDE. The residual loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PDE}}=\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{f}}\left(\frac{\partial u_{\theta}}{\partial t}(x_{i},t_{i})-\frac{\partial^{2}u_{\theta}}{\partial x^{2}}(x_{i},t_{i})+p_{\eta}(t_{i})\,u_{\theta}(x_{i},t_{i})-f(x_{i},t_{i})\right)^{2}.

(5.8)

To enforce the integral overdetermination condition (
1.4
), we define the constraint loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{1}{N_{g}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{g}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}u_{\theta}(x,t_{j})\,\omega(x)\,dx-g(t_{j})\right)^{2},

(5.9)

where
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{g}}\subset[0,T]
are selected quadrature nodes, and the integral is approximated using a numerical quadrature rule (e.g., trapezoidal or Simpson’s).

The total loss functional is:

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\eta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PDE}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}\,\,,

(5.10)

where
\lambda>0
is a penalty parameter controlling the weight of the constraint.

Neural network architecture.

The solution network
N_{\theta}(x,t)
consists of 2 hidden layers with 64 neurons each and hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activations. The coefficient network
p_{\eta}(t)
uses 2 hidden layers with 32 neurons each, also using tanh activations. The networks are trained jointly using a two-phase strategy: an initial training phase using the Adam optimiser to explore the solution landscape, followed by a second phase employing the L-BFGS optimiser to refine convergence with high accuracy.

u_{\theta}(x,t)
, the coefficient network
p_{\eta}(t)
, and their respective outputs—is depicted in Figure
1
.

Advantages.

The PINN framework offers several advantages: it produces smooth approximations of both the solution and the coefficient, handles sparse or noisy data naturally, and is mesh-free. Moreover, it provides a continuous representation of
u(x,t)
and
p(t)
that can be evaluated at arbitrary points in the domain.

Remark 5.2
.

The key challenge in PINNs for inverse problems lies in balancing the different terms in the loss function. In our implementation, the penalty parameter
\lambda
was chosen empirically to ensure that both the PDE residual and the constraint are well enforced. Additionally, automatic differentiation is used to evaluate all derivatives appearing in the loss terms.

6.
Numerical Experiments

To validate the proposed methodologies and assess their accuracy and convergence properties, we present a set of numerical experiments. We consider a manufactured solution to the inverse problem (
1.1
)–(
1.4
), which allows for direct comparison against known ground truth. Two classical methods–the integration-based approach and the Newton-Raphson-based approach–are compared with the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) strategy. And to assess the robustness of these methods, we tested them using noisy integral data
g(t)
and its time derivative.

Test problem with known solution.

We consider the inverse problem on the domain
Q=(0,1)\times(0,1)
, with the exact solution and coefficient given by:

u(x,t)=e^{t}\sin(\pi x),\qquad p(t)=e^{-t}.

From this, the source function
f(x,t)
and the overdetermination data
g(t)
are computed accordingly:

f(x,t)=\sin(\pi x)(1+\pi^{2}e^{t}+e^{t}),\qquad\omega(x)=\sin(\pi x),\qquad g(t)=\frac{1}{2}e^{t}.

This problem satisfies the assumptions of the theoretical framework and provides a smooth benchmark for verifying accuracy.

Error metrics.

To evaluate the accuracy of the numerical solutions, we use both maximum and
L^{2}
norm errors:

\displaystyle\text{Max error in }u(x,t):\quad

\displaystyle Er(u)_{N,M}:=\max_{0\leq i\leq N}|u_{i}^{M}-U_{i}^{M}|,

\displaystyle\text{Max error in }p(t):\quad

\displaystyle Er(p)_{M}:=\max_{0\leq k\leq M}|p^{k}-P^{k}|,

\displaystyle\text{$L^{2}$ error in }u(x,t):\quad

\displaystyle E(u)^{(2)}_{N,M}:=\sqrt{h\sum_{i=0}^{N}(u_{i}^{M}-U_{i}^{M})^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{$L^{2}$ error in }p(t):\quad

\displaystyle E(p)^{(2)}_{M}:=\sqrt{\tau\sum_{k=0}^{M}(p^{k}-P^{k})^{2}},

where
(U_{i}^{M},P^{k})
denote the exact solutions and
(u_{i}^{M},p^{k})
the numerically computed approximations.

6.4.
Discussion

The results clearly show that the Newton–Raphson method delivers the highest precision for both state and parameter recovery. The integration method is simpler and robust, but its accuracy is limited due to numerical differentiation of
g(t)
. The PINN approach offers a flexible and mesh-free alternative, producing continuous approximations and performing reasonably well with a limited number of training points.

Remark 6.1
.

In all cases, the convergence behavior of the schemes aligns with the theoretical order of accuracy, and the residual errors confirm both stability and robustness of the proposed methods.

7.
Conclusion

We studied the inverse problem of identifying a time‐dependent potential
p(t)
and the state
u(x,t)
in a 1D diffusion equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions and an integral overdetermination constraint. After establishing existence and uniqueness via Schauder’s theorem, we implemented three numerical approaches:

•

Integration-based:
Simple to implement, stable under 1–5 % noise, but sensitive to grid resolution and derivative approximation.

•

Newton–Raphson:
Fastest and most accurate on noise-free data, yet unstable even at 1 % noise.

•

PINNs:
Mesh-free and physics-regularised, providing smooth, noise-robust reconstructions (up to 5 % noise) at the cost of longer training and careful hyperparameter tuning.

Overall, classical methods remain highly efficient for low-dimensional, low-noise settings, while PINNs offer a flexible, robust alternative for noisy or higher-dimensional problems. Future work will explore hybrid schemes that combine the speed and accuracy of classical solvers with the noise resilience and mesh independence of PINNs.
