Oncology safety of proximal gastrectomy for advanced Siewert II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction compared with total gastrectomy: a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Qiying Song, Di Wu, Shihe Liu, Ziyao Xu, Yixun Lu, Xinxin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe safety of proximal gastrectomy (PG) for the treatment of advanced Siewert II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) remains debatable. In this study, we aim to evaluate the oncological safety of PG and the metastasis rate of key distal lymph node dissection, which is typically excluded in PG.MethodThis study retrospective collected advanced Siewert II AEG patients who underwent gastrectomy at the First Medical Center of the General Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) from January 2014 to December 2019. A total of 421 patients were enrolled, including 237 PG and 184 total gastrectomy (TG). Propensity score matching (PSM) in a 1:1 ratio was performed to reduce the influence of confounding variables.ResultsAfter PSM, 153 cases were matched in each group. The TG group had longer operation time, more lymph node detection and longer postoperative hospitalization time than the PG group (Both P < 0.05). The postoperative complications of the two groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). For long-term complications, the incidence of reflux esophagitis and anastomotic stenosis were significantly higher in the PG group than in the TG group (Both P < 0.05), but dumpling syndrome and anemia were significantly lower in the PG group compared to the TG group (Both P < 0.05). The 3-year overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) between the two groups were no statistically significant difference (OS: 77.4% and 80.9%, P = 0.223; DFS: 69.7% and 76.1%, P = 0.063). Distal lymph node metastasis rates for No.4d, 5, and 6 were all less than 5%, and the therapeutic value index were also relatively low, with values of 1.09%, 3.26%, and 1.45%, respectively. In addition, the distal No.4d, 5, or No.6 lymph node metastasis rates were significantly higher in patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm and T4 stage (14.29% and 23.40%) than in patients with tumor size < 4 cm and T2-3 (2.78% and 5.11%) (Both P < 0.05). The results of subgroup survival analysis showed that for patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage, the TG group had better DFS compared with the PG group (HR 0.618, 0.387–0.987, P = 0.044), while no significant survival benefits were observed in other subgroups.ConclusionIn summary, for Siewert II AEG with tumor size < 4 cm and T2-3 stage, PG may be a reasonable choice with comparable oncological efficacy to TG. But for higher survival benefits, TG remains gold standard particularly for patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-024-03592-2.Keywords: Adenocarcinoma of esophagogastric junction, Proximal gastrectomy, Total gastrectomy, Siewert II

Introduction

In recent decades, the incidence rate of esophageal cancer and gastric cancer has declined rapidly, but the incidence rate of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) has continued to rise [1]. AEG crosses the esophagogastric junction (EGJ), with the tumor center located within 5 cm of the EGJ line [2]. According to the widely used Siewert classification [3], Type I AEG defines adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus, with the tumor center located within 1–5 cm above the EGJ. Type II is a true “cardia cancer” located within a range of 1 cm above and 2 cm below the EGJ; Type III is located 2–5 cm below the EGJ [4]. In the past 30 years, the prevalence of AEG has rapidly increased in both Asia and Western countries [5–7]. Recently, a study showed that in the past 25 years, the incidence rate of AEG in China has gradually increased from 22.3% to 35.7% [8]. Therefore, there has been an increase in research on understanding and treating AEG.

The best treatment for advanced gastric cancer is radical gastrectomy combined with lymphadenectomy [9]. However, due to the unique anatomical location and biological behavior of Siewert II AEG, the optimal range for Siewert II AEG gastrectomy remains controversial, especially in terms of oncological safety for proximal gastrectomy (PG) in advanced AEG patients. According to the Japanese GC treatment guidelines, total gastrectomy (TG) is the standard surgical method for positive lymph node (cN +) and T2-T4a EGJ and upper one-third gastric cancer in clinical practice [4]. In addition, PG (PG) is only suitable for proximal cT1N0-GC or some early or advanced EGJ cancers ≤ 4 cm, where more than half of the distal stomach can be preserved [4]. That is to say, patients with advanced proximal AEG should receive TG treatment. However, recent increasing evidence suggests that locally advanced proximal GC does not always require TG treatment, and PG and TG treatment for proximal GC have similar survival outcomes [10, 11]. Previous studies have shown that in proximal gastric cancer patients, the lymph node metastasis rates in No. 5, 6, and 4 are lower, and PG is safe and feasible while preserving some of the gastric body, postoperative nutritional status and quality of life are better than TG [12–14]. However, compared to TG, PG reduces the range of lymph node dissection around the stomach, which may increase the risk of tumor recurrence [15, 16].

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to retrospectively analysis the clinicopathologic data of advanced Siewert II AEG patients, evaluate the oncological safety of PG and access the metastasis rate of critical distal lymph node dissection, which is usually excluded from PG.

Method

Patients and study design

We retrospectively collected clinicopathological data of advanced Siewert II AEG patients who underwent potential radical gastrectomy at the First Medical Center of the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital from January 2014 to December 2019. This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2024-263–01). As a retrospective study and the informed consent has been waived. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years, of any sex; (2) Postoperative pathological diagnosis of Siewert II AEG; (3) Received R0 PG or TG (TG);(4) According to the International Union for Gastric Cancer (UICC) 8th edition, the pathological TNM staging is T2 ~ 4N0 ~ 3M0 (According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM staging system [4]). Exclusion criteria: (1) Distant metastasis (M1); (2) combined with other malignant tumors; (3) History of esophageal and gastric surgery (including endoscopic surgery); (4) ASA > 3; (5) Only exploratory surgery and palliative surgery are performed; (6) Insufficient information regarding perigastric station lymph node metastasis status; (7) Preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy; (8) Missing data.

Surgical methods

The patients were divided into PG and TG groups according to the surgical types recorded in the patient’s medical information. The PG group underwent proximal gastrectomy and D1 + or D2 lymphadenectomy, with lymphadenectomy covering groups 1, 2, 3, 4sa, 4sb, 7, 8a, 9, 11p, or 11p and 10. The TG group underwent total gastrectomy and D2 lymph node dissection, with lymph nodes including groups No.1–7, 8a,9,11p, 11d, and 12a [9]. The PG group underwent esophagogastrostomy, tube-type gastroesophageal anastomosis or double tract reconstruction, while the TG group underwent Roux-en-Y anastomosis for digestive tract reconstruction.

Observation indicators

(1) General information: Sex, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history, physical status according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) criteria, tumor size, histological grade, T stage, N stage, TNM stage, vascular invasion, nerve invasion. (2) Intraoperative and postoperative indicators: gastrectomy range, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, number of lymph node dissection, and postoperative hospital stay, therapeutic value index for lymph node dissection (lymph node metastasis rate * 5-year overall survival rate) [17]; (3) Postoperative complications include anastomotic bleeding, anastomotic fistula, intestinal obstruction, cardiovascular. Classification of postoperative complications using the Clavien-Dindo grading system [18]. (4) Long-term prognosis: reflux esophagitis, anastomotic stenosis, overall survival and Disease-free survival.

Follow-up will be conducted through outpatient and regular telephone. Every 3–6 months for the first 2 years after surgery, and every 6–12 months for the following 3 years. The last follow-up was in July 2021. Overall survival was defined as the period from the day of gastrectomy to the date of death or last follow-up. Disease-free survival was defined as the time interval from surgery to the first recurrence or death.

Statistical analysis

Use SPSS 26.0 statistical software for analysis. Students t-test with mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) or Mann–Whitney U test with median (interquartile range, IQR) were applied for continuous variable; The categorical variable was performed using χ2 test or Fisher test. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio with 0.01 caliper value to reduce the influence of confounding variables such as age, sex, tumor size, T stage, N stage. Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test were used to estimate the long-term survival. Univariate Cox regression analysis and multivariate Cox analysis were used to evaluate the risk factors between OS and DFS. P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference.

Results

Figure 1 shows the flowchart of this study. A total of 421 Siewert II AEG patients were included, including 369 males and 52 females. Age ranged from 31 to 84 years old. According to the gastrectomy range, they were divided into the PG group (n = 237) and the TG group (n = 184). The clinical characteristics of the two groups were shown in Table 1. The TG group before PSM had later staging (Both P < 0.05). After PSM using age, sex, tumor size, T stage, N stage and TNM stage, 153 patients were matched in each group. There was no statistically significant difference in characteristic features between the two groups of patients after PSM, including sex, age, BMI, ASA score, tumor size, histological grade, T stage, N stage, TNM stage, vascular invasion, nerve invasion and postoperative chemotherapy (P > 0.05) (Table 1). After surgery, there were slightly more patients in the TG group receiving adjuvant chemotherapy, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.143, Fisher’s exact test).

Survival

Among 306 patients with advanced Siewert II AEG after PSM, the overall 3-year survival rates of PG and TG were 77.4% and 80.9%, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (HR = 0.763, 95% CI = 0.495–1.178, P = 0.223, Fig. 2A). The 3-year Disease-free survival rates of the PG group and TG group were 69.7% and 76.1%, respectively, and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (HR = 0.677, 95% CI = 0.448–1.021, P = 0.063, Fig. 2B). Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that larger tumor size, lower T stage, and lower N stage were significantly associated with poorer OS, while lower N stage and larger tumor size were associated with poorer DFS (Both P < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1A, 2A). Multivariate Cox regression analysis suggested that N stage was an independent prognostic factor for both OS and DFS (Both P < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1B, 2B).

Discussion

The incidence rate of AEG is on the rise worldwide, while Siewert II and type III AEG are common in Asia [1, 6, 8]. Due to their different anatomical locations, appropriate surgical resection ranges and corresponding reconstruction methods about different subtypes of AEG have caused tremendous controversy and discussion. A reasonable surgical option may should meet the following requirements: (1) complete resection of the primary tumor; (2) Adequate lymph node dissection; (3) The safety of oncology, i.e. no residual cancer at the cutting edge; (4) Reduce surgical trauma, reduce postoperative complications, and achieve rapid postoperative recovery; (5) Beneficial for the long-term survival rate of patients after surgery and reducing the recurrence and metastasis of tumors. For Siewert I AEG, due to the higher anatomical plane, transthoracic esophagectomy and PG are required, followed by residual gastroesophageal anastomosis reconstruction, which is currently undisputed. Due to the different anatomical locations and clinical stages of Siewert II AEG patients, as well as the complex lymphatic drainage pathway, the optimal range of gastrectomy has not yet been determined, and there is still controversy.

This study mainly focused on the advanced Siewert II AEG, evaluated the oncological safety of PG and assessed the metastasis rate of key distal lymph node dissection that is typically excluded in PG. Use PSM to balance baseline features between two groups. In the patient data after PSM, the results showed that the operation time in the PG group was significantly shorter than that in the TG group, which may be related to the larger surgical scope in the TG group. At the same time, Roux-en-Y anastomosis was performed on TG, which is more complex than the anastomosis of the lower esophagus and residual stomach. Therefore, the surgery time in TG is longer than that in PG, which is consistent with most research results [19, 20]. In terms of long-term prognosis, there was no statistically significant difference between the two surgical methods in terms of postoperative survival rate, 3-year OS, and DFS. This is consistent with previous research findings [21–23].

After PG, distant lymph node metastasis and remnant gastric cancer are the biggest issues in terms of tumor safety. Because if the tumor is in the progression stage, the risk of both problems will increase. In our study, multivariate Cox regression analysis suggested that N stage was an independent prognostic factor for both OS and DFS (Both P < 0.05). A preoperative comprehensive evaluation of the tumor and intraoperative pathological examination of frozen sections are important measures to avoid tumor residue. The Lymph node dissection is a key focus of surgical procedures, and the results of this study indicate that the TG group has more lymph node dissection than the PG group (P < 0.05). This discovery is similar to most published studies and also aligns with clinical surgical experience [19, 21]. Lymph node dissection requires an increased surgical range, which increases the risk of surgical trauma and complications, and puts a greater burden on the patient’s body. In addition, there are significant differences in the tumor biological characteristics of AEG, such as the degree of tumor differentiation, depth of invasion, and tumor size. These characteristics affect the manner and extent of lymph node metastasis, thereby affecting the effectiveness of lymph node dissection. M Sasako et al. proposed calculating the lymph node dissection benefit index based on lymph node metastasis rate and 5-year survival rate to evaluate the necessity of lymph node dissection at each station, which can provide a reference for tumor resection range [17]. Furthermore, Takaki Yoshikawa et al. included 381 Siewert IIAEG, and the results showed that the lymph nodes with the highest benefit index were in groups 1, 2, 3, and 7. The lymph node metastasis rate in groups 4, 5, and 6 was less than 3%. Siewert II AEG is feasible for PG. A prospective study by Kazumasa Fujitani et al. on 86 patients with advanced Siewert II AEG who underwent TG found that the lymph node metastasis rates in groups No.4d, 5, and 6 were very low. Therefore, it was proposed that PG is an optional treatment option for advanced Siewert II AEG [11]. Meanwhile, Sato et al. included 51 clinically advanced Siewert II and III AEG patients, and the results showed a lymph node metastasis rate of 0 in groups 4, 5, and 6. Gastric invasion length exceeding 40 mm was an important risk factor for 3b LN metastasis. The study recommended that AEG patients with gastric cancer invasion length less than 40 mm should undergo radical PG [24]. Masahiro Yura et al. included 202 patients with T2/T3 upper 1/3 gastric cancer who underwent TG. The metastasis rates for No.4d and No. 12a were very low (0.99% and 0.006%, respectively), while the metastasis rates for No.5 and No.6 were zero. The treatment index for No. 4d, No. 5, No. 6, and No. 12a was zero. The study found that PG is a tumor safe method for T2/T3 proximal gastric cancer [10]. Rui Peng et al. analyzed the tumor safety of PG treatment for advanced proximal gastric cancer, indicating no significant difference in OS between TG and PG. In patients with pathological T2/3 tumors or Borrmann I/II tumors, no metastasis was observed at LN stations 5 or 6 when the tumor size was ≤ 4 cm [25]. A prospective multicenter study in Japan showed that the optimal lymph node dissection range for Siewert II and III AEG can be determined based on metastasis rate. According to the metastasis rate, lymph nodes are divided into three categories, as follows: Type 1 (strongly recommended for dissection), with a metastasis rate > 10%; Type 2 (weakly recommended cleaning), with a transfer rate of 5% −10%; Category 3 (dissection not recommended), metastasis rate < 5% [26]. We analyzed the lymph node metastasis rates at various stations in 184 patients undergoing TG, the distal lymph node metastasis rates in No.4d, 5 and No.6 were all less than 5%. and the therapeutic value index was also relatively low, with values of 1.09%, 3.26%, and 1.45%, respectively. The correlation analysis between No.4d, 5 or 6 lymph node metastasis and clinicopathological characteristics showed that distal lymph node No.4d, 5 or No.6 metastasis rate was significantly higher in patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm and T4 stage (14.29% and 23.40%) than that in patients with tumor size < 4 cm and T2-3 (2.78% and 5.11%) (Both P < 0.05). The metastasis rate in patients with T2-3 stage and tumor size < 4 cm was significantly lower than that in size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage patients (1.69% vs 13.60%, P = 0.023). Therefore, we further analyzed the long-term survival outcomes of the PG and TG groups in tumor size and stage subgroup, no significant difference was observed in patients with size < 4 cm and T2-3 stage, while significantly better DFS were showed in TG group than PG group in patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage (HR = 0.618, 95%CI = 0.387–0.987, P = 0.044). Previous studies also showed that the depth of tumor invasion and lymph node metastasis were significantly associated with recurrence [27]. Thus, for Siewert II AEG with tumor size < 4 cm and T2-3 stage, PG may be a reasonable choice with comparable oncological efficacy to TG, but for patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage, TG should be chosen for higher safety. In addition, Francesco Belia et al. believe that new surgical techniques, such as real-time imaging using near-infrared fluorescence (NIF) and indocyanine green (ICG), may help determine surgical methods as they can detect metastatic lymph nodes with a false negative rate of 1% [28].

Optimizing surgical methods can not only improve prognosis but also contribute to improving the quality of life of patients after surgery [29, 30]. The results of our study showed that the incidence of dumpling syndrome and anemia were significantly lower in the PG group compared to the TG group (Both P < 0.05). Compared with TG, PG preserves the pylorus and some gastric glands, which can preserve some of the digestive and absorption functions of the stomach [31, 32]. A meta-analysis conducted by Tanioka et al. included 883 cancer patients from 11 studies. The results showed that compared with patients who underwent TG, patients who underwent PG had a significant increase in weight loss and hemoglobin levels one year after surgery [33]. According to previous studies, PG has significant advantages in maintaining long-term nutritional status, but the incidence of postoperative complications such as anastomotic stenosis and reflux esophagitis may be higher [19, 21, 23]. Fausto Rosa et al. included 457 patients with proximal gastric cancer, and there was no significant difference in the overall incidence of postoperative complications between the two groups, however, the incidence of reflux esophagitis and anastomotic stenosis after PG was significantly higher than that of TG (P < 0.001 and P = 0.002) [23]. Our study showed that there was no significant difference in perioperative complications between the two groups. However, in terms of long-term complications, the incidence of reflux esophagitis and anastomotic stenosis in the PG group was significantly higher than that in the TG group. The reason for this may be that there is a high-pressure area in the cardia, which can inhibit the reflux of gastric contents. PG surgery will remove the high-pressure area and preserve the pylorus, so reflux is prone to occur during gastric emptying, leading to reflux esophagitis and affecting the patient’s prognosis and recovery. The application of jejunal interposition and other digestive tract reconstruction techniques can effectively reduce the incidence of complications such as gastrointestinal reflux after PG, and promote the promotion and application of PG [34, 23]. In our study, 10 cases of tube-type gastroesophageal reconstruction in PG group did not experience reflux esophagitis, of which 1 case had anastomotic stenosis. No significant difference was observed in postoperative complications between tube-type gastroesophageal reconstruction and esophagogastrostomy. Comparison of complications with different reconstruction methods requires more sample size.

Research strengths: (1) This study adopted a propensity score matching method to reduce the influence of confounding variables and the results were more reliable; (2) This study only included the most controversial advanced Siewert II AEG, providing a theoretical basis for the rational selection of its surgical option; (3): This study estimated the oncology safety for PG, especially the rate of distant lymph node metastasis. However, this study still has some limitations: (1) It is a retrospective cohort study with some selection bias; (2) Lack of comparison of postoperative laboratory nutritional indicators for patients; (3) Comparison of complications with different reconstruction methods requires more sample size. In summary, to compare the efficacy of PG and TG in advanced Siewert II AEG, not only prospective randomized trials but also large-scale clinical data analysis are needed.

Conclusion

In summary, for Siewert II AEG with tumor size < 4 cm and T2-3 stage, PG may be a reasonable choice with comparable oncological efficacy to TG. But for higher survival benefits, TG remains gold standard particularly for patients with tumor size ≥ 4 cm or T4 stage.
