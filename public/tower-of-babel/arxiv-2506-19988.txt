Tipping Point Sensitivity Analysis for Missing Data in Time-to-Event Endpoints: Model-Based and Model-Free Approaches
Ajmal Oodally, Craig Wang, Zheng Li, Arunava Chakravartty

Abstract
Missing data frequently occurs in clinical trials with time-to-event endpoints, often due to administrative censoring. Other reasons, such as loss-to-follow up and patient withdrawal of consent, can violate the censoring-at-random assumption hence lead to biased estimates of the treatment effect under treatment policy estimand. Numerous methods have been proposed to conduct sensitivity analyses in these situations, one of which is the tipping point analysis. It aims to evaluate the robustness of trial conclusions by varying certain data and/or model aspects while imputing missing data. We provide an overview of the missing data considerations. The main contribution of this paper lies in categorizing and contrasting tipping point methods as two groups, namely model-based and model-free approaches, where the latter is under-emphasized in the literature. We highlight their important differences in terms of assumptions, behaviors and interpretations. Through two case studies and simulations under various scenarios, we provide insight on how different tipping point methods impact the interpretation of trial outcomes. Through these comparisons, we aim to provide a practical guide to conduct tipping point analyses from the choice of methods to ways of conducting clinical plausibility assessment, and ultimately contributing to more robust and reliable interpretation of clinical trial results in the presence of missing data for time-to-event endpoints.



Keywords
Time-to-event; tipping point analysis; multiple imputation; sensitivity analysis

1
Introduction

The integrity of clinical trial data is crucial for reliably informing medical decision-making and advancing lifesaving treatment options for patients around the world. However, a persistent challenge is the occurrence of missing data, which can undermine the validity and reliability of study findings, particularly when it occurs frequently or unevenly between experimental arms. The optimal strategy to minimize the impact of missing data is to reduce the probability of their occurrence early on in a trial through data query, compliance training and other operational measures (
Little et al. 2012
). However, despite these efforts, it is often not possible to ensure low levels of missingness.

When analyzing the clinical outcome of interest, missing data can be classified based on their missing mechanism into missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR) and missing not at random (MNAR). Data can be missing for several reasons, such as prematurely dropping out of study, having inadequate trial follow-up, or having inaccurate recording. The assessment and handling of missing data has attracted attention from health authorities and industry working groups. ICH E9 (R1) addendum states "Firstly, the addendum distinguishes discontinuation of randomised treatment from study withdrawal. The former represents an intercurrent event, to be addressed in the precise specification of the trial objective through the estimand. The latter gives rise to missing data to be addressed in the statistical analysis."

For time-to-event endpoints, these are usually caused by patient dropouts which then lead to two types of censoring: informative and non-informative. Non-informative censoring occurs when the reasons are unrelated to the study treatment or the outcome of interest. Examples of such censoring would be patients who remain event-free but still at the risk of an event at the time of study completion. In such cases, the risk of an event for such a patient is not expected to be different from the patients with longer follow up. Hence such cases of missingness do not impact the assumptions for treatment policy estimand estimated using standard methods such as Cox proportional hazard models (
Cox 1972
) and Kaplan-Meier method. Informative censoring, on the other hand, occurs when the reasons are related to the study treatment or the outcome of interest. It is important to note that these dropouts may be driven by various factors of clinical and patient preference. In the experimental arm, discontinuation could be influenced by treatment-related adverse events, lack of perceived efficacy, or patient burden. In contrast, in the control arm, factors such as disappointment at receiving standard of care or desire to switch to a more promising therapy may contribute to dropout. Understanding these underlying reasons through sufficient data collection is the first and essential step to appropriately interpret study results and ensure a comprehensive evaluation of treatment effects. By simply censoring these dropout patients, the estimated effect of the experimental treatment may be biased and raise concerns about the validity of trial conclusions. Informative censoring can lead to violation of the assumptions for estimating treatment policy estimand and thus requires additional consideration. One widely used approach to tackle the issue of informative censoring through sensitivity analysis is the tipping point analysis method. By systematically introducing progressively pessimistic assumptions for the experimental arm or optimistic assumptions for the control arm, this approach allows reviewers to explore scenarios under which the conclusions of a study may change—or "tip"
(
Lipkovich et al. 2016
)
. This structured sensitivity analysis provides insights into the stability of treatment effects and highlights the extent to which results depend on unverifiable assumptions about missing or censored data.

There is extensive literature on tipping point analyses that deal with missing data problems of continuous and binary endpoints (
Yan et al. 2009
,
Sui et al. 2023
,
Gorst-Rasmussen and Tarp-Johansen 2022
,
Jin et al. 2021
,
Liublinska and Rubin 2014
). Recently,
Fallah et al. 2024
summarized the challenges in the interpretation of treatment effect in open-label RCTs from an FDA point of view, mentioning multiple imputation and tipping point analyses as sensitivity analyses to explore the robustness of results under asymmetric censoring.
Torres et al. 2025
emphasized that in the context of regulatory review, sensitivity analyses should target the same estimand as the primary analysis while comprehensively explore a wide space of possible assumptions. While there are several methodological developments for time-to-event endpoints (
Atkinson et al. 2019
,
Lipkovich et al. 2016
), there is a lack of practical guidelines in the literature on the appropriateness of methods under different scenarios. We aim to fill this gap by diving into the root differences between various tipping point methods in the time-to-event setting, and link them with estimand thinking.

In this paper, we focus on patient dropout. This phenomenon may introduce bias in the estimation of the treatment effect under treatment policy estimand and hinder the accurate assessment of therapeutic interventions. Considering those data as missing at random (MAR) is often a strong and unrealistic assumption that can lead to incorrect conclusions. Health authorities such as the US FDA and EMA, guided by principles outlined in ICH guidelines, emphasize the importance of sensitivity analysis to address missing data issues in time-to-event trials and ensure the reliability of trial results for regulatory decision-making. In general, the more extreme the conditions required to tip a positive result are, the more robust that result would be.

In this paper, we address the challenge of informative dropout by proposing a practical guide to selecting appropriate tipping point analysis methods for testing the robustness of statistical results in time-to-event settings. Section 2 introduces two motivating examples—clinical trials that experienced imbalance in patient dropout. In Section 3, we outline the general framework for both model-based and model-free methods, including a flow diagram to help guide the choice of imputation strategies based on the direction of dropout imbalance. Section 4 presents a simulation study to explore different contributing factors that impact the results of the tipping point analysis. In Section 5, we apply tipping point analyses to the two motivating case studies and discuss our findings. Finally, the discussion section summarizes the key findings and provides broader context for the methods and results presented throughout the paper.

2
Motivating examples

In the following subsections, we examine two clinical trials and two simulated examples with an imbalance in dropout rates and timing of dropout between the experimental and control arms. These examples illustrate a common challenge in clinical research, one that underscores the need for more rigorous analysis and a better understanding of the underlying issues. In particular, the imbalance in dropout rates raises concerns about the validity of trial outcomes and the robustness of treatment effects. These examples highlight the importance of employing techniques such as tipping point analysis, which can offer valuable insights into how different dropout scenarios might influence results. However, despite the relevance of such analyses, they are often not consistently applied or fully explored in clinical trials. This paper aims to address this gap by advocating for more thorough and systematic evaluations in order to ensure the reliability and accuracy of conclusions drawn from clinical studies.

Simulated examples

A simulation framework is described in Section

Figure 2:
Simulated data with true marginal HR shown in horizontal dashed lines with two scenarios under different imbalances between informative censoring rate.

The scenario (a) refers to trials where there is a higher rate of dropouts in the experimental arm patients with worse prognosis relative to the control arm. Such scenarios are typical of trials where the patients in experimental arm tend to dropout due to their inability to tolerate the drug. Such propensity of dropout due to intolerability may be further exacerbated by underlying risk factors and patient comorbidities. These early dropouts in the experimental arm may lead to an over-estimation of its event free survival and bias the trial outcome in its favor under treatment policy estimand even when the true HR is 1. Scenario (b) is quite typical of open label studies where control patients with worse prognosis after finding out that they were not assigned to the experimental therapy may be less motivated to continue with the study and are more likely to drop out. This propensity to dropout may be further driven by patient factors such as access to other therapies that may be available to some patients in certain parts of the world, further influencing their decision to drop out. In this scenario, given the available access to care, as more low-prognostic-risk patients in the control arm drop out, the comparison between the experimental and control groups becomes increasingly confounded by differences in patient prognoses. This imbalance may lead to an outcome that favors the experimental arm, even if the true hazard ratio is 1 or higher.

Therefore based on the simulations it can be seen that an informative dropout either in favor of experimental or control arm can lead to inaccurate trial outcomes and a biased estimate in favor of the treatment effect depending on the imbalance of such dropouts.

Statistical methodology

In this section, we describe the key notations and the statistical methodology underlying the tipping point approaches we propose, both in model-free and model-based frameworks.

We denote by
T
the time-to-event random variable with the survival function defined by
S(t)=P(T>t)
and the hazard function
h(t)
as the event rate at time
t
conditional on survival until time
t
. The cumulative hazard function can then be expressed as

H(t)=\int^{t}_{0}h(s)ds=-\log(S(t))

Without loss of generality, we assume a two-arm trial under simple randomization with experimental treatment (
A_{i}=1
) and control (
A_{i}=0
) for
i=1,...,N
.

Tipping point analysis relies on a multiple imputation mechanism which can accommodate one or more varying parameters to impute the missing data, enabling the identification of parameter values for which the favorable treatment effect is lost based on either the point estimation or confidence interval crossing unity, a.k.a, a tipping point. Based on some selection criteria such as censoring reasons and experimental arm assignment, the event or censoring times from a subset of censored patients
\{N_{c}\}
will be imputed. The mainstream methods for tipping point analysis can be classified on the basis of their imputation mechanism as either model-based or model-free approaches. We describe the general approaches for each of them and how they relate to each other.

3.1
Model-based imputation

There exists different classes of models for time-to-event analysis, including parametric (e.g., exponential, Weibull)
Kleinbaum et al. 2012
, semi-parametric (Cox proportional hazard model
Cox 1972
) and non-parametric models (Kaplan-Meier method). Variations of those models could include covariate adjustment and weighting to improve goodness-of-fit under certain scenarios
(
Jin 2024
)
. Generally, in model-based imputation, a specific choice of model is fitted to the subset of patients who did not require imputation. This typically includes patients who had events and those who were censored for administrative reasons. Based on the fitted models, a sensitivity parameter
\delta
is introduced to modify the estimated hazard or the survival function after the censoring time
c_{i}
as

h^{\prime}(t|t>c_{i})=\delta\hat{h}(t);S^{\prime}(t|t>c_{i})=\hat{S}(t)^{\delta},\hskip 11.38109pt0<\delta<\infty

which is then used to generate hypothetical event times for patients in the set
\{N_{c}\}
who require imputation. This is also known as the delta-adjustment method which is thoroughly described in
Lipkovich et al. 2016
and will not be detailed in this paper. Additionally, to account for the inherent variability associated with such imputations, a multiple imputation approach is applied.

Depending on the choice of
\delta
, it is possible for the imputed event times to exceed the maximum follow-up time on the study. Such inconsistences are avoided by introducing a truncation step such that any imputed event time that exceeds this threshold is censored to the maximum follow-up time. For some scenarios, there may be patients whose potential follow-up times are longer than the observed maximum trial follow-up time. This leads to a limitation when imputing event times based on the Cox and Kaplan-Meier methods, which cannot extrapolate the hazard or survival function beyond observed time windows. The parametric approaches are commonly used in practice for model-based imputation compared to semi- and non-parametric approaches due to their simplicity in parametrization for the purpose of tipping point analysis, flexibility of different parametric formulation and ability to extrapolate beyond observed time windows. 3.2
Model-free imputation

The model-based approach described in the previous section is popular in the literature and is often used by sponsors or health authorities. We describe an alternative approach that does not require a choice of model for imputation. In the model-free approach, event/censoring times are imputed either deterministically or randomly sampled from a subset of observed data. The sensitivity parameter
\kappa
can be introduced differently, however both will require multiple imputations. Let
T_{i}
and
C_{i}
be the potential event time and right-censoring time of each
i
th patient, then
Y_{i}
is the event indicator if
T_{i}\leq C_{i}
and the observed outcome is
\min\{T_{i},C_{i}\}
.

Deterministic assignment

Of the patients
\{N_{c}\}
to be imputed, a random subset
\kappa
patients (with
0<\kappa<N_{c}
) will be assigned deterministically an event time at the time of censoring (i.e.,
Y_{i}:=1
and
C_{i}:=T_{i}
) or extend the censoring time to the potential maximum follow-up of each patient (i.e.,
C_{i}:=C_{i}+(T_{\text{data cut-off}}-C_{i})
).

At the patient level, this approach always imputes an extreme case scenario to consider patients immediately having an event (worst case) or being event free up to the longest possible follow-up time (best case), they become equivalent to the model-based imputation with individual adjustment as
\delta_{i}=\infty

\delta_{i}=0

\delta\rightarrow\infty

3.3
Tipping point analysis

The primary objective of conducting tipping point analysis is to adjust the sensitivity parameter
\delta
in model-based or
\kappa
in model-free imputation until the estimated treatment effect no longer favors the experimental arm, e.g., loses nominal significance based on confidence interval or point estimate. For each parameter value, multiply imputed datasets are generated and the results are then averaged using Rubin’s rule as described in
Rubin 1996
. The range of parameter values and their corresponding results help assess to what extent missing data could potentially affect the treatment effect and whether the results remain robust under various assumptions on the nature of dropout. The tipping point analysis identifies the parameter value where the results "tip." To simplify interpretation, a single sensitivity parameter is typically used. However, it is also possible to use arm-specific or covariate-dependent sensitivity parameters to explore a broader range of scenarios.

One of the key considerations in setting up a tipping point analysis is the criteria for selecting
\{N_{c}\}
. Typically, patients censored for non-administrative reasons are included, either in one arm or both. For simplicity, this paper focuses on imputation within a single arm; however, all methods readily extend to two-arm settings where each arm has a different sensitivity parameter for imputations. This choice may vary depending on the scientific objective and the context of the censoring mechanism, as well as the level of conservativeness. In most cases, these choices are made such that the tipping point analysis adequately addresses the impact of the imbalance of such censoring on the robustness of the estimated treatment effect. We propose a flow diagram (see Figure
3
) designed to guide the selection of the appropriate tipping point analysis based on different scenarios. This diagram outlines the decision-making process by considering the imbalance in censoring between arms. By following this structured approach, researchers can choose the most suitable tipping point analysis method for their specific context.

Flow diagram of tipping point analysis.

The consideration of adjusting the sensitivity parameter is common to both imputation approaches. Often, the interest is to impute data such that the treatment effect becomes more pessimistic to stress test the robustness of the original estimation. Consider the scenario where there is a higher dropout rate in the control arm, such as in open-label studies where control arm patients with a better prognosis drop out of the trial in favor of other effective therapies. For model-free imputation, a random subset of patients who dropped out may be selected and considered event-free at the time of data cutoff, effectively extending their censoring time by
t-c
, where
t
is the data cutoff time and
c
is the censoring time. For model-based imputation, patients in the control arm who dropped out can be assigned a sensitivity parameter
\delta<1
indicating deflated hazard compared to the model fitted on patients who did not drop out. In contrast, if there is a higher dropout rate in the experimental arm, a subset of patients may be imputed as having experienced an event at the time of dropout for the model-free imputation or assigned a sensitivity parameter
\delta>1
indicating inflated hazard for the model-based imputation.

Assessing clinical plausibility of tipping points

In addition to tipping point analysis itself, other parameter values may be useful to report, serving as aids to help assess the clinical plausibility of the tipping point results at specific values of the sensitivity parameter. Jump-to-reference imputation (
Atkinson et al. 2019
) is an analysis method in which patients in the experimental arm who require imputation are assumed to switch to the outcomes observed in the control arm (the reference) after dropout. This method models the scenario where, after discontinuing the experimental treatment, patients’ outcomes behave as if they had received the standard of care from that point onward. In the delta-method for model-based imputation,
\delta
can be selected based on an estimated hazard ratio (HR) comparing the experimental and control arms, such that the imputation reflects a scenario consistent with the jump-to-reference method. In this case,
\delta
is set to adjust the imputed outcomes in the experimental arm to align with those expected in the control arm, simulating a switch to the standard of care after dropout. In the case of model-free imputation,
\kappa
can be chosen such that the event rate among
\{N_{c}\}
matches the event rate in the experimental or control arm. In either approaches, such anchoring values (hazard ratio or event rate) help to judge how extreme an imputation is. The anchoring value can also be obtained from the literature on published relevant trial results, although may require additional justifications. Assessing clinical plausibility during tipping point analysis becomes more nuanced when censoring times are extended to the data cut-off, as in the model-free approach for imputing outcomes in the control arm. The data cut-off date is often arbitrarily chosen, and the further it is from the censoring date for a given patient, the more optimistic the underlying assumption becomes. To understand the overall impact of such imputation, one useful approach is to compare the Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves before and after imputation. A significant upward shift in the survival curve post-imputation may indicate an overly optimistic and potentially unrealistic assumption about the outcomes of control arm dropouts.
Visualizing KM curves pre- and post-imputation provides an intuitive method to evaluate the extent and plausibility of the shift caused by imputation. This approach is equally applicable in model-based imputations, where the survival curve can help reveal the impact of the chosen assumptions and highlight potential biases introduced by the imputation strategy. We further illustrate this approach in the two case studies presented in the Section 5.

4
Simulation study

In this simulation study, we investigate the behavior of tipping point analyses methods under different data scenarios and various contributing factors that impact when the tipping point will be reached.

We simulate trials with N = 2000 randomized into either treatment or control arm (
A=0,1
) in 1:1 ratio, such that relatively narrow confidence intervals are estimated justifying the use of tipping point analysis. We denote the time to event and censoring as
T_{i}
and
C_{i}
for the
i
th patient, respectively. The event times are simulated under Weibull distribution with shape parameters
\gamma=\{0.6,1.5\}
and scale parameter
\lambda=\log(2)/8
, indicating decreasing hazard and increasing hazard over time. The hazard function for the event is expressed as follows:

h_{i}(t)=\gamma\lambda(t^{\gamma-1})\exp(\log(\text{trueHR})A_{i}+\log(0.75)X_{i})

where
X\sim N(0,1)
is a continuous prognostic covariate. At the same time, a censoring mechanism is introduced based on the same prognostic covariate (possible interaction with arm assignment) to create the scenario of informative censoring with imbalance in dropout. The hazard function for censoring is based on an exponential distribution in two different scenarios:

1.

For higher censoring rate in the control arm:

h_{i}(c)=\lambda\exp(\log(0.5)A_{i}+\log(2)X_{i}+\log(1)A_{i}X_{i})

2.

For higher censoring rate in the experimental arm:

h_{i}(c)=\lambda\exp(\log(2)A_{i}+\log(1)X_{i}+\log(0.5)A_{i}X_{i})

The informative censoring is considered as an intercurrent event and handled using the treatment policy strategy. In scenario 1, patients with longer survival have increased hazard of being censored due to drop-out. In scenario 2, patients in the experimental arm with shorter survival have increased hazard of censoring due to drop-out. Both scenarios will lead to over-estimated treatment effect under treatment policy estimand as indicated in Figure
2
.

The final time-to-event outcome for patient
i
is
\min(T_{i},C_{i},T_{max})
with a maximum follow-up of
T_{max}=15
months. We simulate 100 trials with varying treatment effects under non-informative censoring as true HR = 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1, 1.1. For each simulated trials, tipping point analysis is carried out by fitting the exponential and Weibull parametric model. For each trial, 100 multiple imputations are performed by deflating the hazard of dropout patients in the control arm until the tipping point of 95% upper CI crosses 1 under treatment policy estimand. We compare the trend when tipping points are reached across different scenarios and imputation approaches. All simulations are implemented under R version 4.3.1.

A simple interpretation of the tipping point results is that the efficacy results are robust (having a lower true HR in favor of the experimental arm) when it is "difficult" to reach the tipping point, i.e., having a relatively extreme sensitivity parameter. 4.1.1
Impact of true HR

Across all the scenarios and imputation approaches, the closer the true hazard ratio (HR) is to 1, the easier it is to tip the result, as the original treatment effect is weaker and easier to counteract. Conversely, when the true HR is significantly lower than 1 (e.g. 0.7), the treatment effect is stronger, requiring more extreme assumptions to tip the results. Thus, we observe a downward trend across all configurations as the true hazard ratio increases as shown in Figure

4.1.2
Impact of shape parameter
(\gamma)

The shape parameter (
\gamma
) significantly influences the amount of hazard inflation required to tip results when applied to early-censored patients in the experimental arm. When
\gamma<1
, the hazard rate decreases over time, meaning early events are more frequent. As a result, inflating the hazard for early-censored patients has a stronger impact, requiring relatively less inflation to shift the conclusions. In contrast, when
\gamma>1
, the hazard rate increases over time, making early events naturally rarer. This reduces the effectiveness of hazard inflation in the early period, necessitating a much stronger increase to achieve the same tipping effect.

Additionally, we observe a higher dropout rate when
\gamma<1
, leading to more patients requiring imputation, which in turn increases the number of potential events that can be artificially introduced through hazard inflation. In contrast, when
\gamma>1
, lower dropout rates means fewer patients require imputation, further reducing the effectiveness of hazard inflation in shifting results.

4.1.3
Impact of parametric distribution in model-based approach

The choice of distribution to model the survival times is crucial in model-based tipping point analysis because it directly influences the hazard of events as the basis for deflation / inflation during imputation. Figure
4
shows a clear difference between the two parametric models in the
\%
hazard deflation required to reach the tipping point of the upper 95% CI crossing 1. Under the exponential model, the
\%
hazard deflation to reach the tipping point is consistently higher compared to the Weibull model when
\gamma=0.6
and is consistently lower when
\gamma=1.5
. When the true model is based on a Weibull distribution with
\gamma<1
, the hazard decreases over time. In this scenario, imputations based on an exponential distribution which assumes constant hazard for patients in the control arm will lead to an overestimation of the hazard and a shorter imputed survival time than imputations based on a Weibull distribution. This overestimation necessitates additional hazard deflation to correct the model’s misspecification, making the tipping point harder to reach. In contrast, the tipping point requires lower hazard deflation in the control arm to be reached for the exponential model than the Weibull model when the true model has a Weibull distribution with
\gamma>1
. The same logic applies to the hazard inflation needed in the experimental arm to tip the results when early censoring is more heavily imbalanced toward the experimental arm. The discrepancy between the Weibull and exponential models can be significant, with the tipping point identified by the Weibull model differing by up to 27% in hazard deflation and up to 175% in hazard inflation in our simulated scenarios.

4.1.4
Impact of different sampling algorithm in model-free approach

A key distinction from the model-based approach lies in the level of conservativeness when addressing imbalances, whether due to higher censoring in the control or experimental arm. As shown in Figure
5
, the percentage of patients requiring imputation is generally lower when imputing the experimental arm. This is because, in that case, an event is imputed at the time of censoring, which has a significantly greater impact than extending the censoring time to the maximum follow-up, as is done when imputing data in the control arm.

Similar observations can be made in Figure
6
, where the model-free approach differs. In this imputation mechanism, early-censored patients in the control and experimental arms have their imputed times drawn from samples of the best or worst event times, respectively. In this model-free approach, where control arm data is imputed by sampling from the best % event times, the distribution of events plays a crucial role. When
\gamma=0.6
, the hazard decreases over time, leading to a higher concentration of events at earlier time points. As a result, the sample used for imputation contains more events compared to the case of
\gamma=1.5
. Consequently, under these conditions, results tend to tip more easily when
\gamma=0.6
. In our simulation study, we imputed using the best/worst patients across both arms. When imputing experimental arm patients using 100% worst patients, this leads to sampling from non-early dropout patients from both arms which is already a pessimistic scenario that can tip the results, as shown by the bottom left panel of Figure
6
.

4.1.5
Impact of other factors

Number of Patients Requiring Imputation

The proportion of patients drop out requiring imputation is higher when
\gamma=0.6
compared to
\gamma=1.5
as shown in Table

. As a result, the impact of imputation increases. In such cases, a smaller sensitivity parameter is sufficient to tip the results, indicating that the analysis becomes more sensitive to the imputation process. This is apparent when the censoring rate in experimental arm is higher. Lower hazard inflation in model-based approach and fewer proportion of patients to be considered as events in model-free approach are sufficient to tip the results when
\gamma=0.6
compared to
\gamma=1.5
.

Relative Timing of Dropouts

Comparing across two different
\gamma
values again, they also impact when patients are typically censored. We see more extreme sensitivity parameters are required to tip the results with lower
\gamma
when control arm patients are imputed and with higher
\gamma
when experimental arm patients are imputed. In our simulation, the median time to dropout is 15 months which is relatively late in the study follow-up. When
\gamma<1
, the hazard rate decreases over time indicating dropout occurs more frequently when the event rate is low. In this scenario, hazard deflation required to reach the tipping point is more extreme to match the already-low event rates than when
\gamma>1
. On the contrary, the hazard inflation for those late dropouts has a larger impact when events are concentrated at the beginning of follow-up with
\gamma<1
, compared to when
\gamma>1
. A similar observation applies to the model-free approach. When dropout tends to occur at follow-up times with a lower event rate (i.e., when
\gamma<1
), a larger proportion of patients need to have their censoring time extended, as events are already rare in both arms. Conversely, when the imbalance shifts in the opposite direction with more drop out in the experimental arm, a smaller proportion of patients need to be classified as events to tip the results, due to the relative rarity of events when drop out occurs compared to when
\gamma>1
.

Sample size

In the simulation, we used a fixed sample size of
N=2000
, but it is expected that tipping the results would be easier with smaller sample sizes due to the wider confidence intervals.

When interpreting tipping point results, it is essential to consider the interplay of these contributing factors. Model specification, true hazard ratio, number of patients requiring imputation, relative timing of events / censoring, and sample size, all interact to influence the results. A comprehensive understanding of these factors is necessary to accurately interpret the findings and drawing informed conclusions.

5.1
CodebreaK200 analysis

For the CodebreaK200 trial (Section

Interestingly, the sponsor presented a different model-free tipping point analysis to showcase the robustness of their results while labeling the assumptions of the sensitivity analyses of the FDA as overly optimistic for the control arm. These findings were discussed during the Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee meeting held on October 5, 2023
U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2023

In this section, we perform our own model-based and model-free tipping point analyses and discuss the results in relation to the ODAC findings. We adopted a Weibull distribution for the event times, as it accommodates a broader range of possibilities compared to an exponential distribution, which assumes a constant hazard over time.

de Langen et al. 2023

The differences between the reconstructed and original data were minimal. Since the timing and reasons for individual censoring were unavailable, we assumed that all censored observations within the first five weeks corresponded to patients who were censored for non-administrative reasons and likely not being followed any further. This approach resulted in approximately 21 (12.1
\%

\%

In contrast, the model-free method which imputes events at the dropout times employs a more conservative approach to imputing event times. By assuming that dropouts experience events at the time of dropout, particularly within one month of starting treatment, the method adopts an overly pessimistic scenario that may not reflect most real-world cases. 6
Discussion

Tipping point analysis is a useful statistical method for sensitivity analysis of time-to-event endpoints when informative censoring is suspected. It is routinely performed during a regulatory review to assess the robustness of estimated treatment effect in the main analysis of clinical trials, providing additional evidence to support the benefit risk assessment of an experimental drug. Before conducting any tipping point analysis, an essential first step is to compare key baseline characteristics between patients who dropped out and those who did not in each arm. If there are meaningful differences, such as in disease severity, comorbidities, or other prognostic factors, these suggests that patients who dropped out may have a different expected outcome than those who remained making the trial susceptible for informative censoring. In such cases, assessing the clinical plausibility of pessimistic or optimistic assumptions in a tipping point framework as well as the choice imputation mechanism can be informed by these observed differences.

In this paper, we classified tipping point analysis into two key categories based on their data imputation mechanism, namely model-based and model-free approaches. We conducted a simulation study to investigate the contributing factors impacting the tipping point results. Through varying the data generating scenarios, we conducted tipping point analyses on the simulated data with model-based and model-free approaches. The patterns on when tipping points were reached underscore the importance of interplay between these factors. Conventionally, the tipping point results are often used to evaluate the robustness of the observed efficacy, i.e., an extreme sensitivity parameter required to tip the result indicates high probability of strong HR favoring the experimental arm. However, additional factors that impact the tipping point results also include imputation approach, model specification, number of patients requiring imputation and the relative timing of those censored patients. Taking into account these factors during interpretation of the result, stakeholders can improve the reliability and validity of their findings, leading to more informed decision-making.

Using the case studies based on CodeBreak2000 and ExteNET from ODAC meetings, we conducted an in-depth discussion and illustration of the differences between model-based and model-free tipping point analyses. For the CodeBreak2000 case study, the model-free and model-based approaches captured a comparable range of resulting hazard ratios (HRs). Since their sensitivity parameters are on a different scale, direct comparisons are not feasible. The two tipping point approaches are not directly comparable but rather provide complementary assessments of the robustness of efficacy under different assumptions. They align only in the most extreme scenarios. Specifically, they become equivalent when there is a higher dropout rate in the experimental arm, with
\delta\rightarrow\infty
in the model-based method, while all dropouts are imputed as events in the model-free approach. Conversely, equivalence occurs when the imbalance favors the control arm,
\delta\rightarrow 0
, and all dropout patients in the control arm are imputed as event-free at the maximum follow-up. The clinical plausibility assessment step provided clinical interpretation of the tipping point results, which can be used to assess the likelihood of different scenarios against external data. The reporting of multiple-imputed Kaplan-Meier curves from the tipping point analyses illustrate the magnitude of change introduced when conducting imputation, offering an alternative presentation of the tipping point results. In the model-based method, the hazard is either inflated or deflated to impute event times, which in turn affects the shape of the Kaplan-Meier curves by modifying the survival or event rate estimates in both arms. On the other hand, the model-free approach, by imputing dropouts as either events or event-free at maximum follow-up time, will also shift the KM curves, but the interpretation of these shifts is driven more by the assumption of how dropouts behave rather than adjusting for their exact timing. While we cannot directly compare the two tipping point approaches, we can compare the resulting shifts in the KM curves they generate. These shifts provide valuable insights into how each approach influences the interpretation of survival outcomes under different assumptions about dropouts. In practice, there is often no unique choice of tipping point method and more than one approaches could be used to complement each other to further testify the robustness of the treatment effect. The imputation approaches are closely linked with the estimand thinking
International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (2019) ICH
;
Siegel et al. 2024
. The key interest in the regulatory context is to conduct sensitivity analysis for the same estimand as the main analysis
Torres et al. 2025
, which often targets treatment policy estimand. In the presence of suspected informative censoring, the assumption for standard analyses methods in time-to-event endpoints are violated to an unknown extent. Tipping point analysis explores this unknown extent to evaluate the robustness of the estimated treatment effect. When examining the tipping point analysis results at specific sensitivity parameters, it is important to interpret the estimates with caution, as different tipping point methods may target different estimands. When there is a higher dropout rate in the experimental arm and they are considered as events at the time of dropout via model-free imputation, this acts as a composite strategy to handle dropout as an intercurrent event, i.e., dropout is considered as an event. When there is a higher dropout rate in the control arm and patients are considered event-free up to their maximum follow-up time, this acts as a hypothetical strategy.

Regardless of the chosen imputation approach, it is important to ensure that the imputations are consistent with the rest of the data to avoid conflicting information. For instance, an imputed progression-free survival (PFS) event or censoring date should occur after the recorded death date. Similarly, if PFS is censored due to the initiation of new antineoplastic therapy, the imputed PFS event date should precede the date of such therapy initiation.

Tipping point analysis is most impactful when a clear clinical interpretation can be assigned to the scenario at which the tipping point occurs. For instance, in our Codebreak200 case study, the tipping point was reached at
\delta=49\%
, approximately corresponding to a hazard ratio (HR) of
0.74
between the early dropout patients in the control arm and sotorasib arm. This translates to a meaningfully more favorable outcome for the patients who dropped out compared to the sotorasib arm making it an improbable scenario in clinical practice, as patients who dropped out were unlikely to access treatments more effective than sotorasib. Importantly, this clinical plausibility assessment step need not rely solely on trial data; comparisons with external data from the literature can also provide valuable insights.

In this paper, we focused on scenarios involving two-arm trials. However, in studies with more than two arms, the model-based approach can leverage the additional data for model fitting, potentially resulting in more precise quantification of the tipping point. This could be particularly useful in capturing nuanced differences between multiple experimental arms.
In non-inferiority trials, the choice of imputation parameters and the specific patient groups to be imputed may differ significantly from those in superiority trials. Non-inferiority settings often demand more stringent clinical plausibility assessment of the tipping point, as the clinical implications hinge on demonstrating that the treatment is not unacceptably worse than the comparator, rather than outright superiority.

Regardless of the chosen approach, the proportional hazards assumption may become increasingly tenuous following imputation, particularly as the number of imputed patients grows. The extent of this violation depends on both the magnitude of the imputed differences and the characteristics of the underlying data. For this reason, alternative population-level summary measures, such as restricted mean survival time (RMST)
(
Royston and Parmar 2013
)
, can provide a robust and assumption-lean metric to evaluate when the tipping point occurs. RMST comparisons are particularly valuable in scenarios where proportional hazards are unlikely to hold, offering a more interpretable assessment of clinical impact. One extension of the tipping point analysis described in our paper is the incorporation of baseline covariates to enable the imputation tailoring to different patient groups.

In summary, tipping point analysis remains a practical method to assess the robustness of efficacy results in the presence of informative censoring, especially in the case of imbalanced censoring across the arms. In order to make the interpretation of tipping point results more reliable, we encourage better reporting and clinical plausibility assessment of these results. In addition to the tipping point itself, visualizations from our case studies and reporting of the factors that impact the tipping point results should be done routinely, such as the fitted parametric model and distribution of censored patients to be imputed. We believe transparent reporting and clinical plausibility of the tipping point against internal and external data together improves the overall reliability of tipping point analyses.
