Solving the Porous Medium Equation with the eXtreme Mesh deformation approach (X– M – es h )
Alexandre Chemin, Jonathan Lambrechts, Nicolas Moës, Jean-François Remacle

Abstract
We introduce a new scheme for solving the non-regularized Porous Medium Equation. It is mass conserving and uses only positive unknown values. To address these typically conflicting features, we employ the eXtreme Mesh deformation approach (X– M – es h ), specifically designed for problems involving sharp interfaces. The method ensures that the interface is always meshed, even in the face of complex topological changes, without the need for remeshing or altering the mesh topology. We illustrate the effectiveness of the approach through various numerical experiments.

1
Introduction

The Porous Medium Equation (PME) is a non-linear partial differential equation of significant interest due to its widespread applicability in modeling various physical phenomena such as gas ﬂow in porous medium, incompressible ﬂuid dynamics and non-linear heat transfer
[
11
,
13
,
2
,
18
]
.

The PME is usually written as the following initial value problem:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa u^{m}\nabla u\right),

(1)

\displaystyle u({\mathbf{x}},t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\geq 0.

(2)

Here,
m>0
is a real parameter,
u({\mathbf{x}},t)
is the unknown scalar function in space
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and time
t\in[t_{0},T]
and
\kappa u^{m}
is homogeneous to a diffusivity coefficient. The case
m<0
corresponds to the Fast Diffusion Equation and is not treated in this paper. In Eq. (
1
),
u
is frequently employed to denote physical quantities such as gas density or temperature, requiring the preservation of non-negativity in solutions.
The case
m=0
leads to the standard heat equation.

The PME is parabolic provided
u\neq 0
, and degenerates when
u=0

[
18
]
. The nature of the solutions to Eq. (
1
) differs fundamentally depending on whether we consider
m=0
(linear case) or
m>0
(non-linear case). In the non-linear scenario, if the initial distribution
u_{0}({\mathbf{x}})
of the quantity of interest
u
has a compact support, then the solution
u(x,t)
will have a compact support for
t>0
. The region
\mathcal{Q}(t)\subset\Omega
where the solution is zero will shrink in size over time but remain present. Thus, in the non-linear case, a sharp interface
\Gamma(u)
may exist, separating
\mathcal{Q}(t)
from the rest of the domain where the solution is positive. This behavior arises from the finite propagation PME’s property which is defined in
[
18
]
as:
disturbances from the level
u=0
propagate in time with finite speed for solutions of the PME
. This constrast with the heat equation for which non-negative solutions are automatically positive everywhere on its domain of definition, and is an argument of the more physical soundness of PME for diffusion process modelization.

Since Eq. (
1
) is not defined for
u<0
, it is typically reformulated for numerical solving as follows:

\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa|u|^{m}\nabla u\right),\quad m>0\\

(3)

called the signed PME. Solving Eq. (
3
) while ensuring non-negativity of the solution over the whole domain
\Omega
is equivalent to solving Eq. (
1
). Conventional numerical methods, such as finite element methods (FEM), face notable challenges when applied to Eq. (
3
). With
\mathbf{n}
denoting the normal vector at the interface
\Gamma(u)
, the quantity
\nabla u\cdot{\mathbf{n}}
may lack regularity, depending on
m
and
u_{0}
. This irregularity in the solution’s derivatives implies that failure to capture
\Gamma(u)
sharply will induce spurious oscillations, compromising non-negativity and accuracy.

To overcome these challenges, we propose here the use of X–
M

–
es
h
method which is specifically designed to handle problems with sharp interfaces in the FEM context. X–
M

–
es
h
has demonstrated its effectiveness in scenarios such as the Stefan problem or two-phase flows
[
15
,
17
]
, particularly in accurately capturing complex interface dynamics without the need for enriched elements or mesh topology modifications. We will show that X–
M

–
es
h
is a valuable tool for accurately simulating the PME while addressing some limitations of traditional FEM approaches.

2
The Porous Medium Equation

In this section, we will detail important properties of PME and state-of-the-art methods to solve it. We consider a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a known function
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega)
such that:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcll}\vskip 5.0ptu_{0}(\mathbf{x})&\geq&0,&\forall\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
\nabla u_{0}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}&=&0,&\forall\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

(4)

The objective is, for fixed
m>0
and
\kappa>0
, to find
u
such that:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcll}\vskip 5.0pt\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(\mathbf{x},t)&=&\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa|u(\mathbf{x},t)|^{m}\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t)\right)&,~~(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\\
\vskip 5.0ptu(\mathbf{x},t_{0})&=&u_{0}(\mathbf{x})&,~~\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\\
\nabla u(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}&=&0&,~~(\mathbf{x},t)\in\partial\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\\
u(\mathbf{x},t)&\geq&0&,~~(\mathbf{x},t)\in\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\\
\end{array}\right.

(5)

As mentioned in the introduction, solutions of the PME can exhibit an interface separating regions where the solution is null from region where the solution is strictly positive. Therefore, problems modeled with the PME fall into the category of
one-phase
problems. For clarity purposes, we are defining in this paper
\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\text{ such as }u(\mathbf{x},t)>0\}
,
\Gamma(u(t))=\partial\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{u(t)}=\Omega\setminus(\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}\cup\partial\mathcal{P}_{u(t)})
.
\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}
will be refered to as
the phase
,
\Gamma(u(t))
as
the interface
and
\mathcal{Q}_{u(t)}
the
empty region
for a given solution
u(t)
on
\Omega
(Figure
1
).

Green areas correspond to
the phase

\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}
(part of the domain where
u(t)>0
). The grey area corresponds to the
empty region

\mathcal{Q}_{u(t)}
(part of the domain where
u(t)=0
) and red lines to
the interface

\Gamma(u(t))
.

PME presents various notable properties that a numerical simulation must be able to ensure. We will highlight the most crucial ones, and a more extensive list can be found in
[
18
]
.

2.1
Mass Conservation

An important property of PME is the invariance in time of the quantity
\int_{\Omega}u\,d\Omega
when homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions are applied on
\partial\Omega
. A comprehensive proof is available in
[
18
]
. In the context of PME modeling for non-linear heat transfer, this corresponds to the conservation of internal energy. In the case of PME modeling for gas flow in a porous medium, it signifies mass conservation. For convenience, we refer to this property as mass conservation in this paper.

2.2
Interface Velocity

Using Reynolds transport theorem and the mass conservation property of PME’s solutions, it is possible to derive the interface normal velocity
\mathbf{v}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.52083pt}\Gamma}

[
18
]
:

\mathbf{v}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.52083pt}\Gamma(u(t))}(\mathbf{x},t)=\lim_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}\rightarrow\Gamma(u(t))}-\kappa\nabla\left(\frac{u^{m}(\mathbf{x},t)}{m}\right)

(6)

This equation highlights that there exists a set of initial conditions for which the interface
\Gamma(u(t))
will not move for a finite amount of time. Indeed, choosing
u_{0}
such that
\nabla(u^{m}_{0})
is continuous and vanishes on
\Gamma(u_{0})
leads to
\mathbf{v}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.52083pt}\Gamma(u_{0})}(\mathbf{x},t_{0})=\mathbf{0}
. This phenomenon is known as the
waiting time
property in the literature. A robust numerical simulation of the PME should accurately reproduce this specific behavior.

2.3
Barenblatt-Pattle solution

Another noteworthy result is the existence of analytical solutions of the form:

u_{BP}(\mathbf{x},t)=t^{-\alpha}\left((C-k\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}t^{-2\beta})^{+}\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}

(7)

with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\vskip 5.0pt\alpha&=&\dfrac{d}{md+2}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\beta&=&\dfrac{\alpha}{d}\\
k&=&\dfrac{m\alpha}{2\kappa d}\\
\end{array}\right.

(8)

and
C
an arbitrary constant. This solution correspond to an initial condition
u_{0}
being a Dirac distribution centered on
\mathbf{0}
for which the total mass
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\,\text{d}\Omega=M(C)
depends on
C
. This analytical solution will be used to characterize the convergence rate of the proposed numerical method. The interface
\Gamma(u_{BP}(t))
is well-defined as:

\Gamma(u_{BP}(t))=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\mid\|\mathbf{x}\|=f(t),f(t)=\left(\dfrac{C}{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}t^{\beta}\right\}

(9)

2.4
Solving PME with Finite Element Method

When solving Eq. (
5
) with FEM, the property of non-negativity is violated, and oscillations appear at interface
\Gamma(u(t))
, propagating throughout the entire domain
\Omega
. An example of such oscillations is illustrated Figure
2
for
d=2
.

Figure 2:
Numerical solution of the PME obtained with a classical FEM. Colored areas correspond to the part of the domain
\Omega
where
u\geq 0
. Left: initial condition at
t=t_{0}
. Right: numerical solution at
t_{1}=t_{0}+\Delta t
.

The manifestation of this behavior coupled with a loss of regularity in the vicinity of the interface, results in a diminished convergence rate of the numerical scheme. The theorical convergence rate using a uniform linear finite element discetization of size
h
in space and a backward-Euler discretization in time established in
[
9
]
is:

\|u^{h}-u\|_{L^{2}([t_{0},T];L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq Ch^{\frac{m^{2}+6m+8}{6m^{2}+14m+8}},\text{ with }\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(h^{\frac{5m+4}{2m}})

(10)

As
m
tends to
0
, the convergence rate reaches its maximum of
1
and gradually approaches
\frac{1}{6}
with increasing values of
m
.

To circumvent this issue, several approaches have been proposed. A first idea is to use a numerical scheme which ensure non-negativity of the solution
[
14
,
19
,
6
]
. For example,
[
19
]
proposes a method where a local discontinuous Galerkin finite element method is coupled with a custom made limiter to ensure non-negativity of the solution. A demonstration is provided showing that this limiter ensure non-negativity of the solution for the discontinuous
\mathbb{P}_{0}
finite elements, and provide numerical 1D results for discontinuous
\mathbb{P}_{2}
finite elements. However, these methods are adding numerical viscosity leading to a loss of precision of the numerical solution near the interface.

A second type of approach is to use a time-dependent spatial discretization. In
[
16
]
is proposed an adaptative moving mesh finite element method. Meshes used for solving the PME are time-dependent but all have the same topology (same number of vertices and elements, and same connectivity). Each mesh is generated such as its elements sizes respect an imposed metric and different metric are investigated. They are showing that using a target metric based on the Hessian of the solution at previous time step allows to retreive a convergence rate in
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})
for
m=1
and
m=2
for the Barenblatt-Pattle solution. It is important to note that with this method, non-negativity of the solution is broken and oscillations at the interface
\Gamma(u(t))
are still showing. However, the target metric used leads to a smaller element size near the interface decreasing significantly oscillations amplitude and propagation in the whole computational domain.

In
[
4
]
is proposed a moving mesh finite element algorithm for PDE with moving boundaries, and is applied to the Barenblatt-Pattle solution of the PME. In that case, only the part of the computational domain where
u>0
is meshed and the mesh evolves in time in order to ensure mass conservation of the solution. This allows to obtain a second-order convergence rate for
m=1
and a first-order convergence rate for
m=3
. However, this approach does not handle topology changes which can lead the mesh to overlap itself and need to be coupled with a remeshing algorithm for such cases.

To the best of our knowledge, all numerical methods for solving the PME described in the literature involve violating either the non-negativity of the solution, mass conservation, or modifying the equation through direct or indirect regularization.

The methodology presented in this paper falls within the moving meshes category. The principle of the X–
M

–
es
h
method is to determine, at each time step
t_{n}
, the solution
u_{n}
and the spatial discretization
\mathcal{T}_{n}
in such a way that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
maintains the same topology as
\mathcal{T}_{n-1}
and fully represents
\Gamma(u_{n})
through its vertices and edges. Key criteria for evaluating the method’s effectiveness include ensuring non-negativity at each time step, improving the convergence rate with respect to the spatial mesh size
h
, enhancing precision in interface localization, maintaining robustness in the face of topology changes in
\mathcal{P}_{u}(t)
, and preserving the mass conservation property of the PME.

Firstly, the X–
M

–
es
h
approach will be presented in more detail, followed by a comprehensive description of the moving mesh strategy. Finally, the results and relevance of the proposed method will be discussed.

3
The X–
M

–
es
h
approach

As previously mentioned, the goal of the X–
M

–
es
h
approach is to use a time-dependent spatial discretization. In order to develop a discrete formulation of the problem with this constraint, we introduce the arbitrary Lagrangian Eulerian frame of reference (
[
5
,
7
]
). We define the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
as
\Omega_{0}=\Omega
, and a mapping
\mathbf{X}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{0}\times[t_{0},T])
:

\begin{array}[]{rll}\mathbf{X}:\Omega_{0}\times[t_{0},T]&\rightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)&\mapsto&\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)\end{array}

(11)

We define
\Omega_{t}=\{\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t),\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\Omega_{0},t\in[t_{0},T]\}
,
\mathbf{w}=\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial t}\right|_{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
,
\mathbf{F}=\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial\mathbf{X}_{0}}\right|_{t}
and
J=\text{det }{\mathbf{F}}
.
Assumptions on the mapping are the following:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t_{0})=\mathbf{X}_{0}\text{,}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\text{the domain shape is unchanged, that is to say }\Omega_{t}=\Omega_{0}\text{, }\forall t\in[t_{0},T],\\
J(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)\geq 0\text{, }\forall(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)\in\Omega_{0}\times[t_{0},T]\text{.}\\
\end{array}\right.

(12)

To obtain a weak formulation of Eq. (
5
), we define the test function space
\bar{\mathcal{U}}
as follows, assuming functions within it have proper regularity on
\Omega\times[t_{0},T]
:

\bar{\mathcal{U}}=\{\bar{u}:\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mid\left.\dfrac{\partial\bar{u}}{\partial t}\right|_{\mathbf{X}}=0\}

(13)

With
\bar{u}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}
, Eq. (
5
) gives:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{crcl}&\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\mathbf{w}.\nabla\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla.(\kappa|u|^{m}\nabla u)\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\mathbf{w}\nabla\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega\\
\\
\Leftrightarrow&\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega&=&-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa|u|^{m}\nabla u.\nabla\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\mathbf{w}\nabla\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega.\\
\end{array}

The time integration of Eq. (
3
) between two instants
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}
is performed using a
\theta
scheme. Noting
\Delta t_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
and
u_{n}(\mathbf{X})=u(\mathbf{X},t_{n})
we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{n+1}\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega\approx\Delta t_{n}\left(\theta\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{n+1}\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega+(1-\theta)\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\bar{u}\,\text{d}\Omega\right)\text{, }\theta\in[0,1]

(18)

In this paper, unless mentioned otherwise,
\theta
is set to
\frac{1}{2}
.

The spatial discretization is done using an order
1
finite element method. The reference domain
\Omega_{0}
is partionned into a triangular mesh
\mathcal{T}_{0}
for which the set of nodes is denoted
\mathcal{N}_{0}
. The spatial discretization at
t
is the triangulation
\mathcal{T}(t)
obtained from applying the mapping
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. By construction, the quantity
\mathbf{w}
corresponds to the mesh velocity and the mesh topology (number of nodes and connectivities) does not depend on time. The set of nodes of
\mathcal{T}(t)
is denoted
\mathcal{N}
, and we note
\mathbf{x}^{p}(t)
the coordinates of
p\in\mathcal{N}
. For convenience purposes, we will also define:

\mathbf{X}(p,t)\equiv\mathbf{x}^{p}(t)=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{x}^{p}(0),t)\text{, }p\in\mathcal{N}

(19)

The discrete solution
u^{h}
at instant
t_{n}
is noted
u^{h}_{n}\equiv u^{h}_{\mid_{t_{n}}}
and verifies:

u^{h}_{n}(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}U^{i}_{n}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X})

(20)

where
\phi_{i}
is the finite element linear basis function associated to node
i
and
U^{i}_{n}
the value of the discrete scalar field
u^{h}_{n}
at node
i
.
The test function
\bar{u}
is discretized using the same process. The discrete test function
\bar{u}^{h}
is:

\bar{u}^{h}(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\bar{U}^{i}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X}).

(21)

and the discrete test function space
\bar{\mathcal{U}}^{h}
is:

\bar{\mathcal{U}}^{h}=\{u:\Omega\times[t_{0},T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mid u(\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}U^{i}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X}),U^{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ }\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\}

(22)

With the spatial discretization described above and the time integration method outlined in Eq. (
18
) over the time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
, Eq. (
3
) becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}r(\bar{u}^{h},u^{h}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1},u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})&\equiv&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}_{n+1}\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}_{n}\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\\
&+&\Delta t_{n}\theta\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa|u^{h}_{n+1}|^{m}\nabla u^{h}_{n+1}.\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}_{n+1}\mathbf{w}_{n+1}\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\right)\\
&+&\Delta t_{n}(1-\theta)\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa|u^{h}_{n}|^{m}\nabla u^{h}_{n}.\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}_{n}\mathbf{w}_{n}\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\right)\\
&=&0\text{, }\forall\bar{u}^{h}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}^{h}\\
\end{array}

The X–
M

–
es
h
approach consists in, knowing
(u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})
, finding
(u^{h}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
such as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\text{ respects conditions Eq.~\eqref{eq:condX}}\\
\vskip 5.0ptu^{h}_{n+1}(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})\geq 0\text{, }\forall(\mathbf{X}_{n+1})\in\Omega\\
r(\bar{u}^{h},u^{h}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1},u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})=0\text{, }\forall\bar{u}^{h}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}^{h}\\
\end{array}\right.

(28)

It is important to note that this set of conditions is equivalent to:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\text{ respects conditions Eq.~\eqref{eq:condX}}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\Gamma_{n+1}^{h}\equiv\Gamma(u^{h}_{n+1})\text{ is fully represented with vertices of }\mathcal{T}_{n+1}\\
r(\bar{u}^{h},u^{h}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1},u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})=0\text{, }\forall\bar{u}^{h}\in\bar{\mathcal{U}}^{h}\\
\end{array}\right.

(29)

which can be rewritten as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{X}_{n+1}\text{ respects conditions Eq.~\eqref{eq:condX}}\\
\vskip 5.0pt\Gamma_{n+1}^{h}\text{ is fully represented with vertices of }\mathcal{T}_{n+1}\\
R((U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n}),(U_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1}))=\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}r^{2}(\phi_{i},u^{h}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1},u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=0\\
\end{array}\right.

(30)

In the following sections will be detailed the general method to solve problem (
30
).

3.1
Solving scheme

We assume that the valid solution
(u^{h}_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})
at time step
t_{n}
is known. The method developed to solve the non-linear problem described in Eq. (
30
) is outlined in Algorithm
1
. The general approach begins by selecting nodes
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
, which will be used to define the interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
at time step
t_{n+1}
. Next, the pair
(U_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
that satisfies Eq. (
30
) is determined through successive updates
k
of the mesh
\mathbf{X}^{k}_{n+1}
and the corresponding solution
U^{k}_{n+1}
. This iterative process continues until the residual
R((U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n}),(U^{k}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}^{k}_{n+1}))
meets a specified tolerance.

. Solution
(U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})
at instant

;

\mathbf{X}^{0}_{n+1}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}_{0}
;

;

\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})\leftarrow localize\_interface(U_{n+1}^{0},\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{0})
;

Computation of solution

associated to
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{k}

The step referred to as

in Algorithm
1
is detailed here. This step aims to compute
U_{n+1}^{k}
for a fixed
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{k}
when
(U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})
is known. To streamline the notations, in this section
U_{n+1}^{k}
and
\mathbf{X}_{n+1}^{k}
will be respectively noted
U
and
\mathbf{X}
, and
\phi_{i}
will refer to the first order finite element basis function associated to node
i\in\mathcal{N}
.

This problem is non-linear and can, for example, be solved using a Newton-Raphson scheme. An initial guess
U_{0}
is made and the solution is updated following:

H_{j}.U_{j+1}=H_{j}.U_{j}-G_{j}

(31)

while
\|G_{j}\|
is greater than a user imposed tolerance, with:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\vskip 5.0ptG_{j}&=&\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r(\phi_{1},U_{j},\mathbf{X},U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})\\
\vdots\\
r(\phi_{N},U_{j},\mathbf{X},U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})\\
\end{array}\right]\\
H_{j}&=&\dfrac{\partial G_{j}}{\partial U_{j}}\\
\end{array}\right.

(32)

As mentioned in Section
2
, if nodes of
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
are not describing
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
, solving Eq. (
31
) will lead to a solution that violates non-negativity and exhibits oscillations propagating throughout the entire domain.

In order to circumvent this issue and ensure non-negativity of the converged solution
U
, the Newton-Raphson iteration Eq. (
31
) is modified by introducing Lagrange multipliers
\lambda^{\mathcal{D}}
to ensure an homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on a well chosen set of vertices
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{N}
. Defining
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{D}
and
I
the identity matrix, the Newton-Raphson iteration with Lagrange multipliers is written:

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{D}}&0\\
H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{D}}&-I\\
0&-I&0\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{j+1}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
U_{j+1}^{\mathcal{D}}\\
\lambda^{\mathcal{D}}_{j+1}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{D}}&0\\
H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{D}}&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{j}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
U_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}\\
0\\
\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}G_{j}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
G_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}\\
0\\
\end{array}\right]

(33)

It should be noted that the application of Lagrange multipliers is targeted solely at nodes where the solution
U
would be negative, necessitating the retention of strictly positive multipliers. Thus, to compute the solution update
U_{j+1}
, we define the set of vertices
\mathcal{D}
in the following manner:

\mathcal{D}=\{m\in\mathcal{N}\mid U_{j}^{m}<0\text{ or }\lambda^{m}_{j}>0\}

(34)

As we know that
U_{j+1}^{\mathcal{D}}=0
, the system of equations
33
can be rewritten in a more convenient way:

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}&0\\
H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}}&-I\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{j+1}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
\lambda_{j+1}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{F}\mathcal{D}}\\
H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}}&H_{j}^{\mathcal{D}\mathcal{D}}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{j}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
U_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}\\
\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}G_{j}^{\mathcal{F}}\\
G_{j}^{\mathcal{D}}\\
\end{array}\right]

(35)

This resolution method, summarized in Algorithm
2
, allows to ensure non-negativity of the solution everywhere and prevents oscillations to propagate in the whole domain
\Omega
, as highlighted Figure
4
.

3.3
Interface location estimation and mesh update

This section focuses on estimating the location of the interface for a given solution
(U_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
and the subsequent mesh update required to capture interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
.

For a given solution
(U_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1})
, it is possible to split the set of nodes
\mathcal{N}
into two subsets
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}&=&\{i\in\mathcal{N}\mid U_{n+1}^{i}>0\}\\
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}&=&\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}\\
\end{array}\right.

(38)

\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
corresponds to the set of nodes which are inside
the phase
at
t_{n+1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}
to the set of nodes which are outside
the phase
. Interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
is localized inside triangles of
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
having vertices belonging to
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and vertices belonging to
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, but the exact location is not known a priori. We now define:

\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})=\{i\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}\mid i\text{ is connected to at least one node of }\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}\}

(39)

\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
is the set of nodes which will be used to describe
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
(Figure
5
). It is defined only once for time step
t_{n+1}
and will remain unchanged during the successive updates of
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
nodes location. This step is called
localize\_interface
in Algorithm
1
. During the mesh update procedure, nodes from
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
will be moved to
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
estimated location.

It is important to note that moving all nodes from
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
to
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
is an ill-posed non-linear problem. The approach adopted is to compute and update an estimated optimal position for each node of
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
independently from each other and reiterate this process until convergence.

Left: in green, nodes of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{P}}
and in black, nodes of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Right: In red are highligted nodes selected to belong to
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
. The blurred blue line represent approximate location of
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
.

Let’s consider
p\in\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
a node which has been flagged to be belonging to the interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
. In order to obtain a good estimation of the interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
location, we are considering the current solution
U_{n+1}
only on the patch of triangles
\mathcal{T}^{p}_{n+1}
sharing
p
as a vertex. We denote
\mathcal{N}^{p}
the set of nodes of
\mathcal{T}^{p}_{n+1}
and
\bar{\mathcal{N}}^{p}=\mathcal{N}^{p}\setminus\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
the set of nodes connected to
p
which does not belong to
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma^{h}_{n+1})
. Finally, we define
\tilde{U}_{n+1}
such as:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{U}_{n+1}^{i}=U_{n+1}^{i}\text{, }\forall i\in\mathcal{N}^{p}\text{, }i\neq p\\
\tilde{U}_{n+1}^{p}=0\\
\end{array}\right.

(40)

Considering definition Eq. (
3
),
p
belonging to the interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
is equivalent to:

r(\phi_{p},\tilde{U}_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1},U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})=0

(41)

We define now a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a new triangulation
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{v}}
obtained from the triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
where the node
p
is translated by
\mathbf{v}
. The problem of finding a new location for
p
such as
p\in\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
can then be formulated as:

\text{Find }\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\text{ such as }r(\phi_{p},\tilde{U}_{n+1},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n+1}^{\mathbf{v}},U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})=0

(42)

This problem does not have a unique solution. In order to simplify the search for a valid
\mathbf{v}
, we are only considering translations along edges connecting
p
to
\bar{\mathcal{N}}^{p}
. Therefore, we only consider vectors
\mathbf{v}=\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}
where:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{v}_{pj}=\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(j)-\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)\text{, }j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}^{p}\\
\eta_{pj}\in[0,1]\\
\end{array}\right.

(43)

In doing so, solving problem Eq. (
42
) is reduced to solving
|\bar{\mathcal{N}}^{p}|
sub-problems:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{With $p$, $\tilde{U}_{n+1}$, $U_{n}$, $\mathbf{X}_{n}$, $\mathbf{v}_{pj}$ fixed, and $j\in\bar{\mathcal{N}}^{p}$,}\\
\text{find }\eta_{pj}\in[0,1]\text{ such as }\\
r(\eta_{pj})=r(\phi_{p},\tilde{U}_{n+1},\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n+1}^{\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}},U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n})=0\\
\end{array}\right.

(44)

The sub-problem is not guaranteed to have a solution, yet given the strict monotonicity of
r(\eta_{pj})
with respect to
\eta_{pj}
, any existing solution would be unique and determined using a bisection method.
Defining
\mathcal{S}_{p}=\{(\eta_{pj},\mathbf{v}_{pj})\mid(\eta_{pj},\mathbf{v}_{pj})\text{ verifies \ref{eq:pbLocInterfaceSub}}\}
the set of possible translations to move node
p
onto the estimated
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
, we choose the one inducing the smallest mesh deformation possible, that is to say the one for which
\|\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}\|
is minimal. The corresponding node
j
for which
\|\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}\|
is minimal will be called node
p
target and is noted
q_{p}
:

q_{p}=\argmin_{\{j\mid\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\}}\|\eta_{pj}\mathbf{v}_{pj}\|

(45)

The method described is illustrated in Figure
6
.

As mentioned previously
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
actually depends on
U_{n+1}

\mathbf{X}_{n+1}

U_{n}

\mathbf{X}_{n}

\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}

p
from solving Eq. (

\mathbf{v}=\min(\eta_{pq_{p}},\alpha)\mathbf{v}_{pq_{p}}
. We found
\alpha=0.6
to be working well.

Ultimately, the translation
\mathbf{v}=\min(\eta_{pq_{p}},\alpha)\mathbf{v}_{pq_{p}}
may result in the creation of inverted elements. If such a situation arises, node
p
will be translated, and any resulting inverted elements will then undergo a corrective procedure referred to as
fix\_inverted\_elements
in Algorithm
3
and illustrated in Figure
7
. This procedure aims to rectify the inverted elements by flattening them, producing elements with null or quasi-null measure. These elements are typically deemed unsuitable for finite element simulations due to their non-compliance with the convergence conditions outlined in
[
3
]
. However, as detailed in
[
10
,
8
]
, convergence of finite element solutions can still be achieved without strictly adhering to these conditions. A more relaxed criterion is developed in
[
12
]
. Additionally, as outlined in
[
8
]
, acceptable patterns of these poorly shaped elements only affect the conditioning of finite element matrices. In the work presented here, all matrix systems are solved with direct solvers, effectively eliminating any concerns regarding the poor conditioning of operators. In cases where an iterative solver for matrix systems is required (e.g., for performance purposes with 3D problems), we recommend the approach proposed in
[
15
]
, which involves bounding from below the determinant of the Jacobian of very small elements.

3.4
Mesh velocity definition

The definition of the mesh velocity
\mathbf{w}
within a time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
remains to be addressed. We know that
\mathbf{w}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)\,\text{d}t=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t_{n+1})-\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t_{n})=\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(\mathbf{X}_{0})-\mathbf{X}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\text{, }\forall\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\Omega

(46)

We have the flexibility to choose any
\mathbf{w}
satisfying Eq. (
46
). The natural modeling choice proposed in
[
15
]
is a mesh velocity
\mathbf{w}
such as
\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial t}\right|_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{0}
in
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. Using definition established in Eq. (
19
), it writes:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{X})=\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbf{W}^{i}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X})\\
\mathbf{W}^{i}=\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(i)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(i)}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}\\
\end{array}\right.

(47)

This option is suitable for addressing
two-phase
problems, however it is not appropriate for
one-phase
problems due to its inadequate representation of the underlying physics. An example of unwanted behavior using this mesh velocity modelization is illustrated Figure
8
. The converged solution obtained after one time step shows that the
phase

\mathcal{P}_{u}
spreads more than expected and on a very thin sheet. As this result is obtained with a fully converged solution (there is no Lagrange multipliers left and

), this behavior is not the consequence of the resolution method but originates from a modelization problem.

4
Numerical results

To evaluate the proposed numerical scheme’s performance, we will perform a three-part assessment. First, we will compare the solution quality obtained with our scheme to that of the classical FEM for the Barenblatt-Pattle family of solutions. Second, we will investigate the scheme’s ability to simulate waiting time phenomena. Finally, we will assess the method’s robustness to changes in the phase topology.

where an explicit expression for the waiting time

h
.

Putting aside the capped, unphysical retrograde movement, waiting times obtained are in good agreement with analytical solutions (Eq. (

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented the application of the eXtreme Mesh deformation (X–
M

–
es
h
) approach to address the challenges associated with the numerical simulation of the Porous Medium Equation (PME). While the X–
M

–
es
h
approach, designed for tracking sharp interfaces without the need for remeshing or altering mesh topology, has previously found success in solving two-phase problems such as the Stefan phase change model and two-phase flows, our objective here was to extend its applicability to the PME, which constitutes a one-phase problem.

A significant challenge discussed in the literature is the numerical resolution of the non-regularized PME while ensuring non-negativity of solutions, mass conservation, and accurate depiction of interface dynamics. The X–
M

–
es
h
approach, as adapted and implemented in this paper, offers a comprehensive solution to these challenges.

Our numerical experiments served to validate the efficiency of the X–
M

–
es
h
approach in addressing various phenomena inherent to the PME. Through simulations on the Barenblatt-Pattle solution, we demonstrated significant improvements in interface localization precision, modest enhancements in
L^{2}
convergence, and the successful preservation of mass conservation. Additionally, our investigation into waiting time phenomena reaffirmed the X–
M

–
es
h
approach’s ability to accurately reproduce analytical results, thereby establishing its reliability and accuracy. Furthermore, we showcased the versatility and robustness of the X–
M

–
es
h
approach in handling interface topology changes, demonstrating its seamless adaptability in accommodating instances of coalescence and vanishing without necessitating additional adjustments or specific treatments.

In conclusion, while the X–
M

–
es
h
approach has been previously utilized for Stefan problems and two-phase flows, our application to the PME domain represents a significant extension of its utility. The demonstrated effectiveness of the approach in addressing longstanding challenges and consistently delivering reliable results establishes it as a valuable tool for solving the PME.

Appendix A
Justification of the mesh velocity definition

In this appendix is detailed the choice made in Section
3.4
for the mesh velocity
\mathbf{w}
inside a time interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. As previously mentioned,
\mathbf{w}
verifies:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t)\,\text{d}t=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t_{n+1})-\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}_{0},t_{n})=\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(\mathbf{X}_{0})-\mathbf{X}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{0})\text{, }\forall\mathbf{X}_{0}\in\Omega

(55)

and we have the flexibility to choose any
\mathbf{w}
satisfying Eq. (
55
). The natural modeling choice proposed in
[
15
]
is a mesh velocity
\mathbf{w}
such as
\left.\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{w}}{\partial t}\right|_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{0}
in
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. Using definition established in Eq. (
19
), it writes:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0pt\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{X})=\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbf{W}^{i}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X})\\
\mathbf{W}^{i}=\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(i)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(i)}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}\\
\end{array}\right.

(56)

This option is suitable for addressing
two-phase
problems, however it is not appropriate for
one-phase
problems due to its inadequate representation of the underlying physics. Indeed, a fully converged solution (there is no Lagrange multipliers left and
R((U_{n},\mathbf{X}_{n}),(U_{n+1},\mathbf{X}_{n+1}))=0
) can exhibit an unwanted phenomenon where the phase
\mathcal{P}_{u}
spreads more than expected and on a very thin sheet (illustrated Figure
8
). This behavior is the result of the fact that Eq. (
56
) is unable to model the interface motion inside a time interval.

To illustrate this, we will examine the behavior of this modelization of mesh velocity on a straightforward 1D transport problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcll}\vskip 5.0pt\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}&=&-\mathbf{v}.\nabla u=-v\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}&\text{, }v\in\mathbb{R}^{+*}\\
\vskip 5.0ptu(x,0)&=&u_{0}(x)&\text{, }\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]\\
u_{0}(x)&=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-ax\\
-bx\\
\end{array}\right.&\text{, }\begin{array}[]{l}\hskip-3.99994pt\text{if }x<0\text{, }a>0\\
\hskip-3.99994pt\text{if }x\geq 0\text{, }b\geq 0\\
\end{array}\\
\end{array}\right.

(57)

which admit as solution:

u(x,t)=u_{0}(x-vt)\text{, }\forall(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]

(58)

The case
b=0
corresponds to a
one-phase
problem (the
phase

\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid u(x,t)>0\}
and the interface
\Gamma(u(t))=\partial\mathcal{P}_{u(t)}
) and the case
b>0
to a
two-phase
problem (the first phase
\mathcal{P}^{1}_{u(t)}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid u(x,t)>0\}
, the second phase
\mathcal{P}^{2}_{u(t)}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid u(x,t)<0\}
and the interface
\Gamma(u(t))=\partial\mathcal{P}^{1}_{u(t)}=\partial\mathcal{P}^{2}_{u(t)}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid u(x,t)=0\}
).

We are now applying to Eq. (
57
) the space and time discretization detailed Section
3
for one time interval
[0,\Delta t]
and with four nodes
\{N_{0},N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\}
and the corresponding three elements
\{[N_{0},N_{1}],[N_{1},N_{2}],[N_{2},N_{3}]\}
. Nodes
\{N_{0},N_{1},N_{3}\}
position will be fixed, node
N_{2}
position will be time-dependent and its velocity
W(N_{2})
is constant.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\vskip 5.0ptX(N_{0},t)=-1\\
X(N_{1},t)=0\\
X(N_{2},t)=1+W(N_{2})t=1+\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}t\\
X(N_{3},t)=2\\
\end{array}\right.

(59)

Finally, we want node
N_{2}
to be located exactly on the interface at
t=\Delta t
, meaning that
U^{2}_{\Delta t}=0
. We therefore have the following initial and boundary conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}U^{0}_{0}=a\\
U^{1}_{0}=0\\
U^{2}_{0}=-b\\
U^{3}_{0}=-2b\\
\end{array}\right.,\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}U^{0}_{\Delta t}=a(1+v\Delta t)\\
U^{2}_{\Delta t}=0\\
U^{3}_{\Delta t}=b(-2+v\Delta t)\\
\end{array}\right.\text{. }

(60)

The two unkowns of the problem are
U^{1}_{\Delta t}
and
\Delta x
. The discretization and constraints are summed up Figure
16
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 5.0pt\text{If }b=0,&\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\vskip 5.0ptU^{1}_{\Delta t}&=&\dfrac{av\Delta t(3v\Delta t+4)}{3v\Delta t+6}\\

b=0

\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\Delta x=-\frac{2}{3}

\mathcal{P}_{u}

The important behavior to notice is that for the
two-phase
problem, the discretized solution inside the time interval exhibits an interface moving from the left to the right as expected, but in the case of the
one-phase
problem the discretized solution inside the time interval exhibits an interface moving from the right to the left. This behavior appears if the node chose to be moved on the interface
\Gamma^{h}_{n+1}
was belonging to the empty region (defined section
2
) at
t=t_{n}
. In other words this behavior occurs if
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{u^{h}_{n}}
. The mesh velocity model described by Eq. (
56
) fails to represent the behavior of the interface for the
one-phase
problem and therefore requires modification.

The choice we are making for
\mathbf{w}
is:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{W}^{p}(t)=(\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})-\mathbf{X}_{n}(p))\delta_{t_{n}}(t)+\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}&\text{, if $p\in\mathcal{Q}_{u^{h}_{n}}$}\\
&\text{and $q_{p}^{0}$ defined Eq.~\eqref{eq:qp}}\\
\mathbf{W}^{p}(t)=\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(p)}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}&\text{, else.}\\
\end{array}\right.

(62)

where
\delta_{t_{n}}
is a Dirac distribution in time centered in
t_{n}
. This expression for
\mathbf{w}
can be simplified when taking into account that the problem is a
one-phase
problem. Indeed, the weak formulation of Eq. (
57
) discretized in space writes:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}^{h}u^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t&=&-\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bar{u}^{h}\mathbf{v}.\nabla u^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t-\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}\mathbf{w}\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t.\\
\end{array}

(63)

The term of Eq. (
63
) involving the mesh speed
\mathbf{w}
can be developed as:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}\mathbf{w}\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t&=&\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W}^{i}(t).\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\,\text{d}t\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}u^{h}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W}^{i}(t).\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}\Omega\\
\end{array}

(64)

Examining the term of the sum corresponding to the node
i=p\in\mathcal{Q}_{u^{h}_{n}}
and utilizing expression Eq. (
62
), we derive:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}u^{h}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W}^{p}(t).\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}\Omega&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{h}_{n}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})-\mathbf{X}_{n}(p)).\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}\Omega\\
&+&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}u^{h}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}.\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}\Omega\\
\end{array}

(65)

As
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{u^{h}_{n}}
, we know that:

u^{h}(\mathbf{X})\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})=0\text{, }\forall\mathbf{X}\in\Omega

(66)

Using Eq. (
66
) in Eq. (
65
) it remains:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}u^{h}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{W}^{p}(t).\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}\Omega=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}u^{h}\phi_{p}(\mathbf{X})\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}.\nabla\bar{u}^{h}\,\text{d}t\,\text{d}\Omega

(67)

The choice for
\mathbf{w}
described Eq. (
62
) is therefore equivalent to the simplier:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{W}^{p}(t)=\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(q_{p}^{0})}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}\text{, if $p\in\mathcal{Q}_{u^{h}_{n}}$}\\
\mathbf{W}^{p}(t)=\dfrac{\mathbf{X}_{n+1}(p)-\mathbf{X}_{n}(p)}{t_{n+1}-t_{n}}\text{, else.}\\
\end{array}\right.

(68)

Using this expression for
\mathbf{w}
, we now obtain the behavior of the solution inside the time interval illustrated Figure

.
