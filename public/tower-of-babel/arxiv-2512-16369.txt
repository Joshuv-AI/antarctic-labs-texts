A first look at common RPKI publication practices
Moritz Müller-Brus, Lisa Bruder, Caspar Schutijser, Ralph Koning

Abstract
. The RPKI ( RPKI ) is crucial for securing the routing system of the Internet. With the RPKI , owners of Internet resources can make cryptographically backed claims, for example about the legitimate origin of their IP space. Thousands of networks use this information to detect malicious or accidental route hijacks. The RPKI consists out of 100 distributed repositories. However, public reports claim that some of these repositories are unreliable. A current Internet-Draft suggests best practices on how to operate these repositories, with the goal to improve deployment quality. Inspired by this draft, we take a first look at the operational practices of repositories of the RPKI . We mainly focus on the distribution of RPKI information. We find that there is a wide variety in deployment practices, of which some might risk the availability of parts of the information in the RPKI . This study creates a baseline for measuring the maturity of RPKI repositories in the future.



1.
Introduction

The
RPKI
enables holders of Internet resources,
like IP address space, to make cryptographically backed claims
about their assets
(
Lepinski and Kent 2012
)
. Currently, the
RPKI
mostly contains claims
about IP prefixes and the networks from which those prefixes may be
announced in so called
ROA
. Networks run
RP
(
RP
) software to fetch and validate these objects and then base their
BGP
(
BGP
) routing decisions on the validation results.
At the moment, almost 60% of all IP prefixes are covered by
a
ROA

(
National Institute of Standards and Technology (2025) NIST
)
,
thereby protecting these
prefixes against malicious attacks and misconfiguration. The validation of
ROA
is also on the rise
(
Li et al. 2023
)
.

The
RPKI
relies on “a distributed repository
system for storing and disseminating” its objects
(
Lepinski and Kent 2012
)
. Most of these objects,
like
ROA
, are managed and distributed by one of the five
RIR

(AFRINIC, APNIC, ARIN, LACNIC, RIPE NCC). At the same time, the
RPKI

enables resources holders to operate their own
CA
(
CA
). Then, resource owners can decide to
publish the objects themselves or, if provided, rely on the
publication service by their
RIR
. The
RIR
or the resource owner
needs to make sure that the objects can be retrieved by
RP
using
the HTTP based
RRDP
(
RRDP
) or rsync.

Besides the repositories by the 5 RIRs, 95 repositories are
currently listed in the
RPKI
. In recent years, operators of
RP

software frequently complained about repositories that were
unreliable
(
Li et al. 2025
)
.
Aside from putting extra burden on
RP

software, unavailable repositories can also negatively impact the
security of Internet resources. If a repository is unavailable for too
long (e.g. several hours), objects published by it can not be updated and potentially expire,
thereby effectively removing the protection by the RPKI. In certain
scenarios, Internet resources covered by objects could even
become unavailable for (parts of) the Internet.

This, likely, motivated
the authors of
RPKI Publication Server Best Current Practices

to formulate a set of practices that operators of publication servers
should consider implementing. The IETF working group “SIDR Operations” has
adopted this document as an Internet-Draft
(
Bruijnzeels et al. 2025
)
.

Inspired by this effort, our goal is to measure the current
RPKI
publication
practices. Thereby, we also create a baseline measurement of the
implementation of the
BCP
.
Note that the goal of this study is not find out
why
some
operators run their own publication server, nor to measure the performance of
the publication servers. Our contributions are as follows:

•

Provide a first look at
RPKI
publication practices, showing that there are only a few common practices most operators implement.

•

Create a baseline measurement for tracking the impact of the recommended best practices.

•

Highlight widely diverging practices between operators, showing that there is not
the
best way to publish objects in the
RPKI
.

2.
RPKI Basics

In the
RPKI
, signed objects are grouped together into publication points based on the signing
CA
. Every group of objects is accompanied by a manifest that lists all
signed objects and a
CRL
(
CRL
) that lists all objects that have been revoked.

The 5
RIR
serve as trust anchors in the
RPKI
and the private keys associated with their
CA
certificates are used to sign certificates for subordinate
CA
as well as resource certificates
(
Labs 2025b
)
.
Resource holders can let their
RIR
manage the signing of their objects (so-called hosted
RPKI
). Alternatively, it is possible to request a delegated
CA
certificate
from an
RIR
and manage signing independently (delegated
RPKI
)
(
Labs 2025b
)
.

Objects also need to be hosted for
RP
to retrieve them. Objects stored in repositories in the
RPKI
are distributed
using rsync and
RRDP
servers
(
Bruijnzeels et al. 2017
)
. When resource holders make use of the “hosted” RPKI service of their
RIR
, the objects are hosted through the
RIR
’s
RRDP
and rsync servers.
However, objects can also be distributed through self-hosted rsync and
RRDP
servers
(
Labs 2025b
)
.

RRDP
is an HTTP based protocol. A CA certificate contains a references to a notification file, which is downloaded by an
RP
, e.g. every 10 minutes.
This file includes a reference to a snapshot file, which contains the full content of the repository.
Additionally, the notification file can contain a list of delta files, listing incremental changes to the repository
(
Bruijnzeels et al. 2017
)
.
An
RP
checks if it can use the delta files to bring its cache up to date or if it needs to download the full snapshot file.
If the
RRDP
server is not reachable, the rsync server can be used to retrieve the objects.

If neither the
RRDP
nor the rsync server are reachable for a prolonged time, no new certificates and signed objects can be distributed.
Then, the objects signed by the
CA
could expire. Because current practice does not advice operators to drop routes with a status of “unknown”,
the impact of an expired
ROA
object is limited
(
Durand et al. 2015
)
. However, in specific situations where a prefix is covered by two
ROA
associated with different
ASN
,
an announcement could become invalid if one of the
ROA
objects expires
(
Hlavacek et al. 2023
;
Labs 2025a
)
.

3.
Methodology

The Internet-Draft “RPKI Publication Server Best Current Practices” (I-D)
(
Bruijnzeels et al. 2025
)
describes best current
practices for the operation of a publication, rsync and
RRDP
servers based on operational experience. This methodology applies to version 05 of the I-D.
In this section, we briefly describe the goal of the recommended practices and
the measurements that we performed, grouped by the section in the I-D they
appear in. Each measurement has a short name that we use to refer to the
measurement in the paper, printed in (
bold
). We refer the reader to the I-D for a more detailed description and motivation for each recommended practice.

We use
RP
software rpki-client v9.6 to download the current data published in the
RPKI
. The
rpki-client cache serves as a basis for our analysis. It contains all files
that have a valid signature path. Our measurement vantage point, located in a
well connected network , supports both IPv4 and IPv6 and we
fetch the content over whatever protocol preferred by rpki-client. For
each
CA
certificate we encounter in the
RPKI
data we extract
the
URL
of the
RRDP
and rsync servers from the Subject Information
Access extension fields.
1
1

1

e.g.
https://rrdp.ripe.net/notification.xml
and

rsync://rpki.ripe.net/repository/DEFAULT/
for the
CA

cert. of the RIPE NCC

We use the
URL
of the
RRDP
server as the identifier for each repository.

For the most part of this paper, we focus on the measurements run on December 6 2025.
We validated that our measurement are representative for normal
repository behaviour by performing two additional measurements two days before and two
days after the initial measurement.

3.1.
Measurements

Hostnames (I-D Section 5)

The I-D recommends the use of different hostnames for the publication,
RRDP
and rsync server to allow for dedicated
infrastructure.
For each
CA
certificate, we compare if the
FQDN
(
FQDN
) of the
URL
of the
RRDP
and the
URL
of the rsync server are different
at different levels of the DNS hierarchy. We compare the difference of the
hostname (left most label,
Different hostnames
), the 2nd level domain name (the first label left
of the
TLD
(
TLD
) - we define the
TLD
using the public suffix list,
Different subdomains
), and the
TLD

(
Different TLDs
).

Also, we verify if the A and AAAA records of the hostnames
are validly signed using
DNSSEC
(
DNSSEC
)
(
DNSSEC (RRDP)
and
DNSSEC (rsync)
).

IP Address Space and Autonomous Systems (I-D Section 6)

If a repository hosts
ROA
that cover the IP address space used for its own
RRDP
and rsync server,
there is a risk of the repository making itself unreachable, without the ability to publish remediating objects.
The I-D therefore recommends that setups like this should be avoided.
To analyse the dependency of a repositories’ reachability on its own
ROA
objects (
Reachability ind. of ROA in repo
), we loop through the ROAs distributed by the repository and look for a
ROA
that covers the prefix(es) we determined for the service endpoints. If we find a matching ROA, we mark this prefix as being dependent on its own repository. A repository is deemed as independent of its own endpoints if none of the IP address space is covered by a
ROA
in the repository.

Furthermore, the
BCP
recommends hosting the
RRDP
and rsync server in different networks.
We query the A and AAAA for the
URI
of the servers and determine the matching routed prefix for each IP address.
We do this using the Network Info API of RIPEstat
(
RIPE Network Coordination Centre (2025) RIPE NCC
)
. Based on this we determine if the
RRDP
and rsync servers
of a repository are hosted in different networks (
RRDP and rsync in diff. networks
). We mark this as fulfilled when the repository uses different prefixes for both servers and both IP address families. Having no IP addresses of a family type for one of the servers does not count as a different network.

Same origin URIs (I-D Section 7.1)

The I-D remarks that as defined in RFC9674, the “uri” attributes in notification files need to refer to the same
URI
for all listed snapshot and delta files
(
Snijders 2024
)
.
We download the content of each notification file and analyse the
URI
in
it pointing to delta and snapshot files. If they point to the same host as
the notification file
URI
, we deem the
RRDP
server as compliant to
this requirement (
Same origin
). In addition, we check if a HTTP
redirect occurs when fetching the content.

Endpoint Protection (I-D Section 7.2)

This
BCP
recommends the use of access control to protect
RRDP
endpoints.
Here, we send an HTTP request to each
URL
pointing towards a notification file and record the returned HTTP status
using the following methods: GET, OPTIONS, HEAD, POST, PUT, PATCH, DELETE (
Endpoint protection
).
The RFCs defining the RRDP protocol do not specify which methods and parameters
are mandatory. We assume that a web server implements endpoint protection if
it only answers with status code 200 for the methods GET and HEAD and with a
status code between 400 and 599 for the methods PATCH and DELETE.
2
2

2

This is in
accordance with Nmap (
https://nmap.org/nsedoc/scripts/http-methods.html
).

Bandwidth and Data Usage (I-D Section 7.3)

This practice recommends to support compression to save bandwidth.
Our test consists of downloading each notification file.
If the server serves the notification file in a compressed form
(i.e., when the
Content-Encoding
header is set to something like
gzip
or
deflate
),
we mark the
RRDP
server as compliant with this requirement (
Compression
).

Content Availability (I-D Section 7.4)

The I-D’s authors recommend to use a
CDN
(
CDN
) to achieve high availability.
We use two methods to determine whether a
RRDP
Server relies on a
CDN
to
distribute its content (
CDN
).

First, we test if the IP addresses of
the
RRDP
Server are announced using
BGP
anycast using a list IP prefixes compiled by LaCeS
(
Hendriks et al. 2025
)
.
We use a list of IP prefixes compiled by LaCeS
(
Hendriks et al. 2025
)
.
Second,
we test whether the IP addresses of the
RRDP
server are announced by

AS
(
AS
) numbers that are categorized as
CDN
by bgp.tools
(
BGP.Tools 2025
)
.
To find out which
AS
(or
AS
) announces a particular IP address,
we use a data set provided by iptoasn.com
(
IPtoASN 2025
)
.

For the most part of the paper, we classify an endpoint as relying on a
CDN
if either of the two methods return a positive result.

Limit Notification File Size (I-D Section 7.5)

This
BCP
recommends practices to reduce the amount of data a
RP
needs to retrieve.
For each repository, we collect the size of the notification, snapshot and
delta file. We take the sum
of the size of each delta files listed in the corresponding
notification file (
Snapshot
>
Deltas
). To save resources of the

RRDP
server, we attempt to look up the file size indicated in the response
header and stop the download afterwards whenever possible.

Also, we measure how long the
RRDP
server keeps individual delta files (
Delta older than 4h
) by downloading each notification file every 30 seconds for 24 hours. We record when we have
observed each delta file for the first and for the last time. We use this
information also to measure the
update frequency of the publication server (
Delta frequency larger than 1min
). Note that we can only identify
update frequencies of 30 seconds or longer due to our measurement frequency.

Manifest and CRL Update Times (I-D Section 7.6)

The I-D discusses up- and downsides of longer and shorter validity periods for manifests and
CRL
.
It does not give a clear recommendation. To understand which validity times are common, we measure
the average for each repository. We collect the update times from manifests and
CRL
(
Manifest/CRL validity
). For both, we extract the “next update” and “this update” fields and report the average difference between the two.

3.2.
Not measured practices and ethical considerations

We focus on practices that we can measure externally, without the need of
ingesting content into the RPKI, thus excluding the publication server. Other aspects can
only be observed in case of problems at the CA (e.g. I-D Section 4.4 Data
Loss). Also, we do not measure Notification File Timing (I-D Section 7.7.1) and
L4 Load-Balancing (I-D Section 7.7.2) as those require frequent, distributed
measurements with low chance of actually finding mis-behaviour.
Finally, we do not measure the implementation of the rsync server. Some
recommendations are not visible for an external observer (e.g. parts of Load
Balancing and Testing, I-D Section 8.3) or require highly frequent
measurements, possibly draining the resources of the server (e.g. Consistent
Content, I-D Section 8.1).

In our measurements, we do not put an unreasonable burden on the measured
infrastructure. One measurement consists of 2 runs of the rpki-client.
Overall, we perform 3 measurements spread over 6 days. The collected data is
public and does not contain personal identifiable information.

IPv4 and IPv6

Repositories use a mean of 3.24 IPv4 addresses and a mean of 3.14 IPv6 addresses to host their repository (rsync and

total size of delta files never exceeds 13.554 KB for 90% of the RRDP servers. See for more details

The RRDP server of ARIN serve the largest snapshot file (623,152 KB),
while a server of
AWS

For the majority of the repositories, the size of the snapshot file stays
stable over the course of our measurements (standard
deviation
phi<26~KB
for
90% of all repositories). However, the total size of the delta files varies
stronger (for 17% of the repositories
phi>100~KB
). This
is mostly the case for repositories with presumably high activity, like the

.

For 44 (49%) repositories, the sum of delta files is always larger than the
snapshot file (5 times larger on average). These repositories thus do not follow the
recommendation. Again, the repositories by
AWS
stand out, with the total size

times. Instead, the
RRDP

AWS

74 (82%) of all repositories keep delta files for at least 4 hours, thus follow
the recommended practice. 6 never follow this recommendation and 10 do
not show consistent behaviour. 58% of all repositories always keep their delta
files for at least 1 day (the maximum time period we measured). Finally, 83 (92%) of the measured repositories always publish new delta files less
frequently than once every minute.

5.
Discussion

5.1.
Common ground and uncommon practices

There is some common ground on how to operate a repository,
even before the best practices document is published. From
Table

1
,
we can derive that serving the content from the
same origin and enabling some basic form of endpoint protection,
are two configuration knobs most operators can agree on.

In contrast, some suggested practices are rarely
implemented. For example, almost none of the operators choose for different
TLD
for
the rsync and
RRDP
servers and also many domain names of
RRDP
endpoints
are not DNSSEC-signed.

Some practices are mostly implemented by repositories that host many
ROA

payloads, for example relying on different hostnames and distributing
RRDP
servers using
CDN
.
While the use of a
CDN
might be perceived as unnecessary for many
small repositories, the security of their network resources depends on
the availability of their repositories. Putting effort into highly
available
RRDP
servers should therefore not be underestimated.

5.2.
Potentially problematic practices

We observed some practices that should be adjusted because they may
cause unnecessary burden for
RP
or put the availability of the
repository at an unnecessary risk.

RRDP servers providing larger snapshot and delta files and their
clients could benefit the most from HTTP compression. However, as discussed
in
Section

4.4
, some RRDP servers do not support compression and
we find that the median snapshot and delta file size that these servers serve
is higher than of the servers that do support compression.

Also, the I-D does not give a clear recommendation on manifest/CRL validity but remarks that longer and shorter durations have certain up- and downsides.

One downside of short validity periods is that
ROA
might become invalid while a repository is unreachable.
Of the 3 repositories with an average validity time of less than 1 day (6 hours), 2 distribute less than 10
ROA
payloads.
One however,
rpki.ccnic.cn
, distributes over 2000 ROA payloads and the short validity time could lead to
ROA
becoming invalid rather quickly if the repository becomes unavailable for example due to unexpected operational issues.

The high average validity periods of 15 and 31 days that we measured for 2 repositories
5
5

5

Seen for
rpki-repository.nic.ad.jp
and
akane.maru.co.jp
respectively.
makes these more vulnerable to potential replay attacks with old
ROA
objects
(
Labs 2025a
)
.

5.3.
Alternatives to
BCP

As also noted in the I-D, there might be good reasons to diverge from the suggested practices.

For example,
not using a
CDN
does not mean a service cannot have a good level of availability.
ARIN’s server, for instance, is classified as not using a
CDN
.
However,
the hostname of that server (
rrdp.arin.net
) resolves to 6 IPv4 addresses and 6 IPv6 addresses.
For both address families, 3 of the 6 IP addresses are announced by
AS
394018,
and the other 3 are announced by
AS
393225,
also connected at different
IXP
.
As such,
in terms of geographical location and connectivity to the Internet,
the 2 groups of servers serving
rrdp.arin.net
seem somewhat independent from each other.

Also, there might be good arguments for not signing the
DNS
(
DNS
) records of
the publication servers with
DNSSEC
.
DNS
spoofing would only have limited effect since the integrity and
authenticity of the information in RPKI itself is cryptographically protected.

5.4.
Measurement limitations

We faced several limitations. First, there is not a definite way of measuring compliance with
Endpoint protection
and the I-D does not provide guidelines.
We picked an interpretation of this recommendation that can be measured (see
Section

3.1
), but acknowledge that the test could cover more scenarios. Failing our test does not mean that the configuration of the server is wrong.

Also, while we did observe that a substantial amount of repositories make use of different hostnames for their
RRDP
and rsync servers, those hostnames could still rely on the same underlying
DNS
infrastructure (
Different hostnames
).

Next, the use of different IP addresses does not necessarily tell us about the redundancy and independency of the underlying infrastructure (
RRDP and rsync in diff. networks
). The different IPs could still lead to the same physical infrastructure.

Finally, some repositories might make use of different networks for their endpoints, where some are covered by a
ROA
in different repositories (
Reachability ind. of ROA in repo
). We did not measure if there are any
ROA
covering (part of) the IP address space in other repositories. These could be used to distribute remediating updates in case a
ROA
in the repository makes its own origination invalid and with that becomes temporarily unreachable.

6.
Related Work

The performance of
RPKI
publication servers has been subject of study before.
Hlavacek et al.
(
Hlavacek et al. 2022
)
performed a measurement study into the resilience of
RP
,
identifying
DNS
as a weak link in the
RPKI
system.
In another study, Hlavacek et al.
(
Hlavacek et al. 2023
)
investigated ways in which
RP
software can be attacked by malicious publication points.
Our work complements Hlavacek et al.’s work because we look at
RPKI
repositories as opposed to
RP
software.

Huston
(
Huston 2025
)
discusses the resilience of the
RPKI
, but does not contain measurements.
Huston concludes that
RPKI
’s resilience is at an acceptable level of resilience at the moment, but needs to be re-evaluated if
RPKI
were to get more or new roles.

Two policy documents at RIPE (published, not yet implemented
(
Snijders and Hilliard 2025
)
)
and APNIC (waiting for endorsement by the Executive Council
(
Snijders 2025
)
)
propose the “revocation of persistently non-functional

RPKI

CA
”. We also encountered
CA
of which the
RRDP
and rsync servers were unreachable or of
which its content was not cryptographically
valid, but in general we focus on the behaviour of the functional CAs and
their publication servers.

Recently, Schulmann et al.
(
Schulmann and Vogel 2025
)
proposed a method to
fingerprint
RPKI
repositories. While not the goal of our research, some of
the implementation details studied in this paper could be useful for
fingerprinting repositories (e.g. the chosen maximum number of delta files
or the update frequency).

Currently, a new protocol called Erik is
being developed in the
IETF
(
IETF
)
(
Snijders et al. 2025
)
.
Erik adds an additional distribution layer which could improve the availability of information in
the
RPKI
, rendering some of the recommended practices in the I-D
(
Bruijnzeels et al. 2025
)
less urgent.

7.
Conclusion

Even before the “RPKI Publication Server Best Current
Practices” I-D has become an official RFC, the major share of objects in
the
RPKI
are already published by servers implementing many of the
suggested best practices. At the same time, there is still plenty room for
improvement, e.g. when increasing resilience against attacks and outages in
the
DNS
. Lastly, there are operational practices that diverge
significantly from the suggested best practices (e.g. the total size of the
published delta files). Future work could study the motivation of these
design choices. As soon as the RFC is published, measuring the uptake of
the suggested best practices at publication servers should be considered.
