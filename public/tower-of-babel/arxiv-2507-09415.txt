Abstract
We study a principal-agent model involving a large population of heterogeneously interacting agents. By extending the existing methods, we find the optimal contracts assuming a continuum of agents, and show that, when the number of agents is sufficiently large, the optimal contracts for the problem with a continuum of agents are near-optimal for the finite agents problem. We make comparative statistics and provide numerical simulations to analyze how the agents’ connectivity affects the principal’s value, the effort of the agents, and the optimal contracts.



1
Introduction

Principal-agent models describe the problem of finding optimal incentives in delegated tasks. A principal, usually playing the role of a higher authority, delegates a project to an agent who, in exchange, receives a payment from the principal (i.e., the contract).
In many situations, the nature of the task prevents the principal from observing the effort that the agent is putting into the project. This lack of transparency makes finding an optimal contract a challenging problem. The asymmetry of information between the two parties, known as moral hazard, is a well-studied phenomenon by economists.

The first paper on this matter in continuous time is written by Holmström and Milgrom
[
HM87
]
, who show that, when the agent maximizes the expected exponential utility of his payoff, it is optimal for the principal to offer a linear contract. Two decades after the finding of Holmström and Milgrom, Sannikov
[
San08
]
proposed a new model in continuous time where the contracts consist of a continuous incentive and a terminal lump-sum payment transacted at a time established by the principal. In addition to the extensive modeling contributions, Sannikov introduced a new method to approach principal-agent problems based on reducing them to a stochastic control framework, allowing the use of dynamic programming techniques. Thereafter, by combining Sannikov’s approach and the use of backward stochastic differential equations (BSDE), Cvitanic-Possamaï-Touzi
[
CPT18
]
provided a general method connecting principal-agent problems to stochastic control theory. The approach of Cvitanic et al. has set the basis for multiple extensions. We highlight the work by Elie and Possamaï
[
ÉP19
]
in which the authors study a contracting problem between a Principal and multiple agents in competition.

On the other hand, mean-field game theory studies stochastic games involving a large number of agents. The main idea is that, when the number of agents is large, the individual actions of every agent have a negligible impact on the stochastic environment. This fact justifies heuristically that the
N
-player game can be approximated only by considering the interactions between a representative agent and the population distribution. The previous argument was rigorously proved by Lasry and Lions
[
LL06a
,
LL06b
,
LL07
]
, and Huang, Malhamé and Caines
[
HMC06
,
HCM07
]
independently by using partial differential equations (PDEs). Since then, the theory of mean-field games has flourished, producing a large number of theoretical and applied results.

A crucial limitation of standard mean-field games is that it is assumed that the agents are symmetric. This situation does not hold in many realistic applications. To address this issue, Parise and Ozdaglar
[
PO19
]
, and Caines and Huang
[
CH21
]
introduced the notion of graphon mean-field games. In their model, a continuum of heterogeneous agents interact via a graphon, a function
G:[0,1]^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
representing the connectivity of the agents. The articles

extend the results from Caines and Huang
[

[

The main advantage of their formulation is to reduce a graphon mean-field game to a standard mean-field game.

The first paper connecting principal-agent problems with mean-field games is authored by Élie, Mastrolia, and Possamaï

We note that in all the works mentioned, it is assumed that the agents interact homogeneously. The latter is crucial, as the previous works are built upon mean-field game theory.

In most practical applications, agents interact in a heterogeneous manner. From this perspective, it is essential to consider the agents’ connectivity when designing optimal incentives, which may need to be tailored to the specific characteristics of each agent. In this scenario, we derive a model with a principal and multiple agents in charge of heterogeneously dependent projects. We describe the interdependence of the agents through an interaction function
G:[0,1]^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
indicating the influence of the agents on each other. We present two versions of the model: a version with
N
agents and a version with a continuum of agents. In each case, we compute the respective optimal contracts and the equilibrium response efforts of the agents (Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
). Furthermore, we establish that the
N
agents problem converges to the problem with a continuum of agents as
N\rightarrow\infty
in the following sense:

i)

The agents’ Nash equilibrium efforts converge as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Theorem
6
.

ii)

The optimal contracts and the principal’s value converge as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Theorem
7
.

iii)

The optimal contract for a continuum of agents generates a near-optimal contract for the
N
agents problem when
N
is large enough. By near-optimal, we mean that a) the contract satisfies the agents’ reservation constraints; b) the contract induces a unique Nash equilibrium effort in the
N
agents problem; c) the contract is
\mathcal{O}(1/N)
-optimal from the principal’s viewpoint. Theorem
8
.

Moreover, we show that the optimal contracts are designed to incentivize more influential agents to exert greater effort (Theorem
3
). In addition, we show that the optimal contracts and agents’ optimal efforts are stable to perturbations in the agents’ connectivity (Theorem
4
, Theorem
5
).

To solve the version of our model with a continuum of agents (Theorem
2
), the approach of Élie et al.
[
ÉMP19
]
needs to be adapted, as their method relies on the homogeneity of the agents. To do so, we use a labeling formulation similar to that of Lacker and Soret
[
LS22
]
. The key idea is to consider the agents’ type as an additional state variable of the problem and model the stochastic game played by a continuum of agents by considering a mean-field game involving a representative agent and the population’s distribution
\mu
. The main difference from a standard mean-field game is that
\mu
denotes the joint distribution of the output process and the agents’ type. Using this formulation of the agents’ problem, we apply
[
ÉMP19
]
to reduce the general principal-agent problem to a mean-field control problem, from which we derive the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation and its corresponding verification lemma (Lemma
2
). Subsequently, we use Lemma
2
in our model to find the optimal contracts with a continuum of heterogeneous agents. We mention that our verification lemma can be applied to general models with a continuum of heterogeneously interacting agents. The latter has numerous potential applications in areas such as finance, macroeconomics, corporate governance, and epidemiology, where contracting a large population of heterogeneous agents is a fundamental problem.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section

Furthermore, we make comparative statistics and numerical simulations to analyze how the agents’ connectivity affects the principal’s value, the effort of the agents, and the optimal contracts. Then, we show that the contracts are stable under perturbations in the agents’ interactions. Finally, in Subsection
3.3

the
N

N\rightarrow\infty
.

2
Verification lemma for the principal-agent problem

In this section, we derive a general procedure to address contracting problems with a continuum of heterogeneous agents. The method presented here provides us with the methodological tools to solve the model introduced in Section
3
. Inspired by Lacker and Soret
[
LS22
]
, we describe the agents’ stochastic game as a standard mean-field game played by a representative agent and a population distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{2}([0,1]\times\mathcal{C})
indicating the joint distribution of the agents’ type and output process. From that point, we apply the methodology introduced in Élie et al.
[
ÉMP19
]
to reduce the principal’s problem to a mean-field control problem.

2.2
Connection to mean-field control

The principal-agent problem described in equation (
2

[
ÉMP19
]

using backward stochastic differential equations (BSDE). This parameterization allows us to characterize, given a contract

Firstly, we define the agent’s Hamiltonian as follows.

\displaystyle h_{t}(u,x,y,{\mu},z,\chi,a)

\displaystyle:=zb_{t}(u,x,\mu,a)+u_{a}(t,u,x,\mu,\chi)-c_{t}(u,x,\mu,a)+k_{t}(u,x,\mu,a)y,

\displaystyle H_{t}(u,x,y,{\mu},z,\chi,a)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}}h_{t}(u,x,y,\mu,z,\chi,a),

where
h:[0,T]\times[0,1]\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}^{2}\left([0,1]\times\mathcal{C}\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.

Assumption 3
.

There exists a unique measurable mapping
\hat{\alpha}:[0,T]\times[0,1]\times\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}^{2}([0,1]\times\mathcal{C})\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}

3
Main results

We introduce a contracting model that involves a principal and multiple competitive agents in charge of heterogeneously interdependent projects. Our model captures how to incentivize agents with different levels of influence. We present two versions of the model: the
N

i)

The agents’ optimal efforts converge as

ii)

iii)

The optimal contract for a continuum of agents generates a near-optimal contract for the

3.2.1
Two examples with a closed-form solution

We study two concrete cases where we can find a closed-form solution to the contracting problem. Those examples allow us to formulate the hypothesis that it is optimal for the principal to incentivize more influential agents to exert more effort, which is consistent with a qualitative analysis. The previous statement is formally proved in Theorem
3
.

Example 1:
Symmetric agents with different levels of influence. We assume that
G(u,v)=\hat{G}(v)
, for all
u,v\in[0,1]
. Then, the output process satisfies

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)x\mu(dv,dx)+\alpha_{t}\right)dt+dW^{\alpha,\mu}_{t}.

The agents are homogeneous but have a different level of influence on each other, represented by the term
\hat{G}(u),u\in[0,1]
. Applying Theorem
2
, we obtain that the solution to following system characterizes the solution to the contract problem.

\displaystyle\dot{Q}(t,u)=-\hat{G}(u)Q(t,u),

\displaystyle Q(t,u)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(u).

Note that the solution to the previous system is given by
Q(t,u)=\exp\left(\hat{G}(u)(T-t)\right)
,
(t,u)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]
.

Using again Theorem
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}(t,u)=\exp\left(\hat{G}(u)(T-t)\right).

Note that, if
0<\hat{G}(u)<\hat{G}(v)
, for
u,v\in[0,1]
, we have
\alpha^{*}(t,u)<\alpha^{*}(t,v)
,
t\in[0,T)
.

The latter shows that the optimal contracts are designed to incentivize more influential agents to exert a higher effort, which is consistent with our intuition. The effort of influential agents influences the majority of the projects.

Example 2:
Heterogeneous agents with the same level of influence. We assume that
G(u,v)=\hat{G}(u)
, for all
u,v\in[0,1]
. Then, the output process satisfies

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\left(\hat{G}(U)\int_{0}^{1}x\mu(dv,dx)+\alpha_{t}\right)dt+dW^{\alpha,\mu}_{t}.

The agents have the same level of influence on each other, but different sensitivities, represented by the term
\hat{G}(u),u\in[0,1]
. Applying Theorem
2
, we obtain that the solution to the contract problem is characterized by the solution to the system

\displaystyle\dot{Q}(t,u)=-\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)Q(t,v)dv,

\displaystyle Q(t,u)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(u).

Note that
Q
is constant in
u\in[0,1]
. Next, we define
P(t):=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)Q(t,v)dv
. The previous linear system can be rewritten as a two-dimensional ODE

\displaystyle\dot{Q}(t,u)

\displaystyle=-P(t),

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)dv\right)P(t),

\displaystyle Q(T,u)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(u),

\displaystyle P(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)dv.

The solution to the previous system is given by
Q(t,u)=e^{\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)dv(T-t)}
. Using again Theorem
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}(t,u)=e^{\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(v)dv(T-t)}.

The latter shows that, from the principal’s perspective, it is optimal to incentivize agents to exert the same effort, which is increasing with respect to the total aggregated influence
\int_{0}^{1}\hat{G}(u)du
. This observation is compatible with our intuition, as all the agents have the same level of influence on each other, and a higher aggregated influence will encourage them to work harder.

3.2.2
Comparative statistics

In this section, we develop a deeper understanding of how the optimal incentives depend on the agents’ influence on each other.
We prove the hypothesis that, from the principal’s viewpoint, it is optimal to incentivize the influential agents to exert a higher effort. To do so, we formalize the notion of influence.

Definition 3
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in[0,1]
be two arbitrary types of agents.
We say that
u_{1}
is at most as influential as
u_{2}
if and only if

\displaystyle G(v,u_{1})\leq G(v,u_{2}),\quad\forall v\in[0,1].

The following theorem proves that the agents with a higher level of influence are incentivized to exert a higher effort.

Theorem 3
.

Let
G:[0,1]\times[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a nonnegative interaction function that satisfies Assumption
4
, and
u_{1},u_{2}\in[0,1]
.
Suppose that
u_{1}
is at most as influential as
u_{2}
. Then,
\alpha^{*}(t,u_{1})\leq\alpha^{*}(t,u_{2})
, for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof:

Using Theorem
2
, we have that for
i\in\{1,2\}
,
\alpha^{*}(\cdot,u_{i})=Q(\cdot,u_{i})
, where
Q
solves the linear system

\displaystyle\dot{Q}(t,u)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}G(v,u)Q(t,v)dv,

\displaystyle Q(T,u)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}(u).

The latter system can be rewritten as

\displaystyle Q(t,u)=1+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G(v,u)Q(s,v)dvds.

Note that,
Q(t,u)\geq 0
, for all
(t,u)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]
by the nonnegativity of
G
. Then,

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}(t,u_{1})

\displaystyle=Q(t,u_{1})

\displaystyle=1+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G(v,u_{1})Q(s,v)dvds

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G(v,u_{2})Q(s,v)dvds

\displaystyle=\alpha^{*}(t,u_{2}),

which proves the statement.

{G}

{G}:[0,1]^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)-Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)|\leq C\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}

Proof:

We start by recalling that
Q_{G_{1}}
, and
Q_{G_{2}}
solve respectively the systems

\displaystyle Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G_{1}(u,v)Q_{G_{1}}(s,v)dvds,

\displaystyle Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G_{2}(u,v)Q_{G_{2}}(s,v)dvds,

for all
(t,u)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]
.
Then, we obtain by adding and subtracting
\int_{t}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}G_{1}(u,v)Q_{G_{2}}(s,v)dvds

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)-Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(T\sup_{(t,u)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]}|Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)|\Big)\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle+\left(\|G_{1}\|_{\infty}+\|G_{2}\|_{\infty}\right)\int_{t}^{T}\sup_{u\in[0,1]}|Q_{G_{1}}(s,v)-Q_{G_{2}}(s,v)|ds,\quad t\in[0,T].

Hence, by Grönwall’s inequality there exists
C>0
(depending on
G_{1}
, and
G_{2}
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in[0,1]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)-Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)|\leq C\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}.

The latter proves the theorem.

Using the previous theorem, we show that the distribution of the optimal contracts, in equilibrium, varies continuously with respect to changes in the Agents’ interactions. The following theorem formalizes the result.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose Assumption
5
hold. Let
G_{1},G_{2}:[0,1]^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be two interaction functions satisfying Assumption
4
. There exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
G_{1},G_{2}
, such that

\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{Q(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{1}}\right)^{-1},\mathbb{P}^{{Q}(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)\leq C\left(\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|^{1/2}_{\infty}+\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}\right),

where,
\xi^{*}_{G_{i}}
denotes the optimal contract defined in Theorem
2
with
G_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof:

Using Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{1}:=\mathbb{P}^{{Q}(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{1}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)^{2}dt,\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)^{2}dt\right),

\displaystyle\eta_{2}:=\mathbb{P}^{{Q}(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{2}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)^{2}dt,\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)^{2}dt\right).

Hence, using the closed-form formula for the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
distance of two Gaussian distributions, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)^{2}-Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)^{2}\right)dt\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\left(\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{1}}(t,u)^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}-\left(\int_{0}^{T}Q_{G_{2}}(t,u)^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}.

Finally, using that
\sqrt{x}
is
1/2
-Hölder continuous and Theorem
4
, there exists a constant
C>0
, (depending on
G_{1},G_{2}
) such that

\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{Q(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{1}}\right)^{-1},\mathbb{P}^{{Q}(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=u}\circ\left(\xi^{*}_{G_{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)\leq C\left(\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}+\|G_{1}-G_{2}\|_{\infty}\right).

The latter completes the proof.

3.3
Convergence results

We start by showing that, as the number of agents grows, the equilibrium effort chosen by each agent in the

N
agents problem converges to the heterogeneous mean-field equilibrium. Recall that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
i\in[N]
,
\alpha^{i,N,*}
, and
Q^{i,N}
are defined in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 6
.

Let Assumptions
4
, and
5
hold. Then, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that

\max_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|\alpha^{*}(t,i/N)-\alpha^{i,N,*}(t)\big|=\max_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg|Q\left(t,\frac{i}{N}\right)-Q^{i,N}(t)\bigg|\leq\frac{C}{N}.

Proof:

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in[N]
, and
t\in[0,T]
. We obtain by adding and subtracting terms

\displaystyle\big|Q^{i,N}(t)-Q(t,i/N)\big|

(20)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t}^{T}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G\left(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N}\right)\left(Q(s,\frac{j}{N})-Q^{j,N}(s)\right)\bigg|ds

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}G\left(\frac{i}{N},v\right)Q(s,v)dv-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G\left(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N}\right)Q(s,\frac{j}{N})\bigg|ds

\displaystyle=:I^{i,N}_{1}(t)+I^{i,N}_{2}(t),

where

\displaystyle I^{i,N}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t}^{T}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G\left(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N}\right)\left(Q\left(s,\frac{j}{N}\right)-Q^{j,N}(s)\right)\bigg|ds,

\displaystyle I^{i,N}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t}^{T}\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}G\left(\frac{i}{N},v\right)Q(s,v)dv-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G\left(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N}\right)Q(s,\frac{j}{N})\bigg|ds.

Using Assumption
4
, there exists
C_{1}>0
, such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big|I_{2}^{i,N}(t)\big|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N}.

Hence, taking the supremum on (
20
) over
i\in[N]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\big|Q(t,\frac{i}{N})-Q^{i,N}(t)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N}+\|G\|_{\infty}\int_{t}^{T}\sup_{j\in[N]}\left|Q\left(s,\frac{j}{N}\right)-Q^{j,N}(s)\right|ds,\quad t\in[0,T]

Finally, using Grönwall’s inequality, there exists
C>0
, such that

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg|Q\left(t,\frac{i}{N}\right)-Q^{i,N}_{t}\bigg|\leq\frac{C}{N}.

The latter proves the theorem.

Next, we show that the principal’s value function and the optimal contract for the
N
agents problem converge to the heterogeneous mean-field solution. We introduce the sequence of mutually independent random variables
\Big(\big(X_{0}^{i,N}\big)_{i\in[N]}\Big)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, satisfying
X_{0}^{i,N}\sim\lambda\left(i/N,dx\right)
, for all
i\in[N]
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
. In the following theorem, we assume that the initial value of the agent
i
project in the
N
agent problem is given by
X_{0}^{i,N}\sim\lambda(i/N,dx)
.

Theorem 7
.

Let Assumptions
4
, and
5
hold. Then, there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[(V_{N}^{P}-V^{P})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{N},\quad\mathcal{W}^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}^{Q(\cdot,U)}\big|_{U=\frac{i}{N}}\circ(\xi^{*})^{-1},\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}\circ(\xi^{i,*}_{N})^{-1}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}},

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof:

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We obtain by adding and subtracting terms

\displaystyle V_{N}^{P}-V_{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Q^{i,N}(0)-Q(0,i/N)\right)X^{i,N}_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q(0,i/N)\left(X_{0}^{i,N}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(i/N,dx)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q(0,i/N)\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(i/N,dx)-\int_{0}^{1}Q(0,u)\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(u,dx)du

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q^{i,N}(t)\right)^{2}dt-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}Q(t,u)^{2}dudt

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{a}(\frac{i}{N})+\int_{0}^{1}R_{a}(u)du

\displaystyle=:\sum_{i=1}^{6}{I}^{N}_{i}.

(21)

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Theorem
6
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[|I_{1}^{N}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Q^{i,N}(0)-Q(0,{i/N})\right)X_{0}^{i,N}\bigg|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in[N]}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\big|Q^{i,N}(t)-Q(t,i/N)\big|^{2}\sup_{u\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{2}\lambda(u,dx)

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N}.

Furthermore, using that
\left(X_{0}^{i,N}\right)_{i=1}^{N}
are independent, we obtain using Assumption
4
, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[|I_{2}^{N}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q(0,i/N)\left(X_{0}^{i,N}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(i/N,dx)\right)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{2}}{N}.

(23)

Additionally, by Assumption
4
, there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
, such that

\displaystyle|I_{3}^{N}|=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Q(0,i/N)\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(i/N,dx)-\int_{0}^{1}Q(0,u)\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\lambda(u,dx)du\right|\leq\frac{C_{3}}{N},

for
N
sufficiently large.
Moreover, using Assumptions
4
, and
5
, there exists
C_{4}>0
, such that

\displaystyle|I^{N}_{4}|+|I^{N}_{5}|+|I^{N}_{6}|\leq\frac{C_{4}}{N}.

(24)

Combining the previous inequalities, we obtain that there exists
C>0
, such that

\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}\left[\left(V_{N}^{P}-V^{P}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C}{N}.

It remains to show the bound on the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-distance of the distribution of the optimal contracts. By Theorem
1
, and
2
, we have

\displaystyle\nu_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}^{{Q}}\big|_{U=i/N}\circ\left(\xi^{*}\right)^{-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt,\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt\right),

\displaystyle\nu_{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}\circ\left(\xi^{i,N,*}\right)^{-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}Q^{i,N}(t)^{2}dt,\int_{0}^{T}Q^{i,N}(t)^{2}dt\right).

Applying the well-known expression for the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-distance of two Gaussian distributions, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right)\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q(t,i/N)^{2}-Q^{i,N}(t)^{2}\right)dt\right)^{2}+\left(\left(\int_{0}^{T}Q^{i,N}(t)^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}-\left(\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}

Finally, by Theorem
6
, and the
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder property of the function
\sqrt{x}
, there exists
C>0
such that
\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{N}}.

Next, we show that the heterogeneous mean-field optimal contract presented in Theorem
2
can be used to generate near-optimal contracts for the
N
agents problem when
N
is sufficiently large.

Theorem 8
.

Let Assumptions
4
, and
5
hold. We introduce the measurable mapping
\hat{\xi}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{C}\times C([0,T],\mathcal{P}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}))\mapsto\mathbb{R}
defined by

\hat{\xi}(U,X,\mu):=R_{a}(U)-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}Q(t,U)^{2}+Q(t,U)\int_{[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}}G(U,v)x\mu_{t}(dv,dx)\right)dt+\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,U)dX_{t}.

We define the contracts
\hat{\xi}^{N}:=\left(\hat{\xi}^{1,N},\ldots,\hat{\xi}^{N,N}\right)
given by

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}^{i,N}:=\hat{\xi}\left(\frac{i}{N},X^{i,N},\mu_{N}\right),\quad i\in[N],\quad\mu^{N}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{(\frac{j}{N},X_{t}^{j})}.

(25)

Then,
\hat{\xi}^{N}
satisfies the agents’ reservation constraint, and there exists
C>0
, such that

\big|J^{N}_{p}(\hat{\xi}^{N})-V^{N}_{p}\big|\leq\frac{C}{N},

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof:

Consider the
N
agents problem introduced in Subsection
3.1
. In this case, the agents’ output process
\bar{X}^{N}
satisfies

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i,N}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G_{i,j}^{N}X^{j,N}_{t}+\alpha_{t}^{i}\right)dt+dW^{i,\alpha}_{t},\quad\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}-a.s.\quad i\in[N],

\displaystyle X_{0}^{i,N}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{i,N}.

Assume the principal offers to the agents the contracts
\hat{\xi}^{N}
. In this case, every agent
i\in[N]
maximizes the following objective

\displaystyle J_{a}^{i}(\alpha^{i},\hat{\xi}^{i,N})=R_{a}(i/N)+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q(t,i/N)\alpha^{i}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{t}^{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}Q(t,i/N)^{2}\right)dt\right].

Hence, it is simple to verify that there exists a unique Nash equilibrium given by

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle:=\big(Q(t,{i}/{N})\big)_{i=1}^{N},\quad t\in[0,T].

Moreover,
V_{a}^{i}(\hat{\xi}^{i,N})=R_{a}(i/N)
, implying
\hat{\xi}^{N}\in\Sigma_{a}^{N}
.
The latter induces the probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}
defined as follows

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}{d\mathbb{W}}:=\mathcal{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N})X_{t}^{j,N}+\alpha^{i}_{t}\right)dX^{i,N}_{t}\right)

Hence, in equilibrium, the output process satisfies the following dynamics

\displaystyle dX^{i,N}_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{N}_{i,j}X^{j}_{t}+Q(t,i/N)\right)dt+dW^{i,\hat{Q}^{N}}_{t},\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}-a.s.,\quad i\in[N].

(26)

Furthermore, from the principal’s point of view

\displaystyle J_{P}^{N}(\hat{\xi}^{N})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(X^{i,N}_{T}-\xi^{i,N})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}\left[-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{a}(i/N)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X^{i,N}_{T}-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}Q(t,i/N)^{2}dt\right].

On the other hand, using Theorem
1
, we know that
the contracts
\bar{\xi}^{N,*}:=(\xi^{i,N,*})_{i=1}^{N}
given by

\displaystyle\xi^{i,N,*}:=R_{a}(i/N)-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2}Q^{i,N}(t)^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}G(\frac{i}{N},\frac{j}{N})X^{j,N}_{t}\right)dt+\int_{0}^{T}{Q}^{i,N}(t)dX^{i,N}_{s},\quad i\in[N],

are optimal for the
N
agents problem. Moreover, using again Theorem
1
, the contracts
\bar{\xi}^{N,*}
induce a unique Nash equilibrium of the agents given by

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{N}(t):=\left(Q^{i,N}(t)\right)_{i=1}^{N},\quad t\in[0,T].

Therefore,

\displaystyle V_{p}^{N}

\displaystyle=J_{p}(\bar{\xi}^{N,*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}}\left[-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_{a}(i/N)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{X}_{T}^{i,N}-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\left(Q^{i,N}(t)\right)^{2}dt\right],

where

\displaystyle d{X}^{i,N}_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{N}_{i,j}{X}^{j}_{t}+Q^{i,N}(t)\right)dt+d{W}^{i,\bar{Q}^{N}}_{t},\quad\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}-a.s.

\displaystyle X_{0}^{i}=x_{0}^{i}.

and
\bar{Q}^{N}:=(Q^{1,N},\ldots,Q^{N,N})
solves the linear
N
-dimensional ODE (
10
). Hence,

\displaystyle\big|J_{p}^{N}(\hat{\xi}^{N})-V_{P}^{N}\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{i\in[N]}\left|Q(\frac{i}{N},s)^{2}-Q^{i,N}(s)^{2}\right|ds+\sup_{i\in[N]}\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}[X^{i}_{T}]-\mathbb{E}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}[X^{i}_{T}]\bigg|.

(27)

Next, for all
t\in[0,T]
, we obtain by applying standard arguments

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}\left[X^{i,N}_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}}\left[{X}^{i,N}_{t}\right]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq T\left(\sup_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|Q^{i,N}(t)-Q(t,i/N)\big|\right)

\displaystyle+\|G\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{i\in[N]}\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}\left[X^{i,N}_{s}\right]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}}\left[{X}^{i,N}_{s}\right]\bigg|ds.

Using Grönwall’s inequality, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{i\in[N]}\Big|\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\hat{Q}^{N}}}\left[X^{i,N}_{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\bar{Q}^{N}}}\left[X^{i,N}_{t}\right]\Big|\leq C_{1}\sup_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|Q^{i,N}(t)-Q(t,i/N)\big|.

Moreover, applying ODE estimates, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\Big|Q(\frac{i}{N},t)^{2}-\left(Q^{i,N}(t)\right)^{2}\Big|\leq 2e^{\|G\|_{\infty}T}\sup_{i\in[N]}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big|Q^{i,N}(t)-Q(t,i/N)\big|.

Plugging the previous two inequalities in (
27
), and using Theorem
6
, we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
, such that

|J^{N}_{p}(\hat{\xi}^{N})-V_{P}^{N}|\leq\frac{C}{N}.

The latter proves our statement.
