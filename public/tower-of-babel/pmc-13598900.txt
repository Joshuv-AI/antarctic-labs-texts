Recent Advances in Food Allergens and Emerging Biosensing Technologies
Authors: Maojie Hu, Youfa Wang, Yuxin Wang, Liqiushi Deng, Geoffrey I N Waterhouse, Vijaya Raghavan, Jin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFood allergies have become a worldwide public health issue, highlighting the critical need for rapid, sensitive, and field‐deployable detection strategies. While traditional detection methods offer high accuracy, their high cost, operational complexity, and lengthy analysis time limit their applicability for rapid screening purposes. Emerging biosensing technologies offer innovative solutions. This review first outlined the characteristics of food allergens and conventional detection approaches and subsequently provided a systematic summary of recent developments in fluorescence, colorimetric, surface‐enhanced Raman scattering, surface plasmon resonance, and electrochemical sensors, classified according to their signal transduction mechanisms. Furthermore, this review examined the underlying principles, signal amplification mechanisms, analytical performance, and practical validation of different sensor platforms in real‐sample analysis. Current challenges include matrix interference, limited multiplexing, poor long‐term stability, and the high cost of recognition elements. Future development directions encompass antifouling interfaces, high‐throughput platforms, self‐powered and wearable technologies, standardized evaluation frameworks, and artificial intelligence algorithms. This review aimed to offer both a theoretical framework and directional guidance to inform future studies and practical implementations of rapid food allergen detection technologies.Keywords: biosensors, conventional techniques, food allergens, on‐site detection, rapid detection

Introduction

Food allergy is an aberrant immune response triggered by the ingestion of specific food protein antigens and is widely recognized as a major threat to human health (Cianferoni 2020; Remington et al. 2020; Warren et al. 2020). Even extremely low doses of allergens can elicit diverse and unpredictable clinical manifestations, including hypotension, respiratory impairment, gastrointestinal symptoms, and skin or mucosal involvement (Bahna 2001; Guo et al. 2021), which not only impose a substantial medical burden on patients but also severely compromise their quality of life. According to reports, approximately 6%–13% of the global population is currently affected by food allergies, with prevalence rates of 2%–10% among adults and 6%–8% among children, and these rates continue to increase (Sampath et al. 2019, 2021; Golding et al. 2021). Notably, the disease burden is particularly pronounced in industrialized countries, where approximately 8% of children and 10% of adults are affected, and relevant studies have indicated that this increasing trend is closely associated with changes in environmental exposure patterns and the transformation of dietary structure (Q. Z. Zhang et al. 2025; Bartha et al. 2024).

Food allergies are predominantly triggered by the “big nine” allergenic foods: tree nuts (TNs), shellfish, fish, eggs, soy, peanuts, milk, wheat, and sesame (Pi, Chen et al. 2025). These allergens can affect multiple organ systems, leading to a variety of clinical manifestations, including skin inflammation, vomiting, diarrhea, and respiratory symptoms (Lisiecka 2025). In severe cases, exposure to these allergens may precipitate life‐threatening reactions, including anaphylactic shock (Muñoz‐Cano et al. 2017). Considering that even extremely low levels of allergens can elicit severe allergic reactions, the development of sensitive and reliable allergen detection methods throughout food processing is essential for ensuring accurate allergen labeling and reducing the risk of unintended exposure among susceptible populations.

In the context of international food trade, inadequate or absent allergen labeling can directly lead to product recalls, resulting in significant economic losses and reputational risks for food manufacturers (Thow et al. 2020). According to the European Union Rapid Alert System, improper or absent allergen labeling is among the primary causes of food recalls, underscoring the high sensitivity and stringent requirements of allergen labeling management in food safety compliance (Pi, Ren et al. 2025). As an effective risk management strategy, allergen labeling plays a critical role in minimizing allergen‐related risks by providing essential information to individuals with food allergies, thereby reducing the likelihood of accidental ingestion and improving food consumption safety (Blom et al. 2021). With respect to regulatory requirements, the European Union requires the mandatory labeling of 14 major food allergens (including eggs, milk, peanuts, nuts, gluten‐containing cereals, lupin, soybeans, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, fish, crustaceans, mollusks, and sulfites) under Regulation (EU) No. 1169/2011 (Curulli 2022). In China, the General Standard for the Labeling of Prepackaged Foods (GB 7718‐2011), issued in 2011, provides recommended guidelines for allergen labeling of eight major food allergen groups, including milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, TNs, peanuts, wheat, and soybeans (Vicentini et al. 2026). In addition to the eight major food allergens specified under Chinese regulations, the United States recognizes sesame as the ninth major food allergen, following the enactment of the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act (FALCPA) in 2004 and the subsequent Food Allergy Safety, Treatment, Education, and Research (FASTER) Act, which took effect in 2023. The implementation of these regulations provides consumers with a clear and standardized framework for identifying potential food allergens, thereby improving transparency within the food supply chain and contributing to the protection of public health.

Moreover, the provision of accurate, standardized, and traceable allergen information is a critical element of effective food safety management. Accurate allergen labeling allows consumers to quickly identify potential risk ingredients in food, thereby minimizing the risk of severe allergic reactions from inadvertent exposure and safeguarding dietary safety. For food manufacturers and distributors, implementing stringent allergen detection systems is essential not only for regulatory compliance but also for building consumer confidence, protecting brand integrity, and ensuring the seamless flow of international trade. Thus, allergen detection has emerged as an essential element of public health management and food safety regulation, with its significance becoming increasingly evident amid the globalization of food supply chains and the escalating burden of allergic diseases worldwide.

Currently, food allergen monitoring in the food industry relies predominantly on established analytical methods, including enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), mass spectrometry (MS), and lateral flow devices (LFDs) (Birse et al. 2023; Sena‐Torralba et al. 2022). However, each of these conventional methods has inherent limitations. ELISA is susceptible to processing‐induced changes in protein conformation and interference from complex food matrices, whereas PCR detects allergen‐associated DNA rather than the allergenic proteins themselves and may therefore not directly reflect actual allergenic risk. MS requires costly instrumentation and technically demanding procedures, while LFDs are generally limited to qualitative or semiquantitative analysis and often provide insufficient sensitivity. More broadly, these methods may also be constrained by reliance on laboratory infrastructure, relatively long analysis times, high analytical costs, and limited comparability of results across different analytical platforms (Birse et al. 2023). Thus, a wide range of emerging biosensing technologies, many of which remain at the laboratory research stage, are being rapidly developed to achieve higher sensitivity, enable multiplexed detection, and provide robust quantitative analysis (Malitha et al. 2026). However, comprehensive and systematic reviews that critically evaluate these emerging sensing technologies remain limited. This article first provides a brief overview of the nine major food allergens (based on US regulatory standards) and conventional detection methods and then systematically reviews recent advances in emerging biosensing technologies for food allergen detection, encompassing colorimetric, fluorescence, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), surface‐enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), electrochemical, and dual‐mode or multimode biosensors. The aim is to provide conceptual insights and a theoretical framework to guide the further development and practical implementation of rapid and accurate food allergen detection technologies.

Overview of Food Allergens

At present, no effective treatment is available for food allergies, and prevention of allergic reactions relies primarily on the strict avoidance of specific allergenic foods. Despite the implementation of mandatory or recommended labeling regulations for major food allergens in many countries, unintended allergen contamination may still occur throughout food processing, production, storage, and distribution due to factors such as cross‐contact, the use of shared manufacturing facilities, and inadvertent ingredient incorporation (Abdelmoteleb et al. 2021; Jan and Naig 2021). Consequently, the precise identification and quantification of allergens in food products are crucial not only for protecting susceptible consumers but also for ensuring regulatory compliance within the food industry.

The accurate detection of food allergens remains challenging because the extraction and identification of allergenic proteins can be significantly affected by the complexity of food matrices and the diverse processing conditions encountered in food production (Lei and Grammer 2019). Specifically, common food‐processing methods, such as thermal treatment, fermentation, and hydrolysis, as well as harsh extraction procedures, can induce protein denaturation, aggregation, and interactions with matrix components. These structural modifications often mask or destroy the target epitopes (L. L. Zhang et al. 2025). Although many of these allergens exhibit high structural stability and retain their allergenic potential after processing (Table 1), it is important to recognize that the biorecognition elements incorporated into biosensors, such as antibodies and aptamers, may exhibit altered binding affinities toward analytes that have undergone processing and extraction. Recognition elements developed against native allergenic proteins often exhibit reduced binding affinity toward analytes that have undergone processing‐induced structural modifications (Gonzalez et al. 2025). Considering this, a comprehensive understanding of the physicochemical properties of food allergens, together with the development of highly sensitive, rapid, and advanced detection technologies, is of great significance for achieving effective allergen monitoring and control. Milk Allergen

The estimated prevalence of allergy to milk from domestic cattle (Bos taurus) in developed countries ranges from 0.5% to 3% at age 1 year (Flom and Sicherer 2019). Notably, in the critical age group of 0–2 years, milk stands out as a prominent trigger for anaphylaxis (N. N. Jiang et al. 2021). Milk protein features several allergenic components, including alpha‐lactalbumin (Bos d 4, α‐LA), beta‐lactoglobulin (Bos d 5, β‐LG), serum albumin (Bos d 6), immunoglobulin (Bos d 7), alphaS1‐casein (Bos d 9), alphaS2‐casein (Bos d 10), beta‐casein (Bos d 11), and kappa‐casein (Bos d 12). The major milk allergens are β‐LG, caseins, and α‐LA. Specifically, β‐LG is responsible for food allergies in approximately 80% of cow's milk allergy patients (Villa et al. 2018). As a lipocalin family protein, β‐LG is encoded by the beta‐lactoglobulin gene, featuring a molecular mass of 18.3 kilodaltons (kDa) in its monomeric state. In addition, the allergenicity of β‐LG increases with heat denaturation at 50°C–90°C due to the exposure of hidden SH‐group epitopes (Bu et al. 2009). Studies have shown that approximately 27.6%–62.8% of milk allergic populations are affected by α‐LA, the regulatory subunit of lactose synthase, which is specifically expressed in the mammary gland and secreted in milk (Hong et al. 2021). To address milk allergies, strategies include substitutes for alternative milk sources. However, common milk substitutes such as sheep, buffalo, and goat milk may have high cross‐reactivity rates (Villa et al. 2018). Therefore, ultrasensitive detection of cattle milk proteins is an important complementary method to ensure food safety.

Egg Allergen

Eggs produced by chickens (Gallus domesticus) are pervasive in various foods and dishes. In the United States, the prevalence of egg allergy is noteworthy, with rates of 0.9% among all children and 1.3% among those under 5 years old (Samady et al. 2020). Egg white comprises several allergens, including ovomucoid (Gal d 1), ovalbumin (OVA) (Gal d 2), ovotransferrin (Gal d 3), and lysozyme C (Gal d 4) (Vapor et al. 2022). Yolk, consisting of 27%–32% egg, contains two minor allergens: alpha‐livetin or chicken serum albumin (Gal d 5) and YGP42 protein (Gal d 6). Among these, Gal d 1 is hypothesized to be the predominant egg allergen due to its heat resistance compared to heat‐labile OVA (Dang et al. 2019). Avian Gal d 1 comprises three homologous tandem Kazal family inhibitory domains linked by disulfide bonds, contributing to its stability under heating (Verhoeckx et al. 2015). The primary peptide segment responsible for the high allergenicity of Gal d 1 is the third domain, containing the most dominant immunoglobulin G (IgG) and IgG‐binding epitope. Moreover, comprising 210 amino acids, Gal d 1 acts as a serine protease inhibitor, specifically inhibiting trypsin. A few biosensors targeting egg white proteins have been developed, and more research on this target is deemed necessary.

Fish Allergen

The production and consumption of wild‐caught and cultured fish products from marine and freshwater sources in China have shown a consistent increase since 2016, as reported in the China Statistical Yearbook. Despite the growing popularity of fish consumption, certain populations may encounter health risks, as all fish species have the potential to be allergenic (Khaneghah and Mostashari 2025). The prevalence of fish allergies varies, ranging from 0% to 7%, with fish species commonly implicated in allergic cases, including cod, salmon, and tuna (Moonesinghe et al. 2016; Ruethers et al. 2018). As major fish allergens, parvalbumin (PV), aldolase A, p‐enolase, tropomyosin (TM), and vitellogenin have been identified (Ruethers et al. 2018). Among these, PV, a small and stable calcium‐binding muscle protein, stands out as the most frequent fish pan‐allergen, contributing to 95% of fish food allergies (Mukherjee et al. 2023). PVs exhibit 17 molecular variations, with weights ranging between 9 and 14 kDa, as derived from the WHO/IUIS database. Although these proteins are highly resistant to thermal denaturation, their extractability, structural conformation, or epitope accessibility can still be influenced by complex chemical interactions within food matrices during processing (Abdelmoteleb et al. 2021). These properties underscore the critical importance of biosensor‐based PV risk assessment to aid consumers in determining if a specific fish might trigger allergic reactions.

Crustacean Shellfish Allergen

In this review, the term “shellfish” refers specifically to crustaceans, including shrimp, crabs, and lobsters. Although mollusks, such as squid, oysters, scallops, and snails, may also be classified as shellfish in a broader biological sense, they are not included among the “big nine” food allergens (Giovannini et al. 2023). A physician‐confirmed assessment indicated a prevalence of 1.2% (with a range of 1.1%–1.3%) within a significant, nationally representative cohort of 40,443 US adults (Warren et al. 2019). Furthermore, allergic reactions to fish and shellfish allergens manifest a strikingly high persistent nature, with up to 90% of cases exhibiting lifelong continuity (Ruethers et al. 2018). Within crustacean shellfish allergens, a collection of seven prevalent and potent components is identified, featuring TM, arginine kinase (AK), myosin light chain, sarcoplasmic calcium‐binding protein, triosephosphate isomerase, and troponin. Of these, crustacean TM emerges as the major allergen responsible for shellfish allergies, acting as the pan‐allergen across various shellfish species, contributing to 72%–98% of shrimp allergies (J. H. Zhao et al. 2023). TM displays notable heat resistance due to its characteristic linear‐dimetric helical coiled‐coil structure with nearly 100% α‐helix along its entire length. Adding to its unique structural characteristics, crustacean TM is distinguished by its high conservation, sharing over 80% amino acid sequence similarities with cockroaches and dust mite allergens due to lineage (Reese et al. 1999). Therefore, it is crucial to create biosensors targeting TM to proactively differentiate potential allergen contamination.

TN Allergen

TN, a collective term for nuts growing on trees, encompasses a variety of common allergenic species, including almond, Brazil nut, cashew nut, hazelnut, macadamia, pecan, pistachio, and walnut (McWilliam et al. 2015). The global prevalence of TN allergy varies, spanning from less than 1% to approximately 3% (McWilliam et al. 2020). In addition, TN and peanut allergies constitute a significant portion of food‐induced anaphylaxis fatalities, with TNs alone accounting for approximately 18%–40% of these cases (Smeekens et al. 2018; Weinberger and Sicherer 2018). Moreover, TNs feature several major allergens, namely, seed storage proteins, including 2S albumin, profilin, vicilin, legumins, and nonspecific lipid transfer proteins. Among these, 2S albumin emerges as the most abundant and potent allergen, eliciting immunoglobulin E (IgE)‐mediated responses in more than 50% of patients (Geiselhart et al. 2018). The heightened allergenicity of 2S albumins can be attributed to their resistance to heating and proteolytic effects by enzymes (Sordet et al. 2009). In addition, despite the primary structure of 2S albumin exhibiting considerable variability across species, a segment with highly conserved six to eight cysteine residues forming disulfide bridges leads to cross‐reactivities within TNs (Geiselhart et al. 2018; Souza 2020). Therefore, to manage the risk of TN allergies and cross‐reactivities, ultrasensitive biosensing platforms are necessary.

Peanut Allergen

Peanut (Arachis hypogaea) ranks as the third most common allergen among children in the United States, impacting approximately 1.25 million (2.2%) children aged 4–17 years (Brown et al. 2022; Z. Sun et al. 2025). Peanut allergy tends to persist throughout the lifetime, with only approximately 20% of children developing tolerance (Kusari et al. 2018). Furthermore, the Australian School Nuts Study revealed that peanuts constituted 38.6% of confirmed anaphylaxis episodes and 30.6% of unconfirmed attack cases (Alshajarah et al. 2022). Peanut allergens can be categorized as cupin (Ara h 1, Ara h 3), conglutin (Ara h 2, Ara h 6, Ara h 7), profilin (Ara h 5), PR‐10 (Ara h 8), nonspecific lipid‐transfer protein type 1 (Ara h 9, Ara h 17), oleosin (Ara h 10, Ara h 11, Ara h 14, Ara h 15), defensin (Ara h 12, Ara h 13), nonspecific lipid transfer protein type 2 (Ara h 16), and cyclophilin (Ara h 18). Among these, the major allergen Ara h 1 is involved in more than 90% of peanut allergic reactions, while 84% of peanut‐allergic patients exhibit adverse responses to Ara h 2 and Ara h 6 (Hemmings et al. 2020; Smeekens et al. 2018). Structurally, Ara h 1 demonstrated the capacity to prevent structural degradation, with epitopes remaining more or less hidden in the natural Lys trimer complex (Hong et al. 2021). In addition, Ara h 2 and Ara h 6 have conserved three‐dimensional (3D) structures, resulting in high stability in the gastrointestinal tract environment and remarkable resistance to thermal processing (Ebisawa et al. 2013; Hemmings et al. 2020). The severity of peanut allergy and its high persistence have led to numerous studies focusing on peanut biosensors.

Wheat Allergen

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is the most widely consumed staple food globally. The prevalence of wheat allergy is documented at 0.63% for self‐reported cases and 0.70% for self‐reported physician‐diagnosed cases (W. Liu et al. 2023). In Europe, wheat stands out as the most common food trigger for anaphylaxis among adults, particularly in central Europe (Kraft et al. 2021). Notably, 82.8% of wheat anaphylaxis cases are associated with exercise, marking a significantly higher proportion compared to other foods (Kraft et al. 2021). Nine out of 28 wheat allergens documented in the WHO/IUIS allergen database are food allergens: profilin (Tri a 12), beta‐amylase (Tri a 17), agglutinin isolectin 1 (Tri a 18), omega‐5 gliadin (Tri a 19), gamma gliadin (Tri a 20), thioredoxin (Tri a 25), high molecular weight glutenin (Tri a 26), low molecular weight glutenin (Tri a 36), and alpha‐purothionin (Tri a 37) (M. Liu et al. 2023). Furthermore, gluten protein, constituted by the major wheat allergens gliadin and glutenin, is the sole food allergen known to trigger autoimmune diseases (Cabanillas 2020; Kraft et al. 2021). Gliadin exists as a monomeric molecule, classified into α/β, γ, and ω‐gliadins, with α/β‐gliadin proteins sharing comparable structures characterized by abundant proline and glutamine. In contrast, glutenin manifests as a polymeric compound, further categorized into high molecular weight glutenin subunits (80–120 kDa) and low molecular weight glutenin subunits (40–80 kDa) (M. Liu et al. 2023). Very few biosensors for detecting wheat allergens have been developed targeting gluten, and further research is required to support the detection of complex wheat allergens.

Soybean Allergen

Soybean (Glycine max), believed to have originated in East Asia, has long been a staple in human diets (G. A. Lee et al. 2011). The prevalence of soybean allergy is estimated to range from 0.3% to 3% in the general population (Wiederstein et al. 2023). Despite its relatively high eliciting dose (40–60 mg) and a natural resolution rate of 70% in pediatric patients by the age of 10, soybean remains an important allergen source, especially in East Asia, where high soybean consumption is a dietary norm (Wiederstein et al. 2023). The array of allergenic proteins in soybean includes profilin (Gly m 3), pathogenesis‐related protein (Gly m 4), beta‐conglycinin (β‐CG; Gly m 5, 7S globulin), glycinin (Gly m 6, 11S globulin), seed biotinylated protein (Gly m 7), and 2S albumin (Gly m 8), with Gly m 4, Gly m 5, and Gly m 6 emerging as the major proteins. In addition, multiple studies have underscored Gly m 5 and Gly m 6 as the most abundant soybean allergens, accounting for 200–500 mg per gram of total soybean protein (T. Wang et al. 2014; Wiederstein et al. 2023). Moreover, in a Japanese study, 17 out of 19 symptomatic soybean‐allergic children exhibited heightened serum IgE levels against 2S albumin (Ebisawa et al. 2013). Therefore, building reliable soybean allergen sensing devices is of enormous importance.

Sesame Allergen

Due to the growing prevalence of sesame allergies, sesame seeds (Sesamum indicum) were designated as a prior food allergen by Health Canada in 2016. In the United States, the estimated prevalence of sesame allergy is approximately 0.1%–0.2% (Sokol et al. 2020). However, the significance of sesame allergy risks is underscored by the fact that less than 30% of pediatric patients naturally develop tolerance (Machnes‐Maayan et al. 2022). Sesame allergenic proteins include 2S albumins (Ses i 1, Ses i 2), 7S vicilin‐like globulin (Ses i 3), oleosins (Ses i 4, Ses i 5), and 11S globulins (Ses i 6, Ses i 7). Among these 2S albumins, Ses i 1 has been identified as a major sesame allergen by numerous studies, exhibiting stability at temperatures up to 90°C. Moreover, Ses i 3 and Ara h 1 have approximately 80% sequence identity between specific epitopes, which has led to documented cross‐reactive IgE binding activities. In addition, studies found a significant increase in sesame antigenicity after boiling or dry roasting (Pi et al. 2024). Hence, it is crucial to prioritize the development of detection methods to effectively identify sesame allergens, particularly in processed commercial food products.

Traditional Methods for Food Allergen Detection

Accurate detection of food allergens is a cornerstone of effective allergen risk management. The performance of detection methods can be assessed using parameters such as detection speed, sensitivity, and specificity; therefore, the development of rapid, highly sensitive, and highly specific analytical methods is of critical importance. Traditionally, the detection of food allergens has primarily focused on molecular components such as proteins and nucleic acids (Tuppo et al. 2022). Current methodologies can be broadly categorized into ELISA, nucleic acid‐based detection methods, and MS‐based techniques (Birse et al. 2023). ELISA is a well‐established immunological analytical technique that exploits the high specificity of antigen–antibody interactions in conjunction with enzyme‐mediated signal amplification, enabling the sensitive and selective detection of target allergens. Owing to its ease of operation, high specificity, and capacity for high‐throughput screening, this method has been extensively applied to the rapid detection of major food allergens, such as peanut, milk, and egg allergens (Liu et al. 2024). For example, Tan et al. generated high‐affinity monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against recombinant alpha‐lactalbumin (ALA) and developed a biotin–streptavidin‐amplified sandwich ELISA (sELISA) using rALA‐mAb 3B20 as the capture antibody and biotinylated anti‐ALA polyclonal antibodies (Bio‐ALA‐pAb) as the detection antibodies. The assay enabled the specific and quantitative detection of ALA in hypoallergenic infant formula powders (Tan et al. 2023). Jayasena et al. purified Ara h 2 from lightly roasted peanut flour and used the purified protein as an immunogen to elicit the production of polyclonal IgG antibodies in rabbits. Following purification of IgG antibodies from high‐titer rabbit antisera, a competitive inhibition ELISA was developed for the quantitative detection of the peanut allergen Ara h2 (Jayasena et al. 2025). Nevertheless, the linear range of ELISA is inherently restricted, frequently necessitating sample dilution, thereby increasing the procedural complexity of the assay (Eischeid et al. 2021). Moreover, the widespread application of ELISA is limited by the high cost, time‐consuming production, and insufficient stability of antibodies. Antibody performance can also be affected by batch‐to‐batch variability, environmental factors, and matrix interference, potentially resulting in cross‐reactivity and reduced analytical accuracy.

Molecular biology‐based detection methods mainly encompass PCR and its derivatives, including quantitative PCR (qPCR), loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), nucleic acid hybridization assays, and gene sequencing technologies. The underlying principle of these techniques relies on the sequence specificity of nucleic acids, whereby target DNA or RNA is identified through amplification‐based strategies or probe‐mediated recognition. Jayathilake et al. identified high‐affinity antibodies from a VHH library and established an animal immunization‐free cD‐IPCR detection platform, thereby alleviating some of the inherent drawbacks of conventional antibody production, such as high cost and prolonged development time (Jayathilake et al. 2020). The results indicated that the proposed method allowed for the quantitative determination of gliadin within a detection range of 0.001–10 µg/mL. However, these methods detect genes derived from allergen source species, whose expression levels do not necessarily correlate with the corresponding allergenic protein content. In addition, the relatively high technical requirements associated with these methods further limit their practical application in allergen detection (Holzhauser 2018).

MS is a powerful analytical approach for the characterization of protein and peptide primary structures, allowing the accurate identification and quantification of allergenic proteins and peptides in complex food matrices (Marzano et al. 2020). The underlying principle of MS is the ionization of peptides or proteins, followed by their separation and detection based on their mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z) within an electromagnetic field (Roder et al. 2021). Owing to its high specificity, superior sensitivity, and multiplexing capability, targeted MS has shown great promise for the simultaneous identification and quantification of multiple food allergens in complex food matrices. Zhang et al. identified and synthesized signature peptides from OVA, α‐LA, β‐LG, alpha S1‐casein (αS1‐CN), and alpha S2‐casein (αS2‐CN), together with their corresponding stable isotope‐labeled analogs. Based on these peptide markers, a liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry method was established for the simultaneous detection and quantification of egg‐ and milk‐derived allergenic proteins (Fan et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the reliance of this method on costly instrumentation, labor‐intensive sample preparation, and specialized technical expertise limits its broader application in field‐based allergen detection, particularly in resource‐constrained settings (Tuppo et al. 2022).

Emerging Biosensing Technologies for Food Allergen Detection

In recent years, emerging biosensing technologies have garnered considerable attention in the field of food allergen detection. These sensing platforms typically integrate highly specific molecular recognition elements, including antibodies, aptamers, nanobodies (Nbs), and molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs), with efficient signal transduction systems. The choice of recognition element plays a pivotal role in determining key sensor attributes, including selectivity, stability, cost‐effectiveness, and compatibility with complex matrices, and thus constitutes a fundamental consideration in sensor design. To facilitate a systematic comparison of different recognition elements, the key advantages and limitations of the major recognition components described above are briefly discussed below. Conventional antibodies offer excellent affinity and specificity but are constrained by high production costs, limited thermal stability, substantial batch‐to‐batch variability, and poor regenerability (Y. X. Zhao et al. 2023). Aptamers combine high affinity with relatively low synthesis costs and exhibit excellent environmental stability, batch‐to‐batch consistency, and regenerability. Nevertheless, their recognition performance in complex food matrices can be influenced by variations in ionic strength and pH. Moreover, the conformational mechanisms governing aptamer‐target interactions remain incompletely understood, potentially compromising their reliability for certain allergen targets (C. Zhu et al. 2025). Nbs, which are derived from the variable domains of heavy‐chain‐only antibodies, retain high affinity and specificity while exhibiting superior thermal and chemical stability compared with conventional antibodies. Their production is also relatively cost‐effective; however, their development typically requires animal immunization followed by phage‐display screening, resulting in relatively lengthy production cycles, and their recognition capability toward small‐molecule haptens remains limited (Y. Wang et al. 2024). In contrast, MIPs, as synthetic recognition receptors, generally exhibit somewhat lower affinity and specificity than biological recognition elements but offer exceptional stability under harsh physicochemical conditions, along with low synthesis costs, excellent batch‐to‐batch consistency, and superior regenerability (Farooq et al. 2024). Overall, the four classes of recognition elements exhibit distinct strengths and limitations across different performance criteria, with no single class offering superior performance across all dimensions. Accordingly, rational sensor design requires a careful trade‐off between recognition performance and practical applicability, tailored to the specific requirements of the intended detection scenario.

The specific binding between recognition elements and target allergens is transduced into measurable signals, including fluorescence, colorimetric, SERS, and electrochemical responses, thereby enabling the quantitative or semiquantitative detection of food allergens (Asif et al. 2019; R. D. Liu et al. 2025; Y. N. Zhao et al. 2026). Owing to their operational simplicity, rapid response, high sensitivity, and portability, these sensors exhibit remarkable advantages for the on‐site screening of allergens in complex food matrices, such as peanuts, milk, and eggs (Qiu et al. 2023; X. Shen et al. 2026; Shin et al. 2022). Notably, the integration of advanced nanomaterials and microfluidic technologies has further enhanced sensing performance through signal amplification, background suppression, and automated visualization (Shin et al. 2022; D. Zhu et al. 2024; A. Wang et al. 2025). These collective advantages effectively overcome the key limitations of traditional immunological and instrumental analytical methods, including laborious procedures, high equipment costs, and prolonged assay times. Benefiting from their miniaturization, high analytical precision, and cost‐effectiveness, biosensors are increasingly recognized as promising alternatives to conventional analytical tools. However, the inherent complexity of food matrices poses substantial challenges to analytical sensitivity and reliability, making efficient sample pretreatment essential for ensuring robust and reproducible detection performance. Thus, this section first discusses pretreatment strategies for complex food samples prior to analysis and then systematically reviews the major emerging biosensing technologies for food allergen detection, encompassing optical biosensing (including fluorescence, colorimetric, SERS, and SPR biosensors), electrochemical biosensing, and dual‐modal/multimodal sensing technologies. Preparation of Food Samples

The complex matrix components inherently present in foods, such as proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and salts, often form intricate interference networks that pose a primary obstacle to the accurate biosensor‐based detection of trace allergens (Hayder et al. 2022). This challenge is further exacerbated during food processing, particularly under thermal treatment, which induces protein denaturation, aggregation, and covalent modifications (e.g., Maillard reaction), leading to irreversible alterations in conformational epitopes. Consequently, for immunosensors that rely on specific antigen–antibody recognition, the disruption or masking of such tertiary structures significantly attenuates signal responses (Shin et al. 2022; Civera et al. 2023).

To overcome these detection bottlenecks arising from matrix effects and processing conditions, systematic optimization of sample extraction parameters, including buffer composition, temperature, time, and additives, is critical for preserving the native conformation of allergens and improving the eventual electrical or optical signal output of the sensor (Shin et al. 2022). For instance, Alves et al. systematically evaluated 36 extraction conditions for the peanut allergen Ara h 6 in complex chocolate matrices, achieving a recovery of up to 94.7% using a Tris‐HNO3 buffer supplemented with 1 mol/L NaCl and skim milk powder at 21°C for 60 min (Alves et al. 2017). Similarly, Lee et al. reported comparable findings in a single‐walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)‐based biosensor for Ara h 1 detection, explicitly demonstrating that optimized extraction parameters directly determine the concentration and conformational integrity of the target analyte entering the detection system, thereby significantly improving the accuracy of the resistance‐change (ΔR) response (Lee 2020).

However, while additives such as NaCl (which increases ionic strength) and skim milk powder (which competitively binds to co‐extracted matrix components such as polyphenols) can effectively facilitate target release, co‐extracted nontarget proteins and other substances tend to induce nonspecific adsorption at the sensor recognition interface. In the absence of subsequent purification steps, this phenomenon inevitably elevates the background noise of the assay (Curulli 2022). Therefore, the integration of advanced sample pretreatment and enrichment technologies into sensing systems has become an imperative strategy to eliminate matrix interference and enhance detection performance. Conventional extraction techniques, including filtration, centrifugation, and liquid–liquid extraction, often suffer from inherent limitations such as poor selectivity and time‐consuming procedures (Ling et al. 2023). Recently, novel solid‐phase extraction (SPE) materials—including metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), MIPs, and magnetic nanomaterials—have offered promising solutions for the efficient separation of allergens from complex matrices owing to their exceptional adsorption specificity and high enrichment efficiency. Such targeted purification mechanisms fundamentally prevent matrix interferents from contaminating the sensing interface (Ling et al. 2023).

The development of biosensing technologies for food allergens should not be confined solely to enhancing the intrinsic sensitivity of the sensing elements. Instead, the evaluation of food processing status and the systematic optimization of pretreatment and extraction protocols must be integrated as fundamental prerequisites for methodological development. These innovative sample preparation strategies open new avenues for the efficient enrichment of food allergens, thereby significantly advancing the practical application of biosensors toward sensitive and reliable detection in real‐world scenarios.

Fluorescent Biosensor

Fluorescence biosensors represent a powerful analytical platform in bioanalysis, operating on the principle that specific chemical reactions or selective interactions between biorecognition elements and target analytes result in measurable changes in fluorescence intensity or fluorescence lifetime, which can be correlated with the presence or concentration of the target (Qi et al. 2023). Through the capture and transduction of these optical signals, biosensing platforms enable sensitive and precise detection and quantification of target analytes. To date, numerous fluorescence‐emitting materials, including organic fluorophores, semiconductor quantum dots (QDs), graphene QDs, and carbon QDs, have been developed and applied as signal‐generating probes in fluorescence biosensing platforms (Wolfbeis 2015). Due to their superior analytical performance, characterized by rapid response times, high sensitivity, strong signal output, and visual detection capability, fluorescence biosensors have emerged as widely adopted tools for the sensitive and reliable detection of food allergens (Shin et al. 2022).

Weng et al. established a microfluidic biosensing platform incorporating a quantum dot (Qdot)/aptamer‐functionalized graphene oxide (GO) nanobiosensor for the rapid and highly sensitive detection of Ara h 1 (Figure 1a) (Weng and Neethirajan 2016). The fluorescence sensing strategy relies on the fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) effect established within the Qdot–aptamer–GO complex, resulting in efficient fluorescence quenching and thereby maintaining the sensor in an “off” state prior to target recognition. Upon target recognition, the aptamer exhibits a stronger binding affinity for Ara h 1 than for GO, thereby inducing the desorption of the Qdot–aptamer complex from the GO surface and restoring fluorescence. This target‐induced fluorescence “turn‐on” mechanism enabled sensitive detection of Ara h 1, with a detection limit as low as 56 ng/mL. Validation in spiked real samples demonstrated recovery rates of 87.5%–92.5%, comparable to those achieved using a commercial ELISA kit (85.8%–90.4%). A key advantage of this platform is its homogeneous microfluidic design, which circumvents complex probe immobilization and achieves quantitative detection within 10 min. Furthermore, its integration with a miniaturized optical analyzer facilitates portability for on‐site applications. However, practical limitations persist. Analyzing complex food matrices, such as biscuits, requires extensive offline pretreatment (including prolonged heating at 60°C, centrifugation, and filtration), which compromises the overall assay rapidity.

(d) MOF‐based fluorescent biosensor. Reprinted with permission from L. Hong et al. (2022).

To further advance the precision and sensitivity of allergen detection in complex food matrices, Qi et al., through a rolling circle amplification (RCA)‐mediated self‐assembly process, engineered multivalent aptamer‐encoded DNA nanoflowers (Mul‐DNFs), which, in combination with the fluorescent dyes Cy3 and Cy5, enabled the construction of a highly sensitive ratiometric fluorescent aptasensing platform for Ara h 1 detection (Figure 1b) (Qi et al. 2024). The Mul‐DNFs possess a 3D flower‐like structure with densely arranged 31‐base Ara h 1 aptamers on their surface, optimized via truncation and point mutations, and the resulting multivalent cooperative interactions markedly strengthen their binding affinity for the target analyte. A FRET‐based sensing platform was constructed through the co‐hybridization of Cy3‐ and Cy5‐labeled complementary DNA (cDNA) strands onto the Mul‐DNFs. Upon binding of Ara h 1, the Cy5‐labeled cDNA was competitively displaced from the Mul‐DNFs, leading to a decrease in fluorescence emission at 668 nm and a corresponding increase at 566 nm. The fluorescence intensity ratio showed a strong linear correlation with Ara h 1 concentration across a dynamic range of 0.05–2000 ng/mL, achieving an exceptionally low limit of detection (LOD) of 0.02 ng/mL. The assay demonstrated robust analytical performance in real food matrices, with spike‐recovery rates ranging from 95.7% to 106.3% in milk, biscuit, and chocolate samples, and the relative standard deviation (RSD) was only 1.4%–5.1%. Notably, the modular design of the platform enables straightforward adaptation to other targets through replacement of the aptamer‐encoding region within the circular template, underscoring its potential as a versatile and universal biosensing platform for precise food allergen detection.

Despite their widespread application, traditional fluorescent labels, including rhodamine and 5(6)‐carboxyfluorescein, are constrained by several shortcomings, such as complex synthetic procedures, broad emission bandwidths, and suboptimal photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs) (Cheng and Lei 2024). These limitations have spurred the exploration of next‐generation fluorescent materials with enhanced photophysical characteristics and sensing performance. Carbon quantum dots (CDs) are emerging as a versatile class of carbon nanomaterials due to their facile synthesis, low cytotoxicity, outstanding fluorescence characteristics, broad excitation range, ease of surface modification, and favorable biocompatibility, which have enabled their widespread application in various fields (Kong et al. 2024). Wang et al. employed water‐soluble QD‐conjugated polyclonal antibodies to establish a QD‐based fluorescent lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA), enabling sensitive detection of myosin. The assay is based on a competitive binding mechanism: as the concentration of myosin rises, more QD‐labeled polyclonal antibodies engage with the target in solution, thereby diminishing the number of complexes captured by immobilized myosin on the test line and resulting in a dose‐dependent decrease in the fluorescence signal. The developed method demonstrated detection limits of 0.5 µg/mL by naked‐eye assessment and 0.05 µg/mL by instrumental analysis, highlighting its potential for both rapid on‐site screening and sensitive quantitative detection. Despite their attractive properties, CDs generally exhibit fluorescence emission in the blue spectral region, where substantial background autofluorescence from food matrices can compromise signal discrimination and detection sensitivity. According to reports, incorporating CDs into a silica shell provides a dual advantage: it enables stable and permanent surface functionalization, thereby overcoming the challenges associated with direct modification of CD surfaces, while also amplifying fluorescence output through the enrichment of CDs within the shell structure, which improves the material's optical performance (Wang et al. 2019). Similarly, Wang et al. developed a dual‐channel fluorescent immunosensor based on an M13 bacteriophage biotemplate, which combines the high specificity of Nb recognition with the fluorescence signaling capability of CDs, enabling the simultaneous detection of the food allergens Ara h 3 and Mac i 1 (Figure 1c) (Y. Wang et al. 2025). The sensing platform operates via a “magnetic separation‐fluorescence readout” mechanism. Target allergens are first captured by Nb‐functionalized magnetic beads, followed by the incorporation of Phage‐CDs@SiO2 probes to construct a sandwich immunocomplex. Magnetic separation is then applied to remove unbound probes, and the fluorescence intensity of the supernatant (or re‐dispersed complexes) is recorded, producing a signal positively correlated with analyte concentration. This method achieved detection limits of 9.5 ng/mL for Ara h 3 and 10.2 ng/mL for Mac i 1, with a sensitivity improvement of two to five times compared to traditional methods and a wide linear range (e.g., Ara h3 exhibited good linearity in the range of 22.1–2000 ng/mL).

Moreover, this coating strategy has been successfully applied to other emerging fluorescent materials. To overcome the poor water stability of perovskite nanocrystals (PNCs), Qi et al. synthesized CsPbBr3@SiO2 core–shell structures via a one‐pot method. The SiO2 shell effectively shields the nanocrystals from water‐induced degradation, enabling them to retain more than 80% of their initial fluorescence intensity after 25 days of storage in water. This provides a robust material platform for biodetection in aqueous environments. Building upon this stable probe, the team developed an intelligent “competitive displacement‐magnetic separation” sensing platform: anti‐Ara h 1 aptamers were covalently immobilized on the surface of CsPbBr3@SiO2 nanoparticles, and cDNA‐modified magnetic nanoparticles were concurrently synthesized (Qi et al. 2023). In the absence of the target, the two components bind together, and following magnetic separation, the fluorescence signal is removed (the “off” state). In the presence of the target, the analyte competes for binding with the aptamer, resulting in the generation of a fluorescence signal in the supernatant (the “on” state). The assay entails an 80‐min incubation and magnetic separation, achieving a 0.04 ng/mL detection limit and a linear dynamic range spanning 0.1–100 ng/mL for Ara h 1. Selectivity was validated against a 10‐fold excess of five nontarget proteins (including β‐LG, α‐LA, IgG, lysozyme, and bovine serum albumin), while intra‐batch and inter‐batch RSDs of 2.3%–4.2% and 3.0%–6.7% confirmed methodological reproducibility. Practical recoveries in spiked, filtered milk and tea drinks ranged from 95.9% to 105.4%. Despite these metrics, reliance on a benchtop spectrometer restricts on‐site application, and parallel validation via standard methods on naturally contaminated samples is lacking. Consequently, this establishes a matrix‐specific proof‐of‐concept for Ara h 1 rather than a universal sensing strategy. Hong et al. utilized the high surface area and well‐defined pore structure of the MOF MIL‐100 to encapsulate upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs), resulting in the successful fabrication of a UCNP@MIL‐100(Fe) core–shell nanocomposite (Figure 1d) (L. Hong et al. 2022). The fabricated nanocomposite not only mitigated the intrinsic limitations of UCNPs, such as poor dispersion and luminescence instability, but also functioned as an effective carrier for the development of fluorescent thermoresponsive MIPs. Leveraging the photoinduced electron transfer (PET) mechanism, β‐LG induces efficient fluorescence quenching of UCNPs, facilitating sensitive quantification with a detection limit as low as 0.043 mg/mL. Although fluorescent biosensors provide the advantage of high sensitivity, background signals originating from the complex matrices of food samples can still compromise the accuracy of detection. Moreover, the stability and biocompatibility of existing fluorescent probes need to be further enhanced, as these limitations hinder their practical application in on‐site detection.

Colorimetric Biosensor

Colorimetric biosensors have attracted considerable interest in food allergen analysis, as they combine operational simplicity with instrument‐free, visually readable outputs, making them highly suitable for rapid and on‐site detection applications. Conventional colorimetric assays typically rely on single‐wavelength absorbance measurements, yielding limited chromatic contrast and thus insufficient visual discrimination, thereby impeding reliable semiquantitative naked‐eye interpretation and constraining their practical utility in real‐time point‐of‐care testing (POCT) (Rao et al. 2019). To address this constraint, attention has gradually shifted toward nanomaterials exhibiting tunable or multicolor optical characteristics. Gold nanorods (GNRs), with their readily adjustable aspect ratios, display tunable color changes ranging from red to the near‐infrared region, along with corresponding SPR absorption peaks. These optical properties provide an excellent foundation for the development of multicolor colorimetric sensing systems (Kermanshahian et al. 2021). Multicolor detection strategies utilizing GNR etching circumvent the drawbacks of traditional aggregation‐dependent colorimetric methods, including environmental susceptibility, by adopting a label‐free, non‐aggregation detection paradigm. This approach permits naked‐eye semiquantitative analysis while markedly enhancing visual resolution and assay precision.

In recent years, GNR‐based multicolor colorimetric biosensors have been successfully applied to the detection of major allergens in foods such as peanuts, milk, and eggs, providing a simple, efficient, and reliable approach for the rapid on‐site screening of food safety hazards (Hua et al. 2021). Sun et al. reported a multicolor visual biosensing platform that combines bifunctional aptamer‐mediated target recognition, G‐quadruplex DNAzyme catalysis, and GNR etching for the rapid detection of β‐LG. By avoiding both enzyme labeling and sophisticated instrumentation, the platform enables facile, instrument‐free allergen analysis with high potential for point‐of‐care applications (Figure 2a) (C. Sun et al. 2025). This platform is based on a target‐induced inhibition mechanism. In the absence of β‐LG, the aptamer assembles with hemin into a catalytically active G‐quadruplex DNAzyme, which promotes the H2O2‐driven oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)‐to‐TMB2+. The generated TMB2+ efficiently etches GNRs, producing pronounced color transitions. Upon target binding, the aptamer undergoes a conformational rearrangement that inhibits DNAzyme formation, thereby reducing TMB oxidation and subsequent GNR etching. The resulting attenuation of the colorimetric response enables concentration‐dependent, naked‐eye semiquantitative analysis of β‐LG without the need for sophisticated instrumentation. Crucially, to mitigate matrix interference during sample pretreatment, the dairy samples required a pH adjustment to approximately 4.7; this specific step precipitates and removes casein, which otherwise disrupts the longitudinal surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) signal. Following this pretreatment, the assay demonstrated quantitative recovery rates of 90.77%–100.99% in cow milk and 94.90%–103.70% in camel milk, with RSDs consistently below 6.0%. In addition, smartphone‐assisted analysis was incorporated into the sensing procedure, whereby the hue value extracted from the solution color was correlated with the β‐LG concentration to enable quantitative determination, achieving an LOD of 2.9 µg/mL and demonstrating considerable potential for portable POCT.

(b) Nanozyme‐based colorimetric biosensor. Reprinted with permission from S. S. Wang et al. (2025). (c) Nanozyme‐based colorimetric biosensor. Reprinted with permission from C. D. Liu et al. (2025).

Beyond GNR etching‐based multicolor colorimetric approaches, the emergence and rapid development of nanozymes—nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme‐like catalytic activity—have significantly promoted the advancement of colorimetric biosensing technologies in recent years. Compared with natural enzymes, nanozymes possess a range of attractive attributes, including robust catalytic activity, low production costs, straightforward synthesis, scalability for mass production, and exceptional operational stability under harsh conditions (R. Q. Yang et al. 2025). These distinctive advantages have sparked extensive interest in the application of nanozymes for biosensing, particularly in colorimetric food allergen detection, where they offer promising opportunities for the development of sensitive, robust, and cost‐effective sensing platforms.

Wang et al. reported a nanozyme‐enabled direct competitive immunoassay for the sensitive detection of Jug r 4, a major walnut allergen, in complex food samples. Specifically, a walnut‐specific mAb was conjugated to a Cu2 − x Se@Bi2MoO6 composite nanomaterial through electrostatic adsorption, yielding an immunosignal probe that combined selective antigen recognition with nanozyme‐mediated signal amplification (Figure 2b) (S. S. Wang et al. 2025). The composite material exhibited good peroxidase‐like activity and could be stably stored at room temperature. Under optimal experimental conditions, the proposed method demonstrated suitable linearity over a concentration range of 137.35–3526.0 µg/L (R 2 ≥ 0.999) with an LOD of 81.63 µg/L (S/N = 3), and spike recovery experiments yielded recoveries between 70.63% and 127.04%, indicating acceptable accuracy and robustness across the tested matrix. Liu et al. reported a facile, economical, and aptamer‐free colorimetric sensing platform utilizing a GeO2/ZnO bimetallic oxide nanozyme for the determination of β‐LG, one of the major milk allergens. The material was synthesized via CTAB‐assisted interlayer growth of ZnO nanoparticles within GeO2 nanosheets, which endowed the GeO2/ZnO nanozyme with enhanced peroxidase‐mimicking catalytic activity as well as increased binding affinity for β‐LG (Figure 2c) (C. D. Liu et al. 2025). Owing to the formation of a specific protein corona driven by multiple recognition interactions, the binding of β‐LG to the GeO2/ZnO nanozyme markedly reduces its peroxidase‐like catalytic activity. The approach allowed for the selective and precise determination of β‐LG in dairy matrices, achieving a detection limit of 0.26 µg/mL, and a portable assay kit was also established to facilitate rapid on‐site testing and naked‐eye colorimetric analysis. Beyond presenting a robust methodology for allergen detection, this study advances the field by offering innovative guidance for the design of nonbiological protein‐affinity sensors, thereby expanding the toolkit for precise and practical food safety analysis.

The integration of tunable nanomaterials and multifunctional nanozymes has significantly advanced colorimetric biosensing, evolving it from basic single‐wavelength assays into high‐resolution, AI‐assisted multimode platforms. However, critical operational bottlenecks still hinder their translation into practical POCTs. Many current systems rely on strict pH and temperature controls, sacrificing maximum sensitivity for assay stability (Zhang et al. 2025). To bridge the gap from laboratory proof‐of‐concept to real‐world application, future research must prioritize robust nanozymes that maintain high catalytic efficiency in complex food matrices under ambient conditions. In addition, integrating sample preparation with detection via microfluidics, paired with miniaturized, AI‐embedded hardware (Li et al. 2026), will be essential for seamless, real‐time food allergen monitoring.

SERS Biosensor

SERS distinguishes itself by coupling the structural specificity of traditional Raman spectroscopy with immense signal amplification. The fundamental principle of SERS relies on the intense localized electromagnetic fields generated around plasmonic nanostructures, such as metallic colloids, upon SPR excitation. This electromagnetic enhancement effect markedly amplifies the inherently weak Raman scattering signals by several orders of magnitude, facilitating the ultrasensitive detection of trace amounts of target molecules (Hu et al. 2026; Jiang et al. 2025; Usman et al. 2023). Integrating SERS with immunosensing technology preserves the core advantages of SERS, namely, high sensitivity and superior spectral resolution, while incorporating the specific molecular recognition capability and operational simplicity inherent to immunosensing platforms, thereby expanding analytical chemistry's methodological toolkit.

The intensity of the SERS signal is largely determined by the properties of the substrate materials, which are typically composed of noble metal (e.g., silver or gold) nanostructures. These nanostructured substrates exhibit strong LSPR characteristics, enabling substantial electromagnetic field enhancement and the generation of highly active “hot spots.” However, individual metal nanoparticles often exhibit limitations, including insufficient structural stability and relatively weak SERS enhancement capability, which impede their broader application in trace‐level detection. In recent years, bimetallic nanostructures with tunable dimensions, morphologies, and compositions have attracted considerable attention as promising platforms for enhancing SERS detection performance, owing to their superior optical properties and highly tailorable structural characteristics (Jiang et al. 2018). For instance, Duan et al. utilized gold–silver nanourchins as the SERS substrate, enabling the ultrasensitive detection of the milk allergen β‐LG (Duan et al. 2022). Owing to the presence of sharp tips acting as nanoantennas and tip junctions serving as nanogap structures, Au–Ag nanourchins possess an ultrahigh density of electromagnetic hot spots and a substantially rougher surface morphology than gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), thereby delivering significantly enhanced Raman signals. Furthermore, Au–Ag nanourchins exhibit superior chemical stability, which enhances the reliability and reproducibility of SERS substrates. Leveraging these advantages, the developed sensing platform achieved a wide linear detection range of 10–1000 ng/mL and a low detection limit of 0.07 ng/mL, surpassing the performance of most previously reported methods. Although SERS‐based immunoassays utilizing SERS tags functionalized with specific recognition elements (e.g., antibodies or aptamers) enable direct signal generation and exhibit high sensitivity and excellent analytical performance, these strategies still encounter inherent challenges in ultrasensitive trace‐level detection. Moreover, intrinsic background signals arising from the recognition elements themselves may introduce complex spectral features into Raman spectra, complicating signal interpretation and limiting their applicability for trace analysis (Ilyas et al. 2023). Thus, in SERS‐based immunoassays, effective suppression of background noise, enhancement of reporter signal specificity, and incorporation of secondary signal amplification strategies are essential for achieving ultrasensitive detection. Driven by the rapid progress of nanotechnology in recent years, nanozyme‐mediated secondary signal amplification strategies have emerged as powerful approaches for overcoming the limitations associated with conventional SERS‐based immunoassays (R. Q. Yang et al. 2025). Su et al. developed an AuNP‐doped COF that exhibited excellent nitroreductase‐mimicking catalytic activity along with remarkable structural stability. Using 4‐nitrothiophenol (4‐NTP) as the substrate, the compound was reduced to 4‐aminothiophenol (4‐ATP) in the presence of NaBH4. The generated 4‐ATP molecules subsequently assembled onto gold nanostars through Au─S bonding and electrostatic interactions, leading to the formation of abundant SERS “hot spots” (Figure 3a) (Su et al. 2019). Concurrently, the characteristic Raman band of 4‐NTP at 1573 cm−1 was attenuated, while a new Raman signal corresponding to 4‐ATP at 1591 cm−1 was significantly enhanced, resulting in a ratiometric SERS response. Building upon this strategy, the authors developed a ratiometric nanozyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (NELISA) platform for β‐LG detection by quantifying the intensity ratio of the characteristic Raman signals (I 1591/I 1573). The proposed method achieved an ultralow detection limit of 0.01 ng/mL and a broad linear response range of 25.65–6.2 × 104 ng/mL while offering several advantages, including low cost, high recovery efficiency, and minimal cross‐reactivity. This work provides a promising new avenue for the SERS‐based ultrasensitive detection of trace allergens.

To address the increasing demand for rapid, accurate, and on‐site detection of food allergens, the integration of SERS with portable LFIA, known as the SERS‐LFIA platform, has been successfully developed (Xing et al. 2022). Although colorimetric LFIA platforms provide significant advantages for rapid and on‐site visual screening, their dependence on optical colorimetric responses typically restricts their application to qualitative or semiquantitative analysis (C. Hu et al. 2026). By incorporating SERS‐active nanolabels, SERS‐LFIA platforms facilitate a seamless transition from conventional visual detection to ultrasensitive quantitative analysis. The integration of SERS with LFIA substantially improves the analytical sensitivity, accuracy, and reliability of portable and point‐of‐care detection systems. Yang et al. constructed an immunochromatographic strip utilizing a dual‐signal amplification strategy based on gold nanobranches/polydopamine (AuNBs@PDA). The 3D multibranched architecture of AuNBs@PDA generated many SERS hot spots, thereby enhancing the signal intensity, whereas the polydopamine layer facilitated efficient antibody immobilization, achieving a conjugation efficiency exceeding 99%. The platform achieved LODs of 0.397 ng/mL for colorimetric detection and 72.2 pg/mL for SERS detection of the peanut allergen Ara h 3, respectively, representing a 300‐fold improvement in sensitivity over conventional AuNP‐based lateral flow assay (LFA) strips (S. Yang et al. 2026). Li et al. developed an LFA platform based on aptamer‐functionalized core–shell SERS tags, in which Raman reporter molecules were encapsulated within the nanogaps of gold‐core/silver‐shell nanoparticles (AuMBA@AgNPs). This design replaced conventional antibody‐based recognition with sequence‐specific DNA aptamers, achieving an LOD of 2.2 ng/mL for β‐LG. Moreover, the platform enabled dual‐target detection, allowing the simultaneous quantification of β‐LG and fish PV in complex food extracts using a single sample port and dual spatially separated test lines (T1 for β‐LG and T2 for PV) on the same strip. The entire assay was completed within 8 min, demonstrating its potential for rapid and multiplexed food allergen detection (H. Li et al. 2025).

In recent years, multimodal detection strategies have attracted increasing attention in the field of analytical science, demonstrating rapid development and successful integration into SERS‐LFIA platforms. By preserving the inherent advantages of single‐mode biosensing systems while enabling cross‐validation of analytical results, these strategies effectively minimize errors associated with manual operation and external environmental variations, thereby enhancing detection accuracy, robustness, and reliability (Xing et al. 2022). Jiao et al. pioneered the development of a dual‐mode lateral flow immunoassay (CM/SERS‐LFI) integrating colorimetric and SERS detection, leveraging phage‐displayed shark nanobodies (PSNs) as biorecognition elements and AuMBA@AgNPs as SERS‐active probes (Figure 3b) (Jiao et al. 2024). Compared to conventional full‐sized antibodies, shark Nbs were specifically selected due to their extraordinarily small size, exceptional thermal and chemical stability, and superior ability to access hidden or cryptic epitopes on the target analyte. Furthermore, utilizing phage display technology enables rapid, low‐cost screening and large‐scale production, overcoming the lengthy cycles and batch‐to‐batch variations associated with traditional animal immunization. In this system, the silver shell thickness was systematically optimized to maximize the Raman enhancement factor. The method established naked‐eye qualitative detection, colorimetric quantitative detection, and SERS quantitative LODs for crustacean TM of 0.01, 0.0057, and 0.0026 µg/mL, respectively, with spike recovery rates ranging from 88.9% to 108.4% across three representative food matrices (bread, cookie, and cheese, which were utilized to evaluate the biosensor's accuracy and resistance to complex background interferences).

Moreover, SERS has been integrated with microfluidic platforms and nanoenzyme‐based catalytic systems to enable multimodal, quantitative, and robust analytical detection. Zhang et al. developed a SERS immunoassay leveraging cerium dioxide (CeO2) nanozyme‐mediated signal amplification, wherein CeO2 catalyzes the oxidation of colorless and Raman‐silent malachite green leuco base to its Raman‐active cationic form while concurrently promoting AuNP aggregation to generate electromagnetic “hot spots,” thereby enabling a robust signal‐on detection strategy (Figure 3c) (Zhang et al. 2021). The method achieves an LOD of 0.01 ng/mL for α‐LA, a linear dynamic range of 0.5–600 ng/mL (R 2 = 0.9992), and spike recovery rates of 98.7%–100.4% in fortified milk matrices. The same research group subsequently developed a dual‐readout microfluidic immunoassay chip utilizing gold@Prussian blue core–shell nanozymes (Au@PBNPs), which exhibit intrinsic peroxidase‐mimicking activity‐catalyzing the oxidation of 2,2′‐azino‐bis(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid) (ABTS) to generate a colorimetric signal and display a sharp, background‐free Raman peak at 2156 cm−1 within the cellular Raman‐silent region, thereby enabling orthogonal signal generation with minimal spectral interference (Figure 3d) (Zhang et al. 2024). The LOD for α‐LA was determined to be 0.011 ng/mL in SERS mode and 0.283 ng/mL in colorimetric mode, thereby supporting field‐deployable dual‐mode detection through the synergistic integration of smartphone‐based RGB colorimetric analysis and a portable Raman spectrometer.

It is worth noting that functional materials employed in SERS‐based food allergen detection are predominantly noble metal nanocomposites, and their high production costs remain a significant economic barrier to large‐scale practical implementation. Consequently, the development of nonprecious metal Raman‐active substrates—as well as reusable and self‐cleaning substrate platforms—has emerged as a key research frontier in this domain (Arun Kumar et al. 2023).

SPR Biosensor

SPR is a label‐free, noninvasive optical sensing technique that leverages the collective oscillation of conduction electrons in noble metal nanostructures to enable real‐time and quantitative monitoring of refractive index variations occurring at the sensor‐analyte interface (Ansari et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023). When integrated with specific biorecognition elements, this approach enables continuous monitoring of refractive index variations in the vicinity of the metal surface, providing a sensitive means of detecting biomolecular interactions. This approach offers notable advantages, such as heightened sensitivity, expedited detection, and the obviation of complex sample labeling procedures. It allows for the prompt completion of detection processes and supports real‐time dynamic monitoring of the interactions between target molecules and immobilized antibodies. Aptamers have increasingly shown promise as alternatives to antibodies in the development of SPR biosensors, owing to their small size, high stability, ease of chemical synthesis, and ability to retain binding affinity following labeling. Tran et al. employed capillary electrophoresis‐SELEX to isolate a high‐affinity DNA aptamer against the peanut allergen Ara h 1 (Figure 4a) (Tran et al. 2013), exhibiting a dissociation constant of 353 nmol/L; the resulting SPR biosensor demonstrated robust discrimination of Ara h 1 across a calibrated concentration range in chocolate candy bars, achieving an LOD of 4.76 µg/mL.

However, complex food matrices can adsorb onto the sensor interface, leading to nonspecific interactions that interfere with target recognition. While sample pretreatment can mitigate this issue to some extent, it is often time‐consuming and labor‐intensive, ultimately diminishing detection efficiency (F. Zhao et al. 2023). Consequently, the development of antifouling interfaces that effectively resist nonspecific adsorption has emerged as a crucial strategy. Xia et al. engineered an antifouling surface for SPR biosensing by immobilizing hierarchical zwitterionic peptides. These peptides functioned not only as a robust antifouling layer, demonstrating exceptional resistance to nonspecific protein adsorption (< 5 ng/cm2) and negligible background interference, but also served as the anchoring substrate for the covalent immobilization of specific anti‐peanut allergen antibodies (Figure 4b) (Xia et al. 2024). Through a rigorous spiking and recovery study, this immunosensing platform enabled specific, label‐free detection of peanut allergens over a linear concentration range of 0.5–20.0 µg/mL. Its analytical reliability and translational potential were successfully validated using complex commercial biscuit samples, achieving spike recovery rates of 98.2%–112.0% and proving its high resistance to matrix interference without the need for complex sample pretreatments. Despite its advancements, SPR technology still faces several practical limitations, including elevated sensor costs, the necessity for enhanced operational stability, and challenges associated with achieving large‐scale, high‐throughput detection.

The selection of suitable nanomaterials plays a crucial role in optimizing the SPR effect, thereby significantly improving the sensitivity and overall performance of food allergen detection. Zhang et al. developed a dual‐wavelength photoelectrochemical (PEC) aptasensor by integrating a D‐TA COF with a hydrogen‐bonded organic framework (HOF‐101), followed by the in situ growth of Au2Pt nanoparticles on the COF surface (Figure 4c) (S. M. Zhang et al. 2025). The SPR effect of Au2Pt nanoparticles induces the generation of abundant hot electrons, which effectively suppresses the recombination of photogenerated electron‐hole pairs and promotes interfacial charge transfer. Furthermore, the G‐quadruplex/hemin complex formed in the presence of hemin functions as a DNAzyme, catalyzing the reduction of O2 to OH−. During this catalytic process, electrons are continuously extracted from the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of hemin, thereby promoting directional charge transfer. This enzymatic catalytic process not only facilitates signal amplification but also induces a photocurrent polarity reversal from anodic to cathodic under 980 nm illumination, thereby enabling dual‐mode signal readout of the sensor. The developed sensor demonstrated high sensitivity and selectivity for the detection of the peanut allergen Ara h 1 and the milk allergen β‐LG, achieving linear dynamic ranges of 1–200 ng/mL (LOD = 0.28 ng/mL) and 1–1000 ng/mL (LOD = 0.5 ng/mL), respectively. Pollet et al. developed a nanomagnetic bead‐enhanced SPR strategy utilizing superparamagnetic nanoparticles conjugated with Ara h1 antibodies as secondary labels. This method achieved an LOD as low as 0.09 µg/mL, representing a two‐order‐of‐magnitude improvement over conventional label‐free approaches (Pollet et al. 2011). Furthermore, MIPs, as a class of artificial recognition materials featuring tunable properties and outstanding stability, have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional antibodies. The integration of MIPs with SPR sensing technology combines the inherent advantages of SPR, including label‐free detection and real‐time monitoring, with the enhanced stability and cost‐effectiveness of MIPs, thus addressing the limitations of conventional antibody‐based recognition systems and demonstrating considerable potential for advanced biosensing applications. Ashley et al. were the first to synthesize nanomolecularly imprinted nanoparticles (nanoMIPs) targeting bovine α‐casein via the solid‐phase imprinting method, subsequently integrating these nanoMIPs with SPR technology to develop a label‐free affinity sensor (Figure 4d) (Ashley et al. 2018). The synthesized nanoMIP demonstrated a nanomolar binding affinity for α‐casein, with a dissociation constant of approximately 10 × 10−9 M. The detection limit was determined to be 127 ± 97.6 ng/mL, which surpassed the detection sensitivity of commercially available ELISA kits and most previously reported methods. Recovery rates in spiked wastewater samples ranged from 87% to 120%, demonstrating the excellent accuracy and practical applicability of the method for near real‐time monitoring of allergen residues in food processing environments. However, while traditional commercial SPR systems often rely on large and costly instruments, recent advancements have led to the development of miniaturized, portable platforms capable of multiplexed analysis (Polonschii et al. 2024). Despite these promising prototype developments, the widespread commercial availability of such portable devices and the ability to robustly and simultaneously analyze multiple allergens in complex food matrices remain areas requiring further investigation.

Electrochemical Biosensor

Electrochemical sensors have demonstrated significant potential in the field of food allergen detection owing to their advantages of high sensitivity, rapid response, low sample consumption, and ease of miniaturization (Lv et al. 2023; Riu and Giussani 2020; X. Zhao et al. 2022). Electrochemical sensing is fundamentally based on the immobilization of specific biorecognition elements, such as antibodies or aptamers, on the electrode surface, where the selective binding between the target allergen and the recognition element triggers interfacial electrochemical responses, converting biological recognition events into measurable electrical signals. After signal amplification and processing, ultrasensitive quantitative detection of the target allergen can be achieved (Ferreira et al. 2019; L. H. Li et al. 2016). Recently, the integration of functional materials, such as porous organic frameworks, metal nanoparticles, and carbon‐based nanomaterials, has markedly expanded the electroactive surface area of electrodes and facilitated electron transfer, thereby further improving the analytical performance of electrochemical sensors (Gupta et al. 2021). Considering their practical deployment capabilities, electrochemical biosensors for food allergen detection can be broadly categorized into laboratory‐based benchtop systems and portable, field‐deployable devices. Laboratory‐based benchtop systems typically rely on conventional electrochemical workstations, providing high‐precision measurements and versatile analytical modes under controlled laboratory conditions. In contrast, portable devices integrate miniaturized electrodes, signal‐processing units, and wireless data transmission modules into compact platforms, enabling rapid on‐site screening while preserving high sensitivity and operational convenience. Laboratory‐Based Electrochemical Biosensor

According to the mode of signal output, laboratory‐based electrochemical biosensors can be broadly classified into two categories: single‐signal electrochemical biosensors and dual‐signal ratiometric electrochemical biosensors. In single‐signal electrochemical biosensors, specific biorecognition elements, such as antibodies or aptamers, are immobilized on the working electrode surface. The selective interaction between the target allergen and the recognition element triggers interfacial electrochemical changes, such as altered electron‐transfer kinetics, current responses, or impedance variations, thereby converting the biorecognition event into a single measurable electrical signal. Following signal amplification and processing, quantitative detection of the target allergen can be achieved (Shalaby et al. 2025; C. C. Wu et al. 2023; X. L. Zhang et al. 2024). For instance, Casian et al. employed aptamers as stable alternatives to antibodies to construct an indirect competitive electrochemical sensor based on the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− redox probe, enabling the detection of the peanut allergen Ara h 1 with a detection limit of 0.052 µg/mL (Figure 5a) (Casian et al. 2024). Wu et al. constructed an electrochemical immunosensor by integrating immunomagnetic nanoparticle‐based target enrichment with an AuNP‐modified screen‐printed carbon electrode. The resulting platform enabled highly specific detection of mango allergens, achieving 100% specificity and 91.7% sensitivity (C. C. Wu et al. 2024).

With the rapid development of nanotechnology, various functional nanomaterials have been introduced into the electrochemical sensing interface, which significantly improves the sensitivity of the detection method. These materials, including carbon‐based nanomaterials, metal nanoparticles, and hydrogen‐bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), contribute to enlarged electroactive surface areas, accelerated heterogeneous electron transfer, and improved immobilization efficiency of biorecognition elements, thereby synergistically enhancing signal transduction performance (X. L. Zhang et al. 2024). Şimşek et al. developed a quaternary sensing interface by integrating titanium dioxide (TiO2), carbon nanofibers (CNFs), gold (Au), and palladium (Pd) within a single nanocomposite architecture. In this design, the TiO2–CNF matrix offers a high electroactive surface area to facilitate efficient biomolecule immobilization, whereas the Au–Pd bimetallic nanoparticles enhance interfacial electron transfer through their superior electrical conductivity and synergistic catalytic properties. Benefiting from these synergistic advantages, the sensor achieved an ultralow detection limit of 0.035 pg/mL for the target allergen (Şimşek et al. 2025). Furthermore, our group developed electrochemical aptasensing and immunosensing platforms for Ara h 1 detection based on conductive porous HOFs. Benefiting from their large specific surface area, excellent water stability, and outstanding electrocatalytic properties, HOFs effectively promoted substrate oxidation and amplified the electrochemical response, enabling ultrasensitive detection of Ara h 1 with a detection limit as low as 0.0165 µg/mL (R. Wang et al. 2024).

To further enhance the accuracy and sensitivity of sensing platforms, the development of ratiometric integrated biosensors capable of effectively minimizing interference from external factors has emerged as a highly promising strategy (Lin et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2024; Z. Y. Yang et al. 2024). Ratiometric electrochemical sensors quantify target analytes by measuring the ratio of peak current intensities generated by two electroactive probes with distinct redox potentials (Z. T. Zhu et al. 2025). Typically, one signal is correlated with the target concentration, whereas the other serves as an internal reference for signal calibration and stabilization (X. C. Hu et al. 2024). By analyzing the ratio of these two signals, intrinsic background interference can be effectively compensated for, thereby significantly improving the accuracy and stability of the analytical results.

Our group developed a ratiometric electrochemical biosensor based on a TF‐COF and methylene blue‐encapsulated multiwalled carbon nanotube (MWCNT@MB) system for the ultrasensitive detection of the sesame allergen Ses i 2 (Figure 5b) (Wu et al. 2025). In this platform, MWCNT@MB served as the first signal unit, reducing charge‐transfer resistance while providing a reference signal through the electrochemical response of methylene blue (MB). Meanwhile, an aptamer‐functionalized TF‐COF was employed as the second signal probe, exploiting its excellent conductivity and porous structure to promote the catalytic oxidation of 4‐aminocatechol (ACP) and amplify the current response. In the presence of Ses i 2, the MB current decreased owing to hindered electron transfer, whereas the oxidation current of ACP increased, generating a dual‐signal ratiometric response with opposite signal trends. The sensor demonstrated excellent analytical performance for Ses i 2 detection, exhibiting a broad linear response range of 1.25 pg/mL to 12.5 µg/mL, an ultralow detection limit of 1.0 pg/mL, and outstanding accuracy. Furthermore, we developed a novel ratiometric sensing interface by integrating a ferrocene‐covalently functionalized silica/AuNP composite with a conductive HOF. The sensor demonstrated excellent accuracy and high sensitivity toward the detection of the peanut allergen Ara h 1, with a low detection limit of 1.29 ng/mL. Moreover, its overall analytical performance was superior to that of most previously reported biosensors (Wang et al. 2026). Based on existing studies, the integration of advanced electrode interface engineering with nanomaterial‐based signal amplification strategies provides an effective approach for regulating the electrode surface microenvironment and enhancing interfacial electron transfer efficiency, thus offering a viable pathway toward ultrasensitive detection using electrochemical biosensors.

Portable Electrochemical Biosensor

Despite their notable advantages in sensitivity and accuracy, the aforementioned laboratory‐based sensors remain largely restricted to use under standardized laboratory conditions. However, practical food allergen monitoring often necessitates rapid on‐site screening in settings such as food processing lines, food service establishments, and market surveillance. Accordingly, the development of portable electrochemical biosensors for on‐site detection has emerged as a major research focus, aiming to extend laboratory‐grade analytical capabilities to resource‐limited, real‐world settings through device miniaturization and automated operation.

Lu et al. developed a portable electrochemical aptasensor based on a screen‐printed carbon electrode (SPCE) for the rapid on‐site detection of β‐LG. The original aptamer was optimized through sequence truncation, while a bimetallic ZnCu‐MOF was synthesized using an aging method and subsequently carbonized and decorated in situ with AuPd nanoparticles to produce a highly conductive ZnCu‐NC@AuPd composite, enhancing electron transfer efficiency. In addition, Pt@Au nanorods loaded with MB were assembled with a DNA wheel structure to form signal probes. Upon target recognition, the specific binding of β‐LG to the aptamer disrupted the DNA wheel structure, causing the release of the signal probe and a corresponding decrease in the square‐wave voltammetric response. The sensor exhibited a linear range of 0.01–1000 ng/mL and a detection limit of 1.85 pg/mL. Coupled with a self‐developed miniature electrochemical workstation, on‐site detection could be completed within 1 h at a cost of only $0.72 per test. Recovery rates in spiked milk samples ranged from 97.3% to 104.4% (Lu, Wen et al. 2025). Sundhoro et al. applied a MIP‐based electrochemical sensor for the rapid on‐site detection of soybean allergens. Using genistein as a characteristic soybean biomarker and o‐phenylenediamine as the functional monomer, an MIP film was fabricated on the SPCE surface through potentiostatic electropolymerization (Sundhoro et al. 2021). Differential pulse voltammetry revealed that the oxidation peak current of genistein at the MIP electrode (0.61 V) was 6.6 times higher than that of the non‐imprinted electrode. The sensor achieved a detection limit of 0.1 µg/mL, which is two orders of magnitude below the clinical sensitization threshold. Furthermore, it effectively discriminated against structurally related compounds and common interferents, achieving 100% qualitative accuracy across more than ten real samples. The sensor also exhibited excellent thermal stability, with no significant signal attenuation after storage at 65°C for 10 days.

Jiang et al. developed an origami microfluidic electrochemical nanoaptasensing platform for the detection of the peanut allergen Ara h 1. Chromatographic paper was folded into a 3D microfluidic architecture, while a screen‐printed three‐electrode system was fabricated on the paper substrate, enabling low‐cost detection without complex instrumentation. Exfoliated black phosphorus nanosheets were combined with poly‐l‐lysine and subsequently functionalized with aptamers through electrostatic adsorption, forming highly conductive sensing probes with large specific surface areas. The sensor exhibited good linearity over the range of 50–1000 ng/mL, with a detection limit of 21.6 ng/mL. Recovery rates in spiked cookie dough samples ranged from 98.3% to 107.9%, and the cost per test was approximately $0.80 (H. Jiang et al. 2021). Furthermore, another research group developed a 3D bioprinted vascular microtissue‐based electrochemical biosensor for the rapid and sensitive detection of fish‐derived parvalbumin (Figure 5c) (D. Jiang et al. 2024). Using photocurable 3D bioprinting technology, mast cells and endothelial cells were co‐encapsulated within a conductive GelMA hydrogel containing dopamine‐modified carbon nanotubes to construct biomimetic vascular microtissues with hollow tubular structures. Graphite rod electrodes were sequentially modified with AuNPs and a copper–organic framework before integration with the vascular microtissues. Specific binding between parvalbumin and IgE on mast cell surfaces induced membrane damage and stimulated endothelial cells to release bioactive substances, resulting in concentration‐dependent increases in differential pulse voltammetry current responses. The sensor demonstrated a linear range of 0.1–2.5 µg/mL and a detection limit of 65 ng/mL.

Recent advances in microfluidic technologies, flexible electronics, and low‐power signal processing have facilitated the miniaturization and integration of sensing devices. Among these, self‐powered sensors, as a critical subset of portable sensing technologies, operate independently without reliance on external power sources, thereby demonstrating distinct advantages for on‐site food allergen detection. This feature offers a viable technological pathway toward achieving genuinely field‐deployable, real‐time, and energy‐autonomous diagnostic testing. A recently reported portable self‐powered sensing strategy integrated a glucose‐driven enzymatic biofuel cell (EBFC)‐based electrochemical biosensor with a smart glucometer chip, thereby establishing a miniaturized “energy‐biosensing‐monitoring” platform that integrates power generation, biosensing, and signal readout (Figure 5d) (Xu et al. 2025). The sensing mechanism relies on β‐LG‐induced recruitment of glucose oxidase (GOD) to the graphdiyne‐based bioanode through a specific aptamer. Upon β‐LG binding, the aptamer undergoes a conformational change that facilitates GOD assembly on the electrode, thereby promoting glucose oxidation and generating an electrical current. Because the extent of GOD recruitment depends on the β‐LG concentration, the resulting current is quantitatively correlated with β‐LG levels and can be converted into a glucometer‐compatible readout, which is further transmitted to a smartphone via Bluetooth. The sensor exhibited a linear response over 0.01–10,000 ng/mL β‐LG (R 2 = 0.9978), with an LOD of 0.0033 ng/mL, while the glucometer‐based readout achieved an LOD of 0.0051 ng/mL. Thus, the EBFC served both as the power source and as the signal‐generation component, whereas the commercial glucometer provided a convenient means of quantitative signal readout, enabling portable and self‐powered β‐LG detection. The development of this novel biosensor represents a significant advancement in portable allergen detection. By integrating a commercial glucometer with a self‐powered biosensor, the proposed platform offers a simple and cost‐effective detection strategy while ensuring excellent portability and ease of storage. These advantages make it a promising tool for rapid allergen detection in food safety and healthcare applications, with considerable potential for practical implementation and the protection of public health.

Dual‐Modal or Multimodal Biosensing

Traditional single‐signal biosensors, which rely on a single signal output, often suffer from limited accuracy and poor stability, making it difficult to meet the requirements for precise allergen detection in complex food matrices (Song et al. 2026; X. W. Wang et al. 2025). To address these limitations, multimodal biosensors have been developed by integrating two or more complementary signal transduction mechanisms within a single functional sensing interface. Through inherent self‐validation and self‐correction capabilities, these systems can substantially improve analytical sensitivity, accuracy, and reliability (Z. J. Chen et al. 2025; Pu et al. 2023; Zhi et al. 2022). Zhang et al. reported a ratiometric fluorescence‐colorimetric dual‐mode aptasensing platform constructed from a UiO‐66‐TCPP bifunctional MOF, providing highly accurate detection of the milk allergen β‐LG (C. Zhang et al. 2025). The synthesized UiO‐66‐TCPP possessed dual functionalities, including red fluorescence derived from the TCPP moieties (emission maximum at ∼666 nm) and inherent peroxidase‐mimicking activity that enabled the catalytic oxidation of TMB for colorimetric signal generation. Owing to electrostatic attraction, positively charged UiO‐66‐TCPP readily adsorbed negatively charged β‐LG aptamers. In this state, the FAM‐labeled aptamers underwent efficient fluorescence quenching through combined PET and FRET mechanisms, whereas the framework's inherent red fluorescence (λ em ≈ 666 nm), serving as an internal standard, remained essentially unaffected. At the same time, aptamer incorporation markedly increased the affinity of UiO‐66‐TCPP for the TMB substrate and facilitated electron transfer, leading to a pronounced enhancement of its intrinsic peroxidase‐like catalytic activity. Upon target recognition, the β‐LG aptamer is released from the UiO‐66‐TCPP surface because of its preferential binding to β‐LG, thereby restoring the fluorescence of the FAM reporter, while simultaneously, the peroxidase‐like catalytic activity of the framework decreases and reverts to its native level. Exploiting this target‐responsive “on–off” switching mechanism, the dual‐mode sensor facilitated both ratiometric fluorescence and colorimetric quantification of β‐LG over a broad concentration range of 0.08–200 µg/mL, yielding low detection limits of 16.19 and 47.69 ng/mL, respectively. Moreover, the approach achieved reliable spike‐recovery results in real milk samples and enabled portable, visual detection through smartphone‐assisted RGB analysis, presenting a new paradigm for multimodal, high‐precision food allergen detection.

Furthermore, some nanozymes possess both excellent photothermal conversion capabilities and enzyme‐like catalytic activity, enabling their use in the construction of photothermal‐colorimetric dual‐mode sensing platforms. Zhang et al. prepared a multifunctional biomimetic nanozyme, Au@His‐MIL‐88, composed of histidine‐doped MIL‐88‐coated gold nanostars. The nanozyme displayed enhanced peroxidase‐mimicking catalytic activity toward TMB oxidation as well as quantitative photothermal conversion efficiency under 660 nm laser excitation. Coupled with catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA)‐mediated signal amplification and artificial neural network‐assisted data processing, this platform was used to construct a colorimetric‐photothermal dual‐mode aptasensor for the highly sensitive detection of β‐CG, a major soybean allergen (Y. M. Zhang et al. 2025). The assay showed a wide linear range of 5–5000 ng/mL with an LOD of 1.31 ng/mL and achieved recoveries of 93.1%–114.0% in spiked soybean milk powder and plant‐based meat samples, with RSDs consistently below 5.3%, indicating satisfactory accuracy and robustness, which highlights the potential of this approach as an intelligent and precise analytical strategy for food allergen detection. Despite these quantitative metrics, the methodology presents specific operational limitations. First, the optimal catalytic pH is strictly constrained to 4.0, necessitating a 16‐h sample extraction followed by a 50‐fold dilution in specific buffering conditions, which complicates direct and rapid analysis. Furthermore, there is a thermal operational trade‐off: while the nanozyme achieves its highest catalytic rate at 60°C, the assay must be conducted at 37°C to preserve experimental stability, thereby sacrificing potential maximum sensitivity.

In addition, square‐wave voltammetry (SWV) with PEC sensing was used for the simultaneous detection of β‐LG and peanut allergen Ara h 1 (S. M. Zhang et al. 2026). HOF‐101 and ferrocene served as the respective signal‐generating components for the PEC and SWV modes, while the G‐quadruplex/hemin complex acted as a signal amplifier to enhance the PEC response. β‐LG triggered the release of ferrocene‐modified In1 (Fer‐In1), which subsequently induced the unfolding of G‐quadruplex‐modified hairpin H1, thereby exposing the hemin‐binding sites and facilitating the formation of G‐quadruplex/hemin complexes for PEC signal amplification. Concurrently, the ferrocene‐derived SWV signal increased proportionally with the β‐LG concentration. Ara h 1 triggered the release of Fer‐In2, which subsequently hybridized with H1, resulting in a similar signal amplification process. The biosensor exhibited excellent linear responses to β‐LG and Ara h 1 over concentration ranges of 20–2000 ng/mL and 20–200 ng/mL, respectively, with corresponding detection limits of 12 and 14 ng/mL. Furthermore, the dual‐mode PEC/SWV sensor retained 92.31% of its initial signal after 20 days of storage and exhibited a low RSD of 4.2% across 10 replicate batches, confirming its excellent storage stability and reproducibility. Nevertheless, the sensor still faces challenges, including tedious sample pretreatment, susceptibility of aptamer–protein binding to temperature/pH fluctuations, and the need for performance validation in complex matrices.

To further enhance analytical performance, Zhu et al. developed a cascade multimodal LFIA platform incorporating multifunctional Fe3O4@Ru@Pt nanocomposites and specific M13 Nbs, enabling highly sensitive detection of β‐CG (Y. T. Zhu et al. 2026). In this study, Fe3O4@Ru@Pt nanozymes were rationally designed and synthesized to integrate rapid magnetic separation, broad‐spectrum optical absorption, high peroxidase‐like catalytic activity, and excellent photothermal conversion efficiency within a single multifunctional nanoplatform. Leveraging these integrated functionalities, the cascade platform achieved an ultralow detection limit of 2.02 pg/mL in the photothermal readout mode, corresponding to a 151‐fold enhancement in sensitivity compared with the direct colorimetric mode. Moreover, the platform demonstrated excellent specificity in real‐sample analysis, with spiked recoveries ranging from 83.21% to 114.72%. The synergistic integration of cascade signal amplification with multimodal signal readout substantially enhanced both detection sensitivity and analytical reliability, highlighting the platform's considerable potential for POCT applications.

Summary and Outlook

Food allergies have become a global food safety concern, posing serious threats to human health and quality of life. To protect susceptible consumers from allergen exposure, the development of accurate, sensitive, and highly selective analytical methods is of critical importance. In recent years, emerging biosensing technologies, including fluorescence, colorimetric, SERS, SPR, and electrochemical sensing, have attracted increasing attention because of their operational simplicity, rapid response, high sensitivity, and compatibility with portable and disposable devices. Compared with conventional instrumental methods, these platforms offer considerable advantages for rapid screening and on‐site monitoring of food allergens and therefore hold great promise for decentralized food safety testing. This review first provides a brief introduction to food allergens and conventional detection methods, followed by a comprehensive overview of recent advances in emerging biosensing technologies, with particular emphasis on their advantages and limitations. Nevertheless, several challenges still hinder their practical application and commercialization, including poor reproducibility caused by the aggregation or uneven distribution of nanomaterials, nonspecific signal amplification, insufficient stability and efficiency of bioreceptor immobilization, matrix interference in complex food samples, and the relatively complicated sample pretreatment procedures required for reliable analysis. In addition, the high cost and limited stability of conventional biorecognition elements remain important constraints. Future research should therefore focus on constructing more robust and reproducible sensing interfaces, developing low‐cost and stable recognition elements such as MIPs and Nbs, and exploring novel biological strategies such as CRISPR‒Cas‐based systems. In particular, the combination of aptamer‐mediated recognition with CRISPR‒Cas signal amplification provides a promising strategy for converting protein recognition events into highly amplified nucleic acid signals, thereby enabling ultrasensitive detection of trace allergens (Y. Chen et al. 2024; Malitha et al. 2026; Y. P. Zhang et al. 2026). At the same time, the development of reversible sensing interfaces, magnetic nanocomposites, and stimuli‐responsive materials may facilitate the regeneration and reuse of sensing elements, reducing material consumption, environmental burdens, and the overall cost of large‐scale applications.

Beyond technological optimization, the future trajectory of biosensor development is fundamentally shaped by current market demands, which are bifurcated into two main fields of application. For industrial sectors, the prevailing trend is geared toward continuous and fast monitoring along the entire food supply chain. This application requires devices that are rapid, exceptionally sensitive, and cost‐effective to facilitate large‐volume screening and ensure manufacturing compliance. Conversely, for end‐consumers, the field is rapidly shifting toward personal use devices, with a strong emphasis on portable and wearable technologies. While current commercial devices, such as the portable Allergy Alert systems or the Allergy Amulet, represent significant milestones in consumer‐oriented safety, a critical evaluation reveals inherent limitations. These early‐stage commercial products often face constraints regarding multiplexing capabilities, long‐term operational stability in diverse environmental conditions, and the requirement for user‐friendly sample pretreatment protocols suitable for untrained individuals (Fakeih et al. 2025; M. Wang et al. 2025). Bridging the gap between laboratory‐based proof‐of‐concept sensors and practical commercial products requires further integration of advanced functional materials, miniaturized technologies, and intelligent data‐processing systems. With the continued integration of advanced functional materials, portable technologies, and intelligent signal‐processing algorithms, these approaches are expected to pave the way for more efficient, reliable, and accessible solutions for food safety monitoring and allergen detection.
