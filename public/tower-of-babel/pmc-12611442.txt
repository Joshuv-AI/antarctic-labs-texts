Pain catastrophizing immediately after surgery predicts postoperative patient satisfaction one year after total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Tomofumi Kinoshita, Tatsuhiko Kutsuna, Kunihiko Watamori, Takashi Tsuda, Yusuke Horita, Kazunori Hino, Masaki Takao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPatient satisfaction after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a key measure of clinical outcome. While preoperative pain catastrophizing is known to affect postoperative results, the impact of early postoperative catastrophizing remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether early postoperative pain catastrophizing is associated with PS one year after TKA.MethodsWe evaluated 254 knees in 254 patients undergoing TKA, assessing the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) and visual analogue scale (VAS) one day before, as well as one, three, and seven days after TKA. The 2011 Knee Society Score (KSS) was assessed preoperatively and one year postoperatively. Based on PS scores in the KSS, patients were classified into satisfaction (PS > 20) and dissatisfaction (PS ≤ 20) groups. PCS and VAS scores were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and multiple regression analysis were used to evaluate relationships among factors.ResultsMean PCS scores were 22.8 (SD 12.6), 21.9 (SD 12.2), 18.3 (SD 10.8), and 15.2 (SD 10.5) one day before, as well as one, three, and seven days after TKA, respectively. PCS scores seven days after TKA were negatively correlated with patient satisfaction one year after TKA (ρ = -0.32, p < 0.001). The dissatisfaction group had significantly higher PCS scores at all postoperative timepoints (p = 0.010, p < 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively.). There were no significant differences in VAS scores between groups at any timepoint. Multiple regression analysis revealed that the PCS score seven days after TKA was significantly associated with PS (β = -0.321, p < 0.001). The cutoff PCS score seven days post-TKA for the dissatisfaction group was 12 (area under the curve: 0.653).ConclusionPatients who were dissatisfied with TKA outcomes persistently exhibited high PCS scores immediately after surgery. Surgeons should consider not only preoperative PCS and VAS scores, but also postoperative PCS scores in order to enhance postoperative patient satisfaction following TKA.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(11):1457–1465.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Pain catastrophizing scale, 2011 Knee Society Score, Patient satisfaction, total knee arthroplasty (TKA), visual analogue scale (VAS), Knee Society Scores, VAS scores, Pain Catastrophizing Scale, knees, Multiple regression analysis, correlation coefficients, measure of clinical outcome, postoperative pain

Introduction

In recent decades, the relationship between pain and pain catastrophizing in knee osteoarthritis has been widely discussed.1,2 Severe pain, particularly chronic long-term pain, can induce catastrophizing, which in turn may lead to depression due to fear of pain.3 Additionally, numerous studies have investigated the impact of preoperative catastrophizing on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA).1,4 However, the relationship between postoperative catastrophizing and clinical outcomes remains poorly understood.

Methods of evaluation play a critical role in assessing psychological factors. Previous studies have commonly used tools such as the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey questionnaire,5 Perceived Stress Scale, and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS).6 Among these, the PCS uniquely assesses the psychological status of individuals suffering from chronic pain.6 Dave et al7 reported that chronic pain due to knee osteoarthritis significantly impacts both depression and pain catastrophizing. Although previous studies have demonstrated an association between preoperative PCS scores and postoperative clinical outcomes such as residual pain,1,3,4,6 few have investigated postoperative PCS scores, and the improvement in catastrophic thinking through surgical intervention remains largely unknown.

To clarify the psychological effects of surgery, it is crucial to conduct comprehensive evaluations of pain catastrophizing in the early preoperative period, particularly in patients with longstanding conditions, and to monitor changes in catastrophizing over time. This study aimed to investigate changes in pain catastrophizing following TKA, as well as to clarify the association of PCS scores measured before and immediately after TKA with postoperative patient satisfaction score, using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). We hypothesized that PCS scores in the early postoperative period would more accurately predict patient satisfaction than preoperative PCS scores.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki,8 and was approved by our Institutional Review Board. Patients with severe osteoarthritis who underwent primary TKA between December 2019 and November 2023 and had provided informed consent were enrolled in the study. The exclusion criteria were patients with paralysis (n = one), a prior surgical history of the affected knee joint (n = four), advanced Parkinson’s disease (n = one), or those undergoing simultaneous bilateral TKA (n = 19).

A total of 301 patients were initially enrolled in the study. However, 34 patients who were either lost to follow-up or had missing Knee Society Score (KSS)9 data at the one-year outpatient visit, as well as an additional nine patients with missing postoperative PCS and visual analogue scale (VAS) data, were excluded. Furthermore, patients who developed paralysis due to lumbar spinal canal stenosis (n = 1), sustained lower limb fractures from falls resulting in gait impairment (n = 2), or developed a postoperative infection in the operated knee (n = 1) were also excluded. Consequently, 254 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). This cohort included patients with a history of insomnia (n = 36), depression (n = 1), and panic disorder (n = 1). The patient characteristics are presented in Table I. The implants used in this study were the Persona CR (Zimmer, USA, n = 59) and Persona PS (Zimmer, n = 195). The mean age of the patients was 76.2 years (48 to 91, SD 7.1) (Table I). All procedures were performed using the measured resection technique with mechanical alignment. After primary bone resections, trial components and the thinnest inserts were used initially. Manual varus-valgus stress testing and pull-out lift-off tests at 90° flexion were used to assess stability.10 Insert thickness was adjusted as needed to correct for laxity, and posterior capsular release or additional bone resection was performed if the extension or flexion gaps were tight.11 Definitive components were implanted with cement. All surgeries were performed by four experienced orthopaedic surgeons (TKi, TKu, KW, KH), each certified as a specialist by the Orthopaedic Association.

Intraoperative pain management included local infiltration with a cocktail of analgesics, consisting of 0.75% ropivacaine hydrochloride hydrate 300 mg (40 ml) and 4.95 mg of dexamethasone sodium phosphate. However, use of dexamethasone sodium phosphate was omitted in patients with diabetes mellitus due to the risk of postoperative hyperglycaemia and a potential increased risk of surgical site infection. The volume was adjusted based on patient body weight and injected intra-articularly. Tranexamic acid (2 g) was also administered intra-articularly during surgery, and an additional 2 g was administered intravenously on the day of surgery. Postoperatively, fentanyl was delivered via continuous intravenous infusion at a rate of 0.25 μg/kg/hr for 48 hours, and celecoxib was administered orally at a dose of 400 mg/day for the first postoperative week, followed by 200 mg/day for an additional week. In patients with renal dysfunction, acetaminophen was used as an alternative to celecoxib. Early mobilization was initiated on the first postoperative day. Patients remained hospitalized at our institution for approximately two weeks, after which they were transferred to rehabilitation facilities for an additional two to four weeks, if necessary.

Pain Catastrophizing Scale

The PCS is a 13-item self-report measure that evaluates pain-related catastrophic thinking and is composed of three subdomains: rumination, magnification, and helplessness. Each item is rated on a five-point scale from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘all the time’), with higher total scores indicating greater levels of catastrophizing. The PCS has been validated in multiple populations and is widely used as a psychological screening tool for pain-related outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated that high PCS scores are associated with increased postoperative pain and dissatisfaction following TKA, making it a valuable preoperative predictor in clinical decision-making.1,3,4,6 A summary of PCS domains is presented in Table II. In this study, PCS measurements were taken one day before TKA, as well as one, three, and seven days after TKA.

Patient-reported outcome measures

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the 2011 KSS and VAS. VAS measurements were taken one day before TKA, as well as one, three, and seven days after TKA. KSS measurements were performed before TKA and one year after TKA. As previously reported, patients were classified into satisfaction (patient satisfaction > 20) and dissatisfaction (patient satisfaction ≤ 20) groups based on the patient satisfaction in the KSS one year after TKA.12

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using JMP software (version 14.0, SAS Institute, Japan). Non-parametric tests were used because the data were non-normally distributed according to the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Friedman test was used to compare KSS and PCS scores across measurement timepoints. The non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between the satisfaction and dissatisfaction groups, whereas the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative statuses within the same patients. A power analysis was conducted using the mean and SD from 30 preliminary consecutive measurements. Based on the observed mean difference between the two groups, a minimum sample size of 52 was required to detect a difference of δ = 8 with a SD of σ = 10, assuming 80% power and a significance level of α = 0.05. Accordingly, a sufficient number of participants were enrolled to account for potential limitations associated with a small sample size. Bivariate analysis of PCS and VAS at each timepoint, as well as patient satisfaction score one year after TKA, was performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient tests. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed using VAS and PCS scores which showed statistically significant correlations with the patient satisfaction score one year after TKA as independent variables. Confounding factors, including age, sex, surgeons who performed TKA, implant design (posterior-stabilized TKA or cruciate-retaining TKA), and the presence of insomnia or psychiatric disorders, were included as adjustment variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table III shows the preoperative and postoperative KSS score. Figure 2 and Figure 3, and Table IV, illustrate the changes in mean PCS and VAS scores one day before TKA and one, three, and seven days after TKA. Based on the mean patient satisfaction one year after TKA, 188 and 66 cases were categorized into the satisfaction (patient satisfaction > 20) and dissatisfaction (patient satisfaction ≤ 20) groups, respectively. The PCS score in the dissatisfaction group was significantly higher than that in the satisfaction group at all timepoints after TKA (Table IV). No significant difference in the VAS score was found between the groups at any timepoint (Table IV). Postoperative knee alignment was similar between the two groups, with a hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA) of 1.6° (SD 3.1°) in the satisfaction group and 1.1° (SD 2.6°) in the dissatisfaction group. The cutoff values for the PCS score at one, three, and seven days after TKA for predicting postoperative dissatisfaction at one year were 30 (area under the curve (AUC): 0.604), 17 (AUC: 0.647), and 12 (AUC: 0.653), respectively (Figure 4).

Bivariate analysis

Table V presents the association of patient satisfaction in the KSS with PCS and VAS scores. The PCS scores after TKA were negatively correlated with patient satisfaction one year after TKA at all timepoints, with the correlation coefficient observed seven days after TKA (ρ = -0.32, p < 0.001). In contrast, only the VAS score at seven days after TKA showed a weaker correlation with patient satisfaction one year after TKA (ρ = -0.13, p = 0.040). VAS scores were statistically correlated with the PCS scores measured on the same day at all measurement times (Table VI). PCS score seven days after TKA was not correlated with preoperative VAS score.

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that the PCS immediately following TKA was statistically correlated with the patient satisfaction one year postoperatively, particularly seven days after TKA. In this study, the relationship between PCS scores, VAS scores after TKA, and patient satisfaction one year after TKA was investigated. Based on these results, greater attention should be paid to a residual high PCS score rather than VAS score. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to clarify temporal changes in the PCS score immediately after TKA.

Preoperative catastrophizing has been shown to impact postoperative clinical outcomes.1,3,4,6,13,14 Riddle et al13 found that among 140 TKA patients, the preoperative PCS score was the only consistent psychological predictor of poor postoperative pain outcomes, suggesting that targeting catastrophizing may improve recovery. Forsythe et al14 reported that preoperative PCS scores and the number of comorbidities were significant predictors of chronic pain at 24 months after TKA. Hasegawa et al4 reported that the postoperative numerical rating scale six months after TKA was higher in patients with a PCS score ≥ 30 than in those with a PCS score < 30, establishing a cutoff value for the preoperative PCS score. While their research identified key preoperative PCS thresholds predictive of postoperative pain, our results further suggest that, in addition to preoperative PCS, postoperative PCS may serve as a crucial indicator for achieving more favourable surgical outcomes.

The ability of patients who experience increasingly severe catastrophizing thinking issues to achieve favourable postoperative outcomes has been widely discussed, but previous research remains controversial, and definitive conclusions have yet to be established.1-4,6 However, these studies primarily compared preoperative PCS score with postoperative outcomes and did not assess changes in mental status in relation to postoperative results. A key aspect of our study is the establishment of cutoff values immediately after surgery, enabling the identification of patient groups that may require postoperative therapeutic interventions. Despite the relatively low correlation coefficients (ρ values) observed between PCS and patient satisfaction, our multiple regression analysis revealed that PCS, particularly the score assessed on postoperative day 7, was independently associated with satisfaction at one year. This finding suggests that the early postoperative psychological response may be more predictive of long-term satisfaction than preoperative catastrophizing alone. Therefore, while simple correlation analysis may underestimate the contribution of PCS, our multivariate approach underscores its potential value as a prognostic indicator during the acute postoperative phase.

Various treatments for pain catastrophizing have been reported in several studies.15-17 Moderate exercise has been shown to positively impact pain catastrophizing thinking.15 Additionally, continuous psychological interventions through smartphone applications have been found to effectively reduce catastrophic thinking.14,15 The results of this study reveal that the PCS score gradually decreased over the first seven days after surgery, suggesting that the catastrophic thinking associated with severe pain from knee osteoarthritis can be improved through surgery. However, it was also found that, despite pain relief, strong residual catastrophic thinking remained in a considerable number of patients. Approaches targeting this group are crucial for further improving the outcomes of TKA. Further investigation is needed to establish effective methods for early postoperative interventions.

This study found a correlation between VAS and PCS scores, both preoperatively and postoperatively, in patients undergoing TKA. Since both VAS and PCS are PROMs, individuals exhibiting stronger catastrophic thinking are more likely to report higher VAS scores. Although using preoperative PROMs to predict postoperative PROMs is a straightforward approach,18-20 it is important to recognize that PROMs themselves may be influenced by psychological factors.21,22 Thus, objectively assessing improvements in activity levels and mobility is essential when evaluating postoperative outcomes. Our findings indicate that individuals whose PCS scores did not improve after surgery experienced a decline in satisfaction one year postoperatively. However, further investigation is needed to understand how PCS scores influence actual changes in activity levels. Previous studies have documented that catastrophic thinking profoundly affects activities of daily living in patients with osteoarthritis.23,24 Therefore, it is important to further investigate whether the enhancement of catastrophizing thinking following TKA contributes to actual improvements in physical activity levels.

This study has several limitations. First, multiple implant designs were used, rather than a single standardized implant. Although posterior-stabilized implants were used more frequently than cruciate-retaining implants, implant type was included in the regression analysis and was not significantly associated with patient satisfaction or PROMs. Therefore, we believe the impact of implant type on our findings is minimal. All procedures were performed by four experienced orthopaedic surgeons (TKi, TKu, KW, KH). Given their comparable expertise and the finding that the choice of surgeon was not significantly associated with patient satisfaction or PROMs in the multiple regression analysis, we believe the influence of inter-surgeon variability on the outcomes was minimal. Second, the R² value in multiple linear regression analysis was low, highlighting the limitations of the model in predicting patient satisfaction and pain catastrophizing. This low R² was likely due to numerous unmeasured factors that affect these constructs, such as individual patient characteristics and external factors, including social or cultural influences. Future research should aim to incorporate additional variables and consider other psychological measures to improve the predictive capability of the model. In addition, as mentioned above, all evaluation methods used in this study, including PCS, VAS, and the KSS, are PROMs. These measures may be influenced by psychological factors beyond PCS alone, potentially affecting KSS and VAS as well. Moreover, we did not assess the use of medications for pain management or psychological interventions after surgery. Further, standardized psychiatric screening by a psychiatrist was not performed in this study; therefore, information regarding psychiatric conditions may be incomplete. However, multiple regression analysis revealed that the presence of pre-existing psychiatric disorders, including insomnia, was not associated with patient satisaction at one year postoperatively. Nevertheless, as the number of patients with conditions such as depression was extremely small, further investigation may be necessary in populations with such psychological backgrounds. Finally, we did not assess preoperative factors related to PCS score seven days after TKA. Preoperative VAS was not correlated with PCS seven days after TKA. Clarifying factors affecting postoperative PCS, such as the duration patients suffered from knee osteoarthritis, walking ability, and muscle strength, is necessary. These limitations may restrict the generalizability of the results. However, the results of this study suggest that evaluating PCS postoperatively is as important as preoperatively. Further verification is also needed regarding whether early intervention in PCS affects postoperative outcomes.

In conclusion, patients dissatisfied with TKA outcomes persistently exhibited high PCS scores immediately after surgery. Surgeons should consider not only preoperative PCS and VAS scores, but also postoperative PCS scores, to enhance postoperative patient satisfaction following TKA.

Take home message

- Patient satisfaction after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is associated with early postoperative catastrophizing thinking.

- In particular, higher Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) scores at seven days postoperatively are associated with lower satisfaction one year after surgery.

- Considering postoperative as well as preoperative PCS scores may help improve patient satisfaction following TKA.
