In-silico discovery of druggable molecular signatures that drive dengue fever to severe dengue fever highlighting common pathogenesis through single-cell RNA-Seq analysis
Authors: Md Al Noman, Md Abdul Latif, Md Foysal Ahmed, Md Shariful Islam, Md Al Amin Pappu, Md Sanoar Hossain, Md Bayazid Hossen, Tasfia Noor, Md Manir Hossain Mollah, Md Hadiul Kabir, Md Nurul Haque Mollah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue, also known as dengue fever (DF), is an infection spread by mosquitoes. It infects over 400 million people globally each year. Sometimes DF progresses to severe DF (sDF) for which patients suffer from life-threatening complications including internal bleeding, failure of cardiovascular systems and death. Although DF is a severe threat to human life, its diagnosis and therapeutic strategies have not yet reached a satisfactory level. In order to address these issues, at first, this study identified six key cell-types (myeloid dendritic cells (DCs), memory B cells, naive B cells, memory CD4 T cells, plasmacytoid DCs, and plasmablasts) associated with both DF and sDF by integrating two single-cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-seq) profile datasets (GSE220969 and GSE154386) and cell–cell communication (CCC) analyses. Top-ranked nine common host key genes (SYK, CYBB, SOCS3, HSPA5, IRF7, NFKB1, IL-6, ISG15, and TNFSF13B) were identified from the selected cell types through differential expression patterns and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analyses, assuming their potential involvement in the development and progression of DF and sDF. The enrichment analysis of common host key-genes (chKGs) with gene ontology (GO) terms and KEGG-pathways disclosed molecular mechanisms about how chKGs are associated with the development and progression of DF to sDF. Finally, chKGs-guided three candidate drug agents (Entrectinib, Imatinib, and QL47) were recommended against dengue virus infection (DENVI) through molecular docking, drug-likeness screening, ADME/T analysis, DFT analysis. Therefore, the findings of this study may provide valuable insights for diagnosis and therapies against DENVI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23000-3.Keywords: Dengue virus infection, Single-cell RNA-Seq profiles, Infection-causing key genes, Drug repurposing, Bioinformatics analysisSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Dengue virus (DENV) is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA flavivirus transmitted by mosquitoes. A person gets infected with the DENV, which belongs to the Flaviviridae family and is transmitted by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, when an infected mosquito bites their skin1. It enters in host cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis by first binding to glycosaminoglycans and receptors like DC-SIGN. Once inside, it hijacks the host’s mRNA machinery to replicate itself altering the gene expression and disrupting the immune signaling2. Thus, DENV manipulates host cellular processes, including transcription, translation, RNA decay, and epigenetic regulation, to enhance its replication and evade immune responses3. It also blocks several immune signaling pathways to spread the disease in the host. Viral nonstructural proteins, specifically, NS3 and NS4a proteins prevent the movement of the RIG-I protein to the mitochondria. NS2a and NS4b proteins hinder the activation of TBK1, thereby blocking the MAVS pathway and stopping the production of IFN-β. Additionally, the NS2b protein blocks CGAS for autophagy–lysosome-dependent degradation and prevents the sensing of mitochondrial DNA. The NS2b protease further suppresses IFN production by cutting the STING protein2 (Figure S1).

Dengue fever (DF), a febrile illness caused by the DENV affects over 400 million people worldwide and DENV includes four distinct but antigenically related serotypes: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-44. In 2023, a total of 6.43 million dengue cases and 6,892 deaths were reported worldwide, with an incidence rate of 56,672 cases and 28.45 deaths per million people5. Some patients may experience serious complications known as severe dengue fever (sDF) which is characterized by plasma leakage, haemorrhagic shock and occasionally death6. The sDF starts from an excessive immune response, including a cytokine storm, antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), and endothelial dysfunction7. During sDF, the critical phase occurs when the fever subsides, as plasma leakage and circulatory failure may develop, increasing the risk of shock8. There are several individual studies under bulk transcriptomics profiles including microarray studies9–14 and RNA-seq studies15–17 that explored DF-causing genes highlighting their pathogenetic processes. Recently single-cell RNA-Seq (scRNA-Seq) profile analysis has become more popular compared to the bulk transcriptomics analysis in identifying disease-causing genes, since scRNA-Seq profile analysis provides detail view at the level of individual cells that can’t be revealed by bulk transcriptomics analysis18. There are some individual studies based on scRNA-Seq profiles that explored DF19–21 and sDF22–24 causing cell-type specific host key-genes (hKGs) to disclose their individual molecular mechanisms. However, so far, there is no study in the literature that has disclosed druggable key molecular signatures and their pathogenesis for which DF progress to sDF though there is no satisfactory treatment available against dengue infection25. Therefore, this study aimed to address these issues in order to disclose common pathogenesis and candidate drugs as the common treatment in both stages of DF and sDF.

Methods and materials

Data source and descriptions

In order to explore DF and sDF causing cell-type specific chKGs, we considered two scRNA-seq count datasets with accession numbers GSE22096924 and GSE15438621 from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) platform of NCBI database. From GSE220969, we selected 4 healthy, 8 DF, and 8 sDF samples, while GSE154386 provided 4 DF samples (Table S1). The datasets were obtained from both infected and healthy children who were uninfected, aged 4 to 17 years, and included participants of both sexes among which eleven children were female (Table S1).

Data pre-processing and integration

The scRNA-Seq count data was pre-processed using Scanpy (version 1.11.0) in Python 3.11.526, a versatile tool for single-cell gene expression analysis, offering pre-processing, visualization, clustering, differential expression, and various other advantages. To identify and remove doublets (cases where two cells may have been mistakenly captured together) we applied SOLO27. After filtering out all detected doublets, only singlet cells were retained for further analysis28. We customized Scanpy’s parameters for quality control, filtering out cells with < 200 genes29, genes in < 3 cells, and cells with > 10% mitochondrial expression to remove low-quality data30. MALAT1 was removed from the transcript list because of its high enrichment, which could produce biasedness in the clustering results31. Then we merged 24 samples to construct a single scRNA-Seq profile dataset. Then a gene was removed if its expression counts in all cells are more than 99%. Cells were normalized using ‘scanpy.pp.normalize_total’ (1 × 104 counts per cell) and log-transformed with ‘scanpy.pp.log1p’. Then, we used scVI (version 1.0), a Variational Autoencoder-based deep-learning tool, to process the data while accounting for covariates32. This method effectively removed unwanted variation and corrected batch effects33. We identified 5000 highly variable genes using ‘scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes’ and stored them for further analysis. After pre-processing, we obtained high-quality single-cell data for analysis. All of these analyses were performed using Scanpy and Python script in Jupyter Notebook34 environment (version 7.4.2).

Cell clustering

We used the ‘scanpy.pp.neighbors’ function with its default settings to compute the nearest-neighbors distance matrix and construct a neighbourhood graph for the cells. To illustrate the graph in low dimension (2D) and improved visualization, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used35. The cells are represented in two axes (UMAP_1 and UMAP_2). To detect distinct cellular groups, we applied the Leiden algorithm (‘scanpy.tl.leiden’), a powerful method for identifying communities in complex networks36. By setting the resolution parameter to 0.937, we effectively divided the neighbourhood graph into well-defined clusters.

Identification of cluster-specific marker genes and corresponding cell-types

To pinpoint the marker genes, we performed differential expression analysis using the ‘scanpy.get.rank_genes_groups_df’ function which extracted the top 15 marker genes from the differential gene expression analysis. The Wilcoxon test was used for this analysis, and only genes with a p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1 were considered differentially expressed38. To ensure a reliable and biologically meaningful annotation of cell types, several databases were integrated. The Human Protein Atlas39 was used to link protein expression patterns, while CellMarker 2.0 provided a comprehensive database of cell-specific markers40. Additionally, PanglaoDB was used to validate gene expression associated with specific cell types41.

Cell–cell communication analysis for exploring DENVI related cell types

To accurately detect DENVI related cell types through ligand-receptor interactions from scRNA-seq data, we analyzed communication between different cell types42,43. This was done using two computational frameworks: LIANA + and Tensor-cell2cell44. LIANA is a flexible analytical platform that consolidates multiple ligand-receptor databases and methodologies to study cell–cell communication (CCC). We utilized these tools to identify context-specific cell–cell communication (CCC)45 patterns for both comparisons: healthy vs. DF and DF vs. sDF to find the key cell types.

Identification of cell-type specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and common DEGs

We identified host DEGs for each key cell type and extracted common DEGs (cDEGs) among DENVI severity groups. We conducted differential expression analysis on scRNA-seq data between case (DF/sDF) and control groups using the t-test, implemented through the “de.test.t_test” function from the diffxpy package in Python 3.11.546. DEGs consistently found in key cell types were selected for functional analysis using a p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC|> 1 as thresholds. Then we selected cDEGs between DF and sDF separately for upregulated and downregulated DEGs obtained. The identified cDEGs were then visualized using Jvenn47, providing a clear comparison of shared and unique differentially expressed genes between healthy vs. DF and DF vs. sDF conditions.

Identification of common host key-genes (chKGs)

To identify chKGs, we built separate PPI networks48 for upregulated and downregulated cDEGs using the STRING database (version 12.0)49. To improve visualization and analysis, we used Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) software50. chKGs were identified through CytoHubba, a plugin in Cytoscape51. This tool ranks proteins in the network using eleven different topological analysis methods based on shortest path calculations. We evaluated the PPI network using four key metrics: CLOSENESS, DEGREE, RADIALITY, and STRESS. Degree is the number of direct connections a node has; in PPI networks, high-degree proteins act as hubs that quickly spread effects to many partners52. Closeness is the reciprocal of a node’s average shortest path to others, indicating how efficiently it can reach the network53. Radiality measures a node’s reachability; proteins with above-average radiality strongly influence others54. Stress centrality counts the shortest paths passing through a node, highlighting key players in network communication55. In this network, nodes represent proteins, while edges indicate interactions between them. The genes that were common in top-ranked ten genes found from each of these measures, were identified as chKGs.

Verification of the association of chKGs with DENVI through independent database

We have conducted a disease-chKGs enrichment analysis using the Enrichr web tool56 in conjunction with the DisGeNET database57. This methodology enabled us to explore the association between chKGs and various viral disorders, including DENVI.

Identification of regulatory factors of chKGs

To identify the key transcription factors (TFs) and micro-RNAs (miRNAs) that regulate chKGs, we performed interaction network analysis using the NetworkAnalyst 3.0 platform58. For TF- chKGs interactions, we used data from the JASPAR database59, while TarBase60 provided information on miRNA-chKGs interactions. To improve network visualization, we utilized Cytoscape 3.9.1 software. The core regulatory elements were determined through two topological analysis methods: Betweenness61 and Degree62, which helped highlight the most influential nodes within the network.

Identification of GO-terms and KEGG pathways related to DF and sDF

To identify the most significant gene ontology (GO) terms covering molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and biological processes (BP)63 as well as relevant Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways64–66 linked to chKGs, we conducted an enrichment analysis.

For this study, we used the DAVID (v2024q4) web tool67 to identify statistically significant GO terms (BP, MF, and CC) and KEGG pathways associated with chKGs related to DF and sDF.

Exploring candidate drug molecules against DF and sDF

We explored target proteins and potential drug agents through molecular docking analysis to identify FDA-approved repurposable drugs for disease. Drug target receptors were considered to be proteins mediated by chKGs and related TFs. We extracted 186 drug agents (Table S8) by reviewing published literatures and other databases (DGIdb, IDG and DrugMatrix using the Enrichr web tool). The 3D structures of the target proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB)68 and AlphaFold69 databases. The PubChem database was used to select the 3D structures of all disease-associated drug compounds70. Receptors were preprocessed (missing hydrogen atoms and Kollman charges were added) using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6)71 and drug molecules were optimized with Avogadro (version 1.2.0)72. Using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.x)73, molecular docking was then performed. The docking results were visualized and analysed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2021(v21.1.0.20298)74. Molecular visualization played a critical role in modelling and analysing the interactions between the selected compounds and the target proteins.

In-silico validation of candidate drugs

To validate the drugs for DENV treatment, we used different software, web-tools, and databases as introduced below:

Drug-likeness and ADME/T analysis

To explore the structural features and chemical characteristics of the top 30 drug molecules, we assessed their drug-like properties and ADME/T (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profiles. The top 30 drug molecules were selected based on the average binding affinity scores (BASs), by which receptors and ligands were ranked. More specifically, let Bmn denote the binding affinity score between mth receptors (m = 1, 2, …, q) and nth ligands/agents (n = 1, 2, …, p). Receptors were sorted based on the descending order of their average scores , while ligands/agents were ranked by the descending order of their average scores The pkCSM75 web tool was employed to determine if the compounds adhered to the Lipinski rule of five76. ADME/T properties were also predicted using the pkCSM75 tool. To evaluate the safety profiles of candidate compounds, in silico toxicity predictions were performed using the pkCSM pharmacokinetics server. Compounds predicted with low acute toxicity, were classified as having minimal toxicity relative to other screened candidates. Compounds meeting both the Lipinski and ADME/T criteria were considered as potential drug candidates.

Cross-validation of candidate drugs

To confirm the pharmacological relevance of our top candidate compounds prior to molecular docking for cross-validation, we applied in-silico QSAR-based biological activity predictions using PASS Online web tool77,78. PASS Online analysis was performed using canonical SMILES of each compound retrieved from PubChem70. Activities with Pa > 0.4 were retained as this threshold can be used to get relevant dengue pathogenesis79.

To validate the docking method and assess its binding accuracy, we applied three conditions to the proteins used for cross-validation. If there was a naturally attached ligand in the receptor protein, we re-docked between them. Then we compared their binding affinity score (BAS) with the BAS of the receptor proteins and the proposed ligands. We used active/decoy ligands from DUDE database80 to validate our docking protocol. In this case, we compared the BAS for the active and decoy ligands and also compared the BAS of the proposed ligands with the BAS of the active ligand. In the absence of a naturally bound ligand, we used experimentally validated ligands known to inhibit the target protein as a standard and then compared with the scores with our proposed ligands.

Then, to validate the selected candidate drugs for DENVI treatment, we collected 37 distinct receptors identified as key-genes (KGs) in published literatures (Table S12) and performed molecular docking with the selected candidate drugs to see the binding affinities. We also identified the top-ranked 5 DEGs for sDF only and performed molecular docking analysis to determine the binding affinities with the selected candidate drugs.

Density functional theory (DFT) analysis

Density Functional Theory (DFT) was applied to evaluate the structural, functional, and quantum chemical properties of the phytocompounds identified in the molecular docking analysis. The Becke-3-parameter Lee–Yang–Parr (B3LYP) method81, along with the 6–311 G82 basis set, was utilized in the Gaussian-09W (version 9.5)83–85 software to calculate the thermodynamic energies of the compounds, as well as their electronic and structural properties, and frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energies. For this study, Gaussian-09W and GaussView 686 were employed to compute the energy levels of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), and the energy gap (ΔE) for the drug molecules associated with the highest-ranking complex87.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To investigate the dynamic behavior of the top protein–ligand complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the Yet Another Scientific Artificial Reality Application (YASARA) software88 with the AMBER14 force field. The simulations were carried out on prioritized complexes, which were selected based on their highest docking BASs. Prior to simulation, each complex was solvated using a TIP3P (Transferable Intermolecular Potential with 3 Points) water model89 and the hydrogen bond network was optimized within the simulation cell. Periodic boundary conditions were applied, maintaining a solvent density of 0.997 g/L. Energy minimization of the systems was performed using simulated annealing with the steepest descent method over 5,000 cycles. Simulations were run under physiological conditions (298 K, pH 7.4, and 0.9% NaCl), using a multiple time-step approach90 with a time-step of 2.5 fs91. Each system was simulated for 100 ns under constant pressure and temperature using the Berendsen thermostat92. Trajectory data were recorded every 100 ps for downstream analyses. Initial trajectory analyses were conducted using YASARA’s built-in macros93 and the SciDAVis program (http://scidavis.sourceforge.net/). Subsequently, all trajectory snapshots were processed for every 100 ps to calculate binding free energies using the MM-PBSA (Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area) method implemented in YASARA94. For further details on the MD simulation protocol, refer to our earlier studies95,96.

Binding free Energy = EpotReceptor + EsolvReceptor + EpotLigand + EsolvLigand − EpotComplex − EsolvComplex.

Here, the binding energy of MM-PBSA was computed using YASARA built-in macros via AMBER 14 as a force field indicating the more positive energy, the better binding.

Results

Obtaining high-quality cells by preprocessing of scRNA-Seq count data

In order to filter out low-quality cells from scRNA-Seq profiles, we performed preprocessing of scRNA-Seq count data generated from pediatric samples across different groups/batches (healthy, DF, and sDF). At first, we identified 1,81,670 cells from raw count data. After removing doublets and other low-quality cells, we obtained 1,53,535 high-quality cells, including 80,596 cells from DF patients, 52,412 from sDF patients, and 20,527 from healthy individuals. Before applying batch correction, a noticeable batch effect was observed in the sample specific scRNA-Seq profiles (Fig. 2A). However, after batch correction, the distribution of cells became more uniform, indicating that technical variations among batches had been effectively reduced (Fig. 2B). After batch correction, distinctions, especially between control and sDF cells, became more defined, improving disease-specific gene expression patterns.

UMAP visualization of scRNA-seq data (A) without and (B) after batch correction for healthy, DF and sDF samples. This UMAP plot shows the scRNA-seq data colored by disease status.

Identification of cell clusters and cluster-specific marker genes

28 distinct cell communities were detected from the high-quality cells as distinct cellular groups (Fig. 3A). To classify the clusters, the top 15 marker genes per cluster, determined by the highest logFC values (Table S2), were examined for cell specificity using well-established databases such as CellMarker 2.040, Human Protein Atlas39, and PanglaoDB41. Following the criteria set by Khan et al. (2022), a cell type was assigned to a cluster if at least 50% of its top marker genes were linked to that specific type97.

UMAP plot showing (A) cell clusters and (B) the annotated cell types from the scRNA-seq dataset. Each dot represents an individual cell, and each color represents a particular cell type.

Identification of cell-types

The classification of the clusters provided 14 distinct cell types (Fig. 3B), distributed as follows: plasmablasts (29.6%), NK cells (18.6%), naive B cells (14.4%), classical monocytes (9.9%), naive CD4 T cells (9.4%), T-reg cells (6.3%), memory B cells (4.9%), CD8 T cells (4.1%), plasmacytoid DCs (0.6%), platelets (0.5%), non-classical monocytes (0.4%), memory CD4 T cells (0.4%), myeloid DCs (0.4%), and progenitor cells (0.4%). Among these, plasmablasts were the most abundant, followed by NK cells, naive B cells, classical monocytes and the remaining cell types.

The key marker genes for each category included: CD8 T cells (CCL5, CD8A), NK cells (GNLY, CST7), plasmablasts (IGHG1, IGLV2-14), platelets (PPBP), progenitor cells (SPINK2), T-reg cells (IL32), classical monocytes (S100A8, S100A9), memory B cells (MS4A1), memory CD4 T cells (IL7R), myeloid DCs (CD1C, CD33), naive B cells (TCL1A), naive CD4 T cells (LEF1, TCF7), non-classical monocytes (MS4A7), and plasmacytoid DCs (LILRA4, CLEC4C) (Table S3).

Cell–cell communication analysis for exploring DENVI related cell types

Context-specific cell–cell communication (CCC) analysis examined cell type interactions between healthy and DF conditions, as well as between DF and sDF. The interactions were decomposed into 12 factors for the healthy vs. DF comparison and 10 factors for the DF vs. sDF comparison. Spearman coefficients quantify the correlation between sample loadings and disease conditions. A positive correlation indicates that the factor-associated interaction pattern is more prevalent in the second state of the comparison, whereas a negative correlation indicates greater prevalence in the first state. This framework allows us to identify the factor that best captures the difference in interactions between the two conditions. In the healthy vs DF comparison, Factor 3 showed a negative correlation (-0.53), indicating that this interaction pattern was more prevalent in healthy individuals and reduced in DF, making it the most significant factor for distinguishing these conditions. In the DF vs sDF comparison, Factor 2 showed a positive correlation (0.41), indicating that this interaction pattern was more prevalent in sDF than in DF (Table 1). These two factors were therefore selected for identifying relevant cell types in subsequent analyses. These shifting communication patterns highlighted changes in ligand-receptor (LR) interactions with disease progression.

Identification of cell-type specific DEGs and cDEGs

We have examined DEGs in selected cell types for both healthy vs. DF (myeloid DCs, memory B cells, naive B cells, memory CD4 T cells, plasmacytoid DCs, and plasmablasts) and DF vs. sDF (myeloid DCs, memory B cells, naive B cells, memory CD4 T cells, and plasmacytoid DCs). DEGs for these cell types, including both upregulated and downregulated genes, are identified using t-tests with a threshold of |log2FC|> 1 and p-value < 0.05.

Finally, we separately merged the upregulated and downregulated DEGs for each comparison and identified 659 cDEGs (282 upregulated, 377 downregulated) (Table S6) shared between the two disease conditions (Fig. 5A–B).

Venn diagram of cDEGs across two different disease comparisons (healthy-DF and DF-sDF). (A) Up-regulated cDEGs and (B) Down-regulated cDEGs.

Identification of chKGs from cDEGs

We used the STRING database98 to build a PPI network for the cDEGs. The network for the downregulated cDEGs had 334 nodes and 2732 edges (Fig. 6A), while the upregulated cDEGs included 250 nodes and 821 edges (Fig. 6B). To identify chKGs, we applied four topological measures CLOSENESS, DEGREE, RADIALITY, STRESS within these networks (Table S7). The genes that were common in top-ranked ten genes found from each of these measures, were identified as the most central genes which were also called chKGs. As a result, we identified nine chKGs including 5 downregulated (Fig. 6A) and 4 upregulated (Fig. 6B) chKGs: HSPA5, SYK, CYBB, SOCS3, IRF7, NFKB1, ISG15, IL-6 and TNFSF13B.

Identification of regulatory factors of chKGs

To determine the transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulators of chKGs, we examined their networks with TFs and miRNAs. First, we identified the top three TFs (YY1, FOXC1, GATA2) as transcriptional regulators by applying two topological measures, degree (≥ 5) and betweenness (≥ 60) (Fig. 7B). Next, using the same measures but with cutoff values of degree ≥ 8 and betweenness ≥ 770, we selected the top three miRNAs (hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-103a-3p, hsa-miR-98-5p) as post-transcriptional regulators of chKGs (Fig. 7A).

Exploring chKGs-guided common drug molecules against DF and sDF

As dengue progression involves multiple host pathways, we docked candidate drug-like molecules into the identified chKGs and TFs to assess their potential across a disease-relevant receptor set. We selected 12 drug target receptors, comprising nine chKGs and three TFs. The 3D structures for eight receptors—IL-6, ISG15, TNFSF13B, SYK, CYBB, GATA2, NFKB1 and HSPA5 were accessed from the Protein Data Bank with their PDB identifiers 4O9H, 2HJ8, 5Y9J, 1A81, 8GZ3, 1u36, 6zyh and 5O9B. The AlphaFold models of the remaining four targets (IRF7, SOCS3, YY1 and FOXC1) were retrieved from UniProt using their respective identifiers: AF-Q92985-F1, AF-O14543-F1, AF-P25490-F1 and AF-Q12948-F1. Receptors were preprocessed (missing hydrogen atoms and Kollman charges were added) using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6)71 and drug molecules were minimized with Avogadro (version 1.2.0)72.

Then, we evaluated binding affinity scores (BAS) between proposed receptors and candidate drugs through molecular docking (Table S9), where drugs and receptors were ranked by average BAS, providing insights into receptor (chKGs)-specific drug efficacy. The top-ranked 30 drug molecules are mapped to their corresponding receptors (Fig. 8A). The goal of this study was to identify multi-targeted drug molecules. That’s why we considered 12 key proteins as drug targets. To investigate their off-targeted effect, we performed molecular docking analysis of the selected top three drugs with the top-ranked 11 independent receptors (Fig. 8b) as well as 5 uncommon receptors (Fig. 8c) associated with sDF only, and found their significant binding affinity scores. We also investigated ADME and toxicity screening (see next section). Further experimental validation will be required to evaluate therapeutic benefit against potential host-related risks.

Cross-validation of candidate drugs

To confirm the pharmacological relevance of our top candidate compounds (ENTRECTINIB, IMATINIB, and QL47) prior to molecular docking, we implemented QSAR-based biological activity prediction using PASS Online tool. We found that all three compounds possessed bioactivity properties relevant to dengue virus-induced pathogenesis (Table S10). ENTRECTINIB demonstrated a fairly high probability (Pa = 0.62) of acting as a platelet-derived growth factor receptor kinase inhibitor, which is associated with antiviral activity99. IMATINIB demonstrated high probability for protein kinase inhibitor (Pa = 0.81), which is also associated with antiviral activity100. Finally, QL47 was predicted as a moderate Cytochrome P450 inhibitor (Pa = 0.41)101. As these compounds showed relevance to antiviral activities, we considered these compounds for performing docking to see binding with the independent receptor proteins.

To validate the reliability of our molecular docking protocol, we evaluated binding energy from docking based on the nature of each receptor. For proteins containing ligands in their PDB structures (TYMS, RELA, and IL12A), we extracted the native ligands and re-docked them using AutoDock Vina. The resulting binding scores indicated strong binding compared to the binding with our candidate drugs, as expected (Table S11). For the homology-modelled protein PPARG, which lacked a known bound ligand, we retrieved active and decoy molecules from the DUDE database. The docking protocol was assessed by its ability to distinguish active compounds from decoys based on binding energy, showing clear differentiation. In cases where neither re-docking nor the DUDE approach was applicable, we used published inhibitors as standard references and demonstrated efficient binding, suggesting strong confidence in our docking results. Detailed information, including affinity scores and compound IDs, was provided in Table S11. 11 genes were used in validating the method as these genes were either validated in wet-lab with their inhibitors or their decoys were found.

Once we had verified the adequacy of the protocol, we assessed the effectiveness of the three candidate drugs against 37 distinct independent receptors identified as key-genes (KGs) for DENVI in several literature (Table S12), which also showed strong binding. Among them, BAS of top 11 independent receptors were showed in Fig. 8B.

The KGs found only for sDF compared to DF were also used to find BAS with the suggested drugs, which had shown strong binding. Thus, the suggested three drugs can also be useful for sDF patients (Fig. 8C).

Density functional theory (DFT) analysis

Through DFT analysis using Gaussian 09, we examined quantum chemical properties of leading compounds (Table 6), analyzing HOMO–LUMO energies and key molecular parameters. The HOMO–LUMO energy gap analysis demonstrates that ENTRECTINIB, IMATINIB, and QL47 exhibit similar lower energy gaps (approximately 2.76 eV). Their HOMO energies range from -7.644 eV to -8.159 eV, while LUMO energies span -4.712 eV to -5.393 eV (Table 6). These lower energy differentials suggest increased molecular stability among these compounds. QL47 demonstrated the highest electron affinity (5.393 eV), followed by IMATINIB (5.266 eV), and ENTRECTINIB (4.882 eV). Higher electron affinity values suggest enhanced electron-accepting capabilities, influencing biological interactions.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

From above sections we noticed that Entrectinib, Imatinib, and QL47 met all drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic criteria. Therefore, these three drugs were selected for stability evaluation through 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The protein–ligand complexes were selected based on the highest docking BASs. The analysis involved monitoring key parameters such as RMSDBb, and RMSF. RMSD measures the average displacement of atoms over time compared to a reference structure, reflecting the overall stability of the ligand-receptor complex. As shown in Fig. 9, all three drug-receptor complexes demonstrated notable stability throughout the simulation, both at the initial stage and during dynamic variations. The average RMSD values for the HSPA5_Entrectinib, SYK_Imatinib and HSPA5_QL47 complexes stabilized at 1.688, 4.517, and 1.332 Å, respectively, indicating moderate structural deviations and relatively stable configurations. HSPA5_QL47 had the lowest RMSD, suggesting stronger binding and enhanced stability compared to the other complexes (Fig. 9A). RMSF quantifies the flexibility of individual amino acid residues in the protein, highlighting regions with significant fluctuations. The RMSF analysis indicates that the HSPA5_QL47 complex has the highest rigidity and stability, with an average value of 1.15 Å, compared to 1.27 Å for HSPA5_Entrectinib, and 2.92 Å for SYK_Imatinib (Fig. 9B). This suggests that HSPA5_QL47 forms the most consistent interaction with its target. These results suggested that Entrectinib, Imatinib, and QL47 exhibit stable and effective binding to their target receptors, highlighting their potential as therapeutic agents for treating both DF and sDF.

Discussion

Dengue virus infection (DENVI) is a serious threat to human health if it is not properly treated at the initial stage. This study identified cell-type specific common host-KGs (chKGs) associated with DF and sDF progression through scRNA-Seq profile analysis, suggesting their potential causal involvement and highlighting their relevance for diagnosis and therapeutic exploration. At first, 153,535 high-quality cells were detected from scRNA-Seq count data by removing doublets, filtering mitochondrial genes and other preprocesses. Batch effects were removed to ensure uniform cell distribution across samples which were visualized using UMAP35 (Fig. 2). Then 28 distinct cell clusters were identified using Leiden36 community detection algorithm of cell clustering. To classify these clusters, top 15 marker genes (differential among clusters) per cluster were determined by the highest logFC values (Table S2). Using these genes and well-established databases, the clusters were annotated into 14 major cell types (plasmablasts, NK cells, naive B cells, classical monocytes, naive CD4 T cells, T-reg cells, memory B cells, CD8 T cells, plasmacytoid DCs, platelets, non-classical monocytes, memory CD4 T cells, myeloid DCs, and progenitor cells). The most abundant cell types were plasmablasts, NK cells and naïve B cells. But myeloid DCs, memory B, naive B cells, memory CD4 T cells, plasmacytoid DCs, and plasmablasts were the cell-types that showed differential ligand-receptor interactions among the disease states (healthy vs. DF and DF vs. sDF) based on context-specific cell–cell communication (CCC) analysis45. These six key cell-types were selected to conduct differential expression analysis to identify DEGs. Then, from these key cell-type specific DEGs for both DF and sDF, chKGs were identified between these two disease states separately for upregulated and downregulated common DEGs (cDEGs). 4 chKGs (SYK, CYBB, SOCS3 and HSPA5) were selected from upregulated DEGs and 5 chKGs (IRF7, NFKB1, IL-6, ISG15 and TNFSF13B) were selected from downregulated DEGs using protein–protein interaction (PPI) network48 constructed by the STRING database49.

The chKGs identified in this study contribute in developing DENVI through several immune signaling pathways. SOCS3 is a critical gene, whose overexpression in DENV-infected cells helps the virus to evade the innate immune response that weakens the host’s antiviral defense102. The expression of this gene is upregulated by IL-10, which serves an anti-inflammatory role and its elevated levels reflect a dysregulated immune state103. The transcription factor Yin Yang 1 (YY1) also promotes the expression of SOCS3104. Upregulation of SOCS3 as well as FOXC1 can suppress NF-κB mediated signaling, indirectly causing downregulation of NFKB1105,106. The NFKB1 gene encodes the transcription factor NF-κB, which regulates immune and inflammatory responses to a wide range of stimuli, including pathogens like DENV107. But the downregulation of this gene may result in poor immune responses activating the development of DENVI. NF-κB activation during DENV infection promotes the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-β, which are essential for antiviral defense and inflammation control108,109. The SYK gene encodes Syk, a tyrosine kinase essential for immune cell (B cell) development and activation110,111. During antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) in DENVI, SYK amplifies IL-1β production and enhances toll-like receptor (TLR) and NF-κB signaling112. The cytokines produced by these factors are central to the early antiviral defense mechanisms and inflammation regulation. But excessive release of these cytokines caused by upregulation of SYK potentially triggers cytokine storms that helps in developing severe case112. The CYBB gene encodes a subunit of the NADPH oxidase complex113 that generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) during immune responses. The production of ROS plays a critical role in activation of NF-κB signaling that increases the inflammatory response114. The IRF7 gene plays a central role in initiating type 1 interferon responses which is crucial in antiviral immunity. DENV can suppress IRF7 to avoid immune detection and promote disease progression115. The remaining chKGs found in this study also have influential contribution in the signaling pathways that develop DENVI116–124. Together, these genes and their associated signaling pathways orchestrate the pathological responses during DF and sDF.

Since these host genes and related pathways are hijacked by DENV to suppress the immune responses, targeting these host factors with known candidate drugs may provide antiviral effects and reduce the chances of disease progression. To identify effective DENV treatments, we screened 186 drugs against 12 target proteins (nine chKGs and three key TFs) using docking analysis. Three top candidates (Entrectinib, Imatinib, and QL47) were selected based on their strong binding affinities, drug-likeness properties, ADME/T analysis and MD simulation. We used top-ranked ten drugs for ADME-Tox prediction. Among these drugs, some were validated in the clinical trials and their ADME-Tox properties were published in database like Drugbank. But other drugs were not validated by the clinical trials. To know their ADME-Tox properties we needed to predict and compare them with already validated reference drugs. We also assessed the effectiveness of these three drug molecules against 37 distinct independent receptors identified as key-genes (KGs) for DENVI in several literatures (Table S12), that also showed strong binding which validated our result. The KGs found only for sDF compared to DF were also used to find association with the suggested drugs, which had also shown strong binding. This validates that our proposed drugs will be useful for treating patients in severe state. Lipinski’s Rule confirmed their drug-like properties, showing excellent oral bioavailability and HIA scores above 90%, indicating efficient absorption. Toxicity screenings found no safety concerns. These compounds exhibited high oral absorption, minimal BBB penetration, and low toxicity, making them strong therapeutic candidates. Some physiochemical properties were also shown by DFT analysis including the HOMO–LUMO energy gap (ΔE), chemical hardness (ɳ), and electrophilicity index (ω). The molecular dynamics simulation results also showed stability suggesting suitability for the treatment of both DF and sDF. Entrectinib stood out as a broad-spectrum antiviral, directly inhibiting DENV with IC50 values of 2.43 μM (DENV2)125. Imatinib combats DENV by inhibiting c-ABL kinase, blocking viral entry and infection. It reverses DENV-2-induced endothelial changes, restores junctional proteins, and suppresses mesenchymal markers, highlighting its potential in sDF treatment126,127. QL47 is a potent antiviral that targets host mechanisms, inhibiting BTK kinase and viral translation. It disrupts the DENV infection cycle at non-toxic levels, offering broad-spectrum potential with a strong therapeutic index128,129. To investigate the off-targeted effect of the proposed drugs, we performed molecular docking analysis for the selected top three drugs with the top-ranked 11 independent receptors (Fig. 8b) as well as 5 uncommon receptors (Fig. 8c) associated with sDF only, and found their significant binding affinity scores. We also investigated the drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties (i.e. ADME/T) of the top three drug molecules and found their suitability as the candidate drugs. The toxicity results in ADME/T analysis showed no toxic risks associated with the proposed three drugs. Therefore, the proposed drugs might be the safe treatment to the patients. Further wet-lab and clinical studies are needed to confirm their effectiveness for DF and sDF treatment.

Conclusion

This study provided a detailed analysis of scRNA-seq profile datasets to disclose both DF and sDF-causing cell-type-specific common host key-genes (chKGs) for diagnosis and therapies. By integrating two scRNA-seq datasets (GSE220969 and GSE154386), this study identified 14 distinct cell types. Among these, six key cell types including myeloid dendritic cells (DCs), memory B cells, naive B cells, memory CD4 T cells, plasmacytoid DCs, and plasmablasts were highlighted through CCC analysis. From these cell types, nine top-ranked chKGs (IL-6, IRF7, NFKB1, ISG15, TNFSF13B, HSPA5, SYK, CYBB, and SOCS3) were identified based on their robustness, underscoring their roles in biological functions, pathways, and regulatory mechanisms relevant to diagnosing, predicting outcomes, and treating DF and sDF patients. Additionally, disease-chKGs enrichment analysis confirmed a strong connection of chKGs with both DF and sDF. The enrichment analysis of chKGs also revealed a strong association between these genes and shared GO terms linked to DF and sDF. Further regulatory analysis of chKGs with TFs and miRNAs identified three key transcriptional regulators (YY1, FOXC1, and GATA2) and three post-transcriptional regulators (hsa-let-7e-5p, hsa-miR-103a-3p, and hsa-miR-98-5p). Furthermore, chKGs-guided drug discovery identified three top-ranked drug candidates Entrectinib, Imatinib, and QL47 through molecular docking, drug-likeness assessment, ADME/T analysis and DFT analysis. Most of the results in this study also received support by the literature review. Therefore, these findings could play a significant role in developing effective treatment strategies against both DF and sDF.
