Abstract
High-redshift galaxies exhibit compact regions of intense star formation, known as “clumps,” which are conspicuous in the rest-frame ultraviolet. Studying them can shed light on how they form and evolve and inform theoretical models of galaxy evolution. We examine the evolution of clumpy galaxies with redshift and stellar mass over 2 < z < 12 2<z<12 with James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) imaging from the JWST Advanced Extragalactic Survey (JADES). Off-center clumps are detected in the rest-frame near-ultraviolet (NUV) using similar techniques to those in earlier studies based on Hubble Space Telescope (HST) images. This is done for a sample of 9,121 star-forming galaxies with stellar masses log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) ≥ 8 \log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 8 . The fraction of clumpy galaxies, f clumpy f_{\rm{clumpy}} , increases from ∼ 20 % \sim 20\% at z > 6.5 z>6.5 to ∼ 80 % \sim 80\% at z ∼ 2.75 z\sim 2.75 at log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) ≥ 9 \log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 9 . Our f clumpy f_{\rm{clumpy}} values are generally higher at fixed redshift and increase faster with decreasing redshift than what studies based on HST data found, which we attribute largely to the higher sensitivity of JWST. Clumps make up 15 − 20 % 15-20\% of the NUV luminosity of clumpy galaxies, with little dependence on redshift or stellar mass. f clumpy f_{\rm{clumpy}} correlates with stellar mass and there is little correlation with specific star-formation rate. Our f clumpy f_{\rm{clumpy}} measurements are compared with those from simulations as well as other observations. At low redshifts ( z ≲ 5.75 z\lesssim 5.75 ) and intermediate-to-high stellar masses ( log ⁡ ( M ⋆ / M ⊙ ) ≥ 9 \log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 9 ), our results suggest gas fragmentation due to violent disk instabilities to be the dominant mechanism for forming clumps. At high redshifts and low stellar masses, compression of gas during mergers appears to dominate.



I
Introduction

Early Hubble Space Telescope (HST) imaging of high-redshift galaxies revealed systems with patchy, clumpy, kpc-scale substructures
(
Cowie et al. 1995
;
Giavalisco et al. 1996
;
van den Bergh et al. 1996
;
Elmegreen & Elmegreen 2005
;
Elmegreen et al. 2007
)
. Later work showed that these substructures (hereafter, “clumps”) are bright in the rest-frame ultraviolet (UV), are fairly massive (
\gtrsim 10^{7}~M_{\odot}
), have young stellar ages (
\sim 100
Myr), and make up about
10-50\%
of the total star-formation rates (SFRs) and about
4-20\%
of the total stellar mass of their host galaxies, on average
(
Elmegreen et al. 2008
;
Elmegreen et al. 2009
;
Förster Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Guo et al. 2012
;
Guo et al. 2015
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Wisnioski et al. 2012
;
Wuyts et al. 2012
;
Wuyts et al. 2013
;
Livermore et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Soto et al. 2017
;
Zanella et al. 2019
;
Mehta et al. 2021
;
Vanzella et al. 2021
;
Meštrić et al. 2022
;
Claeyssens et al. 2025
)
. These results suggest that clumps were significant sites of star formation in galaxies over
0.5<z<3
, during the peak of cosmic star-formation activity
(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
)
.

Studies of observed and simulated clumps have proposed two main formation mechanisms. The first is violent disk instabilities (VDI), wherein gas collapses under gravitational instabilities in marginally stable gaseous disks
(
Noguchi 1998
;
Noguchi 1999
;
Immeli et al. 2004a
;
Immeli et al. 2004b
;
Bournaud et al. 2007
;
Bournaud et al. 2009
;
Agertz et al. 2009
;
Dekel et al. 2009a
;
Ceverino et al. 2010
;
Romeo et al. 2010
;
Romeo & Agertz 2014
;
Inoue et al. 2016
)
. The VDI is sustained by the high gas fractions in high-redshift galaxies
(
Tacconi et al. 2020
, and references therein)
, which are fed by the accretion of cold gas from the intergalactic medium
(
Birnboim & Dekel 2003
;
Kereš et al. 2005
;
Kereš et al. 2009
;
Dekel & Birnboim 2006
;
Bournaud & Elmegreen 2009
;
Dekel et al. 2009b
;
Dekel et al. 2009a
;
Nelson et al. 2013
)
. Observational support for the VDI has been found by examining integral field spectroscopic data, with which maps of gas kinematics may be measured
(
Förster Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Genzel et al. 2011
;
Genzel et al. 2014
;
Wisnioski et al. 2011
;
Wisnioski et al. 2012
;
Newman et al. 2012
;
Mieda et al. 2016
)
. The second mechanism is mergers, during which gas is compressed by tidal forces and/or turbulence due to gravitational interactions between galaxies
(
Di Matteo et al. 2008
;
Renaud et al. 2015
;
Nakazato et al. 2024
)
. Observational support for mergers has come from visual inspection of the morphologies of clumpy and non-clumpy galaxies
(
Straughn et al. 2015
)
and integral field spectroscopy
(
Puech 2010
;
Menéndez-Delmestre et al. 2013
)
.

Clumps could also evolve within their host galaxies and alter their morphologies. Theoretical studies and simulations have predicted that clumps experience dynamical friction and/or interactions, which cause them to migrate to the centers of their hosts and contribute to the growth of bulges
(
Noguchi 1999
;
Immeli et al. 2004a
;
Immeli et al. 2004b
;
Dekel et al. 2009a
;
Krumholz & Dekel 2010
;
Ceverino et al. 2012
;
Inoue & Saitoh 2012
;
Perez et al. 2013
;
Bournaud et al. 2014
;
Mandelker et al. 2014
;
Mandelker et al. 2017
;
Dekel et al. 2022
)
. However, other simulations find that clumps are short-lived and dissipate within
\sim 100
Myr due to stellar feedback, which drives strong outflows of gas (
Murray et al. 2010
;
Genel et al. 2012
;
Hopkins et al. 2012
;
Moody et al. 2014
;
Buck et al. 2017
;
Oklopčić et al. 2017
;
Meng & Gnedin 2020
; see also
Fensch & Bournaud 2021
). In this scenario, stars formed within the clumps could contribute to the growth of the thick disk (
Noguchi 1996
;
Inoue & Saitoh 2014
; see also
Bournaud et al. 2007
).

The evolution of the fraction of clumpy galaxies (
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
) with redshift may also provide clues to the physical processes through which clumps form and evolve
(
Guo et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
)
. Using HST imaging data,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
has been estimated at various redshifts by identifying compact, UV-bright regions within galaxies. The behavior of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
with redshift is well understood at
z\leq 3
, but unclear at higher redshifts. At
z\leq 3
,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
peaks at
\sim 60\%
at
z\sim 2
and declines at lower and higher redshifts
(
Elmegreen et al. 2007
;
Overzier et al. 2009
;
Puech 2010
;
Guo et al. 2012
;
Murata et al. 2014
;
Guo et al. 2015
;
Hinojosa-Goñi et al. 2016
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Adams et al. 2022
;
Sok et al. 2022
;
Martin et al. 2023
;
Sattari et al. 2023
)
. These studies are largely consistent with predictions from simulations of the redshift evolution of galaxies undergoing VDI, based on simulations
(
Cacciato et al. 2012
)
. Support for mergers was also found at lower redshift
(
Puech 2010
)
and lower masses
(
Guo et al. 2015
)
. At
z>3
, there is disagreement on the behavior of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
with redshift. One group of studies found that
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
decreases from
\sim 40\%
at
z\sim 3
to
\sim 20\%
at
z\sim 8

(
Ravindranath et al. 2006
;
Conselice & Arnold 2009
;
Oesch et al. 2010
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
)
. Another found that
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is roughly constant with redshift at
\sim 40\%
from
z\sim 4
to
z\sim 8

(
Jiang et al. 2013
;
Kawamata et al. 2015
;
Curtis-Lake et al. 2016
;
Bowler et al. 2017
;
Ribeiro et al. 2017
)
.

Studies using data from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have revealed a more complex picture of the clumps. Thanks to the higher angular resolution, greater sensitivity, and longer wavelength coverage of the JWST, sub-kpc substructures in faint galaxies at high redshift can now be studied in greater detail with its Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam). JWST imaging of gravitationally lensed galaxies over
1<z<8.5
revealed a significant population of clumps that were not detected by the HST
(
Claeyssens et al. 2023
;
Fujimoto et al. 2024
)
. Un-lensed galaxies at
z>4
also reveal clumps in JWST images
(
Tacchella et al. 2023
;
Hainline et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, some of which are not found in HST observations
(
Bik et al. 2024
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. On average, 76% of the rest-frame UV light from
z\simeq 6-8
galaxies was found to come from the clumps
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
, which is much higher than the
\sim 20-30\%
measured over
0.5<z<3

(
Guo et al. 2015
)
. The rest-optical and rest-NIR properties of clumps have been analyzed out to higher redshifts (
z\sim 2
) than what is possible with HST
(
Kalita et al. 2024
;
Kalita et al. 2025
;
Mercier et al. 2025
)
. Massive (
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 10
) star-forming galaxies at
z<3
were found to have higher
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
when undergoing starburst activity
(
Faisst et al. 2025
)
. Finally, about 70% of spectroscopically selected UV-bright galaxies at
z\sim 7
contain clumps
(
Harikane et al. 2025
)
, in contrast with the
10-20
% inferred from pre-JWST studies
(
Shibuya et al. 2016
, and references therein)
.

While recent studies show that clumpy galaxies might be more abundant than expected at high redshifts, interpretation of these results is difficult due to selection effects and occasional small sample sizes. The largest post-JWST sample at
z>6
to date for which
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
has been measured consists of 53 galaxies
(
Harikane et al. 2025
)
. That sample was selected to be intrinsically bright with absolute UV magnitudes
-23<M_{UV}<-19
mag.
Tan et al. 2024
measured
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
over
0.25<z<5
for a sample of 909 galaxies expected to be progenitors of Milky Way analogs. Their sample was selected with bins in stellar mass that evolve with redshift. This complicates comparisons with pre-JWST studies, which largely selected galaxies with a mass range that was constant with redshift.
Claeyssens et al. 2025
examined the physical properties of 1,751 clumps in 377 galaxies over
0.7<z<5.5
that are gravitationally lensed by the cluster Abell 2744. A measurement of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
as a function of redshift was not performed in that work.

In this paper, we measure
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
over
2<z<12
with JWST data for a sample of 9,121 star-forming galaxies. The selection of the sample is performed such that results from the JWST and HST data may be directly compared. This allows us to determine whether the redshift evolution of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is significantly different with JWST data compared with HST data.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we describe the data used in this work. The selection criteria of our sample are given in Section
III
. Section
IV.1
describes the completeness of the routine used to identify clumps. The redshift evolution of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is discussed in Section
V
. The contribution of clumps to the rest-frame NUV luminosity is shown in Section
VI
. In Section
VII
, we examine the correlation between stellar mass and sSFR with
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
. Predictions from simulations of clumpy galaxies are compared with our observations in Section
VIII
. We discuss the implications for clump formation mechanisms in Section
IX
. We summarize and conclude in Section
X
.

Throughout this work, cosmological parameters measured by
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
are adopted, with
H_{0}=67.7~\rm{km}~\rm{s}^{-1}~\rm{kpc}^{-1},\Omega_{\rm{m}}=0.309
, and
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.691
. All magnitudes are in the AB photometric system

Oke & Gunn 1983
)
.

Data

II.1
Images, Source Detection, and Photometry

This work is based on NIRCam observations of the Great Observatories Origins Deep Survey (GOODS;
Giavalisco et al. 2004
) North and South (hereafter, GOODS-N and GOODS-S) fields, taken by the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (JADES;
Rieke et al. 2023b
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
;
Bunker et al. 2024
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
). These fields were also observed by HST as part of the Cosmic Assembly Near-infrared Deep Extragalactic Legacy Survey
(
Grogin et al. 2011
;
Koekemoer et al. 2011
, CANDELS;)
. The combined area of these fields observed by NIRCam is 124 arcmin
2
. The GOODS-N field was observed in 11 NIRCam bandpasses: F090W, F115W, F150W, F182M, F200W, F210M, F277W, F335M, F356W, F410M, and F444W. GOODS-S was observed in these and three additional bandpasses: F430M, F460M, and F480M. Observations in GOODS-S in the F182M, F210M, F430M, F460M, and F480M bandpasses were taken by the JWST Extragalactic Medium-Band Survey
(
Williams et al. 2023b
, JEMS,)
. The 5
\sigma
point-source limiting magnitude measured in 0.″3 circular apertures is
\sim 29.5
mag in each bandpass.

Rieke et al. 2023b
detected sources in JADES and performed photometry in nine NIRCam and 14 ground- and space-based bandpasses. Briefly, a detection image was created by stacking the F277W, F335M, F356W, F410M, and F444W images and weighting them by their inverse variances. Next, various source detection routines in the
python
module
Photutils

(
Bradley 2023
)
were used to detect and deblend sources and perform photometry. All images are aligned to the same reference frame and drizzled to a common pixel scale of 0.″03 per pixel. Source detection and photometry are performed on HST and JWST images convolved to the F444W resolution. For more details, see Section 4 in
Rieke et al. 2023b
.

This work relies on photometry in HST and JWST bandpasses. Spatially integrated fluxes are measured in Kron apertures with a Kron parameter of
k=2.5
. Spatially integrated errors on the HST and JWST photometry are computed as follows. Two sources of error are considered: errors due to sky subtraction and a combination of source Poisson and instrumental errors. The former are measured in apertures of various sizes that are centered on empty regions of the sky (see Section 4.2 in
Rieke et al. 2023b
, also
Labbé et al. 2005
;
Whitaker et al. 2011
; and
Skelton et al. 2014
). For JWST/NIRCam images, the latter come from the ERR extension, and for HST images, they are measured following the procedure described in Sec. 4.1 by
van der Wel et al. 2012
. Total errors on the JWST photometry are computed by adding these two sources of error in quadrature. For the HST images, the JADES team have measured errors due to sky subtraction and report the average values of the weight, or inverse variance, within the Kron apertures. The error due to the weight for each galaxy is computed by multiplying the average weight value by the area of the Kron aperture in pixels. Total errors on the HST photometry are calculated by adding these two errors in quadrature. A final error is added to all fluxes for each bandpass to account for zeropoint errors and mismatches in the templates between model and observed SEDs. It is equal to 5% of the flux (see, e.g., Table 4 of
Dahlen et al. 2013
and Table 11 of
Skelton et al. 2014
).

Error maps in the JWST bandpasses, which are needed to detect clumps, come from the ERR extension in the images provided by the JADES team. Those in the HST bandpasses are measured following the procedure described in Sec. 4.1 in
van der Wel et al. 2012
.

Axis ratios, half-light radii, segmentation maps, and central coordinates of galaxies were measured from the detection images by
Rieke et al. 2023b
. These measurements and data products are adopted for this analysis. They were calculated by applying
photutils
to the detection image in each field. Half-light radii were measured in circular apertures. Central coordinates were computed by taking the light-weighted centroids along the horizontal and vertical axes of the detection images.

II.2
Photometric and Spectroscopic Redshifts

Photometric redshifts in JADES come from
Hainline et al. 2024
and are estimated using
EAZY

(
Brammer et al. 2008
)
.
EAZY
measures photometric redshifts by fitting non-negative linear combinations of templates to observed fluxes in order to derive probability distribution functions of the redshift. Templates are supplied by the user.
Hainline et al. 2024
develop modified versions of the original galaxy templates used by
Brammer et al. 2008
and seven new templates designed to fit the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of high-redshift galaxies. For more details, see Section 3 by
Hainline et al. 2024
.

Spectroscopic redshifts come from the JADES and the compilation over the CANDELS fields by
Kodra et al. 2023
. Descriptions of the selection of the targets, reduction and calibration of the data, measurements of the redshifts, and presentation of the JADES spectra are provided by
Bunker et al. 2024
and
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
. The provenances of the spectroscopic redshifts gathered by
Kodra et al. 2023
and descriptions of the quality cuts applied can be found in that work and references therein.

) and the latter are measured using

(

. The bias, normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD) scatter, and outlier fraction are measured according to

.

The accuracy of the photometric redshifts measured by the JADES team is assessed by comparing them with spectroscopic redshifts. We find 2,774 galaxies with spectroscopic redshifts in GOODS-N and 3,006 in GOODS-S. Their redshifts span
0<z<13.35
. The accuracies of the photometric redshifts are quantified using the bias, normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD) scatter, and outlier fraction according to
Dahlen et al. 2013
. The bias is given by

\textrm{bias}=\textrm{mean}\left(\Delta z/(1+z_{\rm{spec}})\right),

(1)

where
\Delta z=z_{\rm{spec}}-z_{\rm{phot}}
. It is computed after excluding outliers, which are defined below. The NMAD scatter is:

\textrm{NMAD}=1.48\times\textrm{median}\left(|\Delta z|/(1+z_{\rm{spec}})\right).

(2)

The outlier fraction is given by the fraction of galaxies that satisfy
|\Delta z|/\left(1+z_{\rm{spec}})\right)>0.15
.

Spectroscopic and photometric redshifts are compared in Figure
1
. We measure a bias of 0.002, an NMAD scatter of 0.034, and an outlier fraction of 12.4% for all 5,780 redshifts from both fields. There is good agreement between the spectroscopic and photometric redshifts, given the low bias and NMAD scatter. The outlier fraction is somewhat high, which may be due to the shallower depth of and fewer medium bands available in GOODS-N than those of GOODS-S. Similar NMAD scatter and outlier fractions were obtained by
Puskás et al. 2025
. They built a catalog of spectroscopic redshifts in GOODS-N and GOODS-S and classified them in terms of their quality. Of these, 5,382 in GOODS-S were found to have the highest quality, for which they measured an NMAD scatter of 0.065 and outlier fraction of 6.91%. For the 2,591 highest-quality redshifts in GOODS-N, an outlier fraction of 13.62% was measured.

II.3
Stellar Masses and Star-Formation Rates

Stellar masses and star-formation rates for all galaxies in the full sample (Section
III
) are measured by fitting their SEDs. The SFRs used in this work are averaged over the last 100 Myr. Where available, fits were performed to the HST/Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS) bandpasses F435W, F606W, and F775W combined with all the NIRCam bandpasses listed in Section
II.1
. The number of filters used in each fit ranges from five to 17, with a median of 13. The Bayesian SED-fitting tool
bagpipes

(
Carnall et al. 2018
)
is used. It fits SEDs using the
MultiNest
Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm
(
Feroz et al. 2009
)
and returns marginalized posterior probability distribution functions for each parameter of interest. Stellar masses and SFRs are measured by taking the median stellar mass and SFR from their respective posteriors.

We adopt the priors listed in Table 1 in
Carnall et al. 2020
, adjusting those on the metallicity and the amount of dust attenuation in the rest-frame
V
-band,
A_{V}
. This is done based on arguments for physically motivated priors given by
de la Vega et al. 2025
. A Gaussian prior on the metallicity
Z/Z_{\odot}
with a mean
\mu=0.3
and standard deviation
\sigma=0.5
is assumed. Another Gaussian prior on
A_{V}
is assumed, with
\mu=0.3
and
\sigma=1.0
, over the range
A_{V}\in[0,6]
. This is adopted from
Leja et al. 2019
.

Priors for other physical parameters are as follows. A uniform prior on the log of the stellar mass
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)
is assumed over the range of 1 to 13. We adopt the flexible attenuation curve model by
Salim et al. 2018
. It modifies the shape of the attenuation curve measured by
Calzetti et al. 2000
by multiplying it by a power law that varies with wavelength. For the slope of the power law,
\delta
, a Gaussian prior is set with
\mu=0
and
\sigma=0.1
over the range [-0.3, 0.3]. The
Calzetti et al. 2000
curve is obtained by setting
\delta=0
. The strength of the dust absorption feature at rest-frame 2,175 Å is also modeled by
Salim et al. 2018
. We set a uniform prior on it over the range [0, 5]. Another prior is assumed for the differential attenuation between young (
\leq 10
Myr) and old stars
(
Calzetti et al. 2000
;
Charlot & Fall 2000
, see, e.g.,)
. The former are generally dustier than the latter due to additional attenuation from birth clouds. A uniform prior on
\eta
, the ratio of
A_{V}
for young stars to that for old stars, is assumed over the range [1, 5]. SED fits include nebular emission, the strength of which is determined with the log of the ionization parameter,
\log U
, and the nebular metallicity. A uniform prior is set on the former over the range [
-4,-2
]. The latter is assumed to be the same as that of the stars.

Last, a double power-law star-formation history is assumed. It is given by

\textrm{SFR}(t)\propto\left[\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{-\beta}\right]^{-1},

(3)

where
t
is the time since star-formation began in a galaxy,
\tau
is the time after the Big Bang when the SFR peaked,
\alpha
is the declining slope, and
\beta
is the rising slope. We assume log-uniform priors for
\alpha
and
\beta
, each of which is over the range [0.01, 1000]. A uniform prior on
\tau
is set, which ranges from 0.1 Gyr to the age of the Universe at the observed redshift of a given galaxy.

For the SED fits, we fix the redshift to its spectroscopic value, if available. Otherwise, the photometric redshift from JADES is assumed. We apply upper limits on photometry with signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)
<3
following the procedure by
de la Vega et al. 2025
. This is relevant only for the HST and bluest NIRCam filters, especially at high redshift.

Stellar population synthesis templates from version 2.2.1 of the Binary Population and Spectral Synthesis
(
Eldridge et al. 2017
;
Stanway & Eldridge 2018
, BPASS;)
models are used. The initial mass function is set to the default ‘135_300’ model listed in Table 2 in
Stanway & Eldridge 2018
. The
cloudy

(
Ferland et al. 2017
)
photoionization code is used to model nebular emission. All HST and JWST filter transmission curves, which are used in the SED fits, are taken from the SVO Filter Profile Service
(
Rodrigo et al. 2012
;
Rodrigo & Solano 2020
)
.

In Figure
2
, the relation between stellar mass and SFR, also known as the star formation main sequence
(
Noeske et al. 2007
;
Rodighiero et al. 2010
;
Whitaker et al. 2014
;

;
Schreiber et al. 2015
;
Tomczak et al. 2016
;
Popesso et al. 2023
;

;

, SFMS; e.g.,)
is shown. These properties are derived from the SED fits. The distribution of stellar masses and SFRs measured in this work agrees broadly with the SFMS measured in various studies.

II.4
Stellar Mass Completeness Limits

Here we compute 90% mass completeness limit for our selected sample. The limits are based on the method in
Pozzetti et al. 2010
and follow what was done for galaxies in JADES by
Simmonds et al. 2024
. The stellar masses obtained with
bagpipes
are used.

The completness limits are estimated as follows. First, a limiting stellar mass,
M_{\rm{lim}}
, is computed for all galaxies in the full sample (Section
III
). It is defined to be

\log\left(M_{\rm{lim}}\right)=\log\left(M_{\star}\right)+0.4\left(m-m_{\rm{lim}}\right),

(4)

where
\log\left(M_{\star}\right)
is the stellar mass in solar units of a given galaxy,
m
is its apparent magnitude, and
m_{\rm{lim}}
is the limiting magnitude of the survey. In this work, the magnitude in the F444W band is used to calculate
M_{\rm{lim}}
. This band is used as it is the longest NIRCam filter available in both GOODS-N and GOODS-S and thus best traces the stellar mass. After finding
M_{\rm{lim}}
for all galaxies, they are divided into redshift bins (see Section
III.1
). In each bin, the faintest 20 percent of galaxies are identified. Then, the 90th percentile of
M_{\rm{lim}}
for these is found. We take
m_{\rm{lim}}
to be 29.8, which is the average
5\sigma
point-source depth across the GOODS-N and GOODS-S fields, as measured by
Puskás et al. 2025
.

Completeness limits span
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)=7.16-8.21
. They change monotonically with redshift. These limits are comparable to those derived by other studies where there is overlap in redshift
(
Ciesla et al. 2024
;
Simmonds et al. 2024
;
Puskás et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

III
Sample Selection

III.1
The Full Sample

We start with all sources in the JADES GOODS-N and JADES GOODS-S photometric catalogs. There are 179,709 sources in both fields: 85,709 in GOODS-N and 94,000 in GOODS-S.

All sources that are brighter than or equal to 29th magnitude in the reddest broadband filter, F444W, are selected. This is about 0.5 mag brighter than the limiting magnitudes for all NIRCam filters in JADES
(
Rieke et al. 2023b
, Table 2 in)
. This cut removes 82,606 objects with 97,103 remaining. Next, we remove point sources using the
FLAG_ST
flag. This cut removes 531 sources, leaving 96,572 galaxies. All sources that are contaminated by diffraction spikes from bright stars are removed with the
FLAG_BS
flag. Those that are contaminated by bright neighbors are removed using the
FLAG_BN
flag. The former cut removes 1,504 galaxies and the latter removes 23,672, with 71,396 left.

Galaxies over
2<z<12
are selected. This is done to enable the identification of clumps in the rest-NUV at all redshifts, following pre-JWST studies
(
Wuyts et al. 2012
;
Guo et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
, over the widest possible range in redshift. The lower limit of
z=2
is chosen because the bluest NIRCam bandpass available, F090W, spans the rest-frame NUV starting at
z=2
. The upper limit of
z=12
is chosen such that the reddest bandpasses sample the rest-frame Balmer break. The range
2<z<12
thus spans the rest-UV to rest-optical at all redshifts for the NIRCam bandpasses in JADES. This rest-frame wavelength coverage is optimal for the measurement of stellar masses and SFRs
(
Pacifici et al. 2012
;
Pforr et al. 2012
;
Buat et al. 2014
)
. Applying the redshift cut removes 38,947 galaxies and leaves 32,449.

Further cuts are applied to select galaxies that are brighter than or equal to 29th magnitude in a NIRCam bandpass spanning the rest-frame NUV, which is assumed to cover 0.19 – 0.27
\mu
m (cf. the GALEX NUV bandpass). Galaxies must be brighter than 29th magnitude in F090W over
2<z<3.5
; in F115W over
3.5<z<5
; in F150W over
5<z<6.5
; in F200W over
6.5<z<9
; and in F277W over
9<z<12
. These redshift ranges and choices of bandpasses are shown in Figure
3
. Applying these cuts removes 13,256 galaxies and leaves 19,193.

Cuts are then applied on the morphologies of galaxies. These are done to exclude compact and edge-on galaxies and have often been applied in pre-JWST studies of clumpy galaxies
(
Guo et al. 2012
;
Guo et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. Galaxies with circular half-light radii of at least 0.″1 in the F444W bandpass are selected. This removes 2,848 galaxies with 16,345 remaining. Galaxies with axis ratios of
b/a\geq 0.5
are chosen. This excludes 933 more.

The full sample contains 15,412 galaxies: 7,167 in GOODS-N and 8,245 in GOODS-S. Galaxies in the final sample have a median S/N of about 4.0 in F090W and 13.2 in F277W. For each galaxy in the full sample, its stellar mass and SFR are measured by fitting its combined HST+JWST SED (Section
II.3
). About 8% (1,195/15,412) of the full sample has spectroscopic redshifts. The distribution of the full sample in terms of redshift and stellar mass is shown in Figure
4
. Individual galaxies are represented with small gray dots. The stellar mass completeness limit for each redshift bin is indicated with a light gray shaded region. Galaxies with spectroscopic redshifts are indicated with empty red squares.

IV
Selection of Clumpy Galaxies

IV.1
Identification of Clumps

Clumps are identified for all galaxies in the mass-selected, star-forming sample (Section
III.2
) using the technique developed by
Sattari et al. 2023
. Briefly, it involves taking the Fourier Transform of the rest-frame NUV image of a galaxy, filtering out the low-frequency signal that corresponds to the host galaxy light, and performing source detection on the leftover high-frequency signal, which consists of clumps and noise (see Sec. 3 in
Sattari et al. 2023
for details). To distinguish these, we apply stringent source detection criteria.

Objects are identified as clumps in JWST/NIRCam images if they span at least five contiguous pixels, each of which must be at least two standard deviations brighter than the background-subtracted image, and if the total S/N of the object is at least 3. These criteria are the same as those used by
Shibuya et al. 2016
, who used HST data, and similar to those used by
Claeyssens et al. 2025
, who used JWST data. We use
photutils
to detect clumps. For a given galaxy, clumps must lie at least 0.″1 from the center and within the segmentation map created by the JADES team. This corresponds to a distance of 0.86 kpc at
z=2
and 0.37 kpc at
z=12
. The former criterion is used to avoid confusion with a bulge or active galactic nucleus and the latter ensures that all detected clumps lie within a single galaxy and not within its neighbors projected on the sky.

IV.2
Completeness of Clump Identification

To ensure that a complete sample of clumps is identified, we measure how faint clumps can be in order to detect at least half of them, following pre-JWST studies
(
Guo et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Sattari et al. 2023
)
. This is done by adding fake clumps to images of randomly selected real galaxies, regardless whether they have clumps, and running the clump identification method described above.

Fake clumps are modeled as point sources following previous works
(
Guo et al. 2012
;
Guo et al. 2015
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Soto et al. 2017
;
Sattari et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. We measure empirical point spread functions (PSFs) for all NIRCam bandpasses in GOODS-N and GOODS-S. This is described in Appendix
A
. Positions of the fake clumps are randomly selected to lie within the segmentation map of a given galaxy. Their brightnesses vary randomly between 0.1% and 75% of that of the host galaxy. In order to have sufficient statistics to determine the completeness, 100 galaxies are randomly selected in each of three stellar mass bins (
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9,9<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
, and
10<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<11
) for each redshift bin. If fewer than 100 galaxies exist within a given bin in mass and redshift, all galaxies within that bin are selected. In total, 1,541 galaxies are randomly selected. A fake clump is added to the rest-frame NUV image of each galaxy using the
ImagePSF
routine in
photutils
. The clump identification algorithm is then run on the modified image to determine whether the fake clump is recovered. For each randomly selected galaxy, this procedure is repeated 50 times. The position and brightness of the fake clumps are randomly varied in each iteration. Uncertainties on the completeness are computed in a Bayesian fashion and assume a noninformative Jeffreys prior on the binomial distribution
(
Cameron 2011
)
.

The completeness of the fake clump simulations is shown in Figure

This is further shown as a function of stellar mass (lines of various thicknesses) and redshift (columns). Clumps are more likely to be detected in higher-mass systems and lower redshifts. The minimum fractional luminosities at which clumps are detected at 50% completeness are indicated with the vertical dashed-dotted lines in Figure
5
. For galaxies at
z\sim 2.75
and over
9<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
, 50% of clumps are detected when they are at least 2% of the NUV luminosities of their hosts. At
z\sim 7.75
and over the same range in mass, the same completeness is obtained for a minimum fractional luminosity of 7%. At lower masses, minimum fractional luminosities are higher, and at higher masses, they are similar. At
z\sim 10.5
, no clumps are detected with at least 50% completeness. Because of this, galaxies in the two highest redshift bins at
z\sim 7.75
and
z\sim 10.5
will be combined into one redshift bin in the following Sections. The minimum fractional luminosities at
z\sim 7.75
will be used to find clumps over
6.5<z<12
.

IV.3
Aperture Photometry of Clumps

We measure the fluxes of detected clumps by performing fixed aperture photometry with
photutils
. Each aperture is circular with a radius of 3 pixels. The background is measured in annuli with inner radii of 4 pixels and outer radii of 10 pixels. To perform background subtraction, the mean surface brightness of the light in the annuli is measured, multiplied by the area of the aperture, and subtracted. In each of these steps, for a given galaxy, any nearby clumps are masked using a circular mask of radius equal to 4 pixels. The sizes of the apertures, background annuli, and masks are adopted from
Guo et al. 2018
, who carried out extensive tests on photometry of clumps in HST images.

Because clumps are modeled as point sources, the circular apertures used here enclose the majority of the total light, about 50-70%, depending on the bandpass. We account for this by applying aperture corrections that vary with wavelength. Clump aperture fluxes are multiplied by factors of 1.434, 1.400, 1.422, 1.477, and 1.886 in GOODS-N and 1.471, 1.429, 1.469, 1.502, and 1.806 in GOODS-S for the F090W, F115W, F150W, F200W, and F277W bandpasses, respectively. These are determined using the empirical PSFs that are measured in this work (see Appendix
A
).

V.3
Comparing Clumpy Galaxy Fractions between the HST and JWST Data

Our results on the redshift evolution of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
disagree with those of most studies over
2<z<6.5
. All but two of these are based on HST data, whereas this work is based on JWST data. To determine the cause of the disagreement, we compute
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
using both HST and JWST images for a subset of our sample and vary the minimum fractional luminosity of the clumps. As we demonstrate in this Section, it is largely the difference in sensitivity between the two datasets that is responsible for the disagreement in the
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements shown here. Other factors may have secondary effects on the measurement of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
, such as the differences between photometric redshifts, stellar masses, and SFRs from earlier studies and those used in this work.

Galaxies were selected for this comparison as follows. We match the mass-selected, star-forming sample (Section
III.2
) with photometric catalogs from the CANDELS survey, which were created by
Barro et al. 2019
for GOODS-N and
Guo et al. 2013
for GOODS-S. We use a match radius of 0.″5 and match HST/Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) F160W magnitudes to within 0.5 mag. There are 2,007 matches in GOODS-N and 2,189 in GOODS-S. As studies based on HST data limited their analyses to galaxies with stellar masses
\gtrsim 10^{9}~M_{\odot}
(see Table
2
), we only consider those with masses
\geq 10^{9}~M_{\odot}
. This mass cut leaves a total of 1,245 galaxies (592 in GOODS-N and 653 in GOODS-S) that will be used for our comparison.

Clumps are identified in HST images using the algorithm by
Sattari et al. 2023
and the detection criteria from
Shibuya et al. 2016
. These are the same algorithm and criteria as those used to search for clumps in the JWST images (Section
IV.1
). HST images have a pixel scale of 0.″06 pixel
-1
. We note that the criterion on the minimum number of contiguous pixels that must be spanned by a candidate clump depends on the HST instrument used: it is 5 for ACS images and 7 for WFC3 images. We search for clumps in HST data over
2<z<6.5
to ensure that the coverage in rest-frame wavelength is as similar as possible between HST and JWST (see Section
III
). Clumps are identified in HST bandpasses that span the rest-frame NUV at various redshifts. They are identified in the HST instruments/bandpasses as follows: ACS/F850LP over
2<z<3.5
; WFC3/F125W over
3.5<z<5
; and WFC3/F160W over
5<z<6.5
. Cutting on redshift removes 18 galaxies, with 1,227 remaining. We make sure that
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is measured using the same galaxies for both HST and JWST images. Therefore, any difference in
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is due to discrepancies in depth and resolution between HST and JWST images and not the selection of the sample of galaxies used to compare these data.

VI
Contribution of Clumps to the Rest-Frame NUV Light of Galaxies

We now measure the fraction of the NUV luminosity originating in clumps,
C_{\rm{UV}}
, for our sample of galaxies. For a given galaxy in a given redshift bin, it is calculated by summing the flux of all of its clumps in the NIRCam filter that corresponds to the rest-frame NUV (Section
III
) and dividing by the flux of the host galaxy in the same filter. Only clumpy galaxies are used to compute this. All clumps are selected to be at least as bright as the lowest minimum fractional luminosity listed in Table
1
at a given redshift.

C_{\rm{UV}}
is shown as a function of stellar mass and redshift in Figure
9
. Individual measurements are shown as small gray points. At a given redshift, medians are calculated in three bins in mass and are indicated with large black hexagons and horizontal error bars. In each bin, the 16th and 84th percentiles are also computed and shown as vertical error bars. Medians and percentiles are only calculated for mass bins with at least 10 galaxies.

At all masses and redshifts considered, the median
C_{\rm{UV}}
is
15-20\%
. The 16th percentiles are as low as 4% and 84th percentiles are as high as 35%. They also vary little with mass. We note that several galaxies have very high fractions (
>80\%
) of their NUV light emerging from clumps. These are found predominantly at the lowest masses considered.

Our measurements at
z\sim 2.75
are consistent with results from
Guo et al. 2015
at overlapping redshifts and stellar masses. Their calculations of
C_{\rm{UV}}
over
2<z<3
are shown as small, black diamonds in the leftmost column of Figure
9
. In that work,
C_{\rm{UV}}
was computed as a function of stellar mass for galaxies in two survey fields, GOODS-S and UDS. The running average over both fields is shown in the Figure. Their
C_{\rm{UV}}
values were corrected for incompleteness and averaged over all galaxies, including non-clumpy systems. In contrast, our
C_{\rm{UV}}
values are not corrected for completeness and are computed only for clumpy galaxies. Their
C_{\rm{UV}}
values are systematically higher than ours at fixed mass by
3-12\%
. Despite this, our measurements and theirs are consistent to within the uncertainties.

At higher redshifts, measurements of
C_{\rm{UV}}
from other studies are scarce.
Bowler et al. 2017
mentioned that clumps made up the majority of the UV light in their sample of 25 bright Lyman-break galaxies at
z\simeq 7
.
Chen et al. 2023
examined the JWST/NIRCam images of a sample of 12 UV-bright galaxies over
z\simeq 6-8
. They found that clumps, on average, account for 76% of the light in F200W for the galaxies in their sample. These measurements exceed our median
C_{\rm{UV}}
values of
15-20\%
at similar redshifts. However, these previous works considered clumps at all radii in their samples of galaxies, whereas only off-center clumps are investigated in this work. The higher
C_{\rm{UV}}
reported by these studies is likely the result of light from the centers of galaxies being included in their measurements.

VIII
Comparison with Hydrodynamic Simulations

Here we discuss the implication of our results to galaxy formation scenarios. There are two key results of this work. First, the fraction of clumpy galaxies,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
, increases with decreasing redshift over
2<z<12
. For galaxies with stellar mass
M{\star}\geq 10^{9}M_{\odot}
,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is
\sim 20\%
at
z>6.5
and
\sim 80\%
at
z\sim 2.75
.
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is found to be higher, at fixed redshift, and increase more rapidly with decreasing redshift than what was found in many previous studies (Section
V
and Figure
7
). We interpret this as a consequence of the higher sensitivity of the JWST compared to the HST, which nearly all earlier studies relied on (Section
V.3
). Second, at a fixed redshift, as stellar mass increases, the fraction of clumpy galaxies increases (Section
VII.1
and Figure
10
).

In this Section, we interpret our observations within the context of clump formation over
2<z<12
. Our results are compared with those from simulations of clumpy galaxies. In doing so, we identify what might be the dominant processes for clumpy galaxy formation as a function of mass and redshift. In Section
IX
, our results are studied in the context of other observations.

to
\sim 50\%

z\sim 2
, above which they asymptote. They have uncertainties of
\sim 20\%
, which result from Poisson noise due to a limited number of galaxies examined in that work. Our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements peak at
\sim 70-90\%
at
z\sim 2.75
and decline with increasing redshift, while those from
Mandelker et al. 2017
do not change with redshift within the uncertainties. The discrepancy in
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
as a function of redshift between the theoretical predictions and our observations may arise from differences in the way clumps are identified.
Mandelker et al. 2017
searched for clumps in projected density maps of their galaxies and computed
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
using those with stellar masses
>10^{7}~M_{\odot}
, SFRs of at least 0.1% those of their host galaxies’ disks. In this work, clumps are detected in rest-frame NUV images and selected to be compact objects that are at least as bright as a fraction of their hosts’ NUV luminosity, depending on redshift and stellar mass of the host galaxy. In both studies, clumps are selected to be off-center. However, at the redshifts and stellar masses where the two studies overlap, the measurements are largely consistent within the uncertainties.

Our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements are also consistent with those from
Nakazato et al. 2024
at
z\sim 5.75
and
z>6.5
and
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
. As redshift decreases, our measured
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
increase from 3% at
z>6.5
to 11% at
z\sim 5.75
. Those from
Nakazato et al. 2024
are roughly constant with redshift at
\sim 10\%
. Galaxies studied by that work had stellar masses of
\lesssim 10^{9}~M_{\odot}
at
z\geq 8.5
. Therefore, trends in
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
at the lowest masses considered in this work are in overall agreement with those from
Nakazato et al. 2024
at redshifts where there is overlap. However, clumps were identified differently between these two methods. In
Nakazato et al. 2024
, clumps were found in stellar mass surface density maps of young (
<10
Myr) stars. Detectable clumps had stellar masses of
\gtrsim 10^{7}~M_{\odot}
. This is comparable to the faintest clumps that can be detected in this work at
z\sim 5.75
and over
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
, assuming clumps have similar NUV mass-to-light ratios as their hosts at these redshifts and stellar masses. In
Nakazato et al. 2024
, galaxies were defined to be clumpy if they had at least two clumps. In this work, a galaxy must have at least one off-center clump to be considered clumpy.

It is unclear from the simulations which processes dominate the formation of clumpy galaxies at other combinations of redshift and stellar mass, namely, low redshift (
z<5.75
) and low mass (
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
), and high redshift (
z>5.75
) and high mass (
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 9
). In the case of the former, only two galaxies in the sample of
Mandelker et al. 2017
had such low masses at
z=2
. Predictions for
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
for galaxies at these masses and redshifts are needed. In the case of the latter, predictions from simulations that produce clumps through VDI are needed out to higher redshifts in order to distinguish clumps formed through VDI and those from mergers. Our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements at intermediate stellar masses (
9<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
) are about four times higher than those from
Nakazato et al. 2024
at
z\sim 5.75
. This suggests that major mergers may not significantly contribute to clumpy galaxy formation at these masses and redshifts (see below for support for this from observations). However, confirming this will require
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements from simulations of clumpy galaxies that form via other mechanisms at these redshifts.

IX
Discussion

Comparison of our observations and predictions from simulations suggests that VDI and mergers are the likely dominant mechanisms for clumpy galaxy formation, depending on their redshift and stellar mass, in agreement with other studies
(
Guo et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. VDI appears to be the dominant mechanism for forming clumps at
z\lesssim 5.75
and at intermediate-to-high stellar masses (
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 9
). Mergers likely dominate at
z\gtrsim 5.75
and low stellar masses (
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
). We now assess the plausibility of these mechanisms by considering observations of the morphologies, gas kinematics, and pair fractions of star-forming galaxies with similar redshifts and stellar masses as those studied in this work. We find that observations largely support the scenario described above.

For clumps to form through VDI, their host galaxies must possess marginally stable disk components
(
Bournaud et al. 2007
;
Agertz et al. 2009
;
Dekel et al. 2009a
;
Ceverino et al. 2010
;
Mandelker et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
. Star-forming galaxies over
2<z\lesssim 6
and
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)>8
have typical Sérsic indices of
n\simeq 1

(
van der Wel et al. 2014
;
Shibuya et al. 2015
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Nedkova et al. 2021
;
Ormerod et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Varadaraj et al. 2024
;
Ward et al. 2024
;
Miller et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
, which suggests they have disk-like structures. At higher redshifts, Sérsic indices are generally larger, which implies disk-like structure becomes less common (
Sun et al. 2024
;
Miller et al. 2025
; see also
Ferreira et al. 2023
;
Kartaltepe et al. 2023
).

The stability of gaseous galaxy disks against fragmentation due to gravity is often quantified with the ratio of the circular velocity to velocity dispersion,
V/\sigma
(see
van der Kruit & Freeman 2011
and
Förster Schreiber & Wuyts 2020
for reviews). This is often interpreted to be the ratio of ordered to disordered motions
(
Kassin et al. 2007
;
Kassin et al. 2012
;
Förster Schreiber et al. 2009
;
Epinat et al. 2010
;
Stott et al. 2016
, e.g.,)
. It is usually measured with emission lines from warm ionized gas (e.g., H
\alpha
) or cold molecular gas (e.g., CO). Galaxies are expected to be dominated by VDI when
V/\sigma\leq 2

(
Dekel & Burkert 2014
;
Zolotov et al. 2015
)
. Studies that examined the gas kinematics of star-forming galaxies over
2<z<3.5
found that those with stellar masses
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)>9
have
V/\sigma\sim 0.5-4
, with higher values at lower redshifts and higher masses
(
Gnerucci et al. 2011
;
Simons et al. 2016
;
Turner et al. 2017
;
Wisnioski et al. 2019
)
. VDI may therefore be the prominent process in the evolution of the majority of these systems. At higher redshifts (
4<z<8
), a wide range in
V/\sigma
(
0.1\lesssim V/\sigma\lesssim 20
) has been found in various studies
(
Fraternali et al. 2021
;
Rizzo et al. 2021
;
Parlanti et al. 2023
;
de Graaff et al. 2024
)
. Recently,
Danhaive et al. 2025
modeled the H
\alpha
kinematics of 272 galaxies over
z\approx 3.9-6.5
and
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
with the NIRCam grism. They measured an average
V/\sigma\approx 2
, which suggests the majority of galaxies at these redshifts and stellar masses might be undergoing VDI. Large and deep surveys at high spatial and spectral resolution are needed to determine whether the majority of galaxies at even higher redshifts (
z>7
) could be experiencing VDI.

For clumps to form mainly through mergers, it is expected that the fraction of clumpy galaxies and that of galaxies undergoing mergers are similar. The
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements from the present study are compared with pair fractions measured by
Puskás et al. 2025
in Figure
12
. The latter are shown as thin gray lines. Various bins in stellar mass are indicated with various line styles. In that work, the pair fraction was measured over
3<z<9
and
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
in both of the JADES fields.
Puskás et al. 2025
computed the frequency of galaxies with major companions at projected separations of
5-30
kpc and stellar masses of at least
1/4
those of primary galaxies. Their pair fractions agree with other recent measurements of the pair fraction at high redshift with JWST
(
Dalmasso et al. 2024
;
Duan et al. 2025
)
. Our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements over
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
are consistent with pair fractions from
Puskás et al. 2025
over the same range in mass and the overlapping range in redshift. This suggests that major mergers are the dominant mechanism for clumpy galaxy formation at low stellar masses over the redshift range
3<z<9
.

At higher stellar masses (
9<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<10
),
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
is consistent with the pair fraction only at
z>6.5
. At lower redshifts, the former is three to four times higher than the latter. This implies that major mergers are not the dominant process through which clumps form at these masses and redshifts. This is also the case at the highest masses considered here.
Duncan et al. 2019
calculated pair fractions over
0.5<z<6
and
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)>9.7
using HST data. The pair fraction at
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)>10.3
was found to be
\sim 10-15\%
over
2\lesssim z\lesssim 4
and increases to
\sim 30-40\%
at
z\sim 6
. This is inconsistent with our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
over
10<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<11
, which decreases from
90\%
at
z\sim 2.75
to
73\%
at
z\sim 4.25
.

X
Conclusions

We use JWST/NIRCam imaging from the JADES survey
(
Eisenstein et al. 2023a
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023b
;
Rieke et al. 2023b
;
Bunker et al. 2024
;
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
)
to identify clumpy star-forming galaxies over
2<z<12
. The evolution of the fraction of clumpy galaxies,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
, with redshift and correlations with other physical properties were examined. A sample of 9,121 galaxies with stellar masses
\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)\geq 8
and
\log\left(\rm{sSFR/yr}^{-1}\right)\geq-9.5
was selected by fitting their combined HST and JWST SEDs. Clumps were selected in a way similar to earlier studies based on HST
(
Guo et al. 2015
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Shibuya et al. 2016
;
Sattari et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. The algorithm in
Sattari et al. 2023
was used to find off-center clumps in rest-frame NUV images of galaxies. The completeness of the clump detection procedure was determined by injecting fake clumps into images of real galaxies. Clumps were selected to be
\geq 50\%
complete as a function of redshift and stellar mass.

Our main conclusions are summarized below.

1.

We find that
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
increases with decreasing redshift from
\sim 20\%
at
z>6.5
to
\sim 80\%
at
z\sim 2.75
(Section
V
and Figure
7
). This is similar to the relative increase in the cosmic volume-averaged star-formation density over the same range in redshift. Compared with the bulk of pre-JWST studies,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
from this work is higher at fixed redshift and increases more steeply with decreasing redshift.

2.

The higher
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
found in this work is due to the higher sensitivity of JWST compared with HST (Section
V.3
and Figure
8
). We calculate
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
for a sample of 1,227 galaxies that are detected in both HST and JWST images and compare measurements from both sets of images. When clumps are selected to have NUV luminosities that are
\geq 8\%
those of their host galaxies, both measurements of
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
are consistent with each other. When fainter clumps are selected,
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
from JWST data are systematically higher, which is inconsistent with that from HST.

3.

Off-center clumps make up
15-20\%
of the rest-frame NUV luminosity of clumpy galaxies (Section
VI
and Figure
9
). There is no significant dependence on redshift or stellar mass of the host galaxy. At
z\sim 2.75
, our results are consistent with previous studies.

4.

We examine correlations between
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
and physical properties of galaxies. There is a strong correlation between stellar mass and
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
, such that more massive galaxies have higher
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
(Section
VII.1
and Figure
10
). This is the opposite trend of most previous studies. We find a mild correlation between sSFR and
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
at the lowest masses and little to none at higher masses (Section
VII.2
and Figure
11
). This is consistent with the results of earlier studies.

5.

Our
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measurements are consistent with a physical scenario in which clumps form largely through violent disk instabilities at
z\lesssim 5.75
and over
9<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<11
and through mergers at
z\gtrsim 5.75
and over
8<\log\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<9
(Section
VIII
and Figure
12
). This is done by comparing
f_{\rm{clumpy}}
measured from simulations with the observations. This physical scenario is bolstered when considering the observed morphologies, gas kinematics, and pair fractions of star-forming galaxies at redshifts and stellar masses that overlap with the sample examined in this work (Section
IX
).

Appendix A
Empirical Point Spread Functions

Empirical PSFs were measured using
photutils

EPSFBuilder
routine, which is based on the technique developed by
Anderson & King 2000
. We adopt the parameters used to construct PSFs in
photutils
from
Zhuang & Shen 2024
, who characterized JWST/NIRCam PSFs in Cycle 0 calibration programs. In the present work, PSFs were created using bright (F277W mag
<22
), unsaturated, and isolated point sources that were visually inspected in each NIRCam bandpass. For the wide bandpasses, 16 point sources in GOODS-N and 17 in GOODS-S were used to estimate PSFs. For the medium bandpasses, six to ten point sources in either field were used, depending on the bandpass.

Empirical PSFs and their curves of enclosed energy are shown in Figure
13
. The latter are measured by summing the light from the PSFs in circular apertures at various radii and are plotted as solid lines in the Figure. At each radius, the value of the curve indicates the fraction of the total light enclosed. We compare the PSFs measured here with those from
Ji et al. 2024
, who calculated empirical PSFs in the JADES GOODS-S field. Their enclosed energy curves are shown as dashed lines in the second row. There is good agreement overall. At worst, the enclosed energy curves computed in this work for the F430M, F460M, and F480M bandpasses disagree with those from
Ji et al. 2024
by 5% at most. For all other bandpasses, they disagree at most by about
2-3\%
.
