Renal replacement therapy in patients under extracorporeal membrane oxygenation: a narrative review
Authors: Filip Tomovic, Arnaud Thibault-Baum, Nikola Glogonjac, Caroline P Martens, Slobodan Sajinovic, Vedran Premuzic, Max Bell, Lui Forni, Antoine Schneider, Thomas Rimmelé, Frank Bidar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has expanded for severe respiratory and circulatory failure. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most frequent complications. Up to 85% of ECMO patients develop AKI and approximately half of them require renal replacement therapy (RRT) making a comprehensive understanding of both therapies and their interaction essential for patient management. However, evidence to guide ECMO-specific RRT strategies remains limited.Main bodyECMO-related AKI arises from a complex interplay between patient and circuit factors that promote inflammation, endothelial injury, and tubular damage. Timing of RRT initiation in ECMO patients often relies on criteria used in the general critically ill population. Fluid overload is consistently associated with worse outcomes, and observational data suggest that earlier RRT, primarily to control fluid balance, may improve survival although randomized trials in ECMO patients are lacking. All RRT modalities can theoretically be used, but continuous techniques are preferred. RRT can be delivered via a parallel circuit using a dedicated venous catheter or integrated into the ECMO circuit. Integrated configurations reduce the need for additional vascular access and may prolong filter lifespan but require careful pressure management and team expertise to avoid alarms, hemolysis, and air embolism. Anticoagulation strategies must balance bleeding and thrombosis risk across both circuits; unfractionated heparin remains standard, while regional citrate anticoagulation can safely extend filter lifespan in selected patients although data is lacking in patients under veno-arterial ECMO. RRT during ECMO is associated with higher short-term mortality and an increased burden of chronic kidney disease among survivors.ConclusionsThe management of AKI in ECMO patients remains a major clinical challenge. While RRT is often required in this population, optimal strategies for its initiation, modality selection, and integration with ECMO circuits are still evolving. Current evidence underscores the need for individualized approaches based on patient characteristics. Future research should focus on defining standardized protocols for RRT implementation in ECMO, use of regional citrate anticoagulation, optimizing patient selection, and evaluating long-term renal and survival outcomes.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Hemoadsorption, Renal replacement therapy

Background

Overthe last decades, the use of extracorporeal organ support (ECOS) systems has expanded. Venovenous (VV-) and venoarterial (VA-) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may be required in selected patients with refractory respiratory or cardiac failure. Although survival after ECMO has improved over recent years, complications still contribute importantly to morbidity and mortality. Among them, acute kidney injury (AKI) is frequent, with reported rates between 60 and 85% [1–5]. Yet, the pathophysiology of AKI in the context of ECMO remains poorly understood and incidence rates have remained largely unchanged since the early 2000s [6].

The development of AKI is associated with increased morbidity and in-hospital mortality in ECMO patients [2, 7]. Notably, ECMO patients who develop AKI often require prolonged mechanical ventilation and experience longer intensive care unit (ICU) stays [3]. Many factors including fluid accumulation, refractory acidosis, electrolyte disturbances, uremic complications, and progression of AKI may necessitate the initiation of renal replacement therapy (RRT); this need concerns approximately 50% of ECMO patients [8]. RRT indication and management thus represent a daily concern for clinicians involved in the care of ECMO patients. Despite the growing need to combine multiple ECOS systems in selected patient populations, the optimal strategies for implementing integrated therapies remain poorly defined. In the present review, we explore and summarize the current evidence regarding indications and timing for RRT initiation, RRT modalities, technical considerations concerning ECMO and RRT combination, anticoagulation strategies and outcomes of ECMO patients receiving RRT.

Methods

This narrative review was informed by a structured literature search. We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar for studies published between January 2010 and September 2025; the search was updated in February 2026 prior to manuscript submission. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (when applicable) and free-text terms, using Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”, “NOT”), and focused on three main concepts: ECMO, AKI and RRT. Keywords and variants included: “acute kidney injury”, “renal replacement therapy/kidney replacement therapy”, “extracorporeal membrane oxygenation/extracorporeal life support”, “continuous renal replacement therapy”, “continuous veno-venous hemofiltration”, “continuous veno-venous hemodialysis/hemodiafiltration”, “intermittent hemodialysis”, “prolonged intermittent renal replacement therapy/sustained low-efficiency dialysis”, as well as terms related to fluid overload/ultrafiltration and anticoagulation (“fluid balance/fluid overload”, “ultrafiltration”, “heparin”, “regional citrate anticoagulation”, “bivalirudin”, “argatroban”). We included original studies (randomized and observational), systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines addressing indications/timing for RRT, modality choice, technical integration of RRT with ECMO, anticoagulation strategies, and outcomes in ECMO patients. Small series were retained when reporting technical aspects not covered by higher-level evidence studies. Given the narrative design, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis was planned.

Pathophysiology of AKI in ECMO patients

When considering the pathogenesis of AKI in ECMO patients, contributing factors can be categorized into patient-related determinants, factors related to the underlying cardio-respiratory failure, and ECMO circuit–related mechanisms.

The severity of pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD) is among the strongest predictors of acute kidney injury, likely reflecting diminished renal reserve and a reduced capacity to tolerate physiological stress. Other well-established risk factors include older age and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure. Sepsis also confers substantial risk, as do markers of acute illness severity, including hypotension or shock and the requirement for vasopressor support [9]. In both VV and VA-ECMO patients, hemodynamic instability, systemic venous congestion, and shock prior to ECMO cannulation reduce renal perfusion and create a vulnerable substrate for injury [10–12].

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is the leading indication for VV-ECMO [13]. In this setting, mechanical ventilation may contribute to AKI through lung-kidney cross-talk. Gas-exchange disturbances, including hypoxemia and hypercapnia, can alter renal vascular tone and reduce renal perfusion [14]. Ventilation-induced lung stress may further amplify systemic inflammation, contributing to tubular injury [15]. In addition, positive-pressure ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, which can impair venous return or cardiac output and compromise renal blood flow [16].

In patients with cardiogenic shock who require VA-ECMO, a rapid deterioration in cardiac function can precipitate AKI through a combination of hemodynamic and non-hemodynamic pathways. Reduced forward flow and venous congestion increase renal venous pressure and lower renal perfusion pressure, activating intrarenal mechanisms that can decrease glomerular filtration. In parallel, neurohumoral activation and systemic inflammatory signaling contribute to the bidirectional amplification of cardiac and renal dysfunction [17]. In post-cardiotomy patients requiring VA-ECMO, AKI is multifactorial involving ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurohumoral activation and mechanical factors related to cardiopulmonary bypass [18].

The underlying acute illness and pre-existing comorbidities likely influence individual susceptibility to homeostatic disruption. The contact of blood with non-biological surfaces in extracorporeal circuits and high shear stress activate inflammatory and immunological cascades, further amplifying the risk of developing ECMO-related AKI. Mass spectrometry-based serum proteomic analyses have identified differentially expressed proteins known to be involved in coagulation and inflammation in patients treated with VA-ECMO compared to controls [19]. Activation of the contact and complement system induces leukocyte activation, which leads to endothelial damage and organ injury [20, 21]. Along with coagulation-induced activation of endothelial cells leading to increased expression of P-selectin and E-selectin, this altogether leads to adherence, activation, and transmigration of neutrophils. Neutrophil infiltration is thought to be central for the end-organ damage associated with ECMO [21]. Circuit-related factors such as the induction of shear stress and the occurrence of non-pulsatile blood flow in VA-ECMO contribute to ECMO-related AKI. Shear stress-induced hemolysis leads to the release of cell-free hemoglobin, which promotes oxidative and nitrosative stress, ultimately contributing to tubular injury [22]. Furthermore, high concentrations of cell-free hemoglobin have been clinically associated with a higher incidence of AKI in ECMO patients [23]. Indications and timing for RRT initiation

ECMO patients represent a distinct population, yet robust data regarding the optimal timing for RRT initiation in this group remain lacking. The major trials on RRT initiation were all conducted in the general ICU population with AKI [24–26]. None of these studies demonstrated a survival benefit when using an early initiation strategy. Nevertheless, ECMO patients were either excluded, under-represented, or not explicitly reported. In a recent survey, the primary reported indications for initiating RRT in ECMO patients were persistent anuria (44%) and fluid overload (20%) [27]; according to most respondents (96%), the same criteria as those applied in the general ICU population were used. According to the ELSO (Extracorporeal Life Support Organization) guidelines [28], RRT should be considered when euvolemia cannot be achieved medically or when metabolic disturbances may “decrease the chance of recovery from cardiopulmonary failure”.

Fluid overload (FO) may negatively impact outcomes in ECMO patients as multiple studies have reported a consistent association between FO and increased mortality [5, 29, 30]. More specifically, patients developing early FO—defined as >10% positive fluid balance within 72 hours after ECMO cannulation—experience a two-fold increase in 30-day mortality [5]. Among patients receiving RRT, 90-day mortality appeared lower when a negative fluid balance was achieved at 72 hours post-cannulation. These findings support a potential link between fluid balance management and survival in ECMO patients, yet the extent to which RRT timing influences this relationship is unclear. Several studies have explored this hypothesis. A retrospective study conducted in Japan [31] compared mortality between early (< 48 hours) and late initiation of RRT in 2513 VA-ECMO patients identified from a nationwide health claims database. Early RRT initiation was associated with reduced hospital mortality at both 28 and 90 days (HR 0.59, 95% CI [0.53;0.68] and HR 0.67, 95% CI [0.61;0.75], respectively). However, early initiation of RRT was consistently associated with a higher risk of 90-day RRT dependence. In a single-center randomized controlled trial (RCT), patients with postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock were randomized to early (<12 hours) versus standard RRT initiation timing [32]. Early RRT was associated with significantly improved lactate clearance. These findings support a potential link between fluid balance management and survival in ECMO patients, yet the extent to which RRT timing influences this relationship is unclear. Conversely, a retrospective cohort study found no significant association between the timing of RRT initiation and mortality [33] and a recent meta-analysis could not determine the optimal timing of RRT, as results were not consistent between studies [34]. Although FO is often associated with worse outcomes, this relationship may be confounded by severity: patients with ongoing shock, inflammatory capillary leak, or bleeding often cannot safely undergo early net fluid removal. In these patients, fluid removal is particularly challenging because hemodynamic changes may be both beneficial and harmful: while decongestion and afterload reduction may improve cardiac performance, excessive preload reduction or hypotension may also impair organ perfusion [35]. Overall, the available data do not clearly show that starting RRT earlier improves outcomes in this setting. Starting before recommended indications may simply expose patients to extracorporeal therapy unnecessarily and could even compromise subsequent renal recovery.

Modalities

All RRT modalities can theoretically be used in ECMO patients, including intermittent hemodialysis (IHD), prolonged intermittent renal replacement therapy (PIRRT), and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Current guidelines do not recommend one modality over another [28]. While intermittent modalities such as IHD and PIRRT offer advantages like scheduled circuit downtime, lower costs, and facilitated rehabilitation, they may be associated with hemodynamic instability and disequilibrium syndrome due to higher solute removal rates [36]. In hemodynamically unstable ECMO patients, CRRT remains the preferred option [37]. Two surveys have reported a widespread use of CRRT while IHD is rarely performed, accounting for only 3% and 7% of cases [27, 38]. Although a few small-scale studies have demonstrated the feasibility of PIRRT or peritoneal dialysis in ECMO patients, data remain limited and do not support the use of such techniques as first-line therapies [39, 40]. In contrast, CRRT not only provides superior hemodynamic stability but may also be associated with improved renal recovery compared to intermittent techniques although data specific to ECMO populations are lacking [41, 42].

No large-scale study has assessed the optimal clearance mode during CRRT [43] so that the choice of CRRT modality often relies on local expertise. In ECMO patients, convective modalities appear to be more frequently employed [27, 38]. A retrospective study using Taiwan’s national healthcare database compared continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH) and continuous veno-venous hemodialysis (CVVHD) in patients receiving either VV- or VA-ECMO. CVVH was associated with significantly lower in-hospital mortality (68.4% vs. 76.9%; OR 0.65, 95% CI [0.50; 0.85]), and shorter ICU length of stay (mean difference -4.59 days, 95% CI [-9.15; -0.03]) [44]. The superior clearance of middle-molecular-weight solutes such as cytokines with convective therapies may partly explain these findings however the clinical relevance of this mechanism remains uncertain and the relationship between cytokine removal and patient-centered outcomes has not been consistently demonstrated [45, 46]. Finally, no study has specifically assessed the optimal RRT dose in ECMO patients. In clinical practice, most clinicians prescribe a dose of 25–30 mL/kg/day, in line with the KDIGO (Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes) recommendations for the general ICU population [27].

ECMO and RRT combination

Parallel connection

In a parallel configuration, CRRT runs independently from the ECMO circuit through a dedicated venous catheter [47]. The parallel connection avoids any interference between the ECMO and CRRT circuits. A key advantage is the absence of pressure perturbations between devices: the CRRT can run with standard settings without the pressure alarms being triggered, which can occur when it is connected directly to the ECMO circuit [47]. Furthermore, the connection and disconnection of the CRRT circuit can be performed without specialized training. However, the parallel connection requires the insertion of an additional central venous catheter, which may be challenging in patients already cannulated for ECMO and under anticoagulation therapy [47]. This additional vascular access may also increase the risk of mechanical complications, insertion-related bleeding, and catheter-associated infection or thrombosis [47]. Despite these drawbacks, the use of parallel connections remains common, with 40% of centers reporting its use [27] - possibly due to insufficient team training or the absence of compatible ECMO ports to enable direct connection on the ECMO circuit.

Integrated connection

When using an integrated connection, the CRRT machine is directly connected to the ECMO circuit. Various configurations have been described: both the CRRT access and return are connected to the high-pressure section of the circuit after the pump, both are connected to the low-pressure venous cannula before the pump, or the access is connected to the arterial section and the return to the venous section [36, 39, 47–57]. Integrated connection of the CRRT machine provides full dialysis capabilities (precise ultrafiltration, dialysis or hemofiltration modes, fluid and electrolyte management) with monitoring of pressures and filter integrity. This connection also avoids a second venous access and its associated complications [36]. The major disadvantage is related to the “cross-talk” between the two machines: the CRRT device is exposed to pressures outside its normal range as the ECMO pump’s high pressures (often +150 to +400 mmHg post-pump and up to -100 mmHg pre-pump) can exceed the CRRT machine’s normal operating range, which can trigger frequent high or low pressure alarms depending on the CRRT machine used [36]. With certain configurations, recirculation may occur within the CRRT circuit. However, this phenomenon is considered negligible as the RRT blood flow is typically more than ten times lower than the ECMO flow.

The principles for safe ECMO-CRRT integration include selecting appropriate connection sites with the CRRT return line positioned before the oxygenator, ensuring pressures remain within safe operational limits, preventing air entrainment and hemolysis, and adjusting equipment settings to accommodate non-standard pressure environments [58]. To ensure maximum safety, the connection should always be performed by trained staff; this implies that physicians or perfusionists are readily available to prevent errors in ECMO circuit manipulations. Then, connecting the CRRT after the ECMO pump minimizes the risk of air embolism, which can lead to pump airlock and interruption of ECMO flow. Any connection on the negative section of the ECMO circuit should be performed on one-way valves to prevent air embolism. The CRRT return line should also be positioned before the oxygenator to prevent any arterial or venous embolism to the patient, post-oxygenator return should be avoided. Finally, ECMO circuit modifications should be limited (e.g., added stopcocks or Luer-type sampling/injection ports). In centers that routinely use a dedicated CRRT bridge, it should be built into the initial ECMO tubing at the time of circuit set-up, as each added junction increases circuit complexity, creating structural weak points in the circuit and increasing the risk of blood leak or thrombus formation (Fig. 2).

Technical considerations for integrated circuit connection

Proper connection configuration and pressure management are crucial when combining ECMO with a CRRT device. The goal is to maintain adequate blood flow through the CRRT device without disturbing ECMO function or causing device alarms. In an integrated ECMO-CRRT configuration, ECMO pump flow is the primary determinant of circuit pressures; higher ECMO flows drive proportionally elevated pressures throughout the CRRT circuit [49, 50]. The arterial cannula diameter and any cannula or oxygenator obstruction further modulate pressures, with larger cannula minimizing an increase in pressure [55]. In contrast, changes in CRRT pump flow have a smaller effect, slightly decreasing access pressure while raising return pressure [58]. Monitoring pressures within the ECMO circuit is essential to effectively perform an integrated connection. When the CRRT circuit is connected after the ECMO pump and before the oxygenator, the main limiting factor is often the CRRT return pressure: if this pressure exceeds the maximum threshold of the CRRT machine, therapy cannot proceed. The CRRT blood pump generates an increase in pressure downstream of the CRRT circuit that approximately equals the drop pressure measured by the CRRT machine (∆PRRT). Consequently, the return pressure on the CRRT circuit can be estimated as CRRT return pressure ≈ ∆PRRT + P2, where P2 corresponds to the existing pressure in the ECMO circuit at the connection site. ∆PRRT typically ranges from 20 to 50 mmHg. This estimation allows clinicians to anticipate whether CRRT machine pressure limits will be exceeded prior to connection. If the predicted return pressure exceeds the device’s allowable threshold, an integrated connection at that site may not be feasible [58].

In case of persistent high pressure on the CRRT return line, other adaptations have been proposed. Pressure alarms can be modified on some CRRT devices but this is not feasible for all of them [54]. The use of pressure regulators could help maintain pressure within an adapted range, which is necessary if the CRRT return line is placed before the ECMO pump. A 2024 single-center observational comparative study evaluated adding a pressure control line (regulator) on the CRRT return line in an ECMO-integrated setup [59]. By partially restricting flow at the return line, the pressures within the CRRT circuit changed from strongly negative to mildly positive, thereby protecting the filter. Specifically, when using a regulator, the CRRT circuit filter pressure was maintained around +45 mmHg compared to -69 mmHg without a regulator (p < 0.001) [59]. This nearly eliminated the pressure swings that cause frequent alarms and filter collapse. The impact on filter longevity was significant: filters lasted ~38 hours with a regulator vs. ~27 hours without (mean use time per filter set, p < 0.01) [59]. Similarly, a larger observational study by Na et al. observed that patients with pressure-relief valves on both access and return lines had significantly longer CRRT filter lifespan than those with no pressure control (or one-sided control) [53]. However, the use of clamps on the CRRT circuit could be associated with hemolysis or thrombosis on the RRT line and needs further research [36].

Overall, the configuration choice for integrated connections should be individualized based on the RRT machine and ECMO circuits available (Fig. 3). A thorough understanding of both ECMO and RRT functionality is essential to ensure procedural safety and efficiency.

In a French ICU study (n=34), median filter survival was significantly longer with ECMO-integrated CRRT than with parallel CRRT (48h vs 20 h respectively, p=0.0009), with a 58% lower risk of circuit loss despite lower heparin use [52]. Similarly, a UK VV-ECMO cohort (n=20) reported longer filter life (42 h vs 28 h) and fewer alarms or flow interruptions with integrated connection. By 72 h, 40% of integrated circuits remained functional, compared to only 14% in the parallel group [51]. A Chinese series (n=100) also demonstrated markedly longer filter survival with integrated connection (47h vs 11h, p<0.001) and faster CRRT initiation, without increased oxygenator clotting or embolism [61]. These findings support that high, stable ECMO blood flow protects against flow stasis, alarms, and downtime which are common in parallel catheter-based CRRT. However, evidence is not entirely consistent and could also depend on the anticoagulation used. A retrospective COVID-19 VV-ECMO study (n=105) using dual anticoagulation (systemic heparin plus regional citrate) found no filter survival difference between integrated and parallel connections (~60% reaching 72 h without clotting) [48]. Integrated circuits showed higher access and return pressures, yet transmembrane pressures remained stable and therapy uninterrupted under careful anticoagulation [48]. Large registry data and case series confirm that integration does not increase oxygenator failure or embolic events compared with parallel connection [48]. The randomized E-CRRT trial found no difference in median CRRT circuit lifespan between integrated and separate connections (approximately 72h in both arms) [60]. However, a substantial proportion of circuits were maintained beyond the manufacturer’s 72h, potentially increasing infectious risk [62]. Notably, crossovers from separation to integration were frequent because dialysis catheter placement was unsuccessful or deemed unsafe, likely reflecting that accessible sites for catheter insertion are often more limited in ECMO patients than in the general ICU population. Finally, infectious complications could be associated with the type of connection. The concern for infection risk is twofold: bloodstream infections related to circuit manipulation and local infections at cannulation sites. Interestingly, current data do not demonstrate a clear difference in rates of bacteremia or sepsis between integrated and parallel CRRT strategies [48]. In Schönfelder’s cohort, the incidence of catheter-associated infections was comparable (~10–15%, p = 0.47) regardless of whether a separate dialysis catheter was used [48]. Anticoagulation

Although anticoagulation is required for both the RRT and the ECMO machine, anticoagulation targets may differ depending on the mode of ECMO. The ELSO guidelines [63] do not endorse a specific first-line anticoagulation strategy for ECMO circuits, though unfractionated heparin (UFH) is the most prescribed anticoagulant. An international survey reported exclusive use of UFH in 96% of centers for ECMO patients [64]. Despite its widespread use, UFH has notable limitations, including reduced efficacy in inflammatory states and the risk of heparin induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Other anticoagulants such as direct thrombin inhibitors (DTIs) are also used in this population, notably in case of HIT. Bivalirudin binds to both circulating and clot-bound thrombin. Approximately 80% of the drug is metabolized through proteolysis, while the remaining 20% is eliminated unchanged by the kidney. This metabolism results in a predictable anticoagulant effect according to the administered dose and a short half-life, facilitating precise titration of anticoagulation. However bivalirudin is cleared by RRT and thus requires close monitoring and dose adjustment in this situation [65]. Recent evidence, including systematic reviews and network meta-analyses, suggest that bivalirudin may be associated with lower rates of thrombotic complications compared with UFH without a clear excess of major bleeding. However, the available data remain largely observational so that definitive superiority across all ECMO populations cannot be assumed [66]. Bivalirudin anticoagulation requires constant blood flow, therefore zones of low flow or stasis at connection sites in integrated ECMO–CRRT configurations may represent a specific risk for local thrombosis and should be closely monitored. In case of renal dysfunction or RRT, argatroban is the recommended treatment for HIT [67], the therapy should be started at 2 µg/kg/min up to 10 µg/kg/min, to achieve a 1.5- to 3-fold prolongation of the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT). Since argatroban is metabolized by hepatic cytochrome P450, its half-life may be prolonged in case of hepatic impairment. Several case reports show the feasibility of this anticoagulation in ECMO patients, also with concurrent RRT [68–70]. Although approximately 20% of the drug is eliminated during hemodialysis, the overall impact of RRT on argatroban pharmacokinetics appears clinically negligible. A systematic review confirmed the efficacy and safety of argatroban in both IHD for CKD and in CRRT [71]. A recent randomized controlled pilot trial showed that maintenance anticoagulation with argatroban is feasible and does not increase the risk of thrombosis or hemorrhage in ECMO patients without HIT [72].

In ECMO patients, the RRT circuit is most commonly anticoagulated using UFH, as it simultaneously ensures anticoagulation of the ECMO circuit. Regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) has been associated with longer hemofilter lifespan than UFH in the general ICU population but these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to ECMO patients as comparative data are scarce [73]. RCA offers the advantage of maintaining effective anticoagulation of the RRT circuit, even when systemic anticoagulation must be interrupted due to bleeding. In a single-center retrospective study of 29 patients on VV-ECMO, RCA-CVVH was associated with a mean hemofilter lifespan of 27 hours, with no evidence of citrate accumulation or circuit/oxygenator thrombosis [74]. Similarly, a retrospective study reported a lower rate of RRT circuit clotting (11% vs. 38% in the UFH group, p < 0.001) and an improved hemofilter lifespan in patients receiving RCA + UFH compared to UFH alone, with no reported metabolic complications related to RCA [75]. These findings suggest that RCA is a feasible and safe anticoagulation strategy for the RRT circuit in ECMO patients, particularly when systemic anticoagulation is contraindicated. However, its use in VA-ECMO remains limited, as patients with circulatory failure and refractory shock may be at greater risk of citrate accumulation [76]. In centers experienced with RCA, specific reduced-citrate or stepwise initiation protocols have been proposed to limit citrate load and potentially mitigate the risk of citrate accumulation in selected high-risk patients [77, 78]. Such strategies rely on strict algorithms and close biochemical surveillance and should be applied cautiously, particularly in patients with refractory shock or multi-organ failure in whom the risk of citrate accumulation is increased.

Outcomes

Short-term outcomes

The short-term outcomes of ECMO patients who develop AKI and/or require RRT remain heterogeneous across studies.

Several studies have investigated the impact of AKI occurrence and severity on survival in ECMO patients. A prospective French study involving 60 patients on VV-ECMO found that those who developed KDIGO stage 3 AKI had significantly lower survival at hospital discharge compared to those with stage 0–2 AKI (27.6% vs. 67.7%, p = 0.02) [79]. Similarly, a retrospective Korean study reported increased mortality only among patients with stage 3 AKI, with no significant difference observed for stages 1 or 2 [80]. Notably, this Korean cohort included both VA- and VV-ECMO patients, with a predominance of VA-ECMO (72%) and a high proportion of patients undergoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation within 24 hours before cannulation (43%), reflecting substantial baseline severity. Outcomes also appeared time-dependent: mortality within the first two weeks after ECMO initiation was higher in the VA-ECMO group, whereas overall in-hospital mortality was lower than in the VV-ECMO group. The authors suggested that VA-ECMO patients may follow distinct trajectories, with early deaths clustering among the most severe presentations, while those surviving the initial phase may experience clinically meaningful recovery once cardiac dysfunction is reversed. This illustrates how differences in indication and underlying pathophysiology can modify the observed association between AKI severity and mortality. In contrast, a cohort study of 190 patients, also including both VA- and VV-ECMO with a predominance of VA-ECMO (83%), reported no significant difference in ICU or hospital mortality between patients with and without AKI. A possible explanation for these conflicting results is that, unlike the first two studies, the latter did not stratify patients by AKI severity; stage 3 AKI thus appears to be the main driver of the association between AKI and mortality in ECMO patients [3]. Furthermore, pre-existing AKI is a major determinant of poor short-term outcomes; in a study of 329 patients, those with AKI prior to ECMO initiation faced an in-hospital mortality rate of 73%, which was significantly higher than the 42% mortality seen in patients who developed AKI after initiation [81].

An association between the need for RRT during ECMO and worse outcomes has been repeatedly reported. In two meta-analyses including a majority of VA-ECMO patients, approximately 50% of ECMO patients required initiation of RRT, with overall mortality rates of 63% and 68%. Both studies reported an increased risk of mortality in patients receiving combined ECMO and RRT compared to ECMO alone, with a relative risk (RR) of 1.81 in one and an odds ratio (OR) of 3.73 in the other [6, 8]. However, mortality rates differ markedly between studies, probably due to variations in patient selection, ECMO modality, and underlying clinical profiles. Importantly, VA-ECMO is frequently initiated for clinical phenotypes associated with profound hemodynamic collapse and multi-organ failure, including post-cardiotomy shock after cardiopulmonary bypass and refractory cardiogenic shock, whereas VV-ECMO is primarily used for severe ARDS. Such differences are expected to influence both AKI incidence and the likelihood of requiring RRT. In this context, RRT requirement may primarily represent a marker of illness severity and confounding by indication rather than a direct mediator of outcome. This is illustrated by a Belgian studyof 135 patients in which ICU mortality was comparable among three groups: ECMO without AKI, ECMO with AKI not requiring RRT, and ECMO with AKI requiring RRT (43%, 53%, and 60%, respectively; p = 0.21) [82]. In this study, the absence of an excess mortality signal with CRRT remained consistent when AKI stages and VA/VV-ECMO were analyzed separately and after adjustment for confounders. The authors also suggested that the prognostic weight of AKI may be attenuated in the most severe presentations, such as cardiogenic shock with established multi-organ failure, where outcome is largely driven by the underlying disease. Similarly, a US-based retrospective study found no statistically significant difference in ICU discharge mortality between patients treated with ECMO alone and those receiving both ECMO and RRT (31.4% vs. 39.5%, p = 0.074) [83]. Overall, these findings support interpreting RRT use during ECMO as a marker of illness severity and underlying clinical context rather than a treatment-related determinant of outcome, and highlight the need for modality-specific analyses and adequately adjusted studies when assessing its prognostic impact. Interpretation is also complicated by practice variation across centers, as thresholds and indications for initiating RRT during ECMO are not standardized and may differ substantially between institutions.

Renal recovery and long-term outcomes

Renal recovery in patients who required both RRT and ECMO, remains highly variable across studies. In a large Taiwanese cohort (mean follow-up 2.4 years (SD 2.5)) of VV-ECMO patients, those with dialysis-requiring AKI had significantly higher rates of new-onset CKD (13.7% vs 8.1%) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (5.2% vs 0.5%) compared to those who did not require RRT [84]. In a UK-based cohort of 300 VV-ECMO patients, CKD was observed in 33.3% of those who had received RRT during ICU stay, versus 4.3% among those without AKI (p = 0.004). At 6 months post-discharge, 2.2% remained dialysis-dependent, and 1.4% had progressed to ESRD by 1 year [85]. Long-term data from a French single-center retrospective study (n = 158) showed that major adverse kidney events occurred in 60% of VA-ECMO patients at 30 days, 60% at 90 days, and 85% at 1 year. Among ICU survivors, 6.7% remained dialysis-dependent at 12 months [86]. The most comprehensive long-term data come from a Korean nationwide study of adult ECMO survivors, which reported that 7.6% developed new ESRD within 1-year post-discharge. In this study, the risk of incident ESRD was associated not only with pre-existing renal disease (OR 2.83) and longer duration of in-hospital continuous RRT (OR 1.16), but also with the underlying ECMO indication, with higher risk in the cardiovascular group (vs. respiratory; OR 1.78) and in post-cardiac arrest patients (vs. respiratory; OR 2.52) [87]. These findings underscore that long-term renal outcomes after ECMO are strongly influenced by the pre-cannulation clinical context and the severity of shock-related end-organ injury, variables that are incompletely captured in administrative observational datasets. Accordingly, the observed associations should be interpreted as prognostic signals rather than causal effects of ECMO or RRT exposure. They also emphasize the importance of differentiating VA- and VV-ECMO populations when discussing renal recovery and long-term kidney risk. Perspectives

Recovery or preservation of renal function before VA-ECMO decannulation is an indicator for hospital survival [88]. Consequently, protecting kidney function and limiting AKI progression are crucial to improve outcomes in ECMO patients [89]. This requires timely recognition of AKI, avoidance of nephrotoxic agents, and prevention of FO through judicious fluid administration and promotion of diuresis when appropriate. Beyond these general principles, the timing and practical delivery of RRT on ECMO should be considered within a broader trajectory that integrates cardiac or pulmonary recovery, the strategy for ECMO weaning, and the expected course of renal recovery. In this setting, decongestion is not solely a renal objective but part of a global approach to optimize hemodynamics and treat pulmonary edema, including consideration of left ventricular unloading in VA-ECMO patients [90]. However, the optimal net ultrafiltration target during RRT remains undefined in this population, and the achievable rate is often constrained by hemodynamic tolerance [91]. A major unmet need remains the ability to better predict renal recovery, especially in patients under VA-ECMO, an issue with direct implications for decisions such as transition to durable mechanical circulatory support or heart transplantation. Finally, while biomarker-based approaches are appealing for risk stratification and guide decision-making, available data are largely derived from non-ECMO cohorts; at present, there is insufficient ECMO-specific evidence to support routine clinical implementation or to recommend any biomarker-driven strategy in this population [92].

Conclusions

The management of AKI in ECMO patients remains a major clinical challenge. While RRT is often required in this population, optimal strategies for its initiation, modality selection, and integration with ECMO circuits are still evolving. Current evidence underscores the need for individualized approaches based on patient characteristics and fluid balance status. Moreover, the integration of RRT into ECMO circuits, particularly with appropriate pressure management, appears safe and may improve filter lifespan and circuit efficiency. Future research should focus on defining standardized protocols for RRT implementation in ECMO, use of RCA, optimizing patient selection, and evaluating long-term renal and survival outcomes.
