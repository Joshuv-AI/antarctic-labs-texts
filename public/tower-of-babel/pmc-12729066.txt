Qualitative study on the feeling of accomplishment experienced by nursing students during clinical internships
Authors: Wenhao Tang, Jiaxing Zhou, Wen Yang, Jinlong Zheng, Shangping Zhao, Qiling Tan, Bo Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical internship is a crucial component of nursing education, with the feeling of accomplishment of nursing students playing a significant role in shaping their professional identity and career development. However, qualitative research on nursing students feeling of accomplishment remains limited.DesignA qualitative interpretive phenomenological study.MethodsSeventeen nursing students were recruited using purposive sampling. Semi-structured interviews were conducted, The data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes were identified: (1) Sources of accomplishment, (2) Expression of inner emotions, (3) The significance of accomplishment, (4) The perspectives of accomplishment and emotional conflict.ConclusionsIt is essential for clinical nursing managers and educators to recognize the significance of nursing students’ feeling of accomplishment and the underlying dynamics that influence it. Furthermore, optimizing mentorship approaches during clinical internships can play a pivotal role in advancing nursing practice education and fostering more effective learning environments.Keywords: Nursing students, Accomplishment, Experiences, Qualitative study

Introduction

Clinical internships are a crucial component of the educational process and a critical period for nursing students’ transition into clinical nurses [1]. They play a significant role in bridging the gap between theory and practice, further consolidating theoretical knowledge [2]. Clinical internships serve as a crucial pathway for nursing students to translate theoretical knowledge into patient care, while simultaneously developing clinical competencies, fostering professional identity, and facilitating role transition into nursing practice [3].

In China, clinical internships are a graduation requirement for nursing students, with a mandatory internship period of no less than 10 months [4]. Clinical internships are not merely a process of skill development; they also involve significant emotional and psychological changes [5, 6]. Research has shown that nursing students experiences during clinical internships directly impact their professional identity and their willingness to pursue the profession [7, 8]. Although nursing students’ emotional experiences during clinical internships have been widely examined, most studies have primarily focused on the challenges faced by interns, including issues of adaptation, psychological adjustment, and stress [9–11]. Little attention has been paid to positive emotions such as a feeling of accomplishment, which play a crucial role in shaping students’ professional identity, motivation, and career commitment, understanding how nursing students develop and experience a sense of accomplishment is particularly important for improving clinical teaching quality and student retention in the profession.

Accomplishment is a positive emotional experience, defined as the feeling of pride following the attainment of a goal or success. It involves a feeling of personal competence, goal accomplishment, and social recognition, and can stimulate positive emotions and motivation in individuals [12, 13]. Unlike professional identity, which concerns an individual’s sense of belonging and commitment to the nursing role, accomplishment refers to the emotional experience arising from goal attainment or recognition. It represents an affective outcome rather than a cognitive belief or role perception. From the perspective of positive psychology, a sense of accomplishment is a core component of well-being and intrinsic motivation [14]. This study employs a qualitative research approach, specifically interpretive phenomenology, to explore the accomplishment experience of nursing students during their clinical internships. By gaining a deeper understanding of how nursing students experience and form their feeling of accomplishment, the findings will provide more targeted support strategies for educators and administrators, helping students better cope with challenges during their clinical internships, build self-confidence, and lay a solid foundation for their future careers.

Purpose

The purpose of this study is to explore the experiences of accomplishment perceived by undergraduate nursing students during their clinical internships using a qualitative research approach.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted an interpretive phenomenological design grounded in Heidegger’s existential philosophy. Heidegger’s interpretive phenomenology focuses on understanding human experiences as they are lived, emphasizing the meanings individuals ascribe to their experiences within specific social and cultural contexts. Unlike descriptive phenomenology, which aims to depict phenomena as they appear, interpretive phenomenology seeks to uncover the underlying meanings and contextual influences that shape individual experiences [15]. This approach was chosen because it aligns with the aim of exploring nursing students’ subjective experiences of accomplishment within their clinical learning context.

Setting and participants

The participants in this study were selected through purposive sampling. The inclusion criteria for the interns were: (1) Internship duration of more than 3 months; (2) Voluntary participation in the study; (3) No speech or language disorders. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Students who had already completed their internships; (2) Students with severe psychological health issues. Data saturation was defined as the point when no new codes, meanings, or relationships emerged from the interviews. The thematic range was generally determined by the 16th interviewee. To prevent false saturation of themes, an additional participant was included for data validation. Therefore, a total of 17 participants were included in the study. Data collection

Interview guide

The interviews in this study were conducted between November 2024 and March 2025. Initially, an interview outline was developed, adhering to the principles of simplicity, clarity, and scientific rigor. The outline was designed based on the research objectives and a review of relevant literature. Additionally, nursing experts with teaching experience, clinical practice supervisors, and experts with qualitative research experience were invited to review and revise the initial draft. Following the development of the preliminary interview outline, two participants were selected for a pilot interview. Based on the feedback from the pilot interview, the outline was revised, and the final formal interview outline was established. See Fig. 1.

Interview guide

Interview procedure

Data collection was conducted using semi-structured interviews. The researcher arrived at the interview setting in advance to ensure the environment was clean, quiet, and free from distractions. Prior to the interview, the purpose and significance of the study were fully explained to the participants, and informed consent was obtained. During the interview, the interview guide was adjusted as necessary based on the participants’ emotions, but no topics were omitted. If new perspectives emerged during the interview, probing and follow-up questions were used. After the interview, the content was reviewed with the participant to ensure they had accurately understood the interview questions. Throughout the entire process, no comments were made on the participants’ viewpoints, and an open attitude was maintained to respect each participant’s perspective. With the exception of three nursing students who chose to conduct the interviews online via WeChat due to time constraints, all other interviews were conducted in person. Data collection was conducted between November 2024 and March 2025. Thematic saturation was reached after the 16th interview, when no new codes or concepts emerged from the data. To confirm saturation, one additional participant was interviewed, bringing the total to 17 nursing students.

Data analysis

The researchers transcribed the audio recordings into text within 24 h after the interviews, repeatedly listening to the recordings to correct any typographical errors in the transcripts. After ensuring the accuracy of the transcripts, the data were imported into Word. The data were then analyzed using thematic analysis [16]. Initially, the researchers repeatedly read through the interview transcripts in sequence, combining reflection journals and field notes to immerse themselves as deeply as possible in the interview materials, thereby gaining a holistic understanding of the data. Two researchers independently coded significant ideas and/or concepts from the raw data. Subsequently, similar codes were grouped into themes and sub-themes, and this process was repeated iteratively until the themes were refined. After the preliminary themes and sub-themes were developed, the researchers reread the transcripts, conducting repeated comparisons to identify the potential logical relationships between each theme/sub-theme and the research objectives. Ultimately, the developed themes/sub-themes were defined. Throughout this process, all researchers compared themes and resolved discrepancies through discussions, reaching a consensus.

Quality assurance

To ensure rigor, Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for trustworthiness were applied [17]. Credibility: Prolonged engagement with participants and member checking were used to confirm the accuracy of interpretations. Transferability: Detailed descriptions of participants and settings were provided to allow readers to assess the applicability of findings. Dependability: The reliability of the study was ensured through regular team discussions to reach consensus on themes and by maintaining a detailed audit trail throughout the analysis process. Confirmability: Reflexive journals were kept to document decisions and reduce researcher bias. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) has also been included as an attachment.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Bioethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, with the document number: 2024(2661). The entire research process strictly adhered to ethical protection principles. No specific information that could identify individuals will be used in the research report. In this study, participants’ names, addresses, or any identifying information will be replaced or omitted. Additionally, all collected interview data will be stored securely with encryption. If the research involves content that may cause psychological distress, the research team will provide necessary psychological support resources or recommendations to ensure that participants receive appropriate care throughout the study.

Results

A total of 17 nursing students were interviewed in this study, with an average age of 21.4 years. The interview data were transcribed into 53,369 words, with an average interview duration of 26.3 min. A total of 135 codes were formed, leading to the identification of 4 themes and 10 sub-themes. The four themes are: Sources of accomplishment, Expression of inner emotions, The significance of accomplishment, and The perspectives of accomplishment and emotional conflict.

Theme 1 Sources of accomplishment

“First-time” breakthrough in clinical procedures

The success of clinical operations is generally regarded by nursing students as the most direct and tangible source of accomplishment. This feeling of success often begins with a “first-time” breakthrough. Successfully completing a clinical practice not only serves as a test of their professional skills but also reflects the intern’s personal growth, especially when they accomplish clinical tasks they perceive as challenging or difficult.

N1: “I remember the first time I successfully inserted an intravenous catheter into an elderly man. When I saw the blood return after the catheter entered the vein, I really felt proud of myself.”

N3: “In the emergency department, it was really busy, but once, I successfully obtained an arterial blood gas from a patient with very poor vascular conditions. Although it was a simple procedure, I still felt a strong feeling of accomplishment at that moment.”

Receiving praise and recognition

Nursing students often derive a feeling of accomplishment in clinical practice through positive evaluations from clinical instructors, as well as recognition and appreciation from patients. These external affirmations not only enhance the interns’ confidence but also motivate them to continue their efforts and pursue further self-improvement. This is exemplified by the following participant narratives:

N6

“When I changed a patient’s wound dressing, they told me my technique was gentle and painless, asking me to continue changing their dressings next time. In that moment, I felt my work was affirmed, and I experienced a feeling of accomplishment.”

N13

“After my clinical instructor assessed my intravenous infusion skills, they said, ‘You’re ready to practice independently.’ Those four simple words made me feel incredibly happy.”

Professional capabilities and values were reflected

Nursing students describe how, when confronted with patient care needs or emergencies, they are able to make timely and effective judgments and implement appropriate nursing interventions based on the knowledge and skills they have acquired. This ability, especially when the problem is successfully resolved, provides them with a strong feeling of accomplishment.

N6

“When I was performing an ECG on a patient who was about to be discharged after coronary angiography, I noticed that the waveform was abnormal. I informed my clinical instructor, and later it turned out to be a premature ventricular contraction. At that moment, I felt really proud of myself, thinking that I applied what I had learned in clinical practice. It felt like an accomplishment.”

N2

“When I was administering an IV to a patient, I felt something was wrong with the site where the previous catheter had been placed, as it seemed like phlebitis. Since I had seen phlebitis before, I quickly informed my instructor for further action.”

Theme 2 Expression of inner emotions

Excitement and joy

Excitement and joy are positive emotional responses commonly experienced by nursing students during their internship, particularly when they achieve something rewarding, such as successfully completing their first clinical task or performing a challenging nursing procedure. These emotions evoke a feeling of excitement and uncontrollable joy, which not only enhance their confidence in their abilities but also further motivate them to tackle future challenges.

N5: “When I successfully inserted an indwelling needle for the first time, my hands were shaking, and I was so excited (laughed out loud). It felt like I had taken an important step.”

N8: “My classmates told me that maintaining a PICC line involves many steps and is difficult, but when I completed it under my teacher’s guidance, I felt so happy. It felt like that day was full of meaning, and I was happy all day long.”

Self-recognition and affirmation

Self-recognition and affirmation are internal emotional responses that nursing students experience as a result of their actual performance in achieving a feeling of accomplishment. As they continuously learn, progress, and succeed in clinical practice, interns gradually become aware of their growth and gain confidence in their abilities within the nursing field. This self-recognition and affirmation are not only reflected in the completion of specific tasks but also in the overall enhancement of their professional nursing skills.

N9: “When I first joined the department, I was not very familiar with some of the procedures, but after completing each one, I felt more and more capable and started to recognize my own abilities.”

N13: “In the previous department, it was so busy that I felt like I didn’t learn much, which made me feel insecure when I came to this department and was afraid to perform procedures. But after my mentor demonstrated the procedure for me and I successfully performed it on a patient, I realized I could do it, and I could do it well.”

Theme 3 The significance accomplishment

Increasing professional confidence and identity

The feeling of accomplishment not only allows interns to recognize improvements in their abilities and gain self-recognition but also leads to a deeper understanding of the meaning and value of nursing work. When interns receive recognition from patients and solve clinical problems through their efforts, they perceive their work as meaningful, which boosts their confidence in the nursing profession and strengthens their identification with the role of a nurse.

N3: “When I saw my name on a letter of appreciation from a patient, I felt that my efforts were valuable. This is precious and made me feel that the nursing profession and being a nurse are meaningful.”

N16: “At that moment, I felt that I could truly handle the nursing job (laughing). This feeling of accomplishment filled me with confidence about my future career.”

Promoting mental health

Nursing students mentioned in interviews that, during clinical practice, they often face high levels of work pressure and complex emotional challenges, such as dealing with patient suffering or death, as well as the stress of unfamiliar environments. As a positive emotional experience, the feeling of accomplishment can effectively alleviate these pressures, significantly improve their emotional state, and promote mental well-being.

N9: “This feeling of accomplishment makes me feel that all my efforts are worth it, and the pressure has been greatly reduced. Especially when I first came to the department, everything was unfamiliar—the environment and people—and I felt a lot of pressure, worried I wouldn’t be able to perform well as an intern. But now, it’s much better.”

N1: “Sometimes, I do feel exhausted, and there are times when I almost break down emotionally. But the feeling of accomplishment from those experiences, although brief, really helped me calm my mind. During that time, I felt very fulfilled inside.”

Motivating learning and professional growth

Participants in the interviews noted that the feeling of accomplishment can significantly enhance their interest in and motivation to learn. When nursing students successfully complete a challenging nursing task, they feel that their abilities have been validated, which encourages them to actively acquire new knowledge and skills. Simultaneously, the motivation for learning and professional growth further promotes the intern’s skill development, with each new success leading to further accomplishment, thereby creating a positive cycle.

N6: “When I do something well, it gives me a great feeling of accomplishment. Beyond feeling happy and excited, I always reflect on areas where I can improve. This feeling of accomplishment drives me to set new goals and work hard to achieve them.”

N13:“After each day of my internship, I write down what I’ve learned in a notebook, especially the things I did well. When I get home, I look up additional information and compare it with nursing standards. I feel like I’m making progress little by little every day.”

Theme 4 The perspectives of accomplishment and emotional conflict

No expected feedback or responses

Nursing students described that during their clinical training, when they accomplished something that gave them a feeling of accomplishment, many interns expected strong emotional feedback and recognition. However, in some cases, interns did not receive the anticipated feedback, which led to feelings of disappointment. This situation significantly diminishes the emotional expression of accomplishment among nursing students.

N3: “The teachers did do well, and they would always summarize our strengths and weaknesses, but it just felt very ordinary, not the kind of emotional feedback I had expected.”

N16: “Every time we finish an operation or assessment summary, the teacher always says, ‘Well, good job’ or ‘Overall, very well done, the process is very familiar.’ I feel like she says this to every intern.”

N1: “At the time, I felt really great about it, but the instructor just brushed it off with a single sentence. Maybe they’re used to it, but I felt a bit disappointed.”

Silence in emotional expression

Nursing students described that although they felt a strong feeling of accomplishment and were internally excited and thrilled, external constraints and the lack of anticipated emotional feedback often led them to suppress these emotions. Externally, this was often expressed through silence or a low-key demeanor.

N7: “I feel like the emotions I express are just the tip of the iceberg because you can’t fully display these emotions. Only I know what they truly are.”

N3: “Actually, I wanted to make a victory gesture to motivate myself at the time, but my instructor was standing nearby, and I thought that doing such actions might not be appropriate.”

N11: “I was extremely excited and happy inside, but when I saw my instructor remain calm, I just stayed silent and didn’t show it.”

Discussion

In this study, we identified three primary sources of accomplishment for nursing students: “first-time” breakthrough, receiving encouragement and recognition, and Professional capabilities and values were reflected. Each of these sources plays a crucial role in enhancing interns’ feeling of competence, value, and confidence. During their internships, nursing students perform a variety of clinical tasks under the guidance of preceptors, such as medication administration, physical assessments, or assisting with patient care [18, 19]. Clinical nursing procedures are a core component of the internship and one of the key evaluation criteria. Nursing students place significant emphasis on the role of clinical operations in their training. For those without prior clinical experience, certain nursing procedures can be challenging and difficult [20, 21]. However, when they succeed in performing clinical tasks—especially on their first successful attempt—they often experience a strong feeling of accomplishment. This breakthrough helps them feel as though they have transitioned from outsiders to novices in the clinical environment.

Moreover, encouragement and recognition from patients and preceptors serve as important sources of accomplishment for nursing students. Our study found that such positive affirmations, whether verbal or non-verbal, significantly contribute to the interns’ feeling of accomplishment. Related research also indicates that external motivation and affirmation can enhance university students’ feeling of accomplishment, helping them maintain a positive attitude when facing challenges and boosting their self-worth. This is equally applicable to nursing students. Another key source of accomplishment for nursing students is solving clinical problems. Interns frequently encounter unexpected challenges in patient care, such as handling emergencies, addressing patients’ concerns, or identifying potential nursing risks. Successfully resolving these issues—either independently or with the guidance of preceptors—provides a profound feeling of accomplishment, particularly when clinical reasoning and critical thinking skills are demonstrated. Therefore, during the clinical practice of nursing students, nursing managers and clinical preceptors, while adhering to the principle of standardized teaching, should enhance opportunities and support for clinical skill development, foster an environment of encouragement and recognition, and provide guidance and opportunities for solving clinical problems. Innovative teaching models should be explored to help nursing students gain a deeper feeling of accomplishment through problem-solving [22].

Our study also found that the feeling of accomplishment experienced by nursing students during their internships has profound and meaningful effects. On the one hand, it enhances their feeling of identity and confidence in the nursing profession. Research indicates that positive internship experiences can strengthen interns’ feeling of professional identity and belonging, thereby reducing the risk of burnout and increasing their willingness to pursue long-term careers in nursing [23, 24]. At the same time, nursing students face many pressures and challenges during their clinical internships, which can lead to negative emotions such as anxiety [25, 26]. However, the experience of accomplishment can effectively alleviate their stress and negative emotions, enabling them to adopt a more positive mindset when confronting the complexities of the clinical environment. Furthermore, the feeling of accomplishment plays a significant motivational role in boosting their learning confidence. Through events that provide a feeling of accomplishment, they feel more capable of dealing with the complexities of actual work. This experience and emotion drive them to be more proactive in learning and applying new nursing theories and methods, thereby creating a positive learning cycle.

Our research also uncovered an interesting and previously unstudied phenomenon: a conflict in perspectives and emotional experiences between nursing students and their clinical preceptors during moments of perceived accomplishment. The findings reveal that nursing students often do not receive the emotional feedback they expect, which suppresses their expression of inner feelings. When nursing students accomplish tasks they perceive as significant accomplishments—such as performing a difficult procedure or solving a complex clinical problem—the clinical preceptors may view these actions as routine or procedural. Although preceptors acknowledge the interns’ excellent performance and capabilities, the interns often perceive this feedback as formulaic and lacking in genuine emotional recognition. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that clinical preceptors, having spent years in clinical practice, have become desensitized to routine nursing operations and the resolution of clinical issues. Additionally, preceptors often engage in considerable emotional labor during teaching, which can lead to emotional fatigue [27, 28].

Nursing administrators should address this issue by establishing a tiered feedback mechanism in clinical teaching that not only affirms interns’ feeling of accomplishment but also guides them toward higher professional standards. At the same time, it is essential to strengthen emotional support training for clinical preceptors, including organizing specialized communication and emotional labor training sessions. These efforts will help preceptors better understand the emotional needs of nursing students, thereby enhancing the interns’ feeling of accomplishment and enriching their emotional experience. This study also holds significant theoretical value in the field of nursing education and provides valuable insights for clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single teaching hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Second, as with all qualitative studies, the results reflect the participants’ subjective experiences and interpretations rather than objective measurements. Third, the researcher’s prior background as a nursing intern might have influenced data interpretation despite efforts at reflexivity. Finally, three interviews were conducted online, which may have reduced the richness of non-verbal data.

Conclusion

Our study employs a qualitative research approach to deeply explore the emotional experience of accomplishment among nursing students during their internship. Our findings reveal that the feeling of accomplishment experienced by nursing students is a valuable yet often subtle emotion, and it originates from the recognition by patients and mentors, as well as the success they experience in clinical practice. This emotion holds significant meaning for them, fostering self-confidence and providing positive inner strength. However, there is a phenomenon of conflicting perspectives on accomplishment, where the feeling of accomplishment cannot be fully expressed or released in a timely manner.
