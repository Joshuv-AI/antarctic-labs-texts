The evaluation of hemogram-related systemic inflammatory markers in pterygium recurrence
Authors: Sibel Alişan Alimoğlu, Burçin Çakır, Kübra Özata Gündoğdu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate hemogram-related systemic inflammatory markers and cholesterol levels in patients with primary and recurrent pterygium.MethodsThe records of patients underwent pterygium surgery were investigated. Patients with primary and recurrent pterygium were enrolled as Group 1 and Group 2, respectively. Age and sex-matched healthy individuals formed Group 3. Hemogram results taken one week before surgery were obtained from the medical files. Neutrophil, lymphocyte, eosinophil, monocyte, platelet, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR), monocyte-to-eosinophil ratio (MER), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR), and systemic inflammation index (SII) and HDL, LDL, TG, and total cholesterol were calculated and compared between groups.ResultsThere were 54, 30 patients and 34 healthy individuals, in Group 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The mean vertical and horizontal length of pterygium were not statistically different between patient groups. The mean neutrophil, eosinophyl, monocyte, platelet, were not statistically different between groups (p: 0.43, p: 0.79, p: 0.85, p:0.29, respectively). The mean lymphocyte value was statistically different between Group 2 and 3 (p:0,001). The mean HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, TG were not statistically different between groups. Mean NLR, PLR, ELR, MER, MLR, MHR, and SII were not statistically different between groups (p: 0.63, p: 0.17, p:0.13, p:0.46, p: 0.07, p:0.77, p:0.97, respectively).DiscussionHemogram-related inflammatory markers and serum cholesterol levels were not different between patients with primary, recurrent pterygium, and healthy subjects. Only mean lymphocyte was lower in patients with recurrent pterygium compared with control group. The role of systemic inflammation should be investigated in further studies.Keywords: Recurrent pterygium, Systemic inflammation, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Monocyte-to-HDL ratio (MHR) , Systemic inflammation index (SII)

Introduction

Pterygium is a common ocular surface lesion originating in the limbal conjunctiva within the palpebral fissure with progressive involvement of the cornea [1]. While UV radiation, proximity to the equator, dry climates, outdoor lifestyle are the main risk factors, the exact etiopathogenesis of pterygium is not well-known [2]. Recent studies revealed that inflammatory mechanisms, immunological mechanisms, oxidative stress, genetic factors, and antiapoptotic mechanisms play role in the pterygium development [3–6]. As well as local inflammation, systemic inflammation may also be involved in the development process of pterygium [7]. Recent studies focused on hemogram-related systemic inflammatory markers to find out any effect of these markers on development of pterygium. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), eosinophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR), monocyte-to-eosinophil ratio (MER), mean platelet volume-to-platelet count ratio (MPV/PC), platelet distribution width (PDW), and red cell distribution width (RDW) have been determined as indicators of systemic inflammation [8, 9]. Besides, monocyte to high density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio was investigated as an inflammatory risk factor [7]. In a recent study, HDL and low density lipoprotein (LDL) were found to be risk factors for pterygium in people < 50 years [10].

The major problem in the management of pterygium is the recurrence. Excision with conjunctival autograft is considered the current gold standard because of its low rate of recurrence. As well as the surgical procedure of choice is an important factor in the recurrence of pterygium, race and geographical factors are found to affect recurrence [11]. Recurrent pterygium tends to have more agressive fibrovascular structure. In a recent retrospective, longitudinal study, the preoperative NLR and pterygium grade were found to be significantly associated with recurrence [12].

The aim of the current, retrospective study is to evaluate preoperative hemogram-related systemic inflammatory markers including neutrophil, lymphocyte, eosinophil, monocyte, platelet, NLR, PLR, ELR, MER, MLR, monocyte-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (MHR), and systemic inflammation index (SII, neutrophil* platelet/lymphocyte) and high density lipoprotein (HDL), low density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol in patients with primary and recurrent pterygium and to compare these parameters between patient groups and healthy individuals.

Methods

This retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology. Prior approval from the Institutional Review Board was taken (Sakarya University Medicine Faculty Ethical Commitee, IRB number: E-71522473-050.01.04-300102-313)) and written informed consent was obtained from each subject. The study was performed in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki.

The records of patients who underwent pterygium surgery with temporal limbal conjunctival autograft performed by two ophthalmologists (S.A.A, B.Ç.) between 2014 and 2024 were investigated.

The records of preoperative detailed ophthalmologic examination including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement by Snellen chart, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement by Goldmann Applanation tonometry, slit-lamp biomicroscopy and fundus observation by + 90 diopter lens was investigated. According to this evaluation, patients with any ocular diseases and any additional previous ocular surgeries were not included in this study. Besides, patients with systemic diseases which may affect systemic inflammatory markers (e.g. metabolic syndromes, diabetes mellitus, hypertension obesity, and infections), systemic medical therapies, recent infections were also not included in this study. Smoking habits of patients were also investigated and documented.

In temporal limbal conjunctival autograft technique; pterygium excision was done and conjunctival autograft was obtained from the limbal superotemporal conjunctiva in the same eye. 10.0 polypropylene suture was used for fixing the graft over the exposed scleral bed. One week after surgery, suture material was taken off. Topical steroid, antibiotic eye drops and artificial tears composed of 0,15% hyaluronic acid were prescribed to all patients for two weeks.

Patients with primary and recurrent pterygium were enrolled as Group 1 and Group 2, respectively. Age and sex-matched healthy individuals who have no signs of ocular diseases in biomicroscopic examination and have venous blood sample test results taken within 15 days were included as control group; Group 3. Venous blood sample tests were taken after 8–10 h fasting between 09:00–11:00 a.m., in all groups. Hemogram results of patient groups were obtained from the medical files. Neutrophil, lymphocyte, eosinophil, monocyte, platelet, NLR, PLR, ELR, MER, MLR, MHR, and SII and HDL, LDL, TG, and total cholesterol were calculated and compared between groups. The laboratory conditions and tools were not changed during the timeline of the study.

Statistical analysis were performed by using SPSS for Windows version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons among the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by pairwise comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U test. Effect sizes were calculated using eta squared (η²) for ANOVA and epsilon squared (ε²) for the Kruskal–Wallis test. For pairwise comparisons, effect sizes were expressed as rank-biserial correlation (r). Confidence intervals for median differences were estimated using a bootstrap resampling method. A generalized linear model was used to evaluate group differences while adjusting for age as a covariate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

There were 54, 30 patients and 34 healthy individuals in Group 1 (primary), 2 (recurrent), and Group 3, respectively. A power analysis was performed for a medium effect size (f = 0.25) with an alpha error of 0.05 across three groups, indicating a statistical power of 65%. The mean vertical and horizontal length of pterygium, and follow-up time of patient groups were not statistically different. There were no statistically significant differences among the groups in terms of sex, age, and smoking habits (Table 1).

Discussion

It has been already known that recurrent pterygium tends to contain more and aggressive fibrovascular tissue. The inflammation plays an important role on both primary and recurrent pterygium [13, 14]. An et al. found that the level of MMP-1 in serum-free supernatant of cultured primary and recurrent human pterygium fibroblast (HPF) was higher compared to normal ones. They showed that the levels of MMP-1 and MMP-3 in supernatant secreted by primary HPFs exhibit a different pattern from recurrent HPFs. This means that different pathogenesis may exist between primary and recurrent pterygia [15]. In this current study, we aimed to find out possible more systemic inflammation via hemogram-related inflammatory markers in patients with recurrent pterygium. Hemogram-related systemic inflammatory factors have been investigated for various ophthalmic diseases in recent studies. Because they are inexpensive and easily available parameters that are derived from complete blood count analyses [7, 8, 16].

In this current study; mean neutrophil, platelet, eosinophyl, and monocyte were not statistically higher in patients with recurrent pterygium. Only mean lymphocyte level was statistically different between recurrent pterygium and control groups. But the effect size was moderate. Candan et al. found higher lymphocyte levels in patients with primary pterygium [17]. Elbeyli et al. investigated neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet, and white blood cell counts for pterygium occurence but found no statistical difference [8]. Yılmaz et al. found that hematocrit, WBC, total cholesterol, and TG levels were higher in the primary pterygium group [18]. In a recent study, 196 patients with primary and recurrent pterygium were enrolled and after surgery, patients with relapse were compared with patients with cure. Preoperative NLR ratio and pterygium grade were found to be predictors of relapse in patients with pterygium. The NLR ratio was higher due to high neutrophil levels, in these patients [12]. There was no control group in this study and type of pterygium (primary or recurrent) was not classified but the results are valuable because of the longitudinal style. We might suggest further studies with larger sample sizes and more homogenous groups to deepen our knowledge about this item.

Serum cholesterol levels including HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, and TG were not statistically higher in patients with recurrent pterygium. Zang et al. found that serum HDL and LDL cholesterol levels were risk factors of primary pterygium [1]. Kılıç et al. found lower HDL and higher TG levels in patients with pterygium when compared with healthy individuals [19]. In a recent study, Yılmaz et al. found higher levels of total cholesterol, and TG levels in patients with primary pterygium [18]. The cholesterol levels in patients with recurrent pterygium were not evaluated and compared with healthy individuals, before. Differences in patient characteristics may lead to variations in research results.

The NLR reflects a dynamic relationship between the innate (neutrophils) and adaptive (lymphocytes) cellular immune response. NLR is a valuable marker of systemic inflammation, which is significantly increased in many eye disorders such as glaucoma, dry eye disease, keratoconus, suggesting that inflammation plays a key role in the pathophysiology of these diseases [20–22]. In recent studies, NLR of patients with pterygium was also assessed. There are contradictory findings about the NLR level in pterygium patients compared to healthy individuals [19, 23, 24]. The PLR was also investigated in patients with primary pterygium. While Kılıç et al. found higher PLR in patients with primary pterygium, Gökmen et al. did not find any statistical difference in terms of PLR in patients with primary pterygium and healthy individuals [23, 25]. The ELR, MLR, and MER were evaluated in patients with primary pterygium and no statistical difference was found when compared with healthy individuals [7]. An increased MHR has been observed in ocular diseases but an increase in MHR was not observed in patients with primary pterygium [7, 26, 27]. In a recent study, SII was found to be higher in patients with keratoconus but it was not evaluated in patients with either primary or recurrent pterygium [16]. In our study, hemogram-related inflammatory markers including NLR, PLR, ELR, MER, MLR, MHR, and SII were not statistically different between groups. The contradictory results obtained from clinical studies about the relationship between systemic inflammation and pterygium occurence suggest that local inflammation is more important for pterygium development. The role of systemic inflammation investigated through hemogram-related inflammatory markers on recurrent pterygium should be evaluated with further large sample sized studies.

The major limitation of the study was small sample sizes of groups. In addition, the retrospective manner might affect the results. Although the study spanned a long period of time, all surgeries were performed by the same surgeons, and no changes were made to the laboratory conditions. In this way, efforts were made to ensure standardization.

In conclusion; hemogram-related systemic inflammatory markers including mean, neutrophil, platelet, eosinophyl, monocyte, NLR, PLR, ELR, MER, MLR, MHR, and SII and cholesterol levels including HDL, LDL, total cholesterol, and TG were not statistically different between patients with primary and recurrent pterygium, and healthy individuals. Only the mean lymphocyte level was statistically different between patients with recurrent pterygium and healthy subjects. This difference was statistically significant but the effect size was moderate. This study is the first which investigates and compares systemic inflammation markers including both hemogram and cholesterol related markers, in patients with recurrent pterygium. Further studies with larger sample sizes might enlighten the role of systemic inflammation on pterygium reccurence.
