Monitoring Spatially Distributed Cyber-Physical Systems with Alternating Finite Automata
Anand Balakrishnan, Sheryl Paul, Simone Silvetti, Laura Nenzi, Jyotirmoy V. Deshmukh

Abstract
. Modern cyber-physical systems (CPS) can consist of various networked components and agents interacting and communicating with each other. In the context of spatially distributed CPS, these connections can be dynamically dependent on the spatial configuration of the various components and agents. In these settings, robust monitoring of the distributed components is vital to ensuring complex behaviors are achieved, and safety properties are maintained. To this end, we look at defining the automaton semantics for the Spatio-Temporal Reach and Escape Logic (STREL), a formal logic designed to express and monitor spatio-temporal requirements over mobile, spatially distributed CPS. Specifically, STREL reasons about spatio-temporal behavior over dynamic weighted graphs. While STREL is endowed with well defined qualitative and quantitative semantics, in this paper, we propose a novel construction of (weighted) alternating finite automata from STREL specifications that efficiently encodes these semantics. Moreover, we demonstrate how this automaton semantics can be used to perform both, offline and online monitoring for STREL specifications using a simulated drone swarm environment.

1.
Introduction

Multi-agent and distributed cyber-physical systems (CPS) are becoming
increasingly pervasive in modern society, from IoT networks
(
Xu et al., 2018
;
Latif et al., 2022
;
Pivoto et al., 2021
)
, vehicular
networks
(
Wang et al., 2019
)
, power grids

(
Zhu and Hill, 2020
;
Pagani and Aiello, 2013
)
smart cities
(
Narayanan et al., 2019
;
Habibzadeh et al., 2019
;
Madakam et al., 2018
)
, drone swarms, to
multi-robot configurations used in space exploration
(
NASA, 2024
)
.
Individual
nodes (or agents) in such systems communicate and interact with each other, and
the temporal behavior of one node can affect the behavior of other nodes.
A
common approach to model the behavior of such systems is as a time-varying
connectivity graph where individual nodes are considered as stochastic processes
evolving in time.
As such systems become networked at scale, it has become
increasingly important to monitor them to ensure that they perform tasks as
expected while satisfying safety constraints.
For instance, consider the problem
of detecting a real spike (vs.
bad sensor data) for a given bus in a power grid.
A proposed solution in
(
Zhu and Hill, 2020
)
is to compare if some subset of
neighboring busses also display the same spike.
Monitoring such properties
requires reasoning about both the spatial connectivity and temporal behavior of
nodes in the underlying graph representation of the system.

In systems where agents must coordinate tasks or avoid certain areas, spatial
relationships play a critical role, influencing both safety and performance.
For instance, in multi-robot systems, agents may need to stay within a specific
proximity to maintain communication or avoid obstacles in dynamic environments.
Spatio-temporal logics allow designers to specify these constraints and monitor
systems to ensure they conform to such requirements.
For e.g. in
1
, we have a simulation in the SwarmLab
environment
(
Soria et al., 2020
)
showing a flock of drones that must
navigate through an obstacle-dense environment to reach a central goal.
They must avoid obstacles (indicated by the black polygons), while maintaining
contact with the ground-stations (indicated by the blue regions).
Monitoring such systems requires advanced logic to ensure that agents maintain
connectivity, avoid collisions, and achieve goals efficiently.

Figure 1
.
A spatially distributed system of drones coordinating with each other to
“flock” towards a common goal.
The drones can have requirements to maintain communications with ground stations (blue circles) either directly or through a flock member.

In the CPS context, temporal logics have become a popular tool to describe and
monitor for complex spatio-temporal behavior
(
Taha et al., 2020
)
.
Logics like Metric
Temporal Logic
(
Koymans, 1990
;
Ouaknine and Worrell, 2005
;
Ouaknine and Worrell, 2008
)
and Signal Temporal Logic
(
Deshmukh et al., 2017
;
Bartocci et al., 2022
)
(and their
extensions) have been used extensively in this context due to the various
quantitative semantics proposed for them
(
Haghighi et al., 2019
)
, in
addition to their qualitative or Boolean semantics.
Recently, there has been a
lot of interest in spatio-temporal logics to explicitly reason about the spatial
configuration of a system.
For example, Signal spatial-temporal logic (SSTL)

(
Nenzi and Bortolussi, 2015
;
Nenzi et al., 2015
)
is a modal logic that allows
combining temporal modalities with two spatial modalities, one expressing that
something is true somewhere nearby and the other capturing the notion of being
surrounded by a region that satisfies a given spatio-temporal property.
The
logic SaSTL
(
Ma et al., 2020
)
extends STL by introducing operators that allow
spatial aggregation of real-valued quantities at neighbors and spatial counting
across sets of agents.
The logic SpaTel introduces directional spatial operators
by combining STL with the
Tree Spatial Superposition
Logic

(
Aydin-Gol et al., 2014
)
(TSSL) and assumes a fixed topology over the space
(for example a checkerboard pattern)
(
Haghighi et al., 2015
)
.
Most of the
aforementioned logics assume static spatial configurations.

Of particular relevance to this paper is the logic STREL (Spatio-Temporal Reach
and Escape Logic)
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
.
This logic combines STL with two
spatial operators: reach and escape which allow reasoning about temporal
properties over arbitrary routes in the graph describing the spatial
configuration.
The interesting aspect of STREL is that it allows reasoning about
dynamic spatial configurations, and has been used to model many different kinds
of properties such as bike-sharing systems in smart cities, mobile ad hoc
networks
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
, and disease spread during a pandemic

(
Mohammadinejad et al., 2021
)
.

In the automata theory literature, the connection between logics and automata is
well-studied.
For example, for every Linear Temporal Logic formula, an
equivalent non-deterministic Büchi automaton can be constructed in size
exponential in the length of the formula
(
Esparza et al., 2022
)
, and can be efficiently constructed
(
Pnueli and Zaks, 2008
)
.
Similar constructions exist
for translating computation tree logic (CTL) to weak Büchi tree automata

(
Vardi, 1996
)
, to map metric temporal logic to timed
automata
(
Alur et al., 1996
;
Maler et al., 2006
)
among others.
Curiously, this direction remains
unexplored for spatio-temporal logics.
In this paper, we present the first such
translation from a discrete-time variant of the logic STREL to an
alternating finite-state automaton
(AFA).

In a nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA), the notion of acceptance of a word

w
is that there exists a path from the initial state that reaches a final
state.
Consider a state
q
in the automaton that transitions to one of

\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\}
upon reading the symbol
a
.
Then, starting from
q
we can
say that a word
a.w
would be accepted if it accepts
w
from either
q_{1}
or

q_{2}
or
q_{3}
.
Briefly, we can express that as:
\mathrm{acc}(q,aw)=\mathrm{acc}(q_{1},w)\vee\mathrm{acc}(q_{2},w)\vee\mathrm{acc}(q_{3},w)
.
In an AFA, this acceptance condition can be expressed as an
arbitrary

combination of conjunctions or disjunctions.
For example
\mathrm{acc}(q,aw)=(\mathrm{acc}(q_{1},w)\wedge\mathrm{acc}(q_{2},w))\vee\mathrm{acc}(q_{3},w)
means
that the automaton accepts from
q
if it accepts
w
from
both

q_{1}
and

q_{2}
or it accepts from
q_{3}
.
AFA are a well-studied artifact, see

(
Vardi, 1995
;
Vardi, 2005
)
for a detailed treatment.

In this paper, we show that discrete-time STREL formulas can be converted into
AFA.
This translation is done in two steps: (1) We first convert a discrete-time
STREL formula into an equivalent formula that does not use temporal operators
with intervals.
As we assume full knowledge of the discrete time-stamps, for
example, a formula
\mathbf{F}_{[a,b]}\varphi
can be converted into a
disjunction over
\mathbf{X}
(next time) formulas.
(2) We then convert the
resulting formula into an alternating finite automaton.
We prove that the
complexity of the resulting AFA is linear in the size of the intermediate
formula (with
\mathbf{X}
operators).

A recent development in automata-based monitoring and quantitative satisfaction
semantics for temporal logics is the use of a general algebraic framework to
define semantics of the automaton/logic.
The observation is that the usual
qualitative (Boolean) satisfaction semantics for most temporal logics utilize
the Boolean semiring
(\vee,\wedge,0,1)
.
However, by allowing more general
semirings, a logical formula can be re-interpreted to produce some value in the
chosen semiring
(
Jakšić et al., 2018
;
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
.
For example, the
semiring
(\max,\min,-\infty,+\infty)
recovers the quantitative semantics of
Metric Temporal Logic as defined in
(
Fainekos and Pappas, 2009
)
.
An attractive
aspect of STREL is that it also provides quantitative semantics based on
semiring semantics.
In this paper, we show how the semiring semantics of STREL
can be directly incorporated into the AFA: instead of a standard AFA, we instead
get a
weighted
AFA.
The weighted AFA is especially useful for performing
quantitative monitoring in both online and offline settings.

To summarize, our main contributions in this paper are:

•

We present a linear-time construction for STREL specifications
to alternating finite automaton (AFA).

•

We additionally show how to adapt the quantitative semantics for STREL
presented in
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
into
weighted
AFA.

•

We present how to use this construction for offline and online
monitoring.

•

We present results from applying the automaton-based monitoring approach for STREL on case studies in the SwarmLab environment.
In these case studies, we monitor drone trajectories across various map configurations to evaluate their compliance with reach-avoid specifications, ensuring that drones maintain contact with the flock and can reconnect promptly when temporarily disconnected.

Preliminaries

Definition 2.1 (Semiring
(
Kuich and Salomaa, 1986
;
Golan, 1999
)
).

A tuple,
\mathbb{K}=\lparen K,\oplus,\otimes,\bot,\top\rparen
is
a
semiring
with the underlying set
K
if
\left(K,\oplus,\bot\right)
is a commutative monoid with identity
\bot
;

\left(K,\otimes,\top\right)
is a monoid with identity element

\top
;
\otimes
distributes over
\oplus
; and
\bot
is
an annihilator for
\otimes
(for all
k\in K,k\otimes\bot=\bot\otimes k=\bot
).

A semiring
K
is called a
commutative
if multiplication
\otimes

is also commutative and
simple
if
k\oplus\top=\top
, for all
k\in K
.
A semiring
K
is additively (or multiplicatively)
idempotent
if, for
all
k\in K
,
k\oplus k=k
(or
k\otimes k=k
).
A semiring that is commutative, additively idempotent, and simple is known as a

constraint semiring
or a
c-semiring
.
Additionally, if a c-semiring is also multiplicatively idempotent, the semiring
creates a
bounded distributive lattice
, where
\top
is the supremum
element and
\bot
is the infimum element.

Definition 2.2 (De Morgan Algebras
(
Cignoli and Gallego, 1983
)
).

A De Morgan algebra is a structure
(K,\oplus,\otimes,\ominus,\bot,\top)
such that:

•

(K,\oplus,\otimes,\bot,\top)
is a
bounded
distributive lattice
; and

•

\ominus
is a negation function on
K
such that, for
a,b\in K

–

\ominus(a\oplus b)=\ominus a\otimes\ominus b
,

–

\ominus(a\otimes b)=\ominus a\oplus\ominus b
,

–

\ominus\ominus a=a
,

i.e.,
\ominus
is an
involution
on
K
that follows De
Morgan’s laws.

Examples of De Morgan algebras include

•

The Boolean algebra
(\mathbb{B},\lor,\land,\neg,\bot,\top)
with

\land,\lor,
and
\neg
referring to the usual logical
conjunction, disjunction, and negation respectively.

•

The min-max algebra over the extended reals
(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty,-\infty\},\max{},\min{},-,\bot,\top)
.

Definition 2.3 (Distance Domain).

(D,\bot_{D},\top_{D},+_{D},\leq_{D})
is a
distance domain
such that

\leq_{D}
is a total ordering over
D
with
\bot_{D}
and
\top_{D}
as
infimum and supremum respectively, and
(B,\bot_{B},+_{B})
is a monoid.

The following are examples of distance domains:

•

Counting domain
(\mathbb{N}^{\infty},0,\infty,+,\leq)
, where
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}=\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
is the extended natural numbers,
+

is the usual addition with identity
0
and bounded at
\infty
(
i+\infty=\infty
), and
\leq
is the usual definition of total
ordering.

•

Tropical domain
(\mathbb{R}^{+},0,\infty,+,\leq)
where
\mathbb{R}^{+}=\{d\in\mathbb{R}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}d\geq 0\}\cup\infty

reals, with
+

Spatial Models

Definition 2.4 (Spatial Model
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
).

Given some set
W
, a
W
-spatial model
\mathcal{S}
is a graph
(L,E)

where
L
is a finite set of
locations
, also called a
space
universe
, and
E\subseteq L\times W\times L
is a finite set of labeled
edges between the locations.

We will use
\mathbb{S}_{W}
to describe the set of all
W
-spatial models, while

\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}
indicates the set of
D
-spatial models having
L
as a set of
locations.
For a given
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}
, we abuse notation and let
\mathcal{S}(l_{1},l_{2})=w

refer to the edge
(l_{1},w,l_{2})

\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 2.5 (Paths in
\mathcal{S}
).

Let
\mathcal{S}=(L,E)\in\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}
be a
W
-spatial model.
Then, a

\tau
is a sequence of locations

such that
l_{i}\in L
and
(l_{i},w,l_{i+1})\in E

A

is a path where no location repeats.

Note that since an edge cannot occur more than once in a path, the number of
paths in a spatial model is finite, and the length of a path
\tau
(denoted

\lvert\tau\rvert
) is also finite.
We let
Routes(\mathcal{S})
denote the set of routes in
\mathcal{S}
and
Routes(\mathcal{S},l)
denote the set of routes starting at location
l
.

For a path
\tau=l_{0}l_{1}\cdots
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and
l\in L
, we use

•

\tau[i]
to denote the
i
th node
l_{i}
in
\tau

•

\tau[..i]
to indicate the prefix
l_{0}l_{1}\cdots l_{i}
, and

\tau[i..]
to indicate the suffix
l_{i}l_{i+1}\cdots
, both of
which are also paths in
Routes(\mathcal{S})

•

l\in\tau
when there exists an index
i
such that
\tau[i]=l
, and
l\not\in\tau
otherwise.

Definition 2.6 (Distance functions and Distance over paths).

Let
\mathcal{S}=(L,E)
be a
W
-spatial model and
(D,\bot_{D},\top_{D},+_{D},\leq_{D})
be a distance domain.
A
distance function

f:W\to D
is a mapping from an edge
weight in
\mathcal{S}
to the distance domain, and the distance over a path

d^{f}:Routes(\mathcal{S})\to D
is defined as:

d^{f}(\tau)=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert\tau\rvert}f(\mathcal{S}(\tau[i-1],\tau[i]))&~\text{if}~\lvert\tau\rvert\geq 2\\
\bot_{B}&~\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where the summation is defined over the monoid
+_{D}
.

We use the notation
d^{f}_{\tau}[i]
to refer to
d^{f}(\tau[..i])
, i.e., the
path distance of the prefix of a path
\tau

i
th location on

And we use the following notation to describe the distance shortest path
between two locations
l_{1},l_{2}\in L
in
\mathcal{S}
:

d^{f}_{\mathcal{S}}[l_{1},l_{2}]=\min\{d^{f}_{\tau}[i]\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\tau\in Routes(\mathcal{S},l_{1}),\tau[i]=l_{2}\}.

2.2.
Spatio-temporal Reach and Escape Logic (STREL)

The syntax for STREL
(
Bartocci et al., 2017
;
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
for
discrete-time traces is given by the recursive definition

(1)

\begin{split}\varphi:=&\mu\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\\
&\mid\TNext\varphi\mid\varphi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\varphi\mid\varphi\Reach_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\varphi\mid\Escape_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\varphi.\end{split}

Here,
\mu
is an atomic predicate, negation
\neg
, conjunction
\land
,
and disjunction
\lor
are the standard Boolean connectives,
\TNext
is the
temporal
Next
operator, and
\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
is the
Until

temporal operator with a positive real time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
.
These are the same temporal operators found in Linear Temporal Logic and Metric
Temporal Logic.

Reach

\Reach
and
Escape

\Escape
are the
spatial

operators defined in STREL, where
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
are
distances
in
a domain
D
and
f
is a distance function mapping to
D
.

Informally, the
Reach
operator
\phi_{1}\Reach_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\phi_{2}

describes the behavior of reaching a location satisfying
\phi_{2}
through a
path
\tau
such that
d_{1}\leq d^{f}(\tau)\leq d_{2}
and
\phi_{1}
is
satisfied at all locations in the path.
The
Escape
operator
\Escape_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\phi_{1}
instead describes
the possibility of escaping from a certain region via a path that passes only
through locations that satisfy
\phi_{1}
such that the distance between the
starting location and the last location belongs in the interval
[d_{1},d_{2}]
.
Note that for
Escape
, the distance constraint is not necessarily over the
satisfying path, but the shortest distance path between the start and end
location (but they may be the same).

From the above syntax we can derive the standard temporal
operators
Eventually

\Ev_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
and
Globally

\Alw_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
.
Moreover, we can derive from them additional
spatial operators
:

•

\diamonddiamond_{\leq d}^{f}\varphi=\top\Reach_{\leq d}^{f}\varphi

denotes the
Somewhere
operator

•

\boxbox_{\leq d}^{f}\varphi=\neg\diamonddiamond_{\leq d}^{f}\neg\varphi
denotes the
Everywhere
operator

•

\varphi_{1}\varocircle_{\leq d}^{f}\varphi_{2}
denotes the
Surround

operator.

The
Somewhere
and
Everywhere
operations describe behavior for some
or all locations within reach of a specific location, and the
Surround

expresses the notion of being surrounded by a region that satisfies
\phi_{2}
,
while being in a
\phi_{1}
satisfying region.

To formally define the semantics of a STREL specification, we first define
a
trace
over
W
-spatial models.

Definition 2.7 (
K
-Labeled Trace).

Let
(K,\oplus,\otimes,\ominus,\bot,\top)
be a De Morgan algebra and

L
a space universe.
Then, a
K
-labeled trace
is a pair

(\sigma,\nu)
such that

•

\sigma=\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathcal{S}_{1}\cdots
is a finite
sequence of spatial models

•

\nu(\mathcal{S}_{i},l,\mu)\in K
is a labeling
function that maps the location
l\in L
in
\mathcal{S}_{i}
to a value in

K
for an atomic predicate
\mu
.

We will simply refer to a trace as
\sigma
when the labeling function
\nu

is obvious.

Thus, the algebraic semantics
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)

\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi,t)

of a discrete-time STREL specification
\phi
at an
ego
location
l\in L
for a
K
-labeled trace
\sigma=\mathcal{S}_{0}\mathcal{S}_{1}\cdots
at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
can be defined as:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\rho_{l}(\sigma,\mu,t)&=&\nu(\mathcal{S}_{t},l,\mu)\\
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\neg\phi,t)&=&\ominus\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi,t)\\
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2},t)&=&\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1},t)\otimes\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{2},t)\\
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2},t)&=&\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1},t)\oplus\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{2},t)\\
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1}\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\phi_{2},t)&=&\bigoplus\limits_{\mathclap{t^{\prime}\in t+[t_{1},t_{2}]}}\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{2},t^{\prime})\otimes\bigotimes\limits_{\mathclap{t^{\prime\prime}\in[t^{\prime},t]}}\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1},t^{\prime\prime})\\
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi_{1}\Reach_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\phi_{2},t)&=&\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\bigoplus\limits_{{\tau\in Routes(\mathcal{S}_{t},l)}}\bigoplus\limits_{{i:d^{f}_{\tau}[i]\in[d_{1},d_{2}]}}\left(\rho_{\tau[i]}(\sigma,\phi_{2},t)\otimes\bigotimes\limits_{j<i}\rho_{\tau[j]}(\sigma,\phi_{1},t)\right)\lx@intercol\\[20.00003pt]
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\Escape_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\phi,t)&=&\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\bigoplus\limits_{\tau\in Routes(\mathcal{S}_{t},l)}\bigoplus\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l^{\prime}\in\tau\\
d^{f}_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}[l,l^{\prime}]\in[d_{1},d_{2}]\end{subarray}}\bigotimes\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq j\\
\tau[j]=l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\rho_{\tau[i]}(\sigma,\phi,t)\lx@intercol\end{array}

See that for the above algebraic semantics, using the Boolean algebra
(\mathbb{B},\lor,\land,\neg,\bot,\top)
gets us the Boolean/quantitative semantics of
STREL over finite traces.
For the Boolean algebra, we say that a trace
\sigma
satisfies a
specification
\phi
at the ego location
l
(denoted
\sigma\models(\phi,l)
) if the output of
\rho_{l}(\sigma,\phi,0)
is
\top
.
We also use the same notation,
\mathcal{S}\models(\phi,l)
, to denote if the
specification
\phi
is satisfied for a spatial model
\mathcal{S}
at location

l
.

2.3.
Alternating Automata

For a given set
X
, let
\mathbb{B}[X]
be the set of positive Boolean formulas
over
X\cup\{\top,\bot\}
, i.e., Boolean formulas built over
X
using
only
\land
and
\lor
logical connectives.
We let
X^{*}
denote the set of all finite-length sequences
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots
such that
x_{i}\in X
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

In a non-deterministic automaton
\mathcal{A}=\left(\Sigma,Q,Q_{0},\Delta,F\right)
, with input alphabet
\Sigma
, states
Q
, initial states

Q_{0}\subseteq Q
, and final states
F\subseteq Q
, we usually model the
transition relation
\Delta
as a partial function
\Delta:Q\times\Sigma\to 2^{Q}
.
For example, for a state
q\in Q
and an input symbol
a\in\Sigma
, the
transition relation may output the set of successor states as
\Delta(q,a)=\left\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\right\}
.
The above can also be represented as a function
\Delta:Q\times\Sigma\to\mathbb{B}[Q]
such that the set
\left\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\right\}
is equivalent to the
formula
q_{1}\lor q_{2}\lor q_{3}
.

While non-deterministic automata restrict such formulas to disjunctions only, in
alternating automata, we allow the use of arbitrary formula from
\mathbb{B}[Q]
.
Thus, we can have an
alternating
transition

\Delta(q,a)=(q_{1}\land q_{2})\lor(q_{3}\land q_{4}),

which means that the automaton accepts a word

aw
, where

Such a transition combines

Definition 2.8 (Alternating Automata

An

\mathcal{A}=\left(\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\Delta,F\right)
, where

Due to the nature of the universal transitions, for an input

•

Intuitively, the automaton monitor clones itself and transitions to all the states in a universal transition, and the runs of each cloned automata must all be accepting for the word to be accepted.

3.
Conversion to Alternating Automata

In this section, we will derive a framework for translating STREL specifications to alternating word automata.
We will first look at translating STREL to an intermediate logic,
Spatial Linear Temporal Logic
(SpLTL), from which we will derive the direct translation into an alternating word automaton.

3.1.
Spatial Linear Temporal Logic

Similar to STREL, we define the syntax of a Spatial Linear Temporal Logic (SpLTL) recursively as:

(2)

\begin{split}\varphi:=&\mu\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\\
&\mid\TNext\varphi\mid\varphi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\varphi\mid\varphi\Reach_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\varphi\mid\Escape_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}^{f}\varphi.\end{split}

Here, the various operators maintain the same semantics as in discrete-time
STREL, except that we do not permit intervals on
Until
(and by extension
on the derived
Eventually
and
Globally
operators).
For the SpLTL operators, we retain the same notation for the semantics
\rho

and for denoting accepting traces and models.

Lemma 3.1.

For any discrete-time STREL specification,
\varphi
, there exists a corresponding SpLTL specification
\varphi^{\prime}
such that

(1)

For an input trace
\sigma
and ego location
l
,
\sigma\models(\varphi^{\prime},l)

if and only if

\sigma\models(\varphi,l)
.

(2)

\lvert\varphi^{\prime}\rvert
is in
O(T)
, where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
counts the number of syntactic elements and terms in the formula and
T
is the sum of all the intervals in
\varphi
.

Proof.

We can prove the above by showing the expansion formulas for every temporal operator with an interval to an untimed one
(
Donzé et al., 2013
;
Esparza et al., 2016
;
Vardi, 1996
)
:

•

\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\psi\equiv\Ev_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\psi\land\left(\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},\infty)}\psi\right)
, for
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N},t_{1}<t_{2}

•

\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}_{[t_{1},\infty)}\psi\equiv\Alw_{[0,t_{1}]}\left(\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\psi\right)

•

\Ev\phi\equiv\top\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\phi
and
\Alw\phi\equiv\neg\Ev\neg\phi\equiv\neg\left(\top\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\neg\phi\right)

•

Bounded formulas
\Ev_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\phi
and
\Alw_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\phi
, where
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N},t_{1}<t_{2}
, are equivalent to nested
disjunctions or conjunctions of the
next
operator.
For example,

F_{[0,3]}a\equiv a\lor\TNext(a\lor\TNext(a\lor\TNext a)).

From the above, we can see that for each temporal expression, there is a linear increase in the number of syntactic elements.
Specifically, if an interval for a formula is
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, we see that the formula increases by a factor of
t_{2}-t_{1}
(with some constant overhead).
Thus, if
T
is the sum of all the interval sizes in
\varphi
, the size of
\varphi^{\prime}
is in
O(T)
.
∎

Theorem 3.2 (Linear-sized SpLTL automata).

Given a SpLTL formula
\phi
with atomic propositions
A
and locations

L
, one can build an alternating finite automaton
\mathcal{A}=\lparen\Sigma,Q,q_{0},\Delta,F\rparen
where the language
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A},l)
is exactly the set of

at
ego location

such
that
\lvert Q\rvert
is in
O(\lvert L\rvert\lvert\phi\rvert)
.

Proof.

We can clearly see from the above construction that

corresponding to each subformula in
\phi

corresponding to

.

have, for each location

Remark
.

Note that while, in general, the

through the temporal operators, and the non-reachable states can be pruned.

3.3.
Alternating Weighted Automata

In this section, we look at how we can generalize the above construction to
incorporate the quantitative semantics of STREL.

Notice that for some finite set
X
, formulas
\theta\in\mathbb{B}[X]
that
are in DNF form are effectively polynomials over the Boolean semiring with
support variables
X
, with logical
and
as multiplication and logical

or
as addition.

For a commutative semiring
K
and a set
X=\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right\}
, we use
K[X]
to denote the set of all polynomials with
indeterminates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and coefficients in
K
.
A monomial
m
with indeterminates
X=\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right\}
is
a polynomial in
K[X]
such that
m=cx_{1}^{k_{1}}x_{2}^{k_{2}}\ldots x_{n}^{k_{n}}
for some coefficient
c\in K
and non-negative integers

k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}
where multiplication is under the semiring operation.
A constant
a\in K
is a monomial in
K[X]
with the power of all
indeterminates equal to 0 and coefficient
a
.
The
support
of a polynomial is the set of indeterminates (or variables)
with coefficients not equal to
\bot\in K
.
Moreover, a polynomial
\theta\in K[X]
is a scalar function
\theta:K^{\lvert X\rvert}\to K
.

For idempotent semirings (or, equivalently, bounded distributive lattices),
since
x^{k}=x
for all non-negative integers
k
such that
k>0
, we
can see that the
degree
of the polynomials
K[X]
over a bounded
distributive lattice does not exceed
1
.
This includes the Boolean semiring (equivalent to the previous section) and the

\min-\max
semiring, which defines the quantitative semantics of STREL.

Let us now formally define the alternating weighted automaton, along with the
conversion of a STREL formula to such an automaton while preserving quantitative
semantics.

Definition 3.3 (Alternating Weighted Automata
(
Kostolányi and Mišún, 2018
)
).

Let
K
be a commutative semiring.
Then, an
alternating weighted automaton
over
K
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=\left(\Sigma,Q,\Delta,\alpha,\beta\right)
, where
\Sigma
and

Q
are the input alphabet and the non-empty finite set of states.
Here, the transition function
\Delta
is a polynomial assigning function

\Delta:Q\times\Sigma\to K[Q]
with

\alpha\in K[Q]
as an initial weight polynomial and

\beta:Q\to K
as the terminal weighting function.

While the definition of
\Sigma
remains the same as in the previous section,
for
Q
, we will not populate it with any negations unless they also appear in
the specification
\phi
.
The transition function
\Delta
needs to be redefined in terms of
polynomials over semirings, along with the initial polynomial and terminal
weights,
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Moreover, the labeling function,
\nu
needs to be defined over the semiring

K
.

Initial and Terminal Weights

For a STREL specification
\varphi
and an
ego
location
l_{0}
, the
initial weight polynomial
\alpha\in K[Q]
is exactly the polynomial

q_{\varphi}^{l_{0}}
, i.e,
\alpha
is the polynomial with the initial state
corresponding to the ego location as the support and unitary coefficient

\top
.
The
terminal weighting function
,
\beta:Q\to K
, is a mapping such
that

\beta(q)=\begin{cases}\top,&\text{~if~}q\in F,\\
\bot,&\text{~otherwise.}\end{cases}

These correspond to the initial states
Q_{0}
and final states
F

respectively.

Note
.

Similar to the Boolean case, the initial polynomial
\alpha
of the weighted
automaton can vary depending on the choice of the ego location.
Specifically, the initial polynomial is always the variable for state

q_{\phi}^{l_{0}}
, where
\phi
is the specification being monitored and

l_{0}
is the ego location.

Labeling function
\nu

Depending on the choice of algebra for
K
, let
\nu:\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}\times L\times A\to K
be a mapping of an atomic predicate from the spatial domain to
K
.
An example can be a notion of a “distance” that a location
l
needs to move
for it to satisfy the predicate
\mu
.
For example, as in
(
Fainekos and Pappas, 2009
)
and
(
Donzé and Maler, 2010
)
,
this can be a signed distance such that if the value is greater than 0, the
predicate is satisfied, and negative if the predicate is not, and can be used
with the min-max algebra.

Transition relation
\Delta

We redefine the Boolean transition relation over an arbitrary semiring
polynomial
K[Q]
.
Specifically, for some
\mathcal{S}\in\Sigma
, we define the transition relation

\Delta:Q\times\Sigma\to K[Q]
recursively on states
corresponding to subformulas in
\Phi_{\varphi}
as follows:

(3)

\begin{array}[]{c@{\ =\ }l}\Delta(q_{\top}^{l},\cdot)&\top\\
\Delta(q_{\bot}^{l},\cdot)&\bot\\
\Delta(q_{\mu}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\nu(\mathcal{S},l,\mu)\\
\Delta(q_{\neg\phi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\ominus\Delta(q_{\phi}^{l},\mathcal{S})\\
\Delta(q_{\phi\land\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\Delta(q_{\phi}^{l},\mathcal{S})\otimes\Delta(q_{\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})\\
\Delta(q_{\TNext\phi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&q_{\phi}^{l}\\
\Delta(q_{\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\Delta(q_{\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})\oplus\left(\Delta(q_{\phi}^{l},\mathcal{S})\otimes q_{\phi\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\psi}^{l}\right)\\
\Delta(q_{\phi\Reach^{f}_{[d1,d2]}\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\bigoplus\limits_{\tau\in Routes(\mathcal{S},l)}\bigoplus\limits_{i:\left(d_{\tau}^{f}[i]\in[d_{1},d_{2}]\right)}\left(\Delta(q_{\psi}^{\tau[i]},\mathcal{S})\otimes\bigotimes\limits_{j<i}\Delta(q_{\phi}^{\tau[j]},\mathcal{S})\right)\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\Delta(q_{\Escape^{f}_{\leq d}\psi}^{l},\mathcal{S})&\\
\lx@intercol\hfil\bigoplus\limits_{\tau\in Routes(\mathcal{S}_{t},l)}\bigoplus\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l^{\prime}\in\tau\\

\tau[j]=l^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\Delta(q_{\psi}^{\tau[i]},\mathcal{S})\hfil\lx@intercol\end{array}

Run of an alternating weighted automaton

Similar to a standard alternating automaton, the run of an alternating weighted
automaton is also a tree.
But, unlike the Boolean version, where the branches of the tree terminate in
Boolean values, determining the satisfaction value of a run, the branches of
a weighted run end in leaves taking value in a given algebra
K
.
The
weight

w_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma)
of a run induced by a trace
\sigma
on the automaton
\mathcal{A}
is the sum over paths in the run tree with the semiring
K
, followed by a product over the value of each branch.

4.
Automata-based Monitoring for STREL

Let the states in
Q
be consistently indexed such that
q_{1},\ldots,q_{\lvert Q\rvert}\in Q
.

As an alternating (weighted) automaton is characterized by polynomials over De
Morgan polynomials
K[Q]
, we can see that to keep track of the current state
of the run of the automaton, it is sufficient to keep track of a single
polynomial.
Specifically, let
\theta\in K[X]
be the current state of the automaton.

At the start of the execution, for the specification
\phi
and an ego
location
l_{0}
,
\theta
can be initialized to the state
\alpha

corresponding to the given ego location
l_{0}
.
Then for subsequent input symbol
\mathcal{S}\in\Sigma
, we can compute the next
state of the automaton
\theta^{\prime}
, where

\theta^{\prime}\leftarrow\theta\left\lparen\Delta(q_{1},\mathcal{S}),\ldots,\Delta(q_{\lvert Q\rvert},\mathcal{S})\right\rparen.

That is, for each state variable
q
in the support of
\theta
, we evaluate
the transition function and substitute it into
\theta
, producing a new
polynomial
\theta^{\prime}\in K[Q]
.

Then, to evaluate the satisfaction value or weight of a run at a given state

\theta
at time
t
, we simply use the final mapping
\beta
such that

\rho_{l_{0}}(\sigma_{0}\cdots\sigma_{t},\phi,0)=\theta(\beta(q_{1}),\ldots,\beta(q_{\lvert Q\rvert})).

This method can be used both for online monitoring by keeping track of the
current state
\theta
and evaluating with
\beta
as needed, or in an
offline setting, by evaluating with
\beta
at the end of the trace.

Complexity of Monitoring Spatial Operators

Notice that in the transition definition for nodes corresponding to
Reach

and
Escape
operations, the
Routes(\cdot,l)
searches over all
possible routes originating from location
l
.
In general, this can lead to an explosion in the search space.
Indeed, simply the problem of enumerating all paths between two locations

s,t
in a graph is known to be
\#P
-complete
(
Valiant, 1979
)
.

In
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
, the authors overcome this for offline monitoring as
they compute the satisfaction value for each subformula before evaluating the
spatial operators.
In our case, since we are consuming input
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}
for every time
step, in general, we cannot realize whether a specific subformula for
\Delta

holds true at a location before the end of the trace.

To this end, we adopt a depth-limited search algorithm to
enumerate paths from an ego location
l

(
Agarwal et al., 2000
)
.
Here, the goal is to repurpose the standard depth-first search algorithm to enumerate each path such that we avoid repeating locations in a path by marking them visited, and then removing the mark just before popping from the stack.

Proposition 4.1.

Given a spatial model
\mathcal{S}=(L,E)\in\mathbb{S}_{W}^{L}
, a distance function

, and an interval

, we define
d_{min}=\min\left\{f(w)\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}(l,w,l)\in E\right\}
and
k=\min\left\{i\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}i*d_{min}>d_{2}\right\}

(where
i*d_{min}
indicates the sum of
i

d_{min}
.
Let
b=\max_{l}{(l,w,l^{\prime})\in E}
be the maximum node degree in
\mathcal{S}
.
Then, the complexity to enumerate the list of simple paths from a location

O(b^{d_{min}})
.

Complexity of updating states

When working with Boolean polynomials, a common data structure to use is Binary
Decision Diagrams
(
Bryant, 1992
)
, or Algebraic Decision Diagrams in
the context of finite domains
(
Bahar et al., 1997

.
It is a well-established result that reduced-ordered binary decision diagrams
(ROBDD) are canonical representations of arbitrary Boolean expressions and that
the computing a

(evaluating the expression with Boolean
values for each variable) or a
composition
(substituting a variable with
another expression) can be done in time linear in the size of each expression.

Similar work has been done in the context of representing arbitrary semiring
polynomials as
decision diagrams

(
Wilson, 2005
;
Fargier et al., 2013
)
, where polynomial operations like
restriction and composition are linear in the size of the polynomials.

Lemma 4.2.

For any update to the current state
\theta\in K[Q]

\mathcal{A}=(\Sigma,Q,\Delta,\alpha,\beta)
with state variables

.
Moreover, evaluating the final weight
\theta(\beta)
of a run from a given state

Lemma 4.3.

For a polynomial
\theta\in K[X]
, where
K
is a De Morgan algebra and

\theta

\lvert\theta\rvert

2^{\lvert X\rvert}
.

Proof.

De Morgan algebras have the property of the multiplication and addition
monoids begin idempotent.
Thus, as noted earlier, polynomials over De Morgan algebras have the property
that the degree for any variable in the polynomial is at most 1.
So, for a set of variables
X

2^{\lvert X\rvert}

permutations of exponents for each term with values
0
or
1
.
Thus, for any De Morgan polynomial
\theta\in K[X]

\theta

2^{\lvert X\rvert}
.
∎

3.1

.

To demonstrate our framework, we monitor the execution of a simulation of drone
swarms that are coordinating to flock towards a common goal, while avoiding
obstacles in their path.
Each drone in the swarm can communicate within a certain proximity to other
drones and to stationary ground stations.
This setup allows us to generate diverse set of drone trajectories, with various
obstacle and ground station configurations, while monitoring STREL properties
over their execution.

Experimental Setup

For our experiments, we use the Swarmlab simulator
(
Soria et al., 2020
)
to
generate trajectories for drone swarms under various configurations.
In the simulation, each drone must reach a goal area from some initial location
while avoiding obstacles and passing through regions where they can communicate
with a ground station.
The trajectories are sampled at a
10ms
period.

We vary experimental parameters such as obstacle density, number of drones,
and number and positions of ground stations.
The various maps with their configurations are shown in
Figure

2
.

Spatial Model in Traces

The space universe
L
of the system is the set of drones and ground stations,
each annotated with a unique identifier and position in 3D (although the drones
travel almost along the same altitude, thus the vertical dimension can be
discarded).
An edge is formed between locations
l,l^{\prime}
if they are within communication
reach of each other —
40m
if either of the locations is a ground station,

30m
otherwise.

Specifications

Here, we look at two specifications over the traces of the spatial models.
These specifications are designed to ensure that certain behavioral conditions
are met by the swarm throughout the simulation.
In particular, we focus on specifications that impose connectivity constraints
on each drone — such as being in proximity to a ground station or other drones
that are within the proximity of one.

Establish Communication
\phi_{1}
:
Ensures that each drone can maintain connectivity with either the swarm of
drones or a ground station within a specific distance.
If a drone is isolated from the rest of the swarm it must reconnect within 100
time steps (or 1 second) either with another drone that is within 2 hops from a
drone or a ground station.
This specification is intended to monitor the robustness of the swarm’s
communication network and ability to dynamically reconfigure in response to
disruptions.

\phi_{1}=\Alw\left(\diamonddiamond_{[1,2]}^{hops}\textsf{drone}\lor\Ev_{[0,100]}\diamonddiamond_{[1,2]}^{hops}\left(\textsf{drone}\lor\textsf{groundstation}\right)\right)

Reach Avoid
\phi_{2}
:
This ensures that drones must avoid obstacles throughout their trajectories and
until it reaches the goal, it must remain in communication with a ground station
within two hops.
This specification is designed to assess navigational safety in obstacle avoidance and connectivity reliability with ground stations as the drones move along their trajectories to their goal positions.

\phi_{2}=\Alw\neg\textsf{obstacle}\land\left(\left(\textsf{drone}\Reach^{hops}_{[0,2]}\textsf{groundstation}\right)\mathbin{\mathsf{U}}\textsf{goal}\right)

The predicates
drone
and
groundstation
are enabled at
a location if the location corresponds to a drone or ground station respectively.

In the specifications, we refer to the distance function
hops
that labels
each edge in a spatial model with the weight
1\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}
, and the
distance domain is the counting domain, i.e.,
(\mathbb{N}^{\infty},0,\infty,+,\leq)
.
This distance function applied over a path in the spatial model essentially
captures the number of “hops” some message would make for an agent at the
start location to communicate to the agent at the other end of the path.

For the former, we report the average time taken for each step across all drones

5.1.
Results and Analysis

Our experiments were conducted on a laptop with an M3 chip featuring an 8-core
CPU, 10-core GPU and a 16-core Neural engine.
The framework operates at a sampling rate of 10 milliseconds per step which
allows us to capture fine-grained behaviors.
For each configuration we observe that the mointoring time is influenced by the
obstacle density, number of drones and trace length.
As expected, configurations with fewer drones and obstacles such as Maps 4 and 5
have significantly lower monitoring times.
Configurations with higher obstacle densities and larger number of drones (Map
3) result in increased monitoring times, especially for
\phi_{2}
.
However, even despite the theoretical complexities of monitoring over multiple
agents and a high number of obstacles, the actual computation times per step are
not affected, as seen in
Table 1
in the seconds-per-step
metric.
This indicates that our framework can handle a large number of spatial
locations, agents and map configurations without significant slowdown.

6.
Related Work

In the work by
(
Momtaz et al., 2023a
)
, the authors propose distributed monitoring for an STL formula over the global state of a distributed system.
For a truly distributed system, estimating the global state is a hard problem due to asynchronous communication and lack of a central clock.
The authors show that STL formulas can be monitored under the assumption of bounded clock drift using SMT solvers.
In our approach, we do not consider issues arising from clock asynchrony, and assume a central clock.
It would be interesting to extend our approach with assumptions similar to the above work.

The work by
(
Matos Pedro et al., 2022
)
introduces a method for monitoring continuous-time, continuous-valued signals in CPS by compensating for clock drifts, enabling fast, distributing monitoring suitable for real-time deployment in autonomous systems, while the authors of
(
Momtaz et al., 2023b
)
work on distributed predicate detection using the notion of consistent cuts extended to continuous-time settings.
STL has also been extended to applications in monitoring agent-based epidemic
spread models
(
Bortolussi and Nenzi, 2014
)
.
Similar to our work,
(
Audrito and Torta, 2021
)
focus on integration spatial and temporal logic in distributed monitoring of swarms.
The work
(
Li et al., 2021
)
presents STSL, a spatio-temporal specification language combining temporal and spatial logic
\mathcal{S}4_{u}
for CPS, with applications in runtime verification, falsification, and parameter synthesis.
TQTL has been introduced in
(
Balakrishnan et al., 2019
)
to assess spatio-temporal properties of perception algorithms in autonomous systems allowing quality evaluation even without ground truth labels.
Spatio-Temporal Perception Logic (SPTL)
(
Hekmatnejad et al., 2024
;
Balakrishnan et al., 2021
)
has also been introduced to evaluate perception systems in autonomous vehicles providing spatial-temporal reasoning capabilities and enabling offline performance checks without ground-truth data.
Recently, team Spatio-Temporal Reach and Escape Logic (tSTREL)
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
has been introduced as an extension of STREL with the possibility to evaluate reach and escape operators over a set of locations, which provides more flexibility in expressing multi-agent system properties.

7.
Discussions

7.1.
Limitations

In this paper, we currently only handle monitoring for finite-length traces, where the input word is some
\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}
.
To support infinite-length traces, we would require the alternating word automaton to have Buchi acceptance conditions
(
Vardi, 1995
)
.
In general, while our construction works for translating STREL specifications into such automata, we will explore the semantics and applications for them in future work.

Furthermore, the semantics for STREL in
(
Bartocci et al., 2017
)
and
(
Nenzi et al., 2022
)
are defined for continuous signals from multiple locations.
Handling this would require extending our construction to
alternating timed automata
.

In our construction, we also consider only spatial models with finite, a priori known locations and do not handle edge multiplicities, and will investigate efficient means to do so in the future.
For example, for the former, we can potentially use a symbolic variable for the locations and realize them as we see new locations in the input word.

7.2.
Conclusion

In this work, we address the growing need for the robust monitoring of spatially distributed cyber-physical systems by leveraging STREL. We introduce a novel, linear-time construction of alternating finite automata (AFA) from STREL specifications. Additionally we presented an extension to weighted AFA for quantitative monitoring. Our contributions allow for efficient and scalable, offline and online monitoring as demonstrated by our Swarmlab experiments. By analyzing drone trajectories in various map configurations, we validated our framework’s capability to enforce reach-avoid, and connectivity-maintenance specifications on drones that attempt to navigate their way to the goal in complex environments. To summarize, we highlight the utility of automaton-based monitoring for scalable and dynamic applications of CPS and its efficiency in our experiments indicates promising applications in real-world distributed systems.
