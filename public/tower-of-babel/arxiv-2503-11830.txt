Application of the Pontryagin Maximum Principle to the robust time-optimal control of two-level quantum systems
O. Fresse-Colson, S. Guérin, Xi Chen, D. Sugny

Abstract
We study the time-optimal robust control of a two-level quantum system subjected to field inhomogeneities. We apply the Pontryagin Maximum Principle and we introduce a reduced space onto which the optimal dynamics is projected down. This reduction leads to a complete analytical derivation of the optimal solution in terms of elliptic functions and elliptic integrals. Necessary optimality conditions are then obtained for the original system. These conditions are verified numerically and lead to the optimal control protocol. Various examples, ranging from state-to-state transfer to the generation of a Not gate, illustrate this study. The connection with other geometric optimization approaches that have been used to solve this problem is also discussed.

The model system

We consider a two-level quantum system whose dynamics are governed in a given rotating frame and within the Rotating Wave Approximation by the following Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\alpha\hat{V}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta(t)&(1+\alpha)\Omega(t)\\
(1+\alpha)\Omega(t)&\Delta(t)\end{pmatrix},

(1)

with
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=[-\Delta(t)\hat{\sigma}_{z}+\Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_{x}]/2
and
\displaystyle\hat{V}=\Omega(t)\hat{\sigma}_{x}/2
. The two-dimensional Hilbert space of the system
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
is spanned by the canonical basis
\displaystyle\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
. This system has two control parameters:
\displaystyle\Delta(t)
, the detuning between the frequency of the external electromagnetic field and the frequency transition of the quantum system, and
\displaystyle\Omega(t)
, the Rabi frequency which is proportional to the field amplitude. The term
\displaystyle\hat{V}
, which describes field inhomogeneities, is a perturbation of
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}
with
\displaystyle|\alpha|\ll 1
a small real parameter. We assume that the two control parameters satisfy the constraints
\displaystyle|\Delta(t)|\leq\Delta_{0}
and
\displaystyle|\Omega(t)|\leq\Omega_{0}
where the two bounds
\displaystyle\Delta_{0}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{0}
are fixed by the experimental setup. The goal is to control this system in minimum time while being robust to field inhomogeneities, i.e. with respect to the parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
. In this study, we consider the definition of robustness used in
[
46
,
47
,
49
,
50
]
and briefly recalled below.

The state
\displaystyle|\psi\rangle
of the system is the solution of the time-dependent Shrödinger equation,
\displaystyle i|\dot{\psi}\rangle=\hat{H}|\psi\rangle
(in units where
\displaystyle\hbar=1
). We consider its expansion in power series of the parameter
\displaystyle\alpha
as

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha^{n}|\psi_{n}\rangle=|\psi_{0}\rangle+\alpha|\psi_{1}\rangle+\alpha^{2}|\psi_{2}\rangle+\dots

(2)

The kets
\displaystyle|\psi_{n}\rangle
satisfy the following dynamics

\displaystyle\displaystyle i|\dot{\psi}_{0}\rangle=\hat{H}_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle\displaystyle i|\dot{\psi}_{n}\rangle=\hat{H}_{0}|\psi_{n}\rangle+\hat{V}|\psi_{n-1}\rangle,~\textrm{for}~n\geq 1.

Note that the dynamics of
\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle
is only determined by
\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}
, and that the other kets
\displaystyle|\psi_{n}\rangle
(
\displaystyle n\geq 1
) are not normalized. We use a Dyson series to find the expression of the different kets. At time
\displaystyle t_{f}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\psi(t_{f})\rangle=|\psi_{0}(t_{f})\rangle-i\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,\hat{U}_{0}(t_{f},t)\hat{V}(t)|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\hat{U}_{0}(t_{f},t)\hat{V}(t)\hat{U}_{0}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{V}(t^{\prime})|\psi_{0}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle+O(\alpha^{3}),

where the evolution operator
\displaystyle\hat{U}_{0}
is solution of
\displaystyle i\dot{\hat{U}}_{0}=\hat{H}_{0}\hat{U}_{0}
. Introducing the target state,
\displaystyle|\psi_{f}\rangle
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\psi_{f}|\psi(t_{f})\rangle=1-i\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,\langle\psi_{0}(t)|\hat{V}(t)|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\langle\psi_{0}(t)|\hat{V}(t)\hat{U}_{0}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{V}(t^{\prime})|\psi_{0}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle+O(\alpha^{3}),

where we use
\displaystyle|\psi_{f}\rangle=|\psi_{0}(t_{f})\rangle
. We define the real function
\displaystyle e(t)
as
\displaystyle e(t)=\langle\psi_{0}(t)|\hat{V}(t)|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle
. The propagator
\displaystyle\hat{U}_{0}
can be expressed as

\hat{U}_{0}(t,t^{\prime})=|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{0}(t^{\prime})|+|\psi_{\perp}(t)\rangle\langle\psi_{\perp}(t^{\prime})|,

where
\displaystyle|\psi_{\perp}(t)\rangle
is a state orthogonal to
\displaystyle|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle
,
\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}(t)|\psi_{\perp}(t)\rangle=0
. We obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\psi_{f}|\psi(t_{f})\rangle=1-i\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,e(t)-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,e(t)\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,\bar{f}(t)\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,f(t^{\prime})+O(\alpha^{3}),

with
\displaystyle f(t)=\langle\psi_{\perp}(t)|\hat{V}(t)|\psi_{0}(t)\rangle
. The latter equation can be simplified into

\displaystyle\displaystyle\langle\psi_{f}|\psi(t_{f})\rangle=1-i\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,e(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,e(t)\right)^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,f(t)\right|^{2}+O(\alpha^{3}).

For a state-to-state robust population transfer, the fidelity
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}
of the control is defined as
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}=|\langle\psi_{f}|\psi(t_{f})\rangle|^{2}
. At order 2 in
\displaystyle\alpha
, the fidelity can be written as
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{s}=1-\alpha^{2}\left|\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,f(t)\right|^{2}
where we omit the term
\displaystyle O(\alpha^{3})
. We deduce that the control is robust at order 2 in
\displaystyle\alpha
if
\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,f(t)\right|=0
i.e. if

\Re\left[\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,f(t)\right]=\Im\left[\int_{0}^{t_{f}}dt\,f(t)\right]=0.

For a qubit gate in
\displaystyle SU(2)
, the fidelity
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{g}
is given by
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{g}=\Re[\langle\psi_{f}|\psi(t_{f})\rangle]
where the Cayley-Klein parameters are used to represent the evolution operator as a ket
[
50
]
. We deduce that the implementation of the gate is robust if the conditions
\displaystyle E(t_{f})=F(t_{f})=0
are both satisfied with
\displaystyle E(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e(t^{\prime})
and
\displaystyle F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,f(t^{\prime})
. Only the second relation
\displaystyle F(t_{f})=0
is needed for state-to-state transfers.

3
Application of the PMP

An optimal robust control is a control that is both robust under the conditions given in Sec.
2
and optimal for a given cost functional. In this paper, we study a time-optimal protocol for which the goal is to reach the target state in minimum time. The optimal control problem is formulated from the PMP. For details on the application of the PMP, we refer the interested reader to recent review papers
[
11
,
18
]
.

The PMP is applied in an extended space corresponding to the vectors of coordinates
\displaystyle(|\psi_{0}\rangle,E,F)
in order to account for robustness constraints. Starting from the state
\displaystyle(|\psi_{0}(0)\rangle,0,0)
, the goal is therefore to bring the system to
\displaystyle(|\psi_{f}\rangle,0,0)
in minimum time. We denote by
\displaystyle(|\chi\rangle,p_{e},p_{f})
the corresponding adjoint state with
\displaystyle p_{e}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle p_{f}=p_{1}+ip_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
. Note that
\displaystyle p_{e}=0
for a state-to-state transfer. The PMP is then formulated from the pseudo-Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H_{P}
as

H_{P}=\Re\left(\langle\chi|\dot{\psi}_{0}\rangle+p_{e}\dot{E}+p_{f}\dot{F}\right)-1,

where only the normal extremals are considered
[
11
]
. The final control time being free, we have
\displaystyle H_{P}=0
. The Pontryagin Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H_{P}
can be transformed into

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Im[\langle\chi|\hat{H}_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle]

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\frac{\Omega}{2}\big(p_{e}\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{\sigma}_{x}|\psi_{0}\rangle+\Re[p_{f}\langle\psi_{\perp}|\hat{\sigma}_{x}|\psi_{0}\rangle]\big)-1.

The PMP states that the coordinates of the extremal state and of the corresponding adjoint state fulfill the Hamiltonian’s equations associated to the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H_{P}
. We have

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\dot{\psi}_{0}\rangle=2\partial_{\langle\chi|}H_{P},~\langle\dot{\chi}|=-2\partial_{|\psi_{0}\rangle}H_{P},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{E}=\partial_{p_{e}}H_{P},~\dot{p}_{e}=-\partial_{E}H_{P},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{F}=2\partial_{p_{f}}H_{P},~\dot{p}_{f}=-2\partial_{F}H_{P},

where the factor 2 is added for complex coordinates
[
11
]
. For the state components, we deduce as expected that
\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle
is solution of the Schrödinger equation and that
\displaystyle\dot{E}=e
and
\displaystyle\dot{F}=f
. For the dynamics of the adjoint state
\displaystyle|\chi\rangle
, we show in the Appendix
A
that

\langle\dot{\chi}|=i\langle\chi|\hat{H}_{0}-\Omega\,p_{e}\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{\sigma}_{x}-\Omega\,p_{f}\langle\psi_{\perp}|\hat{\sigma}_{x}.

(3)

Finally, since
\displaystyle H_{P}
does not depend explicitly on
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle F
, we obtain that
\displaystyle p_{e}
and
\displaystyle p_{f}
are constants of motion.

The optimal control problem can be solved by using the PMP formalism. In our case, the goal is to find the initial adjoint state
\displaystyle(|\chi(0)\rangle,p_{e},p_{f})

such that the system described at time
\displaystyle t
by
\displaystyle(|\psi(t)\rangle,E(t),F(t))
reaches the target state
\displaystyle(|\psi_{f}\rangle,0,0)
in minimum time. As such, the optimal solution cannot be integrated analytically. However, the complexity of the dynamics can be drastically reduced by considering the vectors
\displaystyle\vec{I}=(I_{x},I_{y},I_{z})
and
\displaystyle\vec{R}=(R_{x},R_{y},R_{z})
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\displaystyle\vec{I}

\displaystyle=\Im\langle\chi|\hat{\vec{\sigma}}|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(4)

\displaystyle\vec{R}

\displaystyle=p_{e}\langle\psi_{0}|\hat{\vec{\sigma}}|\psi_{0}\rangle+\Re\left(p_{f}\langle\psi_{\perp}|\hat{\vec{\sigma}}|\psi_{0}\rangle\right).

In this reduced space, the dynamics are given by (see Appendix
A
for details)

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{I}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta&0\\
-\Delta&0&-\Omega\\
0&\Omega&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{I}+\Omega\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{R},

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{R}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta&0\\
-\Delta&0&-\Omega\\
0&\Omega&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{R}.

For a given set
\displaystyle(p_{e},p_{f})
, we observe that
\displaystyle R^{2}=\vec{R}\cdot\vec{R}
is a constant of motion. We can then use the projection of the system onto the
\displaystyle\vec{R}
-space, with a state moving on the sphere of radius
\displaystyle R
. The Pontryagin Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

H_{P}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\Delta I_{z}+\Omega I_{x}+\Omega R_{x}\right)-1.

(6)

Furthermore, the second-order robustness for both state-to-state transfer and quantum gate leads to a Necessary Condition to Robustness (NCR) in terms of
\displaystyle\vec{R}
given by

\mathcal{R}(t_{f})=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}R_{x}(t)\,dt=0.

(7)

It is only a necessary condition since
\displaystyle E(t_{f})=F(t_{f})=0
and
\displaystyle\Omega
constant leads to
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t_{f})=0
, but the reverse is not true.

We show in the following that the PMP can be solved exactly in the
\displaystyle\vec{R}
-space. The trajectories in this space are only a projection of the original optimal dynamics. Necessary conditions are thus obtained. In a second step, we check that some candidates for optimality given by the PMP with the
\displaystyle\vec{R}
-coordinates are also solutions of the control problem in the initial space.

4
Regular and singular trajectories

The PMP states that in the optimal case
[
18
,
11
]
, the problem can be restricted to the study of the Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H
given by

H=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\Delta(t)|\leq\Delta_{0}\\
|\Omega(t)|\leq\Omega_{0}\end{subarray}}H_{P}.

Here we are faced with a non-trivial optimal control problem in which the admissible control space is a rectangle
[
58
]
. We are interested in determining the times when controls are equal to their bounds
\displaystyle\pm\Delta_{0}
and
\displaystyle\pm\Omega_{0}
and when they may have values in the open intervals
\displaystyle]-\Delta_{0},\Delta_{0}[
and
\displaystyle]-\Omega_{0},\Omega_{0}[
. Such controls correspond respectively to regular and singular solutions. More precisely, the maximization condition can be solved by introducing two switching functions
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}
and
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Omega}
defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi_{\Delta}=I_{z},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Phi_{\Omega}=I_{x}+R_{x},

and corresponding to the Hamiltonian terms in front of
\displaystyle\Delta
and
\displaystyle\Omega
in Eq. (
6
). We deduce that

\displaystyle\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}\mathrm{sgn}[\Phi_{\Delta}]
and
\displaystyle\Omega(t)=\Omega_{0}\mathrm{sgn}[\Phi_{\Omega}]
when the corresponding switching function is different from zero. The associated control is of constant amplitude and is called a bang control. When the switching function vanishes at an isolated time
\displaystyle t_{0}
, the control changes sign and we get a bang-bang control sequence. The singular solution occurs when the switching function is zero on a time interval
\displaystyle[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Note that
\displaystyle\Delta
and
\displaystyle\Omega
may be independently regular or singular. However, they cannot be simultaneously singular since in this case
\displaystyle H_{P}=-1
, which is in contradiction with the fact that
\displaystyle H_{P}=0
when the final time is free.

We now derive the singular controls denoted
\displaystyle\Delta_{s}
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{s}
by exploiting the fact that the switching functions and their time derivatives are equal to zero. We have

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\Omega(I_{y}+R_{y}),

\displaystyle\ddot{\Phi}_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=-\Delta\Omega(I_{x}+R_{x})-\Omega^{2}(I_{z}-2R_{z}),

and

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\Delta(I_{y}+R_{y}),

\displaystyle\ddot{\Phi}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\Delta^{2}(I_{x}+R_{x})-\Delta\Omega(I_{z}+2R_{z}),

where we assume that the other control is a constant because the singular-singular case is not optimal. For
\displaystyle\Delta_{s}
, starting from
\displaystyle\ddot{\Phi}_{\Delta}=0=-\Delta\Omega(I_{x}+R_{x})-2\Omega^{2}R_{z}
, we deduce from
\displaystyle H_{P}=0=\frac{\Omega}{2}(I_{x}+R_{x})-1
that

\Delta_{s}=-{\Omega_{0}}^{2}R_{z}.

(8)

In the case where
\displaystyle\Omega
is singular, we obtain from
\displaystyle\ddot{\Phi}_{\Omega}=0=H_{P}
that the only solution is
\displaystyle I_{z}=-\tfrac{2}{\Delta_{0}}
and
\displaystyle R_{z}=\tfrac{1}{\Delta_{0}}
. This leads to
\displaystyle\Omega_{s}=0
. This control is not relevant in our study where the goal is to transfer the state to a target from the ground state of the system.

From the analysis of the Pontryagin Hamiltonian, we conclude that the optimal trajectory is the concatenation of bangs and singular arcs for
\displaystyle\Delta
and bangs for
\displaystyle\Omega
. Such trajectories are respectively called regular and singular in the rest of the paper.

5
Integration of the optimal trajectories

In this section, we study the optimal dynamics in the
\displaystyle\vec{R}
-space. We show that the trajectories can be described by cosine and Jacobi functions in the regular and singular cases, respectively. The NCR can be expressed as a sum of elliptic integrals for the singular case, and of cosine functions for the regular case.

5.1
The singular case

We consider the singular situation with
\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{s}
and
\displaystyle\Omega=\Omega_{0}
. The dynamics of
\displaystyle\vec{R}
can be written as

\dot{\vec{R}}=\Omega_{0}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Omega_{0}R_{z}&0\\
\Omega_{0}R_{z}&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{R}.

(9)

We consider the change of coordinates
\displaystyle\tilde{\vec{R}}=\Omega_{0}\vec{R}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{t}=\Omega_{0}t
to obtain the following system of differential equations

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{x}

\displaystyle=-R_{z}R_{y},

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{y}

\displaystyle=R_{z}R_{x}-R_{z},

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{z}

\displaystyle=R_{y},

where we omit the tilde for simplicity. We denote by
\displaystyle\vec{R}^{(0)}
the initial point of the dynamics. A careful inspection of Eq. (
10
) shows that it has two constants of motion,
\displaystyle E_{s}
and
\displaystyle R
which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle R^{2}={R_{x}}^{2}+{R_{y}}^{2}+{R_{z}}^{2},

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{s}=R_{x}+\frac{{R_{z}}^{2}}{2}.

The values of such constants are fixed by the initial conditions on
\displaystyle\vec{R}
.
Starting from Eq. (
10
), we obtain that
\displaystyle R_{z}
is solution of a second order non-linear differential equation as

\ddot{R}_{z}+\left(1-E_{s}\right)R_{z}+\frac{1}{2}{R_{z}}^{3}=0.

(11)

This can further be written has an effective Hamiltonian
\displaystyle E_{z}
of a one-dimensional system as

E_{z}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{R}_{z}}^{~2}+\frac{1-E_{s}}{2}{R_{z}}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}{R_{z}}^{4},

(12)

which leads to

{\dot{R}_{z}}^{~2}=-\frac{1}{4}\left[{R_{z}}^{4}+4\left(1-E_{s}\right){R_{z}}^{2}-8E_{z}\right].

(13)

Note that
\displaystyle E_{z}
can be expressed in terms of the initial conditions
\displaystyle\vec{R}^{(0)}
. Since
\displaystyle E_{z}
may be both positive or negative, the roots of the polynomial
\displaystyle P
of order 2,
\displaystyle P(x)=x^{2}+4(1-E_{s})x-8E_{z}
can have the same or opposite signs (as its discriminant is always positive). In the numerical examples of Sec.
6
and
7
, only the case
\displaystyle E_{z}>0
(i.e. roots of opposite signs) allows to reach the target state and are thus relevant for our control problem. The solutions for which
\displaystyle E_{z}<0
are discussed in Appendix
B
. We introduce the positive and negative roots of
\displaystyle P
as
\displaystyle r^{2}
and
\displaystyle-s^{2}
, with
\displaystyle r,s\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle r^{2}

\displaystyle=-2(1-E_{s})+2\sqrt{(1-E_{s})^{2}+2E_{z}},

(14)

\displaystyle s^{2}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}2(1-E_{s})+2\sqrt{(1-E_{s})^{2}+2E_{z}}.

Note that
\displaystyle(1-E_{s})^{2}+2E_{z}=(1-R_{x})^{2}+{R_{y}}^{2}
. Introducing
\displaystyle\varepsilon=\mathrm{sgn}(\dot{R}_{z})=\mathrm{sgn}(R_{y})
, we obtain

\int_{R_{z}^{(0)}}^{R_{z}(t)}\frac{du}{\sqrt{\left(r^{2}-u^{2}\right)\left(s^{2}+u^{2}\right)}}=\varepsilon\frac{t}{2},

and

R_{z}(t)=\frac{rs}{\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}}}\mathrm{sd}\left(u=\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}}}{2}t+u_{0},m\right),

(15)

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{sd}=\mathrm{sn}/\mathrm{dn}
is a Jacobi elliptic function, and

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sd}^{-1}\left[\frac{\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}}}{rs}R_{z}^{(0)},m\right],

(16)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{r^{2}+s^{2}}.

We introduce the parameter
\displaystyle A=\tfrac{\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}}}{2}
connecting the coordinates
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle t
. Note that the parameter
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is absorbed in a new definition of the sign of the product
\displaystyle rs
.
Using the relations
\displaystyle R_{y}=\dot{R}_{z}
and
\displaystyle R_{x}=E_{s}-{R_{z}}^{2}/2
, we obtain for
\displaystyle\vec{R}

[
59
]

\displaystyle R_{x}

\displaystyle=E_{s}+\frac{s^{2}}{2}-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mathrm{nd}^{2}(u,m),

(17)

\displaystyle R_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{rs}{2}\frac{\mathrm{cn}(u,m)}{\mathrm{dn}^{2}(u,m)},

\displaystyle R_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{rs}{\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}}}\mathrm{sd}(u,m),

and for the NCR at time
\displaystyle t

5.2
The regular case

The same derivation can be made in the regular case for which
\displaystyle\Delta=\Delta_{0}
and
\displaystyle\Omega=\Omega_{0}
. The dynamics of
\displaystyle\vec{R}
are given by

\dot{\vec{R}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Delta_{0}&0\\
-\Delta_{0}&0&-\Omega_{0}\\
0&\Omega_{0}&0\end{pmatrix}\vec{R}\equiv M_{0}\vec{R}.

(20)

It can be shown that
\displaystyle R
and
\displaystyle E_{r}=\Delta_{0}R_{z}-\Omega_{0}R_{x}
are constants of motion. The propagator
\displaystyle e^{M_{0}t}
can be expressed as

e^{M_{0}t}=\mathds{1}+\left[1-\cos\left(\omega t\right)\right]\frac{{M_{0}}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}+\sin\left(\omega t\right)\frac{M_{0}}{\omega},

where
\displaystyle\mathds{1}
is the identity operator, and
\displaystyle\omega=\sqrt{{\Delta_{0}}^{2}+{\Omega_{0}}^{2}}
. We deduce that the dynamic is
\displaystyle 2\pi/\omega
-periodic. This gives
\displaystyle\vec{R}(t)=e^{M_{0}t}\vec{R}(0)

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(t)=R_{x}^{(0)}t+\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\omega^{3}}\left[\sin(\omega t)-\omega t\right]

(21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\times\left[\Delta_{0}R_{x}^{(0)}+\Omega_{0}R_{z}^{(0)}\right]+\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\omega^{2}}\left[\cos(\omega t)-1\right]R_{y}^{(0)}

for the NCR.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we apply the PMP to find robust time-optimal control protocols in a two-level quantum system. We consider both state-to-state transfers and the implementation of quantum gates. Introducing a reduced dynamical space, we show how the optimal equations given by the PMP can be integrated analytically. This derivation provides candidates to optimality for the control problem. The optimal solution is finally found by a numerical integration of the Schrödinger equation describing the system dynamics. In this paper, we focus on a specific class of robustness at order 2, that is the one with respect to Rabi frequency, but this approach can be generalized to other types of robustness and to higher orders following, for example, recent works in this direction
[
50
,
51
]
. In such cases, it is not clear that the optimal solutions can be found analytically, but the reduced space method is expected to drastically simplify the numerical derivation of the control processes.

In the appendices
D
and
E
, we have also shown that two recent geometric optimization approaches used to solve this robustness problem are in fact specific formulations of the PMP and thus equivalent to this work. Such methods are interesting for highlighting the geometric nature of the control problem. However, they suffer from some limitations. The RIO approach only considers singular extremals and assumes additional smoothness conditions on the control. The use of angular coordinates in RIO can also lead to singularities that can be difficult to handle. The geometric optimization process proposed in
[
53
,
54
]
takes into account regular and singular extremals, but in a simplified situation that may be difficult to generalize.

Finally, we hope that our method can be used to solve more complex systems such as those with two or more coupled qubits. Similar approaches have been used, for example, in
[
60
]
to generate gates in Rydberg atoms.
