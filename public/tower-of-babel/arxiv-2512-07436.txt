Abstract
. Recent advances in large reasoning models ( LRM s) have enabled agentic search systems to perform complex multi-step reasoning across multiple sources. However, most studies focus on general information retrieval and rarely explores vertical domains with unique challenges. In this work, we focus on local life services and introduce LocalSearchBench , which encompass diverse and complex business scenarios. Real-world queries in this domain are often ambiguous and require multi-hop reasoning across merchants and products, remaining challenging and not fully addressed. As the first comprehensive benchmark for agentic search in local life services, LocalSearchBench includes over 150,000 high-quality entries from various cities and business types. We construct 300 multi-hop QA tasks based on real user queries, challenging agents to understand questions and retrieve information in multiple steps. We also developed LocalPlayground , a unified environment integrating multiple tools for agent interaction. Experiments show that even state-of-the-art LRM s struggle on LocalSearchBench : the best model (DeepSeek-V3.1) achieves only 34.34% correctness, and most models have issues with completeness (average 77.33%) and faithfulness (average 61.99%). This highlights the need for specialized benchmarks and domain-specific agent training in local life services. Code, Benchmark, and Leaderboard are available at localsearchbench.github.io

In this paper, we propose

Data Augmentation and Synthesis

Data Augmentation

The original merchant seed data contains only 12 basic fields with many missing or unstandardized attributes, which is inadequate for

The enhanced dataset covers: (1) basic information with standardized categories and descriptions; (2) location intelligence including structured geographical data, coordinates, and landmarks; (3) business operations such as operating hours and pricing; (4) service characteristics like facility lists; (5) contact information, including phone numbers and emails; and (6) promotional content, such as product descriptions and discount offers.

Privacy Rewriting

To comply with data protection regulations and enable safe public release, we employ a
Privacy Rewriting Agent
on the augmented data (see Figure

The agent rewrites 6 privacy-related fields to anonymize merchant identity, location, and contact details while keeping the remaining business information usable, as illustrated in Figure

3.1.3.
Data Validation

A

Detailed instructions have been carefully designed for augmented data validation and privacy rewriting validation, as illustrated in Figure

Augmented Data Validation

It evaluates augmented Data quality against original seed data across six core dimensions: (1) Data Completeness (20% weight) assessing field coverage and information richness; (2) Logical Consistency (30% weight) validating business logic coherence and field correlations including name format compliance, category alignment, and pricing rationality; (3) Geographic Accuracy (10% weight) verifying location information correctness and address format consistency; (4) Content Quality (20% weight) evaluating textual descriptions, language expression, and business relevance; (5) Format Standardization (10% weight) ensuring data format compliance and structural consistency; and (6) Innovation Degree (10% weight) measuring content creativity and merchant name diversity through batch-level uniqueness analysis. 3.2.
LocalRAG
Construction

To enable efficient and accurate merchant information retrieval for
LRM
s high-performance tool calling, we further construct a comprehensive Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system, which we term
LocalRAG
, specifically designed as a tool interface for
LRM
s. Built upon our previously generated merchant dataset,
LocalRAG
consists of three core components:
vector embedding generation
,
similarity retrieval
, and
result reranking
, as shown in Figure
6
.

3.2.1.
Vector Embedding Generation

LocalRAG
employs a state-of-the-art embedding model to convert merchant information into high-dimensional vector representations. The process begins by transforming structured merchant data into coherent natural language descriptions. Each merchant record undergoes a text formatting pipeline that systematically concatenates key fields including name, category, address, and service descriptions into readable text. The complete merchant profile is then directly encoded and stored as indexed vector chunks in the vector database, ensuring the embeddings capture both explicit attributes and semantic relationships for nuanced similarity matching.

3.2.2.
Similarity Retrieval

The generated embeddings are organized in an indexed vector database with cosine similarity-based search capabilities for fast approximate nearest neighbor retrieval, implementing dense retrieval methodology. During query processing, user queries are embedded using the same model and matched against the indexed merchant vector chunks to retrieve the top-
N
semantically similar merchant candidates.

3.2.3.
Result Reranking

To improve retrieval accuracy,
LocalRAG
first retrieves the top-
N
merchants from the vector database as a larger candidate set, then applies a specialized reranking model to perform fine-grained relevance scoring between the query and these candidates. The reranking model computes precise relevance scores for each candidate merchant, ultimately selecting the top-
K
most relevant merchants based on these refined scores to generate the final answer for
LRM
s.

3.3.2.
Question Instantiation

We instantiate the collected questions with city-specific information through a two-phase process. First, we construct city profiles for each of the 3 cities (Section
3.1.1
), collecting structured information from public resources including landmarks, cultural events, local cuisine, price bands, etc. The collected city data is preprocessed using automated scripts to remove duplicates, normalize formats, and correct inconsistencies, then validated by native annotators to ensure factual accuracy and cultural authenticity.

Second, annotators from platform M manually instantiate questions into concrete multi-hop queries. For each city, approximately 100 questions are selected from the collected 1,200 questions and grounded with city-specific information. To transform single-hop questions into multi-hop queries, annotators enrich questions with 2–4 reasoning segments (2 for 3-hop, 3 for 4-hop, 4 for 5-hop) based on business relevance and logical coherence. These segments include: cross-merchant comparison, schedule planning, chained events, and constraint resolution. We maintain target intelligence-level and hop-count distributions as shown in Figures

3.3.3.
Answer Collection

For each instantiated question, we generate its reference answer through a systematic two-step process, leveraging our pre-constructed
LocalRAG
described in Section
3.2
along with LLM and human expert validation to ensure comprehensive and accurate ground truth answers.

Step
1:
Merchant Retrieval
. For each question,
LocalRAG
retrieves relevant local merchant from our comprehensive business database.
LocalRAG
maintains an indexed vector database of merchant information, where each merchant record is encoded as high-dimensional vector representations capturing merchant names, categories, addresses, and service descriptions along with their semantic relationships. Using semantic similarity matching combined with geographical proximity analysis, the system retrieves relevant business listings, location metadata, and service information that directly relate to the question context. For complex multi-step questions,
LocalRAG
performs multiple retrieval operations to gather comprehensive merchant information across different categories and locations, providing the necessary contextual foundation for subsequent reasoning steps.

Step
2:
Initial Answer Generation
. With the retrieved merchant context from
LocalRAG
, we utilize state-of-the-art LLMs (GPT-5
(
26
)
and Claude-4.1-opus
(
1
)
), assisted by domain experts using collaborative tools, to generate preliminary answers. This process is guided by prompts that emphasize factual accuracy, completeness, and strict adherence to the provided merchant information. The prompts explicitly direct the models to cite specific merchant details and avoid introducing information not present in the retrieved context, thus minimizing hallucination. The resulting question-answer pairs serve as input for the subsequent validation and refinement stage.

3.3.4.
Data Validation

To ensure the highest quality and reliability of our benchmark, all preliminary QA pairs undergo a rigorous data validation process beyond the initial generation and refinement described in Section
3.3.3
. This section outlines the final quality assurance process designed to meet benchmark-level standards, as illustrated in Figure
7
. This multi-phase procedure is conducted by experienced technical specialists with deep domain expertise in local life services and involves both answer revision and quality filtering.

Step
1:
Expert Answer Revision.

Each generated answer is manually reviewed by our expert team consisting of three paper authors working independently. With the assistance of AI-powered development tools, each reviewer independently verifies the factual accuracy of every claim, assesses the completeness of the explanation, and refines the language for clarity and precision. This human-in-the-loop approach allows for nuanced corrections that automated systems might miss, ensuring each answer is not only correct but also comprehensible and contextually appropriate.

Step
2:
Quality Filtering
.
After revision, the QA pairs are subjected to a final filtering step. QA pairs are retained only if they meet all of the following quality standards: (1) questions are clear, well-formulated, and unambiguous; (2) answers are factually correct and complete after revision; and (3) answers can be sufficiently grounded in real-world local service scenarios and business logic. We also enforce balanced distribution across the service categories and geographic regions defined in Section
3.1.1
. This stringent filtering ensures that only the most clear, correct, and valuable QA pairs are included in the final benchmark.

4.
LocalPlayground

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed benchmark, we design
LocalPlayground
, an agent evaluation framework that employs autonomous agents to assess LLM performance, as shown in Figure
10
. The framework consists of two key components that work collaboratively: a Search Agent that retrieves and synthesizes information, and a Validation Agent that evaluates response quality against ground truth.

Search Agent

The Search Agent retrieves relevant information using two search tools:
LocalRAG
and Web Search.

LocalRAG
performs semantic similarity matching and geographical proximity analysis on vector-indexed merchant databases to retrieve business listings, points of interest and service descriptions.

Web Search queries real-time contextual information such as temporal facts, weather conditions, general knowledge, news and events and supplementary product details that may affect merchant operations and user decisions through web requests to search APIs, which interface with search engines (specifically Baidu Search
(
23
)
in our experiments) to retrieve up-to-date web content.

The Search Agent operates iteratively over
N
rounds. In each round, it invokes
LocalRAG
at most once and Web Search at most once, allowing the agent to progressively refine its information gathering strategy based on intermediate results.

Validation Agent

To ensure comprehensive and objective assessment of generated outputs, we employ a
Validation Agent
implemented as an LLM-as-a-Judge framework, as shown in Figure
10
.
Given the generated outputs and the golden answer, an LLM (specifically Claude-Sonnet-4
(
2
)
in our
experiments) evaluates answer quality across seven dimensions: (1)
Correctness
: measures whether the answer is factually accurate; (2)
Completeness
: measures whether the answer fully addresses the question, (3)
Fluency
: measures the readability and coherence of the response; (4)
Faithfulness
: measures the degree to which the response avoids fabricated or hallucinated content; (5)
Safety
: measures whether the response avoids harmful or inappropriate content; (6)
Avg. tool calls
: measures the average number of tool invocations (
LocalRAG
and web search) and (7)
Avg. rounds
: measures the average number of conversation rounds.

Dimension (1) employs a binary scoring scheme (0 or 1), clearly distinguishing
correct from incorrect responses. For dimensions (2)-(5), each dimension is evaluated using a granular 10-point rating system (0-10), with higher scores indicating superior performance. Scores across dimensions (1)-(5) are normalized and expressed as percentages for standardized comparison. For dimensions Dimensions (6)-(7) capture continuous variables representing
frequency counts. To ensure reliability, each instance is evaluated five times per dimension,
with final scores computed by averaging across trials. Candidate systems are anonymized, question order is randomized, and the judge model is separate from all evaluated models to prevent self-assessment.

Benchmark Results (RQ2)

Impact of web search.

We first analyze the impact of web search, comparing
LocalPlayground
(
LRM
+
LocalRAG
+web search) against

LRM

LRM

LocalRAG
from our comparison, as multi-hop questions in our benchmark require information exclusively available in the
LocalRAG
knowledge base.

As shown in Table
5
, incorporating web search into
LocalPlayground
presents a trade-off between answer quality and faithfulness. On average,
LocalPlayground
improves Correctness by 4.37pp (from 25.58% to 29.95%) and Completeness by 3.95pp (from 73.38% to 77.33%), but reduces Faithfulness by 3.64pp (from 65.63% to 61.99%). This trade-off is most pronounced for DeepSeek-V3.1, which gains 4.57pp in Correctness but loses 8.45pp in Faithfulness. These results suggest that while web search is essential for retrieving critical information in multi-hop reasoning, it also introduces noise that compromises response faithfulness.

Performance across language models.

The choice of underlying
LRM
significantly influences performance across all metrics. Among all models,
DeepSeek-V3.1
achieves the highest Correctness (34.34%), followed by GLM-4.5 (33.78%) and LongChat-Large-32K (33.19%).
Qwen-Plus-Latest
leads in Completeness (80.94%), while
Gemini-2.5-Pro
excels in Faithfulness (78.26%). Closed-source models outperform open-source ones in completeness (79.08% vs. 75.57%) and efficiency (2.25 vs. 2.61 tool calls), though differences in Correctness are more modest. Despite these variations, overall performance remains limited, as even the best model achieves only 34.34% Correctness, underscoring the substantial challenge of multi-hop local search.

5.4.
Sensitivity Study (RQ3)

To investigate the impact of maximum conversation rounds on tool usage and model performance, we conduct a sensitivity study using DeepSeek-V3.1, the best-performing model in correctness from RQ2, with varying max conversation round
N
from 2 to 8.

As shown in Table
6
, the optimal configuration is
N=5
, which achieves the highest Correctness (34.34%). When
N<5
, insufficient rounds limit information gathering, resulting in lower correctness (30.87%–32.11%). When
N>5
, despite increased tool usage, correctness begins to decline (31.32%–32.77%), suggesting that excessive rounds introduce noisy information that hinders answer quality rather than improving it. In terms of computational cost, increasing the maximum rounds initially leads to more tool calls and longer conversations, but the growth saturates beyond
N=5
. The average tool calls plateau around 3.68–3.81 and average rounds stabilize near 4.29–4.48. Therefore,
N=5
represents a critical turning point that balances performance and efficiency (3.43 tool calls, 4.02 rounds).

Regarding other metrics, Fluency remains stable across all settings (71.44%–72.39%), while faithfulness gradually decreases from 64.78% (
N=2
) to 60.80% (
N=5
), suggesting that longer conversations introduce more opportunities for hallucinations. Safety scores stay consistently high (80.39%–82.39%), indicating robustness across different round limits.

6.
Conclusion

We propose
LocalSearchBench
, a new benchmark for
agentic search
in local life services, designed to evaluate multi-hop reasoning in real-world business scenarios. The dataset includes 150,031 samples and 300 multi-hop QA tasks, curated from over 260,000 merchant records in 3 Chinese cities and 6 service categories through data augmentation, privacy rewriting, and rigorous validation. Using the
LocalPlayground
toolkit for search and seven-dimensional validation, our experiments on ten leading LLMs show that current models still struggle. For example, DeepSeek-V3.1 achieves only 34.34% correctness. By open-sourcing
LocalSearchBench
and
LocalPlayground
, we aim to foster progress in domain-specific
agentic search
and encourage future improvements in data coverage and model training.
