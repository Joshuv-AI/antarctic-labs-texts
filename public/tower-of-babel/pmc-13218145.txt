Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common chronic respiratory disease characterised by persistent airflow limitation and progressive decline in lung function. It is associated with high incidence, substantial mortality, a prolonged disease course, difficulty of cure and frequent exacerbations, posing a serious threat to patients’ lives and placing a heavy burden on families and healthcare systems.1 The number of patients with COPD worldwide has reached nearly 100 million. Among adults over 40 years of age in China, the prevalence of COPD is as high as 13.6% and continues to increase.2 It is predicted that by 2030, COPD will become the third leading cause of death globally.3 These figures highlight the urgent need to strengthen comprehensive prevention, treatment and long-term management of COPD.

Increasing evidence suggests that effective COPD control depends not only on pharmacological treatment but also on patients’ capacity to engage in sustained self-management. Educational interventions that enhance self-care agency and rational drug use have been shown to improve patients’ ability to participate in disease control,4 while broader self-management programmes can reduce symptom burden, decrease hospitalisation frequency and improve quality of life.5 Such self-management requires close cooperation between patients, families, communities and medical professionals to control disease symptoms, cope with physical and psychological impacts and adjust lifestyle to meet the challenges of long-term chronic illness.6 However, qualitative and quantitative studies indicate that, in practice, patients often encounter substantial barriers to self-management, including limited disease knowledge, insufficient social support, negative emotions such as anxiety and depression and various technical and resource constraints.6 7

In recent years, qualitative research exploring the self-management experiences and needs of patients with COPD has gradually increased, providing rich insights into how individuals perceive and manage their condition in different cultural and healthcare contexts.6 7 Nevertheless, single qualitative studies usually focus on specific settings or subgroups and therefore cannot comprehensively and deeply reflect broader patterns in patients’ inner feelings, challenges and support needs. To address this gap, the present study conducts a qualitative systematic review and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) meta-aggregation of existing qualitative research on COPD self-management. Our aim is to systematically synthesise patients’ real experiences, problems and needs in the self-management process, thereby generating evidence to inform the design and implementation of more precise and effective self-management interventions and to support patients in actively adapting to their condition and improving their quality of life.

Methods

Design

This qualitative systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42024607051). The conduct and reporting of the review followed the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement.8 The review was conducted using the JBI meta-aggregation approach for qualitative evidence synthesis. The completed ENTREQ checklist for this review is provided in (online supplemental file S1)

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of the literature

This study adheres to the PICoS principle for inclusion criteria. The following are the listed inclusion criteria: (1) Participants: patients with a clear diagnosis of COPD, aged ≥18 years; (2) Phenomena of interest: the real feelings, experiences and needs of patients with COPD during the self-management process; (3) Context: the process of self-management and rehabilitation of patients in medical institutions, communities or at home; and (4) Study type: qualitative research, including ethnography, phenomenology, grounded theory and other qualitative designs.

For mixed-population studies (eg, studies including patients with COPD and other chronic respiratory diseases), we included the study only if data for patients with COPD could be clearly distinguished and extracted separately; otherwise such studies were excluded. For mixed-methods studies, we included articles when the qualitative component focusing on COPD self-management could be identified and analysed independently.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) literature that could not be obtained in full text or had incomplete data, despite attempts to obtain the full text through institutional subscriptions, interlibrary loan and, where appropriate, by contacting the authors; (2) repetitively published literature (in which case the most complete or recent report was retained); and (3) studies not published in Chinese or English.

Literature search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in ten electronic databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Database and CBM, from database inception to March 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) or equivalent controlled vocabulary with free-text terms related to “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”, “self-management”, “self-care”, “experience”, “perception”, “needs” and “qualitative research”, using Boolean operators and truncation as appropriate for each database.

The search strategies were adapted to the syntax and indexing of each database (eg, using MeSH terms in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase), and, where applicable, filters for humans and adults (≥18 years) were applied. No restrictions were placed on publication year, but only studies published in Chinese or English were considered. An example of the search strategy for PubMed is shown in online supplemental S1 figure. The full, precise search strategies for all databases, including field tags, operators, limits and filters, are provided in online supplemental file S2.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers (both having received training in evidence-based nursing) independently screened the literature and conducted cross-checks. After removing duplicate records using EndNote V.21 software, they first read the titles and abstracts to exclude obviously irrelevant studies, then assessed the full texts to determine final eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Data were extracted using a standardised form. The extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, study design, data collection methods, setting and participant characteristics), the phenomena of interest (experiences and needs related to COPD self-management) and qualitative findings relevant to self-management (participants’ quotations and authors’ interpretive statements). These qualitative findings formed the units of analysis for the subsequent meta-aggregation.

Evaluation of methodological quality of literature

Two researchers independently evaluated the quality of the literature based on the 2016 version of the JBI Quality Evaluation Criteria for Qualitative Research in Evidence-Based Health Care. Each of the 10 evaluation items was evaluated with ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’ or ‘Not Applicable’. The quality of the literature was classified into three levels: A level for complete compliance, B level for partial compliance and C level for complete non-compliance. In case of disagreement in the evaluation results, the third researcher participated in the discussion and the literature with quality grades of A and B was finally included.

Literature analysis method

We synthesised the qualitative evidence using the JBI meta-aggregation approach.9 10 In this approach, each extracted finding (participant quotation and/or author interpretation relevant to COPD self-management) was treated as a unit of meaning and assigned a level of credibility. Following the guidance of Lockwood et al10, conceptually similar findings were grouped into categories and these categories were then further aggregated into overarching synthesised findings that captured core meanings across the studies while remaining close to the original data. Two reviewers independently coded the extracted findings and developed the initial categories. A third reviewer then reviewed and refined the coding framework and the grouping of categories into synthesised findings. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. This iterative process enhanced the transparency and rigour of the qualitative synthesis.

Results

Literature search results

The study selection process is summarised in the flow diagram shown in online supplemental S2 figure. The initial search yielded 2474 records. After removal of duplicates, 1628 records remained for screening. Of these, 58 articles were retained based on title and abstract screening, and following full-text assessment, re-screening and quality appraisal, 15 qualitative studies were finally included in the synthesis.11,25 These comprised 3 studies published in Chinese12 21 24 and 12 in English.1113,20 22 23 25

Synthesised findings from the meta-aggregation

Across the included studies, 52 findings were identified and grouped into 13 categories, which were further synthesised into 4 overarching synthesised findings. To improve clarity and avoid a list-like presentation, the findings are presented below as an interpretive narrative supported by selected illustrative quotations, rather than as a comprehensive listing of quotations for every individual point.

Synthesised finding 1: physical symptom burden strongly shapes psychological well-being during COPD self-management

Across the included studies, patients consistently described a close and often reciprocal relationship between physical symptom burden and psychological well-being during COPD self-management. Breathlessness, fatigue, sleep disturbance, reduced exercise tolerance and progressive functional decline were widely reported and frequently triggered fear, frustration, sadness and helplessness. For many patients, these symptoms not only limited physical functioning but also undermined social participation, confidence and emotional stability. However, psychological responses were not entirely uniform. While distress predominated, a minority of participants described gradual acceptance, adaptation and a more hopeful outlook over time, indicating heterogeneity in coping across individuals and care contexts.

Category 1: physical symptom burden and disruption of daily life

Progressive physical decline was described across almost all included studies as a central challenge to self-management. Patients frequently reported that dyspnoea, fatigue, low energy levels, sleep problems and recurrent exacerbations disrupted routine activities and reduced their ability to maintain independence in daily life. These symptoms often affected not only self-care but also family roles, mobility and participation in previously valued social activities. Some accounts also suggested that worsening symptoms created a sense of unpredictability, making patients hesitant to plan ahead or leave the home. One participant captured this loss vividly: “I used to love dancing… Now I can’t even finish a song”.22 Although symptom burden was widespread, its impact appeared more severe among patients with advanced disease and among those living in settings where supportive resources were limited.

Category 2: diverse and evolving psychological responses to chronic illness

Patients described a broad range of emotional responses to living with COPD, although fear, anxiety, frustration and hopelessness were more commonly reported than emotional acceptance. Psychological distress was often linked to the experience of breathlessness, uncertainty about disease progression, fear of exacerbation, loss of autonomy and the cumulative burden of long-term illness. For some, these feelings were intensified by worries about becoming dependent on others or about being unable to fulfil family and social responsibilities. Others responded by minimising symptoms or avoiding engagement with treatment, suggesting that emotional distress could also shape behavioural patterns in self-management. One participant remarked, “I wake up at night struggling to breathe—it’s terrifying when you can’t get air”.18 At the same time, a smaller number of participants described greater optimism and acceptance, often in the presence of family encouragement or trust in professional care, indicating that psychological adaptation existed along a continuum rather than following a single fixed pattern.

Synthesised finding 2: patients face multiple and interrelated barriers to effective self-management

Most studies showed that patients encountered several overlapping barriers in understanding, sustaining and integrating COPD self-management into everyday life. These barriers included limited disease knowledge, difficulty adapting to long-term behavioural change, poor adherence to recommended regimens, financial strain and restricted access to healthcare services. Rather than occurring in isolation, such challenges often reinforced one another. For example, inadequate knowledge could reduce confidence, which in turn affected adherence, while financial difficulties and limited service access could further constrain patients’ capacity to manage symptoms effectively. Although these problems were reported across settings, structural barriers were more prominent in rural or low-resource contexts, whereas patients in better-resourced settings more often emphasised uncertainty, stigma and the hidden burden of chronic illness.

Category 3: inadequate disease knowledge and self-management skills

Insufficient knowledge about COPD and its management was evident in almost all included studies. Patients reported uncertainty about symptom monitoring, exacerbation management, medication use, inhaler techniques, home oxygen therapy, smoking-related risks and pulmonary rehabilitation. These knowledge deficits reduced confidence in decision-making and often left patients unsure how to respond when symptoms worsened. In several studies, participants also felt that information from professionals was incomplete, delayed or difficult to understand, which further limited their ability to manage the disease effectively. One participant explained, “No one ever asks if I need information—they just say, ‘You have COPD, figure it out’”.22 Such gaps were especially apparent among older adults and those with lower educational levels, whereas patients who had received structured education tended to describe greater understanding and confidence.

Category 4: difficulties adapting to lifestyle change and sustaining adherence

Many patients described difficulty adapting to the behavioural and lifestyle changes required for long-term COPD management. These difficulties were reflected in continued smoking, low motivation for rehabilitation or exercise, reluctance to use medicines as prescribed and a tendency to delay active management until symptoms became severe. In some studies, poor adaptation was also linked to altered self-identity, reduced confidence and a sense of diminished capability, which made it harder for patients to incorporate self-management into everyday routines. Adherence problems therefore appeared to be shaped not only by knowledge deficits, but also by emotional resistance and the burden of ongoing lifestyle adjustment. One participant stated, “I’m just a disabled person now—incapable of anything”,23 reflecting the low self-efficacy that often accompanied poor adjustment. Although these barriers were common, some participants gradually renegotiated their roles and routines over time, suggesting variation in how self-management was incorporated into daily life.

Category 5: financial strain and limitations in healthcare access

This category reflects the concrete economic and service-related barriers that patients were already experiencing in daily self-management. Across several studies, the costs of medication, oxygen equipment, consultations and long-term treatment led some patients to reduce dosages, interrupt treatment or delay care. In addition to direct medical costs, some patients also described the wider material consequences of chronic illness, including pressure on household finances and difficulty accessing appropriate services because of distance, mobility limitations or shortages in local provision. Limited access to specialist care, pulmonary rehabilitation and chronic disease support programmes further weakened patients’ capacity to manage COPD effectively, especially in rural or resource-constrained areas. One participant noted, “This medication requires twice daily, but I use it once—it’s too expensive”.17 These accounts indicate that financial hardship and inadequate service availability functioned as immediate barriers to self-management rather than merely perceived future concerns.

Category 6: dual influences of religious beliefs and cultural practices

Religious beliefs and cultural practices had both supportive and constraining effects on COPD self-management. In some studies, spiritual beliefs provided comfort, hope and a sense of meaning that helped patients endure chronic symptoms and maintain motivation. In other cases, however, cultural practices or traditional remedies conflicted with medical recommendations and were associated with delayed treatment, reduced adherence or worsening symptoms. Some patients appeared to move flexibly between biomedical advice and culturally rooted practices, while others relied more heavily on non-medical coping approaches. One participant reported, “During Ramadan, I abstain completely”,14 illustrating how cultural practices could directly affect self-management behaviours. Overall, these influences were context-dependent and varied across cultural and religious settings, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive support.

Synthesised finding 3: some patients develop active coping strategies and self-management resources

The included studies also showed that a proportion of patients developed practical and adaptive ways of managing COPD in daily life. These strategies included monitoring symptoms, adjusting activity levels, learning disease-management skills, maintaining a positive outlook and drawing on support from multiple sources. For these patients, self-management was not only a response to illness but also an active process of learning, experimentation and adjustment. However, such approaches were not universal. Some patients remained reliant on reactive, limited or avoidant forms of management, indicating marked variation in self-management capacity and resilience across the included studies.

Category 7: self-regulation strategies for symptom control

Many patients described practical strategies for reducing symptoms and preventing exacerbations. These included avoiding environmental triggers, pacing activities, monitoring bodily changes, practising breathing or sputum-clearance techniques, participating in rehabilitation and keeping medicines readily available. In some cases, patients also developed highly individualised routines based on prior experience of symptom fluctuation, which helped them feel more in control of daily management. At the same time, the quality and sophistication of these strategies varied, with some approaches appearing more flexible and effective than others. One participant explained, “I track my symptoms, control my activities and diet, and assess disease stability without relying solely on healthcare providers”.19 Although self-regulation strategies were widely described, some patients relied mainly on short-term avoidance or energy-conservation patterns that could help temporarily but also risked reinforcing deconditioning and social isolation.

Category 8: positive reframing and psychological adaptation

A minority of participants described developing greater emotional resilience over time. These patients reframed COPD as a condition to be managed rather than as a purely catastrophic event, and they emphasised hope, acceptance and confidence in ongoing self-management. Positive adaptation was often associated with a gradual process of adjusting expectations, recognising small improvements and focusing on what could still be controlled in daily life. Some patients also appeared to draw strength from supportive relationships or from prior experiences of coping with adversity. As one participant stated, “We must learn to coexist with COPD without surrendering, maintaining hope”.16 Although such positive reframing was not universal, it suggests that psychological adaptation may function as an important internal resource for sustaining self-management over time.

Category 9: active mobilisation of support from multiple sources

This category reflects patients’ active efforts to seek and mobilise support from different sources in order to manage COPD more effectively. Participants described asking healthcare professionals for advice, searching for information, learning from peers and making use of practical help from family members or institutions. In this sense, support was not only something received passively, but also something patients actively pursued when they recognised limits in their own knowledge or capacity. Such efforts could strengthen confidence, improve access to information and make disease management feel less isolating. One participant stated, “If I don’t speak up, how can clinicians help me?”.17 These active help-seeking behaviours were more frequently reported in urban or better-resourced settings, whereas patients in more isolated or resource-constrained environments often described fewer opportunities to access diversified sources of support.

Synthesised finding 4: patients express multidimensional and continuing support needs

Across almost all studies, patients articulated support needs that extended well beyond medication alone. These included clearer and more accessible information, sustained professional guidance, stronger financial and social protection and greater emotional support from family members, peers and healthcare professionals. Rather than being isolated requests, these needs were often interrelated and reflected patients’ attempts to cope with the long-term burden of living with COPD in everyday life. Although these needs were widely shared, their specific forms varied across healthcare systems, social contexts, family structures and levels of available community support.

Category 10: need for accessible and practical disease information

Nearly all patients expressed a strong need for clearer, more practical and more timely information about COPD, including symptom management, treatment options, disease progression and daily self-care. Many also preferred information to be delivered through multiple formats, such as written materials, videos or repeated explanations, rather than through one-off verbal instruction. Patients’ accounts suggested that information was valued not only for knowledge itself, but also for the reassurance and sense of control it could provide. One participant commented, “Knowing the truth about the disease is crucial”.16 Although the desire for information was nearly universal, preferences regarding timing, format and depth of education varied between individuals and some patients reported feeling overwhelmed by technical or inconsistent explanations.

Category 11: need for ongoing professional and personalised rehabilitation support

Patients commonly reported needing more individualised and continuous professional support than they were currently receiving. This included clearer rehabilitation instructions, tailored exercise guidance, assistance with comorbidity management, better communication with clinicians and regular follow-up after discharge. Several studies also suggested that patients valued professional support that took account of their personal circumstances, symptom burden and changing capabilities rather than relying solely on standardised advice. As one participant explained, “Without clear rehab instructions, I can’t persist—experts should explain its benefits”.13 Overall, participants described a gap between their need for ongoing, person-centred support and the episodic, standardised care that they often experienced in practice.

Category 12: need for stronger financial and social support

In contrast to Category 5, which describes barriers already experienced in everyday management, this category captures patients’ expectations for stronger long-term financial protection and broader social support. Participants expressed the need for improved insurance coverage, reduced treatment costs, community-based services, practical family assistance and opportunities to maintain social participation despite COPD. In many accounts, support needs extended beyond symptom control to preserving meaningful social roles, maintaining dignity and continuing valued family relationships. One participant stated, “I want to keep up with my grandchildren”,22 showing that support needs were closely connected to everyday life and social belonging. Although these needs were described across settings, some patients focused more on informal family support, whereas others emphasised formal welfare measures and community resources, reflecting variation in healthcare systems and social expectations.

Category 13: need for emotional and psychological support

Unlike Category 9, which concerns patients’ active efforts to seek support, this category focuses on patients’ unmet need for empathy, reassurance and emotionally supportive relationships. Many accounts reflected loneliness, fear and a sense of being insufficiently understood, particularly when symptoms were severe or support networks were weak. Patients often valued not only practical assistance but also the experience of being listened to, accompanied and emotionally acknowledged by family members, peers or healthcare professionals. One participant said, “To doctors, I’m just another patient—a nurse’s companionship would mean so much”.17 Structured psychological interventions and organised peer-support services were mentioned only rarely, suggesting an important gap between patients’ emotional needs and the support currently available.

Discussion

Addressing challenges in COPD self-management and enhancing comprehensive support

This study showed that patients with COPD face multiple and interrelated barriers during self-management, including physiological limitations (eg, dyspnoea, fatigue, restricted mobility), psychological distress (eg, anxiety, depression stemming from insufficient disease knowledge and treatment uncertainty) and social isolation—reported in most included studies—exacerbating helplessness. These factors collectively contribute to poor self-management behaviours, aligning with prior research.6 26 27 Potential causes include high disease burden, inadequate self-management awareness and insufficient social and professional guidance.28 Domestic studies, such as Zhao et al,29 demonstrated that COPD pulmonary rehabilitation programmes based on the IMB model effectively alleviate dyspnoea, improve exercise tolerance, enhance quality of life and boost adherence and self-management capacity. International research by Benzo and McEvoy30 also highlighted that nurse-led motivational interviewing improves patient self-management. 31Therefore, healthcare providers should use multidimensional, standardised and intelligent assessment tools to evaluate patients’ physical and mental states accurately, implement targeted interventions and foster confidence in self-management. Collaborative efforts among patients, families, clinicians and society are essential to provide holistic support—educational, emotional, financial and social—to mitigate the disease’s impact.

Strengthening positive self-management behaviours to promote adaptation

This study identified that COPD self-management behaviours are influenced by socioeconomic status, education level, health beliefs, social support, disease severity and psychological state, consistent with Sami et al.28 Proactive patients exhibited adaptive strategies such as information-seeking, self-monitoring and skill acquisition.24 Successful self-management experiences reinforce confidence, creating a positive reinforcing cycle of symptom recognition, medication adherence and physical activity.18 Clinicians should adopt a holistic approach, acknowledging both challenges and achievements.32 Such strategies encourage continuous adaptation and growth in disease management.

Identifying multidimensional needs and reducing self-management burden

Effective COPD self-management requires personalised support systems integrating familial, clinical, institutional and societal resources.29 33 However, unmet needs persist, including knowledge gaps, inaccessible resources and mismatched services. Clinicians must prioritise patient-centred care by understanding individual preferences and providing continuous feedback. Personalised strategies should address physical rehabilitation, psychological adaptation and social integration. Internet-based platforms34 could optimise remote monitoring and follow-up,35 while family workshops and self-management diaries strengthen long-term adherence.36 Pendoni et al37 emphasised that robust social support systems enhance access to healthcare resources, disease education and emotional reinforcement, ultimately improving prognosis. 31 38Governments, institutions and communities must collaborate to establish COPD support networks, improve resource accessibility and alleviate self-management burdens.

Methodological considerations and limitations

In interpreting these findings, several methodological limitations of the review should be considered. First, restricting inclusion to studies published in Chinese or English may have introduced language bias and reduced the global representativeness of the evidence. Second, despite attempts to obtain full texts through institutional access and interlibrary loan, three potentially relevant studies could not be retrieved and were therefore excluded, which may have resulted in the loss of additional qualitative insights. Third, the included studies differed in design, setting and participant characteristics (eg, age, disease severity and socioeconomic background), as well as in healthcare system and cultural context, which may have introduced heterogeneity and limited the transferability and generalisability of the synthesised findings. Finally, although the JBI meta-aggregation approach aims to remain close to the primary data, the processes of identifying, grouping and synthesising findings inevitably involve interpretive judgement by the review team.

Conclusion

This study systematically synthesised the lived experiences and unmet needs of patients with COPD in relation to self-management, highlighting important physical, psychological and social challenges, adaptive coping strategies and persistent gaps in support. The findings indicate that patients face multiple and interrelated barriers during self-management, underscoring the need for healthcare professionals to provide comprehensive guidance, reinforce proactive behaviours and strengthen patients’ self-management capacity. Clinicians should identify and address patients’ multidimensional needs through tailored and diversified support in order to reduce self-management burden and promote adaptation to chronic illness. These findings also suggest that stronger coordination across patient, family, community and clinical levels may help better address the multilevel needs identified in this review and improve quality of life for this population.
