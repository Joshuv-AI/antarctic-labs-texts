Revisiting lepton flavor violation: τ \tau and meson decays
Kevin A. Urquía-Calderón, and Oleg Ruchayskiy

Abstract
The minimal type-I seesaw model provides a simple explanation of neutrino flavor oscillations and induces charged lepton flavor violation (cLFV). Despite extensive previous studies, semileptonic cLFV channels remain underexplored. Using updated form factors, decay constants, and oscillation data, we revisit τ \tau and meson decay channels, performing a systematic comparison across the seesaw parameter space. Surprisingly, we find that decays such as τ → ℓ ​ ρ \tau\to\ell\rho can dominate over purely leptonic τ \tau -sector probes, including τ → 3 ​ ℓ \tau\to 3\ell and even τ → ℓ ​ γ \tau\to\ell\,\gamma , in certain regions. In contrast, heavy-meson decays remain far below experimental sensitivity. Considering global constraints on the seesaw parameters, we derive branching ratios for the relevant cLFV processes and identify those within potential reach of next-generation experiments.

Introduction

The discovery of neutrino oscillations in Super-Kamiokande
49

Neutrino oscillations imply that there must be mixing in the lepton sector and that neutrinos have masses. The SM predicts none.
A minimal extension to account for neutrino oscillations is by adding Heavy Neutral Leptons (HNLs, or also known as right-handed neutrinos or sterile neutrinos), which eventually give neutrino masses via the seesaw mechanism

2
Type-I seesaw theory

We extend the SM by including
\mathcal{N}
neutral singlet right-handed fermions,
N_{R}
.
Having no SM quantum numbers, their Lagrangian terms are

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SM}}+i\bar{N}_{R}\not{\partial}N_{R}-\bar{L}_{L}\cdot\tilde{H}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\nu}N_{R}-\frac{1}{2}\bar{N}_{R}^{C}\,\mathbf{M}_{M}\,N_{R}+\mathrm{H.c.}\,,

(1)

where
L_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{L}\\
\ell_{L}\end{pmatrix}
is the SM fermion doublet,
\tilde{H}=i\sigma_{2}H^{\ast}
where
H=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{W}^{+}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(H+i\varphi_{Z}^{0})\end{pmatrix}
, is the SM Higgs doublet.
\mathbf{Y}_{\nu}
is a Yukawa matrix of dimensions
3\times\mathcal{N}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{M}
is a Majorana mass matrix of dimension
\mathcal{N}
.

After spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB), the Higgs field acquires a vacuum expectation value (VEV),
v
, giving tree-level masses to SM particles, and now also providing a mixing term between the neutral singlets and active neutrinos. The mixing term looks like

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mass}}=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\nu}_{L}&\bar{N}_{R}^{C}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{M}_{D}\\
\mathbf{M}_{D}^{T}&\mathbf{M}_{M}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{L}^{C}\\
N_{R}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(2)

where
\mathbf{M}_{D}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}v\mathbf{Y}_{\nu}
.
The diagonalization into mass eigenstates provides us with the two following states

\displaystyle n_{L}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Theta}\,\mathbf{\Theta}^{\dagger}\right)\mathbf{V}\,\nu_{L}+\mathbf{\Theta}\,N_{R}^{C}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{R}

\displaystyle\simeq-\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}\,\mathbf{V}^{\ast}\nu_{L}^{C}+\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}\,\mathbf{\Theta}\right)N_{R}\,,

(3)

where
\mathbf{V}
is the usual unitary PMNS matrix, and
\mathbf{\Theta}\simeq-\mathbf{M}_{D}\,\mathbf{M}_{M}^{-1}
is the mixing angle matrix between the singlets and active neutrinos.
We expanded only up to
\mathcal{\mathbf{\Theta}^{2}}
. From experimental data and precision measurements, we expect the elements of
\mathbf{\Theta}\lesssim${10}^{-3}$
.

The diagonalization of this matrix gives the mass spectrum of neutral fermions.
In the limit where
\mathbf{M}_{D}\ll\mathbf{M}_{M}
, we arrive at the following relation for active neutrino masses

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{m}_{\nu}\,\mathbf{V}^{T}

\displaystyle\simeq-\mathbf{M}_{D}\,\mathbf{M}_{N}^{-1}\,\mathbf{M}_{D}^{T}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{N}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbf{M}_{M}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\simeq-\mathbf{\Theta}\,\mathbf{M}_{N}\,\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}\,.

This is the
type-I seesaw relation
.
We should also highlight here that we considered the first power of
\mathbf{\Theta}
in this relation.

Interactions between neutral leptons and the rest of the SM particles, in the Feynman-’t-Hooft gauge, are

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{W^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathcal{U}_{\alpha i}\,\bar{\ell}_{\alpha}\,\not{W}^{-}\,P_{L}\,n_{i}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{2\,c_{w}}\,\bar{n}_{i}\,\not{Z}\left[\mathcal{C}_{ij}P_{L}-\mathcal{C}^{\ast}_{ij}P_{R}\right]\,n_{j}\,,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi^{\pm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\sqrt{2}\,M_{W}}\,\mathcal{U}_{\alpha i}\,\phi^{-}\,\bar{\ell}_{\alpha}\left[m_{i}\,P_{R}-m_{\alpha}\,P_{L}\right]n_{i}+\mathrm{h.c.}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi^{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\,g}{4\,M_{W}}\,\phi^{0}\,\bar{n}_{i}\left[\mathcal{C}_{ij}(m_{j}\,P_{R}-m_{i}\,P_{L})+\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\ast}(m_{i}\,P_{R}-m_{j}\,P_{L})\right]\,n_{j}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{g}{4\,M_{W}}\,H\,\bar{n}_{i}\left[\mathcal{C}_{ij}(m_{j}\,P_{R}+m_{i}\,P_{L})+\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{\ast}(m_{i}\,P_{R}+m_{j}\,P_{L})\right]\,n_{j}\,,

(9)

where we introduced the matrices
\mathcal{U}\equiv\left[(\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{\Theta}^{\dagger})\,\mathbf{V},\mathbf{\Theta}\right],\mathcal{C}\equiv\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}
. In Appendix
A
we summarize some important relationships regarding the unitarity of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{C}
.

In what remains of the paper, we will solely focus on the case where
\mathcal{N}=2
.
This is the most minimal case that can explain neutrino oscillation data, but it also predicts that the lightest neutrino is massless.

2.1
Parametrization of mixing angles in the minimal model with 2 HNLs

In the vanilla realization of the seesaw mechanism, the smallness of the active neutrino masses is attributed to the largeness of
\mathbf{M}_{N}
compared to that of
\mathbf{M}_{D}
.
This
vanilla
realization of the seesaw model is very hard to probe, since it would also give us extremely small
\mathbf{Theta}
that almost no experiment can probe.

However, we can still have large mixing angles and radiatively small neutrino masses.
We can do this by imposing that the smallness of neutrinos is generated by the small breaking of a symmetry.
In the minimal model with only two additional HNLs, we can achieve this by imposing
M_{N1}=M_{N2}=M_{N}
and
\Theta_{\alpha 1}=\pm i\,\Theta_{\alpha 2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Theta_{\alpha}
.
This will leave neutrinos massless at tree-level.
Thus, neutrino masses are generated from small perturbations to the equalities we just described.

To properly incorporate neutrino oscillation data into our analyses with large mixing angles, we use the Casas-Ibarra parametrization
30
.
Solving for
\mathbf{\Theta}
in Eq. (
4
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}=i\,\mathbf{V}\mathbf{m_{\nu}}^{1/2}\,\mathbf{\Omega}\,\mathbf{M}_{N}^{-1/2}\,,

(10)

where
\mathbf{\Omega}
is an arbitrary semi-orthogonal matrix that is only parametrized by one angle.
This matrix differs for the normal mass ordering (NO) and for the inverted mass ordering (IO) of neutrino masses

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}_{\mathrm{NO}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\cos\omega&\sin\omega\\
-\sin\omega&\cos\omega\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}_{\mathrm{IO}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega&\sin\omega\\
-\sin\omega&\cos\omega\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(11)

the
L
conserved scenario is obtained in the limit where
\imaginary(\omega)\gg 0
.
In this limit, the Casas-Ibarra matrices become

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}_{\mathrm{NO}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{-i\omega}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&-i\\
i&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}_{\mathrm{IO}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{e^{-i\omega}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\
i&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(12)

We can write the total mixing angle
U_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha i}\absolutevalue{\Theta_{\alpha i}}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha}\absolutevalue{\Theta_{\alpha}}^{2}
, in terms of
\omega,M_{N}
and neutrino masses

\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2}=e^{2\imaginary(\omega)}\frac{\sum m_{\nu}}{2\,M_{N}}\,.

(13)

Now we introduce

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\absolutevalue{\Theta_{\alpha i}}^{2}}{U_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2}}=\frac{\absolutevalue{\Theta_{\alpha}}^{2}}{U_{\mathrm{tot}}^{2}}\,,

(14)

these quantities are written only in terms of neutrino oscillation data and a free Majorana phase

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}=\frac{\absolutevalue{V_{\alpha i}\sqrt{m_{\nu,i}} + i\,V_{\alpha j} \sqrt{m_{\nu,j}} }^{2}}{\sum_{k}m_{\nu,k}}\,.

(15)

3.2
Parametric suppression of meson decays

The branching ratios of pseudoscalars and vectors decaying into two different charged leptons will be highly suppressed, because neutral unflavored mesons and quarkonia decay mostly through strong interactions.
We can see this by analyzing the parametric dependence of Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Br}(M\to\ell_{\alpha}\ell_{\beta})\propto\left(\alpha_{W}^{4}\,\frac{m_{M}^{4}}{M_{W}^{4}}\frac{f_{M}^{2}}{m_{M}^{2}}\right)\frac{m_{M}}{\Gamma_{M}}\,.

(33)

The term in the parentheses will be much smaller than
m_{M}/\Gamma_{M}
, since
\Gamma_{M}\propto\alpha_{s}\,m_{M}
.
1
1

1

Neutral pion decay mostly to two photons through a chiral anomaly, so the parametric dependence of its total decay width should be
\Gamma_{\pi}\propto\alpha^{2}m_{\pi}
. cLFV pion decays should therefore be less suppressed than, e.g.,
\eta
or
\eta^{\prime}
decays. Nevertheless, they are still much smaller than
\tau
decays.

This is in direct contrast with
\tau
decays, which will have a similar parametric dependence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Br}(\tau\to\ell_{\alpha}M)\propto\left(\alpha_{W}^{4}\,\frac{m_{\tau}^{4}}{M_{W}^{4}}\frac{f_{M}^{2}}{m_{\tau}^{2}}\right)\frac{m_{\tau}}{\Gamma_{\tau}}\,,

(34)

where the decay width scales as
\Gamma_{\tau}\propto\alpha_{W}^{2}\,m_{\tau}^{5}/M_{W}^{4}
.
The small tau decay width cancels the small values of
\alpha_{W}^{2}\,m_{\tau}^{4}/M_{W}^{4}
.

Flavored mesons, like
K^{0}
,
D^{0}
,
B^{0}
, and
B_{s}^{0}
, decay mostly through weak interactions, and therefore these should not have the same suppression.
However, the GIM mechanism should make their cLFV decays very small.
In
15
, some of these decays were investigated, and it was found that the decays are very suppressed, with
B
and
B_{s}
decays being larger than kaon decays.
This makes sense, since the large coupling between bottom and top quarks alleviates the GIM suppression.

Heavier mesons, such as
\phi,J/\Psi
, and
\Upsilon
have larger decay widths because they are subject to Okubo–Zweig–Iizuka suppression (OZI suppression)
77
;
101
;
59
. However, the decays are still mostly driven by strong interactions and are still parametrically suppressed.

To finish our discussion about meson decays, it is also important to highlight the fact that pseudoscalar decays will also enjoy helicity suppression.
Helicity suppression should be particularly relevant for heavy pseudoscalar states decaying to an
e\mu
final state.

6
Summary and conclusions

We studied different charged lepton flavor violating processes involving mesons in the minimal type-I seesaw with two degenerate HNLs.

We find that meson decays, including light-unflavored mesons and quarkonia, have very small branching ratios in our model.
The largest of light-unflavored mesons are pion decays, where the branching ratio is at least
{10}^{-17}
as allowed by indirect and theoretical constraints;
and for quarkonia
\Upsilon
decays are as large as
{10}^{-14}
. We also explained the reason for their suppression.

\tau
decays into mesons, on the other hand, are much larger.
In particular,
\tau\to\pi\,\ell_{\alpha}
and
\tau\to\rho\,\ell_{\alpha}
have the largest allowed branching ratios, in the order of
{10}^{-10}
.
This is in the ballpark of the sensitivity that Belle-II aims to achieve.

However, it should still be unlikely that Belle-II will see anything, since it is likely that electroweak precision bounds will improve in the coming years with upcoming experiments like PIONEER
10
, which will provide more stringent bounds on electroweak precision bounds from lepton universality decays on pions.

The currently running and upcoming muon facilities
19
;
22
;
5
;
16
will also provide the strongest bounds ever seen in any cLFV process.
In the case of observation of any cLFV process in these current and upcoming facilities, we should then aim to reconstruct mixing angles and masses to pinpoint which regions of the parameter space we could lie at.

It would also be interesting to perform the same exercise with other seesaw variants, like the minimal type-I seesaw with three degenerate HNLs, seesaw models with two or more additional scales, like the inverse or double seesaw.
The bounds from these models are less constraining, and should therefore open up more the possibility of seeing cLFV processes with mesons future facilities, especially Belle-II.

having also the same definition as with charged kaon, with an additional minus sign in the isovector component in Eq. (
99
)

.

We used the vector meson dominated (VMD) models for the parametrization of different form factors
88
, where we assume that interactions that involve hadrons are mediated by a sum of different vector bound states, such as
\rho,\omega
, and
\phi
-like resonances.
Accordingly, we parametrize form factors as a sum of different Breit-Wigner (BW) functions, for a form factor, this looks as

\displaystyle F(s)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\,\mathrm{BW}(s,m_{V_{i}},\Gamma_{V_{i}})\,,

(102)

where
c_{i}
are normalization constants, that in general can be complex, that ensure that
F(s=0)=1
.
Dual-QCD in the limit of
N_{C}=\infty
predicts that there should be an infinite number of these resonances
98
;
27
, but phenomenologically the most relevant ones are the first ones.

We parametrize the
\rho,\omega
, and
\phi
resonances with different BW functions, just as it was used in
39
.
For
\rho
, we used the Gounaris-Sakurai (GS) BW function
53
, for the
\omega
resonances a constant BW, and for
\phi
resonances we used a Kühn-Santamaría (KS) BW function
67
. Following the parametrization of
67
;
39
, the BW functions are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{GS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{V}^{2}+h(0,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})}{m_{V}^{2}-s+h(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})-i\sqrt{s}\,\tilde{\Gamma}_{V}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})}\,,

(103)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{KS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{m_{V}^{2}-s-i\sqrt{s}\,\tilde{\Gamma}_{V}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})}\,,

(104)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{const}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{m_{V}^{2}-s-i\sqrt{s}\,\Gamma_{V}}\,,

(105)

where the auxiliary functions are defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})=\frac{m_{V}^{2}}{s}\left(\frac{s-4\,m_{P}^{2}}{m_{V}^{2}-4\,m_{P}^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\Gamma_{V}\,,

(106)

\displaystyle h(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})=\hat{h}_{V}(s)-\hat{h}_{V}(m_{V}^{2})-(s-m_{V}^{2})\left.\derivative{s}\hat{h}_{V}(s)\right|_{s\to m_{V}^{2}}\,,

(107)

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{V}(s)=\frac{4\,m_{V}^{2}\,\Gamma_{V}}{\pi(s-4\,m_{P}^{2})}\left(\frac{s}{4}-m_{P}^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{4\,m_{P}^{2}}{s}\right)^{1/2}\log(\frac{1 + \left(1 - \frac{4\,m_P^2}{s}\right)^{1/2}}{1 - \left(1 - \frac{4\,m_P^2}{s}\right)^{1/2}})\,,

(108)

where
m_{P}
should be understood as either the mass of the pion or kaon, depending on the form factor in question.
The rest of the parameters,
c_{V},m_{V},\Gamma_{V}
, are fitted from data from different experiments from different processes.

For
F_{\pi\pi}(s)
, we used the fit from the BaBar collaboration
69
, which was written as a sum of four different
\rho
-like vector mesons

\displaystyle F_{\pi\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+c_{\rho^{\prime}}+c_{\rho^{\prime\prime}}+c_{\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}}}\sum_{V=\rho,\rho^{\prime}\dots}^{\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}}c_{V}\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{GS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})\,.

(109)

To account for
\rho-\omega
mixing, we shift the first resonance

\displaystyle\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{GS}}(s,m_{\rho},\Gamma_{\rho})\to\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{GS}}(s,m_{\rho},\Gamma_{\rho})\,\frac{1+c_{\omega}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{const}}(s,m_{\omega},\Gamma_{\omega})}{1+c_{\omega}}\,.

(110)

The different kaon form factors are written as
27
;
34

\displaystyle F_{KK}^{(3)}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{V=\rho,\rho^{\prime}\dots}c_{V}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{GS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})\,,

(111)

\displaystyle F_{KK}^{(8)}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{V=\omega,\omega^{\prime}\dots}c_{V}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{const}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})+\frac{1}{3}\sum_{V=\phi,\phi^{\prime}\dots}c_{V}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{KS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})\,,

(112)

\displaystyle F_{KK}^{(0)}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{V=\omega,\omega^{\prime}\dots}c_{V}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{const}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{V=\phi,\phi^{\prime}\dots}c_{V}\,\mathrm{BW}^{\mathrm{KS}}(s,m_{V},\Gamma_{V})\,.

(113)

We used the output from the fit of
86
(see
39
for details on the parametrization).
We did not use the infinite tower of resonances.
Instead, for the
\rho
-like resonances, we used the first six resonances, and for
\omega

\phi
-like, we used the first five.

The
\phi

The isosinglet component,

We will follow the steps in
