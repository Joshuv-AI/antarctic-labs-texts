In vitro evaluation of Verteporfin and exploration of TEAD palmitoylation inhibition in Piezo1–YAP/TAZ signaling and ECM remodeling
Authors: Zhuang Xiaojun, Qiyong Chen, Shaoqiang Liu, Boling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fibrosis is characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition driven by mechanical stress, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. To investigate whether mechanical stress–induced ECM remodeling is mediated by YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling and whether pharmacological modulation can mimic or reverse these effects. Nucleus pulposus (NP) cells were exposed to mechanical stress and treated with the MST1/2 inhibitor XMU-MP-1 or the YAP/TAZ–TEAD inhibitor Verteporfin (VP). Nuclear localization of YAP/TAZ–TEAD, expression of canonical TEAD target genes (CTGF, CYR61, ANKRD1), and ECM markers (Col1, Col2, α-SMA, FN, CTGF) were analyzed by Western blot, qPCR, and immunofluorescence. Mechanical stress induced nuclear accumulation of YAP/TAZ–TEAD, upregulated TEAD target genes, and promoted profibrotic ECM remodeling. XMU-MP-1 recapitulated these effects, while VP suppressed TEAD-dependent transcription and reversed ECM remodeling without affecting nuclear localization. YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling mediates mechanically induced ECM remodeling. Activation of this pathway drives profibrotic gene expression, and disruption of YAP–TEAD interaction effectively reverses these changes, highlighting a potential therapeutic target for fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28960-0.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a multi-causal condition and a leading cause of chronic low back pain. This musculoskeletal disorder significantly affects the quality of life and work production globally. The classically described structure of the intervertebral disc comprises the nucleus pulposus (NP), an annulus fibrosus, and a cartilaginous and bony rim. Of these parts, the NP is key to maintaining the disc’s biomechanical properties, including hydration, elasticity, and resistance to compressive force1,2. Despite their hypoxic, avascular nature, NP cells are susceptible to mechanical stimuli, most probably because they are uniquely adapted to their niche. Excessive or persistent mechanical loading causes oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and pathological extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, which contribute to the development of IDD3,4.

Mechanical stress is recognized as a major driver of fibrotic remodeling; however, the molecular mechanisms by which cells sense and transduce mechanical cues into profibrotic responses remain incompletely understood6–8. Activation of Piezo1 signals calcium influx and downstream signaling cascades that culminate in cell fate and matrix composition9. Recent studies have shown that mechanical stimulation can promote Piezo1 expression and activity in NP cells, thereby facilitating ECM disorganization and altering cell phenotype10.

The downstream pathways activated by Piezo1-mediated mechano-sensation include the Hippo pathway, especially its terminal effectors Yes-associated Protein (YAP) and Transcriptional co-activator with PDZ binding motif (TAZ)11,12. Dephosphorylated YAP/TAZ translocate into the nucleus and can interact with the transcriptional-enhanced associate domain (TEAD) family of transcription factors to drive gene expression13. Nuclear translocation is also associated with upregulation of fibrotic genes, such as type I collagen (COL1), and downregulation of cartilage markers, including type II collagen (COL2), a characteristic of disc degeneration14–16. Additionally, upregulation of nuclear YAP/TAZ activity is associated with cell proliferation, inflammation, and fibrogenesis in degenerative and neoplastic diseases17,18.

In addition to cytosolic phosphorylation events, the regulation of YAP/TAZ activity includes the availability of transcriptional partners and post-translational modifications. TEAD palmitoylation has been identified as a critical modification for TEAD function and YAP/TAZ-mediated gene transcription. TEAD is palmitoylated, which stabilizes TEAD, promotes its nuclear retention, and increases its interaction with YAP19,20. Most importantly, TEAD palmitoylation is an attractive target for manipulating this pathway, as YAP lacks a canonical enzymatic site to target it directly21. Specific inhibition of TEAD palmitoylation, associated transcriptional activity, and downstream fibrotic signaling is achieved by administering inhibitors such as VT10322.

Its efficacy in photodynamic therapy, as seen with verteporfin (VP), a small molecule, has also been shown to disrupt the YAP–TEAD interaction and simultaneously block YAP nuclear translocation23. Prior work in pulmonary, hepatic, and cardiac fibrotic ECM remodeling models has shown that VP can diminish fibrotic ECM remodeling by lowering YAP-driven transcription24,25. The combined effects of YAP nuclear translocation inhibition and TEAD palmitoylation blockade under mechanical tension in NP cells remain unclear.

Based upon the increasing body of evidence demonstrating that Piezo1–YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling links to fibrogenesis, we hypothesize that this axis will define the role of mechanical stress in NP cell ECM remodeling and that therapeutically targeting this pathway at various nodes of this pathway will modify or reverse the degenerative state of these cells.

Here, we first examined whether mechanical stress activates the YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling. We then focused on how YAP/TAZ–TEAD regulates transcriptional activation and ECM remodeling using pharmacological modulation and functional inhibition approaches. Our findings reveal that YAP activation is both necessary and sufficient to drive TEAD-dependent profibrotic transcription and ECM deposition, while disruption of YAP–TEAD interaction reverses these effects, highlighting YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling as a critical mediator of mechanotransduction and fibrosis.

Methods

Cell isolation techniques of dermatologic interest

Primary nucleus pulposus (NP) cells were isolated from intervertebral disc (IVD) tissue obtained from X patients (age range: 18–21 years; 1 male, and 2 females) undergoing lumbar discectomy for degenerative disc disease (DDD) at Fuzhou Second General Hospital. No healthy donor tissue was used. All procedures were approved by the Fuzhou Second General Hospital Ethics Committee (approval number: 2022152), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Intervertebral disc specimens were obtained during lumbar discectomy procedures, with informed patient consent, and NP cells were isolated using established protocols23,25. Viability NP cells were then collected from NP tissue, enzymatically digested with 0.2% type II collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 4 h at 37 °C, and filtered and centrifuged. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Ham’s F-12 (DMEM/F12, Gibco, USA), containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, was used to culture cells in standard humidified conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Phenotypically stable cells between passages 2 and 4 were used in all experiments.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106), and cDNA was synthesized with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1708891). qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad, 1725125). All primers were designed for human genes: CTGF (forward: 5'-CAGAGTGGGTGTGTGACGAC-3', reverse: 5'-TGGGACAGCTTGGGACTTAA-3'), CYR61 (forward: 5'-TGTGGACGAGGAGGACTGAG-3', reverse: 5'-CTGCTGCTGCTGTTGCTTCT-3'), ANKRD1 (forward: 5'-GAGCTGCTGCTGACGAGATT-3', reverse: 5'-TGCTTCTGCTTCTGCTGAGT-3'), Col1 (forward: 5'-GAGGGCCAAGACGAAGACATC-3', reverse: 5'-CAGATCACGTCATCGCACAAC-3'), Col2 (forward: 5'-AGCAGTTGGAGAGAGGAGGA-3', reverse: 5'-TCTTCTTGGGAGGTGGTGTC-3'), α-SMA (forward: 5'-GTGTTGCCCCTGAAGAGCAT-3', reverse: 5'-GCTGGGACATTGAAAGTCTCA-3'), FN (forward: 5'-TGGAGAACTTCGGGAGAGTG-3', reverse: 5'-CAGTGTTGGTGGTTTGTGGA-3'), and GAPDH (forward: 5'-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTCA-3', reverse: 5'-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3'). Relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH.

Mechanical stretch stimulation

NP cells were seeded onto collagen I-coated BioFlex® culture plates with a flexible silicone membrane (Flexcell International, USA) to simulate mechanical stress conditions. Cells at 70–80% confluence were cyclically mechanically stretched in a custom-designed equiaxial mechanical loading system at 1 Hz with a 1:20 strain ratio for 24 h. The resulting loading condition is consistent with physiological disc dynamic compression in vivo17. Equal mechanical stimulation was applied to the control groups, which were maintained under identical conditions.

Bioinformatics and pathway enrichment analysis

Transcriptomic data from publicly available gene expression databases (NCBI GEO) or RNA-sequencing were used to investigate signaling pathway activation under mechanical stress (GSE245147 and GSE175710). The DESeq2 function was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes (DEG) with adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1. To determine the signaling cascades significantly affected, DEGs were analyzed using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources and KEGG pathway enrichment of mechano-transduction-related Piezo1, YAP/TAZ, and TEAD genes26,27.

Western blot analysis

After experimental treatment, NP cells were harvested in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for lysing (Beyotime, China). For whole-cell lysates, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo-Scientific, USA). For nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation, NP cells were processed using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo-Fisher, 78833) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were resuspended in cytoplasmic extraction reagent containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, incubated on ice, and centrifuged to separate cytoplasmic proteins. The resulting pellet was further treated with a nuclear extraction reagent to obtain the nuclear proteins, and protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay. Protein concentration was measured by a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo-Scientific, USA) kit. Protein (30 µg) was loaded equally onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Primary antibodies against Piezo1 (Abcam, ab128245), total YAP (CST, #14074), nuclear YAP (CST, #4912), TEAD1 (Santa Cruz, sc-373781), COL1 (Abcam, ab34710), and COL2 (Abcam, ab34712) were used to block the membranes in 5% non-fat milk overnight at 4 °C, respectively. Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were run on the same gel, and GAPDH and Histone H3 were used as internal loading controls. Protein bands were visualized after incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, detected with ECL reagents, and quantified with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Grown on sterilized glass coverslips, NP cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following a 1-h incubation at room temperature with 5% BSA as a blocking agent, cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies for YAP, COL1, and COL2. The next day, cells were washed three times with PBS, then incubated with Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) for 1 h. The counterstaining was performed on nuclei with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min, and then the nuclei were mounted. Quantitative intensity analysis of fluorescence images using ImageJ, along with nuclear-to-cytoplasmic YAP ratios and collagen marker distribution, was performed.

Quantitative image and band intensity analysis

YAP localization was analyzed in ImageJ to quantify nuclear and cytoplasmic fluorescence intensities. DAPI staining was used as a mask to identify nuclear regions and to calculate the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic intensity ratio for each condition. For Western blots, band intensities were normalized to respective internal controls (GAPDH or Histone H3), and relative protein levels were expressed as fold change compared to the appropriate control. All experiments were repeated more than three times independently to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t-tests. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were assessed to verify the assumptions for parametric testing. Non-parametric tests (e.g., Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post hoc) were applied when assumptions were not met. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using [GraphPad Prism 9.0]. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The Ethics Committee of the Fuzhou Second General Hospital (2023092808) approved this study. All procedures performed in this study complied with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. The patient gave written informed consent for publication. To ensure confidentiality, all patient data were anonymized. Patients were free to choose whether to participate in the study; if so, they could withdraw without prejudice to their treatment.

Findings were further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining. Under static conditions, YAP was localized predominantly in the cytoplasm, as shown in Fig. 1B. Cyclic stretch, in contrast, caused a pronounced translocation of YAP into the nucleus, consistent with the pathway’s activation. Nuclear YAP intensity increased significantly (Fig. 1C, Quant. Analysis) along with this observation.

Moreover, Western blot quantification revealed increased expression of profibrotic ECM markers, including Col1, α-SMA, FN, and CTGF, along with a concomitant decrease in Col2 levels in Fig. 1D. We confirmed these results by densitometric analysis (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results indicate that mechanical stress activates YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling, leading to transcriptional induction of profibrotic genes and ECM remodeling.

Based on the above findings, mechanical stimulation increased type I collagen (COL1), a marker of fibrosis, and decreased type II collagen (COL2), a marker of a healthy NP matrix. This study demonstrates the effect of mechanical stress on initiating upstream signaling and inducing ECM remodeling to a fibrotic phenotype.

Pharmacological activation of YAP recapitulates ECM remodeling

To determine whether YAP activation is sufficient to drive ECM remodeling, cells were treated with the MST1/2 inhibitor XMU-MP-1, which prevents YAP phosphorylation and maintains it in an active, nuclear-localized state. In Fig. 2A, Western blot analyses showed that XMU-MP-1 slightly increased total YAP, TAZ, and TEAD levels, while markedly reducing YAP phosphorylation and enhancing nuclear accumulation of YAP, TAZ, and TEAD, confirming activation of the YAP/TAZ–TEAD axis. Figure 2B shows that upregulation of canonical TEAD target genes (CTGF, CYR61, ANKRD1) following XMU-MP-1 treatment. Western blot analysis in Fig. 2C shows a decrease in nuclear YAP protein levels after XMU-MP-1 treatment; this reduction was confirmed as statistically significant by densitometry (Fig. 2D), consistent with the immunofluorescence data. Immunofluorescence staining with XMU-MP-1 revealed that this exposure partially reversed the fibrotic changes induced by mechanical loading, as evidenced by decreased COL1 and increased COL2 levels compared to controls. TEAD inhibition attenuates YAP-dependent transcriptional activation

To test whether YAP-mediated transcription relies on TEAD interaction, cells were treated with XMU-MP-1 in the presence or absence of the YAP/TAZ–TEAD inhibitor Verteporfin (VP). Western blot analyses (Fig. 3A) showed that total YAP levels remained unchanged, and YAP phosphorylation was reduced by XMU-MP-1 but unaffected by VP. Nuclear YAP, TAZ, and TEAD levels were elevated upon XMU-MP-1 treatment and remained high in the presence of VP, indicating that VP does not prevent nuclear translocation. In contrast, qPCR analysis revealed that canonical TEAD target genes (CTGF, CYR61, ANKRD1) were upregulated by XMU-MP-1 but substantially reduced with VP co-treatment (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that nuclear accumulation of YAP induced by XMU-MP-1 persisted in VP-treated cells (Fig. 3C). From a therapeutic perspective, targeting the YAP–TEAD interface could block ECM remodeling without altering YAP nuclear distribution. By contrast, Col2 was high in control cells, suppressed by XMU-MP-1, and restored by VP. Immunofluorescence staining further supported these findings, showing robust Col1 and α-SMA signals after XMU-MP-1 treatment that were markedly reduced with VP, while Col2 expression was rescued (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrate that YAP activation drives profibrotic ECM remodeling through TEAD-dependent transcription, and that pharmacologic disruption of the YAP–TEAD complex effectively reverses these changes. This highlights the YAP/TAZ–TEAD axis as a central hub linking mechanical cues to fibrotic gene expression, and suggests that selective blockade of YAP–TEAD interactions could mitigate mechanically induced fibrosis without globally suppressing YAP signaling, offering a potential targeted therapeutic strategy.

Discussion

This study provides in vitro correlational evidence that mechanical stress–induced ECM remodeling in NP cells is associated with activation of the YAP/TAZ–TEAD signaling axis. Mechanical stretch increased nuclear YAP localization and altered collagen expression, while pharmacological interventions with Verteporfin and XMU-MP-1 were correlated with a reversal of these fibrotic changes. Although this preliminary correlational evidence, the direct mechanistic validation should be performed6,10,14.

Importantly, Verteporfin and XMU-MP-13 were used to explore potential pathway interactions under controlled in vitro conditions. The results clearly showed that inhibition of YAP nuclear accumulation or TEAD palmitoylation coincides with reduced fibrotic marker expression and restoration of ECM balance11,12; however, co-immunoprecipitation or palmitoylation assays should be employed to define the specific biochemical interactions between TEAD and YAP where the findings highlight the potential of targeting this signaling axis8,9.

Although the exact molecular bases were unknown, it has long been known that mechano-transduction in NP cells plays a key role in intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD). Recent studies1 have shown that the nuclear translocation of transcriptional regulators such as YAP in response to mechanical loading is not a downstream consequence but a critical determinant of variation in outcomes in mechanically sensitive tissues. We show that YAP nuclear entry is essential for fibrotic gene expression in NP cells, consistent with observations in other fibrotic tissues, including the kidney2, myocardium3, and skin4.

Piezo1 is increasingly recognized as a pliable mechanosensory ion channel that is important across many organ systems. In vascular biology studies, Piezo1 has been found to respond to hemodynamic shear stress by manipulating endothelial permeability and inflammation downstream of transcriptional regulators such as YAP/TAZ5. In addition, Piezo1 has been suggested to undergo adipocyte mechano-sensation and adipogenesis under compressive stress6. Inhibition of TEAD palmitoylation was associated with the expression of Piezo1. This observation suggests a possible crosstalk between Piezo1 functions and YAP/TEAD signaling, but further studies are needed.

In recent years, TEAD palmitoylation has attracted attention and is becoming increasingly important in cancer biology, where TEAD auto-palmitoylation is required for its stability and DNA binding capacity8. Using a MST1/2 inhibitor XMU-MP-1, we found that impairment of TEAD’s post-translational modification prevented YAP from entering the nucleus and restored a balanced ECM in NP cells. These data confirm emerging data that TEAD palmitoylation can be targeted to selectively alter fibrotic and proliferative diseases, including malignant pleural mesothelioma and hepatocellular carcinoma9,10.

Developed initially as a photosensitizer, verteporfin has been repurposed as a pharmacological YAP–TEAD disruptor and can attenuate fibrosis in multiple preclinical models. For instance, verteporfin demonstrated broad-spectrum efficacy by blocking myofibroblast activation and collagen deposition in a murine model of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis without light activation11. In models of systemic sclerosis, Verteporfin diminished dermal fibrosis by inhibiting TAZ/TEAD-mediated transcription12. Finally, the results support these findings and provide the first evidence that Verteporfin can modulate NP cell mechanobiology, extending Verteporfin’s clinical utility beyond optic nerve head diseases to spinal degenerative diseases.

The study findings reflected the significant changes in ECM remodeling, notably a transition from COL2 to COL1 dominance, consistent with the pathological changes observed in degenerated intervertebral discs. Previous work has linked this collagen profile transition to matrix stiffness and associated disc hydration loss, both hallmarks of IDD progression13. This shift is mainly driven by YAP/TEAD-driven transcriptional reprogramming. The activation of YAP in hepatic stellate cells has been associated with COL1 induction via direct TEAD binding to promoter regions of fibrosis-associated genes14. From our results showing that TEAD inhibition reverses COL1 upregulation, we infer that a similar mechanism is active in NP cells.

Furthermore, the study provided mechanistic insights into combination therapeutic strategies. Targeting TEAD, presumably via the exact mechanism, is anticipated to have dual benefits: suppressing downstream fibrotic gene expression and upstream mechanosensory expression (Piezo1). The combinatorial regulatory control of this sort could be critical to prevent the self-amplification that is a hallmark of mechano-transudative signaling in NP cells.

Study limitations

The present study has certain limitations. Regarding Verteporfin, while our data provide correlational evidence that it attenuates YAP/TAZ–TEAD–mediated gene expression and ECM remodeling, which can significantly affect YAP/TAZ localization signaling15, direct mechanistic or biochemical validation, such as a Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), mass spectrometry–based palmitoylation analysis, to demonstrate reduced YAP–TEAD interaction, was not performed. As a result, the proposed effects of XMU-MP-1 on TEAD palmitoylation and the potential feedback from TEAD to Piezo1 cannot be confirmed. In addition, all experiments were conducted in a 2D in vitro model, which may not fully reflect the 3D biochemical environment of the intervertebral disc. Future work shall include these assays to validate the findings using organotypic disc cultures or in vivo animal models to further strengthen the mechanistic link. Extending these studies to 3D hydrogel cultures or organotypic disc explant models would enhance translational relevance. Additionally, dose–response and time-course studies could further clarify the relationship between pharmacological modulation and downstream ECM remodeling, and we are actively planning such experiments for future investigations. (Supplementary 1 and 2).

Conclusion

In summary, we establish a direct link between mechanical stress, Hippo/YAP–TEAD signaling, and ECM remodeling. By dissecting the requirement for YAP activation and TEAD-mediated transcription, our study advances the understanding of mechanotransduction in fibrosis and identifies actionable targets for therapeutic intervention in mechanically driven fibrotic diseases. However, mechanistic exploration and biochemical validation are needed to prove these findings. In addition, future studies shall include experiments that address TEAD palmitoylation status, YAP–TEAD interaction, and possible feedback regulation of Piezo1.
