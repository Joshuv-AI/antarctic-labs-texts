Transapical beating heart septal myectomy learning curve and training of future surgeons: an observational study
Authors: Jiangtao Li, Yue Chen, Rui Li, Jing Fang, Eduard Quintana, Xiang Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Description of the learning curve for transapical beating heart septal myectomy (TA-BSM) helps to understand the potential for wider adaptability. The authors elaborate and examine a competency-based training assessment for TA-BSM that could serve to disseminate septal myectomy expertise.Materials and methods:Data on 177 consecutive patients who underwent the TA-BSM for hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) between April 2022 and June 2023 was collected prospectively, which was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Using the cumulative sum analysis, the authors identified two distinct learning phases based on the procedural time. Outcomes of procedural success and major complications were also compared between phases. Assessment through a Likert scale competency-based assessment of a myectomy surgeon after on-site TA-BSM training.Results:The cumulative sum analysis of procedural time showed two well-differentiated phases: the initial phase (cases 1–44) and the proficiency phase (cases 45–177). After 44 cases, significant improvements in procedural time were observed (113 vs. 134 min; P<0.001). Optimal procedural success (left ventricular outflow tract gradient <30 mmHg and mitral regurgitation grade ≤1+ without mortality or median sternotomy conversion) increased in the proficiency phase (93.2 vs. 79.5%; P=0.018), while complications decreased. The assessment of another surgeon showed consistent agreement in operative decision-making and performance for all evaluated domains.Conclusions:Competence in TA-BSM was achieved after 44 cases, without sacrificing guideline-desired outcomes. Appropriate training of other septal myectomy surgeons could further expedite this learning curve. This new technique may represent an option for wider dissemination of HOCM surgery worldwide and improve disease management.

Transaortic septal myectomy (SM) is considered the gold standard septal reduction therapy (SRT) for hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM)1. The operation performed through a full sternotomy, on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and cardioplegic arrest usually abolishes obstruction resulting in symptom relief2.

Successful surgical techniques for HOCM, under direct surgical vision, have evolved throughout six decades. The operation, SM, performed in specialized centers has shown an excellent safety profile. However, several limitations may hinder the widespread use of this approach to address the high prevalence of HOCM, including the narrow operative window and lack of real-time surveillance during surgery. Therefore, such outcomes have not been reproduced by general cardiovascular surgery practices3. Importantly, the classic transaortic SM implies a steep learning curve with limited room for imperfections, which may explain poor worldwide adaptability4.

In 2023, data from the first-in-man experience with a novel surgical approach to HOCM was reported5. The operation known as transapical beating heart septal myectomy (TA-BSM) pursues the same goal as the classic transaortic operation, which is the resolution of obstruction and secondary mitral regurgitation (MR) due to systolic anterior motion (SAM). The avoidance of sternotomy, CPB, and cardiac ischemia have provided for the first time a truly less invasive surgical option. In addition, real-time echocardiographic guidance allows a tailored resection with immediate confirmation of appropriate septectomy.

Currently, TA-BSM reported experience has preserved gold-standard guideline-desired outcomes for HOCM patients in need of SRT6. Comparatively, data between the transaortic SM operation – at other centers of excellence – and TA-BSM experience suggests that the new approach would match the classic approach observed outcomes7. The novel approach has reached enough maturity to be considered a serious option to be disseminated beyond this single-center achievement. Nonetheless, TA-BSM is a difficult operation that may require a deep understanding of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy pathophysiology and, ideally, pre-existent surgical experience in SM.

As with any new technique, there may be procedural complications during the initial stages of implementation. The learning curve phenomenon, defined as the improvement in clinical outcomes with increasing experience, has been observed in cardiac surgeries8. Therefore, it is important to assess the outcomes throughout the implementation of this experimental approach with TA-BSM. Understanding the learning curve is crucial for identifying patients who may be at risk of suboptimal outcomes during the initial learning period and offering well-informed alternative options to patients. This is even more relevant with the existence of a consolidated surgical option – SM – that provides already excellent outcomes within the frame of established HOCM practices.

In the present study, we aimed to characterize the learning curve of TA-BSM and investigate the relationship between the operator experience and clinical outcomes in a single center. Moreover, we review the training experience of another surgeon embracing this innovative approach. We developed and assessed a training plan for surgeons in preparation for the implementation of this new technique into other existing HCM programs. We discuss the future potential of TA-BSM for the dissemination of surgical HOCM expertise.

Materials and methods

Patients

Data on consecutive patients who underwent the TA-BSM procedure for HOCM between April 2022 and June 2023 were collected prospectively, which was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. This learning curve study was planned in priori and initiated with the launching of the prospective single-center, single-arm, first-in-man clinical trial. Inclusion criteria were patients with 1) a resting or provoked left ventricular intracavitary gradient >50 mmHg and a maximal septal thickness ≥15 mm; 2) drug-refractory symptoms, including chest pain, dyspnea, or syncope/presyncope; and 3) a consent form to approve the TA-BSM procedure. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) pregnancy; 2) younger than 18 years; 3) prior SRT, including conventional SM, alcohol septal ablation, and radiofrequency ablation of the intraventricular septum; and 4) concomitant diseases needing surgery, such as primary valvular disease or coronary artery disease. Data from consecutive patients beginning with their initial surgical procedure were collected, and a total of 177 patients were included in this study. Using the cumulative sum analysis, we identified two distinct learning phases based on the procedural time. Outcomes of procedural success and major complications were also compared between the two phases. All TA-BSM procedures were performed by a single surgeon with more than 20 years of surgical experience, including surgery for HOCM.

To ensure ethical compliance, all patients signed a written informed consent form. This study complied with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards. The work has been reported in line with the strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery (STROCSS) criteria9 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C741).

Preoperative evaluation

Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography was performed to determine the septal morphology, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradient, SAM of the anterior mitral leaflet, and MR grade. Patients with a resting gradient <50 mmHg underwent provocation tests, including the Valsalva maneuver, treadmill test, repetitive squat-to-stand maneuver, and isoproterenol infusion. The MR grades were classified into 0 (none), 1+ (mild), 2+ (moderate), 3+ (moderate to severe), and 4+ (severe). Preoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was additionally performed for patients with unclear mitral valve abnormality to exclude primary mitral valve disease.

The TA-BSM procedure and perioperative management

The BMD (Wei-Xin-Tan Cooperation) was used to perform all TA-BSM procedures. Details of our surgical procedures for TA-BSM have been published previously10. Briefly, patients were supine position and draped for left mini-thoracotomy under general anesthesia. After a fifth intercostal incision and suspension of the pericardium, two sets of purse string sutures supported with Teflon pledgets were placed. After puncturing and enlarging the apical incision, BMD was cautiously introduced under the TEE surveillance. We performed the first resection in the basal anterior septum, located usually 5–10 mm beneath the orifice of the right coronary artery. Left ventricular morphology, SAM, MR, and hemodynamics were re-evaluated after each resection. The second resection was also performed in the basal anterior septum adjacent to the previous. The resection of the basal posterior septum was performed by rotating the BMD anticlockwise from the location of the first resection. For those concomitant hypertrophied midventricular septum, the excisions were made after pulling the instrument further towards the apex. Obstruction resolution was confirmed in resting conditions and after provocation with an induced premature ventricular contraction. Provocation with isoprenaline was utilized when judged clinically appropriate. A repeat resection would be performed in the presence of residual obstruction (a provoked gradient >30 mmHg). Finally, the apical port was closed tying the purse strings and reinforcement sutures were applied. All patients were subsequently transferred to the ICU for postoperative management under mechanical ventilation. Most patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography 1 week after the procedure or before discharge.

Outcome definitions

Procedural time was defined as the time from skin incision to skin closure. Major complications included 30-day mortality, median sternotomy conversion, stroke, cardiac perforation, mitral valve injury, left ventricle apex rupture, or reoperation for bleeding. The primary outcome of this study was in-hospital procedural success, analyzed according to the following definitions:Optimal: LVOT gradient <30 mmHg and MR grade ≤1+ without mortality or median sternotomy conversion.Acceptable: LVOT gradient <50 mmHg and MR grade ≤2+ without mortality or median sternotomy conversion.

New surgeon training and competency-based assessment

An external HCM surgeon with operative experience of >350 SM cases underwent on-site training for TA-BSM, under the supervision of the experienced team for 5 weeks (14–19th September 2023, and January-February 2024) and associated facilities in Wuhan (China). The first week of training included observation of TA-BSM operations, device manipulation training, and TA-BSM practice in a swine model. The following 3 weeks, consisted of immersion in clinical practice, from perioperative evaluation of patients to scrubbing and participation in HCM operations. During the 4th week, the experienced TA-BSM surgeon and the surgeon-in-training itemized the most relevant steps of the procedure, delineating a competency-based training model and assessment for TA-BSM. This included a series of steps to be ranked on a 5-point Likert scale11,12 (Table 1) to quantify the degree of agreement between both surgeons on operative decision-making and performance. This operative component assessment was used to evaluate the external HCM surgeon during the last week of training.

Statistical analysis

To determine the learning curve phases, we used the procedural time as the marker of efficiency. We modeled the association between case number and procedural time using cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis13. The CUSUM is the running total of the deviation between the raw value of individual cases and the mean data of the total cohort. Thus, CUSUM procedural time was defined as ∑i=1n(xi−μ) , where x i is the procedural time of each case and μ is the mean procedural time of the cohort. In this way, the CUSUM curve portrays trends in data that are not discernable with other approaches. The turning point was calculated from the CUSUM chart. After identifying the turning point, patients were divided into two groups: the initial phase (cases 1–44, n=44) and the proficiency phase (cases 45–177, n=133).

Data are shown as median (interquartile range) for continuous variable and n (%) for categoric variables. Continuous variables were analyzed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and categorical variables were analyzed by χ2 test or Fisher exact test.

A multivariable restricted cubic spline regression model with four knots was used to assess the potential nonlinear relationship between case number and operation time, procedural success, and major complications. These relationships were plotted as curves for case number versus clinical outcomes and any given slope along the curve represented the rate of change in clinical outcomes with increasing procedural experience. The covariates in the adjusted mixed models are listed in Supplementary Table 1 (Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/JS9/C740). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was taken to indicate significance.

Learning curve

The raw procedural time was plotted in the chronologic case number (Fig. 1A). The procedural time gradually decreased, but the trend was unclear. Therefore, the CUSUM procedural time-learning curve was plotted and showed that the turning point was 44 cases, which indicated a point of trend transition (Fig. 1B). After identifying the turning point, we obtained two well-differentiated phases. Phase 1 (the initial 44 cases) represented the initial phase. Phase 2 (the proficient 133 cases) represented the period of high skill in the TA-BSM procedure. When the demographics, clinical, and echocardiographic parameters of our cohort were assessed in each of the two distinct phases of the learning curve that we identified, few differences were seen between patients in each learning period confirming the uniformity of the cohort analyzed (Table 2).

Multivariable-adjusted analyses

Multivariable-adjusted learning curves for procedural time, procedural success, and major complications were constructed using case number as a continuous variable (Fig. 2). Multivariable adjusted restricted cubic splines regression models showed a nonlinear association between the case number with the procedural time (P for nonlinearity <0.001). In addition, the inflection point (44 cases) was visually evident and continued improvement in optimal and acceptable procedural success was observed for the entire case sequence up to 177 cases. A similar finding for a relation between a threshold of 44 cases and lower rates of major complications was also evident.

New surgeon training and competency-based assessment

A total of 33 TA-BSM cases culminated in the overall training experience. Observation during five operations and direct participation in the remaining 28 cases. In the last week, this surgeon was submitted to an on-site multilevel assessment on the level of agreeability with the experienced surgeon. During the last phase of training (eight cases) categorical multiple items scoring on the specific surgical components for TA-BSM were used to determine the ability to adopt the technique using the method of the experienced team in TA-BSM. The assessment provided consistent satisfactory results in each evaluated domain. In fact, all items for all patients were scored mainly 5 (and on limited occasions 4) and there were no disagreements on operative decision-making or performance.

Discussion

The present study supports that 44 successive TA-BSM cases were necessary to overcome the preliminary phase and attain proficiency in the first-in-man experience. Notably, procedural time served as a surrogate marker for efficiency; nonetheless, further analysis of clinical outcomes showed a consistent improvement in procedural success and decreased complications as the learning curve progressed. Importantly, this association persisted in multivariable regression models adjusting for baseline characteristics, indicating that these improvements cannot be attributed to patient selection. More resections and weight of myocardium were achieved with increasing operator experience to relieve obstruction and MR.

As with conventional SM at centers of excellence14, the median LVOT gradient after the procedure was 13 mmHg in this study. Only six patients (3.4%) had residual obstruction (LVOT gradient ≥30 mmHg), which disappeared in two of six patients at 3 months follow-up. In this study, we found that optimal procedural success (defined as post-LVOT gradient <30 mmHg with MR grade ≤1+ without mortality or median sternotomy conversion) was achieved in ~80% of patients following TA-BSM procedure in the initial phase. Importantly, even during early experience with TA-BSM, guidelines desired outcomes for septal reduction therapies were fulfilled. This is a very significant consideration, as the ability to maintain satisfactory surgical outcomes appears possible. The reported experience demonstrates the potential to deliver an operation that is equivalent in efficacy to the gold standard, without the need for CPB. The new approach has demonstrated great potential to decrease atrioventricular blockage compared to the already low incidence of pacemakers seen after conventional SM15. Only one patient (0.6%) with preoperative normal intraventricular conduction developed atrioventricular block. These findings suggested that the TA-BSM procedure was a safe surgical technique even in the early stage of this first surgeon experience.

Surgical innovation aims to minimize invasiveness and enhance clinical outcomes. An operation proves further value when it becomes easy to adopt and is reproducible. Assuming proper training and mentorship, it is reasonable to consider that for other experienced HCM surgeons, TA-BSM could be adopted without outcome penalties. In this report, we have also analyzed the learning curve of another surgeon undergoing TA-BSM training that may help understand the practicalities of the adoption of the new technique. The accumulated knowledge from the existent first center experience and proper training pathways may further decrease the observed number of cases (44) required to achieve the proficiency described here.

Training experience from an already-established myectomy surgeon provides a testimony to the potential of TA-BSM for all septal phenotypes and the safety of this operation. Undeniably, validation of this training experience will be obtained after unsupervised independent practice, later, in other institutions and teams. The experience reported here, though, has revealed that it seems possible to approach HOCM surgery with a standardized methodology. Operative management with TA-BSM requires expert echocardiographic support throughout the procedure. To ensure appropriate guidance, training of the echocardiographers involved in these cases is equally essential to avoid errors in the learning phase of technique implementation. In this research, we did not analyze the importance of this accompanying expertise. Our vision is aligned with the need for simultaneous surgical and sonographer training in TA-BSM for the future adoption of the procedure by dedicated teams. We believe that future steps to transfer expertise and neutralize learning curves may require a good theoretical frame, observation of experienced teams, animal or simulation lab training, and mentorship.

How long other HCM surgeons should train before adopting TA-BSM safely could be extrapolated from our experience. We believe 3–4 weeks of intense exposure (or around 30 cases) may enable overcoming parts of the original surgeon’s learning curve. Our data shows that interoperator agreement on operative decision-making and performance was achieved at the end of the training period. This supports that cognitive and operative skill preparation could be attained by myectomy surgeons through a similar process. Our perception and data support that both surgeons demonstrated an equivalent ‘surgical’ thinking process during the operations.

The actual inadequate number of expert myectomy surgeons represents a significant clinical issue for patients with HOCM and a key impediment to optimal management of the disease worldwide. This reality is also a limitation for countries with well-developed healthcare systems where surgery is underutilized16. The combination of lack of surgical expertise and popularization of promising myosin inhibitor therapies may further complicate the surgical training of the next generation of surgeons. This could result in a gradual decline in the ability to offer one of the most effective and rewarding interventions for HOCM patients. The economic burden for healthcare systems imposed by the current price of myosin inhibitors requires in-depth rethinking as it may not be cost-effective17. Conversely, TA-BSM may represent an extremely effective, one-time low-risk intervention, delivered through a minimally invasive strategy. The reach of this new operation could be broadened and serve patients that would never be candidates for SM in developing countries where surgeons are lacking of long-term training period believed necessary to perform SM effectively18.

Another question is whether TA-BSM may facilitate the expansion of surgical treatment for HOCM by new operators without previous experience in HCM surgery. The nature of this real-time echo-guided intervention could overcome some of the limitations found when performing classic transaortic SM. Its ability to observe the gradual impact of each resection and reach enough distally into the ventricle are major benefits. This could translate into better resolution of obstruction and fewer complications in the learning phases for new ‘myectomy’ surgeons. Undeniably, this could finally become a solution to expand surgery for HCM outside the very few selected and insufficient centers and ensure the survivability of SM. Our description of operative steps incorporation followed by supervised practice could be an asset for future training in TA-BSM. Finally, an objective appraisal of technical skills in this surgery could be feasible and useful.

From a practical standpoint, the best phenotypes to safely initiate a TA-BSM program may be basal phenotypes (subaortic septum ideally <40 mm of length >18 mm of septal thickness) and not excessively long mitral valve leaflets. It is probably wise to avoid sigmoid thin septum and apical phenotypes until expertise is gained. In the future, it is likely that such HCM phenotypes and nonobstructive HCM may still benefit from referral to the most experienced centers in TA-BSM.

Despite our enthusiasm towards TA-BSM, it remains to be determined whether large-scale reproducibility will be achieved, as this is not an easy operation. Also, a randomized clinical trial may be required to establish this therapy as a new goal standard for a surgical septal reduction in patients who are candidates for isolated SM.

Study limitations

First, it is a single-center study and despite adjustment for various factors, unmeasured confounders may exist. Second, our analysis was primarily focused on the learning curve of the surgeon, and its interaction with the entire heart team was not specifically assessed, which could have an impact on the learning curve. Third, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon, thereby limiting the generalizability of the learning curve to other surgeons. The results of our study need to be validated in larger cohorts with more surgeons. Lastly, the pre-existent experience of the surgeon receiving training may greatly influence our interpretations and require further validation.

Conclusions

Competence in TA-BSM was achieved after 44 cases without sacrificing guideline-desired outcomes guidelines for SRT. Appropriate training of other SM surgeons could further expedite this learning curve. This new technique may represent an option for wider dissemination of HOCM surgery worldwide and further improve outcomes.
