Investigation of the Research Competence of Applicants Enrolling into Doctoral Programs in Healthcare Professions: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Vitaliy Koikov, Gulmira Derbissalina, Ayagoz Umbetzhanova, Laura Kassym, Natalya Zemlyanskaya, Gulzhan Alibekova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSuccessful doctoral training requires well-developed research competence; however, evidence regarding the preparedness of prospective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions remains limited. This study aimed to assess research competence among prospective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions in Kazakhstan.Patients and MethodsA nationwide multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted among prospective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions in Kazakhstan. Of 539 eligible participants, 500 completed the adapted Research Competence Questionnaire and were included in the final analysis. Descriptive analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. The psychometric evaluation of the adapted questionnaire included assessment of internal consistency, sampling adequacy, and construct validity using exploratory factor analysis in R version 4.6.1. Differences in research competence between master’s students and residents were assessed using Welch’s t-test.ResultsThe mean overall research competence score was 84.37 ± 19.43. Participants demonstrated higher competence in the Knowledge domain (34.07 ± 8.83) than in the Skills (30.32 ± 7.96) and Attitude (19.98 ± 5.28) domains. Master’s students demonstrated significantly higher overall research competence than residents (88.63 ± 15.61 vs 82.72 ± 20.87; p = 0.003), with significantly higher Knowledge and Skills scores, whereas no significant difference was observed in the Attitude domain. The adapted questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency and supported the original three-domain structure.ConclusionProspective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions demonstrated satisfactory overall research competence according to the original scoring system; however, important gaps remained in practical research competencies required for independent scientific work. These findings suggest that strengthening applied research training during postgraduate education may better prepare future doctoral candidates for independent research.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of biomedical knowledge, the increasing complexity of healthcare systems, and the growing emphasis on evidence-based medicine have substantially strengthened the role of research in health professions education. Healthcare professionals are expected not only to critically appraise and apply scientific evidence but also to generate new knowledge capable of improving patient care, healthcare organization, and public health outcomes. Consequently, doctoral education has become the principal mechanism for preparing independent researchers with advanced methodological expertise, critical thinking, research integrity, scientific communication skills, and the ability to translate research findings into clinical practice and health policy. This vision is reflected in several international policy documents, including the Salzburg Principles, the Salzburg II Recommendations, the Vitae Researcher Development Framework, and the UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science, which consistently emphasize that doctoral programmes should prepare competent researchers capable of addressing contemporary scientific and societal challenges.1–3

The internationalization of doctoral education has been strongly influenced by the Bologna Process, which established doctoral education as the third cycle of higher education and promoted competency-based research training. In line with these reforms, Kazakhstan modernized its doctoral education system to strengthen research capacity, increase the international competitiveness of scientific research, and prepare independent researchers capable of contributing to evidence-based healthcare. Consequently, research competence has become one of the key competencies expected from applicants entering doctoral programmes in healthcare.

Despite its widespread use in educational research, research competence remains a concept without a universally accepted definition. Contemporary literature increasingly conceptualizes research competence as a multidimensional construct integrating research knowledge, methodological expertise, critical appraisal of scientific evidence, study design, biostatistics, scientific writing, research ethics, project management, communication, collaboration, and dissemination of research findings. The systematic review by Salahuddin et al highlighted substantial conceptual heterogeneity while identifying common domains underlying research competence across different educational settings.4 Similarly, a systematic review demonstrated that effective research preparation requires integration of methodological, analytical, communication, and professional competencies rather than isolated technical research skills.5 Collectively, these findings support the view that research competence represents a comprehensive educational outcome requiring systematic development and valid assessment.

Accordingly, strengthening research competence has become a strategic priority throughout health professions education. Numerous educational initiatives have been introduced to improve learners’ ability to conduct research, critically evaluate scientific evidence, and participate in scholarly activities. Evidence consistently demonstrates that structured research training improves methodological knowledge, research self-efficacy, scientific productivity, and long-term academic engagement.6–8 Furthermore, studies focusing on doctoral education indicate that successful doctoral education depends not only on curriculum quality but also on applicants entering doctoral programmes with an adequate level of research competence.9–12

In Kazakhstan, strengthening research capacity has become an important component of higher education reform and healthcare development. Medical universities have expanded research infrastructure, encouraged student participation in scientific projects, promoted publication activity, and implemented initiatives aimed at developing research competencies. Previous national studies have examined medical students’ interest in research, the educational impact of research participation, the development of supportive research environments within medical universities, and the scientific performance of higher education institutions.13–15 These studies collectively demonstrate Kazakhstan’s growing commitment to strengthening research culture and scientific capacity. However, these studies primarily focused on undergraduate education, institutional research environments, or research productivity rather than on evaluating the research competence and preparedness of individuals entering doctoral education.

Although previous studies have investigated research interest, research participation, research environments, and research productivity among medical students, doctoral students, and academic institutions, considerably less attention has been paid to research competence as a multidimensional construct among applicants entering doctoral education. Understanding applicants’ baseline research competence is essential because deficiencies identified before enrolment may be addressed through targeted educational interventions during doctoral training. Moreover, assessment of research competence before admission may provide information that cannot be obtained from academic transcripts, publication records, or entrance examinations alone, thereby enabling early identification of educational needs and facilitating the development of targeted training strategies. To the best of our knowledge, no published study has comprehensively evaluated the research competence of applicants seeking admission to doctoral programmes in healthcare in Kazakhstan. Addressing this knowledge gap may contribute to improving admission procedures, optimizing doctoral curricula, and strengthening national research capacity in healthcare.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the research competence of applicants enrolling in doctoral programmes in healthcare professions and to identify strengths and deficiencies across the principal domains of research competence.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This nationwide multicenter observational cross-sectional study was conducted between November 1, 2023, and February 16, 2024, to assess research competencies among prospective applicants to doctoral programs in healthcare in the Republic of Kazakhstan. Data were collected continuously throughout the study period using an anonymous online questionnaire administered via Google Forms. The study involved medical universities and medical faculties of multidisciplinary universities across Kazakhstan.

Participants and Recruitment

Eligible participants included first- and second-year master’s students, final-year medical residents, faculty members, and practicing physicians planning to pursue doctoral studies. Individuals already enrolled in doctoral programs and respondents who submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the study.

The survey link was distributed through dean’s offices, postgraduate education departments, institutional Moodle platforms, and messaging applications. A total of 539 individuals were invited to participate. Of these, 500 completed the questionnaire and were included in the final analysis, corresponding to a response rate of 92.8%.

Survey Instrument

The study employed the Research Competence of Postgraduate Students questionnaire developed by Afolabi et al.16 The instrument was originally designed to assess research competence among postgraduate students across three domains: knowledge, skills, and attitudes. The questionnaire had previously been used to assess research competence among postgraduate healthcare trainees.

The questionnaire consists of 31 items organized into three domains: Knowledge (12 items), Skills (11 items), and Attitudes (8 items). Responses are recorded using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 31 to 124, with higher scores indicating higher levels of research competence. Based on the original scoring system, total scores were categorized into low (31.00–41.33), moderate (41.34–82.66), and high (82.67–124.00) levels of research competence.

The questionnaire comprised two sections. The first section collected demographic and educational characteristics, including age, sex, institution, educational status (master’s student, resident, or practicing physician), and medical specialty. The second section consisted of the 31-item research competence questionnaire.

The original questionnaire was independently translated into Kazakh by two bilingual researchers. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Data Collection

Data were collected electronically using Google Forms. Before accessing the questionnaire, all participants were provided with information about the study objectives and procedures and gave electronic informed consent. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Completion of all questionnaire items was mandatory before submission; therefore, no missing data were present in the final dataset.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Astana Medical University (Protocol No. 3 from 02/11/2023). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. Participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous, and all collected data were treated confidentially throughout the study.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between master’s students and residents were performed using Welch’s independent-samples t-test. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Psychometric analyses were performed using R version 4.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Construct validity was evaluated using exploratory factor analysis based on a polychoric correlation matrix. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The number of factors was determined using parallel analysis.

Results

Psychometric Properties of the Questionnaire

The questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.965 for the Knowledge domain, 0.964 for the Skills domain, and 0.907 for the Attitude domain. The data were highly suitable for factor analysis, as indicated by an excellent Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.945) and a statistically significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2(465) = 22,708.88, p < 0.001). Exploratory factor analysis was performed based on the theoretical three-domain structure of the original questionnaire. Although parallel analysis suggested a possible four-factor solution, the three-factor solution was retained because it was conceptually consistent with the original instrument. Most items demonstrated substantial primary loadings on their intended factors, with only three items showing cross-loadings ≥0.30, supporting the multidimensional structure of the adapted questionnaire.

General Characteristics of the Study Participants

A total of 539 eligible individuals participated in the survey. Of these, 500 completed all questionnaire items and were included in the final analysis, corresponding to a completion rate of 92.8%. Thirty-nine incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis. Participants had a mean age of 27.8 ± 4.3 years, and most were women (353, 70.6%). The majority were residents (350, 70.0%), followed by master’s students (92, 18.4%), faculty members (42, 8.4%), and practicing physicians (16, 3.2%). Most participants specialized in Medicine (405, 81.0%), and 338 (67.6%) reported planning to enroll in doctoral studies. Knowledge Domain

Within the Knowledge domain, respondents demonstrated the highest confidence in explaining the importance of their research (3.02 ± 0.79), formulating research objectives (2.93 ± 0.86), and conducting literature reviews (2.92–2.93). In contrast, the lowest confidence was observed for using modern statistical methods (2.70 ± 0.88), applying relevant theories (2.71 ± 0.90), selecting appropriate research methodology (2.73 ± 0.88), and developing conceptual models (2.78 ± 0.89).

Skills Domain

Within the Skills domain, respondents reported the greatest confidence in explaining the purpose of their research (3.00 ± 0.76), justifying its significance (2.96 ± 0.78), and preparing literature reviews or interpreting study findings (both 2.85 ± 0.83). The lowest confidence was observed for planning research (2.53 ± 0.89), selecting appropriate statistical methods (2.64 ± 0.88), developing conceptual models (2.64 ± 0.88), and preparing the study rationale (2.68 ± 0.88).

Attitude Domain

Within the Attitude domain, respondents expressed the highest mean score in writing literature reviews (2.65 ± 0.84). However, they most strongly agreed that research is a complex and stressful process (2.71 ± 0.86). Moderate levels of concern were also reported regarding writing research rationales (2.46 ± 0.85), formulating research problems (2.47 ± 0.85), developing research questions and hypotheses (2.46 ± 0.86), and understanding research concepts (2.45 ± 0.84). The lowest agreement was observed for stress associated with formulating research aims and objectives (2.37 ± 0.84) and for the statement that selecting an appropriate methodology is easy (2.40 ± 0.85).

Comparison Between Residents and Master’s Students

Master’s students demonstrated significantly higher overall research competence than residents (88.63 ± 15.61 vs 82.72 ± 20.87, p = 0.003). They also achieved significantly higher scores in the Knowledge (36.57 ± 7.73 vs 33.17 ± 9.22, p < 0.001) and Skills (32.65 ± 6.56 vs 29.42 ± 8.45, p < 0.001) domains. Discussion

This nationwide multicenter study provides one of the first comprehensive assessments of research competence among prospective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions in Kazakhstan. Overall research competence corresponded to a high level according to the original scoring system; however, important differences were identified across competency domains. Participants demonstrated stronger research knowledge than practical research skills, whereas responses within the Attitude domain suggested that many participants perceived research as challenging despite recognizing its importance. In addition, master’s students demonstrated significantly higher research competence than residents, particularly in the Knowledge and Skills domains, although the observed effect sizes were small to moderate. Collectively, these findings suggest that prospective doctoral applicants possess an adequate theoretical foundation but require further development of practical competencies necessary for independent scientific work. This observation is consistent with international recommendations emphasizing that successful doctoral education requires not only disciplinary knowledge but also well-developed methodological, analytical, and transferable research skills that support independent research and lifelong scientific development.1,4,11

One of the most important findings of this study was the discrepancy between theoretical research knowledge and practical research skills. Participants generally reported adequate knowledge of fundamental research concepts, including formulating research objectives, identifying research questions, and understanding the principles of scientific inquiry. In contrast, considerably lower scores were observed for competencies requiring practical application, particularly research planning, conceptual model development, selection of appropriate statistical methods, and interpretation of research findings. This pattern suggests that current postgraduate education successfully provides a theoretical understanding of research but offers fewer opportunities to develop practical competencies through authentic research experience. Similar findings have been reported internationally. A systematic review by Lee et al (2020) concluded that although medical graduates generally acquire an adequate understanding of research methodology during their education, many feel insufficiently prepared to conduct independent research.6 Likewise, several studies demonstrated that participation in structured research activities, rather than classroom-based instruction alone, is essential for developing practical research competence.5,7 Comparable findings were also reported by Afolabi et al (2022) using the same research competence instrument, where postgraduate students demonstrated stronger knowledge-related competencies than practical research abilities.16 Together, these findings suggest that strengthening experiential learning through supervised research projects, mentorship, and applied methodological training may better prepare future doctoral applicants for independent scientific work.

Although participants generally recognized the importance of research, several findings from the Attitude domain indicate that research continues to be perceived as a demanding and psychologically challenging process. The highest agreement within this domain was observed for the statement that research is a complex and stressful activity, while lower confidence was reported for tasks related to developing research rationales, formulating research problems, and selecting appropriate methodologies. These findings suggest that awareness of the importance of research does not necessarily translate into confidence in conducting research independently. One possible explanation is that many participants have had limited opportunities to engage in all stages of the research process and therefore feel uncertain when applying methodological knowledge in practice. Similar observations were reported by Meerah (2010), who found that limited research experience was associated with lower research confidence and greater anxiety among postgraduate students preparing for doctoral education.10 Likewise, Syed Mohamed et al (2020) emphasized that successful preparation for doctoral studies depends not only on methodological knowledge and technical skills but also on research self-confidence, motivation, resilience, and a supportive academic environment.12 These studies suggest that postgraduate education should promote earlier and more sustained involvement in research activities, allowing future doctoral applicants to develop both technical competence and confidence in conducting independent research.

Master students demonstrated significantly higher research competence than residents, particularly in the Knowledge and Skills domains, although the magnitude of these differences was small to moderate. This finding is consistent with the different educational objectives of the two postgraduate pathways. Master programmes are primarily research-oriented and typically include formal training in research methodology, thesis preparation, scientific writing, and independent research projects. In contrast, residency programmes focus predominantly on developing clinical competencies, leaving fewer opportunities for structured research training despite intensive clinical practice. Similar observations have been reported in Kazakhstan, where participation in research activities during medical education was associated with greater research interest, stronger methodological competence, and higher scientific productivity.13–15 Although the observed effect sizes indicate that these differences should not be overinterpreted, they nevertheless suggest that educational environment and opportunities for active research participation contribute meaningfully to the development of research competence before doctoral enrolment.

The findings of this study have important implications for postgraduate medical education. The identified gaps in practical research competencies suggest that preparation for doctoral education should begin before admission to PhD programmes. Alongside theoretical instruction, postgraduate curricula should provide greater opportunities for supervised research, training in research methodology and biostatistics, academic writing, scientific communication, and research ethics. Within the context of Kazakhstan, strengthening research competence among healthcare professionals may further support the development of evidence-based practice and the national biomedical research system.17

This study has several strengths, including its nationwide multicentre design, relatively large sample size, inclusion of participants from different postgraduate educational pathways, and psychometric evaluation of the translated questionnaire. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because of its cross-sectional design, this study can identify associations but cannot establish causal relationships. Second, research competence was assessed using self-reported responses and therefore may not fully reflect actual research performance. Third, participants were recruited exclusively from healthcare professions, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other academic disciplines. Finally, although the translated questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency and satisfactory construct validity, further validation in independent populations would strengthen its broader applicability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that prospective applicants to doctoral programmes in healthcare professions possessed an adequate level of research competence according to the original scoring system, while identifying important gaps in practical research competencies required for independent scientific work. The findings suggest that postgraduate education could benefit from greater emphasis on applied research training, including supervised research experience, research methodology, biostatistics, and scientific communication. Strengthening these competencies before doctoral enrolment may better prepare future doctoral candidates for independent research. Further studies are needed to evaluate educational strategies aimed at improving research competence in postgraduate healthcare education.
