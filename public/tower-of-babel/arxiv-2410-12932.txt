The masses of open star clusters and their tidal tails and the stellar initial mass function
Henriette Wirth 1, František Dinnbier 1, Pavel Kroupa 1,2 and Ladislav Šubr 1

Abstract
Context. Unresolved binaries have a strong influence on the observed parameters of stellar clusters (SCs). Aims. We quantify this influence and compute the resulting mass underestimates and stellar mass function (MF). Methods. N-body simulations of realistic SCswere used to investigate the evolution of the binary population in a SCand its tidal tails. Together with an empirically gauged stellar mass-luminosity relation, the results were then used to determine how the presence of binaries changes the photometric mass and MFof the SCand its tails as deduced from observations. Results. Tail 1 (T1), which is the tidal tail caused by gas expulsion, contains a larger fraction of binaries than both the SCand tail 2 (T2), which forms after gas expulsion. Additionally, T1has a larger velocity dispersion. Using the luminosity of an unresolved binary, an observer would underestimate its mass. This bias sensitively depends on the companion masses due to the structure of the stellar mass-luminosity relation. Combining the effect of all binaries in the simulation, the total photometric mass of the SCis underestimated by 15%. Dark objects (black holes and neutron stars) increase the difference between the real and observed mass of the SCfurther. For both the SCand the tails, the observed power-law index of the MFbetween a stellar mass of 0.3 and 0.7 M ⊙ M_{\odot} is smaller by up to 0.2 than the real one, the real initial mass function (IMF)being steeper by this amount. This difference is larger for stars with a larger velocity dispersion or binary fraction. Conclusions. Since the stars formed in SCsare the progenitors of the Galactic field stars, this work suggests that the binary fractions of different populations of stars in the Galactic disc will differ as a function of the velocity dispersion. However, the direction of this correlation is currently unclear, and a complete population synthesis will be needed to investigate this effect. Variations in the binary fractions of different clusters can lead to perceived variations of the deduced stellar MFs.



1
Introduction

Stellar clusters (SCs) are the building blocks of our and other galaxies
(
Kroupa, 1995a
;
Kroupa & Boily, 2002
;
Lada & Lada, 2003
;
Kroupa, 2005
;
Portegies Zwart et al., 2010
)
.
Therefore, understanding their masses and the mass functions (MFs) within them is of the utmost importance for comprehending the Universe as we see it today.
One factor that strongly influences the measurements of these properties are unresolved binaries
(
Kroupa et al., 1991
;
Kouwenhoven & de Grijs, 2008
;
Gieles et al., 2010
;
Kroupa & Jerabkova, 2018
;
Borodina et al., 2019
;
Rastello et al., 2020
)
.

While the binary fraction in the Galaxy is
\approx 0.5

(
Duquennoy & Mayor, 1991
;
Raghavan et al., 2010
;
Marks & Kroupa, 2011
, and references therein)
, SCsare likely born with up to 100 % of their stars in binaries
(
Mathieu, 1994
;
Kroupa, 1995a
;
Kroupa, 1995b
)
.
The difference between the observed distribution functions of binary systems in the Galactic field in comparison to that observed in star-forming regions allowed
Kroupa (1995a)
;
Kroupa (1995b)
to deduce the properties of the initial distribution functions of young binary-rich populations.
There is still some debate around whether this is also true for very low-mass binaries, many of which might dissolve before reaching the main sequence or even not start out as binaries
(
Thies et al., 2015
;
Offner et al., 2023
)
.
Due to the dense environment of the clusters, the initially high binary fractions in SCsare reduced quickly
(
Kroupa, 1995a
;
Kroupa, 1995b
;
Ivanova et al., 2005
;
Goodwin & Kroupa, 2005
;
Kobulnicky & Fryer, 2007
)
.
The stars lost during SCevolution form the field stars observed in the Galaxy today
(
Kroupa, 2002b
;
Marks & Kroupa, 2011
;
Dabringhausen et al., 2022
;
Ferrone et al., 2023
)
.

Dinnbier & Kroupa (2020)
categorised these stars into tail 1 and tail 2 (T1and T2) classes.
T1stars are lost from the birth-embedded cluster due to the decrease in the gravitational potential during gas expulsion.
The T2stars, on the other hand, evaporate slowly from the SCafter this initial phase.
Both regimes overlap in phase space and so are not observed as two separated regions.
They are also contaminated by high-velocity stars ejected due to few-body interactions and supernova (SN) kicks.
These tails are expected to inherit binaries from the SC, and if the binaries are unresolved, this has implications for the observations of the tails and the SC.

One of the most common ways to determine the mass of a star is to use the measured luminosity.
Many studies have determined the mass-luminosity relation (MLR)for main-sequence stars in different mass ranges
(see e.g.
Hertzsprung, 1923
;
Russell et al., 1923
;
Kroupa et al., 1993
;
Henry & McCarthy, 1993
;
Eker et al., 2015
, and references within)
.
However, if a binary is unresolved, only the combined luminosity of its members can be measured.
An unassuming observer would take this combined luminosity and use it to compute the mass of the assumed single star.
This mass, however, does not equal the sum of the masses of the binary components.
Therefore, an observer would arrive at a wrong mass for the binary and the entire SC.

Alternatively, the mass of a virialised SC can be deduced dynamically because its dynamical mass is proportional to the square of the line-of-sight velocity dispersion
(
Illingworth, 1976
;
Spitzer, 1987
, e.g.)
.
However, for this deduction, a binary fraction of 0 is assumed.
While this mass determination might still work if centre-of-mass velocities for binaries are used, many studies have shown that the velocity dispersions for systems with large fractions of unresolved binaries are overestimated
(
Kouwenhoven & de Grijs, 2008
;
Gieles et al., 2010
;
Borodina et al., 2019
;
Rastello et al., 2020
;
Borodina et al., 2021
, see e.g.)
.
Therefore, binaries will cause an overestimate of the SCmass if it is measured dynamically.
However, long-duration observations over multiple epochs can mitigate the effect
(
Cottaar et al., 2012
;
Ramírez-Tannus et al., 2024
)
.

Cottaar & Hénault-Brunet (2014)
suggest that two epochs are sufficient for this in many SCs; however, they also point out that many binaries have periods of thousands of years, making it practically impossible to detect them.

Similarly, the presence of binaries influences the deduced MFfor low-mass stars
(
Kroupa et al., 1991
)
.
Having an unresolved binary means that, when determining the MF, two lower-mass stars are missing and one slightly more massive star is added
(
Kroupa & Jerabkova, 2018
)
.
This makes the MFappear more top-heavy.

Li et al. (2023)
investigated this effect using mock data, and it was further investigated using N-body simulations in this work.

The purpose of this work is, therefore, to determine how the binary fraction affects our understanding of the mass and MFin SCsand their tails.
For the first time, this is done using realistic N-body simulations of SCsto determine the development of the binary population.
Under the assumption that all binaries are unresolved, possible conclusions an observer would draw from these data are computed and compared to the results obtained under the assumption that all binaries are resolved.
This work is structured as follows:
Section
2
explains the methods used for this work.
The results are discussed in Sect.
3
, and Sect.
4
concludes the paper.

It is assumed that the observer is unable to resolve any of the binaries.
Since the stars spend most of their time on the main sequence the MLRfrom
Kroupa et al. (1993, in their Appendix A)
can be used to get the luminosity,

The main advantage of this MLRover others
(

;
Russell et al., 1923
;
Eker et al., 2015

is that it extends down to very low-mass stars (
0.07\penalty\ M_{\odot}

The luminosity of the members of multiple systems is then added and converted back to a mass that represents the mass an observer would assume for the unresolved system:

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{obs}}=L^{-1}\left(L(m_{1})+L(m_{2})\right),

(11)

with the masses of the individual stars,
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, and the apparent mass an observer that cannot resolve the binary would compute from the total luminosity,

Figure
2
shows the used MLR.
The grey line follows the function
M_{V}=(10.8\pm 0.1)\left(m/M_{\odot}\right)^{-0.491\pm 0.006}+(-6.1\pm 0.1)
.
This function was fitted using the data points for
m>0.96M_{\odot}
and is used to compute the luminosities for all stars with
m>1.5M_{\odot}
.
For all other masses the values of the closest red data point are used.
The magnitudes,
M_{V}
, are transformed to luminosities,
L

\displaystyle\frac{L(m)}{L_{\odot}}=10^{-0.4(M_{V}(m)-M_{V,\odot})},

(12)

where
M_{V,\odot}=4.83
is the absolute V-band magnitude of the Sun.

It is important for our work to use an observationally derived MLR, rather than a theoretical one, since the observationally derived MLRcorrectly displays the KTG peak
(
Kroupa et al., 1990
)
of the stellar luminosity function at the transition from partially convective to fully convective stars
(
Kroupa, 2002a
)
.
If not accounted for correctly, this leads to spurious results on the stellar MF
(
Kroupa et al., 1990
)
.

3.1
The apparent stellar masses of unresolved multiples

Figure 3:
Luminosity of a star (Eq.
12
and Fig.
2
) and its first and second derivative with respect to the stellar mass as a function of the stellar mass.

For the purpose of this discussion, all bright stars are assumed to be on the main sequence, that is to say, the short phases between when they are on the main sequence and when they become a black hole (BH), neutron star (NS), or white dwarf are ignored.
Since the luminosity is added instead of the masses the relationship of the luminosity and the mass determines whether or not the mass of a system is over- or underestimated.

In Fig.
3
, the
L(m)
used in this work and its first and second derivative are shown.
We note that the anomaly in the second derivative at
1.5\penalty\ M_{\odot}
stems from the transition from using the next data point from the MLRto using the fitted function.
This leads to a small jump in the derivative of
L(m)
, which is further enhanced through the second derivative.
Since the second derivative is positive,
L(m)
must be a convex function in the given interval.

Figure 4:
Examples on how the non-linearity of
L(m)
can lead to an underestimate or an overestimate of the total mass of a binary star system.

To understand, how this affects our estimate of the total mass of a binary, the following is considered:
Assume two stars with masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
.
If the function is convex in an interval
[0,m_{1}+m_{2}]
(i.e. every point lies below its chord), where
L(0)=0,
it immediately follows that

\displaystyle\frac{L(m_{1}+m_{2})}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle>\frac{L(m_{1})}{m_{1}},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{L(m_{1}+m_{2})}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle>\frac{L(m_{2})}{m_{2}}.

(14)

Multiplying with the denominator on the right side results in

\displaystyle L(m_{1}+m_{2})\frac{m_{1}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle>L(m_{1}),

(15)

\displaystyle L(m_{1}+m_{2})\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle>L(m_{2}).

(16)

Adding both equations together leads to

\displaystyle L(m_{1}+m_{2})\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}}{m_{1}+m_{2}}

\displaystyle>L(m_{1})+L(m_{2}),

(17)

\displaystyle L(m_{1}+m_{2})

\displaystyle>L(m_{1})+L(m_{2}).

(18)

Thus, the luminosities of the individual stars added up is always going to be smaller than the total luminosity of a single star with mass
m_{1}+m_{2}
.
This means that in this case the mass of an unresolved binary is always going to be underestimated.
Analogous it could be shown that for a concave function with
L(0)=0
the mass of an unresolved binary is always going to be overestimated.
An example of this can be seen in Fig.
4
.
It should be noted here that the
L(m)
used in the present work is only known down to
0.08M_{\odot}
.
This can affect whether the mass of the binaries is over- or underestimated especially for the low-mass stars.

5
shows by how much an observer would underestimate the total mass of the multiple systems from the main model (top) and the models M2 (bottom) at the end of the simulation.
The bottom plot shows all stars of the 5 models M2 together.
The real mass of a system is
m_{\rm rl}=m_{1}+m_{2}
.
The observed mass of the apparent star is lower for systems with higher
q
.
The minimum of
m_{\mathrm{obs}}/m_{\mathrm{rl}}
for binaries with both components on the main sequence is
\approx 0.55
.
All systems below this minimum are either of an order higher than two or include BHs, NSs, or other non-main-sequence stars, which are assigned a luminosity of
0\penalty\ \mathrm{L_{\odot}}

The underestimation of the masses of stellar systems leads to an underestimate of the total masses of the SCand its tails.
In all three components (SC, T1, and T2) the fraction of higher-order multiples (more than two stars) is small.
In fact, in the main model, no higher-order multiple composed of only main-sequence stars is found in the last timestep.
While the number of binaries in the SCand its tails differ, the qualitative distribution of binary masses is similar in all three components.

In addition to the underestimate of the SC, T1, and T2masses due to the above-described bias from unresolved multiple systems, the amount by which the mass of a SCwould be overestimated using the velocity dispersion is computed.
As mentioned in the introduction,
Illingworth (1976)
and
Spitzer (1987)
found that the determined dynamical mass is proportional to the square of the velocity dispersion of the centres of mass of the stellar systems in the SC,
\sigma_{\rm COM}
.
Following
Rastello et al. (2020)
it is assumed that an observer would see the centre of light movement, rather than the centre of mass movement.
The proportionality factor between the cluster mass and the velocity dispersion depends on the assumed mass profile of the cluster.
To identify merely the error caused by the velocity dispersion, we therefore used

\displaystyle M_{\rm SC}^{\rm dynamical}=M_{\rm SC}^{\rm total}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\rm COL}}{\sigma_{\rm COM}}\right)^{2},

(19)

with
\sigma_{\rm COL}
being the velocity dispersion of the centre of lights of the stellar systems.
For our calculations we use the velocity dispersions in z-direction (
\sigma_{\rm COL}=\sigma_{{\rm COL},z}
,
\sigma_{\rm COM}=\sigma_{{\rm COM},z}
).

Here it is important to determine which stars an observer would count as members of the SC, as stars with too large radial velocities are often discarded as non-members.
To this end, the stars are sorted by their distance to the SCcentre.
For each star the velocity dispersion
\sigma_{{\rm COL},z}
is computed from its own velocity and the velocities of its 20 neighbours in each direction.
The velocity dispersion is computed for each star individually to account for the velocity dispersion being larger in the centre of the SC, than in the outskirts.
Systems whose velocities are larger than
3\sigma_{{\rm COL},z}
are excluded from the calculations.
This method is applied iteratively until no stars remain outside this variance.

As seen in Fig.
7
, the dynamical mass is significantly larger than the total mass of the SC.
For the main model, we measure an overestimate by a factor of 1.86 (Table
2
) at the end of the simulation.

At the same time, the mass of the SCis underestimated by
3\penalty\ \%
due to the presence of unseen BHsand NSs and a further
15\penalty\ \%
due to unresolved binaries.
The effect caused by BHsand NSs is small since many of them get ejected from the SCdue to SN kicks.
Similarly, the mass in T1(T2) is underestimated by
14\penalty\ \%
(
6\penalty\ \%
) due to not detecting BHsand NSs and a further
14\penalty\ \%
(
14\penalty\ \%
) due to unresolved binaries.
Here, the effect of BHsand NSs is stronger, since the remnants kicked out from the SCby SNe are added to its tails in our model.
However, since these remnants are kicked out with high velocities (
\approx 40\penalty\ \rm km/s
), their phase space positions do not necessarily match those of the other tail members.
Since the most massive stars are short-lived and the majority of SNe explode early on, it is expected that dark objects play a more significant role in T1’s mass underestimate, while in the SCand T2unresolved binaries cause the majority of the underestimate.

The underestimate of the SCcaused by BHs, NSs, and binary systems is similar to that of the main model at 22 %.
We also note that the dynamical mass of the SCincreases at the end of the simulation together with the binary fraction.

Due to the square in Eq.

this would correspond to a mass overestimate by a factor of 44.8.
The main reason for that difference is that in the present work the stars with the largest velocities are removed as explained above, since this work assumes that an observer would probably not count these stars as cluster stars.

A full list of the over- and underestimates of the SCmasses at the end of the simulations can be found in Table

Both the overestimate of the dynamical mass and the underestimate of the visible mass of the SCsbecome more pronounced over time.
It is visible that the dynamical mass is both mass and time dependent.
This is mainly due to the high initial cluster density leading to a large velocity dispersion.
If a SCalready has a large velocity dispersion, binaries are not increasing the perceived velocity dispersion by a large factor.
Low-density, low-mass clusters on the other hand have a low velocity dispersion, which means that binaries increase the perceived velocity dispersion and the dynamical mass by a large factor.

As mentioned in the introduction, observers often use the line-of-sight velocity dispersion to determine the dynamical mass instead of the photometric mass.
This work clearly demonstrates that not only is the dynamical mass overestimated due to binaries; the photometric mass is significantly underestimated.
This increases the apparent discrepancy between the cluster masses determined by different methods.
This is relevant for the Hyades star cluster, which is super-virial by a factor of about two
(
Kroupa et al., 2022
, and references therein)
.
For the main model of this work, the dynamical and luminous mass differ initially by a factor of 1.6, which increases to 2.3 at the end of the simulation.
For the other models this factor is even larger.
However, many of the binaries in Hyades have been resolved
(
Gizis & Reid, 1995
)
, leaving a smaller difference than in our models that assume all binaries are unresolved.

By analysing star-count data,
Li et al. (2023)
deduced apparently systematic variations in the IMFwith the velocity dispersion in
z
-direction (perpendicular to the Galactic plane) and metallicity.
Such a variation in the IMFhas important astrophysical implications
(
Kroupa et al., 2013
)
.
Here we test if the origin of stars in star clusters may not partially or wholly account for this apparent variation.
This may result if the binary fraction and velocity dispersion conspire to lead to apparently systematic variations in the observationally deduced IMF.

The velocity dispersion vertical to the Galactic plane is used as a proxy for the line-of-sight velocity dispersion.
This is done because the velocity ellipsoid in radial direction with respect to the Galactic centre is larger than in tangential direction.
However, the orientation of these axis in the non-rotating x-y plane changes over time.
Therefore, the velocity dispersion in x- and y-direction is expected to vary with time, as the population revolves around the Galaxy
(
Dinnbier & Kroupa, 2020
, for a more detailed description of how the tails develop over time, see)
.
In the case of multiples the velocity of the centre of mass is used for the calculations.
We note that the observer would observe the SCprimarily from within the Galactic disc and, therefore, would have to account for the positions of the SCrelative to the Sun.

Snapshots of the first eight timesteps of the main model are shown in Fig.
9
.
The tidal radius, calculated according to Eq. 1 from
Baumgardt & Makino (2003)
is shown as a black circle.
Only one star escapes the SCat 0.3 Myr, which is clearly dynamically ejected.
However, in the same timestep we also see a star within the cluster with a velocity exceeding
100\penalty\ \mathrm{km/s}
.
This is typical for an ejected star about to leave.
After 0.6 Myr it is difficult to distinguish between stars that left the SCprimarily due to gas expulsion and those leaving primarily due to dynamical effects.
It is clear, however, that only a handful of stars leave the SCbefore gas expulsion.
These stars leaving the SCpropagate with a velocity of a few tens to hundreds of km/s.

The velocity dispersion for stellar systems in the SCand its tails is shown in Fig.
10
.
The high peak in the velocity dispersion at the very beginning for both the SCand T1is caused by stars being ejected due to stellar-dynamical encounters.
As gas expulsion starts the stars lost due to gas expulsion quickly form the majority of the T1stars.
These stars oscillate around the Galactic plane.
Since the T1stars are all expelled during a very short time interval through gas expulsion, this translates to oscillations of the velocity dispersion for T1around about 3 km/s.
They can be distinguished easily from the stars in the SCand T2due to their larger velocity dispersion.

The T1stars complete about 9.9 vertical oscillations over the 300 Myr of simulation.
This translates to a period of about 32 Myr, which is comparable to the vertical period of the Sun around the Galactic plane
(
Bahcall & Bahcall, 1985
)
.
T1ends up far more dispersed in both the Galactic plane and the orthogonal direction, as can be seen from the velocity dispersions.
Therefore, T2would be expected to be much easier to observe than T1.

As can be seen in Table
2
differences between the velocity dispersions of different models are small compared to the differences between T1and T2.
More massive SCsproduce tidal tails with fewer binaries and a larger velocity dispersion.
Only looking at this result, it might seem as if field populations with a higher velocity dispersion would automatically be expected to host fewer binaries.
However, we have also seen that T1hosts a larger fraction of binaries and has a larger velocity dispersion than T2.
For the models investigated here, the difference between different tails is larger than the difference produced by different SCmasses.
To get a complete quantification for a correlation of binary fraction and velocity dispersion in the Galactic field a complete simulation of SCsfollowing the MF of embedded star clusters is needed.
However, it should be noted that
Li et al. (2023)
do find a higher binary fraction in dynamically hot stellar populations than in cold populations observed using LAMOST.
They distinguish the two groups of stars by their action in the z-direction,
J_{z}
, and call those with
J_{z}>20\penalty\ \rm kpc\;km/s
dynamically hot.
Stars with line-of-sight velocities exceeding
{25\penalty\ \rm km/s}
are assumed to be in binaries by them.

Especially in the trailing tail, the number of binaries seems to decrease again at large distances (10°) to the SC.

Overall these results show that there is a correlation between binary fraction and

However, a stellar-dynamical population model of the Galactic field population, consisting of many accumulated populations, would need to be computed to estimate systematic biases in deriving the stellar IMFfrom star counts.

In this work, two main methods for deriving the power-law index,
\alpha,
were employed:

The first method is histogram fitting, which was mentioned by
Maschberger & Kroupa (2009)
.
It is visualised in Fig.
13
.
The stars are first sorted into logarithmic mass bins and their number is divided by the bin width,
w=\log_{10}(m_{j+0.5})-\log_{10}(m_{j-0.5})
(
m
is in units of
M_{\odot}
).

j
is the number of the bin and
w
is equal for all bins.
The mass MF
dN=\xi(m)dm=km^{-\alpha}dm
is a power-law, which becomes linear in log-log.
Therefore, we translated the MFinto the double logarithmic frame and obtain for each bar in the histogram at the position
\log_{10}(m)
of height
N_{j}
:

\displaystyle\xi(\log_{10}(m))

\displaystyle=k10^{(1-\alpha)\log_{10}(m)}\ln(10),

(20)

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\log_{10}(m_{j-0.5})}^{\log_{10}(m_{j+0.5})}\xi(\log_{10}(m))d\log_{10}(m)

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{1-\alpha}\left(10^{(1-\alpha)\log(m_{j+0.5})}-10^{(1-\alpha)\log(m_{j-0.5})}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{1-\alpha}10^{(1-\alpha)\log_{10}(m_{j})}\left[10^{(1-\alpha)w/2}-10^{-(1-\alpha)w/2}\right],

(23)

\displaystyle\log_{10}\left(N_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\log_{10}(m_{j})+const.

(24)

The function in Eq.
24
is then fitted to the data to obtain the MF.

The second method is the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)described in Appendix A of
Khalaj & Baumgardt (2013)

The MLEis based on the work of
Clauset et al. (2009)
;
Maschberger & Kroupa (2009)
who developed it for cases with only a lower boundary.
Their main idea is to formulate the likelihood that a certain distribution of data points results from a MFwith a given
\alpha
and then maximising the function for
\alpha
.
As a first step both the fitting method and the MLEare tested on an optimally sampled canonical IMF
(
Kroupa et al., 2013
;
Yan et al., 2017
)
between
m=0.3
and
0.7\penalty\ M_{\odot}
.
This gives

It is important to note here that there is no ‘correct’ value for the power-law slope here, since the true slope is a two-part power-law instead of the one-part power-law that is fitted here.
The lower value from the MLEstems from the MLEputting more emphasis on matching the low-mass portion of the MFthan fitting a histogram does.
If the low-mass portion of the MFwould have a larger
\alpha

\alpha

4
Conclusions

In the present work, realistic N-body simulations of SCswith masses between
800M_{\odot}
and
6400M_{\odot}
carried out with
nbody6
were used to determine the effect of multiple star systems on the measured masses and MFs.
These masses are near the upper limit of masses of nearby SCs
(
Ebrahimi et al., 2022
)
.
The apparent mass a multiple system would have was computed by adding the luminosities produced by the companion stars and using the MLRfrom
Kroupa et al. (1993, see also

)
.

The main results are as follows:

(1)

Stars in T1have a higher fraction of multiples than those in T2and the SC.
This is due to the T1stars being expelled early, before the multiple fraction in the SCdrops to its final value.

(2)

Due to the presence of remnants (NSs and BHs) and unresolved multiples, the photometric mass underestimates the total mass of the SCby nearly one-fifth.
Binaries are the main contributor to this effect.
In the main model, they cause a 14 - 15 % underestimate of the total photometric mass of the SCand its tails. BHsand NSs have only a small effect in the SC, where they cause a 3 % underestimate, but a very strong effect in T1, the total photometric mass of which is underestimated by an additional 14 % due to dark objects.
The dynamical mass of the SCin the main model is a factor of 1.6 higher than the luminous mass at the beginning of the simulation, and a factor of 2.3 higher at its end.
This is reminiscent of the Hyades-mass problem discussed in
Röser et al. (2011)
and
Kroupa et al. (2022)
.

(3)

The MFin the stellar mass range 0.3 - 0.7
M_{\odot}
appears more top-heavy to an observer who cannot resolve multiple systems.
In both tails and the SC, the difference
\Delta\alpha_{0.3-0.7M_{\odot}}
is
0.2\pm 0.3
.
While this is within 1
\sigma
, the fact that this difference is present at all timesteps, in both the SCand its tails and in all simulations, suggests a systematic effect.

(4)

While there is a positive correlation between
f_{\rm mul}
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{z}}
, and
\Delta\alpha_{0.3-0.7M_{\odot}}
within a single model, when looking at different models one can find a positive correlation between the initial SCmass and the velocity dispersion in the tidal tails.
However, the correlation between the SCmass and binary fraction is negative.

In this work it is assumed that all binaries are unresolved; however, in reality some binaries might be resolved and some not.
Therefore, future studies should take the distance of the SCto the observer into account when studying these effects.

The field stars we observe in the Galaxy consist of a mixture of tidal tails of SCsof many different initial masses.
To recreate the stellar population actually observed in the field, a full population synthesis that includes a range of models with different SCmasses would be necessary
(
Kroupa, 2002b
;
Marks & Kroupa, 2011
;
Dabringhausen et al., 2022
, see e.g.)
.
This would have to be done by including a range of IMFsthat reflect their dependence on the metallicity and density of the gas cloud the SCis born from, as was found by comparing the results of N-body models to observed SCproperties, such as their masses, half-mass radii, and binary fractions
(
Marks et al., 2012
)
, and by comparing the chemical evolution of galaxies computed for different IMFs to the metallicities observed in dwarf galaxies
(
Lee et al., 2009
;
Gunawardhana et al., 2011
;
Yan et al., 2020
)
.
The two methods lead to similar results for the upper end of the IMF.
It is also important to mention that
Baumgardt et al. (2023)
measured the upper end of the IMFof 14 Small and Large Magellanic Cloud clusters directly.
While the masses and metallicities of these clusters should theoretically lead to a flattened IMF, their errors are too large to detect any systematic variation.
Similarly,
Dickson et al. (2023)
investigated 37 Galactic globular clusters without finding any evidence for a variation in the IMFafter fitting multi-mass models to them.
However, they only cite statistical errors and, in contrast to the analysis by
Marks et al. (2012)
,
Dickson et al. (2023)
neglect very early stellar dynamical processes during and before gas expulsion, which makes it difficult to assess the actual errors of the found MFslopes.
Gas expulsion itself is not taken into account in these models.

The work presented here thus points towards the required deep analysis needing to be performed when studying the field-star population in order to infer how the stellar IMFvaries with the physical conditions of star formation.
