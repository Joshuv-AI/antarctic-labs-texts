Unilateral partial-mouth extraction achieves complete clinical remission in cats with unilateral caudal feline chronic gingivostomatitis: a case series
Authors: Kue Hwan Choe, Se Eun Kim, Hyun Min Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS) is a debilitating inflammatory condition characterized by severe caudal oral mucosal inflammation and frequently necessitates extensive dental extractions. Full-mouth extraction (FME) or partial-mouth extraction (PME) is considered standard therapy; however, reported clinical remission rates vary widely, ranging from 51.8% to 67.3%, and many affected cats require long-term immunomodulatory treatment. Although inflammation is typically bilateral, a subset of cats exhibits lesions confined to one side of the oral cavity, raising questions regarding whether FME is invariably required. To date, no clinical studies have evaluated unilateral PME as a therapeutic option for cats with unilateral caudal FCGS. All cats exhibited ulcerative or proliferative lesions limited to one side of the caudal oral mucosa. All cats underwent unilateral PME of the affected side. Background

Feline chronic gingivostomatitis (FCGS) is a severe, chronic inflammatory condition of the oral mucosa that predominantly affects the caudal region. This painful and often debilitating disorder is characterized by persistent mucosal inflammation that may endure for months to years [1]. The reported prevalence of this immune-mediated inflammatory disease varies widely, ranging from 0.7% to 26% in the general feline population [1–5].

FCGS manifests in two principal clinical phenotypes, ulcerative and proliferative, with some cats exhibiting a combination of both forms [6, 7]. While the disease is reported to affect up to 12% of the feline population, the majority of these affected cats experience moderate to severe oral pain, which can lead to substantial morbidity [7, 8]. Frequently observed clinical signs include reduced appetite, diminished grooming, and decreased social interaction [9–11].

Although the precise etiology of FCGS remains incompletely defined, multiple contributing factors have been implicated. Viral infections, bacterial involvement, and alterations in the oral microbiome have all been associated with disease pathogenesis, which is thought to result from an aberrant immune response to persistent antigenic stimulation [12–15]. Immune-mediated mechanisms typically involve dysregulation of T-cell subsets and heightened production of inflammatory cytokines, processes that underpin many chronic inflammatory conditions [16]. Current etiologic hypotheses for FCGS include chronic infection with feline calicivirus (FCV), pronounced CD8⁺ T-cell–mediated immune responses, elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-17, and activation of innate immune pathways involving myeloid-lineage cells [11, 17–19]. Coinfection with feline leukemia virus (FeLV) has also been linked to more severe disease manifestations and poorer therapeutic outcomes. While coinfections with FCV is reported to have an association with specific lesion types, this has not yet been clearly established [19, 20].

Standard treatment for FCGS in general consist of partial-mouth extraction (PME) or full-mouth extraction (FME), often accompanied by adjunctive therapies such as antibiotics, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory medications [10]. The decision between PME, which preserves the canine teeth, and FME depends on the distribution and severity of lesions within the oral cavity.

In most FCGS cases, inflammatory or ulcerative lesions are present bilaterally within the caudal oral mucosa. However, a subset of cats exhibits predominantly unilateral lesions [21], with the contralateral mucosa remaining clinically unaffected. The optimal surgical strategy for managing unilateral caudal stomatitis remains a subject of debate. This case series describes three cats with unilateral caudal mucosal lesions and evaluates the clinical outcomes of cats treated with unilateral PME, contributing to the ongoing evaluation of individualized, lesion-directed treatment approaches for FCGS.

Case presentation

Signalment and diagnostic evaluation

A retrospective case series analysis was conducted using medical records of three client-owned cats diagnosed with FCGS at the Taeil Veterinary Dental Hospital (Seoul, Republic of Korea) between 2020 and 2024.

Unilateral FCGS was operationally defined as the presence of macroscopic inflammatory or ulcerative lesions strictly affecting one side of the caudal oral mucosa, with the contralateral side being macroscopically completely free of any inflammatory changes. To meet this definition, the unilateral mucosal inflammation must not be directly attributed to underlying dental pathologies, such as localized severe periodontal disease, tooth resorption, or physical trauma from malocclusion.

Based on this definition, inclusion criteria consisted of cats with ulcerative or proliferative lesions strictly confined to one side of the caudal oral mucosa, involving the ipsilateral premolar and/or molar teeth regions. Only cases with complete medical records, including preoperative examination findings, surgical details, postoperative follow-up, and a minimum follow-up duration of six months, were included.

Exclusion criteria included cases where localized mucosal inflammation was determined to be secondary to localized severe periodontal disease, tooth resorption, or mechanical trauma, bilateral caudal oral mucosal lesions, concurrent systemic diseases that could influence treatment outcomes, a history of previous dental extractions for FCGS, incomplete medical documentation, or loss to follow-up.

Preoperative diagnostic testing included complete blood count, serum biochemistry profile, and thoracic radiography. Thoracic imaging was routinely performed for comprehensive pre-anesthetic risk assessment, cardiopulmonary screening, and evaluation of the tracheal diameter to guide appropriate endotracheal tube selection. Serologic screening for FeLV, feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV), and heartworm using the SNAP Triple Kit (IDEXX Laboratories), along with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for FCV, feline herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1), and Mycoplasma species, was performed using swab samples collected from caudal oral mucosal lesions.

All cats underwent a comprehensive physical examination and detailed oral cavity assessment under general anesthesia. The oral cavity was systematically evaluated and photographed. To thoroughly rule out underlying dental pathologies and differentiate unilateral FCGS from other localized dental diseases, full-mouth intraoral radiography was performed in all cases. Clinical assessment included evaluation of gingival inflammation, characterization of caudal mucosal lesions (ulcerative or proliferative), and identification of dental pathology.

Treatment

General anesthesia was performed using a standardized protocol. Premedication consisted of intramuscular administration of ampicillin (20 mg/kg; Penbrex inj, Yungjin Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea), butorphanol (0.1 mg/kg; Butophan, Myungmoon Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea), and midazolam (0.2 mg/kg; Bukwang Midazolam, Bukwang Pharm, Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Anesthesia was induced with propofol (1%; Anepol, Hana Pharm, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) at 4 mg/kg intravenously, followed by endotracheal intubation. Maintenance anesthesia was achieved with isoflurane (1.5–2.5% in oxygen) delivered via a semi-closed rebreathing circuit. Dental nerve blocks were administered using bupivacaine (Bupivacaine HCL 0.5% Inj; Myungmoon Pharm) at 1 mg/kg. Intraoperative monitoring included assessment of heart rate, respiratory rate, capnography, pulse oximetry, and blood pressure.

All cats (Cats 1–3) underwent unilateral PME because inflammation was restricted to one side and no significant dental pathology was detected on the contralateral side (Table 1).

All dental extractions were performed by a single surgeon using appropriate instruments and established techniques for feline dental surgery [22–25]. Depending on the condition of individual teeth and the associated alveolar bone, single-rooted and multi-rooted teeth were removed either by simple extraction or surgical extraction. Post-operative intraoral radiographs were obtained prior to closure to confirm complete removal of tooth roots.

Mucoperiosteal flaps were elevated, and inflammatory granulation tissue was meticulously excised from all extraction sites. Alveolar bone was smoothed, and extraction sites were thoroughly lavaged with sterile saline. Primary closure was achieved using absorbable suture material (5 − 0 poliglecaprone 25, Monocryl, Ethicon) in a simple interrupted pattern without tension.

All cats received standardized postoperative care. Antimicrobial therapy consisted of amoxicillin–clavulanate (12.5 mg/kg orally twice daily for 14 days; Amocla Table 375 mg, Kunil Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) or a single subcutaneous injection of cefovecin (8 mg/kg; Convenia, Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ, USA). Postoperative analgesia consisted of a transdermal fentanyl patch (Durogesic D-trans Patch 12 µg/h, Janssen Korea, Seoul, Republic of Korea) applied immediately after surgery and maintained for 3–5 days, together with gabapentin (5 mg/kg orally twice daily; Neurontin Cap. 100 mg, Viatris Korea, Seoul, Republic of Korea) administered for 14 days after surgery. Additional analgesics, such as gabapentin or opioids, were prescribed only when needed beyond the initial 14-day postoperative period. NSAIDs were not included in the postoperative analgesic protocol due to concerns regarding potential adverse effects in feline patients. Owners were instructed to monitor for signs of pain, difficulty eating, excessive salivation, and behavioral changes.

Clinical findings

The Stomatitis Disease Activity Index (SDAI) scores were evaluated for all cats at three timepoints: (1) preoperatively (before dental extractions) (2), at the time of clinical remission (4 to 8 weeks after dental extractions), and (3) at the final follow-up examination (more than 6 months after dental extractions). To ensure consistency, all SDAI assessments were performed and recorded by a single veterinarian. Clinical remission was defined as resolution of oral pain, restoration of normal eating behavior, complete resolution of caudal mucosal ulcerative or proliferative lesions, absence of inflammation at extraction sites, and return to normal grooming and social behavior.

Three client-owned cats with unilateral caudal FCGS were included in this study. All cats were Korean domestic shorthair cats. The study population consisted of two neutered males and one neutered female. The median age was 4 years (range: 1–7 years), and the median body weight was 4.5 kg (range: 4.3–7.0 kg). PCR testing of lesional tissue revealed FCV infection in all three cats. All cats tested negative for FHV-1 and Mycoplasma species. Testing for FeLV, FIV, and heartworm antigen was negative in all cases (Table 2).

Outcome and follow-up

All three cats achieved clinical remission within 1 to 2 months after surgery. The median time to clinical remission was 7 weeks (range, 7–8 weeks). SDAI scores improved substantially in all cases, decreasing from a median preoperative score of 22 (range, 18–24) to 5 (range, 5–6) at the time of remission, and further to 1 (range, 1–2) at the final follow-up evaluation (Table 3).

All cats treated with unilateral PME (n = 3) achieved clinical remission without lesion recurrence during the follow-up period, with a median time to remission of 7 weeks. In all cats, the caudal oral mucosal lesions documented preoperatively had resolved by the postoperative recheck examinations (Fig. 3).

None of the cats required long-term immunomodulatory medications (such as cyclosporine, corticosteroids, interferon, or mesenchymal stem cell therapy) following surgery. Additional analgesic therapy beyond the initial 2-week postoperative period was required in two cats. No major complications were observed.

Follow-up duration ranged from 6 to 12 months (median, 8 months). All three cats maintained complete clinical remission throughout the follow-up period. No recurrence of caudal mucosal inflammation was observed in any cat, and none developed refractory stomatitis requiring further medical or surgical intervention.

Among the three cats that underwent unilateral PME, the retained contralateral teeth showed no macroscopic evidence of gingivitis or adjacent mucosal inflammation on conscious oral examination at the final follow-up.

Owner-reported quality of life was excellent in all cases, with cats demonstrating normal appetite, maintenance of body weight, and typical social behavior.

Discussion

This case series demonstrates that in carefully selected cats with unilateral caudal FCGS, unilateral PME can achieve complete clinical remission with a substantial decrease in SDAI scores. All three cats achieved sustained remission without the need for long-term immunomodulatory therapy, and notably, the cats treated with unilateral extraction exhibited no progression of disease on the contralateral, non-extracted side during the follow-up period.

The 100% remission rate observed in this small cohort should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and retrospective nature of the study. Prior larger studies have reported remission rates ranging from 51.8% to 67.3% following FME or PME in bilateral FCGS [26], and direct comparison with those findings is not appropriate. Several factors may account for the apparent difference in treatment outcomes. First, the cats included in this study presented with unilateral lesions, suggesting a potentially distinct clinical phenotype with a more favorable prognosis compared with the conventional bilateral form of FCGS. In line with this, focal dysbiosis causing localized stomatitis should also be considered as a critical differential diagnosis or a contributing factor to this atypical phenotype. Such localized microbial imbalance might explain the strictly unilateral presentation observed in these cases. Furthermore, this localized nature would favor the rapid clinical recovery observed in our cohort compared to the more protracted courses often seen in typical bilateral FCGS, where a more systemic immune dysregulation is likely involved. Second, the small sample size increases the likelihood that this group contained a disproportionate number of individuals who respond well to extraction alone, potentially leading to overestimation of the treatment effect. Third, the follow-up period of 6 to 12 months, although adequate for short-term outcome assessment, may not have been sufficient to identify late recurrence in some cats.

The successful outcomes observed in cats treated with unilateral extraction indicate that FME may not be necessary in unilateral cases of FCGS. Previous studies have demonstrated that both FME and PME can yield similar remission rates when retained teeth are free of dental disease and adjacent mucosal inflammation [10]. The current findings extend this principle by suggesting that when inflammation is strictly confined to one side of the oral cavity, extraction may safely be limited to the affected side alone.

The etiology of FCGS is incompletely understood but is believed to involve complex interactions among viral infection (particularly FCV), immune dysregulation dominated by CD8⁺ T-cell responses, and alterations in the oral microbiome [11, 17–19]. Bacterial plaque is considered a persistent antigenic stimulus capable of perpetuating the aberrant immune response in susceptible cats [12–15]. Dental extraction is thought to be effective because removal of the plaque–tooth interface eliminates this chronic antigenic burden. In cases of unilateral FCGS, the immune-mediated inflammatory process may remain localized to one side of the oral cavity due to regional differences in viral load, microbial colonization, or local immune factors. Extraction limited to the affected side may therefore be sufficient to eliminate the antigenic stimulus driving the localized inflammation.

An alternative explanation is that unilateral presentation may represent an early or less advanced form of FCGS that has not yet progressed to bilateral involvement. However, the sustained remission observed in the unilateral extraction group, with no progression to contralateral disease during follow-up, suggests that unilateral FCGS may constitute a distinct clinical entity rather than simply an early stage of bilateral disease.

Interestingly, all cats in this series tested positive for FCV, consistent with the high prevalence of FCV reported among FCGS patients in previous studies [11, 19]. The role of FCV in the pathogenesis of FCGS remains controversial, with some investigations proposing a causative contribution and others suggesting that FCV functions as an opportunistic pathogen within immunocompromised oral tissues. The universal FCV positivity observed in this cohort, coupled with successful treatment outcomes, supports the latter interpretation and suggests that FCV alone is insufficient to sustain FCGS once the plaque–tooth interface is eliminated.

The findings of this case series indicate that unilateral PME may be considered a treatment option in carefully selected cases of FCGS. Based on the present observations, the following criteria may assist in identifying appropriate candidates for unilateral extraction:

It is important to emphasize that the decision between unilateral PME and FME should be individualized and made following a comprehensive oral examination under general anesthesia. Dental radiography is essential for detecting subclinical pathology that may not be apparent on gross inspection. Any tooth with significant periodontal disease or evidence of tooth resorption should be extracted regardless of the distribution of mucosal lesions.

Owners should be advised that although unilateral PME may be appropriate in selected cases, there remains a risk of disease progression that could necessitate subsequent extraction of the contralateral teeth. Long-term follow-up and implementation of home oral care (when tolerated) are crucial for monitoring the health of retained teeth.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 3), single-center setting, and inclusion of a single breed limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, because treatment allocation was not randomized and was based on clinical judgment, selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, the relatively short follow-up duration (maximum of 12 months) may not have captured delayed recurrences, as some cats with FCGS experience relapse months to years after initial remission. Accordingly, larger multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to determine whether unilateral extraction provides durable benefit and to better define the patient population most likely to benefit from this approach.

Conclusions

This case series provides preliminary evidence that unilateral PME may represent a viable treatment option for carefully selected cats with unilateral caudal FCGS, achieving successful clinical outcomes. Although FME remains the standard of treatment for typical bilateral FCGS, these findings suggest that a more individualized, lesion-directed surgical strategy may be appropriate when disease is strictly unilateral and the contralateral dentition is free of pathology. Larger prospective studies are required to validate these observations, establish evidence-based criteria for case selection, and determine the long-term durability of remission following unilateral extraction.
