Where Have All the Firewalls Gone? Security Consequences of Residential IPv6 Transition
Erik Rye, Dave Levin, Robert Beverly

Abstract
. IPv4 NAT has limited the spread of IoT botnets considerably by default-denying bots’ incoming connection requests to in-home devices unless the owner has explicitly allowed them. As the Internet transitions to majority IPv6, however, residential connections no longer require the use of NAT. This paper therefore asks: has the transition from IPv4 to IPv6 ultimately made residential networks more vulnerable to attack, thereby empowering the next generation of IPv6-based IoT botnets? To answer this question, we introduce a large-scale IPv6 scanning methodology that, unlike those that rely on AI, can be run on low-resource devices common in IoT botnets. We use this methodology to perform the largest-scale measurement of IPv6 residential networks to date, and compare which devices are publicly accessible to comparable IPv4 networks. We were able to receive responses from 14.0M distinct IPv6 addresses inside of residential networks (i.e., not the external-facing gateway), in 2,436 ASes across 118 countries. These responses come from protocols commonly exploited by IoT botnets (including telnet and FTP), as well as protocols typically associated with end-user devices (including iPhone-Sync and IPP). Comparing to IPv4, we show that we are able to reach more printers, iPhones, and smart lights over IPv6 than full IPv4-wide scans could. Collectively, our results show that NAT has indeed acted as the de facto firewall of the Internet, and the v4-to-v6 transition of residential networks is opening up new devices to attack.



1.
Introduction

Residential networks are of paramount concern for Internet security.
Users all over the world are increasingly deploying vulnerable
smart-home devices in their homes
(
Herwig et al. 2019
;
Sinha 2025
)
.
IoT botnets have taken advantage of vulnerable in-home devices to launch
unprecedentedly large
attacks
(
Antonakakis et al. 2017
;
Krebs 2025
)
, thereby
threatening not only the device owners’ own security and privacy, but
everyone on the Internet.

To date, IoT botnets’ growth has been limited by a small, unlikely
defender: IPv4 Network Address Translation (NAT).
Because IPv4 addresses are in limited supply, almost all IPv4
residential networks run a NAT box at their gateway, translating
between the home’s single public IPv4 address and its many internal
private addresses.
Critically, NAT acts as a sort of firewall, default-denying incoming
connections unless a user has explicitly set up a port forwarding rule
to allow them.
IPv6, conversely, has a virtually limitless number of public addresses
and eliminates the need for NAT altogether.

In this paper, we ask: has the transition from IPv4 to IPv6 ultimately
made residential networks more vulnerable to attack, and empowered the
next generation of IPv6-based IoT botnets?
The answer to this is not obvious: it is not enough that addresses
within an IPv6 network not be behind a firewall—the attacker must
be able to ascertain which IPv6 addresses are assigned to active
devices.
For IPv4, IoT botnets simply scan the entire address space, similar to
ZMap
(
Durumeric et al. 2013
)
, but that is not feasible with IPv6’s

2^{128}
addresses.
The Internet measurement community scans portions of the IPv6 space by
using generative AI techniques, trained on a seed list of known-active
IPv6 addresses, to produce other likely-active addresses.
These techniques are computationally intensive, and are therefore not
feasible on low-resource devices common to IoT botnets.

We show in this paper that it is in fact possible to perform
large-scale scans of IPv6 residential networks in a manner that can be
performed on low-resource devices common in IoT botnets.
We perform the largest measurement of IPv6 residential networks to
date, and compare which devices are publicly accessible to comparable
IPv4 networks.
We make the following three key findings:

Effective, low-computation IPv6 scanning.
We introduce a scanning methodology that is computationally low-cost
yet highly effective.
Given a known-active IPv6 /48 prefix (which are publicly available),
our technique simply scans the initial low-order addresses
(
::1
,
::2
, etc.) within each /56 prefix.
Though this is a narrower portion of the IPv6 space than what AI-based
techniques could potentially access, we show that it still allows us to
reach tens of millions of in-home devices.
Most importantly, it does so in a way that would be easily deployable
on any low-resource IoT device.

Our technique thus shows that scanning large portions of the
residential IPv6 Internet is within reach for IoT botnets.

An analysis of vulnerable services publicly accessible over IPv6.
We applied our scanning methodology to perform the most comprehensive
measurement study of residential IPv6 networks to date.
We scanned for the presence of 25 port/protocol combinations, including
many of those most commonly used by IoT
botnets
(
Antonakakis et al. 2017
;
Herwig et al. 2019
)
, as well as the
iPhone Sync protocol (to our knowledge, this is the first measurement
for the public accessibility of iPhones).
We find millions of publicly accessible, internal residential IPv6
hosts running potentially vulnerable services.
We also compare our IPv6 findings to IPv4, through both our own and
Shodan’s scans of the
entire
IPv4 address space, and find that
we are able to access more of some devices (especially printers and
iPhones) over IPv6.

Collectively, our results show that the v4-to-v6 transition of
residential networks is opening up new devices to attack, empowering a
new generation of IoT botnets, and ultimately putting the entire
Internet at greater risk.
This makes our final finding all the more surprising:

IPv6 traffic is less monitored for malice than IPv4.
IPv6 now constitutes the majority of addresses in the US, and later
this year will be the global majority
(
Google 2025
)
.
It would thus be natural to assume that IPv6 is monitored
for malicious traffic at least as much as IPv4.
Unfortunately, we found this not to be the case.

We ran our experiments using the community-adopted best practices of
ethical Internet scanning, including making our identities clear and
giving users and network operators the ability to opt-out from our
scans.
We received opt-out requests as well as notifications that some of our
traffic appeared malicious.
While this was by no means our measurements’ intent, it does present an
opportunity to compare the Internet’s responsiveness to
malicious-looking traffic on IPv4 versus IPv6.
We compare the responses we obtained from our IPv4 and IPv6 scans.
Whereas our IPv4 scans triggered multiple IDSes across the
Internet—some within four minutes of our scan starting—we never
received a
single
notification for our IPv6 scans.
These results indicate a shockingly low amount of monitoring of
malicious traffic over IPv6, potentially granting IoT botnets an even
greater advantage.

Contributions
Our primary contributions include:

•

We perform the most comprehensive scan of devices within IPv6 residential networks, measuring the public accessibility of 25
distinct port/protocol combinations.

•

We introduce a scanning methodology that is significantly simpler
than prior work, thereby demonstrating that even a low-resource IoT
botnet could propagate across the IPv6
Internet (§

).

•

We compare the reachable services across IPv4 and IPv6 networks,
finding that some devices (including printers, lighting systems,
and IP cameras) are reachable at significantly higher rates in
IPv6 than in IPv4 (§
4
and
§

).

•

As an unintended side-effect of our study, we observe that IPv4 scanning is far more heavily monitored than IPv6 scanning (§
6
).

•

We propose potential remediation based on our measurements and
findings (§
7
).

To facilitate further study and to encourage greater adoption of
defenses, we will be making our code and data available to the extent
that is safe and ethical (§
A
, §
B
).

2.
Background and Related Work

Our effort builds upon a rich and long history of identifying
open and unprotected Internet services. Additionally, we leverage
recent advances in IPv6-specific scanning and network discovery
of residential networks.

2.1.
Finding Open Services on the Internet

ZMap revolutionized Internet measurement by making it possible to exhaustively
scan the IPv4 address space in under an hour
(
Durumeric et al. 2013
)
.
Multiple research and commercial efforts (e.g., Rapid7
(
rap 2025
)
,
Censys
(
Durumeric et al. 2015
)
, and Shodan
(
sho 2024
)
) regularly scan the entirety of
the IPv4 Internet and catalog services.

Attackers have since leveraged such techniques to remotely connect to services
for nefarious purposes ranging from software exploits to denial-of-service
attacks
(
Antonakakis et al. 2017
;
Herwig et al. 2019
)
.
Notably, the Mirai botnet and its many successors use ZMap’s stateless,
randomized approach to rapidly scan the Internet for vulnerable
devices
(
Antonakakis et al. 2017
)
.

Finding open services on the IPv6 Internet is far more difficult, for two key
reasons:
(1) IPv6 has a huge address space, making it impossible to scan in its entirety,
and
(2) IPv6 addresses are often ephemeral and frequently contain 64 bits that are generated
at random
(
Narten et al. 2007
)
, making it difficult to predict which addresses are
in-use and thus worth scanning.
These features have stymied both benign and malicious actors from being able to
scan the IPv6 Internet.
Our work shows a low-effort way to discover open services in IPv6 at an
unprecedented scale.

2.2.
IPv6 Measurements

Though still somewhat nascent, active IPv6 scanning has become more
prevalent
(
Czyz et al. 2013
;
Richter et al. 2022
;
Tanveer et al. 2023
)
.

IPv6 Hitlists
To address the challenge of identifying which IPv6 addresses to scan,
various efforts have been underway to collect and disseminate
hitlists
:
lists of IPv6 addresses that have been observed to be active.
Scans can then be performed by running ZMap6
(
tumi8 2022
)
, an extension of
ZMap that scans all of the addresses in a given hitlist.
We use the ZMap6 tool to identify responsive addresses in this work.

Gasser et al. compose their “IPv6 Hitlist” by running traceroutes to public
open servers, DNS zone enumeration, and other active
techniques
(
hit 2025
)
.
Rye and Levin
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
instead
passively
learn IPv6 addresses by running NTP servers in the NTP Pool—a crowdsourced system for
providing network time.
They reported that their dataset contains far more clients than those obtained
through active scans.
We therefore adopt their methodology—in a superset of the countries they had
originally used—to collect our own seed dataset of active /48 prefixes.
We note that Rye and Levin’s study did not include any active port scans of the
addresses they learned; to the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to
use their client-rich dataset for this purpose.
We discuss in §
3.1
and §
A
the steps we took to
ensure these scans were done in an ethically responsible manner.

Target Generation Algorithms

TGA
have emerged as a popular mechanism for identifying more active IPv6 addresses given an IPv6 hitlist as a
seed
(
Foremski et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Song et al. 2022
;
Cui et al. 2020a
;
Cui et al. 2020b
;
Cui et al. 2021
;
Hou et al. 2021
;
Hou et al. 2023
;
Williams et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
.

TGA
make use of often sophisticated machine learning techniques, such as
variational autoencoders
(
Cui et al. 2020a
)
, language model
transformers
(
Cui et al. 2020b
)
, reinforcement learning
(
Hou et al. 2021
;
Hou et al. 2023
)
,
LSTMs
(
Williams et al. 2024
)
, ensemble learning
(
Sun et al. 2025
)
, and generative
adversarial nets
(
Cui et al. 2021
)
to attempt to learn structure in IPv6 address
training datasets.

Our methodology does not use these sophisticated, computationally expensive
techniques, as we anticipate that they would be beyond the capabilities of IoT
botnets—the likely adversary for residential networks.
Instead, we introduce and use a far simpler scanning methodology.
Nonetheless, we are able to discover several times more
more residential devices than these prior efforts could.
We attribute this primarily to our use of different seed data: whereas most of
these studies used the IPv6 Hitlist
(
hit 2025
)
—which is comprised
largely of router and server addresses—we use NTP Pool-derived data—which
has primarily client prefixes
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
.
It is possible that seeding TGAs with NTP Pool datasets could result in even
more hosts found, but again, we focus in this paper on the simpler techniques
that are within IoT botnets’ capabilities.

Perhaps most directly related to our work among prior art is that by Williams et
al.
(
Williams et al. 2024
)
, who introduced a TGA-based IPv6 scanning system (6Sense)
and applied it to analyze open services on IPv6.
They scanned responsive addresses on several TCP and UDP ports, and found 11M
responsive addresses using a budget of 100M probes.
While Williams et al. detected some LAN-internal hosts by chance (e.g., printers),
our key insight and contribution is a methodology
designed explicitly to
find these types of devices
in the vast expanse of the IPv6 address space.
Additionally, their methodology makes use of computationally-expensive
TGAs—their experiments used a 64-core machine with 512GB of RAM and an NVIDIA
A40 GPU.
By contrast, ours relies only on a set of simple heuristics; our work therefore
demonstrates the feasibility of wide-scale IPv6 even on low-resource IoT
devices.

IPv4 vs. IPv6 reachability
Another closely-related work is that of Czyz et al., who compare the reachability
policies between IPv4 and IPv6 among dual-stack servers and
routers
(
Czyz et al. 2016
)
.
Much like our work, they found significantly higher openness of IPv6 services
as compared to IPv4.
However, they studied only servers and routers; we focus our study on
residential IPv6 networks, who often have drastically different security
postures than enterprise devices.

2.3.
Residential Networks

NAT as a firewall
Vulnerabilities may be particularly acute in residential networks, which
commonly have many poorly managed and consumer-grade IoT devices.
In such environments, where devices may even use well-known passwords (or none
at all), there is an over-reliance on NAT to provide a layer of protection.
As we show in this paper, IPv6’s removal of NAT makes residential hosts more
accessible.

Whereas IPv4 NAT has a default-deny access control, IPv6 router
manufacturers can choose to implement default-allow or default-deny.
Olsen et al.
(
Olson et al. 2022
;
Olson et al. 2023
)
procured and characterized
popular home router models in a controlled setting, finding that some popular
models have both IPv6 enabled and permit unsolicited inbound traffic from the
WAN to the LAN by default.
Their study showed the possibility of publicly-accessible residential devices;
ours demonstrates it by actively measuring residential networks in the wild.

Finding residential IPv6 devices
We are familiar of only two pieces of work that have actively sought to
identify devices on residential IPv6 networks.
Rye et al.
(
Rye and Beverly 2020
)
used
yarrp6

(
Beverly et al. 2018
)
—a port of the
IPv4
yarrp
stateless traceroute tool
(
Beverly 2016
)
—to discover nearly
64M active residential LAN networks and their associated
CPE
(
CPE
).
Rye et al. subsequently used ZMap6 to track
CPE
devices using a legacy
form of IPv6 addressing over time as their ISP-assigned network
changed
(
Rye et al. 2021
)
.
Whereas both of these prior efforts sought to identify residential CPE
(gateway) devices, we seek to identify devices
inside
the home.

To track devices, they require CPE devices’ MAC address to be included in
the lower 64 bits of an EUI-64 IPv6 address
(
Rye et al. 2021
)
.
Our techniques do not require EUI-64 IPv6 addresses, but when they are available
we use them to ascertain manufacturer information for the subset of devices
in this study that use them.

Residential IPv6 IoT vulnerabilities
Complementary to our work, Saidi et al. analyzed IPv6 networks for privacy and
security vulnerabilities related to in-home, IoT hosts
(
Saidi et al. 2022
)
.
Unlike our work, Saidi et al. obtained IPv6 network data from a large ISP,
whereas we demonstrate in this study that an unprivileged attacker can, in many
cases, discover the same types of devices using active methods.

3.
Scanning Methodology

IoT botnets spread by scanning IP addresses for responsive hosts
running potentially vulnerable services.
To understand whether IPv6 residential networks are vulnerable to IoT
botnets, we must ascertain whether it is possible to scan IPv6
residential networks without relying on computationally expensive
methods like AI.

In this section, we present a scanning methodology that can be run by
even extremely low-resource IoT devices, yet, as we show in
§
4
, is able to reach millions of devices internal to
residential networks.
We decompose this problem into two parts: finding in-use residential
/48 prefixes (§
3.1
), and then identifying specific
addresses within those prefixes to scan (§
3.2
).
We close this section with a discussion of our approach’s
limitations (§
3.3
).

3.1.
Finding In-use Residential /48s

The first component of our residential network discovery relies on obtaining
IPv6 networks known to be recently active.
While this can be accomplished using active scans in IPv4, in IPv6,
exhaustive scanning is infeasible and can be cost-prohibitive even at
relatively coarse granularities.
Moreover, space allocated to organizations by RIRs is not necessarily assigned
to subscribers,
and large swathes of addresses may be unassigned and unresponsive.

NTP Pool passive collection
We learn of active IPv6 addresses by running servers in the NTP Pool
(
ntp 2025
)
,
following the methodology of Rye and Levin
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
.
We ran 34 NTP servers in 25 countries (listed in the Appendix).
The NTP Pool uses coarse IP geolocation data to direct NTP clients to a nearby
NTP server.
Thus, we obtain traffic from
outside
the 25 countries our servers
are located in, as well.
Collecting active IPv6 addresses by running NTP Pool servers was shown by Rye
and Levin
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
to result in far more client addresses than
other methods, notably the
IPv6 Hitlist

(
hit 2025
)
.

Why not just scan back the NTP Pool clients?
At this point, it would be trivial to simply scan back the IPv6 addresses
that came to our NTP Pool servers.
However, prior to running our servers, we contacted the NTP Pool operators
to inquire about what forms of data they were comfortable allowing us to
collect, share, and act on.

The NTP Pool operators told us that it would
not
be acceptable to scan
back the /128s.
Back in 2016, Shodan had run NTP Pool servers to discover and scan-back IPv6
/128s, which drew concern and condemnation from the
community
(
seclists.org 2016
)
.
The NTP Pool operators were concerned that allowing this behavior in the future
would jeopardize the trust that users have in the service.
We therefore believe that
no research studies should scan back the full
/128 IPv6 addresses
learned from passively running NTP Pool servers;
otherwise, the academic community risks losing out on NTP Pool data altogether.
Given how valuable these datasets have been shown to be
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
,
we feel that the benefits of port scanning back even once would not outweigh the
potential risks.

However, the NTP Pool operators also told us that sharing the /48s publicly was
acceptable (as with Rye and Levin
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
), and that any
methodology that starts with
only
the /48s and derives a /128 to scan
was also acceptable.
We therefore discard the lower 80 bits altogether from every client IPv6
address that comes to our NTP Pool servers, saving only the /48s.
The methodology we describe in the rest of this section starts with these /48
prefixes and derives /128s to scan.

Narrowing to residential prefixes
We use the /48 networks we learned during the week of December 22, 2024.
This comprised 4,533,027 distinct /48s in total.
Our study focuses on
residential
networks, but the NTP Pool
data contains many non-residential /48s, as well, including infrastructure
and mobile networks.

To focus specifically on /48s allocated for residential networks, we filter
our /48s in two ways:

First
, we use the Stanford ASdb dataset
(
Ziv et al. 2021
)
to
select for networks whose primary category is ‘‘Internet Service
Provider’’
1
1

1

We use the most recent available data from January
2024
. This reduced the number of /48s to 3,621,017.

Second
, we use the MaxMind GeoIP2 Connection Type
database
(
MaxMind Inc 2025
)
to filter all of the ASdb “ISP”
/48s for those MaxMind classifies as “cable/DSL” or “dialup”.
This further reduced the candidate /48s to 2,515,372 (55.5% of the original
dataset).
Applying both of these filters provides a degree of confidence that the
resulting /48 networks are assigned to residential or small business
networks by access ISPs.
For concision, we refer to these networks hereafter as “residential,”
although they may comprise small office/home office (SOHO) networks
from cable and DSL ISPs as well.

Alternatives botnets could make
The NTP Pool operators do not wish server operators to scan-back the
exact IP addresses that access them.
As ethical researchers, we adhere to this limitation, but botnet
operators might not.
Instead, a botnet could operate their own servers in the NTP Pool (or
ostensibly another popular service) and collect not just /48s, but full
/128s.
Additionally, botnets need not (and in general do not) limit themselves
to residential networks; “smart” devices pervade corporate networks,
as well.
Thus, the results we present in this paper are a strict lower bound on
what would be possible with botnets today.

3.2.
Scanning Addresses within /48s

Using the residential /48s discovered from our NTP Pool servers, we
then perform a series of active measurements to identify responsive
addresses within those prefixes, and then to scan them for open
services.

Address selection
Arguably the most difficult problem in IPv6 scanning is identifying
specific /128 addresses that might be responsive.
State of the art IPv6 scans use sophisticated AI techniques
to this end.

We take a far simpler approach that any embedded device could
implement.
For a given /48, we iterate over each of its 256 /56s, and to each one
we contact its first ten addresses (
::1
–
::a
).
While there is no universal standard for how ISPs issue IPv6 subnets to
their customers, recommended best practices are to assign a /48 or /56
to each end customer and to use a unique /64 to address the WAN link of
the CPE from its own /64
(
RIPE 2017
)
.
As we will see, the prevalence of DHCPv6 makes this a surprisingly
effective way of finding responsive devices—even devices that also
assign themselves random IIDs.

Detecting responsiveness and aliases
To each of the addresses we consider for scanning, we first test their
responsiveness by sending an ICMPv6 Echo Request (ping).
We do this using ZMap6
(
tumi8 2022
)
, the IPv6 port of
ZMap
(
Durumeric et al. 2013
)
.
We also send an Echo Request to a random address address in each of the
/56s we probe.
This random target address helps identify
aliased
IPv6 networks—those that respond to probes to
any
address.
We remove aliased networks from further consideration in our results.

Differentiating internal and external responses
We next differentiate between whether the responses we get to our IPv6
pings are coming from the “external” CPE WAN interface (i.e., the
home gateway router) or from an internal device’s LAN interface.
Even with NAT, IPv4 scans can reach CPE WAN interfaces (which is why
IoT botnets commonly exploit vulnerabilities in home
routers
(
Herwig et al. 2019
)
).
We are thus primarily interested in the extent to which the transition
to IPv6 has made internal devices publicly reachable.

We identify two broad classes of responses:

(1) External responses.

Responses originating from addresses
we did not probe
are typically
ICMPv6 Destination Unreachable type error messages, such as Administratively
Prohibited or Address Unreachable.
In IPv6 residential networks, these responses generally originate from
a CPE’s WAN interface
(
Rye and Beverly 2020
;
Rye et al. 2021
)
when the CPE cannot
route the traffic to the destination, either because the destination
address is not in use or its security policy does not permit it.
We refer to these responses as
external responses
hereafter,
because the response originates from the “external” or WAN side of
the CPE device.

(2) Internal responses.

When responses come from one of the first ten addresses within the /56
that we probed, we refer to them as
internal responses
.
In IPv6 residential networks, these addresses may be active on a home
or small-business LAN for one of several reasons.
First, though DHCPv6 is not ubiquitous in IPv6 networks, unlike its
IPv4 cousin, it can be implemented in residential networks.
The home gateway may implement it and allocate addresses to clients
starting at
::1
.
Further, in IPv6 a device may have
several
IPv6 addresses
assigned, including a EUI-64
SLAAC
(
SLAAC
) or
SLAAC
with Privacy
Extensions (PE) address, in addition to a DHCPv6 address.
While it may prefer to initiate new connections with one address, a
device will respond to any.
We show an example of this phenomenon in §
5.1
.
Second, a device on the LAN—likely the gateway—may simply choose a
low-byte address like
::1
when it boots for ease of
administration.
Network administrators are familiar with the gateway device having an
IPv4 address ending in
.1
, and choosing the
::1
for
the LAN interface of the router in IPv6 is a natural extension of this
paradigm.
Networks that return
internal responses
contain at least one

predictable
address that an attacker can guess to connect with
an interface on the LAN.
Figure
1
provides an illustration of a residential network
with internal and external addresses.

Protocol Scans
After identifying responsive addresses,
we aggregate open port and running service
information using ZGrab2. For each responsive address, whether an
internal

or
external
response,
we initiate a series of protocol scans on a variety of common ports and
protocols. We chose 25 ports and protocol combinations that we use ZGrab2 to
interrogate.
We selected them due to their general popularity as well as their being
common targets of IoT botnets
(
Herwig et al. 2019
)
.
Of note, we are the first to develop a ZGrab2 module to discover devices running the “iPhone-Sync” protocol, which is used
to wirelessly sync an iPhone with a computer over port
62078
(
usb 2025a
)
. We will release this ZGrab2 module as a contribution
to the network security community. Descriptions of the port and protocol
combinations we probed are given in Table
6
in the Appendix.

3.3.
Limitations

While our measurement campaign is designed to discover IPv6 addresses within residential networks, several factors complicate
this endeavor.

First, as noted by Rye and Levin
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
, many modern
operating systems (notably Windows, some versions of Android, and
iOS/macOS) do not use the NTP Pool for time by default.
However, many versions of Linux do use NTP Pool by default.
This may bias our passive discovery towards networks that contain
routers or IoT devices running embedded Linux.
However, it does not preclude us finding devices that do not use the
NTP Pool: so long as there is any device in a given /48 that comes to
one of our NTP Pool servers, we will scan all of the /56s within that
/48.
Moreover, it is common for IoT devices running embedded Linux to run
within households that also run Apple, Android, or Windows devices.

Second, IPv6 addressing is highly dynamic. Prior studies report ISPs reallocating
delegated residential prefixes over time intervals as short as 24
hours
(
Padmanabhan et al. 2016
;
Padmanabhan et al. 2020
;
Rye et al. 2021
)
. This complicates measurement
campaigns longer than the prefix rotation period, as probes sent to a network at
one point in time may end up in a different customer’s network at a
later point in time.
To minimize the effects of this churn, we conducted all of our
measurements within the course of two weeks in early January 2025.

Third, some ISPs may delegate residential allocations at a finer granularity than
/56 (e.g., /60 or /64), the granularity at which we probe for active LAN addresses
using ZMap6. In this case, we neglect to probe many of the residential subnets
inside of the ISP /48 networks. However, in the event that this is the case, our
results are merely a lower bound on the reachability of IPv6 LANs.

Fourth, our scans use ICMPv6 responsiveness as a prerequisite for
subsequent scanning, but devices can be responsive to network services
without being reachable via (or responsive to) pings. Our results are
thus a lower bound.

Fifth, in some networks, the CPE may be assigned an address between

::1
and
::a

on its WAN (external) interface
,
rather than using
SLAAC
to assign it, or being assigned an address
outside of our internal probing range.
For CPE that are configured this way, we will erroneously consider the
responsive WAN IPv6 address internal, rather than external.

Finally, we may miss some active hosts during our ZMap6 and ZGrab2 scans due to limiting the range of IPv6 addresses we probe (e.g., from

::1
-
::a
in each /56).
Alternatively, some DHCPv6 implementations may start assigning IPv6 addresses at an address besides
::1
, such as
::1000
.
This scenario also causes our results to be a lower bound on the number
of devices in the IPv6 residential networks we probe.

4.2.
Which Residential IPv6 Networks Respond?

Here, we evaluate how well our scanning methodology

Recall that our scanning methodology takes as input the 2.5M

(

–

Recall further that we remove all aliased networks from consideration;
we found only 75,287 /56 networks that were aliased, a small fraction
of those we probed (0.01%).

In total, 66.9M unique addresses responded to our ICMPv6 pings,
after excluding aliased networks.
This includes both successful responses as well as error responses:
both indicate a responsive host that can be further scanned.
The responsive addresses span 1,191,256 /48s; 3,362

We speculate that some CPE devices self-assign
::1

To test this hypothesis, we computed the hop count distance from the
responsive hosts to our measurement machine.
More precisely, we took the IPv6
hop limit
—IPv6’s analog to
IPv4’s Time-To-Live (TTL)—and assumed a starting hop limit of 64 if
the received hop limit was less than 64, 128 if greater than 64, and
255 if greater than 128.
For example, if the response we received had a hop limit of 118, then
we assume it started at 128 and was thus a distance of 10 hops away.

For networks where we received both an internal
and
an external
response, we computed the difference in hop count distances between
them.
A difference of zero indicates that the external and internal
addresses correspond to interfaces on the same device; a positive
difference indicates a device behind the home gateway.

Figure
2(b)
shows the distribution of the differences in
distance between internal and external responses from the same network.
Indeed, we find that a difference of zero is the most common case,
constituting 3,738,626 (83%) of the internal-external IPv6 pairs that
responded to us.
Another 426,934 (10%) of internal-external IPv6 pairs are at a
distance of +1, indicating the device assigned the internal IPv6 address is one hop behind the residential gateway.
Larger differences in hop limits account for another 7%, and may arise
from path changes, load balancing
(
Bhaskar and Pearce 2024
)

an incorrect assumption about the starting hop limit.

Responsive /48s
Another metric by which to measure the efficacy of our methodology is
to examine the
yield
of the IPv6/48s we probed.
That is, given a /48, what is the probability that there is at least
one responsive IPv6 address?
Of the 2.5M residential IPv6 /48s we probed, 72% responded with an
external address, 34% responded with an internal address, and 1.9M
(76%) responded with one or both.
Figure

shows the number of internal and external
responses in the 1.9M responsive /48s; while most /48s have a small
number of responsive hosts, 12% (3%) of them have more than 100
distinct hosts that give external (internal) responses.
These results show that, despite its straightforward nature, our scanning
methodology is effective at converting /48 datasets into responsive
hosts—and potential targets for attack.

indicating a web server is running on the LAN IP of a Nokia router. The HTTPS
response data indicates that these devices serve their login page on their
internal address; 2.5k also run an SSH server on the internal address. None of
these devices are responsive to our protocol scans on their external addresses.

Internal-only accessibility
Finally, we look at Telnet-accessible devices to reinforce the security
vulnerabilities that arise with permitting unsolicited traffic into
residential LANs.
In our data, we find 3,907 devices with internal IPv6 addresses
running Telnet servers, while the external address does not respond to
any protocol.
These servers are exposed on internal addresses in residential networks
whose external addresses are unresponsive to any application protocol
scan.
This demonstrates the danger in residential IPv6 networks—while
running services may refuse connections on external addresses, there are
many exposed
and reachable
LAN-internal services, likely unbeknownst
to their owners.
This type of vulnerability is almost always prevented in IPv4 by NAT;
in IPv6, a lack of stateful firewalling in some home routers permits
us to find hosts at predictable, low-numbered addresses.

press a button on the lighting systems to effect the upgrade, Internet-accessible IoT devices that accept uploads from arbitrary hosts
constitute a security risk that IPv4’s NAT prevents. A plurality of these
devices (90 (35%)) are found in Romanian networks, and 52 (20%) are found in
Bahrain.

5.4.
MQTT

MQTT is a message queueing protocol commonly supported by IoT devices.
We analyzed internal and external IPv6 responses for MQTT server
support.

Of the addresses we scanned for MQTT, 1,047 successfully responded to our ZGrab2 probes. We categorize these responsive addresses by responsive
address type (internal vs external), and by the response code the responsive
host returned when we probed it. External addresses were 5.5 times more likely to
respond with an MQTT return code 5, which indicates that the connection was
refused due to the client being unauthorized, than return code 0, indicating an
accepted connection (682 vs 125 unique IPv6 addresses). Conversely, internal addresses
were 2.3 times more likely to respond with MQTT return code 0 than return code 5
(168 vs 72 unique IPv6 addresses addresses.)
This demonstrates a substantive difference
in security posture between IPv6 addresses internal to residential LANs, and
residential addresses facing the WAN, and highlights the dangers in allowing
unsolicited traffic into IPv6 LANs by CPE devices.

6.
Responses to Our Scans

During our scans, we received several opt-out requests and notifications of
detected malicious activity.
We emphasize that
this was not an intended goal of our measurements
.
We did not actively seek to trigger IDSes, nor did we set out with the initial
research questions of whether administrators are monitoring their networks for
malicious traffic.
On the contrary, we took steps to minimize how malicious our traffic might
look: we limited our interactions with devices to the bare minimum to ascertain
whether they were publicly accessible and what services they were running.
Moreover, we made our identities clear and honored all opt-out
requests (§
A
).
Nonetheless, the responses we got to our scans represent an important
perspective on the unobserved nature of IPv6, and we feel compelled to present
them here.

VPS
We performed the majority of our scans using VPSes in cloud providers.
We initially found it extremely difficult to perform the IPv4-based scan of the
iPhone-Sync protocol.
Our VPS provider classified our scan traffic as the “source of outgoing
attacks against third parties,” despite the innocuous nature of our scans and
the opt-out web page we hosted.
This led to the VPS provider preventing our continued iPhone-Sync scans on
IPv4.
Based on emails from the VPS provider, they appear to be tracking the number of
IPv4 connections being made by their VPSes using Linux’s
conntrack

utility.
However, despite running IPv6 scans on the same VPSes over the course of a
year, no such warnings from our VPS provider were ever initiated.

University IDS
Because of the difficulties conducting IPv4 scans using VPSes, we eventually
conducted our IPv4 protocol scans using a machine on our institution’s
network.
Within
four minutes
of starting our IPv4-based scan of the iPhone-Sync
protocol from one of our institution’s servers, we received a (presumably
automated) email from a top-tier academic institution
informing us that they were blocking our IP
address’s access to their networks.
Over the next several hours our scanning machine’s IPv4 address was listed as
‘‘suspicious’’ for its scanning activity on several Internet threat
intelligence websites
3
3

3

We ran our other IPv4 scans several weeks later;
the blocking did appear to persist.
.
One of these sites advertises that it currently does not support IPv6.

Takeaways
What these events indicate is that the IPv6 Internet is significantly less monitored
for malicious traffic than IPv4.
Thus, to the extent that IoT botnets (and other attackers) do start to actively
scan and attack residential networks, we are currently not as well positioned to
detect and mitigate their efforts as we are in IPv4.

7.
Mitigations

Our results show that millions of home gateways lack a stateful firewall that
would prohibit unsolicited traffic to their LAN networks.
It is unclear to us why this is still the case.
To the extent that device manufacturers have eschewed implementing this
security feature, we hope that this work demonstrates that discovering devices
on that LAN is no longer a hypothetical attack, but is possible even in IPv6’s
vast address.
Therefore, our strongest mitigation recommendation for device manufacturers is
to implement stateful firewalling to bring IPv6 up to the level of security
that IPv4 home LAN hosts inherit naturally from NAT.

Our results also show that even devices that use SLAAC addressing (random IIDs)
use easy-to-guess aliases (e.g.,
::1
), most likely given to them
by their DHCPv6 server.
We suggest two approaches to mitigate this issue.

First
, we recommend that devices not use their DHCPv6-assigned
address when SLAAC addressing is available.
Android devices already do this: they do not even support
DHCPv6
(
Android Public Issue Tracker 2025
)
.

Second
, to make it more difficult for an adversary to guess an active
/128, we recommend that DHCPv6 servers issue IPv6 addresses with random
IIDs.

Finally, our experiences indicate that IPv6 is less monitored for malicious
traffic than IPv4.
We recommend that network operators monitor IPv6 for malicious traffic at least
as thoroughly as IPv4.

8.
Conclusions

We showed that IPv6 networks can be an easy and effective addition to IoT
botnets’ arsenal.
Using a publicly available dataset of active /48s and a straightforward
scanning methodology that could run on virtually any device, we showed
that we can reach millions of services on residential networks.
We also demonstrated that, unlike with IPv4, we can reach
inside
of the
networks—something that required sophisticated attacks in IPv4 to
accomplish
(
Kumar et al. 2021
)
.

Our results paint a grim picture of a looming threat for the Internet.
At the time of this writing, roughly half of the Internet has transitioned from
IPv4 to IPv6, but most residential users do not realize that their networks
have changed.
NAT was once the de facto firewall for the Internet.
With NAT gone, unwitting users must now rely on firewalls (which they do not
seem to be deploying en masse) and randomized IIDs to protect their networks
from being scanned.
Unfortunately, our results show that randomized IIDs are of limited utility, as
even devices that generate random IIDs (such as iPhones) will still honor
DHCPv6 leases, which often give low-order IIDs, (e.g.,
::1
and

::2
).

Worse yet, we unintentionally discovered that, IPv6 is less monitored for
malicious traffic than IPv4.
Put simply: the Internet is currently not ready for when the IoTv6 botnets
appear.
Our hope is that our results and proposed mitigations can spur action by
network operators and CPE manufacturers so that we might prepare.

Appendix A
Ethical Considerations

Scanning using ZMap and ZGrab2 are both extremely common in IPv4, and the
ethical considerations and best practices are well
established
(
Durumeric et al. 2013
)
.
However, as our results will show, scanning over IPv6 presents unique
considerations because many more devices can be directly connected to in the
absence of NAT boxes.
In this section, we revisit the ethical considerations of scanning through this
new lens of highly reachable devices.
Our institution’s IRB determined our experimentation exempt and not human
subjects research; nonetheless, as we will describe here, we took special care
in data collection and storage to ensure ethically responsible research and
good Internet citizenship.

A.1.
Scanning Considerations

One of the primary ethical considerations in Internet scanning is avoiding
network abuse through overloading networks with too many scanning packets.

Standard considerations
As is standard in IPv4
(
Durumeric et al. 2013
)
, we randomize our scanning
targets and rate-limit, so as not to overload networks.
We also host web pages on our scanning machines that provide information
about our scans and allow network operators to opt their networks out of our
scans—none did.
In addition to these standard considerations, Williams et al.
(
Williams et al. 2024
)

raise two IPv6-specific ethical concerns:

Avoiding aliased networks
First, they suggest actively identifying IPv6 aliases to avoid re-scanning
them.
As described in §
3
, we identify aliased networks that
respond to random-IID addressed probes, and simply remove all aliased networks
from subsequent scanning altogether.

Minimizing seed overheads
Whereas IPv4 scans cover the entire address space, IPv6 scans require

seed
datasets—addresses or prefixes that are believed to be
active—so as to avoid scanning unused portions of the IPv6 address space.
Most prior IPv6 scanning approaches used seed datasets that were collected using
active scans (most commonly the IPv6 Hitlist).
As a result, Williams et al.
(
Williams et al. 2024
)
suggest using public scan datasets
when available, so as to reduce the additional load of active scans.
We avoid this particular ethical concern altogether by collecting our data

passively
by running public NTP servers.

A.2.
Data Sensitivity

Often, data collected from IPv4 scanning is not treated as sensitive.
The content is hosted publicly, and—as with our experiments—data collection
is performed without trying to provide any authentication (e.g., guessing
usernames or passwords).
As a result, the community has operated under the reasonable belief that users
do not have a reasonable expectation of privacy of their publicly-hosted data.

However, the results of our work indicate that this belief might not hold
for IPv6.
We will show, at unprecedented scale, that residential IPv6 transition has
made hosts that were previously shielded from direct scanning in IPv4 now
globally accessible in IPv6.
Given that IPv6 transitions are often performed without users’ knowledge, we
anticipate that many users are likely unaware that their internal devices are
now globally-accessible.
It is therefore possible that some open services we scanned sent information
that the user did not expect was publicly reachable.

We revisit this concern through the three pillars of ethical research as
outlined in the Belmont report:

Respect for persons
Out of respect for users and to protect against any potential PII that may have
been included in the responses, we will not be making data obtained from ZGrab2 scans publicly available; we store them on a secure machine that only the
authors have access to.
Similarly, following the suggestions of Rye and Levin and the NTP Pool
operators
(
Rye and Levin 2023
)
, we will not publish only the /48 prefixes of
the active IPv6 addresses we observed.
Moreover, out of respect for the NTP Pool service operators, we strictly
adhered to their requirements (§
3.1
) and did not directly
scan-back the /128s we learned.

Beneficence
To minimize the potential harm of our work, we make minimal requests necessary
to determine whether a host is publicly running any particular service.
We use the standard ZGrab2 requests, which only make basic requests (e.g., for
HTTP, it sends a GET request for
/
) and minimizes interactions (e.g.,
for MQTT, it only connects, but does not subscribe to any channels).
To maximize the possible benefits of our research, we are actively attempting
to mitigate this (likely unintentional) reachability by disclosing our results
to impacted routing manufacturers.
Thus far, we have disclosed our results to a major router vendor, who has
acknowledged that some of their products may be affected by this vulnerability.
We are in the process of disclosing our results to other manufacturers, as well.

Justice
It would be unjust to subject only specific populations to our scans.
We ran NTP Pool servers in as many countries as we were able to obtain vantage
points in, and we used the entirety of the /48s of which we learned, treating
all regions and networks equally in our work.
We have no reason to believe (nor do our results indicate) that any one
region makes their users more vulnerable than others.

More broadly, with IPv6 scanning, we believe the community should revisit its
assumption that publicly accessible content is not sensitive.
We recommend treating the data as if users had a reasonable expectation of
privacy, and either keeping datasets private (as we are doing) or curating data
to identify and remove any PII before making them public.
