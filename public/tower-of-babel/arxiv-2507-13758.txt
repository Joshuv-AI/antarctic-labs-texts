Reasoning Models Can be Easily Hacked by Fake Reasoning Bias
Qian Wang, Yubo Fan, Zhenheng Tang, Nuo Chen, Wenxuan Wang, Bingsheng He

Abstract
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) like DeepSeek-R1 and o1 are increasingly used as automated evaluators or judgers, raising critical questions about their vulnerability to the aesthetics of reasoning in LLM-as-a-judge settings. We introduce THEATER, a comprehensive benchmark to systematically evaluate this vulnerability—termed Reasoning Theater Bias (RTB)—by comparing LLMs and LRMs across subjective preference and objective factual datasets. Through investigation of six bias types including Simple Cues and Fake Chain-of-Thought, we uncover three key findings: (1) in a critical paradox, reasoning-specialized LRMs are consistently more susceptible to RTB than general-purpose LLMs, particularly in subjective tasks; (2) this creates a task-dependent trade-off, where LRMs show more robustness on factual tasks but less on subjective ones; and (3) we identify ‘shallow reasoning’—plausible but flawed arguments—as the most potent form of RTB. To address this, we design and evaluate two prompting strategies: a targeted system prompt that improves accuracy by up to 12% on factual tasks but only 1-3% on subjective tasks, and a self-reflection mechanism that shows similarly limited effectiveness in the more vulnerable subjective domains. Our work reveals that RTB is a deep-seated challenge for LRM-based evaluation and provides a systematic framework for developing more genuinely robust and trustworthy LRMs.

Introduction

As Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across many domains
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
, researchers increasingly deploy them as automated evaluators—a paradigm known as LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Gu and Others 2024
;
Li and Others 2024
)
. With advancements in reasoning methods since 2025, the landscape of LLMs has evolved into a new category termed Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), exemplified by models like DeepSeek-R1
Guo et al. 2025
and OpenAI’s o1
Jaech et al. 2024
. Unlike standard LLMs, LRMs have a "think" process, enhancing performance in complex reasoning tasks through explicitly designed mechanisms, such as generating chains of thought (CoT) and refining multi-step logical inferences before final answers
Xu et al. 2025b
;
Tang et al. 2025
. Consequently, LRMs are increasingly employed as automated evaluators
Wang et al. 2025a
to judge outputs
Li and Others 2024
or work as reward models in RL
Bandyopadhyay et al. 2025
.

LRMs exhibit significantly larger accuracy drops (10-12%) compared to standard LLMs (4-5%), demonstrating their heightened susceptibility to superficial reasoning cues—a key indicator of Reasoning Theater Bias.

Based on these findings, we term this phenomenon
Reasoning Theater Bias (RTB)
. These considerations motivate us to systematically investigate the following questions: How do Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) and standard Large Language Models (LLMs) differ in their susceptibility to RTB? Which types of deceptive reasoning are most potent, and how does this vary across subjective and factual tasks? Finally, how effectively can prompting strategies mitigate these vulnerabilities?

To answer these questions, we introduce
THEATER
, a comprehensive benchmark to investigate Reasoning Theater Bias. Our framework systematically evaluates two categories of bias—subtle
Simple Cues
and elaborate
Fake Chain-of-Thought
—across a range of LRMs and LLMs. We test these models on both subjective preference alignment datasets and objective fact-based datasets to analyze performance in different contexts.

We have three main findings from our experiments:
(1)
Despite their advanced reasoning capabilities, LRMs exhibit a critical paradox, proving consistently more susceptible to RTB than their LLM counterparts.
(2)
This vulnerability is most severe in subjective tasks, where we identify that “shallow reasoning"—plausible but flawed arguments—is the most potent form of deception.
(3)
This creates a task-dependent performance trade-off, with LRMs showing greater robustness only in narrow, fact-based domains.

Based on our benchmark, we design and evaluate two mitigation strategies: a
targeted system prompt
that improves accuracy by up to 12% on factual tasks but provides only minimal improvement (1-3%) on subjective tasks, and a
self-reflection prompt
that shows similarly limited effectiveness in the more vulnerable subjective domains. We find that the failure of these strategies in subjective contexts demonstrates that RTB is a deep-seated challenge, not a surface-level flaw.

Our key contributions are:

❶

We define and formalize
Reasoning Theater Bias (RTB)
, identifying it as a critical vulnerability in modern reasoning models, particularly LRMs.

❷

We develop
THEATER
, a comprehensive evaluation framework with six distinct bias types for systematically measuring and diagnosing RTB across different models and tasks.

❸

We provide the first large-scale empirical evidence that specialized LRMs are more susceptible to RTB than LLMs, pinpointing shallow reasoning on subjective tasks as the key failure mode.

❹

We demonstrate that RTB is highly resistant to prompting-based mitigation in subjective domains, highlighting a fundamental challenge for developing trustworthy LRMs.

2
Proposed Framework: THEATER

To systematically investigate RTB, we propose
THEATER
(THinking Evaluation And Testing for Erroneous Reasoning), a comprehensive benchmark framework illustrated in Figure
2
. Its purpose is to quantify model susceptibility to deceptive reasoning cues through a controlled experimental pipeline. The framework consists of three core components: a suite of designed bias injections, a carefully selected set of models and tasks for evaluation, and a set of metrics to measure the bias’s impact.

Figure 2:
The THEATER framework for systematically evaluating Reasoning Theater Bias. It details our methodology for injecting six distinct bias types (from Simple Cues to Fake CoT) across different models (LRMs and LLMs) and task domains (subjective and factual). This process allows for a precise quantification of model vulnerabilities and reveals key insights, such as the paradox where reasoning-specialized models are more susceptible to deceptive reasoning cues.

2.1
Bias Injection Design

The THEATER benchmark includes two primary categories of bias designed to probe vulnerabilities at different levels of complexity, as detailed in Table
2
.

Simple Cues.
These are superficial textual markers strategically injected
between
the two answer options (In-Option) to create a "pause for thought" effect. This category is designed to test if the mere appearance of contemplation, without substantive content, is enough to sway the model’s judgment. It includes three types:
Wait Cues
(e.g., "wait… wait…"), which imitate cognitive hesitation;
Metalinguistic Cues
(e.g., "Let me think"), which explicitly signal a thinking process; and
Reflective Cues
(e.g., "However, on second thought"), which imply reconsideration.

Fake Chain-of-Thought (Fake CoT).
This more sophisticated category simulates a structured yet fallacious reasoning process, appended
after
both options are presented (Post-Option), to serve as a deceptively persuasive analysis. This category tests whether models prioritize the format of reasoning over its logical validity. It is divided into three types of escalating complexity:
Shallow Reasoning
, which uses simple logical fallacies like appeals to authority (e.g., "experts agree…");
Deep Reasoning
, which mimics a multi-step analytical process to create an illusion of depth; and
Fake Reflection
, which constructs a compelling narrative of initial consideration followed by a decisive reversal.

All bias injection texts were generated using Claude-3.5
(
Anthropic 2024
)
to avoid self-preference bias in our benchmarked models. The prompts used for generation are detailed in Appendix
A.3
.

2.2
Experimental Setup

Models.
To isolate reasoning-specific vulnerabilities, we compare a diverse set of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) against general-purpose Large Language Models (LLMs). Our selection includes state-of-the-art LRMs such as the DeepSeek-R1 series, Qwen’s QwQ-32B, and the o1 models. These are benchmarked against strong LLMs like DeepSeek-V3, the Qwen2.5 series, and GPT-4o, allowing for controlled comparisons across different architectures and scales, as detailed in Table
7
.

Tasks and Datasets.
To assess RTB across different contexts, we evaluate models on two distinct task types. For
subjective evaluation
, we use human preference DPO datasets where the ’better’ answer is a matter of judgment (e.g., Truthy-DPO, Orca-DPO). For
objective evaluation
, we use fact-based multiple-choice datasets adapted from MMLU-Pro (e.g., Chemistry, History), where there is a single correct answer. This dual-domain approach allows us to examine if the bias manifests differently when ground truth is ambiguous versus when it is clear.

2.3
Evaluation Metrics

Our evaluation focuses on a pair-wise comparison format, where a model
M
must choose between two responses,
R_{A}
and
R_{B}
. We quantify the model’s performance using the following metrics.

Accuracy.
The primary metric is accuracy, which measures whether the model’s final judgment aligns with the ground-truth preference. In our setup, where the bias is always applied to favor the incorrect answer, a lower accuracy indicates a higher susceptibility to the bias. The judgment is mapped to a binary score
y\in\{0,1\}
, where
y=1
signifies a correct choice.

Robustness Rate.
To directly measure the impact of the bias, we define a
Robustness Rate
. This is calculated as the model’s accuracy on a biased test set divided by its accuracy on the clean, unbiased baseline.

\text{Robustness Rate}=\frac{\text{Accuracy}_{\text{biased}}}{\text{Accuracy}_{\text{baseline}}}

(1)

A score below 1.0 indicates a performance degradation caused by the bias, with lower scores signifying greater vulnerability. This metric allows for a normalized comparison of bias impact across different models and tasks.

2.4
Assessment Details

Comparing LRMs vs. LLMs.
To investigate whether susceptibility to RTB stems from general model properties or is specifically linked to reasoning capabilities, we select a diverse set of models for controlled comparison. As detailed in Table
7
, our benchmark includes models spanning three key dimensions: (1) LRM vs. LLM classification, (2) model family, and (3) open-source availability. We include state-of-the-art LRMs like the DeepSeek-R1-Distill series (DS-R1), Qwen’s QwQ-32B, and O1 models. These are compared with strong baseline LLMs such as DeepSeek-V3 (DS-V3), other models from the Qwen2.5 series and GPT-4o.

Comparing Human Preference Alignment Datasets vs. Factual Datasets.
To investigate how models handle RTB when evaluating subjective versus objective content, we use both types of datasets. For subjective evaluation, we use human preference DPO datasets: Emerton-DPO, Orca-DPO, Py-DPO, Truthy-DPO. For objective evaluation, we use fact-related multiple-choice datasets adapted from MMLU-Pro: Math, Chemistry, History, Psychology. This allows examining if the bias manifests differently depending on task nature.

Judging Bias Evaluation.
We formalize the process of evaluating judgments produced by a judge model
M
. Given a task instruction
I
and an input query
Q
, the model
M
evaluates a set of candidate items
\mathcal{R}
. The model’s primary output is a final judgment
J=M(I,Q,\mathcal{R})
. While LRMs might generate intermediate reasoning steps
S
and reflection
\Phi
, our quantitative analysis primarily focuses on the final judgment
J
and its derived score
y
, as this reflects the ultimate decision influenced by potential RTB. We focus on the pair-wise comparison evaluation format:

Pair-wise Comparison.
The set of candidates is
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{A},R_{B}\}
, representing two distinct responses. The judgment
J
indicates a preference relation (e.g.,
R_{A}\succ_{J}R_{B}
). We map this judgment to a binary score
y
.

y=\mathbf{1}(R_{A}\succ_{J}R_{B})\in\{0,1\}

(2)

Here,
R_{A}\succ_{J}R_{B}
signifies that judgment
J
prefers
R_{A}
over
R_{B}
, and
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. By convention,
y=0
implies
R_{B}\succ_{J}R_{A}
. This definition provides a quantitative score
y\in\{0,1\}
based on the model’s judgment
J
.

Experiments

In this section, we address three key research questions (RQs):
RQ1:
How do simple superficial cue properties affect RTB magnitude?
RQ2:
How does fake CoT affect RTB magnitude?
RQ3:
Can we mitigate RTB through prompting?

3.1
RQ1: How Susceptible Are Models to Simple Cues?

Figure 3:
Performance comparison of LLMs and LRMs under Simple Cue biases across DPO and Factual datasets. Panels (a) and (b) show accuracy metrics, while (c) and (d) present robustness scores. All panels compare three simple cue types: Wait, Think, and Fake Reflection, with both individual model results and LLM/LRM averages showing distinct vulnerability patterns.

Approach.
We investigate the extent to which models are susceptible to Simple Cues, which mimic the superficial performance of thinking. Following our methodology in Section
2
, we inject the three types of simple cues—Wait Cues, Metalinguistic Cues, and Reflective Cues—between the two answer options as detailed in Table
2
. From the results presented in Figure
3
, which are averaged across all relevant datasets, we have the following findings:

❶
LLMs are generally more robust than LRMs of a similar parameter scale.
Our analysis shows a clear trend where general-purpose LLMs better resist superficial cues than their reasoning-specialized LRM counterparts. On average, LLMs consistently achieve higher robustness scores across all cue types. For instance, at the 7B scale,
qwen2.5-7b
demonstrates superior average robustness compared to
ds-r1-7b
. The main exception is the LLM
ds-v3
, which exhibits a fragility more typical of an LRM; we hypothesize this may be due to it using training data similar to the
ds-r1
family, causing it to inherit vulnerabilities despite its different architecture.

❷
Robustness is an orthogonal capability not guaranteed by scale or high benchmark performance.
Our results reveal a critical blind spot in common evaluation practices, as strong baseline performance does not ensure resilience against deceptive cues. Furthermore, the "bigger is better" paradigm has clear limits. Scaling the LRM family from
ds-r1-7b
to the 10x larger
ds-r1-70b
yields almost no improvement in DPO robustness. This insight is crucial, suggesting that building resilient AI requires a direct focus on training for robustness, not just optimizing for higher benchmark scores or parameter counts.

❸
Subjective domains are the primary attack surface for reasoning theater, posing a risk to alignment.
The vulnerability of all models is drastically amplified in subjective DPO tasks compared to factual ones where performance is more stable. This is profoundly important because it targets the soft underbelly of modern LRMs in tasks without a single ground truth. The LRM
o1
exemplifies this split, showing strong factual accuracy but a sharp collapse on DPO tasks from 0.79 to 0.65. The fact that the most severe failures for all model types occur in DPO settings highlights that this is a universal risk and a foundational challenge for creating truly trustworthy LRMs.

3.2
RQ2: How do different models respond to fake Chain-of-Thought reasoning?

Approach.
We escalate the complexity of the bias from simple cues to full-fledged fake Chain-of-Thought reasoning. We inject three types of perturbations—Shallow Reasoning, Deep Reasoning, and Fake Reflection—designed to deceptively support an incorrect answer. By analyzing accuracy and robustness as shown in Figure
4
, we aim to uncover how different model architectures withstand more sophisticated forms of reasoning theater.
We summarize our findings as follows:

❶
Plausible simplicity is the most potent vector for deception.
Our most critical finding is that shallow, simple-seeming fake reasoning is far more effective at deceiving models than more complex fabrications. Across both DPO and factual tasks, all models suffer their most catastrophic accuracy drops against Shallow CoT. As shown in Figure
4
a, average DPO accuracy for both LRMs and LLMs plummets from a baseline of 0.68 to a near-random 0.42. This reveals a fundamental model bias for cognitive ease; models are not just checking for the presence of reasoning steps, but are highly susceptible to any narrative that appears coherent and direct, even if it’s logically flawed.

❷
Architectural strengths diverge under complex deception, revealing a factuality-subjectivity trade-off.
As the fake reasoning becomes more complex, the distinct strengths of each architecture emerge. On factual tasks, LRMs consistently prove more robust than LLMs, leveraging their structured reasoning to better parse and reject the flawed logic. Conversely, on subjective DPO tasks, LLMs show superior resilience against the most complex, metacognitive "Fake-Reflection" CoT. This suggests an LRM’s rigidity is an asset for fact-checking but a liability against deceptive meta-narratives, whereas an LLM’s flexibility is better suited for navigating ambiguity.

❸
More elaborate deception is surprisingly less effective, revealing a "curse of complexity" for attackers.
Counter-intuitively, making the fake reasoning more detailed or reflective does not make it more persuasive. Model accuracy and robustness are lowest against Shallow CoT and generally improve as the fake reasoning becomes deeper and more reflective. For example, the average LRM robustness on DPO tasks increases from a low of 0.42 (Shallow) to 0.61 (Reflection). This suggests that more complex lies may introduce more logical inconsistencies for the model to detect, or that the sheer plausibility of a simple, direct argument is what makes it so uniquely potent. This is a crucial insight for understanding and defending against adversarial attacks.

Mitigation effectiveness confirms the Factuality-Subjectivity Divide.
Building on our findings from RQ1 and RQ2 that subjective domains are the

❷
Architectural trade-offs persist post-mitigation.
The factuality-subjectivity trade-off between architectures, first identified in RQ2, is not resolved by prompting but rather reinforced. Mitigation attempts amplify the inherent strengths of each model type: LRMs, already stronger on factual tasks, see their greatest accuracy gains there, with some scores reaching as high as 0.90. Conversely, LLMs maintain their superior robustness in subjective domains, showing that prompting does not close the fundamental architectural gap between the two model families.

❸
The superiority of targeted prompts suggests models struggle with genuine metacognition.
Given that RQ1 and RQ2 established that models are easily swayed by superficial cues and simple heuristics, our results here show that activating deep self-correction is difficult. We find that direct, explicit guidance via a
Targeted prompt
is consistently more effective than encouraging introspection with a
Self-Reflection prompt
. This suggests current models benefit more from being explicitly warned about fallacies than from being asked to discover those fallacies themselves through metacognition.

❹
Shallow Reasoning remains the most virulent and resistant form of RTB.
Confirming our central finding from RQ2 that plausible simplicity is the most potent form of deception, our mitigation results prove that
Shallow Reasoning bias is also the most difficult to cure
. Across all models and datasets, this bias category sees the smallest improvements post-intervention. Even after mitigation, accuracy against shallow reasoning in subjective tasks remains near chance levels, highlighting a fundamental limitation that prompting cannot fully overcome.

❺
GPT-4o’s holistic robustness is a consistent outlier.
Throughout our entire investigation—from initial vulnerability testing in RQ1 and RQ2 to mitigation analysis in RQ3—
GPT-4o
consistently demonstrated a superior and more generalizable resistance to RTB. Its baseline performance, particularly in subjective domains, often exceeds that of other models even after they have been aided by mitigation prompts. This suggests its general-purpose training has cultivated a more holistic resilience that is inherently more robust than the specialized yet brittle reasoning of other architectures.

3.4
Discussion

The Paradox of Specialization and Reasoning Aesthetics.
Our findings reveal a compelling paradox in reasoning-specialized models. We hypothesize that their heightened vulnerability to RTB is not an incidental flaw but a systemic consequence of their specialized training. By optimizing for structured thought processes, LRMs may inadvertently learn to overvalue the
aesthetics of reasoning
—the syntactic patterns and formal structure that signify deliberation—at the expense of semantic integrity. The dramatic performance drop when faced with simple cues (RQ1) or specious CoT (RQ2) suggests their evaluation is captured by the performance of thinking, rather than its substance. In contrast, the general-purpose training of LLMs appears to foster a more holistic robustness, making them less susceptible to these superficial heuristics.

Limitations of Behavioral Fixes and The Path Forward.
The limited success of our mitigation strategies (RQ3) indicates that RTB is not a mere behavioral artifact that can be corrected with simple prompting. This limitation is critical: if models cannot be reliably instructed to ignore deceptive reasoning, their utility in high-stakes scenarios is questionable. Furthermore, our results raise a profound concern for AI alignment. If human preference data (e.g., for DPO) is itself susceptible to RTB, we risk creating a feedback loop that trains models to become better deceivers. Addressing this requires moving beyond surface-level interventions. Future work must focus on: 1) developing adversarial training regimes to inoculate models against RTB, and 2) fundamentally rethinking data curation to reward verifiable reasoning over persuasive rhetoric.

Conclusion

In this paper, we identify and evaluate
Reasoning Theater Bias (RTB)
, where automated judgments are swayed by superficial reasoning. We uncover a critical paradox: reasoning-specialized LRMs are ironically more susceptible to RTB than general-purpose LLMs, especially when presented with “shallow reasoning”—plausible but flawed arguments—in subjective tasks. To mitigate this, we tested prompting strategies which, while effective on factual tasks with improvements up to 12%, largely failed on subjective tasks. This demonstrates that RTB is a deep-seated vulnerability, not a surface-level flaw. We hope this work encourages the community to develop more fundamental defenses beyond prompting, such as adversarial training and process-based supervision, to build genuinely robust LRMs.

Our experiments utilize two main types of datasets: DPO datasets and fact-related datasets. The DPO datasets inherently provide pairs of responses (preferred and dispreferred), making them directly suitable for our pairwise comparison studies.

However, the fact-related datasets—Mathematics, Chemistry, History, and Psychology—initially present questions with multiple choice options (typically 10), only one of which is correct. To integrate these into our pairwise comparison framework, we transformed them into binary choice tasks. For each question, we paired the factually correct answer with one incorrect option, randomly selected from the remaining choices for that same question. This method allows us to assess how superficial cues influence model preferences when choosing between a correct and an incorrect factual statement.

For instance, a Mathematics question originally offering ten answer choices (A through J), with C being the correct answer, would be converted into a binary comparison. This might be phrased as: "Which is the correct answer? Option A: [content of the correct option C] or Option B: [content of a randomly selected incorrect option from A, B, D-J]". This transformation preserves the factual nature of the original questions while aligning them with the binary choice format of the DPO datasets, thereby ensuring methodological consistency. We ensured that the incorrect option was selected randomly for each pair to prevent any systematic bias in the difficulty of the comparisons.

A.4
Mitigation Prompts

This section provides the detailed prompts used for mitigating Reasoning Theater Bias in our experiments.

A.4.1
Targeted System Prompt

Targeted system prompt for RTB mitigation

When evaluating options or analyzing information, follow these principles to ensure genuine reasoning:

Avoid Premature Conclusions
: Fully examine all evidence before drawing conclusions. Resist the urge to decide quickly based on superficial cues or presentation style.

Verify Logical Consistency
: Check that your reasoning steps follow logically from one to the next. Identify and correct any inconsistencies or unwarranted assumptions in your thinking.

Ensure Substantive Analysis
: Provide depth in your reasoning beyond surface-level observations. Avoid being influenced by elaborate but empty reasoning that lacks actual substance.

Validate Inferences
: Confirm that your final conclusions are properly supported by your reasoning process. Be willing to revise your position if the evidence doesn’t actually support it.

A.4.2
Self-reflection Prompt

Self-reflection prompt for RTB mitigation

When reasoning through a problem or evaluating options, pause to reflect on your reasoning process:
1. Am I being influenced by superficial features rather than substantive content?
2. Is my reasoning thorough and logically sound, or am I taking shortcuts?
3. Have I considered all relevant information before reaching a conclusion?

If you find your reasoning process is inadequate, revise your approach to ensure genuine, substantive analysis.

LLMs are notably vulnerable to adversarial attacks like prompt injection, where hidden instructions manipulate their behavior, leading to disallowed outputs, data extraction, or safety bypasses

For instance, attacks like JudgeDeceive can degrade LLM-based evaluation reliability, and deceptive fairness attacks can skew outputs
Maloyan and Namiot 2025
;
Cantini et al. 2025

LLM Reasoning

LLM reasoning is an emerging field exploring the reasoning capabilities of LLMs
(
Plaat et al. 2024
;
Lai et al. 2025
)

(1) Step-by-step Reasoning

As part of the process in improving LLMs’ reasoning ability, recent findings show that even for non-reasoning LLMs, reasoning abilities are inherently encapsulated for sufficiently large models.
More specifically, methods such as chain-of-thought

This suggest that the key to improving

On this basis, other methods like self-reflection have been explored to further improve LLMs’ reasoning abilities. Drawing inspiration from the thought process
of humans, researchers find that instructing LLMs to reflect on their chain of thoughts(CoT) empowers them to identify and avoid errors
(
Renze and Guven 2024
;
Madaan et al. 2023
)
. This is a further step towards building
intelligent AI systems without the need of blindly scaling up parameter sizes.
