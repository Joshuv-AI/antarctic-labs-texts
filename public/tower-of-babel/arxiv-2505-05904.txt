SPECTRAL CLUSTER BOUNDS FOR ORTHONORMAL FUNCTIONS ON MANIFOLDS WITH NONSMOOTH METRICS Thanks: © 2025 by the authors. This paper may be reproduced, in its entirety, for non-commercial purposes.
Jean-Claude Cuenin, Ngoc Nhi Nguyen, Xiaoyan Su

Abstract
. We establish L q L^{q} spectral cluster bounds for families of orthonormal functions associated to the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a compact Riemannian manifold. The metric is only assumed to be of class C s C^{s} , where 0 ≤ s ≤ 2 0\leq s\leq 2 .



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Smooth metrics

Let
(M,{\rm g})
be a smooth compact Riemannian manifold without boundary and of dimension
n\geq 2
. We denote by
\Delta_{{\rm g}}
the (negative) Laplace–Beltrami operator on
M
, whose action on smooth functions, in local coordinates, is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{{\rm g}}u=|{\rm g}|^{-1/2}\partial_{i}(|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm g}^{ij}\partial_{j}u),

(1)

where
|{\rm g}|=\det({\rm g}_{ij})
and the summation convention is in effect.
We consider
-\Delta_{{\rm g}}
as a
non-negative, unbounded self-adjoint operator in
L^{2}(M)
, defined with respect to the Riemannian measure
|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x
. For
\lambda\geq 1
, the spectral projection

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}:=\mathds{1}(\sqrt{-\Delta_{{\rm g}}}\in[\lambda,\lambda+1]),

defined by the functional calculus, is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of functions in
L^{2}(M)
that are spectrally localized to frequencies in the unit length window
[\lambda,\lambda+1]
.
We also define the spectral cluster

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}:=\mathop{\rm Ran}\Pi_{\lambda}\subset L^{2}(M).

(2)

If the metric
{\rm g}
is smooth,
a seminal result of Sogge
[
41
]
states that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{\delta(q)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)},\quad u\in E_{\lambda},

(3)

where
2\leq q\leq\infty
and

\delta(q)=\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)&\text{if}\ 2\leq q\leq q_{n}\,,\\
n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\frac{1}{2}&\text{if}\ q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty,\end{cases}\quad q_{n}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n-1}.

(4)

More recently, also for smooth metrics, Frank and Sabin
[
17
, Theorem 3]
proved the following generalization of (
3
) to families of orthonormal functions
(u_{j})_{j\in J}\subset E_{\lambda}
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta(q)}\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|^{\alpha(q)}\bigg)^{1/\alpha(q)},

(5)

where
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
and

\alpha(q):=\begin{cases}\frac{2q}{q+2}&\text{if}\ 2\leq q\leq q_{n},\\
\frac{q(n-1)}{2n}&\text{if}\ q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty.\end{cases}

(6)

Here and in the following,
J
denotes any countable index set. In comparison, using (
3
) and the triangle inequality, one only obtains (
5
) with
\alpha=1
(without the orthogonality assumption). The crucial feature of (
5
)–(
6
) is that
\alpha>1
.
When
\#J=1
, (
5
) coincides with Sogge’s bound (
3
). On the other extreme, when
\#J=\dim E_{\lambda}
, then (
5
), with
\nu_{j}=1
for all
j\in J
, yields

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\in J}|u_{j}|^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{n-1},\quad 2\leq q\leq\infty,

(7)

due to the fact that
\dim E_{\lambda}\approx\lambda^{n-1}
, by the sharp Weyl law (see, e.g.,
[
42
, Chapter 4]
). The sum in (
7
) is independent of the choice of basis
(u_{j})_{j\in J}
of
E_{\lambda}
and coincides with the spectral function corresponding to the interval
[\lambda,\lambda+1]
. The inequality (
5
) can be seen as an optimal interpolation between the two extreme cases (
3
) and (
7
).

The insight that orthonormality improves the dependence on the number of functions, compared to merely applying the triangle inequality, originally stems from the celebrated work of Lieb and Thirring
[
27
,
28
]
, see also Lieb
[
26
]
, in the context of the Sobolev inequalities. More recently, this idea was extended to Strichartz inequalities in, e.g.,
[
14
,
16
,
2
,
3
,
31
,
4
,
20
]
. Spectral cluster estimates for semiclassical Schrödinger operators with smooth confining potentials were established by the second author in
[
32
, Theorem 7.2]
, generalizing the single function bounds of Koch, Tataru, and Zworski
[
25
]
.

1.2.
Nonsmooth metrics

An interesting question is whether the bounds (
3
) or (
5
) hold for metrics of limited regularity. This is especially relevant for applications to nonlinear equations, where the metric evolves in time.

A breakthrough was achieved by Smith in
[
37
]
, where he proved that (
3
) remains valid for

C^{1,1}
metrics. This level of differentiability ensures uniqueness of the geodesic flow. More recently, Chen and Smith
[
8
]
established the same bounds under the weaker assumption that the sectional curvatures of the manifold are uniformly bounded.

Counterexamples by Smith and Sogge
[
38
]
as well as Smith and Tataru
[
40
]
show that
C^{1,1}
is indeed best possible in the
C^{s}
scale. More precisely, they
constructed examples of
C^{s}
metrics, for each
0<s<2
(Lipschitz in case

s=1
), for which there are functions
u\in E_{\lambda}
such that for all
q\geq 2

\displaystyle\frac{\|u\|_{L^{q}(M)}}{\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}}\geq c\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})(1+\frac{2-s}{2+s})},

(8)

where
c>0
.
Thus, these examples exhibit multiplicative losses of
\lambda^{\delta(q)\frac{2-s}{2+s}}
, compared to the smooth case, for
2<q<2(n+2s^{-1})/(n-1)
; for later reference, we note that in the case
n=2
,
s=1
, this range is
2<q<8
.

In
[
36
]
, Smith proved the upper bounds corresponding to (
8
), i.e.

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q})(1+\frac{2-s}{2+s})},\quad u\in E_{\lambda},

(9)

for
1\leq s<2
(Lipschitz if
s=1
) in the range
2\leq q\leq q_{n}
.
He also proved no loss estimates in the range
q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty
on cubes of sidelength
R=\lambda^{-\frac{2-s}{2+s}}
.
Since the constant in these estimates is uniform over all cubes, he also obtains global
L^{\infty}
bounds for Lipschitz metrics that are as good as in the case of smooth metrics,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}},\quad u\in E_{\lambda}.

(10)

Interpolation between
q=q_{n}
and
q=\infty
yields bounds with exponents that are strictly larger than predicted by the
counterexamples in
[
38
]
. Koch, Smith, and Tataru
[
23
]
developed new techniques that combine energy flow estimates for Lipschitz coefficients with combinatorial arguments and proved no-loss estimates (up to a logarithmic loss) for Lipschitz (
s=1
) metrics in
n=2
dimensions. This is best possible in view of the counterexamples in
[
38
]
.

For Hölder metrics, that is
0<s<1
, Koch, Smith, and Tataru
[
22
]
proved estimates with a further loss of
\lambda^{(1-s)(1/2-1/q)}
in the range
2\leq q\leq q_{n}
. It is currently not known what the sharp bounds are for
q_{n}<q<\infty
.
Davies
[
11
]
showed that for
s=0
, spectral cluster bounds are no better than what can be obtained by Sobolev embedding.

Related results include Blair’s work
[
5
]
on metrics with Sobolev regularity, as well as his work
[
6
]
on restrictions of spectral clusters to submanifolds for low regularity metrics. We should also mention the work of Smith and Sogge
[
39
]
, where sharp spectral cluster bounds on two-dimensional manifolds with boundaries are established. Although the metric is smooth to begin with, after doubling the manifold, one ends up with a manifold without boundary but with a Lipschitz metric.

The aim of this article is to extend (a subset of) the spectral cluster bounds for nonsmooth metrics, discussed above, to families of orthonormal functions. We will focus on the results of Smith
[
37
,
36
]
(for the
C^{1,1}
and
C^{s}
,
1\leq s<2
, case, respectively) and the result of Koch, Smith, and Tataru
[
22
]
for the Hölder case (
0<s<1
). We plan to revisit other results mentioned above at a later stage.

1.3.
Main results

The main results of this paper are the following generalizations of
[
37
,
36
,
22
]
to orthonormal systems.
For
s\in[0,2]
, we set

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}:=\mathds{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[\lambda,\lambda+\lambda^{(1-s)_{+}}]),\quad E_{\lambda}:=\mathop{\rm Ran}\Pi_{\lambda},

(11)

where
P=-\Delta_{{\rm g}}
is defined by quadratic form methods, see Section
2.2
(due to the limited regularity of
{\rm g}
), and
x_{+}:=\max(x,0)
.

Theorem 1.1

(The
C^{1,1}
case)
.

Let
(M,{\rm g})
be a smooth compact boundaryless Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Assume that the metric
{\rm g}
is of class
C^{1,1}
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that for any
\lambda\geq 1
, any orthonormal system
(u_{j})_{j\in J}\subset E_{\lambda}
and any sequence
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, the bound (
5
) holds for all
2\leq q\leq\infty
, with
\delta(q)
and
\alpha(q)
as in (
4
) and (
6
), respectively.

Theorem 1.2

(The case
s\in[1,2)
)
.

Let
(M,{\rm g})
be a smooth compact boundaryless Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Assume that the metric
{\rm g}
is of class
C^{s}
for
s\in[1,2)
(Lipschitz in case
s=1
).
Then there exists
C>0
such that for any
\lambda\geq 1
, any orthonormal system
(u_{j})_{j\in J}\subset E_{\lambda}
and any sequence
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, we have, for all
2\leq q\leq\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta_{s}(q)}\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|^{\alpha(q)}\bigg)^{1/\alpha(q)},

(12)

where
\alpha(q)
is given by (
6
), and

\delta_{s}(q):=\begin{cases}\frac{n-1}{2}\left(1+\frac{2-s}{2+s}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)&\text{if}\ 2\leq q\leq q_{n},\\
n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2-s}{2+s}\frac{1}{q}&\text{if}\ q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty.\end{cases}

(13)

Moreover, for any cube
Q_{R}\subset M
of sidelength
R=\lambda^{-\frac{2-s}{2+s}}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{q/2}(Q_{R})}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta(q)}\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|^{\alpha(q)}\bigg)^{1/\alpha(q)},\quad q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty,

(14)

with
\delta(q)
as in (
4
).

Theorem 1.3

(The case
s\in[0,1)
)
.

Let
(M,{\rm g})
be a smooth compact boundaryless Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Assume that the metric
{\rm g}
is of class
C^{s}
for
s\in[0,1)
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that for any
\lambda\geq 1
, any orthonormal system
(u_{j})_{j\in J}\subset E_{\lambda}
and any sequence
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, we have, for all
2\leq q\leq\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}\bigg\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta_{s}(q)}\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|^{\alpha(q)}\bigg)^{1/\alpha(q)},

(15)

where
\alpha(q)
is given by (
6
), and

\delta_{s}(q):=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\left(1+\frac{2-s}{2+s}\right)+1-s\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)&\text{if}\ 2\leq q\leq q_{n},\\
n\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2-s}{2+s}\frac{1}{q}+(1-s)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)&\text{if}\ q_{n}\leq q\leq\infty.\end{cases}

(16)

Remark 1.4
.

The exponents of
\lambda
in Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
are the same as in the one-function bounds
[
37
,
36
,
22
]
. The constants depend on the manifold
M
, the non-degeneracy of the metric
{\rm g}
and its
C^{s}
norm, but are independent of
\nu_{j}
,
u_{j}
and
\lambda
.

1.4.
Operator formulation

As explained in
[
16
,
32
]
, bounds for systems of orthonormal functions like (
5
) or (
12
) can be expressed
more conveniently using operators. While the one-function estimate (
3
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Pi_{\lambda}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{q}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{\delta(q)},

(17)

the estimate (
5
) for systems of orthonormal functions may be restated as follows (cf.
[
17
, Theorem 3]
). If
(u_{j})_{j\in J}\subset E_{\lambda}
is an orthonormal system and
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
are coefficients, we consider the operator

\displaystyle\gamma:=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\langle u_{j}|,

(18)

where we used Dirac’s notation
(|u\rangle\langle v|)f:=\langle v,f\rangle u
, for all
u,v,f\in L^{2}(M)
. Then

\lVert\rho_{\gamma}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta(q)}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))},

(19)

where
\rho_{\gamma}
is called the density, and the norm on the right is a Schatten norm (see Section
2.3
for definitions),

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(x):=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}(x)|^{2},\quad\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))}:=\bigg(\sum_{j\in J}\left\lvert\nu_{j}\right\rvert^{\alpha(q)}\bigg)^{1/\alpha(q)}.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, one may always assume without loss of generality that
\nu_{j}\geq 0
. Note that
\gamma=\Pi_{\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\lambda}
, which implies that
\gamma
has finite rank (because
\Pi_{\lambda}
does).
We also remark that, in the case of smooth metrics,
\rho_{\gamma}
is a smooth function (by elliptic regularity) and hence is pointwise defined.
Using
[
16
, Prop. 1]
, one sees that (
5
) is also equivalent to the estimate

\displaystyle\|W\Pi_{\lambda}W\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)^{\prime}}(L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lambda^{2\delta(q)}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)},

(20)

that holds for any
W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)
, for some
C>0
independent of
W
and
\lambda>1
.
The operator formulation of (
12
) is
analogous, with
\delta(q)
in (
19
), (
20
) replaced by
\delta_{s}(q)
and
\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}
by
\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}\lambda^{(1-s)_{+}}
.
In the following discussion, we will use (
19
) as a reference, with the understanding that all the arguments work equally well if we replace
\delta(q)
by
\delta_{s}(q)
.

Besides the dual formulation (
20
), another useful observation is that (
19
) would follow from the
quasimode bound

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\gamma}\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta(q)}\left(\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))}+\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))}\right)

(21)

(see Lemma
3.4
), where we assume that
\mathop{\rm Ran}(\gamma)\subset\dom(P)
. However, we do not assume that
\gamma=\Pi_{\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\lambda}
here (in contrast to (
19
)). This means that
\gamma
may have high-frequency components, and hence
q
needs to be sufficiently small (dictated by Sobolev embedding or its analogue for operators, see Proposition
4.3
, which gives
q\leq 2n/(n-2)
.) In any dimension,

q=q_{n}
is allowed, which will be sufficient (see Section
1.5
below). The quasimode formulation is standard in the one-function case; in the many-function case, a similar version appeared in the work of the second author
[
32
]
.
The bound (
21
) may be restated as a
resolvent estimate
,

\displaystyle\big\|\rho\left((P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i})^{2})^{-1}\gamma(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i})^{2})^{-1}\right)\big\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta(q)-2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))}

(22)

(see Remark
3.3
) where we used the notation
\rho(A):=\rho_{A}
. By the duality principle of Frank and Sabin
[
16
, Lemma 3]
(see also Subsection
2.5
), the latter is equivalent to the following bound,

\displaystyle\left\lVert W(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i})^{2})^{-1}(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i})^{2})^{-1}\overline{W}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)^{\prime}}(L^{2}(M))}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta(q)-2}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}

(23)

for all
W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)
,
which is essentially what we will prove.

1.5.
Remarks on the proof

We first note that the case
q=2
in (
12
) is trivial since
\|\rho_{\gamma}\|_{L^{1}}=\|\gamma\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{1}}
and
\delta(2)=0
,
\alpha(2)=1
. As in
[
17
,
32
]
, the
L^{\infty}
estimates in (
12
) follow from the corresponding one-function bounds (see Proposition
2.1
). Thus, as in
[
17
,
37
,
36
]
, we only need to consider the critical exponent
q=q_{n}
, for which
\delta(q_{n})=1/q_{n}
. The bounds for the other values of
q
are obtained by linear interpolation.
Despite these similarities, there are some important differences in our proof compared to
[
17
]
, as we will now explain in more detail. Their approach starts by replacing
\Pi_{\lambda}
by a “smooth projection”
\chi(\sqrt{-\Delta_{{\rm g}}}-\lambda)
(see
[
17
, (18)]
). They then use the Hadamard parametrix to represent the smooth projection as an oscillatory integral operator modulo a smoothing operator. Finally, they apply a generalization of the Carleson–Sjölin theorem
[
17
, Theorem 1]
to prove (
20
). An analogue of this approach in the one-function case can be found, e.g., in
[
42
, Chapter 5]
.

Our approach is closer to that of
[
37
,
36
,
22
]
(whose results we generalize in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
). As in the one-function case considered in these works, the foundation of our result is the
C^{1,1}
bound (Theorem
1.1
). We will comment on this case momentarily, after discussing the bounds for
s<2
, and we recall that we consider
q=q_{n}
throughout.

Estimates for
s\in[1,2)
are derived from the
C^{1,1}
result together with a frequency-dependent scaling argument and an energy flux estimate of Smith
[
36
, equation (15)]
. On the
R=\lambda^{-(2-s)/(2+s)}
scale,
C^{s}
coefficients are well approximated by
C^{1,1}
coefficients, which leads to no-loss estimates on cubes of size
R
, as in (
14
), and to spectral cluster bounds (
12
) on the whole manifold with an
R^{-1/q_{n}}
loss compared to the smooth case (
5
). The loss arises from summing over roughly
R^{-1}
slabs of the form
S_{R}=\{|x_{1}-c|\leq R\}
, which appear in the energy flux estimates and on which one also has no-loss
L^{q_{n}}
bounds. The appearance of
x_{1}
as a privileged direction (by a partition of unity and a linear change of coordinates, there is no loss of generality in fixing this direction) comes from an angular localization argument that reduces the Helmholtz operator
-\Delta_{{\rm g}}-\lambda^{2}
to a frequency-localized first-order pseudodifferential operator (see Section
4
).

For
s<1
, the counterexample of Koch, Smith, and Tataru
[
22
, Section 4]
shows that eigenfunctions can be exponentially localized to tubes of diameter

\lambda^{-2/(2+s)}
and length
\lambda^{s-1}
, so there can be no energy flux bounds above the
T=\lambda^{s-1}
scale. However, at or below this scale,
C^{s}
coefficients are well approximated by Lipschitz ones, which leads to bounds over slabs
S_{R}
with a
T^{-1/2}
loss (see
[
22
, equation (15)]
). Summing over
TR^{-1}
disjoint slabs
S_{R}
intersecting a given cube of sidelength
T
and then summing over a cover of
M
by such cubes yields the
(TR^{-1})^{1/q_{n}}T^{-1/2}
loss in (
12
).

We now comment on the
C^{1,1}
bound. Smith
[
37
]
obtained the one-function bound as a consequence of square-function (i.e.
L^{q}_{x}L^{2}_{t}
) bounds for the wave equation
(\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta_{{\rm g}})u=0
, in the spirit of Mockenhaupt–Seeger–Sogge
[
30
]
. We have not been able to use this approach for orthonormal systems due to an inefficiency of Littlewood–Paley theory in this case.
Essentially, if
\gamma_{k}:=P_{k}\gamma P_{k}
, where
P_{k}
denotes a smooth Littlewood–Paley projection to frequencies of size
2^{k}
, one is faced, among other things, with estimating expressions like

\displaystyle\sum_{k}2^{2\delta(q)k}\|\gamma_{k}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(L^{2}(M))},

(24)

where
\gamma
is the operator associated to an orthonormal system of initial data in
H^{\delta(q)}(M)
. For a single initial datum,
\gamma=|f\rangle\langle f|
, this expression reduces to
\|f\|^{2}_{H^{\delta(q)}(M)}
, and this is one of the reasons why the proof of the main result of
[
37
]
can be reduced to a frequency-localized estimate
[
37
, equation (2.12)]
. However, for a nontrivial orthonormal system consisting of several functions, (
24
) cannot be bounded by
\|\gamma\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha(q)}(H^{\delta(q)}(M))}
unless
\alpha(q)\leq 1
, which for
\alpha(q)
as in (
6
) is only true for the trivial case
q=2
. This means that, on the level of operators, a frequency-localized bound analogous to
[
37
, equation (2.12)]
cannot readily be summed to yield a global bound. Studying square function bounds for orthonormal systems would be an interesting topic in itself.

The alternative strategy we employ here, while similar in many aspects to
[
37
]
, does not rely directly on the wave equation. Although square-function bounds are extremely powerful and yield spectral cluster estimates as an immediate corollary (see
[
37
]
), the latter can also be derived using only the stationary equation. Note, however, that even in the stationary case, locally in phase space, one can always single out a distinguished space direction that plays the role of (fictional) time.
In the single-function setting, this approach is standard and has been used by many authors. For instance, Koch and Tataru
[
24
]
applied it to establish microlocal
L^{q}
estimates for quasimodes of pseudodifferential operators, including non-self-adjoint ones. Semiclassical analogues in the self-adjoint case were later obtained by Koch, Tataru, and Zworski
[
25
]
.
In the single-function setting, if the metric is smooth, one can use Fourier integral operators to construct a parametrix (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
). Smith
[
35
,
37
]
and Tataru
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
used wave packet techniques to construct parametrices for regularized wave operators.
The smoothing errors can be treated as forcing terms, and the equation can be solved iteratively. The wave packet parametrices require roughly two derivatives on the coefficients as this ensures uniqueness of the bicharacteristic flow. This approach works equally well for both time-dependent and stationary problems but has not been used before in the context of orthonormal systems.

The key estimate in our proof of the
C^{1,1}
result is the pointwise kernel bound (
159
) for the propagator of a regularized, frequency-localized, first-order pseudodifferential operator. This bound follows from a special case of the results of Koch and Tataru
[
24
, Proposition 4.7]
and the Dunford–Pettis theorem
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.5]
.

1.6.
Remarks on optimality

There are two aspects of optimality to be considered. First, one can ask whether the exponent
\delta_{s}(q)
in the one-function estimate is optimal (smallest possible). Second, if this exponent is optimal, one can ask whether the Schatten exponent
\alpha(q)
is optimal (largest possible). We already discussed the first aspect, and we focus now on the second aspect. Since
\dim E_{\lambda}\approx\lambda^{n-1}
by the sharp Weyl law, the Schatten exponent may always be increased at the expense of increasing
\delta_{s}(q)
. This is the reason we fix (the optimal) exponent
\delta_{s}(q)
as in the one-function bounds.

As explained earlier, for
C^{1,1}
coefficients, the exponent
\delta(q)
is the same as in the smooth case, hence best possible by the examples in
[
42
, Section 5.1]
.
For the Laplacian on the two-dimensional sphere with the standard round metric, optimality of the Schatten exponent
\alpha(q)
was shown in
[
17
, Theorem 4]
. They showed optimality in a strong sense, namely, for any fixed ratio
\#J/\lambda^{n-1}
of the number of functions to the maximal number of orthonormal functions in the cluster
E_{\lambda}
, inequality (
5
) is saturated. There is strong evidence that
\alpha(q)
is sharp in any dimension
n\geq 2
and on any compact manifold with smooth metric
[
15

. We thus expect that Theorem

is also optimal in this strong sense.

When

(our bounds hold with

2.1.
The

[

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{\infty}(M)}\lesssim\begin{cases}\lambda^{\frac{n-1}{2}}&\text{for }s\in[1,2],\\
\lambda^{\frac{n-s}{2}}&\text{for }s\in[0,1).\end{cases}

(26)

These bounds directly imply the case
q=\infty
in Theorems
1.1

1.3

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,{\rm d}x),(Y,{\rm d}y)
be measure spaces, and let
T:L^{2}(Y)\to L^{\infty}(X)
be a bounded operator with continuous integral kernel.
If
J
is a countable index set,
(u_{j})_{j\in J}
an orthonormal system in
L^{2}(Y)
and
(\nu_{j})_{j\in J}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, then

\|\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|Tu_{j}|^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(X)}\leq\|T\|_{L^{2}(Y)\to L^{\infty}(X)}^{2}\sup_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\sup_{j\in J}|\nu_{j}|=1
. Let
K:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{C}}
denote the integral kernel of
T
. For fixed
x\in X
, set
K^{x}(y)=K(x,y)
. Then

Tu_{j}(x)=\int_{Y}K^{x}(y)u_{j}(y){\rm d}y=\langle K^{x},u_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(Y)}.

By Bessel’s inequality,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}|Tu_{j}(x)|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}|\langle K^{x},u_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(Y)}|^{2}\leq\|K^{x}\|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2}.

Taking the supremum over
x
yields

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|Tu_{j}(x)|^{2}\leq\sup_{x\in X}\int_{Y}|K(x,y)|^{2}{\rm d}y=\|T\|_{L^{2}(Y)\to L^{\infty}(X)}^{2},

(27)

completing the proof.
∎

2.2.
Quadratic forms and operators

If
{\rm g}
is smooth, then
-\Delta_{g}
is essentially self-adjoint on
C^{\infty}(M)
(i.e., has a unique self-adjoint extension). In the case of limited regularity, we will define a self-adjoint extension by quadratic form methods. Here, we only assume that the coefficients of the metric are bounded and measurable. Consider the sesquilinear form

\displaystyle Q(u,v):=\int_{M}{\rm g}^{ij}\partial_{i}\overline{u}\partial_{j}v\,|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x,\quad u,v\in C^{\infty}(M),

(28)

and denote by
Q(u)=Q(u,u)
the corresponding quadratic form.
Since
({\rm g}_{ij})
is positive definite, there exists
c_{\rm ell}>0
such that, in local coordinates near
x\in M
, one has for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\displaystyle c_{\rm ell}^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq{\rm g}^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq c_{\rm ell}|\xi|^{2},

(29)

It follows from standard results (e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 1.2.6]
) that
Q
is closable. We will denote the closure again by
Q
. Then there exists a canonical non-negative self-adjoint operator
P
associated to
Q
, with form domain

\displaystyle\dom(Q)=\dom(\sqrt{P})=\{u\in L^{2}(M):\int_{M}{\rm g}^{ij}\partial_{i}\overline{u}\partial_{j}u\,|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x<\infty\}=H^{1}(M),

(30)

and
Q(u)=\|\sqrt{P}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}
,
see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 1.2.1]
,
[
21
, Theorems VI.2.1, VI.2.6, VI.2.23]
.
Here, the derivatives are taken in the weak sense.
The operator domain of
P
is given by

\displaystyle\dom(P)=\{u\in H^{1}(M):\exists f\in L^{2}(M)\mbox{ s.t. }Q(u,v)=\langle f,v\rangle,\forall v\in H^{1}(M)\},

(31)

and
Pu=f
, see, e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 1.2.7]
,
[
46
, Theorem 2.14]
. If the coefficients
{\rm g}^{ij}
are
C^{1}
, then
\dom(P)=H^{2}(M)
, and

\displaystyle Pu=-|{\rm g}|^{-1/2}\partial_{i}(|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm g}^{ij}\partial_{j}u)=-{\rm g}^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u-|{\rm g}|^{-1/2}\partial_{i}(|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm g}^{ij})\partial_{j}u,\quad\forall u\in H^{2}(M).

(32)

We will call
P
the Laplace–Beltrami operator (or simply Laplacian) on
M
. By the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem,
P
has compact resolvent. We denote the sequence of discrete eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity) by
(\lambda_{j}^{2})_{j=1}^{\infty}
and the sequence of corresponding eigenfunctions by
(e_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
.
Then

\displaystyle\Pi_{\lambda}:=\mathds{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[\lambda,\lambda+\lambda^{(1-s)_{+}}]):=\sum_{\lambda_{j}\in[\lambda,\lambda+\lambda^{(1-s)_{+}}]}|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|.

(33)

The complex square root is defined by the spectral theorem, i.e.,

\displaystyle(P-z)^{-1/2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{j}^{2}-z)^{-1/2}|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|,

(34)

with
\zeta^{1/2}:=|\zeta|^{1/2}{\rm e}^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\arg\zeta}
,
0\leq\arg\zeta<2\pi
, for all
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
.

2.3.
Schatten spaces

If
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are complex separable Hilbert spaces and
\alpha\in[1,\infty]
, the Schatten space
\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
is defined as the space of compact linear operators
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with norm

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}:=\left(\tr_{\mathcal{H}}(A^{*}A)^{\alpha/2}\right)^{1/\alpha}=\left(\tr_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}(AA^{*})^{\alpha/2}\right)^{1/\alpha}

if
\alpha<\infty
and with the operator norm
\|\cdot\|
if
\alpha=\infty
.
When
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
, we write
\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{H})
instead of
\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
. We sometimes omit
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
if the spaces are clear from the context. For background on Schatten spaces, we refer for instance, to
[
34
]
.

In the following, we consider operators on
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(M)
or
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, and the corresponding Schatten spaces.
There are obvious analogues for
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is an open subset of
M
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, or more generally, an arbitrary
\sigma
-finite measure space.

We will repeatedly use
the triangle and Hölder’s inequality in Schatten spaces. The latter states that

\displaystyle\|AB\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha_{1}}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\|A\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha_{2}}(\mathcal{H})}\|B\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha_{3}}(\mathcal{H})}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\alpha_{1}^{-1}=\alpha_{2}^{-1}+\alpha_{3}^{-1}.

(35)

We also recall the Kato–Seiler–Simon inequality
[
34
, Theorem 4.1]
:
For
2\leq p\leq\infty
,

\left\lVert W(x)\beta(-{\mathrm{i}}\nabla)\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{p}\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)}\leq(2\pi)^{-n/p}\lVert W\rVert_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

(36)

Frank and Sabin
[
17
, Appendix C]
proved a version of the Kato–Seiler–Simon inequality for the Laplacian on compact manifolds. Here we state a version for low-regularity metrics.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
s\in[0,2]
, and let
{\rm g}
be of class
C^{s}(M)
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for all
2\leq p\leq\infty
,

\displaystyle\|W\beta(\sqrt{P})\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{p}(L^{2}(M))}\leq C\|W\|_{L^{p}(M)}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{k\leq\tau\leq k+1}|\beta(\tau)|^{p}(1+k)^{n-1+(1-s)_{+}}\bigg)^{1/p}.

(37)

Proof.

The case
p=\infty
is trivial, so we focus on
p<\infty
. The proof of
[
17
, Proposition 24]
carries over with minor modifications. By the Lieb-Thirring inequality (see
[
17
]
for references),

\displaystyle\|W\beta(\sqrt{P})\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{p}(L^{2}(M))}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\tr(|W|^{p}|\beta(\sqrt{P})|^{p})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tr(|W|^{p}|\beta(\sqrt{P})|^{p}\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[k,k+1]))

(38)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{k\leq\tau\leq k+1}|\beta(\tau)|^{p}\tr(\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[k,k+1])|W|^{p})

(39)

\displaystyle\leq\|W\|_{L^{p}(M)}^{p}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sup_{k\leq\tau\leq k+1}|\beta(\tau)|^{p}\|\mathbf{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[k,k+1])\|_{L^{2}(M)\to L^{\infty}(M)}^{2}.

(40)

The claim now follows from the estimates (
26

∎

2.4.
Densities

If
\gamma
is a non-negative finite rank operator on
L^{2}(M)
, then it has a spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}\rangle\langle u_{j}|,

(44)

where
\#J<\infty
,
\nu_{j}\geq 0
and
u_{j}
are normalized in
L^{2}(M)
. If, in addition,
\gamma=\Pi_{\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\lambda}
, then
u_{j}\in E_{\lambda}
, i.e. each
u_{j}
is a finite linear combination of eigenfunctions of
P
. If the coefficients of the metric are bounded and measurable, elliptic regularity (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Theorems 8.15, 8.24]
and the comment after
[
18
, Theorem 8.37]
) implies that
u_{j}\in C^{\alpha}(M)
for some
\alpha>0
. Then, the
density
is pointwise defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(x):=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|u_{j}(x)|^{2},\quad x\in M,

and also belongs to

C^{\alpha}(M)
for the same
\alpha
. In particular,
\rho_{\gamma}\in L^{q/2}(M)
for any
q\in[2,\infty]
.

We will also need to consider the density of the conjugated operator
A\gamma A^{*}
, where
A
is some bounded operator on
L^{2}(M)
. In this case,

\displaystyle\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}:=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|Au_{j}|^{2}

(45)

is a priori only in
L^{1}(M)
, but we still have

\displaystyle\int_{M}\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x=\tr(A\gamma A^{*}).

Indeed, if
(f_{k})_{k}
is any orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)
, then

\displaystyle\tr(A\gamma A^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\langle A\gamma A^{*}f_{k},f_{k}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}|\langle Au_{j},f_{k}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\langle Au_{j},f_{k}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\|Au_{j}\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}=\int_{M}\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x,

where the second to last equality follows by Bessel’s identity. Similarly, if
W
is a measurable function,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}|W|^{2}|{\rm g}|^{1/2}{\rm d}x=\tr(WA\gamma A^{*}\overline{W}).

(46)

2.5.
Duality

We first recall the duality principle
[
16
, Lemma 3]
of Frank and Sabin.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A:L^{2}(M)\to L^{q}(M)
be a bounded operator for some
2\leq q<\infty
, and let
\alpha\geq 1
. Then the following bounds are equivalent:

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq C\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(L^{2}(M))}

(47)

for any non-negative finite rank operator
\gamma
on
L^{2}(M)
, and

\displaystyle\left\lVert WAA^{*}\overline{W}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}(L^{2}(M))}\leq C^{\prime}\|W\|_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}^{2},\quad\forall W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M).

(48)

Moreover, the values of the optimal constants
C
and
C^{\prime}

coincide.

The lemma follows from Hölder’s inequality and the identity

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}=\sup_{W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}\frac{\tr(WA\gamma A^{*}\overline{W})}{\lVert W\rVert_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}^{2}}=\sup_{\lVert W\rVert_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}=1}\left\lVert WA\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2}(L^{2}(M))}^{2}.

(49)

The latter also yields a useful version of the triangle inequality.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
2\leq q\leq\infty
.
Assume that
(A_{j})_{j=1}^{K}
is a family of bounded operators
L^{2}(M)\to L^{q}(M)
, and let
A=\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}
. If
\gamma
is a non-negative finite-rank operator on
L^{2}(M)
, then

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}\leq K\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lVert\rho_{A_{j}\gamma A_{j}^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}.

(50)

Proof.

By applying (
49
) twice,

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{A\gamma A^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lVert W\rVert_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}=1}\left\lVert WA\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2}(L^{2}(M))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\lVert W\rVert_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}=1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left\lVert WA_{j}\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2}(L^{2}(M))}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lVert\rho_{A_{j}\gamma A_{j}^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}^{1/2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq K\sum_{j=1}^{K}\lVert\rho_{A_{j}\gamma A_{j}^{*}}\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)},

where we used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the last line.
∎

2.6.
Pseudodifferential calculus

We will use a modest amount of pseudodifferential calculus.
We will work with the following fixed-frequency symbol classes, as in
[
24
,
23
]
.

Definition 2.7

(Symbol classes)
.

Let
a(t,x,\xi,\lambda)
be a family of symbols in
(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, depending on parameters
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\lambda\geq 1
. For
\sigma\in[0,1]
, we say
a(t,x,\xi,\lambda)\in S_{\lambda,\lambda^{\sigma}}
if, for all multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
, there exist constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}
such that

\displaystyle|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a(t,x,\xi,\lambda)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\lambda^{-|\beta|+\sigma|\alpha|}.

(51)

We say
a(t,x,\xi,\lambda)\in C^{k}S_{\lambda,\lambda^{\sigma}}
if the stronger bound

\displaystyle|\partial_{t,x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}a(t,x,\xi,\lambda)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\lambda^{-|\beta|+\sigma(|\alpha|-k)_{+}}

(52)

holds, where
x_{+}=\max(x,0)
.

These are symbols of order zero. For symbols of order
m\in{\mathbb{R}}
we write
a\in\lambda^{m}S_{\lambda,\lambda^{\sigma}}
.
We will only consider the cases
\sigma=1
,
k=1
and
\sigma=1/2
,
k=2
. The dimension
d
will either be
n
or
n-1
.
In the first case, there will be no parameter
t
. In the second case,
t=x_{1}
will be one of the spatial variables.
The symbol class denoted here by
C^{2}S_{\lambda,\lambda^{1/2}}
corresponds to
S^{2}_{\lambda}
in
[
24
]
. We have adopted the terminology of
[
23
, Definition 2.2]
here. Symbols will initially be defined in the phase space region

\displaystyle B_{\lambda}:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:|x|\leq 1,|\xi|\leq\lambda\}.

(53)

and extended globally to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, consistently with the symbol class.

As noted in
[
23
]
,
a\in S_{\lambda,\lambda}
constitutes a bounded family of zero-order symbols in the Hörmander class
S^{0}_{0,0}
when rescaled via
(t,x,\xi)\mapsto(\lambda t,\lambda x,\xi/\lambda)
. Consequently, both the
Kohn-Nirenberg quantization

a(t,x,D_{x})
and the
Weyl quantization

a^{w}(t,x,D_{x})
are uniformly bounded on
L^{2}
, with bounds independent of
t
and
\lambda

[
19
]
. In what follows, we adopt the Weyl quantization

\displaystyle a^{w}(t,x,D,\lambda)u(x)=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(x-y)\cdot\xi}a\Big(t,\frac{x+y}{2},\xi,\lambda\Big)u(y){\rm d}y{\rm d}\xi,\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

This is convenient but not essential.
We usually simply write
a^{w}
instead of
a^{w}(t,x,D,\lambda)
. The symbol classes
S_{\lambda,\lambda^{\sigma}}
as well as the corresponding classes of operators
OPS_{\lambda,\lambda^{\sigma}}
are algebras. If
\sigma<1
, the usual pseudodifferential calculus applies. For symbols in
a,b\in C^{1}S_{\lambda,\lambda}
,
the asymptotic formulas for composition and adjoint remain valid to first-order,

\displaystyle a^{w}b^{w}=(ab)^{w}+\lambda^{-1}R_{1},\quad(a^{w})^{*}=(\overline{a})^{w}+\lambda^{-1}R_{2},

(54)

where the remainders
R_{1},R_{2}
are Weyl quantizations of symbols in

and are therefore bounded on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We call

a smoothing operator if

for any

, in which case we have
\|R\|\lesssim\lambda^{-N}
for any
N>0
. However, the fixed-frequency calculus does not readily imply trace norm estimates for

, and we resort to the standard
S^{m}_{1,0}
calculus for this purpose. Precisely, if

, then

\displaystyle\|r^{w}(x,\lambda^{-1}D)\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{1}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))}\lesssim\lambda^{-N}.

(55)

This follows from the translation-invariant kernel bound

\displaystyle|r^{w}(x,y)|\leq C_{N}\lambda^{-N}(1+\lambda|x-y|)^{-N},

(56)

where
r^{w}(x,y)
denotes the kernel of

.

3.
Connection between spectral cluster and resolvent estimates

In this section, we elucidate the connection between spectral cluster estimates, quasimode estimates, and two versions of resolvent estimates. We consider spectral clusters
[\lambda,\lambda+\mu]
of size
\mu=\mu(\lambda)
. In the application to our problems, we will choose
\mu(\lambda)=\lambda^{(1-s)_{+}}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
P
be a non-negative self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(M)
,
1\leq\mu\leq\lambda
, and let
\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}:=\mathds{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[\lambda,\lambda+\mu])
and
\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}:=\mathds{1}(\sqrt{P}\in[0,2\lambda])
. Let
q\in[2,\infty]
,
\delta\geq 0
and
\alpha\geq 1
. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle\lVert\rho(\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\gamma\Pi_{\lambda,\mu})\rVert_{L^{q/2}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}},

(57)

\displaystyle\|\rho(\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda})\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta}\left(\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}+(\mu\lambda)^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}\right),

(58)

\displaystyle\|\rho\big((P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\big)\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}.

(59)

Here,
\gamma
is a non-negative finite-rank operator, and
\mathop{\rm Ran}\gamma\subset\dom(P)
in (
58
). The implicit constants are independent of
\gamma,\lambda,\mu
. Moreover,
the above estimates are implied by the following resolvent bound,

\displaystyle\left\lVert W_{1}(P-(\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}W_{2}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-1}\mu^{-1}\|W_{1}\|_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}\|W_{2}\|_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}

(60)

for all
W_{1},W_{2}\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)
, and for both choices of the sign
\pm
.

Remark 3.2
.

Since the resolvent
(P-(\lambda\pm\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}
is not of the form
AA^{*}
, the duality principle
[
16
, Lemma 3]
does not apply to (
60
), i.e., there is no equivalent formulation in terms of a density.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Since
\mathop{\rm Ran}\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\subset\mathop{\rm Ran}\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}
, this follows from Hölder’s inequality in Schatten spaces,

\displaystyle\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\gamma\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}\leq\|\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}(P-\lambda^{2})\|^{2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}\lesssim(\mu\lambda)^{2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}.

(61)

Since
P
and
\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}
commute, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}\gamma\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}

\displaystyle=(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})\gamma(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}

(62)

whenever
\mathop{\rm Ran}\gamma\subset\dom(P)
, and therefore,

\displaystyle\|\rho(\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}

\displaystyle\gamma\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda})\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}

(63)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\left\lVert(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})\gamma(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}

(64)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\lambda^{1+(1-s)_{+}}+\lambda^{2(1-s)_{+}})\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}

(65)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}(\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}+\lambda^{2(1+(1-s)_{+})}\left\lVert\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2})

(66)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2\delta}(\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}+(\mu\lambda)^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}),

(67)

where we used (
59
) in the first inequality, and the triangle inequality
in the second inequality.

By duality (Lemma
2.5
), with
A=(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}
there, it suffices to prove

\displaystyle\left\lVert W(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\overline{W}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}

(68)

for all
W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)
,
where we observed that
A^{*}=\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}
and that
\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}^{*}=\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}=\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}^{2}
.
By Schatten space duality (see, e.g.,
[
34
, Theorem 3.2]
) the latter is equivalent to proving

\displaystyle\tr(WAA^{*}\overline{W}\gamma)\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)}\|\gamma\|_{\mathfrak{S^{\alpha}}}

(69)

for all
W\in L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}(M)
and finite-rank
\gamma\geq 0
.
Using the spectral decomposition (
44
) and completing the system
\{u_{j}\}_{j\in J}
there to an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(M)
, we compute the trace

\displaystyle\tr(WAA^{*}\overline{W}\gamma)=\sum_{j\in J}\langle u_{j},WAA^{*}\overline{W}\gamma u_{j}\rangle=\sum_{j\in J}\nu_{j}\|A^{*}\overline{W}u_{j}\|^{2}.

Let
(e_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty},(\lambda_{k}^{2})_{k=1}^{\infty}
denote the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
P
, i.e.
Pe_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{2}e_{k}
. Expanding the last display in eigenvectors, we have

\displaystyle\tr(WAA^{*}\overline{W}\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu_{j}|\langle e_{k},A^{*}\overline{W}u_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{\lambda_{k}\leq 2\lambda}\nu_{j}|\lambda_{k}^{2}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2}|^{-2}|\langle e_{k},\overline{W}u_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

For
\ell\in\mu{\mathbb{Z}}
,
0\leq\ell\leq 2\lambda
and
\lambda_{k}\in[\ell,\ell+\mu]
, we have

\displaystyle|\lambda_{k}^{2}-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2}|^{-2}\lesssim\lambda^{-2}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}.

(70)

Thus, by Bessel’s identity and Tonelli’s theorem,

\displaystyle\tr(WAA^{*}\overline{W}\gamma)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-2}\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{\ell\in\mu{\mathbb{Z}}\cap[0,2\lambda]}\sum_{k\>\lambda_{k}\in[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\nu_{j}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}|\langle e_{k},\overline{W}u_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\sum_{j}\sum_{\ell}\nu_{j}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}\|\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\overline{W}u_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\sum_{\ell}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}\sum_{j}\nu_{j}\|\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\overline{W}u_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\sum_{\ell}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}\tr(\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}|W|^{2}\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\sum_{\ell}(\mu+|\ell-\lambda|)^{-2}\|\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}|W|^{2}\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\|\gamma\|_{{}_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\|\gamma\|_{{}_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}},

where in the last inequality, we used the dual version of (
57
) to bound

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}|W|^{2}\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}=\|W\Pi_{[\ell,\ell+\mu]}\overline{W}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta}\|W\|^{2}_{L^{2(q/2)^{\prime}}},\quad\forall\ell\leq 2\lambda.

follows from the resolvent identity.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

An inspection of the proof
shows that for any bounded operator
A
on
L^{2}(M)
, the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle\|\rho(A\gamma A^{*})\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta}\left(\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}+(\mu\lambda)^{-2}\|(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}\right),

(71)

\displaystyle\|\rho\left(A(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}\gamma(P-(\lambda-\mathrm{i}\mu)^{2})^{-1}A^{*}\right)\|_{L^{q/2}(M)}\lesssim\lambda^{2\delta-2}\mu^{-2}\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}.

(72)

The next lemma shows that, if

is the Laplacian and
\mu=1
, then we may add a gradient term
\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert\nabla_{{\rm g}}\gamma\nabla\mathrlap{{}^{*}}_{\textup{{\rm g}}}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}
to the expression in the parenthesis of (

This form is convenient since gradient terms naturally arise in localization arguments (see Subsection
4.1

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
P
be the Laplacian, defined in the sense of quadratic forms, with bounded and measurable coefficients (see Subsection
2.2
), and
let
\alpha\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert\nabla_{{\rm g}}\gamma\nabla\mathrlap{{}^{*}}_{\textup{{\rm g}}}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(L^{2}(M))}\lesssim\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(L^{2}(M))}+\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}(L^{2}(M))}.

for any finite rank operator
\gamma\geq 0
with
\mathop{\rm Ran}\gamma\subset\dom(P)
and for any
\lambda\geq 1
.

Proof.

For
f\in\dom(\sqrt{P})
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\rm g}}f\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}=Q(f)=\|\sqrt{P}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(M)}\implies\|\nabla_{{\rm g}}(P+\lambda^{2})^{-1/2}\|\leq 1.

Thus,

\displaystyle\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert\nabla_{{\rm g}}\gamma\nabla\mathrlap{{}^{*}}_{\textup{{\rm g}}}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert\nabla_{{\rm g}}\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}\leq\lambda^{-2}\|\nabla_{{\rm g}}(P+\lambda^{2})^{-1/2}\|^{2}\left\lVert(P+\lambda^{2})^{1/2}\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert(P+\lambda^{2})^{1/2}\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-2}\|(P+\lambda^{2})^{1/2}(1+\lambda^{-1}|P-\lambda^{2}|)^{-1}\|^{2}\left\lVert(1+\lambda^{-1}|P-\lambda^{2}|)\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}

(75)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert(1+\lambda^{-1}|P-\lambda^{2}|)\sqrt{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha}}^{2}

(76)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}+\lambda^{-2}\left\lVert(P-\lambda^{2})\gamma(P-\lambda^{2})\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{S}^{\alpha}}.

(77)

In the penultimate line we used the spectral theorem to estimate

\displaystyle\|(P+\lambda^{2})^{1/2}(1+\lambda^{-1}|P-\lambda^{2}|)^{-1}\|=\sup_{\tau^{2}\in\spec(P)}\frac{(\tau^{2}+\lambda^{2})^{1/2}}{1+\lambda^{-1}|\tau^{2}-\lambda^{2}|}\lesssim\lambda.

(78)

∎

4.1.
Spatial localization and regularization

By Proposition

4.3.

To complete the proof of Proposition

Lemma 4.6

Let

Proof.

If

\displaystyle\langle f,Pf\rangle

\displaystyle\geq(c_{\rm ell}^{-1}-C\lambda^{-1})\|\nabla f\|^{2}

(118)

Hence, if we choose

\displaystyle\langle f,Pf\rangle\geq\lambda^{2}c^{-2}(2c_{\rm ell})^{-1}\|f\|^{2}.

If we further require

.
In other words, for such a choice of

\displaystyle\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}=\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}\mathbf{1}(|D|\leq c^{-1}\lambda).

(120)

By taking adjoints, we also find that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}=\mathbf{1}(|D|\leq c^{-1}\lambda)\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}.

(121)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|W\Pi_{\leq 2\lambda}(P-(\lambda+\mathrm{i})^{2})^{-1}\|_{\mathfrak{S}^{2\alpha(q_{n})^{\prime}}}^{2}

(124)

∎

(125)

for all

.

Lemma 6.1

\displaystyle\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}[P,\phi](P-z)^{-1/2}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Im}\,z|^{1/2}}.

(221)

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle|\langle u,[P,\phi]u\rangle|\leq 2\|\nabla_{{\rm g}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}\|\nabla_{{\rm g}}u\|_{L^{2}(M)}\lesssim\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}Q(u)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}[P,\phi](P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}\|

(223)

\displaystyle\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}.

(224)

\displaystyle\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{1/2}(P-z)^{-1/2}\|=\sup_{\tau^{2}\in\spec(P)}\frac{(\tau^{2}+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{1/2}}{|\tau^{2}-z|^{1/2}}\lesssim\frac{(\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{1/2}}{|\mathrm{Im}\,z|^{1/2}},

hence

\displaystyle\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}[P,Q](P-z)^{-1/2}\|

(226)

\displaystyle\leq\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}[P,Q](P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-1/2}\|\|(P+\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{1/2}(P-z)^{-1/2}\|\lesssim\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Im}\,z|^{1/2}}.

(227)
