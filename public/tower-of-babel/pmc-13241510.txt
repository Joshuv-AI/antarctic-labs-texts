Metabolotheranostics of pancreatic cancer by targeting choline, glutamine, and glucose transporters with photoimmunotherapy
Authors: Puneet Khandelwal, Jiefu Jin, James D. Barnett, Yelena Mironchik, Balaji Krishnamachary, Saleem Yousf, Raj Kumar Sharma, Hisataka Kobayashi, Zaver M. Bhujwalla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Altered choline, glutamine, and glucose metabolism form a triumvirate of metabolic reprogramming in most cancers that significantly influences growth, progression, and response to treatment. Photoimmunotherapy (PIT) is a highly target-specific treatment where a targeting antibody (Ab) is conjugated to a photosensitizing dye, IR700, that damages the target only when exposed to near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation. The requirement of an extracellular target has restricted PIT targeting to cell surface receptors and antigens. Here, for the first time, we exploited the extracellular domain of three metabolic transporters, CTL1 for choline, ASCT2 for glutamine, and GLUT1 for glucose for PIT, to demonstrate metabolotheranostics of cancer cells. We analyzed the TCGA database to establish increased expression of the three transporters in human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). For the PIT studies, we used two patient-derived PDAC cell lines selected for differences in transporter expression and demonstrated an expression-dependent reduction of cell viability following PIT. A single CTL1-PIT treatment of Pa04C tumors resulted in the eradication of four out of five established tumors. In PDAC, the PIT of metabolic transporters would be most effective in the intraoperative setting, where it could significantly impact cancer cells that may have invaded critical structures.

Introduction

With an overall 5-year survival of 13%1, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) currently ranks fourth in cancer-related mortality, and is predicted to become the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality by 20302. Termed the silent killer, PDAC is frequently detected when it is inoperable because of its proximity to major blood vessels or critical structures. Only 20% of patients have resectable disease at presentation, 30–40% of patients present with borderline resectable disease, and 40–50% of patients present with distant metastasis2. Microscopically positive margins have been associated with reduced overall survival and recurrence-free survival3. Some advances are being made with inhibitors of mutant KRAS, one of the most commonly mutated oncogenes in PDAC4. The expression of HER2 in 2% of PDAC has also led to some partial responses with trastuzumab4. Despite these advances, PDAC remains a lethal disease with few target-specific treatments.

Our purpose here was to investigate, in proof-of-principle studies, the use of the extracellular domain of choline, glutamine, and glucose transporters for transporter-targeted near infrared (NIR) photoimmunotherapy (PIT) of PDAC. In NIR-PIT, a water-soluble and photo-stable phthalocyanine dye IRDye700DX (IR700) conjugated to an antibody binds to its cellular target, with subsequent NIR light exposure damaging the cell membrane and causing cell death5. The light penetration depth of PIT, however, limits its use to superficial cancers or for intraoperative therapy6. An international multicenter randomized phase 3 clinical trial of NIR-PIT using cetuximab-IR700 (ASP-1929) to treat recurrent head and neck cancer patients is ongoing7. Target-specific cell death occurs only when antibody binding and light exposure are combined. Several cancer targets have been investigated with NIR-PIT, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)8–13, human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (Her2)14,15, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)16, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)17,18, CD4419, PD-L120,21, Gr122, and fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP-α)23–25.

While the present study focuses on IR700-based PIT targeting of metabolic transporters, significant advances have also been made in the development of antibody-photosensitizer conjugates and nanophotoactivatable liposomes to overcome the dense desmoplastic stroma characteristic of PDAC26,27. Studies using benzoporphyrin derivative (BPD)-based photoimmunoconjugates have demonstrated superior efficacy in heterotypic PDAC models with high stromal content compared to non-targeted photosensitizers28. Nanophotoactivatable platforms that synchronize PDT with the sustained release of multikinase inhibitors such as cabozantinib are also being developed27. The translational significance of these approaches is underscored by ongoing clinical efforts, such as the Phase II clinical trial at the Mayo Clinic (e.g., NCT06381154), which evaluates photodynamic priming with verteporfin in combination with immune checkpoint blockade with pembrolizumab and standard-of-care chemotherapy for unresectable PDAC29.

Here, for the first time, we exploited the extracellular domain of three metabolic transporters, CTL1 for choline, ASCT2 for glutamine, and GLUT1 for glucose, that form the crux of aberrant metabolism in PDAC30–32 and in most cancers33, for PIT. Dysregulated choline metabolism has been previously identified in patient-derived PDAC cancer cells, with increases of CTL1 and choline-kinase alpha (Chk-α)30. Choline is required for membrane synthesis and cellular signaling34. The choline transporter CTL134,35, also known as SLC44A1, is an extracellular choline transporter. CTL1 is often overexpressed in cancers such as PDAC, prostate, lung, and breast cancers, where it supports the rapid proliferation and survival of tumor cells by facilitating choline uptake30. With a Km value for choline in the range of 5–40 μM, CTL1 has a high affinity for its substrate, allowing cancer cells to efficiently import choline even at low concentrations36–38.

Glutamine has long been observed to play an important role in cancer metabolism39. ‘Glutamine addiction’ of cancer cells was observed as early as 195540. PDAC cells have been identified as glutamine-avid, that was attributed to an increase in the expression of the ASCT2 glutamine transporter41. Like CTL1, the glutamine transporter, ASCT2, has a Km value typically ranging from 20 to 40 μM, providing effective transport at low extracellular concentrations42,43. ASCT2 plays a significant role in the metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells, including PDAC, where it is often overexpressed44,45. This overexpression supports the aggressive growth and survival of tumors by facilitating glutamine uptake, which fuels critical metabolic pathways.

The aberrant glucose metabolism of cancers with increased glycolysis occurring even in the presence of oxygen was first identified by Otto Warburg in the 1930s and is a common occurrence in cancers, including PDAC31. This increased glucose requirement is largely mediated by increased expression of the glucose transporters, including GLUT131, and has resulted in the development of FDG PET imaging as a major diagnostic test for cancer. The glucose transporter, GLUT1, also known as SLC2A1, is a major glucose transporter overexpressed by many cancers, including PDAC. GLUT1 has a Km value of approximately 1–5 mM for D-glucose46,47. The glucose avidity of cancers sustains tumor growth and survival48. In PDAC and other cancers like breast, lung, and colorectal cancer, GLUT1 supports the high glycolytic activity (Warburg effect) typical of cancer cells49.

In these studies, we mined TCGA data to validate our approach of using metabolic transporter-mediated PIT for human PDAC. We then evaluated the use of the extracellular domain of CTL1 for choline, ASCT2 for glutamine, and GLUT1 for glucose, for PIT using Pa04C and Pa20C human PDAC cells selected for differences in these three transporters. Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy were performed to explain the treatment behavior in the cell lines. We performed in vivo CTL1-PIT with Pa04C tumors to demonstrate the effects on tumor growth in vivo. Our studies may lead, in the future, to the ability to eliminate PDAC cells intra-operatively to debulk the tumor and eliminate residual cancer cells by PIT, potentially increasing survival from this lethal disease. Depletion of substrates due to transporter damage can combine with the PIT effects to further damage the cancer cell. The studies presented here are primarily proof-of-principle studies to determine the feasibility of eliminating PDAC cells with PIT using a single dose of light. These studies can also pave the way for metabolic transporter targeted antibody drug conjugate (ADC) therapy50 or for radiotheranostics51,52 for systemic primary and metastatic cancer treatment.

Discussion

We evaluated the extracellular domain of three metabolic transporters, CTL1 for choline, ASCT2 for glutamine, and GLUT1 for glucose, for PIT in patient-derived PDAC cells. TCGA analysis confirmed the predominance of increased CTL1, ASCT2, and GLUT1 expression in human PDAC tissues that was consistent with results from previous studies35,54,55. CTL1 mRNA expression tended to be higher in PDAC patients, with significantly longer survival in the low-/medium-CTL1 expression group compared to the high-CTL1 expression group35. ASCT2 has been previously reported to be overexpressed in PDAC, with high ASCT2 expression associated with unfavorable survival outcomes54. Similar to CTL1 and ASCT2, increased GLUT1 expression has been previously reported in PDAC; GLUT1 overexpression levels significantly correlated with poor prognosis, and GLUT1 was an independent risk factor in the prognosis of patients with PDAC55. GLUT1 was also correlated with larger tumor size ( > 2 cm) and the presence of lymph node metastasis56. These studies highlight the importance of the three transporters in prognosis and for targeted PIT therapy.

Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy established significantly higher levels of all three transporters in Pa04C cells compared to Pa20C cells. Incubation of antibody-IR700 conjugates, performed to determine the direct effects of the antibodies alone on affecting the function of the transporters in human PDAC cells at 24 h and 48 h, resulted in minimal effects on cell viability. PIT-induced cell death was observed with the targeting of all three transporters in an expression-dependent manner, with significantly higher cell death observed in Pa04C cells compared to Pa20C cells. Cell death was most likely caused by a combination of cell membrane damage and the consequences of metabolic transporter and pathway disruption. Both are dependent upon the number of transporters present, but the latter will also depend upon the avidity of the cell line for the substrate and pathway. The light dose and antibody conjugate concentration required were higher compared to prior IR700-PIT reports that have used receptor expression19,23. This could reflect the binding ability of the antibody or the lower concentration of the extracellular domain of the transporter compared to receptor expression levels. Our approach represents a combined attack of damaging the cell with PIT and disrupting metabolic pathways that are critically important for cancer cells. We evaluated individual conjugates, as our purpose was to assess each transporter as a therapeutic target rather than use a cocktail that may also potentially increase toxicity. Also, targeting transporters individually may uncover varying degrees of metabolic vulnerability associated with specific transporters. Because light exposure is only directed to the tumor region, this minimized any potential normal tissue damage, although the limitations of light penetration primarily restrict its use to superficial tumors or intra-operative applications.

We performed CTL1-IR700 PIT of Pa04C tumors to determine the effect on tumor growth. Whole body in vivo optical imaging confirmed that CTL1-IR700 accumulated in high-CTL1 expressing Pa04C tumors.

Following PIT, four of the five tumors showed no tumor growth until euthanization at 140 days, identifying CTL1-targeted PIT as an effective treatment capable of eradicating established tumors. One CTL1-IR700-treated Pa04C tumor showed an initial decrease in size but subsequently regrew that may have occurred due to inadvertently shielding the tumor with aluminum foil during PIT. Because of its small size, the tumor most likely retracted under the aluminum foil during NIR irradiation, which may have resulted in a lower dose.

Previous CTL1-targeted therapeutics included the inhibitor, Amb4269951, and its derivative Amb4269675. Amb4269951 and Amb4269675 showed tumor growth inhibition in a mouse MIA PaCa-2 xenograft model without adverse effects such as weight loss, supporting our data35. However, most normal cells express choline, glutamine, and glucose transporters57,58. One limitation of our study was that, because these studies were performed with a human-specific CTL1 antibody, normal tissue damage was not evaluated. Future studies with syngeneic tumors using mouse-specific antibodies or peptides should address this limitation. Additionally, in vivo collateral damage from the cytotoxicity of the unbound conjugate should be evaluated in future studies, as the clearance of the unbound conjugate relies on lymphatics and blood flow. Future studies should also evaluate treatment effects in normal tissue to further investigate therapeutic selectivity. PIT does provide specificity because light exposure can be directed only to the tumor region to minimize potential normal tissue damage. It does, however, suffer from the limitations of light penetration, restricting its use to superficial tumors or intraoperative applications. Blocking the extracellular domain in normal cells with a low ‘cold’ dose may also be possible before giving the cancer-targeted treatment. In addition, it is also possible that the cancer cells are more sensitive to depletion of choline, glutamine, or glucose that will occur with the damage of the transporters compared to normal cells.

Tumor eradication with a single dose of Ab-IR700 and PIT creates new opportunities and directions for intraoperative applications. In human applications, ASP-1929 was used to target the EGFR receptor in patients using a clinical dose of ~17 mg/kg with up to eight cycles for disease management. In our study of the CTL1 metabolic transporter in Pa04C tumor-bearing mice, we used a single 100 µg dose, approximately equivalent to ~5 mg/kg59–61. However, our preclinical protocol utilized a higher localized fluence of 200 J/cm2 compared to the clinical use of 50 J/cm262. In future studies, we intend to evaluate targeting a combination of the transporters at the same time to see if this increases cell kill efficacy in tumors. Since choline, glutamine, and glucose are dysregulated in most cancers, our studies also create the possibility of targeting cancers such as triple-negative breast cancer that do not express cell surface targets for ADC or radiotheranostics. To the best of our knowledge, there are no published studies investigating the use of the extracellular domain of metabolic transporters for PIT. PIT of metabolic transporters in PDAC would be most effective in the intra-operative setting, where it could have a significant impact on cancer cells that may have invaded critical structures. Advances in this area may also potentially render inoperable PDAC to become operable. For this approach to succeed, PIT would have to eliminate cancer cells in a single setting. Our data indicate that the combination of cell membrane damage with PIT and the consequences of metabolic transporter and pathway disruption result in established tumor eradication with a single treatment. If successful, these studies can significantly expand the scope of targeted cancer treatments with PIT. Companion PET diagnostics are available to image choline, glutamine, and glucose metabolism for patient selection and to monitor response to therapy for future translational applications.

Methods

TCGA analysis

We used publicly available PDAC datasets (TCGA, Firehose Legacy) that were profiled, curated, and analyzed using the cBioportal platform63. The TCGA Firehose Legacy data, analyzed using cBioportal in the present study, was originally sourced from the BroadGDAC Firehose study (previously known as TCGA provisional). This pancreatic cancer dataset, comprising clinical data from 185 patients, was gathered as a part of the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard Firehose initiative. The original dataset is available from https://gdac.broadinstitute.org/. Within cBioPortal, cohorts corresponding to pancreatic adenocarcinoma (TCGA, Firehose Legacy) can be uniquely identified by the study ID and can be obtained from the following URL: https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=paad_tcga. Out of the 185 patients, 176 samples were pancreatic adenocarcinoma tumors. We applied clinical and genomic filters to create a list of 93 PDAC samples that were treatment naïve. We then grouped the tumor samples into high (expression > than in normal) and low (expression < than in normal) cohorts based on mRNA expression. Expression data obtained from cBioPortal is based on a frequently employed analysis technique called RNA-seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM) that considers the transcript length and provides acceptable and accurate results64. RNA sequencing by RSEM was used to quantify transcripts65. We used RSEM values for the genes of interest (GOI) CTL1, ASCT2, GLUT1, and EGFR, and converted them to Log2 values. Log2 values of the GOI from patient-derived tumor samples were grouped as higher or lower than the Log2 RSEM values for the same gene in the normal pancreas, as indicated by the dotted line in the corresponding figures.

Cell culture and tumor implantation

The Pa04C cell line was derived from PDAC lung metastasis in a 59-year-old male patient, while the Pa20C cell line was derived from primary PDAC. Both Pa04C and Pa20C cell lines were kindly provided by Dr. Anirban Maitra. Pa04C and Pa20C cells were cultured in DMEM (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were cultured in standard cell culture incubator conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Subcutaneous tumors were derived by inoculating 2 × 106 Pa04C cells in 50 μL of HBSS in the left flank of NCI athymic nude male mice (Charles River, Wilmington, MA, USA). Experiments were performed when the tumors reached volumes of ~100–200 mm3. All surgical procedures and animal handling were performed following protocols approved by the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC) and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the NIH.

Flow cytometry

Pa04C and Pa20C cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 in 100 mm tissue culture dishes. Twenty-four hours later, cells were washed with PBS buffer containing 5 mM EDTA and harvested. Approximately 5 × 105 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed, and resuspended in PBA buffer (PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin and 0.02% sodium azide). The cells were incubated at room temperature (RT) for 30 min with the primary antibodies and washed in PBA thrice. The cells were then incubated with PE-conjugated secondary Ab at RT for 30 min in the dark and washed thrice with PBA buffer. Rabbit anti-human ASCT2 polyclonal Ab, 20350-1-AP, and rabbit anti-human GLUT1 polyclonal Ab, 21829-1-AP, were purchased from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Mouse anti-human CTL1 monoclonal mouse Ab, MBS1801817, was purchased from mybiosource.com (San Diego, CA, USA). Rabbit and mouse IgG isotype controls were purchased from Bio X Cell (Lebanon, NH, USA). PE-conjugated secondary Ab was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Flow cytometry was performed with a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA). The data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (FLOWJO, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Confocal microscopy

Pa04C and Pa20C cells were seeded onto 16-well Thermo Scientific™ Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ Chamber Slide™ at a density of 10,000 cells/well. Once cells reached 70 ~ 80% confluency, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then washed with PBS three times. The cells were incubated with CTL1 or ASCT2 or GLUT1-Ab or rIgG or mIgG Ab at a concentration of 20 μg/mL for 30 min at RT. The cells were washed with PBS, and PE-conjugated secondary antibody was added. After 30 min, the cells were washed with PBS, and the samples were mounted using DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium, and coverslips were placed gently to avoid air bubbles. The cells were imaged with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 700, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena) at 63X magnification. All the images were obtained under identical microscope settings.

RNA isolation, cDNA preparation, and q-RT-PCR

RNA was isolated from the tumor using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Chatsworth, CA, Cat. No. 74106) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For muscle tissues, RNA was isolated using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, Cat. No. 15596026). cDNA was prepared using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA, Cat. No. 1708891). Quantitative Real-time PCR (q-RT-PCR) of cDNA samples was performed using IQ SYBR Green supermix and gene-specific primers in the iCycler real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad). The difference between the expression of the target gene mRNA relative to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was calculated as ΔCt. A lower ∆Ct reflected higher expression of the target gene mRNA66 .

Synthesis of IR700-conjugated antibodies

CTL1, ASCT2, GLUT1 Ab, rIgG, and mIgG Ab, as rat and mouse IgG Ab isotype controls, were incubated with IR700 in 1X PBS buffer (pH = 7.4) at 4 °C overnight. Water-soluble phthalocyanine dye IR700 was obtained from Li-Cor Bioscience (Lincoln, NE, USA). The mixture was purified with an Amicon centrifuge filter unit (MWCO = 30 kDa) to obtain Ab-IR700-conjugates. The concentrations of Ab and IR700 were determined by measuring their absorption at 280 nm and 689 nm. The number of fluorophore molecules per Ab was adjusted in the range of 3.5 to 4.4.

In vitro PIT and cytotoxicity assay

Pa04C and Pa20C cells were seeded on 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing CTL1-IR700, ASCT2-IR700, or GLUT1-IR700 (20 µg/mL). Cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, washed with HBSS, and fresh medium was added. Cells were irradiated with NIR light using a 690-nm continuous wave LED lamp (purchased from Marubeni, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The dose of irradiation was 64 J/cm2. After irradiation, cells were incubated for 1 h, followed by a CCK8 assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) to determine cell viability.

In vivo studies

All in vivo studies were performed under a protocol approved by the JHU ACUC. Mice bearing ~ 100-mm3 Pa04C tumors were randomly assigned to three groups of five mice each that received an i.v. injection of PBS or 100 μg of mIgG-IR700, or 100 µg of CTL1-IR700. NIR fluorescence imaging was performed on a Li-Cor Pearl® Impulse imager (LI-COR Biosciences). Fluorescent images were obtained over a 24-h period. Twenty-four hours post-injection, tumors were exposed to NIR irradiation at 200 J/cm2 (provided by an LED from Marubeni, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at a peak wavelength of 690 nm. The rest of the body was covered by aluminum foil. Tumor volumes were measured by caliper twice a week until they reached ~500 mm3 in the PBS-treated group. Ellipsoidal tumor volumes were calculated as 0.52 × length × width × depth. All imaging, i.v. injections, and PIT were performed with mice anesthetized with 1% isoflurane delivered by inhalation. During PIT, animals were monitored visually. Mice were euthanized by an i.v. injection of a mixture of ketamine (62.5 mg/kg) and acepromazine (6.25 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation performed on the euthanized animal as per the ACUC protocol.

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as Mean ± standard error (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired two-sided t-test, assuming unequal variance for comparison between two groups, unless otherwise stated. Values of P ≤ 0.05 were considered significant unless otherwise stated.
