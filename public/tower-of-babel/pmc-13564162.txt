A protocol for collaborative research to understand protection, recovery, and resilience after youth suicide attempt in Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland)
Authors: Arnarak Patricia Bloch, Christina Viskum Lytken Larsen, James Allen, Tenna Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a PhD protocol for an Indigenous-led qualitative study exploring protective factors and recovery processes among Indigenous Kalaallit Inuit (Greenlandic Inuit) youth in Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland) following suicide attempts. The majority of the population in Kalaallit Nunaat is Indigenous Kalaallit Inuit. The study is grounded in Kalaallit Inuit values and inspired by selected principles of community-based participatory research. Thirty interviews will be conducted in Kalaallisut (Greenlandic) across five regions through in-person and online modalities, as structured through a culturally and linguistically grounded interview guide. The project centers the principle of “Nothing About Us Without Us,” emphasizing community ownership, language-based access, and reciprocal accountability. The project methodologically explores the feasibility of trauma-informed, trust-based interviewing in small Arctic communities and investigates the importance of spirituality, land, and cultural revitalization in healing. In addition to methodological and thematic insights the project aims at creating a transferable model for ethical, community-engaged suicidology in Arctic Indigenous contexts. Thematic findings will be published in forthcoming articles. As this article presents a PhD protocol, no empirical findings are reported. The manuscript describes the study rationale, Indigenous theoretical foundations, methodology, ethical considerations, planned analyses, and dissemination strategy guiding the doctoral project.

Introduction

The suicide rate in Greenland remains among the highest in the world, especially prevalent among young men aged 20–24 and young women aged 15–19. Regional differences also exist, with the lowest suicide rates in Nuuk and the highest rates found in the sparsely populated regions of Tunu (East Greenland), and Avanersuaq, (the northernmost part of Greenland) [1,2]. A recent systematic review summarising all Greenlandic suicide research [3] shows that several well-known social determinants such as limited access to education, the labour market, and healthcare significantly impact suicide rates in Greenland at both individual, family and community level. Thus, the high incidence of suicide in Greenland is closely tied to fundamental social and geographical inequalities in living conditions and health.

In 2018, the World Health Organisation (WHO) strongly recommended that effective suicide prevention can be achieved if an entire community collaborates [4]. WHO emphasises that suicide prevention should not rely solely on psychiatric services but requires coordinated action across schools, healthcare services, social services, families, community organisations, local authorities, and civil society. Community engagement approaches are especially important in small Arctic communities where help-seeking often occurs through informal social networks before formal healthcare systems are accessed.

Consequently, it is essential that frontline workers and professionals working with health promotion at the local level in Kalaallit Nunaat, are trained to address sensitive topics like sexual abuse and suicide collaboratively within the community [5]. An evaluation of community certified gatekeeper training [6] shows that training professionals central to children and young people's lives, such as teachers and social and healthcare staff, is crucial for implementing youth-targeted suicide prevention work.

In 2023, Kalaallit Nunaat launched its current national suicide prevention strategy, Qamani [7], with the overarching goal of strengthening protective factors for young people's mental health and prevention of suicidal behaviour. Qamani emphasises strengthening protective factors through citizen involvement, accessible support pathways, destigmatization of suicide, safe environments for youth, and strengthened community engagement. While these priorities are strongly aligned with Indigenous and international suicide prevention recommendations, relatively little qualitative research has investigated how Kalaallit Inuit themselves describe recovery processes and protective factors following suicidal crises. However, the specific protective factors against suicidal behaviour are not thoroughly documented in Greenlandic suicide research [3], which poses a challenge to fully achieving the objectives of Qamani (2023). The present protocol also builds upon a preceding independent qualitative pilot study [8] among Kalaallit Inuit with lived experience of suicide attempts who described themselves as recovered in their mental health and are thriving today. Preliminary findings from the study indicated that recovery was often described through relational safety, belonging, cultural grounding, language-accessible support, connections to nature, and opportunities for meaningful participation. These preliminary insights informed the development of the current PhD study and highlighted the need for a larger and more geographically diverse investigation of protective processes following suicidal crises.

This study aims to contribute qualitative insights into suicide prevention by focusing on how young people who have survived suicide attempts and have managed to move forward in life and seeks to describe what factors have influenced their success. Beyond making a significant scientific contribution nationally and internationally, the project seeks to produce practical educational materials for institutions regarding protective factors and to provide a set of specific, clear recommendations for practical suicide prevention aimed at authorities, professionals, and citizens, grounded in the voices of the youth.

Background and context

Research on suicide in Greenland [9] has highlighted that the rapid process of modernisation during colonisation after 1953 negatively impacted the mental health of the population, resulting in one of the highest suicide rates globally. Suicide among Kalaallit Inuit cannot be understood solely through individual risk factors or social determinants of health. Structural and political determinants, including colonisation, rapid modernisation, language marginalisation, forced relocations, disruption of traditional livelihoods, and unequal distribution of resources, have also shaped contemporary mental health experiences. The social transition in Kalaallit Nunaat occurred within a relatively short historical period, contributing to profound cultural and social change across generations. Similar health challenges exist among Inuit populations in Canada [10] and Alaska [11] and among Indigenous Sápmi in the Nordic countries [12]. Recent Greenlandic suicide research [1] identifies various risk factors associated with suicide and suicidal behaviour, including difficult childhood conditions, problematic alcohol use, social inequality, mental health issues, illness, and traumatic or conflict-laden relationships. Recent Arctic health research [13] emphasises the need for a more holistic understanding of health determinants that, beyond well-known social factors, includes family, social, cultural, historical, linguistic, and environmental aspects that promote and sustain well-being of Arctic communities.

In Alaska, research has documented individual risk and protective factors for suicide among Indigenous populations [14]. Protective factors for youth include having ambitions for the present and future, good physical and emotional health, positive self-image, resilience, a sense of connection with others, and the perception of positive opportunities, social norms, and cultural belonging. Many Indigenous communities in Alaska have developed strategies to build resilience in youth by implementing wellness-promoting measures and leveraging cultural strengths within the community [15]. This approach emphasises fostering protective factors rather than focusing solely on preventing risk factors in suicide prevention efforts. The results of these efforts suggest that interventions focused on protective mechanisms offer a valuable alternative or supplement to the conventional focus on risk factors [16]. Over the past two decades, Non-Indigenous researchers including James Allen, and Lisa Wexler and Indigenous researcher Stacy Rasmus [15–18], have contributed significantly to culturally grounded suicide prevention science among Alaska Native communities. This work demonstrates how community knowledge, cultural continuity, storytelling, Indigenous identity, belonging, and land-based practices can be integrated into prevention research and intervention development. A notable example is the Qungasvik (Tools for Life) [15] intervention, which was developed collaboratively with Yup’ik communities and aims to strengthen culturally meaningful reasons for life and protective factors among youth. This body of scholarship highlights the importance of understanding community-defined resilience, recovery, and wellbeing before developing and evaluating interventions. Inspired by this growing Indigenous prevention science literature, the present study seeks to contribute context-specific knowledge from Kalaallit Nunaat. However, unlike intervention studies, this PhD project is designed as a narrative ethnographic study exploring lived experiences of recovery, resilience, and protection following suicidal behaviour among Kalaallit Inuit.

A notable example in Alaska includes cultural camps featuring outdoor activities as a core part of suicide prevention. Since 2010, such programs have shown that cultural camps emphasising relationship-building with other youth and adult role models significantly improve mood scores, enhance the sense of belonging, and increase participants’ capacity to handle life stressors [11]. Research in Greenland also shows that one key factor for physical and mental health is a sense of belonging and relationships between younger and older individuals and being part of a community [19]. Additionally, studies highlight that activities in and connection to nature are fundamental to Greenlandic culture, contributing positively to mental health and the feeling of a good life [20]. A 2011 survey among the oldest primary school students in Greenland [21] revealed that most respondents (82%) knew where to seek help and support for suicidal thoughts. However, the survey also found that only 58% of those who had experienced suicidal thoughts had disclosed them to anyone. Only 5% had contacted a public institution in connection with a suicide attempt. Although several suicide prevention resources are available in Kalaallit Nunaat, young people do not always make use of formal services when experiencing suicidal thoughts. Understanding these patterns is important for strengthening both community and professional suicide prevention initiatives. More recent data indicates that Kalaallit Inuit are increasingly seeking help for suicidal thoughts, for instance, by contacting Tusaannga, Greenland's national helpline. Tusaannga’s 2022 annual report shows that 44% of inquiries were related to current suicidal thoughts [7]. The remaining inquiries were about previous suicide attempts, concerns for others, suicide bereavement, and individuals at risk of suicide.

Gaining insights into what motivates people to seek help and how they regain a desire for life is essential for advancing suicide prevention efforts in the future.

Positionality, credibility, and accountability

The first author's position as a Kalaallit Inuit researcher is central to this research, combined with more than a decade of engagement and work in suicide prevention activities across Kalaallit Nunaat, contributed directly to the feasibility of recruitment, interviewing, and interpretation. The qualitative approach was chosen because the study relies heavily on culturally grounded meaning-making, Indigenous-language interviewing, and longstanding community relationships that support trust when discussing highly sensitive experiences related to suicidality. These contextual conditions are considered an important part of the knowledge production process rather than simply a researcher characteristic. Together, this drives an explicit reflexivity about power, expectations and representation. Credibility is strengthened through Indigenous-language interviewing, transparent documentation of analytic decisions, iterative reflexive memoing, and participant-informed dissemination choices. The protocol aligns with Inuit Circumpolar Council guidance on equitable and ethical engagement (43). It is additionally informed by the principle “Nothing About Us Without Us”, a phrase widely associated with disability rights activism and participatory approaches to social justice, which emphasises the meaningful Inuit involvement of affected populations in decisions, knowledge production, and dissemination. The study is grounded in Inuit-led and community-engaged approaches, where all participants are Kalaallit Inuit from Kalaallit Nunaat, ensuring that the research is informed by Indigenous perspectives and priorities throughout. The project recognises Indigenous Knowledge in its own right and emphasises meaningful and reciprocal partnerships, ongoing dialogue, and culturally appropriate communication and dissemination. The research is further informed by the researcher’s positionality as an Indigenous Kalaallit Inuit scholar which supports relational accountability, culturally grounded interpretation, and adherence to Inuit-defined ethical practices.

Ethics

International suicide theory by researcher Thomas Joiner [22] encourages professionals to be attentive to individuals who express their suicidal thoughts, as this openness can be preventive. Given the sensitive nature of the topic, this research project implements several measures to maintain a high ethical standard. First and foremost, participant well-being and safety are prioritised by the first author, who is trained as a supervisor and counsellor in difficult conversations with suicidal individuals, with eight years of practical experience. The first author has extensive experience focusing on the trauma informed approach and healing process during carrier in suicide prevention work in Kalaallit Nunaat. Additionally, the first author is trained to assess suicide risk and prepared to provide support if a participant shows suicidal tendencies, either through a direct conversation or by referring them to the national helpline Tusaanga, available 24/7. Alternatively, healthcare services, including psychiatry or the police, can be engaged, as they are available around the clock in all Kalaallit Nunaat towns.

Recruitment ethics

Recruitment procedures were designed to minimise potential distress and avoid undue influence. Participation is entirely voluntary and based upon self-identification. Individuals are not recruited through medical records, professional referrals, or disclosure by third parties. Potential participants contact the researcher directly in response to recruitment materials. Recruitment materials focus on recovery, coping, and wellbeing rather than the suicide attempt itself in order to minimise potential distress associated with recruitment.

Participants receive both written and oral information before participation. Informed consent is regarded as an ongoing process and participants may withdraw from the research component at any point before analyses are finalised.

Participants are asked to sign an informed consent form and have the opportunity to review, edit, and approve their stories before inclusion in the research project. They may withdraw from the study at any time, with the understanding that data already included in published articles or presentations cannot be retracted post-publication.

The Ethics Committee for Scientific Research (VEK) in Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland) and RIO at SDU Denmark have approved the study protocol, which follows Ilisimatusarfik’s research ethics guidelines [23]. Data will be managed in compliance with GDPR regulations.

Participants will be invited to the PhD defence as part of reciprocal accountability and recognition. Attendance is entirely voluntary. Hybrid participation options will be available, including participation without camera use. Participants will receive information about Tusaannga, Greenland's national 24-hour helpline, and other relevant support services.

Participant wellbeing and safety during dissemination activities

The study will apply a trauma-informed approach throughout recruitment, interviewing, analysis, and dissemination activities. Particular attention is given to participant wellbeing during public presentations and dissemination events where lived experiences of suicidality may be discussed.

Documentary film ethics

The PhD research findings are planned to be disseminated to the public of Kalaallit in Kalaallit Nunaat through a documentary film. The interviews from data collection will not be filmed. Participants will have the option to choose whether they wish to take part in the development of documentary film to support narrative ownership and meaningful dissemination of project materials, including sharing knowledge with the broader public as part of community-oriented knowledge dissemination. This approach aligns with Naalakkersuisut’s (Government of Greenland) research strategy [24], emphasising that research in Kalaallit Nunaat should ensure local ownership, benefit, and accessibility for the Kalaallit Inuit in Kalaallit Nunaat.

Participation in the documentary dissemination component is entirely voluntary and separate from research participation. Participants will be informed that anonymity cannot be guaranteed if they choose to appear in the documentary. Participants may choose from different levels of visibility, including full anonymity, voice-only participation, visual participation without identification, or only citations with nature as background or full public identification. The informed consent process includes explicit discussion of the public and potentially permanent nature of documentary media. The purpose is to ensure that newly generated knowledge on suicide prevention is communicated jointly by participants and researchers, recognising participants as co-creators of knowledge rather than positioning researchers as the sole knowledge producers.

Ethical considerations for documentary dissemination

All documentary dissemination activities will follow WHO recommendations for responsible communication about suicide by avoiding detailed descriptions or visual portrayals of suicide methods and will not portray suicidal behaviour in ways that could contribute to normalisation, imitation, or glorification. Instead, the documentary will focus on recovery processes, protective factors, coping resources, help-seeking, resilience, and pathways from suicidal crisis toward wellbeing. The purpose is not to retell suicide attempts in detail, but to create greater public awareness that suicidal thoughts and suicidal crises can be survived, that recovery is possible, and that meaningful pathways toward healing exist.

In line with WHO recommendations, emphasis will be placed on hope, community resilience, supportive relationships, cultural strengths, and access to help. Participants will be encouraged to reflect on experiences of recovery, coping, belonging, and healing rather than methods of self-harm or detailed descriptions of suicide attempts in the documentary film.

This approach aligns with the overall purpose of the study, which seeks to understand and communicate participant-defined protective factors and recovery processes rather than the suicidal act itself.

Aim of the study

The main purpose of this Ph.D. project is, therefore, to investigate the factors that protect against suicidal behaviour among Kalaallit Inuit who experienced suicide attempts during youth and early adulthood in the age between (15–35 years) who self-identify as currently thriving, recovering, or experiencing positive wellbeing despite previous suicidal behaviour.

The first author has worked with suicide prevention for many years across all municipalities in Kalaallit Nunaat with Inuit who work in various regions and government position.

During these meetings, the lack of evidence-based research on factors that protect against suicidality and support renewed hope and a desire to live following suicide attempts was frequently discussed. In response, many has approached the first author to share their own experiences of recovering hope and life courage following suicidal crises. Many of them haveexpressed a wish for such experiences to be documented and studied, so that this knowledge could benefit others facing similar challenges. Their willingness to share experiential knowledge reflected collective responsibility and contributing knowledge for the benefit of the wider community, and it informed the development of the present study.

Theoretical framework

The theoretical framework of this PhD project is grounded in The Interpersonal Theory of Suicide [22,25], which posits that the most dangerous form of suicidal desire arises from the presence of two interpersonal factors: a lack of belonging and a feeling of being a burden to others, coupled with hopelessness associated with each of these conditions. This theory differentiates between the capability for suicide and the desire for suicide. According to the theory, the capability for suicidal behaviour develops through habituation after repeated exposure to physically painful or fear inducing experiences, which cultivates fearlessness toward self-destruction and pain, as well as technical proficiency in one or more methods of suicide.

Fearlessness, as a learned skill, allows a person to carry out the act of suicide. Additionally, the capability for suicide is reinforced when one’s pain tolerance increases or when one becomes accustomed to suppressing the strong natural drive for self-preservation.

The theory also suggests that while psychological pain and a sense of hopelessness are necessary conditions for suicidal thoughts, they are not sufficient for suicide actions. As long as a person fears death and the actions leading to suicide [22,25] attempts are unlikely. The theory asserts that serious suicide attempts occur only when three specific conditions align: a feeling of being a burden to one’s surroundings, a lack of belonging, and the capability to carry out the act.The research questions specifically draw upon the theory’s understanding that discussing suicide can be preventive, as it encourages openness about suicidal thoughts. Joiner emphasises that a sense of belonging is a relatively accessible factor to address and is crucial for human functioning. Therefore, linked through an emphasis on belonging, the interview guide in this project is inspired by this theoretical framework.

This approach, informed by Joiner’s Interpersonal-Psychological Theory of Suicide and particularly emphasising the role of sense of belonging as a key target for prevention, has also been used in suicide prevention courses in Greenland, with positive results, as participants continued to use the learned tools for three years post-training [6]. However, suicide must also be understood within a life course perspective [26], where individual life stories, as well as developmental and relational issues, are considered in the analysis.

In addition to psychological capability, international suicide research has highlighted access to lethal means as an important factor influencing suicide mortality. A substantial body of evidence demonstrates that access to firearms, medications, and other highly lethal methods increases the likelihood that suicidal crises result in death and that reducing access to such means represents an effective suicide prevention strategy [27]. While this topic has received limited attention within Greenlandic suicide research, it may be relevant to consider in the Greenlandic context, where access to hunting firearms forms part of everyday life in many communities. The present study does not specifically investigate access to means but acknowledges this broader suicidological literature.

Methodology

This manuscript presents a PhD protocol rather than empirical findings. While recruitment and data collection activities are planned, analyses are pending and no study findings are reported within this paper.

Design

The data foundation for this project consists of personal narratives. Hjelmeland emphasises in her book on suicide prevention [28] that qualitative research has often been excluded from suicide studies, which have largely focused on quantitative studies of risk factors for suicide over the past 25 years. Hjelmeland argues that this approach has not succeeded in reducing suicide rates, highlighting the need to broaden our understanding of valid research and evidence.

Qualitative research is thus chosen as the method for this PhD protocol to capture these elements. The study is grounded in an Indigenous research perspective that recognises lived experience, community knowledge, storytelling, relationships, language, land, and cultural practices as valid knowledge systems in their own right. The study does not seek to claim ownership of community knowledge but to document and share knowledge contributed by participants.

The methodological approach is further informed by findings from the pilot study [8], which demonstrated that participants often described recovery as relational, contextual, and culturally embedded rather than as an individual psychological characteristic. These observations reinforced the relevance of a narrative ethnographic approach centred on lived experience and Indigenous ways of knowing.

During previous professional activities and community engagement related to suicide prevention, community members have shared reflections on recovery and wellbeing following suicidal crises. These experiences contributed to the development of the present research questions but do not constitute data for the current study [29]. This inspired the researcher to bring these narratives into academic focus through this study.

Participants and recruitment

Participants are adults aged 18 years and older who attempted suicide during youth/early adulthood in the age 15–35 years and who describe themselves as thriving today. Recruitment has been initiated through a national Sermitsiaq newspaper announcement and social media dissemination through the first author's community network. Interested individuals voluntarily contacted the researcher. Most participants were previously unknown to the researcher and had no personal relationship with the researcher.

Data generation

Thirty semi-structured in-depth individual interviews [30] will be conducted by the first author in Kalaallisut (Greenlandic native language). The first author is also responsible for verbatim transcription of all interviews, ensuring continuity between interviewing, transcription, interpretation, and analysis At the time of protocol publication, approximately thirty individuals have expressed interest in participation through the researcher’s extensive network built over six years of work in the field and contacted the researcher following recruitment efforts. Eligibility assessment and final inclusion will occur according to the study protocol. [29]. The age range 15 to 35 years is based on a prior health report on mental health in Greenland: Mental Health and Health Among 15–35-Year-Olds in Greenland [31], which is the typical age range in suicide risk groups [3].

The present PhD protocol builds directly upon a previously completed qualitative pilot study [8] and contributed important methodological insights regarding recruitment, trust-building, Indigenous-language interviews, and the feasibility of conducting interviews both online and face-to-face across geographically dispersed regions of Kalaallit Nunaat.

For this project, it is essential to include all regions of Greenland. Therefore, interviews will be conducted both face-to-face and via video call to ensure inclusion of participants from across Kalaallit Nunaat.

Interview guide

The interview guide used in this study was developed to explore protective factors after suicidal behaviour among Greenlandic youth through a culturally grounded and relational lens. The interview guide used in this study was developed to explore protective factors after suicidal behaviour among Greenlandic youth through a culturally grounded and relational lens. It is inspired by Indigenous methodologies that centre lived experience and relational knowledge [32], emphasising knowledge as co-created, context-dependent, and grounded in relationships between people, place, and community. This perspective informs the study’s methodological approach by prioritising participants’ own interpretations, narrative forms, and meanings, rather than imposing predefined analytical categories. In addition, the study draws on cultural theory emphasising the significance of everyday life and embedded norms, highlighting how what matters in people's lives is shaped through daily practices, social expectations, and culturally situated values. Together, these approaches guide the exploration of protective factors as emerging from lived, relational, and culturally meaningful contexts rather than as abstract or individual-level constructs. In line with critiques of the protective factors literature that highlight definitional inconsistency and overly individualistic approaches [16], protective factors are in this study understood as relational, contextual, and culturally embedded processes rather than fixed or universal individual attributes. This conceptualisation was informed by empirical insights from a preceding qualitative pilot study, in which participants identified relational safety, cultural grounding, connection to nature, belonging and participation, language access, and culturally relevant support as central resources in their pathways from crisis to recovery (kilde).

The interview guide includes themes such as childhood context, transitions to resilience, belonging, nature, and professional support. It also integrates perspective on hopelessness as relational rather than pathological by inviting participants to situate experiences of hopelessness within relationships, environments, and life histories and Indigenous epistemological insights [33] into how people interpret and evaluate help. The structure encourages participants to narrate their lived experiences and reflect on meaning-making across time, aligning with best practices in qualitative suicidology [34] and land-based healing literature [35]. This dialogical and trauma-informed approach reflects the study’s commitment to ethical engagement and co-created understanding.

Analysis

Data will be analysed using an ethnographic, iterative and abductive analytic approach [36]. NVivo software will be used to support data management, coding, memo-writing, and thematic development. The primary analysis will be conducted by the first author. Selected anonymized excerpts will be translated into English and discussed with supervisors to support analytical transparency and reflexive dialogue.

Analysis will proceed through repeated cycles of close readings of transcribed data, memo-writing, and interpretive refinement, moving between participants' narratives, relational/contextual knowledge, and evolving analytic questions to foreground Inuit worldviews and local explanatory models on their own terms. This involves attending closely to participants’ own language, metaphors, and narrative structures, and allowing locally meaningful categories and interpretations to emerge inductively rather than being imposed from predefined theoretical frameworks. The purpose of this analytical process is to develop insights into participants’ thoughts and experiences regarding their coping resources, examining protective factors such as turning points to the process of recovery and how they are utilised in daily life. Although the interview guide is fixed, data will be analysed using an open and thematic approach to avoid constraining the exploration of the data’s content.

Beyond the academic contributions of this dissertation, educational materials will be developed, including a co-created documentary film, developed in dialogue with participants to ensure narrative ownership and meaningful dissemination for senior students in primary schools and higher education institutions in Greenland as well as to the public by sending it to Greenland’s national tv broadcaster KNR to ensure the Governments strategy for research protocols where research results will be owned by the Greenlandic people.

The study is inspired by selected principles of community-based participatory research (CBPR), particularly relational accountability, reciprocal knowledge exchange, and co-creation during dissemination activities. However, the study should not be considered a full CBPR design, as participants are not involved in all stages of data collection and analysis. CBPR has proven meaningful among Indigenous populations, including Inuit, First Nations, and Métis in the Arctic [37,38]. This approach fosters community involvement in health research, building co-ownership throughout the research process and communicating results back to the community at each stage. In this study, CBPR is operationalized through principles of co-creation, relational accountability, and reciprocal knowledge exchange.This will include involving participants, creating spaces for dialogue and reflection on findings, and ensuring that knowledge generated is returned to and accessible for the communities involved.

Communication, implications, and practical application

This PhD project will use a co-created documentary film as a dissemination strategy. This was partly inspired by previous youth-led initiatives in Kalaallit Nunaat, including the documentary Qamani produced in 2023 [39] by MIO (Greenland's National Children's Rights Institution), in which young people publicly shared experiences and recommendations regarding suicide prevention. The documentary has been shown at national television in Kalaallit Nunaat also had a national premier at the movie theatre Katuaq Nuuk. This demonstrated how visual storytelling can facilitate public dialogue, participation, and community engagement concerning suicide prevention and youth wellbeing. The documentary should primarily be understood as a dissemination strategy rather than an intervention. Within Kalaallit Nunaat there is increasing emphasis that research concerning Kalaallit people should be accessible and returned to the communities [24] from which the knowledge originates. The documentary film is planned to be developed in close collaboration with participants who choose to engage in dissemination activities, to ensure narrative ownership and ethically grounded storytelling. Care will be taken to align with WHO guidelines on media reporting of suicide [40], which emphasise avoiding detailed methods for suicidal act and focusing instead on community resilience, mental health awareness, and prevention.

The documentary film is planned to be available via YouTube channel and can serve as an educational tool containing concrete, relevant recommendations for suicide prevention. It will be designed for use across multiple levels of the educational system, schools, universities, and training programs and reaches broader audiences, including professionals, policymakers, and the public.

The documentary film will serve as visual educational material and will be developed and refined in collaboration with suicide prevention instructors, participants, and an educational institution. The material will be tested in diverse community and school settings, with teachers and suicide prevention instructors allowing their feedback to iteratively improve format and content. This is to ensure narrative ownership and meaningful dissemination, while also including English subtitles to engage other Arctic populations.

Overall, this communication strategy ensures that the research has tangible, long-term impact by embedding culturally grounded suicide prevention knowledge into everyday educational and community practices across Greenland.

Limitations

Participants were recruited through voluntary self-selection following public recruitment efforts and may therefore differ from individuals who are less connected to social networks, less comfortable discussing suicidal experiences, or less willing to engage in research on sensitive topics. Although transferability may exist across some Arctic Indigenous contexts, the findings are expected to be most directly relevant to Kalaallit Inuit communities in Kalaallit Nunaat. Any transferability to other settings will depend on historical, cultural, linguistic, and social similarities, and the study therefore relies on thick description, reflexive documentation, and transparent analytic processes to support readers' assessment of contextual relevance.

Additional limitations relate to the documentary dissemination component. Participants who choose to appear publicly may differ from individuals who prefer anonymity, potentially influencing which perspectives become visible and how experiences are narrated. The presence of cameras may also shape participant narratives. From a narrative ethnographic perspective, such influences are understood as part of the context within which stories are produced rather than solely as methodological bias. Nevertheless, visibility choices may affect both participation and the public representation of recovery experiences. To address these considerations, dissemination follows a tiered consent model (anonymous, named, voice-only, or full visibility), renewed consent procedures, and community-informed risk assessments.

A further limitation concerns the use of selected Western theoretical frameworks alongside Indigenous epistemologies and Kalaallit Inuit understandings of wellbeing, belonging, recovery, and relational life. While theories such as the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide provide useful analytical perspectives, tensions may arise when applying externally developed concepts within Indigenous contexts. To address this challenge, participants' own narratives, meanings, and explanatory models remain central throughout the analysis, while ongoing reflexive attention is used to avoid privileging Western interpretations over Inuit understandings and lived experiences. This approach aligns with Inuit Circumpolar Council principles emphasising Inuit leadership, relational accountability, and respect for Indigenous knowledge systems.

Discussion and conclusion

This protocol positions Kalaallit Inuit language and worldview as essential evidence in suicidology, especially where quantitative approaches have not reduced suicide rates. It centres recovery as relational and culturally situated, making explicit space for spirituality, tradition and land/nature-based wellbeing as lived elements of Kalaallit Inuit life. By detailing ethical relational practices and a flexible analytical framework, it provides a transferable model for Indigenous-led suicide prevention research in Arctic contexts. Substantive results will be published in forthcoming empirical articles.
