Effectiveness of pharmacist-led management of candidemia in a tertiary care hospital without a full-time infectious disease specialist
Authors: Teruhisa Kinoshita, Shoko Sahara, Atsuya Kondo, Yuki Kondo, Yoichi Ishitsuka, Norio Takimoto, Keisuke Oka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere are limited reports on the effectiveness of promoting pharmacist-led management of candidemia in tertiary care hospitals without a full-time infectious disease specialist. We aimed to assess the effectiveness of promoting pharmacist-led management of candidemia in a tertiary care hospital without a full-time infectious disease specialist and to determine the type of support provided by pharmacists in the antimicrobial stewardship team. This retrospective cohort study evaluated patients with Candida spp. detected in blood cultures between April 2013 and March 2024 at our hospital. To compare the effect of bundled intervention on patients with candidemia, patients enrolled during the periods from April 2013 to March 2018 and April 2018 to March 2024 were classified in the pre- and post-antimicrobial stewardship team pharmacist intervention groups, respectively. There were no significant differences in the baseline clinical characteristics or Candida spp. distribution between the two groups. Compliance to the candidemia treatment bundle significantly improved in the post-antimicrobial stewardship team pharmacist intervention group (4.5% vs 39.6%); however, there was no difference between the two groups in 30-day mortality. The antimicrobial stewardship team pharmacist primarily contributed by recommending treatment initiation, drug selection, and checking for bundle compliance. Pharmacist-led management of candidemia did not improve outcomes for patients with candidemia in a tertiary care hospital with no full-time infectious disease specialist. However, this management may have helped improve the quality of antifungal therapy in terms of drug selection, dosing, and drug interactions, thus contributing to improved candidemia treatment bundle compliance.

INTRODUCTION

Candidemia is an infection with a high mortality rate.1,2 When treating this infection, the presence of complications and organ infection must be properly assessed, and blood culture negativity must be confirmed before the treatment duration is determined. Although guidelines vary in content, bundled interventions are recommended for treating candidemia.1,2 Promoting candidemia treatment bundles shown to improved treatment outcomes.3,4

Guidelines recommend the appropriate use of antimicrobial and antifungal drugs by infectious disease specialists and pharmacists.5 In Japan, the effectiveness of antimicrobial stewardship team (AST) support for candidemia has been reported in secondary and tertiary medical institutions with infectious disease specialists,6,7 but there are limited reports of AST activities in tertiary medical institutions without full-time infectious disease specialists.8

Therefore, this study investigated the effectiveness of promoting pharmacist-led management of candidemia in a tertiary care hospital without a full-time infectious disease specialist. We also aimed to determine the type of support provided by pharmacists in the AST.

METHODOLOGY

Overview of pharmacist-led candidemia management activities at our hospital

Our hospital is a tertiary care hospital with 704 sickbeds. The hospital has no hematology department and no full-time infectious disease specialist. The AST was established in April 2018, and management of candidemia was initiated.

The AST comprises an emergency specialist, urologist, gastrointestinal surgeon, pediatrician, part-time infectious disease specialist, full-time pharmacist, nurse, and clinical laboratory technician. The role of the part-time infectious disease specialist included attending weekly morning conferences for blood culture-positive patients and weekly consultation responses for difficult cases; they were not involved in any other aspect of the management of candidemia.

The AST comprises five pharmacists (two pharmacists are certified as both Infection Control Pharmacy Specialist and Infectious Disease Chemotherapy Pharmacist, and one pharmacist is certified as an Infectious Disease Chemotherapy Pharmacist), one of whom is dedicated full-time to AST responsibilities. At the core of the team is a full-time pharmacist, who conducts daily morning conferences for patients with positive blood cultures and oversees their follow-up care. The team also performs prospective audit and feedback of patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as carbapenems, piperacillin-tazobactam, and fluoroquinolones. One of the key interventions implemented by the team is a bundled approach to the management of patients with candidemia. Data collection and evaluation items

All data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records. The key items assessed included patient characteristics, risk factors for candidemia, Candida species, diagnosis, and initial treatment. The primary outcome was compliance with the treatment bundle. Secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality due to candidemia, transition to oral therapy, and pharmacist support. The AST pharmacist interventions were evaluated based on recommendations for treatment initiation and discontinuation, drug selection, dose modification, loading dose design (caspofungin or fluconazole), route of administration, and testing recommendations. Bundle elements such as “confirmed negative blood culture” and “14 days of treatment after negative blood culture” can only be achieved in surviving patients. To exclude patients who died or dropped out before completing bundle elements that should be achieved after treatment initiation, a subpopulation analysis was performed on patients who survived >30 days after positive blood culture.

Statistical analysis

JMP Pro 18.0.1 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA) was used to conduct the statistical analysis. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare nominal variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to assess survival for up to 30 days, and the log-rank test was used to identify differences between the groups.

As a supplemental analysis, we employed an interrupted time series approach to assess trends in bundle completion rates with AST pharmacist intervention. Changes in levels and trends were analyzed using a segmented regression model. The model included outcomes as dependent variables and indicators for pre-intervention, post-intervention, time elapsed since the start of the study, and time since the intervention as independent variables. The offset was the natural logarithm of either the cumulative number of patients per 4-month period or the number of bundle items targeted. No transition period or comparison group was included. For the interrupted time series analysis, we utilized generalized linear models with a Poisson distribution. The analysis included 15 data points from the pre-intervention phase and 17 from the post-intervention phase, excluding the period from April to July 2019 (during which no candidemia cases occurred).

RESULTS

Patients’ characteristics

Between April 2013 and March 2024, Candida spp. were detected in the blood cultures of 112 patients. After excluding 10 patients who died before their blood cultures became positive, 6 patients who were not treated as end-of-life care at the time their blood cultures became positive, and 4 patients who were transferred to other hospitals for treatment, 92 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The pre- and post-AST pharmacist intervention groups comprised 44 and 48 patients, respectively. The patient characteristics in both groups are shown in Table 2. No significant differences were observed in patient characteristics between the two groups, except for β-D-glucan levels.

DISCUSSION

This study examined the effectiveness of pharmacist-led management of candidemia in a hospital that does not have a full-time infectious disease specialist. With severe infections such as Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia,9 Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteremia,10 and candidemia,11 early consultation with an infectious disease specialist is associated with reduced mortality and improved patient outcomes; therefore, early and appropriate therapeutic intervention is crucial. However, Japan has a shortage of infectious disease specialists, and most specialists work at university hospitals.12 Therefore, pharmacists must play a central role in ASTs in many hospitals, and their intervention in infectious disease treatment is important.13,14

The mortality associated with candidemia is considerably high, with international studies reporting rates between 10% and 50%.1,2 In reports from Japan, the mortality rate is estimated to be approximately 20% to 60%, although it varies widely from one report to another.6-8 National guidelines recommend bundle management to improve the prognosis of candidemia. Reports from Japan also indicate that promoting the candidemia treatment bundle improves clinical outcomes.3 A candidemia treatment bundle, based on the Infectious Diseases Society of America and European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases guidelines,1,2 was developed at our hospital (Table 1), and its effectiveness was studied.

Although the promotion of candidemia management by the pharmacist-led AST significantly increased compliance with the candidemia treatment bundle, no significant differences in 30-day mortality rates were observed. The candidemia treatment bundle (all six items) compliance rate improved significantly from the pre-AST pharmacist intervention group (4.5%) to the post-AST pharmacist intervention group (39.6%); however, the compliance rate remained low. This may be related to the high mortality rate from candidemia. The treatment bundle includes negative blood culture confirmation followed by 14 days of treatment. Therefore, it is difficult to achieve the bundle if the patient dies before negative blood culture confirmation or within 14 days after the confirmation. In addition, tests may not be performed for patients with severe symptoms and scheduled for end-of-life care. However, when the analysis was limited to patients who survived 30 days or more after a positive blood culture, compliance with the candidemia treatment bundle (all six items) improved. Although there were slight differences in the bundle content, the completion rate in Kawaguchi et al’s report,7 which was based on a university hospital with an on-site infectious disease specialist, was approximately 50%, a comparable level. Considering that the bundle completion rate in Takesue et al’s report was 21.4%,3 our pharmacist-led initiative is highly valuable. Furthermore, although the report by Murakami et al,8 based on a tertiary medical institution without an infectious disease specialist, did not include the overall bundle completion rate, it focused solely on physician-led AST. Our study’s inclusion of the diagnostic process of echocardiography, and the fact that it was conducted as a pharmacist-led AST, are significant.

A detailed analysis of bundle components (Table 3) revealed the specific aspects of care most impacted by pharmacist-led AST. The most significant improvements were seen in procedures that pharmacists could review and recommend without strict deadlines, such as transthoracic echocardiography, repeat blood cultures, and negative blood culture confirmation. These items align closely with the pharmacist’s main activities, such as inspection recommendations (Table 5). Conversely, “central venous catheter removal within 24 hours” showed the least improvement. This may be because this item was already implemented at a relatively high rate (83.9%) pre-intervention. Unlike electronic orders, it requires a physician-dependent procedure, has a strict 24-hour time limit that can be missed during weekends or nights (when pharmacist intervention may be delayed), and involves complex clinical decision-making in severe cases (eg, patients on high-dose catecholamines). Similarly, “treatment for at least 14 days after a negative blood culture” (36.4% to 64.6%), while improved, was not fully achieved. This highlights a limitation of intervention timing; if a pharmacist notices a premature discontinuation (eg, on day 13) retrospectively, it is difficult to recommend restarting therapy. These findings suggest that items requiring strict time adherence and physician-dependent procedures (central venous catheter removal) or retrospective review (treatment duration) are challenging to manage solely through case-by-case pharmacist intervention. To further improve compliance, these individual interventions must be supplemented by continuous, hospital-wide education and awareness campaigns regarding the candidemia bundle.

Six possible reasons for the lack of significant reductions in the 30-day mortality rates may be proposed. (1) The rates of central venous catheter removal within 24 hours of diagnosis, ophthalmological examination, and blood culture retesting were considerably high compared to those previously reported,6-8 even before the AST pharmacist intervention was implemented. Candidemia may have been appropriately treated to some extent even before the AST pharmacist intervention. (2) Treatment rates were significantly higher in the post-AST pharmacist intervention group within 14 days of negative blood culture results than in the pre-AST pharmacist intervention group. However, treatment duration was similar between the two groups. (3) The increase in echocardiography examination rates was associated with a significant improvement in bundle compliance rates. The complication rate of endocarditis resulting from candidiasis is 2% to 8% in patients with candidemia, and it is rare.15-17 In the present study, only one case (2.2%) of infectious endocarditis was noted in the pre-AST pharmacist intervention group, while two cases (4.2%) were identified in the post-AST pharmacist intervention group. (4) The number of patients treated during the study period and included in the analysis was small. Takesue et al analyzed a large number of cases and reported that compliance to the candidemia treatment bundle led to improved clinical outcomes.3 Other studies involving a small number of cases reported that improved compliance to treatment bundles did not improve 30-day mortality rates.6-8 (5) The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic occurred in the period after the AST intervention was implemented. Although the present study did not include any patients who tested positive for coronavirus disease 2019, the prevalence of this disease may have had an unforeseen impact on the outcomes because the hospital has been designated as a medical institution for Class II infectious diseases. (6) β-D-glucan levels were higher in the post-AST pharmacist intervention group compared to the pre-AST pharmacist intervention group.

Previous research has shown that high β-D-glucan levels are associated with higher mortality rates.18,19 Therefore, it is possible that this may have negated the effect of the AST pharmacist intervention.

Support from AST pharmacists for candidemia treatment mainly consisted of recommendations for drug selection, dose adjustment, and checking for bundle compliance. The content of support from pharmacists was similar to that reported in other countries, where healthcare systems and pharmacists’ remit differ.20,21 Our hospital had a high support acceptance rate, suggesting that a pharmacist-led AST is an important component in a hospital that does not have an infectious disease department. The cooperation between various departments contributed to the high proposal acceptance rate, as the AST included doctors from several departments and could participate in the blood culture conferences every morning. Since April 2020, the AST pharmacist has provided education regarding infectious disease treatment to residents at the hospital; we consider that a good relationship has been established with the AST pharmacist, particularly among younger doctors.

The drugs used to treat candidemia pose a number of challenges. For example, several drug interactions with azoles must be addressed appropriately.22 A loading dose is recommended for fluconazole, voriconazole, and caspofungin, and the patient’s renal function and weight are relevant to dose setting. Appropriate dosage adjustment by a pharmacist is necessary, especially in critically ill patients, because pharmacokinetic parameters may change daily.

This study had several limitations. First, the study was a single-agency management of candidemia initiative. Currently, and for various reasons, hospital medical staff are under-resourced,12,23,24 and the number of medical staff available for involvement in the management of candidemia varies widely from facility to facility. Therefore, extrapolating these findings to initiatives at other facilities may not be possible. Second, the drugs available in Japan differ from those available in other countries. Consequently, the choice of antifungal drugs and methods of administration may differ, leading to differences in interventions provided by pharmacists. Finally, the number of patients included in the study was small.

CONCLUSION

Pharmacist-led management of candidemia did not improve treatment outcomes for patients with candidemia in a tertiary care hospital that had no full-time infectious disease specialist. However, this strategy may have helped improve the quality of antifungal therapy in terms of drug selection, dosing, and drug interactions, thus contributing to improved candidemia treatment bundle compliance.
