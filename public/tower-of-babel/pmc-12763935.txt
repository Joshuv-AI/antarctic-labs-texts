Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics reveal TNC-positive cancer-associated fibroblasts that mediate immunosuppression and promote tumor progression in basal cell carcinoma
Authors: Min Luo, Wei Tian, Qianwei Zhuo, Zhenjian Zou, Jinger Lin, Lihang Lin, Rong Li, Yue Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global incidence of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is steadily increasing. Although immunotherapy has emerged as a treatment option, its efficacy remains limited. This underscores the need for further investigation of immune remodeling mechanisms in BCC. Here, we aimed to characterize fibroblast subsets and their interactions with other components of the tumor microenvironment in BCC.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA sequencing on nine surgical specimens from six patients, including BCC tumors and adjacent normal tissues. Key findings were further validated using spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunohistochemistry.ResultsWe identified the C01_TNC fibroblast cluster as a population of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs). These cells exhibited prominent peritumoral infiltration and were characterized by matrix-associated and inflammatory gene expression profiles. We propose that C01_TNC CAFs contribute to tumor progression, possibly by upregulating collagens that engage integrins on tumor cells, thereby facilitating extracellular matrix remodeling and invasion. Furthermore, C01_TNC CAFs were spatially associated with Tregs and expressed ligands that interact with Treg receptors, suggesting a role in enhancing immunosuppression. Notably, this subset was significantly enriched in peritumoral regions and could establish an immune barrier favoring tumor progression.ConclusionsOur findings shed light on the role of fibroblasts in orchestrating interactions between tumor and immune cells in BCC. This enhanced understanding of the tumor microenvironment may inform novel therapeutic strategies to improve treatment outcomes for patients with BCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07491-2.Keywords: Basal cell carcinoma, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Spatial transcriptomics, Immunosuppression, Cancer-associated fibroblast

Introduction

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common malignancy worldwide, and its incidence continues to rise across diverse populations [1]. Most lesions are effectively controlled with local therapies—surgical excision or topical approaches—with excellent long-term outcomes [2, 3]. A minority of cases, however, present as locally advanced, deeply infiltrative, or metastatic disease that threatens survival and function. Because BCCs frequently occur on the head and neck, invasion of periocular, nasal, or auricular structures can render surgery challenging and potentially disfiguring [4]. Systemic options include Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HHIs; vismodegib, sonidegib) and immune checkpoint blockade, particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibition with cemiplimab [5, 6]. Nevertheless, adverse events and both primary and acquired resistance often limit the durability of HHI therapy [7]. Moreover, cemiplimab achieves objective responses in only about one-third of patients with locally advanced BCC, leaving many without clinical benefit [8]. These limitations underscore the need for more effective strategies.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), comprising immune cells, stromal populations, and the extracellular matrix (ECM), profoundly shapes tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance [9]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute a major stromal compartment and have emerged as key modulators of antitumor immunity and potential therapeutic targets [10]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has begun to resolve CAF heterogeneity in BCC; however, the phenotypic states, spatial organization, and functional contributions of CAF subsets remain incompletely defined [11–14]. In particular, how CAF-immune-tumor interactions establish the immunosuppressive milieu characteristic of BCC requires clarification.

Here, integrating scRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics (ST-seq) and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), we systematically delineate fibroblast diversity in BCC. We identify five fibroblast states and highlight a C01_TNC subset that is enriched at tumor borders and exhibits matrix-associated and inflammatory features. These C01_TNC CAFs localize near tumor nests and regulatory T (Treg) cells and display ligand and ECM signatures consistent with potentiating integrin-mediated remodeling and immunosuppression. This work refines understanding of the BCC TME and nominates CAF-directed interventions as potential avenues to mitigate tumor progression and overcome immune barriers.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and clinical characteristics

Nine human tissue specimens (six BCC tumors and three matched adjacent normal tissues) were obtained from diagnostic biopsies at Union Hospital, Fujian Medical University (Fujian, China). No patient received any treatment prior to biopsy. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of Union Hospital, Fujian Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (approval No. 2025KY002).

Visium Spatial transcriptomics

RNA quality from optimal cutting temperature (OCT)–embedded sections was assessed by RNA integrity number (RIN) using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies); only samples with RIN > 7 proceeded to library preparation. Tissue optimization was performed with the Visium Spatial Tissue Optimization Slide & Reagent Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000193) following the manufacturer’s user guide (CG000238). Sections were permeabilized for the empirically determined optimal duration, and spatial gene-expression libraries were prepared using the Visium Spatial Gene Expression Reagent Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000184). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Spatial transcriptomics data processing and integration

Visium libraries were processed with Space Ranger (v2.1.1, 10x Genomics) and aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome. Space Ranger outputs were imported into R and analyzed with Seurat (v4.4.0). To integrate spatial and single-cell data, we co-embedded the scRNA-seq and ST expression matrices. CellTrek (v0.0.94) was used to infer cell-level spatial coordinates with the following parameters: intp_pnt = 5000, nPCs = 30, ntree = 1000, top_spot = 10, spot_n = 5, repel_r = 20, and repel_iter = 20 [15].

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded BCC sections (case BCC1_P1) were stained using the Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (PerkinElmer, NEL801001KT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibodies included: FAP (1:100; Abmart, M079003), TNC (1:500; Abmart, PC1039), COL1A2 (1:200; Abmart, PS04211), EPCAM (1:500; Abmart, M069058), PTCH1 (1:400; Abmart, TA5202), integrin α2 (1:500; Abcam, ab181548), integrin β1 (1:50; Abcam, ab52971), MIF (1:200; Abmart, T56829), LAMB1 (1:100; Abmart, P60054), FOXP3 (1:500; Abmart, PC23074), CD74 (1:100; Abmart, T56704), and CD44 (1:100; Abmart, T55122). Slides were scanned on a TissueFAXS SpectraS multispectral system (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria).

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Fresh skin tissues were rinsed twice with pre-chilled 1× DPBS (Gibco, 14190144) and minced into 2–4 mm³ fragments. Samples were dissociated using the MACS Human Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-095-929), filtered through 70-µm strainers (MACS SmartStrainer), and centrifuged at 300 rpm for 7 min. Pellets were treated with 1× Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-094-183), and dead cells were removed using the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-090-101). Cells were resuspended in 1× DPBS containing 0.04% BSA (Invitrogen, AM2616) at 700–1,200 cells/µL for scRNA-seq.

10x single-cell RNA sequencing library construction and sequencing

Single cells were captured on the Chromium platform (10x Genomics). Gel bead-in-emulsions were generated, polyadenylated transcripts were reverse-transcribed, and full-length barcoded cDNA was amplified. Libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ v3 Reagent Kit (10x Genomics) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150) per the manufacturer’s protocols.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and quality control

Raw reads were processed with Cell Ranger (v7.2.0) using default settings. FASTQ files were generated with mkfastq, aligned to GRCh38, and filtered by unique molecular identifiers and valid barcodes to produce gene-barcode matrices. Matrices were imported into Seurat (v4.4.0) for downstream analysis [16]. Cells with < 200 detected genes or >20% mitochondrial transcripts were excluded. Doublets were removed using DoubletFinder. A total of 115,414 high-quality cells were retained for analysis.

Data integration and dimensionality reduction

Gene expression matrices were normalized with Seurat’s NormalizeData (default parameters). The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified, centered, and scaled. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these features. To mitigate batch effects across patients, datasets were integrated using Harmony (first 30 PCs) [17].

Cell clustering and annotation

Graph-based clustering (Louvain algorithm via Seurat FindClusters) was applied, and clusters were visualized with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell identities were assigned using canonical markers from the literature. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for fibroblast subpopulations were identified with Seurat FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) using thresholds of min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.5, and adjusted P < 0.01. Copy number variation (CNV) in malignant cells was inferred with inferCNV using immune cells as reference.

Pathway and functional enrichment analyses

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was used to estimate pathway activities across subtypes, using Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler (v4.2.2) under default parameters to characterize functions of each subcluster.

Single-cell trajectory inference

Fibroblast lineage relationships were inferred using Monocle 2 (v2.22.1) to construct pseudotime trajectories [18]. Trajectory and gene-expression dynamics were visualized with plot_cell_trajectory and plot_genes_in_pseudotime. CAF differentiation states were further evaluated with CytoTRACE (v0.3.3) using default settings.

Gene regulatory network analysis

Single-cell gene regulatory networks and key transcription factors (TFs) were inferred with SCENIC (v1.1.2) [19]. GRNBoost identified TF–target co-expression modules; RcisTarget detected enriched motifs to define regulons. Regulon activity was quantified per cell using AUCell, and cell-type specificity was summarized by Regulon Specificity Scores.

Cell-cell communication

Intercellular signaling networks among subpopulations were analyzed with CellChat (v1.6.1) under default settings [20]. We assessed major signaling inputs/outputs and estimated ligand-receptor communication probabilities across cell types. Detailed parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Supporting Information).

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of major cell types

To investigate the TME landscape in BCC, we performed scRNA-seq and ST-seq analyses on six tumor samples and three matched adjacent normal tissues from six BCC patients (Fig. 1A; Table S1). After quality control and removal of doublets, we obtained a total of 115,414 high-quality cells for downstream analysis. The gene expression matrices were then normalized and integrated, and batch effects were corrected. Unsupervised clustering revealed fourteen distinct cell clusters, visualized by UMAP (Figure S1A-B).

Eleven major cell types were identified based on canonical marker genes, including myeloid cells (LYZ, CD68), keratinocytes (KC; KRT14, KRT5), T cells (ITK, CD69), mast cells (TPSAB1, CPA3), sweat gland cells (DCD, AQP5), B cells (MZB1, CD79A), endothelial cells (CD34, PECAM1), lymphatic endothelial cells (CCL21, PROX1), fibroblasts (DCN, COL1A1), melanocytes (PMEL, MLANA), and parietal cells (MYL9, MYH11) (Fig. 1B and E-F; Figure S1C). The distribution of these cell types varied among patients, highlighting heterogeneity in the TME’s cellular composition (Fig. 1D). Notably, no significant batch effect was observed across samples (Fig. 1C). In addition, cell cycle analysis revealed an even distribution of cells across the three cell-cycle phases (Figure S1D).

Single-cell analysis uncovers fibroblast heterogeneity in BCC

The plasticity and heterogeneity of fibroblasts are critical in shaping the TME. We defined five fibroblast subclusters based on their gene expression profiles: C01_TNC, C02_COCH, C03_WNT5A, C04_APOD, and C05_PTPRC (Fig. 2A). Cluster C01_TNC was more abundant in tumors than in adjacent normal tissues, representing the CAFs. In contrast, clusters C02_COCH, C03_WNT5A, C04_APOD, and C05_PTPRC were enriched in adjacent normal (non-tumor) tissues (Fig. 2B). Notably, C01_TNC CAFs constituted the largest proportion of the fibroblast population.

DEGs were used to further characterize the unique features of each fibroblast subset (Fig. 2C-D). C01_TNC CAFs exhibited characteristics of both matrix-associated CAFs (mCAFs) and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), with upregulated expression of ECM genes (e.g., TNC, FAP, MMP1, MMP11) and inflammatory markers (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL2) [21, 22]. The C04_APOD cluster, characterized by high expression of inflammatory genes (e.g., APOD, CFD, PLA2G2A, CXCL14, C3, CXCL12, APOE), was classified as an inflammatory fibroblast subset [23]. C02_COCH fibroblasts were uniquely defined by high COCH expression, similar to a newly identified fibroblast subtype in skin tissues [24]. C03_WNT5A fibroblasts, marked by elevated expression of vascular-related genes (particularly VEGFA), were designated as vascular fibroblasts. Finally, C05_PTPRC fibroblasts expressed high levels of PTPRC, SKAP1, and ITK—key molecules involved in T cell activation and immune responses [25–29].

We next performed GO enrichment analyses to elucidate the functions of these fibroblast subpopulations (Fig. 2E). C01_TNC CAFs were enriched in biological processes such as “extracellular matrix organization,” “regulation of angiogenesis,” and “leukocyte migration,” implying essential roles in ECM remodeling and pro-invasive processes. C04_APOD fibroblasts showed enrichment in regulation of inflammatory responses and cell chemotaxis, suggesting involvement in humoral inflammation. C05_PTPRC fibroblasts were enriched in pathways related to T cell activation and immune response regulation. Interestingly, C05_PTPRC cells were predominantly found in adjacent normal tissues and were seldom observed in tumors, suggesting they may have an antineoplastic (anti-tumor) phenotype. Furthermore, C02_COCH fibroblasts were mainly associated with axon development and trans-synaptic signaling processes, while C03_WNT5A fibroblasts were implicated in coagulation and vascular development processes.

Subsequently, we performed SCENIC analysis to identify master transcriptional regulators in each fibroblast cluster. By evaluating regulon specificity scores (Fig. 3A), we inferred key regulons for each subset. In C01_TNC CAFs, the top-ranked regulons were BACH1, FOSB, CREB3L2, and NFIL3 (Fig. 3B). Notably, BACH1 has been shown to be involved in ECM remodeling and tumor invasion [30].

(C-E) Differentiation trajectory of fibroblasts as predicted by CytoTRACE and Monocle2. (F) Spatial trajectory of fibroblasts in BCC. BCC, basal cell carcinoma; RSS, Regulon Specificity Score

To explore the dynamic transitions of fibroblasts, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis using the Monocle 2 algorithm, alongside CytoTRACE for stemness evaluation. CytoTRACE revealed that the C04_APOD cluster exhibited the highest stemness score (Fig. 3C). Cells from this C04_APOD subset were positioned at the beginning of the differentiation trajectory, with APOD and CXCL12 expression gradually decreasing along pseudotime (Fig. 3D). Cells from clusters C01_TNC, C03_WNT5A, and C05_PTPRC occupied intermediate states, whereas C02_COCH fibroblasts were located at the terminal state of the trajectory, showing increasing expression of their marker genes (Fig. 3E). These findings suggest a potential differentiation path from C04_APOD fibroblasts toward C01_TNC CAFs. We further projected the single-cell trajectories onto spatial transcriptomics data, and observed that the spatial distribution of fibroblast states was consistent with the single-cell pseudotime results (Fig. 3F).

Tumor-specific C01_TNC CAFs are associated with BCC progression

To further resolve BCC heterogeneity, we analyzed the KC compartment. KC were reclustered into nine distinct subclusters (Fig. 4A). We first applied InferCNV to assess CNV profiles in each KC, using immune cells as a reference. This analysis revealed pronounced chromosomal alterations and elevated CNV scores in KC clusters 0, 3, 4, and 7 (Fig. 4B-C). Consistently, clusters 0, 3, 4, and 7 showed higher expression of BCC markers PTCH1 and GLI2, along with increased Hedgehog and NOTCH pathway activity [4] (Figs. 4D-F and 5A). Accordingly, clusters 0, 3, 4, and 7 were classified as tumor KC populations.

Among the remaining KC subpopulations, clusters 5 and 7 expressed proliferation markers, indicating heightened proliferative capacity in those cells. Cluster 8 was specifically enriched for hair follicle signature genes, whereas cluster 2 was enriched for basal epidermis signatures. Clusters 1 and 6 expressed spinous or granular differentiation markers, consistent with their identity as differentiated KC (Fig. 4D-F).

We further identified genes upregulated in the tumor KC clusters. In addition to the Hedgehog pathway genes (PTCH1, PTCH2, GLI2), tumor KC overexpressed several other genes (e.g., SPON2, CASC15, ABI3BP, LRRTM4) (Fig. 4G). Notably, CASC15, a long non-coding RNA with potential oncogenic roles in BCC, was among these upregulated genes [31]. We also observed enrichment of Wnt/β-catenin, KRAS, and epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) signaling pathways within the tumor KC subpopulations. These findings suggest that beyond the classical Hedgehog pathway, additional molecular pathways may contribute to BCC pathogenesis.

CellChat analysis of cell-cell communication revealed extensive interactions between tumor cells and all other cell populations in the TME. The overall interaction network was markedly more complex in tumor regions compared to normal tissue. Notably, tumor cells received a majority of incoming signals from C01_TNC CAFs (Fig. 5B-D).

To understand how C01_TNC CAFs might drive tumor progression, we examined their specific interactions with tumor cells. C01_TNC CAFs expressed high levels of type I and type VI collagens (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL6A1, COL6A2, COL6A3), which can bind to integrins (ITGα2β1, ITGα3β1, ITGα5β8) and syndecan-1 (SDC1) on tumor KC (Fig. 5E). Integrin engagement is known to stimulate tumor cell proliferation and progression, and is critical for fibroblast-stroma interactions [32, 33]. This suggests that C01_TNC CAFs may facilitate tumor growth and invasion by depositing collagens that interact with tumor cell receptors.

Interactions between C01_TNC CAFs and T cell subpopulations contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment

To investigate the immune microenvironment in BCC, we analyzed T cell subsets based on their marker gene expression (Fig. 6A-D). Three major T cell subpopulations were identified: Tregs, effector NKT cells, and CD4+ effector memory T cells (TEM). The Treg subset was defined by high expression of FOXP3, IL2RA, IKZF2, TNFRSF18, CTLA4, and TIGIT [34]. Effector NKT cells were characterized by expression of CD8A and cytotoxic molecules (GZMB, GZMA, IFNG, CCL5), as well as NK cell markers (NKG7, GNLY) [35]. The CD4+ TEM subset displayed elevated levels of CD40LG and IL7R [35].

Tregs were notably enriched in BCC, consistent with an immunosuppressive TME. According to CellChat, Treg cells received a large proportion of their incoming signals from C01_TNC CAFs (Fig. 6E). To pinpoint the molecular basis of this crosstalk, we examined ligand-receptor interactions between C01_TNC CAFs and T cells (Fig. 6F). The analysis suggests that C01_TNC CAFs may recruit Tregs and enhance their immunosuppressive function through multiple ligand-receptor pairs, including CXCL12-CXCR4, MIF-(CD74/CD44), and laminin-CD44 interactions where laminin subunits LAMA4, LAMB1, LAMC1 on CAFs bind CD44 on T cells.

Spatial mapping reveals heterogeneity in BCC

Spatial localization of cells within the TME is generally associated with their function. To explore the spatial context of C01_TNC CAFs, we mapped our single-cell data onto ST sections from six BCC specimens using CellTrek (Fig. 7A). We found that C01_TNC CAFs were predominantly localized at the tumor border (Fig. 7B). Additionally, collagen ligands produced by these CAFs showed a high degree of co-localization with their corresponding integrin receptors in the spatial maps. COL1A2 from C01_TNC CAFs co-localized with ITGA2 and ITGB1 (Fig. 7C; Figure S3A-B; Figure S4A). This spatial co-localization underscores a close interaction between C01_TNC CAFs and tumor cells, potentially promoting tumor progression.

We next validated the presence and distribution of C01_TNC CAFs in human BCC tissues using mIHC. Consistent with our spatial transcriptomic data, C01_TNC CAFs were observed in proximity to tumor nests. Remarkably, COL1A2 and ITGα2β1 showed strong co-localization in these tissues (Fig. 7D).

Spatial mapping of immune and stromal components further revealed that regions enriched in C01_TNC CAFs had reduced infiltration of effector NKT and CD4+ TEM cells into the tumor core, compared to regions with fewer fibroblasts. This suggests that C01_TNC CAF-rich regions may form a physical and/or functional barrier limiting effector T cell entry (Fig. 8A; Figure S5A-B). Moreover, C01_TNC CAFs were spatially co-localized with Tregs in the tumor microenvironment. We observed, for instance, that MIF produced by C01_TNC CAFs co-localized with its receptors (CD74 and CD44) on nearby cells, and LAMB1 from CAFs co-localized with CD44, corroborating our mIHC results (Fig. 8B-C; Figure S4B; Figure S5C). Collectively, these observations indicate the formation of a complex interaction network between C01_TNC CAFs, tumor cells, and Treg cells, which likely establishes an immunosuppressive microenvironment and promotes BCC progression.

Discussion

Localized BCC is generally considered an “easy-to-treat” lesion [6]. However, locally advanced or metastatic BCC often requires more complex therapeutic interventions. The advent of HHIs and immunotherapies has dramatically altered the management of BCC that is not amenable to local treatments [36, 37]. Nonetheless, a substantial proportion of patients fail to respond to these therapies or eventually develop resistance [37]. Consequently, current systemic treatments are unlikely to provide a durable solution for advanced BCC. The selection of subsequent treatment regimens remains an open question that requires further investigation. In this study, we performed single-cell and ST analyses, as well as mIHC staining, on BCC tumors and adjacent normal tissues from six patients. Our findings provide insight into the TME of BCC, including the identification of distinct fibroblast subpopulations at single-cell resolution. Moreover, our data suggest that interactions between the C01_TNC CAF subset and Treg cells, as well as between C01_TNC CAFs and tumor cells, play a crucial role in fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports tumor progression.

CAFs, which are the most abundant cells in the tumor stroma, play critical roles in helping tumors evade the immune system, progress, metastasize, and develop resistance to therapy [38]. They promote these malignant processes through multiple mechanisms: secreting growth factors and chemokines, remodeling the ECM, altering tumor metabolism, interacting with other cell types, and inducing angiogenesis [39, 40]. Therefore, CAFs have emerged as potential targets for cancer therapy. In our BCC samples, we identified five distinct fibroblast subsets and systematically characterized their heterogeneity and functional properties within the TME. Compared to normal skin, the C01_TNC CAF subset was more abundant in tumor samples and exhibited higher expression of ECM-related genes (TNC, MMP1, MMP11) along with inflammatory genes (CXCL1, CXCL2). Notably, C01_TNC CAFs were predominantly linked to biological processes involved in ECM remodeling and invasiveness, such as extracellular matrix organization, angiogenesis, Wnt signaling, and hypoxia response. We also identified several top regulons active in C01_TNC CAFs, including BACH1, FOSB, CREB3L2, and NFIL3. Previous studies indicate that BACH1 is overexpressed in a variety of tumors and promotes malignant phenotypes such as increased invasion, metastasis, and proliferation [30]. Specifically, BACH1 has been shown to upregulate collagen and fibronectin expression, thereby modulating ECM remodeling to promote tumor invasion [41].

ST revealed that C01_TNC CAFs were predominantly enriched at the tumor periphery in BCC samples, a finding we confirmed with mIHC staining. Interestingly, regions with high densities of C01_TNC CAFs showed reduced infiltration of effector NKT cells, suggesting that these fibroblasts might form a physical or functional barrier to immune cell entry. Together, these findings highlight a central role for C01_TNC CAFs in shaping a tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive TME.

In our cell-cell communication analysis, C01_TNC CAFs exhibited the highest level of interaction with tumor KC compared to other fibroblast subsets. Specifically, we identified a novel crosstalk between BCC tumor cells and CAFs mediated by type I and VI collagens (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL6A1, COL6A2, COL6A3) interacting with corresponding integrin receptors (ITGα2β1, ITGα3β1, ITGα5β8). Integrins serve as key molecular links between cells and the ECM and also act as mediators of cell-cell interactions [32]. In addition, integrins are involved in tumor cell invasion, proliferation, and migration [32]. Furthermore, ITGα5β1 binding to fibronectin has been reported to promote BCC cell migration [42]. Consequently, collagen-integrin interactions represent a potential target for therapeutic intervention in BCC.

Recent studies have shown that both laminin family proteins and macrophage MIF are overexpressed in many cancers [43, 44]. For example, LAMC2 can promote the migration of triple-negative breast cancer cells by directly binding to CD44 on the cell surface [45]. MIF has also been implicated in fostering a tumor-supportive microenvironment that promotes cancer growth and metastasis [43]. Moreover, a recent study by Lee et al. identified elevated levels of MIF and its receptor CD74 in cancer tissues, suggesting that the MIF-CD74/CD44 axis could serve as a biomarker and therapeutic target [46]. However, the role of this ligand-receptor pair in the immune microenvironment of BCC remains unclear.

Our data suggest that C01_TNC CAFs engage in crosstalk with Tregs via MIF and laminin signaling. In this model, MIF secreted by C01_TNC CAFs can bind to the CD74/CD44 receptor complex on Tregs, while laminin can bind to CD44 on Tregs. Intriguingly, C01_TNC CAFs appear to create an immune barrier within the tumor, effectively excluding effector NKT cells and CD4+ TEM cells from the tumor core. This immunosuppressive microenvironment likely limits lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor, thereby potentially diminishing the efficacy of PD-1 blockade therapy. Although our spatial transcriptomic data suggest that C01_TNC CAFs colocalize with Tregs, potentially via MIF-CD74/CD44 interactions, this mechanism remains speculative without functional validation. Future studies should test whether disrupting these pathways can reverse the immunosuppressive state.

Our study has several limitations. First, although we identified and characterized fibroblast subpopulations in BCC, their functional roles still need to be verified in vitro and in vivo. Second, our sample size (n = 6) is small and may not capture the full heterogeneity of BCC; larger cohorts will be needed to confirm and extend these findings. Most importantly, the mechanisms we propose—such as collagen-integrin-mediated tumor progression and MIF/CD44-driven immunosuppression—are speculative, being derived from multi-omics data, and will require direct experimental validation. Future studies could use blocking antibodies or inhibitors targeting the collagen–integrin and MIF–CD74/CD44 pathways, or co-culture models of C01_TNC fibroblasts with tumor cells or Tregs, to directly test the causal role of these interactions in tumor progression and immune suppression.

In summary, our study provides insights into the BCC tumor microenvironment and highlights how the C01_TNC CAF subset is associated with tumor progression and immunosuppression. Targeting these CAF-mediated interactions presents a potential avenue for future therapeutic development.
