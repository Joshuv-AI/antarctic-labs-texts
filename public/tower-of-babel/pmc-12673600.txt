Multispin Photoexcited State and Generation of Dynamic Electron Spin Polarization Observed in Anthracene–Radical-Linked Systems with Extended π‑Conjugated Spin Coupler
Authors: Yoshio Teki, Akihiro Shimizu, Nobutoshi Kimura, Ken Kato, Akitaka Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding multispin photoexcited systems and their excited-state dynamics is crucial for modern technological applications of organic radicals and related spin systems. In this study, the multispin photoexcited states and excited-state dynamics of anthracene–radical-linked π-conjugated spin systems (OV2 and IN2) with an extended π-conjugated spin coupler were investigated using time-resolved electron spin resonance (TRESR), pulse ESR, and transient absorption measurements. The results were compared to those of a system with a phenylene spin-coupler (OV1). Solution- and solid-phase TRESR measurements revealed the occurrence of electron spin polarization transfer (ESPT) from the doublet photoexcited state (D; trip-doublet state) to the doublet ground state (DG state) in OV2 and IN2. The solid-phase TRESR measurements identified a doublet–quartet (D–Q) mixed state (Q; trip-quartet state) in OV2 and IN2 in addition to the spin-polarized DG state, and ESPT from the photoexcited state to the DG state was directly observed as a time-dependent resonance field shift of the central emissive peak (g ≈ 2.0). Pulse ESR experiments showed that the decay time of dynamic electron spin polarization (DEP) and the characteristic time of DEP generation strongly depended on the expected magnitude of the intramolecular exchange coupling between the triplet-state chromophore and the radical substituent. This finding supports that DEP generation in the examined π-radical systems proceeds predominantly via radical spin–orbit borrowing-enhanced intersystem crossing.

Introduction

The potential of multispin photoexcited systems has expanded to include modern technological applications. , The generation of dynamic electron spin polarization (DEP) in photoexcited states is important for quantum information science, − and its subsequent transfer to the ground state is expected to have applications in high-sensitivity magnetic resonance imaging. , Corvaja et al. reported the multispin photoexcited states of the chromophore–radical-linked systems (C60–nit), in which C60 is σ-bonded to a nitroxide radical (nit). This research field has been developed by several research groups, leading to the discovery of the multispin photoexcited states in a metal (Zn)–porphyrin–radical coordination complex (ZnTPP-nitpy), phthalocyaninatosilicon (SiPC)–radical covalently linked compound (SiPC-TEMPO), , π-conjugated spin systems constructed from anthracene and stable radicals, ,− donor–acceptor–radical-linked three-spin systems, − and others. , Although the existence of the quartet (Q; trip-quartet) states formed by an exchange coupling between the triplet excited state and stable radical was predicted by the radical–triplet pair mechanism (RTPM) , of DEP generation in solution, direct observation of the Q states was reported in the various types of triplet–radical exchange–coupled systems by the above-mentioned studies. Recently, various photogenerated multispin molecular systems have been intensively investigated as molecular spin-qubit model compounds for quantum computing ,,− and spin-optical interface devices.

In chromophore–radical-linked systems, the Q and D (trip-doublet) states are generated after intersystem crossing (ISC) of the chromophore. When the exchange interaction (J) between the radical substituent and triplet-state chromophore is much stronger than the fine structure and Zeeman interactions, pure D and Q states are generated. If J is comparable to or smaller than the fine-structure interaction, then spin-state mixing between the D and Q states (D–Q mixing) is induced by the fine-structure interaction, leading to a D–Q mixed state. The excited-state dynamics are expected to change significantly with D–Q mixing. This spin-state mixing is thought to be involved in the DEP generation of the doublet ground state (DG state), − ,,,,,,,− the reverse quartet mechanism, and the acceleration of ISC. Although DEP generation and electron spin polarization transfer (ESPT) to the ground state have been observed in several systems, it is difficult to say that the specific mechanisms have been sufficiently clarified. Because DEP generation in the ground state is critically important for future technologies, further studies on the DEP generation in the multispin photoexcited states and its ESPT to the ground state are important.

Various ISC Mechanisms

Figure shows the various ISC mechanisms expected in the multispin photoexcited states of chromophore–radical-linked spin systems. Hereafter, “n-ISC” is used to denote the normal ISC due to the spin–orbit coupling (SOC) in the chromophore. The SOC is known to generate occupations in the zero-field (B = 0) spin sublevels, leading to zero-field DEP. In our previous study, based on the following experimental results, we proposed an additional ISC mechanism, in which the relatively strong SOC of radical substituents is borrowed via π-conjugation. (1) Pure quintet and quartet time-resolved electron spin resonance (TRESR) spectra with zero-field DEP were clearly observed, when π-radical substituent(s) were linked to diphenylanthracene and phenylanthracene chromophores via π-conjugation. (2) The ISC efficiency in diphenylanthracene itself was known to be significantly low. (3) The DEP patterns of the diphenylanthracene–biradical and phenylanthracene–radical-linked systems were inverted compared to the DEP pattern of the triplet-state anthracene chromophore (Figure S32 in Supporting Information (SI)). These findings indicate that the ISC is enhanced by a π-radical substituent under negligible D–Q mixing. In addition, the DEP appears to be SOC-driven, but the DEP patterns cannot be explained by the SOC of the chromophore itself. Hereafter, we refer to this mechanism as “radical SOC borrowing-enhanced ISC (RSO-EISC)”. RSO-EISC selectively populates the zero-field spin sublevels of the excited Q state with M S ′ = ±1/2 (the corresponding wave function is denoted as |Q ±1/2 ′⟩ in the following), because it is also SOC-driven. Here, the prime (′) denotes the zero-field spin sublevels. In addition, chromophore–radical-linked spin systems are expected to exhibit conventional enhanced ISC (EISC) , owing to the electron-exchange integral between the unpaired electron in the radical substituent and two unpaired electrons generated by the one-electron photoexcitation from highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) to lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the chromophore. The roles of each exchange interaction between the radical spin and two unpaired electrons in the excited-state chromophore during EISC and that of the wave function mixing to net DEP generation were clarified by Kirk and Shultz et al. , EISC mechanism selectively populates the excited D state because of the angular-momentum conservation law (EISC is not SOC-driven). However, it is unclear whether the DEP generation by EISC occurs in the spin sublevels under an external magnetic field (B ≠ 0) (|D ±1/2⟩) or in zero-field spin sublevels (|D′±1/2⟩), because this mechanism is not caused by SOC. Therefore, for the DEP generation by the conventional EISC, we treat both cases as EISC1 and EISC2. Following the angular-momentum conservation law, the dominant spin-allowed pathway to the ground state is expected to originate from the D state (|D ±1/2⟩). Thus, the n-ISC and RSO-EISC mechanisms can be distinguished from EISC1 based on the TRESR spectral pattern (zero-field or high-field DEP). The n-ISC mechanism leads to both |Q ±1/2 ′⟩ and |Q ±3/2 ′⟩ with zero-field DEP, but in the case of an anthracene chromophore, the pathway to |Q ±3/2 ′⟩ is dominant. Therefore, it is possible to determine which of the n-ISC and RSO-EISC is dominant. EISC2 can also be distinguished based on its TRESR spectral pattern.

Multispin Systems Studied in This Work

The π-radical systems studied herein were constructed from a triplet excited chromophore (anthracene moiety), a π-conjugated spin coupler, and a radical substituent (Figure (a)). The expected relationships of the several spin states in the excited states are depicted in Figure (b). An extended π-conjugated linker (1,2-diphenylacetylene moiety) was used in OV2 and IN2, instead of replacing the phenylene linker in OV1. The photoexcited states and excited-state dynamics of these π-radicals were investigated by TRESR, pulse ESR, and transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy, as well as molecular-orbital calculations. Although we previously reported the TRESR study on the Q state of OV1, more detailed studies of the excited-state dynamics, including pulse ESR experiments, were performed for comparison with OV2 and IN2. D–Q mixing in the photoexcited states of π-radical systems was first observed for OV2 and IN2. For both in solution and in solid phases of OV2 and IN2, we observed characteristic TRESR spectra and excited-state dynamics due to D–Q mixing, as well as the spin-polarized DG-state) generated by rapid ESPT from the D–Q mixed multispin photoexcited states. In many systems reported to exhibit DEP, only polarized multispin photoexcited states have been detected, and the polarized DG state has not been observed by solid-phase TRESR, or vice versa. In contrast, the present systems provide a rare example in which the polarized multispin photoexcited states and polarized DG states are simultaneously observed by solid-phase TRESR. Furthermore, the ESPT from their excited state to the ground state in the solid phase was directly detected by TRESR. By comparing the results for OV2 and IN2 to those for OV1, which shows a pure Q state with negligible D–Q mixing, we clarified the role of D–Q mixing in the DEP generation and the characteristic excited-state dynamics of the photoexcited multispin systems: specifically, the mixed state led to the ESPT to the DG state.

Results and Discussion

Estimation of J

The J between the radical substituent and the triplet excited state of the chromophore moiety was calculated. The results are presented in section 6 in SI. The magnitude of J substantially decreased in the order OV1 ≫ OV2 > IN2. However, the calculated value was still overestimated even for IN2, which was too large for the quantum mixing between the D and Q states.

Synthesis and Characterization

OV2 and IN2 were synthesized from anthracene-9-ylboronic acid via the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling followed by the Sonogashira coupling, a condensation reaction, and oxidation (see SI for details). The formation of radical species was confirmed by ESR spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HRMS) for both compounds and elemental analysis for OV2. For OV1, the basic data, including the synthesis, were already published in our previous work.

UV–Vis and Conventional ESR Spectra

The main data for this study were obtained using the radical species diluted in poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA). To compare data in environments that are as similar as possible for various measurements, we used PMMA as a matrix. Their solution-phase spectra of these radicals acquired at room temperature are shown in sections 3 and 4.1 of SI. The radical species were identified based on solution-phase ESR spectra. The presence of pronounced vibronic bands (Figure (a)) and hyperfine splitting (Figure (b)) indicated that each radical species is sufficiently diluted in the PMMA matrix.

TRESR Spectroscopy

TRESR measurements were performed to characterize the photoexcited states and their dynamics. Figure (a–c) shows typical solution-phase TRESR spectra of OV1, OV2, and IN2 measured in toluene-d 8 at room temperature. Although deuterated toluene was used, the reaction at the radical sites in the activated molecules in the photoexcited state, which extracts hydrogen atoms from the solvent, was not completely suppressed. Therefore, intensive accumulation of the measurement data in solutions was difficult, leading to relatively low signal-to-noise ratios for the spectra shown in Figure . The photoexcited states of OV1, which feature an exchange coupling considerably stronger than the Zeeman and fine-structure interactions, are expected to have very short lifetimes in solution. , Therefore, the weak TRESR signal in Figure (a) may originate from the RTPM based on the collision of the ground-state radical species with the triplet excited state of the nonradical compound formed by the hydrogen abstraction reaction. A broad signal with microwave emission (e) was clearly observed in the solution of OV2 at 0.20 μs (Figure (b)). In the exchange–coupled spin systems of the triplet-state chromophore and doublet-state radical species, the g values of the D and Q states are given by 1 g(D)=(4/3)gT−(1/3)gR and 2 g(Q)=(2/3)gT+(1/3)gR Using g T = 2.0030 and g R = 2.0040 (verdazyl radical), g(D) and g(Q) of OV2 were calculated as 2.0027 and 2.0033, respectively, corresponding to the resonance fields of 336.50 and 336.39 mT, respectively. The resonance field of the DG state of OV2 (g R = 2.0040) was calculated as 336.27 mT, and that of the decomposed triplet state of OV2 (g T = 2.0030) as 336.44 mT. Based on the peak resonance field position, the signal at 0.20 μs (peak position: 336.8 mT) was assigned to the D state of OV2, and the signal at 0.60 μs (central-peak position: 336.26 mT) was attributed to the DG state (Figure (b)). The signal at 0.35 μs is the superposition of the D- and DG-state signals. Similarly, using g T = 2.0030 and g R = 2.0060 (iminonitroxide radical), g(D) and g(Q) of IN2 were calculated as 2.0020 and 2.0040, respectively, corresponding to resonance fields of 336.55 and 336.21 mT, respectively. The resonance field of the DG-state signal of IN2 (g R = 2.0060) is 335.87 mT, and that of the decomposed triplet state (g T = 2.0030) is 336.38 mT. Therefore, the broad signal at 0.20 μs (peak position: 336.7 mT) was assigned to the D state of IN2, and the seven sharp signals due to the hyperfine splitting at 0.60 μs (central-peak position: 335.93 mT) were attributed to the DG state of IN2 (Figure (c)). As shown later, quantum mixing between the D and Q states (D–Q mixing) occurred in the solid phase for OV2 and IN2 by the fine-structure term and not for OV1 with a large J. The time-averaging of the fine-structure term by molecular rotation in the solution reduces the effective magnitude, leading to the suppression of the D–Q mixing, because the exchange interaction becomes much larger than the time-averaged fine-structure interaction. Therefore, the observed solution TRESR spectra of the photoexcited states of OV2 and IN2 were assigned to the D state with the characteristic g value (g(D)) of the pure doublet excited state. Figure (d) shows the time profile of the signals of IN2 at 336.258 mT (dominantly the DG-state signal with an overlap with the D-state signal) and that at 336.506 mT (D-state signal). As the D-state signal decayed, the DG-state signal appeared. This is direct evidence of ESPT from the D state to the DG state. In contrast, in ZnTPP-nitpy, and SiPC-TEMPO system, , only the Q and DG states were detected by the solution TRESR measurements, and the DEP generation in these states was explained by the ESPT from the Q state to the DG state and a radical–quartet pair mechanism. , The present observation of our π-radicals (evidence of the ESPT from the D-state to the DG-state) differs from the results obtained for the SiPc-NO system, etc. Herein, the ESPT from the D state to the DG state was directly detected as the time variation of the emissive DEP of OV2 and IN2. The DEP generation for the D state in solution possibly originated from the residual effect of D–Q mixing in the early stage before the complete time-averaging of the fine-structure term or the residual fine structure due to incomplete averaging. In addition, encounters (collisions) between the ground-state radical species and photoexcited states (D and Q) can be reasonably expected in solution, and ESPT from the Q state to the DG state may also occur through an indirect pathway via the D state. In this case, the DEP of the D state in solution is due to an indirect pathway from the Q state spin-polarized via the radical–quartet pair mechanism, although the Q state with DEP was not detected in this work (possibly because of the broadening of the Q-state signal by residual fine-structure splitting). In the case of DEP for the DG state observed for the solution TRESR spectra of OV2 and IN2, it is not possible to rule out the contribution from RTPM via collisions between the ground-state radical species and triplet excited state of the nonradical compound formed by the hydrogen abstraction reaction. However, the observation of the D-state signal with DEP supports the possibility of the above-mentioned two mechanisms. There are usually no off-diagonal terms between the D and Q states, because their states are energy-different eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, including Zeeman, fine-structure, and exchange coupling terms. However, the modulation in J value and fine-structure tensor due to the vibrational conformation fluctuations can cause the time-dependent off-diagonal fluctuations between the D and Q states. Recently, the contribution of the vibrational fluctuations to singlet fission was clarified. − Although the conformation of the π-conjugated spin systems is more rigid than that of the single σ-bonded systems or coordination complexes, a contribution to the DEP generation in the D and Q states induced by the conformational fluctuation cannot be completely ruled out in solution, especially for OV2 and IN2 with small J values. ,, For the DEP generation mechanism, a D–Q conversion proportional to the matrix element of the dipolar coupling between the D and Q states after EISC1 is also proposed. Since this mechanism is also thought to be induced by structural fluctuations like J-fluctuation, the possibility cannot be excluded in solution.

The spectral features of each radical species in PMMA were identical to those in the frozen glass matrix. The following changes in spectral features should be noted: The XY peak interval of OV2 and IN2 was smaller than that of OV1, as expected for species with an extended linker because of the decrease in the dipolar interaction between the triplet excited-state moiety and radical substituent due to a distance longer than that in OV1. In contrast, the Z-peak intervals showed almost no change for these species (Figure S20, the expanded version of Figure (a)). As discussed later, these spectral features of OV2 and IN2 can be reproduced by D–Q mixing, which occurs when the fine-structure interaction is comparable with or larger than the exchange interaction (J). In addition to the wing signal peaks assigned to the Z and XY transitions, central peaks (g ≈ 2.0) corresponding to the microwave emission (e) were observed. The central-peak intensity relatively increased in the order of OV1 < OV2 < IN2, i.e., in the order of decreasing the expected magnitude of J. Furthermore, the central peaks of OV2 and IN2 shifted slightly toward the low magnetic field region over time (Figures S17 and S19). The central peaks of OV2 and IN2 were not reproduced in the spectral simulation of the multiplet TRESR but were reproduced by the superposition of the spin-polarized DG signal, as shown later. In contrast, the TRESR spectrum of OV1 was well reproduced by the spectral simulation of the pure Q state (Figure S31), as reported in our previous work. The central peak of OV1 was not the superposition of the DG-state signal, and it was assigned to an off-axis extra-line (dominant) with the overlap of the weak canonical XYZ peaks due to the |Q –1/2⟩ ↔ |Q +1/2⟩ transition of the Q state. , The TRESR spectrum of OV1 and those of our other previously reported π-radicals constructed from a triplet excited-state chromophore and radical substituent were well reproduced by the spectral simulation with the selective population (DEP) of the zero-field spin sublevels of M S ′ = ±1/2 in the pure Q state (|Q′±1/2⟩) (Figure S31). This selective DEP cannot be explained by conventional n-ISC originating from the SOC of the chromophore itself because of the microwave absorption/emission (a/e) pattern being opposite to the TRESR of the chromophore itself (Figure S32). Thus, the observed DEP pattern was ascribed to the DEP induced by the RSO-EISC mechanism. In addition, the conventional EISC mechanism, which selectively populates the D state according to the angular-momentum conservation law, is expected to occur in the chromophore–radical-linked systems. The signal intensity of TRESR is proportional to the difference between the occupation numbers of the sublevels. When J is large, D–Q mixing can be neglected, and the population of the D state by EISC does not contribute to the TRESR spectrum of the Q state. Furthermore, when D–Q mixing is neglected, the sublevel difference of EISC for the D state can be neglected. Therefore, in the case of a large J (e.g., for OV1), the population of the D state does not contribute to the TRESR spectrum. In the later section, we will discuss in more detail the RSO-EISC and the conventional EISC , expected in the chromophore–radical-linked multispin photoexcited states.

Pulse ESR Spectroscopy

Pulse ESR experiments were performed to probe the excited-state dynamics. Figure shows the time profiles of DEP generation and decay in OV1 (blue), OV2 (red), and IN2 (black). The generation and decay rates decreased in the order OV1, OV2, and IN2; that is, the characteristic time constants increased in this order, which is the expected order of the decrease in J. This finding strongly supports that DEP generation in our π-radical systems is dominantly due to the RSO-EISC mechanism and not n-ISC mechanism based on the SOC of the chromophore (anthracene moiety) itself. In our previous work, we proposed a possible mechanism of the RSO-EISC. Dn (→ D3(CT) or D4(CT)) ⇒ Q2(CT) → Q (Figure and section 10 in SI for comparison to our original proposal in 2001). Here, CT and Q2 mean the charge-transfer state and nπ* quartet state (“n” denotes the nonbonding lone-pair orbital). The transitions denoted by → and ⇒ involve intramolecular electron transfer (ET) and ISC based on the SOC at the radical substituent, respectively. CS and CR represent the charge separation and charge recombination, respectively. The order of the DEP generation rate (OV1 ≫ OV2 > IN2) can be understood by this mechanism. As the spin-coupler becomes longer, the characteristic time (τET) required for ET between the anthracene moiety and the radical substituent increases, leading to τEΤ(OV1) ≪ τEΤ(OV2) ∼ τEΤ(IN2). The spin-coupler with the butoxy groups in IN2 decelerates the ET process due to its weaker π-overlap arising from steric hindrance. Furthermore, since the OV substituent has two n orbitals, the characteristic time of the ISC by SOC (τRSO) at the OV substituent is expected to be shorter than that of IN, leading to τ­(OV1) (τEΤ(OV1) + τRSO(OV1)) ≪ τ­(OV2) < τ­(IN2). The observed DEP generation of OV2 and IN2 is μs time scale, which is much slower than the typical lifetimes of singlet excited states of anthracene derivatives (e.g., 6.5 ns for 9-phenylanthracene at room temperature). This finding is reasonably understandable when the ET­(CS) process is significantly faster than the ET­(CR) process. Since the CR process is usually slower by 1–3 orders of magnitude than that of the CS process, and this event occurs at low temperatures in solid phase, τEΤ(CR) in a cooled-down derivative is expected to be slow, which may reach near to μs region as the spin-coupler length becomes longer. Furthermore, ISC enhancement is governed by ET­(CS) (→ D3(CT) or D4(CT)) and ISC­(R) (⇒ Q2(CT)). In the ISC enhancement based on the proposed mechanism, the rate of transport of the hole from the HOMO to the adjacent phenylene unit is important. Although τEΤ increases with the spin-coupler length, ET rate per unit length (equivalent to the hole transport rate per unit length) does not change drastically. Consequently, unlike J, the enhancement factor also does not change drastically. We consider that this DEP generation dependence cannot be explained by mechanisms such as n-ISC and so on. In the covalently linked SiPC–radical systems, the spin-coupler dependence was reported only for the decay time. This finding can be understood as intramolecular ET occurring via the π-conjugation. The fast ET­(CS) is reasonable owing to the nature of the intramolecular ET through the π-skeleton. The spin-coupler dependence of the decay time was ascribed to the fact that the decay was due to the conventional EISC mechanism transferring to the ground state. Similarly, the dependence of the excited-state decay in the present systems can be understood using the EISC mechanism on the ground state, because the decay occurs dominantly from the D-state component in the D–Q mixed state, according to the spin-allowed nature of this transition. The phase memory time (T M) and spin–lattice relaxation time (T 1) were also estimated using pulse ESR spectroscopy (Figures S21–S26). Their T M of the photoexcited states did not substantially differ among the radical species (within a factor of 2) but were 2–5 times shorter than those of the ground state. These short T M values suggest that the influence of the lifetime decay of the photoexcited states was included. Results of Pulsed ESR Measurements

To obtain more information on the spin state and quantum mixing, the XY peaks of the photoexcited multiplet states and ground-state signals with g ≈ 2.0 were examined using spin–echo-detected electron spin transient nutation (TN) spectroscopy , (Figures S27–S29). For the Z-peak signals of the photoexcited states, these measurements were difficult because of their weak peak intensities, and for the central peak at g ≈ 2.0, information on the photoexcited state was not obtained because of the overlap with the strong ground-state signal. Under the condition of ω1 ≪ ω ZFS , the nutation frequency (ω TN ) of the M S ↔ M S ′ transition is given by ωTN=S(S+1)−MSMS′ω1 , where ω1 = gμ B B 1/ℏ, ωZFS = D/ℏ, and B 1 is the microwave-field strength. , For all three species, the ratios of the nutation frequency of their XY transitions in the photoexcited state to that of the DG state (ω G = ω1), ωTN/ωG, were close to 1.73, which is the value expected for the pure Q state (S = 3/2), although the solid-phase TRESR spectra suggested the occurrence of D–Q mixing for OV2 and IN2. However, as shown below, this finding does not contradict to the results of TRESR experiments, which clearly showed the resonance field shift of the Z transitions due to D–Q mixing. As shown in Table S2 of the SI, we calculated the ratios obtained from the expectation values of S and M S for each transition and those also from the transition moment, which were close to 1.7 for the wing XY and wing Z transitions, even when J was comparable with the fine-structure interaction. Furthermore, the nutation frequency of the X, Y, and Z transitions near the center of the spectrum became close to 2, which is the ratio expected for the M S = −1/2 ↔ +1/2 transitions of the pure Q state (S = 3/2). These findings indicate that the nutation frequency was less sensitive to D–Q mixing than the fine-structure splitting pattern of the spectrum (resonance field positions). This phenomenon occurs because, in the zeroth-order approximation, the effect of the D–Q mixing on the resonance field is a function of the coefficient |C 1|, whereas the transition intensity is affected by |C 1|2 (see section 9 in the SI). In the case of OV2 with C 1 = 0.221, the effect of D–Q mixing on the nutation frequency is less than 5%. Thus, the spectral simulation approach is a more powerful tool than transient nutation spectroscopy for characterizing D–Q mixing.

TA Spectroscopy

To estimate the lifetimes of the photoexcited states, we carried out TA measurements at 90 K using PMMA. Figures and S30 show the typical TA spectra of OV2 and IN2, respectively. Although low-temperature TA measurements were difficult to perform for PMMA because of light scattering, we succeeded in determining the lifetimes of the photoexcited states. In our previous study, the TA decay profile of OV1 in a frozen glass matrix (2-MTHF) was better fitted by biexponential decay than by monoexponential decay. Reliable TA data for OV1 in PMMA at 90 K could not be obtained in our apparatus, because of the short lifetime of this species and weak intensity. For OV2 and IN2, no substantial difference between the mono- and biexponential fittings of the TA decay profiles was observed. However, in the D–Q mixed states (as confirmed by TRESR spectroscopy), the decay is expected to be monoexponential, because it is not possible to clearly distinguish between the D and Q states. Therefore, we estimated the excited-state lifetimes of OV2 and IN2 by assuming monoexponential decay. In addition, the intensity rise of the TA signal due to the ISC of the chromophore (n-ISC, RSO-EISC, and EISC) was also analyzed using a monoexponential function. The lifetimes of OV2 and IN2 were estimated as 2.3 ± 0.1 and 1.7 ± 0.4 μs, respectively, using biexponential time profile fitting as well as Figure . The lifetime of OV2 was close to the DEP decay time determined by the pulse ESR experiments, and that of IN2 estimated from the TA decay curve was approximately half of the DEP decay. However, the DEP decay time, including the lifetime decay, should be shorter than the lifetime itself. This discrepancy observed for IN2 could be due to the poor quality of the corresponding TA data. Therefore, it can be mentioned that the DEP decay observed by using pulse or time-resolved ESR measurements was predominantly ascribed to the lifetime decay of the photoexcited states. Based on the rising profile of the TA signal, the ISC rates of OV2 and IN2 were estimated as k ISC = 5.5 ± 0.4 × 106 s –1 (τISC = 184 ± 13 ns) and 2.9 ± 0.1 × 106 s –1 (τISC = 346 ± 14 ns), respectively, assuming a small nonradiative decay to the ground state (the radiative decay to the ground state could be neglected because of the negligible fluorescence). Thus, τ ISC increases in the order of OV2 < IN2, which is the order expected for the decrease in J. The times at which the TA signal intensity reaches a maximum are 0.99 and 1.06 μs for OV2 and IN2, respectively, which also follow the expected order based on their J. Additionally, the rate of ISC to the triplet state of the chromophore apparently exceeds the DEP generation rate of OV2 or IN2 itself. This finding suggests that indirect pathways are involved in DEP generation in OV1, OV2, and IN2. Specifically, mechanisms such as from higher-energy doublet or quartet state such as the CT states (D2(CT) or Q2(CT) in Figure ) discussed already, followed by the transition to |Q′±1/2⟩ by ESPT or by ET, rapid EISC2 to |D′±1/2⟩ or the decoherence process in the D–Q mixed state, etc., are considered likely. For example, in the case of the possible RSO-EISC mechanism proposed in our previous work, D state can be generated both by EISC from Dn state and ET­(CR) from D2(CT) state, which is constructed from the triplet nπ* state of the radical moiety and the unpaired spin of LUMO of the anthracene moiety. Q state can be generated by ET­(CR) from the Q2 state. When the EISC is faster than ET­(CR), the delay of DEP generation can be expected compared to the rising of the TA signal (Figures and ).

DEP Generation via Various ISC Mechanisms

In this section, we discuss DEP generation in the multispin photoexcited states of the chromophore–radical-linked π-conjugated spin system, with the ISC mechanisms expected to be operational in these systems shown in Figure . The dominant spin-allowed pathway to the ground state is expected to originate from the D state (|D ±1/2⟩) because of the spin conservation nature. In OV2 and IN2, the D–Q mixing was confirmed by TRESR spectroscopy. Therefore, although it is difficult to strictly distinguish between the D and Q states, we discuss them hereafter based on their occupancy states and treat them as different states. The state selectively occupied by only the zero-field |Q′±1/2⟩ component of the D–Q mixed state was denoted as the zero-field spin-sublevel populated quasi-Q state (q-Qzf). Similarly, the states selectively occupied by only the zero-field |D′±1/2⟩ component or |D ± 1/2⟩ component under a magnetic field were denoted as the zero-field spin-sublevel populated quasi-D state (q-Dzf) or the quasi-D state under a magnetic field (q-Df), respectively. The n-ISC mechanism is expected to give the opposite a/e pattern of the TRESR spectra of q-Qzf because of the dominant population of the zero-field T X and T Y sublevels of the triplet state of the anthracene chromophore (Figure S32). Although competition between the RSO-EISC and n-ISC mechanisms may occur, the former mechanism was predominant in the observed TRESR spectra. Therefore, the following discussion focuses on the TRESR spectra caused by the RSO-EISC and EISC mechanisms. The solid-phase TRESR spectral patterns of each quasi-D and quasi-Q states under a magnetic field were simulated using the eigen field/exact diagonalization hybrid method for each state with populations (DEP) generated using the projection operator (Λ) corresponding to each mechanism. The detailed procedure of the spectral simulation is provided in section 7 of the SI.

The TRESR spectra of the D–Q mixed state could be obtained by simply superimposing the TRESR spectra of the quasi-D and quasi-Q states according to their mixing weights. The representative J dependences of the TRESR spectra of q-Qzf, q-Df, and q-Dzf states are given in Figures S33, S37, and S38, respectively. The TRESR spectra of OV2 and IN2, except for the central emission peak, and that of OV1 were well simulated by assuming only the RSO-EISC mechanism (Figures , S34–S35, and S31). Thus, the observed TRESR spectra of OV2 and IN2 exhibited the characteristic of the q-Qzf state except for the relatively strong central emission peak. As shown in Figure , the central peaks at late times (1.45 μs for OV2 and 2.3 μs for IN2) were dominated by the spin-polarized DG state. Simply thinking about it, one can assume that the spin-polarized quasi-D state was generated first and then experienced rapid ESPT affording the DG state. For the rapid ESPT mechanism, spin-selective decay (k DG in Figure ) from the D-state component by internal conversion (EISC1 to DG) is the most reasonable pathway. The results of the solution-phase TRESR measurements indicate the presence of spin-polarized D states (not quasi-D states). In addition, in the region of J/h = 107 – 109 Hz, both the q-Dzf and q-Df states give rise to a peak near the center (g ≈ 2) of the solid-phase TRESR spectrum. For J/h ≈ 109 Hz, the q-Dzf state gives rise to a central emissive peak that is stronger than that derived from the q-Df state (Figures S39 and S40). The difference between the q-Dzf (by EISC2) and q-Df (by EISC1) states is owing to numerical elimination of the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix in the zero-field basis or the eigen field basis (corresponding to dephasing of quantum coherence), after selective occupation of the D state component using the projection operator (Λ D ). Thus, in any case, the DEP generation of the quasi-D states (spin-polarized quasi-D states) via D–Q mixing was supported by the spectral simulations. However, RTPM in the polymer chains covalently linked to nitroxide radical was reported in solution. Therefore, a contribution of intramolecular RTPM by the J-modulation (based on vibrational conformation fluctuations) cannot be completely ruled out in solution, although the conformation for the π-conjugated spin systems is more rigid than that for the single σ-bonded systems. However, in the solid phase, structural fluctuations are expected to be suppressed. Furthermore, as shown in Figure , similar spectral patterns were observed in a frozen glass matrix and PMMA, and the J-sensitive Z peaks were observed without broadening for OV1, OV2, and IN2 in PMMA. Although large structural fluctuations immediately after excitation are generally expected events, the observed TRESR spectra could be simulated using a single J value. The DEP generation can be reasonably understood by assuming the RSO-EISC mechanism. Therefore, we consider that the effects of J-fluctuation do not persist to the current phenomenon, at least for Q-state or q-Q state signals, although the actual contribution is unknown. Thus, DEP generation of the quasi-D states via D–Q mixing is the most reasonable mechanism. However, the peak shift over time observed in the solid-phase TRESR spectra recorded in PMMA could not be simply ascribed to the ESPT from these quasi-D states, as the resonance field position of their quasi-D state obtained by the spectral simulations differed from the observed one. That is, in the q-Dzf and q-Df states, the simulated central peaks appeared at a lower field than the experimentally observed ones. Although the peak field positions of the observed spectra were ∼ 327.3 mT for OV2 at 0.15 μs and 327.7 mT for IN2 at 0.40 μs (Figures S17 and S19), those of the simulated spectra were 324.3 mT for OV2 and 324.6 mT for IN2 (Figures S39 and S40). This issue remained an open question in the present study. Possibly, the quasi-D state at an early time was not correctly reproduced, unlike the quasi-Q state. Pulse ESR data show that DEP generation is delayed after the ISC of the chromophore in OV2 and IN2. One possible explanation is that the signal at an early time comes from a higher-energy D state (with a g value smaller than g R). The most possible candidate of the higher-energy D state is D2(CT) in Figure . D2(CT) and Q2(CT) states in OV2 and IN2 are also expected to be D–Q mixed states because they are constructed from the nπ* triplet state of the radical substituent and anionic anthracene moiety. Therefore, the emissive central TRESR signal like that in Figure S39­(c) is expected by the D–Q mixing. The reason for the missing Q2(CT) signal may be due to a large zero-field splitting (large D value) or a relatively small population due to the transition to the Q state. The missing q-D-state signal can be reasonably understood by the direct decay from the D2(CT) state or the rapid decay from the q-D state to the DG state. If the D2(CT) state (or the q-D2 (CT) state) has a g value smaller than gR, the resonance field shift observed in the central peak of the solid-phase TRESR can be explained. However, for the DEP in the central peak at early time, the contribution from the intramolecular RTPM based on the impulsive large J fluctuation immediately after photoexcitation cannot be completely ruled out. Since the RTPM gives net polarization in contrast to D–Q mixing, a small amount of the contribution gives a relatively larger effect compared to D–Q mixing. Therefore, the possibility cannot be entirely ruled out that the effects of events occurring in a short period at an early time may remain. Another possibility of the mysterious quasi-D-state signal is that the initial states immediately after ISC may retain quantum coherence. The present simulations were performed for the D–Q mixed states after decoherence. Thus, it is possible that the quasi-D state was not correctly simulated in the initial period. To clarify this issue, the excited-state dynamics of the present systems, including the early stage, should be investigated in greater detail in further experimental and theoretical studies.

Finally, we discuss the various possibilities of indirect processes in the RSO-EISC mechanism. We already discussed the leading possibility from the CT state to a high-energy |Q'2±1/2⟩ followed by ET­(CR) to |Q′±1/2⟩. However, the direct evidence of the D2(CT) and Q2(CT) states, such as the characteristic TA spectra, was not obtained in the present experiments; we will discuss the other possibilities as shown below. One of the other possibilities is rapid EISC2 to |D′±1/2⟩ followed by ESPT to |Q′±1/2⟩. There are usually no off-diagonal terms between |D′±1/2⟩ and |Q′±1/2⟩ states because they are energy-different eigenstates of the zero-field spin Hamiltonian, including fine-structure and exchange coupling terms. This is true even for the doubly degenerate D–Q mixed states with different energy eigenvalues. Therefore, the above-mentioned mechanism can be ruled out when additional terms connecting their states do not exist. However, J-modulation or vibronic SOC can cause time-dependent off-diagonal terms between their states. However, the former mechanism is usually considered under an external magnetic field, ,, and therefore, is not applicable to the spin-sublevel selective zero-field ESPT from |D′±1/2⟩ to |Q′±1/2⟩. Vibronic SOC may cause nonradiative decay from |D′±1/2⟩ to |Q′±1/2⟩, although the spin-sublevel selectivity is unknown. Therefore, the possibility that vibronic coupling to the SOC (probably coupling to the SOC in the radical substituent) causes after the rapid EISC2 to |D′±1/2⟩, followed by ESPT from |D′±1/2⟩ to |Q′±1/2⟩, cannot completely rule out other candidates of RSO-EISC mechanism. In addition, the possibility of nonradiative decay from |D′±1/2⟩ to |Q′±1/2⟩ owing to SOC mixing with higher-energy Q and D states was also theoretically proposed, although the origin of the ISC was the SOC of the chromophore itself. , This kind of SOC mixing with stronger SOC of the radical substituent is also a candidate for ESPT to |Q′±1/2⟩ from |D′±1/2⟩ that is occupied by rapid EISC. Another possibility is that other double states (with higher energy than the D state and g values smaller than g R) is generated by rapid EISC or rapid internal conversion, followed by RSO-EISC to |Q′±1/2⟩. In addition, the possibility of a spectral transformation from q-Dzf to q-Qzf related to the decoherence process is relevant only for OV2 and IN2. Previously, using stochastic Liouville equations, we discussed the ESPT between different states in anthracene–radical-linked systems with functionality groups, based on electron transfer or energy transfer accompanied by decoherence. Here, we mention the possibility of spectral conversion caused by decoherence within the D–Q mixed state. Rapid EISC2 may lead to a retained zero-field D-state nature in the initial period (|ψ′±1/2⟩ C = |D′±1/2⟩ + 0|Q′±1/2⟩). By a decoherence process, |ψ′ ±1/2⟩C will change to the D–Q mixed state (|ψ′±1/2⟩DC = C 1|D′±1/2⟩ + C 2|Q′±1/2⟩). Under external magnetic field, this state changes to |ψ±1/2⟩DC = C 3|D ±1/2⟩ + C 4|Q ±1/2⟩. When the selective decay occurs from the D-state component (|D ±1/2⟩), the TRESR spectrum is similar to the q-Qzf spectrum. However, this mechanism cannot occur in OV1, because D–Q mixing is required. The delay of the DEP generation from the rising profile of the TA spectra suggests indirect pathway. Although we think that the mechanism (Dn (→ D3(CT) or D4(CT)) ⇒ Q2(CT) → Q) is the most leading candidate for RSO-EISC, further studies are required to elucidate the specific details. Anyway, to comprehensively understand the results for OV1, OV2, and IN2, it is considered that the SOC of the radical substituent is involved, regardless of whether the mechanism is a direct or indirect process.

Conclusions

We studied the multispin photoexcited systems of anthracene radical-linked π-conjugated spin systems (OV2 and IN2) with an extended π-conjugated spin-coupler (1,2-diphenylacetylene moiety) and compared them with those of OV1, which featured a phenylene moiety as the spin coupler. The D–Q mixed state was detected in OV2 and IN2 together with the spin-polarized DG state by solid-phase TRESR spectroscopy. The observed spectra were well reproduced by superimposing the spin-polarized ground state (DG state) signal and the simulated spectra of the quasi-Q state (q-Qzf) obtained by assuming the RSO-EISC mechanism. In both solution- and solid-phase TRESR experiments, ESPT to the DG state was detected for OV2 and IN2. That is, in the solution TRESR experiments, the D-to-DG ESPT was directly detected as increasing the DG-state signal with decreasing D-state signal. ESPT from the photoexcited state to the DG state was also observed in the solid phase as a resonance field shift of the emissive (e) central peak (g ≈ 2.0) in the solid-phase TRESR spectra. Not only the decay time of DEP but also the characteristic time of the DEP generation strongly depended on the expected magnitude of the intramolecular exchange coupling (J) between the triplet-state chromophore and radical substituent. This finding strongly suggests that DEP generation in the present π-radical systems proceeds dominantly via the RSO-EISC mechanism and not n-ISC based on the SOC of the chromophore itself. The contributions of the various ISC mechanisms and the role of D–Q mixing in the generation of DEP to the (quasi-)­D state were discussed together with DEP generation in the multispin photoexcited states based on the simulation of the solid-phase TRESR spectra. These findings provide useful insights for future technologies utilizing the excited state of π-radical or related spin systems.
