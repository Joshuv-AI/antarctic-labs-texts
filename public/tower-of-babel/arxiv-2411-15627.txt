Community detection for binary graphical models in high dimension
Julien Chevallier, Guilherme Ost

Abstract
Let N N components be partitioned into two communities, denoted 𝒫 + {\cal P}_{+} and 𝒫 − {\cal P}_{-} , possibly of different sizes. Assume that they are connected via a directed and weighted Erdös-Rényi (DWER) random graph with unknown parameter p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) . p\in(0,1). The weights assigned to the existing connections are of mean-field-type, scaling as N − 1 N^{-1} . At each time step, we observe the state of each component: either it sends some signal to its successors (in the directed graph) or remains silent otherwise. In this paper, we show that it is possible to find the communities 𝒫 + {\cal P}_{+} and 𝒫 − {\cal P}_{-} based only on the activity of the N N components observed over T T time units. More specifically, we propose two simple methods, an aggregated method and a spectral method, whose misclassification rates vanish as long as T ≫ N T\gg N (up to log terms). This condition is proved to be near-optimal in the minimax sense. Moreover, under the stronger condition T ≫ N 2 T\gg N^{2} (up to log terms), the aggregated method is shown to achieve exact recovery with probability tending to 1 1 . Interestingly, these simple methods do not require any prior knowledge of the other model parameters (e.g. the edge probability p p ). The key step in our analysis is to derive an asymptotic approximation of the 1-lagged covariance matrix associated to the states of the N N components, as N N diverges. This asymptotic approximation relies on the study of the behavior of the solutions of a Stein-type matrix equation satisfied by the simultaneous (0-lagged) covariance matrix associated to the states of the components. This study is challenging, especially because the simultaneous covariance matrix is random since it depends on the underlying DWER random graph.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider a system of
N
interacting
\{0,1\}
-valued chains
X=\{X_{i,t},t\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq i\leq N\}
that are coupled via a random matrix
\theta=(\theta_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
of i.i.d entries distributed as
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
with
{{0<}p}\leq 1
. Let
X_{t}=(X_{1,t},\ldots,X_{N,t})
denote the configuration of the system
X
at time
t
. The dynamics of the model is as follows. Conditionally on
\theta
, the system
X
evolves as a stationary Markov chain on the
state space
\{0,1\}^{N}
in which the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given that
X_{t-1}=x
is that of
N
independent
\text{Ber}(p_{\theta,i}(x))
random variables,
i=1,\ldots,N
, where

p_{\theta,i}(x)=\mu+(1-\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{+}}\theta_{ij}x_{j}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{-}}\theta_{ij}(1-x_{j})\right),\ x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}).

(1)

The sets
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
form a partition of
[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and the values
0<\lambda<1
and
0{<}\mu\leq\lambda
are fixed.
Neither the sets
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
nor the values
\mu
and
\lambda
depend on the random environment
\theta
.

In this model, the transition probabilities
p_{i,\theta}(x)
defined in (
1
) are increasing with respect to
x_{j}
for
j\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
and decreasing for
j\in\mathcal{P}_{-}
, so that the components

j\in\mathcal{P}_{+}
have an excitatory role, whereas those belonging to
\mathcal{P}_{-}
have an inhibitory role. In that respect, the sets
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
partitioning the set of components
[N]
of the system
X
introduce a
community structure
into the model: components in the same partition
have the same role.
Hereafter, for
a\in\{-,+\}
, we denote
r^{N}_{a}=|\mathcal{P}_{a}|/N
the fraction of components of the system that belong to the community
{\cal P}_{a}
,
and assume that there are values
0<r_{+},r_{-}<1
satisfying
r_{+}+r_{-}=1
such that

|r^{N}_{+}-r_{+}|\vee|r^{N}_{-}-r_{-}|\leq KN^{-1}
for some constant
K
. In particular, we do not assume that the communities are balanced, i.e., the choice
r_{+}\neq r_{-}
is allowed.

Problem formulation

The goal of this paper is to address the following
community detection problem
.
By observing a sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T+1}
of the system
X
, can we find the communities
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
?
We do not assume any prior knowledge of
\mu,\lambda,p,r_{+},r_{-}
and
\theta
.

1.1
Application motivation

Part of our motivation to investigate this community detection problem comes from Neuroscience. Nowadays, the simultaneous activity of large neuronal networks can be routinely recorded and understanding which structural properties of these networks can be inferred from the recordings is an important question.
In several cases, neuronal recordings have been modeled by discrete-time
\{0,1\}
-valued stochastic chains that share some similarities with our model (see for example
(
Galves and Löcherbach, 2013
;
Duarte et al., 2019
)
and references therein). In these cases, the time interval in which the neuronal network has been recorded is binned in small intervals and in each of these intervals, and for each recorded neuron, we assign the symbol 1 if the neuron has fired an action potential in that time interval and the symbol 0 otherwise.
In this context, the sets
\mathcal{P}_{+}

and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
correspond respectively to the excitatory and inhibitory neurons of the network, and the community detection problem formulated above asks whether these two populations can be discriminated using only the recorded activity.

1.2
Related literature

The model considered here was recently introduced in
(
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost, 2026
)
. In their main result, the authors show that it is possible to estimate the connectivity parameter
p
from the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}
of the system
X
with rate
N^{-1/2}+N^{1/2}/T+(\log(T)/T)^{1/2}
, provided that the asymptotic fraction of components in each community (the values
r_{+}
and
r_{-}
) is known a priori. As a consequence, the proposed estimator is shown to be consistent provided that
N^{1/2}/T\to 0
.
In this paper, we complement these results by showing that one can also find the communities defined by the sets
\cal{P}_{+}
and
\cal{P}_{-}
.
More specifically, we propose

two simple methods, an aggregated method and a spectral method, for which we establish theoretical guarantees on exact recovery and misclassification rate control.

Interestingly, these simple methods
do not require the knowledge of the asymptotic fraction of components that belong to each community.

Community detection problems have been extensively investigated over the past decades. A large part of the known results were established for the stochastic block model (SBM) - also known as planted partition model -
(
Abbe, Bandeira and
Hall, 2016
;
Mossel, Neeman and
Sly, 2015
;
Mossel, Neeman and
Sly, 2016
)
and its generalizations such as the degree-corrected SBM
(
Zhao, Levina and
Zhu, 2012
;
Lei and Rinaldo, 2015
)
and mixed-membership SBM
(
Airoldi et al., 2008
)
. We refer the reader to
(
Abbe, 2018
)
for a recent review of the classical results in this framework.
The problem of detecting the communities in such stochastic block models is different from ours for at least two reasons. First, for such models, one has access to the adjacency matrix with a block structure in terms of the edge probabilities and uses a function of this matrix to find the communities.
In our framework, the adjacency matrix counterpart is the random matrix
A^{N}
defined in (
3
). This matrix is a normalized and signed version of the random matrix
\theta
in which all columns in
\mathcal{P}_{-}
have a negative sign. Since the matrix
A^{N}
depends on the matrix
\theta
that is not observed, one cannot use the matrix
A^{N}
in our detection problem. Instead, we can only use the information present in the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T+1}
which only gives us a skewed view of
A^{N}
(this is better justified in (
4
)).
Second, given the community assignments, there is no interaction between the components of those models: they behave independently of each other.
Here, on the contrary, the components of the model do interact and these interactions are crucial for finding the communities.

More recently, the problem of community detection has been also addressed in the context of graphical models
(
Berthet, Rigollet and
Srivastava, 2019
)
and G-latent models
(
Bunea et al., 2020
)
.
In both papers, the observations are assumed to be i.i.d. copies of some
N
-dimensional random vector
Z
. In
(
Berthet, Rigollet and
Srivastava, 2019
)
, the authors suppose that the distribution of
Z
is that of an Ising model without external field in which the interaction strength is
\beta N^{-1}
between sites of the same block and
\alpha N^{-1}
otherwise, where the real values
\alpha
and
\beta
satisfy
\beta>\alpha
. In particular, the block structure in this model is imposed directly on the interactions, similar to our model.
In the framework of G-latent models considered in
(
Bunea et al., 2020
)
, the community structure is imposed directly on the covariance matrix of the random vector
Z
, unlike the model considered in
(
Berthet, Rigollet and
Srivastava, 2019
)
and ours. In the present paper, we go beyond the assumption that the observations are i.i.d by addressing the community detection problem for dependent variables.
In this setting, part of the literature has focused on dynamic SBM
(
Pensky and Zhang, 2019
;
Lin and Lei, 2024
;
Matias and
Miele, 2016
)
- a framework fundamentally different from ours - where one observes sequences of adjacency matrices that slowly vary their community structures over time and the goal is to estimate these time varying communities.
Closer to our setting, we are only aware of the works of
(
Peixoto, 2019
)
and
(
Hoffmann et al., 2020
)
. Both papers adopt a Bayesian approach for detecting communities from time series data. Their focus is on discussing the properties
of the proposed methods using synthetic and empirical data, rather than developing theoretical guarantees leading to
exact recovery or controlling the misclassification rate.
Establishing such results in a similar context is the goal of our paper.

1.3
Our contributions

In this paper, we first establish structural results on the model. These
are then used to derive theoretical guarantees for two community detection methods.

1.3.1
Structural results

The key step in our analysis is to derive an asymptotic approximation for
the 1-lagged
covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
whose
(i,j)
-entries are defined as

\Sigma^{(1)}_{ij}=\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,1},X_{j,0}),\ i,j\in[N],

(2)

where
\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,1},X_{j,0})
denotes the covariance between the random variables
X_{i,1}
and
X_{j,0}
, conditionally on
\theta
.
To describe this approximation, let
A^{N}
be the random matrix
whose
(i,j)
-entries are given by

A^{N}_{ij}=\begin{cases}N^{-1}\theta_{ij},\ \text{if}\ j\in{\cal{P}_{+}}\\
-N^{-1}\theta_{ij},\ \text{if}\ j\in{\cal{P}_{-}}\end{cases},\ i,j\in[N].

(3)

With this notation, we establish in Theorem
2.1

that there are explicitly known constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}
(both depending on
\lambda,p,
and
r_{+}
) such that,
with high probability,

\max_{i,j\in[N]}\big|\Sigma^{(1)}_{ij}-(c_{1}A^{N}_{ij}+c_{2}N^{-1})\big|\leq K\sqrt{\log(N)/N^{3}},

(4)

where the constant
K>0
depends on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
.
This means that all entries of the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
are uniformly close to those of the matrix
c_{1}A^{N}+c_{2}N^{-1}1_{N}1_{N}^{\top}
(with high probability), where

1_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional vector full of ones so that
1_{N}1_{N}^{\top}
is the
N
-by-
N
matrix full of ones.
The term
c_{2}N^{-1}
can be interpreted as a bias term present in all the entries of the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
.
We note that the sign of this bias term is the same as the sign of
r_{+}-r_{-}
. In particular, the bias is
0
if and only if the communities are balanced, i.e.
r_{+}=r_{-}=1/2.

Once the entry-wise approximation (
4
) is established, we then derive an approximation for
\Sigma^{(1)}
in the spectral norm, in terms of the matrix

\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}:=c_{1}\mathbb{E}[A^{N}]+c_{2}N^{-1}1_{N}1_{N}^{\top},

(5)

where
\mathbb{E}[A^{N}]
denotes the expectation of
A^{N}
, taken with respect to the distribution of the random matrix
\theta
.
Specifically, we establish in Corollary
2.4
that, with high probability,

\vvvert\Sigma^{(1)}-\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\leq K\sqrt{\log(N)/N},

(6)

for some constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
.

The matrix
A^{N}
defined in (
3
) is a normalized and signed version of
\theta
in which the entries associated with columns in
{\cal P}_{-}
have a negative sign. Hence the columns of
A^{N}
, and also of
\mathbb{E}[A^{N}]
, carry information about the communities
\mathcal{P}_{+}
and
\mathcal{P}_{-}
.
The approximations in (
4
) and (
6
) suggest that the same is true for the columns of the random matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
for large values of
N
.

To formalize this, let
\sigma^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
be the vectors defined as

\sigma^{\rm ag}:=(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}\ \text{and}\ \overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}:=(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}.

(7)

The superscript
{\rm ag}
in both vectors
\sigma^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
stands for
aggregated
, since these vectors are obtained by summing over the columns of the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
, respectively.

Note that the random vector
(A^{N})^{\top}1_{N}
is expected to concentrate around its mean
\mathbb{E}[(A^{N})^{\top}]1_{N}
sufficiently fast (this is formalized in Inequality (
37
)). Combining this fact with Equation (
4
), we show in Corollary
2.3
that the vectors
\sigma^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
satisfy,
with high probability,

\max_{i\in[N]}|\sigma^{\rm ag}_{i}-\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{i}|\leq K\sqrt{\log(N)/N},

(8)

for some constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda
,
p
and
r_{+}
.

Using (
5
) and the fact that the random variables
\theta_{ij}
’s are i.i.d with distribution

\text{Ber}(p)
, one can check that
the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
defined in (
7
)
can be rewritten as

\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}=\overline{\sigma}_{+}1_{{\cal P_{+}}}+\overline{\sigma}_{-}1_{{\cal P_{-}}},

(9)

where
1_{{\cal P}_{+}}
(resp.
1_{{\cal P}_{-}}
) is the
N
-dimensional vector whose entries are equal to 1 in
{\cal P}_{+}
(resp.
{\cal P}_{-}
) and
0
elsewhere,
and the real values
\overline{\sigma}_{+}
and
\overline{\sigma}_{-}
are given by

\overline{\sigma}_{+}=c_{2}+c_{1}p\ \text{and}\ \overline{\sigma}_{-}=c_{2}-c_{1}p.

(10)

Since
\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}=2c_{1}p>0
, it follows from (
9
) that the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
discriminates the communities
{\cal P_{+}}
and
{\cal P_{-}}
, in the sense that the entries in
{\cal P}_{+}
take a larger value than those in
{\cal P}_{-}
.

1.3.2
Aggregated method

In view of the upper bound (
8
), it is natural to expect that the vector
\sigma^{\rm ag}
also discriminates these communities in the limit as
N
diverges.
However, the vector
\sigma^{\rm ag}
cannot be used in practice, since the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
is not observed.
In light of (
7
), we propose to estimate
\sigma^{\rm ag}
by

\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}=(\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N},

(11)

where
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
, defined below in (
17
), is the empirical estimate of
\Sigma^{(1)}
.
Given the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
, it is then natural
to estimate the communities
{\cal P}_{+}
and
{\cal P}_{-}
by clustering its coordinates.
We call the resulting method
aggregated method
.

Its theoretical guarantees are studied in Section
2.4
.
There, we first prove the convergence of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
towards
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
, both in the sup-norm (Theorem
2.5
) and
\ell_{2}
-norm (Theorem
2.6
).
The control in the sup-norm relies on (
8
) and
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
, Theorem 3)
, whereas
the one in
\ell_{2}

follows from (
6
) and a sharp estimate for the distance between
\Sigma^{(1)}
and its empirical estimate
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
, stated in Theorem
D.1
.
Then, using Theorem
2.5
, we show in Corollary
2.7
that the aggregated method achieves exact recovery with high probability as long as
T\gg N^{2}
(up to log factors).
In Appendix
F.2
, we study the optimality of this condition in a similar statistical setting in which conditionally on
\theta
, the system
X
starts from fixed initial conditions (and therefore is not stationary). In this setting, in Proposition
F.5
, we derive an information-theoretic lower bound showing that exactly recovering the communities
{\cal P_{+}}
and
{\cal P_{-}}
is impossible in the minimax sense if
T/(N\log(N))\to 0
. This result suggests that the condition
T\gg N^{2}
may not be optimal for exact recovery.

Finally, as a consequence of Theorem
2.6
, we show in Theorem
2.9
that the misclassification rate of the aggregated method vanishes as long as
T\gg N
(up to log factors). Combined with the information-theoretic lower bound stated in Theorem
2.12
, this result shows that the aggregated method is near-optimal with respect to the misclassification rate.

1.3.3
Spectral method

In this paper, we also consider a
spectral method
for finding the communities
{\cal P_{+}}
and
{\cal P_{-}}
.
One key observation behind our spectral method is that the matrix
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
has rank 1 and that the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
(up to renormalization) is a leading right singular vector of it (see Equation (
21
)).
Since singular vectors are only defined up to a
\pm 1
factor, any leading singular vector of
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
can estimate
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
only up to its sign, and clustering the entries of such a vector is prone to label ambiguity as it is well known in the spectral methods literature
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
.
Interestingly, we show that in our case this ambiguity can be resolved by using
the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
to determine the correct sign,
yielding an improved spectral method presented in Section
2.5
.

In Theorem
2.11
, we prove that the misclassification rate
of our spectral method vanishes as long as
T\gg N
(up to log factors). This result together with Theorem
2.12
shows that our spectral method is also near-optimal with respect to the misclassification rate.
To prove Theorem
2.11
, we combine the perturbation theory of matrices with the approximation results stated in Equation (
6
) and Theorem
D.1
.

1.3.4
Proof strategy of our key step

The asymptotic approximation (
4
) is achieved by first showing that the 1-lagged covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
can be computed from the simultaneous (0-lagged) covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
whose
(i,j)
-entries are defined as

\Sigma^{(0)}_{ij}=\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,0},X_{j,0}),\ i,j\in[N].

(12)

Next, we show that the simultaneous covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
satisfies some Stein matrix equation (see Proposition
3.2
). To conclude, we then study the asymptotic behavior of the solutions of the matrix equation satisfied by
\Sigma^{(0)}
as
N\to\infty
. This analysis is challenging, specially because the matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
depends on the random matrix
\theta
and, hence, it is itself a random matrix.
The analysis involves two main ingredients: identifying an "almost" eigenvector of the Kronecker product
A^{N}\otimes A^{N}
, and a control
of averages over entries of the matrix
\theta
that are obtained via Hoeffding’s inequality.

1.4
Concluding remarks, extensions and future work

The present paper focuses on the case of two communities
{\cal P}_{+}
and
{\cal P}_{-}
.
Let us stress that

our proposed methods

for finding these communities do not rely on the exact values of the two limits
\overline{\sigma}_{+}
and
\overline{\sigma}_{-}
defined in (
10
);
knowing that they are well seperated is sufficient.

From a theoretical viewpoint, the case of two communities is a natural and widely studied setting in the community detection literature
Abbe (2018)
;
Abbe, Bandeira and
Hall (2016)
.
Moreover, as noted by
Berthet, Rigollet and
Srivastava (2019)
in the context of the Ising blockmodel, extending to multiple communities is particularly challenging when the population covariance matrix cannot be directly computed from the model parameters. A similar difficulty arises in our setting: the 1-lagged covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
is not directly given in terms of the model parameters, and we must derive a suitable approximation for it (Theorem
2.1
). Deriving such an approximation is by itself a difficult task, because the environment encoded in the matrix
\theta
is random and observations are not i.i.d. in our model.

In a suitably modified version of our model with
K>2
communities, we expect the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
to have
K
distinct values, so that it should be possible to extend our analysis of the aggregated method to the case of
K
communities, though the details remain to be worked out.
However, we conjecture that the analysis of the spectral method would be more challenging.
Indeed, with
K>2
communities, we expect the matrix
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
to have rank
K-1
and we would need to track
K-1

singular vectors simultaneously, instead of only one as in our case. Moreover, the resolution of the label permutation ambiguity, which in our case reduces to a simple sign ambiguity, would become significantly more challenging for
K>2
.

As mentioned earlier, a key property in our analysis is that the community
structure only affects the columns of
A^{N}
, and in particular its rows
share a common distribution. The aggregated method heavily relies on this property
and should perform poorly if it is violated.
Although the spectral method also relies on this property,
we believe that a spectral approach is more robust to generalizations of the present model.

Furthermore, throughout our analysis we assume that the parameter
p
does not scale with the number of chains
N
in the system
X
.
Despite the fact that some probabilistic tools used in our proofs are available for cases where
p
scales with
N
(e.g. Hoeffding’s inequality), extending our results for such cases is not straightforward for at least two reasons. First, the model has to be suitably modified, since the normalization factor
N^{-1}
is no longer appropriate. Second, and more importantly, our proof of Theorem
D.1

relies on Lemma 3.8 of our companion paper
(
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost, 2026
)
, which has been proved only for
p
constant. This lemma controls the covariances between components of our model and is a key ingredient to control a variance term (this is done in Lemma
D.4
) appearing in the Bernstein-type concentration inequality derived in Lemma
D.3
, which, in turn, is used to prove Theorem
D.1
.
Extending Lemma 3.8 to regimes where
p
scales with
N
would require a substantially different analysis and is beyond the scope of the present paper.
The study of the case
p
scaling with
N
is particularly relevant for applications where the underlying graph is sparse and dynamics of the model is sensitive to local graph structures (contrary to the mean-field assumption in (
1
)).

To conclude, we note that both aggregated and spectral methods are near-optimal in terms of the misclassification rate and that the aggregated method achieves exact recovery.
Whether the spectral method also achieves exact recovery remains an open question.
A related open question is to understand whether the condition
T\gg N^{2}
required by the aggregated method is optimal for exact recovery. Our lower bound in Proposition
F.5
suggests that the sharp threshold may indeed be
T\gg N\log(N)
. Settling this issue is left for future work.

2
Model definition, notation and main results

2.1
Model definition

We consider a system of
N
interacting chains
X=\{X_{i,t},t\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq i\leq N\}
taking values in
\{0,1\}
denoting the presence or the absence of a signal at a given time. This system evolves in a random environment which is given by the realization of a directed Erdös-Rényi random graph via the selection of
N^{2}
i.i.d.​ random variables
\theta_{ij},1\leq i,j\leq N
, distributed as a Bernoulli of parameter
p
with
0{<}p\leq 1
.
Conditionally on
\theta
, the evolution of the system
X
is that of a stationary Markov chain on the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{N}
with transition probabilities (which depend on
\theta
) given as follows.
Writing
X_{t}=(X_{1,t},\ldots,X_{N,t})
and
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, we have, for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

I\!\!P_{\theta}(X_{t}=y|X_{t-1}=x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(p_{\theta,i}(x))^{y_{i}}(1-p_{\theta,i}(x))^{(1-y_{i})},

(13)

where
p_{\theta,i}(x)=I\!\!P_{\theta}(X_{i,t}=1|X_{t-1}=x)
is defined in (
1
).
The existence and uniqueness of a stationary version of the Markov chain having transition probabilities as defined in (
13
) and (
1
) is granted by
(
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost, 2026
, Theorem 3.3)
. Only the stationary regime is considered throughout this paper.

2.2
Notation

Throughout the paper, we use the letters
t,s
to denote some time indices whereas the letters
i,j
denote some spatial values, that is the index of the corresponding component. In agreement with the left-hand side of (
13
), we write
I\!\!P_{\theta}
to denote the probability measure under which the environment
\theta
is kept fixed and the process
X
is distributed as the unique stationary version of the Markov chain having transition probabilities as defined in (
13
) and (
1
). The expectation taken with respect to
I\!\!P_{\theta}
is denoted by
I\!\!E_{\theta}
. The variance and covariance computed from
I\!\!E_{\theta}
are denoted
\var_{\theta}
and
\cov_{\theta}
respectively.
Moreover, we write
I\!\!P
to denote the probability measure under which the random environment
\theta=(\theta_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
is distributed as a collection of i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
and the conditional distribution of the process
X
given
\theta
is that of the process
X
under
I\!\!P_{\theta}
, i.e., the following identity holds
I\!\!P(X\in\cdot|\theta)=I\!\!P_{\theta}(X\in\cdot)
. Finally, we denote
I\!\!E
the expectation taken with respect to the probability measure
I\!\!P
, and
\var
and
\cov
the variance and covariance, respectively, computed from
I\!\!E
.
When we need to emphasize that the partition
({\cal P}_{+},{\cal P}_{-})=(S,S^{c})
, where
S\subset[N]
with
|S|=\lceil Nr_{+}\rceil
, we add an additional subscript to the previous notation. For example,
I\!\!P_{S,\theta}
denotes the probability measure under which the environment
\theta
is fixed and the process
X
is distributed as the unique stationary version of the Markov chain having transition probabilities given by (
13
) and (
1
), where the communities
({\cal P}_{+},{\cal P}_{-})=(S,S^{c}).

The corresponding expectation is denoted by
I\!\!E_{S,\theta}
.

For any
N
-by-
N
matrix
M
, we shall denote
\vvvert M\vvvert_{p}
, with
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the matrix norm induced by the vector norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}
, and
\vvvert M\vvvert_{\max}=\max_{i,j\in[N]}|M(i,j)|
. Recall that, although the matrix norm
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{\max}
is not sub-multiplicative, it satisfies

\vvvert AB\vvvert_{\max}\leq\min\left\{\vvvert A\vvvert_{\infty}\vvvert B\vvvert_{\max},\vvvert A\vvvert_{\max}\vvvert B\vvvert_{1}\right\},

(14)

for any
N
-by-
N
matrices
A
and
B
. We write
I_{N}
to denote the
N
-by-
N
identity matrix.

Hereafter, for any subset
V
of
[N]
, we write
1_{V}
to indicate the
N
-dimensional vector having value
1
in each coordinate belonging to
V
and value
0
in the remaining coordinates. To alleviate the notation, we will simply write
1_{N}
instead of
1_{[N]}
, to indicate the vector full of ones. Along the text, the letter
K
denotes a constant that is independent of
N
and
T
and that may change from line to line. Also, for any real values
a,b
, we let
a\wedge b
and
a\vee b
respectively denote the minimum and the maximum between
a
and
b
. Finally, all the logarithms are in base
e
.

2.3
Structural results

Let us denote

m=m(\mu,\lambda,p)=\frac{\mu+(1-\lambda)pr_{-}}{1-{D}}\ \text{with}\ {D=D(\lambda,p,r_{+})=(1-\lambda)p(r_{+}-r_{-})}

(15)

which was proved in
(
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost, 2026
, Theorem 2.1)
to be the limit, in probability, of the spatio-temporal mean
(TN)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{i,t}
as
T\wedge N\to\infty
.

Furthermore, denote

\begin{cases}c_{1}=(1-\lambda)m(1-m),\\
c_{2}=\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}(r_{+}-r_{-})}{1-D^{2}}c_{1}.\end{cases}

(16)

For later use, let us observe that
c_{1}>0
since
p>0
and
\lambda<1
and that
c_{2}
is non-null as long as the network is unbalanced (i.e.
r_{+}\neq r_{-}
).

Recall the random matrices
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
A^{N}
defined in (
2
) and (
3
) respectively. The matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
is the
1-lagged
covariance matrix and
A^{N}
is a normalized and signed version of random matrix
\theta
.
Remark that
\Sigma^{(1)}
also depends on
N
but we chose to not highlight this dependence in the notation. Moreover, note that this matrix is not symmetric.

In our first structural result, namely Theorem
2.1
below, we show that with high probability the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}

is close to the sum of two matrices: the first one is the matrix
c_{1}A^{N}
and the second one is the matrix
c_{2}N^{-1}1_{N}1_{N}^{\top}
which is interpreted a bias term.
The proof of Theorem
2.1
is given in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists a positive constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

I\!\!P\left(\left\vvvert\Sigma^{(1)}-\left(c_{1}A^{N}+\frac{c_{2}}{N}1_{N}1_{N}^{\top}\right)\right\vvvert_{\max}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N^{3}}}\right)\leq\delta,

where the matrices
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
A^{N}
are defined in (
2
) and (
3
) respectively, and the real values
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are defined in (
16
).

Remark 2.2
.

The constant
K
appearing in Theorem
2.1
remains bounded for fixed values of
\lambda
(i.e. as functions of
p
and
r_{+}
only). However, this constant diverges when
\lambda\to 0
(the ergodicity of dynamics breaks) or
\lambda\to 1
(the dependence between the components of the model becomes very weak).
In such regimes, the result stated in Theorem
2.1
is vacuous.
The same remark applies to several other results, although we will not mention this explicitly.

Recall the matrix
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
defined in (
5
). Our second structural result establishes that the vectors
\sigma^{\rm ag}=(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}=(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}
are close in the sup norm, with high probability.
This result is the basis for the aggregated method presented in Section
2.4
. Its proof, given also in Section
3
, follows from Theorem
2.1
and concentration properties of the random vector
(A^{N})^{\top}1_{N}
around its mean
I\!\!E[(A^{N})^{\top}]1_{N}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

There exists a positive constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

I\!\!P\left(\left\|\sigma^{\rm ag}-\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

where the vectors
\sigma^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
are defined in (
7
).

Our final structural result shows that the matrices
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
are close in the operator norm, with high probability. This result will be important in the analysis of the misclassification rate for both aggregated and spectral methods.
Its proof, presented in Section
3
, follows from a combination of Theorem
2.1
with concentration properties of the random matrix
A^{N}
around its expectation
\mathbb{E}[A^{N}]
.

Corollary 2.4
.

There exists a positive constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

I\!\!P\left(\left\vvvert\Sigma^{(1)}-\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}\right\vvvert_{2}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

where the matrices
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
are defined in (
2
) and (
5
) respectively.

2.4
Aggregated method

Recall the vectors
\sigma^{\rm ag}=(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}=(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}.
Observing that
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
can be rewritten as
\overline{\sigma}_{+}1_{{\cal P}_{+}}+\overline{\sigma}_{-}1_{{\cal P}_{-}}
(recall Equation (
9
))
where
\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}=2c_{1}p>0
, we immediately see that the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
discriminates the communities
{\cal P}_{+}
and
{\cal P}_{-}
. By Corollary
2.3
, we then expect that the communities can also be discriminated by the vector
\sigma^{\rm ag}
, with high probability.
In practice, however, the vector
\sigma^{\rm ag}
cannot be used to that end because the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
is not known. To circumvent this issue, one can try to find these sets by using the empirical estimate of
\sigma^{\rm ag}
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T+1}
be a sample of a stationary Markov chain with transition probabilities given by (
13
) and (
1
), associated with some realization
\theta
of the random environment which is not observed. Given the sample, we propose to estimate the vector

\sigma^{\rm ag}=(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}
by the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}=(\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)})^{\top}1_{N}
, where
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
denotes the empirical estimate of the matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
, which is defined as

\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(X_{t+1}-\widehat{m})(X_{t}-\widehat{m})^{\top},\ \text{with}\ \widehat{m}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}.

(17)

In Theorem
B.1
, stated and proved in Appendix
B
, we show that the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}

concentrates around

\sigma^{\rm ag}
in the sup-norm, as both
T
and
N
diverge provided that
T\gg N^{2}
(up to log factors).
Combining this result with Corollary
2.3
, we can show that the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
also converges to
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
in the sup-norm, provided that
T\gg N^{2}
(up to log terms). This is the content of the result below.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
be the vectors defined in (
11
) and (
7
) respectively. There exist positive constants
K_{1},K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending on
\lambda
such that

I\!\!P\left(\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\|_{\infty}\geq K_{1}\Bigg(\frac{N\log(TN)}{\sqrt{T}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(N)}{N}}\Bigg)\right)\leq K_{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{N}\Bigg),

for all pairs
(N,T)
satisfying
N\geq 2
and
T\geq K_{3}\log(N)
.

Theorem
2.5
is the key ingredient in establishing the exact recovery guarantees of the aggregated method defined below. Its proof is given in Section
4
.

To control the misclassification rate, we need a control similar to that of Theorem
2.5
with
\ell_{2}
-norm replacing the sup-norm.
A naive approach that combines the crude bound
\|x\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}\|x\|_{\infty}
with Theorem
2.5
leads to a sub-optimal estimate for the convergence rate of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
towards
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
in the
\ell_{2}
-norm.
To achieve optimal convergence rate, we use Corollary
2.4
and a sharp estimate
for the distance between the 1-lagged covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
and its empirical estimate
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
(Theorem
D.1
).

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
be the vectors defined in (
11
) and (
7
) respectively.
Then there exist positive constants
K_{1},K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending on
\lambda
such that

I\!\!P\left(\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\|_{2}\geq K_{1}\Bigg(N\sqrt{\frac{\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}}+\sqrt{\log(N)}\Bigg)\right)\leq K_{2}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{N}\Bigg),

for all pairs
(N,T)
satisfying
N\geq 2
and

T\geq(K_{3}N\log(T)\log(N\log(T)))\vee 2.

(18)

The proof of Theorem
2.6
is given in Section
5
. Note that Condition (
18
) requires the time horizon
T
to be sufficiently large when compared to the number
N
of interacting chains composing the system.

In light of the properties of the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and of Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
, it is natural to estimate the communities
{\cal P}_{+}
and
{\cal P}_{-}
by clustering the entries of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
. Although our approach extends to several clustering procedures, in what follows we analyze in detail only two of them.

The first is the
k
-means
clustering with
k=2
, which computes the global minimizer of the
k
-means criterion.
Since the entries of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
are real numbers, this can be done efficiently (in time linear in
N
) via the dynamic programming algorithm developed in
Wu (1991)
(see also
Grønlund et al. (2018)
for a review of the existing approaches to one-dimensional
k
-means clustering).
Note that, in general, its output is different from that of Lloyd’s algorithm.
We denote by
(\widehat{\cal P}_{+}^{\rm ag,km},\widehat{\cal P}_{-}^{\rm ag,km})
the estimated communities obtained by applying
k
-means clustering to the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
.

The second procedure, which we refer to as
mean threshold
clustering, estimates the communities
{\cal P_{+}}
(resp.
{\cal P_{-}}
) as the
set of indices
i\in[N]
for which
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{i}
is above (resp. below)
the mean value
N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}^{\rm ag}
. We denote the resulting
communities by
(\widehat{\cal P}_{+}^{\rm ag,mt},\widehat{\cal P}_{-}^{\rm ag,mt}).

Our next result provides the theoretical guarantees for these clustering procedures with respect to exact recovery.

Corollary 2.7
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
be the vector defined in (
11
) and denote by
(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{-})
the estimated communities obtained by applying
k
-means or mean threshold clustering to
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
. Then there exist an integer
N_{0}
and positive constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
depending only on
\lambda,p,r_{+}
such that

I\!\!P\left((\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+}^{\rm ag,\bullet},\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-}^{\rm ag,\bullet})\neq({\cal P_{+}},{\cal P_{-}})\right)\leq K_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{T}\Big)

for all
(N,T)
such that
N\geq N_{0}
and

T\geq K_{1}N^{2}\log^{2}(TN).

(19)

Remark 2.8
.

As our proof reveals, the conclusion of Corollary
2.7
also holds if the
k
-means clustering is replaced by Lloyd’s
k
-means algorithm, initialized with the means
m_{1}=\min_{i\in[N]}\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{i}
and
m_{2}=\max_{i\in[N]}\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{i}
. Indeed, whenever
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
is sufficiently close to
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
in the sup-norm, we show that Lloyd’s algorithm converges to the
true partition
({\cal P}_{+},{\cal P}_{-})
in one iteration.

The analysis for
k
-means clustering is more involved as it requires to show that the global minimizer of the
k
-means criterion is the true
({\cal P}_{+},{\cal P}_{-})
, provided that the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
are close in the sup-norm. This is done in Lemma
H.1
of Appendix
H
.

Like Theorem
2.5
, the proof of Corollary
2.7
is given in Section
4
. It follows from Corollary
2.7
that we can exactly recover the communities
{\cal P}_{+}
and
{\cal P}_{-}
by clustering the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
via either
k
-means or mean threshold, as long as
T\gg N^{2}
(up to log factors).
In Section
6
, we perform a numerical study to compare the performance of these two clustering procedures (and also include hierarchical clustering in this study).
We note that the information-theoretic lower bound derived in Proposition
F.5
of Appendix
F.2
shows, in a slightly different setting, that exactly recovering the communities
{\cal P_{+}}
and
{\cal P_{-}}
is impossible in the minimax sense if
T/(N\log(N))\to 0
. This suggests that the condition
T\gg N^{2}
may not be optimal, and resolving this issue is left for future work.

In what follows, we focus on the performance of the aggregated method with

k
-means or mean threshold clustering in terms of its misclassification rate. Here,
the misclassification rate of an estimated partition
(\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+},\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-})
is defined by

\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+},\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-})=N^{-1}(|\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+}\cap{\cal P}_{-}|+|\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-}\cap{\cal P}_{+}|).

(20)

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
be the vector defined in (
11
) and denote by
(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{-})
the estimated communities obtained by applying
k
-means or mean threshold clustering to
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
.
Then there exist an integer
N_{0}
and positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending only on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that

I\!\!P\Big(\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{-})\geq K_{1}\Big(\frac{N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}+\frac{\log(N)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{N}\Big),

for all pairs
(T,N)
for which
N\geq N_{0}
and (
18
) is satisfied.

The proof of Theorem
2.9
is provided in Section
5
. The key step in the proof is to obtain an estimate for missclassification rate in terms of
\ell_{2}
-distance between
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
. This is the purpose of Lemma
5.1
, stated and proved in Section
5
. Given this estimate, we then conclude by Theorem
2.6
.

Finally, let us observe that since condition (
18
) matches the condition
T\gg N
up to log terms, the minimax lower bound stated in Theorem
2.12
of Section
2.6
implies that the estimated partitions
(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,\bullet}_{-})

are both near-optimal, with respect to the misclassification rate.
An interesting open question is to understand whether the log factors in condition (

) can be removed.

2.5
Spectral method

Recall the constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
defined in (
16
).
Using that
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}=c_{1}I\!\!E[A^{N}]+c_{2}N^{-1}1_{N}1_{N}^{\top}
and that
I\!\!E[A^{N}]=pN^{-1}1_{N}(1_{{\cal P}_{+}}-1_{{\cal P}_{-}})^{\top}
, we can rewrite
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
as

\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}=N^{-1}1_{N}(\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag})^{\top},

(21)

where
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
is the vector given by (
9
).

Starting from (
21
), one can verify after simple calculations that

\overline{\sigma}^{\rm sp}:=\frac{\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}}{\|\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\|_{2}},

(22)

is a right singular vector of
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
with corresponding singular value

\sigma_{1}\big(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}\big)=\sqrt{(\overline{\sigma}_{+})^{2}r^{N}_{+}+(\overline{\sigma}_{-})^{2}r^{N}_{-}}.

(23)

Except in trivial cases,
\sigma_{1}\big(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}\big)>0
so that
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
has rank 1 and
\sigma_{1}\big(\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}\big)
is its unique non-null singular value.

Next observe that Corollary
2.4
and Theorem
D.1
together imply that the matrices
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
and
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
are close in operator norm with high probability. In view of the above properties of
\overline{\Sigma}^{(1)}
and of Wedin’s
\sin\Theta
theorem (see for instance Inequality (
50
)), it then follows that, with high probability, any leading right singular vector
\widecheck{v}
of the matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
is close to
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm sp}
, up to sign.
The sign ambiguity can be resolved using
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
as follows. Let
\widecheck{{\cal P}}
be a preliminary estimated community defined as

\widecheck{{\cal P}}=\left\{i\in[N]:\widecheck{v}_{i}\geq N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widecheck{v}_{j}\right\}.

(24)

Depending on the ambiguous sign,
\widecheck{{\cal P}}
is expected to be close to
{\cal P}_{+}
or
{\cal P}_{-}
, and consequently the average of the coordinates of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
over
\widecheck{{\cal P}}
is expected to be close to
\overline{\sigma}_{+}
or
\overline{\sigma}_{-}
. Hence, we propose to estimate the vector
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm sp}
by the vector

\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}=\begin{cases}\widecheck{v}&\text{, if }|\widecheck{{\cal P}}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\widecheck{{\cal P}}}\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{\rm ag}\geq|[N]\setminus\widecheck{{\cal P}}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]\setminus\widecheck{{\cal P}}}\widehat{\sigma}_{i}^{\rm ag},\\
-\widecheck{v}&\text{, else}.\end{cases}

(25)

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}
be defined in (
25
).
There exist an integer
N_{0}
and positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending only on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that

I\!\!P\Big(\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}-\overline{\sigma}^{\rm sp}\|_{2}\geq K_{1}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(N)}{N}}\Big)\Big)\leq K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{N}\Big),

for all pairs
(T,N)
for which
N\geq N_{0}
and (
18
) is satisfied.

Theorem
2.10
is proved in Section
5
. As anticipated above, its proof follows from the perturbation theory of matrices (Wedin’s Sin
\Theta
theorem), Corollary
2.4
and Theorem
D.1
. The most challenging part in the proof is to show that the sign of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}
is correct with high probability.

Finally, the estimated partition is defined as

\widehat{{\cal P}}^{\rm sp}_{+}=\left\{i\in[N]:\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}_{i}\geq N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}_{j}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{{\cal P}}^{\rm sp}_{-}=[N]\setminus\widehat{{\cal P}}^{\rm sp}_{+},

(26)

and its misclassification rate is upper bounded as follows.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
(\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm sp}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm sp}_{-})
be the partition defined in (
26
).
There exist an integer
N_{0}
and positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending only on
\lambda,p
and
r_{+}
such that

I\!\!P\Big(\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm sp}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}^{\rm sp}_{-})\geq K_{1}\Big(\frac{N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}+\frac{\log(N)}{N}\Big)\Big)\leq K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{N}\Big),

for all pairs
(T,N)
for which

The proof of Theorem

2.6
Lower bound for the misclassification rate

In this section, we establish lower bounds on the sample size

needed to recover the partition
({\cal P}_{+},\cal{P}_{-})
, with respect to the either misclassification rate.
For each
K>0

u\geq 0

\tau_{K}(u)\to 1

\tau_{K}(u)\to 0

Theorem 2.12
.

There exist positive constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}

\mu
and
\lambda
such that for all

\min_{(\widehat{\cal P}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}_{-})}\max_{S\in{\cal S}}I\!\!E_{S}\Big[\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{\cal P}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}_{-})\Big]\geq\frac{(r_{\min}-N^{-1})}{2}\Bigg(1-\tau_{K_{1}}\Big(\frac{T}{N}\Big)-2\tau_{K_{2}}\Big(\frac{\log(2N)}{N}\Big)\Bigg),

The proof of Theorem
2.12

F.1

Note that if both

3.2
Asymptotics for the simultaneous covariance matrix

Let us denote
v^{N}
the variance vector given by

v^{N}=(m^{N}_{1}(1-m^{N}_{1}),\ldots,m^{N}_{N}(1-m^{N}_{N})).

(34)

According to the end of previous section, we expect that

Let us denote
\Sigma^{(0)}=I\!\!E_{\theta}[(X_{0}-m^{N})(X_{0}-m^{N})^{\top}]

\Sigma^{(0)}_{ij}=\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,0},X_{j,0}),\ i,j\in[N].

(35)

The main objective of this section is to provide an asymptotic approximation of the matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
as
N\to\infty
(see Proposition
3.3
).

The reason to study the simultaneous covariance matrix is that it serves as a proxy for getting the asymptotics of the 1-lagged
covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}

Proposition 3.1
.

\Sigma^{(1)}=(1-\lambda)A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}
.

By conditioning with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{0}
in Equation (
29

31

\Sigma^{(1)}=I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[(p_{\theta}(X_{0})-m^{N})(X_{0}-m^{N})^{\top}\right]=I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[(1-\lambda)A^{N}(X_{0}-m^{N})(X_{0}-m^{N})^{\top}\right],

which ends the proof.
∎

For a
N
-dimensional vector
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})
, we write
\diag(u)
to denote the
N
-by-
N
diagonal matrix having value
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
associated with its
i

\diag(u)_{ii}=u_{i}

For a
N
-by-
N
matrix

\d 0(M)
the same matrix where the diagonal terms are replaced by zeros, i.e.
\d 0(M)_{ij}=M_{ij}\mathbf{1}_{i\neq j}
.
The simultaneous covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
satisfies the following Stein-type matrix equation.

Proposition 3.2
.

The simultaneous covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(0)}
is equal to
v^{N}
on the diagonal and satisfies
\Sigma^{(0)}=(1-\lambda)^{2}A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}
out of the diagonal, i.e.

\Sigma^{(0)}=(1-\lambda)^{2}\d 0\left(A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}\right)+\diag(v^{N}).

Let
i\in[N]
. Under the stationary distribution,
X_{i,0}
is a Bernoulli variable with parameter
m^{N}_{i}
. Hence,
\Sigma^{(0)}_{ii}=\var_{\theta}(X_{i,0})=m^{N}_{i}(1-m^{N}_{i})=v^{N}_{i}
.

Let
i,j\in[N]
with
i\neq j
. By conditional independence between
X_{i,0}
and
X_{j,0}
given
X_{-1}
and stationarity of the process, we have that

\Sigma^{(0)}_{ij}=\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,0},X_{j,0})=\cov_{\theta}(p_{\theta,i}(X_{-1}),p_{\theta,j}(X_{-1}))=\cov_{\theta}(p_{\theta,i}(X_{0}),p_{\theta,j}(X_{0})).

Yet, the centered version of
p_{\theta,i}(X_{0})
is
p_{\theta,i}(X_{0})-m^{N}_{i}
so that using Equation (
31

\Sigma^{(0)}_{ij}=(1-\lambda)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[A^{N}(X_{0}-m^{N})(X_{0}-m^{N})^{\top}(A^{N})^{\top}\right]_{ij}=(1-\lambda)^{2}[A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}]_{ij}.

∎

Let us denote
a^{N}=1_{N}(1_{N})^{\top}-I_{N}
the
N
-by-
N
matrix full of ones except on the diagonal. Here is our main asymptotic result regarding the simultaneous covariance matrix.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists a constant

depending only on
\lambda,p
and

such that for all

and

I\!\!P\left(\left\vvvert\Sigma^{(0)}-\left(\diag(v^{N})+\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}m(1-m)}{1-D^{2}}\frac{a^{N}}{N}\right)\right\vvvert_{\max}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N^{3}}}\right)\leq\delta,

where the real values

The proof of Proposition

(see Proposition

Proofs

that

(43)

Claim 1.

(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,km}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}^{\rm ag,km}_{-})

Lemma

. Hence, under this event, we must have

Let

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\overline{\sigma}=b_{+}1_{{\cal P}_{+}}+b_{-}1_{{\cal P}_{-}}
for some
b_{+}\neq b_{-}
and
\widehat{\sigma}
be an estimator of
\overline{\sigma}
. Let
(\widehat{\cal P}_{+},\widehat{\cal P}_{-})
be the partition defined as

\widehat{\cal P}_{+}=\left\{i\in[N]:\widehat{\sigma}_{i}\geq N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\sigma}_{j}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\cal P}_{-}=[N]\setminus\widehat{\cal P}_{+}.

Then the following inequality holds:

\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{-})\leq\frac{2\|\widehat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}\|_{2}^{2}}{|b_{+}-b_{-}|^{2}\cdot(r_{+}^{N}\wedge r_{-}^{N})^{2}}N^{-1}.

Let
v
and
\overline{v}
be the centered versions of
\widehat{\sigma}
and
\overline{\sigma}
, that is

x_{i}=\hat{\sigma}_{i}-N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{x}_{i}=\overline{\sigma}_{i}-N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\overline{\sigma}_{j}.

Remark that the set of misclassified components is
{\cal M}=\{i\in[N]:\text{sign}(v_{i})\neq\text{sign}(\overline{v}_{i})\}
. Note that the coordinates of
v
and
\overline{v}
have different signs in
{\cal M}
. As a consequence, for all
i\in{\cal M}
,

|v_{i}-\overline{v}_{i}|\geq|\overline{v}_{i}|\geq|b_{+}-b_{-}|(r_{+}^{N}\wedge r_{-}^{N}),

and so
\|v-\overline{v}\|_{2}^{2}\geq|\mathcal{M}|\cdot|b_{+}-b_{-}|^{2}\cdot(r_{+}^{N}\wedge r_{-}^{N})^{2}
. Yet, by triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities,

\displaystyle\|v-\overline{v}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}-N^{-1}\langle\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma},1_{N}\rangle 1_{N}\|_{2}

(59)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}\|_{2}+N^{-1}|\langle\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma},1_{N}\rangle|\cdot\|1_{N}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}\|_{2}+N^{-1}\|\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}\|_{2}\cdot\|1_{N}\|_{2}^{2}=2\|\hat{\sigma}-\overline{\sigma}\|_{2}.

To conclude, recall that
\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{+},\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{-})=|\mathcal{M}|/N
.

6
Simulation study

The numeric experiments were made using Julia programming language and are available in the package
MeanFieldGraph.jl
, as well as the material used to produce the figures below.

The simulation framework follows the one of
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost (2026)
. In particular, we consider the parameter
\beta=\mu/\lambda
instead of
\mu
because the set of admissible values of
\beta
is independent of
\lambda
(which is not the case for
\mu
). If not specified otherwise, the following parameter values are used:

N=50,\quad r_{+}=.5,\quad\beta=.5,\quad\lambda=.5,\quad p=.5,\quad N_{\rm simu}=1000.

Furthermore, the fractions of excitatory and inhibitory components are chosen as
r_{+}^{N}=\lceil r_{+}N\rceil
and
r_{-}^{N}=N-|\mathcal{P}_{+}|
.

The performance of any community detection method is evaluated via two metrics: the probability of exact recovery
(
PER =
I\!\!P(\{\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+}={\cal P_{+}}\}\cap\{\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-}={\cal P_{-}}\})
)
and the mean misclassification rate
(
MMR =
I\!\!E[\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+},\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-})]
)
, recall the definition of the misclassification rate
\operatorname{MR}(\widehat{{\cal P}}_{+},\widehat{{\cal P}}_{-})
given in (
20
).
Both metrics are estimated via Monte Carlo method (
N_{\rm simu}
indicates the number of simulations).

We evaluate 8 methods, obtained as the combination of the two methods (aggregated and spectral) developed in the paper with four clustering algorithms (
k
-means, mean threshold and hierarchical clustering with two linkage functions).
Before comparing the performance of the different methods in terms of PER and MMR, we highlight that the spectral method is computationally more expensive than the aggregated method. Indeed, the spectral method requires estimating the full 1-lagged covariance matrix rather than just its column sums and finding its leading singular vector. For instance, with
N=100
and
T=10000
, the computation of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm sp}
takes around
130
ms, whereas
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
takes only
0.5
ms.

Tables
1
and
2
give a comparison of these 8 methods in terms of PER and MMR respectively.
We selected specific couples
(N,T)
to produce the tables.
The aggregated method combined with mean threshold clustering (denoted by ag_threshold in the following) is always among the best methods. This is also true for the full dataset. To be exhaustive, let us mention that, out of 420 couples
(N,T)
, there are:

•

Lemmas based on Hoeffding inequality

The two lemmas stated here rely on Hoeffding concentration inequality.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\theta_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
be i.i.d. entries distributed as
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
and
A,B\subset[N]
be two subsets. Then,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[N]}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A}(\theta_{ij}-p)\right|\geq\sqrt{\frac{|A|\log(2N/\delta)}{2N^{2}}}\right)\leq\delta,

(60)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,j\in[N]:\ i\neq j}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}-p^{2}\right|\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\delta)}{2N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(61)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i,j\in[N]:\ i\neq j}\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in A\times B}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})\right|\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\delta)}{2N}}\right)\leq\delta.

(62)

The independent and centered random variables
\theta_{ij}-p
have range equal to 1 so Hoeffding inequality combined with the union bound gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[N]}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in B}(\theta_{ik}-p)\right|\geq z\right)\leq 2Ne^{-2N^{2}z^{2}/|A|}.

Taking
z=(|A|\log(2N/\delta)/(2N^{2}))^{1/2}
proves (
60
).

The proofs of the two last statements are similar. We only prove the last one which is a bit more complex.
Let
i\neq j
. First of all, observe that we can write

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in A\times B}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in A}\theta_{ik}\right)\sum_{\ell\in B}\frac{(\theta_{j\ell}-p)}{N}+\frac{p|B|}{N}\sum_{k\in A}\frac{(\theta_{ik}-p)}{N}.

Next, using that
(|A|\vee|B|)/N\leq 1
,
0\leq p\leq 1
and
|\theta_{mn}|\leq 1
, we obtain from the previous inequality the following upper bound

\max_{i,j\in[N]:i\neq j}\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in A\times B}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})\right|\leq\max_{j\in[N]}\left|\sum_{\ell\in B}\frac{(\theta_{j\ell}-p)}{N}\right|+\max_{i\in[N]}\left|\sum_{k\in A}\frac{(\theta_{ik}-p)}{N}\right|.

Using Equation (
60
), the union bound and substituting
\delta
by
\delta/2
conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Remind that
D
is defined in Equation (

). There exists a constant
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that for all
b\in\{-,+\}
,
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|L^{N,\bullet b}-bpr_{b}1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(63)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|L^{N}-p(r_{+}-r_{-})1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(64)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|C^{N}-p(1_{\mathcal{P}_{+}}-1_{\mathcal{P}_{-}})\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(65)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|\ell^{N,\bullet b}-\left(1_{\mathcal{P}_{b}}+\frac{b(1-\lambda)pr_{b}}{1-D}1_{N}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(66)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|\ell^{N}-\frac{1}{1-D}1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(67)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|m^{N}-m1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta,

(68)

\displaystyle I\!\!P\left(\left\|v^{N}-m(1-m)1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta.

(69)

Remark A.3
.

The asymptotics above are related to
(
Chevallier, Löcherbach and
Ost, 2026
, Lemmas F.1. and F.4.)
. There are three differences: 1) control in expectation there is replaced by control in probability here, 2) the 2-norm there is replaced by the infinity one here, 3) the rate of order 1 there is replaced by a rate of order
(\log(N)/N)^{1/2}
here (remind the natural upper bound
\|x\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}\|x\|_{\infty}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
).

Let us start with the proof of (
63
). By definition, for all
i\in[N]
and
b\in\{-,+\}
,

L^{N,\bullet b}_{i}-bpr_{b}^{N}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}(A^{N}_{ij}-bp)=\frac{b}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}(\theta_{ij}-p).

Hence, Lemma
A.1
and the fact that
r^{N}_{b}\leq 1
, imply that

I\!\!P\left(\left\|L^{N,\bullet b}-bpr^{N}_{b}1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log(2N/\delta)}{2N}}\right)\leq\delta.

Combining the above inequality with the fact that
|r^{N}_{b}-r_{b}|\leq KN^{-1}
, and then adjusting the constant
K
suitably, we obtain (
63
).
The proofs of Equations (
64
) and (
65
) follow the same lines and are therefore omitted.

We now turn to (
67
). Let us denote
\varepsilon^{N}=L^{N}-p(r_{+}-r_{-})1_{N}
and remind that
\ell^{N}=Q^{N}1_{N}
. Starting from
1_{N}=Q^{N}[I_{N}-(1-\lambda)A^{N}]1_{N}
, one gets that

\ell^{N}=1_{N}+(1-\lambda)Q^{N}L^{N}=1_{N}+(1-\lambda)Q^{N}[p(r_{+}-r_{-})1_{N}+\varepsilon^{N}],

\ell^{N}=\frac{1}{1-D}\left(1_{N}+(1-\lambda)Q^{N}\varepsilon^{N}\right).

\left\|\ell^{N}-\frac{1}{1-D}1_{N}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1-\lambda}{1-D}\vvvert Q^{N}\vvvert_{\infty}\|\varepsilon^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1-\lambda}{\lambda(1-D)}\|\varepsilon^{N}\|_{\infty},

since
\vvvert Q^{N}\vvvert_{\infty}\leq\lambda^{-1}
by Equation (

67
) then follows from (

).

Along the same lines, one can prove that

\ell^{N,\bullet b}=1_{\mathcal{P}_{b}}+b(1-\lambda)pr_{b}\ell^{N}+(1-\lambda)Q^{N}\varepsilon^{N,\bullet b},

with
\varepsilon^{N,\bullet b}=L^{N,\bullet b}-bpr_{b}^{N}1_{N}
,
and in turn that (
66
) follows from (
63
) and (
67
).

Starting from (

) and using the fact that

\frac{\mu}{1-D}1_{N}+1_{\mathcal{P}_{-}}-\left(1_{\mathcal{P}_{-}}-\frac{(1-\lambda)pr_{-}}{1-D}1_{N}\right)=m1_{N},

one easily deduces (

) from (
66
) and (
67
).

Finally, remark that

\|(m^{N})^{2}-m^{2}1_{N}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|m^{N}-m1_{N}\|_{\infty},

so that (

) follows from (

).

B
Convergence of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}

In this section, we prove the convergence of
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
towards
\sigma^{\rm ag}
stated in the result below.

Theorem B.1
.

There exist positive constants
K_{1},K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending on
\lambda
such that for all
N\geq 2
and
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
,

I\!\!P_{\theta}\left(\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-\sigma^{\rm ag}\|_{\infty}\geq K_{1}\frac{N\log(TN)}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\leq K_{2}T^{-1},

provided that
T\geq K_{3}\log(N)
.

Note that Theorem
B.1
holds for all realizations of the random matrix
\theta
. Clearly, integrating with respect to
\theta
leads to a similar result with respect to the probability measure
I\!\!P
, under which the environment is random.
The proof relies on
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
, Theorem 3)
to control the convergence of the empirical mean and empirical covariance.

Along this proof, we use the following notation: for a vector
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\overline{v}
denotes the spatial mean
\overline{v}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}
.
First of all, observe that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\rm ag}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Sigma^{(1)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\cov_{\theta}(X_{i,1},X_{j,0})

\displaystyle=N\cov_{\theta}(\overline{X_{1}},X_{j,0})=N\left(I\!\!E_{\theta}(\overline{X_{1}}X_{j,0})-\overline{m^{N}}m^{N}_{j}\right),

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\overline{X}_{t}X_{j,t-1}-N\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}.

Hence,

\|\sigma^{\rm ag}-\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\|_{\infty}=N\max_{j\in[N]}\left|\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\overline{X}_{t}X_{j,t-1}-I\!\!E_{\theta}(\overline{X_{1}}X_{j,0})+\overline{m^{N}}m^{N}_{j}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}\right|.

By the triangle inequality and the facts that
\overline{m}^{N}\leq 1
and
T^{-1}Z_{j,T}\leq 1
, we have that

\max_{j\in[N]}\left|\overline{m^{N}}m^{N}_{j}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}\right|\leq\max_{j\in[N]}\left|m^{N}_{j}-\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}\right|+\left|\overline{m^{N}}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\right|,

which together with the previous inequality gives

\|\sigma^{\rm ag}-\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}\|_{\infty}\leq N\max_{j\in[N]}\left|\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\overline{X}_{t}X_{j,t-1}-I\!\!E_{\theta}(\overline{X_{1}}X_{j,0})\right|\\
+N\max_{j\in[N]}\left|m^{N}_{j}-\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}\right|+N\left|\overline{m^{N}}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\right|.

(70)

Now, we use Theorem 3 of
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
)
to control each terms in the right hand side above.

First, for each
j\in[N]
, let
f_{j}:\{0,1\}^{N}\to\{0,1\}
be the projection on the
j
-th coordinate:
f_{j}(x)=x_{j}
and denote

V(f_{j})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_{j}(X_{t})-I\!\!E_{\theta}(f_{j}(X_{t})))=\frac{Z_{j,T}}{T}-m^{N}_{j}.

By Theorem 3 of
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
)
with
m=M=1
,
{\cal F}=\{f_{j}:j\in[N]\}
,
F=[N]
and
\log(1/(1-\lambda))/2
as their
\theta
, we have that there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that for all realization of
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N},

I\!\!P_{\theta}\left(\cup_{j\in[N]}\left\{|V(f_{j})|>\sqrt{C_{1}\frac{(1+\log(TN))\xi}{T}}\right\}\right)\leq C_{2}T^{-1}+2Ne^{-\xi},

(71)

for all
\xi>0
and
T
such that

T\geq 1+\frac{2}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}(2\log(T)+\log(N)).

Since,
\left|\overline{m^{N}}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\right|\leq\max_{j\in[N]}|Z_{j,T}/T-m^{N}_{j}|
, Inequality (
71
) implies that

I\!\!P_{\theta}\left(\left|\overline{m^{N}}-\frac{\overline{Z}_{T}}{T}\right|\leq\sqrt{C_{1}\frac{(1+\log(TN))\xi}{T}}\right)\geq 1-(C_{2}T^{-1}+2Ne^{-\xi}),

(72)

for all realization of
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
.

Second, let us define
g_{j}:\{0,1\}^{N}\times\{0,1\}^{N}\to\{0,1\}
for each
j\in[N]
as
g_{j}(x,y)=x_{j}\overline{y}
and denote

\displaystyle W(g_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(g_{j}(X_{t-1},X_{t})-I\!\!E_{\theta}(g_{j}(X_{-1},X_{t})))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(T-1)}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(\overline{X}_{t}X_{j,t-1}-I\!\!E_{\theta}(\overline{X}_{1}X_{j,0})\right).

By Theorem 3 of
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
)
with
M=1
,
m=2
,
{\cal F}=\{g_{j}:j\in[N]\}
,
F=[N]
and
\log(1/(1-\lambda))/2
as their
\theta
, there exist constants
C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{4}>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that

I\!\!P_{\theta}\left(\cup_{j\in[N]}\left\{W(g_{j})\leq\sqrt{C^{\prime}_{1}\frac{(1+\log(TN))\xi}{T}}\right\}\right)\leq 1-(C^{\prime}_{2}T^{-1}+2Ne^{-\xi}),

(73)

for all realization of
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
,
\xi>0
and
T
such that

T\geq 2+\frac{2}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}(2\log(T)+\log(N)).

Putting together Equations (
70
)-(
73
) with
\xi=\log(TN)
, we show that the conclusion of the theorem is true for all
N\geq 2
and
T\geq C_{3}\log(TN)
for some
C_{3}
depending only on
\lambda
, with
K_{i}
depending on
C_{i}
and
C^{\prime}_{i}
, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
, which in turn implies that
K_{i}
depends only on
\lambda
.
Hence, to conclude the proof, it remains to show that the conclusion of the theorem is also true for all
N\geq 2
and
T\geq K_{3}\log(N)
for some
K_{3}
depending only on
\lambda
. To see that, take
T_{0}=T_{0}(C_{3})
such that
T/2\geq C_{3}\log(T)
for all
T\geq T_{0}
, define
K_{3}=(T_{0}2C_{3})/\log(2)
, and observe that if
T\geq K_{3}\log(N)>T_{0}
then
T=T/2+T/2\geq C_{3}\log(T)+(K_{3}/2)\log(N)>C_{3}\log(T)+(T_{0}C_{3}/\log(2))\log(N)>C_{3}\log(T)+C_{3}\log(N)
.
∎

C.1
Vectorization and Kronecker product

Two mathematical objects are known to be very useful to deal with Stein-type matrix equations: vectorization and Kronecker product. Here are their definition and useful properties.

The
vectorization

\operatorname{vec}(M)
of a
N
-by-
N
matrix
M
is the
N^{2}
-dimensional vector constructed column by column. More precisely,
M_{ij}
associated with its
k
-th coordinate, where
k=i+(j-1)N
with
i,j\in[N]
.
With slight abuse of notation, will write
\operatorname{vec}(M)_{ij}
to refer to the coordinate
k=i+(j-1)N
of the vector
\operatorname{vec}(M)
.
Recall that the
\operatorname{vec}
operator is an invertible linear operator on the set of all matrices with real value entries.
Given two
N
-by-
N
matrices
A
and
B
, we denote
A\bigotimes B
the
Kronecker product
between
A
and
B
. It has the following block representation:

{A}\otimes{B}={\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}{B}&\cdots&A_{1n}{B}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{m1}{B}&\cdots&A_{mn}{B}\end{bmatrix}}.

More precisely, this is a
N^{2}
-by-
N^{2}
matrix having value
A_{ik}B_{j\ell}
associated with entry
(u,v)
, where
u=N(i-1)+j
and
v=N(k-1)+\ell
with
i,j,k,\ell\in[N]
. With slight abuse of notation, will write
(A\bigotimes B)_{ij,k\ell}
to refer to the entry
(u,v)=(N(i-1)+j,N(k-1)+\ell)
of the matrix
A\bigotimes B
.

Their most useful property regarding matrix equations is that, for all
N
-by-
N
matrices
A,B,M
,

\operatorname{vec}(AMB)=\left(B^{\top}\bigotimes A\right)\operatorname{vec}(M).

(74)

Furthermore, remark that
\vvvert M\vvvert_{\max}=\|\operatorname{vec}(M)\|_{\infty}
.

The main idea is to apply the vectorization operator to the matrix equation of Proposition
3.2
. In order to emphasize the difference between vectors of size
N
(for instance
m^{N}
,
v^{N}
) or size
N^{2}
(for instance
\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma^{(0)})
), we use bold notation for the latter. The same convention is used for
N^{2}
-by-
N^{2}
matrices.
Let us then introduce the notation:

\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}:=\operatorname{vec}(\Sigma^{(0)}),\ \mathbf{v}^{N}:=\operatorname{vec}(\diag(v^{N})),\ {\mathbf{1}^{N}:=\operatorname{vec}(\diag(1_{N}))},\ \mathbf{a}^{N}:=\operatorname{vec}(a^{N})

(75)

and
\mathbf{I}
denotes the
N^{2}
-by-
N^{2}
identity matrix. Obviously, the abuse of notation described above for the coordinates of the vectorization and Kronecker product is inherited to the bold notation introduced here.

According to Equation (
74
), the starting idea to tackle the matrix equation satisfied by
\Sigma^{(0)}
is to consider its vectorization and the Kronecker product
(1-\lambda)^{2}(A^{N}\bigotimes A^{N})
. Here is a slight modification in order to tackle the
\d 0
operator.
Let
\mathbf{L}=(\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell})_{i,j,k,\ell\in[N]}
be the
N^{2}
-by-
N^{2}
matrix defined as

\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell}=\begin{cases}(1-\lambda)^{2}(A^{N}\bigotimes A^{N})_{ij,k\ell},\ \text{if}\ i\neq j,\ k,\ell\in[N]\\
0,\ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In particular, remark that

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}},\,i\in[N],\quad[\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}]_{ii}=0.

(76)

Moreover,
\vvvert\mathbf{L}\vvvert_{\infty}\leq(1-\lambda)^{2}<1
so that
\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L}
is invertible. Finally, remark that the expectation of
\mathbf{L}
is given by

I\!\!E\left[\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell}\right]=\begin{cases}((1-\lambda)pN^{-1})^{2},\ \text{if}\ i\neq j,\ (k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{+}^{2}\cup\mathcal{P}_{-}^{2},\\
-((1-\lambda)pN^{-1})^{2},\ \text{if}\ i\neq j,\ (k,\ell)\notin\mathcal{P}_{+}^{2}\cup\mathcal{P}_{-}^{2},\\
0,\ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Step 1.
Here we prove that

\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{v}^{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{L}^{k}\mathbf{v}^{N}.

Proof of Step 1.
By Proposition
3.2
and linearity of
\operatorname{vec}
, we have

\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}=(1-\lambda)^{2}\operatorname{vec}\left(\d 0\left(A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}\right)\right)+\mathbf{v}^{N}.

(77)

On the one hand, observe that for any
i,j\in[N]
with
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}\operatorname{vec}\left(\d 0\left(A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}\right)\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)^{2}[A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}A^{N}_{ik}\Sigma^{(0)}_{k\ell}A^{N}_{j\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell}\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}_{k\ell}=[\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}]_{ij}.

On the other hand, for
i=j
, the definition of
\d 0
implies

(1-\lambda)^{2}\operatorname{vec}\left(\d 0\left(A^{N}\Sigma^{(0)}(A^{N})^{\top}\right)\right)_{ii}=0=[\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}]_{ii},

where the second equality comes from (
76
). All in all, we have proved that

\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\Sigma^{(0)}}+\mathbf{v}^{N},

which, in turn, concludes Step 1.

The rest of the proof is devoted to the study of the iterates of the form
\mathbf{L}^{k}\mathbf{v}^{N}
. Since we expect that
\mathbf{L}
is close to
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbf{v}^{N}
is close to
m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N}
, let us look at the iterates
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]^{k}\mathbf{1}^{N}
as a preliminary step. It turns out that their study is simple since
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N}
is colinear to
\mathbf{a}^{N}
and
\mathbf{a}^{N}
is an eigenvector of
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]
as proven in Step 2 below.

Step 2.

Here we prove that

I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N}=\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}}{N}\mathbf{a}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N}=(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}\left((r_{+}^{N}-r_{-}^{N})^{2}-N^{-1}\right)\mathbf{a}^{N}.

Proof of Step 2.

Let
i,j\in[N]
. Let us first study
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N}
. On the one hand,
(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N})_{ii}=0=\mathbf{a}^{N}_{ii}
by (
76
) and the definition of
\mathbf{a}^{N}
. On the other hand, if
i\neq j
,

(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N})_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}I\!\!E\left[\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell}\right]\mathbf{1}^{N}_{k\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}I\!\!E\left[\mathbf{L}_{ij,kk}\right]=((1-\lambda)p)^{2}N^{-1}.

We now turn to
I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N}
. Again, we have
(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N})_{ii}=0=\mathbf{a}^{N}_{ii}
. To consider the terms with
i\neq j
, let us denote

•

k\leftrightarrow\ell
if and only if
(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{+}^{2}\cup\mathcal{P}_{-}^{2}
and
k\neq\ell
,

•

k\not\leftrightarrow\ell
if and only if
(k,\ell)\notin\mathcal{P}_{+}^{2}\cup\mathcal{P}_{-}^{2}
.

Remark that the two relations above are not complementary. Indeed,
k=\ell
does not satisfy
k\leftrightarrow\ell
nor
k\not\leftrightarrow\ell
. The cardinality of the sets
\{k\leftrightarrow\ell\}
and
\{k\not\leftrightarrow\ell\}
are respectively
N^{2}[(r_{+}^{N})^{2}+(r_{-}^{N})^{2}-1/N]
and
2N^{2}r_{+}^{N}r_{-}^{N}
. Hence, if
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(k,\ell)\in[N]^{2}:\ \ell\neq k}I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}_{ij,k\ell}]=\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}}{N^{2}}N^{2}\left([(r_{+}^{N})^{2}+(r_{-}^{N})^{2}-1/N]-2r_{+}^{N}r_{-}^{N}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}\left((r_{+}^{N}-r_{-}^{N})^{2}-N^{-1}\right)

which concludes the proof of Step 2.

According to Step 2 and the paragraph above it, we expect that the two following quantities are negligible:

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}:=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{v}^{N}-\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}m(1-m)}{N}\mathbf{a}^{N}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}:=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{a}^{N}-D^{2}\mathbf{a}^{N},

where we remind that
D=(1-\lambda)p(r_{+}-r_{-})
.

Step 3.
Here we prove that there exists a constant
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

I\!\!P\left(\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N^{3}}}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof of Step 3.

According to Step 2, we write and decompose
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}
as

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{v}^{N}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}](m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})=\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{v}^{N}-m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})+(\mathbf{L}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}])(m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})

On the one hand, since
(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}^{N})_{ii}=(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}^{N})_{ii}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{L}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}])(m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\neq j}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{L}_{ij,kk}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}_{ij,kk}]\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}}{N}\max_{i\neq j}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}-p^{2}\right|.

By Equation (
61
),

I\!\!P\left(\|(\mathbf{L}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}])(m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})\|_{\infty}\geq 2(1-\lambda)^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(4N/\delta)}{2N^{3}}}\right)\leq\delta.

(78)

On the other hand, since
(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{v}^{N})_{ii}=(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}^{N})_{ii}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{v}^{N}-m(1-m)\mathbf{1}^{N})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\neq j}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{L}_{ij,kk}\left(v_{k}^{N}-(m(1-m))\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)^{2}}{N^{2}}N\|v^{N}-m(1-m)1_{N}\|_{\infty}.

Using (
38
) and gathering with (
78
) conclude the proof of this step.

Step 4.
Here we prove that there exists a constant
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
N\geq 2
,

I\!\!P\left(\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}\|_{\infty}\geq K\sqrt{\frac{\log(N/\delta)}{N}}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof of Step 4.

We decompose
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}
as

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}=(\mathbf{L}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}])\mathbf{a}^{N}+\left(I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N}-D^{2}\mathbf{a}^{N}\right).

On the one hand, using Step 2, it is easy to check that

\|I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{a}^{N}-D^{2}\mathbf{a}^{N}\|_{\infty}=\left|(r_{+}^{N}-r_{-}^{N})^{2}-N^{-1}-(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2}\right|\leq KN^{-1}.

(79)

On the other hand, remind the notation
k\leftrightarrow\ell
and
k\not\leftrightarrow\ell
introduced in the proof of Step 2, and check that

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{L}-I\!\!E[\mathbf{L}])\mathbf{a}^{N}\|_{\infty}=(1-\lambda)^{2}\max_{i\neq j}\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k\leftrightarrow\ell}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k\not\leftrightarrow\ell}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})\right|.

By definition of
k\leftrightarrow\ell
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leftrightarrow\ell}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{+}^{2}}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})+\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{-}^{2}}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})-\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}-p^{2}),

\displaystyle\sum_{k\not\leftrightarrow\ell}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{+}\times\mathcal{P}_{-}}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2})+\sum_{(k,\ell)\in\mathcal{P}_{-}\times\mathcal{P}_{+}}(\theta_{ik}\theta_{j\ell}-p^{2}).

Using Lemma
A.1
on the five terms in the right hand side above and gathering with (
79
) conclude the proof of this step
(after adjusting the constant
K
to get a simpler form for the lower bound).

We are now in position to end the proof of the proposition. Let us write

\mathbf{L}\mathbf{v}^{N}=\frac{\zeta}{N}\mathbf{a}^{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{L}\mathbf{a}^{N}=D^{2}\mathbf{a}^{N}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2},

where
\zeta=(1-\lambda)^{2}p^{2}m(1-m)
. By standard linear algebra, we have
\mathbf{L}^{0}\mathbf{v}^{N}=\mathbf{v}^{N}
, and by induction for all
k\geq 2
,

\mathbf{L}^{k}\mathbf{v}^{N}=\frac{\zeta}{N}\left(D^{2(k-1)}\mathbf{a}^{N}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}D^{2\ell}\mathbf{L}^{(k-2)-\ell}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}\right)+\mathbf{L}^{k-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}.

In particular, denoting
\nu=\max\{D^{2},1-\lambda\}<1
and reminding that
\vvvert\mathbf{L}\vvvert_{\infty}\leq(1-\lambda)^{2}
, one has for all
k\geq 1
,

\left\|\mathbf{L}^{k}\mathbf{v}^{N}-\frac{\zeta}{N}D^{2(k-1)}\mathbf{a}^{N}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta(k-1)\nu^{k}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,2}\|_{\infty}}{N}+(1-\lambda)^{k-1}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{N,1}\|_{\infty}.

(80)

To conclude the proof, it suffices to remind the result of Step 1, remark that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}D^{2(k-1)}=1/(1-D^{2})
, use the summability of the sequence
(k\nu^{k})_{k\geq 0}
and the inequalities of Steps 3 and 4.
∎

D
Convergence of
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}

The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
\Sigma^{(1)}
be the matrix defined in (
2
) and
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
its empirical estimate defined in (
17
).
There exist positive constants
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
and
K_{3}
depending only on
\lambda
such that

I\!\!P_{\theta}\Bigg(\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\geq K_{1}\sqrt{\frac{N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}}\Bigg)\leq K_{2}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{N\log(T)}\Big),

(81)

for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
, as long as the pair
(N,T)
satisfies
N\geq 2
and

T\geq(K_{3}N\log(T)\log(N\log(T)))\vee 2.

An important feature of this result is that, like Theorem
B.1
, it also holds for all realizations of the random matrix
\theta
. By integrating (
81
) with respect to
\theta
, we deduce that the conclusion of Theorem
D.1
is also valid for the probability measure
I\!\!P
, under which the environment is random.

The starting point of the proof of Theorem
D.1
is the following observation.
Let
\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}
be the matrix defined as

\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top},

(82)

where
m^{N}
is defined in (
28
). The matrix
\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}
can be seen as an intermediate matrix between the covariance matrix
\Sigma^{(1)}
and its estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}
. Then, observe that

\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\leq\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}+\vvvert\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}.

(83)

Hence, the proof of Theorem
D.1
reduces to controlling each one of the terms on the RHS of (
83
).

The first term on the RHS of (
83
) is controlled by bounding the difference between
\widehat{m}
and the (conditional) mean vector
m^{N}
. This is done in Lemma
D.2
.

To control the second term,
the proof follows a similar strategy to that of Theorem
B.1
.
Nevertheless, a direct application of
(
Ost and Reynaud-Bouret, 2020
, Theorem 4)
would lead to a looser upper-bound here.
Indeed, their result relies on Hoeffding inequality, which requires no assumption on the covariances. Bernstein inequality is sharper in our case, since we know that the covariances are of order
N^{-1}
(Lemma
G.1
). Adapting their proof to our framework leads to Lemma
D.3
below. Finally, Lemma
D.4
is used to control a variance term appearing in Lemma
D.3
.

Lemma D.2
.

There exists a constant
K>0
depending on
\lambda
such that the following bounds hold for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
:

\displaystyle I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[\|\widehat{m}-m^{N}\|^{2}_{2}\right]\leq K\frac{N}{T}.

(84)

\displaystyle I\!\!P_{\theta}\Bigg(\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\geq\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}\Bigg)\leq K\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}.

(85)

The proof of (
84
) follows from the observation that

\displaystyle I\!\!E_{\theta}[\|\widehat{m}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}I\!\!E_{\theta}[|\widehat{m}_{i}-m^{N}_{i}|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{Var}_{\theta}\Big(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{i,t}\Big)=\frac{1}{T^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\text{Cov}_{\theta}\Big(X_{i,t},X_{i,s}\Big)

and
G.1
.

For any vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
u(T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}-\widehat{m})^{\top}=u(\widehat{m}-\widehat{m})^{\top}=0_{N\times N}
, where
0_{N\times N}
is the matrix with all entries equal to 0, so that we can write

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(X_{t+1}-\widehat{m})(X_{t}-\widehat{m})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-\widehat{m})^{\top}+(m^{N}-\widehat{m})\Big(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}-\widehat{m}\Big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}+\Big(\frac{1}{T}(X_{T+1}-X_{1})+(\widehat{m}-m^{N})\Big)(m^{N}-\widehat{m})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}+\frac{1}{T}(X_{T+1}-X_{1})(m^{N}-\widehat{m})^{\top}-(\widehat{m}-m^{N})(\widehat{m}-m^{N})^{\top}.

As a consequence, we obtain that

\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\|X_{T+1}-X_{1}\|_{2}\|m^{N}-\widehat{m}\|_{2}+\|m^{N}-\widehat{m}\|_{2}^{2}.

(86)

By combining (
84
), (
86
) with the chain of inequalities
\|X_{T+1}-X_{1}\|_{2}\leq\|X_{T+1}\|_{2}+\|X_{1}\|_{2}\leq 2\sqrt{N}
and then using Jensen inequality, we obtain for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
, that

\displaystyle I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{T}\sqrt{I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[\|\widehat{m}-m^{N}\|^{2}_{2}\right]}+I\!\!E_{\theta}\left[\|\widehat{m}-m^{N}\|^{2}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{K}N}{T^{3/2}}+K\frac{N}{T}\leq\frac{N}{T}(2\sqrt{K}+K).

The inequality above combined with Chebyshev inequality implies Inequality (
85
), concluding the proof.
∎

Combining a coupling result (Lemma
E.1
) with Matrix Bernstein inequality, we can show that
\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}
and
\Sigma^{(1)}
are also uniformly close in the operator norm.

Lemma D.3
.

Take
1<R\leq T
. For all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
\zeta>0
, the following inequality holds:

I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}>\frac{2NR}{T}+\zeta\Big)\leq TN(1-\lambda)^{R-2}+2NR\exp\left\{-\frac{\lfloor T/R\rfloor\zeta^{2}}{2(\sigma_{\theta}+2N\zeta/3)}\right\},

where
\sigma_{\theta}=\sigma_{\theta,0}\vee\sigma_{\theta,1}
and for
t\in\{0,1\}
,

\sigma_{\theta,t}=\vvvert I\!\!E_{\theta}\Big[\|X_{t}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}(X_{1-t}-m^{N})(X_{1-t}-m^{N})^{\top}\Big]-(G_{0}1_{\{t=0\}}+G_{1}1_{\{t=1\}})\vvvert_{2},

with
G_{0}=\Sigma^{(1)}(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}
and
G_{1}=(\Sigma^{(1)})^{\top}\Sigma^{(1)}
.

The proof of Lemma
D.3
is provided in Appendix
E
.
The lemma below is used to control the constant
\sigma_{\theta}
appearing in Lemma
D.3
.

Lemma D.4
.

For each
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
, let
\sigma_{\theta}
be as in Lemma
D.3
.
Then, there exists a constant
K>0
depending only on

such that

for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
.

We need to prove that there exists a constant
K>0
such that
\sigma_{\theta,t}\leq KN
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
t\in\{0,1\}
. We will only prove that
\sigma_{\theta,0}\leq KN
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
for some suitable constant
K>0
. The analysis for the case
t=1
is very similar.

In the rest of the proof, let
Y_{i,s}=X_{i,s}-I\!\!E_{\theta}[X_{i,s}]
for

and
s\in\mathbb{Z}
. Also, denote

.

For all

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[\|X_{0}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}(X_{1}-m^{N})(X_{1}-m^{N})^{\top}_{ij}\big]-G^{\top}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\text{Cov}_{\theta}[Y_{k,0}Y_{i,1},Y_{k,0}Y_{j,1}].

G.1
, we then deduce that for all

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[\|X_{0}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}(X_{1}-m^{N})(X_{1}-m^{N})^{\top}_{ij}\big]-G^{\top}_{ij}|

\displaystyle\leq N\Big(\frac{K}{N}\Big)=K,

(87)

for some constant
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
, and that for all

,

|d_{i}|\leq N,\ \text{where}\ d_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[\|X_{0}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}(X_{1}-m^{N})(X_{1}-m^{N})^{\top}_{ii}\big]-G^{\top}_{ii}.

(88)

Let
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{N})
and define

) and (

\sigma_{\theta,0}=\vvvert\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[\|X_{0}-m^{N}\|_{2}^{2}(X_{1}-m^{N})(X_{1}-m^{N})^{\top}\big]-G^{\top}\vvvert_{2}\leq\vvvert D\vvvert_{2}\\

We are now in position to prove Theorem
D.1
.

Combining (
83
), (

) and Lemmas

and

, we obtain that there exists a constant
K>0
depending only on
\lambda
such that for all
1<R\leq T
,
\zeta>0
and
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N},
it holds that

I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert\widehat{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\geq\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{2NR}{T}+\zeta\Big)\leq\Bigg(K\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+TN(1-\lambda)^{R-2}\\
+2NR\exp\Big\{-\frac{n\zeta^{2}}{2N(K+2\zeta/3)}\Big\}\Bigg),

(90)

where
n=\lfloor T/R\rfloor.

Let
R=2(1+\log(TN)/\log(1-\lambda)^{-1})
,
and
\zeta=\sqrt{\frac{8KN\log(2NR)}{n}}
and suppose that the pair
(N,T)
is such that
N\geq 2
and

T\geq K_{3}N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))\vee 2,

where
K_{3}>0
is a constant satisfying the following properties:

1.

K_{3}\log(2)\log(2\log(2))\geq 1
.

2.

K_{3}2\log(2)\log(2\log(2))\geq(1-\lambda)^{-1/2}\vee T_{1}\vee\exp(8/\log((1-\lambda)^{-1}))
,
where

.

3.

K_{3}\geq\frac{1024K^{3}}{9\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}

Claim.
For such a pair
(N,T)
, one can check that following properties holds:

a)

T\geq N
.

b)

R\leq 2R\leq 16\log(T)/\log((1-\lambda)^{-1}))\leq T
.

c)

N\log(T)\geq 16/\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})
.

d)

n\geq T\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})/(16\log(T))
.

\zeta\leq 3/(2K)
.

Proof of the Claim.
Item a) follows immediately from property 1.
Property 2 ensures that
T\geq(1-\lambda)^{-1/2}\vee T_{1}
. This, in turn, implies that
2\log(T)/\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})\geq 1
. Using this inequality and item a), we then deduce that

\displaystyle R\leq 2R

\displaystyle\leq 4(1+\frac{1}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}\log(TN))

\displaystyle\leq 4(1+\frac{2}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}\log(T))\leq\frac{16}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}\log(T).

Using the definition of
T_{1}
and the fact that
T\geq T_{1}
, we obtain the last inequality of item b).

Item c) follows from the fact that
N\geq 2
and

. As for item d), it follows from b) and the inequality
\lfloor z\rfloor\geq z/2
which is valid for all

. Finally, using first d), then b) and c), we have that

\displaystyle\zeta=\sqrt{\frac{8KN\log(2NR)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{128KN\log(T)\log(2NR)}{T\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{128KN\log(T)\log(\frac{16N\log(T)}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})})}{T\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{256KN\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}}.

Hence, using the fact that
T\geq K_{3}N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))
and Property 3, we deduce that

\zeta\leq\sqrt{\frac{256KN\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{256K}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})K_{3}}}\leq 3/(2K),

proving e).

We now conclude the proof using Claim 1.
By Items b) and e) of Claim 1, we have that
R\leq T
and
\zeta\leq 3/(2K)
, so that

\displaystyle K\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+TN(1-\lambda)^{R-2}

\displaystyle+2NR\exp\Big\{-\frac{n\zeta^{2}}{2N(K+2\zeta/3)}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq K\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{1}{TN}+\frac{1}{2NR}\leq(K+1)\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}{4N\log(TN)},

(91)

where in the second inequality we have also used that
2\log(TN)/\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})\leq R
.

Moreover, using the definition of
R
, a), b), d) and c), we can also show that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{2NR}{T}+\zeta

\displaystyle\leq 5\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{8}{\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}\frac{N\log(T)}{T}+\sqrt{\frac{8KN\log(2NR)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq 5\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{8N\log(T)}{T\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}+\sqrt{\frac{8KN\log(\frac{16N\log(T)}{\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}})16\log(T)}{T\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq 5\sqrt{\frac{N}{T}}+\frac{8N\log(T)}{T\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}+\sqrt{\frac{256KN\log(N\log(T))\log(T)}{T\log(1-\lambda)^{-1}}}

\displaystyle\leq K_{1}\sqrt{\frac{N\log(T)\log(N\log(T))}{T}},

where
K_{1}>0
depends only on
\lambda
and
K
.
Putting together (
90
), (
91
) and the above inequality, the result follows.
∎

E
Coupling and Concentration

The goal of this section is prove the concentration result stated in Lemma
D.3
. To that end, we will use the
coupling result below. In words, we couple our model of interest with a similar Markov chain that independently starts anew every
R
time steps.
We show that the coupling error vanishes exponentially with respect to
R
.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
R>1
and denote
{\cal R}=\{3+aR:a\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Take
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and let
(Z_{t})_{t\in{\cal R}}
be a sequence of i.i.d random vectors with distribution
\pi_{\theta}
, where
\pi_{\theta}
denotes the invariant distribution of the Markov chain with transition probability defined in (
13
), conditionally on
\theta
.
Let
(U_{i,t})_{i\in[N],t\geq 2}
be a sequence of i.i.d random variables uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, independent of everything else. Let
(X_{t},\tilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be the bi-variate stochastic chain defined in the following way: for
t=1
, set
\tilde{X}_{1}=X_{1}\sim\pi_{\theta}
and recursively for all
t\geq 2
and
i\in[N]
, define

X_{i,t}={1}_{\big\{U_{i,t}\leq p_{\theta,i}(X_{t-1})\big\}}\ \text{and}\ \tilde{X}_{i,t}={{\mathbf{1}}}_{\{t\not\in{\cal R}\}}{1}_{\big\{U_{i,t}\leq p_{\theta,i}(\tilde{X}_{t-1})\big\}}+{{\mathbf{1}}}_{\{t\in{\cal R}\}}Z_{i,t},

where
p_{\theta,i}(x)
is defined in (
1
). Then, the following properties hold:

1.

The chain
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a stationary version of Markov chain with transition probability defined in (
13
), conditionally on
\theta
.

2.

The random vectors
(\tilde{X}_{t-2},\tilde{X}_{t-1})_{t\in{\cal R}}
are independent.

3.

For all
t\in{\cal R}
,
(\tilde{X}_{t-2},\tilde{X}_{t-1})
is distributed as
(X_{t-2},X_{t-1})
. Also, if
X_{t-2}=\tilde{X}_{t-2}
, then
X_{t-1}=\tilde{X}_{t-1}
.

4.

Denoting
\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}
the joint distribution of
(X_{t},\tilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
built as above, then for all
t\in{\cal R},

\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\big(X_{t-2}\neq\tilde{X}_{t-2}\big)\leq N(1-\lambda)^{R-2}.

Items 1,2 and 3 follow from classical arguments of Markov chain theory. Hence, we only prove Item 4.

Throughout the proof, we write
I\!\!E_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}
to denote the expectation taken with respect to
\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}.

Note that by construction,
p_{\theta,i}(X_{t-1})
and
p_{\theta,i}(\tilde{X}_{t-1})
are independent from
U_{t,j},j\in[n]
for all
i\in[N]
and
t\geq 2
, so that for all
i\in[N]
and
t\not\in{\cal R},

\displaystyle I\!\!E_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t}-\tilde{X}_{i,t}|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\{X_{i,t}\neq\tilde{X}_{i,t}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\{U_{i,t}\mbox{ lies between }p_{\theta,i}(X_{t-1})\mbox{ and }p_{\theta,i}(\tilde{X}_{t-1})\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|p_{\theta,i}(X_{t-1})-p_{\theta,i}(\tilde{X}_{t-1})|].

(92)

Next using the definition of
p_{\theta,i}
, i.e. Equation (
1
),
we obtain that

\displaystyle|p_{\theta,i}(X_{t-1})-p_{\theta,i}(\tilde{X}_{t-1})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{ij}|X_{j,t-1}-\tilde{X}_{j,t-1}|.

Taking the expectation in both sides of the above inequality and then using (
92
), we deduce that for
t\not\in{\cal R}
and
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle I\!\!E_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t}-\tilde{X}_{i,t}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{j,t-1}-\tilde{X}_{j,t-1}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)\max_{j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{j,t-1}-\tilde{X}_{j,t-1}|].

In the second inequality above, we have also used that
N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\theta_{ij}\leq 1
. Now, since
i\in[N]
is arbitrary, it follows that for
t\not\in{\cal R}
,

\max_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t}-\tilde{X}_{i,t}|]\leq(1-\lambda)\max_{j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{t-1,j}-\tilde{X}_{t-1,j}|].

Finally, take
t\in{\cal R}\setminus\{3\}
, we observe that
t-2-s\not\in{\cal R}
for all
s=0,\ldots,R-3
, so that applying the above inequality recursively yields

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t-2}-\tilde{X}_{i,t-2}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)^{(R-2)}\max_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t-2-(R-3)}-\tilde{X}_{i,t-2-(R-3)}|]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\lambda)^{(R-2)}.

which, in turn, implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\big(X_{t-2}\neq\tilde{X}_{t-2}\big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}}[|X_{i,t-2}-\tilde{X}_{i,t-2}|]\leq N(1-\lambda)^{R-2}.

For
t=3
, we have
X_{t-2}=X_{1}=\tilde{X}_{1}=\tilde{X}_{t-2}
by construction. Hence the result holds automatically also for
t=3
.
∎

Once we have Lemma
E.1
at our disposal, we are in position to prove Lemma
D.3
.

Let
n=\lfloor T/R\rfloor
. For each
1\leq r\leq R
, denote

\mathbb{T}_{r}=\{r+aR:a\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\}
and define

M_{r}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{r}}(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}-\Sigma^{(1)}.

Also, define

E=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=nR+1}^{T}\left((X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}-\Sigma^{(1)}\right),\ \text{if}\ nR<T,\\
0_{N\times N},\ \text{if}\ nR=T,\end{cases}

where
0_{N\times N}
is the
N
-by-
N
matrix with all entries equal to
0
.

With this notation, we can write

\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}=\frac{nR}{T}\Big(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{r}\Big)+E.

Since
nR/T\leq 1
we then deduce from the above identity that

\vvvert\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\leq\Big\vvvert\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{r}\Big\vvvert_{2}+\vvvert E\vvvert_{2}.

(93)

To deal with the second term on the RHS of (
93
), we first observe that for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\vvvert(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}\vvvert_{2}\leq N\vvvert(X_{t+1}-m^{N})(X_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}\vvvert_{\max}\leq N,

which also implies that
\vvvert\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}\leq N
, and then combine these inequalities with the fact that
T-nR\leq R
to obtain
\vvvert E\vvvert_{2}\leq 2NR/T.

To deal with the first term on the RHS of (
93
), we proceed as follows. By the union bound and the stationarity of the chain conditionally on
\theta
, we have that for all
\zeta>0
and
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
,

I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\Big\vvvert\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{r}\Big\vvvert_{2}>\zeta\Big)\leq\sum_{r=1}^{R}I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert M_{r}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta\Big)=RI\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert M_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta\Big).

Putting together (
93
) and the above upper bounds, we obtain for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
\zeta>0
,

I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert\tilde{\Sigma}^{(1)}-\Sigma^{(1)}\vvvert_{2}>\frac{2NR}{T}+\zeta\Big)\leq I\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\Big\vvvert\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{r}\Big\vvvert_{2}>\zeta\Big)\leq RI\!\!P_{\theta}\Big(\vvvert M_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta\Big).

(94)

Let
(\tilde{X}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
the chain built in Lemma
E.1
and define

\tilde{M}_{1}=n^{-1}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{1}}(\tilde{X}_{t+1}-m^{N})(\tilde{X}_{t}-m^{N})^{\top}-\Sigma^{(1)}.

By Items 1, 3 and 4 of Lemma
E.1
, we have that

\displaystyle I\!\!P_{\theta}(\vvvert M_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}(\vvvert M_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}(\vvvert\tilde{M}_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta)+\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}\Big(\cup_{t\in\mathbb{T}_{1}}\{Y_{t}\neq X_{t}\}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}(\vvvert\tilde{M}_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta)+nN(1-\lambda)^{R-2}.

(95)

Items 2 and 3 of Lemma
E.1
imply that the matrix
\tilde{M}_{1}
is a sum of i.i.d random matrices. Therefore, we can apply matrix Bernstein inequality
(
Tropp, 2015
, Theorem 6.1.1)
, to deduce that

\mathbb{Q}_{\theta}(\vvvert\tilde{M}_{1}\vvvert_{2}>\zeta)\leq 2N\exp\left\{-\frac{n\zeta^{2}}{2(\sigma_{\theta}+2N\zeta/3)}\right\}.

Combining (
94
), (
95
) and the above inequality, the result follows.
∎

F
Minimax lower bounds

F.1
Misclassification rate

The goal of this section is to establish the minimax lower bound for the misclassification rate stated in Theorem
2.12
.

Throughout this section, we denote
N_{+}=\lceil Nr_{+}\rceil
and
N_{-}=N-N_{+}
. In what follows, we will assume that
1/2\leq r_{+}<1
. The analysis is similar for the case
0<r_{+}<1/2
.
For later use, let us observe that
N_{+}\geq N/2\geq N_{-}
under the regime
1/2\leq r_{+}<1
.

Recall that
{\cal S}=\{S\subset[N]:\ |S|=N_{+}\}
. In the sequel, each subset
S\in{\cal S}
plays the role of the community
\mathcal{P}_{+}
.
Recall that for each
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
S\in{\cal S}
, we write
I\!\!P_{\theta,S}
to denote the probability measure under which the sequence of random vectors
X=(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is distributed as
our model of interest, where the communities
({\cal P}_{+},{\cal P}_{-})=(S,S^{c}).
In particular, for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, it holds that

P_{\theta,S}(y|x):=I\!\!P_{\theta,S}(X_{t}=y|X_{t-1}=x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(p_{\theta,S,i}(x))^{y_{i}}(1-p_{\theta,S,i}(x))^{1-y_{i}},

(96)

where for each
i\in[N]
,

p_{\theta,S,i}(x)=\mu+\frac{(1-\lambda)}{N}\Big(\sum_{j\in S}\theta_{ij}x_{j}+\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\theta_{ij}(1-x_{j})\Big).

(97)

Moreover, let
\pi_{\theta,S}
denote the unique stationary distribution of the sequence
X
under
I\!\!P_{\theta,S}
, that is, the distribution such that
\pi_{\theta,S}(y)=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\pi_{\theta,S}(x)P_{\theta,S}(y|x)
for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, where
P_{\theta,S}(y|x)
is the transition matrix defined in (
96
).

Furthermore, let
I\!\!P_{S}
denote the probability measure under which
\theta=(\theta_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
is an i.i.d. sequence of random variables with distribution
\text{Ber}(p)
and the sequence
X
is distributed according
I\!\!P_{\theta,S}
conditionally on
\theta
, i.e.,
I\!\!P_{S}(X\in\cdot|\theta)=I\!\!P_{S,\theta}(X\in\cdot)
.
Also, for
T\geq 1
, we denote
I\!\!P_{\theta,S}^{(1:T)}
and
I\!\!P_{S}^{(1:T)}
the law of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{T})
under
I\!\!P_{\theta,S}
and
I\!\!P_{S}
respectively.

Finally, we note that
|S\setminus V|=|V\setminus S|
for any
S,V\in\mathcal{S}
. To see this, first write
|S|=|S\setminus V|+|S\cap V|
and
|V|=|V\setminus S|+|S\cap V|
and then use the fact that
|V|=N_{+}=|S|.

The key technical step of this section is to study the modifications of our model as
S
varies in
\mathcal{S}
. These modifications are measured in terms of total variation distance
d_{TV}
or Kullback-Leibler divergence.

Recall that for each
K>0
and
u\geq 0
, we denote
\tau_{K}(u)=\sqrt{1-\exp(-Ku)}
.
The main step in deriving the lower bound stated in Theorem
2.12
is to prove the following result.

Proposition F.1
.

Let
T\geq 0
be an integer. There exist positive constants
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
depending only on
\mu
and
\lambda
such that

d_{TV}\Big(I\!\!P_{S}^{(1:T+1)},I\!\!P_{V}^{(1:T+1)}\Big)\leq\tau_{K_{1}}\Big(\frac{T}{N}\Big)+2\tau_{K_{2}}\Big(N^{-1}\Big\lceil\frac{\log(2N)}{\log((1-\lambda)^{-1})}\Big\rceil\Big).

for all
S,V\in\mathcal{S}
such that
|S\setminus V|=1
.

To show Proposition
F.1
, we will use three auxiliary results which involve the Kullback-Leibler divergence denoted by
\operatorname{KL}(\cdot\,\|\,\cdot)
.

Lemma F.2
.

There exists a positive constant
K
depending only
\mu
and
\lambda
such that

\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|x)\big)\leq KN^{-1}.

for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
,
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
S,V\in\mathcal{S}
such that
|S\setminus V|=1
.

Fix
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
,
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
and
S,V\in\mathcal{S}
such that
|S\setminus V|=1
.
Using that
P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|x)=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\text{Ber}(p_{\theta,S,i}(x))
and
P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|x)=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\text{Ber}(p_{\theta,V,i}(x))
, we can check that

\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|x)\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{KL}\big(\text{Ber}(p_{\theta,S,i}(x))\,\big\|\,\text{Ber}(p_{\theta,V,i}(x))\big).

(98)

Next, using that for all
p,q\in(0,1)
,

\operatorname{KL}\big(\text{Ber}(p)\,\big\|\,\text{Ber}(q)\big)\leq\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{q(1-q)},

and that, since
0<\mu\leq p_{\theta,S,i}(x)\leq 1-(\lambda-\mu)<1
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
,
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
i\in[N]
,

\min_{W\in\{S,V\}}\min_{i\in[N]}p_{\theta,W,i}(x)(1-p_{\theta,W,i}(x))\geq C:=\min\{\mu(1-\mu),(\lambda-\mu)(1-(\lambda-\mu))\}>0,

we obtain from (
98
) that

\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|x)\big)\leq\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(p_{\theta,S,i}(x)-p_{\theta,V,i}(x)\big)^{2}.

(99)

Now, since
|V\setminus S|=|S\setminus V|=1
, we have that

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{j\in S}\theta_{ij}x_{j}-\sum_{j\in V}\theta_{ij}x_{j}\Bigg|

\displaystyle=\Bigg|\sum_{j\in S\setminus V}\theta_{ij}x_{j}-\sum_{j\in V\setminus S}\theta_{ij}x_{j}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in S\setminus V}|\theta_{ij}x_{j}|+\sum_{j\in V\setminus S}|\theta_{ij}x_{j}|\leq|S\setminus V|+|V\setminus S|=2.

(100)

Similarly, we also have that

\Bigg|\sum_{j\in S^{c}}\theta_{ij}(1-x_{j})-\sum_{j\in V^{c}}\theta_{ij}(1-x_{j})\Bigg|\leq 2.

(101)

Combining (
97
), (
100
) and (
101
), we then deduce that

|p_{\theta,S,i}(x)-p_{\theta,V,i}(x)|\leq\frac{4(1-\lambda)}{N}.

Finally, using the above inequality in (
99
), we obtain that

\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|x)\big)\leq\frac{1}{C}N\Big(16(1-\lambda)^{2}N^{-2}\Big)=\frac{16(1-\lambda)^{2}}{C}N^{-1}.

∎

In the sequel, let us denote

\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|x}(y)=I\!\!P_{\theta,S}(X_{t}=y|X_{0}=x),

for all
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{N},S\in{\cal S},t\geq 0
and
\theta\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
.

There exists a constant

\displaystyle d_{TV}\Big(\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|x},\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|y}\Big)\leq N(1-\lambda)^{t}{{\mathbf{1}}}_{\{x\neq y\}},

,

,

.

,

and

).

Clearly, the chains
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
and
(\widehat{X}_{t})_{t\geq 1}

y

Moreover, denoting

\mathbb{Q}_{\theta,S,y,x}\Big(X_{t}\neq\widehat{X}_{t}\Big)\leq N(1-\lambda)^{t}{{\mathbf{1}}}_{\{x\neq y\}}.

Now, since

d_{TV}\Big(\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|x},\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|y}\Big)\leq\mathbb{Q}_{\theta,S,y,x}\Big(X_{t}\neq\widehat{X}_{t}\Big),

and
S\in\mathcal{S}
,
\theta
,

,

and

Hence, it only remains to prove (

The key step to prove (

Next, using (

Then, using now (

\operatorname{KL}\big(\nu^{(t,t+1)}_{\theta,S|x}\,\|\,\nu^{(t,t+1)}_{\theta,V|x}\big)=\operatorname{KL}\big(\nu^{(t+1)}_{\theta,S|x}\,\|\,\nu^{(t+1)}_{\theta,V|x}\big)+\\

(108)

Combining (

) and using the positivity of the Kullback-Leibler divergence, it then follows that

\leq\operatorname{KL}\big(\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|x}\,\|\,\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,V|x}\big)+\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\nu^{(t)}_{\theta,S|x}(y)\operatorname{KL}\big(P_{\theta,S}(\cdot|y)\,\|\,P_{\theta,V}(\cdot|y)\big).

Applying Lemma

In this section, we collect some remarks concerning

2.7
. In what follows, for any vector

v_{(1)}\leq v_{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq v_{(N)}

Recall the definition of the vector
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
given in (

Lemma H.1
.

Consider the vectors
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
and
\overline{\sigma}^{\rm ag}
defined in (
11
) and (

) respectively. There are at most
N-1
possible outcomes for
k
-means clustering applied to
\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}

. Moreover, these outcomes have the form

Furthermore, for all

For all

\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-(\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}}1_{{S^{(k)}_{+}}}+\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}}1_{{S^{(k)}_{-}}})\|^{2}_{2}>\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-(\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{+}}1_{{S^{(k+1)}_{+}}}+\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{-}}1_{{S^{(k+1)}_{-}}})\|^{2}_{2}.

(115)

2.

For all

\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-(\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}}1_{{S^{(k)}_{+}}}+\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}}1_{{S^{(k)}_{-}}})\|^{2}_{2}<\|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}-(\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{+}}1_{{S^{(k+1)}_{+}}}+\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{-}}1_{{S^{(k+1)}_{-}}})\|^{2}_{2}.

(116)

It is well known that the partition of
k
-means is a Voronoi diagram. In our case, Voronoi cells are intervals (dimension equals one) and the optimal partition of
k
-means, denoted by
(S_{+},S_{-})
, is of the form
S_{-}=\{i\in[N]:\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{i}\leq c\}

. This collection of partitions corresponds to
\{(S^{(k)}_{+},S^{(k)}_{-}):1\leq k\leq N-1\}

Hence, it remains to prove (
115
) and (

). Since both proofs are similar, we only prove (
115
). Therefore, in the rest of the proof, we fix
1\leq k<|{\cal P}_{-}|
.

Using that
\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j\in S}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-c)^{2}=\sum_{j\in S}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{S})^{2}
for any
S\subseteq[N]
and that
\sum_{j\in S^{(k)}_{+}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-c)^{2}-\sum_{j\in S^{(k+1)}_{+}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-c)^{2}=(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-c)^{2}
, for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
, one can check that

\sum_{j\in S^{(k)}_{+}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}})^{2}-\sum_{j\in S^{(k+1)}_{+}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{+}})^{2}\geq(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}})^{2}.

(117)

Arguing similarly, we can also check that

\sum_{j\in S^{(k+1)}_{-}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k+1)}_{-}})^{2}-\sum_{j\in S^{(k)}_{-}}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}})^{2}\leq(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}})^{2}.

(118)

Combining (
117
) and (
118
), we obtain that (
115
) holds, whenever

|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}}|>|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}}|.

(119)

To prove that (
119
) holds on the event
E
, we argue as follows. On the one hand,

|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{-}}|=\frac{1}{|S^{(k)}_{-}|}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(j)})\leq\epsilon.

(120)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{N}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(j)}-\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N-k}\sum_{j=|{\cal P}_{-}|+1}^{N}(\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(j)}-\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|{\cal P}_{+}|}{N-k}(\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}-2\varepsilon)\geq\frac{|{\cal P}_{+}|}{N-1}(\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}-2\varepsilon)>r^{N}_{+}(\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}-2\varepsilon).

Assuming that
N\geq N_{0}:=\lceil 2/(r_{+}\wedge r_{-})\rceil
so that
r_{+}^{N}\wedge r_{-}^{N}\geq(r_{+}\wedge r_{-})/2
, it gives

|\widehat{\sigma}^{\rm ag}_{(k+1)}-\widehat{\mu}_{S^{(k)}_{+}}|>\frac{(\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-}-2\varepsilon)r_{\rm min}}{2}.

Finally, combining the above inequality with (
120
), we see that (
119
) holds on the event
E
, for all
N\geq N_{0}
as soon as
\varepsilon\leq\frac{(\overline{\sigma}_{+}-\overline{\sigma}_{-})r_{\rm min}}{2(1+r_{\rm min})}=\frac{c_{1}pr_{\rm min}}{(1+r_{\rm min})}
.
∎
