Uncovering the role of tumor cGAS expression in predicting response to PD-1/L1 inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yuichi Ozawa, Yasuhiro Koh, Ryota Shibaki, Yuhei Harutani, Hiroaki Akamatsu, Atsushi Hayata, Takeya Sugimoto, Yuka Kitamura, Junya Fukuoka, Masanori Nakanishi, Nobuyuki Yamamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe impact of cGAS/STING tumor expression on PD-1/L1 inhibitor efficacy and the tumor microenvironment remain to be elucidated.MethodsIn a post-hoc analysis of a prospective biomarker study with 106 advanced NSCLC patients treated with PD-1/L1 inhibitors from December 2015 to September 2018, tumor tissue before treatment from 68 patients was analyzed. cGAS and STING expression were measured using immunohistochemical staining and H-scores. Additionally, 40 serum proteins were quantified before and 4–6 weeks after treatment initiation.ResultsMedian cGAS and STING H-scores were 220 (range, 5–300) and 190 (range, 0–300), respectively. There were no differences in cGAS or STING H-scores between the high (tumor proportion score [TPS] ≥ 50) and low (TPS < 50) PD-L1groups (p = 0.990 and 0.283, respectively). Unexpectedly, patients with high cGAS (H-score ≥ 220) demonstrated significantly shorter progression-free survival (PFS) of PD-1/L1 inhibitors when the PD-L1 TPS was high (median PFS: 143 days vs. not reached; p = 0.028); PFS at 18 months was 7% and 53% in the high and low cGAS groups, respectively while STING expression did not impact PFS. In serum protein analyses, high cGAS H-score was associated with significantly higher TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 before PD-1/L1 inhibition (47.5 vs. 22.3 ng/l, p = 0.023; 2118 vs. 882 pg/ml, p = 0.037); additionally, the cGAS H-score significantly correlated with TGF-β1 (r = 0.451, p = 0.009) and TGF-β2 (r = 0.375, p = 0.031) basal levels.ConclusioncGAS expression, but not STING, predicts poor PD-1/L1 inhibitor efficacy in NSCLC with high PD-L1, potentially due to a TGF-β-mediated immunosuppressive environment (UMIN000024414).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-024-03861-9.Keywords: Lung cancer, cGAS, STING, TGF-b, PD-L1, Immune checkpoint inhibitor

Introduction

Lung cancer, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) as its predominant form, remains a major cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. In recent years, the management of NSCLC has experienced significant advancements through the use of immunotherapies—especially PD-1/L1 inhibitors—offering renewed hope for patients. However, the efficacy of PD-1/L1 inhibitors varies substantially among patients, highlighting the need for reliable biomarkers to identify patients that are most likely to benefit from immunotherapy. The PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS), particularly when > 50%, is a valuable predictor of PD-1/L1 inhibitor efficacy [1–3]. However, relying solely on PD-L1 expression is not sufficient, making the exploration of additional predictive factors essential for accurately identifying patients who will benefit from immunotherapy.

The signaling pathway involving cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) plays a crucial role in detecting cytosolic double-stranded DNA and triggering the innate immune response. This cGAS/STING pathway is thought to be a key factor in the immune-driven initiation, progression, and spread of cancer [4, 5]. The DNA damage-induced accumulation of cytosolic DNA activates cGAS, leading to cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP) production. In tumors and antigen-presenting cells, cGAMP activates STING, initiating a kinase cascade involving IκB kinase (IKK) and TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). This cascade phosphorylates interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) [6–9], a transcription factor that regulates immune-stimulated genes and type 1 interferons (IFNs) [4, 10, 11]. Cytokines released from tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells can activate cytotoxic CD8 + T cells and regulate the differentiation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), downstream of this pathway [10, 12, 13].

Activation of STING and anti-PD-1/L1 combination therapy results in synergistic tumor growth inhibition in animal models of breast cancer and melanoma [14–17]. However, overactivation of STING results in the sustained production of cytokines, leading to an inflammatory tumor microenvironment that facilitates resistance to PD-1/L1 inhibitors [9] and promotes tumor growth [18, 19]. This strongly suggests a close link between the cGAS/STING pathway and the effectiveness of PD-1/L1 inhibitors. However, there are currently no reports on the relationship between cGAS/STING expression and the efficacy of PD-1/L1 inhibitors.

In this study, we semi-quantitatively analyzed the expression of cGAS and STING proteins in the tumor tissues of patients with NSCLC using immunohistochemistry, to explore their correlation with immunotherapy efficacy and cytokine production.

Materials and methods

Patients and materials

We enrolled 106 patients with advanced NSCLC, who were treated with PD-1/L1 inhibitors monotherapy and who provided written informed consent, in this prospective biomarker study in Wakayama Medical University between December 2015 and September 2018 (UMIN000024414). A post-hoc analysis was performed on 68 patients who consented to utilize residual tissue samples that were collected prior to PD-1/L1 inhibitor administration. Detailed information regarding the prospective biomarker study and initial analysis results have been reported previously [20–22]. Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients before PD-1/L1 inhibitor treatment, during weeks 4–6, and thereafter. The administration of PD-1/L1 inhibitors and assessment of their effectiveness and toxicity were determined by individual investigators. Effectiveness was evaluated through radiological examinations conducted every 6–8 weeks, using the RECIST version 1.1 criteria. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the duration from the initiation of PD-1/L1 inhibitors to the occurrence of disease progression or death from any cause. Survival time was defined as the time from the initiation of immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment to the date of death or the most recent visit. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Wakayama Medical University (#2654).

Immunohistochemistry staining and evaluation

Immunohistochemical staining was used to assess the expression of cGAS, STING, and PD-L1 in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissue specimens. The BenchMark XT system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) was used for cGAS and STING immunostaining, which involved tissue section deparaffinization at 75 °C using EZ Prep buffer (Ventana Medical Systems, Oro Valley, Arizona, USA). Antigen retrieval was achieved by incubating the specimens in Tris–EDTA base buffer at pH 8.5 (Cell Conditioning 1; Ventana Medical Systems) for 60 min at 95 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 10 min. The primary antibodies used were Anti-MB21D1 (HPA031700; Atlas Antibodies, Stockholm, Sweden) and STING/TMEM173 (19,851-1-AP; Proteintech, Chicago, Illinois, USA), diluted to 1:500 and 1:1500, respectively. The antibodies were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. The immunohistochemical reaction was visualized using the Ultraview Universal Diaminobenzidine IHC Detection Kit (Ventana Medical Systems), following the manufacturer’s protocol. For PD-L1 immunostaining, PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx (SK00621-5 J; Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) is used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Positive controls with known antibody reactivity were included, and negative controls were obtained by omitting the primary antibody. Two pathologists (Y. K. and J. F.) independently evaluated all pathological data, and a consensus was reached to determine the results. Scoring was performed in a blinded manner using both clinical and pathological data.

The H-score was used to assess the cGAS and STING expression; this score was derived by multiplying the staining intensity (ranging from 1 to 3) by the percentage of the stained area (ranging from 1 to 100%).

The TPS was used to evaluate PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, indicating the percentage of viable tumor cells that displayed either partial or complete membrane staining. Scoring was conducted in a blinded manner for both clinical and pathological data.

Serum protein analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected to quantify serum proteins using a Luminex 200 analyzer (Luminex Corporation, Austin, Texas, USA) and a Milliplex MAP system (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA). The assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, utilizing the human cytokine/chemokine panel 1, human angiogenesis/growth factor panel 1, and a multispecies transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) panel. The 40 proteins analyzed in this study are shown in supplementary Table 1. Standards or serum samples were mixed with chemically dyed beads bound to antibodies and then incubated overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed and incubated with biotinylated detection antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the beads were incubated with phycoerythrin-labeled streptavidin for 30 min at room temperature, and the median fluorescence intensities were quantified. All the samples were measured in duplicates.

Statistical analysis

The clinical characteristics of enrolled patients with high (≥ 50) and low (< 50) PD-L1 TPS were compared using the χ2 and Mann–Whitney U tests. PFS with ICI treatment and survival after ICI initiation were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. A log-rank test was used to compare PFS between the high- (≥ 220) and low- (< 220) cGAS H-score groups, as well as between the high- (≥ 190) and low- (< 100) STING H-score groups. The same comparisons were performed between the high- and low-PD-L1 groups.

The correlation between the cGAS H-score, STING H-score, and serum protein values (predose), as well as the fold change (calculated by dividing each value obtained 4–6 weeks after ICI treatment by the baseline value) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs). Regression analysis was also conducted for proteins that exhibited significant correlations. Additionally, for baseline cytokine concentrations, groups with high and low expression of cGAS or STING were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. JMP software version 14.0 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The clinical characteristics of the 68 patients included in this study were previously presented [22] and are shown in Table 1. Their median age was 70 (range: 31–91) years. Fifty-two patients (76%) had a good performance status (PS) of 0 or 1; 35% of the tumors were squamous cell carcinomas, and 56% were adenocarcinomas. All patients were treated with PD-1/L1 inhibitors (including pembrolizumab, n = 26; nivolumab, n = 33; and atezolizumab, n = 9). PD-L1 expression was high (TPS ≥ 50) in 27 patients (40%) and low (TPS < 50) in 41 patients (60%). Twenty-two patients (81%) in the high-PD-L1 group received ICI as first-line therapy, while only four patients (10%) did in the low-PD-L1 group (p < 0.001). As expected, pembrolizumab was the predominant choice for patients in the high-PD-L1 expression group and was used in 89% of the cohort. Conversely, in the low-PD-L1 group, the selection of treatments varied more: nivolumab was used in 59% of patients, pembrolizumab in 22%, and atezolizumab in 20% of the patients. Beyond these preferences in treatment choice, no other notable differences were observed between the high- and low-PD-L1 groups.

In the high- and low-PD-L1 groups, the H-scores for cGAS were 220 (80–220) and 235 (5–235), respectively; those for STING were 220 (0–300) and 175 (0–300), respectively, with no significant differences (p = 0.990 and 0.283, respectively).

We divided the cases into two groups based on median cGAS H-scores (220) and STING H-scores (190), respectively, and compared their PFS with ICI. There were no statistically significant differences between the cGAS high and low groups (median PFS of 64 and 130 days, p = 0.924), and the STING high and low groups (median PFS of 113 and 86 days, p = 0.213) (Fig. 1c). However, the PFS curves for cGAS high and low groups intersected, prompting further analysis on whether PD-L1 affects the impact of cGAS and STING on PFS. The results indicated that cGAS high cases had significantly shorter PFS compared to cGAS low cases when PD-L1 expression was high (≥ 50%) (median PFS: 143 days (95% confidence interval (CI) 43–203) vs. not reached (95%CI 77–not reached); p = 0.028), and PFS at 18 months was 7% and 53% in the high and low cGAS groups, respectively. In contrast, cGAS expression did not affect PFS when PD-L1 TPS was low (< 50%) [47 days (95%CI 39–127) vs. 61 days (95%CI 35–166), p = 0.798]. STING expression did not impact PFS regardless of PD-L1 expression (Fig. 1d).

Next, the correlation between cGAS expression and the concentrations of the 40 different proteins measured in serum samples collected prior to ICI administration was comprehensively investigated. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients for each protein are presented in Fig. 2a. Notably, significant correlations were observed for five proteins: Fit 3L, GRO, IFN-γ, TGF-β1, and TGF-β2 (p = 0.043, 0.047, 0.0009, 0.0085, and 0.031, respectively).

When comparing serum protein concentrations between groups with high- and low-cGAS expression levels, statistically significant differences were only observed for TGF-β1 and TGF-β2 (p = 0.012 and p = 0.019, respectively), as shown in Fig. 2b. Figure 2c illustrates the correlations between the H-scores for cGAS and STING, and the serum concentrations of TGF-β1 and TGF-β2. cGAS expression was significantly correlated with serum concentrations of TGF-β1 and β2 before ICI administration (R2 = 0.200, p = 0.009; R2 = 0.149, p = 0.027, respectively). Unlike cGAS, STING expression did not show a clear association with either TGF-β1 or TGF-β2 (R2 = 0.001, p = 0.883; R2 = 4.66 × 10–5, p = 0.883, respectively).

In a comprehensive analysis of the association between STING expression and serum protein concentrations, significant correlations were observed with three proteins: Bone Morphogenetic Protein 9 (BMP-9), Heparin-Binding Epidermal Growth Factor-like Growth Factor (HB-EGF), and Regulated upon Activation, Normal T Cell Expressed and Presumably Secreted (RANTES) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Upon comparing the serum concentrations prior to ICI administration between the high- and low-STING expression groups, a significant difference was only observed for BMP-9 (p = 0.0041) (Supplementary Fig. 1b), and there was also a significant correlation between the STING H-Score and the serum concentration of BMP (R2 = 0.126, p = 0.006) (Fig. 1c).

Discussion

This study identified a potential link between high cGAS protein expression in tumors and the reduced efficacy of PD-1/L1 inhibitors, particularly in cases with high PD-L1 TPS. Additionally, our data revealed a correlation between tumor cGAS protein levels and serum TGF-β concentrations, including notable changes in serum TGF-β levels, following PD-1/L1 inhibitor treatment.

In our analysis, we found that cGAS expression, but not STING expression, was associated with PFS. This suggests that cGAS expression is more indicative of the status of the cGAS/STING pathway, as cGAS plays an initial role in detecting cytosolic double-stranded DNA in this pathway, thus functioning upstream of STING. Numerous reports have indicated the impact of cGAS/STING pathway activation on the tumor microenvironment. In a mouse model, Schadt et al. [23] reported that tumors with high cGAS expression showed increased infiltration of CD8-positive cells around the tumor, and a positive correlation with the effectiveness of PD-1/L1 inhibitors. Conversely, activation of the cGAS/STING pathway has also been associated with the induction of MDSCs and TAM, which have immunosuppressive effects on the tumor microenvironment [12, 13]. Our findings in human NSCLC indicate that cGAS expression correlates with a suppressive tumor microenvironment.

Recent findings indicate that the tumor microenvironment plays a crucial role in the effectiveness of PD-1/L1 inhibitors [24]. A vital component of the tumor microenvironment is the activity of TGF-β signaling. This signaling pathway inhibits immune surveillance by cytotoxic T cells, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells. Furthermore, it promotes the activation of TAMs and MDSCs [25, 26]. Our analysis revealed a significant correlation between pretreatment serum TGF-β levels and tumor cGAS expression. While reports on the association between the cGAS/STING pathway and TGF-β are scarce, it is plausible that TGF-β secreted by cancer cells, fibroblasts, and bone marrow-derived suppressive cells like TAMs, may be linked to TAMs, induced by the activation of the cGAS/STING pathway.

Furthermore, the variation in serum TGF-β levels post-PD-1/L1 inhibitor administration demonstrated an inverse correlation with tumor cGAS expression. The reduction of serum TGF-β levels after PD-1/L1 inhibitor administration (change ratio < 1) was observed in 43% of cases with high cGAS expression, and only 20% of cases with low cGAS expression. PD-1/L1 inhibitors activate macrophage activity and phagocytosis, including TAMs [27]. The cGAS/STING pathway in macrophages can be activated upon the engulfment of cancer cells that contain cyclic dinucleotides (cGAS/STING pathway stimulator) [28]. Macrophages can be classified as M1 and M2, with the immunosuppressive M2 type primarily producing TGF-β. Activation of the cGAS/STING pathway in macrophages is capable of reprograming M2 macrophages into the M1 type [13, 29, 30]. Considering these factors, the activation of the cGAS/STING pathway in macrophages—resulting from the enhanced activation and phagocytosis induced by PD-1/L1 inhibitors in the tumor microenvironment—could lead to macrophage reprogramming and a subsequent reduction in serum TGF-β levels. Interestingly, the internalization of cGAMP into macrophages can also activate the cGAS/STING pathway [23]. Because cGAMP is produced by cGAS, it is presumed to be more abundant in cancer cells with high cGAS expression. This could potentially explain the greater reduction in TGF-β levels observed in the group with high cGAS expression.

The predictive significance of cGAS expression in tumors is limited to cases with high PD-L1 expression. PD-L1 suppresses the acute response to Type I IFNs like IFN-β by enhancing the IFN-related DNA damage resistance signature in some tumors. Additionally, in such tumors, the chronic activation of the cGAS/STING pathway leads to the continuous secretion of low levels of IFN-β, which further amplifies the IFN-related DNA damage resistance signature [31]. Another possibility is that activation of the cGAS/STING pathway itself may have contributed to the enhanced expression of PD-L1. Typically, PD-L1 expression is upregulated by IFN-γ from T cells infiltrating the tumor microenvironment, thus serving as a useful marker of the immune response against the tumor. However, secondary enhancement of PD-L1 expression due to activation of the cGAS/STING pathway has been reported [32]. In such cases, the increased PD-L1 expression may not necessarily indicate the presence of infiltrating T cells or IFN-γ. Consequently, the effectiveness of PD-1/L1 inhibitors may not be high in these scenarios.

This study has some limitations; first, the limited number of cases was a significant constraint. Further studies with larger cohorts are required to validate these findings. Additionally, using the H-score method to evaluate cGAS/STING expression may not be optimal, and alternative methods may offer more precise assessment. Moreover, reconsidering the method for defining high and low expression levels based on median values would be beneficial. Conducting a more extensive analysis with a larger sample size may enable the determination of a more suitable threshold for categorization. Furthermore, our analysis did not directly examine the infiltrating lymphocytes or macrophages in the tumor tissue. A comprehensive analysis of PD-L1 immune cells and infiltrating immune cells within tumor tissues could expand our understanding and provide additional insights.

In summary, the tumor expression of cGAS may predict poor efficacy of PD-1/L1 inhibitors, in cases exhibiting high PD-L1 expression, with TGF-β possibly playing a key role in this process. Identifying patients who exhibit a poor response may lead to the development of more personalized treatment strategies, such as the adoption of therapies typically used in patients with low PD-L1 expression. Moreover, there is a possibility that cGAS tumor expression may play a role in predicting the efficacy of TGF-β inhibitors, which are currently in development. These findings hold great promise for future studies.
