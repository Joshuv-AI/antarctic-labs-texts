A Rotating-Wave Comagnetometer Detector for Particle Physics
Itay M. Bloch, Or Katz

Abstract
Many extensions of the Standard Model propose the existence of new particles or forces, aiming to answer mysteries such as the identity of the elusive dark matter. Atomic-based detectors are at the forefront of technologies designed to search for these particles or forces through their couplings to fermions, enabling the testing of well-motivated models, such as axion-like particles, which could form dark matter. These detectors also probe new long-range interactions between the detectors and spin-polarized objects, as well as interactions mediated by light particles that break CP symmetry, introducing a coupling between the detector and an unpolarized object. However, the sensitivity of these detectors is often constrained by magnetic noise, limiting their effectiveness to a narrow region of parameter space. We propose and develop a technique, which we name the Rotating Wave comagnetometer (RoW comag), that can suppress magnetic noise at tunable frequencies while maintaining high sensitivity to target signals, significantly expanding the potential reach of these detectors. We analyze its operation for testing various extensions to the Standard Model and show how it could improve current sensitivities by several orders of magnitude. This work paves the way for a new class of tabletop experiments aimed at searching for new physics, including the exploration of well-motivated axion-like particle dark matter models at higher masses than previously attainable.

Noise Suppression Mechanism.
The detector sensitivity is limited by background noise, typically magnetic fields
\delta{\bf B}_{\perp}
oscillating at
\omega_{\textrm{DM}}
in the
xy
plane, which would rotate the noble-gas or alkali-metal spins off axis, leading to false detection. For example, in a magnetically-shielded environment at finite temperatures, the electrically conductive shields produce magnetic field noise at levels of
|\delta{\bf B}_{\perp}|\sim 10\,\textrm{fT}/\sqrt{\textrm{Hz}}

[
64
]
. Self-compensating comagnetometers suppress such fields at low frequencies by leveraging the fact that, unlike ALP DM fields, the magnetic noise couples simultaneously to both the noble gas and the alkali-metal spins at strengths proportional to their gyromagnetic ratios
\gamma_{\textrm{N}}
and
\gamma_{\textrm{s}}
, respectively.

Accounting for the hybrid alkali-metal and noble gas dynamics, we can express the total alkali-metal spin response to magnetic field noise in the frequency domain as

s_{x}(\omega)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{B}(\omega)\delta B_{+}(\omega)+\chi_{B}^{*}(-\omega)\delta B^{*}_{+}(-\omega)\right).

(1)

Using complex notation,
\delta B_{+}=\delta B_{x}+i\delta B_{y}
represents the magnetic field noise vector that rotates counter-clockwise in the
xy
plane, and
\delta B_{+}^{*}(-\omega)\equiv(\delta B_{+}(-\omega))^{*}
is the orthogonal field vector that rotates clockwise. The complex response function
\chi_{B}(\omega)
is approximately given by

\chi_{B}(\omega)=\frac{\Delta_{\textrm{N}}+\nu}{-J^{2}+\Delta_{\textrm{N}}(i\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}+\Delta_{\textrm{s}})}{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z},

(2)

where
\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}
denotes the alkali-metal spin decoherence rate,
J
is the collective spin-exchange rate
[
65
,
66
]
and
\Delta_{\textrm{N}}(\omega,B)=\omega_{0\textrm{N}}+\gamma_{\textrm{N}}B-\omega
and
\Delta_{\textrm{s}}(\omega,B)=\omega_{0\textrm{s}}+l+\gamma_{\textrm{s}}B-\omega
are the relative detunings of the field oscillating at frequency
\omega
from the noble-gas and alkali-metal magnetic (Larmor) frequencies, respectively.
l
represents light induced shift by optical beams.
\omega_{0\textrm{N}},\,\omega_{0\textrm{s}}
represent the spin-exchange shifts applied by one ensemble on the other and
\nu\equiv\omega_{0\textrm{s}}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}/{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}
.

The response to transverse magnetic field noise is significantly suppressed at low frequencies when a static magnetic field
B_{c}\hat{z}
satisfying
\Delta_{\textrm{N}}(B_{c})=-\nu
is applied, resulting in
\chi_{B}(\omega=0)=\chi_{B}^{*}(-\omega=0)=0
in Eq. (
2
), as shown in Fig.
1

xy

B=B_{c}

II
Rotating-Wave Comagnetometer

II.1
Self compensation of magnetic noise

Suppressing magnetic noise at finite frequencies would seem to require the cancellation of both the clockwise and counter-clockwise rotating noise components, which have response amplitudes
\chi_{B}(\omega)
and
\chi_{B}^{*}(-\omega)
, respectively. However, for stationary fields at
\omega=0
, both terms are cancelled simultaneously. This observation motivates us to consider the Self-compensating comagnetometer equations of motion in a rotating frame of reference that rotates counterclockwise at frequency
\Omega_{\rm R}
. By analyzing this non-inertial frame, we propose a straightforward scheme that achieves noise suppression without requiring any physical rotation. The coordinates in the rotating frame are given by

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{\prime}=\cos(\Omega_{\text{R}}t)\hat{x}+\sin(\Omega_{\text{R}}t)\hat{y},

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{\prime}=\cos(\Omega_{\text{R}}t)\hat{y}-\sin(\Omega_{\text{R}}t)\hat{x}.

(4)

In this frame of reference, any fields oscillating counterclockwise at frequency
\omega=\Omega_{\rm R}
would appear stationary, as illustrated in Fig.
1
c. The equations of motion in the rotating frame include a non-inertial ”rotation field”
-\Omega_{\textrm{R}}\hat{z}
, which couples to both the noble-gas and alkali-metal spins. This coupling results in a uniform shift in their resonance frequencies
\Delta_{\textrm{N}}
and
\Delta_{\textrm{s}}
by
-\Omega_{\rm R}
. We express the
\hat{x}^{\prime}
spin in the rotating frame,
s_{x}^{\prime}
, as a function of the
laboratory
noise fields

\displaystyle s_{x}^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\chi_{B}(\omega+\Omega_{\textrm{R}})\delta B_{+}(\omega+\Omega_{\textrm{R}})

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\chi_{B}^{*}(\Omega_{\textrm{R}}-\omega)\delta B^{*}_{+}(\Omega_{\textrm{R}}-\omega)\bigr),

where crucially, both the clockwise and counter-clockwise rotation frequencies are shifted equally by the same negative rate
\Omega_{\textrm{R}}
. Consequently, the stationary spin signal in the rotating frame,
s_{x}^{\prime}(\omega=0)
, is susceptible to laboratory magnetic fields oscillating at
nonzero
frequency
\Omega_{R}
in the laboratory frame, with the same susceptibility magnitude
|\chi_{B}(\Omega_{\textrm{R}})|
. Tuning the static magnetic field to

B_{\textrm{R}}=B_{c}-\Omega_{\textrm{R}}/\gamma_{\textrm{N}},

(6)

enables correction for the rotation fields, ensuring maximal suppression
\chi_{B}(\Omega_{\textrm{R}})\rightarrow 0
to compensate for noisy fields oscillating at the target frequency
\Omega_{\textrm{R}}
, unlike the self-compensating comagnetometers which only suppress nearly static magnetic field noise. The suppression of this rotating frame magnetometer for nonzero rotation frequencies is presented in Fig.
2
a.

Operating the detector at a magnetic field
B_{\textrm{R}}
affects the magnetometer’s response to both noisy and anomalous fields. While the change in
\Delta_{\textrm{N}}
for the noble-gas spins is compensated by adjusting
B
, it shifts the resonance frequency of the alkali-metal spins
\Delta_{\textrm{s}}(B)
. Nevertheless, the application of a light-shift term

l_{R}=-(\gamma_{\textrm{s}}/\gamma_{\textrm{N}}-1)\Omega_{\textrm{R}},

(7)

compensates for the alkali-metal frequency shift without affecting the noble-gas spins, thereby restoring the magnetometer’s sensitivity to its maximal value. This renders the response to ALP fields at
\omega=\Omega_{\rm R}
independent of
\Omega_{\textrm{R}}
, as shown in Fig.
2

Notably, the rotating frame signal can be reconstructed in the laboratory frame without any physical rotation. Instead, simultaneous measurement of
s_{x}(t)
and
s_{y}(t)
is required. The rotating frame signal,

3

III
Discussion

In this work we presented a novel method relying on the effective (or physical) rotation of a comagnetometer to drastically reduce magnetic noise at a controlled frequency. Other comagnetometry systems have been placed on rotating stages in the past
[
41
,
54
]
. However, in those cases, a static signal was modulated, and the need for physical rotation required adding mechanical stages to the basic comagnetometer setup. We demonstrated how by modifying the detector parameter, and adding a second probe beam, a search for oscillating signals is facilitated, which could be of great benefit in many particle physics searches.

The presented detector can potentially improve current sensitivities by four orders of magnitude through suppression of magnetic field noise which is a major limiting factor. Reaching this performance would require optimization of the optical magnetometer readout, which is influenced by factors such as the finite number of photons in the polarimeter, the degree of circular birefringence in the medium, and technical electronic noises. However, the only major change in the detector setup which is necessary for demonstrating the new method is the addition of a second probe beam. With this simple modification, the RoW comag should be able to explore models which no terrestrial or astrophysical probe is sensitive to. Additional feasible improvements would allow the detector to probe several highly motivated extensions to the Standard Model
[
9
,
10
,
16
]
. We focused on three extensions to the Standard Model: ALP-DM coupled to neutrons, ALP-mediated spin-spin long-range interactions, and ALP-mediated spin-mass long-range interactions. However, other configurations of the RoW comag, or generalizations of it in other systems could potentially probe other extensions to the SM as well.

The true fundamental quantum noise of comagnetometers is associated with the finite number of noble-gas atoms, and is exceptionally low (on the order of
0.01~\rm{aT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}
). Reducing practical noises is of key importance in achieving that sensitivity. Therefore, this work represents a significant milestone towards a system that could utilize the full potential of atomic spin detectors.

Materials and Methods

Time dependent dynamics of the Rotating Wave comagnetometer detector

The comagnetometer consists of two overlapping spin ensembles: an alkali-metal vapor with a mean spin vector
{\bf{s}}
, and a noble-gas ensemble with a mean spin vector
{\bf{N}}
. Their dynamics, described in Ref.
[
50
]
, evolve according to the Bloch equations:

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf{s}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}\left({\bf B}+B_{\textrm{N}}{\bf{N}}+{\bf b}_{\textrm{s}}+\mathbf{l}/{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}\right)\times{\bf{s}}+\left(R_{\rm pu}{\bf s}_{\rm pu}+R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}{\bf{N}}-\mathcal{R}_{\textrm{s}}{\bf{s}}\right),

(8)

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf{N}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}({\bf B}+B_{\textrm{s}}{\bf{s}}+{\bf b}_{\textrm{N}})\times{\bf{N}}+R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}{{\bf{s}}}-\mathcal{R}_{\textrm{N}}{\bf{N}}.

(9)

We focus on a configuration where the applied magnetic field, pump beam, and applied light-shift are aligned along the
\hat{z}
-axis, similar to other comagnetometers. In the limit of small transverse fields, the steady-state axial spin components are given by:

s_{z}=\frac{R_{\rm pu}\Gamma_{\textrm{N},z}}{2(\Gamma_{\textrm{N},z}\Gamma_{\textrm{s},z}-R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}})},

(10)

and

N_{z}=s_{z}R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}/\Gamma_{\textrm{N},z}.

(11)

Assuming these axial components remain constant during the dynamics allows us to simplify the problem and focus on the evolution of the transverse spin components. For conciseness, we use the complex notation

s_{+}=s_{x}+is_{y}
, and
N_{+}=N_{x}+iN_{y}
for the transverse spin components, and

\delta B_{+}=\delta B_{x}+i\delta B_{y}
,
b_{\textrm{N},+}=b_{\textrm{N},x}+ib_{\textrm{N},y}
for the transverse noise and signal fields. With this notation, the dynamics can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(i\omega_{\textrm{s}}-\Gamma_{\textrm{s}})s_{+}+(R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}-i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{N}}s_{z})N_{+}-i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}\delta B_{+}

(12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(i\omega_{\textrm{N}}-\Gamma_{\textrm{N}})N_{+}+(R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}-i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{s}}N_{z})s_{+}-i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}N_{z}\left(\delta B_{+}+b_{\textrm{N},+}\right),

(13)

where we defined

\displaystyle\omega_{\textrm{s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}\left(B+B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}\right)+l={\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B+\omega_{0\textrm{s}},

(14)

\displaystyle\omega_{\textrm{N}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}\left(B+B_{\textrm{s}}s_{z}\right)={\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B+\omega_{0\textrm{N}}.

(15)

Owing to the spectrally narrow nature of ALPs, applying a Fourier transform to these equations allows us to focus on the response to these frequencies, which is relevant for signal detection. This transformation eliminates terms related to initial conditions and can be expressed as:

\begin{pmatrix}i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{s}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}&i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{N}}s_{z}-R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}\\
i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{s}}N_{z}-R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}&i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{N}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}s_{+}(\omega)\\
N_{+}(\omega)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}\delta B_{+}(\omega)\\
-i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}N_{z}\delta B_{+}(\omega)-iN_{z}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}b_{\textrm{N},+}(\omega).\end{pmatrix}

(16)

Solving this system by matrix inversion results in the response of the spins to the anomalous and noise fields. Since only the alkali-metal spins are measured, we focus on their response (which incorporates the coupling to noble gas spins),

s_{+}(\omega)=(\chi_{B}(\omega)\delta B_{+}(\omega)+\chi_{b}(\omega)b_{\textrm{N},+}(\omega)).

(17)

The linear response function to anomalous fields coupled directly to the noble-gas spins is given by

\chi_{b}(\omega)=-\frac{\left({\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{N}}s_{z}+iR_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}\right)}{\left(i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{s}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}\right)\left(i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{N}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}\right)-\left(i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{s}}N_{z}-R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}\right)\left(i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{N}}s_{z}-R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}\right)}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}N_{z},

(18)

and the linear response function to magnetic field noise is given by

\chi_{B}(\omega)=\frac{(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{N}}-{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z})-i(\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}+{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}N_{z}R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}/({\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}))}{\left(i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{s}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}\right)\left(i(\omega-\omega_{\textrm{N}})+\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}\right)-\left(i{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{s}}N_{z}-R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}\right)\left(i{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{N}}s_{z}-R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}\right)}{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}.

(19)

Therefore, the expression for
\chi_{B}
indicates the sensitivity of the comagnetometer to magnetic noise, while
\chi_{b}
reflects its sensitivity to anomalous signals. These parameters define the response characteristics of the detector. The expression for
\chi_{B}
given in the main text is the result when neglecting the noble-gas related relaxation mechanisms (
\Gamma_{\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}
), which practically set a lower limit on the detector bandwidth and maximal magnetic field suppression.

IV
Optimal control fields

Maximizing the sensitivity of the detector to anomalous fields requires optimizing the ratio
|\chi_{b}(\omega)/\chi_{B}(\omega)|
. From Eq. (
18
) and Eq. (
19
), this ratio is maximized for
\omega=\Omega_{\rm R}
when
\omega_{\textrm{N}}=\Omega_{\rm R}-{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}
. This corresponds to a magnetic field
B=B_{R}
with

B_{R}\equiv-B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}-B_{\textrm{s}}s_{z}+\frac{\Omega_{\rm R}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}=B_{c}+\frac{\Omega_{\rm R}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}.

(20)

In a frame rotating at
\Omega_{\rm R}
, one reproduces the picture of the standard self-compensating comagnetometer of Ref.
[
50
]
, where static fields are suppressed. Signals at
\Omega_{\rm R}
shift to zero, while the noble gas perceives an effective shift equivalent to a change in the magnetic field amplitude by
\Omega_{\rm R}/{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}
relative to the compensation point. Adjusting the external field by this amount reproduces the compensation-point behavior, with the alkali-metal ensemble experiencing a modified field. This adjustment ensures an optimal ratio between the sensitivity to signal and magnetic noise at
\Omega_{\rm R}
.

While tuning
B_{R}
effectively minimizes sensitivity to ambient magnetic fields, other noise sources (such as electronic or probe polarization noise) become more dominant when the amplitude of
\chi_{b}(\omega)
is decreased. To ensure that
\chi_{b}(\omega)
is not decreased by the change of magnetic field to
B=B_{R}
, we optimize it as a function of
\mathbf{l}=l\hat{z}
, yielding the optimal value

l_{\rm R}(\Omega_{\rm R})=-\left(\frac{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}-1\right)\Omega_{\rm R}+\frac{\left(B_{\textrm{N}}R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}+B_{\textrm{s}}\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}\right)\left({\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}s_{z}+{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}N_{z}\right)}{\left(\Gamma_{\textrm{N}}^{2}+{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}^{2}B_{\textrm{N}}^{2}N_{z}^{2}\right)}\approx-\left(\frac{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}-1\right)\Omega_{\rm R}.

(21)

For the parameters we consider,
\Gamma_{\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}
are small and their effect on the signal response is negligible, leading to the approximate response

s_{+}(\Omega_{\rm R})\approx-\frac{s_{z}{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}b_{\textrm{N},+}}{(i\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}+(l-l_{\rm R}(\Omega_{\rm R}))}.

(22)

Achieving maximal sensitivity thus requires tuning
l
with a resolution of approximately
\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}
, which corresponds to the alkali-magnetometer bandwidth. As the light-shift is proportional to
\Omega_{\rm R}
, achieving this for large
\Omega_{\rm R}
would require high-power and far-detuned lasers. However, as many noise sources (including the atomic shot noise and magnetic noise) are suppressed in the same manner when
l=0
, in some cases tuning
l=l_{\rm R}
may not be crucial for optimal performance.

V
Sensitivity to Transient Signals

We briefly address the detector’s response to transient signals, which may arise from sudden changes in system conditions. While the steady-state solution in Eq. (
17
) describes the long-term behavior, transient noise can be seen as producing exponentially decaying homogeneous solutions. Efficiently vetoing periods of transient noise in a practical search is crucial, and longer veto durations reduce the overall measurement time. A shorter coherence time for the system’s modes allows for quicker recovery from transient events.

At the optimal control parameters, coupling between the two spin ensembles leads to large decoherence rates for both hybrid modes of the spins. These rates can be approximated as
\frac{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{s}}s_{z}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{s}}s_{z}+{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}}\Gamma_{\textrm{s}},\frac{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}}{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}B_{\textrm{s}}s_{z}+{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}}\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}
. These are identical to the decoherence rates in the self-compensating comagnetometers of Ref.
[
50
]
. This indicates that both spin ensembles have coherence times primarily determined by the alkali-metal ensemble, enabling efficient noise vetoing and rapid system recovery. If light-shift compensation is not applied, at higher
\Omega_{\rm R}
s, one of the modes becomes longer lived, providing another motivation for applying light-shift compensation.

Anomalous magnetic-like fields

Modifications to the Standard Model can introduce anomalous fields, which can manifest as either irreducible backgrounds or as signals under specific conditions. Here, we examine three such models: ALPs as DM, long-range spin-spin interactions, and spin-mass couplings. Throughout this section, we use natural units where
\hbar=1
and
c=1
.

V.1
Axion-Like Particles as Dark Matter

In the model we consider in this work, ALP DM couples to neutron spins through a coupling constant
g_{n}
. The coherence time of the ALP-induced field is inversely proportional to its energy uncertainty, approximately
\sim 2\pi\cdot 1.6\times 10^{6}/m_{\rm DM}
, allowing it to be treated as a monochromatic field over short measurement periods. The anomalous field generated by the ALP is given by
[
62
,
19
]

{\bf b}_{\textrm{N}}=\epsilon_{nN}g_{n}\sqrt{{2\rho_{\rm DM}}}{\bf v}_{\rm DM}\cos(\langle E_{\rm DM}\rangle t+\theta_{0})/{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}},

(23)

with
{\bf v}_{\rm DM}
denoting the DM velocity relative to the lab-frame,
\rho_{\rm DM}
denoting the local DM energy density, and
\theta_{0}
is an unknown initial phase. We take
|{\bf v}_{\rm DM}|=238{\rm km}/{\rm sec}
, and
\rho_{\rm DM}=\sqrt{0.4{\rm GeV}/{\rm cm}^{3}}
. For practical purposes, the ALP energy
\langle E_{\rm DM}\rangle
is approximated by
m_{\rm DM}
. The parameter
\epsilon_{nN}
represents the fraction of the noble gas spin due to neutron-spin contributions, which is approximately 100% for
{}^{3}\textrm{He}

[
70
]
.
This effective coupling arises from an interaction described by the Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}=g_{n}\partial_{\mu}a\bar{n}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}n,

(24)

where
a
represents the ALP field,
n
is the neutron Dirac spinor, and
\gamma^{\mu}
are the Dirac gamma matrices.

V.2
Long-Range Spin-Spin Interactions

New long-range spin-spin interactions are predicted by a variety of SM extensions
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In particular, ALPs, whether they are DM or not, introduce such interactions. We focus on ALP-induced forces, though our proposed experimental setup could be reanalyzed in the context of other extensions as well. We consider a spin-polarized source based on nuclear spins with large neutron fraction
{\bf I}_{\rm source}
located at distance
{\bf r}
from the RoW comag. The induced anomalous field coupled to the noble gas nuclei in the detector is given by
[
14
]

{\bf b}_{\textrm{N}}\approx\frac{\epsilon_{Nn}g_{\rm ann}^{2}}{2\pi{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}\lambda}+\frac{1}{r^{3}}\right){\bf I}_{\rm source}-(\hat{r}\cdot{\bf I}_{\rm source})\hat{r}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}r}+\frac{3}{r^{2}\lambda}+\frac{3}{r^{3}}\right)\right)e^{r/\lambda},

(25)

where
\lambda=1/m_{\rm mediator}
is the interaction range, with
{m}_{\rm mediator}
as the ALP mediator’s mass. When
\lambda\ll r
, the interaction decays exponentially, while for
\lambda\gg r
, it approximates a dipole-dipole interaction, scaling as
1/r^{3}
. We assume for simplicity that the spin source is a sphere of radius
R
with a uniform spin density
n_{\rm source}
, and a uniform spin-polarization
P_{\rm source}
(taken to be 100%). We take the contribution of neutron spins to the spin of the nuclei as
\epsilon_{n,{\rm source}}
. Because at low frequencies, various noise sources are considerably larger, we assume that the spin source precesses under a magnetic field at a frequency
\Omega_{\rm R}
such that its polarization along the
\hat{x}
axis is modulated by
\cos(\Omega_{\rm R}t)
. While all three components of
{\bf I}_{\rm source}
contribute to the anomalous field in all directions, for simplicity we focus on the contribution from
I_{{\rm source},x}
to the anomalous field. Considering this component only, and
\Omega_{\rm R}\ll 1/r,m_{\rm mediator}
, the induced anomalous field is given by

b_{\textrm{N},x}=\frac{1}{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}g_{ann}^{2}n_{\rm source}\epsilon_{n,{\rm source}}\epsilon_{nN}\frac{(R+\lambda)e^{-(r+R)/\lambda}+(R-\lambda)e^{-(r-R)/\lambda}}{2r^{3}}\left((r+\lambda)^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)\cos(\Omega_{\rm R}t).

(26)

For simplicity, in our estimates we take
\cos(\Omega_{\rm R}t)\hat{x}\to\hat{x}^{\prime}(t)
(see Eq. (
3
)).

V.3
Spin-Mass Couplings

ALPs or QCD axions with CP-violating couplings can produce spin-mass interactions. Assuming a single atom of mass number A at distance
r
from the detector, the induced field is
[
16
]

{\bf b}_{\textrm{N}}=\frac{Ag_{s}^{N}g_{p}^{n}}{8\pi m_{n}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}}\epsilon_{nN}\left(\frac{1}{r\lambda}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)e^{-r/\lambda}\hat{r},

(27)

where
\lambda=1/m_{\rm mediator}
,
m_{n}
is the mass of a neutron,
g_{p}^{n}=2m_{n}g_{n}
is the dimensionless (pseudoscalar) coupling of ALPs to neutron spins, and
g_{s}^{N}
denotes the (CP-violating) scalar coupling to nucleons in the massive object (assumed to be equal for protons and neutrons). For
r\gg\lambda
, the interaction decays exponentially, while for
r\ll\lambda
, it decays like
1/r^{2}
.

We assume for simplicity the mass is always placed in the
\hat{x}^{\prime}(t)
(see Eq. (
3
)) direction. Similar sensitivity is achieved if the distance (rather than relative direction) between the mass and RoW comag is modulated. We further take the mass to be a uniform sphere of radius
R
and density
\rho_{\rm source}
, and so it generates a field at the detector’s center for
r\gtrsim R

{\bf b}_{N}=\frac{g_{s}^{N}g_{p}^{n}\rho_{\rm source}}{4m_{n}^{2}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}r^{2}}\epsilon_{nN}\lambda(r+\lambda)\left((R+\lambda)e^{-(r+R)/\lambda}+(R-\lambda)e^{-(r-R)/\lambda}\right)\hat{x}^{\prime}(t).

(28)

This estimate assumes a point-like detector and
\Omega_{\rm R}\ll 1/r,m_{\rm mediator}
.

In this section, we detail the proposed configurations that we considered in the calculation of the projected performance shown in Fig.
3

1
.

The comagnetometer parameters, such as the decoherence rates and magnetization, are based on the detector in Ref.
[
14
]
, except where otherwise specified. We consider two levels of white magnetic noise within the relevant parts of the spectrum: (1)
|\delta B|\approx 10~{\rm fT}/{\sqrt{Hz}}
achievable using high-permeability
\mu
-metal shielding limited primarily by Johnson noise
[
64
]
, and (2)
|\delta B|\approx 1~{\rm fT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}

[
71

. Ferrites are likely to provide such sensitivity only for higher frequencies
[
71
]

Another source of noise stems from the limited number of alkali-metal atoms and the variance in their collective spin, known as the Atomic Shot Noise (ASN). Neglecting the effects of
\Gamma_{\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}}
and
R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}
, the ASN is given by
[
14

\delta s_{x}=\sqrt{{\xi}/{(2\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}n_{s}V_{\rm eff}})},
where
\xi
is a dimensionless parameter which depends on
s_{z}
, and equal to
0.65
for
s_{z}=0.25
.
n_{\textrm{s}}
is the number density of the alkali-metal ensemble, and
V_{\rm eff}
is the effective probe beam volume in the cell. For the RoW comganetometer detector operating at
l_{\rm R}
and
B_{R}
, our ASN remains consistent with that of the non-rotating system in Ref.
[
14
]
. In units of magnetic field, this noise can be expressed as

\delta B_{\rm ASN,eff}=\frac{1}{{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}}\sqrt{\frac{\xi\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}}{2n_{\textrm{s}}V_{\rm eff}}}.

(29)

Photon shot noise, associated with the finite number of photons, can be tuned to be smaller than ASN
[
72
]
, making ASN the limiting noise source. We examine two regimes: one with a mild
10\%
improvement over Ref.
[
14
]
(
|\delta B_{\rm ASN,eff}|\approx 0.1~{\rm fT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}
) and another with improved sensitivity
|\delta B_{\rm ASN,eff}|\approx 1~{\rm aT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}
which could be achieved using larger cells and higher temperatures
[
73
]
.

We now discuss the parameters for Fig
3
. For the long-range interaction searches, the oscillation frequency is known and chosen to minimize technical noise. However, when searching for ALP DM we scan over
\Omega_{\rm R}
to search for the signal, because the mass of ALP-DM is unknown. Therefore, we consider the bandwidth of our sensor for a fixed
B_{R},l_{\rm R}
, as well as the range of frequencies we expect to measure, determined by practical considerations of
l_{\rm R}
.

Since the maximal magnetic subtraction is below the lowest quantum noise, the dependence of
\chi_{B}
on
\Gamma_{\textrm{N}},R_{\textrm{N}\textrm{s}},R_{\textrm{s}\textrm{N}}
plays no role. Therefore, we can estimate the square root of the power spectral density of the alkali-metal spin
s_{+}
at the vicinity of
\omega=\Omega_{\rm R}
as

\delta s_{+}(\omega=\Omega_{\rm R}+\delta\omega)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\xi}{2\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}n_{s}V_{\rm eff}}+\left(\frac{\delta\omega{\gamma_{\textrm{s}}}s_{z}}{\Gamma_{\textrm{s}}{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}}\delta B(\omega)\right)^{2}},

(30)

with the first term associated with ASN, and the second term with imperfect suppression of the magnetic noise
\delta B
at a frequency
\Omega_{R}+\delta\omega
. Assuming a frequency independent
\delta B(\omega)
, we consider the effective bandwidth, BW, as the range of
\delta\omega

{\rm BW}=2\cdot\left(\frac{\delta B_{\rm ASN,eff}}{{\rm aT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}}\right)\left(\frac{\delta B}{{\rm fT}/{\sqrt{\rm Hz}}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{{\gamma_{\textrm{N}}}B_{\textrm{N}}N_{z}}{2\pi\times 17{\rm Hz}}\right)\cdot 2\pi\times 17~{\rm mHz}.

(31)

Therefore, lower magnetic background noise
\delta B
, would effectively increase the bandwidth around a given
\Omega_{\rm R}
. A broader measurement bandwidth would allow to fully scan a predetermined region of frequencies
[\omega_{\rm min},\omega_{\rm max}]
with fewer points, thus decreasing the required total measurement time needed for a fixed targeted sensitivity.
