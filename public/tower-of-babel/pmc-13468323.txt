Chitosan as a Vaccine Adjuvant: From Material Determinants to Innate Immune Activation and Formulation Optimization
Authors: Ziyi Li, Xiuli Zhang, Nan Liu, Ningshao Xia, Ying Gu, Shaowei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review summarizes the current understanding of chitosan as a chemically tunable vaccine adjuvant, with a particular focus on the relationships between material determinants, innate immune activation, and translational formulation design. From a materials science perspective, we discuss how molecular weight, charge density, and composite construction with organic or inorganic components shape the physicochemical behavior of chitosan-based systems. It then summarizes, from an immunological perspective, the pathways through which chitosan-based adjuvants activate immune responses—including the cGAS–STING pathway, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and TLR2/TLR4—and how they balance humoral and cellular immunity. Finally, we also analyze the context-dependent applicability of various chitosan-based formulations, such as nanoparticles and hydrogels, along with the design rationale for synergistic “chitosan + X” (e.g, TLR agonists, cytokines) adjuvant systems. We highlight key translational challenges, including poor solubility at neutral pH, structural heterogeneity, endotoxin and impurity control, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) requirements. Collectively, this review provides a theoretical framework for rational formulation selection, critical quality attribute standardization, and the prioritization of clinically feasible chitosan-based adjuvant platforms.

Introduction

Adjuvants enhance the immunogenicity of vaccines, help maintain the structural stability of antigens, balance the types of immune responses, and at the same time reduce the required antigen dose and the number of immunizations. Although aluminium salts remain the most widely used adjuvant in licensed vaccines, their ability to activate cellular immunity and mucosal immunity is limited, and therefore they are not suitable for vaccines targeting intracellular pathogens or mucosal vaccines.1,2 The hydroxyl and amino groups on the chitosan backbone can form hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions with glycoproteins on mucosal surfaces, enabling strong adhesion to mucosal epithelia in the intestine, respiratory tract, and other sites.3,4 This improves the bioavailability of vaccines administered via mucosal routes and highlights the potential of chitosan as both a delivery carrier and an immunostimulatory adjuvant. Chitosan is a low-toxicity polysaccharide widely recognized and approved for use in food and medical products in many countries,5,6 and it possesses favorable physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility, making it a highly promising, precisely tunable adjuvant platform for next-generation vaccines.

Despite these advantages, the clinical translation of chitosan-based adjuvants remains limited. To date, most evidence has been generated in preclinical studies, while only a small number of chitosan-containing formulations have advanced to early clinical evaluation. Importantly, “chitosan” does not refer to a single chemically defined material; rather, its molecular weight (MW) distribution, degree and pattern of deacetylation, salt form, derivatization chemistry, and residual impurity burden can vary substantially across sources and processing methods. These variations can profoundly influence physicochemical behavior, including aggregation, antigen loading and release, stability at physiological pH, and interactions with biological barriers, with direct consequences for innate pathway engagement, reproducibility, and CMC feasibility.7 In parallel, standardized characterization parameters such as MW distribution, degree of deacetylation (DD), polydispersity index, zeta potential, particle size, endotoxin level, and residual impurities are increasingly recognized as essential quality attributes for evaluating chitosan-based vaccine systems.

Compared with the previous review, we adopt a translational, framework-oriented perspective that integrates material determinants, formulation architecture, innate immune activation, and CMC-relevant challenges. This review places particular emphasis on the structure–function relationships linking chitosan material properties to delivery performance, immune activation, and translational feasibility. We first examine the material determinants of chitosan performance, including MW, charge properties, and composite design, and discuss how these features are associated with distinct delivery scenarios. We then summarize mechanistic evidence for chitosan-induced activation of innate immune pathways, including cGAS–STING, NOD‑like receptor family, pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome signaling, and Toll‑like receptor (TLR) TLR2/TLR4-mediated responses. Finally, we review formulation strategies and modular “chitosan + X” co-adjuvant systems, with emphasis on context-dependent applicability, translational bottlenecks, and practical directions toward scalable and regulatory-compatible designs. By integrating materials science, innate immunology, and translational considerations, this review aims to provide a framework for rational formulation selection and the prioritization of clinically feasible chitosan-based vaccine adjuvants.

Determinants Underpinning Chitosan Adjuvant Performance

Materials Determinants

MW Tailoring

The MW of chitosan is one of the key parameters determining its adjuvant efficacy across different vaccine delivery routes. Precise control of MW provides a practical basis for optimizing its functional performance. In injectable vaccine studies, high-MW chitosan exhibits pronounced positive charge, strong nucleic acid-interfering capacity, and a robust ability to stimulate cellular immunity. In a study employing chitosan as a tumor vaccine adjuvant8 low-, medium-, and high-molecular-weight chitosan were used as adjuvants respectively; the higher-molecular-weight chitosan (340 kDa) produced the greatest immune-stimulatory effect. In Anna’s study on an influenza A virus vaccine, both high- and low-MW chitosan were tested.9 The results showed that high-molecular-weight chitosan markedly upregulated activation markers such as CD80, CD86, and MHC II on the surface of dendritic cells (DCs), and significantly enhanced T-cell immune responses. In contrast, in mucosal vaccine studies, low-molecular-weight chitosan displays superior mucosal uptake. This may be because high-molecular-weight chitosan is more readily protonated in the mildly acidic mucosal environment,10 thereby crosslinking with mucins to form a three-dimensional network structure.11 This leads to local gelation and hinders antigen diffusion and absorption. Subrata et al reported12 that the group receiving 42 kDa chitosan showed significantly higher levels of serum IgG and intestinal sIgA than the 74 kDa and 106 kDa groups. However, the effect of MW on adjuvant activity is not universal and may depend on the route of administration, antigen type, formulation structure, and immune endpoint. Accordingly, no single molecular-weight range can be regarded as optimal across all vaccine systems.

Mature process technologies are now available to precisely tailor the MW of chitosan. For example, chemical degradation methods (acid hydrolysis, oxidative degradation) allow directional reduction of MW by adjusting reaction time, temperature, and reagent concentration.13,14 Enzymatic degradation takes advantage of the specific catalytic activity of chitosanases or the synergistic action of enzyme cocktails,15 enabling efficient depolymerization under mild conditions and yielding products with a relatively narrow molecular-weight distribution. Compared with the original high-molecular-weight chitosan, these products exhibit markedly improved water solubility and antimicrobial performance. Physical methods (such as microfluidic approaches16) induce chain scission via mechanical forces or radiation energy, and are simple to operate and readily scalable. Collectively, these processes provide solid technical support for obtaining chitosan within defined molecular-weight ranges.

Charge Density and Surface Potential Regulation

Chitosan is a linear polysaccharide composed of β-(1→4)-linked D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units, characterized by a high density of primary amino groups along the backbone.17,18 The protonation state of these amino groups governs the net positive charge and thereby dictates electrostatic interactions with antigens, cell membranes, and the mucus layer. For unmodified chitosan, charge density is primarily determined by the DD

and environmental pH relative to the amino-group pKa: under mildly acidic conditions (pH < pKa), amino groups are protonated to form –NH3+, whereas increasing pH reduces protonation and weakens condensation of negatively charged cargo such as DNA; The pKa will change with the degree of acetylation within a certain range.19 This protonation behavior renders chitosan a cationic polyelectrolyte with substantial positive charge in dilute acidic media. In addition, for chitosan derivatives, the degree of substitution (DS)—for example, the degree of quaternization in trimethylated chitosan—becomes an additional and often decisive determinant of charge density and charge stability. DD refers to the percentage of free amino groups relative to the total sugar units along the chitosan chain.20 A high DD implies more free amino groups on the backbone, which in turn leads to greater protonation under acidic conditions and thus a significantly higher positive charge density on the chitosan surface. In adjuvant development, selecting chitosan with a high DD (eg, ≥85%, and in some cases as high as ≥99.9%) is a key strategy to strengthen its binding to negatively charged antigens. Environmental pH also plays a critical role in shaping the charge properties of chitosan. At pH values above its isoelectric point, the positive charge of chitosan progressively diminishes, reducing its ability to condense negatively charged species such as DNA.21 The charge characteristics of chitosan and its derivatives are therefore central to its function as a vaccine adjuvant. By deliberately adjusting the DD and the surrounding pH, one can achieve precise control over its positive charge density and stability.

In chitosan derivatives, charge regulation is additionally governed by the degree of substitution and the type of substituent introduced, and these factors should be considered alongside DD and environmental pH. For example, N,N,N-trimethyl chitosan and hydroxypropyltrimethyl ammonium chitosan carry permanently cationic groups, so increasing the degree of quaternization typically increases positive charge density and improves charge stability under neutral conditions.22 By contrast, carboxymethyl chitosan and sulfated or sulfopropylated chitosan introduce anionic groups and therefore shift the net charge toward negative or pH-dependent behavior, with the degree of substitution determining the balance between residual cationic amino groups and the newly introduced anionic moieties.23,24 Polyethylene glycol–grafted chitosan and glycol chitosan do not necessarily add fixed ionic groups, but they can reduce the effective surface charge through steric shielding and hydration, thereby weakening electrostatic interactions in a substitution-dependent manner.25–27 Therefore, the degree of substitution is a key parameter for defining charge density, charge stability, and downstream antigen binding in chitosan-based adjuvant design.

Chitosan-Based Composite Systems: Design and Optimization

Combining chitosan with natural organic components is one of the most fundamental and well‑established strategies for constructing multifunctional vaccine adjuvants. This “organic–organic” composite mode not only preserves chitosan’s intrinsic bioactivity and mucoadhesive capacity, but also leverages the structural features and functional complementarity of the co‑components to further optimize antigen loading, sustained‑release behavior, and biocompatibility of the carrier.28 An example of chitosan–organic composites is its combination with sodium alginate. Sodium alginate is a biodegradable and biocompatible polysaccharide29 bearing abundant carboxyl groups, which are negatively charged under physiological pH. When chitosan solution is mixed with sodium alginate solution, strong electrostatic attraction drives the formation of a crosslinked network. The modified chitosan-alginic acid hydrogel microcapsules can serve as a programmable oral drug delivery system for the transportation of tumor nanovaccines.30 These microcapsules protected the engineered bacteria in the stomach environment and achieved targeted intestinal release, resulting in a 5.9-fold increase in cumulative release amount within 20 hours. In vitro, the nanovaccine promoted DC maturation and T-cell activation; in vivo oral administration induced both intestinal mucosal and systemic immunity, increased T-cell responses by over twofold, and achieved 75% inhibition of tumor growth in CT26 tumor-bearing mice. In addition to the oral delivery method, the composite system of chitosan and alginate is also applicable to the muscle injection protocol. When chitosan nanoparticles coated with alginate are used as an adjuvant for the hepatitis A vaccine, the antigen loading efficiency reaches 78.5%, and a 100% seroconversion rate can be achieved after immunization, significantly increasing the antibody level.31 The “organic-organic” composite of chitosan has great potential for application in oral vaccines and injectable vaccines, and it also has good compatibility with other organic materials such as carrageenan32 and xanthan gum.33

The “organic–inorganic” hybrid strategy that combines chitosan with inorganic nanomaterials can achieve a synergistic enhancement in immunostimulation by integrating the advantageous physicochemical properties of inorganic materials with the bioactivity of chitosan. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs), characterized by ordered mesoporous architectures, high specific surface areas, and readily functionalizable surfaces, are considered ideal carriers for drug and antigen delivery.34,35 Chitosan modification imparts a positive surface charge to MSN-based adjuvant particles, facilitating antigen transport across the gastrointestinal barrier and sustained release, thereby eliciting robust systemic as well as mucosal immune responses.36 Combining chitosan derivatives with metal nanoparticles can solve problems such as particle aggregation and insufficient mucosal adhesion. In a mucosal vaccination model employing Newcastle disease virus antigens, this hybrid platform exhibited favorable acid resistance, colloidal stability, and biocompatibility.37 Functionalized Graphene oxide (GO) can enhance both humoral and cellular immune responses.38 The synergistic incorporation of chitosan with nanoscale GO enhances GO biocompatibility while concurrently promoting stronger immune activation. Chitosan-functionalized, GO-based nanoparticles carrying the hepatitis E virus P239 protein overcome intrinsic material limitations of pristine GO, including its uniformly negative surface charge (ie, an inherently negative ζ-potential) and strong interlayer van der Waals interactions that promote restacking.39 After chitosan modification, the chitosan–GO composite exhibited charge reversal, thereby improving antigen-binding ability. Meanwhile, the chitosan layer introduced steric hindrance, disrupting the driving force for aggregation of GO sheets and significantly improving the dispersion stability of the composite particles (even after high-speed centrifugation at low temperature, they remained stably dispersed; atomic force microscopy showed a thickness of about 12.5 nm, about eight times the thickness of a pristine GO monolayer (1.5 nm)).

In summary, through a multilayered materials-design strategy of “precise molecular-weight tailoring – electrical charge-oriented modulation – composite system optimization” chitosan has been transformed from a single material into a multifunctional carrier (Figure 1). This approach meets the functional requirements of different vaccine delivery routes and provides materials-based solutions for improving key performance metrics of vaccine adjuvants, including encapsulation efficiency, delivery precision, and immunostimulatory capacity. Immunological Determinants

cGAS–STING Pathway

Among the many signaling pathways activated by chitosan, the cGAS–STING pathway has attracted particular attention because of its central role in driving potent Th1‑type cellular immune responses40 (Figure 2). The cGAS–STING axis is a key innate immune surveillance system for sensing cytosolic DNA. It operates by activating the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS, which in turn induces a conformational change in the adaptor protein STING. Activated STING then promotes downstream transcription factors that initiate the transcription of type I interferons.41 Th1‑type responses are characteristically marked by the secretion of interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ),42 which is crucial for the clearance of intracellular pathogens (such as viruses and certain bacteria) and for antitumor immunity. In addition, STING‑activating molecules used as vaccine adjuvants in mucosal settings have demonstrated notable advantages.43 Following phagocytic uptake of chitosan by DCs, mitochondrial stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production are induced, promoting opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore and release of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol, where cytosolic DNA is sensed by cGAS to catalyze cGAMP generation and subsequently activate the STING pathway.44 In bone marrow–derived DCs from mice with cGAS or STING gene knockout (Mb21d1−/− or Tmem173−/−), chitosan of any MW fails to induce secretion of IFN‑β and CXCL10, and the upregulation of DC maturation markers (CD40/CD86) is completely abrogated. This indicates that the cGAS–STING pathway is absolutely essential for chitosan‑induced type I interferon responses and DCl maturation. Nlrp3

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a large cytosolic protein complex that functions as an alarm system of the innate immune response. It senses a wide range of danger signals, including pathogen‑associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs).46 Chitosan has been demonstrated to be an effective activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, with mitochondrial stress playing a pivotal role in this process (Figure 3). Treatment with chitosan leads to increased mitochondrial ROS production, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and leakage of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol, all of which are well‑recognized triggers for NLRP3 activation.47 Both in vitro and in vivo experiments conducted by Li48 showed that the absence of NLRP3 significantly attenuates chitosan‑induced IL‑1β secretion and caspase‑1 activation. Interestingly, Li’s study also revealed intimate crosstalk between the NLRP3 pathway and the cGAS–STING pathway. First, they share upstream activating signals such as mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).49 Second, activation of the cGAS–STING pathway itself can provide the priming signals required for NLRP3 activation. This interdependent relationship means that signals triggered by chitosan via one pathway can effectively amplify the response of the other, thereby achieving a robust mobilization of the innate immune system. TLRs

The TLR family recognizes molecular signatures of microbial pathogens and plays a critical role in innate immune responses. TLRs transmit signals through a common downstream pathway, ultimately inducing the expression of various inflammatory genes.50 Chitosan can specifically bind to the extracellular ligand‑binding domain of TLR2, thereby initiating the myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88) signaling cascade. MyD88 recruits and activates interleukin-1 receptor–associated kinase (IRAK) and TRAF6 (TNF receptor–associated factor 6), triggering a series of phosphorylation events that culminate in the activation of NF‑κB, which then regulates the transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6, TNF‑α, and IL‑1β.51 This process is not solely dependent on TLR2; it also involves synergistic interactions with other receptors such as DC–associated C-type lectin 1 (Dectin‑1) and fibrinogen C domain–containing protein 1 (FIBCD1), which further amplify TLR2‑mediated signaling.52,53 TLR4 is another key target through which chitosan derivatives activate immune responses. As a transmembrane receptor, TLR4 plays a central role in innate immunity. It can recognize exogenous ligands at the cell surface as well as endogenous ligands, thereby initiating complex intracellular signaling cascades.54 Through a series of experiments, Zhang55 showed that chitosan oligosaccharides (COS) bind to TLR4 on the surface of macrophages, mediating the phagocytic uptake of COS and ultimately enhancing macrophage immune function, as evidenced by upregulated mRNA levels of TLR4 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). The interaction between COS and TLR4 is likely due to structural similarities between COS and the saccharide portion of lipid A in chitosan oligosaccharide,56 the canonical ligand of TLR4. Taken together, chitosan and its derivatives activate a multi‑receptor, multi‑pathway immune activation network through specific interactions with TLR2 and TLR4 within the TLR family (Figure 4). This provides molecular‑level theoretical support for their use as vaccine adjuvants to enhance antigen‑specific immune responses. Biocompatibility and Safety Determinants

As a natural cationic polysaccharide, chitosan has been experimentally demonstrated to exhibit low toxicity, biodegradability, and good biocompatibility. In terms of in vivo toxicological evaluation, 16 in vivo toxicity studies on chitosan nanoparticles conducted between 2014 and 2024 indicate57 that, in acute and subacute toxicity assessments using small rodents, chitosan nanoparticles do not show significant acute toxicity even under single high-dose administration, thereby meeting the criteria for a “low-toxicity or non-toxic” drug delivery carrier. In most subacute studies, no pronounced adverse effects were observed. Across different routes of administration (oral, intravenous, inhalational, and topical), chitosan nanoparticles consistently demonstrated a favorable safety profile, and their toxicity was generally lower than that of the corresponding free drugs.58 A series of zebrafish embryo in vivo toxicity models provide convergent evidence supporting these conclusions.59–61 In vitro safety evaluation systems are a critical component for assessing the clinical translation potential of chitosan-based adjuvants and currently rely primarily on standardized assays of cytotoxicity and hemocompatibility. Cytotoxicity testing using L929 cells and hemolysis assays have shown that all chitosan-treated groups exhibit a hemolysis rate of < 5%, a relative cell proliferation rate of >80%, and normal cell morphology, indicating a lack of cytotoxicity.62 A Phase I clinical trial evaluated the safety of an intranasal norovirus virus-like particle (VLP) vaccine formulated with chitosan as an adjuvant in healthy adults.63 The results showed that the vaccine effectively induced elevations in both IgG and IgA antibody levels without triggering serious adverse events. Collectively, these safety evaluations provide preliminary evidence supporting chitosan and its derivatives as safe and reliable vaccine adjuvants. However, most available safety evidence remains formulation-specific, and data on repeated mucosal exposure, long-term local inflammation, and potential immune overstimulation are still limited. Therefore, further systematic investigation is required before broad clinical translation can be confidently pursued.

Formulation Design Strategies and Scenario Adaptability

Particle Size–Adjuvanticity Relationship

Once the material properties and immunological activity of chitosan have been clarified, the next step in precise modulation lies in using advanced materials engineering techniques to construct these polymers with defined attributes into delivery systems of different morphologies. Particle size is a key physical parameter that determines the behavior of nano‑ or micron‑scale carriers: biodistribution, cellular uptake efficiency, and antigen‑release profile, thereby profoundly shaping the immune response. Nanoparticles (typically 1–500 nm) are currently the most extensively studied chitosan‑based delivery systems. Their core advantage lies in an exceptionally high surface area–to–volume ratio, which confers excellent antigen adsorption and encapsulation capacity.64 More importantly, this size range falls precisely within the sensitive window for cellular endocytosis.65 Design strategies for chitosan nanoparticles generally aim to maximize uptake by antigen‑presenting cells (APCs), thereby amplifying the initial immune signaling. In this way, nanoparticles can simultaneously function as highly efficient antigen delivery vehicles (through APCs targeting and serving as an antigen depot) and as immune activators (ie, exerting adjuvant effects).66,67 Chitosan nanoparticles in the range of 100–500 nm, particularly 200–300 nm, can be efficiently internalized by macrophages and DCs. For example, approximately 300‑nm OVA‑loaded chitosan particles achieved a macrophage uptake efficiency as high as 76%.68 At the same time, chitosan nanoparticles also show great promise as mucosal adjuvants (eg, for intranasal vaccines). A chitosan‑modified nano-emulsion (167.2 nm) exhibited prolonged retention in the nasal mucosa and achieved a cellular uptake efficiency of 59.7% in Madin–Darby canine kidney cells, compared with only 8.9% for the unmodified nano-emulsion. Currently, microfluidic and microemulsion technologies make it possible to prepare chitosan nanoparticles with highly uniform particle sizes and stable antigen encapsulation tailored to the requirements of antigen or drug delivery.69

In contrast to nanoparticles, microparticles (1 μm–1 mm) exhibit distinctly different functional characteristics. Particles <5 μm can be efficiently internalized by APCs and transported via the lymphatic system to immune organs such as the spleen.70 However, particles >10 μm cannot be directly taken up by APCs. This property constitutes a unique advantage—the “antigen reservoir” effect. When microparticles are injected subcutaneously or intramuscularly, they can form a stable sustained‑release platform at the injection site, allowing APCs prolonged and repeated access to antigens. This extends the temporal window of immune stimulation and enhances and sustains the immune response. For instance, chitosan/calcium phosphate composite microparticles (particle size 3121–4265 nm) not only exhibit high encapsulation efficiency and structural stability but also show a favorable long‑term sustained‑release profile (maintaining an IL‑4 release rate of approximately 68% and a ginsenoside Rb1 release rate above 45% over 4 weeks), thereby avoiding the “burst release” effect that can lead to immune tolerance.71 Chitosan microparticles also possess distinctive mucosal‑targeting capabilities: M cells in the Peyer’s patches of the intestine can efficiently internalize chitosan microparticles <10 μm, enabling oral vaccines to precisely target key inductive sites of mucosal immunity. A similar targeting mechanism is applicable to intranasal mucosal vaccines.72

Differences in particle size among chitosan nanoparticles lead to distinct antibody kinetic profiles. Smaller particles (272.5 nm) induced higher levels of neutralizing antibodies in the early phase of immunization, whereas slightly larger particles (343.2 nm) elicited higher antibody titers at later time points.73 This suggests that by finely tuning the particle size distribution within a single adjuvant system, it is possible to further modulate the kinetics and polarization of the immune response in a nuanced manner. Moreover, in a subcutaneous vaccination study,74 chitosan particles with an average size of 1 μm outperformed other size ranges in terms of APCs uptake, activation, and antigen presentation. This apparent inconsistency does not indicate a contradiction per se, but rather underscores the strong dependence of particle-size effects on administration route, antigen type, formulation architecture, and immune readout. Therefore, there is no universally optimal particle size across all vaccine platforms; rather, the most appropriate size or size distribution should be selected according to the intended delivery scenario and immunological goal.

Morphology-Driven Formulation Optimization

Hydrogels

The multifunctionality of chitosan as a vaccine adjuvant is largely attributable to its highly tunable morphology and structure, which can be rationally engineered to optimize antigen delivery, stability, and immunogenicity across diverse physiological environments. Chitosan-based hydrogels represent a transformative class of vaccine delivery systems.75 Their design essence lies in mimicking the physiological properties of biological mucosa and achieving intelligent responsiveness to the external microenvironment (primarily pH-responsive and thermo-responsive systems), thereby enabling prolonged antigen retention and precise release.76 Chitosan hydrochloride–sodium alginate composite hydrogels are a typical example of pH-responsive hydrogel adjuvants: they can maintain structural integrity over a wide pH range of 3–11, implying robust sustained-release performance in blood, muscle tissue, and even acidic intracellular compartments.77 In addition, this adjuvant exhibits favorable safety, injectability, and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (inhibition rate >60%), effectively reducing the risk of microbial contamination during vaccine manufacture and administration.

Thermo-responsive hydrogels exist as free-flowing sols at room temperature, which facilitates injection, and undergo rapid sol–gel transition at body temperature to form a stable gel network. Andrew et al78 developed a thermo-sensitive chitosan hydrogel that remains in solution state at room temperature, allowing convenient mixing of antigens (eg, ovalbumin, OVA, and TRP2 peptide) with the adjuvant Quil‑A. Upon injection in vivo (37 °C), it rapidly forms a gel, enabling sustained antigen release and increasing the frequency and duration of antigen contact with DCs, thereby laying the foundation for the establishment of long-lasting immune memory. Wu et al79 designed two HTCC hydrogels (hydrogels based on quaternized chitosan derivatives) as formulations for an H5N1 split influenza vaccine. Both the intranasal hydrogel and the intramuscular hydrogel microspheres demonstrated prolonged release and robust, well-balanced immune responses. Collectively, these examples highlight a key design principle: for scenarios requiring strong and durable immune responses, leveraging the depot effect of hydrogels may represent a superior strategy.

However, the application of hydrogels is not without challenges. Hydrogels can enhance antigen permeation by disrupting tight junction proteins (such as ZO‑1) in the nasal mucosa. While transient disruption can facilitate antigen absorption, long-term or high-dose exposure may compromise mucosal barrier function. This risk can be mitigated through optimized dosing strategies, such as limiting the amount per administration and extending dosing intervals. Existing studies have confirmed that the short-term effects of chitosan on mucosa are reversible and that barrier function can gradually recover after cessation of administration.80 In the future, molecular modification of hydrogel-forming chitosan derivatives may offer a more fundamental solution to this limitation.

Nanoparticles

Distinct from the macroscopic depot effect of hydrogels, the core advantage of chitosan-based nanoparticles (CNPs) lies in the unique biological properties conferred by their nanoscale dimensions. As a natural cationic polysaccharide, chitosan can form nanoparticles that efficiently encapsulate negatively charged biological macromolecular antigens (such as proteins, DNA, and mRNA) via electrostatic interactions, resulting in stable complexes.81 This encapsulation not only provides a physical barrier protecting fragile antigens from degradation by various endogenous enzymes, but also endows the nanoparticles with intrinsic adjuvant activity capable of actively engaging and activating the innate immune system.82 Antigen protection, sustained release, and stimulus-responsive release are key strengths of chitosan nanoparticles. They can preserve antigen immunogenicity while achieving high loading efficiency. For example, chitosan nanoparticles encapsulating inactivated Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV) antigens achieved a loading efficiency of 93.75%. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that RVFV–CNPs were regular, smooth-surfaced spheres, a morphology that reduces direct contact between the antigen and degradative enzymes and ensures stability of antigen delivery.83 Their sustained-release capacity has been validated in vivo: after intranasal administration of chitosan nanoparticles used as an anti-coccidioidomycosis vaccine, fluorescently labeled chitosan nanoparticles remained on the upper respiratory mucosa for up to 96 hours with stable signal, in sharp contrast to the control group, in which fluorescence intensity dropped from 5.5×1012 to 1.1×1012 within 24 hours.84

This sustained-release behavior has also been demonstrated in vitro. In a Mycobacterium tuberculosis vaccine using chitosan nanoparticles as adjuvant, Hspx–PPE44–EsxV (HPE) in HPE/resiquimod/chitosan nanoparticles (HPERC) was labeled with radioactive iodine,85 revealing excellent controlled-release characteristics. The initial release rate was moderate, with gradual increase over time, reaching a plateau at around 72 hours, thus avoiding the excessive peak exposure associated with “burst release” and overly strong immune stimulation. Another formulation, a nanoparticle system based on an acidified sucralfate–chitosan derivative (SA@N‑2‑HACC/CMCS/BSA NPs),86 combined potent immunostimulatory capacity with intelligent antigen delivery and protection: it released almost no payload in simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.2), but showed continuous release in simulated intestinal fluid (pH 6.8). This stimulus-responsive design ensures that the antigen is not prematurely released before reaching its intended site of action, thereby improving drug utilization and targeting, and is well-suited to the physiological requirements of oral vaccines.

In addition, chitosan-modified lipid nanoparticles as adjuvants for mRNA vaccines are also a feasible strategy. Chitosan optimized the physicochemical properties of lipid nanoparticles by enhancing dispersion uniformity (PDI < 0.2) and improving mRNA encapsulation efficiency (>95%).87 This strategy also significantly enhances mucosal adhesion and mRNA stability.88 After intranasal administration, this formulation can penetrate the pulmonary mucus barrier, prolong the residence time in the lungs, and target immune cells, thereby inducing strong systemic and mucosal immune responses, as well as a Th1-biased immune memory response. For injectable delivery, co-administration of a chitosan-based biodegradable hydrogel scaffold with mRNA lipid nanoparticles enables sustained mRNA release and efficient transfection, thereby enhancing the cellular and humoral immune responses of mRNA vaccines.89

Taken together, these examples illustrate that CNPs, through a coordinated mechanism of “high-efficiency loading–physical protection–controlled release–stimulus responsiveness”, provide a robust solution for antigen delivery in mucosal, oral, and other vaccination routes, and lay an important foundation for the functional optimization and clinical translation of future vaccine carriers.

Microneedles

Chitosan-based microneedle arrays (MNs) are emerging as one of the most attractive future directions owing to their distinctive features of minimal invasiveness, convenience, and precision. Compared with conventional hypodermic needles that penetrate several millimeters into the skin, microneedles typically penetrate only about 480 μm, reducing pain by approximately 90%.90 A dissolvable microneedle array composed of chitosan and polyvinyl alcohol has been shown to exhibit good biocompatibility with keratinocytes and fibroblasts and to achieve sustained drug release over a period of 48 hours.91 Chitosan MNs also possess intrinsic antibacterial activity and pro-angiogenic effects,92 effectively promoting wound healing—features that may, in the future, allow safe vaccination in populations at high risk of localized infection. Existing studies have provided substantial evidence for the immunopotentiating effects and application potential of chitosan-based microneedles in tumor vaccine delivery. Guo et al93 developed a biodegradable chitosan–trehalose microneedle system co-loading hepatitis B core protein VLPs as tumor antigens and mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) as an adjuvant. This platform exhibited mechanical properties suitable for skin penetration and enabled complete sustained antigen release within 16 hours. In vitro, it significantly promoted DC maturation and antigen presentation, and in vivo it effectively elicited antigen-specific humoral and cellular immune responses, thereby achieving efficient tumor prophylaxis in a melanoma model. Co-administration with CpG further induced robust abscopal antitumor effects and durable immunological memory. Chen et al94 fabricated chitosan-polyvinylpyrrolidone composite microneedles loaded with a nanovaccine based on functionalized MSNs. They demonstrated a synergistic interplay between the adjuvant activity of chitosan and the nanocarrier’s functions in APC targeting and endosomal escape, which markedly enhanced MHC-I–mediated cross-presentation efficiency. Compared with subcutaneous injection, these microneedles increased antigen accumulation in draining lymph nodes and induced stronger cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, resulting in superior tumor prevention/therapy and enhanced memory formation in a melanoma model.

Despite their promising outlook, chitosan microneedles are still in an early stage with respect to vaccine application: comprehensive vaccine studies remain scarce, and the rate of technological translation is relatively low. Critical issues requiring resolution include ensuring that each individual microneedle meets stringent specifications for geometry, mechanical strength, and payload capacity. In the future, the integration of advanced manufacturing methods such as 3D printing may accelerate the commercialization and large-scale deployment of this potentially revolutionary technology.

The “Chitosan + X” Synergistic Adjuvant System

The adjuvant properties of chitosan arise from its distinctive physicochemical characteristics and its complex interactions with the host innate immune system. However, when used alone, chitosan exhibits certain functional limitations. For example, chitosan by itself induces only a “partially activated” state in DCs: these DCs are unable to effectively drive T-cell proliferation and activation, nor can they induce the secretion of Th1-type cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-2.95 This suggests that chitosan provides an incomplete activation signal for DCs—one that likely focuses on the initial enhancement of antigen-presentation capacity, while the subsequent and more decisive T-cell-polarizing cues require additional signals. Such a “signal gap” has inspired the use of modular design platforms enabled by modern nanotechnology. By co-encapsulating chitosan with other functional components within a single nanocarrier, it becomes possible to achieve precise temporal and spatial co-delivery, thereby forming a “chitosan + X” synergistic adjuvant system (Figure 5). TLR Agonists

Combining chitosan with specific TLR agonists has proven to be a successful strategy. The excellent delivery capacity of chitosan nanoparticles allows TLR agonists to effectively drive and enhance immune responses in vivo. Trimethyl chitosan nanoparticles combined with a TLR9-specific agonist (CpG-1826)96 demonstrated a marked immunoenhancing effect in an injectable influenza vaccine. Compared with using the TLR agonist or chitosan nanoparticles alone, the combined formulation significantly increased both antibody titers and cellular immune responses in the early phase after immunization. In mucosal immunization, sublingual vaccination with a formulation combining methyl glycol chitosan (MGC) and the TLR4 agonist CRX-601 for sublingual delivery of an influenza vaccine in mice yielded serum anti-influenza IgG titers that were equal to or higher than those achieved by intramuscular injection.97 At the same time, the delivery-enhancing effect of MGC allowed for a reduction in the effective dose of CRX-601, thereby lowering the risk of adverse effects associated with TLR agonists.

In the context of cancer vaccines, the combination of chitosan and TLR agonists has also shown promise. poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)–chitosan composite nanoparticles co-loaded with two TLR agonists—poly I:C (a TLR3 agonist) and R848 (a TLR7/8 agonist)98—exhibited clear antitumor efficacy and potent immunoenhancing effects in both prophylactic and early therapeutic immunization against pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. In these systems, the delivery capability of chitosan is crucial for enabling TLR agonists to overcome biological barriers and activate immune pathways, ultimately achieving a coordinated “carrier–adjuvant–antigen” enhancement effect. Across multiple application scenarios, this approach has shown good adaptability, underscoring its flexibility and customizability.

Cytokines

Beyond activating PRRs to initiate immune responses, chitosan and its nanocarriers can also be used to precisely deliver key immunoregulatory molecules—cytokines—to target sites, thereby amplifying and fine-tuning local immune signaling. This “chitosan + cytokine” design concept goes beyond the traditional role of adjuvants as mere providers of primary stimulation and moves toward active shaping of the immune microenvironment. Chitosan/γ-polyglutamic acid (Ch/γ-PGA) nanoparticles have been used as carriers for IFN-γ delivery.99 In this strategy, IFN-γ-loaded Ch/γ-PGA nanoparticles are administered via peritumoral subcutaneous injection, delivering the cytokine directly to the tumor and its surrounding microenvironment. This approach successfully activated local immune cells, amplified and finely regulated local immune signaling, and ultimately produced antitumor effects. IL-2 is a pivotal factor for T-cell growth and differentiation, but systemic high-dose IL-2 therapy can cause severe toxic side effects.100 Chen et al101 prepared chitosan nanoparticles encapsulating plasmids encoding IL-2, thereby protecting the cytokine gene from nuclease degradation and ultimately inducing a Th1-biased immune response. These examples collectively demonstrate that chitosan-based carriers can efficiently deliver cytokines and elicit robust immune responses.

Chemokines

Chemokines are an essential component of the immune system. Functionally, they act as “chemotactic cytokines”, regulating the migration and localization of immune cells by establishing concentration gradients.102 Leveraging their ability to precisely control immune-cell trafficking, researchers have begun to employ chemokines as a new class of adjuvants in vaccine development.103 At present, a common strategy is to co-deliver chemokines as independent adjuvant molecules alongside antigens.104 This approach allows separate optimization of chemokine and antigen dosage while avoiding the complexity of constructing fusion proteins. In one study, a DC2.4/Hepa1-6 fusion-cell vaccine was co-administered with folate-modified chitosan/mIP-10 plasmid nanoparticles (Folate-Modified Chitosan/mIP-10 Plasmid Nanoparticles),105 illustrating the potential of combining the two as a “vaccine adjuvant system”. The folate modification enables the chitosan nanoparticles to specifically bind folate receptors, which are highly expressed on the surface of hepatocellular carcinoma cells, thus delivering the mIP-10 plasmid precisely to the tumor site and avoiding systemic chemokine dissemination and associated side effects. At the same time, this strategy achieves sustained expression of mIP-10 protein within the tumor.

Another study co-administered chemokine LTN and VP1(from Coxsackievirus B3) antigen particles— “chitosan-pVP1” and “chitosan-pLTN”—via the intranasal route, further demonstrating the feasibility of combining chemokines with chitosan particles.106 Compared with immunization using chitosan-pVP1 alone, co-immunization with chitosan-pLTN + chitosan-pVP1 harnessed the chemotactic activity of LTN to achieve comprehensive enhancement of immune responses and effective protection (increased survival: following lethal CVB3 challenge, 28-day survival was 75% in the co-immunization group, 41.67% in the antigen-only group, and 0% in the control group). In addition to co-delivery strategies, fusing the gene encoding a chemokine with that encoding the antigen to generate a chimeric fusion protein is another line of exploration. However, related studies remain relatively scarce, and further application-oriented research along this line is anticipated. Technical Bottlenecks and Potential Solutions

Although chitosan and its derivatives show considerable potential in mucosal delivery and immune enhancement, their clinical translation is still constrained by a combination of physicochemical, immunological, and manufacturing bottlenecks. First, unmodified chitosan exhibits pronounced pH-dependent solubility: deprotonation of amino groups under physiological neutral conditions reduces solubility and promotes aggregation or formation of poorly soluble particulates, thereby destabilizing formulation size distribution, surface charge, and mucosal interactions (adhesion/penetration/retention), ultimately compromising delivery efficiency and batch-to-batch reproducibility.108,109 A common strategy to address this issue is to develop water-soluble derivatives, including quaternized chitosan derivatives such as N,N,N-trimethyl chitosan, as well as carboxymethyl chitosan, sulfated/sulfopropylated chitosan, and glycolated or PEGylated chitosan. In addition, constructing composite systems with polysaccharides such as alginate, co-assembling with nanoscale components, or adopting crosslinking/coating approaches can improve dispersibility at neutral pH while modulating antigen-release kinetics and enhancing system stability. Second, the mechanisms by which chitosan functions as an intranasal vaccine adjuvant are not yet fully defined,110 and available evidence suggests that its innate immune activation is more likely to be multi-pathway, indirect, or context dependent. However, compared with classical immunostimulants with well-defined molecular targets, its receptor specificity and predictability remain relatively limited. Accordingly, when more consistent induction of mucosal IgA or stronger cellular immunity (particularly Th1/CTL responses) is desired, chitosan is often combined with immunostimulants with defined molecular targets to enhance the magnitude and controllability of immune responses.111 More critical translational barriers arise from the high heterogeneity of chitosan feedstocks and structures and the potential risk of endotoxin/impurity contamination. Although chitosan can be produced from low-cost shell resources, variations in source, DD, MW/PDI, acetylation patterning, and residual proteins/ash/metal ions can markedly affect physicochemical properties, in vivo behavior, and immunological readouts. If pyrogenic contaminants such as endotoxin (LPS) are present, their potent innate immunostimulatory activity may lead to overestimation of adjuvant effects or misinterpretation of immune polarization.112 Moreover, because chitosan is not a single, well-defined chemical entity, a quantitative and traceable quality-control system is required to rigorously control parameters such as MW, DDA, PDI, residual impurities, and endotoxin levels.113 Otherwise, the difficulty of CMC standardization, batch consistency, and regulatory review will increase substantially.

In summary, the core translational bottleneck for chitosan adjuvants is not insufficient immunostimulatory potency, but rather limited “controllability”: poor solubility at neutral pH can be mitigated by derivatization or composite systems, yet increased formulation complexity often introduces challenges in characterization and scale-up. Because their immune effects are strongly influenced by material attributes and processing parameters, the mechanisms and immunophenotypes are difficult to predict, and even co-administration with CpG/MPLA to improve robustness can increase formulation complexity and the burden of toxicological justification. More importantly, feedstock heterogeneity and endotoxin/impurity contamination can simultaneously amplify both “positive experimental signals” and “batch-to-batch variability”. Taken together, this structure–property–immunity framework provides a practical basis for rational formulation screening, standardization of critical quality attributes, and prioritization of chitosan-based adjuvant platforms with greater translational potential.107 Going forward, priority should be given to simplifying formulation design, standardizing critical quality attributes, and improving manufacturing consistency while preserving the minimum level of functionality required for in vivo efficacy.

Conclusion and Outlook

This review has systematically elucidated the core capabilities and application potential of chitosan as a novel vaccine adjuvant. Chitosan-based systems can be tailored through MW, charge characteristics, particle size, morphology, and composite design, and they have been shown to activate immune responses via the cGAS–STING pathway, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and TLRs. Despite current technical bottlenecks, chitosan-based adjuvants remain promising candidates for future vaccine development, provided that material controllability and manufacturing feasibility can be better balanced. This article aims to provide a theoretical framework to support the precise development and clinical translation of vaccine adjuvants, thereby fostering innovation in next-generation adjuvant systems.
