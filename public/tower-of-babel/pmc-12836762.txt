The added value of distal radius bone mineral density for the diagnosis of osteoporosis in patients with a history of fragility fracture
Authors: Pegah Heydari, Alireza Mirzaei, Pouria Tabrizian, Mozhdeh Zabihiyeganeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreventing osteoporotic fragility fractures is a critical health priority, yet there is limited data on the utility of distal radius bone mineral density (‎BMD) for this purpose. This study aimed to evaluate the added value of distal radius BMD in osteoporosis diagnosis in patients with a history of fragility fractures.MethodsIn a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data, 1205 who were enrolled in the Fracture Liaison Service of our institute were included in the analysis. The main outcome was the BMD classification of ‎patients using a 3-region BMD (femoral neck, lumbar spine, and distal radius) compared to the conventional 2-region BMD (femoral neck and lumbar spine). The lowest T-score from ‎any skeletal site was used to classify patients into normal, osteopenia, or ‎osteoporosis categories.‎.ResultsUsing a 2-region BMD, normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis were identified in 11.1%, 42.7%, and 46.2% of patients, respectively. After adding distal radius BMD, normal, osteopenia and osteoporosis were detected in 5.6%, 32.9%, and 61.6% of patients, respectively. Two-region and 3-region BMD were discordant in 185 patients. A significant moderate correlation was found between the distal radius T-score and both the lumbar spine T-score (r = 0.470, P < 0.001) and the femoral neck T-score (r = 0.528, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe addition of distal radius densitometry to the conventional BMD regions can result in an earlier diagnosis of osteoporosis in patients with a high risk of fragility fracture. Consequently, it has the potential to be integrated into future osteoporosis classification systems.Keywords: Bone mineral density, Distal radius, Fragility fracture

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a prevalent skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, resulting in heightened bone fragility and susceptibility to fractures [1]. Globally, osteoporosis affects 19.7% of the population, with an additional 40.4% classified as having osteopenia [2].

Osteoporotic fragility fractures are estimated to affect approximately 40–50% of women and 13–22% of men aged 50 and older during their lifetime [3]. The risk of a secondary fragility fracture is reported to be 86% higher in patients with a prior fragility fracture [4]. Given the serious health and socioeconomic consequences of osteoporotic fragility fractures [5, 6], it is crucial to optimize diagnostic and therapeutic approaches to effectively reduce the risk of future fractures.

Traditionally, the diagnosis of osteoporosis relies on bone mineral density (BMD) assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) at specific anatomical sites such as the lumbar spine and proximal femur [7]. These measurements are generally used for fracture risk assessment and guiding therapeutic interventions [7, 8]. However, DXA can be inaccurate in specific scenarios, including overestimation of BMD in degenerative changes of the spine, osteoarthritic hip joints, fractures, or any other artifact like osteosclerosis and aortic calcification [9, 10].

In recent years, the distal radius has garnered attention as an additional site for BMD assessment [11, 12]. This region is less affected by degenerative changes than the spine and hip. Moreover, the distal radius is relatively free of artifacts and confounding factors, especially in older populations, making it more reliable for BMD measurements [13]. Specifically, the 1/3 radius (1/3 R), which refers to the one-third distal part of the radius, has emerged as a potentially valuable complementary location for BMD measurement [14]. Despite this advantage, the clinical utility of 1/3R BMD measurements in osteoporosis diagnosis remains an active area of investigation. This study aimed to assess the additional diagnostic value of 1/3R BMD measurements in the BMD classification of patients with a history of fragility fracture who were enrolled in the Fracture Liaison Service (FLS) of our institute.

Methods

This study received approval from the ethics committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences under code IR.IUMS.REC.1399.1256. Before enrolment in the FLS, patients provided a comprehensive review to use their medical data for anonymous publication.

We retrospectively reviewed the medical profiles of patients who were prospectively enrolled in our institute’s FLS between April 2021 and March 2023. Prior to registration with the FLS, patients signed a comprehensive consent form allowing anonymous use of their medical data for publication. Inclusion criteria were inpatient postmenopausal women and men aged over 50 years who were referred with a fragility fracture and the presence of a 3-region BMD in the patient’s record, covering the lumbar spine, femoral neck, and 1/3R of nondominant forearm. Patients with bilateral radius fractures were excluded from the study. In patients with nondominant forearm fracture, the dominant radius was for BMD assessment. Finally, 1205 patients who met the study criteria were included in the analysis. A fragility fracture was defined as a low-energy fracture caused by a minimal force, such as a fall from standing height or less [15].

Given that our study was conducted in an orthopedic hospital, patients with severe systemic diseases, such as end-stage renal disease (ESRD), were typically referred to general hospitals for multidisciplinary management. In this cohort, cases classified as having chronic renal failure had previously undiagnosed moderate chronic kidney disease (glomerular filtration rate 30–59 mL/min) [16], which was incidentally diagnosed during hospitalization.

A comprehensive secondary osteoporosis workup was performed for all patients, and no cases of secondary osteoporosis (e.g., malignancy or multiple myeloma) were identified. Therefore, secondary osteoporosis was not explicitly categorized in our analysis.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was utilized to evaluate BMD at the spine (L1-L4), femoral neck, and 1/3R using a Hologic Discovery machine (Fig. 1). BMD was quantified in mg/cm², and T-scores were calculated. T-scores greater than − 1 were categorized as normal, T-scores between − 1 and − 2.5 were regarded as osteopenia, and T-scores less than − 2.5 were considered osteoporosis [17].

An example of BMD result

The main outcome of the study was to assess changes in the BMD classification of patients when using a 3-region (femoral neck, lumbar spine, and 1/3R) compared to a 2-region (femoral neck, lumbar spine) BMD. The lowest T-score from any skeletal site was used for the classification of patients into the normal, osteopenia, or osteoporosis category. The secondary outcome of interest was the evaluation of the correlation between 1/3R T-score, lumbar spine T-score, and femoral neck T-score.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS for Windows, version 16. Quantitative variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation, while qualitative variables were presented with numbers and percentages. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using a Chi-square test. The correlation between 1/3R BMD and various clinicodemographic characteristics of the patients was examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all analyses.

Discussion

Given the substantial economic and health burdens associated with osteoporotic fragility fracture, extensive research is dedicated to optimizing osteoporosis diagnosis and treatment [18–20]. In this study, we evaluated the effect of adding distal radius BMD (3-region) on osteoporosis diagnosis in a series of patients with a history of osteoporotic fragility fracture. The addition of the distal radius region significantly changed the BMD classification of patients. This change mainly included shifting the patient’s category from normal and osteopenia toward the osteoporosis diagnosis. To put it differently, when a 3-region BMD was used, a considerably higher number of patients were classified as osteoporosis compared to a conventional 2-region BMD. Interestingly, no significant correlation was observed between the femoral neck and distal radius T-scores, while a significant moderate correlation was observed between the lumbar spine and distal radius T-score.

The added value of distal radius in BMD classification has been reported in a small number of earlier studies. Tripto-Shkolnik et al. evaluated the added value of 1/3R on osteoporosis diagnosis and fracture risk assessment. The addition of 1/3R to the conventional BMD regions increased the osteoporosis rate from 32% to 40%. Moreover, the addition of 1/3R slightly but significantly improved the performance of the Fracture Risk Assessment (FRAX) tool for the prediction of major osteoporotic fractures and hip fractures [14]. In the present study, adding distal radius BMD increased the osteoporosis rate from 46.2% to 61.6%, which was almost twice as the study by Tripto-Shkolnik et al. This discrepancy may be explained by the diversity of patient populations included in the study. We specifically enrolled patients with a history of fragility fractures, where a higher prevalence of osteoporosis is anticipated compared to the general population. Furthermore, this distinction suggests that distal radius BMD may hold even greater clinical significance among individuals at heightened risk of fragility fractures compared to the general population. This could lead to earlier detection of osteoporosis and prompt initiation of treatment, thereby mitigating the risk of primary or future fragility fractures.

Azami et al. compared the 2-region and 3-region BMD in patients suspicious of osteoporosis. Distal radius T-score was significantly correlated with the hip and lumbar T-score. With 2-region BMD, 23.7% were diagnosed as osteoporotic. After the addition of distal radius BMD, the osteoporosis rate increased to 33%. There was a concordance rate of 81.9% between the 2 and 3-region BMD [21]. In this study, incorporating distal radius BMD increased the osteoporosis diagnosis rate by 15.4%.

Eftekhar-Sadat et al. evaluated the utility of wrist BMD in the diagnosis of hip osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. The rate of osteoporosis based on the hip, lumbar spine, and wrist BMD was 24.2%, 49.5%, and 59.6%, respectively. X-ray study showed lumbosacral arthritis in 84.8% of patients. Wrist BMD was significantly correlated with both hip and lumbar spine BMD. They concluded that wrist BMD provides better accuracy than lumbar spine BMD in diagnosing osteoporosis in postmenopausal women [22]. We also observed an increased rate of osteoporosis after the addition of distal radius BMD to conventional BMD sites. Similarly, distal radius BMD significantly correlated with both hip and lumbar spine BMD in the present study.

Patients who experience a primary fragility fracture face a heightened risk of subsequent fractures. Effective osteoporosis treatment is crucial for these individuals, as it significantly lowers the risk of future osteoporotic fractures and improves overall clinical outcomes [23, 24]. According to the American College of Endocrinology (ACE) guidelines, patients who have experienced hip or spine fractures should receive osteoporosis treatment even without a densitometry assessment [25]. However, for fragility fractures other than those of the hip and spine—which represent the majority of patients in this study (76.1%)—a BMD assessment is generally required before starting treatment, while both osteopenia and osteoporosis classifications warrant treatment initiation [25]. Therefore, evaluating BMD at the distal radius is crucial in FLS patients, as this assessment can lead to a more accurate BMD classification, potentially shifting diagnoses from normal to osteopenia or osteoporosis, which can reduce the risk of secondary fragility fractures.

Many countries continue to rely on WHO criteria for diagnosing osteoporosis [26], and insurance coverage for osteoporosis treatment frequently depends on BMD results. If the BMD does not indicate osteopenia or osteoporosis in patients with fragility fractures, many insurance companies will not support the treatment, leading to delays for this high-risk population. This underscores the importance of including distal radius densitometry in FLS evaluations, as it can shift many patients from a normal classification to osteopenia, ensuring that they receive timely and necessary osteoporosis treatment.

The utility of distal radius BMD in refining osteoporosis diagnosis has been investigated across various conditions, with many studies reporting enhanced accuracy when distal radius measurements are added to conventional BMD sites [27–29]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no earlier study has reported the utility of distal radius addition to the conventional 2-region BMD in patients with a history of fragility fracture. Despite its significant benefit, it is important to note that distal radius densitometry requires a high degree of accuracy. Additionally, there are instances where distal radius BMD measurements may be unreliable, such as in patients with hand tremors [9, 30]. Moreover, the accuracy of distal radius BMD measurement can be influenced by the level of experience of the operator performing the scan. Unlike central DXA scans of the spine and hip, which are more standardized, peripheral sites like the distal radius require precise positioning and proper region-of-interest selection. Operator expertise plays a crucial role in minimizing errors related to patient positioning, scan acquisition, and analysis [31, 32].

Our findings demonstrate that including distal radius BMD in the assessment of patients with fragility fractures reclassifies a significant proportion of patients into a higher-risk category. While osteoporosis is traditionally diagnosed based on BMD measurements at the hip or lumbar spine, distal radius BMD may offer valuable insights, especially in patients at high risk of fragility fracture. These findings could lay the groundwork for future redefinition of osteoporosis classification by relevant organizations, advocating for the integration of distal radius BMD into diagnostic algorithms.

The present study was not without limitations. A key limitation of our study was the absence of a control group comprising patients without a history of fragility fractures. Such a control group would have better demonstrated the specific value of distal radius BMD among FLS patients. Our study exclusively focused on FLS patients who had a history of fragility fracture. Additionally, the number of vertebral fractures was too small in the present study. This can be attributed to the institutional setting of our orthopedic hospital, where vertebral fractures are generally managed conservatively rather than surgically. As a result, these patients are often treated on an outpatient basis and, therefore, not captured by our FLS. These limitations could have adversely affected the generalizability of our findings to broader patient populations with different clinical conditions.

Moreover, while our analysis demonstrates that including the 1/3 radius T-score significantly increases the percentage of patients classified as having densitometric osteoporosis, we acknowledge that this does not inherently translate into improved clinical outcomes. The critical question remains whether incorporating the 1/3 radius measurement enhances fracture risk prediction or better identifies patients who will benefit from osteoporosis treatment. Future studies should focus on assessing whether the addition of the 1/3 radius improves predictive performance or whether it refines treatment stratification. Acknowledging this limitation, our study primarily highlights the impact of incorporating distal radius BMD on osteoporosis classification rather than making assumptions about its direct effect on clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

Adding distal radius BMD to the conventional BMD locations significantly improves the osteoporosis diagnosis in patients with a history of osteoporosis fragility fracture. This observation suggests the utility of employing a 3-region BMD for earlier diagnosis and treatment initiation, thereby preventing future fragility fractures in high-risk patients. This approach is particularly valuable when a 2-region BMD assessment does not indicate osteopenia or osteoporosis, highlighting its potential to improve clinical outcomes through more comprehensive risk assessment and management strategies.
