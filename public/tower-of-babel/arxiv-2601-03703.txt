TreeAdv: Tree-Structured Advantage Redistribution for Group-Based RL
Lang Cao * Hui Ruan * Yongqian Li * Peng Chao Wu Ning Haonan Song Renhong Chen † Yitong Li † Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd., China {caolang4,liyitong3}@huawei.com

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with group-based objectives, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), is a common framework for aligning large language models on complex reasoning tasks. However, standard GRPO treats each rollout trajectory as an independent flat sequence and assigns a single sequence-level advantage to all tokens, which leads to sample inefficiency and a length bias toward verbose, redundant chains of thought without improving logical depth. We introduce TreeAdv (Tree-Structured Advantage Redistribution for Group-Based RL), which makes the tree structure of group rollouts explicit for both exploration and advantage assignment. Specifically, TreeAdv builds a group of trees (a forest) based on an entropy-driven sampling method where each tree branches at high-uncertainty decisions while sharing low-uncertainty tokens across rollouts. Then, TreeAdv aggregates token-level advantages for internal tree segments by redistributing the advantages of complete rollouts (all leaf nodes), and TreeAdv can easily apply to group-based objectives such as GRPO or GSPO. Across 10 10 math reasoning benchmarks, TreeAdv consistently outperforms GRPO and GSPO, while using substantially fewer generated tokens under identical supervision, data, and decoding budgets.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training has become a key mechanism for eliciting reliable and multi-step reasoning behavior in large language models (LLMs), especially in mathematics and formal problem-solving tasks
32
;
16
.
As pure next-token prediction reaches diminishing returns
23
, RL provides a way to move beyond surface-level pattern matching toward more deliberate problem solving
14
.
Within the broad landscape of post-training methods, including supervised fine-tuning, RL with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO;
15
;
20
), and offline preference optimization such as DPO
17
, group-based RL methods, in particular Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO;
21
) and Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO;
33
), have emerged as a practical foundation for training reasoning-oriented LLMs
27
. By estimating baselines from group-wise statistics instead of a separate value network, these methods substantially reduce memory overhead, enabling the training of large, long-context reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1
5
.

Despite this progress, GRPO-style methods still rely on sequence-level advantage assignment where supervision is aggregated across entire generated sequences
10
.
Each sampled output
y_{i}
receives a single scalar reward, which is converted to a sequence-level advantage
A_{i}
and applied uniformly to all tokens, sometimes after collapsing finer-grained scores into one objective.
This induces a fundamental limitation for reasoning that any segment appearing in a successful trajectory is rewarded, regardless of whether it is actually helpful
30
;
32
.
Concretely, this leads to (i) degraded reasoning behavior, where long, redundant, or logically inconsistent chains of thought are treated as equally desirable as concise, sound ones; and (ii) noisy optimization signals, since genuinely useful decisions, early mistakes that are later corrected, and exploratory detours all share the same scalar reward.

These issues motivate a token-level view of advantages, where intermediate reasoning fragments are evaluated rather than inheriting a single score determined only by the final outcome
10
;
30
. However, assigning such token-level advantages is non-trivial.
Rather than introducing an additional reward model or requiring dense process supervision, we obtain fine-grained learning signals from terminal scalar rewards via Monte Carlo advantage assignment
29
;
6
;
28
.
Specifically, we aggregate outcomes across multiple sampled continuations to estimate the expected return of intermediate prefixes, and convert these estimates into structure-aware, segment-level advantages that are redistributed to corresponding tokens. Consequently, policy updates reflect how intermediate decisions affect downstream outcomes instead of applying a single sequence-level score uniformly.

To address these challenges, we propose TreeAdv (Tree-Structured Advantage Redistribution for Group-Based RL), a training framework for token-level advantage assignment in reasoning models.
At a high level, TreeAdv explicitly models the reasoning process as a tree of alternative continuations and uses this structure to assign advantages to individual reasoning tokens while correcting for shared prefixes.
Rather than viewing highly correlated rollouts as a source of gradient noise, TreeAdv turns their tree-like topology into a source of informative signal about which local decisions truly contribute to success.

Empirically, TreeAdv improves post-training performance on long-form reasoning benchmarks.
It maintains the stability and computational practicality of Group-Based RL baselines while delivering higher accuracy and substantially reduced token usage, suggesting that token-level structure-aware optimization is an effective lever for training compact and efficient reasoning behaviors in LLMs.

Our main contributions are threefold:

1.

We introduce TreeAdv, a drop-in modification to GRPO-style group-based RL that adds structure-aware token-level advantages while keeping the training pipeline and memory footprint essentially unchanged.

2.

On long-form mathematical reasoning benchmarks, TreeAdv achieves higher final accuracy than GRPO-style baselines and significantly reduces reasoning length, without degrading training stability in large-scale runs.

3.

We analyze how sequence-level rewards lead to undesirable reasoning behavior and noisy optimization signals, as well as how tree-structured and token-level advantage can alleviate these issues from both behavioral and optimization perspectives.

Related Work

2.1
Group-based Policy Optimization

The computational and memory cost of PPO
20
, with its separate value network and GAE-style estimators
19
, has motivated group-based
4
;
13
alternatives for training large reasoning-oriented LLMs. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
21
estimates the baseline from the mean reward of a group of sampled outputs, removing the critic and substantially reducing memory usage in practice, which has enabled large long-context reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1
5
. However, GRPO still assigns a single scalar reward to each sampled response and applies it uniformly to all tokens, making supervision inherently sequence-level and prone to reinforcing locally suboptimal reasoning whenever the final answer is correct
30
;
32
.

GSPO
33
refines this paradigm while preserving the same linear sampling scheme as GRPO. Instead of directly using one per-sequence advantage, GSPO smooths token-level signals by averaging token-wise weights along each sequence, which reduces gradient variance and improves training stability for large reasoning models
27
. Nonetheless, supervision is still aggregated at the level of whole sequences: all tokens in a response are ultimately updated according to one trajectory-level signal, and highly correlated tokens are treated as if they provided independent evidence.

Our work is motivated by these sequence-level failure modes and recent token-level RL studies
10
. We depart from GRPO- and GSPO-style objectives by performing token-level reward assignment, so different parts of a trajectory receive qualitatively different advantages even within the same response. Rather than merely smoothing a single sequence-level signal over tokens, we organize overlapping reasoning paths into a tree and modulate token-level advantages using the induced topology (shared prefixes and branching structure). This yields a structure-aware objective that directly addresses the two issues identified in our Introduction: rewarding only segments that genuinely contribute to success, and reducing noise from treating highly correlated steps as independent.

2.2
Tree-based Reasoning and Rollout Strategies

Tree structures are widely used to improve LLM reasoning at inference time. Methods such as Tree of Thoughts (ToT)
29
and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
6
explore multiple branches for a given prompt and select or aggregate among them, yielding substantial gains on challenging mathematical and logical benchmarks
26
;
31
. These approaches show that explicitly modeling branching structures is beneficial when searching for high-quality reasoning trajectories.

On the training side, rollout strategies that generate branched trajectories complement GRPO/GSPO-style loss-level improvements. TreeRPO
28
, for example, grafts shallow trees onto GRPO by treating different partial continuations as branches and aggregating gradients across them. Such tree-shaped rollouts bring some of the benefits of ToT/MCTS into training by exploring multiple reasoning directions in parallel, but branches are typically combined by simple summation or averaging, without correcting for shared prefixes or leveraging the full tree topology in the learning rule. As a result, tree structure mainly serves as a search scaffold, while supervision remains effectively sequence- or partial-trajectory-level.

TreeAdv is related to both lines of work but differs in emphasis. Like tree-based rollouts, it uses multiple reasoning paths per prompt; however, it (i) constructs a reasoning tree by branching only at high-entropy tokens, and (ii) performs topology-aware, token-level reward assignment on this tree. In TreeAdv, tree structure is not only a vehicle for exploration but a first-class object in the objective itself: it directly shapes how advantages are computed and propagated, aligning the training signal with the internal structure of the reasoning process.

3
Method

We present TreeAdv, a tree-structured extension to group-based RL for multi-step reasoning.
TreeAdv
keeps the standard clipped surrogate objective unchanged
and only substitutes the per-sequence advantages with token-level, structure-aware advantages computed via entropy-guided tree construction.

3.1
Background: Group-Based RL

For a prompt
x
, we sample a group of
K
rollout trajectories, where the
i
-th trajectory at step
t
is denoted as
s_{i,t}=(x,a_{i,1},\cdots,a_{i,t})
from a policy
\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})
.
Group-based objectives (e.g., GRPO) rely solely on a scalar sequence-level reward per rollout,
R_{i}
, and form a group-normalized sequence advantage without a value network:

A_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\mu_{R}}{\sigma_{R}}\,,

(1)

where
\mu_{R}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}R_{j}
, and
\sigma_{R}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}(R_{j}-\mu_{R})^{2}+\delta}
.

In the following, we take GRPO as an example and show how TreeAdv can apply a token-level optimization.
Let
\pi_{\text{old}}
denote the behavior policy for generating rollouts.
GRPO optimizes a PPO style clipped surrogate with per-token likelihood ratios
r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})}
, and
\bar{r}_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)
, and the final objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{|s_{i}|}\min\!\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)A_{i},\bar{r}_{i,t}(\theta)A_{i}\right)\right]

(2)

In this setup, the advantage score
A_{i}
is applied at the sequence level and to all tokens within the rollout
s_{i}
.

3.2
TreeAdv: Entropy-Guided Trees and Token-Level Advantage

Research indicates that tokens vary in their importance and should consequently receive distinct advantage scores
25
;
28
.
Empirically, we observed that current RL models often make inference errors during a few intermediate steps, and most of the reasoning steps are correct.
Standard group-based RL fails to distinguish these critical steps, leading to sample inefficiency and a tendency to generate unnecessarily long rollouts.

Motivated by these observations, we proposed a method, TreeAdv, which considers token-level advantages.
Specifically, our method samples rollout trees (rather than independent sequences) using an entropy-driven branching strategy.
Then, TreeAdv converts leaf (complete-rollout) advantages into token-level advantages by organizing the
K
rollouts of each prompt into a prefix forest and aggregating advantages on shared prefix segments.

Entropy-guided branching.

We measure uncertainty at a prefix state
s_{t}
, omitting the index
i
, by the token entropy under the behavior policy:

H(s_{t})=-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\text{old}}(a_{t}\mid s_{<t})\log\pi_{\text{old}}(a_{t}\mid s_{<t}).

Starting from the prompt, we perform linear rollouts under
\pi_{\text{old}}
.
Positions where entropy
H(s_{t})
exceeds a threshold
\tau
are treated as
branching points
. At these points, we sample multiple distinct next tokens to create child nodes, expanding the frontier (Appendix
A.1
).
This process yields (i) a set of completed rollouts (leaves) for reward evaluation, and (ii) internal nodes representing shared reasoning segments.

Assigning token advantages

For each rollout trajectory
s_{i}
, we compute the standard group-normalized sequence advantages
A_{i}
as in Eq.
1
.
The token advantage
A_{i,t}
of token
a_{i,t}
is defined by summing up the advantage scores of the trajectory set
S(a_{i,t})
, consisting of all trajectories sharing
a_{i,t}
, and then normalized by the number of trajectories,

A^{\text{tok}}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{\left|S(a_{i,t})\right|}\sum_{\ell\in S(a_{i,t})}A_{\ell}.

(3)

The normalize operation can prevent advantages near the root (with many descendants) from dominating by scale.

TreeAdv objective in GRPO

TreeAdv integrates
\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t}
into the same clipped surrogate as GRPO, the objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{TreeAdv}}(\theta)
is defined as,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{|s_{i}|}\min\!\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t},\bar{r}_{i,t}(\theta)\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t}\right)\right]

(4)

All other components, e.g., KL regularization to a reference policy, remain unchanged, and TreeAdv only modifies the construction of advantages on the token level.

TreeAdv objective in GSPO

TreeAdv integrates
\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t}
into the same clipped surrogate form, while replacing a length-normalized sequence-level importance ratio (PPO-style) with the per-token likelihood ratio.
We follow the definition of GSPO-token and define the length-normalized sequence ratio for trajectory
i
as

w_{i}(\theta)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|s_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|s_{i}|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})}\right)

(5)

and construct a token-wise ratio

w_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[w_{i}(\theta)\right]\cdot\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})}{\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,<t})\right]},

(6)

where
\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]
denotes the stop-gradient operator.
We further define the clipped ratio
\bar{w}_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)
.
Then, the objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{TreeAdv}}(\theta)
for GSPO is defined as

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{|s_{i}|}\min\!\left(w_{i,t}(\theta)\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t},\bar{w}_{i,t}(\theta)\tilde{A}^{\text{tok}}_{i,t}\right)\right].

(7)

Similarly, TreeAdv only modifies the construction of advantages on the token level while adopting a GSPO-style sequence ratio for clipping and optimization.

4.3
Training Dynamics

We next analyze training-time behavior to understand how the improvements emerge.
Figure
4
compares TreeAdv against GRPO/GSPO across model scales, reporting reward (top) and entropy (bottom) over training steps.
Reward provides a direct optimization signal under the training objective, while entropy serves as a proxy for exploration and update volatility.

On
Qwen3-8B-Base
, TreeAdv increases reward faster than GRPO in the early stage, after which the gap narrows and the two methods become closer later in training.
At the same time, entropy under TreeAdv decreases more rapidly, indicating earlier policy concentration.
While faster concentration can reduce exploration, it is consistent with the observed rapid early reward gains on the base model and suggests more decisive updates under the same training budget.

In contrast, on instruct models, TreeAdv often shows a short warm-up phase: reward increases more slowly than GRPO/GSPO early on, but TreeAdv consistently catches up and eventually surpasses the baselines as training proceeds.
We attribute this to an adaptation effect of token-/segment-level advantage assignment: redistributing terminal outcomes into more localized advantages can yield more conservative (lower-variance) early updates, which may delay short-term reward gains yet improves long-horizon advantage assignment and stability.
Consistent with this, entropy trajectories under TreeAdv are typically smoother than GRPO/GSPO, indicating lower training volatility.

Across these datasets, accuracy broadly tracks the reward dynamics: TreeAdv improves more steadily, while GRPO exhibits a noticeable regression in the mid-training stage.

Notably, between steps

5.1
Allocating a fixed rollout budget

Figure
5
analyzes training dynamics under a fixed rollout budget of
K{=}16
completed trajectories per question.
GRPO / GSPO sample
K
independent rollouts without shared prefixes.
In our tree-structured variants, we allocate the same
K
rollouts into
M\in\{1,2,4\}
entropy-guided trees.
Smaller
M
induces stronger prefix sharing (more branches per shared prefix), whereas larger
M
increases diversity at root by spreading the budget over more independent prefixes.
Varying
M
therefore isolates a trade-off between within-prefix comparability and global exploration under the sampling budget.

5.2
Regime-dependent preference: sharing vs. exploration

The optimal allocation depends on the model regime.
On the instruct model (Figure
5
a),
M{=}1
yields the best and most stable accuracy improvements, with
M{=}2
and
M{=}4
trailing slightly.
On the base model (Figure
5
b),
M{=}4
achieves the best final accuracy, outperforming
M{=}2
and especially
M{=}1
, which plateaus early.
This pattern is consistent with an exploration–variance trade-off: base models benefit more from discovering high-reward prefixes via root-level diversity, whereas instruct models, already producing more coherent solutions, benefit more from lower-variance learning signals enabled by stronger prefix sharing.

5.3
Accuracy versus token usage

Token-usage trends suggest that gains are not merely driven by longer generations.
On the instruct setting, GRPO becomes increasingly verbose at later steps while accuracy decreases (Figures
5
a,c), indicating that additional tokens do not necessarily improve correctness.
Tree-structured variants keep token usage more controlled and avoid the late-stage regression.
On the base setting, token usage increases with larger
M
(Figure
5
d), reflecting the cost of broader exploration; notably, the best-performing configuration (
M{=}4
) is also the most token-expensive, suggesting that increased exploration is beneficial in this regime despite longer trajectories.
Since training-time compute is approximately proportional to the number of processed tokens (ignoring first-token latency and fixed overheads), these curves also serve as a proxy for training efficiency.
Appendix
A.5
(Figure
6
) reports rollout token statistics across additional model scales.

5.4
Why TreeAdv is more stable

We observe that the GRPO/GSPO baselines can sometimes yield worse post-training performance than the corresponding pre-trained checkpoints on several benchmarks.
A plausible explanation is distribution mismatch, where the RL training set and reward signal may deviate from the model’s original instruction-tuning distribution, such that sequence-level optimization amplifies spurious correlations and causes policy drift.
This issue can be exacerbated by sequence-level advantage assignment, where all tokens in a successful rollout receive the same advantage, potentially reinforcing redundant or format-specific fragments that are not causally related to correctness.

In contrast, TreeAdv performs tree-structured token-level advantage redistribution, assigning based on outcome variation among branched continuations that share the same prefix.
This yields more comparable learning signals on shared segments, reduces variance, and suppresses reinforcement of non-informative tokens.

Finally, on the instruct model under non-think mode, we observe a GRPO-specific instability between the second and third epoch in which the policy collapses toward stalling tokens (e.g., “wait”) and accuracy degrades.
We hypothesize that this arises from style leakage between implicit think and non-think behaviors, where sequence-level advantages inadvertently reward slow-thinking templates when they co-occur with successful trajectories, causing them to propagate into non-think generation (see Appendix
A.7
).
We do not observe the same collapse under TreeAdv, consistent with structure-aware, token-level advantage assignment mitigating spurious template amplification.

6
Conclusion

This work studies how to improve RL fine-tuning under a fixed rollout budget by changing how rollouts are sampled and how advantage is assigned.
We propose TreeAdv, including tree-structured sampling which organizes
K
completed trajectories into
M
entropy-guided trees, and a structure-aware token-level advantage redistribution that uses outcome differences among continuations sharing the same prefix.
This yields more comparable learning signals on shared segments and reduces spurious reinforcement of non-informative tokens.

Across model families, our method consistently increases average accuracy while
reducing
average token usage under the same rollout budget, demonstrating that gains are not driven by longer generations.
Training is also more stable across epochs and settings, with lower variance and fewer late-stage regressions.

Limitations

Our experiments primarily focus on math-heavy long-form reasoning benchmarks and a small set of Qwen-family backbones. While TreeAdv is not inherently math-specific, additional evaluations on other domains (e.g., code, tool use, dialogue) and model families are needed to characterize generality.

TreeAdv also depends on practical rollout-tree construction choices (entropy thresholding, branching constraints, and a no-branch token list).
These heuristics may require tuning across decoding settings, and our entropy estimate is approximated from top-
k
probabilities.

Finally, TreeAdv introduces extra systems complexity (tree/forest rollout management and segment-level aggregation). Although it often reduces redundant generation, realizing wall-clock gains may require careful implementation and scaling studies.

no-branch token list

H(s_{t})>\tau
is satisfied, we suppress branching if the token at position
t
matches any entry in this list (see the tcolorbox below).

k
candidates (with
k=20
in our setup). As a result, we approximate the token entropy using this truncated support rather than the full vocabulary:

A.2
Training Details

Training data.

All RL experiments are trained on the same supervision pool to enable a controlled comparison. We sample a fixed 10K subset from the DeepMath103K corpus and reuse it for all GRPO, GSPO, and TreeAdv runs. The subset is
not
reshuffled across methods: each method observes the same examples in the same order. This shared data schedule simplifies the analysis of optimization dynamics and helps isolate the effect of the learning algorithm.

Optimization and learning-rate schedule.

All methods are implemented in the VERL framework, with optimization settings matched across objectives whenever possible. For all
Base
models, we use a peak learning rate of
5\times 10^{-6}
; for all
Inst
models, we use
1\times 10^{-7}
. The minimum learning rate is set to
1\times 10^{-7}
in all runs. Training lasts 57 optimization steps with a global batch size of 512, corresponding to approximately 3 epochs over the 10K subset. We use 2 warmup steps with a linear ramp from 0 to the peak learning rate, followed by cosine decay to the minimum learning rate. This schedule (total steps, warmup length, and cosine decay) is kept identical for GRPO, GSPO, TreeAdv-GSPO and TreeAdv-GRPO.

Checkpointing and model selection.

We save checkpoints every 5 optimization steps throughout training. For each method and model, we evaluate every saved checkpoint and report the best-performing checkpoint on the held-out benchmarks in the main results. This early-stopping-style selection is applied uniformly to GRPO, GSPO, TreeAdv-GSPO and TreeAdv-GRPO, ensuring that each method is compared at its best point along an otherwise shared training trajectory.

TreeAdv-specific hyperparameters.

TreeAdv introduces a small set of additional hyperparameters beyond the GRPO/GSPO baselines. We use an initial entropy threshold of
H_{0}=1.4
to determine branching points in the tree. The threshold is annealed with a decrement of
0.05
over training, with a floor at
H_{\min}=1.0
. Unless otherwise specified, for each prompt TreeAdv constructs 4 trees. Each prompt is allotted a total of 16 rollouts, distributed across these trees, and this tree/rollout configuration is held fixed across all TreeAdv experiments. To avoid premature branching at the very beginning of generation, we additionally impose an earliest-branching constraint: branching is only allowed at the first position that satisfies the entropy threshold
after
encountering one of the following delimiters in the generated text:
.\n\n
,
,
, or
.\n
.

Sampling for rollouts and inference.

During training rollouts, we use top-
k
and nucleus (top-
p
) sampling with
k=20
and
p=0.7
. We use the same sampling configuration for test-time inference on the evaluation benchmarks (top-
k
=
20
, top-
p
=
0.7
) to keep generation stochasticity consistent between training and evaluation.

Reward design and other settings.

Reward design and other RL-specific settings (e.g., correctness scoring, shaping, and penalty terms) follow the recommended configuration in the DeepMath103K release. We use the same reward function for GRPO, GSPO, TreeAdv-GSPO and TreeAdv-GRPO so that observed differences are attributable to the training objective and advantage construction rather than changes in supervision. To prevent excessively long generations, we apply a length penalty consistently across all experiments: if the output exceeds 16K tokens, we set the original reward to
-1
.

Computational resources.

The comprehensive experimental suite, including all training phases, hyperparameter tuning, and inference evaluations, was executed on a high-performance cluster equipped with
128 NVIDIA A800 GPUs
. The total computational cost for this study, covering all baselines and our proposed method, amounted to approximately
1,500 GPU hours
.
