Battling time restrictions with collective discourse: collaborative quizzes in a condensed human anatomy course
Authors: Zi Guo, Natascha Heise
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUndergraduate medical education is currently undergoing a remarkable period of transformation. The exponential growth of medical knowledge, accompanied by societal changes and expectations for the upcoming generation of physicians, is placing immense pressure on academic institutions to reform their curricula, particularly foundational courses such as human anatomy. Consequently, instructors are grappling with the challenge of striking a balance between a new curriculum and maintaining the time-honored benchmarks of medical education.MethodsThis study proposes a 9,5-week medical gross anatomy course containing collaborative quizzes to improve the efficacy in a condensed pre-clerkship curriculum. It was hypothesized that the implementation of collaborative quizzes facilitates group learning while ultimately helping students to be better prepared for the NBME (National Board of Medical Examiners) unit examinations used as summative assessment within the course. During the four collaborative quizzes, medical and medical masters student groups rotated through twenty dissected donor stations, each containing a short clinical or anatomy-related multiple-choice question formatted in NBME style. Students individually answered the question first and then collaboratively discussed the question in their group. Success was measured by individual student outcomes of four collaborative quizzes, two NBME anatomy-focused unit examinations, and overall course evaluations.ResultsOverall, all students (n = 203) passed the course and performed well on collaborative quizzes and NBME anatomy-focused unit examinations. Analysis of the quizzes revealed that questions involving tagged structures, blood supply, and nerves resulted in the most answer changes after group discussion. Course evaluations proved the collaborative quizzes to be enjoyable and beneficial to identify lack of knowledge areas.ConclusionsTo address time constraints within the pre-clerkship curricula effectively, it is crucial for educators to explore smaller adjustments to core learning principles. Collaborative quizzes in the gross anatomy laboratory provided students with opportunities to assess their knowledge in a low-stakes setting while simultaneously benefiting from peer learning. The collection and analysis of quiz grades offered a method of identifying struggling students before their first summative examination, enabling timely academic support. In the future, the research team hopes to continue using this assessment and integrate content from various other pre-clerkship courses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06416-9.

Background

Graduate and undergraduate medical education is constantly evolving. As new challenges emerge, previously invisible problems transpire and drive a restructuring of policies that flows from the healthcare system to the training of new physicians [1]. Among the many trends of changing medical school curricula, includes a shortening of the pre-clerkship phase timeline from 24 to 18 or 12 months [2, 3]. Naturally, a reduction in the amount of time available to be exposed to the foundational knowledge necessary for clinical practice increases the pressure on faculty to provide innovative teaching methods that focus on efficiency while maintaining the high standards of knowledge retention and depth of understanding that is required of the physician profession [1]. Shortening of the curriculum and the following demand of change in teaching methods is particularly difficult for anatomy teaching faculty as knowledge in anatomy is generally seen as foundational for medical and health profession students [4].

The current pedagogy of medical education is somewhere between the rigorous two-pillar model championed by Flexner and Osler in the early twentieth century resulting in the creation of the preclerkship and clinical clerkship model, and the curricular reforms pushed by discontent among the academic and professional community [5, 6]. In a 2021 survey encompassing 145 responses from first and second-year medical students, it was revealed that merely 63% of first-year students and 53% of second-year students chose to attend non-mandatory lectures [7]. This declining popularity of traditional lecture formats has prompted numerous institutions to explore alternative approaches, such as problem-based learning, small group sessions, and flipped classrooms, all centered around active learning and collaboration [8]. Scoping review of problem-based learning has shown equivalent knowledge retention and academic performance with high satisfaction but requires greater human resources [9]. Small group sessions, particularly in medical education, have yielded mixed results, with academic performance either remaining equivalent or decreasing when compared to didactic lectures. Student preferences also fluctuate between the two methods [10, 11]. Flipped classrooms, while generally well-received by students, lack substantial evidence supporting increased knowledge acquisition over traditional learning methods [12]. Among the many alternative learning approaches, Team-based learning (TBL) has been shown of specific interest due to its efficient use of instructors, high effectiveness and student satisfaction. TBL focuses on the usage of sequential activities to create opportunities for the application of knowledge in a small group environment and is broken down into six consecutive steps: Advance Assignment, Individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT), Team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT), Instructor Clarification Review, Team Application, and Appeal. The strategy begins with students receiving a list of learning activities and objectives to prepare in advance. The class starts with an iRAT, where each student completes a set of multiple-choice questions. This is followed by a tRAT, in which students collaborate to answer the same questions as a group. Afterward, the instructor reviews the material that posed challenges for the students. The session continues with a new, related scenario or set of questions that the teams work on together. Outside of class, students have the option to appeal and request the instructor to reconsider alternative answers that they believe are valid [13].

Compared to the other courses of pre-clerkship education, anatomy is more difficult to restructure due to its traditional inclusion of a physical laboratory component [11]. Anatomy is derived from the ancient greek anatomḗ and is translated as “a cutting up” [14]. It is no surprise that cadaveric dissection has been a staple as a primary educational tool as early as the fifteenth century [15]. In the past two decades, many modifications have been implemented in US medical school anatomy education, notably consisting of reduced total course and dissection time [11]. These alterations possess the potential to yield adverse learning outcomes for students as they result in a condensed time frame for dedicated learning. The early identification of negatively impacted students is essential for the implementation of successful academic support [16, 17]. The traditional teaching methods of didactic lectures and dissection are being reduced with alternatives ranging from prosection to multimedia resources [18]. Among these alternatives, online based student learning was comparable to traditional face-to-face teaching methods for medical school anatomy in terms of academic performance but led to lower levels of satisfaction [19]. These curricular changes were only further spurred on by the era of COVID-19 and social distancing which highlighted the importance of maximizing interactive learning [11]. As medical school curricula continue to evolve, it is inevitable that new challenges will emerge. Along with these challenges, innovative solutions are needed to uphold the high standards of education.

The role of an educator is not to simply present a large amount of information, but rather to transfer the information as well as its understanding and application. Expanding upon the evidence-based shifts in medical education, teachers must continue to innovate and implement new strategies that further improve the student, in both ability and knowledge. Nevertheless, it is crucial to remain vigilant for latent shortcomings in these untrodden concepts and proactively support students to prevent any academic setbacks. In response to the dynamic medical education landscape and a novel opportunity to assess the potential of collaboration and peer-learning in anatomical coursework, this study introduced a modified TBL course approach in a shortened and condensed human anatomy course for first-year medical and medical master’s students at Macon and Joan Brock Virginia Health Sciences Eastern Virginia Medical School (EVMS) at Old Dominion University in Norfolk, VA. The aim of this study was to evaluate the benefits and implications of group assessments in a gross anatomy laboratory, pioneering a fresh perspective in the realm of long-standing teaching methods. It was hypothesized that the implementation of collaborative quizzes facilitates group learning while ultimately helping students to be better prepared for their summative assessments within the course.

Methods

Collaborative quizzes were introduced in a 9.5-week long human anatomy course at EVMS in Norfolk, VA.

Course structure and grading

In fall 2022, the first-year anatomy course for medical and medical master’s students at EVMS consisted of 9.5 weeks and focused on providing students with a comprehensive overview of clinically relevant human anatomy and emphasis on form, structure, function, and development of anatomical structures. The content was divided into two blocks: Block one focused on the anatomy of the lower limb, upper limb, and back whereas the second block covered the head and neck, thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. The structure of the course was split into two components: Lectures and anatomy laboratory sessions. Lectures consisted of didactic teaching sessions either in-person or online that provided students with the relevant information. Anatomy laboratories were in-person during which students learned to dissect donors and utilize the knowledge learned from lectures to identify anatomical structures. Evaluation of students’ understanding was composed of two summative examinations of NBME questions (midterm and final) and four graded formative collaborative quizzes (group score) in the anatomy laboratory spread throughout the course. At the end of the 9.5 weeks, students were asked to fill out an evaluation as part of the course.

Quiz structure and grading

Collaborative quizzes consisted of 20 single-best answer multiple choice questions focusing on human anatomy and some neuroanatomy that tested the concepts and material presented in previous lectures and laboratories. The remaining anatomical sciences such as histology and embryology were only briefly covered as the curriculum at EVMS includes these in future courses. Question stems typically included a clinical vignette or image and commonly included a tagged structure (unidentified structure pinned on a donor). Faculty met weekly to review vetted practice questions from previous courses and accumulate collaborative quiz questions. An example quiz can be found in the Supplemental Materials (Supplemental Materials 1). Due to human anatomy being the first course at EVMS, the setting of the collaborative quizzes in the gross anatomy laboratory, and the increased difficulty of applying knowledge to tags on anatomical donors, first and second-order questions were used. No NBME-style questions were included in the collaborative quizzes since these students already encounter NBME questions in their summative unit examinations. Tagging of the structures took place as a team the day before the quiz. This enabled faculty to unanimously vote on the tagged structures and ensure fairness of the quiz. The first quiz focused on the anatomy of the lower limb. The second on the upper limb, the third on the head and neck, and the fourth and last quiz focused on the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. At the beginning of each quiz, the class cohort was separated into groups of five to six and assigned a station, which was either a donor, bone, or medical image. During the first one and a half minutes, the students had the chance to quietly work through the question individually. During the following one and a half minutes, the students discussed the question and answers with their group. Answers were recorded on a bubble sheet containing two sets of answer choices: one for their individual answer and one for their answer after group discussion. Students then rotated as a group to the next station during which they again had 3 min to note their answers. After the quiz, each student turned in their bubble sheet for grading. ZipGrade (ZipGrade LLC, New Lenox, IL) was used for grading and the answers selected after the group discussion counted towards the student’s grade.

Participants

For this study, the sample was composed of students at EVMS enrolled in either the Medical Master’s (MS) program or Doctor of Medicine (MD) program (Table 1). Both programs followed nearly identical coursework for the first year, including learning resources, quizzes and examinations. All registered students for the 2022–2023 academic year of the MS program and first year students in the MD program were a part of this study, thus forming an opportunity sample. Students from the MS program (n = 52) and students from the MD program (n = 151) were used to create a total sample population of 203. Data collection

Data was collected both quantitatively and qualitatively for this study. Quantitative data consisted of quiz answers, both before and after group discussion of each question, and summative examination grades. Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis consisted of a comparison of quiz answer sheets before and after group discussion using a paired t-test. These answer sheets were used to determine the quiz grades and identified which answers were modified after group discussion. For each student, quiz grades were separated into individual and group, which was compared with their examination grades. Software utilized includes GraphPad (Prism, Boston, MA) and Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). For each quiz question, the number of changed answers from individual to group answer sheets was tallied and recorded. Additionally, each quiz question was divided based on three categories: question type, number of tags, and subject. Question type pertains to the format of the question, categorized as either "Image-based," where the question is centered around a picture, or "Clinical Vignette," where there is no image and the question stem is solely composed of a brief clinical patient presentation. Number of tags indicates the count of unidentified structures used in the question, presented either as an image or an anatomical structure on donors. Subject divides the questions based on the type of material being inquired about such as “Muscle”, “Blood Supply”, “Nerve”, or “Other”. "Other" encompasses subject matter not falling into the predefined categories, such as anatomical landmarks, bones, and organs. These categorizations allowed a comparison of the number of changed answers across different question categories. Qualitative data analysis was composed of a blinded thematic analysis of open-ended questions performed by two researchers. Initial codes were reviewed and finalized to themes.

Discussion

In this study, the results from implementing collaborative quizzes in a gross human anatomy course demonstrated a positive and statistically significant relationship between the average quiz grades before and after group discussion. This was expected due to the collaborative nature of the discussions and sharing of ideas. This relationship is further substantiated by previous studies, which showed that individual assessment immediately followed by group assessment resulted in group grades outperforming individual grades [20]. However, when comparing the percentage difference in quiz grades before and after group discussion with examination grades for each student in this study, a negative correlation was revealed. For this analysis, linear regression was utilized to attempt to quantify the ability of quiz grades to predict student performance on examinations. With an R2 of 0.427, this study reports a moderate relationship between the two. This indicates that students who made more changes to their answers after group discussion were more likely to score lower on the midterm and final examinations. The extent to which answers are altered following group discussions typically mirrors a student's level of confidence in their grasp of the subject matter. Students who exhibit a greater propensity to modify their responses are often indicative of a weaker comprehension of the material, and they are consequently more likely to achieve subpar results in the examination. Metacognitive ratings of response confidence have a positive correlation with response accuracy [21, 22]. Moreover, revisions of low-confidence answer choices are more likely than high-confidence answer choices [21, 23, 24]. This finding can be leveraged to identify students at higher risk of performing poorly on examinations and enable early intervention, both of which are well recognized as important factors in facilitating academic support [20, 25]. Rather than relying solely on the results of the initial major examination to identify students facing difficulties, these collaborative quizzes can serve as an early performance indicator. Moreover, participation in formative assessments have shown to be predictive of greater success for summative assessments [20]. With the current trend of reducing the pre-clerkship timeline, collaborative quizzes could be an efficient tool in monitoring student performance and increasing peer learning [2]. It’s important to note that the statistical analyses in this study combined two distinct student groups into a single cohort, despite differences in their grading and program standards. These grading differences may have led to varied impacts of the intervention on each group.

Additionally, an analysis of question categorization and the variation in the percentage of students changing their answers after group discussion shed light on the impact of different question types. Questions with multiple tags resulted in more students changing their individual answers in three of the quizzes. These tags were unlabeled and linked to specific anatomical structures on the donor, serving as key elements in the quiz question. These questions likely sparked additional peer discussions due to their higher order nature requiring both identification of the structures and application of that knowledge to answer the question, contributing to increased changes in answers. Furthermore, questions related to blood supply and the nervous system in three out of four quizzes also led to a higher percentage of students changing their answers after group deliberation. This highlights areas of weakness among the students and emphasizes aspects for potential curriculum restructuring. When the results of all four quizzes were considered, the aforementioned categories of “Multiple Tags”, “Blood Supply”, and “Nerve” all displayed a greater percentage of students with answer changes after group discussion, with the addition of “1 Tag” questions. Interestingly, the format of the question, whether in a clinical vignette style or based on an image, did not appear to significantly impact the percentage of change in answers following the group discussion.

Lastly, the qualitative analysis of course evaluation survey responses yielded overwhelmingly positive feedback. Many students expressed that the quizzes were enjoyable and helpful, specifically mentioning that the application of knowledge and increased interaction through group discussions facilitated their learning. This was particularly surprising as the course evaluations did not specifically consist of a question focusing on the collaborative quizzes. Nevertheless, 44 students, which is about one fourth of the cohort, shared positive feedback about this assessment tool demonstrating the impact on the students. Negative responses were limited and mainly centered around logistical changes to the quizzes or requests for more frequent quizzes. Amidst the growing shift of educational institutions towards innovative methodologies centered around active learning and collaboration, it is crucial to consider the perspective of the students [8]. In light of this, collaborative quizzes within the anatomy laboratory emerge as a promising choice within the realm of innovative learning, fostering student engagement and enjoyment. A course passing rate of 100% with similar means to previous cohorts demonstrates comparable outcomes in terms of knowledge acquisition making this collaborative assessment tool an effective active learning method.

Overall, the nature of the collaborative quizzes utilized in this study enhanced the traditional Team-Based Learning (TBL) approach by implementing several modifications. Rather than having students complete the individual Readiness Assurance Test (iRAT) and then the team Readiness Assurance Test (tRAT) in separate sessions, this method involved tackling each question individually and then consecutively as a team. This sequence aimed to boost learning and engagement through heightened challenge and surprise. Moreover, while the tRAT typically provides immediate feedback on whether a group's answer is correct or incorrect, these quizzes withhold this feedback until the end of the entire examination. This strategy encouraged students to maintain an open mind about their peers' responses and prevented them from forming judgments based on individual performance during the quiz. Additionally, these quizzes did not include instructor clarification reviews, team applications, or appeal processes. Unlike many TBL studies that only allow a single group answer per tRAT question, this approach permitted individual responses even during group discussions, fostering a more nuanced and comprehensive learning experience.

Limitations

To comprehensively evaluate the impact of collaborative quizzes on medical students' academic performance and their learning of anatomy, a randomized control trial featuring two distinct groups is essential. One group with access to collaborative quizzes, while the other follows traditional learning methods, would allow for a comparative analysis of their outcomes. The current design predominantly focused on observing how students interacted with the collaborative quizzes and lacked a control group, making it challenging to definitively establish the quizzes' positive impact on academic performance. To enhance the analysis of collaboration effects, it would be beneficial for students to individually retake the quiz one week after their initial attempt, enabling a comparison with their original individual quiz grades. It is worth noting that students from the two academic programs enrolled in this course received varied grading approaches, which may have led to varied impacts of the intervention on each group. Consequently, this discrepancy might have influenced students' motivations and performance, leading to a potential bias towards higher individual performance and group participation. To strengthen validity, future studies should more thoroughly address this issue by standardizing grading criteria or incorporating additional measures to assess and control for motivation. Alternatively, subgroup analyses could offer insights into how these differences affect the study outcomes. Additionally, students may have not entered their individual answer correctly and waited until the group discussion took place. Furthermore, it is essential to recognize that various external factors beyond the academic curriculum contribute to students' examination success. These factors encompass individual motivations, learning commitments, styles, and other variables that can significantly vary at both individual and cohort levels.

Future directions

The research team will persist in integrating collaborative quizzes in the anatomy curriculum as a valuable tool to provide students with additional opportunities to evaluate their knowledge while fostering peer learning. To enhance the student learning experience and boost teaching efficiency, adjustments to formatting, analytical methods, and quiz structure will be diligently made. Continuing forward, the team remains committed to analyzing student success and gathering feedback, persistently pushing the boundaries of teaching methods in pursuit of the ideal learning environment.

Conclusions

Collaborative quizzes emerge as a valuable and potentially indispensable tool for medical school anatomy courses across the nation. With pre-clerkship curriculums being condensed and clinical knowledge expanding rapidly, time becomes a limiting factor. While medical curricular reform trends involve significant changes like flipped classrooms and abandoning traditional lectures, it is equally crucial for future educators to explore smaller adjustments to core learning principles to address time constraints effectively. This study advocates for the incorporation of collaborative quizzes in a compact anatomy curriculum. The uniqueness of these quizzes lies in their ability to provide students with opportunities to assess their knowledge in a low-stakes setting while simultaneously benefiting from peer learning. Instead of having a dedicated teacher for each group, students take turns guiding their peers through individual thought processes, reinforcing their own understanding in the process. Moreover, the collection and analysis of quiz grades offer a potential method of identifying struggling students before their first examination, enabling timely academic support. By implementing this assessment tool, anatomy educators may enhance both learning and teaching efficiency and effectiveness in an academic environment emphasizing reduction. Collaborative quizzes prove to be a promising solution for optimizing the learning experience amid the constraints of condensed curricula.
