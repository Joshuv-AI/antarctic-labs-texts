New matrices for the spectral theory of mixed graphs, part I
G. Kalaivani, R. Rajkumar

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a matrix for a mixed graph, called the integrated adjacency matrix. This matrix uniquely determines a mixed graph, as long as the indices of the matrix are specified. Additionally, we associate an (undirected) graph with each mixed graph, enabling the spectral analysis of the integrated adjacency matrix to connect the structural properties of the mixed graph and its associated graph. Furthermore, we define certain mixed graph structures and establish their relationships to the eigenvalues of the integrated adjacency matrix. Keywords: Mixed graph, Integrated adjacency matrix, Spectrum Mathematics Subject Classification: 05C50



1
Introduction

All graphs (directed graphs) considered in this paper may contain multiple loops and multiple edges (multiple directed loops and multiple arcs). A mixed graph generalizes both graphs and directed graphs. That is, a mixed graph may contain multiple loops, multiple edges, multiple directed loops and multiple arcs.

A fundamental problem in spectral theory of mixed graphs is assigning an appropriate matrix to a mixed graph and analyzing the mixed graph’s properties through the eigenvalues of that matrix. To achieve this, researchers have introduced various matrices that capture the spectral properties of mixed graphs.

For a mixed graph having no multiple edges, loops, multiple arcs, directed loops, and digons (i.e., a pair of arcs with the same end vertices but in opposite directions), the following matrices have been explored: the Hermitian adjacency matrix, independently introduced by Liu and Li
[
9
]
and Guo and Mohar
[
7
]
; the Hermitian Laplacian matrix defined by Yu and Qu
[
15
]
; the Hermitian quasi-Laplacian matrix defined by Yu et al.
[
14
]
; the Hermitian normalized Laplacian matrix defined by Yu et al.
[
13
]
; the mixed adjacency matrix defined by Adiga et al.
[
2
]
; the
k
-generalized Hermitian adjacency matrix for each positive integers
k
, proposed by Yu et al.
[
16
]
; the index weighted Hermitian adjacency matrices defined by Zheng Wang et al.
[
11
]
; the
\alpha
-Hermitian adjacency matrix for
|\alpha|=1
defined by Abudayah et al.
[
1
]
; the
\gamma
-signless Laplacian adjacency matrix introduced by Alomari et al.
[
3
]
; the Hermitian Laplacian matrix of second kind and the Hermitian (quasi-)Laplacian matrix of second kind introduced by Xiong et al.
[
12
]
.

For a mixed graph having no directed loops, mixed Laplacian matrix was defined by Bapat et al.
[
5
]
.
For a mixed graph having no loops, directed loops and digons, a generalization of the Hermitian adjacency matrix was introduced by Guo and Mohar
[
7
]
.
In the case of a mixed graph where an edge is treated as a digon, the Hermitian adjacency matrix of the second kind was introduced by Mohar
[
10
]
.
For a mixed graph having no digons, loops and directed loops, another form of Hermitian adjacency matrix was defined by Yuan et al.
[
17
]
.

However, there are certain limitations: (i) the matrix defined in
[
2
]
is not symmetric, and as a result, their eigenvalues can include complex numbers; (ii) some of these matrices are only defined for specific types of mixed graphs; and (iii) in some matrices, undirected edges are represented as digons. This representation makes it unclear from the matrix entries whether two vertices are joined by an edge or a digon, even when the index set of the matrix is provided. Furthermore, the number of edges, loops, arcs, and the orientation of arcs cannot be clearly determined from the entries of the matrix defined in
[
5
]
. Consequently, not all mixed graphs can be determined from their associated matrices.

To address these issues, in this paper, we introduce a matrix, named the integrated adjacency matrix for a mixed graph. We study the properties of a mixed graph through the eigenvalues and characteristic polynomial of its integrated adjacency matrix.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. We present some preliminary definitions and notations in Section
2
that are used throughout the paper. In Section
3
we define a matrix for a mixed graph, namely the integrated adjacency matrix, and associate a graph to it. We show that the integrated adjacency matrix of a mixed graph is identical to the adjacency matrix of the associated graph.

Section
4
presents some basic results which relate the mixed graph invariants, such as number of edges, number of arcs to the eigenvalues of its integrated adjacency matrix. Additionally, we define a new structure called an alternating walk in a mixed graph and use it to determine the entries of the
k
-th power of the integrated adjacency matrix of a mixed graph.

In Section
5
we determine the eigenvalues of the integrated adjacency matrix of some special mixed graphs, such as complete
k
-partite mixed graph, complete
k
-partite directed graph, and complete mixed graph.
We also provide a necessary and sufficient condition on the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of the integrated adjacency matrix for a mixed graph being
(r,s)
-regular. Furthermore, we derive the characteristic polynomial of the integrated adjacency matrix of a mixed graph formed by adding a new vertex in a specific manner to a given mixed graph.

In Section
6
, we introduce a new structure in a mixed graph, namely a mixed component. We prove that the number of mixed components of a mixed graph equals the number of components of the associated graph.

Section
7
provides the determinant of the integrated adjacency matrix for specific types of mixed graphs. Finally, in Section
8
, we establish bounds for the largest, second largest, smallest, and second smallest eigenvalues of the integrated adjacency matrix in relation with the mixed graph invariants, such as the number of edges, arcs, and degrees. Additionally, we derive upper bounds for the clique number and the independence number of a mixed graph using the eigenvalues of its integrated adjacency matrix.

Definition 3.1

Let
G

\{v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,v^{\prime}_{n},v^{\prime\prime}_{1},v^{\prime\prime}_{2},\ldots,v^{\prime\prime}_{n}\}

For

\textnormal{the }(v^{\prime}_{i},v^{\prime}_{j})\textnormal{-th entry of }\mathcal{I}(G)=\textnormal{the }(v^{\prime\prime}_{i},v^{\prime\prime}_{j})\textnormal{-th entry of }\mathcal{I}(G)=\begin{cases}2r,&\text{if}~i=j\textnormal{ and}~v_{i}\overset{r}{\sim}v_{j};\\

\textnormal{the }(v^{\prime}_{i},v^{\prime\prime}_{j})\textnormal{-th entry of }\mathcal{I}(G)=\textnormal{the }(v^{\prime\prime}_{j},v^{\prime}_{i})\textnormal{-th entry of }\mathcal{I}(G)=\begin{cases}t,&\text{if}~v_{i}\overset{t}{\rightarrow}v_{j};\\

The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{I}(G)
are called the

-

of

. We denote these eigenvalues as
\bm{\lambda}_{i}(G)
for

. Since
\mathcal{I}(G)
is real symmetric, these can be arranged, without loss of generality as
\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)\geq\bm{\lambda}_{2}(G)\geq\cdots\geq\bm{\lambda}_{2n}(G)
. The spectrum of
\mathcal{I}(G)
is referred to as the

-

It can be seen that a mixed graph

, as long as the indices of the matrix are specified.

By arranging the rows and columns of

\mathcal{I}(G)=\begin{bmatrix}A(G_{u})&\vec{A}(G_{d})\\
\vec{A}(G_{d})^{T}&A(G_{u})\end{bmatrix}.

(3.1)

Since the matrix
\mathcal{I}(G)
integrates the matrices

and
\vec{A}(G_{d})
, it justifies its name.

Now we define a graph, called the

of

, denoted by

Let

. The adjacency among the vertices of

(i)

6
Mixed components of a mixed graph

Definition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a mixed graph. A submixed graph
G^{\prime}
of
G
having at least one arc is said to be a
special submixed graph
of
G
, if it satisfies the following conditions.

(i)

Any two distinct vertices of
G^{\prime}
are joined by an alternating walk;

(ii)

If a component
H
of
G^{\prime}_{u}
has at least one vertex having in-arc in
G^{\prime}
, and at least one vertex having out-arc in
G^{\prime}
, then for each vertex
v
of

, there exists a closed alternating walk in
G^{\prime}
which starts and ends at
v
, and containing odd number of arcs.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
G
be a mixed graph. A submixed graph
G^{\prime}
of
G
is said to be a
mixed component
of
G
if it satisfies one of the following three conditions.

(i)

G^{\prime}
is a component of
G_{u}
, and each vertex of
G^{\prime}
has out-degree zero in
G
.

(ii)

G^{\prime}
is a component of
G_{u}
, and each vertex of
G^{\prime}
has in-degree zero in
G
.

(iii)

G^{\prime}
is a maximal special submixed graph of
G
.

We call a mixed component satisfying the conditions (i), (ii) and (iii) mentioned above as a
mixed component of Type I, Type II
and
Type III
, respectively.

Observation 6.1

Let

(i)

For a mixed component
H
of a mixed graph

\Theta_{H}:=\{v\in V_{H}\mid\text{there exits an alternating path in }H\text{ which contains }v\text{ twice}\}
,

\Omega_{H}:=\{e\in E_{H}\mid\text{there exits an alternating path in }H\text{ which contains }e\text{ twice}\}

t(H):=|E_{H}|+|\Omega_{H}|+|\vec{E}_{H}|
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
G
be a mixed graph, and
H
a mixed component of
G
. Then the associated component of
H
in
G^{A}
has
|V_{H}|+|\Theta_{H}|
vertices, and
t(H)
edges.

Proof.

Let
H^{\prime}
denote the associated component of
H
in
G^{A}
. First, we determine the number of vertices in
H^{\prime}
. It is obvious that each vertex of
V_{H}
corresponds to at least one vertex and at most two vertices in
H^{\prime}
.
Now, we claim that
v\in\Theta_{H}
if and only if
H^{\prime}
has two vertices corresponding to
v
.
Let
v\in V_{H}
. Suppose
H^{\prime}
has two vertices corresponding to
v
. According to the proof of Theorem
6.1
, the component of
H_{u}
containing
v
includes a vertex with non-zero in-degree, and a vertex with non-zero out-degree in
H
. As per the definition of mixed component, there exists an alternating walk in
H
starting and ending at
v
and containing odd number of arcs. This implies that there exists an alternating path in
H
starting and ending at
v
and containing odd number of arcs. Therefore, we have
v\in\Theta_{H}
. By retracing the above we can prove the converse. Thus there are
2|\Theta_{H}|+|V_{H}\setminus\Theta_{H}|
vertices in
H^{\prime}
. Since
\Theta_{H}\subseteq V_{H}
, we have
|V_{H}\setminus\Theta_{H}|=|V_{H}|-|\Theta_{H}|
. So, the total number of vertices of
H^{\prime}
is
|V_{H}|+|\Theta_{H}|
.

Next, we determine the number of edges in
H^{\prime}
. Each arc in
H
corresponds to exactly one edge in
H^{\prime}
. Each edge in
H
corresponds to at least one edge and at most two edges in
H^{\prime}
.
Now, we claim that
e\in\Omega_{H}
if and only if
H^{\prime}
has two edges corresponding to
e
.
Let
e\in\Omega_{H}
. Suppose
H^{\prime}
has two edges corresponding to
e
. According to the proof of Theorem
6.1
, the component of
H_{u}
containing

includes a vertex with non-zero in-degree, and a vertex with non-zero out-degree in
H
. As per the definition of mixed component,

has an alternating walk (in particular, an alternating path) starting and ending at an end vertex of

and containing odd number of arcs. Suppose the above path does not contain the edge
e
twice, we can extend it by taking
e
as the first and last edge of the path.
Therefore, we have
e\in\Omega_{H}
. By retracing the above we can prove the converse.
Thus there are
2|\Omega_{H}|+|E_{H}\setminus\Omega_{H}|+|\vec{E}_{H}|
edges in
H^{\prime}
. Since
\Omega_{H}\subseteq E_{H}
, we have
|E_{H}\setminus\Omega_{H}|=|E_{H}|-|\Omega_{H}|
. So, the total number of edges in

is

.
∎

Theorem 8.2

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)

\displaystyle\underset{\lVert X\rVert=1}{\max}\{X^{T}\mathcal{I}(G)X\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{X\neq 0}{\max}\bigg\{\frac{X^{T}\mathcal{I}(G)X}{X^{T}X}\bigg\}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{1}_{2n}^{T}\mathcal{I}(G)\bm{1}_{2n}}{\bm{1}_{2n}^{T}\bm{1}_{2n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\bigg(\underset{u\in V_{G}}{\sum}(d(u)+d^{+}(u))+\underset{u\in V_{G}}{\sum}(d(u)+d^{-}(u))\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{1}(G)+\delta_{2}(G))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min\{\delta_{1}(G),\delta_{2}(G)\}.

By Theorem
8.1
,
\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)\leq\max\{\Delta_{1}(G),\Delta_{2}(G)\}
. Hence, the result follows.
∎

The largest
\mathcal{I}
-eigenvalue of a mixed graph
G
, i.e.,
\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)
is referred to as the
\mathcal{I}
-
spectral radius of
G
.

Theorem 8.11
.

Let

and
p^{+}
denote the number of

-eigenvalues of a simple mixed graph

which are less than, equal to and greater than
0
, respectively and let
q^{-},q^{0}
and
q^{+}
denote the number of
\mathcal{I}
-eigenvalues of
G
which are less than, equal to and greater than
-2
, respectively. Let
s=\min\left\{1+\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)}{2},\frac{p^{0}+p^{-}+1}{2},p^{0}+p^{+}-1,\right.

\left.q^{0}+q^{-}+1,\frac{q^{0}+q^{+}}{2}\right\}
. Then
\omega(G)\leq s
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
contains a clique
H
on
k\geq 1
vertices. Then
\mathcal{I}(H)
is of the form

.
Since
H
is an induced submixed graph of

, by Theorem
4.2
, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{I}(H)
interlace the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{I}(G)
. Since the eigenvalues of

are

with multiplicity

,
0
with multiplicity

, and
-2
with multiplicity

, we have the following:

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{2n-2k+1}(G)\leq 2(k-1)\leq\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G);

(8.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{2n-2k+i}(G)\leq 0\leq\bm{\lambda}_{i}(G)~\text{for}~i=2,\ldots,k+1;

(8.2)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{2n-2k+i}(G)\leq-2\leq\bm{\lambda}_{i}(G)~\text{for}~i=k+2,\ldots,2k.

(8.3)

From the second inequality of (
8.1
), we get
k\leq 1+\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\lambda}_{1}(G)}{2}
.
By (
8.2
), we get

From (

. Since

, as desired. ∎

9
Concluding remarks

The integrated adjacency matrix of a mixed graph, introduced in this paper, accommodates entries corresponding to multiple edges, multiple arcs, and multiple loops. Furthermore, any mixed graph can be uniquely determined from its integrated adjacency matrix. We have established results that reveal the interplay between the eigenvalues of the integrated adjacency matrix of a mixed graph and its structural properties.

It is evident that some of the spectral graph theoretic results for simple graphs, utilized in this paper, can be extended to graphs with loops and/or multiple edges. Consequently, Theorems
4.2
,
6.2
,
6.4
,
8.1
,
8.2
,
8.5
,
8.6
,
8.7
,
8.8
, and
8.10
, which were proved for simple mixed graphs, can also be extended to mixed graphs with loops, directed loops, multiple edges, and/or multiple arcs. Additionally, Theorem
4.4
can be extended to loopless mixed graphs. Furthermore, in part II of this paper
[
8
]
, we introduce and study three additional matrices for mixed graphs: integrated Laplacian matrix, integrated signless Laplacian matrix, and normalized integrated Laplacian matrix, all defined using the integrated adjacency matrix.
