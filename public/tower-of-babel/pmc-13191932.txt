Preliminary results from a phase II trial of spatially fractionated radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy in patients with bulky solid tumors: early evidence of promising efficacy and favorable safety
Authors: Yuntao Zhou, Yanwei Li, Hui Zhu, Siyi Yang, Chengwen Yang, Shengpeng Jiang, Shiping Shen, Lujun Zhao, Jing Luo, Zhiyong Yuan, Ningbo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeBulky solid tumors present significant therapeutic challenges. Spatially fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT), a technique delivering alternating high- and low-dose subvolumes, alters the tumor microenvironment while minimizing toxicity. This phase II trial assesses the efficacy and safety of SFRT combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and anti-angiogenic agents in advanced malignancies.Methods and materialsThis prospective phase II trial enrolled 34 patients with bulky solid tumors between October 2024 and July 2025. All patients underwent SFRT using GRID, LATTICE, or Stereotactic central/core ablative radiation therapy techniques. Multimodal therapy, incorporating pre-radiotherapy administration of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor and thymalfasin, as well as concurrent ICIs and anti-angiogenic agents during SFRT, was administered according to clinical recommendations and patient preferences. The endpoints were treatment-related adverse events and the objective response rate (ORR). In addition, a prognostic analysis was performed to identify factors associated with clinical outcomes.ResultsAmong the 37 treatment courses in 34 patients, 4 patients did not complete the planned therapy, and 1 was lost to follow-up. The median follow-up duration was 6.0 months. Of the 32 evaluable lesions from patients who completed the study, the ORR was 65.63% (21/32). Of the 22 evaluable patients, 5 (22.73%) exhibited abscopal effects. Common adverse events included anemia (18.92%), followed by skin/mucositis and pneumonia, each occurring in 10.81% of patients.ConclusionsThis phase II trial demonstrates that the multimodal approach of SFRT combined with ICIs and anti-angiogenic agents is a promising strategy for bulky solid tumors, while highlighting the need for larger trials to validate long-term efficacy and refine personalized treatment protocols. Introduction

Bulky solid tumors remain a major challenge in oncology, as their large size and aggressive behavior often lead to local invasion and distant metastasis, limiting the effectiveness of surgery, chemotherapy, and conventional radiotherapy [1, 2].

Spatially fractionated radiotherapy (SFRT), which partitions tumor volumes into alternating high-dose and low-dose subvolumes, offers a promising solution [3]. SFRT, encompassing GRID, LATTICE, stereotactic central/core ablative radiation therapy (SCART), micro-beam and mini-beam modalities, creates a peak-to-valley dose effect, concentrating ablative doses (> 15 Gy peaks) in specific regions while sparing surrounding tissues (3–5 Gy valleys) [4]. Clinical evidence underscores SFRT’s efficacy, with Huhn et al. [5] reporting 92% local control in bulky head/neck cancers using GRID and Mohiuddin et al. [6] observing 91% overall response rates (ORR) across palliative sites (pelvis, abdomen) while maintaining minimal organs at risks (OARs) toxicity.

Radiotherapy combined with multiple therapies is increasingly recognized in oncology. Growing evidence shows that radiotherapy can synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to enhance anti-tumor immunity [7–10]. For example, a phase II trial of neoadjuvant hypofractionated stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) plus durvalumab in HPV-negative head and neck cancer achieved an 89% major pathological or complete response rate [8]. GM-CSF enhances dendritic cell and macrophage function, improving antigen presentation. Thymalfasin (Tα1) promotes T cell maturation and activates dendritic cells via Toll-like receptors, boosting anti-tumor immunity [11, 12]. The PRaG5.0 study further supports the clinical potential of combining radiotherapy with immunomodulatory agents such as GM-CSF and Tα1 [13]. In addition, anti-angiogenic agents have also demonstrated synergistic effects when combined with radiotherapy, reinforcing the value of multi-modal strategies [14–18].

Despite substantial advances in the field of SFRT, to our knowledge, the combination of SFRT with pre-radiotherapy administration of GM-CSF and Tα1, followed by concurrent delivery of ICIs and anti-angiogenic therapy alongside SFRT, has not been previously reported. Building on emerging evidence supporting multimodal immuno-modulation, this prospective, single-center, phase II trial (NCT06775678) represents a novel effort to translate this integrative strategy into clinical practice. By synergizing advanced dose-painting techniques with mechanistically guided immunomodulation, this study aims to establish SFRT-based combination therapy as a potential paradigm shift in immuno-radiotherapy—particularly for the management of historically challenging bulky tumors.

Methods and materials

Patients

This prospective pilot study (NCT06775678), conducted at Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital between October 2024 and July 2025, enrolled patients meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histopathologically confirmed malignancy; (3) Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥ 60 and expected survival > 3 months; (4) primary tumor diameter > 5 cm or deemed suitable for SFRT treatment by the multidisciplinary team (MDT); (5) provision of signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) radiotherapy-unsuitable conditions (e.g., malignant effusions, severe organ dysfunction, or active systemic infections); (2) significant comorbidities (e.g., myocardial infarction within 6 months, severe arrhythmia, or life expectancy < 3 months); (3) active autoimmune diseases or prior grade 3 or higher cardiac/hepatic toxicity/grade 4 immunotherapy-related toxicities; (4) other protocol-inconsistent factors per investigator judgment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute & Hospital (No. E20240009, Approval Date: February 2, 2024), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment.

Systemic therapy and monitoring

Figure 1A illustrates the idealized treatment pathway. Patients were enrolled based on predefined criteria and received pre-treatment administration of GM-CSF and Tα1. They then underwent SFRT—delivered using GRID, LATTICE, or SCART techniques (Supplementary Fig. 1)—selected by experienced radiation oncologists based on clinical judgment, primarily considering tumor size and location. SFRT was delivered concurrently with anti-angiogenic and immunotherapy agents to enhance efficacy. Post-SFRT, patients entered a maintenance phase with continued systemic therapy to consolidate response and prevent recurrence. Trial consort diagram. A: Ideal Clinical Trial Design; B: Flowchart of our Clinical Study

The selection of SFRT technique was guided by predefined clinical and anatomical criteria, including tumor size, tumor geometry, anatomical location, and proximity to OARs. Specifically, SCART was preferentially applied to smaller and more geometrically regular lesions, allowing high-dose irradiation of the tumor core with minimal margin expansion. In contrast, GRID or LATTICE techniques were more commonly used for larger bulky tumors, where spatial dose modulation was required to safely deliver heterogeneous high-dose regions while sparing surrounding normal tissues. Peak and valley dose regimens were selected based on prior institutional experience, published radiobiological and clinical evidence, and safety considerations, with the aim of maximizing tumor control and immune modulation while minimizing normal tissue toxicity.

Dosimetry and planning

Per international SFRT consensus, the gross tumor volume (GTV) was defined as the macroscopic tumor on axial imaging. The planning GTV (PGTV) was generated by expanding the GTV by 0.5 cm. The GTV was subdivided into high-dose (Dpeak) and low-dose (Dvalley) regions, delineated either manually or via in-house software based on established methods [19]. Planning adhered to OARs constraints from the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group 101 guidelines for 5-fraction SBRT [20]. Peak-to-Valley Dose Ratio (PVDR: PVDR1 = Dpeak/Dvalley; PVDR2 = D10%/D90%) (21), and Ablative Dose Ratio (ADR), defined as the volume ratio of high- to low-dose subregions [21]. Fractionation was tailored to tumor size and patient performance status.

Treatment plans were generated using Eclipse or Pinnacle. Quality assurance included multidisciplinary SBRT protocol reviews and cone-beam CT (CBCT) verification before every alternate-day fraction. All patients received SFRT via IMRT, VMAT, tomotherapy, or CyberKnife, delivered on linear accelerators (TrueBeam, Agility, Synergy, or CyberKnife), all equipped with CBCT for precise image guidance—despite none being specifically designed for Lattice RT.

Endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence and severity of adverse events, graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.0, assessed at 3 months after treatment. Secondary endpoints included ORR per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. The abscopal effect (AE) is defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in the size of distant, non-irradiated metastatic lesions. Exploratory endpoints encompassed the time interval from enrollment to initiation of subsequent systemic therapy and time to metastasis-directed therapy initiation. Pre-treatment hematologic indices [red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), platelets (PLT), hemoglobin (HB), neutrophils (NEUT), lymphocytes (LY)] and tumor marker levels were obtained from blood tests prior to simulation CT. Based on these results, we calculated the pan-immune-inflammation value [PIV = (neutrophil count × platelet count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count], systemic immune-inflammation index (SII = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count), lymphocyte/monocyte ratio (LMR), platelet lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and neutrophil lymphocyte ratio (NLR).

Post-radiotherapy, patients underwent active surveillance for ≥ 1 year, with imaging every 3 months. Beyond year one, follow-up continued every 3–6 months with clinical and imaging assessments.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; http://www.Rproject.org), along with Zstats v1.0 (www.zstats.net). Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics and treatment parameters. Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range), categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For univariate analysis of target lesion control and AE, the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum) was used for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, due to the small sample size. Given the limited sample size and low event rate, multivariable analyses were performed using Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias. Variables were selected based on univariable Firth analyses, with the number of covariates restricted to minimize overfitting. Associations between treatment response and clinical/treatment factors were assessed. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Between October 2024 and July 2025, 34 patients (37 treatment courses) were enrolled and analyzed at the Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (Fig. 1B). As shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2, the median age of the study cohort was 62.50 years, with the majority being male (61.76%). Clinically, the majority of patients were diagnosed with stage IV cancer (70.59%). 35.29% of patients reported a history of smoking. Among histological subtypes, adenocarcinoma was the most frequently observed (32.35%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (29.41%).

Treatment response assessment

Median follow-up was 6.0 months. Four patients discontinued treatment (one due to viral pneumonia; three withdrew consent), and one was lost to follow-up. Among the 32 evaluable lesions (28 target, 4 non-target), the ORR was 65.63% (Fig. 2A). All four non-target lesions exhibited non-SD/PD responses. In target lesions, 60.71% achieved partial response (PR), 28.57% stable disease (SD), and 10.71% progressive disease (PD) (Fig. 2B). Subgroup analyses are shown in Fig. 2C. The waterfall plot (Fig. 2E) illustrates tumor regression across the cohort, with notable responses observed particularly in patients receiving combination regimens of SFRT, ICIs, and anti-angiogenic therapy. Abscopal effect

Among 22 patients evaluable for AE, 5 (22.73%) demonstrated a ≥ 30% reduction in non-irradiated metastases (Fig. 2D and F). Notably, the subgroup involving GM-CSF and Tα1 administration prior to SFRT, followed by concurrent immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy, demonstrated an abscopal response rate of 50%. Representative imaging responses are provided in Fig. 3D.

Adverse events

No grade ≥ 3 toxicities were observed across the 37 treatment courses, with no treatment-related deaths (Table 3). The most common adverse event was anemia, occurring in 7 patients (18.92%). Skin/mucositis and pneumonia were the next most frequent, each reported in 4 patients (10.81%). Anorexia, nausea, and thrombocytopenia each occurred in 3 patients (8.11%). Fatigue, low-grade fever, and rash were observed in 2 patients each (5.41%). Abdominal distension was the least common, reported in 1 patient (2.70%).

Discussion

SFRT is a novel approach that delivers heterogeneous high- and low-dose radiation within the same tumor to modulate anti-tumor immunity, contrasting with conventional fractionated radiotherapy, which often exerts immunosuppressive effects through immune cell depletion, vascular disruption, and chronic interferon signaling [22]. Preclinically, sub-ablative SFRT enhances systemic immunity by upregulating intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) in CD8 + T cells and tumor endothelium, promoting T-cell infiltration into non-irradiated lesions [9]. In addition, high-dose regions within SFRT may induce bystander and abscopal signaling through cytokines such as TNF-α and TRAIL, as shown in lung adenocarcinoma models [23, 24].

Consistent with these biological insights, emerging clinical evidence supports the efficacy and safety of SFRT in patients with bulky or advanced tumors. In the present study, we observed an ORR of 60.71% across 28 target lesions at a median follow-up of 6.0 months.This result aligns with findings from the LATTICE-01 trial, which reported an 89% clinical response rate and an 86.4% one-year overall survival using 18F-FDG PET-guided SFRT in patients with stage IV disease [25]. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated favorable tolerability and encouraging tumor control in bulky head and neck malignancies treated with SFRT [26]. Additionally, Yang et al. further introduced SCART, delivering 15–24 Gy × 3 fractions to tumor cores with non-uniform dosing in surrounding regions to spare normal tissue. In 18 patients with recurrent/metastatic bulky tumors, median survival was 16.3 months with 6 deaths, demonstrating favorable safety [4]. Compared with these prior reports, our study included a more heterogeneous population with respect to primary tumor types, metastatic burden, and treatment history, and uniquely integrated SFRT with immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy. These differences in patient populations and treatment protocols limit direct cross-study comparisons but also underscore the potential generalizability of SFRT-based combination strategies in real-world clinical settings.We await the results of ongoing trials to further clarify the role of SFRT in clinical practice.

A growing body of evidence supports the synergistic interaction between hypofractionated radiotherapy and ICIs. In our study, the protocol incorporated several “peak dose” regimens, including 8 Gy × 3 fractions (n = 13), 10–15 Gy × 3 fractions (n = 19), and other individualized schedules [7–10]. While local tumor control was similar between the 8 Gy× 3 fractions and 10–15 Gy× 3 fractions groups (ORR: 63.64% vs. 61.54%, p > 0.99), AEs were higher with 8 Gy × 3 fractions (37.5% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.25), which aligns with previous reports suggesting this fractionation may favor systemic immune activation [10]. This warrants further investigation.Our exploration of dose de-escalation within the SFRT framework aims to improve patient safety and preserve the opportunity for subsequent radiotherapy, potentially enabling a more sustainable and integrative approach to combined modality treatment.

In addition to peak-dose effects, low-dose irradiation within the valley regions of SFRT may also contribute to immune modulation. In our study, we observed a trend toward higher AEs rates in the valley doses ≤ 15 Gy group compared to the > 15 Gy group (40.00% vs. 8.33%, p = 0.14), although this did not reach statistical significance. This aligns with evidence showing enhanced ICI efficacy when combined with incidental low-dose RT, such as in liver/lung metastases [27] or intestinal irradiation (ILDR), where gut microbiota modulation may contribute to improved PD-L1 inhibitor responses and survival [28].

In our cohort, combining SFRT with CFRT in 13 patients yielded an ORR of 76.92%. This hybrid approach aims to enhance immune activation while preserving normal tissue [1]. Supporting this, Olabumuyi et al. [29] reported 86.4% negative surgical margins and 32.4% pathologic complete response (pCR) with neoadjuvant SBRT-PATHY (Stereotactic Body RadioTherapy of PArtial Tumor irradiation targeting HYpoxic segment) in soft tissue sarcoma—outcomes superior to historical controls. Similar strategies in NSCLC and cervical cancer also show improved tumor control [30, 31]. Notably, our pilot data in bone metastases demonstrated 100% symptom relief, representing a novel application of SFRT (Supplementary Table 6).

Regarding anti-angiogenic therapy, 37.84% (14/37) of patients received concurrent treatment during RT, achieving a 64.29% ORR in target lesions. Most (64.29%) received anlotinib, which not only directly suppresses tumor cell proliferation and migration, but also remodels the tumor microenvironment by promoting vascular normalization—improving perfusion, reducing hypoxia, and thereby enhancing radiotherapy sensitivity [18, 32–34]. Clinical evidence supports this synergy: Nan Wei et al. reported 19-month progression-free survival in advanced lung cancer using sequential RT and anlotinib without pulmonary hemorrhage [35], and combinations of anlotinib with whole-brain RT or SBRT show promise in brain metastases [14–16]. These findings highlight anlotinib’s potential to enhance RT outcomes via vascular and immune modulation, warranting further investigation in combination strategies.

The efficacy and safety of radiotherapy combined with ICIs and GM-CSF have been confirmed by the PRaG study [36]. In our study, GM-CSF was used to boost T-cell and dendritic cell functions before radiotherapy. Of nine patients who received standard treatment, eight completed it. With a non-curative radiotherapy regimen (8 Gy×3f + 1.2 Gy×5f), we achieved an ORR of 62.5%, including five PRs and two SDs. The PRaG 1.0 protocol combined radiotherapy, PD-1 inhibitors, and GM-CSF for advanced solid tumors post-first line chemotherapy, enrolling 54 patients with a median PFS of 4 months and OS of 10.5 months. It was well-tolerated with few grade 3–4 adverse events [36]. The improved PRaG 5.0 study, based on T-lymphocyte counts, showed good efficacy and safety with thymopentin doses prior to radiotherapy [13, 37].

Tα1 enhances antitumor immunity by augmenting dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation, activating T-cell responses, and inducing Th1-type immune responses, thereby promoting CD8 + T-cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment [38–41]. It also reduces treatment-related toxicities, thereby improving the efficacy of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [42–49]. Notably, prior studies have demonstrated that Tα1 enhances distant responses in esophageal cancer patients (9.7% PR, 35.5% SD) [46]. In contrast, our study observed a 50% distant response rate in patients who received Tα1 before radiotherapy. In locally advanced rectal cancer, Tα1 combined with long-course chemoradiotherapy plus tislelizumab significantly improved the pathological complete response (pCR) rate (61.5% vs. 38.5%) and promoted tumor regression [49, 50]. Furthermore, Tα1 combined with the Bragg regimen in patients with advanced refractory solid tumors prolonged median progression-free survival (3.97 months) and improved the tumor immune microenvironment [51].

Historically, the AE was considered an exceptionally rare clinical phenomenon during the conventional radiotherapy era, with an incidence rate substantially below 10% and predominantly documented as isolated case reports [52]. However, the advent of immunotherapy has markedly elevated the incidence of AEs, with rates now consistently reported between 10% and 40% in combined radiotherapy-immunotherapy regimens [52]. For instance, in patients with progressive disease following ipilimumab therapy, subsequent radiotherapy induced AEs in 43% of cases [53]. Similarly, investigators administering subcutaneous GM-CSF concurrently with radiotherapy and chemotherapy observed an AE rate of 27% [54]. Notably, SBRT-PATHY alone has been reported to elicit AE in up to 52% of patients, with minimal associated toxicity. In alignment with these contemporary findings, our cohort analysis revealed an AE rate of 22.73% (5 of 22 evaluable patients). Strikingly, 80% (4 of 5) of responders received SFRT combined with ICIs and anti-angiogenic therapy. In the subset of 6 patients who underwent standardized priming with GM-CSF and Tα1 prior to SFRT, followed by SFRT (the peak dose is 8 Gy*3f and the valley dose is 1.2 Gy*5f) combined with ICIs and anti-angiogenic therapy, the AE rate was 50%. Mechanistically, Zhang et al. recently established that irradiated tumor cells secrete PAI-1, inducing SFRP2 overexpression in distant lesions via the LRP1/p65 axis [55].

Beyond the overall incidence of abscopal effects, our exploratory multivariable Firth regression analysis identified post-RT LMR and GTV volume as independent predictors of the abscopal effect, and post-RT HB as an independent predictor of treatment response. These findings suggest that both host-related factors and tumor burden may influence treatment outcomes following SFRT-based multimodal therapy. Higher post-RT LMR may reflect a more favorable systemic immune environment, whereas larger tumor volumes may limit the development of abscopal responses. In parallel, adequate post-RT HB levels may support effective local tumor control by improving tumor oxygenation and radiosensitivity. Collectively, these readily available clinical parameters may help inform patient stratification and treatment optimization in future studies; however, given the exploratory nature of the analyses, these associations should be interpreted with caution.

However, András Piffkó et al. reported “badscopal effect” where non-irradiated metastases paradoxically progressed after high-dose RT, mediated by amphiregulin overexpression that weakens immune surveillance and enhances tumor defense [56]. These findings underscore the need for further exploration of optimal RT doses, fractionation schedules, treatment timing, and combination therapies.

This investigator-initiated, single-arm, phase II trial demonstrates SFRT’s favorable safety profile and efficacy in treating bulky recurrent/metastatic tumors, with encouraging anti-tumor activity when combined with immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic agents. However, limitations include: (1) enrollment of heavily pre-treated advanced patients without randomization; (2) target volume delineation based on clinical judgment rather than standardized criteria, subject to tumor heterogeneity; and (3) limited sample size and short follow-up duration;4)the multivariable Firth regression analyses were exploratory in nature and constrained by the limited number of events, which required restricting the number of variables included in the models; therefore, the identified associations should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are essential to optimize this promising, safe, and feasible treatment modality.

Conclusions

In the current study, SFRT demonstrated favorable tolerability and safety when administered to patients with recurrent or metastatic bulky tumors. Furthermore, its combination with ICIs and anti-angiogenic treatments also exhibited a favorable safety profile. These findings underscore the potential of SFRT as a promising, safe, and feasible therapeutic modality for challenging clinical scenarios. However, future studies involving larger patient cohorts are warranted to further optimize its efficacy and refine treatment strategies.
