Whole‐body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) screening for reducing morbidity and mortality from multiple diseases in asymptomatic adults
Authors: Ziheng Chen, Minna Johansson, Loai Albarqouni, Steven Woloshin, Karsten Juhl Jørgensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate the benefits and harms of whole‐body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scans for screening in asymptomatic adults, compared with no intervention, usual care, or alternative screening strategies not involving whole‐body MRI or whole‐body CT.

Background

Description of the condition

Cancer and cardiovascular disease together remain the leading causes of death worldwide, and account for most premature deaths [1, 2]. Many individuals are still diagnosed at advanced disease stages, when opportunities for effective interventions may be limited, though the relevance of stage to treatment benefit differs across diseases [3]. Early detection is therefore promoted as a way to enable timely treatment, with the expectation that this could improve outcomes and sometimes reduce the aggressiveness of the treatment needed. The major target conditions for whole‐body imaging screening include cancers, cardiovascular lesions, and other potentially life‐threatening abnormalities that may be asymptomatic in early stages [4]. In this Cochrane review, “asymptomatic adults” refers to adults from the general population who do not have symptoms prompting diagnostic imaging for a suspected condition. This may include adults who consider themselves healthy and who seek screening to identify otherwise unknown disease or abnormalities. However, unlike targeted screening programmes (e.g. mammography, colonoscopy), whole‐body screening is often driven by commercial availability and direct‐to‐consumer marketing, rather than as an officially endorsed intervention following evidence‐based critical review of benefits and harms.

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Whole‐body scans with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) have been proposed as a comprehensive approach to identify asymptomatic cancers, vascular lesions, and other serious conditions before clinical symptoms emerge [5]. Private companies already offer these tests to the public, with a growing use among healthy adults, and they are sometimes promoted by influencers and celebrities [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. The promise of such an approach lies in its ability to detect multiple potentially life‐threatening diseases at an earlier stage in a single examination, and that this might reduce morbidity, mortality, and sometimes the need for aggressive treatment. However, earlier diagnosis and treatment are not necessarily beneficial and may also lead to harms. Potential benefits may include earlier diagnosis and treatment of clinically important disease when this improves patient‐relevant outcomes. Potential harms may include unnecessary follow‐up testing, invasive procedures, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, radiation exposure from CT or subsequent follow‐up imaging, psychological distress, false reassurance, and financial costs. Detection rates and downstream consequences may also vary according to scanner technology, imaging protocols, reporting thresholds, and the interpretation and recommendations of individual radiologists. The performance and consequences of whole‐body imaging may vary according to imaging modality and technology, including whether MRI or CT is used, scanner generation, MRI field strength, image resolution, anatomical coverage, reporting thresholds, and the individual radiologist. These differences may influence detection rates, false‐positive findings, and downstream follow‐up.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

Whole‐body MRI provides excellent soft‐tissue resolution, making it particularly suitable for detecting abnormalities including tumours and organ pathology [11]. The intervention involves systematic imaging of multiple body regions in a single session, typically covering the brain to mid‐thigh or further. An important advantage of MRI over CT is that it achieves this without ionizing radiation exposure.

Computed tomography (CT) scan

The CT scan offers rapid image acquisition and high sensitivity for calcifications and structural abnormalities, including pulmonary nodules and vascular pathology [12]. Similar to MRI, the CT scan involves comprehensive imaging from the head to pelvis or lower extremities in a single examination.

Why it is important to do this review

Whether whole‐body MRI or CT scans can reduce morbidity and mortality from disease in asymptomatic adults has not been established. However, the potential harms are multifaceted. Screening using scanning technology incurs risks that extend beyond financial costs to include false positives, false negatives, incidental findings, and overdiagnosis [13]. The psychological reassurance provided by a ‘negative’ scan may be misleading, as false‐negative results can create a sense of false reassurance that dangerously delays the diagnosis and treatment of an underlying disease. Even a true negative scan, meaning that no relevant abnormality is detected at the time of screening within the limits of the imaging test, may also contribute to over‐reassurance if individuals or clinicians interpret it as excluding future disease risk more broadly than the scan can support. Conversely, other individuals face significant psychological distress arising from ambiguous or false‐positive results [14]. False‐positive findings—where an abnormality is suspected but ultimately ruled out—can trigger a cascade of necessary but potentially harmful diagnostic procedures, including invasive biopsies, extra radiation exposure from follow‐up imaging, and significant inconvenience. The resource use from these follow‐up examinations, both financial and in terms of the time required from skilled personnel, is also a concern as healthcare systems are already under pressure to meet demands.

Other major concerns regarding these scans are the detection of incidental findings and the risk of overdiagnosis. Overdiagnosis involves the detection of abnormalities (e.g. indolent cancers, slow‐evolving conditions, or benign findings) that would never progress to cause symptoms or mortality [15]. Overdiagnosis is one of the most important potential harms of screening and is highly relevant to decision‐making because it may lead to further testing, labelling, treatment, and anxiety without improving health outcomes. Clinical commentators have observed that the mere detection of these anomalies, and the subsequent drive to investigate them, can paradoxically lead to harm. The harm often initiates at the point of discovery before escalating through unnecessary diagnostic workup and overtreatment [16].

Existing evidence regarding the prevalence of incidental findings has been derived largely from observational contexts. A systematic review of whole‐body MRI by Kwee and Kwee (2019), which aggregated data from 12 observational studies, reported that the prevalence of critical and indeterminate incidental findings in asymptomatic subjects is overall substantial (pooled prevalence 32.1%), though with significant variability dependent on the scanning protocol. Of these detected findings, verification data are lacking. However, among the subset of findings that were followed up, 16.0% were confirmed as false positives. Furthermore, in these observational studies, it remains impossible to distinguish what percentage of true positives represents overdiagnosis, as it requires long‐term follow‐up to determine if the condition would have ever become symptomatic [13].

As the Kwee 2019 systematic review highlighted significant heterogeneity in imaging protocols, individual observational studies show a wide range of results, with some reporting relatively low detection rates while others show much higher frequencies of findings [13]. One study of asymptomatic adults who voluntarily underwent whole‐body imaging frequently reported incidental finding rates exceeding 60%, with a substantial proportion requiring additional testing [17]. While quantifying the potential reductions in morbidity and mortality requires randomized trials, the downstream consequences of screening—such as recall rates, false‐positive findings, and the utilization of invasive follow‐up tests—can be characterized using these observational data. Quantifying these consequences is essential to understanding the full impact of whole‐body scanning on both the individual and the healthcare system.

Previous evidence syntheses have mainly focused on observational studies of whole‐body MRI and the frequency of abnormal findings [13], rather than directly evaluating whether whole‐body MRI or CT screening reduces morbidity or mortality in asymptomatic adults. Evidence on downstream consequences, false‐positive findings, overdiagnosis, and the comparative benefits and harms of whole‐body MRI or CT screening remains uncertain. In addition, whole‐body CT screening is not well covered by existing evidence syntheses with an overlapping population, intervention, comparator, and outcome framework. No recent systematic review has comprehensively evaluated whether whole‐body MRI or CT screening in asymptomatic adults reduces morbidity and mortality across multiple diseases, despite rising commercial availability and growing public demand. This Cochrane review is therefore needed to address this gap in knowledge and to clarify whether whole‐body scanning should be considered for broader public health adoption.

Objectives

To evaluate the benefits and harms of whole‐body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scans for screening in asymptomatic adults, compared with no intervention, usual care, or alternative screening strategies not involving whole‐body MRI or whole‐body CT.

Methods

We will follow the guidance from the Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review [18], and PRISMA 2020 for reporting the review [19]. We will also follow the guidance of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention (in short, the Cochrane Handbook) [20].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include studies that evaluate the effects of whole‐body MRI or CT scans for screening in asymptomatic adults. Eligible reports will be full‐text records from the included electronic databases. There will be no restriction by language or year of publication.

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including individually randomized and cluster‐randomized trials. We will also include observational studies, including prospective and retrospective cohort studies and cross‐sectional studies. We will follow the guidance in Chapter 3 of the Cochrane Handbook [21].

Types of participants

We will include studies of asymptomatic adults (≥ 18 years of age) from the general population. We will include participants regardless of gender, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, geographic location, or insurance status. Studies including mixed populations will be eligible if we can extract data for asymptomatic adults separately or if we can obtain these by contacting the study authors. We will exclude studies that include participants based on a specific high‐risk profile for a single disease (e.g. cohorts of heavy smokers recruited for lung cancer screening, or carriers of specific genetic mutations such as the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes), even if the imaging protocol used covers the whole body. We will follow the guidance in Chapter 3 of the Cochrane Handbook [21].

Types of interventions

The interventions of interest are whole‐body MRI or CT scans undertaken as a screening strategy to advance the time of detection for multiple diseases or risk factors. Eligible interventions include single or repeated scans, regardless of imaging protocol, provided the scan is a non‐contrast whole‐body MRI or non‐contrast whole‐body CT scan used for screening purposes. We will consider repeated scans to be part of the same screening intervention.

Eligible comparators will include no intervention, usual care, or alternative screening strategies. Alternative screening strategies may include standard disease‐specific screening, targeted imaging, laboratory testing, physical examination, risk‐based assessment, or general health checks. Studies comparing different eligible whole‐body MRI or CT screening strategies, such as single versus repeated scans, will also be eligible if they inform the relative benefits or harms of whole‐body imaging as a screening strategy. We will exclude studies limited to imaging of specific body regions (e.g. chest CT, brain MRI), scans performed to look for single specific conditions, scans using contrast injection, or other imaging modalities (e.g. ultrasound, magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), Positron Emission Tomography (PET), PET‐CT) [21].

Outcome measures

We will follow the guidance in Chapter 3 of the Cochrane Handbook [21]. We will extract all prespecified outcomes from RCTs, where reported, including mortality, overdiagnosis, diagnostic accuracy, detected diseases and risk factors, and downstream consequences. We will use observational studies to provide descriptive evidence on diagnostic accuracy in terms of false positive and false negative rates, detected diseases and risk factors, and downstream consequences, but we will not use them to estimate the effects of whole‐body MRI or CT screening on mortality or overdiagnosis.

Searching other resources

We will screen the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews on whole‐body imaging and population screening to identify additional eligible studies. Backwards and forwards citation searching will be performed for all studies included in the review using Scopus as the primary platform.

For included and eligible studies, we will check for post‐publication amendments, including expressions of concern, errata, corrigenda, and retractions. We will do this by checking the study record in the database from which it was identified and the journal website where available. Where we identify a post‐publication amendment, we will record this and consider its implications for study eligibility, data extraction, risk of bias assessment, and interpretation of findings [22].

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

Two review authors (ZC and KJJ) will independently screen references by title and abstract, and will exclude any irrelevant references. They will assess the full‐text articles of any potentially relevant references using Covidence [23]. The two review authors will resolve any disagreements through discussion, or by consulting a third review author (LA). We will collate reports of the same study so that each study, rather than each report, is the unit of interest in the review [22].

Data extraction and management

We will extract the following data from each included study: study design, study location, sampling design, population demographics, sources of funding and conflict of interest, scanning modality, scanning protocol, number and timing of screening rounds, and duration of follow‐up. We will also capture the study's specific definition of the ‘asymptomatic’ population (e.g. criteria for exclusion based on symptoms, pre‐existing conditions, or known genetic risk).

For scanning protocols, we will extract scanner technology characteristics where reported, including MRI field strength, CT scanner generation, or other indicators of image resolution, and whether scans were interpreted by one or more radiologists.

For studies evaluating repeated screening scans, we will extract first‐round or cumulative data, or both, according to the nature of the outcome, as prespecified in the Outcome measures section. When studies report outcomes separately by screening round, we will extract first‐round data as the baseline screening round. When studies only report data cumulatively across screening rounds, or when cumulative data are most relevant to the outcome, we will extract all‐round data. If both first‐round and cumulative data are available, we will extract both and specify which data we use in each analysis. All data will be extracted into Covidence [23]. We will use a standardized data extraction form, which we will pilot on a small sample of eligible studies and refine as needed before full data extraction [24]. Two review authors (ZC and KJJ) will independently extract data from each included study, and will resolve any discrepancies they identify during comparison through discussion. We will attempt to contact study investigators to obtain relevant unreported data.

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Two review authors (ZC and KJJ) will independently evaluate the risk of bias in each included study, using designated risk of bias tools tailored to the study design. The authors will resolve any disagreements through discussion or adjudication by a third review author (LA).

We will use the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for each specific outcome [25, 26]. We will assess risk of bias for the main results contributing to the review conclusions, including all‐cause mortality, all‐cancer mortality, cardiovascular disease mortality, other disease‐specific mortality, and overdiagnosis, where reported. For each outcome, we will assess the specific outcome measure and time point used in the analysis.

The effect of interest will be the effect of assignment to the intervention, corresponding to the intention‐to‐treat effect. If studies report only per‐protocol or as‐treated analyses, we will assess the risk of bias for the reported effect and consider the implications when interpreting the evidence.

For cluster‐randomized trials, we will use the RoB 2 tool for cluster‐randomized trials and assess the additional considerations relevant to cluster designs [27]. We will determine the overall risk of bias judgements for each outcome result according to the Cochrane RoB 2 guidance.

Regarding observational studies, we will not conduct a formal risk of bias assessment. We will include observational studies to provide descriptive evidence on diagnostic accuracy, detected abnormalities, and downstream consequences, rather than to estimate causal effects of whole‐body MRI or CT screening. We expect detection rates and downstream consequences to vary substantially according to radiologist reporting thresholds, scanner technology, scanning protocols, local follow‐up pathways, and study populations. These sources of variation are inherent to radiology‐based screening studies and are unlikely to be separable using a formal risk of bias tool. We will therefore interpret observational findings descriptively, extract relevant study and protocol characteristics where reported, and discuss these limitations when interpreting the evidence.

Measures of treatment effect

For mortality outcomes assessed in randomized trials (all‐cause, all‐cancer, and cause‐specific), we will extract or calculate risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [28]. Where appropriate and if sufficient homogeneous studies are available, we will pool mortality estimates using random‐effects meta‐analysis to account for expected heterogeneity across studies.

For critical outcomes, we will report the number of events, the denominator used, and proportions with 95% CIs. Denominators may include the number of participants screened, the number with positive scan findings, or another study‐defined denominator; we will report the denominator used by each study. Where studies report these outcomes as rates over time, we will extract the number of events and the person‐time at risk and report rates, rate ratios, or rate differences with 95% CIs, as appropriate.

We will assess overdiagnosis where data permit using the definition used in this protocol. Given the challenges in measuring overdiagnosis, we will also extract and report any estimates of overdiagnosis provided by study authors. If included studies provide quantitative estimates of overdiagnosis, we will first evaluate whether their operational definitions align with ours. When discrepancies exist, but relevant data are available with sufficient follow‐up (e.g. numbers of detected cases, follow‐up outcomes, or competing‐risk information), we will attempt to recalculate estimates consistent with our definition. If necessary data are not reported, we will contact study authors to clarify their methodology or to obtain data that enable re‐estimation according to our criteria. We will describe narratively, but will not pool quantitatively, any study author‐reported estimates that deviate from our definition.

Unit of analysis issues

For RCTs with multiple intervention arms, we will either combine relevant experimental groups or split the shared control group proportionally to avoid double‐counting participants in meta‐analyses. We will follow the guidance in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook [28]. When studies report mortality outcomes at multiple time points, we will select the longest available follow‐up for the primary analysis. For cluster‐randomized trials, we will use effect estimates that account for clustering where reported. We will follow the guidance in Chapter 23 of the Cochrane Handbook [27]. We do not anticipate major unit of analysis issues for observational studies, as these will primarily contribute descriptive data rather than comparative effect estimates.

Dealing with missing data

We will contact study authors to request missing or unreported data. For RCTs, we will use intention‐to‐treat (ITT) data where available. We will not impute missing outcome data for individual participants and will analyze available data as reported. If missing data varies substantially across studies, we will conduct sensitivity analyses comparing results with and without studies having high levels of missing data. We will clearly document all assumptions and methods, in accordance with the guidance in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook [29].

Reporting bias assessment

We will assess the risk of bias due to missing evidence using the Risk of Bias due to Missing Evidence (ROB‐ME) tool [30, 31]. This assessment will consider potential bias arising from missing studies (publication bias), missing outcomes (selective outcome reporting), and missing analyses (selective analysis reporting).

If sufficient RCTs (at least 10 studies) are available for meta‐analysis of mortality outcomes, we will assess publication bias and other relevant biases using funnel plots [32]. If fewer than 10 studies are available, we will not use funnel plots but will provide a narrative discussion of potential reporting biases.

Synthesis methods

We will perform our analyses in Review Manager (RevMan) [33]. We will synthesize data separately for RCTs and observational studies due to differences in study design and outcomes assessed.

For dichotomous outcomes from randomized trials, including mortality outcomes and overdiagnosis, we will use a random‐effects inverse‐variance approach on the log RR scale. We will use the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to estimate between‐study variance [29]. The Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method will be used to calculate CIs for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies and the estimate of between‐study heterogeneity is greater than zero [29]. In other scenarios, including pooled analyses of two studies or where the estimate of heterogeneity is zero, we will use the Wald‐type method [29].

For rare dichotomous outcomes, we will examine the event frequency and the presence of single‐zero or double‐zero studies before selecting the analysis method. We will avoid applying arbitrary continuity corrections where these are likely to introduce bias. If standard RevMan methods are not appropriate, we will consider alternative methods outside RevMan, such as generalized linear mixed models, and will report the rationale for the method used.

For descriptive outcomes from observational studies, we will summarize proportions or rates with 95% CIs for detected abnormalities, clinically important findings, false‐positive findings, false‐negative findings, invasive follow‐up procedures, radiation‐based or ultrasound follow‐up imaging, complications from diagnostic work‐up, cumulative radiation exposure, and treatment initiation. Where appropriate, and if studies are sufficiently comparable, we will pool proportions or rates using a random‐effects model. We will display results in forest plots where we perform meta‐analysis, and in structured tables for narrative synthesis.

We will conduct separate analyses for whole‐body MRI and whole‐body CT given their distinct technological characteristics and clinical applications. We will use the test for subgroup differences to assess whether effects differ across subgroups. For observational studies, if we observe substantial heterogeneity in pooled proportions, we will explore variation through structured tabulation examining differences by scanning modality and population characteristics. If fewer than 10 studies are available for subgroup analysis, we will provide a narrative description of potential sources of heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis

We will conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings, and will follow the guidance in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook [29]. For RCTs, we will perform sensitivity analyses by restricting the primary mortality analyses to studies judged at overall low risk of bias. We will present sensitivity analysis results alongside the main analysis. If substantial differences emerge, we will discuss the implications for the reliability and interpretation of our findings.

Certainty of the evidence assessment

Two review authors (ZC and KJJ) will independently assess the certainty of the body of evidence from RCTs using the GRADE approach [34, 35]. The review authors will resolve any disagreements through discussion or, if needed, through adjudication by a third review author (LA).

We will prepare summary of findings tables for the main comparison: whole‐body MRI or CT screening versus no whole‐body MRI or CT screening. Comparators may include no screening, usual care, or alternative screening strategies not involving whole‐body MRI or whole‐body CT.

For RCTs, we will assess the certainty of evidence for all prespecified outcomes reported in the included trials. For mortality outcomes and overdiagnosis, we will present results at five years and 10 years after completion of the intervention phase, or the closest available follow‐up time point, and at the longest available follow‐up. For other outcomes, we will present the time point reported by the study authors and specify the time point used.

For each outcome included in the summary of findings tables, we will present the effect estimate, the number of participants and studies, the time point of assessment, and the certainty of the evidence. We will rate the certainty of evidence as either high, moderate, low, or very low. Evidence from randomized trials will initially be considered high certainty and may be downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

We will only use observational studies to provide descriptive evidence on diagnostic accuracy, detected abnormalities and disease‐related findings, and downstream consequences. Observational studies will not be used to estimate the effects of screening on mortality or overdiagnosis, and we will not apply the GRADE approach to evidence from observational studies. We will present findings from observational studies separately in either evidence tables or narrative synthesis, rather than in the summary of findings tables.

Equity considerations

We will assess equity considerations by examining the sociodemographic characteristics of participants in the included studies, including socioeconomic status (income and education level), race/ethnicity, geographic location, and insurance status where reported[36]. We will evaluate whether whole‐body screening programmes reach diverse populations or are primarily accessible to higher socioeconomic groups. If data are available, we will examine whether outcomes and harms differ across these equity‐relevant subgroups.

Consumer involvement

We did not involve consumers in the development of this Cochrane protocol, as the intervention is not targeted at a specific patient group but the general population. To our knowledge, no developed core outcome set has been developed for this screening intervention. The protocol author team, as potential users of the intervention, designed this review to characterize whole‐body MRI or CT scans for clinical utility.
