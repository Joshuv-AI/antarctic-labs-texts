Lifetime cumulative effect of reproductive factors, “Life’s essential 8” health status and risk of chronic kidney disease: a nationwide prospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle has been found regarding the lifetime cumulative effect of reproductive factors on chronic kidney disease (CKD), and how this relationship varies with cardiovascular health (CVH) remains unexplored. This study aimed to assess the relationships of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure reflected by reproductive factors, and LE8 CVH status with incident CKD among postmenopausal women.MethodsThe study included 33,700 postmenopausal participants from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4 C) Study, enrolled between 2011 and 2012 with follow-up assessments conducted between 2014 and 2016 (median follow-up: 3.1 years). Lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure due to reproductive factors was evaluated through reproductive lifespan (RLS), endogenous estrogen exposure (EEE), and total estrogen exposure (TEE). Health behaviors information in Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) was collected by questionnaire and laboratory examination. The risk of CKD was analyzed with Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsShorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure was associated with an increased risk of CKD (RLS, HR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.19–1.70; EEE, HR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.12–1.72; TEE, HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.06–1.54). Moreover, the risk decreased gradually with increasing number of optimal cardiovascular health metrics (OCVHMs). Participants with shorter duration of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure combined with 5 or more OCVHMs no longer conferred risk for incident CKD (RLS, HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.70–1.47; EEE, HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 0.80–1.65; TEE, HR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.65–1.37).ConclusionsShorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure was correlated with an elevated risk of CKD. It should be encouraged for postmenopausal women to preserve good CVH status for prevention of CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27933-w.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Cumulative estrogen exposure, Cardiovascular health

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global health challenge with increasing prevalence [1]. Accumulating evidence indicates that female patients with CKD tend to have a better renal prognosis [2–4]. Previous studies have also identified that postmenopausal status acts as an independent risk for CKD, with a decrease in sex hormones proposed as one of the contributing factors to its development [5, 6]. Females with a longer duration of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure are correlated with a reduced risk of metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and overall mortality [7–9]. However, knowledge about the effect of the lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure on the incidence of CKD in female after menopause is insufficient. Only one recent study found that the reproductive lifespan (RLS), a simple indicator of cumulative estrogen exposure, is related to a lower possibility of kidney damage in postmenopausal women [10]. Importantly, endogenous estrogen exposure (EEE) and total estrogen exposure (TEE) have been proposed to accurately assess the cumulative impact of a woman's reproductive experiences throughout her lifespan [11, 12]. To date, limited studies have comprehensively explored the association between cumulative estrogen exposure from reproductive factors and CKD risk after menopause.

Several factors, including diet, lifestyle, and comorbid conditions, have been proposed as potential mechanisms responsible for CKD risk [13]. The American Heart Association (AHA) unveiled the "Life’s Essential 8 (LE8)" in 2022, aimed at developing a composite score for evaluating, tracking, and improving cardiovascular health (CVH) [14]. Recently, studies have shown a negative correlation between CVH and the prevalence of CKD [15, 16]. Promoting adherence to optimal CVH levels may be beneficial in reducing the burden of CKD [17]. Uncovering the link between cumulative estrogen exposure, CVH status, and the onset of CKD is highly significant, as it could offer valuable insights for preventing CKD through long-term behavioral modification.

To fill this knowledge gap, we aimed to investigate the relationships of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure, CVH factors, and CKD outcomes in postmenopausal women, based on data from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study.

Methods

Study participants and design

The current analysis utilized data from the 4 C Study, a comprehensive prospective cohort study conducted on a national scale. The study’s protocol and standard operating procedures have been previously published [18, 19]. Between 2011 and 2012, a baseline survey was conducted that included 193,846 participants over 40 years in mainland China from 16 provincial administrative regions. After an average follow-up time of 3.8 years, 170,240 participants (87.8%) of the initial participants took part in the follow-up assessments (between 2014 and 2016).

For the current study, we first excluded 58,830 men and 27,230 premenopausal women, leaving 84,180 postmenopausal women included. Then, we excluded 2,824 participants with CKD at baseline, 8,724 participants with missing data regarding reproductive factors, 21,081 participants with missing data on any metric of CVH, and 17,851 participants with missing data on the level of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at the follow-up visit. Eventually, there were 33,700 postmenopausal women included in the current analysis. Figure S1 illustrates the flow of sample selection in the current study.

Data collection

Data were systematically gathered in local community clinics by qualified personnel adhering to established protocols [18, 19]. Briefly, information on demographic characteristics, education, lifestyle habits, medical history, and personal history was gathered using standardized questionnaires through face-to-face interviews. Height, body weight, and blood pressure were measured by trained staff. Blood and serum samples were collected for standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), lipids profiles and creatinine tests. Finger capillary whole blood was used for Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) test.

Definition of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure

The components contributing to lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure from reproductive factors include age of menarche, age of menopause, duration of lifetime live births, duration of lifetime miscarriages or termination, duration of lifetime lactation, and duration of hormone use. The duration of lifetime live birth was quantified as the total number of live births multiplied by 0.75 years. The duration of lifetime miscarriages or terminations was quantified as the number of miscarriages or terminations multiplied by 0.25 years. RLS was determined by calculating the interval between menarche and menopause. EEE and TEE were calculated as follows: EEE (years) = RLS (years) - duration of lifetime lactation (years) - duration of hormone use (years) - duration of lifetime live births (years) - duration of lifetime miscarriages or terminations (years), this formula estimates the net duration of endogenous estrogen exposure from ovulatory cycles, subtracting periods when endogenous estrogen production is suppressed (pregnancy and lactation) or replaced by exogenous hormones; TEE (years) = RLS (years) - duration of lifetime lactation (years) + duration of hormone use (years) + duration of lifetime live births (years) + duration of lifetime miscarriages or terminations (years). Study participants were stratified into 4 groups for analysis according to their RLS, EEE, and TEE quartile cutoff.

Definition of LE8 CVH score and status

In accordance with the definition unveiled by the AHA in 2022 [14], LE8 CVH score was calculated based on 2 domains of 8 health components: behaviors (avoidance nicotine exposure, physical activity, healthy diet, and healthy sleep) and biological factors (healthy level of blood pressure, body mass index, blood lipids and blood glucose). Each of the 8 metrics was assigned a score between 0 to 100, where a score of 100 indicates optimal cardiovascular health metrics (OCVHM). To determine the total CVH score, the average of the scores from all 8 metrics was computed. Participants were categorized into 3 CVH status subgroups according to their overall LE8 CVH score: scores of 80–100 defined as high, 50–79 as moderate, and 0–49 as low.

Ascertainment of CKD

We defined CKD as one or more of the following: a decrease in eGFR exceeding 50% from the baseline, an eGFR level below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, initiation of receiving renal replacement therapy, or CKD-related death [20, 21].

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for the continuous variables, and counts with percentages for the categorical variables. Differences between continuous variables were compared using the t-test, with skewed data log-transformed prior to analysis. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test.

The association between new-onset CKD and indicators of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure from reproductive factors was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. Survival time was determined as the interval from the baseline survey date to either the date of CKD diagnosis, the date of the last follow-up visit, or the date of death, whichever occurred first. The crude model 1 was adjusted for age, education level, diabetes family history, marriage status, and research center. Model 2 was further adjusted for the number of live births, lifetime lactation duration, hormone replacement therapy (HRT), history of pregnancy-induced hypertension, and history of gestational diabetes mellitus based on the adjustments made in Model 1. Model 3 was fully adjusted for LE8 CVH scores based on the adjustments made in model 2. To investigate potential non-linear relationships between each component of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure and CKD outcomes, restricted cubic spline models with three knots were employed, fully adjusting for covariates in model 3. Additionally, to evaluate the association between the combination of RLS and age of menarche with incident CKD, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by stratifying participants based on the median value of age of menarche (≤ 15 or > 15 years).

To assess the relationship between lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure combined with CVH and incident CKD, we initially examined the association between RLS and CKD events across various CVH components. We also explored the relationships of lifetime cumulative estrogen categories and overall CVH status (high, moderate, and low) with incident CKD outcome. Moreover, we estimated the associations between shorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure (RLS, EEE, and TEE) with different numbers of OCVHM and incident CKD. Additionally, to further explore the effect of exogenous estrogen, we performed stratified analyses by HRT use status (ever vs. never use) to compare the associations between lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure and incident CKD risk between these subgroups.

We utilized SAS software (version 9.4) for statistical analysis and employed R software (version 4.3.3) for graph generation. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline population characteristics

As described in Table 1, the median age of the participants at baseline was 58.2 years (IQR: 54.4–63.3), and the median ages of menarche and menopause were 15.0 years (IQR: 14.0–17.0) and 50.0 years (IQR: 47.0–52.0), respectively. Among the 33,700 postmenopausal participants, 34.4% had completed high school or higher education, 15.3% had a family history of diabetes, and 11.8% were unmarried. The median lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure from reproductive factors among these postmenopausal participants was 34.0 years (IQR: (31.0–37.0) for RLS, 31.0 years (IQR: 27.3–34.0) for EEE, and 34.3 years (IQR: 31.0–36.9) for TEE. The median CVH score of the participants was 62.0 (IQR: 54.0–70.0). Participants with new-onset CKD were often older, less educated, and lived alone. Furthermore, they often had shorter durations of RLS, EEE and TEE, along with lower CVH score compared to those without CKD.

Association of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure due to reproductive factors with CKD

As presented in Table 2, participants with a shorter RLS (< 31.0 years) had a significantly higher risk of developing incident CKD (HR: 1.42, 95% CI: 1.19–1.70) related to those with a longer RLS (≥ 37.0 years) (P for trend = 0.001). Comparable relationships were found with EEE (HR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.12–1.72, P for trend = 0.002) and TEE (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.06–1.54, P for trend = 0.005). Additionally, Fig. 1 showed a linear relationship between cumulative estrogen exposure and the risk for incident CKD (RLS: P for nonlinearity = 0.491; EEE: P for nonlinearity = 0.272; TEE: P for nonlinearity = 0.370).

As shown in Figure S2, compared to participants with a longer RLS (≥ 37.0 years) and an older age of menarche (> 15 years), those with the same longer RLS (≥ 37.0 years) but an earlier age of menarche (≤ 15 years) had an increased risk of incident CKD (HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.06–2.04). The findings were consistent across the different lengths of RLS and those with both the shortest RLS and an earlier age at menarche conferred the highest risk of CKD (HR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.48–3.01).

Subgroup analyses stratified by HRT use showed that shorter RLS, EEE, and TEE were associated with increased CKD risk in HRT non-users (RLS: HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16–1.70; EEE: HR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.11–1.72; TEE: HR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.03–1.52). In HRT users, effect estimates were directionally consistent (RLS: HR: 1.62, 95% CI: 0.63–4.14; EEE: HR: 1.83, 95% CI: 0.56–6.02; TEE: HR: 1.72, 95% CI: 0.77–3.84). Nevertheless, no significant interaction was detected between HRT use and estrogen exposure indicators (P for interaction = 0.800 for RLS, 0.330 for EEE, and 0.260 for TEE) (Table S2).

Combined associations of lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure and CVH status with the risk of CKD

We found that shorter RLS with high CVH no longer conferred a significantly increased risk for incident CKD, compared with RLS ≥ 37.0 years combined with high CVH (Fig. 2). Participants who had moderate CVH did not have a significantly increased risk of incident CKD when their RLS ≥ 37.0 years (HR: 1.60, 95% CI: 0.79–3.26), while moderate CVH combined with RLS < 31.0 years was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident CKD (HR: 2.13, 95% CI: 1.05–4.32). The risk of incident CKD increased by 301% (HR: 4.01, 95% CI: 1.92–8.37) among participants with RLS < 31.0 years and low CVH, compared with those with RLS ≥ 37.0 years and high CVH. Similar findings were observed in EEE and TEE, with the risk of incident CKD increasing by 203% (HR: 3.03, 95% CI: 1.59–5.78) for EEE and 267% (HR: 3.67, 95% CI: 1.87–7.18) for TEE in participants with low CVH and shorter durations of EEE or TEE exposure, compared with participants who had high CVH and longer durations of exposure to EEE or TEE, respectively. As shown in Table S1, regarding CVH components, a significant interaction was detected in diet pattern strata and RLS duration on risk of CKD (P for interaction = 0.023), which showed that significantly increased CKD risk was detected among shorter RLS duration with non-optimal diet pattern (HR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.33–2.05).

Discussion

This large-scale prospective study revealed that postmenopausal women with shorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure due to reproductive factors were more likely to developing new-onset CKD. The positive relation between shorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure and CKD outcomes was more robust in those with low CVH or fewer OCVHMs. Moreover, achieving more OCVHMs in postmenopausal women could attenuate the adverse effect of shorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure on incident CKD. These findings supported the significance of estrogen exposure on CKD outcome and the importance of optimal CVH in health improvement for postmenopausal women, especially for those with a substantially shorter duration of estrogen exposure.

Estrogen, as indirectly reflected by cumulative exposure to reproductive factors, has been demonstrated to exert long-term immediate effects on systemic metabolism [22, 23]. The Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study, which included 3,155 postmenopausal women in the longitudinal analyses, has shown that a longer RLS was consistently related to a lower risk of CKD [10]. Animal models have also demonstrated that renal injury progresses more gradually in female animals compared to males [24, 25]. Additionally, estrogen itself confers a protective effect against progressive renal injury, as evidenced by significant reductions in glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis observed in rats treated with estrogen [26]. Our current study provided further supporting evidence on endogenous estrogen’s potential protective effect against CKD risk.

In addition, it is worth noting that the RLS is determined by both the age of menarche and the age of menopause. Early menarche has been reported to be correlated with a heightened risk of various conditions, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and mortality [27–29], which may interfere with the protective effect of a longer RLS on health. However, the relationship between age at menarche and CKD is still unknown. Among previous population studies, only one cross-sectional study involving 8,510 postmenopausal women reported that a later menarche age was correlated with a higher incidence of CKD, which contradicted the findings about age at menarche and other cardiometabolic diseases and was limited to their cross-sectional design [30]. Our study is the first to demonstrate that an earlier age of menarche is related to an increased risk of incidence CKD based on the participant data from the prospective cohort and further suggests that an older menarche age combined with a longer cumulative estrogen exposure is correlated with a decreased risk of incident CKD.

Moreover, estrogen exposure is influenced by various endogenous and exogenous factors, which requires a more detailed evaluation. The EEE and TEE assessments were considered more accurate because their calculation formulas included many details about the participants' reproductive history [9]. Specifically, EEE emphasizes estrogen exposure from endogenous sources by subtracting periods of suppressed endogenous estrogen production (pregnancy and lactation) and exogenous hormone use from RLS, whereas TEE represents total estrogen exposure by adding back pregnancy-related and exogenous hormone exposure [11]. In a 2-year longitudinal study of 5,845 menopausal women aged 66 years or older, participants with HRT were found to have a greater loss of eGFR than those without HRT [31]. Recently, findings from the UK Biobank also reported a notable association between HRT and an elevated risk of CKD, especially among women who started HRT at an earlier age [32]. Consistently, our findings do not support the hypothesis that TEE involving HRT offers a superior protective effect against CKD compared to EEE. Subgroup analyses by HRT use further supported this finding, showing similar patterns for RLS, EEE, and TEE, with no significant interactions, indicating that exogenous estrogen use does not modify these associations.

Hence, assessing the modifiable risk factors and define strategies for health and behavior modifications is vital, especially among postmenopausal women with relatively shorter RLS, to minimize the related CKD risk. The updated LE8 construct provides a comprehensive depiction of the entire spectrum of CVH [14]. Recent studies report that the LE8 construct could be an effective instrument in predicting CKD outcomes. In a prospective study involving 147,988 participants from the UK Biobank, individuals with moderate CVH exhibited a 39% reduced risk of developing CKD, while those with high CVH demonstrated a 57% lower risk of CKD incidence compared to those classified as having low CVH [15]. Our team also reported that ideal CVH could modify the adverse impacts on diabetes related to exposure to early risk factors, such as early-life famine exposure and midlife weight change [33, 34]. Our current study extended the evidence for the benefit of maintaining CVH in improving kidney health in postmenopausal women.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths include a large sample size, a nationwide prospective cohort design, and detailed information about lifetime cumulative estrogen indicators and CVH metrics. Nevertheless, several limitations of the current study are worth noting. First, albuminuria level was not available in our study, so incident CKD might be underestimated. Second, information regarding reproductive factors was predominantly gathered through participants’ recollections, which could potentially introduce inaccuracies attributable to recall bias. Third, the CVH metrics were presented only at baseline, and the relationship between CVH changes and clinical outcomes was not evaluated. Finally, the data used for analysis were completed in 2016. Although the biological mechanisms linking lifetime estrogen exposure with CKD risk are not expected to change over time, longer-term follow-up will be valuable to confirm our findings and investigate the long-term effects of lifetime estrogen exposure on CKD progression and end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

Conclusions

The current study demonstrated that the shorter lifetime cumulative estrogen exposure due to reproductive factors was correlated with a higher risk of incident CKD among postmenopausal women. The risk was much higher in those who had lower CVH scores or fewer optimal CVH metrics. The results also suggest that individuals who experienced a substantially shorter duration of estrogen exposure should be encouraged to preserve good CVH status to prevent the new onset of CKD after menopause.
