Effect of solid-state fermentation on the release and antioxidant activity of soluble polyphenols from wheat bran
Authors: Xiaomin Tian, Yuqiu Guo, Linlin Sun, Lirong Chen, Kaichang Liu, Kuijie Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using wheat bran as substrate, four solid-state fermentation methods were compared for soluble polyphenol release. The effect of Bio-enzymatic synergy (BES) is the most significant, producing 4.85 mg GAE/g of total phenolic substances, which is 45.77% higher than the control group (CK). LC-MS analysis revealed that ferulamide and 2,4,6-trihydroxybenzoic acid predominated in positive ion mode, while salicylic acid dominated in negative mode. Notably, salicylic acid was found almost exclusively in ester-bound and glycoside-bound fractions (>88%), indicating its release requires cleavage of covalent linkages to cell wall components. The release of salicylic acid results from the synergistic action of Bio-enzymatic synergy and alkaline hydrolysis. Both in vitro and in vivo assays confirmed that BES-released polyphenols enhanced antioxidant activity, reducing ROS and MDA levels while increasing GSH-Px activity and extending C. elegans lifespan. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for developing targeted fermentation-enzymatic processes to produce functional wheat bran extracts.

Introduction

Wheat is an important food crop widely cultivated in Asia, particularly in northern regions. As a by-product of wheat processing, wheat bran contains various nutritional components, including dietary fiber, polyphenols, and proteins (G., H.G, et al., 2016). Due to the phenolic hydroxyl groups in their molecular structure, polyphenols possess multiple functional activities and serve as natural antioxidants. For example, the hydrogen atom in the phenolic hydroxyl group can dissociate as a hydrogen radical, forming a phenoxyl radical, or depart as a proton, yielding a phenolate anion. The phenoxyl radical and phenolate anion can interconvert through electron transfer, and the phenoxyl radical may further react with other radicals, thereby exhibiting strong free radical scavenging activity (Tuncel & Yılmaz, 2011). This mechanism effectively reduces the occurrence of oxidative reactions.

Phenolics exist in three structural forms: free, soluble-bound, and insoluble-bound (Liu et al., 2016). Insoluble or soluble-bound phenolics are located within the cell wall (Liyana-Pathirana & Shahidi, 2006), where they are linked to cell wall components via ester, ether, or glycosidic bonds, thereby enhancing the mechanical strength of the cell wall (Robbins, 2003). In contrast, free phenolics are distributed in the vacuoles of plant cells. Soluble polyphenols (both free and soluble bound forms) are the main forms that are beneficial for human absorption and use, and they have greater health benefits. As for insoluble bound phenols, because they require extra destructive treatments like alkaline or acid hydrolysis and aren't compatible with the extraction method used in this study. So this study only focuses on soluble polyphenols.

The structure of phenolics influences food color and flavor. For instance, the bitter and astringent taste of free phenolics is closely related to their molecular weight. Low molecular weight free phenolics (e.g., gallic acid) from grape seeds exhibit intense bitterness (Elías et al., 2011), whereas high molecular weight free phenolics (e.g., epicatechin) from fruit skins contribute primarily to astringency (Robichaud & Noble, 1990). During beer brewing, esterified ferulic acid in malt is hydrolyzed during the boiling stage, generating 4-vinylguaiacol, which imparts a vanilla-like aroma (Szwajgier et al., 2012). Certain glycosylated flavonoids can provide sweetness. For example, the glycoside formed between epicatechin and glucose (e.g., epicatechin-3-O-β-D-glucoside) (Dixon, 2009) has lower bitterness intensity than free epicatechin, and produces a slight sweet aftertaste. Future research should further elucidate the dynamic conversion mechanisms of phenolic binding forms to develop improved technologies for food quality enhancement. By recycling high-value components such as polyphenols, low-value by-products can be transformed into functional food ingredients, thereby enhancing economic benefits and promoting sustainable resource utilization.

Solid-state fermentation improves the nutritional quality and application value of wheat bran by modifying its fiber structure, promoting the release of bioactive compounds, and enhancing its antioxidant capacity (Li et al., 2022). Arte et al. (Arte et al., 2015) found that the increase in total phenol content in fermented wheat bran was most significant. Cellulose and hemicellulose are the main components of wheat bran cell walls. Cellulase facilitates the breakdown of chemical bonds between cell wall constituents and bound polyphenols (Zhao et al., 2017).Weissella species produce acidic substances that inhibit the growth of contaminating microorganisms, while Lactobacillus helveticus secretes feruloyl esterase. The combined use of Weissella confiusa and Lactobacillus helveticus more effectively promotes the release of phenolic compounds. As both are lactic acid bacteria, they collectively maintain an acidic environment, which is conducive to polyphenol extraction. Furthermore, the oligosaccharides or monosaccharides generated during enzymatic hydrolysis can be utilized by microorganisms, supporting their growth. Bio-enzymatic synergistic solid-state fermentation integrates the advantages of microbial fermentation and enzymatic hydrolysis, achieving a synergistic effect (Jiang et al., 2021).

During cellular metabolism, free radicals continuously undergo reversible redox changes-being generated and eliminated. An imbalance in redox homeostasis occurs when excessive free radicals accumulate, leading to oxidative stress and consequent oxidative damage. Caenorhabditis elegans serves as a model organism for assessing antioxidant capacity and the extent of oxidative damage. Approximately 60–80% of human genes have homologs in C. elegans (Calvo Daniel et al., 2016), making it a widely used animal model in laboratory research (Martorell et al., 2012). Polyphenolic compounds, particularly free polyphenols, were identified as replenishable endogenous antioxidants that could reduce lipid oxidation and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2021) found that by suppressing lipid oxidation and rancidity in FWB through stabilization treatment (BassinelloI et al., 2012), its antioxidant activity was preserved to the maximum extent.

While previous studies have confirmed that solid-state fermentation can enhance the extraction yield of wheat bran polyphenols, most have focused solely on total phenolic content and in vitro antioxidant activity, lacking systematic investigation into the transformation patterns of different bound-state polyphenols during fermentation, the release mechanisms of specific phenolic compounds, and their true in vivo antioxidant effects. This study improved the extraction technology by hydrolyzing soluble bound phenolics with acid or alkali. Using wheat bran as the substrate, we focused on analyzing the differential release effects of solid-state fermentation on free, ester-bound, and glycoside-bound polyphenols. LC-MS was employed to reveal the variation patterns of key phenolic compounds, particularly the release mechanism of characteristic components such as salicylic acid. Using *C. elegans* as a model, we verified the in vivo antioxidant efficacy of the polyphenol extracts. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the high-value utilization of wheat bran.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wheat bran has been purchased from Weiliang Food Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). This study used whole wheat bran as the fermentation substrate. After fermentation, the bran samples were freeze-dried for 48 h and then ground into powder; they were then passed through a 40-mesh sieve, and the samples were collected for subsequent measurements and analysis. Weissella confiusa and Lactobacillus helveticus have been obtained through laboratory isolation. Weissella confiusa preservation number: CCTCCNO: M20221635. Lactobacillus helveticus preservation number: CCTCCNO: M20221634. Pentosanase and cellulase have been acquired from Vinnox (China) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cellulase at 10,000 U/g and pentosanase at 100,000 U/g. Caenorhabditis elegans has been procured from Fujian Shangyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). All other chemicals have been of analytical grade and have been supplied by Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Extraction of free phenolic compounds

The free phenol extraction method was referenced from Nicoletti et al. (Nicoletti et al., 2013) with minor modifications. One gram of the sample has been mixed with 10 mL of 85% acidified methanol extraction solution in a conical flask. The mixture has been extracted ultrasonically at room temperature for 2 h, then centrifuged at 9000 r/min at 4 °C for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. The residue has been re-extracted twice, and the combined supernatants have been concentrated by rotary evaporation. The concentrate has been reconstituted to 25 mL with ultrapure water, then extracted six times with ethyl acetate. The organic phases have been combined, concentrated by rotary evaporation at 45 °C, and finally made up to 25 mL with ultrapure water as the free phenolic fraction.

Extraction of soluble Ester-bound phenolics

The extraction methods for soluble ester-bound phenolics and soluble glycoside-bound phenolics were adapted from those described by Ma et al. (Ma et al., 2014) with minor modifications. The aqueous phase obtained after free phenolic extraction has been mixed with 10 mL of 2 M NaOH and extracted ultrasonically at room temperature for 2 h. The mixture has been centrifuged at 9000 r/min at room temperature for 10 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant has then been extracted six times with ethyl acetate. The organic phases have been combined, concentrated by rotary evaporation at 45 °C, and made up to 50 mL with ultrapure water as the soluble ester-bound phenolic fraction.

Extraction of soluble glycoside-bound phenolics

The aqueous phase remaining after soluble ester-bound phenolic extraction has been concentrated by rotary evaporation at 45 °C and made up to 50 mL with ultrapure water as the soluble glycoside-bound phenolic fraction. The free phenolics, soluble ester-bound phenolics, and soluble glycoside-bound phenolics have been collectively referred to as soluble polyphenols. The results should be seen as relative extraction content.

Determination of phenolic content and analysis of polyphenol composition

The content of soluble polyphenols has been determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method, with modifications based on the procedure described by Abozed et al. (Abozed et al., 2014). The polyphenol content has been expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry weight (mg GAE/g DW), calculated using the standard curve y = 0.0552× + 0.0847 (R2 = 0.9998). All samples have been analyzed in triplicate.

Prior to analysis using the LC-ESI-MS system, the aqueous solution of soluble polyphenols has been filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to remove impurities. Separation has been performed on a Hypersil Gold column (C18, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). Mobile phase A has consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B has been methanol. The gradient elution program has been set as follows: 0–1.5 min, 2% B; 1.5–3 min, 2–85% B; 3–10 min, 85–100% B; 10–10.1 min, 100–2% B; 10.1–11 min, 2% B; 11–12 min, 2% B. The column temperature has been maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate has been 0.2 mL/min. Spray voltage (ESI+) set at 3500 V, ESI– set at −3000 V; mass scanning range m/z 80–1400; capillary temperature at 320 °C; capillary voltage at 40 V; collision energy at 15–30 V; and injection volume at 10 μL. This study used a lab-built comprehensive database, with matching criteria of a retention time deviation of ±0.2 min and a secondary fragment match of ≥70%. This study employed non-targeted metabolomics without the addition of external or internal standards for absolute quantification. Thus, the values of polyphenolic compounds represent relative abundance based on peak area response intensity and are intended solely for inter-sample comparative analysis.

In vitro antioxidant activity studies

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging assay has been conducted following a modified method based on Abozed et al. (Abozed et al., 2014). The mixture has been incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance at 517 nm has been recorded as A1. The absorbance of the blank group has been measured as A2, and that of the control group as A0. The scavenging activity has been expressed as the percentage inhibition per gram of dry weight. All samples have been analyzed in triplicate using the following formula.

DPPH Radical Scavenging Activity%=1–A1−A2/A0−A2×100%

Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity

The hydroxyl radical scavenging activity has been determined using the Fenton reaction system, adapted from the method of Xie et al. (Xie et al., 2022) with modifications. The mixture has been incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min, centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 5 min at room temperature, and the absorbance of the supernatant at 510 nm has been measured as A1. The control group absorbance has been recorded as A0, while the blank (A2) has been prepared by replacing the sample with sterile water. The scavenging activity has been expressed as the percentage inhibition per gram of dry weight. All determinations have been performed in triplicate using the following formula.Hydroxyl Radical Scavenging Activity%=1−A1−A0/A2×100%

Study on in vivo antioxidant functional activity

Nematode cultivation and synchronized culture

Following the method of Pasrija and Kumari et al. (Pasrija & Kumari, 2025) with modifications, nematodes have been maintained on NGM plates seeded with OP50 at a constant 20 °C. Gravid adults have been washed off plates using M9 buffer into sterile centrifuge tubes. The samples have been centrifuged at 1000 r/min, and the supernatant has been discarded; this washing step has been repeated twice, leaving approximately 1 mL of buffer. Lysis buffer has been added to the pellet, which has been resuspended. Tubes have been shaken vigorously for 8 min to fully lyse adult worms while preserving eggs intact. The tubes have been centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 2 min to pellet the eggs, and the supernatant has been carefully removed.

The egg pellet has been resuspended in 1 mL of M9 buffer and centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 2 min; this washing step has been repeated three times. Finally, the eggs have been maintained in M9 buffer in a centrifuge tube and incubated at 20 °C for 12 h. Due to the absence of food in M9 buffer, the hatched larvae have arrested at the L1 stage.

The L1 larvae have been transferred onto NGM plates seeded with food and allowed to develop until they reached the L4 stage (48–60 h), at which point they have been used for subsequent experiments.

Determination of ROS levels

Following the method of Moliner et al. (Moliner et al., 2020) with modifications, synchronized L4-stage nematodes have been washed three times with M9 buffer, and the wash solution has been collected. A 0.1 mL aliquot of the nematode suspension has been transferred to a light-impermeable 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 0.09 mL of 0.1 mmol/L H2DCF-DA solution and 0.01 mL of the sample. A control group has been prepared using 0.1 mmol/L H2DCF-DA solution. The plate has been immediately placed in a microplate reader to measure fluorescence intensity at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and a reaction temperature of 25 °C. The reaction has been monitored over 12 h, with measurements taken every 15 min. Each experimental group has been replicated six times.RelativeROScontent%=AUCsample group/AUCcontrol group

Note: AUC sample group and AUC control group represent the integrated area of the fluorescence signal for the sample and control groups, respectively.

Determination of antioxidant enzyme levels under stress conditions

Synchronized L1 larvae have been transferred to NGM plates containing the sample, with a blank control group containing only OP50 bacterial lawn. Once the nematodes developed to the L4 stage, they have been washed off with M9 buffer into centrifuge tubes, and the plates have been rinsed three times. The tubes have been centrifuged at 3000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C, and the pellet has been collected. The nematodes have been resuspended in 0.5 mL of M9 buffer and sonicated on ice for 2 min. The supernatant has been collected, and the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and antioxidant enzymes in the nematodes have been determined according to the kit instructions.

Lifespan assay in nematodes

For the sample group, L4-stage nematodes have been transferred to NGM plates containing food, supplemented with 0.5 mL of the sample and 0.5 mL of 12.5 mg/L 5-fluorouracil. The control group has been identical but without phenolic compounds. The survival and death of nematodes have been recorded daily, with the day of transfer designated as 0 day. The nematodes have been transferred to new, labeled plates every 3 days until all nematodes died.

Data processing and analysis

Origin software have been used for statistical analysis and graphing. Data have been presented as mean values, and Duncan's test has been applied for significance analysis (P < 0.05).

BES treatment yielded the highest free phenolic content (1.79 mg GAE/g), representing a 2.4-fold increase over natural fermentation (CK), 1.8-fold higher than GC, and 1.3-fold higher than VL (Table 1). This substantial enhancement suggests that the synergistic action of microbial fermentation and enzymatic hydrolysis effectively disrupts cell wall structures, facilitating the release of otherwise inaccessible phenolics. Similar values have also been reported by Vaher et al. (Vaher et al., 2010). Webber et al. (Webber et al., 2014) have also found that fermentation can significantly increase phenolic components and free radical scavenging capacity.

After alkaline hydrolysis, the soluble ester-bound phenolic content has been 0.85 mg GAE/g, and the soluble glycoside-bound phenolic content has reached 2.21 mg GAE/g. This demonstrates that bound phenolic substances have been predominant in wheat bran and have been difficult to extract using acidified methanol solution. In the BES group, soluble bound phenolics have accounted for 63.09% of the total phenolics, representing more than half of the total content. Research by Liyana et al. (Liyana-Pathirana & Shahidi, 2006) has also shown that soluble bound phenolics are often linked to cell wall components via ester, ether, or glycosidic bonds. These results confirm that BES treatment has effectively disrupted the bonds connecting bound phenolics to the cell wall, more efficiently decomposing the cell wall structure of wheat bran and increasing the content of soluble polyphenols. Consequently, all subsequent experiments have been conducted based on comparisons between the CK and BES groups.

The numerical value of polyphenolic compounds represents the relative abundance based on peak area. Compositional changes of soluble polyphenols in CK and BES under negative ion mode.

A considerable number of ferulic acid and ferulic acid derivative components have been observed. In positive ion mode, ferulic acid derivatives have shown both greater variety and higher relative content (Table 2), with four main compounds detected: 5-Hydroxyferulic acid, Feruloylputrescine, Ferulamide, and Trans-Isoferulic acid. In negative ion mode, Ferulic acid has been the primary form detected (Table 3). Lu et al. (Lu et al., 2022) found that the ferulic acid content after fermentation was approximately 12.06 times higher than that of unfermented wheat bran, thereby correspondingly enhancing its antioxidant capacity. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2016) have identified Ferulic acid as the major phenolic compound in raw bran. In contrast, Verma et al. (Verma et al., 2009) have found that Trans-Isoferulic acid is the predominant phenolic acid and the main contributor (66.4–95.5%) to the antioxidant capacity of wheat bran extracts. This discrepancy has been potentially attributed to differences in the geographical origin and variety of wheat, as Verma studied Canadian wheat bran, whereas this research has focused on Chinese wheat bran.

In positive ion mode, Ferulamide and 2,4,6-Trihydroxybenzoic acid have been the predominant phenolic acids with relatively high content in the bran extract (Table 2). In negative ion mode, Salicylic acid has been the main phenolic acid (Table 3). In positive ion mode, both Ferulamide and 2,4,6-Trihydroxybenzoic acid have exhibited their highest relative content in the free phenolic fraction, with lower levels in the soluble ester-bound and soluble glycoside-bound phenolic fractions. Specifically, Ferulamide has been most abundant in the BES free phenol fraction, accounting for 62.99% of the BES free phenols. 2,4,6-Trihydroxybenzoic acid has been most prevalent in the CK free phenol fraction, constituting 46.23% of the CK free phenols.

The biosynthetic pathway of salicylic acid utilizes filamentous bodies produced in chloroplasts as precursors (Zhang et al., 2025). Ester bonds and glycosidic bonds are key to salicylic acid binding. In negative ion mode, Salicylic acid has shown the highest relative content. Relevant studies have widely employed the negative ion mode for quantitative analysis of salicylic acid (Liu et al., 2017). The levels of free Salicylic acid have been low in both CK and BES, whereas its relative content in soluble bound phenolic fractions has been high. Salicylic acid has accounted for 88.14% of the CK soluble ester-bound phenols and 83.75% of the CK soluble glycoside-bound phenols. Similarly, it has comprised 93.81% of the BES soluble ester-bound phenols and 90.66% of the BES soluble glycoside-bound phenols.

The predominance of salicylic acid in the ester-bound and glycoside-bound fractions, coupled with its near absence in the free phenolic fraction, indicates that salicylic acid in wheat bran is primarily covalently linked to cell wall polysaccharides via ester or glycosidic bonds. Cellulase and pentosanase can degrade the main chain of arabinoxylan, followed by lactic acid bacteria acidifying the substrate and secreting esterase to cleave the ester bonds (Tang et al., 2024). Alkaline hydrolysis effectively cleaves these linkages, releasing free salicylic acid. As Salicylic acid primarily participates in the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway (Zhang et al., 2025), it has been hypothesized that this may be related to the specific chemical bond structure of Salicylic acid within this metabolic pathway (Fig. 1). After salicylic acid is synthesized here, it binds with cell wall components in storage or structural forms. Effects of soluble polyphenols on ROS levels in nematodes

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been normal metabolites produced by physiological metabolism, and the body's intrinsic antioxidant system has been capable of clearing ROS to maintain a balanced state free from damage (Ray et al., 2012). However, with advancing age, the balance between ROS production and clearance has been disrupted, leading to a gradual increase in ROS concentration that has caused cellular oxidative damage and ultimately shortened lifespan. Therefore, ROS has served as an important indicator reflecting the degree of cellular oxidation.

This experiment has employed the H2DCFDA fluorescent probe detection method to measure the antioxidant reactive oxygen species content in nematodes, with results shown in Fig. 3. Brighter fluorescence has indicated higher ROS levels in the nematodes. Comparison of fluorescence intensity in Fig. 3A has revealed the following order among the three groups: Blank > CK > BES, with BES showing lower fluorescence intensity than the other two groups. This has demonstrated that polyphenolic compounds can effectively reduce ROS levels in nematodes during growth and decrease the degree of oxidative damage. Mengoni et al. Malondialdehyde (MDA), as the end product of lipid oxidation, has been used as an indicator to assess the degree of oxidative damage in organisms (Zheng et al., 2024). Polyphenolic compounds have primarily inhibited lipid oxidation by scavenging free radicals, chelating metal ions, and suppressing oxidase activity (Balasubramaniam et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 3C, no significant difference has been observed between Blank and CK (P > 0.05), while BES has shown a marked difference compared to the other two groups (P < 0.05). The MDA value for Blank has been 3.9 nmol/mL, and for CK, it has been 2.87 nmol/mL. The MDA content in BES has been 2.43 nmol/mL, with the MDA level in Blank being 1.6 times that of BES and 1.3 times that of CK. These results have indicated that higher levels of phenolic compounds can reduce MDA content in nematodes, thereby diminishing oxidative damage and exerting antioxidant effects.

Effects of soluble polyphenols on glutathione peroxidase activity in nematodes

Natural antioxidants such as plant polyphenols have enhanced antioxidant capacity by indirectly regulating enzyme expression. Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) has protected cells from oxidative damage by catalyzing the reaction between reduced glutathione and hydrogen peroxide or lipid peroxides (Zduńczyk et al., 2006). GSH-Px, involved in the reaction between glutathione and reactive oxygen species in vivo, has cleared excessive free radicals and protected the organism from ROS damage, serving as a crucial indicator of antioxidant capacity (Zheng et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 3D, the GSH-Px enzyme activity in Blank has been 9.33 nmol/min/mL, and in CK, it has been 9.58 nmol/min/mL, with no significant difference between the two groups. In contrast, BES has shown an activity of 15.33 nmol/min/mL, which is 1.6 times higher than the other two groups, representing a significant increase (P < 0.05). This has demonstrated that GSH-Px activity rises with increasing polyphenol content, thereby enhancing the protective effect against ROS-induced damage.

Effects of soluble polyphenols on nematode lifespan

The lifespan assay has served as the most intuitive indicator for studying the antioxidant properties in Caenorhabditis elegans. The antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds has been verified through the lifespan experiment, with results presented in Fig. 4. Under normal culture conditions, the lifespan of Blank nematodes has been approximately 19 days. By day 14, the survival rate of Blank nematodes has been significantly lower than that of the other groups. By day 20, all Blank nematodes have died, while some CK and BES nematodes have remained alive, with survival rates of 6% and 11%, respectively. By day 25, all CK nematodes have died, while the survival rate of BES nematodes has been 8%. By day 30, all BES nematodes have died, with their lifespan extended by 5 days and 10 days compared to CK and Blank nematodes, respectively. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that bio-enzymatic synergistic (BES) fermentation is an effective strategy for enhancing the release of soluble polyphenols from wheat bran, particularly the bound forms. LC-MS analysis revealed a distinct dichotomy in phenolic profiles between positive and negative ion modes, with ferulamide and 2,4,6-trihydroxybenzoic acid dominating in positive mode, while salicylic acid was the predominant phenolic acid in negative mode. Notably, salicylic acid was found almost exclusively in the ester-bound and glycoside-bound fractions, indicating that its release requires the cleavage of covalent linkages to cell wall components.

The differential distribution of phenolic compounds across free and bound fractions provides insights into their localization and bonding within the bran matrix. The effectiveness of alkaline hydrolysis in releasing salicylic acid suggests that this compound is primarily deposited in the cell wall as conjugates, likely serving structural or storage functions. BES fermentation, by partially degrading cellulose and hemicellulose, may increase the accessibility of these conjugates to subsequent hydrolysis.

Both in vitro and in vivo assays confirmed that the polyphenols released by BES fermentation possess superior antioxidant activity, as evidenced by enhanced free radical scavenging, reduced oxidative stress markers (ROS, MDA), and extended lifespan in C. elegans. This process is simple to operate, requires mild conditions and no complex equipment, demonstrates excellent scalability, and provides an economically viable technical pathway for the high-value utilization of wheat bran. These findings not only provide a scientific basis for the valorization of wheat bran but also offer a mechanistic understanding of how fermentation and enzymatic treatment can be optimized to release specific bioactive compounds from agricultural by-products.
