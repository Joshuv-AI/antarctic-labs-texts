Lymph Node-Targeted PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine for Synergistic Immunity Against Echinococcus granulosus Infection
Authors: Mayire Aizezi, Ayinula Tuohetali, Mutailipu Maimaiti, Xuan Li, Ya Song, Xia Chen, Guangfeng Chen, Saifuding Abula, Huijing Gao, Ruofeng Yan, Kalibixiati Aimulajiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCystic echinococcosis (CE), a zoonosis caused by Echinococcus granulosus, severely endangers human and animal health in western pastoral China. Conventional surgery and chemotherapy suffer from high recurrence and toxic side effects, so immunization is urgently needed for CE prevention.MethodsWe constructed a PLGA nanovaccine loading EgG1Y162 antigen (PLGA-EgG1Y162) via double emulsion-solvent evaporation. Its physicochemical features, DC activation, in vivo lymph node retention, immune response, anti-infection protection and CD4⁺ T cell transcriptome were comprehensively detected.ResultsThe nanovaccine showed uniform spherical morphology with an average particle size of 394 nm and 79% encapsulation efficiency, presenting favorable sustained-release performance and excellent biocompatibility. In vitro experiments verified that PLGA-EgG1Y162 was efficiently taken up by dendritic cells and effectively facilitated DC maturation. In vivo tracking demonstrated that the nanovaccine achieved prolonged retention within lymph nodes. Compared with the free antigen group and Freund’s adjuvant-adjuvanted group, PLGA-EgG1Y162 induced significantly higher and longer-lasting antigen-specific IgG antibodies, markedly upregulated splenic IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10 and TNF-α expression, and maintained elevated proportions of splenic CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells. At 16 and 24 weeks post infection, vaccinated mice had remarkably fewer hepatic cysts, reduced liver-to-body weight ratios and lower serum transaminase levels, with relatively intact hepatic tissue architecture. Transcriptomic data illustrated that the vaccine reshaped CD4⁺ T cell transcriptional profiles, alleviated infection-triggered chronic inflammation, and suppressed aberrant activation of hepatic autophagy-related genes.ConclusionPLGA-EgG1Y162 realizes lymph node-targeted sustained antigen delivery to elicit balanced long-lasting humoral and cellular immunity, producing prominent protection against E. granulosus. This work offers a promising immunoprophylactic candidate for cystic echinococcosis.Keywords: Echinococcus granulosus, EgG1Y162, PLGA, nanovaccine, immune response

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis (CE), also known as hydatid disease, is a globally distributed zoonotic parasitic infection. In China, it is predominantly endemic in the western pastoral regions, where it imposes a substantial burden on both animal husbandry and public health.1 After adult worms residing in the intestines of definitive hosts (primarily canids) release embryonated eggs that are excreted with feces, accidental ingestion by intermediate hosts—including artiodactyls such as sheep and cattle, or humans who are incidental hosts—triggers the activation of oncospheres within the eggs. These oncospheres then penetrate the intestinal wall, migrate via the portal vein, and colonize parenchymal organs, most commonly the liver (accounting for 75% of cases) and lungs. This colonization process induces the formation of pathognomonic structures and concomitant immune responses in the host.2

Current clinical management of CE is limited by high surgical recurrence rates and notable chemotherapy side effects, making prophylactic vaccination a critical strategy for disease control.2 A previous study reported that infection with Echinococcus elicits a series of cellular and humoral immune responses in the host, most critically involving the regulation and activation of dendritic cells (DCs), as evidenced by the upregulation of costimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86) and the secretion of key cytokines (IL-12, IFN-γ).3 This well-defined immunomodulatory potential of E. granulosus-derived antigens provides a robust foundation for the development of EgG1Y162 as a candidate vaccine antigen. Zhang et al4 divided the EgG1Y162 protein into two fragments based on epitope prediction, naming the corresponding gene segments EgG1Y162-1 (1-53aa) and EgG1Y162-2 (54-118aa). This classification was further supported by animal experiments, which showed that EgG1Y162-2 provides better immunoprotection than EgG1Y162-1. This enhanced protection is likely due to the immunodominant properties of EgG1Y162-2, which contains two of the three dominant T-B combined epitope regions.

In recent years, poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanovaccines have attracted increasing attention owing to their unique advantages and represent a promising strategy for preventing and treating parasitic diseases. This biodegradable polymer has been extensively investigated as a delivery carrier for drugs, proteins, and macromolecules such as DNA and RNA.5 Both humoral and cellular immunity are essential in the host defense against intracellular parasites. Studies have demonstrated that a Th2-type response is linked to the production of the IgG1 antibody subclass, while a Th1-type response is predominantly associated with the IgG2a subclass.6 Previous studies have demonstrated that the encapsulation of Leishmania multi-epitope chimeric protein LeishChim together with the immunostimulatory molecule MPLA in PLGA nanoparticles significantly elevates the levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α cytokines, as well as the proportions of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in mice.7 In another study, the E. granulosus antigen EgFABP1 was encapsulated in PLGA polymers, and oral administration was performed to simulate the gastrointestinal infection microenvironment. The results confirmed that the PLGA/Eudragit nanovaccine could effectively preserve the structural integrity of the EgFABP1 antigen and be efficiently internalized by intestinal M cells and enterocytes.8

To date, various CE vaccine platforms have been reported, including EG95 nanovaccines, ferritin-CpG formulations, and CTLA-4-based multi-epitope designs.9,10 However, none of these approaches have investigated the integration of EgG1Y162 with a lymph node-targeted PLGA delivery carrier. Collectively, these merits highlight the significant potential of PLGA nanocarriers in the development of antiparasitic vaccines. Therefore, this study aimed to fabricate a PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine and systematically evaluate its immunogenicity and protective efficacy, to offer a novel strategy for the immunoprophylaxis of CE.

Materials and Methods

Animals

In this study, BALB/c mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 20 ± 2 g) were utilized. The animals were obtained from the Medical Laboratory Animal Center at Xinjiang Medical University (Xinjiang, China). All animal-related experiments described in this manuscript, including immunization, hepatic portal vein infection, tissue collection and primary dendritic cell isolation, complied with standard laboratory animal welfare standards and were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (Approval No. 20230321-14). All hepatic portal vein injection procedures were carried out under continuous inhalation anesthesia with 2–3% isoflurane (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) mixed with oxygen. Anesthetic depth was monitored via loss of pedal and corneal reflexes during the operation. At the designated experimental time points, mice were deeply anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation; after confirming the absence of withdrawal reflexes, cervical dislocation was performed as a secondary euthanasia procedure.11 All animal procedures were performed in strict accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Cell Lines

The RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cell line (Cat No. CL-0190) and DC2.4 dendritic cell line (Cat No. CL-0545) were purchased from Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Both cell lines were purchased uniformly by members of our research group and shared within the laboratory for subsequent in vitro experiments. All cell-related in vitro assays were covered under the IACUC approval (No. 20230321-14). Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (DCs) were isolated and cultured from the femurs and tibiae of BALB/c mice following an established protocol.12 The use of primary cells from mice was covered under the IACUC approval (No. 20230321-14).

Preparation of PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine

The nanovaccine was fabricated via a double emulsion (W/O/W)-solvent evaporation method. Briefly, 50 mg of PLGA was dissolved in 1 mL of dichloromethane. To this organic solution, 2 mL of a 3% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) aqueous solution was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 min and then sonicated on ice (40 W, 5s on/5s off) for 5 min to form the primary water-in-oil (Inland water phase) emulsion. Subsequently, 4 mL of an aqueous solution of EgG1Y162 protein (1 mg/mL) was added dropwise under vortexing (W/O). The mixture was immediately sonicated on ice under the same conditions for an additional 5 min to form the double (W/O/W) emulsion. Then, 2 mL of 3% (w/v) PVA solution was added, followed by another 6 min of ice-bath sonication using identical pulse parameters. The resulting double emulsion was stirred overnight in a fume hood to allow complete evaporation of the organic solvent. Nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 4000 r/min for 30 min, washed, and resuspended in deionized water. The suspension was frozen at −80 °C for 2 h and then lyophilized for 24 h to obtain the nanovaccine powder, which was stored at 4 °C until use.

Formulation Optimization (PVA Concentration Screening)

The polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) concentration was optimized by assessing its impact on the physicochemical properties of the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine. Four concentrations of PVA (2%, 3%, 4%, and 6% w/v) were evaluated while keeping other parameters constant: PLGA concentration at 50 mg/mL, EgG1Y162 protein amount at 4 mg, and fixed sonication conditions. Each formulation was prepared in triplicate. Key characterization parameters, including particle size, zeta potential, polydispersity index (PDI), and entrapment efficiency (EE), were measured. The optimal PVA concentration was selected based on the best overall performance across these metrics.

Hydrodynamic diameter, zeta potential, and PDI were measured in triplicate using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a nanoparticle analyzer, and the average values were reported.

Morphological Analysis

The morphology and dispersion state of the nanoparticles were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, after gold sputtering) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, after negative staining with phosphotungstic acid) using resuspended lyophilized powder.

In vitro Release Profile

In vitro release studies were performed by dispersing 20 µg of lyophilized nanovaccine in 5 mL of PBS (pH 7.4, containing 0.02% sodium azide) under constant shaking (180 rpm) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (0, 6, 12 hours; 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 days), samples were centrifuged. The protein concentration in the supernatant was measured by the BCA method, and the cumulative release percentage was calculated.

Protein Integrity Assessment

To verify protein stability, encapsulated protein was released from the nanovaccine by demulsification and analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Protein bands were visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining.

Animal Grouping and Immunization Protocol

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5–6 per group) as follows: (1) Control: received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); (2) Free EgG1Y162: administered 25 µg of the antigen per mouse; (3) FCA-adjuvanted EgG1Y162: administered 25 µg of EgG1Y162 emulsified with an equal volume of Freund’s complete adjuvant (FCA); (4) PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine: administered the PLGA-encapsulated formulation containing 25 µg of EgG1Y162. All immunizations were delivered via subcutaneous injection on days 0, 14, 28, and 42 for a total of four doses.

Establishment of Animal Infection Model

Following the last immunization, each group of mice was injected via the hepatic portal vein with 0.2 mL of a saline suspension containing 3000 viable protoscoleces to establish the infection model. Sample collection and subsequent analyses were performed at 16 and 24 weeks post-infection.

qRT-PCR

Spleen or liver tissues from mice were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a powder. Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China) and reverse‑transcribed into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (reaction conditions: 42 °C for 60 min, followed by inactivation at 70 °C for 15 min). The cDNA was stored at −20 °C until use. For quantitative PCR, reactions were performed using ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a real‑time PCR system. The mRNA expression levels of IFN-γ, IL‑4, IL-10, and TNF-α in spleen tissues, as well as the autophagy‑related genes p62 and Atg5 in liver tissues, were measured (Table 1). β-actin was used as the internal reference gene, and relative expression was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method.

HE Staining

For HE staining, tissue sections were first deparaffinized in xylene (twice, 10 min each) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to distilled water. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin for 5–10 min, rinsed in running water, differentiated in acid-alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol) for 30 seconds, and blued in running water for 15 min. Following this, sections were counterstained with eosin for 2–5 min, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a synthetic resin medium.

CCK-8

Cytotoxicity Assay

RAW 264.7 macrophage and DC2.4 cells were seeded separately in 96-well plates. Empty PLGA nanoparticles and PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine were added at various concentrations (62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, CCK-8 reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added to each well, followed by an additional 2 h of incubation. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated accordingly.

Lymphocyte Proliferation Assay

On the 45th day after the initial immunization, spleen lymphocytes were isolated and obtained. The spleen lymphocytes were adjusted to 105 cells per well in the culture medium and cultured in a 96-well plate. The culture plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 hours. Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was added to each well, followed by an additional 2 h of incubation. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the proliferation rate was calculated accordingly.

ELISA

Serum samples were collected from immunized mice on days 14, 28, and 42 post-immunization. Total IgG antibody levels were measured by ELISA. Briefly, microtiter plates were coated with EgG1Y162 protein (2 μg/well) and incubated overnight at 4°C. After blocking with 5% skim milk, serially diluted serum samples were added and incubated. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was added, followed by color development with TMB substrate (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The reaction was stopped with 2 mol/L H2SO4, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence

DC2.4 cells were seeded into confocal dishes. After cell adherence, they were incubated with Cy5.5-labeled PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine and an equal amount of free EgG1Y162 protein (as a control) for 24 hours. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound materials and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, 0.1–0.5% Triton X-100 permeabilization solution (approximately 500 µL/well, Absin Bioscience, Shanghai, China) was added, and cells were permeabilized at room temperature in the dark for 5 minutes. After three 5-minute PBS washes in the dark, diluted fluorescently labeled phalloidin working solution (200 µL/well, Absin Bioscience, Shanghai, China) was added, and cells were incubated in a humidified chamber at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. The staining solution was then discarded, and cells were washed three times with PBS in the dark for 5 minutes each to remove unbound dye. Next, DAPI staining solution (diluted 1:1000 in PBS, Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to counterstain the nuclei for 10 minutes at room temperature in the dark, followed by three 5-minute PBS washes in the dark. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope, and intracellular fluorescence intensity was quantitatively measured using ImageJ software.

Flow Cytometry

Splenocyte preparation and staining: At the end of the immunization protocol (prior to E. granulosus infection), as well as at 16 or 24 weeks post-infection, mice were euthanized and spleens were aseptically harvested. Single-cell suspensions were prepared by mechanical dissociation through a 200-mesh sieve. Erythrocytes were lysed, and the remaining cells were washed and resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Aliquots (100 µL) were stained in the dark for 30 min with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies: anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-PE, and anti-CD8-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend, California, USA). After staining, cells were washed three times and resuspended in 350 µL PBS for analysis.

BMDCs were harvested after 6–7 days of culture, washed, and counted. Cells were then stained in the dark for 30 min with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD80-PE, anti-CD86-CY7, and anti-MHC II-APC (BioLegend, California, USA) antibodies, followed by three washes and resuspension in 350 µL PBS.

All samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer, and the data were processed using FlowJo software.

In vivo Fluorescence Imaging

The PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine, FCA-EgG1Y162 adjuvanted vaccine, and free EgG1Y162 protein were fluorescently labeled with Cy5.5, and the labeled formulations were confirmed to retain stability and bioactivity. SPF-grade BALB/c mice received a subcutaneous injection of 100 µL of the labeled sample, each dose containing 15 µg of Cy5.5. At 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 3 d, 5 d, 7 d, 9 d, and 11 d post‑injection, the mice were placed in the dark chamber of an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system. Mice were anesthetized and placed in the dark chamber. Fluorescence signals (excitation/emission: 675/710 nm) from the injection site and draining lymph nodes were captured. Signal intensity was quantified using LivingImage software.

WB

At 16 and 24 weeks post-infection, mouse liver tissues were collected and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to extract total protein. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Solarbio, Beijing, China) Equal amounts of protein samples (20 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-p62/SQSTM1 (1:1000, Abmart, Shanghai, China), anti-Atg5 (1:1000, Abmart, Shanghai, China), and anti-GAPDH (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated for 2 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:500). Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent (Biosharp, Hefei, China), and band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Transcriptomic Sequencing (RNA‑seq) Analysis

Spleen tissues from mice in each group were mechanically homogenized and passed through a 200‑mesh sieve to obtain a single‑cell suspension. CD4⁺ T cells were isolated from splenocytes using a mouse CD4 (L3T4) MicroBeads kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted from the sorted cells with TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (RIN score) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified samples were then processed for library construction following a standard workflow: poly(A) mRNA was enriched and fragmented with Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by cDNA synthesis with random hexamers and reverse transcriptase. The cDNA underwent end‑repair, adapter ligation, and size selection (~200 bp) before amplification. After quality control, the libraries were subjected to paired‑end sequencing on the DNBSEQ‑T7 platform using cPAS technology, with a required Q30 score of ≥ 85%.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and error bars in all figures represent SD. Intergroup differences were assessed via one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). Statistical significance thresholds were defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Flow cytometry datasets were analyzed with FlowJo software (Version 10, USA).

Results

Formulation Optimization, Physicochemical Properties and Functional Evaluation of PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) concentration was evaluated as a key formulation parameter by monitoring its effects on particle size, zeta potential, PDI, and entrapment efficiency (EE). The results indicated a direct correlation between PVA concentration and particle size, and an inverse correlation with EE. Consequently, 3% PVA was chosen as the optimal concentration for fabricating the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine, which subsequently showed a particle size of 394.4 ± 7.6 nm and an EE of 79.05 ± 0.3% (Table 2 and Figure 1A). The average particle size of the nanoparticles slightly increased over time, from an initial 397.8 nm to 408.9 nm on day 21, with a minimal increment. Meanwhile, neither the polydispersity index (PDI) nor the zeta potential exhibited significant changes. These results indicate that the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanoparticles maintained excellent colloidal dispersion stability at 4°C within 21 days (Figure 1B and D). SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed the integrity of the EgG1Y162 protein within the formulation, indicating no structural damage occurred during encapsulation (Figure 1C). In vitro release studies showed an initial gradual release over the first three days, which plateaued after day seven, demonstrating favorable sustained-release properties (Figure 1E). Morphological examination by scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM/TEM) revealed uniformly spherical particles with a smooth surface (Figure 1F and G).

PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine Enhances Dendritic Cell Uptake and Promotes Their Maturation

The in vitro interactions of the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine were assessed by evaluating its cellular uptake by DC2.4 cells, its cytotoxicity, and its capacity to induce the maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Fluorescence-based uptake assays revealed significantly enhanced internalization of the PLGA-encapsulated formulation by DC2.4 cells compared to free EgG1Y162, which showed minimal fluorescence signal and the lowest uptake efficiency (Figure 2A and B). Cytotoxicity evaluation via the CCK-8 assay indicated that PLGA-EgG1Y162 exhibited no significant toxicity toward DC2.4 cells across the tested concentrations, supporting its biocompatibility (Figure 2C). Furthermore, phenotypic analysis demonstrated that treatment with PLGA-EgG1Y162 markedly upregulated the expression of maturation markers CD80, CD86, and MHC II on BMDCs (Figure 2D–G). Taken together, these data indicate that PLGA-EgG1Y162 is efficiently internalized by antigen-presenting cells, exhibits a favorable safety profile, and effectively promotes dendritic cell maturation.

PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine Demonstrates Favorable Safety and Induces Sustained Humoral and Cellular Immune Responses in Mice

The in vivo safety and immunogenicity of the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine were systematically evaluated by assessing cytokine mRNA expression, histopathological features, cytotoxicity, and antibody levels. On days 28 and 42 post-immunization, the mRNA expression levels of IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, and TNF-α in mouse spleens were measured.

On day 28, compared with the control group, the EgG1Y162-alone group, and the FCA-EgG1Y162 group, PLGA-EgG1Y162 significantly upregulated only the expression of IL-10 (P < 0.05) (Figure 3C), with no significant effects on IFN-γ (Figure 3A), IL-4 (Figure 3B), or TNF-α (Figure 3D) expression. In contrast, by day 42, PLGA-EgG1Y162 significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of all four cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-10, and TNF-α) (P < 0.05) (Figure 3E–H). Histopathological analysis revealed no significant structural alterations, abnormal cellular morphology, or inflammatory cell infiltration in HE-stained sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys across all experimental groups (Figure 3I). Both empty and antigen-loaded PLGA nanoparticles showed no severe toxicity to RAW264.7 macrophages within the tested concentration range. Cell viability remained above 100% at nanoparticle concentrations of 62.5–500 μg/mL. However, when the concentration of antigen-loaded particles increased to 1000 μg/mL, cell viability decreased to 90% (Figure 3J). High levels of total IgG were detected in the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group on days 14, 28, and 42, all of which were significantly higher than those in the EgG1Y162 and FCA-EgG1Y162 groups (P < 0.01). The FCA-EgG1Y162 group induced relatively high total IgG levels on days 14 and 28, but these declined significantly after peaking on day 28. In contrast, the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group maintained high antibody levels even on day 42, demonstrating sustained immunogenicity (Figure 3K–M).

PLGA-EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine Exhibits Prolonged Retention and Lymph Node Targeting in vivo

To evaluate the safety of the nanoformulation, hematological tests were performed on mice after the completion of immunization. The results showed that neither the EgG1Y162 antigen alone nor its PLGA-EgG1Y162 formulation induced significant changes in hematological parameters compared with the control group. In contrast, the FCA-EgG1Y162 group exhibited significantly elevated levels of AST and ALT relative to the control (Figure 4A and B), suggesting a potential risk of hepatotoxicity. On the 45th day after the initial immunization, spleen lymphocytes were prepared from different groups and their proliferation responses were analyzed. Compared with the blank group, mice inoculated with PLGA-EgG1Y162 exhibited a significant proliferation response (Figure 4C). We first illustrated the dosing regimen and detection time points for the three formulations (EgG1Y162, FCA-EgG1Y162, and PLGA-EgG1Y162) in a schematic diagram (Figure 4D).

In vivo fluorescence imaging was used to evaluate the biodistribution and delivery kinetics of the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine in mice. Initially (0–12 h post-injection), strong fluorescence at the injection site indicated effective local retention. By 24 h, the signal had shifted and intensified markedly within the draining lymph nodes, where it persisted until at least day 5, confirming targeted lymph node accumulation (Figure 4E and H). In contrast, free EgG1Y162 protein diffused rapidly from the injection site and showed minimal lymph node signal. Notably, the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group maintained a significantly higher lymph node fluorescence intensity than the FCA-EgG1Y162 adjuvant group at day 9, underscoring its sustained-release profile (Figure 4F and G).

PLGA‑EgG1Y162 Nanovaccine Regulates Hepatic Autophagy-Related Gene and Protein Expression

To investigate the regulatory effect of the PLGA‑EgG1Y162 nanovaccine on the hepatic autophagy pathway, we analyzed the expression changes of autophagy-related genes and proteins in mouse liver tissues at 16 and 24 weeks post-infection using transcriptomic heatmap analysis and targeted molecular detection.

Transcriptomic heatmap analysis (Figure 7G and H) revealed significant clustering differences in autophagy-related genes between the model group and the PLGA‑EgG1Y162 group. At 16 weeks after infection (Figure 7G), the core autophagy-related genes such as Atg5, Atg10, Atg9b, Atg12, Gimap7, ATP6V0E, and Atg16l2 were all upregulated in the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group (indicated by red enrichment); conversely, the expression of these genes in the model group was significantly inhibited. It is noteworthy that the key autophagy markers Map1lc3a (encoding LC3 protein) and p62 (encoded by the Sqstm1 gene) were all downregulated in the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group, while their expression increased in the model group. In combination with the upregulation of the upstream Atg genes, this result suggests that the treatment with the nanovaccine may have promoted the smooth flow of the autophagy pathway and accelerated the degradation of substrate proteins; while the initiation of autophagy in the model group may have been inhibited, resulting in substrate accumulation. At 24 weeks after infection (Figure 7H), this regulatory trend still persisted: in the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group, the expressions of genes such as Mfge8, Atg7, Pmaip1, Gadd45g, Alox5, Klf4, and Gadd45b were upregulated, while they showed a decreasing trend in the model group. These findings indicate that the nanovaccine has a persistent regulatory effect on the autophagy pathway.

At 4 months post infection, a total of 1641 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the model group compared with the PLGA‑EgG1Y162 group, including 1151 u repository.pregulated genes and 490 downregulated genes (Figure 8A). GO enrichment analysis of the upregulated DEGs in the model group revealed significant enrichment in adaptive immunity related functions, such as antigen binding, MHC class I/II receptor activity, immunoglobulin complex formation, and B cell mediated immunity (Figure 8B). By 6 months post-infection, the transcriptional differences between the two groups were markedly reduced, with only 55 DEGs identified (1 upregulated, 54 downregulated) (Figure 8D). These genes were mainly enriched in pathways associated with cytokine/chemokine activity, extracellular space, and positive regulation of apoptosis (Figure 8E), suggesting that persistent inflammatory and cellular stress signals existed in the infected model group, whereas PLGA‑EgG1Y162 group administration alleviated such pathological processes. Discussion

Cystic echinococcosis (CE), a zoonotic disease caused by E. granulosus, is predominantly endemic in western regions of China.13,14 The typical clinical manifestation of CE is hepatic cyst formation. The life cycle within the host begins with the oral ingestion of parasite eggs, which hatch into oncospheres in the stomach or duodenum. These larvae then penetrate the intestinal wall, enter the portal circulation, and migrate to the liver, where they eventually develop into hydatid cysts.15,16 In severe cases, these cysts may become infected or rupture, triggering hypersensitivity reactions. Current clinical management of cystic echinococcosis primarily relies on surgery and chemotherapy, both of which carry substantial side effects. Thus, prophylactic vaccination is considered a pivotal strategy for disease control. PLGA, recognized for its superior biocompatibility and biodegradability, is an FDA- and EMA-approved material for nanoparticle fabrication.17,18 In this study, we developed and optimized a PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine. The vaccine demonstrated stable physicochemical properties, excellent biocompatibility in vitro and in vivo, and the capacity to induce durable humoral and cellular immunity. By facilitating lymph node-targeted delivery and enhanced antigen presentation, it conferred significant protection against E. granulosus infection in a murine model.

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is a water-soluble polymer valued for its biocompatibility and low irritancy.19 Meanwhile, PLGA, a biodegradable and clinically approved polymer, offers excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and strong encapsulation and film‑forming properties.20,21 In this study, formulation screening led to an optimized PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine prepared with 3% PVA, which exhibited favorable physicochemical properties: a suitable hydrodynamic diameter (394.4 ± 7.6 nm), high encapsulation efficiency (79.05 ± 0.3%), and uniform spherical morphology with good colloidal stability. More importantly, the nanovaccine demonstrated typical sustained-release characteristics: in vitro release studies revealed gradual antigen release over the initial 3 days, with the release plateauing after day 7. In vivo imaging further confirmed that, compared with free antigen and FCA-adjuvanted vaccine, PLGA-EgG1Y162 exhibited significantly prolonged retention at the injection site, specific accumulation in draining lymph nodes, and detectable fluorescence signals up to day 9. Meanwhile, SDS-PAGE and protein stability assays verified that the encapsulation process did not compromise the structural integrity of the EgG1Y162 protein, thereby preserving its immunogenicity. In vitro cellular experiments further showed that the nanovaccine significantly enhanced dendritic cell uptake, exerted no obvious cytotoxicity on BMDCs, and upregulated the expression of maturation markers (CD80, CD86, MHC II). Consistent with our findings, previous studies have confirmed that PLGA-encapsulated antigens can markedly upregulate the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC class II molecules on the surface of BMDCs, thereby promoting dendritic cell maturation and enabling efficient antigen presentation.3,22 These results collectively indicate that the PLGA nano-delivery system not only protects the antigen but also actively facilitates its recognition and processing by innate immune cells, thereby initiating subsequent adaptive immune responses. In direct comparison with free EgG1Y162 and FCA-adjuvanted vaccine, our lymph node-targeted PLGA nanovaccine retains antigen longer and boosts dendritic cell activation more effectively. Distinct from existing EG95 PLGA vaccines relying on basic antigen encapsulation, our construct combines immunodominant EgG1Y162 with lymph-node-homing carriers to sustain antigen release and durable immune memory. This translated into significantly reduced hepatic cyst burdens and preserved liver function at both 16 and 24 weeks post-infection, whereas the protective effects of free antigen and FCA-adjuvanted formulations waned over time.

Following challenge with E. granulosus, specific IgG responses against hydatid fluid and protoscolex antigens emerge as early as 2 weeks in mice and 11 weeks in sheep, respectively, with these antibodies playing a dominant role in parasite clearance.23 In terms of humoral immunity, the nanovaccine induced significantly higher total IgG levels on days 14, 28, and 42 compared to both the free antigen group and the FCA-adjuvanted group. Notably, while IgG titers in the FCA-adjuvanted group peaked at day 28 and then declined sharply, the PLGA‑EgG1Y162 group maintained high antibody levels even at day 42, highlighting its unique ability to elicit long-lasting humoral immunity. Regarding cellular immunity, flow cytometric analysis of splenic T lymphocyte subsets revealed that PLGA-EgG1Y162 significantly increased the proportions of both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, an effect that remained stable up to 24 weeks post-infection. EgG1Y162 itself exhibits strong antigenicity and immunogenicity, capable of promoting lymphocyte proliferation and cytokine secretion, whereas the PLGA carrier can induce memory CD8⁺ T cell responses,24 thereby providing key support for the long-term maintenance of cellular immunity. At day 28 post-immunization, the vaccine primarily upregulated the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. By day 42, it significantly enhanced the expression of IL-4, IFN-γ, IL-10, and TNF-α. This cytokine profile aligns well with the inherent immunostimulatory activity of EgG1Y162, which is known to trigger the release of multiple cytokines including IFN-γ and IL-4,25 further supporting the synergistic role of antigen and carrier in modulating immune responses.

CD4⁺ T cells, as master regulators of adaptive immunity, exhibit transcriptional signatures that faithfully reflect vaccine-induced immune homeostasis and mechanisms of anti-infective protection.26,27 To further elucidate the immunomodulatory properties of the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine at the transcriptomic level, we performed RNA sequencing on splenic CD4⁺ T cells isolated from mice. At 4 months post-infection, 1151 genes were significantly upregulated in the model group compared to the vaccinated group. These differentially expressed genes were prominently enriched in adaptive immunity-related pathways, including antigen binding, MHC molecule activity, and B cell-mediated immune responses. By 6 months post-infection, the transcriptional disparity between the two groups had markedly narrowed, with only 55 differentially expressed genes identified. The model group continued to exhibit enrichment in inflammatory stress pathways, such as cytokine activity and positive regulation of apoptosis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the nanovaccine not only elicits durable adaptive immunity but also effectively curbs infection-driven chronic inflammation and excessive immune activation, thereby preserving immune homeostasis. Notably, transcriptional remodeling of splenic CD4⁺ T cells directly supports vaccine-mediated anti-parasitic protection. Vaccinated mice exhibited suppressed pro-inflammatory transcription accompanied by balanced Th1/Th2 cytokines and persistent CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell expansion, jointly limiting cyst growth and liver injury. This observation agrees with recent nanovaccine research demonstrating that nanoparticle-carried antigens program protective T cell transcriptional landscapes for sustained anti-pathogen immunity.28,29 Moreover, the restored hepatic autophagy signature represents a direct regulatory effect of PLGA-EgG1Y162 vaccination rather than a passive consequence of relieved infection. Mechanistically, the nanovaccine likely modulates innate-autophagy crosstalk through sustained antigen presentation and moderate T cell activation.

The ultimate objective of this study was to evaluate the in vivo protective efficacy and underlying mechanisms of the nanovaccine. In echinococcosis research, hepatic parasite burden, histopathological alterations, and liver function parameters are routinely employed as core indicators for evaluating the in vivo efficacy of vaccines or therapeutics.30–32 Zhang et al33 employed the significant reduction of hepatic parasitic load, amelioration of histopathological lesions, and restoration of liver function parameters as key efficacy criteria in their evaluation of orally administered PLGA nanoparticles against alveolar echinococcosis. In the present study, vaccination with PLGA-EgG1Y162 led to a remarkable decrease in both the number of hepatic cysts and the liver-to-body weight ratio in infected mice. Furthermore, histopathological analysis confirmed effective suppression of cystic lesion progression and maintenance of normal hepatic architecture, accompanied by a significant reduction in serum AST and ALT levels. Together, these three lines of evidence robustly demonstrate the potent anti-infective protection conferred by the vaccine.

Autophagy plays a crucial role in the host cell-mediated clearance of parasites, while parasites have evolved mechanisms to evade such elimination and may even exploit autophagy for their own energy supply.34,35 Transcriptomic heatmap analysis in this study confirmed at the genome-wide level that Echinococcus granulosus infection extensively activates hepatic autophagy-related genes, including core autophagy regulatory genes such as Atg5, Atg10, Atg12, and Atg16l2, as well as inflammation-related genes including Tlr9. This expression pattern was further verified at the mRNA and protein levels of key molecules including p62 and ATG5. Notably, the PLGA-EgG1Y162 nanovaccine persistently and significantly reversed such infection-induced abnormal expression and exerted a long-lasting regulatory effect on the autophagy pathway. Meanwhile, transcriptomic analysis of splenic CD4⁺ T cells further revealed that at 4 months post-infection, the PLGA-EgG1Y162 group displayed a controlled adaptive immune profile, whereas the model group maintained high expression of inflammation- and stress-related genes. At 6 months post-infection, the model group remained enriched in pathways including cytokine/chemokine activity and positive regulation of apoptosis, indicating a persistent inflammatory microenvironment, whereas these pathological processes were markedly alleviated in the vaccinated group. As an antigen delivery system, PLGA nanoparticles have demonstrated potent immunoprotective efficacy in various parasitic infection models, including Toxoplasma gondii36,37 and Leishmania spp.,38 where they induce mixed Th1/Th2 immune responses and significantly reduce target organ parasite burden.

Conclusion

We have successfully developed an EgG1Y162-based vaccine utilizing a PLGA nano-delivery platform. This vaccine exhibits favorable physicochemical properties and a strong safety profile. By combining lymph node-targeted delivery with sustained antigen release, it synergistically elicits durable and balanced humoral and cellular immune responses, conferring significant protection against E. granulosus infection. These findings provide a promising vaccine candidate for the prevention and control of cystic echinococcosis. From a One Health perspective, this nanovaccine could be applied to susceptible livestock (sheep and cattle) in endemic areas, and integrated with dog deworming and public health surveillance to interrupt the parasite life cycle.
