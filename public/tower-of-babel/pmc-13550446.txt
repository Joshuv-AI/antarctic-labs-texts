Fetal programming is associated with candidate lipidomic signatures in skeletal muscle of Nellore beef cattle offspring
Authors: Felipe Eguti de Carvalho, Guilherme Henrique Gebim Polizel, Artur Oliveira Rocha, Daniel Silva Antonelo, Arícia Christofaro Fernandes, Édison Furlan, Luiz F Brito, José Bento Sterman Ferraz, Miguel Henrique de Almeida Santana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal nutrition during gestation can shape offspring skeletal muscle development, but its long-term effects on the beef cattle muscle lipidome remain poorly understood. This study investigated whether gestational protein-energy supplementation is associated with lipidomic signatures in the Longissimus thoracis muscle of Nellore offspring. Targeted multiple reaction monitoring-based lipidomics was applied to 14 samples, including 7 non-programmed (NP) and 7 fetal-programmed (FP) animals. Of 3,437 multiple reaction monitoring transitions evaluated, 408 lipid features were retained for downstream analyses. Lipidomic profiles were explored using multivariate analyses, univariate filtering, pathway enrichment, triacylglycerol chain-length and unsaturation summaries, and correlations with meat-quality traits. Gestational supplementation was associated with subtle but detectable shifts in the skeletal muscle lipidome. Ten lipid features met nominal p-value and fold-change criteria, although none remained significant after false-discovery-rate correction. These features involved membrane-related and bioactive lipid classes, including phosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylethanolamines, sphingomyelins, ceramides, diacylglycerols, phosphatidylglycerols, and selected triacylglycerols, whereas triacylglycerol chain-length and unsaturation distributions remained stable. Overall, this study highlights lipidomics as a promising approach to identify candidate molecular signatures of fetal programming in beef cattle, requiring validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63063-4.Keywords: Lipidomics, Meat quality, Prenatal nutrition, Postmortem, Oxidative stabilitySubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Physiology

Introduction

Maternal nutrition during critical windows of developmental plasticity can exert lasting effects on skeletal muscle growth and maturation, hallmarks of fetal programming (FP) within the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework1,2. In ruminants, such early-life cues influence myofiber number and type, mitochondrial biogenesis, intramuscular adipogenesis, connective-tissue deposition, and postnatal growth, with downstream consequences for carcass composition and meat-quality traits3–5. Yet, the molecular mediators through which FP is retained in skeletal muscle remain unknown resolved. Therefore, defining the skeletal muscle lipidomic signature associated with FP can contribute to elucidating bioenergetic mechanisms and prediction of meat quality attributes. By identifying lipid classes and metabolic pathways influenced by FP, targeted nutritional interventions and more informed management decisions can then be applied in the beef production sector3,4.

Within FP, membrane lipids can modulate bilayer architecture and biophysics with direct repercussions for mitochondrial organization6,7. Bioactive lipid-derived molecules, such as diacylglycerols (DG) and ceramides (CER), also tune central kinases (AKT and PKC), linking membrane remodeling to metabolic signaling8,9. Together, these properties position lipids as integrative sensors of FP across nutritional levels. Accordingly, prenatal-nutrition effects could be captured by lipidomic analyses, with FP leaving specific signatures in membrane classes and bioactive species that could serve as sensitive readouts of nutritional status10,11.

These mechanisms suggest that subtle alterations in membranes, mitochondria, and bioactive lipids may influence skeletal muscle physiology and postmortem stability12. Moreover, FP effects are often subtle and distributed across multiple lipid species and classes rather than concentrated in a few large changes, requiring rigorous standardization, multiple-testing correction, and ontology-based interpretation13–15. In Nellore cattle (Bos taurus indicus), comparisons across different levels of FP revealed overlapping principal component analysis (PCA) clusters and a limited number of differentially expressed genes, indicating that treatment effects were small16. Additionally, during early gestation, maternal hepatic lipid classes have been reported to exhibit robust alterations whereas FP related effects in the fetus were minimal, supporting the notion that fetal signatures are often small-to-moderate and difficult to detect in isolation10.

As FP is expected to induce subtle lipidomic changes spread across multiple lipid species and classes, approaches that jointly evaluate lipid species, lipid classes, and their potential biological relevance may be useful for detecting coordinated multivariate FP-related signatures under different nutritional conditions14,15,17,18. Likewise, early gestational maternal nutrition in cattle can remodel tissue-specific windows of sensitivity in adipose tissue10,19. We hypothesized that gestational nutrition associated with FP would be associated with detectable differences in the skeletal muscle lipidomic profile at 24 h postmortem, particularly involving membrane-related and bioactive lipid classes, without necessarily producing broad changes in neutral-lipid composition. To test this, we (i) profiled the Longissimus thoracis muscle lipidome using a targeted lipidomic approach based on discovery-scale multiple reaction monitoring (MRM); (ii) compared NP and FP samples at the global, class, and lipid-feature levels; (iii) evaluated the PC ratio as an exploratory lipid index; (iv) summarized the chain-length and unsaturation distributions of TG species; and (v) identified candidate lipid features for future targeted validation.

Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the skeletal muscle lipidomic profile of Nellore offspring exposed to different gestational nutritional regimens and to identify candidate lipid features associated with fetal programming. Because direct measurements of mitochondrial activity, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and lipid metabolic flux were not performed, the lipidomic findings were interpreted as exploratory signatures and hypothesis-generating associations rather than direct evidence of functional alterations.

Methods

The present study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Animal Science and Food Engineering, University of São Paulo (FZEA-USP; Pirassununga, SP, Brazil; protocol number: 1843241117) and adhered to the guidelines of the National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA; Brasilia, Distrito Federal, Brazil).

Animals and experimental design

A total of 28 young Nellore bulls, born to nulliparous heifers that underwent fixed-time artificial insemination using sex-sorted semen from a single Nellore sire, were produced under two gestational nutritional treatments. Heifers were randomly assigned to treatments after stratification by body weight (BW) and body condition score (BCS). Treatments consisted of either mineral supplementation (0.3 g/kg BW; control group, abbreviated as NP; n = 14) or protein–energy supplementation (5 g/kg BW; supplemented group, abbreviated as FP; n = 14). Although the original prenatal nutrition experiment included 28 offspring, the targeted lipidomics analysis was performed using a subset of Longissimus thoracis samples from 14 animals, with 7 samples per treatment group (NP, n = 7; FP, n = 7). Therefore, all lipidomic analyses, multivariate analyses, differential lipid abundance tests, pathway analyses, and lipid–phenotype correlations reported in this study refer to this lipidomic subset.

Throughout gestation, heifers were managed in treatment-specific paddocks with Urochloa brizantha cv. Marandu pasture (Table 1) and received the assigned mineral or protein–energy supplement once daily at 1 p.m. (UTC − 3 BRT; Table 2).

Postnatal management

After parturition, cows and their offspring (regardless of the prenatal nutrition group), were managed together on a single Marandu pasture (Urochloa brizantha cv. Marandu) with ad libitum access to a mineral supplement until weaning at 8 months of age. Following weaning, calves from both groups were raised on pasture for 12 months and supplemented during the dry season with an energy concentrate [dry matter (DM) basis: 30.19% crude protein (CP); 65.80% total digestible nutrients (TDN) at 3 g/kg BW], and during the rainy season with a protein supplement (DM basis: 30.04% CP; 56.80% TDN) at 1 g/kg BW.

The finishing phase was conducted in a feedlot, with the bulls housed for 97 days in individual pens measuring 12 m². Prior to this, the animals were weighed after a 24-hour fasting period and then underwent a 15-day adaptation period to the facilities and diet, after which they were fully adapted to the final diet, consisting of corn silage and concentrate in a 30:70 ratio (13.50% DM CP and 75.40% DM TDN; Table 3).

Slaughter and sample collection

After 97 days of feedlot finishing, bulls were slaughtered at the Campus Fernando Costa Slaughterhouse (Pirassununga, SP, Brazil), located approximately 500 m from the feedlot. At slaughter, NP group had a final body weight of 564.6 ± 58.8 kg and an average daily gain of 1.80 ± 0.32 kg/day, whereas FP group had a final body weight of 568.3 ± 54.7 kg and an average daily gain of 1.72 ± 0.26 kg/day. Before entering the slaughter line, animals were rendered unconscious by mechanical stunning using a pneumatic captive-bolt stunner and no chemical anesthetic agent was administered. Stunning was followed immediately by jugular vein incision for exsanguination, in accordance with humane slaughter practices established by the Brazilian Regulation for the Industrial and Sanitary Inspection of Products of Animal Origin.

Slaughter order was defined based on subcutaneous fat thickness (SFT), assessed by carcass ultrasonography between the 12th and 13th ribs using an Aloka SSD-500 machine with a 17.2 cm linear transducer (3.5 MHz) and vegetable oil as the coupling medium. The scans were performed by a certified technician following the Beef Industry Federation (BIF) guidelines. Images were processed using Lince® software. Animals were slaughtered after a 16-hour fasting period, and carcasses were chilled at 0–2 °C for 24 h before sampling. The Longissimus thoracis et lumborum was collected for meat-quality analyses and standardized into steaks.

Meat-quality phenotyping

The meat-quality traits included in this study have been described in detail by Fernandes et al.20. In the present study, these variables were used to complement the lipidomic characterization, to compare phenotypic responses between NP and FP animals, and to explore biological associations between lipid markers and meat-quality outcomes. Muscle pH was measured at 1 h and 24 h postmortem using a portable pH meter equipped with a penetration electrode, following the procedures described by Fernandes et al.20. These pH measurements were included in the phenotypic dataset used for direct NP–FP comparisons and exploratory lipid–phenotype association analyses.

Maturation, cooking loss, and warner–bratzler shear force

After collecting the shelf-life samples, while maintaining the craniocaudal orientation, three additional 2.5-cm-thick steaks were collected from the sirloin steak, individually identified, vacuum-packaged, and aged for 0, 7, or 14 days, with one steak assigned to each aging period. After the maturation times in a refrigerated chamber (2–5 °C), the samples were removed from the vacuum packages and exposed to the environment for 30 min to allow for blooming. Then, an objective evaluation of meet color and pH was carried out according to the CIELab system21. This methodology was applied for the three maturation times at 0, 7, and 14 days.

After the color and pH analyses, the samples were weighed on a precision scale and cooked at 170 °C in an industrial electric oven (Model F130/L; Golden Arrow Electric Ovens Industry and Commerce Ltd., São Paulo, SP, Brazil) equipped with a thermostat. The internal temperature of the steaks was monitored using individual thermometers (Inkbird Tech C.L., Model IBT-6XS, Smart BBQ thermometer, Shenzhen, China). Upon reaching an internal temperature of 40 °C, the samples were turned over and remained in the oven until they reached an internal temperature of 71 °C, as recommended by the American Meat Science Association22. The samples remained at room temperature until cooling at ± 25 °C, when they were weighed again to determine cooking loss (CL).

Subsequently, the samples were wrapped in plastic film and refrigerated (4–6 °C) for 24 h to start the Warner–Bratzler shear force (WBSF) methodology23. Afterward, six cores (1.27 cm in diameter) were obtained from each sample, parallel to the muscle fibers, for WBSF determination using a TMS-PRO texture analyzer (Food Technology Corporation, Sterling, VA, USA) fitted with a WBSF blade at a crosshead speed of 200 mm/min22. The WBSF of each sample was considered as the average of the six repetitions.

Lipidome analyses

Lipid extraction

Approximately 50 mg (± 1 mg) of each Longissimus thoracis sample from the lipidomic subset (NP, n = 7; FP, n = 7) were weighed into 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tubes and cryo-ground in liquid nitrogen before lipid extraction using a Bligh and Dyer-based protocol24. Briefly, 150 µL of ultrapure water was added to the macerated tissue in polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes and the mixtures were vortexed vigorously to promote cell lysis. Then, 250 µL of chloroform and 500 µL of methanol were added and vortexed for 15 s. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 15 min, followed by the addition of 250 µL of chloroform and 250 µL of ultrapure water and centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min to form a biphasic system, with lipids partitioning into the lower (organic) phase. The organic phase was transferred to a new tube, dried in a centrifugal vacuum concentrator (Genevac™ miVac, Genevac Ltd., Ipswich, UK) and stored at − 80 °C until analyses.

Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM)-profiling

Targeted lipid profiling was performed using discovery MRM-profiling strategies and instrumentation as reviewed by Xie et al.25. Dried lipid extracts were diluted in 50 µL of methanol/chloroform (3:1, v/v) and 250 µL of injection solvent (acetonitrile/methanol/300 mM ammonium acetate, 3:6.65:0.35, v/v/v) to prepare a stock solution. Data was acquired by flow-injection (no chromatographic separation) from 8 µL of stock solution, diluted 300-fold in injection solvent that was spiked with EquiSPLASH™ LIPIDOMIX®Quantitative Mass Spec Internal Standard (0.1 ng/µL of each of the internal standards) prior to delivery to the electrospray ionization (ESI) source of an Agilent 6410 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) via a micro autosampler (G1377A). A capillary pump was coupled to the autosampler and operated at 10 µL min⁻¹ (150 bar), within the microflow operating range for Agilent capillary systems. Instrument settings included a capillary voltage of 4 kV and a drying-gas flow of 5.1 L min⁻¹ at 300 °C (Agilent 6400-series QqQ platform documentation). Additional mass spectrometry acquisition parameters, including ionization mode, MRM transitions, precursor and product ions, fragmentor voltage, collision energy, dwell time, and cell accelerator voltage, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Moreover, quality control (QC) samples (EquiSPLASH™ lipidomix quality control mixture) were analyzed periodically throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument performance and signal stability. Solvent blank and wash injections were included to reduce background contamination and minimize carryover between injections.

The MRM-profiling method was used to interrogate 3,437 MRMs spanning 12 lipid classes. The transition set comprised 1,752 triacylglycerols (TG), 482 diacylglycerols (DG), 179 free fatty acids (FFA), 178 phosphatidylcholines (PC), 145 phosphatidylethanolamines (PE), 141 phosphatidylinositols (PI), 141 phosphatidylglycerols (PG), 139 phosphatidylserines (PS), 120 ceramides (CER), 84 acylcarnitines (CAR), 42 sphingomyelins (SM), and 34 cholesteryl esters (CE). The TGs were monitored using precursor ions and product ions diagnostic of specific fatty-acyl residues (FA16:0, FA16:1, FA18:0, FA18:1, FA18:2, and FA20:4), whereas the remaining lipid classes were monitored using class-specific MRM transitions from the reference library. Raw ion-intensity data for each MRM in each sample were exported using in-house scripts. To reduce background noise, only MRMs with signal intensity at least 1.3-fold higher than that of the corresponding transition in the injection-solvent blank were retained for analyses. Although no specific experiments were performed to evaluate potential leaching of plastic-derived contaminants, the blank-filtering strategy minimized the contribution of background signals to the final dataset. MRMs meeting this threshold were considered detectable. For each retained MRM, relative ion intensity was calculated by dividing its signal by the total summed intensity of all retained MRMs within the same sample (sum normalization). Similar to other relative normalization approaches, this method does not correct for differences in ionization efficiency among lipid classes. Lipid-class distributions, as shown in Fig. 1, were summarized as feature counts per class among the retained MRM features and were not corrected for lipid-class-specific ionization efficiency. Therefore, these distributions should not be interpreted as absolute lipid-class abundances. These normalized values were used in downstream statistical and bioinformatic analyses. Further procedural details on lipid extraction and MRM profiling are described by Antonelo et al.26. Lipid annotations were assigned according to the reference MRM transition library and are reported at the highest level of structural confidence supported by the MRM workflow (Table S1). Since this approach does not provide complete structural characterization, annotations regarding ether bond type (O-/P-), double-bond position or geometry, sphingoid base composition, and the complete molecular composition of some lipid species should be regarded as putative.

Statistical and bioinformatics analyses

Lipidome data were uploaded to MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca/)14,27 and were sum-normalized and Pareto-scaled prior to statistical and bioinformatics analyses. Volcano plot analysis was performed to identify lipid features showing treatment-related differences based on combined fold-change and nominal significance criteria. Lipid features with a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.66 and nominal P-value ≤ 0.05 were classified as nominal differentially abundant lipids (DALs) between treatments at 24 h postmortem. FDR-adjusted P-values were also evaluated to account for multiple testing, and DALs not retained after FDR correction were interpreted as exploratory candidate features for biological interpretation and future targeted validation. In addition, treatment effects on TG distribution were evaluated using three grouping strategies for Student’s t-test: (1) total number of carbons [e.g., TG (n = 54)]; (2) total degree of unsaturation, irrespective of carbon number [e.g., TG species with four total double bonds]; and (3) degree of unsaturation grouped into low-unsaturation (up to 2 double bonds) and high-unsaturation (3 or more double bonds) TGs. Differences in TG distribution were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Unsupervised PCA and supervised partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed to investigate group-wise clustering patterns. The statistical significance of group separation in the PCA was evaluated using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. The PLS-DA model was validated by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), and R² (cumulative explained variance), Q² (predictive ability), and classification accuracy were used as initial indicators of model performance. In the PLS-DA, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were used to rank lipids according to their contribution to group discrimination. Lipids with VIP > 1.0, indicating the greatest contribution to model separation and classification between treatments, were highlighted.

Direct comparisons of carcass and meat-quality traits between NP and FP animals were performed to distinguish treatment-level phenotypic differences from exploratory lipid–phenotype association analyses. Because only two prenatal treatment groups were evaluated in the lipidomic subset, each phenotype was analyzed using a linear model with treatment as the fixed effect, and NP and FP means were compared using Student’s t-test. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation for each treatment group, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Associations between VIP-selected lipids and meat quality traits were evaluated using within-group Pearson correlations, conducted separately for NP and FP groups. Lipidomic samples were matched to individual animal IDs, treatment groups, and meat quality phenotypes. For each group, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and p-values were calculated for each lipid–phenotype pair using pairwise complete observations. Lipid–phenotype pairs with fewer than three valid observations (n < 3) were not tested. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, and only significant correlations were considered in the subsequent interpretation of lipid–phenotype associations.

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using RaMP-DB28, an integrated relational database for metabolomics pathway analysis that incorporates pathway annotations from HMDB29, Reactome30, WikiPathways31, and KEGG32. To maintain consistency with the definition of DALs used above, the enrichment analysis was based on DALs identified by volcano analysis (FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.66 and P ≤ 0.05) together with lipids with VIP > 1.0 in the PLS-DA. Enriched pathways associated with FP were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

The targeted lipidomics workflow initially evaluated 3,437 MRM transitions. After filtering against the injection-solvent blank, 408 lipid features were retained for downstream analyses in the lipidomic subset (NP, n = 7; FP, n = 7). These retained features were mainly represented by TG, PC, PE, and SM lipid classes. The lipidomic data were then evaluated at three complementary levels: global multivariate structure, individual lipid features, and TG chain-length/unsaturation distributions (a complete list of the retained lipids is provided in Table S1).

Based on feature counts, the 408 retained MRM features were assigned mainly to TG (146), PC (102), PE (43), and SM (34) lipid classes (Fig. 1). The statistical comparison of the Longissimus thoracis lipidomic profiles highlighted 10 lipid features meeting the nominal p-value and fold-change filtering criteria, of which nine showed higher relative abundance in NP animals and one showed higher relative abundance in FP animals (Fig. 2; Tables 4, 5). Although these features did not remain significant after FDR correction, they represent a focused set of candidate lipids contributing to the NP–FP lipidomic contrast and were therefore interpreted as exploratory features for biological interpretation and future targeted validation. Moreover, there were no differences in TG carbon chain length or degree of unsaturation between NP and FP animals (Table S2).

Discussion

In the present study, gestational protein-energy supplementation was associated with a focused set of lipidomic differences in skeletal muscle, involving membrane-related and bioactive lipid classes. These lipid features met the predefined fold-change and nominal significance criteria and showed a consistent treatment-related pattern, with most features showing higher relative abundance in NP animals and one sphingomyelin feature showing higher relative abundance in FP animals. Although these features did not remain significant after FDR correction, their biological coherence and contribution to the NP–FP lipidomic contrast support their interpretation as candidate lipid signatures for future targeted validation. Importantly, these data do not directly demonstrate changes in mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, or lipid metabolic flux; therefore, the biological interpretation of these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Maternal nutrition during critical stages of gestation has been shown to influence epigenetic regulation and expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, mitochondrial activity, and myogenesis4,33. Such early-life programming can lead to long-term modifications in postnatal metabolism, tissue composition, and muscle lipid architecture34,35. Therefore, the lower relative abundance of specific PC/PE features in FP animals may be considered a candidate lipidomic pattern potentially related to postmortem lipid stability, but direct measurements of oxidative stability and sensory deterioration were not performed in the present study39,40.

This interpretation is supported by studies showing that improved maternal nutrition enhances fetal antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial development, which may help mitigate oxidative stress in skeletal muscle later in life4,41. Within this framework, the reduced levels of DGs and TGs observed in FP animals are more appropriately interpreted as reflecting differences in the in vivo metabolic state of skeletal muscle that were carried into the early postmortem period, rather than active de novo lipogenesis at 24 h postmortem. In this context, lower DG and TG relative abundances in FP animals may be compatible with differences in lipid partitioning, although β-oxidation and energy metabolism were not directly measured, together with differential remodeling of membrane lipids during the transition from the living muscle state to postmortem metabolism26,42,43. Rather than suggesting lipotoxicity or insulin resistance, these differences indicate that fetal programming altered how lipids were partitioned and used for storage, signaling, and membrane-related functions, whereas NP animals more closely followed the baseline postmortem metabolic trajectory26,42,43.

From a meat quality standpoint, these shifts in neutral lipids may have implications for sensory traits, although this interpretation should be made cautiously because classical carcass and meat-quality phenotypes (e.g., intramuscular fat, tenderness, juiciness, and sensory scores) were not directly measured in the present study. In cattle, intramuscular fat is consistently associated with greater perceived juiciness and flavor intensity44,45, and neutral-lipid pools (including TG) are the primary chemical reservoir for marbling-related lipid deposition. Therefore, the lower DG and TG abundances in FP animals may be compatible with a leaner intramuscular lipid phenotype46. Importantly, the absence of differences in TG chain length and degree of unsaturation between treatments suggests that the main contrast was not driven by broad changes in neutral-lipid composition, but rather by differences in lipid pool size, turnover, and partitioning. In this context, the lower DG and TG abundances in FP animals may also reflect differential use of fatty acids for oxidative metabolism and membrane-lipid remodeling, indicating that fetal programming altered how lipids were mobilized between storage, signaling, and structural functions47–49.

The concurrent reduction in CER(d18:1/24:0) and SM(d17:1/26:1) in FP animals, relative to NP, is consistent with lower activation of inflammatory or apoptotic lipid signaling, given that increased ceramide abundance is commonly associated with metabolic dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment, whereas reduced ceramide levels may indicate attenuation of pro-inflammatory pathways50. Conversely, SM(d16:1/22:0) emerged as the only sphingomyelin feature with higher relative abundance in FP animals, making it a relevant candidate contributor to the NP–FP lipidomic contrast. This finding is biologically meaningful because sphingomyelins are structural components of membrane microdomains and their acyl-chain composition can influence lipid–protein interactions and membrane organization51. However, we do not interpret the change in this single SM species as direct evidence of altered membrane fluidity, signaling capacity, or mitochondrial function. Instead, SM(d16:1/22:0) should be viewed as a candidate sphingolipid feature that may reflect treatment-associated differences in membrane-related lipid composition. Its abundance relative to the broader sphingomyelin pool and its functional impact on membrane properties remain to be determined in targeted validation studies. Therefore, these changes suggest that maternal supplementation was associated with lipid features that may be linked to inflammatory or stress-related lipid pathways, but inflammatory markers were not directly measured, favoring cell survival and metabolic homeostasis52.

Although LPC(18:1) and CAR(2:0) did not differ individually between groups, their contribution to the pathway enrichment analysis highlighted a biologically coherent pattern involving phospholipid metabolism, lysophosphatidylcholine hydrolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and carnitine-related processes in the NP–FP contrast. This pattern is relevant because LPC(18:1) is generated through phospholipase A₂-mediated hydrolysis of phosphatidylcholines and has been linked to membrane lipid turnover and oxidative or inflammatory processes, whereas acetylcarnitine, CAR(2:0), is involved in mitochondrial acetyl-group buffering and CoA availability53. Together, these features suggest candidate lipidomic links between membrane-related lipid remodeling and energy-metabolism-associated pathways. However, pathway enrichment does not demonstrate pathway activity, and mitochondrial acetyl-CoA handling, β-oxidation flux, oxidative stress, and inflammatory markers were not directly measured in the present study. Therefore, the LPC- and CAR-related findings should be interpreted as biologically plausible hypotheses for future validation rather than confirmed functional alterations induced by fetal programming. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that the maternal nutritional environment can influence offspring skeletal muscle development and metabolic phenotype, while direct functional validation remains necessary4,54.

Therefore, NP animals showed lipidomic features associated with pathways related to phospholipid degradation and carnitine metabolism. However, these pathway-level interpretations require direct metabolic validation. Thus, these results reinforce the concept that prenatal nutrition can reprogram lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dynamics, influence postnatal muscle bioenergetics and potentially beef quality. Notably, prior beef lipidomic studies (e.g., Antonelo et al.26, linking phospholipid remodeling to oxidative stress and quality-related phenotypes (e.g., ultimate pH differences) support the hypothesis that membrane-centric lipid signatures can help track postmortem stability and quality variation, reinforcing the plausibility of the mechanistic links38.

The direct comparison of carcass and meat-quality traits showed that treatment-level phenotypic differences were limited. Most carcass, color, maturation, cooking-loss, and organ-weight traits did not differ between NP and FP animals, which is consistent with previous evidence in Nellore bulls showing that fetal programming had limited effects on broad meat-quality outcomes20. In the present lipidomic subset, however, FP animals showed greater subcutaneous fat thickness measured by ultrasound, higher WBSF-2, and higher pH after 5 days of simulated retail display. These differences suggest that gestational protein-energy supplementation may be associated with specific postmortem phenotypic responses, particularly involving fat deposition, tenderness-related measurements, and pH dynamics, rather than broad shifts in meat quality.

The greater subcutaneous fat thickness measured by ultrasound in FP animals is consistent with the biological premise that prenatal nutrition can influence adipose tissue development and later fat deposition. Adipose and connective tissues begin to develop during fetal life, and maternal nutrition can affect offspring skeletal muscle, adipogenesis, and postnatal tissue composition4,11. In addition, recent multi-omics studies in beef cattle indicate that prenatal nutritional treatments can influence molecular pathways in carcass-related tissues, including lipid metabolism and tissue-development processes56,57. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the lipidomic subset was small and broad carcass traits did not differ between treatments.

The higher WBSF-2 observed in FP animals indicates a treatment-level difference in one tenderness-related measurement. Tenderness is a multifactorial trait influenced by postmortem proteolysis, muscle structure, connective tissue, intramuscular fat, and the biochemical environment during aging. Warner–Bratzler shear force is widely used as an instrumental measure of beef tenderness, and its interpretation depends on standardized sampling, cooking, and coring procedures22,23. Therefore, this result should not be interpreted as evidence that fetal programming broadly worsened tenderness, but rather as a specific phenotypic signal supporting the integration of lipidomic features with direct meat-quality measurements. This interpretation is reinforced by the positive within-group correlation between PG(16:0) and WBSF-2 in FP animals, suggesting that selected membrane-related lipid features may help explain variation in tenderness-related traits.

The higher pH after 5 days of simulated retail display in FP animals also suggests subtle treatment-associated differences in postmortem pH dynamics. Postmortem pH is closely related to muscle energy metabolism, mitochondrial oxygen consumption, water-holding capacity, and color stability43,63,64. Lipidomic and metabolomic studies in beef have also shown that pH-related phenotypes are associated with differences in postmortem metabolic and lipid profiles, supporting the biological relevance of integrating pH dynamics with lipidomic signatures26,42,55. However, this pH difference was not accompanied by broad treatment effects on instrumental color or cooking loss, indicating that it should be interpreted as a specific pH-related response rather than evidence of generalized meat-quality alteration.

Together, the direct NP–FP phenotypic comparisons clarify that fetal programming did not produce broad treatment-level changes in meat quality in the lipidomic subset. Instead, the significant differences were restricted to EGS_US, WBSF-2, and pH after 5 days of shelf life. This distinction strengthens the interpretation of the lipid–phenotype correlations as exploratory within-group associations that may help identify candidate lipid features linked to specific carcass and meat-quality traits, rather than as proof of widespread fetal-programming effects on meat quality.

The group-specific correlation results suggest that selected lipid features may be associated with variation in meat-quality traits within treatment groups. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the analysis. The negative correlation between TG(48:7)_FA16:0 and pH at 1 h postmortem suggests that FP animals with higher abundance of this TG exhibited a faster early pH decline, consistent with differences in early postmortem glycolytic dynamics. Early pH trajectories reflect the balance between muscle glycogen availability, glycolytic flux, and mitochondrial oxygen consumption immediately after slaughter, mechanisms that are repeatedly implicated in postmortem meat quality. pH-related phenotypes can change with metabolic state and mitochondrial function, which can influence both pH development and downstream appearance43. In parallel, recent lipidome metabolome profiling of Longissimus muscle samples stratified by ultimate pH, together with analyses of the mitochondrial fraction, indicate that lipid signatures can serve as informative proxies of pH-linked postmortem physiology and color-stability responses, reinforcing their value as biomarkers of underlying mitochondrial function during aging42,55.

Importantly, prenatal nutrition can reprogram metabolic pathways in meat-relevant tissues, with multi-omics studies demonstrating that prenatal nutritional treatments impact lipid metabolism modules and stress/immune signaling, supporting a mechanistic basis for FP-driven differences in postnatal muscle biochemistry56,57. Under this framework, TG(48:7)_FA16:0 is better interpreted not as a direct causal driver, but as a sentinel marker of an FP-linked metabolic context associated with variation in early pH decline. Notably, recent work in Nellore beef with different ultimate pH also identified TG(48:7) related species among lipids associated with pH-linked postmortem responses, which strengthens the interpretation of this feature as a proxy of broader mitochondrial and postmortem metabolic state55.

The same TG species was also negatively associated with b (yellowness) at day 0, that is, prior to retail-display storage or vacuum aging, in both the shelf-life and maturation assessments. In beef, instrumental color coordinates reflect surface redox and light-scattering conditions and are strongly influenced by oxidative processes that link lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial activity, and pigment chemistry. Recent work continues to strengthen the connection between lipid peroxidation pathways (including ferroptosis-like processes) and postmortem meat color stability, emphasizing that lipid oxidation can modulate meat color trajectories58,59. Thus, the TG(48:7)_FA16:0–b* association in FP is consistent with a scenario where FP modifies lipid substrate availability or oxidative susceptibility, which can translate into detectable differences in reflectance along the b* axis during early display or aging, linking early pH decline and b*, suggesting a coordinated FP dependent lipid, energy, and meat color relationship.

In the FP group, PG(16:0) was positively correlated with WBSF, indicating that higher PG(16:0) abundance was associated with greater toughness within FP. While PG is not a dominant lipid class in muscle, it is biologically relevant because phosphatidylglycerol is a key precursor of cardiolipin, a mitochondria-specific phospholipid essential for inner mitochondrial membrane architecture and respiratory function60,61. Experimental evidence shows that altering PG availability can modify mitochondrial morphology and cardiolipin composition, indicating that PG-related variation can reflect mitochondrial membrane remodeling62.

Tenderness is multifactorial characteristic influenced by proteolysis, muscle structure, and the postmortem biochemical environment. Lipidomic studies of vacuum-aged beef (wet aging) emphasize that glycerophospholipids (structural membrane lipids) are among the most dynamic lipid subclasses during aging and can track quality differences (tenderness versus oxidative stability trade-offs) across muscles and aging time62. In this context, the PG(16:0)–WBSF association in FP is plausibly interpreted as a membrane/mitochondria-related signature linked to the postmortem environment that modulates proteolysis efficiency or oxidative tone, pathways that ultimately influence tenderness trajectories.

In the NP group, significant lipids and phenotype associations were overwhelmingly dominated by L* (lightness) at D0 (both shelf life and maturation), with PG(16:0) and multiple TG species showing consistent negative correlations with L* (r ≈ − 0.79 to − 0.92). This indicates that, in NP samples, a higher relative abundance of these lipids was associated with lower L values, consistent with a darker surface appearance. Such clustering of multiple lipid features around a single-color coordinate suggests a coordinated underlying biological driver rather than isolated, lipid-specific effects. Mechanistically, L* is strongly influenced by the interplay between postmortem oxygenation dynamics, myoglobin redox chemistry, and light scattering, which in turn are modulated by muscle metabolic state and mitochondrial activity63,64.

The particularly strong association of PG(16:0) with L* is notable because PG is an anionic glycerophospholipid that is metabolically linked to mitochondrial membrane remodeling, including pathways that feed into cardiolipin-related processes. Experimental evidence shows that altering PG availability can change mitochondrial morphology and cardiolipin composition, supporting PG as a proxy of mitochondrial membrane lipid homeostasis62. In beef, a growing body of work highlights that mitochondrial function (e.g., oxygen consumption and reducing capacity) is a key determinant of objective color attributes and color stability63,65. Recent mitochondrial proteome studies in beef (including in Nellore cattle) show that differences in mitochondrial proteins and respiration-related pathways are associated with variation in color parameters and color stability under different pH categories and postmortem handling conditions65,66.

The consistent negative correlations between several TG species [TG(48:7)_FA16:0, TG(49:7)_FA16:0, TG(46:0)_FA16:0, TG(48:2)_FA16:1, TG(51:7)_FA18:0] and L* in NP suggest that neutral lipid signatures also track color variation. In lipidomic workflows where abundances are often expressed as relative intensities, TG signals can behave as compositional markers of broader lipid remodeling and metabolic state rather than direct proxies of intramuscular fat content alone. In parallel, recent studies on darker beef phenotypes (e.g., dark-cutting severity classes) demonstrate that objective color differences (including lower L*) co-occur with strong metabolic shifts and lipidome differences, indicating that lipid profiles can reflect the biochemical context underlying darker appearance67,68.

The NP reveals a broad lipid color coupling centered on L*, whereas FP shows a more focused pattern involving early pH decline (pH 1 h), b*, and WBSF-2. In meat color science, L* is often a strong discriminator of “darker vs. lighter” appearance and can relate to pH, mitochondrial oxygen consumption, and postmortem redox state63,64,66. Meanwhile, recent lipidomic work specifically in dark-cutting contexts shows that lipidome profiles differ across visual severity levels where L* changes substantially, indicating that lipid markers can track darker appearance phenotypes67.

These results should be framed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Many lipids converged on the same phenotypic endpoint, namely L (lightness), and the membrane lipid PG(16:0) fits a mitochondria-centered color mechanism that has strong support in the beef color literature63,65,66. A practical next step to strengthen inference would be testing whether the TG/PG–L* relationships persist after adjusting for pH or other covariates and validating key candidates in a larger cohort or with targeted quantification.

Implications

Our findings indicate that gestational protein-energy supplementation was associated with subtle differences in the skeletal muscle lipidomic profile and may provide an initial basis for developing a candidate targeted panel (~ 10–12 lipid species) for future validation. Beyond discrimination between NP and FP, the within-group correlation layer suggests that VIP ranked lipids may also relate to biologically meaningful variation in quality-related phenotypes, particularly within the FP group. Specifically, TG(48:7)_FA16:0 was associated with early pH decline and b (yellowness), whereas PG(16:0) was correlated with WBSF, suggesting that FP-linked lipid states may couple energy/redox dynamics and membrane composition to tenderness and color related attributes.

Changes in the PC: PE ratio may provide insight into membrane-related lipid organization because PC and PE are major structural phospholipids. However, in the absence of direct mitochondrial measurements, this ratio should be interpreted as an exploratory lipid index rather than evidence of altered inner mitochondrial membrane curvature, crista remodeling, or mitochondrial function. Together, the bioactive and membrane-related lipid features identified in this study may represent candidate markers of prenatal nutritional exposure and provide a focused starting point for future targeted MRM-based validation. When integrated with key product-quality indicators, such as pH kinetics, tenderness, instrumental color, and shelf-life traits, these candidate lipid features may help clarify the molecular links between gestational nutrition, skeletal muscle lipid composition, and meat-quality variation. Nevertheless, larger datasets and complementary physiological measurements are required before these lipidomic signatures can be translated into practical tools for gestational management or beef production systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that gestational nutritional management is associated with detectable lipidomic differences in the skeletal muscle of Nellore offspring. By applying targeted MRM-based lipidomic profiling to a fetal programming model in beef cattle, this work contributes to a still limited understanding of how prenatal nutrition may leave long-term molecular signatures in postnatal muscle tissue. The lipidomic patterns observed here support the relevance of membrane-related and bioactive lipid classes as candidate markers of prenatal nutritional exposure. Although the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and do not establish direct functional effects on mitochondrial activity, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, or meat quality, they provide a biologically meaningful basis for future validation studies. In this context, the candidate lipid signatures identified in this study may help guide more focused investigations integrating lipidomics with physiological, carcass, and meat-quality measurements. Overall, this study advances the use of lipidomics as a promising approach to investigate fetal programming in beef cattle and highlights candidate lipid features that may contribute to future strategies for understanding the molecular links between maternal nutrition, skeletal muscle biology, and product-quality variation.
