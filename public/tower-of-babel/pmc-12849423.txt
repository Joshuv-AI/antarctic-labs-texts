Virtual reality therapy for adolescent depression: a prospective observational study
Authors: Huimin Gao, Rui Li, Shiling Wu, Wanling Huang, Liju Qian, Meng Zhang, Kun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent depression represents a significant public health challenge, often characterized by emotional instability and poor adherence to conventional therapies. Virtual reality (VR) offers a novel, immersive tool designed to enhance engagement and emotional regulation. This study aimed to evaluate this approach and identify predictors of treatment remission.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted at Shandong Daizhuang Hospital from December 2024 to February 2025, enrolling 100 adolescents (≤19 years). This study employed a VR platform to deliver a combined intervention of relaxation therapy and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). The intervention consisted of 10 sessions of 15-minute VR therapy over two weeks. The change in the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) score from baseline to post-treatment was assessed, with remission defined as a post-treatment SDS score of less than 53. At baseline, we collected demographic characteristics, clinical features, and inflammatory markers. Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the SDS and Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) at both baseline and the 2-week endpoint. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify predictors of remission.ResultsOf the 100 participants recruited, 84 completed the study. The study cohort consisted of 68 females (81.95%) and 16 males (19.05%), with a median age of 15.00 years (interquartile range: 14.00–16.00). Following the VR intervention, a statistically significant reduction in SDS scores was observed (t = 10.61, p < 0.001). The overall remission rate was 36.90% (n = 31). In univariate analyses, the remission rate was significantly higher in males than in females (χ2 = 5.56, p = 0.018). Additionally, the remission group had lower baseline SAS (t = 3.17, p = 0.002) and SDS scores (t = 3.63, p < 0.001) compared to the non-remission group. Baseline inflammatory markers did not differ between groups. In the multivariate logistic regression model, none of the variables that were significant in the univariate analysis, such as gender, baseline SAS score, and baseline SDS score, emerged as independent predictors of remission.ConclusionOur study found that VR-based relaxation therapy combined with CBT achieved a remission rate of 36.90% in adolescents with depression. Although baseline severity and gender showed potential associations with remission in univariate analysis, they were not independent predictors in the multivariate model. Given the absence of a control group in this study, subsequent randomized controlled trials are warranted to verify its efficacy and to identify biomarkers associated with treatment remission.Trial registrationThe trial was registered prospectively in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092919) on 26 November 2024.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07729-9.Keywords: Adolescent depression, Virtual reality therapy, Cognitive-behavioral therapy, Remission, Predictors

Introduction

Adolescent depression is a major growing public health issue globally [1]. The World Health Organization considers it a leading cause of illness and disability in the 10–19 age group [2]. Traditional treatments for adolescent depression primarily involve psychotherapy, such as Cognitive-behavioral Therapy (CBT), and pharmacological interventions [3]. However, psychotherapy is often costly and difficult to access [4]. Meanwhile, medication carries risks such as suicidal ideation and sleep disturbances [5]. Studies indicate that non-adherence to medication among individuals with mental disorders is as high as 44%, which may be a contributing factor to the lack of response to conventional treatments in some patients [6, 7].

In recent years, virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising tool in psychiatry, demonstrating efficacy in treating mental health disorders such as anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder [8, 9]. Its application in adolescent mental health is particularly compelling due to VRs immersive, interactive, and customizable features, which allow for highly personalized therapeutic experiences [10]. Moreover, VR can provide a private, stigma-free environment for treatment, which is especially valuable for adolescents hesitant to engage in traditional therapies [4]. Advances in smartphone and information technology have further improved the accessibility and cost-effectiveness of VR, accelerating its integration into mental health care [8].

Depression in adolescents often manifests as significant irritability and agitation [11]. VR-based relaxation therapy addresses this by immersing patients in simulated natural environments (e.g., beaches or forests) that are seldom accessible in clinical settings [12]. This immersive disengagement from stressors has been shown to effectively reduce anxiety and promote psychophysiological calm, thereby alleviating agitation and supporting mood improvement [13]. Furthermore, adolescent depression is marked by negative cognitive biases, which lead patients to selectively attend to negative stimuli and favor negative interpretations [14]. These biases amplify emotional distress by distorting information processing and impairing social functioning. Although CBT is designed to challenge and restructure these maladaptive patterns, its efficacy as a standalone treatment remains limited [7]. VR offers a promising complementary approach. By fostering relaxation within an immersive and safe environment, VR helps stabilize the patient’s psychophysiological state. This calm state thereby enhances their readiness to engage in CBT. Moreover, by improving patient engagement and adherence, VR may strengthen the delivery and effectiveness of cognitive bias modification during the therapeutic process [15]. Despite its potential, VR remains underexplored in adolescent depression treatment. Integrating VR with established approaches such as relaxation therapy and CBT offers a novel strategy that leverages technological immersion to improve outcomes. Identifying individuals most likely to respond to specific VR-based interventions will be essential for personalizing and optimizing treatment.

Studies on predictors of treatment outcomes in adolescent depression have examined various variables, including demographic (e.g., age, sex), clinical (e.g., baseline severity, anxiety, suicidal ideation), and psychosocial factors (e.g., parent-child conflict, trauma history) [16]. Baseline symptom severity consistently emerges as a predictor, with lower severity often correlating with improved outcomes [17]. While sex differences in depression are well-documented, their role in predicting treatment response remains less clear [18].

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in the role of inflammation in the pathophysiology of depression. Peripheral inflammatory markers, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), are being explored as potential biomarkers [19]. While certain VR therapies have demonstrated efficacy in reducing inflammatory markers in chronic pain conditions [20], their potential as predictors of remission from depression in adolescents undergoing VR therapy remains uninvestigated.

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a novel short-term adjunctive VR therapy, combining relaxation and CBT principles, for adolescents with depressive disorders in terms of remission rate. Additionally, the study aimed to identify potential demographic, clinical, and peripheral inflammatory predictors of treatment remission. The hypothesis posited that the immersive intervention would result in a substantial remission rate, with

lower baseline symptom severity and lower levels of inflammatory markers predicting a favorable outcome.

Materials and methods

Design and population

The study utilized a prospective observational design and was conducted at Shandong Daizhuang Hospital from December 2024 to February 2025.

Participant recruitment was systematically conducted by two trained researchers. They screened the hospital’s VR therapy appointment registry and reviewed electronic medical records to identify potential participants. The research team then contacted these potentially eligible individuals and their legal guardians. Following the informed consent process, potential participants underwent a structured clinical interview to confirm the diagnosis based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), codes F32–F33. A detailed review of their current medications was also conducted, which included consultation with their treating physician to confirm medication stability. Final enrollment was contingent upon meeting all inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 19 years or younger; (2) a primary diagnosis of depressive disorder confirmed by ICD-10, codes F32–F33; and (3) stable doses of antipsychotic and antidepressant medications throughout the VR intervention period.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) meeting the ICD-10 diagnostic criteria for any other mental disorder; (2) severe somatic diseases; (3) history of autoimmune diseases or severe infections; (4) receiving or having received electroconvulsive therapy or transcranial magnetic stimulation during or within 4 weeks prior to the study; (5) unwillingness or inability to comply with the study protocol.

To ensure ethical rigor, informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to enrollment. Participants were provided with detailed information about the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. They were also assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. Additionally, measures were taken to ensure the confidentiality and security of participant data throughout the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Daizhuang Hospital (No0.202410KS-1). The trial was registered prospectively in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400092919).

VR therapy treatment protocol

The VR-based intervention integrated relaxation therapy and CBT, delivered via a pre-established protocol on VR devices. All sessions were conducted in a dedicated, quiet, and comfortable hospital therapy room. A therapist guided each participant to sit comfortably and assisted them in putting on the VR headset. Each session lasted 15 minutes and was administered once daily from Monday to Friday over a two-week period (10 sessions).

VR Relaxation Therapy: Each session began with a relaxation module designed to alleviate stress and induce a calm psychophysiological state. Participants were immersed in a serene underwater environment accompanied by tranquil music. Through audio guidance, they were encouraged to practice mindfulness and deep breathing to facilitate emotional regulation and promote a state of mental and physical relaxation. This phase lasted for 5 minutes.

VR-Based CBT: Following relaxation, participants transitioned to a CBT-oriented module. In this segment, they embarked on a symbolic therapeutic journey, navigating an underwater world with a school of fish and discovering pearls hidden within caves. This module was designed to parallel the core principles of CBT by encouraging participants to: (1) identify negative thought patterns; (2) examine associated emotions from a detached perspective; and (3) challenge irrational beliefs by completing goal-oriented tasks. The act of “seeking pearls” served to reinforce cognitive restructuring and the development of more adaptive thinking patterns to alleviate depressive symptoms (see Fig. 1).

Clinical assessment

At baseline, we collected demographic data including gender, age, years of education and Body Mass Index (BMI), along with clinical characteristics such as age at onset, duration of illness, diagnosis, family history of mental illness, and dosages of antidepressants and antipsychotics. Daily antipsychotic and antidepressant doses were converted to chlorpromazine and sertraline equivalents, respectively, to enable standardized comparisons [21–23]. Side effects were documented in detail throughout the study.

Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and the Zung Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), respectively [24]. Both scales comprise 20 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale. The raw score (sum of all 20 items) is multiplied by 1.25, and the integer portion is taken as the index score, yielding a total score range of 25 to 100. SDS scores were categorized as follows: < 53 for the normal range, 53–62 for mild depression, 63–72 for moderate depression, and > 72 for severe depression. Similarly, SAS scores were defined as: < 50 for the normal range, 50–59 for mild anxiety, 60–69 for moderate anxiety, and ≥ 70 for severe anxiety. In this study, assessments were conducted at baseline and after 2 weeks of treatment. Treatment remission for depression was defined as achieving an SDS score of < 53 [24].

On the morning of the first VR session, and following an 8-hour fast, a 5-mL blood sample was collected from each participant. The collected tube was immediately gently inverted 8 times to ensure proper mixing with the anticoagulant. The sample was then transported to the laboratory department for complete blood count analysis within one hour of collection. Based on routine blood results, inflammatory markers were calculated, including the NLR, platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-monocyte ratio (NMR), and SII [25]. The calculations were performed as follows: NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, PLR = platelet count/lymphocyte count, MLR = monocyte count/lymphocyte count, NMR = neutrophil count/monocyte count, and SII = (neutrophil count × platelet count)/lymphocyte count. All calculated values were rounded to two decimal places.

Statistics

First, paired t-tests were used to compare the changes in SDS and SAS scores before and after treatment to determine the efficacy of VR therapy. Second, patients were categorized into remission and non-remission groups based on SDS scores at 2-week post-treatment. Initial univariate analyses were performed: Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, while continuous variables underwent independent samples t-tests when meeting assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance; otherwise, Mann-Whitney U tests were employed.

Variables previously identified as predictors of remission or demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.05) in univariate analyses were entered into multivariate logistic regression. Prior to regression modeling, linearity between continuous independent variables and the logit of the outcome was verified using Box-Tidwell procedures. Multicollinearity was assessed via tolerance values or variance inflation factors (VIF). A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted by entering all eligible variables simultaneously using the enter method. Model fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (adequate fit: p ≥ 0.05). To evaluate potential bias due to participant dropout, baseline characteristics were compared between study completers and those who dropped out. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis using the worst-case scenario approach was conducted to verify the robustness of the primary outcome. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

Sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) with a two-tailed alpha of 0.05, a power of 0.95, and an effect size d of 0.35. The analysis indicated that 90 participants were required. Accounting for a 10% attrition rate, a total of 100 participants were recruited. The final analytic sample comprised 84 participants who completed the protocol. 16 participants were excluded from the analysis for the following reasons: eight withdrew consent due to personal reasons (e.g., inability to adhere to the treatment schedule), three discontinued because of dizziness experienced during VR sessions, and five were excluded following a change in their therapeutic regimen. Efficacy

Paired t-tests (two-tailed) revealed significant differences in both SDS (t = 10.61, p < 0.001) and SAS scores (t = 8.98, p < 0.001) following the VR intervention. The mean SDS score decreased from 73.43 [Standard Deviation (SD) = 10.70] at pre-intervention to 58.89 (SD = 16.08) at post-intervention. Similarly, the mean SAS score decreased from 66.43 (SD = 14.20) to 54.32 (SD = 15.82) following the intervention.

Comparison between remission groups and non-remission groups

Patients were stratified into non-remission (n = 53) and remission (n = 31) groups based on SDS scores, yielding a 36.90% remission rate. Overall, 81.95% of the participants were female. However, the remission rate was significantly higher in males than in females (χ2 = 5.56, p = 0.018). No statistically significant differences were detected in other demographic variables, including age (U = 808.00, p = 0.898), years of education (U = 756.50, p = 0.532), and BMI (U = 671.00, p = 0.163). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in disease characteristics between the groups, such as age of onset (U = 806.00, p = 0.885), duration of illness (U = 783.00, p = 0.717), diagnosis (χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.843), or family history of mental illness (χ2 = 0.37, p = 0.544). The use of antidepressants (U = 788.00, p = 0.755) and antipsychotics (U = 736.50, p = 0.427) was not significantly different between the remission and non-remission groups.

Univariate analysis of baseline clinical scale scores identified lower SAS (t = 3.17, p = 0.002) and SDS scores (t = 3.63, p < 0.001) as predictors of treatment-induced remission. Inflammatory markers including NLR (U = 716.00, p = 0.328), PLR (U = 726.50, p = 0.379), MLR (U = 787.50, p = 0.753), NMR (U = 737.50, p = 0.436) and SII (U = 715.00, p = 0.324) showed no significant intergroup differences. Predictors of VR therapy effectiveness

The initial univariate analysis demonstrated significant associations between gender (χ2 = 5.56, p = 0.018), baseline SAS scores (t = 3.17, p = 0.002), and baseline SDS scores (t = 3.63, p < 0.001) with treatment remission. Sensitivity analysis

A comparison of study completers and dropouts revealed no significant differences in baseline characteristics (p > 0.05), as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. This finding alleviates concerns about potential attrition bias. The VR intervention significantly reduced SDS scores in the analysis of all 100 participants. The mean score decreased from 73.10 (SD = 10.27) at baseline to 60.89 (SD = 15.71) post-treatment, a statistically significant reduction (p = 0.001). This result was consistent with the findings from the 84 study completers, confirming the robustness of the treatment effect.

All dropouts were classified as non-remission group. Both univariate and multivariate analyses under this conservative approach yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. While gender, baseline SAS scores, and baseline SDS scores showed statistical significance in univariate analyses, these factors were no longer significant in the multivariate model. This consistency across analytical methods further substantiates the robustness of the primary findings.

Side effects

The VR-based intervention was well tolerated by the vast majority of participants. Among the 84 participants, a total of 4 (4.8%) reported transient and mild side effects. These included dizziness (n = 2, 2.4%) and eye strain (n = 2, 2.4%). All symptoms resolved spontaneously within minutes following the VR session. No serious adverse events were reported.

Discussion

This prospective observational study provides preliminary evidence for the efficacy of a novel, short-term VR therapy in adolescents with depression. The two-week intervention was associated with a depression remission rate of 36.90%. In univariate analyses, lower baseline severity of anxiety and depression, as well as male gender, were associated with better outcomes. Contrary to our hypothesis, there were no statistically significant differences in inflammatory markers between the groups.

However, in multivariate regression analysis, none of these factors showed statistical significance, indicating that they were not independent predictors of remission. These findings suggest that while some baseline characteristics may be linked to treatment response, other factors may also influence the effectiveness of VR therapy, and further research is needed to identify reliable predictors.

The remission rate observed in our study was 36.90%, achieved after only two weeks of intervention. This rate is comparable to those of standard therapies assessed over longer periods in clinical trials [26, 27]. For instance, a pivotal trial reported remission rates of 37.90% for CBT and 39.40% for supportive therapy after 12–16 weeks [26]. While these comparisons underscore the promising efficacy of VR therapy, they should be interpreted cautiously due to methodological differences across studies, with particular regard to the assessment tools and criteria used to define remission. Notwithstanding these limitations and the open-label, single-arm design of our study, the rapid and substantial remission observed suggests that VR-based therapy may serve as a potent tool to expedite treatment response.

The efficacy of our brief VR intervention aligns with emerging evidence on the acute benefits of nature-based VR environments for adolescent mental health. In a recent pilot study by Björling et al. [13], adolescents using a self-administered nature VR application at home reported significant immediate reductions in momentary stress following each session. Qualitative findings from that study further revealed that participants frequently described their VR experiences with terms such as “Calm Down” “Relaxation” and “Escape”. These themes resonate with the core therapeutic components of our VR protocol, suggesting that the sense of presence and immersion inherent in VR can effectively foster a state of calm and provide a mental respite from stressors, thereby creating an optimal psychological context for emotional regulation and cognitive restructuring [28].

This immersive quality appears to enhance emotional regulation and create an optimal setting for cognitive restructuring, a benefit which may be particularly salient for adolescents. The engaging, technology-mediated format of VR can overcome motivational barriers often encountered in traditional talk therapy, fostering sustained participation and potentially accelerating treatment response [29]. Taken together, these factors help explain our findings.

In our investigation of predictors of remission, univariate analyses revealed results that partly align with existing literature. The correlation between lower baseline depression and anxiety severity and a higher probability of remission is consistent with findings from various studies using different therapeutic approaches [16, 17], indicating that this VR intervention, like other therapies, may be most beneficial for individuals with mild-to-moderate initial symptoms.

Our findings indicate that VR-based relaxation therapy combined with CBT shows particular promise for male adolescents with depression, suggesting gender plays a significant role in treatment response. This is similar to the outcomes of certain pharmacological treatments [30, 31]. The gender-based difference in the response to VR therapy for depression could be attributed to several factors. One credible explanation points to gender disparities in both the incidence and clinical presentation of depressive symptoms, with females often experiencing more severe symptoms [32]. These differences could influence the respond to VR therapy. Moreover, the way males and females process emotions and stress could also be a contributing factor. For instance, Males tend to be more visually and spatially oriented, they might engage more effectively with the immersive and interactive elements of VR, leading to better treatment outcomes [33]. On the other hand, females might require more emotionally focused or narrative-driven VR content to achieve similar benefits. However, there is a gender imbalance in this study, with male patients constituting only 17.90%. Further research is warranted to explore the influence of gender and to inform personalized treatment approaches for patients.

Baseline peripheral inflammatory markers (NLR and SII) did not predict remission, contrary to our hypothesis, despite the established association between immune system dysregulation and depression [34]. This unexpected result may be due to the limited sensitivity of peripheral markers in capturing the nuanced neuroinflammation linked to depression. Additionally, the relationship between inflammation and treatment response could be specific to certain interventions, such as pharmacotherapies, rather than the VR-based psychological therapy employed in this study. Furthermore, the null finding may also be attributed to the potential inadequacy of our sample size to detect subtle effects.

The most notable result of our predictor analysis was the lack of significance of any univariate predictors in the multivariate logistic regression model, indicating likely interrelationships among the variables. For example, baseline anxiety and depression scores often exhibit high correlation. The robust predictive ability of baseline SDS in the univariate analysis (t = 3.63, p < 0.001) may have masked the influences of gender and SAS in the comprehensive model. The inability of the model to identify independent predictors underscores the intricacies of predicting treatment response in a diverse population such as adolescents with depression, emphasizing the necessity for larger, more robustly designed studies.

Finally, the VR-based intervention was well tolerated by the vast majority of participants in our study. Only four participants reported mild and transient side effects, including slight dizziness and eye strain. Therefore, the following considerations should be emphasized in VR therapy: (1) implementing pre-treatment risk screening and adaptation training; (2) establishing a clear protocol for monitoring and managing adverse reactions; and (3) future efforts should focus on developing personalized VR treatment plans to further enhance its safety and applicability.

This study has several notable limitations. Firstly, the single-arm, observational design lacking a control group prevents definitive attribution of the observed improvements to the VR intervention. Factors such as the placebo effect, therapeutic attention from research staff, or the natural progression of the illness could have influenced the high remission rate, although a 36.90% remission rate in two weeks is unlikely solely due to the natural course of the illness. Secondly, the absence of a follow-up assessment raises uncertainty about the long-term sustainability of the treatment effects. Thirdly, the study’s confinement to a single center may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations and settings. Fourthly, reliance on self-report measures (SDS and SAS) as primary outcomes introduces the potential for reporting bias; inclusion of blinded, clinician-rated measures would have enhanced the study design. Lastly, the sample size of 84, while appropriate for a preliminary study, was probably insufficient for the multivariate regression analysis, increasing the likelihood of a Type II error in the predictor analysis.

The study’s promising results underscore the necessity for further investigation. The next crucial step involves conducting a large-scale, multi-center randomized controlled trial with an active control group (e.g., sham VR experience or alternative digital intervention) and a treatment-as-usual group to discern the specific effects of VR therapy. Long-term follow-up assessments at 3, 6, and 12 months are imperative to ascertain the sustainability of benefits. Subsequent studies should include a broader range of outcome measures, encompassing clinician-rated assessments (including for suicidal ideation) as well as measures of functional improvement, such as social functioning. To enhance predictive models, future research should utilize larger sample sizes and explore various potential predictors, such as neurobiological markers from imaging or genetics, cognitive profiles, and psychosocial factors.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence for the effectiveness of a novel, short-term adjunctive VR therapy in adolescents with depressive disorders. Although lower baseline symptoms and male gender were associated with remission, no strong independent predictors of treatment success were identified, underscoring the challenge of personalizing interventions. The promising outcomes warrant further validation through large-scale randomized controlled trials to support clinical integration of this approach.
