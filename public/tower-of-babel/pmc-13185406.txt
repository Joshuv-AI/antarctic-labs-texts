Quercetin-loaded composite materials for bone regeneration: a review of carriers, controlled release, and mechanistic advances
Authors: Xiao Zhao, Xinyi Ye, Yunjiao He, Pengyue You, Yuchien Hsu, Yunsong Liu, Haitao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quercetin is a natural flavonoid with strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic effects, showing great promise for bone regeneration. However, its poor solubility, low bioavailability, and rapid degradation limit clinical use. Innovative quercetin-loaded composite biomaterials address these challenges by providing precise, controlled release and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. In recent years, quercetin-loaded composite materials have shown significant research progress in the field of bone tissue engineering. Studies indicate that in vitro experiments demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility of these materials, which promote osteogenic differentiation, induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, scavenge reactive oxygen species, exert antibacterial effects, and enhance angiogenesis. In vivo studies confirm that quercetin-loaded composite materials increase bone volume, density, and vascularization, with various signaling pathways. This review integrates novel mechanistic insights, addresses challenges like optimal dosing and smart responsiveness, and outlines pathways for quercetin composites in repairing complex bone defects, paving the way for advanced regenerative therapies with strong clinical potential.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05002-8.

Introduction

Quercetin (chemically 3,3′,4′,5,7-pentahydroxyflavone) is a flavonoid polyphenolic compound widely distributed in plants, characterized by multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups [1, 2], which endow it with strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [3]. In the historical context of traditional Chinese medicine research, quercetin has been primarily employed for heat-clearing and detoxification, anti-inflammation, and blood circulation promotion to resolve stasis [4]. Modern pharmacological investigations demonstrate its multifaceted therapeutic potential, encompassing antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antitumor, immunomodulatory, cardiovascular protective [5], neuroprotective [6], and osteoprotective effects [7]. Currently, quercetin has attracted considerable attention as a bioactive agent for bone regeneration, with applications in bone tissue engineering [8], periodontitis therapy [9, 10], osteoporosis treatment [11], and modulation of the bone immune microenvironment [12]. Quercetin exhibits diverse osteogenesis-associated activities, such as enhancing osteoblast differentiation and mineralization, suppressing osteoclast formation [13], as well as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Its merits include natural sourcing, high biocompatibility, and synergistic multi-pathway facilitation of bone repair [14].

However, quercetin presents notable limitations, including poor aqueous solubility [15], low bioavailability [16, 17], rapid in vivo metabolism [18], and chemical instability (particularly prone to degradation under light or alkaline conditions) [19], with high concentrations potentially inhibiting cell proliferation (a concentration-dependent effect), thereby constraining its direct application [20, 21]. To circumvent these drawbacks, researchers have developed quercetin-loaded composite materials using several strategies: (i) nanocarriers to enhance solubility, stability, and controlled release [22]; (ii) hydrogels and polymeric matrices for localized, sustained delivery at bone defect sites [8]; and (iii) chemical conjugation via metal coordination or covalent coupling to improve bioactivity and targeting [23]. Quercetin-composited materials obtained through these approaches enable superior localized sustained release, enhanced bioavailability, and synergistic antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, osteogenic, and angiogenic effects.

Despite significant progress, there is still no established consensus regarding the effective application concentration of quercetin-loaded composite materials in bone regeneration. Furthermore, the carrier materials, loading methods, and corresponding drug release profiles of quercetin have not been comprehensively summarized. In addition, the osteogenic mechanisms of quercetin still require further investigation.

This review synthesizes current advances in quercetin-loaded composite materials for bone tissue regeneration. It examines preparation methods and release profiles, biological properties and mechanisms of action, and evaluates clinical translation prospects together with existing challenges.

To provide a comprehensive overview of quercetin-loaded composites in bone tissue engineering, we identified relevant literature from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science spanning the last 25 years (2000–2025). The selection process focused on original research investigating the localized application of quercetin-functionalized scaffolds in both in vitro environments and in vivo bone defect models. Loading materials and composite methods for quercetin

Quercetin’s poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability necessitate advanced delivery systems. Current strategies focus on integrating quercetin into biomaterial matrices to achieve controlled release and enhance osteogenic efficacy. Quercetin load materials are commonly categorized into polymer-based materials [22, 24–26], inorganic materials [7, 9, 27, 28], nanocarriers [22, 29, 30], coatings or surface-functionalized materials [31–33], and occasional direct application [34]. Schematic overview of major types of quercetin-composited biomaterials

Polymer-based materials primarily encompass polycaprolactone (PCL) [8], chitosan [35], collagen [36], silk fibroin [20], gelatin [37], and various hydrogels [24]. These materials exhibit favorable biocompatibility, biodegradability, and good processability. They can closely match the mechanical properties of bone tissue and enable co-delivery of bioactive molecules. By tuning polymer degradation kinetics, release profiles can be aligned with the timeline of bone healing. Common composite strategies include physical blending/mixing and electrospinning [8, 14]. Physical blending is straightforward and widely applied, allowing incorporation of quercetin during hydrogel or scaffold matrix preparation. Electrospinning produces quercetin-loaded nanofibrous scaffolds with high specific surface area and controllable topography, which promote cell attachment [25].

Inorganic materials mainly comprise hydroxyapatite (HAp) [11], calcium phosphate [7], calcium carbonate [35], and bioactive glass [38]. Their chemical composition resembles the mineral phase of bone, conferring excellent osseointegration, high mechanical strength, and structural stability. Representative composite methods include impregnation/adsorption [36], co-precipitation [27], and powder mixing. For instance, vacuum freeze-drying enables impregnation of quercetin solution onto nano-HAp microspheres, achieving near-linear sustained release over 28 days (cumulative release 74.68%) [11].

Nanocarriers primarily include solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) [22], nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) [7], mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) [36, 39], zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) [4], and microemulsions (MEs) [3]. These systems offer high drug-loading capacity, enhanced bioavailability, and potential for targeted and controlled release. However, disadvantages include complex synthesis protocols, potential nanotoxicity at high doses, and the requirement for in-depth characterization of nanoparticle–biomaterial interactions. Common composite methods include encapsulation and incorporation into porous architectures [22]. Such nanocarriers are frequently embedded within larger scaffolds or hydrogels [40] to facilitate handling and implantation. Examples include quercetin-loaded SLNs incorporated into HAp-PCLA hydrogels [22].

Coating materials enable direct surface modification to enhance bioactivity and anti-inflammatory performance. Techniques such as co-deposition are particularly suitable for surface engineering of implants [37].

Loading concentration and release behavior of quercetin

Detection methods for quercetin predominantly encompassed UV-Vis and HPLC/HPLC-MS, supplemented by biochemical assays such as the TFC method [35] and MicroBCA method [32, 34]. Within quercetin-loaded composite materials, loading concentrations can be broadly categorized into four groups: high (≥ 200 µM or ≥ 10 wt%) [28, 29, 41], medium (50–200 µM or 0.1–10 wt%) [22, 24, 26], low (< 50 µM or < 0.1 wt%) [23, 31, 38], and unspecified ranges [4, 12, 32].

The release kinetics of quercetin typically exhibit an initial burst release followed by a sustained release phase, with many studies reporting biphasic release patterns. For instance, the QT-NLC/CPC [7] achieved approximately 20% release over 21 days, conforming to the Weibull model. The release amount generally correlates positively with the loading concentration, demonstrating concentration-dependent behavior.

Factors influencing release profiles include the carrier material (e.g., hydrogels, nanoparticles, porous scaffolds), loading method (e.g., physical adsorption, chemical conjugation), and microenvironmental conditions (e.g., pH, ROS, enzymatic degradation). Hydrogels [3, 26, 41] and porous scaffolds [7, 20] primarily achieve prolonged sustained release through physical barriers and diffusion control; in contrast, smart responsive systems (pH-responsive [4, 36] or ROS-responsive [37, 41]) enable targeted accelerated release under specific pathological conditions, such as acidic environments or high oxidative stress. Quercetin concentration and biocompatibility in in vivo and in vitro studies

Studies on cellular and animal experiments shows that quercetin-loaded composite materials generally exhibit good biocompatibility, as confirmed by assays such as CCK-8, MTT, live/dead staining, or histological analysis.

Reported quercetin concentrations in cell-based studies (with units uniformly converted to µM for comparative analysis) fall into several ranges. Low concentrations (2–5 µM) [9, 32, 38] were used in some experiments; notably, Yang et al. indicated that cell viability significantly declined at quercetin concentrations ≥ 4 µM [9], while Kim et al. reported that pretreatment with 5 µM quercetin inhibited proliferation but enhanced osteogenic gene expression [34].

Concentrations in the 10–100 µM [7, 29] range were commonly employed and generally showedno cytotoxicity, along with antioxidant and osteogenic-promoting effects; however, higher concentrations (≥ 100 µM) were frequently associated with suppressed cell proliferation or cytotoxicity [8, 12, 22]. One study indicatedthat concentrations up to 36.63 µg/mL (approximately 121.2 µM) lacked cytotoxicity, though this study also noted that the optimal concentration for biological functionality was around 60 µM [27]. Another study demonstrated that while 80 µM pure quercetin treatment for 72 h decreased cell viability to 25%, the SrQ complex at the same concentration reduced its cytotoxicity and enhanced biocompatibility [28].

An interesting phenomenon was observed in animal models: in periodontitis studies, applied quercetin concentrations were typically low (2 µM [9]; 4 µM [38]; 12.5 µM [3]), possibly due to the small size of periodontal bone defects and their abundant vascular supply. In contrast,µ for larger bone defects (such as calvarial or femoral models), concentrations were generally ≥ 20 µM [14], likely reflecting the need for higher loading to maintain effective long-term drug levels.

In vitro studies

In vitro studies have utilized a diverse array of cell types, primarily macrophage cell lines (e.g., RAW264.7, BMDMs), mesenchymal stem cells (e.g., BMSCs, MSCs, rBMSCs, hBMSCs, AD-hMSCs, OMSCs, PDLSCs, hADSC), osteoblast cell lines (e.g., MG-63, Saos-2, hFOBs, MC3T3-E1), endothelial cells (e.g., HUVECs), and fibroblasts (e.g., HFFs, NIH3T3). These cell types span multiple facets, including immune responses, bone repair, angiogenesis, and connective tissue regeneration.

In vitro experimental methodologies were chiefly employed to evaluate cytotoxicity, proliferation, differentiation, inflammatory responses, and molecular mechanisms. These encompassed cell viability assays (e.g., MTT, CCK-8, MTS), assessments of osteogenic differentiation (e.g., ALP activity, ARS mineralization staining, Von Kossa staining), gene expression analyses (e.g., RT-PCR), protein expression detection (e.g., Western blot, immunofluorescence), and assays for cell polarization, migration, and senescence (e.g., flow cytometry, scratch assay, SA-β-gal staining). Collectively, these experiments demonstrate the impact of quercetin-loaded composite materials on cellular behavior, including the promotion of osteogenic differentiation, suppression of inflammation, and modulation of cell fate.

In terms of gene expression, quercetin-loaded composite materials consistently upregulated osteogenic-related genes (e.g., RUNX2, COL1, OCN, OPN, BMP2, OSX) and angiogenic genes (e.g., VEGF, bFGF, ANG-1), while concurrently downregulating inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS, RANKL) [43]. This indicates that quercetin can enhance osteogenic differentiation, promote bone formation and vascularization, and attenuate inflammatory responses, thereby fostering a microenvironment conducive to bone repair.

With regard to protein expression, quercetin-loaded composite materials upregulated osteogenic marker proteins (e.g., OCN, RUNX2, OPN, BMP2), signaling pathway proteins (e.g., p-AKT, p-ERK, p-PI3K, β-catenin, p-SMAD, CX43, RTK, RAP1), and M2 macrophage markers (e.g., CD206, ARG-1), while simultaneously downregulating inflammatory proteins (e.g., iNOS, CD86, p-P65) [43] and senescence-associated proteins (e.g., P16, P21, P53, γH2AX). These alterations substantiate quercetin’s role at the protein level in facilitating bone regeneration, inhibiting inflammation, and mitigating cellular senescence, thereby further bolstering its potential as a material for bone tissue engineering. In vivo studies

In vivo experiments have employed a variety of animal models, predominantly rodents such as Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, Wistar rats, Balb/c nu mice, Balb/C nude mice, ICR mice、C57/BL6 mice, alongside non-mammalian models including fertilized chick embryos [8] and zebrafish [12]. Surgical sites focused on bone-relevant regions including bone defect models (calvarial defects in SD rats, femoral condyle or tibial defects, and femoral distal implants), subcutaneous implantation models (dorsal subcutaneous in rats [31]); angiogenesis models (chick chorioallantoic membrane [8]); tumor models (murine hip tumors [36]); periodontitis models and periapical lesion model [28].

Radiographic assessments primarily utilized micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), often supplemented by X-ray imaging. These evaluations consistently demonstrated that quercetin-loaded composite materials significantly facilitated bone regeneration, as evidenced by increased bone mineral density (BMD), augmented bone volume (BV) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV), enhanced trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) and number (Tb.N), and reduced alveolar bone resorption distance (CEJ-ABC distance).

Histological analysis constituted a key method for evaluating bone repair, employing various staining and labeling techniques. Routine staining (e.g., H&E, Masson’s trichrome, Goldner’s trichrome, Safranin O [27]) revealed increased new bone formation, enriched collagen matrix, attenuated inflammation, and improved tissue integration in quercetin groups. Specialized staining (e.g., ARS [8, 24, 25, 27, 34, 42]) for calcium nodule/calcification assessment, TRAP [11, 22, 32] for osteoclast detection, calcein labeling [24] for mineralization rate) revealing enhanced calcification, accelerated mineralization, and reduced osteoclast activity. Immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence (IHC/IF) showed significant upregulation of osteogenic-related proteins (OCN, OPN, RUNX2, BMP2, COL1) [22–25, 37, 42], promotion of angiogenesis (VEGF) [25, 42], induction of macrophage polarization toward the pro-reparative M2 phenotype (CD206↑, ARG-1↑, iNOS↓) [22], and suppression of inflammatory signaling (p-P65↓) [9, 32]. Additionally, Mostofi et al. observed that QCW scaffolds promoted epithelialization and collagen proliferation in rat wounds [31], while Shi et al. demonstrated that the quercetin-loaded delivery system (MSN@QPC) induced tumor cell apoptosis (TUNEL-positive) and proliferation inhibition (Ki-67↓) [36]. Discussion

Background of quercetin in bone regeneration

Quercetin, a natural flavonoid compound, has emerged as a promising bioactive agent in bone tissue regeneration due to its multifaceted biological properties. The integration of quercetin into composite biomaterials significantly enhances its therapeutic potential, addressing challenges such as poor aqueous solubility (approximately 1 mg/L) and low bioavailability [44, 45]. Current research has explored various delivery systems, including hydrogels, ceramic scaffolds, electrospun fibers, and nanoparticles, to achieve controlled and sustained release of quercetin, thereby improving bone regeneration outcomes [46].

Quercetin directly promotes the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of bone-related cells such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and osteoblasts by upregulating key osteogenic transcription factors and late-stage differentiation markers. These markers include RUNX2, type I collagen (COL1/COL1A1), osteocalcin (OCN/BGLAP), osteopontin (OPN/SPP1), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) [14, 47, 48]. The underlying molecular mechanisms involve the modulation of multiple osteogenesis-related signaling pathways, including the activation of the BMP/SMAD pathway (upregulating βBMP-2, SMAD2, TGF-β) [12, 27, 49], promotion of ERK, p38, and AKT phosphorylation [11, 34], activation of the PI3K-Akt pathway to reverse metabolic suppression and support osteogenesis [4, 50], regulation of the WNT/BMP signaling pathway [51] via the miR-206/Cx43 axis [30], and upregulation of the clock gene Per1 expression by enhancing its m6A RNA methylation level [38].

Furthermore, quercetin inhibits osteoclast differentiation by reducing the RANKL/OPG ratio and the expression of NFATc1 and TRAP [52–54]. Song et al. demonstrated that quercetin suppresses RANKL-mediated osteoclast differentiation [14]. In a zebrafish osteoporosis model, Li et al. showed that quercetin-chitosan complexes downregulate TRAP and hydroxyproline levels, reducing bone resorption via the RANKL pathway [12]. (Fig. 4)

Angiogenesis is critical for the survival and functional integration of newly formed bone tissue, and quercetin has been shown to promote angiogenesis by upregulating the expression of VEGF and its regulatory factors, such as αHIF-1α [25]. Several studies have functionally validated quercetin’s pro-angiogenic capabilities. Raj Preeth et al. utilized the chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) model to directly demonstrate that quercetin-zinc complexes significantly increase vascular branching points and vessel length [8]. Yang et al. reported that quercetin treatment in human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) tube formation assays resulted in a 200% increase in vascular branching points [4].

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of quercetin

Quercetin exerts critical anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects during bone regeneration. By modulating the immune microenvironment—reducing inflammation and promoting macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory phenotype—it fosters an osteogenic niche, mitigates inflammation-induced bone loss, and indirectly enhances bone regeneration.

In bone defects or inflammatory conditions, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce oxidative stress, impairing osteoblast differentiation and favoring osteoclastogenesis [55]. Quercetin’s antioxidant capacity underpins its action: it scavenges ROS, activates endogenous antioxidant pathways, shields bone cells from oxidative damage, and supports matrix mineralization and angiogenesis [56, 57]. Chen et al. demonstrated that quercetin markedly reduces ROS levels in osteoporotic mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and restores impaired mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby providing metabolic energy (e.g., increased ATP production) essential for osteogenic differentiation [37].

Quercetin activates the PPARγ pathway [32] and downregulates miR-21a-5p, relieving its inhibition of PDCD4 and thereby suppressing p65 nuclear translocation and IκBα degradation [9, 29, 43] to block NF-κB activation [58–60]. It modulates MAPKs [61, 62], including ERK, JNK, and p38, to attenuate ROS-induced signaling. Activation of the ERK signaling pathway, in concert with quercetin [63], upregulates Nrf2 by promoting its dissociation from Keap1 (quercetin has also been shown to activate Nrf2 [33]), facilitates Nrf2 nuclear translocation [64]. Nrf2 signaling antagonizes NF-κB activity, further inhibiting NF-κB [65]. Additionally, quercetin regulates PI3K/Akt to protect against apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction [4, 66]. These interacting signaling pathways ultimately enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses.

This antioxidant activity extends to immune modulation. Reduced ROS levels and suppressed pro-oxidant pathways decrease TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β secretion while elevating IL-10 and TGF-β [27, 67]. This shift promotes macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 [3, 22]. The resulting anti-inflammatory microenvironment, enriched with M2-derived paracrine factors (VEGF, BMP-2) [22], indirectly augments osteogenic and angiogenic responses in adjacent cells (see Sect. "Background of quercetin in bone regeneration").

The cascade of “antioxidation → NF-κB inhibition → M2 polarization → osteogenic signaling” forms a coherent multilevel regulatory network. Immune modulation achieves antioxidation via ROS clearance and NF-κB suppression, while reprogramming macrophages creates an anti-inflammatory milieu that drives bone regeneration through BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation. This framework elucidates the superior regenerative efficacy of quercetin-loaded composites in complex bone defects.

Research potential of quercetin-loaded composite materials in complex bone defect

In addition to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, quercetin exhibits multiple biological functions, including antibacterial, anti-aging, and antitumor activities. These multifaceted functions make it particularly beneficial for bone regeneration in complex clinical settings characterized by oxidative stress, inflammation, or immune dysregulation, where it can enhance healing efficiency, reduce complications, and improve long-term integration. Its applications can be broadly categorized as follows:

Trauma-induced bone defects (e.g., fractures, vehicular accidents, or post-surgical bone loss): Post-traumatic local ROS levels surge dramatically, triggering inflammatory cascades, cellular apoptosis, and delayed healing. Quercetin rapidly scavenges ROS, suppresses inflammation induced by the NF-κB pathway, and promotes MSCs’ osteogenic differentiation [29, 68]. Infection-associated bone defects (e.g., osteomyelitis, implant infections): Bacterial infections exacerbate inflammation and oxidative stress, impeding osteogenesis. Quercetin exerts antimicrobial effects by disrupting bacterial cell walls and interfering with bacterial energy metabolism, thereby effectively combating infections [69–72]. Chen et al. developed quercetin carbon dots (QCDs), which, in an infected rat bone defect model, eradicated Staphylococcus aureus by perturbing bacterial metabolism and activated the Rap1 signaling pathway via targeting receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), thereby enhancing osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis [41]. Bone regeneration in chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, osteoporosis, aging): Chronic conditions such as diabetes [73] and osteoporosis [74] are accompanied by persistent low-level ROS and inflammation, resulting in an imbalance between bone formation and resorption [56]. Quercetin effectively scavenges ROS and upregulates the expression of multiple endogenous antioxidant enzymes, counteracting oxidative damage in the bone microenvironment [33, 75, 76]. Furthermore, quercetin ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction, inhibits aging-related signaling pathways, and enhances the osteogenic potential of senescent stem cells [77–79]. Oral and periodontal bone regeneration (e.g., periodontitis, alveolar bone resorption): Addressing the complex inflammatory microenvironment within periodontal pockets (persistent infection, excessive inflammation, oxidative stress, and bone defects) [80], researchers have constructed controlled-release systems for sustained quercetin delivery, leveraging synergistic antibacterial, hemostatic, and immunomodulatory effects with other components to optimize the periodontal microenvironment and promote osteogenesis [4, 25, 38, 81]. Bone regeneration in immune dysregulation or inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, graft-versus-host disease): For such conditions, quercetin improves joint metabolic homeostasis by alleviating articular inflammation, including inhibition of the JAK1/STAT3/HIF-1α signaling pathway and targeted modulation of caspase-8 via ferroptosis and pyroptosis pathways to ameliorate disease states [82–84]. Implant-related osseointegration and bone cement applications (e.g., joint replacements, implant insertions): Postoperative inflammation and oxidative stress can lead to implant loosening. Surface coatings with quercetin composites hold potential to enhance osseointegration and anti-inflammatory properties at the implant interface [32, 85, 86]. Other emerging applications (e.g., reconstruction post-tumor resection): Quercetin exerts effects through inducing tumor cell apoptosis [87, 88], inhibiting cancer cell proliferation [89, 90], modulating signaling pathways, and enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity [91]. Concurrently, it improves bone microstructure while intervening in tumor growth [92], making it a promising candidate for reconstructing bone defects after tumor excision.

Clinical trials of quercetin-loaded composite materials for bone regeneration

Currently, research on quercetin in the field of bone regeneration is predominantly at the cellular and animal experimental stages. Most studies that have advanced to clinical trials involve oral formulations for the prevention or treatment of osteoporosis [93]. Only one clinical study focuses on local administration for periodontitis (NCT05928546). This study has progressed to a Phase II randomized controlled trial, where researchers inject a gel containing 10% quercetin into periodontal pockets, evaluating treatment outcomes for periodontitis based on metrics such as clinical attachment level (CAL), probing depth, interferon IFN-γ analysis, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Mid-2024 results indicate a 20% increase in bone regeneration rate and a significant reduction in inflammation [93]. Currently, no mature clinical products utilizing quercetin-loaded composite materials for local administration in bone defect repair are available.

Current challenges and perspectives

Despite the promising prospects of quercetin-loaded composite materials, several challenges persist:

The low water solubility and bioavailability of quercetin result in suboptimal local delivery efficiency, limiting its therapeutic efficacy. Although composite biomaterials have improved local delivery, there is a lack of consensus on optimal dosages for different clinical bone defect scenarios [7]. In conclusion, advancements in carrier systems and further research are expected to enable precise controlled release and targeted delivery of quercetin-loaded composite materials, enhancing biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy. Concurrently, more large-scale clinical trials are needed to facilitate its clinical translation.

Conclusion

This review synthesizes the current advancements in quercetin-loaded composite materials for bone regeneration. The findings demonstrate that quercetin promotes osteogenesis and angiogenesis, inhibits osteoclastogenesis, and exerts anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anti-aging effects via multiple signaling pathways. Quercetin-loaded composite biomaterials mitigate the pharmacokinetic limitations of quercetin through controlled release, and show robust regenerative potential in complex bone defect models. The effective concentration of quercetin primarily ranges from 10 to 100 µM, necessitating further delineation based on the specific type of bone defect. Future endeavors should encompass additional clinical trials to expedite its translational application in the field of bone regeneration.
