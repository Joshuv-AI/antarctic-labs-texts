Endovascular Rescue of Type III Endoleak From an Abandoned Fenestration After Fenestrated Endovascular Aneurysm Repair
Authors: Mingzhou Jiang, Yulong Huang, Lixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType III endoleak after fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair of an abdominal aortic aneurysm is uncommon and difficult to treat when direct sac catheterization is not feasible.Case SummaryA 78-year-old man presented 1 year after fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair with 4 fenestrations. During the initial procedure, a fenestration for a right accessory renal artery could not be bridged and was occluded with an embolization device, leaving an abandoned fenestration. Follow-up computed tomography angiography showed sac enlargement, perigraft contrast, and perianeurysmal high attenuation fluid, consistent with type III endoleak. Transgraft sac access was achieved with dual steerable sheaths and a puncture needle, followed by coil embolization and relining with a modified stent graft. Computed tomography angiography 2 weeks later showed marked reduction of the endoleak.DiscussionThe abandoned fenestration was the likely source of late type III endoleak, and transgraft puncture enabled controlled access to an otherwise inaccessible cavity.Take-Home MessageTransgraft puncture, coil embolization, and stent graft relining may provide a feasible rescue strategy for selected cases.

History of Presentation

A 78-year-old man presented to the emergency department with persistent left lower abdominal pain for 1 day. Given the history of complex aortic repair and acute abdominal symptoms, recurrent aneurysm sac perfusion or contained rupture was suspected.

Past Medical History

Approximately 1 year earlier, the patient had undergone fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair for an abdominal aortic aneurysm at our institution. The previously implanted main graft was an Ankura aortic stent graft measuring 36 × 160 mm (LifeTech Scientific). The previous repair involved 4 fenestrations with visceral branch reconstruction. Visceral branch reconstruction was performed using Viabahn stent grafts (Gore), including an 8 × 50-mm stent for the superior mesenteric artery, a 6 × 25-mm stent for the left renal artery, and a 5 × 50-mm stent for the right renal artery. One fenestration had been created for a right accessory renal artery. However, repeated attempts at bridging stent placement through this fenestration were unsuccessful, and the fenestration was subsequently occluded with an embolization device, leaving an abandoned fenestration.

This prior abandoned fenestration was considered a potential structural weak point and a possible source of late type III endoleak.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included type I endoleak, type III endoleak, contained aneurysm rupture, and perigraft hemorrhage.

Investigations

Surveillance computed tomography angiography (CTA) performed several months after the initial repair and repeated shortly before presentation demonstrated contrast outside the abdominal aortic stent graft, enlargement of the excluded aneurysm sac to 74 × 78 mm compared with the prior study, and high attenuation perianeurysmal fluid concerning for blood seepage (Figures 1A and 1B). Given the previously abandoned fenestration and the imaging findings of perigraft contrast with sac enlargement, the diagnosis was most consistent with type III endoleak related to the abandoned fenestration after fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair.

Diagnostic angiography demonstrated a localized endoleak cavity adjacent to the mid segment of the abdominal aortic stent graft, measuring approximately 2 × 5 cm (Figure 1C).

Management

Because direct catheterization of the endoleak cavity was not feasible, a transgraft approach was undertaken. Through left common femoral access, dual steerable sheaths, 8 F, 55 cm (H&H Medical), were advanced to the target segment. A FuThrough endovascular puncture needle system (LifeTech Scientific) was then used to traverse the stent graft fabric and establish access to the endoleak cavity. After the tract was crossed, a catheter was advanced into the sac, and contrast injection confirmed intrasac position (Figure 1D).

Dense coil embolization was performed until dense packing of the endoleak cavity was achieved (Figures 1E and 1F). Embolization was performed using 23 detachable fibered coils (Boston Scientific), including a 1 F-IDC 2D 14 mm × 20-cm coil, 1 0.018-inch Interlock 2D 14 mm × 50-m coil, 6 0.018-inch Interlock 2D 18 mm × 50-cm coils, 3 0.018-inch Interlock 2D 20 mm × 50-cm coils, 3 0.035-inch Interlock 2D 10 mm × 20-cm coils, 6 0.035-inch Interlock 2D 12 mm × 20-cm coils, and 3 0.035-inch Interlock 2D 18 mm × 40-cm coils.

To seal the transgraft access site and reinforce the repair, an abdominal aortic stent graft measuring 40 × 200 mm (LifeTech Scientific) was modified on the table by removing the distal covered segment and preserving a 60-mm proximal covered segment. After exchange to an extra stiff guidewire (Boston Scientific) and placement of a 14-F sheath (Cook Medical), the modified graft was deployed with its proximal landing zone distal to the right renal artery and its distal end proximal to the bilateral iliac limbs (Figure 1G).

Completion angiography demonstrated brisk flow through the main lumen and satisfactory exclusion of the endoleak cavity without significant residual opacification (Figure 1H).

Follow-Up

Early follow-up CTA 2 weeks later demonstrated marked reduction of the endoleak, with stable positioning of the relining stent graft and embolization coils (Figures 1I and 1J).

Discussion

Type III endoleak is a clinically important complication after endovascular aneurysm repair because it represents a high-pressure endoleak that can expose the aneurysm sac again to systemic arterial pressure and increase the risk of sac expansion or rupture.1,2 The rates of type III endoleak range from approximately 0.7% in contemporary standard endovascular aneurysm repair series to 3.0% to 4.5% in earlier or mixed device cohorts.1,3 Type III endoleak is generally caused by component separation or structural failure of the endograft fabric, and therefore usually require active treatment once identified.4

In complex fenestrated or branched repair, endoleak mechanisms may be more difficult to define than in standard infrarenal repair. The Society for Vascular Surgery reporting standards emphasize the need for accurate classification of endoleak source in complex aortic repair involving renal and mesenteric arteries.5 Target vessel–related endoleaks after fenestrated or branched endovascular aneurysm repair may arise from distal target vessel seal failure, bridging stent integrity failure, or loss of connection between the bridging stent and the fenestration or branch interface.6 These mechanisms are relevant to the present case because the previous repair involved multiple fenestrations and visceral branch reconstruction.

In this patient, the key anatomic feature was an abandoned fenestration created for a right accessory renal artery during the initial fenestrated repair. Because bridging stent placement through this fenestration was unsuccessful, the fenestration was occluded with an embolization device. This residual fenestration and closure interface likely represented a vulnerable site for late type III endoleak. Although abandoned fenestration–related endoleak is rarely described as a distinct entity, its mechanism is consistent with an iatrogenic graft defect or fenestration interface failure.

The main technical challenge was the inability to catheterize the endoleak cavity through a conventional route. Previous reports and reviews of type III endoleaks emphasize relining or secondary endovascular reconstruction as major treatment strategies.7 In this case, transgraft puncture created controlled access to the endoleak cavity, coil embolization reduced sac perfusion by densely packing the cavity, and relining with a modified stent graft sealed the transgraft access site and reinforced the repair. Because transgraft puncture itself creates a new fabric defect, subsequent relining was an essential step rather than an optional adjunct.

This case illustrates a stepwise endovascular rescue strategy for selected type III endoleaks arising from abandoned fenestrations after fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair. The technique may be considered when conventional catheterization routes are not feasible, provided that the puncture site can be safely relined and essential branch perfusion can be preserved.
