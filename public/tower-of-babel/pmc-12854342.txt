Flashcards versus educational video: A comparison in order to promote laparoscopic surgical instrument learning
Authors: Setareh Sadeghi, Mohammad Javad Tarrahi, Ahmad Ghadami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Since laparoscopic surgical instruments are sensitive and expensive; Therefore, it is necessary to use new methods to learn these tools, and due to the ease of use and the availability of flash cards and videos, as new educational tools, the present study aims to compare the effects of teaching laparoscopic tools with The use of flash cards and video training on the level of student ‘s education Operating room nursing was performed.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This semi-experimental, two-group pre-test–post-test study was conducted on 52 nursing students in the operating room program in 2023, who were included in the study through convenient sampling. Participants were randomly assigned into two groups of 26 using simple random allocation. The names of two classes, semesters 4 and 6, were provided to the statistics instructor, who evenly divided the individuals into two groups for training using flashcards and educational videos. The flashcards included 13 double-sided cards, with the tool’s image and the tool’s name along with its application. The educational video was a 6-minute researcher-made film. The content of both methods included tools used in laparoscopic cholecystectomy, such as the clip applier, lens, Veress needle, Metzembaum scissors, fan-street, Maryland, J-hook, endo-clinch, wire cauter, and various trocars and ports. The data collection instrument included a researcher-made test with 20 multiple-choice questions, the content and face validity of which were assessed by 10 faculty members from Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, laparoscopic surgeons, and experienced operating room technologists. Additionally, 10 CVI forms were provided to validate the content of the learning test questionnaire, which assessed each question individually based on five criteria: necessity, importance, relevance, clarity, and simplicity. The final assessment was based on a formula. The minimum score for students at the end of each test was 0, and the maximum was 20. The reliability of the instrument was calculated using the Spearman-Brown score, yielding a result of 0.615, which was approved. Data were collected in two phases: before the intervention and two weeks after it. The data were analyzed using Chi-square tests, independent t-tests, paired t-tests, and a significance level of P < 0.05.RESULTS:The two groups did not show significant differences in terms of gender, age, GPA, academic term, or scores in theoretical courses on operating room tools and equipment, and surgical technology in gastrointestinal and endocrine areas (P > 0.05). Before the intervention, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean learning scores between the two groups (P = 0.607). The mean learning score for students in the flashcard training group before and after the intervention (3.98 ± 11 vs. 1.97 ± 15.17) was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Additionally, results showed that the mean learning score for students in the video training group before and after the intervention (3.69 ± 10.92 vs. 1.06 ± 18.57) was also statistically significant (P < 0.05). The mean learning score for the video training group after the intervention was significantly higher than that of the flashcard group (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:The results of the study indicated that training on laparoscopic tools in cholecystectomy using both flashcards and educational videos led to an increase in the students’ learning outcomes. Therefore, it is recommended to use both methods as complementary educational tools in the training of operating room nursing students.

Introduction

The clinical environment for surgical technology students is the operating room, which is a unique setting characterized by crowding, stress, noise, a variety of surgical procedures, and the simultaneous presence of emergency and elective surgeries. This environment poses serious challenges to education while ensuring patient safety.[1] One of the issues in clinical education is the mismatch between the number of instructors and students. Due to the gap between theoretical and practical training, students may struggle to perform effectively in clinical settings without guidance from their instructors. This shortage of mentors and the lack of continuous access to instructors can result in inconsistent and ineffective training for students.[2] Consequently, quality education outside the operating room is essential for students to adequately prepare for acquiring clinical skills.[3] One of the major challenges in teaching operating room nursing students is providing effective training to develop skilled professionals who can deliver services effectively in clinical environments.

Currently, minimally invasive surgeries have become the most important part of general surgery, with laparoscopic surgery being one of these techniques. Laparoscopic surgery allows surgeons to examine internal organs without the need for large incisions in the skin. During laparoscopic procedures, surgeons make several small incisions, typically a few millimeters long. Additionally, a laparoscope (a long, thin tube with a camera and light) is inserted through one of the incisions, displaying endoscopic images on the operating room monitors, allowing surgeons to have a clear and magnified view of the surgical area. Subsequently, specialized and complex surgical instruments are introduced through other small incisions for exploration, removal, or repair of issues within the body.[4] While these minimally invasive techniques offer significant benefits for patient recovery, they also require advanced surgical skills that are challenging for operating room nursing students to master, placing them at risk of making mistakes. Ideally, universities should familiarize students with modern technical skills, including laparoscopic skills, to complement their educational learning.[5] This is one of the important topics in surgical technology training; however, due to the vast number and diversity of these tools, visual methods should be employed to facilitate effective retention.[3]

Several methods exist for teaching surgical instruments to students, including video training, face-to-face instruction, flashcards, and group training.[6] Among modern educational methods, video training and flashcards are noteworthy.[3] Flashcards are a creative method that assists learners in reflecting on their learning.[7] However, disadvantages of flashcards include the difficulty of learning hard and abstract words and the lack of auditory elements in the flashcard system.[8]

Another innovative educational method is video training. The results of a systematic review study in 2018 indicated that video-based training has potential in surgical education, as trainees face significant barriers in their practice and should use educational videos alongside standard techniques. Advantages of video training include the ability to store information, continuity of content, reduced anxiety during training, the addition of new materials to previous content, and the possibility of immediate editing.[9] However, due to the lack of interactive elements, video training cannot replace traditional in-person classrooms. Some disadvantages of video-based education include the requirement for equipment (such as mobile phones or laptops), the difficulty of editing videos, lack of learner control, and promotion of individualism. The use of educational films may not engage some learners due to a lack of experience, especially when learners need to perform essential tasks or be hands-on.[10] Given the difficulties and shortcomings of traditional methods in teaching surgical instruments, researchers have sought to employ modern educational methods such as flashcards and video training in teaching operating room nursing students. Additionally, due to the absence of studies comparing the superiority of these methods in teaching surgical instruments, this project aims to compare the effectiveness of laparoscopic instrument training in cholecystectomy surgery using flashcards and video training on the learning outcomes of operating room nursing students at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in Iran [Figure 1].

Materials and Methods

Study designing and setting

This study was a quasi-experimental two-group pre-test-post-test design, conducted over two phases: the preparation of educational content and the assessment of its effectiveness on the learning of operating room nursing students at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences from April 13 to April 27, 2023.

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was determined using the mean formula and a previous study, resulting in (3) 26 participants. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the total sample size for each group was calculated to be 30 participants. The Z1 value for a 95% confidence level was 1.96, Z2 for an 80% power was 0.84, S1 was an estimate of the standard deviation of the learning score in the intervention group, equal to 1.2, S2[11] was an estimate of the standard deviation of the learning score in the control group, equal to 1.1, and d was the minimum difference in mean learning scores between the two groups, which was 0.9.

Data collection tools and technique

Initially, the researchers designed the educational content for laparoscopic cholecystectomy surgical instruments based on the experiences of operating room technologists[1213] with backgrounds as scrub and circulating nurses, as well as using reference books in operating room nursing and through careful observation of surgical procedures by the researchers. The educational content was clinically oriented and included information related to the suction-irrigation set, clip applier, lens, veress needle, metzenbaum scissors, fan-strait, maryland, J-hook, endoclamp, coagulation wire, various trocars, and port placement.

The researchers first obtained images of laparoscopic instruments used in cholecystectomy surgery from reference books.[1213] The images were then edited using the “Paint” software. Next, black-and-white images were scanned to create 13 cardboard flashcards measuring 8 × 10 cm. The front side of each flashcard contained images of the instruments from various angles, along with coded sections represented numerically. The back side included the names of the coded parts and their applications.

The researchers first visited the operating room unit at Al-Zahra Hospital in Isfahan. They arranged the desired instruments on a work table covered with a green drape. Using a professional camera, they recorded a video while simultaneously demonstrating each instrument and explaining its name and application. Finally, the 6-minute video was edited using the “Filmora” software.

In this study, 52 students from the 4th and 6th semesters of the continuous bachelor›s program in Operating Room Technology at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, who met the inclusion criteria, were enrolled in the study after obtaining informed consent. The students were randomly allocated to two groups: flashcards and educational videos. Initially, the list of both 4th and 6th semester classes was provided to the statistics instructor, who then used a random number table to divide the participants from both classes into the two training groups. In both groups, the participants were evenly distributed regarding their semester, gender, age, GPA, and scores from the courses on tools and equipment in the operating room and gastrointestinal and endocrine technology. Inclusion criteria included all second- and third-year students who had taken and passed relevant theoretical courses, including surgical instruments and operating room equipment and gastrointestinal and endocrine technology, as well as providing full consent to participate in the study and possessing a smartphone. Exclusion criteria encompassed unwillingness to continue collaborating with the research team, failure to watch the educational video, non-use of flashcards, and non-participation in pre- and post-intervention tests. After designing and preparing the educational content, a coordinated session was arranged via the Telegram messaging app with students from each group on the same date and time. Two separate classrooms in the school of nursing and midwifery were designated for conducting the pre-intervention test (pre-test) and training on laparoscopic instruments in cholecystectomy surgery (flashcard group and video training group). The pre-test was administered in person and written in two separate classrooms for each group. Following the completion of the test, a one-hour class session was held for the flashcard training group. The researcher provided the prepared flashcards to the students while simultaneously explaining the instruments. Subsequently, a pre-test was conducted for the video training group in a separate classroom, followed by a lesson using the researcher-made educational video. Students from both groups were instructed to review the educational materials using the flashcards and video at least twice a week over the following two weeks. After a two-week interval from the pre-test, a post-test was administered to both groups. At the end of the study, to ensure educational equity, the educational materials from the other group were provided to both groups [Figure 2].

Flow of participants through each step of the study

The data collection instrument consisted of a 20-question multiple-choice written test, created by the researcher, regarding laparoscopic cholecystectomy surgical tools. The minimum score a student could achieve at the end of each test was 0, and the maximum was 20. The content and face validity of the test questions (pre-test and post-test) were assessed by 10 faculty members from Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, laparoscopic surgeons, and experienced operating room technologists in laparoscopic surgery. Additionally, 10 CVI forms were provided to confirm the content validity of the learning assessment questionnaire, which contained an evaluation of each question based on five criteria: necessity, importance, relevance, clarity, and simplicity. Finally, the validity was measured based on a specific formula. The reliability of the instrument was calculated using the Spearman-Brown formula, resulting in a value of 0.615, which was confirmed as acceptable.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee in Medical Sciences at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The researchers then introduced themselves to all second- and third-year nursing students present at the School of Nursing and Midwifery in Isfahan, explaining the study process and research objectives. Students were invited to join a shared group created on the telegram messaging platform. Those who met the inclusion criteria subsequently entered the study after providing written informed consent.

Statistical analysis method

The obtained data were analyzed using Chi-square tests, independent t-tests, and paired t-tests with SPSS software version 16, at a significance level of P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to answer the question of which educational method—flashcards or videos—could be more effective in teaching laparoscopic surgical instruments to nursing students in the operating room. The results indicated that both methods were effective in improving the average learning scores, but the mean learning score in the video training group was higher than that in the flashcard group. A study by Sadati et al. (2021) titled “Comparing the Impact of Two Teaching Methods on Learning and Satisfaction of Surgical Technologists (flashcards vs. mobile-based learning)” found that, given the effectiveness of both methods, it is recommended to use these methods, especially mobile applications (videos), for teaching surgical instruments.[3] Although the results of Sadati’s study align with the current research, it is noteworthy that in Sadati’s study, the video was presented through a mobile application, which students can access anytime and anywhere, positively impacting learning. It should also be mentioned that in the current study, the method of recording videos of surgical instruments by researchers and providing them to students also enhanced their learning. In line with the present research, a study by Abrishami Zadeh et al. (2021) showed that teaching through illustrated flashcards affects the learning of students with learning disabilities.[14] This finding can be explained by the fact that flashcards, as a type of mnemonic device, make unfamiliar content more familiar, turn meaningless information into meaningful data, and make abstract information more concrete.[15] Aligned with the current research, a study by Rahmavati et al. (2024)[16] showed that teaching catheterization for benign prostatic hyperplasia through video and electronic models increased student learning. Additionally, Mota et al. (2018)[17] demonstrated in a study that the use of simulation videos in teaching surgical instruments to surgical residents improved their learning. Yeh et al. (2024)[18] stated in their research that the use of simulation videos was more effective than other teaching methods in enhancing self-efficacy in patient care and reducing anxiety in nursing students. In a study by Chotiyarnwong and colleagues (2021)[19] comparing slide presentations with video presentations for teaching osteoporosis, the video method yielded better results, which aligns with the present research. In contrast to the current study, a study by Tsai et al. (2021)[20] showed that the use of electronic flashcards for teaching obstetrics and gynecology, although it increased scores, did not result in significant scores after graduation. The differing results between the current study and Tsai’s research may be attributed to the type of educational content used. In the present study, surgical instruments were taught through illustrated flashcards, while in Tsai’s study, the concepts of obstetrics were taught using electronic flashcards. Esposito et al. (2022)[21] concluded in a review study that teaching surgical instruments through videos is effective but poses challenges such as the quality of the educational film, camera quality, and duration of the video. The results of this study do not align with the current research; the difference may be due to content, video duration, and quality. Although both methods were effective in the present study, it seems that the higher learning outcomes associated with video training, based on previous studies, result from the fact that video training can cover a larger number of students with less energy expenditure.[11] Additionally, the repeatability of videos helps prevent distractions and enhances focus on work processes, significantly increasing individuals’ efficiency and productivity during tasks.

The preliminary results of this study indicated that there was no significant difference in the pre-test learning scores between the two groups (P = 0.607). However, after the training, the mean scores of both groups increased, showing a significant difference (P = 0.001). Additionally, the learning score in the video training method was higher than that in the flashcard method. Based on the results of this study, it appears that flashcards and videos can serve as complementary tools to traditional education in laparoscopic surgical instruments. Since these methods are accessible at any time and place can be used on laptops, tablets, and mobile phones (especially for video training), and are low-cost, a wide range of universities and students can utilize them for training in surgical and operating room nursing students, as well as newly qualified operating room nurses, thereby enhancing their education and learning. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the generalizability and impact of these educational methods on students' skills and clinical performance.

Among the strengths of the present study are the use of a standardized, researcher-developed test to assess learning of laparoscopic surgical instruments, the implementation of a new, low-cost educational method that can be used anywhere and anytime with any smart device (especially for video training), and the random allocation of samples to the study groups.

The results of this research indicate that training in laparoscopic instruments for cholecystectomy using both flashcards and educational videos is effective in enhancing the learning of nursing students in the operating room. However, learning is more pronounced with the video educational method. Therefore, if educational videos and flashcards are designed based on scientific principles, they can be effective as complementary educational tools in improving students’ learning. Similarly, by providing effective training to operating room students as future surgical assistants, higher quality services can be offered to patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Limitation and recommendation

Limitations of the study include the varying individual talents and abilities in acquiring skills, which were uncontrollable. Additionally, the small sample size relative to the available population is a factor that threatens the validity of this study and it’s recommended that future studies be conducted with a larger sample size.
