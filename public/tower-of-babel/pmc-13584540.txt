Programming Intrinsic States of Solvent-Free Glycolipids through Molecular Architecture and Thermal History
Authors: Wonhyeong Kim, David E. Hogan, Raina M. Maier, Minkyu Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amphiphilic molecules enable the formation of diverse ordered structures across soft materials systems. The solution-phase self-assembly of glycolipids has been extensively investigated. However, the structural organization of these materials in the absence of solvent remains less systematically understood. It remains unclear whether solvent-free intrinsic states are uniquely dictated by molecular architecture or permit processing-dependent state selection. By systematically varying sugar headgroup identity and alkyl-chain multiplicity under solvent-free conditions, the effects of molecular architecture and thermal history on intrinsic states are mapped. Time–temperature mapping across the glycolipid series distinguishes structure-dominated systems that converge toward a single outcome from transitional regimes where prior temperature–time history influences intrinsic state selection. Within the processing-sensitive regime, designed heat-treatment trajectories direct structural development and generate distinct mesoscale organizations. Spectroscopic analysis further reveals that this trajectory-dependent structural evolution involves strengthened intermolecular hydrogen-bonding networks within the constrained packing environment of the double-tail architecture. The resulting physical states range from viscous liquids to gels with tunable domain architectures. Rheological measurements demonstrate that bulk mechanical response is governed by mesoscale organization, with mechanical strength spanning approximately 3 orders of magnitude without altering chemical composition. These findings establish a structure–processing–property framework for solvent-free glycolipids and position thermal history as a programmable design parameter for engineering adaptive soft materials.

Introduction

Biosurfactants have attracted increasing attention as environmentally friendly alternatives to petrochemical-derived surfactants. , Among them, glycolipids represent one of the most widely studied classes. , In contrast to conventional petrochemical-derived surfactants, which typically possess relatively simple headgroup–tail architectures, glycolipids exhibit substantially greater structural complexity arising from diverse sugar chemistries, stereochemistry, and multifunctional headgroup interactions. Advances in microbial production and biobased chemical synthesis have enabled the preparation of structurally well-defined glycolipids with tunable sugar headgroups and alkyl-chain configurations. − These features extend the relevance of glycolipids beyond conventional application-driven uses, − establishing them as architecturally defined amphiphilic materials for investigating structure–processing–property relationships in soft matter.

Extensive efforts have examined glycolipid self-assembly and mesophase formation under lyotropic conditions, where hydration and concentration serve as primary organizing factors. Ordered structures including micellar, lamellar, and hexagonal phases have been characterized using scattering, microscopy, and rheological techniques. , Variations in headgroup chemistry and alkyl-chain architecture modulate lyotropic phase stability and morphology. , However, this body of work predominantly considers equilibrium structures formed in the presence of solvent and implicitly assumes that glycolipids enter hydration from equivalent solvent-free intrinsic states, without accounting for potential effects of molecular architecture or processing history.

In solvent-free amphiphilic materials, internal organization may depend not only on molecular architecture but also on thermal processing history, − raising the possibility that intrinsic states are not uniquely defined by structure alone. If intrinsic states are strictly architecture-determined, molecular design would fully dictate thermotropic behavior. Conversely, if multiple kinetically accessible states exist, processing history may act as a selection mechanism, enabling thermally programmable structural outcomes. For glycolipids, whether solvent-free intrinsic states fall into a structurally deterministic regime or permit processing-dependent state multiplicity remains unclear. Resolving this distinction requires direct examination of glycolipids under solvent-free conditions with controlled architectural variation and defined thermal histories.

In this study, we systematically investigate the intrinsic thermotropic states of solvent-free glycolipids by varying molecular architecture, including sugar headgroup identity and alkyl-chain number. By mapping how architectural parameters and thermal history govern physical state and internal organization, we distinguish between structurally deterministic intrinsic states and processing-dependent state multiplicity. For glycolipids exhibiting processing-dependent behavior, we further demonstrate how defined temperature–time pathways program mesoscale organization and the resulting mechanical response. Together, this work establishes a materials-level framework for understanding how molecular architecture and thermal history jointly govern intrinsic state accessibility in glycolipids.

Experimental Section

Materials

Architecturally defined glycolipids including Rha-C10 (rhamnosyl-β-hydroxydecanoate), Rha-C10–C10 (rhamnosyl-β-hydroxydecanoyl-β-hydroxydecanoate), Xyl-C10 (xylosyl-β-hydroxydecanoate), and Xyl-C10–C10 (xylosyl-β-hydroxydecanoate-β-hydroxydecanoate) were obtained from GlycoSurf, Inc. (UT, USA). These glycolipids were produced synthetically as single congeners. All samples had a purity of >95% and were used without further purification. To ensure completely solvent-free intrinsic states prior to thermal processing, all samples were dried under vacuum at room temperature for 48 h. The removal of residual solvents and thermal stability were confirmed by verifying negligible weight loss after heating the samples to 120 °C.

Polarized Optical Microscopy and Thermal Processing

Thermotropic phase behavior and mesoscale structural evolution were observed using a polarized optical microscope (Eclipse Ti2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) operated under crossed polarizers. Solvent-free glycolipid samples were placed on a bare glass slide and heated on a hot plate maintained at 120 °C for 30 min. Under these conditions, all four glycolipids became optically homogeneous melts, providing a common initial state for all subsequent thermal processing experiments (Figure S1). Immediately after heating, a coverslip was applied to sandwich the molten sample and produce a uniform thin film. The prepared samples were then transferred directly from the 120 °C hot plate to temperature-controlled environments pre-equilibrated at the target aging temperatures. Hot plates were used for aging at 20–80 °C, whereas a cold room and laboratory freezer were used for 4 °C and −20 °C, respectively. For sequence-programmed thermal trajectories, samples were likewise transferred directly between the corresponding pre-equilibrated environments. No programmed heating or cooling ramps were applied.

For time–temperature–transformation (TTT) mapping, the prepared samples were transferred to temperature-controlled environments preset at the target aging temperatures (80, 60, 50, 40, 30, 20, 4, and −20 °C). The temperatures of these environments were periodically verified using an external thermometer (TP-30, ThermoPro, Toronto, Canada). Samples were isothermally aged for up to 240 h, during which structural changes were periodically observed using POM, typically once per day.

For experiments involving specific sequence-programmed thermal trajectories, the temperature histories were controlled according to the thermal processing protocols (see Figures a and a).

Mesoscale Domain Identification and Analysis

Quantitative analysis of mesoscale domain size and number was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The spatial scale of the optical micrographs was calibrated using a standard micrometer scale, yielding 4.13 μm per pixel. To ensure objective quantification and minimize manual selection bias, images were binarized and domain parameters were extracted using the automated “Analyze Particles” function. A lower size threshold of 100 μm2 was applied to all images to exclude segmentation noise and small artifacts generated during the binarization process. Using this automated filtering process, only fully developed mesoscale domains were included to quantify the average domain size (mean ± standard deviation) and total domain number under each thermal processing condition.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Thermal transitions of representative thermally programmed Xyl-C10–C10 states were characterized using a Discovery DSC 250 (TA Instruments, DE, USA). Samples corresponding to an amorphous gel state without detectable mesoscale domains (aged at 4 °C for 240 h) and a gel-like state containing mesoscale domains (aged at 20 °C for 240 h) were prepared according to the thermal processing protocols described above. Approximately 25 mg of each sample was sealed in standard aluminum DSC pans, while an empty sealed pan was used as the reference. Samples were first equilibrated at 10 °C for 10 min and subsequently heated to 120 °C at a rate of 1 °C min–1 under a nitrogen atmosphere. Thermograms were analyzed using TRIOS software (TA Instruments), and transition temperatures were determined from the peak positions of the endothermic transitions.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to analyze the functional groups and intermolecular interactions of Xyl-C10–C10 across representative thermally evolved states selected from the TTT map. Following the respective thermal treatments, spectra were recorded at room temperature using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50R, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) operated in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. All spectra were acquired over a wavenumber range of 4000–600 cm–1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1 with an accumulation of 64 scans.

Oscillatory Rheology

The viscoelastic mechanical properties of Xyl-C10–C10 samples were characterized using small-amplitude oscillatory shear (SAOS) rheology on a Discovery Hybrid Rheometer (TA Instruments, DE, USA) equipped with a 20 mm stainless-steel parallel plate geometry (Smart-Swap) and a Peltier temperature-controlled stage. Instrument calibration procedures, including inertia, friction, and rotational mapping calibrations, were performed prior to each experiment. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C. Samples were carefully placed onto the lower plate and the upper geometry was lowered to the measurement position with a gap size of 1000 μm. Excess material around the edge of the plate was trimmed to ensure uniform sample loading. To evaluate the strain-dependent viscoelastic response, oscillatory strain sweep measurements were performed over a strain range of 0.01–100% at a constant angular frequency of 1 rad s–1 with 10 points per decade. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were recorded as a function of applied strain to determine the linear viscoelastic region and the onset of strain-induced structural softening.

Results and Discussion

Molecular Architecture-Dependent Physical States of Solvent-Free Glycolipids

The four glycolipids, Rha-C10, Rha-C10–C10, Xyl-C10, and Xyl-C10–C10 (Figure ), were selected to examine how controlled variations in headgroup chemistry and tail multiplicity influence amphiphilic packing and thermotropic behavior under solvent-free conditions. The rhamnose and xylose headgroups differ only by a single methyl substituent; however, this subtle structural difference modifies the steric profile and intermolecular interactions. In parallel, the transition from a single C10 chain to a double C10–C10 tail architecture increases the hydrophobic volume (v) while maintaining constant chain length (l). These modifications shift the packing balance described by the critical packing parameter 1 CPP=vl×a0 where a 0 denotes the effective headgroup area, providing a framework for comparing intrinsic packing tendencies across the series.

Chemical structures of glycolipids with varying headgroup identity and tail multiplicity. (a) Rha-C10 and (b) Rha-C10–C10 contain a rhamnose headgroup, while (c) Xyl-C10 and (d) Xyl-C10–C10 contain a xylose headgroup. C10 denotes a linear decyl alkyl chain.

At room temperature, the four glycolipids exhibit distinct solvent-free physical states. Xyl-C10 appears as an opaque solid, whereas Rha-C10 forms a transparent solid. In contrast, Xyl-C10–C10 exhibits a soft gel-like state, while Rha-C10–C10 behaves as a viscous liquid. These differences reflect systematic variations in intrinsic organization among the glycolipids. ,

For the single-tail molecules, hydrophobic chain volume (v) and length (l) are identical, and differences arise primarily from headgroup-dependent variations in a 0. The methyl substituent in rhamnose reduces headgroup polarity and modifies hydrogen-bonding and steric interactions relative to xylose, enabling more compact headgroup packing and a smaller effective a 0. Conversely, the more polar xylose headgroup can impose a larger effective a 0. With v and l held constant, these differences lead to a smaller vl×a0 ratio for Xyl-C10 than for Rha-C10, consistent with their distinct solvent-free physical states.

Introducing a second alkyl chain increases hydrophobic volume (v), thereby shifting the packing balance for both headgroups. Xyl-C10–C10 retains a relatively larger effective headgroup area than Rha-C10–C10, resulting in an intermediate packing balance and gel-like behavior. Rha-C10–C10 combines increased hydrophobic volume with a comparatively smaller effective headgroup area, exhibiting the largest vl×a0 among the four glycolipids and behaving as a viscous liquid.

Collectively, these observations indicate that controlled structural variations give rise to distinct intrinsic physical states under solvent-free conditions. The progression from opaque solid to viscous liquid reflects architecture-dependent organization established prior to thermal processing. Whether these states are uniquely determined by molecular structure or allow processing-dependent state selection is examined through time–temperature–transformation mapping.

Processing-Dependent Structural Evolution of Glycolipids Revealed by Time–Temperature–Transformation Mapping

To investigate whether solvent-free intrinsic states of glycolipids evolve through uniquely determined transformation pathways or permit processing-dependent divergence, time–temperature–transformation (TTT) maps were constructed. In each experiment, samples were first heated above the melting temperature to obtain optically homogeneous melts that served as a common initial state (Figure S1). The samples were then transferred to selected isothermal temperatures and aged for extended periods, while structural evolution was periodically monitored by polarized optical microscopy (POM) (Figure ). Accordingly, the resulting maps were used to compare the dominant structural outcomes under different temperature–time histories rather than to quantitatively determine transformation kinetics.

Time–temperature–transformation (TTT) maps and representative polarized optical microscopy (POM) images for solvent-free glycolipids. TTT maps are shown for (a) Rha-C10, (b) Rha-C10–C10, (c) Xyl-C10, and (d) Xyl-C10–C10. Symbols denote the dominant outcome observed under POM during isothermal aging: open circles, no detectable structural evolution; filled circles, sparse particle formation; triangles, needle-like crystalline domains; red squares, anisotropic mesoscale textures. Representative POM images corresponding to each symbol are shown in (e) open circles, (f) filled circles, (g) triangles, and (h) red squares. Scale bars: 100 μm.

Among the four glycolipids, Rha-C10 and Rha-C10–C10 exhibit a single structural class in the TTT maps, each characterized by a C-shaped transformation window (Figure a,b). Outside the transformation window, no detectable structural evolution is observed (open circles), whereas within this window, sparse micron-scale particle formation occurs with limited growth (filled circles), as represented by the corresponding POM images (Figure e,f). Although both glycolipids differ in macroscopic physical state at room temperature, their solvent-free intrinsic organization remains within the same structural class across the explored thermal conditions. Thermal processing influences whether sparse micron-scale particles are observable; however, the samples remain predominantly optically homogeneous, and no other distinct structural state emerges.

In contrast, Xyl-C10 exhibits strongly structure-dominated transformation behavior. Across the explored temperature range below its melting point, needle-like crystalline domains form rapidly (triangles; Figure c,g), without a clearly defined region of delayed structural evolution. Thermal history does not alter the structural outcome, and the system consistently evolves toward the same crystalline domain morphology, consistent with its lower CPP and its opaque solid appearance. These observations indicate that, for Xyl-C10, molecular architecture largely determines the solvent-free intrinsic state.

Xyl-C10–C10 exhibits temperature-dependent structural evolution that both overlaps with and diverges from the other glycolipids (Figure d). Across much of the explored temperature range, regions of no detectable changes (open circles) or sparse particle formation (filled circles) are observed, similar to the Rha systems. However, within a specific temperature window (approximately 20–30 °C), extended aging leads to the development of anisotropic mesoscale textures (red squares, Figure h), suggesting access to an additional transformation pathway. In this window, particle-type features initially appear but subsequently evolve into anisotropic mesoscale textures, marking a clear distinction from the other glycolipids, which remain confined to subtle structural variations. Although regions outside the 20–30 °C window exhibit POM features comparable to the Rha systems, the resulting macroscopic physical states differ substantially.

Importantly, TTT maps are based on POM observations and do not directly capture the corresponding macroscopic physical states. Samples that appear structurally similar may nonetheless differ in macroscopic behavior, such as solid-, gel-, or liquid-like states. This distinction is particularly relevant for Xyl-C10–C10, which exhibits mesoscale textures within the 20–30 °C window but also shows broader variation in bulk behavior across thermal conditions.

To determine whether these distinct states established during isothermal processing persist under practical conditions, the same samples were brought to room temperature following isothermal aging (Figure ) and monitored after ∼3 weeks (Figure S2). Rha-C10, Rha-C10–C10, and Xyl-C10 retained their characteristic macroscopic states across the explored thermal conditions: transparent solid, viscous liquid, and opaque solid, respectively. Xyl-C10–C10 exhibited distinct states determined by the aging temperature and preserved at room temperature. Samples aged at higher temperatures remained in a viscous liquid state, whereas samples aged at ≤4 °C formed amorphous gels that remained kinetically arrested upon warming. Gel-like states containing mesoscale textures formed within the 20–30 °C window remained unchanged during storage at room temperature. The distinction between these two gel states was further supported by DSC, which showed two distinguishable endothermic transitions for the domain-containing gel and one dominant transition for the amorphous gel (Figure S3). Overall, even when TTT mapping indicates similar structural categories among the glycolipids, the intrinsic state of Xyl-C10–C10, encompassing both internal organization and macroscopic physical state, depends strongly on prior thermal history. This intrinsic state multiplicity suggests that multiple transformation pathways may be accessible and motivates investigation of how thermal history influences intrinsic state selection, including mesoscale development.

Thermally Programmed Intrinsic States in Xyl-C10–C10

To investigate how thermal history influences intrinsic state selection in Xyl-C10–C10, we designed multistep heat-treatment trajectories guided by the mesoscale window identified in the TTT map (Figure a). Samples were first heated to 120 °C to obtain an optically homogeneous melt, then isothermally aged at selected temperatures (80, 60, or 40 °C) for controlled durations before being transferred to room temperature (∼20 °C). Structural evolution was monitored by POM after transfer, and the time to first appearance of distinct anisotropic texture was recorded. This approach enables direct evaluation of how temperature–time trajectories influence intrinsic states through mesoscale structure development.

Thermal-history-dependent emergence of mesoscale organization in Xyl-C10–C10. (a) Samples were melted at 120 °C (Δt 1 = 0.5 h), isothermally aged at 80, 60, or 40 °C for Δt 2 = 2 h, Δt 3 = 24 h, or Δt 4 = 240 h, and subsequently transferred to room temperature (∼20 °C) for further aging (Δt 5). Δt values denote dwell durations at each temperature and are not cumulative. (b–j) POM images correspond to representative Δt 5 time points at which distinct birefringent contrast first becomes apparent, reflecting the onset of mesoscale organization rather than fully developed structures. Scale bars: 100 μm.

Xyl-C10–C10 exhibits markedly different structural emergence behaviors depending on both aging temperature and aging duration, ranging from fine domains to larger structures, or no observable organization within the experimental time window. After short isothermal aging, distinct anisotropic domains appear at room temperature within relatively short times, forming fine mesoscale features (Figure b–d). With increasing aging time at the same temperature, the appearance of these domains is progressively delayed, and the resulting structures become visibly coarser (Figure e–i). In contrast, after aging at 80 °C for 240 h (Δt 4), only sparsely dispersed particles are observed with no clear mesoscale texture developing even after extended aging at room temperature (Δt 5 = 500 h; Figure j).

These observations indicate that aging temperature and duration act as kinetic filters that control whether mesoscale organization emerges upon entering the mesoscale temperature window. In this manner, the prior thermal trajectory biases structural development, providing a practical means to program mesoscale architecture. This thermal history-dependent behavior motivates further examination of how distinct processing pathways translate into fully developed structural states.

When structural evolution is allowed to proceed to its end point, Xyl-C10–C10 converges to distinct mesoscale architectures that depend systematically on the prior aging temperature (Figure ). To isolate the role of aging temperature while avoiding complete suppression or early stage bias, an intermediate aging duration of 24 h was selected based on the emergence behavior observed in Figure . After transferring to 20 °C and extended aging, fully developed domains are obtained. Domain size and number vary systematically with aging temperature. Conditions near the apparent kinetic-nose region (∼30 °C, Figure d) produce highly subdivided small domains (Figure c), whereas temperatures further from this condition yield fewer, larger domains (Figure b,d). This trend is consistent with classical temperature-dependent nucleation-growth behavior. ,

Fully developed mesoscale structures of Xyl-C10–C10 following controlled thermal trajectories. (a) Schematic of the heat-treatment protocol: samples were first melted at 120 °C for Δt 1 = 0.5 h, isothermally aged at selected temperatures (20, 30, 50, or 80 °C) for Δt 2 = 24 h, and then transferred to 20 °C for extended aging (Δt 3 = 400 h). Times indicate dwell durations at each processing step. (b–e) Representative POM images of fully developed mesoscale structures after extended aging at 20 °C, corresponding to 24 h isothermal aging at 20, 30, 50, and 80 °C, respectively. Images were acquired using a low-magnification objective (4×) to capture a larger field of view for domain counting and area analysis. The number of domains (n domain) is indicated in each panel and was determined by image-based domain identification (see Experimental Section). Corresponding domain area analysis is provided in Table S1. Scale bars: 500 μm.

Aging at 80 °C does not follow this temperature-dependent trend. Conditions away from the apparent kinetic-nose region are typically expected to produce fewer and larger domains, or suppress domain formation. Prolonged isothermal aging at 80 °C indeed favors particle-type states in the TTT map (Figure d), consistent with this expectation. However, after a controlled 24 h isothermal aging step, transfer to 20 °C still produces fine mesoscale formation (Figure e). Quantitative analysis using image-based domain identification (Table S1) shows that the average domain area is comparable to that at ∼30 °C, while the number of domains is lower by approximately 6-fold. This indicates a reduced, but still significant, domain population. This deviation from classical TTT behavior suggests that high-temperature isothermal aging modifies the structural state prior to cooling, thereby altering the effective structural evolution pathway upon entry into the mesoscale-forming window. Thermal trajectory therefore does not follow a simple monotonic nucleation-growth trend, but enables access to distinct mesoscale organizations.

To elucidate the underlying molecular-level interactions associated with these trajectory-dependent pathways, we examined the functional groups of the structurally evolved Xyl-C10–C10 states using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Figure S4). The characteristic vibrational bands corresponding to the primary chemical backbone remain unchanged across all structural states. These invariant bands include the aliphatic C–H stretching of the lipid tails (2923 and 2854 cm–1), the CO stretching of the ester groups (1727 cm–1), and the C–O–H stretching of the carbohydrate moiety (1034 cm–1). , In contrast, the O–H stretching band exhibits a distinct, domain-dependent red shift, moving progressively from 3392 cm–1 in the no-domain state to 3383 cm–1 in the fully developed small-domain state. This shift indicates a transition from weakly to more strongly hydrogen-bonded states. Consequently, the FTIR results indicate that the hydrophobic lipid-tail environment remains stable, whereas mesoscale structural evolution involves the progressive strengthening of the hydrogen-bonding networks within the constrained packing environment of the double-tail architecture.

Altogether, these results demonstrate that controlled temperature–time trajectories enable programmable selection among distinct mesoscale architectures in Xyl-C10–C10. Rather than converging to a single equilibrium structure, the system retains access to multiple structural outcomes depending on its prior thermal history. By modulating aging temperature and duration before entry into the mesoscale-forming window, domain size and density can be reproducibly tuned. This trajectory-dependent structural multiplicity establishes thermal processing as a practical handle for engineering intrinsic states of solvent-free glycolipids.

Mesoscale Domain-Dependent Mechanical Response of Thermally Programmed Xyl-C10–C10

Thermal trajectory in Xyl-C10–C10 produces not only distinct internal structures, but also macroscopically different physical states at room temperature, ranging from flowable materials to rigid, nonflowing gels. While these differences are evident by visual inspection, appearance alone does not resolve the underlying mechanical state. In particular, samples containing mesoscale domains can appear either fluid-like or gel-like, raising the question of how domain architecture defines bulk mechanical strength. To address this, rheological oscillatory strain sweep measurements spanning linear and nonlinear deformation regimes were performed on representative fully developed structures.

Oscillatory strain sweep measurements were conducted on four representative intrinsic states: viscoelastic materials containing large mesoscale domains (Figure a), viscoelastic materials with smaller domains (Figure b), an amorphous gel (Figure c), and a viscous liquid containing sparsely dispersed particles (Figure d). During shear deformation, the storage modulus (G′), reflecting elastic or solid-like response, and the loss modulus (G″), reflecting viscous or liquid-like response, were measured as functions of strain. In the low-strain (linear) regime, where internal structure remains largely undisturbed, materials with G′ greater than G″ exhibit gel-like behavior, whereas materials with G″ exceeding G′ behave in a liquid-like manner. At higher strain, entering the nonlinear regime, a reduction in G′ signals progressive structural breakdown.

Mesoscale domain-dependent mechanical response of thermally programmed Xyl-C10–C10. Oscillatory strain sweep measurements (0.01–100% strain at 1 rad s–1) showing storage modulus (G′, black squares) and loss modulus (G″, red circles) for (a) large-domain state, (b) small-domain state, (c) amorphous gel, and (d) viscous liquid containing sparsely dispersed particles. Insets show POM images (left) and corresponding vial inversion tests (right), illustrating mesoscale organization and macroscopic flow behavior. All scale bars in the POM images are 300 μm.

The mechanical behavior of the domain-containing states clearly correlates with domain size. In the linear regime, the large-domain state (Figure a) is characterized by the largest G′, reflecting a stiffer elastic network. In contrast, the small-domain state (Figure b) yields a lower G′ yet sustains its linear viscoelastic response to greater strain before yielding. Although this state appears flowable upon visual inspection (inset in Figure b), the measured dominance of G′ over G″ at low strain confirms the presence of an elastic structure. These results indicate that increasing domain size enhances stiffness while reducing strain tolerance, revealing a trade-off between rigidity and deformability governed by mesoscale architecture.

To further distinguish the role of mesoscale domains, the mechanical responses of an amorphous gel (Figure c) and a viscous liquid lacking ordered domain structure (Figure d) were examined. The amorphous gel exhibits G′ greater than G″ in the linear regime, confirming the presence of an elastic network despite the absence of visible mesoscale domains. In contrast, the viscous liquid containing sparsely dispersed particles shows G″ exceeding G′ across the measured strain range, indicating viscous-dominated behavior and the absence of a load-bearing network. These responses establish mechanical baselines for comparison with the domain-mediated states.

Taken together, the results demonstrate that thermal trajectory defines mesoscale domain size (Figure ), which in turn governs bulk mechanical stiffness (Figure ). G′ spans approximately 3 orders of magnitude across the programmed states, ranging from on the order of 102 Pa in the small-domain state to exceeding 105 Pa in the large-domain state. By comparison, the amorphous gel exhibits an intermediate G′ near 104 Pa. Because domain size is controllable through defined temperature–time pathways, the mechanical response of Xyl-C10–C10 can be systematically tuned within this stiffness range. In comparison, the amorphous gel and liquid states represent fixed mechanical end points lacking domain-mediated adjustability. These findings establish thermal processing as a practical route to program mechanical properties in solvent-free glycolipids through controlled mesoscale organization.

Structure–Processing Interplay and Implications for Intrinsic State Selection in Solvent-Free Glycolipids

The obtained results address whether solvent-free intrinsic states are uniquely determined by molecular architecture or modulated by processing history. Certain glycolipids display structure-dominated behavior that converges toward a single thermotropic outcome, whereas others demonstrate pronounced state multiplicity in which thermal trajectory directs structural selection. These findings indicate that intrinsic structural organization in solvent-free glycolipids cannot be universally predicted from molecular packing considerations alone, but instead reflects the additional influence of thermal processing. In systems exhibiting such sensitivity, the starting condition is therefore not uniquely defined but reflects prior pathway selection. Accordingly, in glycolipid materials characterized by such processing sensitivity, careful definition of the initial intrinsic state, rather than assuming it is fully dictated by molecular structure, may be important when interpreting subsequent structural evolution under external perturbations.

The processing-dependent behavior observed for Xyl-C10–C10 can be interpreted within the packing framework defined by the critical packing parameter (CPP). , The combined increase in hydrophobic tail volume while maintaining a relatively large effective headgroup area positions this architecture at an intermediate packing balance within the series, between crystalline solid-forming and liquid-like regimes. Under such transitional conditions, more than one thermotropic pathway can remain kinetically accessible, enabling thermal processing to regulate intrinsic state selection. Because glycolipid architecture can be tuned through variation of tail number, tail length, headgroup identity, and headgroup multiplicity, such transitional packing regimes are unlikely to be unique to Xyl-C10–C10. Systematic architectural tuning therefore provides a practical route to engineering processing-sensitive intrinsic states in solvent-free glycolipid systems.

More broadly, such processing-sensitive regimes provide a direct route to programmable mesoscale organization and bulk mechanical response in glycolipid materials. , Thermal trajectory thus functions as a controllable processing variable within a structure–processing–property framework, enabling tunable mechanical properties without altering molecular composition. , Remelting resets the intrinsic state, and subsequent thermal trajectories reprogram the mesoscale outcomes (Figure S5), highlighting that these systems offer a pathway toward reconfigurable soft materials.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that solvent-free intrinsic states of amphiphilic glycolipids are not universally dictated by molecular architecture alone, but instead span distinct degrees of sensitivity to thermal processing. While certain architectures converge toward a single thermotropic outcome, others access multiple kinetically selectable states within transitional packing regimes, as exemplified by Xyl-C10–C10. These findings establish that intrinsic state accessibility in solvent-free glycolipids emerges from the cooperative influence of molecular design and thermal history. By extending the role of bioderived amphiphiles beyond conventional surfactant functions, this work positions architecturally defined glycolipids as programmable soft materials. Enabled by advances in green synthetic strategies that yield structurally tunable glycolipids from renewable feedstocks, , such systems provide a sustainable and chemically versatile foundation for the rational engineering of adaptive soft matter.
