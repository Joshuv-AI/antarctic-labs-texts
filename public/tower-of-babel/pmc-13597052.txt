Evaluation of the Medication Never Events and Related Factors: A Nationwide Database Study
Authors: Mehdi Nasr Isfahani, Behrooz Keleidari, Parvaneh Mahmoodi, Amin Salehi, Asieh Maghami Mehr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The present study was conducted to evaluate the prevalence of medication errors regarded as never events in Iran and the related factors and develop a strategic operation plan (SOP) for its prevention.Materials and Methods:A nationwide cross-sectional study with a documentary approach was conducted. For this purpose, all documented reports of medication-never events (over 515 cases) were extracted from the hospital Information system (HIS) of Iran between 2018 and 2020. After reporting each error, using root cause analysis (RCA), factors related to each medication never event were identified.Results:According to the evaluations, the errors caused by the name/type of medication and medication administration errors had the highest percentages, 38.4% and 25%, respectively. 14.8% of patients were infants, 13.4% children, 4.3% youth, 43.7% adults, and 23.9% were elderly. Among healthcare providers (HCP), nurses were individuals who committed these errors the most (72.2%). Analyzing the causes and factors related to error incidence, the negligence of health-care personnel, the negligence of the patient or his companion, work environment problems, name problems, packaging and drug labels, negligence in responsibilities, problems of computer information systems, and handwritten prescriptions were found to be the most common factors related to the occurrence of medication never events.Conclusion:The results revealed that strategies contributing to the minimization of each type of error may include education of the patient or healthcare provider. By implementing a computer-based system for medication review and reconciliation process, prioritizing areas that can be quickly modified, might be more feasible.

INTRODUCTION

The health and treatment sector are crucial areas in socioeconomic activities of countries, where huge resources are spent every year. The ultimate goal of the healthcare system is to improve people’s health and the quality of healthcare services provided to them. Today, clinical errors, patient dissatisfaction, and complaints are still reported despite the treatment staff efforts and more sophisticated facilities providing healthcare services.[1] Thus, the World Health Organization (WHO) considers “healthcare safety” to be a major global concern. Totally, 3–16% of hospitalized patients around the world suffer from injuries resulting from medical interventions.[2] In Iran, medical errors’ complaints referred to the Tehran Forensic Medicine Organization are associated with an annual growth of 95% in a decade[3]; a brief review of these statistics in the last three decades indicates the increase and severity of this phenomenon as a social problem. In healthcare fields, this situation is assumed as a health-related situation or behavior considered undesirable by society or parts of it and represents some kind of threat.[4] In fact, medical errors can have negative effects on various aspects of the patient’s life (physical, emotional, and financial harms). Consequences such as job loss, permanent disabilities, and loss of quality of life are examples of possible negative effects.[5]

Although the error occurrence risk is an inherent part of healthcare, a significant part of them can be prevented. Moreover, a small part of these errors occurs in healthcare environments, which leads to serious and potential complications including mortality or disability for patients. These never events can be prevented as much as possible by identifying them and improving the prevention knowledge and methods. For this purpose, several jurisdictions, including the American Society for Quality and the National Health Service in Britain, have reported a list of never events including errors caused by care management defects, surgery, product, device, clinical errors, etc.[67] These reports are mostly focused on preventable errors.[89]

One of these error types, apparently more common than other errors that can also be more under the control of the treatment staff and be prevented, is the medication error.[1011] Since medicines are an integral part of healthcare services worldwide, their risks and harms also increase with increasing use of drugs.[12]

Most medication errors occur by physician’s prescription (39%) and medication administration (38%).[13] However, pharmacists and nurses prevent at most 70% of all errors by the physician before being administered to the patient.[14] In general, medication errors may be accompanied by mild complications or without complications, with severe or serious complications, and with “death”. Errors associated with serious complications or death are known as never events.

Although few studies have evaluated medication never events, numerous studies have reported medication errors. For example, 12%[15] of errors in England, 42%[16] in Sweden, and 58%[17] in Mexico have been reported due to medication error. Overall, the rate of serious medication errors in primary healthcare (physicians’ offices) seems reasonably low. However, due to the large number of prescriptions provided in primary healthcare, there is still the possibility of significant adverse outcomes, which, in turn, may affect the health and economic aspects of the individual and the country.[18]

Furthermore, despite focusing on medication never events in many countries, studies in Iran have mainly focused on medical errors, and no study has focused on medication errors or never events errors caused by medicines. Minimizing medical errors (especially high-risk errors for the patient) can be accompanied by improving the quality of healthcare services to patients, safe patient healthcare, and increasing the trust of the society in healthcare sector and developing medical knowledge. Hence, the present manuscript aimed to take an inside look at medication never events in the whole country and increase awareness among healthcare providers (HCPs) and create a clinical guideline to reduce the occurrence of errors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A nationwide cross-sectional study with a documentary approach was conducted. For this purpose, all documented reports of medication never events were extracted from the hospital information system (HIS) of Iran from 2018 to 2020. There were over 816 cases of never events, extracted from the country’s HIS in the form of a census. To identify and classify medication never events and to provide a strategic document as a clinical guide to be presented in the hospital, first, a team consisting of emergency medicine specialists, health information managers and pharmacists (preferably clinical pharmacists), nursing and organizational stakeholders were formed on specific issues (e.g., accreditation, professional regulations, drug safety priorities, and pharmacovigilance organization). During numerous sessions, the error types was determined through discussion and analysis to reach an agreement on summarizing the sub-categories of medication never events. A list of possible medication never events was prepared after solving the existing gaps, removing duplicates and non-serious or unpreventable events, and gaining collective agreement; this list involves over 515 cases of medication errors.

Moreover, the patient baseline characteristics (including age, gender), and outcome of patients (such as Serious but reversible complication, Minimal or no complication and Death) were obtained. Also, places where the error occurred (university academic hospitals, university non-academic hospitals, social security hospitals, private sector hospitals, and other hospitals [e.g., those of the military, reinforcement forces, Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC)] and the person committing the error (physician, nurse, pharmacist or pharmacy technician, patient or his companion, etc., (e.g., care givers, midwives) were recorded.

Finally, the collected data were coded and recorded in SPSS 26. The statistical report of data was based on frequency (percentage) or mean ± standard deviation (SD). Then, root cause analysis (RCA) was used to identify factors related to the occurrence of each medication never event and their prevention strategies.

RESULTS

The results obtained from the data recorded in the country’s HIS revealed that in general 515 medication never events have been reported in the whole country between 2018 and 2020. The medication name/type error and wrong-route administration error had the highest percentage, 38.4% and 25%, respectively. Other errors included medication administration error (13.0%), injection dose error (12.8%), wrong-time administration errors (6.6%), and dispensing error without a physician’s prescription (2.5%). According to the medication, never events classification based on patient outcome, 6.8% of errors induced death or irreversible complication (s), 11.1% serious but reversible complications, and 44.1% minimal complication(s) or no complication [Figure 1].

Nurses were individuals who committed never event errors the most (72%). Physicians, pharmacies (prescription dispensers), patient/patient’s companion, service personnel, and midwives are among others committing never event errors. Also, 49.9% of never events were reported in educational-university hospitals and 36.5% in medical-university hospitals. The patient outcome after the occurrence of never events involved mild to severe complications (17.7%), skin complications (18.8%), cardiac-pulmonary arrest (2.1%), and death (6.8%). DISCUSSION

The current study indicated that the highest percentage of medication never events occurred in adults and the elderly, 43.7% and 23.9%, respectively. However, to identify the high-risk group, to minimize the risk for patients, and to avoid error occurrence, the patient’s outcome facing the mentioned medication never events is of crucial importance. The results revealed that adults and the elderly are the main group exposed to medication never events. The medication name/type and wrong-time administration error in adults and the elderly have been the most frequent compared to other groups. Additionally, wrong-route errors or medication administration error (e.g., wrong venipuncture, parenteral instead of oral use, vaginal use instead of anal use, etc.) were more common in infants. The most responsible persons were nurses or patient companions. Errors in determining and calculating drug dosage are more prevalent in children. Prescribing medicine without a physician’s prescription is much more prevalent in the elderly than other errors.

In this regard, an article on selected formulations for the pediatric population (EMEA/CHMP/PEG/194810/2005) recommended a wide range of approaches for developing pediatric formulations.[19] Also a guideline for the pharmaceutical development of drugs for pediatric use (EMA/CHMP/QWP/805880/2012 Rev. 2), and guidelines on some aspects of drug development suitable for children and specific to these patients have been developed.[20]

Infants may be at high risk of medication never events, with dose administration errors being the most common type of error. This may be related to their variation in age, size and weight, body surface area (BSA), and degree of growth. Many studies have reported drug overdose as the most common medication error.[21] In our country, this error is the most frequent in children, in addition to the fact that the wrong-route administration error was also significant in infants.

Additionally, some evidence suggests that an oral syringe may be the most accurate dose measurement tool for oral liquid medicines (the formulation commonly used in children), especially viscous medicines.[22] However, if an inappropriate dosing device is used to deliver drugs with liquid formulations, drug hazard is possible. The risk of mixing up oral liquid drugs with different strengths can be reduced via ensuring different medicine appearances with different strengths and providing different packages for each.

Other researches have also acknowledged that it is better to refrain from medication administration to the child by his companion or to ensure the literacy and correct understanding of his companion in a hospital environment.[2324]

In other studies, elderly people make up 34% of written prescriptions and they are at high risk of medication errors as mentioned about the need for a reflective article on quality aspects of medicines for the elderly.[23]

Elderly patients may face physical and cognitive impairments, so they may have problems in taking their medications, such as swallowing pills, opening packages, or reading user instructions and package leaflets. Such aspects should be taken into consideration when producing drugs used by elderly patients. Furthermore, the elderly patients frequently use several medicinal products (polypharmacy), which may cause problems that can be resolved by the appropriate design of the drugs used (e.g., using a wider range of colors, sizes, and shapes of tablets to help identify drugs and ultimately reduce the risk of errors). Also, the possibility of impaired vision in elderly patients (especially diabetic patients) may be higher than young patients, which may have a negative effect on the correct use of the insulin pen, so patients should be encouraged to be supported by a caregiver.

The priority of the current study is providing strategies to minimize errors made in the hospital environment. In the first step, it is recommended to avoid medicine administration without a doctor’s order in this group of patients due to their older age and the possibility of having several underlying diseases. It is also necessary to be very careful in determining the medication dose and its administration as the current study findings demonstrated that this group of patients with overdose errors can face serious risks such as cardiorespiratory arrest. Furthermore, wrong administration and lack of skill in venipuncture have led to numerous skin complications in these patients. Therefore, many adverse consequences in this group of patients can be easily controlled and prevented; thus, extra care should be taken by the treatment staff in handling and taking care of these patients.

In this regard, strategies that can be used to minimize the medication never event errors include strategies to minimize medication name/type errors, strategies to minimize medication administration errors, strategies to minimize medication dosage error, strategies to minimize errors made by the patient or his companion, strategies to minimize pharmaceutical errors and those of educational strategies [Table 4]. Key solutions in addition to strengthening the health system

Strategies to minimize medication name/type errors

In the first step, as the most important strategy, we can mention drug naming, that is, similar names in terms inscription or pronunciation may cause a risk to patient safety and must be avoided. In this regard, the world health organization (WHO) has issued guidelines on the creation of a new international non-proprietary name (INN) to facilitate the identification of pharmaceutical substances (active medicinal substances); for example, INNs should be distinct in terms of sound and spelling. INNs should be derived from common roots for products in related drug classes (e.g., azepam for diazepam derivatives, bactam for beta-lactamase inhibitors).[24]

Regarding medication never events, Herve-Bazin et al.[25] evaluated medication errors related to the use of medical gases in France and showed that 38 medication errors were detected in the use of oxygen gases and equimolar mixture of oxygen and nitrous oxide (EMONO) gas from January 1985 to November 2014. The high similarity (80%) between these two products caused 53% error, 34% surgical errors, and 13% human errors by the patient contributing to the mentioned 38 errors. It is suggested to use a label for EMONO gas cylinders so as not to be confused with oxygen gas cylinders. Also, the medical error risk of using gas cylinder for health-medical professions should be well explained in a poster with a short and precise description on their use.

In the first step, when taking delivery of medicines from the pharmacy, they better be checked by the patient or his companion, since most errors can occur by misreading the drug name due to emergency conditions and hurry, specialists and healthcare-medical workers’ sleepiness, or the illegibility of the doctor’s prescription. Also, when delivering the medicines to the hospital, they should be checked by healthcare specialists, and the correctness of the medicine name should be ensured. In case the drugs are available in the hospital and are going to be directly used by the personnel, it is suggested to separate and segregate those with similar names in terms of pronunciation or orthography, etc. to minimize errors in emergency cases and anxiety of healthcare-medical workers despite their precision in this regard.

Some studies have also stated that medication errors can be effectively minimized in different hospital departments through defining and specifying appropriate color distinctions and teaching them to healthcare professionals. For example, medicines with red labels only indicate intravenous use. Medicines with white labels indicate medicines for gastric lavage.

Strategies to minimize medication never events

If a product is administered by injection or intravenous injection, the product information should explain its different preparation and administration methods, and if an injectable product should not be administered through a specific route (e.g., IV, SC, or IM), the related information must be included. It is better to prepare posters with suitable illustrations regarding the key points and warnings in administering medications and place them in different hospital departments for continuous emphasis and training healthcare professionals, especially nurses.

Rarely, it may be necessary to include additional warnings on the labeling for some products, which are not required for other products [e.g., the generic label of piperacillin/tazobactam contains the statement that it must not be mixed or co-administered with any aminoglycosides and prepared or diluted with ringer’s lactate solution (Hartmann’s solution)]. But no such warning is required for the product formulated to eliminate these incompatibilities.

Strategies to minimize medication dosing errors

It is highly important to assess whether the essential information in the medicinal product such as the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), the product information (PIL), and labeing to prevent medicinal errors may be ignored by healthcare professionals, patients, or their companions. According to Article 54 of Directive 2001/83/EC, specifying specifications for the outer and immediate packaging, a template has been established for quality review of documents (QRD) that includes key information on product use, for example, use template or other required special warnings on the outer packaging. It is crucial to consider these warnings in situations where they are assumed critical to minimize the risk (e.g. a warning to prevent overdose: “use this drug only in this specific case; otherwise, there is a possibility of severe overdose”.

In this regard, the Canadian Never Events Action team has provided a list of 15 never events for hospital care, categorizing them into five sub-groups: wrong-route administration, intravenous administration of concentrated potassium solution, accidental injection of epinephrine for local use, hydromorphone overdose or administration of the solution higher than the desired amount concentration or lack of attention to the required dilution/dose, neuromuscular blocking without sedation, and airway management and ventilation.[26]

Strategies to minimize errors made by the patient or patient’s companion

Healthcare-medical professionals are responsible for ensuring correct prescriptions for patients and ensuring that they receive the right, errorless medicine. In cases where patients are responsible for the self-administration of medication, healthcare-medical professionals must ensure that the patient understands how to self-administer drugs appropriately to minimize the risk of medication never events. The preparation and availability of appropriate materials are essential to facilitate their interaction with patients (or caregivers). Different booklets on drug information or technical information can be prepared for products mainly provided in hospitals so that a copy can be provided to every user.

Strategies to minimize errors by pharmacies

Pharmacies may play an important role in confirming the appropriateness of the treatment practice for the patient and informing the patient of safe medication use and any special instructions for providing or prescribing the product. Prescription dispensers can also play a notable role in advising patients regarding medication use, such as dosage, time, along with other medications or foods, the use of devices such as inhalers, and responding to patients’ questions.

Some prevalent errors in distribution departments of hospital pharmacies include delivering wrong medicinal product or with similar names to the patient or his companion, delivering wrong amount of medicine, giving wrong pharmaceutical form, or providing wrong oral information to the patient or his companion. At the first stage to avoid the so-called errors, dispensing medicine and delivering it to the patient must be performed under the supervision of at least two experts in the pharmacy; after the final check, it can be delivered to the patient or his companion. Moreover, when delivering medicine to the hospital or medical personnel, the medicine received better match the patient’s prescription tables; after being confirmed, it should be delivered so that it is not misused by nurses or other healthcare-medical professionals.

Educational strategies

Training healthcare professionals is assumed a key action to improve safety in ambulatory care. This often reduces medication errors when education is considered as part of multifaceted interventions. A review of 47 studies revealed that educational interventions aimed at improving antibiotic prescription and distribution can influence the behavior of specialists and cause their further adherence to the guidelines.[27]

Also, modern technologies can be applied in prescription and distribution systems to improve safe medical practices (such as smartphone software applications, barcoding, and pill identifier websites).

Therefore, holding training sessions and various journal clubs may considerably increase the executive knowledge and awareness of healthcare-medical professionals. Besides discussing medical personnel experiences and dealing with never events, avoiding prescriptions without physicians’ order in emergencies and other information exchange can play a significant role in minimizing never events.

To sum up, the current study was carried out, collecting evidence and experiences, to understand medication never events better and take an effective step through identifying causes and factors related to their occurrence and effective strategies to control and minimize never event errors and promote patient safety. It seems that the following issues can play an effective role in minimizing medical errors, especially never event errors: regional prioritization aimed at identifying the frequency of medical errors, promoting hospital care safety, emphasizing the significance of treatment safety, focusing on creating a positive safety culture, strengthening measurement and monitoring practices for patient safety, boosting the use of electronic tools, making patients and families involved and creating a sense of responsibility, focusing on more vulnerable people, celebrating successes, and sharing experiences with others.

CONCLUSION

RCAs showed that the factors related to the healthcare personnel, patient or his companion, work environment, medication, responsibility, and factors related to computer information systems or prescription handwriting were mostly attributed to the occurrence of medication-never events. Understanding the causes related to each of these categories can help healthcare organizations develop effective strategies to prevent medication never event errors. Some possible solutions to address these causes may include improving communication and collaboration among healthcare personnel, enhancing patient education and involvement in their care, creating a safe and organized work environment, implementing medication safety protocols, clarifying roles and responsibilities, and improving the accuracy and legibility of prescription handwriting or computerized medication orders.
