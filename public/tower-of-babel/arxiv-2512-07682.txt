Abstract
. In this work, we investigate optimal control of a Brinkman equation couple with sixth-order Cahn-Hilliard equation. The Cahn-Hilliard equation is endowed with a source term accounting for mass exchange and the velocity equation contains a non divergence-free forcing term, which act as distributed control variable. We consider the aforementioned system with constant mobility, viscosity and nonlinearity of double-well shape is regular. The cost functional of the optimal control problem contains a nondifferentiable term like the L 1 L^{1} -norm with sparsity constant κ \kappa , which leads to sparsity of optimal controls. We study the first order necessary optimality condition for both the case κ = 0 \kappa=0 and κ > 0 . \kappa>0. When the cost functional is differentiable, first order necessary optimality conditions are characterized by Lagrange multiplier method and for nondifferentiable case we have used the idea of Casas and Tröltzsch from the paper (Math. control Relat. Fields, 10(3):527-546, 2020).

Introduction

In recent years, sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard equations have attracted considerable attention.
Models of this type appear in several physical contexts, including phase separation with pronounced anisotropy effects, atomistic descriptions of crystal growth, the evolution of crystalline surfaces with small slopes exhibiting faceting phenomena, ternary systems such as oil–water–surfactant mixtures, and blends of polymeric materials.
We also refer to
[
39
,
37
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
, for further analytical and numerical investigations of these higher-order models. When coupled with fluid dynamics, these models offer a robust framework for analyzing the interaction between interfacial evolution, transport mechanisms, and hydrodynamic effects. In this article we are interested in one such coupling where we consider the Brinkman equation couple with sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard equations.

1.1.
Model and problem description.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded and connected open set with smooth boundary
\Gamma
and unit outward normal
\mathbf{n}
. Further, let
T>0
denote some final time and we set

Q=\Omega\times(0,T),\quad\Sigma=\Gamma\times(0,T)
.In this article we study the following optimal control problem:

\displaystyle\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}\quad\min_{\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}}\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})

subject to

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\lambda(\varphi)\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla p=\mu\nabla\varphi+\boldsymbol{g},\quad\text{div}~\boldsymbol{v}=0,\quad\text{in }Q,\\
&\partial_{t}\varphi+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi-\text{div}(m(\varphi)\mu)=S(\varphi),\quad\text{in }Q,\\
&\mu=-\Delta w+f^{\prime}(\varphi)w+\nu w,\quad\text{in }Q,\\
&w=-\Delta\varphi+f(\varphi),\end{aligned}\right.

complemented with boundary and initial conditions

(1.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}=0,\text{ and }\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}w=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi=0\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=\varphi_{0},\quad\text{in }\Omega.

In the above
\mathcal{J}
is a quadratic cost functional and
\mathcal{U}_{ad}
is admissible control; precisely define as below

(1)

\beta_{i}
and
\kappa
are nonnegative real numbers for
i=1,2,3,4.

(2)

\boldsymbol{v}_{Q}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H})
,
\varphi_{Q}\in L^{2}(Q)
and
\varphi_{T}\in H
.

Furthermore, we define the cost functional, the control space and admissible control space by setting

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})=\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}\int_{Q}|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\int_{Q}|\varphi-\varphi_{Q}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\varphi(T)-\varphi_{T}|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{4}}{2}\int_{Q}|\boldsymbol{g}|^{2}+\kappa\int_{Q}|\boldsymbol{g}|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad=J(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi,\boldsymbol{g})+\kappa j(\boldsymbol{g}),

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H}),\text{ and }\mathcal{U}_{ad}:=\{\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}:\|\boldsymbol{g}\|_{\mathcal{U}}\leq M\},\text{ $M$ being a positive constant}.

The corresponding functional spaces will be specified in section 2.

The model (
1.1
) is studied in
[
8
]
. The velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}
is governed by a Brinkman-type equation see
\eqref{CHB}_{1}
.
The scalar field
\varphi
, which acts as an order parameter, denotes the local fraction
of one component in a binary mixture. For analytical convenience, it is typically scaled
so that the pure phases correspond to
\varphi=-1
and
\varphi=1
, whereas values
in
(-1,1)
represent the diffuse interfacial region. This interfacial layer is
concentrated in a tubular neighborhood of the physical interface and has thickness
proportional to a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
.

The dynamics of
\varphi
are described by a sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard-type equation,
modified by a nonconservative source term

S(\varphi)=-\sigma\varphi+h(\varphi),

where
h
is smooth and bounded and
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. This term introduces
mass exchange and therefore destroys the standard mass-conservation property of
\varphi
.

The quantities
\mu
and
w
appearing in
\eqref{CHB}_{3}-\eqref{CHB}_{4}
correspond to the variational
derivatives of the total free energy
\mathcal{E}
and the Ginzburg–Landau energy
\mathcal{G}
,
respectively:

\mu:=\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\varphi},\qquad w:=\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta\varphi},

where

\mathcal{E}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(\varphi)\right)^{2}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\varphi)\right).

Here,
F
is a double-well potential defined on
\mathbb{R}
,
f=F^{\prime}
,
\eta>0
is viscosity coefficient and

\nu\in\mathbb{R}
is a (possibly nonpositive) constant. A typical example is the
classical quartic potential

F(s)=\frac{1}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.

The function
\boldsymbol{g}
in
\eqref{CHB}_{1}
acts as an optimal control.
\eta
and
m
denotes the viscosity and mobility of the fluids. The nonlinear term

\mu\nabla\varphi
represents the Korteweg force, which accounts for capillarity effects
in the mixture. Due to the smoothness of
f
, the boundary condition

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}w=0
in (1.5) is equivalent to

\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\Delta\varphi=0
on
\Sigma
.

We next turn to the sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard equation incorporating curvature effects. Well-posedness results for this higher-order system, under various choices of potentials and mobilities, are presented in

None of the optimal control paper cited above is concerned with the aspect of
sparsity
, i.e. the possibility that locally optimal control may vanish in subregion of positive measure of the space time cylinder
Q
that are controlled by sparsity parameter
\kappa.
The sparsity structure follows from the variational inequality arising in the first-order necessary optimality conditions, together with the particular form of the subdifferential
\partial j
. In this work, our interest lies in sparsity effects, and for simplicity, we restrict our attention to the case of full sparsity associated with the
L^{1}(Q)
- norm functional
j
introduced in (
1.4
).

Sparsity in optimal control for partial differential equations has emerged as a highly active research area. The introduction of sparsity-promoting functionals can be traced back to inverse problems and image processing. A landmark contribution was made in the seminal study
[
34
]
on elliptic control problems, which ignited the investigation of sparsity in PDE-based optimal control. After that, numerous results on sparse optimal controls for PDEs have appeared. For instance, sparse control problems for the viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation, the Allen–Cahn equation, and certain tumor growth models are addressed in
[
10
,
33
,
32
,
19
]
. To the best of our knowledge, however, sparse optimal control for the Brinkman flow coupled with a curvature-driven sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard equation has not been investigated in the literature. The present work appears to be the first to consider this setting.

In this article, we study the associated optimal control problem and incorporate an
L^{1}(Q)
-norm of the control into the cost functional in order to capture sparsity effects. We begin by establishing the differentiability of the control-to-state operator and then derive the optimality system using a Lagrange multiplier framework. Employing standard arguments, we show the existence of an optimal control for problem
(CP)
and obtain the first-order necessary optimality conditions. Because of the intrinsic complexity of the sixth-order Cahn–Hilliard structure, second-order sufficient optimality conditions are not addressed here and will be treated in a separate work.

4.
The optimal control problem.

In this section, we study the existence of an optimal control problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
with the cost functional defined in (
1.4
) and characterized it using first order necessary optimality condition.
Note that, if
\kappa=0
, the cost functional
\mathcal{J}=J
is differentiable, and the optimality system together with the first-order necessary optimality condition can be derived by the Lagrange multiplier method; see
[
17
, Chapter 2, Theorem 1.9]
.
However, when
\kappa\neq 0
, this approach is not applicable, and we instead rely on
[
6
, Theorem 3.2]
to obtain the optimality condition.
Thus, we consider the two cases
\kappa=0
and
\kappa>0
separately.
We begin by proving the existence of a solution to the control problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
.

4.1.
Existence of an optimal control.

We note from the operator
\mathcal{S}
that control can uniquely define the solution
(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi,\mu,w)
of the system (
1.1
). Then the cost functional given in (
1.4
) can be defined in terms of control only. Therefore, now we define the reduced cost-functional
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}):=\mathcal{J}((\boldsymbol{v},\varphi),\boldsymbol{g})=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}),\boldsymbol{g});=\tilde{J}(\boldsymbol{g})+\kappa j(\boldsymbol{g}).

In the following theorem we show that the optimal control problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
admits at least one solution.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exist at least one solution for the control problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
. That is, there exists a
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}
such that

\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}),\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})\leq\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}),\boldsymbol{g})\quad\text{ for every }\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}.

Proof.

From (
4.1
), we can write the optimal control problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
as

\min_{\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}).

First note that
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
is bounded from below and nonnegative as
\beta_{i}\geq 0
, for
i=1,\cdots,4
. As a consequence, the infimum exists and finite, so, let us take
j=\inf_{\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g})
. Also, since
\mathcal{U}_{ad}
is nonempty, there exists a minimizing sequence
\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{n})=j.

Thanks to the definition of
\mathcal{U}_{ad}
, there exists a subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{n_{k}}\}
(still denoted by
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\}
) such that

\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}\quad\text{ weakly
in }L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H}),

for some
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}
, as
\mathcal{U}_{ad}
is weakly closed subset of
\mathcal{U}
(cf.
[
21
, Proposition 5.1.4]
). Now, for the weakly convergent subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{g}_{n}\}
we consider
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}_{n})=(\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\varphi_{n}),
where
\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\varphi_{n}
are the first two component of the solution state (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) corresponding to control
\boldsymbol{g}_{n}.
Then, from the stability estimate (
(ii)
), we obtain a subsequence of
(\boldsymbol{v}_{n_{k}},\varphi_{n_{k}},\mu_{n_{k}},w_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(still denoted by
(\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},w_{n})
) such that

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{v}^{\ast}\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{\text{div}}),\\
&\varphi_{n}\to\varphi^{\ast}\quad\text{ weakly in }C^{0}([0,T];V)\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{4}),\\
&\mu_{n}\to\mu^{\ast}\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;H),\\
&w_{n}\to w^{\ast}\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;W).\end{aligned}\right.

Above convergence property of
\varphi_{n}
and well-known strong convergence results (cf.
[
31
, Section 8, Corollary 4]
imply

\varphi_{n}\to\varphi^{\ast}\quad\text{ strongly in }L^{\infty}(Q).

Using these convergence results on
(\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\varphi_{n},\mu_{n},w_{n})
, we can easily pass to the limit in the weak formulation of (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) (see (
2.3
)-(
2.6
)) and conclude
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})=(\boldsymbol{v}^{\ast},\varphi^{\ast}).

Since
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}
is weakly lower semi-continuous on
\mathcal{U}_{c}
, we have (see
[
21
, Corollary 5.1.5]
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{n})=j.

Therefore,
j\leq\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathcal{J}}(\boldsymbol{g}_{n})=j,

which yields that
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}
is a solution of the problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
.
∎

Definition 4.2

We call

to be an optimal control and the corresponding solution of the system (

) denoted by

is refer as the optimal solution of the problem

4.2.
The optimality system.

In this subsection we particularly take
\kappa=0
. Having prove existence of optimal solution, we now use Lagrange multiplier principle to obtain an optimality system that an optimal solution and Lagrange multiplier should satisfy. For that, let us recall abstract Lagrange multipliers method from
[
20
, Theorem 1, Theorem 3, Chapter 1]
and
[
17
]
.

Let
X_{1},

X_{2}
be two Banach spaces. Let
f:X_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:X_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be two functionals and
G:X_{1}\to X_{2}
be a mapping. We find a
z\in X_{1}
such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle f(z)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{Z}_{ad}}f(u),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{ad}=\{u\in X_{1}:G(u)=0,g(u)\leq 0\}.

The Lagrange functional for the minimization problem (
4.3
) is defined by

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(z,\lambda_{0},\lambda,q)=\lambda_{0}f(z)+\langle G(z),q\rangle+\lambda g(z)

for all
z\in X_{1},\lambda_{0},\lambda\in\mathbb{R},
and
q\in X_{2}^{\prime},
where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality pairing between
X_{2}
and
X_{2}^{\prime}.
Then we have following abstract Lagrange principle:

Theorem 4.3
.

(
[
17
, Chapter 2, Theorem 1.9]
) Let
z
be a solution of (
4.3
). Assume that the mapping
f,g,G
are continuously differentiable and that the image of the operator
G^{\prime}(z):X_{1}\to X_{2}
is closed. Then there exist
q\in X_{2}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{0},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that the triplet
(q,\lambda_{0},\lambda)\neq(0,0,0)
. Moreover, it satisfies the relations

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}_{z}(z,\lambda_{0},\lambda,q),h\rangle=0\quad\forall\,h\,\in\,X_{1},

\displaystyle\lambda_{0},\lambda\geq 0,\text{ and }\lambda g(z)=0,

where
\mathcal{L}_{z}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to first variable. Furthermore, if
G^{\prime}(z):X_{1}\to X_{2}
is an epimorphism and the constraint
g(z)\leq 0
is absent in (
4.3
), then
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
and can be taken as 1.

We now formulate the above theoretical framework in the context of our problem. To this end, we consider the following functional spaces:

(4.5)

\displaystyle X_{1}:=\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\times\mathcal{U},\quad X_{2}:=L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{V}_{\text{div}}^{\prime})\times L^{2}(0,T;W^{\prime})\times L^{2}(0,T;H)\times L^{2}(0,T;W^{\prime}),

We also denote

(4.6)

\displaystyle\varkappa=(\boldsymbol{v},\varphi,\mu,w),\quad\varkappa^{\ast}=(\boldsymbol{v}^{\ast},\varphi^{\ast},\mu^{\ast},w^{\ast}),\quad\ell=(\boldsymbol{w},\psi,\theta,\xi),\,\text{ and }\,\wp=(\boldsymbol{v}^{a},\varphi^{a},\mu^{a},w^{a})

.
Let
\mathbb{P}
be the Leray projector defined as
\mathbb{P}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}\to\mathbf{V}_{\text{div}}^{\prime}
, and consider the mappings

and

given by:

(4.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g})=J(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g}),\\
&G\begin{pmatrix}\varkappa\\
\boldsymbol{g}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{P}[-\eta\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+\lambda(\varphi)\boldsymbol{v}-\mu\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{g}]\\

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(\varkappa^{\ast},\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})=\inf_{(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g})\in\mathcal{W}_{ad}}J(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g}),\\

The Lagrange functional of the extremal problem (

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g},\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\wp)=\lambda_{0}f(\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g})+\langle G((\varkappa,\boldsymbol{g})^{t}),\wp^{t}\rangle_{X_{2},X_{2}^{\prime}}+\lambda_{1}(\|\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H})}-M^{2}).

4.8

), expanding the inner product in (
4.4
), using integration by parts and after some cancellation we get

\displaystyle\int_{Q}(\beta_{4}\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}+\kappa\varsigma-\boldsymbol{v}^{a})\cdot(\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})\geq 0\quad\text{ for every }\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad},

which conclude the theorem.
∎

The convex function
j
in cost functional accounts for the sparsity of optimal control, that is, the possibility that any locally optimal control may vanish in some region of space time cylinder
Q.
The form of this region depends on the particular choice of the functional
j
. The sparsity property can be deduce from the variational inequality (
4.38
) and particular form of subdifferential
\partial j
. For the functional
j
introduced in (
1.4
) and its subdifferential is given by
[
20
, Section 0.3.2]

(4.40)

\displaystyle\partial j(\boldsymbol{g})=\begin{cases}\{1\}&\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}(x,t)>0\,\text{ for a.e. }(x,t)\in Q,\\
\{\varsigma\in L^{2}(Q):\varsigma(x,t)\in[-1,1]\}&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{g}(x,t)=0\text{ for a.e. }(x,t)\in Q,\\
\{-1\}&\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}(x,t)<0\,\text{ for a.e. }(x,t)\in Q.\end{cases}

Using this subdifferential in the variational inequality (
4.38
), we obtain the following result:

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}
be the locally optimal control for the problem
\textbf{(CP)}_{\eta}
with
\kappa>0
and functional
j
as defined in (
1.4
). Also,
\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})=(\boldsymbol{v}^{\ast},\varphi^{\ast})
be the first two component of solution of (
1.1
) and the adjoint state
(\boldsymbol{v}^{a},\varphi^{a},\mu^{a},w^{a})
solving (
4.20
). Then there exists a function
\varsigma\in\partial j(\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast})
satisfying the variational inequality (
4.38
), and it also holds that

(4.41)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}^{a}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbf{H})}\leq\kappa,\quad\text{if }\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}=0.

Further, optimal control
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}
can be obtain by the projection formula

(4.42)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{ad}}\left(-\beta_{4}^{-1}(\kappa\varsigma+\boldsymbol{v}^{a})\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{U}_{ad}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathcal{U}
onto
\mathcal{U}_{ad}.

Proof.

The projection formula (
4.42
) is a direct consequence of (
4.38
) (cf.
[
36
, Theorem 2.33]
). It remains to show (
4.41
). In (
4.38
), we take
\boldsymbol{g}=0
and
\boldsymbol{g}=\frac{M\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}}{\|\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}\|_{\mathcal{U}}}
, respectively, we deduce that

(4.43)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}(\beta_{4}\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}+\kappa\varsigma-\boldsymbol{v}^{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}=0.

This immediately gives

(4.44)

\displaystyle\|\beta_{4}\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}+\boldsymbol{v}^{a}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\kappa.

Then, clearly
\|\boldsymbol{v}^{a}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\kappa
if
\boldsymbol{g}^{\ast}=0.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

In this discussion control is taken in first equation of (
1.1
). The same analysis holds by taking control in second equation of (
1.1
). In this case, the variational inequality (
4.38
) takes the form

\displaystyle\int_{Q}(\beta_{4}g^{\ast}+\kappa\varsigma-\varphi^{a})(g-g^{\ast})\geq 0\text{ for every }g\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}

where the control and admissible control space taken as
\mathcal{U}=L^{2}(0,T;H)
,
\mathcal{U}_{ad}=\{g\in\mathcal{U}:\|g\|_{\mathcal{U}}\leq M\}
, respectively, with
M
being a suitable positive constant.
