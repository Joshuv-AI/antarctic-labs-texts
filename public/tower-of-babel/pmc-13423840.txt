A Pediatric Resident Physician Coaching Program With Faculty Retreat Developed in Partnership With a Master Certified Coach
Authors: Oluwatomini Fashina, Megan Thorvilson, Stephanie Mavis, Patti M. Thorn, Siobhan Pittock, Kayla Munger, Justin McKone, Emily Raevsky, Katie L. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn recent years, the Association of Pediatric Program Directors and American College of Graduate Medical Education surveys have highlighted challenges in pediatric residency, including burnout and suboptimal faculty-trainee relationships. Coaching has emerged as a promising strategy to support learner-centered professional development, but most medical faculty lack formal training in coaching. We developed a faculty coaching retreat to train a select set of faculty members who would then participate in a longitudinal pediatric residency coaching program.MethodsThe retreat consisted of a 1-day (7.5-hour) training event including introductions to program structure, a keynote presentation on coaching mindset, and interactive small-group role-plays. We collected qualitative and quantitative data from faculty coaches before and after the retreat, measuring their ability to differentiate coaching versus advising interactions and to describe attributes of an effective coach. Quantitative data were compared using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and qualitative data were organized into themes.ResultsEighteen faculty coaches participated in the coaching retreat. Faculty demonstrated improvements in their ability to distinguish between coaching and advising, with a median score of 90% (interquartile range 70–92.5) to 100% (interquartile range 90–100) (P = .003) on the knowledge assessment. Comparing pre- to postretreat qualitative data, new themes emerged, including curiosity and a resident-driven approach, both key concepts emphasized during the retreat.DiscussionThis faculty coaching retreat increased participants’ knowledge and understanding about what it means to be an effective pediatric residency coach, effectively setting the stage for initiation of a longitudinal residency coaching program.

Educational Objectives

By the end of this activity, learners will be able to: 1.Recognize resident coaching as a self-driven process distinct from mentoring, advising, and teaching.2.Differentiate descriptions of faculty-resident interactions that are more characteristic of coaching versus advising.3.List at least 3 features of an effective pediatric resident coach.4.Demonstrate the ability to carry out a coaching conversation in a role-play setting.

Introduction

Coaching is a process where an individual is supported to maximize their personal and professional potential.1 Coaching is distinct from advising in that the coachee is the driver of the end goals and mechanisms for achieving them. Although widely used in the business world, physician coaching has only recently been introduced to medical education.2–5 During residency, a time where the unique demands of medical education and practice collide, there is a need for dedicated and innovative methods to support professional growth.

In 2023, the Mayo Clinic Pediatric and Adolescent Medicine Residency Program leadership team conducted a needs assessment using data from 2 sources: the Association of Pediatric Program Directors (APPD) burnout survey and the American College of Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) survey. From these surveys, we identified opportunities for growth nationally and within our program pertaining to resident burnout, faculty relations, career preparation, and learning environment. In response to this data, we sought to transform our existing advisor program into a coaching program that could help supplement these areas for growth.

Literature on the implementation of coaching programs in graduate medical education is emerging.6–21 MedEdPORTAL publications on coaching include activities for residents10 and faculty11 that help them learn clinical coaching skills, a curriculum on coachability targeted toward medical students,12 a self-assessment tool that can be used by faculty advisors to help coach struggling trainees,13 a “Mentor-Advisor-Coach Program” implemented within the Yale Internal Medicine Residency Program,14 and 2 examples of peer coaching programs.15,16 Educators at Stanford University implemented a pediatric residency coaching program that includes direct observation, feedback and evaluation, and participation in the Clinical Competency Committee.17

Our coaching program is unique from past published programs in graduate medical education because it prioritizes the partnership role of a coach over the hierarchical role of an advisor and thus removes coaches from evaluative roles toward their coachees as much as possible.

To create a strong foundation for our coaching program, we developed a faculty coaching retreat aimed at training a select set of faculty members to understand the difference between coaching and advising, articulate what it means to be an effective resident coach, and demonstrate ability to carry out coaching conversations in a role-play setting.

Methods

Implementation

We consulted with a Master Certified Coach within our institution to optimize the curriculum. We presented a proposal to the residency leadership team and department chair, both of whom approved it. We obtained department funding, which was beneficial but not required to operate the program. The program structure was for postgraduate year (PGY)-1 residents to participate in small-group coaching with associate program director (APD) coaches quarterly, and PGY-2 and PGY-3 residents to be paired with individual faculty coaches for one-on-one coaching quarterly. Enrollment in the program was mandatory, meaning every resident was assigned a coach, but engagement was at the discretion of the residents, meaning there were no formal consequences for nonengagement.

We developed an implementation checklist (Appendix A) outlining the key steps taken during the 6 months leading up to the launch of our coaching program. This included recruiting coaches, creating handbooks (Appendices B–D), developing the faculty retreat curriculum (Appendices E–H), establishing continuing medical education (CME) credit for the faculty retreat, and developing material for the quarterly intern coaching groups (Appendix I). To make our program material accessible to any residency leadership team regardless of access to a professional coach, we included scripted presentations (see Appendices F and G), suggested reading material (see Appendix F, slide 10), a detailed role-play guide (see Appendix H), and an extensive list of lessons learned (see the Discussion section).

We invited faculty coaches into the program based upon selection by three residency APDs who had trained within the residency program. The APDs’ combined experiences allowed for broad knowledge of department faculty members’ strengths and limitations in embracing the coaching mindset and partnering with residents. Invited faculty members were given the option to train as active coaches or backup coaches, allowing for redundancy and flexibility within the program.

We initiated the program at the beginning of an academic year by hosting 2 identical 1-day (7.5-hour) faculty retreats and 2 resident education sessions to orient everyone to the program. Handbooks were distributed to residents and faculty (in both printed and electronic form), and pediatric residents were given coach biographies to review. Residents submitted coach preferences to the residency leadership team, who used these preferences to match coaches with coachees. For the remainder of the academic year, residents met with their respective faculty coaches quarterly, and coaches participated in optional monthly faculty development meetings including reviewing recommended reading, sharing successes, and troubleshooting challenges.

The coaching retreat curriculum highlighted fundamentals about coaching and introduced the process of master adaptive learning as a core component of the program.18,19 Master adaptive learning is a systematic approach to learning that encourages a growth mindset and lifelong learning.18,19 The concept of master adaptive learning was introduced in the coaching orientation (see Appendix F), included in the handbooks (see Appendices B–D), reviewed in optional monthly faculty development meetings, and reinforced in the intern small-group meetings (see Appendix I). The retreat was required for all new coaches in their first year and highly encouraged for returning coaches in subsequent years. To accommodate this requirement for a large number of new coaches in the first year, the retreat was offered on 2 separate days with an identical curriculum in the first year and on a single day in subsequent years.

Evaluation

We used a fixed, convergent mixed-methods design with a parallel-databases variant to evaluate the impact of the coaching program. In this design, qualitative and quantitative variables were defined in advance (“fixed”) and collected separately (“parallel-databases”). Qualitative and quantitative data were merged (“converged”) in a narrative review to allow for a more complete understanding of the program's impact.20 This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Educational Research Committee (24-036) and Institutional Review Board (24-004689).

Quantitative Data

We administered a brief quiz before and after the retreat to assess faculty coaches’ ability to differentiate “coaching” from “advising” based upon a series of described resident-faculty interactions. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare scores from before versus after the retreat.

Qualitative Data

We asked faculty coaches before and after the retreat to “List three qualities of an effective pediatric resident coach.” Their responses were consolidated into themes by the first and senior authors (Oluwatomini Fashina and Katie Johnson) collaboratively. Themes before versus after the retreat were compared.

Early Measures of Program Impact and Engagement

To track the success of coach pairing, metrics were recorded including the proportion of residents who were paired with their top choice of APD coach (for PGY-1s) and 1 of their top 5 choices of individual coaches (for PGY-2s and PGY-3s).

To assess early perspectives of program impact, we hosted 2 small, voluntary resident focus groups after the first year of implementation (Appendix J). An education program coordinator without an evaluative role over residents (Emily Raevsky) hosted these. Deidentified transcripts were shared with coauthors. Residents were asked how (if at all) the coaching program had affected burnout, stress, resident-faculty relations, career preparation, learning environment, and culture. Responses were reviewed with an intention of continuous quality improvement.

To estimate engagement with the program, we surveyed faculty after the first year of implementation and reviewed budget documentation after the first 18 months of implementation. The budget documentation, in particular, was anticipated to be a minimum estimate of engagement; not all coaching meetings used funding, and not all receipts were turned in through our education program coordinator.

Results

Quantitative Data

Eighteen faculty coaches participated in the pilot faculty coaching retreat(s). Of these, 16 coaches filled out the preretreat survey (16/18 = 89%) and 17 coaches filled out the postretreat survey (17/18 = 94%). When presented with statements describing resident-faculty interactions, faculty improved in correctly identifying coaching behavior from a median score of 90% (interquartile range [IQR] 70–92.5) preretreat to 100% (IQR 90–100) postretreat (P = .003; Figure 1).

Qualitative Data

When asked to list 3 qualities of an effective coach before the retreat, 16 out of 18 faculty responded (16/18 = 89%), identifying a total of 47 qualities. At the conclusion of the retreat, we again asked faculty to list 3 qualities of an effective coach. Seventeen out of 18 faculty responded (17/18 = 94%), identifying 52 qualities. All listed qualities were coded by the first and senior authors (Oluwatomini Fashina and Katie Johnson) and consolidated into themes (Table and Figure 2). In the postretreat surveys, 2 new themes emerged: curiosity and a resident-driven approach. In addition, several “subthemes” were identified that reflected key concepts of the retreat content. These included acknowledging, noticing, appreciating, building trusting relationships, speaking less than the coachee, and prioritizing the coachee's own goals, insight, experience, and needs (see Table).

Early Measures of Program Impact and Engagement

Forty-four residents were enrolled in the coaching program: 15 PGY-1s, 15 PGY-2s, 13 PGY-3s, and 1 PGY-4 chief resident. All interns who submitted preferences for their group coach (12/15 = 80%) were granted their first choice. All senior residents who submitted preferences for their individual coach (26/29 = 90%) were matched with 1 of their top 5 choices.

Voluntary resident focus groups after the first year of implementation consisted of 6 residents (6/44 = 14%). PGY 1s, 2s, and 3s were represented in the focus groups. Deidentified resident comments highlighted appreciation for the relationships developed with faculty and the dedicated time and space allowed for vulnerability. While they were cautious about attributing broad culture change to the coaching program, they acknowledged its potential to influence culture over time. Illustrative quotations from the transcripts are as follows: •“[Our group coach] was able to relate and empathize with what we were going through … I didn't feel as alone.” (Resident 1, focus group 2)•“By all means, it's definitely increased my respect and trust in faculty.” (Resident 1, focus group 2)•“The coaching program provides this structured space to be vulnerable about the experiences we have in medicine…I think that's positively impacted the culture – having that space.” (Resident 2, focus group 1)•“[My coach] created space for a lot of vulnerability unlike other [attendings] I work with…I think that goes a long way to create a supportive environment.” (Resident 3, focus group 1)•“I think there has been an obvious shift in the program to focus more on a growth mindset…the coaching program has been a shift in line with that growth edge mindset.” (Resident 2, focus group 2)

After the first year of implementation, we asked faculty coaches to fill out a survey indicating how many of their quarterly coaching meetings they completed. This survey yielded 6 responses: 2 from APD intern group coaches (2/3 = 67%) and 4 from individual PGY-2 and PGY-3 resident coaches (4/15 = 27%). The percentage of meetings completed varied widely from 0% to 100%. Specifically, both APDs indicated 100% completion of their quarterly meetings but noted incomplete attendance by intern group members. The remaining coach responses ranged from 0% (“Coachee never engaged in my attempts to schedule meetings”) to 100%.

Review of budget data indicated 50 receipts processed for use of department-designated funds for coaching meetings over 18 months. During this time, the expected number of coaching meetings was 190, yielding a minimum program engagement estimation of 50/190 = 26%.

Discussion

Our faculty coaching retreat effectively set the stage for initiation of a longitudinal pediatric residency coaching program. The aims of the retreat—to train a select set of faculty members to understand the difference between coaching and advising, articulate what it means to be an effective coach, and demonstrate ability to carry out coaching conversations—were achieved as evidenced by quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitative data demonstrated a statistically significant increase in faculty participants’ understanding of the role of a coach compared to an advisor. Qualitative data demonstrated increased emphasis on attributes such as curiosity and a resident-driven approach, which mirrored the content of the retreat and encouraged faculty to show up for residents in a unique way, allowing residents to be truly seen and known. The creation of these safe and vulnerable spaces within the residency—with intentional prioritization of coaching over advising, removal of coaches from evaluative roles, and structured materials created in collaboration with a Master Certified Coach—is what sets this program apart from past published coaching programs in graduate medical education.

During the planning, implementation, and evaluation of the coaching program, several key observations emerged that we believe will be helpful to any leadership team planning to replicate this effort.

Early Preparation

If seeking CME credit for the faculty retreat and desiring hard copies of handbooks, we recommend starting the process several months in advance. Be aware that there may be costs associated with printing handbooks. Electronic handbooks can be used if printing is not an option.

Incorporate Redundancy

Building redundancy into the program is essential. We chose to train multiple backup coaches each year in case unexpected events prompted faculty to need to step away from their coaching role.

Participation Model

Consider whether to adopt an opt-in, opt-out, or “everybody in” enrollment model. We chose an “everybody in” model. Residents were encouraged to engage meaningfully, but there were no disciplinary consequences for nonengagement. Coaches were advised that if they did not hear from their coachee after 2 or 3 attempts to schedule a meeting, they could simply continue informal check-ins and remain a supportive presence.

Program Rollout Strategy

Each program must decide whether to roll out the program to interns exclusively (so all residents receive the full 3-year program) or to all classes simultaneously (meaning senior residents will receive only part of the 3-year program). We chose the latter to offer the program to all of our residents.

Offering Coaching to PGY-4 Chief and Dual-Training Residents

Residency programs may differ on whether they choose to have their PGY-4 chief residents or dual residents (ie, pediatric neurology residents entering neurology years) participate in the coaching program. We chose to offer these residents an opt-in model.

Coach Pairing Process

Consider whether to pair residents and coaches on a first-come, first-serve basis favoring the fastest-responding residents or to shuffle pairings attempting to achieve “the best for the most.” We chose the latter and have been consistently able to match most residents with 1 of their top 5 choices.

Funding and Full-Time Equivalent

We applied for full-time equivalent for our faculty coaches but were unable to obtain it. As a result, 2 potential faculty coaches declined enrolling. We did secure funding which covered food and refreshments for the faculty retreats ($1000 per retreat), funding for coaching meetings ($50 per dyad and $150 per group meeting), and the purchase of coaching books for faculty ($10 per book).3 While the coaching program can be implemented without funding, the impact this will have on faculty and resident engagement remains uncertain.

Meeting Setting

Over time, we have learned that the best space for coaching conversations is a place that allows for: •Privacy (ie, for authentic, spontaneous expression of emotions)•Professional boundaries (ie, some dyads may not be comfortable meeting at the faculty member's home or one-on-one in a restaurant)•Well-being (ie, residents appreciated the opportunity to go “off campus,” away from academic offices and exam rooms)

Some of our coaches found that all of these factors were met with virtual meetings. Others used coffee shops or casual restaurants. Some reserved rooms on campus with favorable views or greenery and picked up a meal or drinks to share in the space.

Limitations

Our pediatric residency coaching program, while promising, has several limitations. First, the program's design as a single-center, single-specialty initiative restricts its reproducibility across different graduate medical education programs. Moreover, the successful and prompt implementation of this coaching program at a high-resource institution poses a challenge for external validity. Even with these resources available, engagement by residents was suboptimal. Estimations of program engagement have been incomplete and imprecise, and assessment of impact was limited by very small, voluntary focus groups. Lastly, the fact that the faculty involved in the program were not professional coaches themselves is another limitation; however, this is very common in ACGME coaching programs.6,21

Future Directions

Our next step is to better understand the impact this residency coaching program has had on resident culture, as measured by open-ended feedback, engagement metrics, and APPD and ACGME survey metrics. In addition, we encourage expansion of its implementation across the field of graduate medical education, extending it to various specialties, institutions, and training levels. Each specialty has unique demands, and adapting the program to address these nuances will be crucial. Expanding the program to institutions with varying resource levels will provide valuable insight into how it can be optimized to accommodate diverse environments. Furthermore, adapting the program to different stages of postgraduate training (residency, fellowship, advanced fellowship) would maximize its effectiveness to the unique goals and expectations of all learners.
