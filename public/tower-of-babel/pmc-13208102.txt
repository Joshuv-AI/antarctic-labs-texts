Survival after severe aluminum phosphide poisoning complicated by concurrent insulin overdose: A case report
Authors: Maryam Rasouli, Rasoul Goli, Neda Mohammadi, Nasrin Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAluminum phosphide (ALP) poisoning remains highly lethal due to rapid cardiotoxicity, refractory hypotension, and metabolic disturbances. We report a rare case of a 31-year-old male presenting with severe hypoglycemia and decreased consciousness following intentional insulin overdose, who was later found to have concurrent ALP ingestion. Prompt correction of hypoglycemia, early gastrointestinal decontamination, and intensive supportive care—including vasopressors, magnesium supplementation, and continuous hemodynamic monitoring—were initiated. Despite transient hypotension and metabolic acidosis, the patient achieved complete recovery without organ dysfunction. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of overlapping toxicological conditions, in which insulin-induced hypoglycemia may obscure the early manifestations of ALP poisoning. It also emphasizes the importance of repeated clinical reassessment and early, aggressive supportive care. This report adds to the limited literature on mixed toxic exposures and underscores the critical role of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in atypical presentations, particularly in regions where ALP is prevalent.Keywords: Aluminum phosphide poisoning, Survival, Intensive care, Hypoglycemia, Cardiovascular support, Case report

Introduction

Aluminum phosphide (ALP) remains one of the most lethal toxic agents encountered in clinical toxicology due to its rapid and profound systemic effects following ingestion [1]. Once exposed to the acidic environment of the stomach, ALP releases phosphine gas, a highly toxic compound that disrupts mitochondrial function, impairs oxidative phosphorylation, and induces severe oxidative stress, ultimately leading to cellular energy failure and multiorgan dysfunction [2], [3]. The cardiovascular system is particularly vulnerable, and patients frequently present with refractory hypotension, metabolic acidosis, and circulatory collapse [4]. In the absence of a specific antidote, management relies primarily on early recognition and aggressive supportive care [5].

The diagnosis of ALP poisoning can be especially challenging when the clinical presentation is atypical or confounded by coexisting conditions [6]. Altered mental status is a common but nonspecific manifestation that may arise from various metabolic and toxicological causes [7]. Among these, insulin overdose is a critical consideration, as it can induce profound hypoglycemia leading to neuroglycopenia and impaired consciousness [8], [9]. In such cases, the initial clinical focus is often directed toward rapid correction of hypoglycemia, which may obscure the presence of additional toxic exposures [9].

To our knowledge, this is a rare case of concurrent insulin overdose and ALP poisoning, presenting with overlapping and potentially misleading clinical features. Such dual toxic exposures are rarely reported and pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in acute care settings. This case adds to the existing literature by highlighting the complex pathophysiological interplay, diagnostic pitfalls, and management challenges associated with mixed toxicological presentations. It also underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and performing repeated clinical reassessment in patients with atypical or unexplained presentations.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old previously healthy male was brought to the emergency department by family members due to decreased level of consciousness. According to relatives, the patient had been experiencing severe emotional distress following the death of his father three days earlier. Approximately one hour prior to presentation, he was found lying on his bed in a confused and minimally responsive state, with an empty insulin syringe nearby, suggesting acute hypoglycemia as the initial working diagnosis.

There was no known history of chronic medication use or substance abuse.

Disclosure of aluminum phosphide ingestion

Approximately 90 min after stabilization, the patient became fully responsive and disclosed intentional ingestion of one aluminum phosphide tablet approximately 3 h prior to hospital arrival, in addition to injecting insulin.

He reported nausea and one episode of vomiting prior to arrival.

Given the known high lethality of aluminum phosphide poisoning, immediate toxicological management was initiated.

Gastrointestinal decontamination

A nasogastric tube was inserted.

Gastric lavage was performed using normal saline, followed by administration of 60 mL olive oil via nasogastric tube. The use of olive oil was based on its proposed ability to reduce phosphine gas release and limit further absorption of aluminum phosphide by creating a protective lipid layer over gastric contents.

Cardiovascular findings

Six hours after ICU admission, ECG demonstrated sinus tachycardia with nonspecific ST-segment changes.

Transthoracic echocardiography performed 6 h after ICU admission revealed mild global left ventricular hypokinesia with an estimated ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45%.

A follow-up echocardiogram on day 3 demonstrated significant improvement, with LVEF increasing to 55% and normalization of global ventricular function.

No regional wall motion abnormalities were observed.

Clinical management approach

Management was conducted in a monitored ICU setting following established toxicological and critical care principles. Continuous ECG monitoring, serial laboratory assessments, and frequent glucose measurements were performed.

Therapeutic interventions, including vasopressor support, sodium bicarbonate infusion, and magnesium sulfate administration, were initiated based on predefined clinical thresholds (e.g., MAP <65 mmHg, pH <7.30) and adjusted according to the patient’s physiological response.

Given the nature of a case report, no formal control group was applicable; however, management decisions were guided by standard clinical practice and ongoing reassessment.

Discussion

ALP poisoning remains one of the most lethal toxicological emergencies worldwide, primarily due to its rapid cardiovascular collapse and profound cellular toxicity [10], [11]. The liberation of phosphine gas following ingestion results in severe mitochondrial dysfunction, inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation, and generation of reactive oxygen species, ultimately leading to cellular energy failure and multiorgan dysfunction [2], [3]. Despite historically high mortality rates, emerging evidence suggests that early recognition and aggressive supportive care can significantly improve survival outcomes [5], [12], [13].

A key aspect of this case lies in the pathophysiological interplay between hypoglycemia, insulin overdose, and ALP toxicity. Insulin-induced hypoglycemia deprives cells—particularly neurons—of glucose, their primary energy substrate, resulting in neuroglycopenia and impaired consciousness [8], [9]. Simultaneously, ALP toxicity disrupts mitochondrial ATP production and exacerbates oxidative stress. The combination of these two mechanisms may have led to a compounded state of bioenergetic failure, contributing to early metabolic instability and hemodynamic compromise.

In addition, insulin overdose likely altered the clinical trajectory by masking the early manifestations of ALP poisoning. The patient’s initial presentation with severe hypoglycemia and decreased level of consciousness appropriately prompted immediate glucose correction. However, this initial diagnostic focus may have delayed recognition of concurrent phosphide toxicity, a condition in which early supportive interventions are time-sensitive and potentially lifesaving [5], [6]. This overlap highlights the importance of repeated clinical reassessment following initial stabilization, particularly in patients with unexplained or disproportionate clinical findings.

Comparison with previously reported survival cases provides important clinical context. Survivors of severe ALP poisoning frequently share common features, including early ICU admission, continuous hemodynamic monitoring, timely vasopressor support, and correction of metabolic derangements [14], [15]. Pediatric reports have similarly demonstrated that even severe cardiotoxicity and metabolic acidosis can be reversible with prompt and sustained supportive care, and in some cases, advanced modalities such as extracorporeal support [16]. In our patient, early gastrointestinal decontamination, rapid correction of hypoglycemia, and vigilant ICU management likely played pivotal roles in preventing progression to irreversible shock and multiorgan failure.

From a management standpoint, the coexistence of insulin overdose added a layer of complexity to the clinical course. Continuous glucose monitoring and dextrose infusion were required to prevent recurrent hypoglycemia, while simultaneous hemodynamic instability necessitated aggressive fluid resuscitation, vasopressor support, and correction of metabolic acidosis. Such overlapping priorities can complicate clinical decision-making and require a dynamic, multidisciplinary approach in the intensive care setting. Previous studies have also explored the therapeutic role of insulin in ALP poisoning in the form of high-dose insulin euglycemia therapy; however, in this case, insulin exposure occurred as a toxic overdose rather than a controlled therapeutic intervention, further complicating interpretation of its physiological impact [10], [11].

Importantly, this case carries significant implications for clinical practice, particularly in regions where ALP is readily available and commonly implicated in poisoning. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ALP exposure in patients presenting with unexplained hypotension, metabolic acidosis, or altered mental status—even when an alternative explanation, such as hypoglycemia, is identified. Mixed or overlapping toxicological presentations are diagnostically challenging and require repeated clinical evaluation, thorough history-taking after stabilization, and early initiation of supportive care.

Overall, this case reinforces a critical principle in toxicology and critical care: early recognition, continuous reassessment, and aggressive supportive management can significantly alter outcomes, even in conditions traditionally associated with high mortality. The coexistence of insulin overdose and ALP poisoning further underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance in atypical presentations and highlights the importance of individualized, physiology-driven management strategies.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the findings may not be generalizable to broader patient populations. Second, certain confirmatory diagnostic measurements, such as serum phosphine levels, were not available, which is a common limitation in ALP poisoning cases. Third, although the temporal association between insulin overdose and ALP ingestion was established based on patient disclosure, the exact timing and dose could not be independently verified. Finally, the absence of comprehensive longitudinal biochemical trends may limit detailed interpretation of the dynamic metabolic changes observed during the clinical course.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that survival is possible in severe ALP poisoning, even when complicated by concurrent insulin overdose. Early recognition, prompt supportive care, and continuous clinical reassessment were critical to the favorable outcome. This report highlights the diagnostic challenges of mixed toxicological presentations and underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis. These findings have important implications for clinical practice, particularly in settings where ALP exposure is common.
