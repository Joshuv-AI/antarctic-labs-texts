Solving “pseudo-injective” polynomial equations over finite dynamical systems
Antonio E. Porreca, Marius Rolland

Abstract
We consider the semiring of abstract finite dynamical systems up to isomorphism, with the operations of alternative and synchronous execution. We continue searching for efficient algorithms for solving polynomial equations of the form P ⁡ ( X ) = B P(X)=B , with a constant side B B , with the goal of decomposing complex behaviors into simpler systems. Taking inspiration from the characterization of injective polynomials P P over dynamical systems, which is based on a condition on the lengths of limit cycles of their coefficients, we introduce a more general notion of pseudo-injectivity by relaxing this constraint. We prove that the associated equations can be solved efficiently, even in certain cases where the input is encoded in an exponentially more compact way.



1
Introduction

Abstract finite discrete-time dynamical systems (FDDS) are pairs
(X,f)
where
X
is a finite set of states and
f\colon X\to X
is a transition function (when
f
is implied, we denote
(X,f)
simply as
X
). These objects abstractly represent the dynamic of concrete deterministic models such as finite cellular automata
[
1
]
, automata networks
[
2
]
or reaction systems
[
3
]
.

We often identify these systems with their transition digraphs, which have uniform out-degree one (Fig.
1
).
These graphs are also known as functional digraphs.
Their general shape is a collection of cycles, where each node of a cycle is the root of a finite in-tree (a directed tree with the edges pointing toward the root).
The nodes of the cycles are periodic states, while the others are transient states.

The set
(\mathbb{D},+,\times)
of FDDS up to isomorphism, with the alternative execution of two systems as
addition
and the synchronous parallel
execution of two FDDS as
multiplication
, is a commutative semiring
[
4
]
.
As all semirings, we can define a semiring of polynomials over
\mathbb{D}
and the associated polynomial equations.

Although it has already been proven that general polynomial equations over
\mathbb{D}
(with variables on both sides of the equation) are undecidable, it is easily proved that, if one side of the equation is constant, then the problem becomes decidable (there is just a finite number of possible solutions)
[
4
]
.
This variant of the problem is actually in
{\mathsf{NP}}
: since sums and products can be computed in polynomial time, we can just guess the values of the variables (their size is bounded by the constant side of the equation), evaluate the polynomial, and compare it with the constant side (isomorphism of functional digraphs can be checked in linear time
[
5
]
).

However, more restricted equations are not yet classified in terms of complexity.
For example, we do not know if monomial equations of the form
AX=B
are
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard, in
{\mathsf{P}}
, or possible candidates for an intermediate difficulty.
However, it has been proved that we can find in polynomial time with respect to the size of the inputs (
A
,
B
and
\log k
) the (unique) connected solution of
AX^{k}=B
for all positive integer
k

[
6
]
.
It has also been shown that, when the polynomial
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
is
injective
, the equation
P(X)=B
can be solved in polynomial time. The injective polynomials are exactly those where the coefficient
A_{i}
of a non-constant monomial (
i.e.
, with
i>1
) is
cancelable
(Fig.
1
), that is, it has a fixed point
[
7
]
.
We are interested in finding a generalization of this result to a larger class of polynomials.

We can reformulate the condition of injectivity by saying that there exists an
i\geq 1
such that
A_{i}
is cancelable (
i.e.
,
A_{i}B=A_{i}C
if and only
B=C
for all
B,C
)
[
7
]
.
If an FDDS
A
is cancelable, then it satisfies the following property: denoting by
L(A)
the set of all cycle lengths in
A
, it is always the case that
\min(L(A))
divide all elements of
L(A)
.
We call
pseudo-cancelable
a element which verifies this property, without being necessarily cancelable (Fig.
1
).

In this paper we first prove that equations of the form
AX=B
can be solved in polynomial time if
A
is pseudo-cancelable, then generalize this result to an efficient algorithm for equations of the form
P(X)=B
where
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
is a polynomial such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}
(
i.e.
, the sum of coefficient of non constant terms) is pseudo-cancelable. We refer to this kind of polynomials as
pseudo-injective
.

2
Definitions

The set of FDDS
up to isomorphism
, with mutually
exclusive alternative execution as
sum
and synchronous parallel execution as
product
, forms a semiring
[
4
]
, denoted by
\mathbb{D}
.
The two operations above define a notion of algebraic decomposition of FDDS.

The semiring of function digraphs, with disjoint union for sum and direct product for multiplication, is isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}
.
We recall that the direct product of two graphs
A,B
is the graph
C

such that
V(C)=V(A)\times V(B)
and

E(C)=\{((u,u^{\prime}),(v,v^{\prime}))\mid(u,v)\in E(A),(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in E(B)\}

[
8
]
.
Then, an FDDS can be seen as the sum of connected components, where each component consists of a cycle, representing the periodic behavior, and in-trees (trees directed from leaf to root) rooted in the cycle, representing the transient behavior. An example of the product of two FDDS is depicted in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Product of two FDDS
A
and
B
, where the states are given a temporary label in order to show how the result is computed. Remark that
B
is cancelable (state
6
is a fixed point) and
AB
is pseudo-cancelable (
L(AB)=\{2,4\}
and
\ell(AB)=2
).
A
is also trivially pseudo-cancelable, since it is connected (
L(A)=\{2\}
and
\ell(A)=2
).

The structure of the product is algebraically rich.
For example, this semiring is not factorial,
i.e.
, there exist four irreducible FDDS
A,B,C,D
such that
A\neq C
and
A\neq D
but
AB=CD
.
Moreover, while the periodic behavior of a product is easy to analyze, its transient behavior demands much more work.
Indeed, the product of two connected FDDS
A,B
, having cycles of length
p
and
q
respectively,
generates
\gcd(p,q)
connected components, each with a cycle of size
\lcm(p,q)

[
8
]
.
In order to analyze the (generally more complex) transient behaviors, we use the notion of unroll introduced in
[
9
]
.

Definition 1
(Unroll)

Let
A=(X,f)
be an FDDS.
For each state
u\in X
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
f^{-k}(u)=\{v\in X\mid f^{k}(v)=u\}
the set of
k
-th preimages of
u
.
For each
u
in a cycle of
A
, we call the
unroll tree of
A
in
u
the infinite tree
\mathbf{t}_{u}=(V,E)
having vertices
V=\{(s,k)\mid s\in f^{-k}(u),k\in\mathbb{N}\}
and edges

E=\big\{\big((v,k),(f(v),k-1)\big)\big\}\subseteq V^{2}
.
We call
unroll of
A
, denoted
\mathcal{U}(A)
, the set of its unroll trees.

An unroll tree contains exactly one infinite branch, onto which are periodically rooted the trees representing the transient behaviors of the corresponding connected component.
Remark that the set of unrolls
up to isomorphism
(which will be used in the rest of this paper, unless otherwise specified) with the disjoint union as sum and the levelwise product defined in
[
9
]
(and already exploited in
[
6
,
7
]
) is also a semiring, denoted by
\mathbb{U}
.
In addition, the operation of unrolling an FDDS is a semiring homomorphism
\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{U}
.

An interesting aspect of
\mathbb{U}
is the existence of a total order compatible with the product
[
9
]
, that is, for three unroll trees
\mathbf{t}_{1},\mathbf{t}_{2},\mathbf{t}
we have
\mathbf{t}_{1}\leq\mathbf{t}_{2}
if and only if
\mathbf{t}_{1}\mathbf{t}\leq\mathbf{t}_{2}\mathbf{t}
.
This features has been exploited in order to describe polynomial algorithms for solving certain classes equations of the form
AX=B
or
P(X)=B
with specific condition on
A,X,P
or
B

[
9
,
6
,
7
]
.

Remark that an order compatible with the product does not exist on FDDS.
Indeed, if such an order existed then all FDDS would be cancelable;
however, the cancelable elements in
\mathbb{D}
, are exactly the FDDS that admit a fixed point
[
9
, Theorem 30]
.

In this article we focus our attention on the lengths of the cycles of an FDDS. Denote by
L(A)
the set of cycle lengths of a connected component of
A
, and denote
\ell(A)=\min(L(A))
.
Since least common multiples are important when dealing with the product of connected components, it is useful to introduce the following notion of “anti-lcm”:

Definition 2
(anti-lcm)

Let
a,b
be positive integers with factorizations into primes
\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
and
\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
respectively and such that
a
divides
b
. Then, the
anti-lcm of
b
with respect to
a
is

\alcm_{a}b=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
b_{i}>a_{i}\end{subarray}}^{n}p_{i}^{b_{i}}.

That is,
\alcm_{a}b
is the product of the primes in the factorizations of
a
and
b
with their exponents in
b
, but only if they are strictly larger than their exponents in
a
. For instance, if
a=12=2^{2}\times 3^{1}
and
b=252=2^{2}\times 3^{2}\times 7^{1}
then
\alcm_{a}b=3^{2}\times 7^{1}=63
. An important aspect of this notion (which justifies the name “anti-lgm”) is that the anti-lcm of
\lcm(a,b)
with respect to
a
is the smallest integer
c
such that
\lcm(a,c)=\lcm(a,b)
.

From the characterization of cancelable elements of
\mathbb{D}
, we remark that if
A
is cancelable then every
a\in L(A)
is a multiple of
\ell(a)
.
By dropping the cancelability requirement, we say that an FDDS
A
is
pseudo-cancelable
if every
a\in L(A)
is multiple of
\ell(a)
(Fig.
1
).
Moreover, we call a polynomial
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X_{i}
over
\mathbb{D}

pseudo-injective
if the sum of the coefficients of its non-constant terms
\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}
is pseudo-cancelable; notice that this is indeed a generalization of the actual injectivity condition
[
7
]
.

3
The complexity of solving polynomial equations

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1

Let
B
be an FDDS and
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X_{i}
be a pseudo-injective polynomial.
Then, we can solve
P(X)=B
in polynomial time with respect to the sizes (number of states) of
P
and
B
.

We begin by proving that this is true for equations
AX=B
where
A,X
and
B
are sums of cycles (
i.e.
, their underlying transition functions are permutations).
To this end, we prove that every cycle in
X
must satisfy a certain condition on its size.
For brevity, in the following we denote by
C_{p}
the cycle with length
p
and by
\mathrm{Div}(p)
the set of divisors of
p
.

Lemma 2

Let
A
,
B
and
X
be sums of cycles with
A
pseudo-cancelable.
If
AX=B
then there exists a divisor
k
of
\ell(A)
satisfying
k\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)\in L(X)
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B))=1
.

Proof

Since
AX=B
, by definition of product there exist
a\in L(A)
and
x\in L(X)
such that
\ell(B)=\lcm(a,x)
.
Furthermore,
\lcm(\ell(A),x)\in L(B)
.
It follows that
\lcm(\ell(A),x)\geq\ell(B)
.
Since
A
is pseudo-cancelable, necessarily
\ell(A)
divides
a
.
Thus,
\lcm(\ell(A),x)\leq\lcm(a,x)=\ell(B)
.
Then
\lcm(\ell(A),x)=\ell(B)
and therefore
x=k\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
with
k\in\mathrm{Div}(\ell(A))
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B))=1
.

∎

Corollary 3

If
AX=B
, where
A
and
B
are two sum of cycles and
A
is pseudo-cancelable, then for each cycle
C_{x}\in X
which verifies
C_{\ell(B)}\in AC_{x}
, then there exists a divisor
k
of
\ell(A)
satisfying
k\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)\in L(X)
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B))=1
.

Although Lemma
2
and Corollary
3
partially describe a cycle of
X
, there still remains an unknown, namely the value of
k
.
Nevertheless, as we show in the following lemma, we can fix
k=1
.

Lemma 4

Let
A=\sum_{i\in L(A)}a_{i}C_{i}
and
B,X
be sums of cycles with
A
pseudo-cancelable and let
k\in\mathrm{Div}(\ell(A))
such that
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B))=1
. Then,

A(X+C_{k\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)})=B
if and only if for all
d\in\mathrm{Div}(k)
we have
A(X+dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)})=B
.

Proof

Let
x=k\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
,
d\in\mathrm{Div}(k)
and
a\in L(A)
.
Since
A
is pseudo-cancelable, we have
\ell(A)\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
.
Furthermore, from the definition of
k
, we d educe that
k\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
and also
d\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
.
Consequently,

\gcd(x,a)=\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)\gcd(k,a)=k\times\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a).

In addition, we have
k=\gcd(k,a)=\gcd(d\times(k/d),a)=d\times\gcd(k/d,a)
and
\lcm(x,a)=\lcm(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)
.
By consequence,

\displaystyle C_{a}C_{x}

\displaystyle=\gcd(x,a)C_{\lcm(x,a)}

\displaystyle=k\times\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)C_{\lcm(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

\displaystyle=d\gcd(k/d,a)\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)C_{\lcm(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

\displaystyle=d\gcd((k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)C_{\lcm((k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

\displaystyle=C_{a}dC_{\lcm((k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

This implies

\displaystyle AC_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L(A)}a_{i}C_{i}C_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in L(A)}a_{i}dC_{i}C_{\lcm((k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

\displaystyle=AdC_{\lcm((k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)}

and the lemma follows.

∎

We can now formulate an algorithm for the resolution of
AX=B
working under the hypothesis that
A
and
B
are sums of cycles and
A
is pseudo-cancelable.

Algorithm 5

Given two sums of cycles
A
and
B
with
A
pseudo-cancelable, we can compute
X
such that
AX=B
, if any exists, in the following way:

1.

Check if the number of states of
A
is bounded by the number of state of
B
.

2.

Let
C
be a cycle of length
\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
.

3.

Check if
AC
is a subset of
B
.

4.

Add
C
to
X
.

5.

Set
B\leftarrow B-AC
.

6.

If
B\neq\emptyset
then go to
1
; otherwise return
X
.

This algorithm runs in polynomial time with respect to the number of states of
A
and
B
.
In fact, if we denote by
n
the number of state in
B
, the number of iterations is bounded by
n
.
Moreover, all the instructions in the loop only require polynomial time.
Indeed, line
1
is
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Then, we can compute
\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
simply by counting up to
\ell(B)
and construct
C
in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time, since
n\geq\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)
.
Thus, line
2
is executed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.
Then, we can compute
AC
in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time, and its size is bounded by
n^{2}
.
It follows that checking if
AC
is a subset of
B
and removing
AC
from
B
requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time.
We deduce that lines
3
and
5
are executed in
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
time.
All the other instructions only require constant time.

Furthermore, the solution
X
computed by Algorithm
5
is correct.
Indeed, if
m
is the number of iterations of the algorithm,
C_{i}
the cycle computed in the
i
-th iteration and
B_{i}
the value of
B
at the beginning of the
i
-th iteration, we have
AC_{m}=B_{m}
.
Inductively, we have
B_{i}=AC_{i}+B_{i+1}
.
Since
B_{i+1}=A\sum_{j=i+1}^{m}C_{j}
, it follows that
B_{i}=A\sum_{j=i}^{m}C_{j}
.

All that remains to prove in order to show the correctness of the algorithm is that, if a solution exists, then the algorithm finds it.

Lemma 6

Let
A
and
B
be sums of cycles with
A
be a pseudo-cancelable. If the equation
AX=B
has solutions, then Algorithm
5
finds one maximizing the number of connected components (equivalently, minimizing the cycle lengths).

Proof

Suppose that
AX=B
.
Thus, by Lemma
2
, there exist
k_{1}
and
X_{1}
such that
X=X_{1}+C
with
C=C_{k_{1}\times\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)}
.
Accordingly, we have
AX_{1}=B_{1}
with
B_{1}=B-AC
.
Consequently, by Lemma
4
, the sum of cycles
Y=X_{1}+k_{1}C_{\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)}
satisfies
AY=B
.
This implies
B_{1}=B-Ak_{1}C_{\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)}
.

The number of cycles with length
\ell(B)
generated by
AC
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in L(A)\\
a\mid\ell(B)\end{subarray}}\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a)k_{1}

whereas the number generated by
AC_{\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)}
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in L(A)\\
a\mid\ell(B)\end{subarray}}\gcd(\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B),a).

We deduce that the value of
\ell(B)
in line
2
of the algorithm is the same between the first iteration and the
k_{1}
th iteration of the loop.
This implies that the result contains
k_{1}C_{\alcm_{\ell(A)}\ell(B)}
at the end the
k_{1}
th iteration of the loop.

The lemma follows by induction. Remark that the solution
Y
constructed at each step replaces one cycle by the maximum number of cycles of minimum compatible length while still satisfying the equation; this implies that the solution found by the algorithm maximises the number of connected components

∎

This proves the correctness of Algorithm
5
:

Proposition 7

Given two sums of cycles
A
and
B
with
A
pseudo-cancelable, we can solve in polynomial time the equation
AX=B
.

We can now extend our result to the case of equations
P(X)=B
where
B
and all coefficients of
P
are sums of cycles.
First, we generalize Lemma
2
as follows:

Lemma 8

Let
B
and
Y
be two sums of cycles and
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
be a pseudo-injective polynomial where each coefficient is a sum of cycles.
Let
A_{c}
be a coefficient of
P
such that
\ell(A_{c})=\ell(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i})
.
If
P(Y)=B
then there exists
k\in\mathrm{Div}(\ell(A_{c}))
such that
k\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B-A_{0})\in L(Y)
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B-A_{0}))=1
.

Proof

We assume that
P(Y)=B
.
Thus,
P(Y)-A_{0}=B^{\prime}
with
B^{\prime}=B-A_{0}
.
Therefore, there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
a\in L(A_{i})
and
y\in L(Y)
such that
\ell(B^{\prime})=\lcm(a,y)
.
Furthermore, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
we have
\lcm(\ell(A_{j}),y)\in L(B^{\prime})
.
It follow that
\lcm(\ell(A_{j}),y)\geq\ell(B^{\prime})
for all
j
.
However, by hypothesis on
P
, we have
\ell(A_{c})
divide
a
.
Thus,
\lcm(\ell(A_{c}),y)\leq\lcm(a,y)=\ell(B^{\prime})
.
We deduce that
\lcm(\ell(A_{c}),y)=\ell(B^{\prime})
and therefore
y=k\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B^{\prime})
with
k\in\mathrm{Div}(\ell(A_{c}))
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B^{\prime}))=1
.

∎

Thus, we can generalize Lemma
4
.

Lemma 9

Let
B,Y
be two sums of cycles and
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
be a pseudo-injective polynomial whose each coefficient are a sum of cycles.
Let
A_{c}
be a coefficient of
P
such that
\ell(A_{c})=\ell(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i})
.
Let
k\in\mathrm{Div}(\ell(A_{c}))
such that
k\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B-A_{0})\in L(Y)
and
\gcd(k,\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B-A_{0}))=1
. Then,

P(Y+C_{k\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})=B
if and only if
P(Y+dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})=B
for all
d\in\mathrm{Div}(k)
.

Proof

Let
y=k\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)
,
d\in\mathrm{Div}(k)
and
a\in L(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i})
.
Then
\ell(A_{c})\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
.
Furthermore, from the definition of
k
, we deduce that
k\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
and
d\in\mathrm{Div}(a)
.
Consequently, from the proof of Lemma
4
, it follows that

C_{a}C_{y}^{j}=C_{a}C_{y}^{j-1}(dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})=C_{a}(dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})^{j}

for all natural numbers
j
.
Thus by summing,
A_{j}C_{y}^{j}=A_{j}(dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})^{j}
for all
j
.
We conclude that
P(Y+C_{y})=P(Y+dC_{(k/d)\times\alcm_{\ell(A_{c})}\ell(B)})
.

∎

We deduce that an adaptation of Algorithm
5
can solve in polynomial times the equation
P(Y)=B
if
P
is a pseudo-injective polynomial (Fig.
2
shows an example run).

Algorithm 10

Given a sum of cycles
B
and a pseudo-injective polynomial
P=\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}X^{i}
where each coefficient is a sum of cycles, we can compute
Y
such that
P(Y)=B
, if any exists, in the following way:

1.

Set
B\leftarrow B-A_{0}
,
P\leftarrow P-A_{0}
and
Y\leftarrow\mathbf{0}
.

2.

Check if the number of states of
\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}
is bounded by
|B|
.

3.

Let
C
be a cycle of length
\alcm_{\ell(\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i})}\ell(B)
.

4.

Compute progressively
P(Y+C)
, by checking after each operation that the number of states does not exceed the original right-hand side.

5
Conclusions

In this paper we enlarged the class of efficiently solvable polynomial equations over FDDS by generalizing the notion of injectivity of polynomials. Furthermore, we developed a procedure that allows us to carry over these results to a more compact representation when the FDDS involved are sums of cycles, not only without incurring in an exponential slowdown, but even improving the efficiency of one of the aforementioned algorithms.

This work also highlighted the structure of solutions to equations over pseudo-injective polynomials (Lemmas
12
and
13
), which we expect will be useful for efficiently counting and enumerating the set of solutions. We also believe that a similar structure might be found among the solutions of other equations of the form
P(X)=B
, if the conditions on
P
are relaxed but other conditions on the divisibility of the lengths of the cycles of
B
are introduced.

A more in-depth investigation of compact representations of FDDS in the context of polynomial equations is also interesting, since in most applications the dynamics of the systems are not described explicitly, and the detection of simple dynamical properties usually becomes intractable, notably with a succinct encoding by Boolean circuits.

Another open problem is finding complexity lower bounds for different classes of equations. The exact complexity of solving equations of the form
AX=B
or, more generally,
P(X)=B
of a single variable, but without other restrictions, is still open. Is it an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard problem or does it admit an efficient algorithm? Does this generalize to any constant number of variables? Furthermore, how much can we relax the constraints on the number of variables and the fact that the right-hand side of equations must be constant without them becoming intractable or even undecidable
[
4
]
?
