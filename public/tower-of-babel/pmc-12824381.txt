Application of varied datum in time-to-depth conversion for high resolution coal seam micromorphology characterization
Authors: Qifan Zhang, Dameng Liu, Suoliang Chang, Yidong Cai, Sheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coal seam burial depth is a key factor evaluating coal seam sweet spots, and research on coalbed methane (CBM) exploration and development, particularly the deployment of horizontal CBM wells, requires high-precision characterization of coal seam morphology. However, conventional time-to-depth conversion methods for three-dimensional (3D) seismic data exhibit limited accuracy under complex surface conditions. In this study, the floating datum in the depth domain was accurately defined within the depth domain based on static correction values, and that a high-resolution spliced velocity model was successfully constructed by splicing inversion and migration velocities. On this basis, a novel time-to-depth conversion method using the floating datum was proposed, improving the prediction accuracy of coal seam burial depth. The results demonstrate that the proposed method reduced the depth prediction error for the No. 3 coal seam from 2% to 0.87% within the study area, providing a novel methodological framework support for the development of CBM horizontal wells. Furthermore, the obtained burial depth of the No. 3 coal seam exhibited a strong correlation with its physical properties and gas content, demonstrating that accurate characterization of coal seam burial depth effectively guides evaluation of CBM sweet spots.

Introduction

Coal seam burial depth is a critical factor influencing its physical properties and gas-bearing characteristics, playing a vital role in guiding the evaluation of CBM sweet spots1. Moreover, the development of horizontal CBM wells demands high-precision characterization of the microscopic morphology of coal seams, primarily because accurate burial depth provides a scientific basis for targeted deployment of CBM development plans2. Therefore, accurate prediction of coal seam burial depth is critical in coalbed methane (CBM) exploration and development. Such precise prediction relies on geophysical methods, such as logging and seismic data. 3D seismic technology, which has been widely applied, offers significant advantages in controlling the spatial occurrence of coal seams3,4. As most seismic data are processed in the time domain, time-to-depth conversion is required to characterize coal seam morphology in the depth domain during interpretation. There are three fundamental factors that affect the accuracy of time-to-depth conversion: accurate time, precise speed and appropriate methods. Due to the influence of seismic data resolution and subjective interpretation on the time, the discussion holds limited practical significance. Therefore, this paper emphasizes velocity and methodological developments instead. With increasing geological and surface complexity in exploration areas, the selection of an appropriate datum and the construction of an accurate average velocity have become key factors influencing the accuracy of time-to-depth conversion5. Therefore, accurately determining the datum and constructing a reliable velocity field are essential for improving time-to-depth conversion precision based on seismic and logging data.

There are two primary types of datum used in time-to-depth conversion: a fixed datum, which serves as the zero-time reference plane in the final result, and a floating datum, which is a smoothed surface derived from the fixed datum values across different common midpoint (CMP) gathers6. Traditionally, time-to-depth conversion is conducted using a fixed datum with methods such as time-depth fitting and the layer stripping technique7. However, correcting the velocity to a fixed datum can introduce seismic wavefield distortion due to the influence of complex surface topography, reducing the accuracy of the derived average velocity and limiting its ability to reflect true geological structures. As surface conditions become more complex and demands for high-resolution seismic interpretation increase, a fixed datum is no longer sufficient to support fine-scale exploration. Consequently, the floating datum has been introduced8.

In seismic data processing, static correction and velocity analysis are commonly performed on a floating datum in the time domain to avoid velocity distortion. However, time-to-depth conversion based on a floating datum requires defining this datum in the depth domain. The floating datum in depth domain could be represented by a smoothed version of the surface elevation9. Nevertheless, this method has been considered inadequate for interpreting subtle tectonic features. Alternatively, it has been proposed that the depth-domain floating datum depends on surface elevation, low-velocity zone anisotropy, and high-velocity crest morphology. However, a precise definition has yet to be established10.

Another critical issue influencing time-to-depth conversion accuracy is obtaining a reliable average velocity. Migration velocity obtained through pre-stack time migration is rich in spatial information and serves as an important input for velocity construction11. Stacking velocity can be converted into average velocity using the Dix formula, followed by mathematical correction12. However, this method does not account for structural influences. Therefore, model-based iterative inversion was introduced to offer better results than direct layer velocity derivation via the Dix formula, although it remains highly model-dependent13. Besides, a CMP-coherent velocity inversion method based on ray tracing can better capture lateral variations in layer velocity14. Yet, while ray-based methods avoid the hyperbolic assumption, they suffer from cumulative velocity errors with increasing depth and perform poorly in complex geological settings15.

Obtaining high-precision velocity remains a key challenge for accurate time-to-depth conversion. The average velocity is often derived from velocity spectra using the Dix formula, but significant errors can be introduced due to outliers and its assumption of horizontal, isotropic layers16. In contrast, migration velocity from pre-stack time migration provides richer spatial detail and serves as a key input for velocity modeling. Additionally, seismic inversion velocity offers even higher resolution compared to drilling interpolation velocity, velocity spectra, and pre-stack migration velocity17. However, its use is limited by log data availability and typically covers only a narrow range around the target formation, requiring integration with other velocity sources18. Thus, constructing a high-precision composite velocity model is essential for improving prediction accuracy of coal seam floor elevation.

This study targets the No. 3 coal seam in the Qinshui Basin. First, a method for optimizing the floating datum in the depth domain under complex surface conditions was proposed. Subsequently, a high-resolution velocity field was constructed by splicing seismic inversion velocity with offset velocity under the floating datum. Based on this, a new time-to-depth conversion method incorporating the floating datum was developed to predict the floor elevation of the No. 3 coal seam. The reserved verification wells demonstrated the high accuracy of this approach. Comparison with conventional conversion methods confirmed the superiority of the proposed method in improving coal seam morphological prediction accuracy. These findings support intelligent mining and horizontal well development. Furthermore, correlation analysis between the burial depth of the No. 3 coal seam and its physical properties and gas content highlights the importance of accurate depth characterization, offering practical guidance for evaluating CBM sweet spots and enrichment areas.

Geological background

The study area is located in the southern Qinshui Basin, where the surface terrain is complex and the strata generally dip northeastward. The dip angles are typically less than 10°, but can locally reach up to 20° due to structural deformation19–21.

The main coal-bearing strata comprise the Lower Permian Taiyuan and Shanxi Formations, with the No. 3 coal seam representing a laterally stable and economically mineable layer. The stratigraphic boundary between the Shanxi Formation and the overlying Lower Shihezi Formation is delineated by the K8 sandstone, which consists of medium-grained quartz sandstone overlain by a distinctive aluminous mudstone, and is in conformable contact with the underlying strata. Analysis of stratigraphic cyclicity within the coal-bearing sequences of the North China Plate indicates that the Shanxi Formation was deposited in a transitional deltaic environment between continental and marine settings, during which the coal-forming environments underwent multiple evolutionary stages22. Due to the influence of such transitional sedimentary conditions, the lithology and seismic velocity of coal-bearing strata exhibit rapid lateral variations23.

Data sources

Static correction quantity

Significant variations in surface elevation, formation thickness, and lateral velocity within near-surface low-velocity zones result in time discrepancies between seismic traces, thereby introducing challenges in static correction. Static correction values are typically obtained through chromatographic analysis and are used to restore reflected waves to an approximate hyperbolic time–distance relationship, thereby minimizing associated errors24.

The static correction quantity can be decomposed into two components: a high-frequency component and a low-frequency component. The high-frequency component, which is closely related to abrupt changes in surface elevation, primarily determines whether seismic data can be properly imaged. In contrast, the low-frequency component reflects broader variations in the trend surface elevation near the surface and governs whether the seismic data can accurately represent the structural morphology of subsurface layers25.

Seismic inversion velocity and migration velocity

Seismic inversion is commonly employed for lithological interpretation, reservoir prediction, and sedimentary facies analysis. In addition, the velocity derived from seismic inversion can also be utilized for time-to-depth conversion. Among various velocity types, inversion velocity provides the highest resolution, followed by pre-stack migration velocity.

In recent years, seismic facies-controlled inversion has advanced rapidly. By incorporating seismic facies to constrain the inversion process and reduce the degrees of freedom, this method improves accuracy by integrating structural and lithological characteristics. Seismic facies-controlled inversion directly yields the acoustic impedance volume, which is the product of formation density and P-wave velocity. Because the density difference is much smaller than the P-wave velocity difference, separate the P-wave velocity from the inversion wave impedance by using Gardner formula based on the statistical relationship of rock physics. Compared to conventional velocity extraction methods, inversion velocity is more accurate, capable of capturing thin-layer features, and possesses clearer geological significance26.

Various methods have been proposed for constructing migration velocity; in this study, a ray-tracing-based approach was adopted27. This method involves generating CMP gathers via ray tracing. An initial velocity model is established through iterative dip moveout (DMO) corrections on the velocity spectrum. Subsequently, offset stack profiles generated using different percentage velocities are compared and integrated with the CMP gathers to identify the optimal velocity, thereby establishing the migration velocity field28. As the velocity analysis is referenced to a floating datum, the derived migration velocity is not affected by vertical time shifts, enabling it to more accurately reflect the true subsurface velocity structure.

Drilling data

The coal seam elevation obtained from drilling data is considered the most accurate. However, due to the limited number of wells, the lateral resolution remains low. An effective and cost-efficient approach is to perform time-to-depth conversion using seismic data while constraining the conversion results with drilling data. In this study, 24 wells within the study area were utilized to constrain the time-to-depth conversion. Among them, 19 wells were selected as calibration wells, while the remaining 5 were reserved for verification purposes, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c). The verification wells are distributed uniformly across the study area and cover various terrains, including complex areas such as high-slope regions and fault zones.

Calculation of floating datum in depth domain

Currently, two types of datums are commonly used in time-to-depth conversion. The first is the fixed datum, which is defined by a constant elevation. The second is the floating datum, which is essentially a smoothed surface derived from adjacent CMP points with varying elevations.

CMP means common midpoint. In seismic data acquisition, the combination of a single source activation and its reception is termed a trace. During seismic data processing, multi-fold coverage is employed to suppress noise. The traces with the same midpoint for each shot and receiver points are extracted and formed into a gather, known as the CMP gather. This midpoint is called the CMP point. Within a CMP gather, both shot and receiver points are ideally located on the same horizontal plane, to which seismic data are corrected. However, under complex surface conditions, significant elevation differences among shot and receiver points can result in static correction errors. To address this, a floating datum is introduced as part of a two-step static correction process25.

Static correction is typically performed in the time domain, where the floating datum exists as a temporal concept without a defined spatial elevation. Accordingly, the floating datum in the depth domain must be determined based on the results of the two-step static correction.

Two-step static correction

The two-step static correction process begins with determining the elevation of the floating datum. Subsequently, the static correction is decomposed into two components: a high-frequency component (RS) and a low-frequency component (RG). The original seismic data are first corrected to the floating datum by applying the high-frequency component. Following this correction, velocity analysis and stacking imaging are performed. Finally, the low-frequency component is applied to shift the seismic data onto the fixed datum, ensuring consistency across the dataset29.

Crucially, the accurate definition of the floating datum elevation Hp is fundamental to the effectiveness of static correction.

Calculation methods of floating datum in depth domain

A calculation method for determining the floating datum in the depth domain is proposed for areas with complex surface conditions and multiple low-velocity layers. First, the seismic time difference between the fixed datum and the bottom of the low-velocity zone is calculated, and the low-frequency component is then subtracted to obtain the time difference between the floating datum and the bottom of the low-velocity zone. Average velocity calculation based on inversion velocity and migration velocity

The velocities obtained through different methods represent distinct velocity types; therefore, the average velocity between the floating datum and the No. 3 coal seam must be calculated for time-to-depth conversion. Since velocities derived based on the floating datum accurately reflect the subsurface velocity structure, all velocities referenced to the floating datum are utilized for velocity splicing to obtain the average velocity. Among these, seismic inversion velocity is preferred due to its highest accuracy. However, seismic inversion is typically performed only for target layers where logging data are available and within a limited range above and below these layers. As a result, inversion velocity does not extend to the zero time of the seismic data, leading to a lack of velocity information for shallow layers.

Migration velocity, which starts from zero time and exhibits high accuracy, is used to supplement the seismic inversion velocity. Since migration velocity is the root mean square (RMS) velocity, it must be converted into layer velocity—consistent with seismic inversion velocity—using the Dix formula for subsequent velocity splicing.

A method for splicing these two velocities to form a complete layer velocity has been proposed18. To maximize retention of the high-resolution inversion velocity, the K8 time horizon was selected as the splicing interface, chosen for its proximity to zero time. Ultimately, the complete velocity model was constructed by combining inversion velocity below this interface and migration velocity above it. Time-to-depth conversion method

(1) Obtaining layers in the time domain.

Time-to-depth conversion aims to transform seismic interpretation results from the time domain into the depth domain. The first step involves acquiring accurate seismic horizons in the time domain. By performing layer tracking after well-seismic calibration, the time-domain horizon of the No. 3 coal seam can be delineated. Since seismic interpretation is referenced to the zero-time surface, it is necessary to subtract the low-frequency component of the static correction to obtain the No. 3 coal seam horizon relative to the floating datum.

(2) Calculating average velocity.

As described in Sect. “Average velocity calculation based on inversion velocity and migration velocity”, part of the layer velocity is derived from seismic inversion, while the remainder is obtained by converting migration velocity into layer velocity using the Dix formula. These two components are then spliced to construct a complete layer velocity profile. Based on this profile, the average velocity between the floating datum and the No. 3 coal seam is calculated.

(3) Calculating the floating datum in the depth domain.

Once the total static correction is obtained, it is decomposed into high-frequency and low-frequency components. These components are used to calculate the elevation of the floating datum in the depth domain, as described in Sect. “Calculation methods of floating datum in depth domain”.

(4) Determining the floor elevation of the No. 3 coal seam.

After aligning all relevant data to the floating datum, the depth difference between the No. 3 coal seam and the floating datum is computed using the time and average velocity. This depth difference is then added to the depth-domain elevation of the floating datum to derive the coal seam floor elevation, as shown in Eq. 8. The relative error between the calculated and observed coal seam elevations is evaluated using calibration wells, as defined in Eq. 9. Results and discussion

Application and effectiveness of the new method

Fine-scale structural interpretation of seismic data was carried out to delineate the No. 3 coal seam in the time domain, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Subsequently, seismic inversion velocity and migration velocity were spliced to compute the average velocity of the No. 3 coal seam, as shown in Fig. 6. In parallel, the floating datum in the depth domain was calculated based on the static correction components. Time-to-depth conversion was then performed using this floating datum, ultimately yielding the floor elevation of the No. 3 coal seam. By employing drilling data from 19 calibration wells, the depth-converted results were calibrated, and the final elevation of the No. 3 coal seam floor was determined, as shown in Fig. 7(a).

Accuracy comparison and applicability of the new method

The calculated floor elevation of the No. 3 coal seam was compared with results from two conventional time-to-depth conversion methods to comprehensively verify the accuracy and effectiveness of the proposed method, as summarized in Table 2. The first conventional method involves calculating the coal seam floor elevation using a spliced velocity based on a fixed datum. The second method uses a velocity derived from drilling data interpolation, combined with the previously defined floating datum. The effectiveness of time-to-depth conversion under the floating datum was evaluated by comparing the proposed method with Method One. Furthermore, the effectiveness of using the spliced velocity—composed of seismic inversion and migration velocities—was assessed by comparing the proposed method with Method Two.

(1) Method One: Time-to-depth conversion based on fixed datum and spliced velocity.

The fixed datum in the study area was set at 1350 m. Following the procedure outlined in Sect. “Average velocity calculation based on inversion velocity and migration velocity”, a spliced velocity was constructed and combined with the No. 3 coal seam horizon in the time domain to perform the time-to-depth conversion. The results are shown in Fig. 7(b).

(2) Method Two: Time-to-depth conversion based on floating datum and drilling-interpolated velocity.

The floating datum was computed using the approach described in Sect. “Calculation of floating datum in depth domain”. Then, an average velocity model was constructed based on interpolation of drilling data. This velocity model was combined with the No. 3 coal seam time-domain interpretation for time-to-depth conversion. The resulting floor elevation map is shown in Fig. 7(c).

A comparative analysis of the horizontal distribution results for the No. 3 coal seam floor elevation, obtained using the three different time-to-depth conversion methods, reveals that the overall morphological trend is consistent across all methods—higher in the southwest and lower in the northeast. However, notable differences emerge in the finer structural details. The new method demonstrates a stronger response to subtle variations in elevation compared to the two conventional methods. For instance, while both conventional methods depict the coal seam morphology as a gently inclined monocline, the new method reveals the presence of local secondary folds. This enhanced structural resolution is also evident in the cross-sectional morphology of the No. 3 coal seam, as shown in Fig. 8.

Furthermore, verification boreholes Z03 and Z14—located in areas exhibiting these micro-amplitude structural features—indicate that the new method yields the smallest prediction errors. This comprehensive evidence confirms that the proposed method is capable of finely and accurately characterizing local structural variations, particularly those associated with subtle folding or undulation.

It was observed that the new method yielded the smallest errors at all five verification wells, thereby demonstrating the highest overall accuracy, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Additionally, the new method showed notable improvements in accuracy across most areas, with the accuracy gains at the verification wells ranging from 28% to 76%. In particular, the improvement at well Z14 exceeded 96%, indicating a significant enhancement in predictive performance.

We attribute the large error observed in Method One to the pronounced difference between the fixed datum and the floating datum at well Z14, where the floating datum is the lowest among all verification points. This substantial discrepancy led to a considerable variation in the calculated inversion and migration velocities, thereby increasing the average velocity error. In Method Two, the primary source of error lies in the velocity interpolation. At well Z14, the No. 3 coal seam exhibits the shallowest burial depth and the greatest variation in depth, which exceeds the interpolation capacity of the drilling-based velocity model, resulting in substantial inaccuracies in the average velocity.

Our data is specific to a certain region, but our proposed method can be applied to other regions. Our method is designed for situations with complex surface conditions and high requirements for coal seam burial depth. And our method requires 3D seismic data and surface elevation, as well as data from static correction and seismic inversion. As long as these data can be obtained, our method can be extended to other regions.

Typically, the 3D seismic data for a certain region remains fixed, as reacquiring high-resolution seismic data is prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the resolution of the available seismic data is effectively constrained. The time horizons interpreted using different methods yielded minimal discrepancies, exerting a negligible impact on time-to-depth conversion results. Hence, we focus on the construction of the velocity and the methodology for floating datum calculation. Our approach maximizes the utility of existing 3D seismic data, which remains fixed. Advancing seismic technology may enable reprocessing of legacy data for enhanced resolution, which would improve time-to-depth conversion accuracy pending future methodological progress.

Even with accurate prediction of coal seam depth, drilling horizontal wells still requires assistance from near-bit gamma log to improve the rate of penetration (ROP)30. However, some challenges remain. When the coal seam is thin or has a complex shape, there is very limited space for the drill bit to respond. Additionally, conventional near-bit gamma log has inherent limitations: it remains at a certain distance from the drill bit, and the tool itself has measurement accuracy constraints. During high-speed drilling, these factors can cause a detection delay - the drill bit may have already entered the surrounding rock before the logging tool detects the change.

Therefore, accurately predicting the coal seam is highly valuable. It helps in planning the trajectory of horizontal wells and guides the drill bit to adjust direction in advance, thereby ensuring a high ROP.

Given these findings, we conducted separate, focused discussions on the roles of datum selection and velocity construction in the time-to-depth conversion process to further evaluate their respective influences on the accuracy of the predicted coal seam morphology.

Advantages of time-to-depth conversion based on floating datum

According to the principles of multiple-coverage seismic exploration, the shot points and receiver points within a CMP gather must be situated on the same horizontal plane, and seismic data interpretation requires the data to be corrected to this reference plane. In earlier 3D seismic surveys of coalfields, a fixed datum was typically used for seismic data processing across the entire study area. This approach was sufficient due to the relatively simple surface conditions and gentle terrain. However, in areas with complex surface topography, the large vertical separation between shot (or receiver) points and the fixed datum can introduce significant errors during static correction. These errors may distort seismic waveforms and degrade the accuracy of time-to-depth conversion13. Consequently, the use of a fixed datum alone is no longer adequate to meet the precision demands of seismic interpretation in fine-scale exploration, prompting the introduction of a floating datum for two-step static correction25.

In this correction process, the total static correction is decomposed into high-frequency and low-frequency components31. Initially, the high-frequency component is applied to correct seismic data to the floating datum, and velocity analysis is conducted on this adjusted reference. Subsequently, the low-frequency component is added to shift the seismic data to the fixed datum32. Because the velocity analysis is based on the floating datum, the resulting velocity model more accurately reflects the true velocity structure of the subsurface, thereby ensuring improved reliability in time-to-depth conversion. In contrast, applying a correction to the fixed datum—especially under complex surface conditions—can distort the velocity field and degrade accuracy33. Hence, velocities derived under the floating datum framework are generally more precise and more suitable for time-to-depth conversion.

To verify the accuracy of the floating datum calculated by the proposed method, we conducted a correlation analysis between the time-to-depth conversion error and the magnitude of the floating datum correction. Specifically, we computed the difference between the floating and fixed datums for each of the five verification wells. We then analyzed the relationship between this difference and the conversion errors obtained using Method One and the proposed method, as illustrated in Fig. 10(a). The results revealed that larger differences between the floating and fixed datums correspond to greater time-to-depth conversion errors. Moreover, for the same datum difference, Method One consistently yielded larger errors compared to the proposed method. This suggests that as the magnitude of the low-frequency component increases (due to greater surface complexity), so too does the inaccuracy in the velocity field used for conversion—particularly under a fixed datum framework. In contrast, the new method proposed in this study mitigates these issues and enables more accurate velocity estimation and time-to-depth conversion.

The necessity of introducing inversion velocity for time-to-depth conversion

Time-depth conversion involves various sources of velocity data, such as interpolated average velocity and RMS velocity obtained through seismic data processing. However, interpolated average velocity typically suffers from low accuracy due to its simplistic interpolation methods34. RMS velocity, while more robust, must be transformed into layer velocity using the Dix formula. The limitation here is that the Dix formula assumes horizontally layered, homogeneous, and isotropic media—conditions that are rarely met in real-world geological formations35. This mismatch introduces errors into the derived velocities and consequently degrades the precision of time-to-depth conversion.

In contrast, inversion velocity offers a more accurate alternative that avoids the assumptions required by the Dix formula and provides significantly higher resolution. Specifically, seismic facies-controlled inversion enhances inversion resolution by integrating seismic phase control with structural and lithological information36. The inversion process begins with constructing a geologically meaningful initial model based on well log data and seismic facies interpretation37. Seismic traces are then extrapolated in a trace-by-trace manner. This methodology is particularly effective in delineating thin layers and anomalous bodies, which often exhibit clear geological significance.

Furthermore, seismic inversion combines the mid-frequency content of seismic data with the low- and high-frequency components derived from well logs, producing an inversion result with improved resolution across a broad frequency range. The Gardner equation is subsequently employed to extract velocity from the inversion result. The resulting inversion velocity thus has superior accuracy in both vertical and horizontal directions, providing a solid foundation for reliable time-to-depth conversion.

To further assess the accuracy of combining inversion velocity and migration velocity, a correlation analysis was conducted between the error of time-to-depth conversion and the average velocity accuracy. Specifically, the true average velocity for five verification wells was first calculated. Next, we statistically analyzed the deviation between this true average velocity and the interpolated average velocity, as well as the deviation between the true average velocity and the spliced velocity (composed of inversion and migration velocities). We then examined how these deviations correlated with the time-to-depth conversion errors of Method Two and the proposed method, respectively, as shown in Fig. 10(b).

The results show a clear trend: the greater the deviation from the true average velocity, the larger the resulting error in time-to-depth conversion, regardless of the method used. However, Method Two consistently exhibits higher errors than the proposed method. This indicates that the spliced velocity—obtained by combining inversion velocity and migration velocity—produces a more accurate average velocity, thereby significantly enhancing the precision of time-to-depth conversion.

The influence of burial depth on the physical properties and gas content of the reservoir

The burial depth of coal seams is a critical factor influencing their physical properties and gas content38. As burial depth increases, temperature and pressure rise, accelerating the coalification process. These changes in the reservoir environment significantly affect the development characteristics of the reservoir. Therefore, accurately characterizing the burial depth of coal seams is essential for effectively guiding the evaluation of coal seam sweet spots and the identification of CBM enrichment zones39.

Using the floor elevation of the No. 3 coal seam predicted by the new method, the burial depth was calculated. Subsequently, the relationships between burial depth and key parameters, including density, porosity, maximum vitrinite reflectance (Ro, max), and gas content—were analyzed. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the regression line was calculated and plotted using standard methods based on the t-distribution, as shown in Fig. 11.

Due to the complex factors affecting the physical and gas content of coal seam, the coefficient of determination was moderate, but the narrow CI around the regression line suggests a reliable estimate of the underlying relationship. The results indicate that as the burial depth of the No. 3 coal seam increases, density increases while porosity decreases. Conversely, both gas content and Ro, max increase with depth. Deeper burial depth leads to higher formation pressure, enhancing coal compaction and thus increasing density. This compaction reduces the pore space, causing porosity to decrease. Moreover, increasing burial depth raises temperature and pressure simultaneously, intensifying coal metamorphism and gas generation capacity, ultimately resulting in higher gas content.
