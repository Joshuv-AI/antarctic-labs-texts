Effective serum concentration of high-dose methotrexate therapy for osteosarcoma with dose adjustment for pharmacokinetic parameters: a retrospective study
Authors: Ayumu Nagamine, Takuya Araki, Hideaki Yashima, Akane Kamimura, Takumi Shiraishi, Takashi Yanagawa, Kyoko Obayashi, Koujirou Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn high-dose methotrexate (MTX) (HD-MTX) therapy, a dosage regimen utilizing the pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters of individual patients (PK method) has been developed to successfully reduce the intra- and inter-individual variability of serum MTX concentration. In this study, we evaluated the relationship between the therapeutic effect and serum MTX concentration in HD-MTX therapy performed under conditions in which the serum MTX concentration was precisely controlled using the PK method.MethodsHD-MTX therapy consisted of 1 h of rapid dosing and 5 h of maintenance dosing, with a target serum concentration of 700–1000 µmol/L 1–6 h after the start of administration. The initial MTX dose was determined based on the body surface area, and subsequent courses were determined using PK parameters calculated from changes in MTX concentration at 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after the start of MTX administration. The relationship between the maximum serum MTX concentration (Cmax) and mean concentration from 1 to 6 h after MTX administration [Cmean(1−6)] and efficacy was evaluated. The cut-off values for Cmax and Cmean(1−6) were the values with the best sensitivity and specificity in terms of 5-year survival in the receiver operating characteristic curve.ResultsWe included 33 patients with osteosarcoma without distant metastasis who underwent HD-MTX therapy. Those with a Cmean(1−6) > 768 µmol/L had significantly longer overall survival (OS) (p = 0.010) and tended to have longer event-free survival (EFS) (p = 0.061), with a 5-year survival rate as high as 92.9% (p = 0.077). In contrast, prolonged OS, EFS, and 5-year survival rates were observed in patients with Cmax >936 µmol/L, but none of these differences were significant (p = 0.088, 0.200, and 0.103, respectively). Moreover, OS and EFS improved for Cmean(1−6) > 768 µmol/L regardless of Cmax. In a subgroup analysis of 24 patients aged < 20 years, OS and EFS were significantly prolonged in patients with a Cmean(1−6) > 768 µmol/L (p = 0.010 and 0.025, respectively).ConclusionsThe usefulness of the serum MTX concentration was demonstrated by designing dosing using the PK method. Furthermore, Cmean(1−6) contributed more to prolonging OS and EFS than Cmax.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15437-6.

Background

Osteosarcoma is the most commonly diagnosed primary bone tumor in children and adolescents [1–5]. The treatment of osteosarcoma consists of surgery and systemic chemotherapy to prevent and treat distant metastases. With advances in combined systemic chemotherapy and surgical resection, the cure rate of osteosarcoma has exceeded 70% [6, 7]. One chemotherapy regimen reported to be highly effective for osteosarcoma is high-dose methotrexate (MTX) (HD-MTX) therapy.

MTX targets dihydrofolate reductase, an important metabolic component of nucleotide biosynthesis, and induces cell death by arresting the folate cycle. Treatment of osteosarcoma involves the inhibition of cancer cell growth by delivering MTX to cancer cells in a concentration-dependent (passive) manner by administering high doses of MTX and minimizing the effect on normal cells by administering leucovorin, an active folate that only normal cells have an uptake mechanism for (leucovorin rescue therapy), thereby minimizing the effect of MTX on normal cells [8–10]. Adverse events associated with HD-MTX therapy can be controlled by controlling the serum MTX concentration; therefore, it is recommended to monitor the serum concentration of MTX at the time of HD-MTX therapy and adjust the dose such that the serum MTX concentration is less than 10, 1, and 0.1 µmol/L at 24, 48, and 72 h after the initiation of MTX therapy, respectively [9, 11, 12]. However, because MTX is delivered to tumor cells in a concentration-dependent manner and its antitumor effect is time-dependent, controlling MTX at the highest possible serum concentration is also required. Several studies have reported the maximum serum MTX concentration (Cmax) for high therapeutic effect and the relationship between Cmax and adverse events [13–16]; in clinical practice, the target concentration range for Cmax is 700–1,000 µmol/L for administration by continuous infusion for 6 h [15, 17–20]. However, there are many opposing and controversial views regarding the serum MTX concentration required to achieve the maximum therapeutic effect [13–16].

In 2010, Fujita et al. [21] developed a dosing design method that used the pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters of individual patients (PK method). By combining a 1-h loading dose with a 5-h maintenance dose, they could design dosing that reached and maintained the effective concentration range early. We previously reported that the PK method allows more precise control of serum MTX levels than the conventional method, which uses MTX doses calculated from body surface area (BSA) [22]. The PK method was also confirmed to provide more optimal control of serum MTX concentrations 72 h after administration and to increase the probability of controlling serum MTX concentrations within 6 h of administration to the target concentration range of 700–1,000 µmol/L [22]. Although our previous studies demonstrated that the PK method improves the attainment rate of target serum MTX concentrations, it has not been fully clarified whether maintaining MTX levels within the target range contributes to improved therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, this study was designed as a pilot investigation to explore the relationship between precisely controlled serum MTX concentrations, achieved through pharmacokinetically guided dosing, and clinical outcomes in patients with osteosarcoma.

Methods

Participants and HD-MTX regimen

All patients newly diagnosed with osteosarcoma who underwent HD-MTX therapy at the Department of Orthopedic Surgery, Gunma University Hospital, between April 2004 and March 2020 were enrolled in this study. Patients with recurrent or multiple lesions were excluded from the study. Patients with distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Gunma University (Approval No.: HS2020-002) and Takasaki University of Health and Welfare (Approval No.: 2007) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective study using data collected since 2004, the ethics committees approved the use of an opt-out approach, and all study information was made publicly available to allow participants or their families to decline participation if they wished.

During HD-MTX therapy, the MTX dose during the first course was determined using the BSA method. Of the total dose (8–12 g/m²), approximately 45% (range, 40–50%) was administered as a 1-h loading dose, and the remaining 55% (range, 50–60%) was administered as a 5-h maintenance infusion. In the subsequent courses, loading and maintenance infusion doses were calculated by the PK method using the PK parameters of each patient, which were calculated based on their serum concentration profiles from the previous course, according to a report by Fujita et al. [21]. The target serum MTX concentration was defined as 700–1000 µmol/L after 1–6 h and less than 10, 1, and 0.1 µmol/L at 24, 48, and 72 h, respectively, from the start of administration. The loading and maintenance doses were slightly adjusted by approximately ± 10% from the calculated values. This adjustment range was determined in advance through discussions between our research group and the physicians in the Department of Orthopedic Surgery when the PK-based dosing method was introduced, and the same principle was applied consistently throughout the study. Leucovorin rescue was initiated 24 h after starting HD-MTX therapy. Leucovorin was started at a dose of 21 mg administered every 3 h and adjusted according to the serum concentration of MTX at 48 and 72 h. After 72 h of MTX treatment, leucovorin was continued until the serum MTX concentration reached 0.1 µmol/L. Sodium bicarbonate was administered to maintain a urine pH >7 and hydration and acetazolamide were administered to maintain urine volume.

Data collection

Electronic medical records from Gunma University Hospital were used to retrospectively survey the patient history, concomitant chemotherapeutic agents, and MTX-related laboratory data. The following characteristics were surveyed: age, sex, height, weight, BSA, diagnosis, site of onset, MTX dose, serum MTX concentration (a total of 10 points at 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after the start of MTX treatment, as C1‒C72), number of courses of HD-MTX therapy, laboratory data (hemoglobin [Hb], white blood cell [WBC], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], and serum creatinine levels), and treatment of patients with toxic MTX levels. Serum MTX concentrations were measured using fluorescence polarization immunoassay (TDxFLx Analyzer; Abbott Laboratories, Chicago, IL, USA).

Assessment of efficacy and safety

Cmax is the highest serum concentration within C1‒C6, and Cmean(1−6) is the mean concentration from 1 to 6 h after MTX administration. Efficacy was evaluated based on the overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS). OS was defined as the period from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause, whereas EFS was defined as the period from the date of diagnosis to the occurrence of recurrence, distant metastasis, or death due to any cause, whichever occurred first. The incidence of anemia, leukopenia, hepatic dysfunction, and renal dysfunction within 1 week of MTX administration was assessed to determine safety. Hb, WBC, AST, ALT, and creatinine clearance were used as indices of anemia, leukopenia, hepatic dysfunction, and renal dysfunction, respectively. Creatinine clearance was estimated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation. The Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0, was used to evaluate the grade of adverse events.

Calculation of dosage in the PK method

The PK parameters and dosages were calculated using the method described by Fujita et al. [21]. Briefly, serial blood samples were obtained at 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after the start of MTX infusion. To estimate the PK parameters, the serum concentration profiles of MTX were assumed to be a linear two-compartment model and analyzed by the non-linear least square method using the MULTI program [23]. The target concentration of C1‒C6 was set to 700 µmol/L.

Setting cut-off values

The sensitivity and specificity of Cmax and Cmean(1−6) in terms of 5-year survival were assessed using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the optimal cut-off value was defined as the point at which the sum of sensitivity and 1 minus specificity was the greatest. Patients with Cmax and Cmean(1−6) above the detected cut-off values were designated as Cmax-H and Cmean(1−6)-H, respectively, and those below the cut-off values were designated as Cmax-L and Cmean(1−6)-L, respectively. For subsequent analyses, the following designations were used to categorize patients into four groups according to the combinations of high or low values for Cmax and Cmean(1–6): Cmax-L/Cmean(1–6)-L, Cmax-H/Cmean(1–6)-L, Cmax-L/Cmean(1–6)-H, and Cmax-H/Cmean(1–6)-H.

Statistical analysis

The Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the rates between the two groups. The Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney’s U test were used to compare the means of the two groups. The log-rank test was used to determine the differences between the two groups in the survival curve. Adverse effects were categorized as CTCAE grade 2 or higher and less than 1. However, as there were no patients with an elevated serum clearance of grade 2 or higher, they were categorized as grade 1 or higher and grade 0. Statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In log-rank tests of survival curves for patients with Cmax-H/Cmean(1−6)-L or Cmax-L/Cmean(1−6)-H versus Cmax-H/Cmean(1−6)-H, p < 0.025 was considered significantly different by Bonferroni correction.

Results

Characteristics of patients

The patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Of the 43 patients who were newly diagnosed with osteosarcoma and treated during the study period, 10 with distant metastases at diagnosis were excluded. The remaining 33 patients without metastasis were included in the analysis (median age, 16.6 years). The male-to-female ratio was 3:2, and the site of onset was the lower extremity in 60% of patients. Among the 33 patients, 29 underwent wide or radical resection, and complete surgical resection was achieved in all of these patients. The remaining four patients had unresectable sacral osteosarcoma and were treated with carbon-ion radiotherapy. The median follow-up period from the date of osteosarcoma diagnosis was 91.1 months (interquartile range: 31.0–128.8 months). The 5-year survival rate for 28 patients was 75.0% (21/28), excluding 1 patient who was treated for less than 5 years and 4 who were lost to follow-up. Sex and site of onset (peripheral vs. central) were not associated with OS (p = 0.253 and 0.624, respectively; log-rank test; data not shown). In contrast, there was a trend toward shorter OS in younger patients, with 100% (8/8) of patients aged > 20 years and 65% (13/20) of patients aged < 20 years surviving for 5 years (p = 0.075; Fisher’s exact test).

The median MTX clearance was 5.21 L/h (interquartile range: 4.33–5.82 L/h), with high interindividual variability; however, the median Cmax and Cmean(1−6) were 955.2 µmol/L and 769.2 µmol/L, respectively, with an interquartile range of 700–1,000 µmol/L (Additional File 1).

All patients received HD-MTX therapy in combination with several chemotherapy regimens, which were administered before or after surgery or carbon-ion radiotherapy. The multi-drug regimens included doxorubicin (30 mg/m² on days 1, 2, and 3) + cisplatin (120 mg/m² on day 1); pirarubicin (30 mg/m² on days 1 and 2) + cisplatin (90 mg/m² on day 1); pirarubicin (30 mg/m² on day 1) + ifosfamide (3 g/m² on days 1–3) + vincristine sulfate (1 mg/m² on day 1); and bleomycin (15 mg/m² on days 1 and 2) + ifosfamide (3 g/m² on days 1 and 2) + actinomycin D (0.6 mg/m² on days 1 and 2). Additionally, some patients received doxorubicin monotherapy (30 mg/m² on days 1, 2, and 3) depending on their clinical condition. Patients with recurrent or metastatic disease were also treated with combinations of doxorubicin plus ifosfamide or gemcitabine plus docetaxel.

Setting cut-off values

Drawing an ROC curve using Cmax and Cmean(1−6) as predictors for efficacy in 5-year survival resulted in a cut-off value of 936 µmol/L in Cmax (0.667 sensitivity; 0.714 specificity) and 768 µmol/L in Cmean(1−6) (0.619 sensitivity; 0.857 specificity). The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.667 for Cmax and Cmean(1–6), showing no statistically significant difference (p = 0.147 and 0.105, respectively).

Relationship between serum MTX concentration and OS and EFS in all patients

The relationship between serum MTX concentration and efficacy is shown in Fig. 1A, B for all 33 patients, with no significant differences in OS or EFS between patients with Cmax-H and those with Cmax-L (p = 0.088 and 0.200, respectively; log-rank test). In contrast, there was a significant difference in OS between patients with Cmean(1−6)-H and those with Cmean(1−6)-L, and a significant trend was observed in the EFS (p = 0.010 and 0.061, respectively; log-rank test).

Comparison of Cmax and Cmean(1−6) in efficacy assessment

All patients and those aged <20 years were classified into four categories: Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-H, Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-L, Cmax-L/Cmean(1-6)-H, and Cmax-L/Cmean(1-6)-L, based on the Cmax and Cmean(1-6) cut-off values. The OS and EFS of patients with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-H and those with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-L and Cmax-L/Cmean(1-6)-H were evaluated (Fig. 3A, B). In the analysis of all patients, one patient with Cmax-L/Cmean(1-6)-H had no relapse or death, and OS was similar to that of patients with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-H (p = 0.789; log-rank test). In contrast, four patients with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-L, two of whom relapsed and died, had poorer OS than those with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-H (p = 0.036; log-rank test). Furthermore, a trend toward poorer OS was also observed in patients aged <20 years with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-L (p = 0.094; log-rank test). Among all patients and those aged <20 years, patients with Cmax-L/Cmean(1-6)-H had similar EFS to those with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-H (p = 0.506 and 0.567, respectively; log-rank test), whereas those with Cmax-H/Cmean(1-6)-L had poorer EFS (p = 0.359 and 0.262, respectively; log-rank test).

Adverse events

The relationship between the blood MTX concentration and adverse events in all 217 courses is shown in Table 2. Increased liver function values were observed in Cmax-H and Cmean(1-6)-H, especially in Cmean(1-6)-H, with significant increases in AST and ALT values above grade 3 (p = 0.008 and 0.013, respectively; chi-square test). However, these elevations in liver function tests were transient and reversible, and no patients developed chronic hepatic dysfunction. Cmax-H and Cmean(1-6)-H were not associated with anemia, leukopenia, or increased serum creatinine levels. Patients with elevated AST, ALT, and serum creatinine levels had a significantly higher AUC and lower MTX clearance than those without elevated levels (p < 0.05; Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test; Additional File 2).

Discussion

The PK method used in this study estimated MTX clearance values from the concentration data of each individual patient. Consequently, the control rate within the target concentration range was improved, and intra-individual variability was reduced. This allowed for a more accurate evaluation of serum MTX concentrations, demonstrating that not only Cmax but also Cmean(1–6) can be used as indicators of therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, when comparing Cmax and Cmean(1−6), we found that Cmean(1−6) had a stronger association with efficacy and could be an effective indicator of efficacy.

Since MTX exhibits time-dependent drug effects, it is important to evaluate serum concentrations, including temporal parameters. Most previous studies have used the AUC for this purpose. Indeed, AUC evaluation has been applied to many time-dependent anticancer drugs, including 5-fluorouracil [24, 25], and some reports have attempted to evaluate drug efficacy by AUC in HD-MTX therapy; however, a high AUC is strongly correlated with adverse events, such as high Cmax. Hegyi et al. [26] evaluated Cmax and the AUC versus OS and EFS in 105 pediatric patients with osteosarcoma treated with HD-MTX therapy and reported that Cmax and the AUC were not associated with efficacy and were significantly correlated with adverse events, including hepatotoxicity. In addition, Comandone et al. [27] investigated the association between Cmax, AUC, and efficacy in 25 patients with osteosarcoma treated with HD-MTX therapy and reported that a favorable clinical response was predicted by Cmax and that severe toxicity was dependent on AUC.

Since AUC reflects not only early exposure (Cmax) but also the late elimination phase (e.g., C72), it can be strongly influenced by delayed MTX clearance; therefore, it may overestimate toxicity rather than efficacy. Consequently, AUC may not be an appropriate indicator for evaluating therapeutic efficacy in HD-MTX therapy, whereas early-phase parameters such as Cmax and Cmean(1–6) may better reflect pharmacological effects. This study used a combination of loading and maintenance dosing to increase the Cmax in a short period and maintain it at a high concentration for 6 h. Therefore, Cmax and Cmean(1–6) showed weaker correlations with adverse events than did the AUC (Additional File 2). Nevertheless, AUC remains an important parameter for assessing overall drug exposure and ensuring safety, particularly to prevent toxicity related to delayed MTX elimination.

The usefulness of Cmean(1−6) for the effective concentration range of MTX in HD-MTX therapy has not yet been reported and, to the best of our knowledge, is mentioned for the first time in this study. Patients with Cmean(1−6)-H showed significantly prolonged OS (p = 0.010; log-rank test) and a trend toward longer EFS (p = 0.061; log-rank test), suggesting that Cmean(1−6) may be a useful indicator of treatment efficacy (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the effect of the serum MTX concentration was more pronounced in patients aged < 20 years than in all patients. In general, younger patients are reported to be more sensitive to chemotherapy than older patients. In this study, Cmean(1−6)-H was associated with significantly longer OS and EFS (p = 0.010 and 0.025, respectively, log-rank test; Fig. 2). It is possible that increasing the target concentration setting of MTX in the PK method, especially in younger patients, may help reduce the number of patients with Cmean(1−6)-L and lead to improved treatment efficacy, as long as chemotherapy is tolerated. However, because only 8 patients were aged 20 years or older, these age-related differences should be interpreted with caution. Thus, further validation in larger studies is warranted.

Patients were classified into four groups based on Cmax and Cmean(1−6) cut-off values, and efficacy was compared. As expected, the prognosis was more favorable for Cmean(1−6)-H, regardless of Cmax (Fig. 3). Since high Cmax is negatively correlated with MTX clearance, and Cmax ≥1,500 µmol/L is a poor prognostic factor [13, 16], it is important to design dosing based on Cmean(1−6) from a safety perspective. In contrast, Cmean(1−6)-H is associated with deteriorating hepatic functions. Although hepatic dysfunction with MTX is a frequently experienced adverse event [28–30], it is rarely fatal. As no association between Cmean(1−6)-H and adverse effects affecting prognosis, such as bone marrow suppression, has been confirmed, it is important to adjust the dosage to achieve Cmean(1−6)-H and continue treatment while monitoring liver function.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study; therefore, confounders may have been overlooked. Surgical procedures and concomitant anticancer agents varied among patients, and magnetic resonance imaging and pathological data were not available for all cases, making uniform evaluation difficult. Moreover, pharmacogenetic data were not obtained. Second, the small number of samples made it difficult to calculate the optimal cut-off value and may have limited the statistical power of the ROC analysis, as reflected by the modest AUC values. However, despite this limitation, the log-rank test showed significant differences in OS and EFS between the high-concentration and low-concentration groups, suggesting a potential association that warrants further investigation and providing meaningful pilot data for future prospective studies. Finally, because the target maintenance concentration for the PK method was set at 700 µmol/L in this study, the usefulness of Cmean(1−6) at concentrations higher than 800 µmol/L could not be evaluated. Therefore, it is desirable to verify the efficacy and tolerability of higher concentrations of Cmean(1−6) in a larger number of patients to determine a more reliable cut-off value.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the usefulness of Cmax and Cmean(1−6) in designing dosing using a PK method with low intra-individual variability. Furthermore, Cmean(1−6) contributed more to the prolongation of OS and EFS than Cmax, and there were no tolerability issues despite the presence of hepatic dysfunction; thus, to the best of our knowledge, the usefulness of Cmean(1−6) with MTX acting in a time-dependent manner was proven for the first time. As the present study was a pilot study, further large-scale prospective studies are needed to determine more accurate and clinically applicable cut-off values for serum MTX concentrations.
