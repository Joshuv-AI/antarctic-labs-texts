Fluctuation-induced Hall-like lateral forces in a chiral-gain environment
Daigo Oue, Mário G. Silveirinha

Abstract
Here, we demonstrate that vacuum fluctuations can induce lateral forces on a small particle positioned near a translation-invariant uniform non-Hermitian substrate with chiral gain. This type of non-Hermitian response can be engineered by biasing a low-symmetry conductor with a static electric field and is rooted in the quantum geometry of the material through the Berry curvature dipole. The chiral-gain material acts as an active medium for a particular circular polarisation handedness, while serving as a passive, dissipative medium for the other polarisation handedness. Owing to the nonreciprocity and gain characteristics, momentum is continuously exchanged in a preferred direction parallel to the surface between the test particle and the surrounding electromagnetic field, giving rise to lateral forces. Interestingly, the force can be viewed as a fluctuation-induced drag linked to the nonlinear Hall current. Indeed, although the gain is driven by an electric current, the resulting force acts perpendicular to the bias—unlike conventional current-drag effects. This effect stems from the skewed propagation characteristics of surface modes and gain-momentum locking. Our theory reveals a Hall-like asymmetry in the field correlations and establishes a novel link between quantum geometry and fluctuation-induced phenomena, offering new possibilities for nanoscale control via tailored electromagnetic environments.



I
Introduction

Non-Hermitian systems have attracted the attention and curiosity of researchers and have been extensively studied across a broad range of fields, including optics and photonics, acoustics, electronic circuits and condensed matter physics
El-Ganainy et al. 2018
;
Ashida et al. 2020
;
Bergholtz et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022

Non-Hermiticity—arising from loss and/or gain—may dramatically modify the system’s response, giving rise to phenomena that have no counterparts in Hermitian platforms.
These include exceptional points and related non-trivial topological structures.
The distinct characteristics of non-Hermitian systems have enabled a variety of novel effects in optics and photonics, including enhanced lasing, unidirectional transmission, and perfect absorption.
Moreover, in condensed matter physics, the non-Hermiticity gives rise to novel phase transitions, such as PT-symmetric phonon lasing
Zhang et al. 2018
, re-entrant superconductivity
Yamamoto et al. 2019
, and non-Hermitian many-body localisation
Hamazaki et al. 2019
.

Over the past decade, it has been shown that non-Hermitian electromagnetic responses—such as optical gain—can be induced and controlled by driving nonequilibrium dynamics, enabling active tuning of material properties in optical and photonic systems.
For example, setting a lossy medium in motion at a constant speed can lead to optical gain

;
Silveirinha 2014b
;
Lannebere and Silveirinha 2016
;
Lannebère and Silveirinha 2016

;
Zel’Dovich 1972
and vacuum-fluctuation-induced noncontact frictional forces—quantum friction
Schaich and Harris 1981
;
Høye and Brevik 1992
;
Brevik et al. 1993
;
Pendry 1997
.
Similarly, instead of physically moving the system, an electrostatic bias can be applied to induce electric carrier drift, leading to optical gain
Morgado and Silveirinha 2017
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2018
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2020
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2021
;
Morgado and Silveirinha 2022

;
Shapiro 2017
;

Applying an electrostatic bias can also induce electro-optic effects, such as the Pockels and DC Kerr effects.
These phenomena manifest as changes in the refractive index (or more generally, the permittivity tensor) in response to an applied electric field.
Of particular relevance to this study, recently, it was theoretically shown that an electrostatic bias may give rise to gain terms in the dielectric response function
Lannebère et al. 2022
;
Rappoport et al. 2023
;
Morgado et al. 2024
;
Fernandes et al. 2024
, a phenomenon referred to as the non-Hermitian electro-optic effect (NHEO).
This effect originates from the quantum geometry of low-symmetry materials, where a nonlinearity in the transport equation arises due to the coupling between the Berry curvature of Bloch electrons and an applied electric field.
Specifically, the combination of a static bias with Berry curvature dipoles in low-symmetry conductors—such as twisted bilayers
Rappoport et al. 2023
and trigonal tellurium
Morgado et al. 2024
—generally results in polarisation-dependent optical gain.
As the Berry curvature acts as an effective magnetic field in the presence of the electrostatic bias, electric carriers in such materials follow skewed trajectories under the applied bias, and the dielectric response tensor also gains a magneto-optical-like conservative component
Morgado et al. 2024
.
Thereby, reflecting the nonequilibrium nature due to the bias-induced carrier motion, the dielectric response of such systems acquires non-Hermitian and nonreciprocal components, which lack not only Hermitian but also transpose symmetries (i.e.,
\epsilon^{\dagger}\neq\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\top}\neq\epsilon
).
From these observations, the polarisation-dependence of the gain in these low-symmetry conductors may be interpreted as a consequence of the Hall current following the skewed paths.

Interestingly, for some material symmetry groups, the non-Hermitian response exhibits chiral properties, such that left- and right-handed circularly polarised fields experience different effects—for example, one handedness may be dissipated while the other is amplified.
It has been shown that such
chiral-gain
responses can enable transistor-like distributed behaviour, which may be exploited for optical isolation, amplification
Lannebère et al. 2022
;
Rappoport et al. 2023
and terahertz lasing

II.1
Decomposition of the response tensor

It is instructive to perform an eigen-decomposition of the electro-optic contribution
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}
to the dielectric tensor.
Since the matrix
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}
is normal [i.e,
\commutator{\epsEO'}{\epsEO''}=0
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\prime}:=(\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\dagger})/2
and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\prime\prime}:=(\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\dagger})/(2i)
are the Hermitian and non-Hermitian parts], it is unitary-diagonalisable (the eigenvectors of the matrix
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}
are mutually orthogonal),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,+}}\mathbf{u}_{+}\mathbf{u}_{+}^{*}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,-}}\mathbf{u}_{-}\mathbf{u}_{-}^{*},

(4a)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO},\pm}=\pm\epsilon_{\mathrm{g}},\

(4b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{+}=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}+i\mathbf{u}_{z}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\mathbf{u}_{-}=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}-i\mathbf{u}_{z}}{\sqrt{2}},

(4c)

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO},\alpha}
is an eigenvalue, and
\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}
is the corresponding eigenvector (
\alpha=+,-
).

The non-Hermitian part of the matrix
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}
has the same structure as the full permittivity tensor [see Eq. (
4a
)] when it is eigen-decomposed,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO}}^{\dagger}}{2i}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,+}}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{u}_{+}\mathbf{u}_{+}^{*}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,-}}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{u}_{-}\mathbf{u}_{-}^{*},

(5)

Here,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,\pm}}^{\prime\prime}
denotes the imaginary part of
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,\pm}}
.
Note that the Drude contribution
\epsilon_{\mathrm{D}}^{\prime\prime}
is already diagonal.
For convenience, we write

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{D}}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},x}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{u}_{x}\mathbf{u}_{x}^{*}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},+}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{u}_{+}\mathbf{u}_{+}^{*}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},-}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{u}_{-}\mathbf{u}_{-}^{*},

(6)

where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},x}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},\pm}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime\prime}
.
It is also useful to write

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{\ell}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\ell,\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\quad(\ell=\mathrm{D},\mathrm{EO}).

(7)

For passive systems, the matrix describing the dielectric response
\epsilon_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
must be positive definite to ensure that non-Hermitian light-matter interactions always result in material absorption.
Accordingly, the eigenvalues of
\epsilon_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
are required to be positive.
In contrast, gain media can supply energy to the wave, allowing for gain interactions that may produce negative eigenvalues.

In the case under analysis, the eigenvalues
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}
determine whether the corresponding eigen-polarisation experiences dissipation (
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}>0
) or gain (
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}<0
) in the channel specified by
\ell
.
In the Drude channel (
\ell=\mathrm{D}
), all polarisations should experience dissipation as
\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},x}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{D},\pm}^{\prime\prime}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}^{\prime\prime}>0
.
In contrast, in the electro-optic channel (
\ell=\mathrm{EO}
), the field may undergo either dissipation or gain:
the “right-handed” field experiences dissipation (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,+}}^{\prime\prime}=+\epsilon_{\mathrm{g}}^{\prime\prime}>0
), while the “left-handed” field experiences gain (
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EO,-}}^{\prime\prime}=-\epsilon_{\mathrm{g}}^{\prime\prime}<0
).
Note that the terms “right-handed” and “left-handed” are defined with respect to the optical axis of the material (
+x
axis).

Evidently, reversing the direction of the applied electric bias interchanges the circular polarisations that experience gain and dissipation.
We underline that the polarisations associated with gain and loss are determined by the material properties themselves—they are not dictated by the field distribution within the material or the direction of propagation.
The actual response of the material, whether dissipative or active, depends on the overlap between the field distribution and the eigen-polarisations that govern the non-Hermitian response.

II.2
Surface plasmon resonance

It is useful to note that the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) frequency becomes direction-dependent under the chiral gain, analogous to the behaviour observed in a passive magnetised plasma
Silveirinha et al. 2018
;
Hassani Gangaraj et al. 2018
.
In the absence of the static bias, the resonance occurs for
\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}\approx\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/\sqrt{2}\ (\approx 0.7\omega_{\mathrm{p}})
, corresponding to
\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}+1=0
, which is independent of the propagation direction.
The poles of the reflection and transmission coefficients determine the dispersion relation of the surface plasmon polariton (see Appendix
B
for the derivation of the reflection and transmission coefficients).
Within the quasi-static approximation, the poles can be found by solving

(\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}+1)\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}-\epsilon_{\mathrm{g}}k_{y}=0.

The solutions are:

\displaystyle\omega=-i\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{\omega_{0}k_{y}}{2\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}}\pm\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}^{2}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}+\quantity(\frac{\omega_ 0 k _ y}{2\abs{\vb{k}}})^{2}+i\gamma\frac{\omega_{0}k_{y}}{\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}}}.

(8)

If the electro-optic effect is sufficiently weak (i.e.,
\omega_{0}
is much smaller than any other relevant frequencies), we can approximate

\displaystyle\omega\approx\pm\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathrm{sp}}+\frac{\omega_{0}k_{y}}{2\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}}-i\frac{\gamma}{2}\quantity(
1-\frac{\omega_ 0 k _ y}{\widetilde\omega_ \mathrm{sp}\abs{\vb{k}}}
),

(9)

where we defined
\widetilde{\omega}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}^{2}-\gamma^{2}/4}

The real part of the complex dispersion relation clearly exhibits the direction dependence of the SPR.
Remarkably, the SPP dispersion in Eq. (
9

y

In particular, the imaginary part, which governs the plasmon lifetime, is sensitive to the sign of

As we will further discuss in Section

, this asymmetry underlies the emergence of a fluctuation-induced Hall-like lateral force.
The direction dependence arises from the spin-momentum locking of surface plasmons

;
Van Mechelen and Jacob 2016
combined with our polarisation-dependent gain, which originates a “gain-momentum locking”

;
PrudÃªncio and Silveirinha 2024

Specifically, since the handedness of the plasmons is determined by their propagation direction, and the non-Hermitian effects are governed by the handedness, the gain becomes locked to a particular propagation direction, while the opposite direction experiences enhanced dissipation.

The imaginary part of the complex dispersion relation determines the system’s stability.
One can show that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Max}\imaginary\quantity{\omega}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\quantity(
1-\frac{\omega_ 0}{\widetilde\omega_ \mathrm{sp}}
)<0.

(10)

Thus, the system is stable when the electro-optic effect is weak (

).

III
Field quantisation

The objective of this article is to study fluctuation-induced forces when a qubit is placed nearby the chiral-gain environment considered in the previous section.

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}}\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{a},

(15)

works if the system is purely dissipative, where all the eigenvalues of the non-Hermitian part of each response tensor are positive [i.e.
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{\ell,\alpha}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}^{*}
such that
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}>0
for all
(\ell,\alpha)
].
Note that we introduced
\mathbf{a}=\sum_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}f_{\ell,\alpha}
, and the matrix root can be written in terms of the eigendecomposition,

\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}=\sum_{\ell,\alpha}\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}^{*}
.
With Eq. (
15
), we can readily evaluate the symmetrised current correlation function,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\acomm{\vb{j} ^ -(\pos{r}{1})}{\vb{j} ^ +(\pos{r}{2})}}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\delta_{\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}},

(16)

where we have defined
\mathbf{j}^{+}=(\mathbf{j}^{-})^{\dagger}
.
The anticommutation relation is defined as
\anticommutator{\vb{A}}{\vb{C}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}+\quantity(\vb{C}\vb{A})^{\top}
.
Equation (
16
) can be viewed as a fluctuation-dissipation relation in that the current-current correlation is connected to the non-Hermitian part of the response.
However, setting the fluctuating current operator as in Eq. (
15
) does not work in the presence of gain:
The non-Hermitian part
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}
of the response is no longer positive definite, but the correlation function should be positive definite by definition so that Eq. (
16
) becomes inapplicable.
The fundamental reason for this breakdown is that the square-root decomposition of the matrix,
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}
, is no longer valid in the presence of gain
1
1

1

For any
\mathbf{E}
, we have
\mathbf{E}^{*}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathbf{E}=\absolutevalue{\sqrt{\epsilon''} ^ \dagger\cdot\vb{E}}^{2}>0
so that
\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}}^{\dagger}
is positive definite, while
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}
is not.
, and Eq. (
15
) becomes ill-defined.

To address this issue, we shall proceed with a generalised prescription
Scheel et al. 1998
;
Raabe and Welsch 2008
, which remains valid even in the presence of gain.
In Refs.
Scheel et al. 1998
;
Raabe and Welsch 2008
, it was proved that taking the absolute value of the non-Hermitian part of the permittivity and swapping the roles of annihilation and creation operators does the trick,

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}}\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\epsilon''}}\cdot(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}^{\dagger}),

(17)

where the root is defined in terms of the channel-wise eigen-decomposition,

\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\epsilon''}}=\sum_{\ell,\alpha}\sqrt{\absolutevalue{\epsilon_ {\ell, \alpha}''}}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}^{*},

and the vector-valued operators are respectively defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}=\sum_{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}>0}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}f_{\ell,\alpha},\quad\mathbf{b}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}<0}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}f_{\ell,\alpha}^{\dagger}.

(18)

With these vector-valued operators, the Hamiltonian (
14
) can be written in a compact form,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{f}}=\int\hbar\omega\quantity[
\vb{a} ^ \dagger(\vb{r},\omega) \cdot\vb{a}(\vb{r},\omega)
-\vb{b} ^ \dagger(\vb{r},\omega) \cdot\vb{b}(\vb{r},\omega)
]\differential{\vb{r}}\differential{\omega}.

(19)

The generalised prescription (
17
) reproduces Eq. (
15
) in the absence of gain.
Moreover, we obtain a modified fluctuation-dissipation relation,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\acomm{\vb{j} ^ -(\pos{r}{1})}{\vb{j} ^ +(\pos{r}{2})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\absolutevalue{\epsilon''(\pos{r}{1})}\delta_{\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}}.

(20)

Note that the matrix
\absolutevalue{\epsilon''}
on the right-hand side is positive definite, as it should be.
It is also worth noting that

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vb{j} ^ -(\pos{r}{1})\vb{j} ^ +(\pos{r}{2})}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\absolutevalue{\epsilon'' _ >(\pos{r}{1})}\delta_{\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}},

(21)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vb{j} ^ +(\pos{r}{2})\vb{j} ^ -(\pos{r}{1})}^{\top}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\mu_{0}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\absolutevalue{\epsilon'' _ <(\pos{r}{1})}\delta_{\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}},

(22)

where the positive (negative) component of the non-Hermitian part of the response tensor is defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{>(<)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}>0\\
(\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}<0)\end{subarray}}\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}\mathbf{e}_{\ell,\alpha}^{*}.

(23)

The “absolute values” are defined as
\absolutevalue{\epsilon'' _ {>,<}}=\sqrt{\quantity(\epsilon'' _ {>,<})^{2}}
. Note that the Drude term of the permittivity only contributes to the dissipative part of the decomposition
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{>}
, whereas the electro-optic term has polarisation dependent contributions to both the dissipative (
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{>}
) and gain (
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{<}
) parts.

As the electric field operator is written in terms of the fluctuating current operator and of the system Green’s function (
13
), we can readily determine the field correlation functions from Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
).
There are two distinct field correlation functions,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ - (\pos{r}{1}) \vb{E} ^ +(\pos{r}{2})}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\epsilon_{0}}\bar{G}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\not{\mathbf{r}})\cdot\absolutevalue{\epsgrt(\slashed{\vb{r}})}\cdot\bar{G}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\not{\mathbf{r}}),

(24)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ + (\pos{r}{2}) \vb{E} ^ -(\pos{r}{1})}^{\top}=\frac{\hbar}{\pi\epsilon_{0}}\bar{G}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\not{\mathbf{r}})\cdot\absolutevalue{\epslss(\slashed{\vb{r}})}\cdot\bar{G}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\not{\mathbf{r}})

(25)

where we defined
\bar{G}=(\omega^{2}/c^{2})G
.
The integration over the slashed variable
\not{\mathbf{r}}
is implicit.
As discussed above, the non-Hermitian part
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}
of the dielectric tensor characterises the fluctuations in the system, whereas the Green’s function
\bar{G}
is responsible for propagating the fluctuations from one point to another.
From Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
), it is evident that both correlation functions are positive definite as it should be.
We can recognise that the order of
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
appearing on the right-hand side is different for Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
).
In addition, one can see that the loss contribution
\absolutevalue{\epsgrt}
appears in the first correlation function (
24
) while the gain counterpart
\absolutevalue{\epslss}
appears in the second one (
25
).
This observation suggests that the two correlation functions represent “inverse” processes.
Indeed, it is well-known from perturbation theory (Fermi’s golden rule) that the first one
\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ - \vb{E} ^ +}
characterises the spontaneous decay rate
Novotny and Hecht 2012
, whereas the second one
\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ + \vb{E} ^ -}
controls the rate of the inverse process (i.e. photon absorption by the system), as we shall see in the following section.

Quasi-static approximation

Next, we calculate the field correlation functions [Eqs. (

We note that the non-Hermitian part
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}
vanishes for the upper-half space (

In Appendix

The Green’s function is calculated in the spectral domain (denoted by
{\bf{k}}

x

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})=-\frac{t_{\mathbf{k}}/\epsilon_{\mathrm{d}}}{2\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}}\mathbf{k}_{+}\mathbf{k}_{+}e^{-\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}(z_{1}-z_{2})}\quad(z_{2}<0<z_{1}),

\displaystyle\bar{G}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\int\bar{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(z_{1},z_{2})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2})}\differential{\vb{k}},

IV
Reduced master equation and steady-state

Following a standard procedure for open quantum systems
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Rivas and Huelga 2012
, next we develop an effective description of the two-level system, namely we obtain the evolution equation of the reduced density matrix,
\rho_{\mathrm{q}}:=\tr_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho)
.
Here,
\tr_{\mathrm{f}}
represents the partial trace of the total density matrix over the electromagnetic field degrees of freedom.

Since the field operator behaves like a harmonic oscillator and evolves as
\mathbf{E}^{-}(\mathbf{r},\omega;t)\sim e^{-i\omega t}
in the interaction picture, it is convenient to introduce

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{-}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}},t)=\int\mathbf{E}^{-}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}},\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\differential{\omega}.

(32)

As we are going to focus on the field evaluated at the position
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}
of the particle, the position argument may be suppressed in this section for conciseness [e.g.
\mathbf{E}^{-}(t)=\mathbf{E}^{-}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}},t)
].

We start with the evolution equation for the total system (the integral form of the von Neumann equation),

\rho(t)=\rho(0)+(i\hbar)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\commutator{H _ \mathrm{int}(t-s)}{\rho(t-s)}\differential{s}.

Up to the second order in the interaction Hamiltonian, we can write

\displaystyle\derivative{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}{t}=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tr_{\mathrm{f}}\commutator{H _ \mathrm{int}(t)}{\comm{H _ \mathrm{int}(t-s)}{\rho(t)}}\differential{s},

(33)

where we have assumed
\commutator{H _ \mathrm{int}(t)}{\rho(0)}=0
and also adopted the Markov approximation,
\rho(s)\rightarrow\rho(t)
, letting the upper limit of the integral go to the infinity (i.e.,
\int_{0}^{t}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}
).
We assume the electromagnetic field state approximately stays the same upon the evolution of the two-level system, factorising
\rho(t)\rightarrow\rho_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{f}}
.
This is nothing but the Born approximation.
Note that the interaction Hamiltonian is given in Eq. (
11
).
In the following, we will assume that the electromagnetic subsystem stays in the vacuum state,
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}=\outerproduct{0}{0}
.
The temperature effect may be taken into account by modifying the density matrix
Silveirinha and Oue 2025
.
Then, the integrand in Eq. (
33
) becomes

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathrm{f}}\commutator{H _ \mathrm{int}(t)}{\comm{H _ \mathrm{int}(t-s)}{\rho(t)}}

\displaystyle=\tr_{\mathrm{f}}\commutator{\vb{p} ^ +(t) \cdot\vb{E} ^ -(t)}{\comm{\vb{p} ^ -(t-s) \cdot\vb{E} ^ +(t-s)}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(t)\otimes\rho_ \mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle+\tr_{\mathrm{f}}\commutator{\vb{p} ^ -(t) \cdot\vb{E} ^ +(t)}{\comm{\vb{p} ^ +(t-s) \cdot\vb{E} ^ -(t-s)}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(t)\otimes\rho_ \mathrm{f}}},

(34)

where we have retained only the relevant terms: the conjugate pair of the field operators [e.g.,
\mathbf{E}^{-}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}^{+}(t-s)
] should be picked within the nested commutators to yield a finite contribution after the partial trace is taken.
If an irrelevant pair [e.g,
\mathbf{E}^{-}(t)
and
\mathbf{E}^{-}(t-s)
] is chosen, it will eventually result in
\tr_{\mathrm{f}}\quantity{\rho_ \mathrm{f} \vb{E} ^ -(t)\vb{E} ^ -(t-s)}=0
, and therefore, no contribution to the integral.
Expanding the nested commutators and using
\mathbf{p}^{\pm}(t)=\mathbf{p}^{\pm}e^{\pm i\omega_{\mathrm{q}}t}
, the contribution from the first term in Eq. (
34
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i}\Big(

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{-}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{G}}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{+}+\mathbf{p}^{+}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{-}\rho_{\mathrm{q}}

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{G}}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{+})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{-}-(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{-})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{+}\Big),

(35)

and the second term in Eq. (
34
) yields similar but conjugate results,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2i}\Big(

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{-}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{G}}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{+}\rho_{\mathrm{q}}+\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{+}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{-}

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{G}}^{*}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{+})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{-}-(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\dagger}\cdot\mathbf{p}^{-})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{p}^{+}\Big).

(36)

Here, we have introduced

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}}=2i\int_{0}^{\infty}\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ -(t) \vb{E} ^ +(t-s)}e^{+i\omega_{\mathrm{q}}s}\differential{s},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{G}}=2i\int_{0}^{\infty}\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ +(t-s) \vb{E} ^ -(t)}^{\top}e^{+i\omega_{\mathrm{q}}s}\differential{s},

(38)

where we have written
\expectationvalue{\ldots}=\tr_{\mathrm{f}}(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}\ldots)
.
Note that these correlation functions are eventually independent of
t
, as shown in Appendix
C
.
Substituting the two contributions, (
35
) and (
36
), into the right-hand side of Eq. (
33
), we obtain a Lindblad-type equation,

\displaystyle\derivative{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}{t}

\displaystyle=-i\commutator{\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar} \cdot S _ {\mathrm{L}} \cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar}}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}+i\commutator{\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar} \cdot S _ \mathrm{G} ^ \top\cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar}}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}

\displaystyle+\quantity(\Gamma_ \mathrm{L} \cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{+}}{\hbar}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar} \cdot\Gamma_ \mathrm{L} \cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar}}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}},

\displaystyle+\quantity(\Gamma_ \mathrm{G} ^ \top\cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar})\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{q}}\frac{\mathbf{p}^{-}}{\hbar}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar} \cdot\Gamma_ {\mathrm{G}} ^ \top\cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar}}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}

(39)

where we defined the non-Hermitian parts of the correlation functions,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}=(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}-\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}^{\dagger})/(2i)
, and the Hermitian parts,
S_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}=-(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{G})}^{\dagger})/4
.
As shown in Appendix
C
, they can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}=2\pi\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ -(\Rq,\omegaq) \vb{E} ^ +(\Rq,\omegaq)},

(40)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}=2\pi\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ +(\Rq,\omegaq) \vb{E} ^ -(\Rq,\omegaq)}^{\top},

(41)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{L}}=\PV\int\frac{\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ -(\Rq,\omega) \vb{E} ^ +(\Rq,\omega)}}{\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega}\differential{\omega},

(42)

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{G}}=\PV\int\frac{\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ +(\Rq,\omega) \vb{E} ^ -(\Rq,\omega)}^{\top}}{\omega_{\mathrm{q}}-\omega}\differential{\omega}.

(43)

While the non-Hermitian parts
\Gamma_{\mathrm{L,G}}
are responsible for irreversible evolution, the Hermitian parts give an additional unitary evolution of the two-level system due to the interaction with the surrounding environment.
The effect of this additional unitary evolution can be interpreted as a type of Lamb shift, which is typically negligibly small
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.
However, in passive environments it is responsible for the Casimir-Polder force
Casimir and Polder 1948
;
Sukenik et al. 1993
, which is
normal
to the surface.
As we will focus on the fluctuation-induced
lateral
forces in the following, the Lamb-type shifts may be neglected.
In the second line of Eq. (
39
), we can recognise that the relaxation operator
\mathbf{p}^{-}
operates at the left to the reduced density matrix
\rho_{\mathrm{q}}
together with
\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}
.
From this observation, the matrix
\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}
describes a relaxation process that promotes downward transitions in the qubit.
A similar argument can be made for
\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}
, which describes an excitation process that promotes upward transitions.
In other words, the correlation function
\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ - \vb{E} ^ +}
, which is proportional to
\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}
, controls the (spontaneous) photon emission of the two-level system, while the correlator
\expectationvalue{\vb{E} ^ + \vb{E} ^ -}
, which is proportional to
\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}
, governs the photon absorption by the two-level system.

As we shall further discuss in the next section, the fluctuation-induced forces are determined by the qubit’s steady-state properties, where the system no longer evolves in time.
The steady-state populations can be found from the reduced master equation (
39
).
Since the Lindblad-type terms, which are responsible for the irreversible evolution [i.e., the second and third lines in Eq. (
39
)], spoil the off-diagonal elements (coherence) of the density matrix, one may expect that the density matrix becomes diagonal in the steady state (
t\to\infty
).
The commutators in Eq. (
39
), which are responsible for the Lamb-type shifts, vanish if the density matrix is diagonal.
Thus, we may safely neglect the Lamb-type shifts in evaluating the steady-state density matrix
\rho_{\mathrm{q}}(\infty)
:
At the steady state, we can set
\derivative*{t}=0
and solve the equation for
\matrixelement{\ell_ 1}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(\infty)}{\ell_ 2}\,(\ell_{1,2}\in\quantity{0,1})
to get

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(\infty)}{0}=\frac{\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}},

(44a)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(\infty)}{1}=\frac{\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}+\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}^{*}\cdot\Gamma_{\mathrm{L}}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{e}}}.

(44b)

Note that we used
\tr\quantity{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}=1
.
It is clear that the ground-state (excited-state) population becomes unity (zero) in the absence of optical gain as it should be.
It can be analytically confirmed that, in the steady state,

\displaystyle\matrixelement{0}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(\infty)}{1}=\matrixelement{1}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(\infty)}{0}=0.

(44c)

Thus, the steady-state density matrix is indeed diagonal, and the steady-state populations are not affected by the Lamb-type shifts, as anticipated.

V
Fluctuation-induced Hall-like lateral force

The fluctuation-induced lateral force can be found from the expectation value of the derivative of the interaction energy with respect to the lateral position
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{q}}
of the particle,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\parallel}=\tr{-\rho(t)\partial_ {\xq} H _ \mathrm{int}(t)}.

(45)

Here, we first construct a reduced force operator, which is compatible with the Lindblad description of the two-level system.
We use the approximation
\rho(t)\approx\rho(0)+(i\hbar)^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\commutator{H _ \mathrm{int}(t-s)}{\rho_ \mathrm{q}(t)\otimes\rho_ \mathrm{f}}\differential{s}
, as we did in deriving the reduced master equation.
We suppose that
\tr\quantity{\rho(0)\partial_ {\xq} H _ \mathrm{int}}=0

and therefore the term
\rho(0)
can be ignored [note that the interaction Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{int}}
contains a single relaxation (or excitation) operator so that taking the trace with the initial density matrix
\rho(0)
gives a trivial contribution].
After some algebra, the lateral force expectation can be expressed in terms of the qubit density matrix as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{\parallel}=\tr\quantity{
\qty(
\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar} \cdot\qty[\mathcal{F} _ \mathrm{G} ^ \parallel] ^ \top\cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar}
-\frac{\vb{p} ^ +}{\hbar} \cdot\mathcal{F} _ \mathrm{L} ^ \parallel\cdot\frac{\vb{p} ^ -}{\hbar}
)
\rho_ \mathrm{q}(t)
}.

(46a)

Here
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G(L)}}
are matrices that determine the momentum transfer in the gain (loss) channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G}}^{\parallel}=\quantity[
\qty{-i\hbar\partial_ {\xq}}
2\pi\expval{
\vb{E} ^ +(\Rq', \omegaq)
\vb{E} ^ -(\Rq, \omegaq)
} ^ \top]_{\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}},

(46b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\parallel}=\quantity[
\qty{-i\hbar\partial_ {\xq}}
2\pi\expval{
\vb{E} ^ -(\Rq, \omegaq)
\vb{E} ^ +(\Rq', \omegaq)
}
]_{\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}}.

(46c)

Recall that we defined
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{q}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{q}}+z_{\mathrm{q}}\mathbf{u}_{z}
.
It is also useful to mention that the normal component of the force contains additional terms related to the principal-value integrals
S_{\mathrm{G,L}}
, which represent the Lamb-type shifts (see, e.g., Ref.
Buhmann et al. 2004
;
Hassani Gangaraj et al. 2018
;
Silveirinha et al. 2018
).
This result for the lateral forces agrees with the previous literature
Hassani Gangaraj et al. 2018
;
Silveirinha et al. 2018
, when the material is passive.
In the passive case, the lateral force always vanishes when the qubit is in the ground state (i.e.,
\expectationvalue{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}{0}=1
).
In contrast, with the chiral gain, the lateral force can remain finite even if
\expectationvalue{\rho_ \mathrm{q}}{0}=1
.

We can regard the quantity inside the curved parentheses in Eq. (
46a
) as the reduced force operator.
The first (second) term within the parentheses has the excitation (relaxation) operator
\mathbf{p}^{+(-)}
on the far right, which acts on the density matrix, so that we can understand that it corresponds to the gain (loss) contribution.
The operator in the curly brackets in Eqs. (
46b
) and (
46c
) corresponds to the lateral component of the canonical momentum operator of the electromagnetic field.
As the field correlators can be written in terms of the momentum spectra [see Eqs. (
III.3
) and (
III.3
)], the expression can be simplified in the reciprocal space,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G,L}}=\int\hbar\mathbf{k}\,2\pi\gamma_{\mathrm{G,L}}(\mathbf{k},z_{\mathrm{q}})\differential{\vb{k}}.

(47)

Remind that we defined
\differential{\vb{k}}:=\differential{k _ x}\differential{k _ y}/(2\pi)^{2}.

The expression (
47
) shows that the force can be expressed as the integral of the transferred momentum
\hbar\mathbf{k}
multiplied by the spectra
\gamma_{\mathrm{G,L}}
of photon absorption and emission in each channel.
The matrices
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{G,L}}
represent the momentum transfer rates in the respective processes.

As illustrated in FIGs.
3
and
4
, the matrices
\gamma_{\mathrm{G,L}}
are even functions of
k_{x}
.
This implies that the overall integrands in Eq. (
47

k_{x}
, and thereby the
x
component
F_{x}=\mathbf{u}_{x}\cdot\mathbf{F}_{\parallel}
of the force vanishes.
This result is somewhat counterintuitive, given that the gain response is induced by a current bias along
x
.
Therefore, one might expect a lateral force component perpendicular to the bias field, whereby the qubit experiences drag from the carriers in the chiral-gain medium, akin to a frictional effect.

In the calculation, we use the “equilibrium” density matrix [
\rho_{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rightarrow\rho_{\mathrm{q}}(\infty)
], as the two-level system eventually relaxes to the steady state.
The fluctuation-induced lateral force is exerted due to the chiral optical gain and is significantly enhanced near the surface plasmon resonance condition
\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/\sqrt{2}
.
The peak slightly deviates from the ‘bare’ resonance condition due to the NHEO effect.
The lateral force is along the negative
y
direction.
This is consistent with the following qualitative discussion.
As we have seen in the momentum spectra representing the photon emission (absorption), the two-level system is more likely to emit (absorb) photons propagating in the positive (negative)
y
direction.
Thus, the recoil force generated during photon emission is directed along the negative
y
-direction.
Similarly, the ‘kick’ imparted to the two-level system during photon absorption is also directed in the negative
y
-direction.
Consequently, the net force acting on the two-level system points towards the negative
y
direction, in agreement with the numerical simulations.
It is also worth noting that the
+y
-direction corresponds to the propagation direction of plasmons that experience gain due to the NHEO effect.
The emission of such plasmons is stimulated by the qubit, which in turn experiences a corresponding recoil force.

As previously noted, the lateral force is perpendicular to the expected direction of frictional drag arising from carrier motion in the conductor. However, it can be interpreted as a drag-like force associated with the anomalous current generated by the nonlinear Hall effect in the low-symmetry conductor
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Du et al. 2021
.
Consistent with this interpretation, the sign of the lateral force reverses when the direction of the electric bias is flipped.

To conclude, we note that in principle, a force component along the direction of motion may also exist.
However, such a contribution typically depends on Doppler shifts in the material response due to particle motion, which are neglected in our calculation for simplicity
Shapiro 2010
;
Volokitin and Persson 2011
;
Dean et al. 2016
;
Shapiro 2017
;
Oue et al. 2025
.

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we have uncovered fluctuation-induced lateral forces
acting on qubit placed near a translation invariant gain medium substrate.
Unlike in passive environments, we found that the qubit experiences a persistent lateral force even when it is in the ground state.
Moreover, the sign of the force is independent of the atomic state.
This suggests that gain-assisted environments can be used to induce and control the lateral motion of small particles, opening up intriguing possibilities for optical manipulation based on fluctuation-induced fields.

In our system, the gain arises due to the non-Hermitian electro-optic effect, which produces a chiral gain response.
We demonstrated how this chiral gain modifies the propagation of surface plasmon polaritons in the material and, in turn, shapes the momentum spectra of emission and absorption processes.

The momentum transfer associated with its relaxation and excitation processes produces a lateral force on the qubit.
Specifically, our analysis shows that the momentum transfer rates associated with the relaxation and excitation processes are described by two distinct field-correlation functions, which can exhibit pronounced asymmetry in the direction perpendicular to the applied electric bias, coinciding with the direction of the Hall current induced by the nonlinear Hall effect.
Consequently, the lateral force can be interpreted as a drag-like force rooted in the nonlinear Hall response, establishing a novel and exciting connection between the quantum geometry of the material and its manifestations in fluctuation electrodynamics.
