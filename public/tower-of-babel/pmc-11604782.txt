Prenatal dexamethasone exposure reduces osteoprogenitor proliferation in mice via histone modifications at the Mkp-1 gene locus
Authors: Yongheng Xie, Jianwen Su, Mankai Yang, Zixian Liu, Te Chen, Jikun Qian, Bin Yu, Xianrong Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal dexamethasone exposure (PDE) has long-term consequences in bone development, though the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Our results show that PDE offspring exhibit reduced bone mass, fewer osteoblasts and diminished osteoprogenitors proliferation. Further analyses show that PDE increases MKP-1 expression, while decreasing H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) and H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) at the Mkp-1 gene locus. Mechanistically, dexamethasone suppresses osteoprogenitors proliferation by upregulating MKP-1 expression, notably through the inhibition of H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 modifications, which promote demethylation and transcriptional activation of the Mkp-1 gene. Importantly, restoring histone methylation balance with PFI-90 or GSK-J4 treatment blocks the inhibitory effects of PDE on MAPK signaling in osteoprogenitors, and mitigates the detrimental impact of PDE on osteoprogenitor proliferation and bone development in the offspring. This study provides new insights into the epigenetic mechanism by which PDE disrupts long-term programming of fetal osteoprogenitor proliferation, ultimately impairing long bone growth in offspring.Subject terms: Bone development, Bone

Introduction

Prenatal exposure to glucocorticoids, whether due to maternal stress or synthetic glucocorticoid treatments, is associated with a low birth weight and reduced linear growth of children1,2. Clinical and epidemiological data support a link between a low birth weight and reduced bone mass in both children and adults3–5. Although one follow-up study of clinical trial reported no alterations in bone mass during mid-childhood after repeated antenatal glucocorticoids exposure6, experimental models highlight potential detrimental effects of prenatal dexamethasone exposure (PDE) on fetal bone development and bone mass in later life7–9. Despite these findings, the mechanisms underlying the prolonged adverse effects of PDE on bone mass in adult offspring remain poorly understood.

Our recent research demonstrates that PDE retards postnatal long bone growth and is associated with a persistent reduction in the number of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) in young mice offspring10. Considering that continuous osteoblastogenesis from BMSCs and osteoprogenitors is essential for both bone modeling during embryonic development and bone remodeling in adults11,12, a reduced number of BMSCs or osteoprogenitors may contribute to low bone mass in later life due to PDE. Notably, PDE has also been linked to reduced neuroprogenitor proliferation and neuropsychiatric disorders13,14. While there is growing evidence connecting epigenetics to glucocorticoid-induced fetal programming and subsequent diseases in adult life15,16, the specific mechanism by which PDE persistently reduces osteoprogenitors proliferation remain unclear.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-1 (MKP-1), encoded by the dual specificity phosphatase-1 (Dusp-1) gene, is a key negative regulator of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling. Elevated MKP-1 levels have been associated with impaired self-renewal of stem-like carcinoma cells and dermal stem cells17,18. Several studies have reported increased MKP-1 expression and decreased MAPK signaling in rodents and cell cultures following dexamethasone treatment19,20. Furthermore, activated MKP-1 and suppressed MAPK signaling have been observed in human diseases treated with dexamethasone21,22. These observations suggest that MKP-1 may be a critical mediator in PDE-induced suppression of osteoprogenitors.

Epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, histone acetylation, and histone methylation, play a crucial role in glucocorticoid-mediated fetal programming and postnatal health outcomes15,23. In particular, histone modifications such as H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3), H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3), and H3 lysine 9 dimethylation/trimethylation (H3K9me2/me3) are tightly linked to transcriptional repression in stem cells24,25. These findings raise the possibility that histone modifications may contribute to the long-term adverse effects of PDE on offspring.

In this study, we found that PDE impairs bone formation and significantly decreased bone mass, accompanied by a reduced number of osteoprogenitor in adult female mouse offspring. Further investigations revealed that dexamethasone activates transcription of the Mkp-1 gene by suppressing histone H3K27me3 and H3K9me2 at its promoter, thereby leading to persistent activation of MKP-1 and suppressed osteoprogenitors proliferation. Moreover, blocking H3K27me2/me3 using GSK-J4 or H3K9me2 using PFI-90 ameliorated the bone developmental retardation observed in young PDE offspring. Thus, the demethylation state of H3K27 and H3K9 at the Mkp-1 genes may play a critical role in fetal programming, resulting in reduced osteoprogenitors proliferation and bone growth retardation in PDE offspring.

Results

PDE impairs bone remodeling in adult female mice offspring

In a recent study utilizing the same mouse model, we demonstrated that PDE significantly retards bone development and reduces bone mass in young (12-week-old) female mice offspring10. Here, we examined whether these toxic effects persist in adult (28-week-old) female mice offspring. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of micro-computed tomography (microCT) images revealed markedly less trabecular bone in PDE mice offspring compared with the control group (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a considerable reduction in bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) in PDE mice offspring (Fig. 1B). This reduction in bone mass was mainly due to a notable decrease in trabecular number (Tb. N) rather than in trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) (Fig. 1C, D). Additionally, trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) was increased in PDE mice offspring compared with controls (Fig. 1E).

Next, we investigated the cellular mechanisms underlying the reduced trabecular bone mass in PDE adult mice offspring. H&E staining and quantitative analysis of osteoblasts on the trabecular bone surface showed a notable decrease in the number of osteoblasts per bone perimeter (N. Ob/B. Pm) in PDE adult mice offspring (Fig. 1F, G). Consistently, immunostaining and quantification of osteocalcin (Ocn), a marker for osteogenesis, revealed a significantly reduction in the number of Ocn-positive cells per bone perimeter (N. Ocn+/B. Pm) in PDE adult mice offspring (Fig. 1H, I). Interestingly, the number of TRAP-positive osteoclasts per bone perimeter (N. TRAP+/B. Pm) was unchanged in PDE mice offspring compared with controls (Fig. 1J, K). These findings indicate that the reduced trabecular bone mass in adult mice offspring is primarily due to impaired osteogenesis resulting from PDE.

PDE reduces the number of BMSCs and osteoprogenitors in adult female mice offspring

BMSCs are multipotent progenitors essential for maintaining osteogenesis and osteoblastic bone formation. Our recent study showed that young mice offspring with PDE had significantly decreased numbers of BMSCs and osteoprogenitors10. We hypothesized that this reduction might persist and mediate the toxic effect of PDE on osteogenesis in adult offspring. As shown in Fig. 2A–C, both the number and size of CFU-F were significantly decreased in bone marrow stromal cells from PDE adult mice offspring compared with controls. Surface markers CD29, CD105, and Sca1, combined with the absence of CD45, have been used to identify mouse bone marrow mesenchymal progenitor in bone marrow26,27. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed a significant decrease in the percentage of CD45−CD29+CD105+Sca1+ cells in bone marrow from PDE adult mice offspring compared with controls (Fig. 2D, E).

Given our previous findings that PDE induces cellular senescence in the long bones of mice offspring10, we investigated whether PDE affects the proliferation and/or senescence of BMSCs or osteoprogenitors. Long bone sections from Prx1Cre-tdTomato mice offspring (2-week-old) in control and PDE groups were processed for SA-β-Gal staining to detect senescence and Ki67 staining to detect proliferation. Results showed a significant reduction in the number of Prx1+ cells and an increase in SA-β-Gal+ cells within the femoral metaphyseal bone area of mice offspring in PDE group (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). However, we did not observe any Prx1+ and SA-β-Gal+ double-stained cells in either PDE-affected offspring or controls (Supplementary Fig. 1A), indicating that PDE does not induce cellular senescence in Prx1+ BMSCs or osteoprogenitors. Additionally, the number of Prx1+Ki67+ cells was significantly reduced in the long bones of PDE mice offspring compared with controls (Fig. 2F, G). Flow cytometry analysis using Annexin V and 7-AAD revealed a significant increase in both early apoptotic (Annexin-V+7-AAD−) cells and late apoptotic (Annexin-V+7-AAD+) Prx1+ cells in the PDE-affected offspring at 4 weeks of age, with values of 3.12 ± 0.82% and 5.35 ± 0.39%, respectively (Fig. 2H–J, Supplementary Fig. 1D). Additionally, cell cycle analysis via 7-AAD staining showed a significant accumulation of G2/M phase DNA content in Prx1+ cells from PDE-affected offspring (Fig. 2K, L). Given that apoptosis affected less than 10% of Prx1+ cells in the PDE-affected offspring, the reduction in Prx1+ BMSCs or osteoprogenitors is likely due to suppressed proliferation, potentially through G2/M cell cycle arrest.

Dexamethasone suppresses osteoprogenitor proliferation by modulating the MKP-1/MAPK pathway in vitro

To determine whether dexamethasone directly affects osteoprogenitor proliferation, we treated bone marrow cells from mice long bones with various concentrations of dexamethasone (0, 50, 250 and 500 nM) for CFU-F staining. Dexamethasone suppressed the number and size of CFU-F colonies in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3A–C). Further examination of Ki67 expression in Osterix+ cells confirmed a concentration-dependent suppression of Osterix+Ki67+ cells by dexamethasone (Fig. 3D, E). In addition, dexamethasone reduced the expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), a proliferation marker (Fig. 3F, G). These results confirm that dexamethasone directly impedes the proliferative capacity of osteoprogenitors in vitro.

Previous studies have shown that the MAPK signaling, a major positive regulator of proliferation in various stem cells28–30, plays a critical role in embryonic limb development and bone homeostasis31,32. We then investigated whether dexamethasone modulates MAPK signaling in osteoprogenitors. Dexamethasone significantly decreased the phosphorylation levels of JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 (p-JNK, p-ERK1/2 and p-p38) without altering the total protein levels of these kinases (Fig. 3H, I). RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of dexamethasone-treated osteoprogenitors identified 535 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a Log2(fold change) > 0.8 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Gene Oncology (GO) analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed significant enrichment in multiple biological processes, including the ERK1 and ERK2 cascade and the negative regulation of phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 2B). A Venn diagram analysis pinpointed an overlap of 4 DEGs between these two processes (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, heatmap analysis demonstrated a substantial increase in the expression of DUSP1/MKP-1 in response to dexamethasone treatment (Fig. 3K). Recognizing MKP-1’s role as a key negative regulator of MAPKs, we examined the effect of dexamethasone on MKP-1 expression and found that dexamethasone significantly increased the protein levels of MKP-1 (Fig. 3L, M). Considering the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of dexamethasone for osteoprogenitors, which was determined to be 389.8 nM from the CFU-F assay (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C), we chose a concentration of 250 nM for dexamethasone in our subsequent experiments.

To determine whether up-regulated MKP-1 mediates the suppression of MAPK signaling by dexamethasone, we treated osteoprogenitors with dexamethasone in the presence or absence of RO31-8220, an MKP-1 expression inhibitor33. RO31-8220 rescued the dexamethasone-induced reduction in the activity of JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 pathways and PCNA expression (Fig. 4A, B). CFU-F analysis showed that RO31-8220 restored the number and size of colonies reduced by dexamethasone (Fig. 4C–E). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining for Osterix and Ki67 showed that RO-31-8220 blocked the reduction in Osterix+Ki67+ osteoprogenitors caused by dexamethasone (Fig. 4F, G).

To further test whether MKP-1 plays a role in mediating PDE’s suppression of osteoprogenitors proliferation, MKP-1 was knocked down using siRNA in primary osteoprogenitors. The knockdown efficiency was verified by qPCR and western blot assays (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). MKP-1 knockdown rescued the inhibitory effects of dexamethasone on JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 activity and PCNA expression (Fig. 4H, I). In line with these findings, the reduction in Osterix+Ki67+ osteoprogenitors by dexamethasone was also rescued siMkp-1 treatment (Fig. 4J, K). Together, the above data indicate that dexamethasone suppresses osteoprogenitor proliferation by downregulating MAPK signaling through the upregulation of MKP-1.

PDE activates the persistent expression of MKP-1 through the demethylation of H3K9me2/H3K27me3

As early as postnatal day 2, a substantial reduction in Prx1+ cells was observed in the femoral metaphysis of PDE-affected mice offspring compared with the control group (Fig. 5A, B). This observation suggests that PDE may exert a detrimental effect on Prx1+ cells in long bone during the embryonic stage. By 4 weeks post-birth, while the control offspring showed an increase in the abundance of Prx1+ cells with growth, the number of these cells in the femoral metaphysis of PDE-affected offspring significantly decreased (Fig. 5A, B). Similarly, on postnatal day 2, as well as at 2 and 4 weeks post-birth, the PDE-affected mice offspring exhibited a significant reduction in the number of Osterix+ osteoprogenitors within the femoral metaphyseal bone area compared with control group (Supplementary Fig. 5A,B). Notably, there was no significant difference in the level of perilipin, a marker of adipocyte differentiation between the PDE-affected and control offspring in the metaphyseal bone area (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). These findings indicate that the reduction in Osterix+ osteoprogenitors is not a result of adipogenic differentiation of Prx1+ cells. Instead, it may be attributed to the loss of Prx1+ cells during embryonic stage, an effect that could potentially continue into early adulthood.

To further explore the possibility that PDE may persistently activate the expression of MKP-1 in osteoprogenitors from embryonic bone, we analyzed the expression of MKP-1 in Prx1+ cells in the long bones of offspring. Immunofluorescence staining with an anti-MKP-1 antibody showed elevated expression of MKP-1 in the Prx1+ cells from the distal femoral metaphysis of 4-week-old PDE mice offspring compared with controls (Fig. 5C, D). In keeping with these results, we further found that the percentage of CD45−tdTomato+ (Prx1+) cells sorted from PDE mice offspring at 4 weeks old was lower compared with that from controls (Fig. 5E, F).

Considering the critical role of histone H3 modifications in developmental processes and the lasting effect of glucocorticoid on organ growth and maturation15,34, we analyzed H3 modifications in osteoprogenitors isolated from the bone marrow of PDE and control mice offspring. Utilizing a multiplex colorimetric assay for histone H3 modifications, we observed significant elevations in the levels of H3K9me1 and H3K27me1 modifications, concomitant with notable reductions in H3K9me2, H3K27me2, H3K14ac, and H3ser28P modifications (Fig. 6A). Given the importance of methylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9) and lysine 27 (H3K27) in gene repression during development35,36, we further evaluated the protein levels of H3K9me2/me3 and H3K27me2/me3 in these osteoprogenitors. The results revealed significantly reduced levels of H3K9me2, H3K27me2, and H3K27me3 in osteoprogenitors from the bone marrow of PDE mice offspring (Fig. 6B, C).

Since the H3K9me2 protein level was suppressed intensively in osteoprogenitors from the bone marrow of PDE mice offspring, we performed ChIP-seq for H3K9me2 to identify its role in related epigenetic changes. The results showed relatively lower enrichments in the intron (32.54%) and promoter (9.66%) regions in osteoprogenitors from PDE mice offspring compared with controls (37.1% and 11.05%, respectively) (Fig. 6D). We then measured H3K9me2-binding intensity spanning promoter region and intron regions (±3 kb of transcription start sites). We observed a considerable loss of H3K9me2 binding in these regions in osteoprogenitors from PDE mice offspring (Fig. 6E, F). Specific analysis of the Mkp-1 locus revealed that PDE induced a reduction of H3K9me2 occupancy on its promoter region and gene body (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these findings indicate that PDE reduces H3K9 and H3K27 methylation and activity at genes, including Mkp-1, in bone marrow osteoprogenitors from young mice offspring.

Next, we used chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) to verify the effect of PDE on H3K9me2 and H3K27me2/3 modifications at the Mkp-1 gene locus. The results revealed a dramatic decrease in the deposition of H3K9me2 on the Mkp-1 locus (Fig. 7A). Given that PDE can suppress the protein levels of H3K27me2 and H3K27me3 in osteoprogenitors from the bone marrow of young mice offspring, we also explored their enrichment levels at the Mkp-1 gene locus. We observed that PDE had no effect on the enrichment level of H3K27me2 at the Mkp-1 gene locus (Fig. 7B). However, PDE dramatically decreased the enrichment level of H3K27me3 at the Mkp-1 gene locus in the Prx1+ cells from 4-week-old mice offspring (Fig. 7C). Since the H3K27me3 levels are tightly regulated by UTX and JMJD3, which play essential roles in skeletal development as highly expressed demethylases in embryonic stem cells37,38, we explored the effect of dexamethasone on the expression of UTX and JMJD3 to confirm the effect of dexamethasone on H3K27me3 enrichment level at the Mkp-1 gene locus. The results showed considerably increased mRNA expression of UTX and JMJD3 (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results suggest that PDE reduces H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 modification at the Mkp-1 gene locus, thereby activating its expression in osteoprogenitors.

We then tested whether the upregulated expression of MKP-1 and the corresponding suppression of MAPK signaling pathways by dexamethasone could be alleviated by blocking the activities of H3K9me2 and H3K27me3. Indeed, treatment with PFI-90, a selective KDM3B (H3K9 demethylase) inhibitor, and GSK-J4, a selective H3K27 demethylase inhibitor, both blocked the activation of MKP-1 by dexamethasone. Consistently, these treatments also rescued the suppressed protein levels of p-JNK, p-ERK, p-p38, and PCNA (Fig. 7D–G). These results suggest that dexamethasone might induce a persistent increase in MKP-1 expression by reducing H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 histone modifications.

Blocking H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 rescues developmental retardation in PDE offspring

To determine whether H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 modifications might mediate the long-term suppressive effect of PDE on osteoprogenitors and bone formation, we treated pregnant females with dexamethasone in the presence or absence of PFI-90 or GSK-J4. The results showed that both PFI-90 and GSK-J4 treatments led to substantial rescue in the trabecular area in the metaphyseal bone of PDE mice offspring (Fig. 8A, B, D, E). In consistent, both PFI-90 and GSK-J4 treatments dramatically restored the Osterix+ staining area in the metaphyseal bone of PDE young mice offspring (Fig. 8A, C, D, F). Collectively, PDE may activate the expression of MKP-1 by suppressing H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 at the Mkp-1 gene locus, thereby leading to long-lasting decreased proliferation of osteoprogenitors and reduced bone formation.

Both PFI-90 and GSK-J4 restore osteoblasts and osteoprogenitors in PDE offspring.

Discussion

Prenatal exposure to excessive glucocorticoid can adversely affect bone development, leading to low bone mass in offspring. However, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. Our study presents evidence that PDE may lead to long-term adverse consequences on osteoprogenitor proliferation due to epigenetic modifications of MKP-1 expression in the long bone of mouse offspring. Specifically, dexamethasone activates MKP-1 expression by reducing H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 modifications at the Mkp-1 gene locus. The up-regulated MKP-1 expression subsequently suppresses the activity of the JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 pathways, leading to reduced proliferation of osteoprogenitors. Furthermore, blocking H3K9me2 with PFI-90 or H3K27me3 with GSK-J4 effectively rescues the numbers of osteoblasts and osteoprogenitors decreased by PDE. All our data provide new insights into the developmental origins of low bone mass and suggest potential therapeutic targets for treating bone developmental retardation in PDE offspring.

Glucocorticoids administration at pharmacological doses is known to impair bone formation and stimulate bone resorption in the mature skeleton39. However, our previous studies have shown that PDE not only drastically reduces osteoblast number but also suppress osteoclast numbers in the long bones of fetal rats and young offspring mice8,10,40, indicating that the adverse effects of dexamethasone on bone development differ from its effects on mature bone. In addition, although PDE suppresses osteoclast formation by impairing RANKL expression and activating OPG expression in fetal bone8, we did not observe changes of the osteoclast number in adult offspring of PDE compared with controls. Given that osteoclast formation is regulated by various bone cells41,42, our findings indicate that PDE does not have long-term effects on osteoclast formation. Therefore, defects in osteoprogenitor proliferation may be a core adverse event in the skeleton of PDE mouse offspring.

Studies from other groups have investigated the consequences of PDE on stem cells in various tissues, proposing that the anti-proliferative effects of PDE on stem and progenitor cells could be a fundamental cause of developmental abnormalities in offspring organs13,14,43. Our recent work has shown that PDE leads to a decreased population of osteoprogenitors in young mice offspring and a subsequent impairment in bone remodeling in 12-week-old young adult offspring10. Despite consistently observed an increase in SA-β-Gal+ cells within the femoral metaphysis of PDE-affected mice offspring at 2, 4, and 6 weeks of age, we found few co-staining of Nestin+ cells or Osterix+ cells with SA-β-Gal+ cells10. Here, we noted a scarcity of SA-β-Gal+ staining in Prx1+ cells within the femurs of PDE-affected offspring at 2 weeks of age. This suggests that the reduction in Prx1+ cells and Osterix+ osteoprogenitors, induced by PDE, may not be attributed to cellular senescence. Recent literature indicates that glucocorticoid treatment in adult mice can rapidly induce cellular senescence in bone marrow adipocyte lineage cells, with a subsequent spread to vascular cells and osteoblasts44,45. This suggests that multiple cell populations may be involved in glucocorticoid-induced senescence. Further investigation is warranted to identify the specific cell populations marked by SA-β-Gal that are affected by PDE in the developing long bones.

Our data indicated that young mice offspring of PDE group had a notably decreased number of CD45−CD29+CD105+Sca1+ cells. Considering that common skeletal progenitors (bone, cartilage, and stromal progenitors; BCSP) and their subsequent progenitors exhibit differential expression of CD90, CD105, 6C3 and CD20027, and that the endosteal mesenchymal progenitors CD45−CD29highSca1high population is critical for bone formation26, our data suggest that the osteoblastic lineage BCSP might be a primary target of PDE. Additionally, it is important to note that heterogeneous bone marrow mesenchymal progenitor subpopulations vary with age46,47. Besides Prx1, markers such as Leptin receptor (Lepr)12, c-x-c motif chemokine 12 (Cxcl12)48, and early B cell factor (Ebf3)49 have been proposed for bone marrow mesenchymal progenitors. However, these markers label bone marrow mesenchymal progenitors in adult mice but not during the pre- and postnatal period12,48,49, making them unsuitable for studying the developmental toxicity of PDE on bone. Although Prx1+ cells appear as early as embryonic day 8.5 and represent stem cells required for bone homeostasis, they also account for nearly half of the osteoblasts in the adult bone50. Thus, further studies are required to identify the target BMSCs subpopulations contributing to PDE-induced loss of osteoprogenitors and reduced bone mass in adult mice offspring.

During long bone development, osteoblasts are present in the limb at GD 12.5, and the primary ossification center occurs after GD 14.5 in fetal mice51,52. Therefore, GD 12-14 is a critical period for osteoprogenitor proliferation and differentiation. Our previous work10 and present data demonstrated that PDE during GD 12-14 has a long-term toxic effect on osteoprogenitor numbers in young and adult mice, indicating that toxic insult during this period may result in permanent programming of osteoprogenitors activity. A reduction in osteoprogenitors may occur as a result of reduced cell proliferation or increased differentiation. Nevertheless, the observed decrease in osteoblasts and Ocn+ cells, along with the unchanged Perilipin+ cells in the femoral metaphysis of PDE offspring, does not indicate osteogenesis nor adipogenesis. Our recent study showed a marked reduction in Ki67+ cells in the femoral metaphysis of young PDE mice offspring compared with controls10, and the present study confirms the reduction of Prx1+Ki67+ cells in PDE mice offspring. These data support our conclusion that PDE has a long-lasting toxic effect on osteoprogenitor proliferation in mice offspring. Based on the dose conversion factor between mouse and human53, the dose of dexamethasone used in pregnant mice in this study (1.2 mg/kg/d) is comparable to the dose and regimen of dexamethasone (4 × 6 mg, 12-hourly) for indicated preterm labor9,54. Therefore, our study highlights the necessity of optimizing antenatal glucocorticoid administration for women at risk of preterm delivery.

Notably, stronger staining of MKP-1 was detected in Prx1+ cells in the femoral metaphysis of adult PDE mice offspring compared with controls. Although previous work has documented the essential role of MKP-1 in negatively modulating MAPK signaling and subsequent changes in cellular proliferation and differentiation19,55,56, the role of MKP-1 in proliferation of osteoprogenitors remains unclear. There is evidence that dexamethasone may inhibit the self-renewal and proliferation of cancer stem cells by activating MKP-1, thereby inactivating JNK and p3819,55. Our study reveals that JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 mediate the impaired proliferation of osteoprogenitors due to PDE as the major downstream pathways of MKP-1. The discrepancy in MKP-1 downstream pathways regulated by dexamethasone may be due to differences in cellular properties and/or the mode of dexamethasone administration. These findings suggest an important role for MKP-1 in the suppressed proliferation of osteoprogenitors in PDE mice offspring.

The persistent up-regulation of MKP-1 in osteoprogenitors of PDE mice offspring indicates stable programming of genes expression by early life exposure to dexamethasone. Accumulated evidence has indicated that glucocorticoid may mediate the fetal programming of later onset of disease via alteration of epigenetic processes15,57. Histone-modifying enzyme expression has been implicated as a long-lasting effect of glucocorticoid on genes expression58. Our studies provide evidence that PDE promotes the expression of histone demethylases, including UTX and JMJD3, contributing to the persistent upregulation of MKP-1 in osteoprogenitors, leading to reduced proliferation and decreased bone mass in mouse offspring. However, further experiments are required to assess the long-term effects of PFI-90 or GSK-J4 on osteoprogenitors proliferation and bone metabolism in adult mice offspring.

Conclusions

This study has extended our understanding about dexamethasone-mediated programming in bone metabolism of offspring. Although previous study has documented extensively the essential role of PDE in adult-onset diseases16,59, its impacts on postnatal bone metabolism and underlying regulations remain poorly clarified. Here we have demonstrated that PDE may strongly activate MKP-1 expression followed by reducing p38 and ERK pathways in osteoprogenitors, contributing to persistent low bone mass in mice offspring. Furthermore, our evidence clearly indicates that PDE may result in lasting up-regulation of MKP-1 by reducing H3K9me2 and H3K27me3 histone modifications at the Mkp-1 gene locus. We have thus concluded that the H3 demethylation-mediated modulation of MKP-1/MAPK signaling could be responsible for the long-term suppressive effect of PDE on proliferation of osteoprogenitors and the resultant low bone mass in mice offspring.

Methods

Animals and treatments

All animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanfang Hospital Southern Medical University (No. L2019211). The animal experiments adhered to IACUC guidelines and ARRIVE guidelines, and complied with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Male and female C57BL/6 mice (8 to 10 weeks old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center at Southern Medical University in Guangzhou, China. Prx1Cre mice (strain# 005584)60 and Rosa26-CAG-LSL-tdTomato (Rosa26-tdTomato) reporter mice (strain# 007909)61 were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Primers for Prx1Cre genotyping were 5′-GCGGTCTGGCAGTAAAAACTATC -3′ and 5′-GTGAAACAGCATTGCTGTCACTT-3′. Primers for Rosa26-tdTomato genotyping were 5′-CTGTTCCTGTACGGCATGG-3′ and 5′-GGCATTAAAGCAGCGTATCC-3′. Mice were housed in ventilated cages under specific pathogen-free conditions of the animal facility at Southern Medical University and were maintained under standard conditions with a constant temperature (25°C), a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and access to food and water ad libitum.

Our previous work had demonstrated the course-, dose-, and stage-dependent toxic effect of PDE on long bone development in fetal mice9. Therefore, PDE mouse models were generated according to the procedure described in that work. Briefly, pregnant mice were serially numbered: odd-numbered mice were assigned to the PDE group and received repeated dexamethasone exposure (1.2 mg kg−1 per day, #HY-14648, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA), while even-numbered mice were assigned to the control group and received the same volume of vehicle (normal saline) by subcutaneous injection during gestational days 12-14 (GD 12–14). One or two female offspring were randomly selected from each litter of control and PDE groups, with no criteria for inclusion or exclusion. The minimum number of mice for each in vivo experiment was based on our previous study, which found that a sample size of five to six mice per group produced statistically significant and reliable results10. Mice were anaesthetized with 2.5% tribromoethanol (250 mg kg−1, intraperitoneal injection) before euthanasia by cervical dislocation. After euthanasia, femurs and tibias were immediately collected for further analysis.

To assess the effect of PDE on bone microstructure and metabolism in the long bone of adult offspring, femurs and tibias were collected from 28-week-old female mice offspring in the control and PDE groups for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) measurements, histochemistry and immunohistochemistry analysis. To evaluate the effect of PDE on the proliferation of osteoprogenitors in the long bones of 28-week-old mouse offspring, bone marrow cells were flushed out from collected femurs and tibias of control and PDE groups for colony forming unit-fibroblast (CFU-F) assays and flow cytometry analysis.

Considering that the paired-related homeobox gene-1 (Prx1) is a marker for bone marrow mesenchymal progenitors, that is expressed in the earliest limb bud on embryonic day 8.5–9.550,60, we choose Prx1Cre mice for cell lineage analysis in the PDE mouse model. To evaluate the effect of PDE on bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells in the long bones of offspring, female Rosa26-tdTomato mice were crossed with male Prx1Cre mice. PDE models and controls were produced in Rosa26-tdTomato pregnant mice. Female Prx1Cre-tdTomato offspring identified in control and PDE groups were euthanized at day 2, 2- or 4-week-old, and femurs and tibias were collected for histochemistry and immunohistochemistry analysis. For chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assays with Prx1+ cells, cells were sorted from the bone marrow of Prx1Cre-tdTomato mouse offspring in control and PDE groups.

To explore the role of histone H3 lysine 9 (H3K9) methylation in bone development in PDE offspring, PDE pregnant mice were randomly assigned to treatment with either PFI-90 (20 mg kg−1 per day, #S9882, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) or the same volume of vehicle (10% DMSO in corn oil) by intraperitoneal infection from GD12 until labor. Female mouse offspring at 2-week-old were selected from each litter for long bone growth analysis.

To explore the role of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27) methylation in bone development in PDE offspring, PDE pregnant mice were randomly assigned to treatment with either GSK-J4 (100 mg kg−1 per day, #S7070, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) or the same volume of vehicle (2% DMSO) by intraperitoneal injection from GD 12 until labor. Female mouse offspring at 2-week-old were selected from each litter for long bone growth analysis.

Micro-CT measurement

Left femurs dissected from 28-week-old mouse offspring were fixed with 70% ethanol, scanned using microCT80 (Scanco Medical, AG, Switzerland) with Session Manager and FileView (Version 1.7, Hewlett-Packard Development Company, L.P.). Scanning parameters included 55 kV, 145 μA, 12 μm voxel size, integration time 400 ms, and averaging twice. Images were re-constructed and visualized using DataViewer (Version 1.5.1.9, Bruker) and CTVox software (Version 3.0.0r1122, Bruker). Quantitative analysis was performed using CT Analyser software (Version 1.15.4.0+, Bruker). The region of interest (ROI) in bone was defined as a region starting at a distance of 0.215 mm from the growth plate of the distal femur and extending a further longitudinal distance of 1.72 mm in the proximal direction. Morphometric parameters of trabecular bone were calculated, including trabecular bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Histochemistry and immunohistochemistry

Tibias were dissected free of soft tissue, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, decalcified in 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA) (pH = 7.4) for one week, processed for paraffin embedding, and sectioned at 4-μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, or immunohistochemistry according to standard procedures. Osteoblasts were counted on H&E-stained sections using an inverted light microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan) with CellSens Standard 1.6 software (Olympus) and quantified as the number of osteoblasts per length of trabecular bone from 0 to 0.5 mm below growth plate using ImageJ (Versions 1.8.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For TRAP staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were stained with TRAP stain kit (#294-67001, Wako, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. TRAP+ multinucleated cells containing at least three nuclei were identified as osteoclasts, and TRAP+ cells per mm length of trabecular bone from 0 to 0.5 mm below the growth plate were quantified.

For immunohistochemistry analysis, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated before antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After quenching endogenous peroxidase activity with 0.3% H2O2, sections were blocked in 5% BSA for 1 h, followed by incubation with anti-osteocalcin (#ab93876, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, sections were rinsed in PBS and incubated with a biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 30 min, followed by an avidin-biotinylated horseradish peroxidase complex according to the manufacturer’s manual (VECTASTAINABC Kit, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, USA). Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB substrate (#DA1010, SolarbioLife Sciences, Beijing, China). Ocn positive-stained cells per length of trabecular bone from 0 to 0.5 mm below the growth plate were quantified.

To detect senescent cells, frozen sections were stained with a senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining kit (#9860, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) following manufacturer’s manual. Blue-stained senescent cells were observed and imaged using a light microscope (BX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with CellSens Standard 1.6 software (Olympus).

Isolation and culture of mouse bone marrow mesenchymal progenitors

C57BL/6 mice, at 4- to 6-week-old, were used for the isolation and culture of bone marrow mesenchymal progenitors as previously described26. After removing both ends of tibias and femurs, bone marrow was flushed out with αMEM, passed through a 70-µm cell strainer, and plated at 1 × 106 per well on a 6- or 12-well plate for colony forming unit-fibroblast (CFU-F) assay, in a 0.2 × 106 per 35 mm dish for immunofluorescence staining, in a 2 × 106 per 60 mm dish for western blotting, or in a 4 × 106 per 100 mm dish for ChIP-qPCR. For UTX/JMJD3 and KDM3B inhibition experiments, cells were treated with 250 nM dexamethasone in the presence or absence of 1 µM GSK-J4 and 100 nM PFI-90.

CFU-F assay

To evaluate the effect of PDE on the number of osteoprogenitors in offspring, bone marrow cells were flushed out of the bone from PDE mice offspring and control ones at 28 weeks old, after both ends of long bones were removed. Flushed cells were passed through a 70-µm strainer and seeded at 1 × 106 per well on a 6-well plate. Cells were cultured in growth media (αMEM with 15% FBS, 1% glutamine, 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol, 100 IU ml−1 penicillin and 100 µg ml−1 streptomycin). On days 7–9, wells were stained with 3% crystal violet after methanol fixation. CFU-F colonies per well and their diameters were quantified. Only colonies more than 50 cells were counted.

To determine the effect of dexamethasone on the proliferation of osteoprogenitors, freshly isolated mesenchymal progenitors were cultured in growth media with various concentrations of dexamethasone (0, 50, 250 and 500 nM). For MKP-1 inhibition experiments, cells were treated with 250 nM dexamethasone in the presence or absence of 5 µM RO31-8220. Medium was replenished every 3 days. CFU-F colonies were stained on days 7–9 and quantified as described above.

Flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting

To investigate the effect of PDE on number of BMSCs in the long bone of offspring, bone marrow cells were collected from the long bones of 28-week-old PDE and control offspring. The cells were filtered through 40-µm strainers, collected by a centrifuge, depleted of erythrocytes using an ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer, resuspended, and blocked in 1% BSA. Subsequently, 1 × 106 cells per sample were stained with antibodies against CD105/Endoglin-CFS (#967207, Clone 209701), CD29/Integrinβ1-PE (#967205, Clone 265917), Sca-1-APC (#967206, Clone 177228), and CD45-PerCP (#967208, Clone 30-F11), using the Mouse Mesenchymal Stem Cell Multi-Color Flow Cytometry Kit (#FMC003, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA), following manufacturer’s protocol. To define CD45−CD29+CD105+Sca1+ cells, we established gating strategies based on the exclusion of background staining using labelled isotype control mAbs supplied in the Kit. A total of 1 × 105 cells per sample were counted using a FACSCanto™ II FlowCytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) with BD FACSDiva Software (Version 8.0.1). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (BD LifeSciences San Jose, CA, USA).

For sorting Prx1+ cells from femur bone marrow, cells were collected from the femurs of Prx1Cre-tdTomato mice offspring in the control and PDE groups. After erythrocytes depletion, cells were sorted based on Tomato+ fluorescence after negative selection of CD45-BV421 (#103134, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) using a MoFlo XDP high speed cell sorter (Beckman Coulter).

To characterize the death pattern of mesenchymal progenitors induced by PDE, bone marrow from tibias and femurs of 4-week-old PDE and control Prx1Cre-tdTomato mice offspring was flushed out, passed through a 70-μm strainer, washed with ice-cold PBS, and then stained with the Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (#KGA1024, KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 2 × 105 cells per sample were counted using the Beckman CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer with CytExpert software (Version 2.5.0.77, Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.4, BD LifeSciences San Jose, CA, USA). The percentage of early apoptosis Annexin V+7-AAD− cells and late apoptosis Annexin V+7-AAD+ in Prx1+ cells was calculated.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining of bone mesenchymal progenitors, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for permeation. After blocking non-specific binding sites with donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature, cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: mouse anti-Osterix (#sc-393325, 1:50, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) and rabbit anti-Ki67 (#ab16667, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). The following day, cells were incubated with 488-conjugated goat anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#GB25303, 1:200, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and 594-conjugated goat Anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (#8890S, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with 4, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, #S2110, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Images were acquired using a BX63 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with CellSens Dimension 1.18 software (Olympus) and analyzed using ImageJ. The number of Osterix+ and Osterix+Ki67+ stained cells was counted in five fields per well.

For immunofluorescence staining in bone, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in descending grades of alcohol, blocked with 5% donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies used were: rabbit anti-MKP-1 (#ET1701-82, 1:100, HuaAn Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), rabbit anti-Ki67 (#ab16667, 1:100, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), rabbit anti-Osterix (#DF7731, 1:200, Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China). The next day, sections were incubated with 488-conjugated donkey anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#A-21206, 1:200, ThermoFish Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or 594-conjugated goat Anti-Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#8889S, 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All sections were observed and imaged using a BX63 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with CellSens Dimension 1.18 software (Olympus) and analyzed using ImageJ. Positive-stained area or the number of positive-stained cells per square millimeter of the metaphyseal area was measured from 0 to 0.5 mm below a growth plate.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (#K1020, ApexBio, Shanghai, China) containing proteinase inhibitors (#K1007, ApexBio, Shanghai, China) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#K1015, ApexBio, Shanghai, China). After centrifugation, protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23225, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). In total, 20 µg of total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) Immobilon-P membrane (#IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) before incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used were: PCNA (PC10) mouse mAb (#2586, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2)(#4376, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2)(#4695, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Phospho-p38 MAP Kinase (#9211, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-p38 MAP Kinase (#8690, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), rabbit monoclonal anti-MKP-1 (#ET1701-82, 1:1000, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China), Di-Mythyl-Histone H3K27 rabbit mAb (#9728, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Tri-Methyl-Histone H3K27 rabbit mAb (#9733, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Di-methyl-Histone H3K9 rabbit mAb (#4658, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Tri-methyl-Histone H3K9 rabbit mAb (#13969, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Histone H3 (D1H2) rabbit mAb (#4499, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-GAPDH (#ET1601-4, 1:1000, HuaBio, China), anti-Tubulin (#RM2007, 1:1000, Ray Antibody Biotech, Beijing, China). The membranes were washed with TBS-T solution and then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit (#HA1001, 1:3000, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China) or anti-mouse (#HA1006, 1:3000, HuaBio, Hangzhou, China) secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. All blots were developed using Western Lightning Plus ECL (#NEL105001EA, PerkinElmer), and signals were detected by a chemiluminescence apparatus (Tanon Biotechnology, China).

Small interfering RNAs

Bone marrow osteoprogenitors were isolated and seeded at 1 × 105 per well in a 6-well plate. The next day, siRNA transfection was performed with Lipofectamine 2000 (#11668027, ThermoFish Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The control and mouse-specific Mkp-1 siRNAs were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). The following oligonucleotides of siRNA duplexes were used: control siRNA, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; siMkp-1#1, 5′-AGUGGUUAGGACAAUUGGCTT-3′; siMkp-1#2, 5′-AUCCAACAACAGGCACUGCTT-3′; siMkp-1#3, 5′-AAUGAACAAACACUCUCCCTT-3′.

ELISA assay of Histone H3 modification

Bone marrow cells collected from the long bones of 4-week-old mice offspring from the PDE and control groups were cultured for 2 weeks to expand the bone mesenchymal progenitors as aforementioned in 2.4. The levels of histone H3 modification in bone tissue were quantified using a Histone H3 Modification Multiplex Colorimetric Assay Kit (#ab185910, Abcam). A total of 100 ng per well extract was used, optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm in a Microplate Reader (#ELX808, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), and the relative change in each histone H3 modification in progenitors from the PDE group compared with control group was calculated following the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Bone mesenchymal progenitors were treated with dexamethasone or a vehicle for 48 h. Total RNA samples were extracted using RNAex Pro (#DP424, TIANGEN, China) for RNA-Seq analysis. The RNA was purified using RNAprep Pure (#DP441, TIANGEN, China), quantified with NanoDrop ND-1000, and assessed for quality using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation system to ensure RNA integrity. RNA libraries were prepared using the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode RNA Library Prep kit (#12310ES, YEASEN, China) and sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a Log2 foldchange > 0.8 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 using the SRplot online platform62.

ChIP-sequencing and analysis

Bone mesenchymal progenitors were isolated from the long bones of PDE and control mice offspring and cultured for 2 weeks as aforementioned in 2.4. Cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to crosslink proteins to DNA. After nuclei isolation, chromatin was sonicated in nuclei lysis buffer to achieve the desired DNA fragment, followed by immunoprecipitation with a Histone H3K9me2 rabbit monoclonal antibody (#4658, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). DNA fragments were purified using the SimpleChIP® Plus Kit (#38191S, CST) and quantified with the Qubit® DNA Assay Kit on a Qubit® 3.0 Flurometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). The ChIP-seq libraries were prepared using the Novogen DNA Library Prep Kit (Novogene, Beijing, China) and their quality assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Subsequently, libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 plateform (Illumina, CA, USA) with paired-end 150 base-pair sequencing. Two biological replicates were analyzed for each group. Raw fastq data were processed with fastp software (version 0.19.11)63. The range of sequencing depth was 21–22 million raw reads per sample. Reads shorter than 15 nucleotides were discarded. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse mm39 genome using Burrows Wheeler Aligner (v0.7.12-r1039). Only reads that mapped uniquely were used for the analysis. The number of uniquely mapped reads for each input sample was approximately 10 million. H3K9me2 enriched peak regions were identified using MACSs peak calling software (version 2.2.7.1) with a q-value < 0.05, and data visualization was performed with Integrative Genomic Viewer (IGV 2.17.4).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA from bone mesenchymal progenitors was isolated using RNAex Pro (#AG21102, Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quantity and purity were confirmed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop2000c, ThermoFish Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA was reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (#RR036A, TaKaRa Biotechnology, Japan). PCR reactions were conducted on the ABI PRISM® 7900HT System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) with SYBR Premix ExTaqTM II (#RR820B, TaKaRa Biotechnology, Japan). Murine primers used in this study were as follows: UTX (forward, 5′-TTGCCAAGATTGTGCACGAAAA-3′; reverse, 5′-CAAGATGAGGCGGATGGTAA-3′), JMJD3 (forward, 5′-GGAGGAGAGGGAGAGTGAGG-3′; reverse, 5′-CTCCTGTAGCTGTGGCTTCC-3′) and GAPDH (forward, 5′-TGTCAAGCTCATTTCCTGGTATG-3′; reverse, 5′-TTATGGGGGTCTGGGATGGA-3′). The relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔct method with glyceraldehyde 3-phosphatede hydrogenase (GAPDH) for normalization.

ChIP-qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed according to the Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Protocol (#9003, Simple ChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Briefly, bone marrow tissue from 1-month-old control and PDE mice offspring, primary bone marrow stromal cells treated with vehicle or dexamethasone, and sorted Prx1+ cells from Prx1Cre-tdTomato mice offspring in control and PDE groups were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to crosslink proteins to DNA. After nuclei preparation and chromatin digestion, chromatin was immunoprecipitated with Tri-Methyl-Histone H3K27 (C36B11) rabbit mAb (#9733, 1:20, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Di-Mythyl-Histone H3K27 rabbit mAb (#9728, 1:20, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Di-methyl-Histone H3K9 rabbit mAb (#4658, 1:20, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and normal rabbit IgG (#2729, 1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Protein-DNA cross-links were reversed, and DNA was purified using the SimpleChIP® Plus Kit (#38191S, CST).

H3K27me3 modifications at the Mkp-1 gene locus were analyzed using data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically focusing on H3K27me3 modifications in BMSCs at day 0 (GSM1973731), day 7 (GSM1973733), and day 21 (GSM1973738) of osteogenic differentiation64. The aligned data were visualized and presented using the IGV (integrative genomics viewer) (http://software.broadinstitute.org/software/igv/home) (Supplementary Fig. 7). For H3K27me3 antibody immunoprecipitated DNA, primers specific to the Mkp-1 gene were designed as follows: Forward, 5′-GCCACTCCTGGTATCACGTT-3′; Reverse, 5′-ACTCTCGCCATTTGTCCTCG-3′. These primers target the promoter region, as identified by the genes sequence in the NCBI database, and their location was illustrated in Fig. S1. Finally, the enrichment of DNA sequences was quantified using standard PCR methods.

Statistics and reproducibility

Experimental data were analyzed using Student’s t-test, Welch’s t-test, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Following ANOVA, the Bonferroni post hoc test or Dunnett T3 post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± S.E.M., and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data from at least three independent experiments were used for analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 24.0, International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
