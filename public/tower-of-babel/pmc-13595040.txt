13 | Short‐Term Maternal and Neonatal Microbiome Effects of Intrapartum Azithromycin Exposure: A Multi‐Country RCT Ancillary Study

Carlo1 On behalf of NICHD Global Network for Women's and Children's Health

1University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL; 2UMass Chan Medical School, Worcester, MA; 3University Teaching Hospital–Children's Hospital, Lusaka, Zambia; 4Lata Medical Research Foundation, Nagpur, India; 5Boston University, Boston, MA; 6Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Belagavi, India; 7Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA; 8The Aga Khan University, Karachi, Pakistan; 9Columbia University, New York, NY; 10Kinshasa School of Public Health, Lemba, Congo, (Congo–Kinshasa); 11University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC; 12Moi University School of Medicine, Eldoret, Kenya; 13Indiana University, Indianapolis, IN; 14International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Dhaka, Bangladesh; 15University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA; 16Instituto de Nutrición de Centro América y Panamá, Guatemala City, Guatemala; 17University of Colorado, Denver, CO; 18RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC; 19Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, Bethesda, MD

Objective: In the Azithromycin Prevention in Labor Use (A‐PLUS) RCT, oral azithromycin (AZI) reduced maternal sepsis/death by 33% compared to placebo. We assessed the effect of AZI vs. placebo on the nasopharyngeal (NP) and rectal microbiome composition of mothers and infants at 42 days postpartum (PP).

Study Design: In this planned A‐PLUS ancillary study, women and their infants at 8 sites in 7 countries had serial rectal and NP swabs obtained at baseline (0–1), 7, and 42 days PP. DNA was extracted and 16S sequencing performed uniformly in the US (for 6 of 8 A‐PLUS sites) or India (2 sites due to India regulations); all sequence data were analyzed by the US center using validated protocols. Community‐level measures −alpha and beta diversity – were compared between AZI and placebo groups at each timepoint and longitudinally (test of interaction) with adjustment for select covariates (eg. maternal infection, see Table) from the A‐PLUS RCT.

Results: From 469 maternal‐infant dyads, 4764 samples were analyzed: 2376 AZI, 2388 placebo; 1591 at baseline, 1588 at 7 days, 1585 at 42 days PP. Baseline, alpha and beta diversity were similar between groups for all microbiomes. In the maternal rectal microbiome, alpha diversity was significantly reduced in the AZI group (Figure); beta diversity also significantly differed between groups at 7 days PP, 42 days PP, and over time. (Table). Alpha diversity of the maternal NP, neonatal rectal, or neonatal NP microbiome was similar between groups over time. While there were differences in beta diversity at 7 days (all PERMANOVA p < 0.05) in the maternal NP and neonatal rectal microbiome, these were not present by 42 days PP. Neonatal NP microbiome beta diversity was different between AZI and placebo at 42 days PP (all p < 0.05) but did not demonstrate a consistent AZI treatment effect over time (pinteraction = NS).

Conclusion: Intrapartum AZI was associated with significant community‐level changes in the maternal rectal microbiome and possibly, the neonatal NP microbiome. Further analyses will evaluate if these changes persist long‐term and the clinical implications of these findings.

14 | Expansion of the Hepatitis C Transitions‐To‐Treatment (HCV TOT) Program During Postpartum Hospitalization

Wendy Tian; Marie Johanna J. Boller; Lucy Ward

Oregon Health & Science University, Portland, OR

Objective: Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection in pregnancy is increasing in the United States, yet postpartum treatment initiation remains low due to loss to follow‐up. We evaluated whether initiating HCV treatment during the postpartum hospitalization improves treatment uptake.

Study Design: We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary care center comparing treatment initiation before and after implementation of a postpartum HCV treatment program. Beginning February 2025, individuals with chronic HCV delivering at our institution were offered standardized inpatient evaluation, bedside counseling, and initiation of direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs) prior to discharge, with coordinated postpartum follow‐up. The pre‐implementation cohort (June 2022–January 2025) was compared to the post‐implementation cohort (February 2025–December 2026). The primary outcome was HCV treatment initiation within 12 months postpartum.

Results: Among 40 eligible individuals, treatment initiation increased from 14% (4/28) pre‐implementation to 67% (8/12) post‐implementation. Ongoing analyses include treatment completion, sustained virologic response at 12 weeks (SVR12), and postpartum care engagement.

Conclusion: Initiation of HCV treatment during postpartum hospitalization was associated with a nearly five‐fold increase in treatment uptake. Leveraging the delivery hospitalization—when patients are highly engaged in care—represents an effective, scalable strategy to reduce loss to follow‐up and improve maternal health outcomes. This model has potential for broad implementation in obstetric settings caring for patients with HCV.

15 | Positive Placental Culture in Term Clinical Chorioamnionitis: Association With Neonatal Morbidity

Maayan Bas Lando1; Tujan Nasser Edeen2; Orna Riechman2; Hen Y. Y. Sela1; Maskit Bar Meir2; Rivka Farkash2; Sorina Grisaru‐Granovsky2

1Hebrew University and Shaare Zedek Medical Center, Jerusalem, Israel; 2Shaare Zedek Medical Center, Jerusalem, Israel

Objective: Clinical chorioamnionitis is a heterogeneous diagnosis that may represent true intrauterine infection or sterile inflammation, and the clinical implications of positive placental cultures remain unclear. We evaluated whether placental culture positivity in term clinical chorioamnionitis is associated with neonatal morbidity at the index delivery and with outcomes in subsequent deliveries.

Study Design: Retrospective cohort study of singleton term deliveries complicated by clinical chorioamnionitis between 2005–2022. Women were grouped according to placental culture status (positive vs negative). Maternal, neonatal, and subsequent delivery outcomes were compared. A composite neonatal morbidity outcome was defined as any of the following: 5‐minute Apgar <7, neonatal infection, respiratory complications, hypoglycemia, NICU stay >3 days, or neonatal death. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate independent predictors of composite neonatal morbidity.

Results:

A total of 2322 deliveries met inclusion criteria; 531 (22.9%) had positive placental cultures.

Composite neonatal morbidity was significantly higher in the positive culture group (24.8% vs 15.3%, p < 0.001).

Neonates in the positive culture group had higher rates of Apgar score <7 at 1 minute (12.4% vs 8.5%, p = 0.006), Apgar <7 at 5 minutes (4.1% vs 2.2%, p = 0.017), NICU admission (13.0% vs 8.1%, p < 0.001), prolonged NICU stay (11.2% vs 5.9%, p < 0.001), and neonatal infection (8.4% vs 2.9%, p < 0.001).

In multivariable analysis, positive placental culture remained independently associated with composite neonatal morbidity (aOR 1.82, 95% CI 1.42–2.33, p < 0.001), after adjustment for maternal age, gestational age, parity, and mode of delivery.

In subsequent deliveries, placental culture status at the index delivery was not associated with recurrent chorioamnionitis, preterm birth, NICU admission, or neonatal infection.

Conclusion: Positive placental culture in term clinical chorioamnionitis is associated with increased neonatal morbidity at the index delivery but does not appear to affect outcomes in subsequent deliveries.

16 | Dual‐Compartment Sampling Unmasks Discordant Blood and Vaginal Inflammatory Cytokine Dynamics in Pregnancy

Carolynn Dude1; Stephanie A. Fisher2; Annie Dude3; Emily Gascoigne3; Carmen Beamon4; Hadley Hartwell5; Sarah Heerboth3; Matthew K. Hoffman6; Lynn M. Yee7; Rebecca Fry5; Tracy A. Manuck3

1Northwestern University, Chicago, IL; 2Northwestern University, Chicago, IL; 3University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, NC; 4Wakemed Health, Raleigh, NC; 5Gillings School of Global Public Health, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC; 6Columbia University Medical Center, New York, NY; 7Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago, IL

Objective: Inflammation is a key driver of spontaneous preterm birth (SPTB). Prior studies have found elevated inflammation is associated with eventual PTB among clinically asymptomatic pregnant individuals, but it is unknown if systemic (blood) and local (cervicovaginal) inflammatory marker levels sync or diverge during pregnancy. Thus, we tested the correlation between cytokines within and across blood and vaginal compartments.

Study Design: Secondary analysis of a prospective, longitudinal cohort of multipara with singleton gestations enrolled <22 wks, enriched 2:1 for high SPTB risk. Up to 2 blood and 2 vaginal samples per participant were collected 12+0–27+6 wks. 12 cytokines were quantified via Luminex and normalized using the low‐risk subset. Pearson correlations were assessed between blood‐blood, vaginal‐vaginal, and paired blood‐vaginal samples.

Results: 437 participants contributed 502 blood (collected @ median 20, IQR 17–23 wks) and 649 vaginal (collected @ median 19, IQR 16–22 wks) samples. Blood pro‐inflammatory and T‐helper cytokines clustered tightly (many |r| = 0.4–0.7, p < 0.001), but associations were weaker for IL‐10 & G‐CSF. Vaginal cytokines were even more tightly linked (many |r| >0.6, p < 0.001; e.g., IL‐4, IL‐8, TNF‐α, G‐CSF, GM‐CSF, IL‐1α, IL‐2, IL‐6), but IL‐1Ra & IL‐10 associations were weaker (Figure 1). Strikingly, blood‐vaginal pairs were de‐coupled in the overall cohort (most |r| < 0.15); in the subset at high‐risk for SPTB, select pairs (IL‐1Ra, IL‐4, IL‐8) were modestly inversely correlated (r ≈–0.3 to −0.5), signaling compartmentalized and/or compensatory regulation of IL‐1 and TNF‐ driven pathways (Figure 2).

Conclusion: Cytokines cluster tightly within – but decouple across – systemic blood and local vaginal compartments, unmasking pregnancy‐specific inflammatory dynamics. This discordance suggests that blood and vaginal cytokines are unreliable proxies for each other. Further, it provides critical insights into the importance of dual‐compartment sampling during pregnancy to obtain a complete picture of the contribution of sub‐clinical inflammation to an individual's SPTB risk.

17 | Effect of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Vaccination on Placental and Maternal Inflammation in Pregnancy

Luke P. Burns1; Stephanie Schreiber2; Gregory E. Miller3; Alexa A. Freedman4; Kate Honeyfield5; Linda Ernst6; Sunitha Suresh6

1University of Chicago, Chicago, IL; 2Advocate Christ Medical Center, Oak Lawn, IL; 3Northwestern University, Chicago, IL; 4Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago, IL; 5University of Colorado, Denver, CO; 6Endeavor Health, Evanston, IL

Objective: Safety studies of maternal respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccination demonstrated a signal towards increased risk of preterm birth. We evaluated the association between RSV vaccination and placental and maternal systemic inflammation at delivery to examine if there was a biologic basis for the increased risk.

Study Design: Prospective cohort study of singleton pregnancies between 32 0/7 and 36 6/7 weeks’ gestation at a single institution, comparing patients who received RSV vaccination with vaccine‐eligible and desiring patients outside the administration window. Patients with conditions strongly associated with placental pathology were excluded. Placentas were assessed for acute and chronic inflammation by a perinatal pathologist. Maternal inflammatory markers IL‐6, IL‐10, TNF‐α and C‐reactive protein were obtained at delivery. Univariate analysis was conducted using chi‐squared, Wilcoxon rank sum and Student's t‐test as applicable. Logistic regression was utilized for multivariable analysis adjusting for nulliparity and labor.

Results: Placental pathology and delivery data were available for 49 RSV vaccinated patients and 26 controls. RSV vaccinated patients were more likely to be nulliparous (44.9% vs 15.4%, p = 0.01, Table 1) and trended to less likely to have labored (84.6% vs 65.3%, p = 0.08). Groups were otherwise demographically similar. Gestational age at delivery, mean birth weight and NICU admission did not differ between groups. There was no difference in acute or chronic placental inflammation between those with and without RSV vaccination (for acute inflammation 49.0% vs 50.0%, aOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.33–2.60; for chronic inflammation 42.9% vs 34.6%, aOR 1.02, 95% CI 0.35–3.02). There was no difference in any maternal inflammatory markers between those with and without an RSV vaccination.

Conclusion: In this prospective cohort study, maternal RSV vaccination in pregnancy was not associated with significant differences in maternal serum or placental inflammation at delivery. These results are encouraging for continued utilization of RSV vaccination in pregnancy (see Table 2).
