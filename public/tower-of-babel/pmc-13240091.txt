Effects of the Intensity, Duration, and Muscle Mass of Isometric Handgrip Exercise on Neuromuscular Fatigue and Post‐Exercise Blood Pressure Responses: A Crossover Study
Authors: Jiawei Wang, Changsi Zhang, Jiaqi Guo, Mansoureh Samadi, Yih‐Kuen Jan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIsometric handgrip exercise (IHE) has emerged as a promising nonpharmacological intervention for blood pressure management. The objective of this study was to investigate the influence of the intensity, duration, and muscle mass factors of IHE protocol variation on the relationship between neuromuscular fatigue and blood pressure changes (systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate (HR)).MethodsSixteen participants completed one standard protocol (A) and three protocol variations (B, C, and D) in four visits separated by 7 days between visits. The protocols included: (A) unilateral IHE at 30% maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) for 2 min for four bouts, (B) unilateral IHE at 45% MVC for 2 min for four bouts, (C) unilateral IHE at 30% MVC for 3 min for four bouts, and (D) bilateral IHE at 30% MVC for 2 min for four bouts. The root mean square (RMS) and median frequency (MDF) of surface electromyography were used to quantify muscle fatigue of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), extensor carpi radialis longus (ECR), flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU), and extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU).ResultsDuring Protocol D, FDS RMS displayed a moderate negative correlation with DBP (r = −0.53, p < 0.05). Additionally, ECR demonstrated moderate positive correlations with HR (r = 0.58, p < 0.05), SBP (r = 0.52, p < 0.05), and DBP (r = 0.51, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe results of this study suggest that fatigue of ECR and FDS may affect the efficacy of cardiovascular responses by isometric contraction exercise training.Keywords: blood pressure, electromyography, heart rate, isometric training, muscle fatigue

Introduction

Hypertension is a significant global health issue and a primary risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1]. The American Heart Association (AHA) estimates that within the next 30 years, more than 61% of US adults will have some form of CVD affecting over 184 million individuals and underscoring an urgent public health imperative [2]. First‐line therapy of hypertension is lifestyle modifications and physical activity [3]. Various modes of physical activity and exercise have been clinically prescribed, and isometric exercise training has been endorsed by AHA as level C evidence [4, 5, 6, 7]. Isometric exercise, including isometric handgrip exercise (IHE), is defined as a sustained muscle tension without the muscle length change [8, 9]. Due to its simplicity and safety profile, IHE is especially well‐suited for the elderly population with a high incidence of hypertension and limited mobility [10].

Although the American College of Cardiology and AHA recognize isometric exercise training as an adjunctive therapy for blood pressure management, the acceptance of IHE in clinical practice remains limited due to mixed outcomes across studies [6, 11]. While some studies have demonstrated significant reductions in blood pressure through IHE [5, 12, 13, 14, 15], others reported minimal or nonsignificant effects [16, 17, 18, 19]. The inconsistent conclusions across studies may stem from the differences in exercise protocols consisting of exercise intensity, duration, muscle mass, and overall program structure [20, 21]. For example, Gessler and colleagues used a protocol of four bouts of 2‐min bilateral IHE at 30% of maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) with 1‐min rest between bouts [22], while Carlson and colleagues employed four bouts of 2‐min IHE using the nondominant hand at 5% MVC with 3‐min rest between bouts [23]. Another study used four bouts of 2‐min exercise at 30% MVC, alternating hands with 1‐min rest between bouts [24]. The overall training programs also varied ranging from 12 weeks with five sessions weekly [16], 8 weeks with three sessions weekly [25], to 5 weeks with three sessions weekly [14]. The duration of IHE—whether used as an acute, single session or as a long‐term intervention—also appears to influence blood pressure outcomes. Given the variations in IHE protocols, further exploration is necessary to identify appropriate protocols for effective blood pressure reduction. The intensity, duration, and muscle mass factors of IHE have been demonstrated to affect the efficacy of IHE on cardiovascular responses [20, 21].

The intensity of isometric exercise is a factor associated with cardiovascular responses [26]. Studies demonstrated that variations in exercise intensity can yield distinct effects on blood pressure regulation [27]. Hess and colleagues compared 5% and 10% MVC of IHE and found that both exercise intensities reduced systolic blood pressure (SBP) but neither reached the statistical significance [28]. In contrast, Javidi and colleagues compared the traditional isometric handgrip protocol at 30% MVC (four bouts of 2‐min IHE) with a 60% MVC protocol (8 bouts of 30‐s contraction) and found that both exercise protocols significantly reduced resting blood pressure [29]. Notably, the 60% MVC protocol produced a greater reduction in diastolic blood pressure (DBP). These findings highlight the influences of varying exercise intensities of isometric contraction on blood pressure outcomes. While previous studies have explored the effects of IHE on blood pressure regulation, the appropriate and optimal intensity for achieving effective blood pressure reduction remains largely unknown. Furthermore, it is unclear whether exercise intensity affects the extent of local neuromuscular fatigue of contracting muscles, which in turn influences blood pressure responses [7, 21].

Additionally, the duration of isometric exercise is considered a factor influencing blood pressure responses. Studies have shown that under a similar exercise intensity, a shorter duration elicited higher local blood flow compared to a longer duration [30]. The increase in local blood flow of contracting muscles may contribute to systemic blood pressure reduction by decreasing total peripheral vascular resistance and improving vascular relaxation [7]. This framework demonstrates a link between the length of exercise duration and the degree in blood pressure changes. Recent research by Lin and colleagues indicates that under the same muscle mass, exercise duration has a more significant impact on the forearm muscle oxygenation response and systemic blood pressure regulation than the exercise intensity factor [20]. The study further suggests that local muscle oxygenation changes following isometric contractions may contribute to mediate systemic blood pressure regulation. The effect of the duration of IHE requires further research for effective outcomes.

Lastly, the involved muscle mass of isometric exercise is another factor influencing blood pressure regulation. Van Daele and colleagues suggested that an individual's grip strength is closely associated with the amplitude of blood pressure fluctuations during exercise [31]. Considering that the muscle mass of isometric contraction is a major determinant of grip strength, this further implies a potential link between muscle mass and blood pressure responses. Olher and colleagues emphasized that, compared to isometric exercise duration and intensity, muscle mass played a more prominent role in regulating muscle oxygenation levels [32]. Greater muscle mass usually requires more blood flow during activity or recovery to meet metabolic demands, leading to a more pronounced hemodynamic response.

In summary, evidence suggests that the intensity, duration, and muscle mass of isometric exercise affect blood pressure regulation and isometric exercise with different exercise modes may induce distinct changes in blood pressure regulation [20, 21]. This may partly contribute to the mixed results in the literature regarding the effectiveness of isometric exercise on blood pressure reduction because there are no standard protocols used in these clinical trials. To date, several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain the effect of isometric contraction on blood pressure regulation, including the cardiac autonomic mechanisms [7], neural regulation of vascular tone [33], vascular functional adaptations [34], and vasomotion by intramuscular pressure [35]. Among these potential mechanisms, the sympathetic nervous system may be involved in the regulation of isometric exercise on blood pressure reduction.

Studies have shown that sympathetic activation is associated with increased electromyographic activity (EMG) during fatiguing exercise [36], while local blood flow is linked to peripheral fatigue [21, 37, 38]. Based on these findings, it can be hypothesized that neuromuscular fatigue caused by IHE is associated with post‐exercise blood pressure reductions. Neuromuscular fatigue can be defined as an exercise‐induced reduction in muscle force or power output, which can be categorized into two main types: central fatigue (e.g., reduced voluntary activation and inhibitory afferent feedback) and peripheral fatigue (e.g., metabolic wastes and calcium handling dysfunction) [39, 40]. Different intensities, durations, and muscle masses of isometric exercise may result in different degrees of neuromuscular fatigue, which in turn causes mixed responses of post‐exercise blood pressure [41]. Hikosaka and Aramaki demonstrated that the decline in grip strength during bilateral tasks was significantly smaller than that during unilateral tasks involving the dominant hand, which may be attributed to compensatory mechanisms that reduce the degree of neuromuscular fatigue [42]. Furthermore, Seals highlighted that during isometric exercise, muscle sympathetic nerve activity—a measure of sympathetic activation—increases with larger muscle mass [43]. Additionally, blood pressure (particularly SBP) rises proportionally with the mass of the contracting muscles. After exercise, activation of baroreflex mechanisms may lead to reduced systemic vascular resistance, suppressing sympathetic activity and ultimately inducing post‐exercise hypotension.

Currently, the specific effects of various factors of isometric handgrip on inducing neuromuscular fatigue and regulating blood pressure remain largely unknown. This knowledge gap may cause suboptimal choices for commonly used isometric handgrip protocols in clinical blood pressure management. To investigate the interactions between neuromuscular fatigue and systemic blood pressure regulation under varying conditions of intensity, duration, and muscle mass of IHE, this study employed EMG to quantify neuromuscular fatigue. This quantification of neuromuscular fatigue could be used to elucidate the influence of neuromuscular fatigue of contracting muscle on blood pressure regulation following IHE and further evaluate the potential association between exercise modes and blood pressure changes. To investigate the influence of isometric handgrip protocols on managing hypertension, it is essential to first explore the mechanisms in a healthy young cohort. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to quantify the relationship between the changes in neuromuscular fatigue and systemic blood pressure under different IHE protocols in individuals without hypertension. We hypothesized that all three factors of the intensity, duration, and muscle mass of IHE cause different levels of neuromuscular fatigue of the forearm muscles, which in turn could trigger distinct post‐exercise blood pressure responses. Specifically, we hypothesized that (a) four bouts of 45% MVC IHE causes more fatigue and blood pressure reduction compared to four bouts of 30% MVC IHE (intensity factor), (b) four bouts of 3‐min IHE causes more fatigue and blood pressure reduction compared to four bouts of 2‐min IHE (intensity factor), and (c) four bouts of bilateral IHE causes more fatigue blood pressure reduction compared to four bouts of unilateral IHE (muscle mass factor). The results of this study conducted in healthy individuals could contribute to understand the mechanism of action of isometric contraction on managing hypertension.

Materials and Methods

This study involving human subjects was conducted with the approval of the Institutional Review Board at the University of Illinois at Urbana‐Champaign (IRB #24‐0784). This study was part of broader research investigating the potential mechanisms of action of isometric exercise on blood pressure regulation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Eligibility for participation was determined using the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire Plus (PAR‐Q+). Any participant who responded affirmatively to any of the seven questions on the PAR‐Q+ was excluded from the study. The inclusion criteria were the age between 18 and 40 years and not athletes. Participants were asked to refrain from exercise before the experiment at 48 h. Exclusion criteria included those diagnosed with CVD, neuromuscular injuries, hypertension (defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg), diabetes or taking any medications. The sample size estimation was based on the assumption of a large effect size based on the literature findings [11] (f 0.4), power of 0.8 for four groups for repeated measures, and the needed sample size was 16. Participants were recruited from the University of Illinois at Urbana‐Champaign via flyers. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation in the study.

Study Design

This study employed a crossover, repeated‐measures design, comprising four visits separated by 1 week. During each visit, participants were asked to perform one of four IHE protocols including:

A. Four bouts of unilateral IHE at 30% MVC for 2 min with 2‐min rest between bouts;

B. Four bouts of unilateral IHE at 45% MVC for 2 min with 2‐min rest between bouts;

C. Four bouts of unilateral IHE at 30% MVC for 3 min with 2‐min rest between bouts; and

D. Four bouts of bilateral IHE at 30% MVC for 2 min with 2‐min rest.

By comparing Protocols A and B, we investigated the influence the intensity factor of IHE on muscle fatigue and blood pressure reduction. By comparing Protocols A and C, we investigated the influence of the duration factor of IHE. By comparing Protocols A and D, we investigated the influence of the muscle mass factor of IHE. Protocols B, C, and D were not compared because of more than two factors of IHE protocol variation. Protocols A, B, and C were performed using the dominant hand and Protocol D was performed using both hands. The rationale for choosing these protocols was based on existing exercise protocols reported in the literature [20, 21]. Protocol A (four bouts of IHE at 30% MVC for 2 min) is commonly prescribed. Protocols B, C, and D are variations used to examined the efficacy of IHE protocol on blood pressure reduction [6, 20, 21]. The 24 crossover sequences including [(A, B, C, D) (A, B, D, C) (A, C, B, D) (A, C, D, B) (A, D, B, C) (A, D, C, B) (B, A, C, D) (B, A, D, C) (B, C, A, D) (B, C, D, A) (B, D, A, C) (B, D, C, A) (C, A, B, D) (C, A, D, B) (C, B, A, D) (C, B, D, A) (C, D, A, B) (C, D, B, A) (D, A, B, C) (D, A, C, B) (D, B, A, C) (D, B, C, A) (D, C, A, B) (D, C, B, A)] were randomly assigned to research participants. The counter‐balanced design was implemented to ensure each sequence was first tested once and not repeated from all sequences.

Experimental Procedures

During the first visit, participants' demographic data, including age, weight, height, and baseline SBP and DBP, were measured and recorded. Participants were instructed not to exercise for at least 48 h and to arrive at the laboratory 30 min prior to each experimental session to acclimatize to room temperature. The participant was also told not to take any caffeine nor medication for the date of test. They were seated in an upright position with their arm resting naturally on a table in front of them, shoulders relaxed, forearms semi‐pronated, and wrists in a neutral position, remaining seated quietly for 5–10 min. This posture was maintained throughout the experiment to ensure consistency (Figure 1). Blood pressure measurements were obtained using a blood pressure monitor (BP785, Omron, Hoffman Estates, IL), recording both SBP and DBP and heart rate (HR). Participants then performed three MVC trials using their dominant hand on a handgrip dynamometer (EH101, Digital Hand Dynamometer, Camry, Amazon Store; maximum capacity 110 kg), with each MVC trial lasting 2–3 s, followed by a 1‐min rest between trials. The adjustable handgrip device was then configured to a fixed resistance level based on the average MVC and specific protocol requirements. Following blood pressure measurements and MVC testing, four bipolar electrodes (circular Ag‐AgCl wet gel electrodes) were affixed to a three‐lead electromyography amplifier (EMG100C, Biopac Systems Inc., Goleta, CA; CMRR 110 dB, bandwidth 1–5000 Hz) and placed over the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS), extensor carpi radialis longus (ECR), flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU), and extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU) muscles of the participant's dominant hand. The EMG signals were collected at 1000 Hz to assess neuromuscular activity during the four distinct IHE protocols.

A computer‐based timing device was utilized throughout each protocol to cue participants when to initiate contraction and relaxation phases. Participants were instructed to grip the preset fixed‐resistance handgrip device with their dominant hand (Protocols A, B, and C) or both hands (Protocol D). The order of protocols was assigned to each participant based on a counter‐balanced study design [21]. Upon completion of each exercise protocol separated by 7 ± 2 days, immediate post‐exercise and 10‐min post‐exercise blood pressure and HR measurements were recorded. Each blood pressure measurement was repeated twice and the values were averaged for reporting. During IHE, participants were instructed to breathe normally without holding breath.

Data Analysis

This study employed a high‐pass filter at 2 Hz using a fourth‐order zero‐phase Butterworth filter to process surface electromyography signals [21, 44]. The root mean square (RMS) and median frequency (MDF) values of EMG were calculated to quantitatively reflect the level of muscle activation during isometric contractions. A Hamming window function was utilized for the calculations, with a window length of 0.512 s and a time sampling interval of 75% of the window length [21, 44]. The derived RMS values represent the overall amplitude of the sEMG signal, providing an objective measure of muscle electrical activity. In this study, the RMS values of four muscle groups were calculated for the first and last contraction bouts in each protocol. The normalized RMS ratio (the last bout to first bout) was used to quantify changes in muscle activation, serving as an indicator of neuromuscular fatigue induced by different exercise protocols. This approach ensured consistent evaluation of muscle activity levels across protocols while preserving the integrity of the EMG data.

After obtaining the power spectrum, MDF was calculated based on the following equation [45].

In this study, the mean value of the MDF and RMS during the first and last contraction periods was utilized to quantify the level of neuromuscular fatigue for each participant in each protocol [46, 47]. When muscle is fatigued, EMG RMS values become larger and EMG MDF values become smaller. Furthermore, SBP, DBP, and HR were measured pre‐exercise and immediate post‐exercise (SBP_0, DBP_0, HR_0) and 10‐min post‐exercise (SBP_10, DBP_10, HR_10) to analyze post‐exercise blood pressure changes. The differences between immediate and 10‐min post‐exercise in HR and SBP and DBP were calculated. The calculation process was implemented in MATLAB (2025 version, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA).

Statistical Analysis

The study design of this study was to compare Protocols A and B (intensity factor), Protocols A and C (duration factor), and Protocols A and D (muscle mass factor). Thus, two‐sided paired t tests were used to evaluate the differences in RMS and MDF values of each of four forearm muscle groups between Protocols A and B, Protocols A and C, and Protocols A and D, respectively. These tests aimed to assess the fatigue levels of each muscle group. Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between the fatigue levels (i.e., RMS and MDF in this study) of the four muscle groups and changes in HR, SBP, and DBP immediate and 10‐min post‐exercise. The study also assessed the significance levels and effect sizes of the correlations, categorizing the absolute correlation coefficient ≥ 0.7 as strong, 0.3–0.7 as moderate, and ≤ 0.3 as weak correlations as well as their significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (v.29, Chicago, IL, USA), with the significance level set at p < 0.05. When there was a repeated measure comparison, Bonferroni correction was applied for statistical interpretation.

Results

Sixteen healthy participants (nine males and seven females) were recruited for this experiment. Their characteristics (mean ± standard deviation) were as follows: age 25.07 ± 0.55 years, height 174.40 ± 1.76 cm, weight 67.83 ± 3.88 kg, and body mass index 22.11 ± 0.97 kg/m2. Among the participants, 15 were right‐handed and 1 was left‐handed.

The changes in RMS values for four forearm muscles under different IHE protocols are presented in Figures 2, 3, 4, 5. Figure 2 shows the RMS values of the FDS during the first and last sets across four protocols (A, B, C, and D). In Protocol A, the RMS value significantly increased from 17.69 ± 1.68 mV in the first set to 19.85 ± 1.58 mV in the last set (p < 0.05). Similarly, Protocol B exhibited a significant increase, with RMS values rising from 19.31 ± 1.67 mV to 22.94 ± 2.25 mV (p < 0.05). Figure 3 highlights the changes in RMS of ECR during the first and last sets. While all protocols showed an upward trend in RMS values, only Protocols A and B demonstrated statistically significant increases (p < 0.05). Specifically, in Protocol A, the RMS value increased from 53.26 ± 6.53 mV to 65.47 ± 9.67 mV, and in Protocol B, it rose from 61.91 ± 7.67 mV to 75.50 ± 10.0 mV. Figure 4 presents the RMS of FCU. All protocols showed an increasing trend, but significant changes were observed only in Protocols A, B, and C (p < 0.05). In Protocol A, the RMS increased from 31.13 ± 4.13 mV to 37.59 ± 4.84 mV, in Protocol B from 38.03 ± 5.34 mV to 43.82 ± 5.61 mV, and in Protocol C from 27.01 ± 3.09 mV to 30.18 ± 3.3 mV. In contrast, Protocol D did not show a statistically significant change. Figure 5 illustrates the RMS of ECU. No statistically significant changes were observed across the protocols.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that the increased intensity (45% MVC vs. 30% MVC for four bouts of 2‐min IHE) resulted in a greater neuromuscular fatigue but a lower blood pressure reduction. The results also demonstrated that the longer duration (3 min vs. 2 min at 30% MVC for four bouts of IHE) resulted in the most significant blood pressure reduction and had moderate correlations between neuromuscular fatigue of ECR and FDS and both SBP and DBP reductions. A short duration of IHE (e.g., 3‐min isometric contraction with 2‐min rest for four bouts) can reduce blood pressure that may benefit wheelchair users who cannot participate in walking and jogging. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the influence of individual factors of IHE (i.e., the intensity, duration, and muscle mass) on neuromuscular fatigue and reductions in blood pressure and HR.

The results of the duration protocol (compared to the standard protocol) demonstrated a significantly greater blood pressure‐lowering effect, surpassing even those protocols involving higher intensity or greater muscle mass. The finding suggests that the duration factor of isometric exercise may be the most effective factor modulating post‐exercise blood pressure reduction. The results of this study further indicate that the longer duration results in significant reductions in both immediate and 10‐min post‐exercise SBP and DBP but not HR. This phenomenon may be attributed to the effect of IHE on improving endothelial function of resistance vessels but not on any heart rate variability indices [48]. These findings suggest that the acute effect of the duration protocol is unlikely to activate the cardiac sympathetic control significantly. This is supported by the results from the study conducted by Seals and Enoka indicating that muscle sympathetic nervous activity is associated with the increase in EMG during fatiguing exercise [36]. Specifically, during submaximal IHE (e.g., 30% MVC until exhaustion), muscle sympathetic nerve activity has been shown to correlate positively with muscle fatigue levels. Our study further revealed a moderate negative correlation between muscle fatigue and blood pressure reduction. Based on these results, we propose that local muscle fatigue associated with IHE promotes muscle sympathetic nervous activation for increasing local muscle blood flow and reducing vascular resistance, which ultimately leads to a reduction in blood pressure. Notably, under the similar intensity conditions, longer duration of isometric exercise involves not only the peripheral mechanism but also potentially the central mechanism [49]. Specifically, central fatigue mediated by group III/IV afferent nerves from the forearm muscles may further regulate blood pressure by evoking the motor pressor reflex for post‐exercise hypotension [41]. These findings collectively suggest that neuromuscular fatigue induced by longer duration IHE may involve both the peripheral and central mechanisms, resulting in a greater impact on blood pressure reductions.

The results of this study demonstrated that contraction intensity increased from 30% to 45% of MVC of four bouts of 2‐min IHE resulted in the most severe neuromuscular fatigue but the smallest reduction in blood pressure. This finding suggests that the contraction intensity of IHE may not be an effective driver of blood pressure changes. Bhambhani and colleagues reported similar findings about the intensity factor of isometric contraction from electromyographic responses at different intensities (e.g., 20%, 40%, and 60% MVC) and concluded that the intensity of isometric contraction may not significantly affect EMG RMS fatigue because the participants performed a fixed number of isometric contractions rather than until voluntary fatigue [35]. These observations align with the findings of this study indicating the relationship between the contraction intensity and neuromuscular fatigue is weak. The results can be explained by hemodynamic responses indicating that oxygenated hemoglobin levels of the contracting muscles decreased and deoxygenated hemoglobin levels increased. However, total hemoglobin remained stable within the range of 40%–60% MVC [35]. The steady total hemoglobin indicates that when peripheral blood flow surpasses a specific threshold, further increases in blood flow are restricted. At or above 40% MVC, elevated intramuscular pressure causes mechanical compression of intramuscular blood vessels. This vascular compression limits blood inflow to the muscles, even as muscle demand for oxygen increases, thereby constraining blood delivery to the contracting muscles [35]. At 60% MVC, the onset of fatigue was accompanied by a reduction in muscle oxygen supply, while cerebral blood flow and oxygenation remained stable. These findings collectively support that the effect of the contraction intensity of IHE on the fatigue mechanism is primarily attributed to the peripheral limitation (i.e., restricted oxygen delivery and utilization within the muscle) rather than central fatigue [49].

The results also demonstrated that during a bilateral IHE (30% MVC for four bouts of 2‐min IHE), muscle mass also exhibited a blood pressure‐lowering effect. Compared to other protocols, the muscle mass protocol exhibited minimal immediate changes in HR and BP, but a more significant decline in 10‐min post‐exercise changes. The results demonstrated a moderate positive correlation between ECR RMS fatigue and HR (r = 0.58), SBP (r = 0.52), and DBP (r = 0.51), suggesting that greater muscle fatigue may be associated with slower cardiovascular recovery. Lee and colleagues indicated that delayed recovery of HR and BP is related to autonomic regulation, potentially due to reduced vagal reactivation and sustained sympathetic activation immediately post‐exercise, leading to delayed cardiovascular recovery [50]. Furthermore, the author demonstrated that a reduction in total peripheral resistance (TPR), mediated by enhanced autonomic vasomotor control, may be the primary mechanism for BP reductions following isometric exercise training [51]. During isometric exercise involving larger muscle groups, a significant accumulation of metabolic byproducts, such as lactate, hydrogen ions, and potassium, occurs. These metabolic wastes can cause local vasoconstriction or increased vascular resistance. The clearance of these metabolic byproducts during the recovery phase requires more time, potentially leading to a slower reduction in TPR and consequently delayed BP recovery. Compared to the standard protocol, the muscle mass protocol induced a more significant decrease in blood pressure. Olher and colleagues have indicated that when larger muscle groups are engaged in isometric exercise, tissue oxygenation demands increase, requiring enhanced blood flow [32]. This physiological adaptation promotes the release of nitric oxide and enhances antioxidant responses, both of which act on the vascular system to improve vasodilation and reduce vascular resistance, ultimately leading to a decrease in blood pressure [32]. Additionally, based on the central command theory, the recruitment of larger muscle mass activates more motor units, which send signals to the cardiovascular control center in the brainstem [52]. It is thus reasonable to hypothesize that in isometric contraction tasks, the greater the number of motor units recruited by the skeletal muscle, the higher the degree of integration of these signals by central control neurons. This integration leads to more pronounced commands sent to the brainstem cardiovascular center, resulting in a stronger cardiovascular response following isometric exercise [52]. Notably, the EMG RMS values of the FDS and ECU muscle showed a gradual downward trend. This result aligns with the findings of Hikosaka and Aramaki, suggesting that bilateral handgrip exercise exhibited less fatigue accumulation compared to unilateral handgrip exercise due to the compensatory mechanism [42]. Nevertheless, the complex interplay between muscle mass and neuromuscular fatigue, and how they collectively influence blood pressure regulation, warrants further investigation.

Based on the previous findings from our laboratory [21], we further discovered that among the four specific forearm muscles (ECR, ECU, FCU, and FDS), the EMG RMS values indicating neuromuscular fatigue showed a decreasing trend from ECR to FDS. Notably, the duration protocol demonstrated the most significant association with blood pressure reduction. Through a comprehensive analysis of the four electromyographic indices across all experimental protocols, we observed that in the duration protocol, the EMG RMS fatigue levels of ECR and FDS showed the largest moderate correlations with blood pressure. These findings highlight the necessity of incorporating the specific neuromuscular fatigue mechanism into IHE training designed for blood pressure management. For duration exercise protocols, the specific fatigue states of the ECR and FDS were significantly associated with blood pressure reductions. This discovery provides a theoretical foundation for the development of exercise prescriptions aimed at lowering blood pressure, emphasizing the importance of precisely designing exercise protocols to induce targeted muscle fatigue for optimized blood pressure control. To further uncover the intrinsic relationships between various factors of IHE, neuromuscular fatigue, and blood pressure reduction, researchers should further explore various exercise protocols and the dose–response relationship between IHE modes and blood pressure regulation.

This study has several limitations. First, this study focused only on the acute changes in neuromuscular fatigue and blood pressure. It is usually accepted that the accumulation of acute response leads to long‐term adaptation. Because we intended to explore the influence of various factors (exercise intensity, duration, and muscle mass) associated with IHE, we assessed the acute blood pressure response. Based on these acute changes, we intended to identify potent factors for future longitudinal research. The long‐term effects of various modes of IHEs on neuromuscular fatigue and blood pressure adaptations remain to be investigated. Second, the study population was limited to healthy adults without hypertension; therefore, it is unclear whether the findings could be generalized to individuals with hypertension. Future studies should investigate the acute and long‐term neuromuscular fatigue responses and blood pressure changes in hypertensive patients. The sample size of this study was relatively small. Because we implemented a cross‐over study design, there was no control group in this study. Future study may need to test our protocols in a larger sample size and use a control group for comparison. Lastly, during the experiment, some participants could not maintain full contractions due to muscle fatigue, and slight relaxation led to an inability to sustain the required grip force for some protocols. This could affect the accuracy of the neuromuscular fatigue responses and blood pressure changes. To overcome this limitation, it is recommended to use intelligent handgrip devices equipped with real‐time force monitoring and feedback to improve the reliability and validity of the experimental design.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of the intensity, duration, and muscle mass factors of IHE on the relationship between neuromuscular fatigue and blood pressure changes. The results of this study demonstrated that 45% MVC of 2‐min isometric contraction for four bouts had a significant correlation with neuromuscular fatigue but minimal impact on blood pressure reduction compared to 30% MVC, indicating that the intensity factor of IHE is not the primary driving factor for blood pressure regulation. Notably, a longer duration (3 min at 30% MVC for four bouts) had the most significant correlation with blood pressure reduction compared to 2 min for four bouts, suggesting that the duration factor of IHE may be the most effective factor modulating blood pressure regulation. The results of this study also demonstrated the moderate correlation between neuromuscular fatigue of ECR and FDS and systolic and diastolic blood pressure reductions, indicating that targeted fatigue in certain muscles may improve blood pressure‐lowering effects. These findings highlight the duration factor and the primary muscles (ECR and FDS) of IHE are major factors for an effective exercise program using IHE.
