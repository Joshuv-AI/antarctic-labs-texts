Schrödinger bridge for generative AI: Soft-constrained formulation and convergence analysis In celebration of Prof. Xun Yu Zhou’s 60th birthday We thank helpful comments and discussions from Beatrice Acciaio, Rama Cont, Huyên Pham, Xin Zhang, and Xun Yu Zhou.
Jin Ma, Ying Tan, and Renyuan Xu

Abstract
Generative AI can be framed as the problem of learning a model that maps simple reference measures into complex data distributions, and it has recently found a strong connection to the classical theory of the Schrödinger bridge problems (SBPs) due partly to their common nature of interpolating between prescribed marginals via entropy-regularized stochastic dynamics. However, the classical SBP enforces hard terminal constraints, which often leads to instability in practical implementations, especially in high-dimensional or data-scarce regimes. To address this challenge, we follow the idea of the so-called soft-constrained Schrödinger bridge problem (SCSBP), in which the terminal constraint is replaced by a general penalty function. This relaxation leads to a more flexible stochastic control formulation of McKean–Vlasov type. We establish the existence of optimal solutions for all penalty levels and prove that, as the penalty grows, both the controls and value functions converge to those of the classical SBP at a linear rate. Our analysis builds on Doob’s h h -transform representations, the stability results of Schrödinger potentials, Γ \Gamma -convergence, and a novel fixed-point argument that couples an optimization problem over the space of measures with an auxiliary entropic optimal transport problem. These results not only provide the first quantitative convergence guarantees for soft-constrained bridges but also shed light on how penalty regularization enables robust generative modeling, fine-tuning, and transfer learning.

However, the classical SBP imposes hard terminal constraints on the marginal distributions, which can result in computational difficulties, instability in high-dimensional settings, and limited adaptability when aligning with empirical data in generative tasks. In practice, most numerical schemes for solving the SBP rely on iterative procedures that alternately relax the initial and terminal constraints. These algorithms can exhibit instability, particularly when the two constraints differ significantly, and their convergence guarantees for general target distributions remain an open problem in the literature.

In this paper, in light of Garg et al.

Mathematically, we consider a penalty function

1.1
Outline of the Main Results and Contributions

The soft-constrained Schrödinger bridge problem (SCSBP) studied in this paper replaces the terminal distribution constraint by a general penalty function, which leads to a McKean–Vlasov type stochastic control problem. The main results include the existence of the optimal solution to the SCSBP at each penalty level
k
, and the convergence of the solutions to the SCSBP to that of the SBP, in terms
of both the optimal policy and the corresponding value function, as
k\to\infty
.

More precisely, we begin with the special case where the initial measure is a delta measure. In this setting, we derive the explicit form of the optimal control policy for SCSBP via Doob’s
h
-transform (Proposition
3.6
) and more importantly, we establish a linear convergence rate for the optimal control (Theorem
4.1
). To the best of our knowledge, such a rate of convergence is novel in the literature. Moreover, by applying the so-called
early stopping
, we are able to obtain the linear convergence results for the corresponding value functions (Proposition
4.4
) as well as the Wasserstein distance between the target distribution and the output distribution of the SCSBP (Proposition
4.5
).

The similar results in the case of a general initial distribution is much more involved. Among other things, we establish and/or extend some recently observed stability results of the solutions to the SBP, as the foundation for a fixed-point argument. A key element in our argument is the continuous dependence (or stability) of a mapping that is well known in the (static) optimal transport literature. Specifically, fix
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and consider any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
7
]

-finite measures
(\nu_{0},\nu_{T}):={\cal T}(\mu)
such that the measure

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

\displaystyle\pi(E)=\int_{E}p(T,y;0,x)\nu_{0}(dx)\nu_{T}(dy),\qquad E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(1.1)

has the marginals
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
, where
p(\cdot,\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
is the transition density of a given diffusion process. Then it turns out that the solution to the SCSBP is the fixed-point of the mapping
\Gamma:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mu):={\argmin}_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{kG(\bar{\mu})+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\bar{\mu}}[\log\rho^{\mu}(X)]\right\},

(1.2)

where

\sigma

The successful application of Schauder’s fixed-point theorem on the Wasserstein space relies on several key elements, in particular a crucial continuous dependence result of the mapping

, and identify its solution to the measure

By utilizing some important stability results of the corresponding Schrödinger potentials (Proposition

As a direct consequence, we establish that the optimal control of the SCSBP converges linearly with respect to the penalty parameter

1.2
Closely Related Literature

Our general formulation is largely inspired by Garg et al.
[
29
]
, which investigates the SCSBP with the KL divergence as the penalty function
G
. Within that framework, the authors established the asymptotic convergence of the optimal policies as the penalty parameter
k
tends to infinity, under the assumption that the initial measure is a delta measure. However, the use of KL divergence presents practical difficulties: if the model distribution
\mu
assigns zero probability to any region where the data distribution
\mu_{\rm tar}
has positive mass, then
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{\rm tar}\|\mu)=+\infty
, rendering the divergence ill-posed under support mismatch
[
15
,
38
]
. Moreover, a delta initial measure is rarely employed in generative tasks, as it lacks the diversity and randomness required for effective training. Finally, no convergence rate is quantified therein.

On a technical level, our formulation is closely related to Hernández-Tangpi
[
35
]
, in which
the authors use a probabilistic approach to recast a mean-field Schrödinger bridge into a stochastic optimization problem with McKean-Vlasov dynamics, and connect the optimal control to a solution to a forward backward SDE of McKean-Vlasov type (MKV FBSDE). However, given the generality of the drift, diffusion, and running cost functions, the associated MKV FBSDE is derived without a discussion of uniqueness. In fact, it is not completely obvious that a McKean-Vlasov-type SBP can be converted to a McKean-Vlasov stochastic control problem via the usual Girsanov theorem argument, as we show in Remark
2.1
below. Moreover, the conditions imposed on the penalty function

G
are abstract and can be difficult to verify in common examples. By contrast, our framework leverages the PDE formulation and Doob’s
h
-transform representation, requiring only mild growth conditions on

G
and control of density gaps (see Assumption (
3.2
)). Several concrete examples of admissible

G
are provided in Example
3.4
and
3.5
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the necessary concepts and notations. In particular, we present the connection between the underlying SBP and its stochastic control formulation, and introduce the notion of the SCSBP together with some potential applications. Section
3
is devoted to the existence of optimal policies for the SCSBP at each penalty parameter
k
, while Section
4.1
establishes that the penalized optimal policies converge to those of the original SBP as
k\to\infty
, with a linear rate of convergence. In addition, we prove convergence of the corresponding value functions and quantify the distance between the terminal distribution and the target distribution in terms of the Wasserstein distance. Sections
5
and
6
are devoted to the case with a general initial distribution. A crucial stability result is established in Section
5
, via the stability of Schrödinger potentials of an auxiliary entropy optimal transport problem, and in Section
6
we complete the fixed-point argument.

k\in\mathbb{N}
, find
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}\in{\cal A}
such that
V^{k}:=J^{k}(\widehat{\alpha}^{k})=\inf_{\alpha\in{\cal A}}J^{k}(\alpha)
, where

\displaystyle J^{k}(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{s}|^{2}ds+kG({\mathbb{Q}_{X^{\alpha}_{T}}})\right],

(2.10)

and
G(\cdot)=G(\cdot;{\mu_{\rm tar}})
is the given penalty function satisfying Definition
2.3
and
\mathbb{Q}\sim\alpha
.
∎

Applications in Generative AI.

We remark that the SCSBP Problem
2.4

offers a general framework that can be applied to address multiple problems in generative AI. We briefly mention a few motivational examples.

Example 2.5

(Data generation)
.

The goal of generative AI is to train a data generation procedure using a finite number of iid. data samples
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}\}
under a (unknown) target distribution
{\mu_{\rm tar}}
, in order to simulate unlimited number of data samples whose underlying distribution is close to
{\mu_{\rm tar}}

[
58
,
36
,
33
]
.

To cast this problem into our framework, we can take, for example,
{\mu_{\rm ini}}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and
{\mu_{\rm tar}}=p_{\rm data}
in the theoretical framework (or
{\mu_{\rm tar}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
in the practical implementation). Then the optimal control
\widehat{\alpha}
of SCSBP leads to a controlled process
(X_{t}^{\widehat{\alpha}})_{0\leq t\leq T}
that simulates the data output
X_{T}^{\widehat{\alpha}}
. Our key results (see Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
6.4
below) show that the terminal measure
\mathbb{Q}_{X_{T}^{\widehat{\alpha}}}
is close to
{\mu_{\rm tar}}
, when
k
is sufficiently large.
∎

Example 2.6

(Fine-tuning under a reward signal)
.

Fine-tuning a diffusion model means taking a pre-trained model and training it further on a smaller, task-specific dataset so it learns to generate outputs more suited to that new context
[
60
,
70
,
62
,
34
]
. For example, a diffusion model trained on general images can be fine-tuned to generate a specific style (evaluated via a reward function). This process updates the model’s parameters just enough to adapt to the new data, without starting training from scratch.

In terms of our framework, we can consider (
2.3
) as a pre-trained model with the drift
b(t,x):=s_{\widehat{\theta}}(t,x)
being a well-trained score function, and
\widehat{\theta}
is the trained parameter. Note that, as the result of pre-training, the output measure
\mathbb{Q}_{X_{T}}
is sufficiently close to some data distribution
{\mu_{\rm tar}}
. We then introduce a fine-tuning procedure through a reference measure
p_{\rm ref}
with density
\frac{\exp(r(x))}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(r(x))dx}
, where
r:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a given reward function satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(r(x))dx<\infty
. Now replacing
{\mu_{\rm tar}}=p_{\rm ref}
, the optimal control
\widehat{\alpha}
of the corresponding SCSBP can then serve as the fine-tuning score function; and consequently, the new drift term
b(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})+\sigma(t)\widehat{\alpha}
acts as a combined score function.

Clearly, in this application the penalty parameter
k
should not be chosen too large; otherwise, the effect of the preference function may dominate the fidelity to the original data distribution. With an appropriately selected
k
, the resulting measure
\mathbb{Q}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T}}
not only reflects
p_{\rm data}
but also integrates the reward function
r
. In contrast, we remark that the classic SBP (
2.1
) is not capable of handling this application as it has a
pre-fixed
target distribution.
∎

Example 2.7

(Transfer learning)
.

Transfer learning is a machine learning approach where knowledge gained from a “source task” is reused to improve learning in a related but different “target task”
[
9
,
61
,
48
]
. In what follows we shall consider transfer learning in the context of data generation.

Let us consider a source task
(Y_{\rm sou})
, characterized by a distribution
p_{\rm sou}
, and a target task
(Y_{\rm tar})
with distribution
p_{\rm tar}
. Typically,
p_{\rm sou}
and
p_{\rm tar}
are assumed to be close under a certain divergence or distance function
G(p_{\rm tar};p_{\rm sou})
(assuming
G\geq 0
), such as the Wasserstein distance
[
48
]
.

To fit the transfer learning into our framework, we can take
{\mu_{\rm ini}}=p_{\rm sou}
,
{\mu_{\rm tar}}=p_{\rm tar}
, and set
b\equiv 0
for simplicity. In this case, if we choose
\alpha=0
, and
X_{0}\sim p_{\rm sou}
, then
X_{T}=X_{0}+W_{T}\sim p_{\rm sou}*\mathcal{N}(0,T\mathbb{I}_{d})
, where
\mathcal{N}(0,T{\mathbb{I}_{d}})=\mathbb{P}^{0}_{W_{T}}
and
\mathbb{I}_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix. Thus, denoting the optimal control by
\widehat{\alpha}
and noting that
\alpha\equiv 0
is sub-optimal, we must have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|^{2}ds\right]\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|^{2}ds+kG(\mathbb{Q}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T}})\right]\leq kG(p_{\rm sou}*\mathcal{N}(0,T{\mathbb{I}_{d}});p_{\rm tar}).

This implies that the optimal control
\widehat{\alpha}
has a small
L^{2}
-norm, indicating only minor adjustments are required during sampling—provided
k
is not too large.
∎

Next, we make the following assumptions on the penalty function

Assumption 3.2
.

(i) There exists some small constant

such that

(3.7)

(ii) There exist

\displaystyle|f_{\mu}(x)-f_{\rm tar}(x)|\leq C\phi(x)G(\mu),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.8)

Remark 3.3
.

(i) The function
G(\mu)
on the right hand side of (

) should read

, which essentially states that if

is close to

in

.

(ii) A slightly stronger consequence of (

]

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,{\mu_{\rm tar}})=\sup_{\varphi\in Lip(1)}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)(f_{\mu}(x)-f_{\rm tar}(x))dx\right\}\leq CG(\mu)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|x|)\phi(x)dx.

This suggests that
G(\mu)\sim 0
implies that
\mu
is close to

in the sense of

.

Before we proceed, let us give two examples that justify Assumption

.

Example 3.4
.

We consider the class of
\mu
and

such that

Clearly, Definition

\displaystyle\phi(x)\leq\frac{|f_{\mu}(x)-f_{\rm tar}(x)|}{c\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}|f_{\mu}(x)-f_{\rm tar}(x)|dx},

\mu
in the collection one may consider.

Another natural example of

satisfying Assumption
3.2
would be the Wasserstein distance or the KL divergence, augmented with a small “guardrail” term that enforces the uniform (weighted) pointwise control in (

Example 3.5
.

Consider the case that
{\mu_{\rm tar}}\in\mathscr{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

.
We define, for

\phi(x)=\exp(-\lambda|x-x_{0}|^{2})

\displaystyle G(\mu):=W_{2}(\mu,{\mu_{\rm tar}})+c\Big\|\frac{f_{\mu}-f_{\rm tar}}{\phi}\Big\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}.

Then, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle|f_{\mu}(x)-f_{\rm tar}(x)|\leq\Big\|\frac{f_{\mu}-f_{\rm tar}}{\phi}\Big\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\phi(x)\leq\frac{1}{c}\phi(x)G(\mu).

Thus (

\phi(x)e^{\lambda|x-x_{0}|^{2}}\leq C

Let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

Integrating against

\displaystyle\|\mu_{n}\|^{2+\varepsilon}\leq\|{\mu_{\rm tar}}\|^{2+\varepsilon}+M^{\prime}\int|x|^{2+\varepsilon}\phi(x)dx<\infty,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

This contradicts the fact that

{\mu_{\rm ini}}=\delta_{x_{0}}

To begin with, we first claim that for each

, there exists
\widehat{\mu}_{k}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle D_{k}(\widehat{\mu}_{k})=\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}D_{k}(\mu).

Indeed, let
X
be the solution to uncontrolled SDE (

be given such that

\displaystyle m:=D_{k}({\mu_{\rm tar}})=D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm tar}}\|\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}})+kG({\mu_{\rm tar}})=D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm tar}}\|\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}})<\infty.

Next, let us define, for fixed

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{k}:=\Big\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):D_{k}(\mu)\leq m\Big\}.

Clearly,

Therefore, in order to find the optimal control for Problem

4
Convergence Results under Delta Initial Distribution

To show the convergence of both policies and value functions, we first make the following observations. First, if we denote
g(x):=\frac{f_{\rm tar}(x)}{p(T,x;0,x_{0})}
and
\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|X_{t}=x]
, then by (
3.6
) we can write
h(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[g(X_{T})]
, where
X
is the solution to (
3.1
) with
X_{0}=x_{0}
.
By Feynman-Kac formula, we see that
h
satisfy the PDE:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h(t,x)+\mathscr{L}_{t}h(t,x)=0;\\
h(T,x)=g(x)=\frac{f_{\rm tar}(x)}{p(T,x;0,x_{0})},\end{cases}

(4.1)

where the infinitesimal generator
\mathscr{L}_{t}
is defined by
\mathscr{L}_{t}:=b(t,x)\cdot\nabla+\frac{1}{2}\Delta
. Similarly, we define
g_{k}(x):=\frac{f_{k}(x)}{p(T,x;0,x_{0})}
, then the function

h^{k}(t,x)
can also be represented as the solution of the PDE:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h^{k}(t,x)+\mathscr{L}_{t}h^{k}(t,x)=0;\\
h^{k}(T,x)=g_{k}(x)=\frac{f_{k}(x)}{p(T,x;0,x_{0})}.\end{cases}

(4.2)

Another useful observation is that for
\widehat{\mu}_{k}\in{\cal S}_{k}
, we have
kG(\widehat{\mu}_{k})\leq D_{k}(\widehat{\mu}_{k})\leq m
, or

G(\widehat{\mu}_{k})\leq m/k.

(4.3)

With the above observations, the key is to establish the convergence rate of
|h(t,x)-h^{k}(t,x)|
and
|\nabla h(t,x)-\nabla h^{k}(t,x)|
as
k
tends to infinity.

4.1
The Convergence of Optimal Policies

We shall now argue that the optimal controls for Problem
2.4
,
\{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(\cdot,\cdot)\}
, given by Proposition
3.6
, actually converges to the solution of the original SBP
\widehat{\alpha}(\cdot,\cdot)
given by Lemma
3.1
, and also establish its rate of convergence. More precisely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that the Assumption
3.2
is in force, and that
{\mu_{\rm ini}}=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Furthermore, assume that there exists constants
C,\delta>0
, such that
\delta\leq g(x),g_{k}(x)\leq C
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)
and
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)
,
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the optimal controls given in Lemma
3.1
and Proposition
3.6
, respectively. Then, it holds that

\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|dt\leq\frac{C}{k},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
C>0
is some constant independent of
k
.

Remark 4.2
.

We note that the assumption
\delta\leq g(x)=\frac{f_{\rm tar}(x)}{P(T,x;0,x_{0})}\leq C
(resp.
\delta\leq g_{k}(x)\leq C
) amounts to saying that
f_{\rm tar}(x)
(resp.
f_{k}(x)
)
\propto\,P(T,x;0,x_{0})
as
x\to\infty
, which is not particularly a stringent condition in light of the general estimate (
3.3
), and the arbitrariness of the sample data selection for the data generation procedure.
∎

Proof.

First, by definition
\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)=\nabla\log h(t,x)
and
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)=\nabla\log h^{k}(t,x)
, where
h^{k}
and
h
are the solution to (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), respectively, and
\nabla=\partial_{x}
. We can easily deduce that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\nabla\log h^{k}(t,x)-\nabla\log h(t,x)|=\left|\frac{\nabla h^{k}(t,x)}{h^{k}(t,x)}-\frac{\nabla h(t,x)}{h(t,x)}\right|

(4.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nabla h^{k}(t,x)h(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)h(t,x)+\nabla h(t,x)h(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)h^{k}(t,x)}{h^{k}(t,x)h(t,x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\nabla h^{k}(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)}{h^{k}(t,x)}\right|+|\nabla h(t,x)|\left|\frac{h(t,x)-h^{k}(t,x)}{h^{k}(t,x)h(t,x)}\right|=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

We now estimate
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, respectively. To this end we first apply the well-known Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula
[
8
,
24
]
(see also the representation formula in
[
27
,
43
]
) to get

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla h(t,x)=\partial_{x}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[g(X_{T})]=\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\big[g(X_{T})N_{T}\big],\\
\nabla h^{k}(t,x)=\partial_{x}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[g_{k}(X_{T})]=\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\big[g_{k}(X_{T})N_{T}\big],\end{array}\right.

where,

\displaystyle N_{s}=N^{t,x}_{s}:=\frac{1}{s-t}\int_{t}^{s}(\nabla X^{t,x}_{r})^{\top}dW_{r},\qquad s\in[t,T],

and
\nabla X=\nabla X^{t,x}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
-valued variational process satisfying the (random) ODE:

\displaystyle\partial_{x^{j}}X^{i}_{s}=\delta_{ij}+\int_{t}^{s}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\partial_{x^{\ell}}b^{i}(r,X_{r})\partial_{x^{j}}X^{\ell}_{r}dr,\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d,\quad s\in[t,T].

Furthermore,
one can easily check that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|\nabla X^{t,x}_{s}|^{2}\big]\leq Ce^{C(s-t)},\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[|N^{t,x}_{s}|^{2}\big]\leq{C\over s-t}e^{C(s-t)},\qquad 0\leq t\leq s\leq T.

Therefore, denoting
C>0
to be a generic constant that is allowed to vary from line to line, and applying Assumption
3.8
and estimate (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\nabla h^{k}(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)|&\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|g(X^{t,x}_{T})-g_{k}(X^{t,x}_{T})||N^{t,x}_{T}|\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{f_{k}(X^{t,x}_{T})-f_{\rm tar}(X^{t,x}_{T})}{p(T,X^{t,x}_{T};0,x_{0})}\Big||N^{t,x}_{T}|\Big]\\
&\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}[|N^{t,x}_{T}|^{2}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{f_{k}(X^{t,x}_{T})-f_{\rm tar}(X^{t,x}_{T})}{p(T,X^{t,x}_{T};0,x_{0})}\Big|^{2}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{Ce^{C(T-t)}}{\sqrt{T-t}}G(\widehat{\mu}_{k})\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{\phi(X^{t,x}_{T})}{p(T,X^{t,x}_{T};0,x_{0})}\Big|^{2}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{C}{k\sqrt{T-t}}.\end{split}

(4.8)

Next, we note that by assumption
\delta\leq g(x),g_{k}(x)\leq C
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by the weak maximum principle we conclude that as the solutions to the PDEs (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), respectively, it holds that
\delta\leq h(t,x),h^{k}(t,x)\leq C
, for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T)
.
Consequently, we have

I_{1}\leq\frac{C}{\delta k\sqrt{T-t}}\leq\frac{C}{k\sqrt{T-t}}.

(4.9)

Similarly, we can argue that
|\nabla h(t,x)|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T-t}}
, and that

\displaystyle|h^{k}(t,x)-h(t,x)|\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{f_{k}(X^{t,x}_{T})-f_{\rm tar}(X^{t,x}_{T})}{p(T,X^{t,x}_{T};0,x_{0})}\Big|\Big]\leq CG(\widehat{\mu}_{k})\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{\phi(X^{t,x}_{T})}{p(T,X^{t,x}_{T};0,x_{0})}\Big|\Big]\leq\frac{C}{k},

(4.10)

where the last inequality is due to (
4.3
) and hence
I_{2}\leq\frac{C}{k\sqrt{T-t}}
. This, together with (
4.9
) and (
4.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|\leq\frac{C}{k\sqrt{T-t}}

(4.11)

and hence convergence result :

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|dt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{C}{k\sqrt{T-t}}dt\leq\frac{C\sqrt{T}}{k},

proving the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

A particular example is when we take the penalty function
G(\mu)=D_{\rm KL}(\mu\|{\mu_{\rm tar}})
. In this case, it is known
(see, e.g.,
[
29
, Theorem 2]
) that the optimal control for (
2.9
)-(
2.10
) is given by

\widehat{\alpha}_{t}^{k}=\nabla\log h^{k}(X_{t}^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}},t)
, where

\displaystyle h^{k}(t,x)=d_{k}^{-1}\int p(T,z;t,x)\Big(\frac{f_{\rm tar}(z)}{p(T,z;0,x_{0})}\Big)^{\frac{k}{k+1}}dz,

with
d_{k}=\int f_{\rm tar}(x)^{\frac{k}{1+k}}p(x,T;\,x_{0},0)^{\frac{1}{1+k}}dx
. Consequently, Assumption
3.2
-(ii) can be reduced to that
\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\frac{f_{\rm tar}(X_{T})}{p(T,X_{T};0,x_{0})}\big|^{2}\Big]
is bounded (see Assumption
5.4
for similar conditions); and the linear rate of convergence can be proved with the same arguments.
∎

4.2
The Convergence of the Value Function

Having worked out the convergence analysis for the optimal controls, it is natural to extend the results to the convergence of value functions. However, the singularity at the terminal time
T
in (
4.11
) requires some technical care. It turns out that the popular notion of
early stopping
in diffusion models as well as the flow-based method literature
[
4
,
41
,
33
]
is exactly the remedy to this issue.

To be more precise, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce the following
\varepsilon
-value function.

\displaystyle J_{\varepsilon}(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}dt\right].

There are many practical reasons, mainly for computational purposes, to invoke the notion of early stopping, as elaborated in

[
4
,
41
,
33
]
. But on the other hand, it is clear that the
\varepsilon
-value function effectively excludes the singularity at the terminal time
T
. This leads to the following straightforward result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assume that all the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
are in force. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a generic constant
C:=C(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}})>0
, independent of
k
, such that

\displaystyle|J_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\alpha}^{k})-J_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\alpha})|\leq\frac{C}{k},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.12)

where
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}
and
\widehat{\alpha}
are the optimal controls in Theorem
4.1
, respectively.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}
and
\widehat{\alpha}
be the optimal controls in Theorem
4.1
, respectively. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, applying (
4.11
) we have

\displaystyle|J_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\alpha}^{k})-J_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\alpha})|\negthinspace

\displaystyle\negthinspace\leq\negthinspace

\displaystyle\negthinspace\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon}\big||\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|^{2}-|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|^{2}\big|ds\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon}\big|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}-\widehat{\alpha}_{s}\big|\big(|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|+|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|\big)ds\Big]

(4.13)

\displaystyle\negthinspace\leq\negthinspace

\displaystyle\negthinspace{\frac{C}{k}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\sqrt{T-s}}\big(|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|+|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|\big)ds\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{k\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|+|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|\big)\,ds\Big],

where
the last inequality is due to the fact that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{T-s}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
for
s\in[0,T-\varepsilon]
. To further bound (
4.13
), we recall that the definitions of
J(\cdot)
(
2.5
) and
J^{k}(\cdot)
(
2.10
),
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and define

\displaystyle V^{*}=J(\widehat{\alpha})=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}J(\alpha);\qquad V^{k,*}=J^{k}(\widehat{\alpha}^{k})=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}J^{k}(\alpha).

We should note that
X^{\widehat{\alpha}}
follows the constrained dynamics (
2.6
), whereas
X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}
follows the soft-constrained dynamics (
2.9
). Clearly, by definition (
2.10
) we have

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 1}J^{k}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{t}|^{2}\dd t\Big]&\textrm{ if }\,\,\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}^{\alpha}}={\mu_{\rm tar}}\\
\infty&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, since
\widehat{\alpha}
satisfies the constrained dynamics (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle V^{*}=J(\widehat{\alpha})=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{k\geq 1}J^{k}(\alpha)\geq\inf_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}J^{k}(\alpha)=J^{k}(\widehat{\alpha}^{k})=V^{k,*},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.14)

Consequently, we have, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a simple application of Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the fact (
4.14
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\big(|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|+|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|\big)ds\Big]\leq\frac{{\sqrt{T}}}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{s}|^{2}ds\Big]\Big)^{1/2}+\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}_{s}|^{2}ds\Big]\Big)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{T}\sqrt{V^{*}}.

(4.15)

Combining (
4.13
) and (
4.15
), we obtain (
4.12
).
∎

Besides the convergence of the value functions, another important convergence, that relies crucially on the convergence of the optimal controls, is the convergence of the terminal law
\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T}}
(with respect to the target distribution
{\mu_{\rm tar}}
), measured, for instance, in the Wasserstein distance. Again, to avoid the technicalities that the singularity at terminal time
T
might cause, we shall focus on the early stopped state
X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}
, which is a commonly used criterion in statistical estimation results for generative diffusion models (see, e.g.,
[
28
,
33
,
12
]
). More precisely, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let all assumptions in Theorem
4.1
be in force. Assume further that the optimal policy
\widehat{\alpha}
of the original SBP is Lipschitz in
x
: there exists
\kappa>0
, such that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,y)|\leq\kappa|x-y|,\qquad t\in[0,T].

(4.16)

Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on the Lipschitz constants
L
in Assumption
2.2
and
\kappa
in (
4.16
), but independent of
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle{W}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}},{\mu_{\rm tar}})\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\ln T-\ln\varepsilon}}{k}+C\varepsilon.

(4.17)

In particular, if we choose
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{k}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle{W}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}},{\mu_{\rm tar}})\leq\frac{C}{k}\big(\sqrt{\ln k}+\sqrt{\ln T})+1\big)=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\ln k}}{k}\Big).

(4.18)

Remark 4.6
.

(i) The linear (i.e.,
\sim\frac{1}{k}
) “closeness” between the law of the optimal state and
{\mu_{\rm tar}}
has appeared several times so far. For example, (
4.3
) implies that
G(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T}})=G(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T}};{\mu_{\rm tar}})\leq\frac{c}{k}
, and by
Remark
3.3
-(ii), this implies that
W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T}},{\mu_{\rm tar}})\sim\frac{1}{k}
. The result in (
4.18
) is in the same spirit, by under the stronger
{W}_{2}
-distance, but compensated by an early stopping.

(ii) The Lipschitz condition (
4.16
) for the optimal control
\widehat{\alpha}
is not unusual in the diffusion model literature (see, e.g.,
[
60
,
11
,
12
]
). In fact, this can be argued via regularity of the solution to the PDE (
4.1
) combined with the speed of decay of the density
f_{\rm tar}
, which can be assumed and analyzed rigorously (see Assumption
5.4
below). We therefore consider such an assumption non-stringent.
∎

[
Proof of Proposition
4.5
.]
First note that
X^{\widehat{\alpha}}
and
X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}
satisfy the following SDEs, respectively:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t}=[b(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})+\widehat{\alpha}_{t}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})]dt+dW_{t}\qquad\quad&X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{0}=x_{0};\\
dX^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t}=[b(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t})+\widehat{\alpha}_{t}^{k}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t})]dt+dW_{t},&X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{0}=x_{0}.\end{cases}

(4.19)

Let us now denote
\widehat{\alpha}_{t}(x)=\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)
,
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{t}(x)=\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)
, and define

b^{\widehat{\alpha}}(t,x)=b(t,x)+\widehat{\alpha}_{t}(x),\quad\Delta\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{t}(x)=\widehat{\alpha}^{k}_{t}(x)-\widehat{\alpha}_{t}(x),\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then we see that SDE (
4.19
) can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t}=b^{\widehat{\alpha}}(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})dt+dW_{t}\qquad\quad&X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{0}=x_{0};\\
dX^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t}=[b^{\widehat{\alpha}}(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t})+\Delta\widehat{\alpha}_{t}^{k}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t})]dt+dW_{t},&X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{0}=x_{0}.\end{cases}

That is,

\displaystyle X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}[b^{\widehat{\alpha}}(s,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{s})-b^{\widehat{\alpha}}(s,X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{s})+\Delta\widehat{\alpha}_{s}^{k}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{s})]ds,\qquad t\in[0,T]

Note that by Assumption
2.2
and (
4.16
),
b^{\widehat{\alpha}}
is uniform Lipschitz in
x
(with Lipschitz constant
L+\kappa
),
and applying the estimate (
4.11
), we deduce easily that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t}|^{2}]\leq 2T\int_{0}^{t}\Big[(L+\kappa)^{2}\mathbb{E}[|X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{s}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{s}|^{2}]ds+\frac{2c^{2}}{k^{2}}\ln\Big[\frac{T}{T-t}\Big].

(4.20)

In what follows let us denote
C>0
to be a generic constant depending only on
L
,
\kappa
,
c
, but independent of
k
, and we allow it to vary from line to line. Then, by a simple calculation using Gronwall’s inequality, we see that (
4.20
) lead to that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{t}|^{2}]\leq\frac{C}{k^{2}}(\ln T-\ln(T-t))e^{Ct},\qquad t\in[0,T).

(4.21)

Furthermore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, using the monotonicity of the log function we deduce from (
4.21
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}|^{2}]\leq\frac{C}{k^{2}}(\ln T-\ln\varepsilon)

It then follows that

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}})\leq\mathbb{E}[|X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}-X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}|^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{C}}{k}\sqrt{\ln T-\ln\varepsilon}.

Finally, since the function
b^{\widehat{\alpha}}=b+\widehat{\alpha}
is Lipschitz, by standard
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-continuity result of SDE, we have

\displaystyle{W}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T}})\leq C\varepsilon,

and consequently, noting that
\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T}}={\mu_{\rm tar}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{W}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}},{\mu_{\rm tar}})\leq W_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}_{T-\varepsilon}})+{W}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T-\varepsilon}},\mathbb{P}_{X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T}})\leq\frac{\sqrt{C(\ln T-\ln\varepsilon)}}{k}+C\varepsilon,

proving (
4.17
), whence the proposition.
∎

5
Stability of the Solutions to the SBP

We note that all the results in the previous section are based on an important assumption:
{\mu_{\rm ini}}=\delta_{x_{0}}
, for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this and the next section, we shall extend the results to more general initial condition
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with density
f_{\rm ini}
, and establish a similar rate of convergence.

We shall begin an important aspect in probability theory, which is the basis for the so-called stability issues of the solutions to the classic Schrödinger bridge problem. For notational convenience, we still denote
p(\cdot,\cdot;\cdot,\cdot)
to be the transition density of a standard
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued diffusion (
3.1
). We begin with the following well-known result in diffusion theory (cf. e.g.,
[
7
]
).

Proposition 5.1

(
[
7
]
)
.

For any
{\mu_{\rm ini}},\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists a unique pair of
\sigma
-finite measures
\nu_{0},\nu_{T}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

\displaystyle\pi(E)=\int_{E}p(T,y;0,x)\nu_{0}(dx)\nu_{T}(dy),\qquad E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

(5.1)

has marginals
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
. Furthermore,
\nu_{T}
and
\mu
(resp.
\nu_{0}
and
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
) are mutually absolutely continuous,
denoted by
\nu_{T}\simeq\mu
(resp.
\nu_{0}\simeq{\mu_{\rm ini}}
).
∎

Fix
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
apriori. Let us denote a (well-defined) mapping

{\cal T}:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
{\cal T}(\mu)=(\nu_{0},\nu_{T})
. Note that in Proposition
5.1
the measures
(\nu_{0},\nu_{T})
are only
\sigma
-finite in general, to facilitate our discussion, we shall consider, the following set:

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{{\mu_{\rm ini}}}:=\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):{\cal T}(\mu)\ll Leb(\cdot);\,{\cal T}(\mu)(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty\}.

Here
Leb(\cdot)
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We note that if
\mu\in\mathscr{D}_{{\mu_{\rm ini}}}
and
(\nu_{0},\nu_{T})={\cal T}(\mu)
, then

\nu_{T}
must have a density function, which we shall denote by
\rho^{\mu}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, we define an operator
S:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\displaystyle S[\mu](dy)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(T,y;0,x)\mu(dx)dy,\qquad\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(5.2)

Clearly, if
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
S[\mu](dy)=f_{X^{0,\mu}_{T}}(y)dy
, where
X^{0,\mu}=\{X^{0,\mu}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
denotes the solution to (
3.1
) with
X^{0,\mu}_{0}\sim\mu
.
But the operator
S
can be naturally extended to any
\mu\in\mathscr{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, provided the right-hand side of (
5.2
) is well-defined.

Let us now recall a well-known analogue of Lemma
3.1
in the case of general initial condition
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 5.2

(
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

Let
{\mu_{\rm ini}},{\mu_{\rm tar}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
(\nu_{0},\nu_{T})={\cal T}({\mu_{\rm tar}})
. Assume that
D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm ini}}\|\nu_{0})<\infty
and
D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm tar}}\|S[\nu_{0}])<\infty
. Then, the optimal control for the (original) SBP (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) is given by
\widehat{\alpha}_{t}=\nabla\log h(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})
where, denoting
\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(\cdot)
to be the density function of
\nu_{T}
,

\displaystyle h(t,x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(T,z;t,x)\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(z)dz.

(5.3)

Moreover, it holds that

J(\widehat{\alpha})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y){\mu_{\rm tar}}(dy)-D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm ini}}\|\nu_{0}).

(5.4)

We note that in the above
D_{\rm KL}({\mu_{\rm ini}}\|\nu_{0})=\int\log\frac{{\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)}{\nu_{0}(dx)}{\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)
(see footnote 1), and (
5.1
) implies that
\frac{{\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)}{\nu_{0}(dx)}=\int p(T,y;0,x)\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)dy
. Therefore (
5.4
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J(\widehat{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y){\mu_{\rm tar}}(dy)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(T,y;0,x)\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)dy\Big){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T})]-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log h(0,x){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)=\mathbb{E}\big[\log\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T})\big]-\mathbb{E}[\log h(0,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{0})].

Moreover, for a fixed
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
we define
h^{\mu}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(T,z;t,x)\rho^{\mu}(z)dz
. Then, we have the following result.

Lemma 5.3

(
[
29
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathscr{D}_{{\mu_{\rm ini}}}}
. Then, for any
\{\alpha_{t}\}\subset\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}^{0}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it holds that

\displaystyle J(\alpha)\geq\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\mu}(X^{\alpha}_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[\log h^{\mu}(0,X^{\alpha}_{0})].

The equality holds when
\alpha_{t}=\alpha^{\mu}_{t}=\nabla\log h^{\mu}(t,X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{t})
,
t\in[0,T]
and
X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{T}\sim\mu
.
∎

From Proposition
5.2
and Lemma
5.3
we see that the density function
\rho^{\mu}
plays an important role in the structure of the solution of SBP. We shall be particularly interested in the continuous dependence of
\rho^{\mu}:=\Gamma_{1}(\mu)
on
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which we shall refer to as the
Stability of the
SBP
, borrowing the well-known concept of the SBP theory (cf. e.g.,
[
46
,
22
,
10
]
).

To continue our discussion, we shall identify a set
\mathscr{E}\subset\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

on which an argument based on Schauder’s fixed-point theorem can be carried out. We begin by denoting

\displaystyle{\cal K}:=\Big\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\mbox{$\mu$ has density $f_{\mu}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}\Big\}.

Furthermore, we shall make use of the following assumption.

Assumption 5.4
.

(i) There exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that
|\log f_{\mu}(x)|\leq C|x|^{2}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(ii) There exists a function
g\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};(0,1])
, with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}g(x)ds<\infty
, and a constant
K>0
, independent of
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that
\Big\|\frac{f_{\mu}}{g^{2}}\Big\|_{\infty}\leq K
.

We shall consider the following two sets that will play a crucial role in our discussion.

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}:=\left\{\mu\in{\cal K}:\text{ Assumption \ref{assum:g} holds}\right\}\subset\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\quad{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}:=\left\{f_{\mu}:\mu\in\mathscr{E}\right\}\subset\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(5.5)

Remark 5.5
.

We note that Assumption
5.4
-(i) provides a uniform lower bound for the densities
f_{\mu}\in{\cal K}
, whereas Assumption
5.4
-(ii) controls the decay of
f_{\mu}\in{\cal K}
as
x\sim\infty
, hence mutually non-inclusive. A typical example of the function
g
is
e^{-c|x|^{2}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
c>0
.
In fact, in light of the estimate (
3.3
), such a property holds essentially for all transition probabilities of diffusion processes.
∎

The following lemma lists some basic properties of the set
\mathscr{E}
(or Assumption
5.4
).

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume that Assumption
5.4
is in force. Then it holds that

(i) The set
\{f_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(ii) The set
\{f_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}
is uniformly integrable in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in the sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}|x|^{2}f_{\mu}(x)dx=0.

(5.6)

(iii) If
\{\mu_{n}\}_{{n\geq 1}}\subset\mathscr{E}
such that
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
, as
n\to\infty
, then
\|f_{\mu_{n}}-f_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\to 0
.

Proof.

For any
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
, we note that
0<g(x)\leq 1
, and by assumption,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{\mu}(x)|^{2}dx\leq K^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(x)|^{4}ds\leq K^{2}\|g\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

That is
\{f_{\mu}\}_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}
is uniformly bounded (by
K\|g\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}
) in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, proving (i).

Similarly, for any
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
, by the absolute continuity of the integral we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}|x|^{2}f_{\mu}(x)dx\leq K\int_{\{|x|\geq R\}}|x|^{2}g(x)dx\to 0,\quad\mbox{\rm as $R\to\infty$,}

This proves (
5.6
), whence (ii).

The proof of part (iii) is slightly more involved, which is in the spirit of the so-called Scheffé’s theorem (cf.
[
59
]
).
We note that
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
amounts to saying that
f_{\mu_{n}}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightharpoonup}\limits^{w}}}f_{\mu}
, as
n\to\infty
, in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To show
f_{\mu_{n}}\to f_{\mu}
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we first consider, for each
m>0
, the smooth mollifiers
\varphi^{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi^{m}(z)dz=1
,
m\geq 1
, and denote

f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(x)=[\varphi^{m}*f_{\mu_{n}}](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi^{m}(x-z)f_{\mu_{n}}(z)dz;\quad f^{m}_{\mu}(x)=[\varphi^{m}*f_{\mu}](x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then it is clear that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(x)=f_{\mu_{n}}(x)
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}f^{m}_{\mu}(x)=f_{\mu}(x)
, for a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We should remark that the convergence is uniform in
n
. Indeed, by Assumption
5.4
and Dominated Convergence Theorem we have, as
m\to\infty
, for all
n\geq 0
,
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(x)-f_{\mu_{n}}(x)|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi^{m}(x-z)-\delta_{x}(z)|f_{\mu_{n}}(z)dz\leq K\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi^{m}(x-z)-\delta_{x}(z)|g^{2}(z)dz\to 0.

Furthermore, since
\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}|f_{\mu}|\leq Kg^{2}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by Dominated Convergence Theorem we have
\lim_{m\to\infty}f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}=f_{\mu_{n}}
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, uniformly for
n\geq 0
. That is, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists

M(\varepsilon)>0
, such that for all
n\geq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}-f_{\mu_{n}}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3};\quad\|f^{m}_{\mu}-f_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\quad\mbox{whenever $m>M$.}

(5.7)

In the sequel we fix
m>M(\varepsilon)
, and take a closer look at the sequence
\{f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}\}_{n\geq 1}
. Clearly, each
f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}
is still a density function, and it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{n}|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(y)|\leq\sup_{n}[\varphi^{m}*|f_{\mu_{n}}|](y)\leq K.

(5.8)

Moreover, since
\varphi^{m}
is continuous, thus for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem we have

\displaystyle|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(x+y)-f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi^{m}(x+y-z)-\varphi^{m}(x-z)||f_{\mu_{n}}(z)|dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi^{m}(z^{\prime}+y)-\varphi^{m}(z^{\prime})|dz^{\prime}\to 0,\quad\mbox{as $y\to 0$.}

Clearly, the convergence above is uniform in
n
. That is, the sequence
\{f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}\}_{\{n\geq 1\}}
is so-called
asymptotically equi-continuous
in the sense of Sweeting
[
59
]
. This, together with (
5.8
), implies that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}=f^{m}_{\mu}
, uniformly on compacts in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(cf.
[
59
, Theorem 1]
). Applying the Dominated Convergence Theorem again we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}-f^{m}_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}=0
. That is, for the given
\varepsilon>0
in (
5.7
), there exists
N>0
such that

\|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}-f^{m}_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
, whenever
n>N
. This, together with (
5.7
), yields

\|f_{\mu_{n}}-f_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\leq\|f_{\mu_{n}}-f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}+\|f^{m}_{\mu_{n}}-f^{m}_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}+\|f^{m}_{\mu}-f_{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}=\varepsilon,\quad n>N,

proving (iii), whence the Lemma.
∎

We are now ready to study our main stability result. More precisely, we shall argue that the mapping
\Gamma_{1}:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous. That is, that
\mu_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
weakly converges to some
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Prohorov metric would imply that
\rho^{\mu_{n}}
converges to
\rho^{\mu}
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Such a result, to the best of our knowledge, is novel in the literature.

To simplify our discussion, in what follows, we assume
T=1
. Recall that
\pi
in (
5.1
) has marginals
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
, and in what follows, we shall assume that
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
is fixed and
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
. Let us now consider the following
entropic optimal transport problem
:

I(\mu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi({\mu_{\rm ini}},\mu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\textbf{c}}(x,y)\pi(dxdy)+D_{\rm KL}(\pi\|{\mu_{\rm ini}}\otimes\mu),

(5.9)

where
\Pi({\mu_{\rm ini}},\mu)
is the set of all coupling probability measures
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
; and
{\textbf{c}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuous
cost function
. It is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
16
,
31
,
46
]
) that the minimization (
5.9
) admits a unique solution
\widehat{\pi}
, whose density takes the form:

\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)=\exp\big(-{\textbf{c}}(x,y)+\phi^{\mu}(x)+\psi^{\mu}(y)\big){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)\mu(dy),

(5.10)

where
\phi^{\mu},\psi^{\mu}
:
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are two measurable functions, often referred to as the
Schrödinger potentials
. It is clear that the pair
(\phi^{\mu},\psi^{\mu})
is unique up to an additive constant. That is, if
(\phi^{\mu},\psi^{\mu})
is a pair of Schrödinger potentials, then so is
(\phi^{\mu}+c,\psi^{\mu}-c)
. Furthermore, since both
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
are probability measures, we can easily choose a constant
c
so that
the following
symmetric normalization
holds:

\displaystyle\int\phi^{\mu}(x){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)=\int\psi^{\mu}(y)\mu(dy).

(5.11)

(Otherwise we take
c=\frac{1}{2}\big[-\int\phi^{\mu}(x){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)+\int\psi^{\mu}(y)\mu(dy)\big]
.) Note that under the symmetric normalization, the Schrödinger potentials is unique. The following stability result for the mappings
\mu\mapsto(\phi^{\mu},\psi^{\mu})
is crucial for our discussion.

Lemma 5.7

(
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Assume that the cost function
{\textbf{c}}(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists
C>0
depending only on
\|{\textbf{c}}\|_{\mathbb{C}^{k+1}}
, such that for all
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it holds that

\|(\phi^{\mu_{1}}-\phi^{\mu_{2}},\psi^{\mu_{1}}-\psi^{\mu_{2}})\|_{*}\leq CW_{2}(\mu_{1},\ \mu_{2}),

where
\|(\phi,\psi)\|_{*}:=\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\|\phi-c\|_{\mathbb{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\psi+c\|_{\mathbb{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right\}
.
∎

We now proceed to prove the main result of this section. To begin with, let us consider the entropic optimal transport problem (
5.9
) with
{\textbf{c}}(x,y):=-\log p(1,y;0,x)
,
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
p(s,y;t,x)
,
0\leq t<s\leq 1
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is the transition density of the diffusion (
3.1
). By (
5.10
), for fixed
{\mu_{\rm ini}},\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the unique solution for this entropic optimal transport problem is given by (see also
[
46
]
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}(dxdy)=p(1,y;0,x)e^{\phi^{\mu}(x)+\psi^{\mu}(y)}f_{\rm ini}(x)f_{\mu}(y)dxdy,

where
\phi^{\mu}
and
\psi^{\mu}
are the Schrödinger potentials, and we shall enforce the symmetric normalization so that
they satisfy (
5.11
). Since
\widehat{\pi}
has the marginals
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
and
\mu
, by the uniqueness of
(\nu_{0},\nu_{T})={\cal T}(\mu)
,
in Proposition
5.1
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle p(1,y;0,x)\rho_{0}(x)\rho^{\mu}(y)=p(1,y;0,x)e^{\phi^{\mu}(x)+\psi^{\mu}(y)}f_{\rm ini}(x)f_{\mu}(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\rho_{0}
is the density function of
\nu_{0}
.
An easy argument of separation of variables then yields that

\displaystyle\rho^{\mu}(y)=e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}f_{\mu}(y);\quad\rho_{0}(x)=e^{\phi^{\mu}(x)}f_{\rm ini}(x),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5.12)

Now note that the transition density
p(\cdot,\cdot\,;\cdot,\cdot)
is a classical solution to the Kolmogorov PDE. Thanks to Assumption
2.2
, we can assume without loss of generality that
{\textbf{c}}(\cdot,\cdot)=-\log p(1,\cdot\,;0,\cdot)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus
according to Lemma
5.7
and noting the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{*}
, we see that, modulo some constant normalization, we have that the
Schrödinger potential
(\phi^{\mu_{n}},\psi^{\mu_{n}})
itself satisfies the estimate:

\|{\phi}^{\mu_{n}}-\phi^{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}+\|{\psi}^{\mu_{n}}-\psi^{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\leq CW_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu).

(5.13)

Here in the above the constant
C>0
depending only on
\|{\textbf{c}}\|_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
, but independent of
n
.

Furthermore, we note that
{\textbf{c}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
also lead to the following
a priori
estimate of the Schrödinger potential (see, e.g.,
[
46
, Lemma 2.1]
):

\displaystyle\psi^{\mu}(y)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\textbf{c}}(x,y){\mu_{\rm ini}}(dx)=:\xi(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5.14)

Recall the fundamental estimate (
3.3
) and the definition of
{\textbf{c}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, it is readily seen that
|\xi(y)|\sim\lambda^{\prime}|y|^{2}
, as
y\to\infty
, for some constant
\lambda^{\prime}>0
depending only on the coefficient
b(\cdot,\cdot)
in SDE(
3.1
). In light of Remark
5.5
and
[
46
, Lemma 2.1]
, in what follows we shall impose some explicit assumptions on the Schrödinger potentials
\{\psi^{\mu}(\cdot)\}_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}
and their upper bound
\xi(\cdot)
in (
5.14
), which are in line with Assumption
5.4
.

Assumption 5.8
.

(i) There exists a constant
C>0
, such that for any
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|\psi^{\mu}(y)|\leq C|y|^{2},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(5.15)

(ii) In Assumption
5.4
, the control function
g
satisfies

\displaystyle\eta(\cdot):=e^{\xi(\cdot)}g^{2}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(5.16)

Now for any
f_{\mu}\in{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}
, by Assumption
5.4
and (
5.14
) we have

0\leq\rho^{\mu}(y)=e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}\ f_{\mu}(y)\leq e^{\xi(y)}f_{\mu}(y)\leq e^{\xi(y)}Kg^{2}(y)=K\eta(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Consequently, we conclude that
\rho^{\mu}\in\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
, thanks to (
5.16
).

Bearing the above discussion in mind, we are now ready to present the main result of this section.

Proposition 5.9
.

Assume that Assumptions
2.2
and
5.4
are in force. Assume further that
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathscr{E}
and
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
in Prohorov metric. Then
\|\rho^{\mu_{n}}-\rho^{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}=\|\Gamma_{1}(\mu_{n})-\Gamma_{1}(\mu)\|_{\mathbb{L}^{1}}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Assume
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathscr{E}
, and
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
, in Prohorov metric. By Lemma
5.6
-(ii),
\{\mu_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
, thanks to Assumption
5.4
, and thus by the relationship between Wasserstein distance and Prohorov metric (see,
[
65
, Theorem 7.12]
), we have
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Thus, if follows from (
5.13
) that
\|\psi^{\mu_{n}}-\psi^{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

Next, for each
\mu_{n}\in\mathscr{E}
,
n\geq 1
, and
\mu
, we apply (
5.12
) and write

\displaystyle\rho^{\mu_{n}}(y)=e^{\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)}f_{\mu_{n}}(y),\quad\rho^{\mu}(y)=e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}f_{\mu}(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Therefore, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle|\rho^{\mu}(y)-\rho^{\mu_{n}}(y)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big|e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}f_{\mu}(y)-e^{\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)}f_{\mu_{n}}(y)\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big|e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}-e^{\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)}\big|f_{\mu_{n}}(y)+e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}\big|f_{\mu_{n}}(y)-f_{\mu}(y)\big|=:I^{1}_{n}(y)+I_{n}^{2}(y),

where
I^{i}_{n}
,
i=1,2
are defined in an obvious way. It then suffices to show that both
I^{1}_{n}
and
I^{2}_{n}\to 0
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}
, as
n\to\infty
.

To this end, we first recall that
\|\psi^{\mu_{n}}-\psi^{\mu}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{\infty}}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
. Hence there exists
N>0
, such that
\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)\leq\psi^{\mu}(y)+1
, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whenever
n\geq N
.
Thus, for
n\geq N
, we have

0\leq I_{n}^{1}(y)\leq\big(\big|e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)}\big|+\big|e^{\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)}\big|\big)f_{\mu_{n}}(y)\leq 2e^{\psi^{\mu}(y)+1}f_{\mu_{n}}(y)\leq 2e\cdot e^{\xi(y)}g^{2}(y)=2e\eta(y).

Here in the above, the last inequality holds due to Assumption
5.4
and (
5.16
). Since
\eta\in\mathbb{L}^{1}
by (
5.16
), the Dominated Convergence Theorem implies that
I_{n}^{1}(\cdot)
converges to
0
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
, because
{\psi^{\mu_{n}}}
converges uniformly to
\psi^{\mu}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Finally, since

I_{n}^{2}(y)\leq 2K\eta(y)
, and
f_{\mu_{n}}\to f_{\mu}
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, thanks to Lemma
5.6
-(iii), we can apply Dominated Convergence again to get
I_{n}^{2}(\cdot)
converges to 0 in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, as
n\to\infty
, proving the proposition.
∎

6
Existence of optimal control and convergence for general
{\mu_{\rm ini}}

In this section, we shall extend the results of Section
3
and show that the Problem
2.4
has solution for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
when
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
is an arbitrary distribution with density
f_{\rm ini}
in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To be more precise, for fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
J^{k}(\alpha)
be the cost functional in Problem
2.4
. Applying Lemma
5.3
, for any
\mu\in\mathscr{D}_{\mu_{\rm ini}}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}J^{k}(\alpha)&=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|\alpha_{s}|^{2}ds+kG(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\alpha}_{T}};{\mu_{\rm tar}})\Big]\\
&\geq kG(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\alpha}_{T}};{\mu_{\rm tar}})+\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\mu}(X^{\alpha}_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[\log h^{\mu}(0,X^{\alpha}_{0})]\end{split}

(6.1)

and the equality holds when
\alpha_{t}=\alpha^{\mu}_{t}=\nabla\log h^{\mu}(t,X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{t})
and
X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{T}\sim\mu
.
Our main goal of this
section is to determine the probability measure
\widehat{\mu}
, such that
\widehat{\alpha}_{t}=\nabla\log h^{\widehat{\mu}}(t,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{t})
is the optimal control to Problem
2.4
, where
h^{\widehat{\mu}}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\rho^{\widehat{\mu}}(X_{T})]
.

We now give the heuristic idea of the construction of "solution mapping"
\Gamma
. Let
{\mu_{\rm ini}}
be given. For any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
first apply Lemma
5.3
to get the feedback control

\alpha^{\mu}_{t}=\nabla\log h^{\mu}(t,X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{t})
so that
\mathbb{P}_{X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{T}}=\mu
and

\displaystyle J(\alpha^{\mu})=\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\mu}(X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[\log h^{\mu}(0,X^{\alpha^{\mu}}_{0})].

(6.2)

In what follows we fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. To find the
\widehat{\mu}^{k}
such that
J^{k}(\alpha^{\widehat{\mu}^{k}})=\inf J^{k}(\alpha)
, we consider a mapping:

\Gamma_{2}:\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\mu})=\mu^{\prime}
where

\mu^{\prime}=\argmin_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Big\{kG(\bar{\mu})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu}(y)\bar{\mu}(dy)\Big\}.

(6.3)

Finally, we define
\Gamma=\Gamma_{2}\circ\Gamma_{1}:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and we shall argue that the mapping
\Gamma
has a fixed point
\widehat{\mu}\in\mathscr{E}
, where
\mathscr{E}
is defined by (
5.5
). Clearly, if
\Gamma(\widehat{\mu})=\widehat{\mu}
, then we can still define
\widehat{\alpha}=\alpha^{\widehat{\mu}}
, and by Lemma
5.3
we have
\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}^{\alpha^{\widehat{\mu}}}}=\widehat{\mu}
. Thus by (
6.2
), for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}^{2}_{\mathbb{F}^{0}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle J^{k}(\widehat{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J(\widehat{\alpha})+kG(\widehat{\mu})=kG(\widehat{\mu})+\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\widehat{\mu}}(X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[\log h^{\widehat{\mu}}(0,X^{\widehat{\alpha}}_{0})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle kG(\mathbb{P}_{X^{\alpha}_{T}})+\mathbb{E}[\log\rho^{\widehat{\mu}}(X^{\alpha}_{T})]-\mathbb{E}[\log h^{\widehat{\mu}}(0,X^{\alpha}_{0})]\leq J^{k}(\alpha).

Here in the above the first inequality is due to (
6.3
), and the last inequality is due to (
6.1
). This shows that
\widehat{\alpha}
is the minimizer of
J^{k}(\cdot)
.

We now show that the set
\mathscr{E}\subset\mathscr{D}_{{\mu_{\rm ini}}}
defined by (
5.5
) satisfies all the necessary properties, thanks to the Lemma
5.6
and Proposition
5.9
that we established in the last section, so that the mapping
\Gamma
possesses a fixed point on
\mathscr{E}
by Schauder’s fixed-point theorem.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that Assumptions

5.4

is in force. Consider the set
\mathscr{E}
defined by (
5.5
). Then the following hold:

(i)
\mathscr{E}
is convex and closed under Prohorov metric, and
{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}
is convex and closed in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
;

(ii)
\Gamma(\mathscr{E})\subseteq\mathscr{E}
, and is precompact in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, under both Prohorov and Wasserstein metric;

(iii)
\Gamma
is continuous on
\mathscr{E}
, under Prohorov metric.

Consequently, the mapping
\Gamma
has a fixed point in
\mathscr{E}
.

Proof.

Since the last statement is a direct consequence of Schauder’s fixed point theorem, applying to the space
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with Prohorov metric, we need only prove the properties (i)-(iii).

(i) is obvious.

(ii) By definition of
{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}
we rewrite (
6.3
) as

f_{\mu^{\prime}}=\argmin_{f_{\bar{\mu}}\in{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}}\Big\{kG({\bar{\mu}})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu}(y)f_{\bar{\mu}}(y)dy\Big\}.

Since
{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}
is convex and closed in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it follows that
f_{\mu^{\prime}}\in{\cal S}_{\mathscr{E}}
, and thus
\Gamma(\mathscr{E})\subseteq\mathscr{E}
.
We are to show that
\Gamma(\mathscr{E})
is precompact in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

To this end, let
\{\mu_{n}\}\subseteq\Gamma(\mathscr{E})
be any sequence, we shall find a subsequence
\{\mu_{n_{k}}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{n_{k}}=\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, under both Prohorov metric and
W_{2}
-metric. Since
\Gamma(\mathscr{E})\subseteq\mathscr{E}
, by Lemma
5.6
-(i),
\{f_{\mu_{n}}\}
is bounded in

\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Thus by Banach-Alaoglu Theorem and noting that
\mathbb{L}^{2}
is reflexive,
\{f_{\mu_{n}}\}
is weakly compact, that is, there exists a subsequence
\{f_{\mu_{n_{k}}}\}
such that
f_{\mu_{n_{k}}}\mathop{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\rightharpoonup}\limits^{w}}}f_{\mu}
in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, as
k\to\infty
. But this amounts to
saying the
\mu_{n_{k}}\Rightarrow\mu
in Prohorov metric. This, together with Lemma
5.6
-(ii) and the relationship between Wasserstein distance and weak convergence (see, e.g.,
[
65
, Theorem 7.12]
), leads to that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mu_{n_{k}}=\mu

in
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, proving (ii).

(iii) Let us assume that
\{\mu_{n}\}\subset\mathscr{E}
such that
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
in Prohorov metric.
The stability result in Proposition
5.9
shows that
\rho^{\mu_{n}}=\Gamma_{1}(\mu_{n})\to\Gamma_{1}(\mu)=\rho^{\mu}\in{\cal S}
in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Next, we show that
\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\mu_{n}})\Rightarrow\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\mu})
in Prohorov metric. Recall the definition of
\Gamma_{2}
, we define a family of
functionals on
\mathscr{E}
: for each
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bar{\mu}\in\mathscr{E}
,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle F^{k}_{n}(\bar{\mu}):=kG(\bar{\mu})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu_{n}}(y)\bar{\mu}(dy);\\
\displaystyle F^{k}(\bar{\mu}):=kG(\bar{\mu})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu}(y)\bar{\mu}(dy).\end{array}\right.

Then
\mu_{n}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\mu_{n}}):=\argmin_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathscr{E}}F^{k}_{n}(\bar{\mu})
and
\mu^{\prime}=\Gamma_{2}(\rho^{\mu}):=\argmin_{\bar{\mu}\in\mathscr{E}}F^{k}(\bar{\mu})
.

To show that the minimizers
\mu^{\prime}_{n}\Rightarrow\mu^{\prime}
, we shall invoke
the notion of
\Gamma
-convergence (cf.
[
19
]
). To be more precise, to prove the sequence
\{F^{k}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

\Gamma
-converge to

F^{k}
as
n\to\infty
, it is sufficient to show

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mbox{For every sequence $\bar{\mu}_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{\mu}$, it holds that $\displaystyle F^{k}(\bar{\mu})\leq\mathop{\underline{\rm lim}}_{n}F^{k}_{n}(\bar{\mu}_{n})$};\\
\mbox{There exists a sequence $\bar{\mu}_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{\mu}$, such that $\displaystyle F^{k}(\bar{\mu})\geq\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{n}F^{k}_{n}(\bar{\mu}_{n})$.}\end{array}\right.

Now, note that
G
is convex and
\mathscr{E}
is compact under Prohorov metric, we see that both
\{F^{k}_{n}\}
and
F^{k}
are
coercive
(in the sense that there exists minimizing sequence in
\mathscr{E}\subset\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). Thus, in light of the
\Gamma
-convergence result (see
[
19
, Theorem 7.1]
), in order to show
\mu_{n}^{\prime}\Rightarrow\mu^{\prime}
, it suffices to check that
\{F^{k}_{n}\}

\Gamma
-converges to
F^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. To see this, let
\{\bar{\mu}_{n}\}\subset\mathscr{E}
such that

\bar{\mu}_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{\mu}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\log\rho^{\mu_{n}}(y)\bar{\mu}_{n}(dy)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\rho^{\mu}(y)\bar{\mu}(dy)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\log\rho^{\mu_{n}}(y)-\log\rho^{\mu}(y)|f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}(y)(dy)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\log\rho^{\mu}(y)||f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}(y)-f_{\bar{\mu}}(y)|dy=:I_{1}+I_{2},

where
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are the two integrals on the right side above. We now prove that both
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
converge to 0, as
n\to\infty
. To see this, first note that by (
5.12
) we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}|\psi^{\mu_{n}}(y)-\psi^{\mu}(y)|f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}(y)}dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}|\log f_{\mu_{n}}(y)-\log f_{\mu}(y)|f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}(y)}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}(y)}dy+K\int_{\mathbb{R}_{d}}|\log f_{\mu_{n}}(y)-\log f_{\mu}(y)|g(y)dy=:I_{11}+I_{12},

where the last inequality is due to (
5.13
) and Assumption
5.4
. Since
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
, again by Lemma
5.6
-(ii) and
[
65
, Theorem 7.12]
, we have
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
. Hence
I_{11}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, by Assumption
5.4
-(i) we see that, for
\mu\in\mathscr{E}
, one has
|\log f_{\mu}(y)|\leq C|y|^{2}
for some constant
C>0
, that is,
|\log f_{\mu_{n}}(y)-\log f_{\mu}(y)|g(y)\leq 2C|y|^{2}g(y)
for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. On the other hand, applying Lemma
5.6
-(iii), we have
f_{{\mu}_{n}}\to f_{{\mu}}
in

\mathbb{L}^{1}
. Thus, possibly along a subsequence (may assume itself, by virtue of Heine’s Lemma), it holds
\log f_{{\mu}_{n}}(y)\to\log f_{{\mu}}(y)
, a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, thanks to the continuity of
\log
function. Therefore, by Assumption
5.4
and Dominated Convergence Theorem, we see that
I_{12}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, whence
I_{1}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Similarly, by (
5.12
), (
5.15
), and Assumption
5.4
-(i), we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\psi^{\mu}(y)||f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}(y)-f_{\bar{\mu}}(y)|dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\log f_{\mu}(y)||f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}(y)-f_{\bar{\mu}}(y)|dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|y|^{2}|f_{\bar{\mu}_{n}}(y)-f_{\bar{\mu}}(y)|dy\to 0,\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$},

again thanks to Assumption
5.4
-(ii) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem. Finally, since
G(\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by definition (
6
) we see that
F^{k}_{n}(\bar{\mu}_{n})\to F^{k}(\bar{\mu})
whenever
\bar{\mu}_{n}\Rightarrow\bar{\mu}
. Thus, by (
6
),
\{F^{k}_{n}\}

\Gamma
-converges to
F^{k}
, as
n\to\infty
. The proof is now complete.
∎

Finally, we shall establish an
analogue of Theorem
4.1
in the case of general
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For technical convenience, in what follows we shall make use of the following extra assumptions to facilitate our discussion. Recall the function
\xi
and
\eta
defined by (
5.14
) and (
5.16
), respectively.

Assumption 6.2
.

(i) The penalty function
G
satisfies
G(\mu;\mu_{\rm tar})\geq W_{2}(\mu,\mu_{\rm tar})
;

(ii) In Assumption
3.2
-(ii), the function
\phi
satisfies
\|\phi(\cdot)e^{\xi(\cdot)}\|_{\infty}<\infty
;

(iii) For any
R>0
, there exists
M_{R}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta^{2}(X^{t,x}_{T})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta^{2}(y)p(T,y;t,x)dy\leq M_{R},\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R},

(6.10)

where
B_{R}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|\leq R\}
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

(1) Assumption
6.2
-(i) is not overly restrictive, and can be justified by Example
3.5
.

(2) Note that the function
\phi
in Assumption
3.2
-(ii) satisfies
\phi(y)e^{\lambda|y|^{2}}\leq C
, for some
c>0
and
\xi(y)\sim c|y|^{2}
, as
|y|\to\infty
, Assumption
6.2
-(ii) amounts to saying that
\phi
and
\xi
are compatible.

(3) While Assumption
6.2
-(iii) is slightly stronger than the requirement (
5.16
), it would be trivial if the mapping
(t,x)\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\eta^{2}(X^{t,x}_{T})]
is continuous, which is by no means stringent.
∎

Our last result of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that the Assumptions
3.2
,
5.4
and
6.2
are in force. Let
{\mu_{\rm ini}}\in\mathscr{E}
be given, and
let
\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)
and
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)
,
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
are optimal controls given in Proposition
3.6
and Lemma
5.3
, respectively. Then, for any
R>0
, there exists
C_{R}>0
, such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|dt\leq\frac{C_{R}}{k},\qquad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}.

(6.11)

Proof.

We begin by denoting
\mu_{k}:=\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}^{\widehat{\alpha}^{k}}}
,
{\mu_{\rm tar}}:=\mathbb{P}_{X_{T}^{\widehat{\alpha}}}
, and let
\rho^{\mu_{k}}=\Gamma_{1}(\mu_{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}=\Gamma_{1}({\mu_{\rm tar}})
, respectively, as we defined before. Next, applying (
5.12
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\rho^{\mu_{k}}(y)-\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)|&=|e^{\psi^{\mu_{k}}(y)}f_{\mu_{k}}(y)-e^{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}f_{\rm tar}(y)|\\
&\leq e^{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}|f_{\mu_{k}}(y)-f_{\rm tar}(y)|+f_{\mu_{k}}(y)|e^{\psi^{\mu_{k}}(y)}-e^{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}|.\end{split}

(6.12)

Let us now recall some facts from Section
4.1
. First, note that the optimality of
\mu_{k}
implies that
G(\mu_{k})\leq\frac{C}{k}
(cf. (
4.3
)), for some generic constant
C>0
independent of
k
, which we shall allow to vary from line to line below. Thus, by virtue of Assumption
3.2
-(ii), we can write

|f_{\mu_{k}}(y)-f_{\rm tar}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{k}\phi(y),\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\phi(y)e^{\xi(y)}\leq C
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, thanks to Assumption
6.2
-(ii). Furthermore, under Assumption
6.2
-(i), we can assume without loss of generality that the Schrödinger potentials
\psi^{\mu_{k}}
and
\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}
all satisfy estimates (
5.13
) and (
5.14
). Consequently, by Assumption
6.2
-(i) and an easy application of Lemma
5.7
and Newton-Leibniz formula we have

\displaystyle|e^{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}-e^{\psi^{\mu_{k}}(y)}|

\displaystyle=|{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}-{\psi^{\mu_{k}}(y)}|\int_{0}^{1}\exp\{\psi^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)+\theta(\psi^{\mu_{k}}(y)-\psi^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)\}d\theta

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{\xi(y)}W_{2}(\mu_{k},{\mu_{\rm tar}})\leq Ce^{\xi(y)}G(\mu_{k})\leq\frac{C}{k}e^{\xi(y)},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Summarizing above and recalling Assumption
5.4
and (
5.16
), we derive from (
6.12
) that

\displaystyle|\rho^{\mu_{k}}(y)-\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{k}\left(e^{\psi^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(y)}\phi(y)+f_{\mu_{k}}(y)e^{\xi(y)}\right)\leq\frac{C}{k}\left(e^{\xi(y)}\phi(y)+g^{2}(y)e^{\xi(y)}\right)\leq\frac{C}{k}(1+\eta(y)),

and therefore, given
R>0
, and
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}
, we apply Assumption
6.2
-(iii) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\rho^{\mu_{k}}(X^{t,x}_{T})-\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(X^{t,x}_{T})|]\leq\mathbb{E}[|\rho^{\mu_{k}}(X^{t,x}_{T})-\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(X^{t,x}_{T})|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{C_{R}}{k},

(6.13)

where
C_{R}>0
is some constant depending on the generic constant
C
above and
M_{R}
in (
6.10
).

To complete the proof, let us recall that optimal strategies are of the form
\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)=\nabla\log h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)=\nabla\log h(t,x)
, and
h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)
and
h(t,x)
are the solutions to the respective PDEs:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)+\mathscr{L}_{t}h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)=0;\\
h^{\mu_{k}}(T,x)=\rho^{\mu_{k}}(x).\end{cases}\quad\quad\begin{cases}\partial_{t}h(t,x)+\mathscr{L}_{t}h(t,x)=0;\\
h(T,x)=\rho^{\mu_{\rm tar}}(x),\end{cases}

(6.14)

Furthermore, noting that
h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{\mu_{k}}(X^{t,x}_{T})]
,
h(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(X^{t,x}_{T})]
, and by the Bismut-Elworthy-Li formula we have

\nabla h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{\mu_{k}}(X^{t,x}_{T})N^{t,x}_{T}];\quad\nabla h(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\rho^{{\mu_{\rm tar}}}(X^{t,x}_{T})N^{t,x}_{T}].

Thus, we have whenever
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}
,

|\nabla h(t,x)|\leq K\mathbb{E}[\eta^{2}(X^{t,x}_{T})]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[|N^{t,x}_{T}|]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{C_{R}}{\sqrt{T-t}},

and a similar argument as in (
4.8
) and (
4.10
), together with the estimate (
6.13
), leads to that

\displaystyle|\nabla h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)|\leq\frac{C_{R}}{k\sqrt{T-t}};\quad|h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)-h(t,x)|\leq\frac{C_{R}}{k},\quad(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}.

(6.15)

Finally, we note that by definition the function
h
is positive everywhere, and being the classical solution to the PDE (
6.14
) it is continuous. Thus, given
R>0
, there exists
\delta_{R}
>0, such that
h(t,x)\geq\delta_{R}
, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}
. Since (
6.15
) implies that
h^{\mu_{k}}
converges to
h
uniformly on compacts, thus it must hold that

h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)\geq\delta_{R}/2
, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}
, and
k
large enough. We therefore conclude,
similar to (
4.4
), that

\displaystyle|\widehat{\alpha}^{k}(t,x)-\widehat{\alpha}(t,x)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\nabla h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)-\nabla h(t,x)}{h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)}\right|+|\nabla h(t,x)|\left|\frac{h(t,x)-h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)}{h^{\mu_{k}}(t,x)h(t,x)}\right|\leq\frac{C_{R}}{k\sqrt{T-t}},

as
k\to\infty
, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times B_{R}
, where
C_{R}
depends on
M_{R}
and
\delta_{R}
above, but independent of
k
. Integrating in
t
we obtain (
6.11
).
∎

7
Conclusion

We study the soft-constrained Schrödinger bridge problem (SCSBP) as a flexible alternative to the classical formulation for generative modeling. By replacing hard terminal constraints with a general penalty function, the SCSBP potentially offers greater flexibility and stability for generative AI tasks. Moreover, we establish linear convergence of both the value functions and the optimal controls as the penalty parameter tends to infinity, thereby providing a theoretical guarantee for the framework.

In future work, we will develop efficient algorithms for learning the SCSBP solutions and test the performance on benchmark generative AI tasks. This will allow us to translate the theoretical framework into practical tools, further demonstrating the potential of regularized stochastic control formulations for modern generative modeling.
