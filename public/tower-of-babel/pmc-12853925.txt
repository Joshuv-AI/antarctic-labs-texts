Tools and methods for assessing the usability and related aspects of usability of extended reality and telerehabilitation technologies in stroke rehabilitation: a scoping review
Authors: Fatimata Ouédraogo, Marika Demers, Karla Vanessa Rodrigues Soares Menezes, David Labbé, Karina Lebel, Simon Brière, Mindy F Levin, Michel Tousignant, Dahlia Kairy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeExtended reality (XR) and telerehabilitation (TR) technologies are increasingly being used into stroke rehabilitation. These technologies have the potential to enhance therapy intensity, motivate users through engaging and interactive environments, and improve access to rehabilitation services in both clinical and home settings. Usability assessment is essential to ensure effective, engaging, and accessible interventions. This scoping review aims to identify tools used to evaluate XR and TR technologies in stroke rehabilitation.Materials and methodsThis scoping review was conducted following the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley, further refined by Levac et al. and the Joanna Briggs Institute. A literature search was performed across five databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, and Web of Science) using keywords and their variations related to stroke, virtual reality, augmented reality, mixed reality, telerehabilitation, and usability evaluation. Peer-reviewed articles and conference abstracts published up to December 2024 were included if they reported on the usability evaluation of XR or TR technologies in neurological rehabilitation. Two reviewers independently screened studies for eligibility. Relevant data were extracted using a standardized data charting framework.ResultsThe search yielded 2,290 articles, of which 111 were included in the review. Twenty-eight tools were identified, encompassing both direct usability assessments and complementary tools addressing related aspects. These tools were grouped into six categories: (1) standardized questionnaires, (2) custom questionnaires, (3) semi-structured interviews, (4) task-based usability testing, (5) modified standardized questionnaires, and (6) think-aloud protocols. The most frequently used tool was the System Usability Scale (SUS), followed by custom questionnaires. Among studies explicitly evaluating usability, 55.9% combined two to six tools to capture multiple facets of usability. Usability was assessed in 67.6% of publications using quantitative methods (e.g., questionnaires), in 4.5% using qualitative methods (e.g., interviews, focus groups), and in 27.9% using mixed methods approaches.ConclusionUsability of XR and TR technologies is assessed with a wide variety of tools. Combining tools helps capture different aspects of usability, highlighting the importance of addressing its multifaceted nature in stroke rehabilitation. Future research could develop and validate a framework integrating multiple aspects of usability to ensure both relevance and comparability across studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01861-z.Keywords: Usability evaluation, Stroke rehabilitation, Extended reality, Telerehabilitation

Introduction

Neurological disorders rank among the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. For instance, stroke affects approximately 12 million people globally each year, and nearly 94 million people are currently living with its long-term consequences, which may include impairments in the upper and lower limbs, balance disorders, and a wide range of other physical, cognitive, and emotional difficulties [2, 3]. These impairments can substantially limit individuals’ ability to perform activities of daily living, restrict social participation, and negatively affect overall quality of life. In this context, intensive, targeted, and sustained rehabilitation is essential to optimize functional recovery and promote social reintegration [4–7]. This need extends beyond the acute and subacute phases of stroke care, persisting into the chronic phase, when individuals may continue to face lasting deficits even after returning home [8].

An important challenge with conventional rehabilitation exercises is their tendency to be monotonous and difficult to tailor to individual needs, ultimately reducing motivation and adherence to therapeutic regimens [9]. In many low-resource settings, sustained and intensive access to rehabilitation is often limited by human, financial, or geographic constraints [10]. Together, these factors are driving an increased and sustained demand for neurological rehabilitation services, underscoring the urgent need for innovative, scalable, and engaging strategies to address current limitations and better meet the needs of individuals living with the consequences of stroke or other neurological conditions.

In recent years, neurorehabilitation technologies have emerged as promising adjuncts to conventional rehabilitation. Among these, extended reality (XR), an umbrella term encompassing virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR) that digitally modifies or enhances the user’s perception of the real environment, together with telerehabilitation (TR) have gained increasing attention in neurological rehabilitation [11, 12]. These technologies offer several potential advantages, including increased intensity and personalization of interventions; enhanced patient engagement and motivation through immersive and stimulating environments [13–16], and improved access to rehabilitation services, particularly when delivered remotely via TR platforms [17, 18]. Specifically, VR allows users to interact with simulated environments mimicking real-life activities, while AR overlays virtual elements onto the real world in real-time [12, 19]. MR further integrates VR and AR to enable dynamic interaction between virtual objects and the physical environment [17]. Although exergames (e.g., Nintendo Wii) are not part of the XR spectrum, we included them in this review because they are frequently used in neurorehabilitation as technology-mediated, interactive exercise platforms that share functional similarities with XR-based interventions [20]. TR leverages information and communication technologies such as smartphones, tablets, internet-based platforms, and in some cases XR devices to deliver rehabilitation services remotely [21]. In a Delphi study, Hill et al. [22], defined TR as the use of information and communication technology to deliver rehabilitation services remotely by connecting healthcare providers with beneficiaries, caregivers, or care partners for screening, assessment, intervention, consultation, or monitoring. In this review, we operationalize TR as the synchronous or asynchronous delivery of rehabilitation activities performed at a distance through digital communication technologies, including situations in which XR systems are used remotely to support rehabilitation. This conceptualization justifies the inclusion of both XR and TR within the same review.

While these technologies offer potential benefits and are transforming clinical practice in neurological rehabilitation, their successful implementation may depend on factors such as their usability. According to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 9241-11:2018), usability refers to “the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use” [23]. This definition highlights the need for technologies to be easy to learn, pleasant to use, and appropriately tailored to the abilities and expectations of their users. However, in stroke rehabilitation, the notion of ease of use can be perceived differently. Rehabilitation tasks are intentionally challenging to promote motor recovery, and this therapeutic difficulty may influence perceptions of effort, comfort, and satisfaction in ways that differ from non-clinical contexts. Persons with stroke may present motor, cognitive, perceptual, communication, or psychological impairments (e.g., depression) that influence how they interact with technology. As a result, and beyond the core usability components emphasized in the ISO framework, additional factors such as motivation, cognitive load, and emotional responses play an important role in shaping how individuals with stroke engage with and accept rehabilitation technologies.

Usability can be assessed at different points in the development and implementation process. For example, it may be evaluated before users actively engage with the technology, to identify potential barriers and optimize the design, or after hands-on use, when feedback reflects actual user interaction and experience [24]. Moreover, usability evaluation can occur at various stages of the technology development lifecycle, from early-stage prototyping (low Technology Readiness Level, TRL), where it helps guide design iterations, to later stages (high TRL), where it serves to assess the technology’s practical utility in clinical or home settings [25].

The literature reveals a wide diversity in the tools and methods used to assess usability. This heterogeneity makes it challenging for researchers and clinicians to identify and select appropriate usability evaluation tools and approaches. A comprehensive synthesis is therefore needed to clarify which tools and strategies are currently used, in what contexts, and for what purposes, to support more consistent and rigorous usability evaluation practices in stroke rehabilitation. A scoping review is particularly well suited to map existing evidence and identify knowledge gaps. A literature survey conducted in 2016 by Klaassen et al. [26] focused on usability, but only within the context of telemedicine systems. More recently, Nguyen et al. [27] conducted a scoping review to identify questionnaires used to evaluate neurorehabilitation technologies, including VR, robotics, and gaming systems. However, their review did not focus specifically on usability assessment and excluded qualitative studies that explored user experience through individual interviews and focus groups. Including both qualitative and quantitative studies, as well as a wide range of methodological approaches, could offer a more comprehensive understanding of how usability is assessed in neurorehabilitation technologies.

Identifying the research question

The primary research question guiding this scoping review is: What tools and methodological approaches have been used in the literature to assess the usability of XR and TR technologies in the context of stroke rehabilitation?

Materials and methods

Design

A scoping review was chosen as the appropriate method to address the objectives of this project, as it enables a comprehensive exploration and mapping of existing knowledge on a given topic. This type of review is designed to examine the breadth, scope, and volume of scientific literature, without evaluating the methodological quality of the included studies [28].

The present review was conducted following the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [29], enhanced by Levac et al. [30] and further refined by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [28]. The planning, conduct, and reporting of the review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [31]. The main steps included: (1) defining the research objectives; (2) developing the methodological protocol; (3) conducting a comprehensive search for relevant articles; (4) systematically selecting the studies; (5) extracting data in a structured manner; (6) synthesizing and presenting the results descriptively; and (7) Consultation (optional). The scoping review protocol, including key methodological details, was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) to enhance transparency (https://osf.io/wshbv).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This scoping review was guided by the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework [28].

The population primarily consisted of persons with stroke, regardless of age, gender, sex, or chronicity. Studies that included persons with stroke in combination with other neurological conditions, or that included persons with stroke alongside therapists or other healthy participants, were also included. Studies that only involved healthy participants without any individual with stroke were excluded.

The concept of interest focused on the evaluation of the usability of XR and TR technologies. XR encompassed VR, AR, MR, and exergames. Studies that did not evaluate usability in any form were excluded.

The context of the review covered all strategies and tools used to assess usability for XR and TR technologies for people with stroke, including but not limited to standardized questionnaires, custom questionnaires, individual interviews or focus groups. Full-text publications and conference abstracts published in peer-reviewed journals were included. However, non-peer-reviewed conference abstracts and publications and study protocols were excluded. Concerning the study design, quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies were all eligible for inclusion. Lastly, studies written in English were included, while those published in languages other than English were excluded.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in collaboration with a rehabilitation sciences academic librarian. Searched medical subheadings and key words included VR, AR, MR, TR, telehealth, usability, stroke, cerebrovascular accident, and cerebrovascular disease. The search was performed in five databases on December 15, 2024: Medline, Embase, CINAHL, PsycInfo, and Web of Science. The preliminary search yielded a very large number of peer-reviewed studies, so grey literature (e.g., theses, dissertations, preprints) was not searched at this time. There was no time restrictions applied. The detailed search strategy for Medline is included in the supplementary material. These databases were selected for their broad and complementary coverage of the fields most relevant to this review, including health sciences, rehabilitation, and technology. The search strategy was developed for Medline and adapted to the other databases. Search results were imported into Covidence (Level 5, Melbourne, Australia), where duplicates were identified and removed through both automated and manual processes.

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted independently using Covidence, by two reviewers, based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The first stage involved a preliminary screening of titles and abstracts. Each abstract was categorized as included, excluded, or uncertain. Discrepancies were discussed for each study until a consensus was reached. The second stage consisted of a full-text review of the remaining articles to confirm their eligibility. Reasons for exclusion were systematically recorded for all excluded studies. The involvement of two independent reviewers for all screening phases was expected to enhance the reliability and consistency of the study selection process, even though no formal calculation of inter-rater agreement (e.g., Cohen’s Kappa) was performed. All screening was conducted manually using Covidence without the assistance of machine learning algorithms, artificial intelligence, or other automated screening tools. Reviewers were not blinded to study details during the screening process; full bibliographic information (including titles, abstracts, author names, journal names, and publication years) was visible to reviewers at all stages of screening.

The reviewers collaboratively developed a data extraction framework to systematically capture key information from each included study. The framework was initially piloted on a subset of 10 articles to ensure its clarity and comprehensiveness. Based on this pilot testing and subsequent discussions between the reviewers, modifications were made to refine the framework. The extraction framework documented the following elements:

Once finalized, data extraction was performed by one reviewer and independently reviewed by the second reviewer. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion to reach consensus.

All included studies provided sufficient information relevant to the review; No author contact was necessary. All full-text articles were accessible, and no missing or unclear data required clarification.

Summarising and reporting the results

The results were synthesized descriptively to provide an overview of the number of studies involving XR and TR technologies for neurological rehabilitation. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges) were computed using R software, version 4.4.1, to summarize key characteristics of the included studies. Graphical representations (e.g., bar charts) were created using Microsoft Excel to visually illustrate trends and distributions across the data. Findings were presented in the form of descriptive tables and narrative summaries to facilitate interpretation.

We classified each tool based on the following definitions: Standardized usability and related aspects of usability questionnaires: tools used in their original validated format; Custom Usability Questionnaires: questionnaires specifically developed by the study authors when standardized tools did not fully meet their evaluation needs; Semi-Structured Interviews: individual interviews or focus groups used to explore participants’ perceptions and experiences; Task-Based Usability Testing: assessments of users’ ability to complete predefined tasks with the system (e.g., starting a game, modifying a setting, exiting the program), with common performance indicators including task completion rates, time on task, and user satisfaction ratings; Modified Standardized Usability Questionnaires: adaptations of existing tools to better fit specific study contexts; and the Think-Aloud Protocol: situations where participants verbalized their thoughts in real time while interacting with the system. The study designs were categorized into four main groups to facilitate analysis.

Consultation

The results were presented and discussed with the members of the research team with expertise in neurological rehabilitation, engineering, XR and TR to validate the findings. An additional meeting was held with a team member (DL), who specialize in XR technologies and their usability assessment. This consultation helped refine and validate the results, particularly by clarifying the distinction between tools that directly assess usability and those that evaluate related or influencing factors.

Type of technologies used and TR modalities

Various types of XR technologies (VR, AR, MR, and exergames) were used across the included studies. Some studies combined these technologies with TR approaches (Fig. 4A). More than half of the included studies used VR technologies.

When TR was employed (n = 28), various delivery modalities were reported, with the asynchronous modality being the most frequently used (n = 14), followed by the combined synchronous–asynchronous modality (n = 8) and the synchronous modality (n = 6).

When the technologies were used in clinical settings, sessions typically involved the presence of a clinician, most commonly a physiotherapist (PT) or an occupational therapist (OT), or a member of the research team. In TR contexts, clinician involvement varied depending on the delivery modality. For synchronous TR, individuals with stroke or other conditions used the technology while being virtually supervised by a clinician or research team member. This real-time interaction allowed the clinician to monitor performance, provide feedback, correct movements, and address questions during the rehabilitation session via remote platforms. In contrast, asynchronous TR involved participants using the technology independently, without real-time supervision. In these cases, rehabilitation programs were typically pre-recorded or prescribed in advance, and data (e.g., performance metrics, movement data) were collected for later review by clinicians. Feedback, if provided, was often delivered later based on the review of the recorded sessions or data logs.

Functions targeted by the interventions

Most interventions identified in the included studies focused on upper limb rehabilitation (Fig. 4B). Several studies also addressed global post-stroke rehabilitation, encompassing multiple functional domains simultaneously (motor rehabilitation, cognitive and balance). Additional targeted areas included spatial neglect, cognitive rehabilitation (e.g., attention, memory), balance and postural control, gait training, aphasia, and social interaction facilitated through VR environments.

Studies designs and settings

The first and most common category consisted of usability and/or feasibility studies included usability evaluations, feasibility evaluations, and user-centered design approaches. The second category encompassed experimental studies, including pre–post designs without control groups, single-case designs, and repeated measures designs, to evaluate the effects of interventions on specific outcomes, including usability-related metrics. The third category comprised observational studies, such as cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, prospective observational studies, exploratory cohort studies, descriptive studies, comparative cross-sectional studies, matched-pair case-control designs, and pilot cohort studies. These studies were primarily descriptive in nature and explored associations without experimental manipulation. The fourth category included clinical trials, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), single-blind RCTs, assessor-blinded RCTs, pilot randomized studies, and randomized crossover trials. Most interventions were delivered in clinical environments, including rehabilitation centers and hospital-based settings. As shown in Table 2, the included studies varied widely in sample sizes and intervention parameters. Interventions generally ranged from short-term protocols to longer programs, with a median intervention duration of 4 weeks and a typical session length of approximately 30 min.

Methods of data collection

Usability evaluation was based on three methodological approaches to data collection, with most studies using quantitative methods through questionnaires (67.6%). A mixed-methods approach combining questionnaires with individual interviews, focus groups, or open-ended questions added to questionnaires was used in 27.9% of the studies. Finally, 4.5% of the studies used an exclusively qualitative approach (individual interviews or focus groups).

Usability evaluation and related aspects of usability tools

A total of 28 distinct tools were identified, including those directly evaluating usability and complementary tools assessing related aspects of usability (Fig. 5). As some studies used multiple usability assessment tools, the percentages in Fig. 5 and elsewhere in this paragraph reflect every occurrence of tool use, which can result in totals greater than 100%. The most frequently used tool was the System Usability Scale (SUS) (22.6%), followed by custom usability questionnaires (20.1%) and semi-structured interviews, either individually or in focus groups (13.6%). Complementary tools such as the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI, 7.0%), the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ, 6.5%), and the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX, 3.0%) were also frequently used. These latter tools were not used alone; rather, they were administered as complements to usability measures to assess related aspects such as intrinsic motivation, cybersickness, and cognitive workload, factors that may influence usability.

Overall, 55.9% of the studies that specifically evaluated usability combined two to six tools to capture multiple facets of usability, whereas 44.1% relied on a single usability tool. However, even in the latter case, these studies typically combined usability evaluation with measures of effectiveness, such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment, the Berg Balance Scale, the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), or functional measures such as the Barthel Index, depending on the targeted function of the technology. Among the tools most used in combination with effectiveness measures, custom usability questionnaires ranked first (38.8%), followed by semi-structured interview (14.4%), the SUS (12.2%), the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (8.2%), the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) (6.1%), modified standardized questionnaires (6.1%), including adapted versions of the SUS, TUQ, TAM, the Short Feedback Questionnaire (SFQ) (4.1%), the Telehealth Usability Questionnaire (TUQ) (2.0%), and task-based usability testing (2.0%). Feasibility concepts were also used alone in some studies to complement effectiveness (6.1%).

Discussion

The objective of this scoping review was to identify the usability tools used to assess XR and TR technologies, describe the methodological approaches employed for usability evaluation, and characterize the contexts in which these technologies were implemented. In the following discussion, we structure our findings explicitly according to these objectives.

In terms of context, of the 111 included studies, most were published between 2021 and 2024, reflecting a rapid increase in the use of these technologies in neurological rehabilitation across both high-income and low- and middle-income countries. XR and TR technologies were implemented in clinical settings, at home, or in both settings, highlighting the flexibility of these interventions and their potential for both in-person and remote rehabilitation.

Regarding methodological approaches, quantitative methods predominated, with most studies relying on standardized or custom usability questionnaires. As noted by Klaassen et al. [26], quantitative methods remain the most common approach for usability evaluation, likely due to their efficiency and capacity to collect data from larger samples, and their ability to easily compare scores between conditions or studies. However, questionnaires may overlook subjective aspects of user experience, such as motivation, acceptability, or encountered challenges. Although qualitative approaches can provide rich, in-depth insights into user experiences, perceptions and challenges that may not be captured by questionnaires, only 4.5% of studies included in this review relied solely on qualitative approaches, likely because purely qualitative methods are less easily generalizable across larger samples. To address these strengths and limitations, about one-third of studies used mixed-methods approaches, combining questionnaires with individual interviews or focus groups. Mixed-methods approaches may require more time, resources, and expertise for data collection and analysis, but the triangulation of qualitative and quantitative sources allows for a more nuanced understanding of users’ perceptions, expectations, and recommendations. This illustrates the added value of integrating qualitative insights with quantitative measures. Overall, the choice of methodological approach was guided primarily by the study objectives and the characteristics of the technology being evaluated. Building on these observations, our review identified 28 usability evaluation tools, which were categorized into six groups: standardized usability and related aspects of usability questionnaires, custom usability questionnaires, semi-structured interviews, task-based usability testing, modified standardized usability questionnaires, and the Think Aloud protocol.

Among the 23 standardized questionnaires identified, the SUS emerged as the most frequently used tool. Its widespread adoption can be attributed to several factors. It is a validated questionnaire, short (10 items), easy to administer, quickly interpretable, and applicability across a wide range of technologies, and available in multiple languages, facilitating its use in multicultural and multilingual contexts [107, 108]. The SUS is well-suited for evaluating technologies in real-world settings, which is particularly relevant for emerging technologies such as XR. Nguyen et al. [27], also found that the SUS was the most commonly used questionnaire for evaluating neurorehabilitation technologies, including VR, robotics, and gaming systems. More than half of the included studies used between two and six complementary tools to assess usability, reflecting the multifaceted nature of this construct. In fact, other well-established tools were frequently used alongside the SUS, such as the IMI, the SSQ, and the NASA-TLX. The use of these complementary tools illustrates researchers’ interest in evaluating not only usability itself, but also related factors such as intrinsic motivation, cybersickness, perceived cognitive workload, and other dimensions that can strongly influence usability [109]. This approach is particularly relevant in neurological rehabilitation, where patient engagement, comfort, and perception of the technology are critical for intervention adherence [110]. While tools such as the SUS were applied across a wide variety of technologies, others were more specific to the type of XR or TR technology being investigated. For example, the SSQ was primarily used in studies involving immersive VR systems (head-mounted displays). Thus, the choice of usability assessment tools depends not only on the general aim of capturing usability, but also on the specific technology under study, the research objectives, and the user-related factors of interest.

Although 23 standardized tools were identified in this review to evaluate usability, custom usability questionnaires were the second most frequently used tools after the SUS. Timmins et al. [111], recommended using or adapting existing validated questionnaires rather than creating new ones, in order to enhance the credibility and comparability of research findings. However, when no suitable instrument is available, Leon et al. [112], advocated for following a structured five-step process to develop a new questionnaire, comprising (1) preliminary considerations (including review of existing tools and analysis of qualitative data, if available), (2) drafting the questionnaire, (3) expert panel review, (4) pilot testing, and (5) reliability analysis. In this current review, authors typically developed custom usability questionnaires using 5- or 7-point Likert scales [113]. They designed these tools to meet specific evaluation needs that standardized tools may not adequately address. Our results are aligned with those of Nguyen et al. [27] showing that self-developed questionnaires were the most common usability tools. The frequent use of custom usability questionnaires could be attributed to the emerging and diverse nature of the technologies being assessed, which often have unique features (e.g., level of immersion, interactivity, ease of use, social interaction) requiring more contextualized evaluation approaches. Custom usability questionnaires could offer several advantages, including high flexibility, the ability to target elements relevant to specific technologies or populations, and the potential to complement standardized tools by addressing more specific aspects of usability. However, most of the custom questionnaires used in the studies reviewed here were neither validated nor demonstrated to be reliable. This raises significant concerns regarding the quality, comparability, and interpretability of the data collected. Such methodological heterogeneity complicates cross-study comparisons, undermines scientific rigor, and limits the generalizability of findings. Although custom usability questionnaires may be valuable in specific contexts, their widespread use without validation represents a critical limitation for synthesizing evidence in the field. Therefore, their use should ideally be accompanied by efforts to improve methodological rigor or to develop validated versions tailored to the evaluation of emerging rehabilitation technologies.

Some studies modified standardized questionnaires by adding open-ended questions to collect qualitative data on user’s experience. These tools were categorized as modified standardized usability questionnaires. Adapting existing instruments, rather than developing new ones is encouraged when appropriate tools are available, as this strategy enhances methodological consistency while allowing for contextual relevance [112]. This approach illustrates the complementarity of quantitative and qualitative data in usability assessment. The qualitative data collected allow researchers to go beyond numerical scores by capturing subjective elements such as user perceptions, emotions, barriers encountered, and suggestions for improvement. This greatly enriches the interpretation of results by providing a more nuanced understanding of the interaction between the user and the technology. In the context of neurorehabilitation where user needs can be complex and highly variable, these modified tools may be relevant to inform technology development, adaptation, and implementation.

Other usability assessment tools included task-based usability testing, which allows real-time observation of interaction difficulties, and the Think Aloud protocol, which explores the cognitive processes underlying system use. These methods provide complementary insights by focusing on user behaviors and thought processes during technology use, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive and context-sensitive evaluation of usability. Although this review focused on usability tools, some studies also evaluated aspects of feasibility, such as recruitment rates, retention, adherence, fidelity, and missing data, which may be particularly important for determining whether a technology can be effectively integrated into care settings, used by specific target populations, or implemented in larger-scale studies. Therefore, the combined evaluation of usability and feasibility offers a more holistic and comprehensive perspective, considering both the immediate user experience and the practical conditions of implementation.

In most of the studies included in this review, the usability evaluation tools had been validated for use after interaction with the technology, regardless of the TRL, as the technologies evaluated ranged from level 3 (proof of concept) to level 9 (deployment in real-world settings). These findings highlight the importance of assessing usability even in the early stages of development (TRL 3–5), as such evaluations can inform iterative improvements, enhance the user experience, and support successful implementation in rehabilitation contexts. However, post-use assessments may have limitations, such as overlooking initial challenges with learning, intuitiveness, or accessibility, factors that are particularly critical at lower TRL. It may therefore be relevant to assess usability at multiple time points: prior to use (to capture expectations), during use (to evaluate the user experience), and after use (to assess satisfaction and perceived effectiveness).

Usability evaluation plays a pivotal role in shaping technology implementation. Accordingly, selecting suitable usability tools is essential for generating comprehensive and meaningful information about how the technology is experienced by users. Choosing the right usability tool depends on several factors: who uses the technology, what aspects of usability you aim to measure, and the characteristics of the technology itself. This relationship between usability and implementation is well recognized in several implementation science frameworks. For example, within the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), usability is embedded in the “intervention characteristics,” which directly influence the likelihood of adoption [114]. Similarly, in the RE-AIM framework, a highly usable technology facilitates adoption, effective implementation, and long-term maintenance [115].

Limitations

This scoping review presents certain limitations. It was restricted to peer-reviewed articles and abstracts published in English, which may have led to the exclusion of studies published in other languages. As a result, valuable insights on usability evaluation from non-English-speaking contexts may have been overlooked. Additionally, grey literature such as theses, dissertations, and preprints were not included. Although the large number of peer-reviewed studies already provided substantial coverage of the field, this exclusion may have led to omission of emerging work not yet published in peer-reviewed outlets. However, we included peer-reviewed conference abstracts, which are common venues for disseminating early-stage technological research. Notably, most of these were published in IEEE conference proceedings, recognized for their rigorous peer-review standards and relevance to engineering and health technology. Including these abstracts helped capture innovative studies, as early usability evaluations are often presented at conferences prior to journal publication.

Conclusion

Our scoping review identified a variety of tools used to evaluate the usability of XR and TR technologies, offering practical guidance for clinicians and researchers aiming to develop, evaluate or implement XR and TR technologies in neurological rehabilitation. Custom usability tools were frequently used, though standardized questionnaires were also commonly used, particularly within quantitative, qualitative or mixed-method approaches. These technologies were most often implemented in clinical settings, followed by home-based contexts, or a combination of both. These findings highlight the need for validated and context-sensitive usability assessments to support the effective integration of XR and TR technologies into neurorehabilitation practice, ultimately enhancing patient engagement and optimizing rehabilitation outcomes.
