Choice-Model-Assisted Q-learning for Delayed-Feedback Revenue Management


Abstract
We study reinforcement learning for revenue management with delayed feedback, where a substantial fraction of value is determined by customer cancellations and modifications observed days after booking. We propose choice-model-assisted RL : a calibrated discrete choice model is used as a fixed partial world model to impute the delayed component of the learning target at decision time. In the fixed-model deployment regime, we prove that tabular Q-learning with model-imputed targets converges to an O ⁡ ( ε / ( 1 − γ ) ) O(\varepsilon/(1-\gamma)) neighborhood of the optimal Q-function, where ε \varepsilon summarizes partial-model error, with an additional O ( t − 1 / 2 ) O(t^{-1/2}) sampling term. Experiments in a simulator calibrated from 61,619 hotel bookings (1,088 independent runs) show: (i) no statistically detectable difference from a maturity-buffer DQN baseline in stationary settings; (ii) positive effects under in-family parameter shifts, with significant gains in 5 of 10 shift scenarios after Holm–Bonferroni correction (up to 12.4%); and (iii) consistent degradation under structural misspecification, where the choice model assumptions are violated (1.4–2.6% lower revenue). These results characterize when partial behavioral models improve robustness under shift and when they introduce harmful bias.

Introduction

(1) Operational phenomenon.
In revenue management—a domain with decades of operations research literature
5
;
30
—customer modifications and cancellations (
shocks
) occur 1–14 days after initial booking, constituting 20–40% of total revenue. These delayed outcomes substantially affect the value of pricing decisions.

(2) Learning friction.
Reinforcement learning for such systems faces a credit assignment problem: the learning label
r_{t}=r_{t}^{\mathrm{imm}}+r_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
for the action at time
t
requires the delayed component
r_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
, which is revealed only after a delay
\Delta\in\{1,\dots,14\}
days. Standard approaches must wait for shocks to resolve before updating.

(3) Available structure.
Order features (room type, customer characteristics, booking date) are known immediately at decision time. Discrete choice models (DCMs), calibrated offline via maximum likelihood estimation, provide probabilistic predictions of shock outcomes
P(z|o;\theta)
from these features
(
22
;
32
)
.

(4) Method.
We propose
choice-model-assisted RL
: use a fixed, pre-trained DCM to impute
r_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
at decision time, enabling immediate Q-learning updates without waiting for ground truth. The DCM acts as a
partial world model
—it predicts shock outcomes for pending orders while the Q-function learns the value of pricing actions.

(5) Boundary condition.
This approach helps under
in-family parameter shifts
(demand scaling, competition intensity) where the DCM structure remains valid, but hurts under
structural misspecification
where the true behavior violates model assumptions (IIA violations, segment heterogeneity). The same inductive bias enabling extrapolation becomes a liability when the model family is wrong.

Two evaluation regimes.
We analyze this framework in two complementary settings: (i) a
continuous-operation
regime where we prove asymptotic convergence bounds for tabular Q-learning with fixed DCM; and (ii) a
fixed-window operational
regime where we empirically quantify deployment-time impact using a simulator calibrated from 61,619 hotel bookings.

Feedback Semantics and Notation

We distinguish (i)

at calendar time
t

3.1
Revenue Management with Delayed Feedback

The Full Operational Environment.
Consider the complete hotel revenue management setting. At each decision epoch
t
, the environment state includes:

•

Inventory
: Available rooms
s_{t}\in\{0,1,\ldots,C\}
where
C=26

•

Customer features
: Arriving customers characterized by
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(days-to-arrival, booking channel, loyalty tier, search history;
d=12
)

•

Competitive prices
: Competitor room prices
\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{c}}
observed from market

•

Pending orders
: Set
\mathcal{P}_{t}
of past bookings awaiting modification/cancellation outcomes, each with order features
x_{o}

The full state is thus
\tilde{s}_{t}=(s_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t})
. Customer behavior—booking decisions
j_{t}\sim P(j|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{p}_{t},\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t};\theta)
and shock outcomes
z_{o}\sim P(z|x_{o};\theta)
—depends on features and competitive context. Rewards decompose as
r_{t}=r_{t}^{\mathrm{imm}}+r_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
, where the delayed component
r_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
is revealed only after
\Delta\in\{1,\ldots,14\}
days.

Complexity Challenge.
Learning directly on
\tilde{s}_{t}
is intractable: (i) customer features
\mathbf{x}_{t}
are high-dimensional (
d=12
) and vary per arrival; (ii) competitive prices
\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t}
change frequently; (iii) pending orders
\mathcal{P}_{t}
grow exponentially (
|\tilde{\mathcal{S}}|=O(|\mathcal{S}|\cdot 2^{|\mathcal{P}|})
). However, we observe a key structural property:
customer features and competitive prices affect only customer decisions, not the inventory dynamics directly
. This enables state space reduction.

Three Modeling Approaches.
We contrast how different approaches handle this complexity:

Approach 1: Augmented MDP (Full State + Delayed Structure).

Retain the full state
\tilde{s}_{t}=(s_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(\tilde{s}_{t+1}|\tilde{s}_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=P(\mathbf{x}_{t+1})\cdot P(\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t+1})\cdot P(j_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t},a_{t},\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t})\cdot\prod_{o\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}P(z_{o}|x_{o})

(2)

This is a proper MDP with Markov transitions, but the state space is exponential in feature dimension and number of pending orders—intractable for tabular methods and challenging for function approximation due to non-stationarity in
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t}
.

Approach 2: Reduced State, Ignore Structure (MB-DQN).

Project to inventory only:
s_{t}\in\{0,\ldots,C\}
, treating customer features, competitive prices, and pending orders as unmodeled noise:

\displaystyle P(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}^{c},\{z_{o}\}}\left[P(j|s_{t},a_{t})\cdot\mathbf{1}[s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},j,\{z_{o}\})]\right]

(3)

State space is tractable (
|\mathcal{S}|=27
), but we lose predictive information in customer characteristics and must wait for delayed outcomes to mature.

Approach 3: Model-Based Imputation (CA-DQN, Our Method).

Key observation:
Customer features
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and competitive prices
\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t}
affect only customer decisions, not inventory dynamics directly. We can
pre-process
them via a discrete choice model (DCM) calibrated offline:

\displaystyle s_{t}

\displaystyle\in\{0,\ldots,C\}\quad\text{(reduced state)}

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{N}_{t}}r^{\mathrm{shock}}(o,\hat{z}_{o}),\quad\hat{z}_{o}\sim P(z|x_{o};\theta^{*})\quad\text{(imputed via DCM)}

(5)

The DCM absorbs the high-dimensional customer/competitive context: it takes
(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{p}_{t},\mathbf{p}^{c}_{t},x_{o})
as input and outputs choice/shock predictions. This enables a tractable 27-state MDP while leveraging rich feature information through the partial world model. Theorem
3
bounds the approximation error from using imputed
\hat{r}_{t}^{\mathrm{del}}
instead of true

3.2
Partial World Model: Discrete Choice Model

Define the multinomial logit DCM for room-type choice:

P(j|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p},\theta)=\frac{\exp(V_{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p};\theta))}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}}\exp(V_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p};\theta))}

(6)

where
V_{j}=\theta^{\top}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
is the systematic utility,
\phi_{j}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are feature maps, and
\mathcal{J}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{K},\text{outside}\}
represents
K
room types plus an outside option (no purchase).
1
1

1

K=6
in our experiments. Shocks are modeled as switching probabilities
P(z|o;\theta)
conditional on order characteristics
o
. See Appendix
15
.

The DCM provides tractable modeling by:

•

Reducing dimensionality:
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\ll|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|

•

Imposing structure: Utilities decompose as
V_{j}=\alpha_{j}-\beta p_{j}+\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{x}

•

Enabling closed-form gradients:
\nabla_{\theta}\log P(j|\cdot)=\phi_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{k\sim P}[\phi_{k}]

DCM as Pre-Processing Layer.
The DCM serves as a
pre-processing layer
that absorbs the high-dimensional customer and competitive context. It takes as input: (i) customer covariates
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
(days-to-arrival, booking channel, loyalty tier, etc.); (ii) our price vector
\mathbf{p}
; (iii) competitor prices
\mathbf{p}^{c}
(captured via relative price features); and (iv) order characteristics
x_{o}
for shock prediction. By calibrating
\theta^{*}
offline, we compress this rich context into probabilistic predictions that enable immediate reward imputation while maintaining a tractable 27-state RL problem.

Method: Embedding Partial World Models

4.1
Fixed-DCM Deployment

In practice, we deploy a two-phase system:

1.

Calibration phase
: Estimate
\theta^{*}=\argmax_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log P(j_{i}|s_{i},a_{i};\theta)
on historical data

2.

Deployment phase
: Fix
\theta^{*}
and run Q-learning with model-imputed sampling

This separation is computationally practical: DCM calibration requires batch processing of complete customer journeys, while Q-learning operates online. The Q-update uses synthetic samples:

Q_{t+1}(s,a)=Q_{t}(s,a)+\alpha_{t}\bigl[r^{\prime}_{t}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q_{t}(s,a)\bigr]

(9)

where
(r^{\prime}_{t},s^{\prime}_{t+1})
are generated by model-imputed sampling with the fixed DCM
\theta^{*}
. Theorem
3
provides the finite-time bound for this regime.

5
Theoretical Analysis

5.1
Convergence Analysis

The central theoretical contribution is proving convergence when using a pre-trained choice model to assist Q-learning. Our main theorem covers the
deployment regime
: the DCM is calibrated from historical data and held fixed during Q-learning (see Appendix for the general two-timescale extension).

Theorem 3

(Convergence under Fixed Choice Model)
.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta^{*}}
denote the approximate MDP induced by the fixed choice model, with reward function
\tilde{r}_{\theta^{*}}
and transition kernel
\tilde{P}_{\theta^{*}}
. Let
\tilde{Q}^{*}
be its optimal action-value function, and let
Q^{*}
be the optimal value of the true MDP. Define
\varepsilon_{r}:=\|\tilde{r}_{\theta^{*}}-r\|_{\infty}
(reward approximation error) and
\varepsilon_{P}:=\max_{s,a}\|\tilde{P}_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot|s,a)-P(\cdot|s,a)\|_{1}
(transition approximation error). Under standard coverage and boundedness assumptions (A1–A4 in Appendix):

(i)

Q-learning convergence:
Tabular Q-learning driven by samples from
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\theta^{*}}
converges to
\tilde{Q}^{*}
at rate
O(t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(|S||A|t/\delta)})
with probability
1-\delta
.

(ii)

Simulation lemma:
The model-induced optimal value differs from the true optimal value as

\|\tilde{Q}^{*}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{r}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma R_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right)

Combining via triangle inequality:
\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q_{t}-\tilde{Q}^{*}\|_{\infty}+\|\tilde{Q}^{*}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}
, yielding the full bound. Part (ii) follows standard simulation lemma arguments
(
17
)
; see Appendix
9

Corollary 4

(Asymptotic Neighborhood)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
, in continuous operation:

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{r}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma R_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right)

As
t\to\infty
, sampling noise vanishes and long-run performance is controlled by an irreducible bias floor determined by partial-model error.

The bound has three terms: reward bias, transition bias, and sampling noise. In our setting, transition error dominates. Full proof in Appendix

.

Remark 2

(Extrapolation under Distributional Shift)

We introduced choice-model-assisted RL for delayed-feedback environments. Theory establishes
\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}=O(\varepsilon/(1-\gamma)+t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(\cdot)})
for fixed DCM deployment.

Experiments (1,088 runs) show: (i) no detected difference in stationary settings; (ii) gains in 5/10 shift scenarios (up to +12.4%), degradation in 2/10; (iii) consistent underperformance under misspecification (1.4–2.6% worse). Partial world models help when structural assumptions hold but hurt when violated.

10
DCM Identifiability and Properties

10.1
Identifiability Conditions for Multinomial Logit Model

Before establishing the strict concavity of the MNL log-likelihood, we formalize the identifiability conditions that ensure parameter uniqueness and well-posedness of the estimation problem.

Assumption [
.

Identifiability Conditions for MNL]

Consider a multinomial logit model with
J
alternatives indexed by
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,J-1\}
, where alternative
0
is the baseline with utility normalized to zero. Let each alternative
j\geq 1
have utility:

u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{p})=\beta_{j}+\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}-\alpha_{j}p_{j}

(13)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are customer covariates,
p_{j}
is the price,
\beta_{j}
is the alternative-specific constant,
\mathbf{w}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are covariate coefficients, and
\alpha_{j}>0
is the price sensitivity.

The following conditions ensure identifiability:

(ID1)

Normalization
: Alternative
0
(outside option or no-purchase) has zero utility:
u_{0}=0
, implying
\beta_{0}=0
,
\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\alpha_{0}=0
. This normalization breaks location invariance.

(ID2)

Alternative-Specific Parameters
: Price coefficients
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J-1}
are alternative-specific (not a single shared coefficient). This prevents confounding between price effects and alternative preferences.

\alpha_{j}\neq\alpha_{k}\quad\text{for some }j\neq k,\quad\text{or}\quad\alpha_{j}=\alpha\text{ for all }j\text{ with known }\alpha

(14)

If all
\alpha_{j}
are constrained to be equal (
\alpha_{j}=\alpha
for all
j
), this coefficient must be treated as a known constant or estimated separately via price variation.

(ID3)

Covariate Variation
: The covariate matrix
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
has full column rank:

\text{rank}(\mathbf{X})=d

(15)

This ensures no perfect collinearity among covariates, preventing flat directions in the parameter space.

(ID4)

Sufficient Outcome Variation
: Each alternative
j\in\{1,\ldots,J-1\}
is chosen at least once in the data:

\exists\,i:y_{i}=j\quad\text{for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,J-1\}

(16)

Without this, parameters for unchosen alternatives are not identifiable.

(ID5)

Bounded Choice Probabilities
: For all parameter values
\theta
in the parameter space
\Theta
and all covariate-price combinations
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
in the support of the data distribution:

\epsilon_{0}\leq P_{\theta}(j|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\leq 1-\epsilon_{0}\quad\text{for some }\epsilon_{0}>0

(17)

This ensures no alternative has probability zero or one uniformly, preventing degeneracies.

(ID6)

Compactness of Parameter Space
: The parameter space is a compact subset of Euclidean space:

\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\quad\text{is compact}

(18)

where
p=(J-1)(d+2)
is the total number of parameters (excluding the normalized baseline). This ensures existence of maximizers and prevents parameters from diverging to infinity.

(ID7)

Full-Rank Fisher Information
(Consequence): Under conditions ID1-ID6, the expected Fisher information matrix:

\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},y\sim P_{\theta^{\star}}}\left[-\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\ln P_{\theta}(y|\mathbf{x})\Big|_{\theta=\theta^{\star}}\right]

(19)

is positive definite, i.e.,
\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})\succ 0
.

Remark 1 (Necessity of Conditions).

•

ID1 (Normalization)
: Without normalization, adding a constant
c
to all utilities leaves choice probabilities unchanged (location invariance). Normalizing the baseline alternative breaks this symmetry.

•

ID2 (Alternative-Specific Prices)
: If all alternatives share a single price coefficient
\alpha
and all have the same price (
p_{j}=p
for all
j
), then
\alpha
is not separately identified from the intercepts
\{\beta_{j}\}
. Alternative-specific coefficients
\{\alpha_{j}\}
or exogenous price variation resolves this.

•

ID3 (Covariate Variation)
: Perfect collinearity (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}_{i,1}=2\mathbf{x}_{i,2}
for all
i
) creates infinitely many parameter combinations yielding the same log-likelihood, violating uniqueness.

•

ID4 (Outcome Variation)
: If alternative
j
is never chosen, its utility could be arbitrarily low without changing the likelihood, making
\beta_{j}
,
\mathbf{w}_{j}
, and
\alpha_{j}
unidentifiable.

•

ID5 (Bounded Probabilities)
: If some alternative has probability 1 for all observations in the support, the log-likelihood becomes insensitive to other parameters, violating strict concavity.

•

ID6 (Compactness)
: Without boundedness, parameters can diverge (e.g.,
\beta_{j}\to\infty
to fit a probability approaching 1), preventing convergence of MLE.

•

ID7 (Full-Rank Fisher)
: This is a consequence of ID1-ID6 and ensures asymptotic normality of the MLE with well-defined covariance.

Remark 2 (Verification in Hotel Application).

In our hotel revenue management setting, the DCM models customer modification behavior with
J=4
outcomes (keep booking, modify, cancel, no-show). For each outcome type, the reward mapping is deterministic (e.g., cancellation yields refund penalty). This identifiability verification uses a simplified illustration with
J=7
room types to demonstrate the general MNL conditions:

•

J=7
(6 room types + outside option) in the illustrative example

•

Normalization: Outside option (no booking) has
u_{0}=0
✓

•

Alternative-specific price sensitivities:
\alpha_{\text{standard}}\neq\alpha_{\text{deluxe}}
✓

•

Covariate variation:
N=61,619
observations with
d=12
features (days-to-arrival, search history, etc.), ensuring
\text{rank}(\mathbf{X})=12
✓

•

Outcome variation: All modification outcomes have
\geq 1000
observations in the data ✓

•

Bounded probabilities: Empirically,
0.02\leq\hat{P}(j|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\leq 0.85
across all alternatives ✓

•

\Theta=[-10,10]^{p}
in estimation ✓

All identifiability conditions are satisfied for both the modification DCM used in experiments and this illustrative example.

10.2
Bias-Variance Properties of the Doubly-Robust Reward Target

Algorithm 1 (main paper) uses
model-imputed sampling
—generating synthetic samples
(r^{\prime}_{t},s^{\prime}_{t+1})
from the DCM. An alternative formulation is the
doubly-robust reward target
, which we analyze here for theoretical insight. While model-imputed sampling generates unbiased synthetic samples via the DCM, the doubly-robust target interpolates between model imputation and realized outcomes. Both approaches address delayed feedback; we present the doubly-robust analysis to justify the term “doubly-robust” and quantify the statistical benefits of model-assisted reward estimation.

10.2.1
Target Construction

Consider a state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})
at time
t
. The total reward decomposes as:

r_{t}=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+r_{t}^{\text{delay}}

(20)

where:

•

r_{t}^{\text{imm}}
: Immediate revenue (e.g., booking fees, observed at time
t
)

•

r_{t}^{\text{delay}}
: Delayed revenue from modifications/cancellations (termed
shocks
in the main paper), observed at
t+D_{t}

The delayed component
r_{t}^{\text{delay}}
matures after random delay
D_{t}\in\{0,1,\ldots,D_{\max}\}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denote the information available at time
t
. Define:

•

m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t},\theta]
: DCM-based imputation of delayed reward

•

\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured at }t\}}:=\mathds{1}\{D_{t}\leq 0\}
: Indicator that delay has matured by time
t

The
doubly-robust target
is:

\tilde{r}_{t}:=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})+\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured at }t\}}\bigl(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr)

(21)

10.2.2
Bias Analysis

Lemma 5

(Conditional Unbiasedness of Doubly-Robust Target)
.

Suppose the DCM imputation satisfies
m_{\theta}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[r^{\text{delay}}|s,a]
(i.e.,
\theta=\theta^{\star}
is the true parameter). Then, conditional on
(s_{t},a_{t})
:

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{imm}}|s_{t},a_{t}]+\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]

(22)

That is, the target is conditionally unbiased for the true expected reward.

Bias with DCM Approximation Error:
If
m_{\theta}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[r^{\text{delay}}|s,a]+\varepsilon_{\text{DCM}}(s,a)
where
|\varepsilon_{\text{DCM}}(s,a)|\leq\varepsilon
, then:

\bigl|\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]\bigr|\leq(1-p_{\text{mat}})\cdot\varepsilon

(23)

where
p_{\text{mat}}:=\mathbb{P}(\text{delay matured at time }t)
is the maturation probability.

Proof.

Expand the doubly-robust target:

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{t}

\displaystyle=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})+\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}\bigl(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+r_{t}^{\text{delay}}&\text{if delay matured}\\
r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})&\text{if delay not matured}\end{cases}

Taking conditional expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{imm}}|s_{t},a_{t}]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})\cdot(1-\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}})+r_{t}^{\text{delay}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{imm}}|s_{t},a_{t}]+(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})+p_{\text{mat}}\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]

When
m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{imm}}|s_{t},a_{t}]+\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]

With approximation error
\varepsilon_{\text{DCM}}(s_{t},a_{t})
:

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]-\mathbb{E}[r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t}]\bigr|

\displaystyle=(1-p_{\text{mat}})|\varepsilon_{\text{DCM}}(s_{t},a_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-p_{\text{mat}})\varepsilon

∎

Interpretation:
The bias scales with
(1-p_{\text{mat}})
, i.e., the fraction of delays that have not yet matured. As more delays mature over time, the effective bias shrinks. When all delays mature (
p_{\text{mat}}=1
), bias is zero regardless of DCM accuracy.

10.2.3
Variance Analysis

Lemma 6

(Variance of Doubly-Robust Target)
.

Let
\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}:=\text{Var}(r_{t}^{\text{imm}}|s_{t},a_{t})
and
\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}:=\text{Var}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t})
be the conditional variances of immediate and delayed rewards, and let
m_{\theta}:=m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]
denote the DCM’s expected delayed reward. Then:

\text{Var}(\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t})=\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}^{2}

(24)

Under centered rewards (
m_{\theta}=0
), this simplifies to
\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}
.

Comparison to Naive Estimators:

1.

Imputation-only
(ignore delayed realizations):
\tilde{r}_{t}^{\text{imp}}:=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})

\text{Var}(\tilde{r}_{t}^{\text{imp}}|s_{t},a_{t})=\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}\quad\text{(lower variance but biased)}

(25)

2.

Wait-for-maturity
(use
r_{t}
only when delay matures):

\text{Var}(r_{t}|s_{t},a_{t},\text{matured})=\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}\quad\text{(unbiased but slower learning)}

(26)

The doubly-robust target achieves a
bias-variance tradeoff
:

\text{MSE}(\tilde{r}_{t})=\underbrace{(1-p_{\text{mat}})^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}_{\text{Squared bias from DCM error}}+\underbrace{\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}^{2}}_{\text{Variance}}

(27)

Proof.

We make the following assumptions, which are standard in revenue management:

(i)

Independence
:
r_{t}^{\text{imm}}
,
r_{t}^{\text{delay}}
, and
\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}
are mutually independent conditional on
(s_{t},a_{t})

(ii)

Correct DCM specification
:
m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}|s_{t},a_{t}]
, so that the correction term
r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})
has conditional mean zero

Decompose the variance (all expectations and variances are conditional on
(s_{t},a_{t})
):

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\bigl(r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})+\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t}))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(r_{t}^{\text{imm}})+\text{Var}\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t}))\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+2\text{Cov}\bigl(r_{t}^{\text{imm}},\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t}))\bigr)

where the
m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})
term contributes zero variance since it is deterministic given
(s_{t},a_{t})
.

By assumption (i), the covariance term vanishes. For the second variance term, since
m_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})
is deterministic:

\text{Var}\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta})\bigr)=\text{Var}\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}r_{t}^{\text{delay}}\bigr)

For the product of independent random variables
\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}
and
r_{t}^{\text{delay}}
:

\displaystyle\text{Var}\bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}r_{t}^{\text{delay}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[(r_{t}^{\text{delay}})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}]^{2}\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}]^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{\text{mat}}(\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}+m_{\theta}^{2})-p_{\text{mat}}^{2}m_{\theta}^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{\text{mat}}\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}^{2}

where we used
\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}]=p_{\text{mat}}
and
\mathbb{E}[r_{t}^{\text{delay}}]=m_{\theta}
by assumption (ii).

Remark:
The term
p_{\text{mat}}(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}^{2}
arises from the variance of the indicator variable
\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}
. This term vanishes when rewards are centered (
m_{\theta}=0
) or when
p_{\text{mat}}\in\{0,1\}
. In practice, for revenue management applications where
m_{\theta}
represents expected delayed revenue, this term may be non-negligible.

Combining all terms yields the stated result:

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\tilde{r}_{t}|s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}+p_{\text{mat}}(1-p_{\text{mat}})m_{\theta}^{2}

∎

Interpretation:
The doubly-robust target has variance between imputation-only (
\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}
) and wait-for-maturity (
\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}
), controlled by the maturation probability
p_{\text{mat}}
. As delays mature, variance increases but bias decreases.

10.2.4
Connection to Doubly-Robust Estimation in Causal Inference

The term “doubly-robust” originates from causal inference, where estimators combine:

1.

Outcome regression model
:
\mathbb{E}[Y|X,A]
(analogous to our DCM imputation
m_{\theta}(s,a)
)

2.

Propensity score model
:
\mathbb{P}(A|X)
(not applicable in our deterministic pricing setting)

Our formulation simplifies the classical doubly-robust structure because:

•

Actions
a_{t}
are chosen by the agent (no propensity score needed)

•

The “treatment effect” is the delayed reward realization
r_{t}^{\text{delay}}

•

The correction term
\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta})
removes imputation bias when true outcomes materialize

Robustness Property:
In causal inference, doubly-robust estimators are unbiased if
either
the outcome model
or
the propensity model is correct. In our setting, the target is unbiased when:

1.

m_{\theta}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}[r^{\text{delay}}|s,a]
(correct DCM),
or

2.

p_{\text{mat}}=1
(all delays matured),
or

3.

Both partially hold (bias degrades gracefully with
(1-p_{\text{mat}})\varepsilon
)

This dual protection against model misspecification and incomplete data justifies the “doubly-robust” terminology.

10.2.5
Empirical Calibration

In our experiments:

•

p_{\text{mat}}(t)
grows from
\approx 0.05
(early training) to
\approx 0.85
(late training) as historical delays mature

•

DCM prediction error on delayed component:
\varepsilon\approx 0.12
(12% MAE relative to delayed reward magnitude; corresponds to $2.30 absolute MAE on total reward, or
<2\%
of mean booking revenue)

•

Effective bias:
(1-0.85)\times 0.12=0.018
(1.8% of reward scale)

•

Variance inflation:
1+0.85\times(\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}/\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2})\approx 1.3
(30% increase)

The bias-variance tradeoff empirically favors doubly-robust targets over wait-for-maturity (slower) and imputation-only (biased).

Remark 4

(Relationship Between Model-Imputed Sampling and Doubly-Robust Targets)
.

Both
model-imputed sampling
(Algorithm 1) and the
doubly-robust target
(analyzed above) leverage the DCM to handle delayed feedback, but they differ in mechanism:

1.

Model-imputed sampling
: At each step, sample
j^{\prime}\sim P(j|s_{t},a_{t};\theta)
from the DCM and use the synthetic reward
r^{\prime}_{t}=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+r_{j^{\prime}}^{\text{delay}}
. This generates unbiased synthetic samples when the DCM is correct, but variance equals that of the full reward (no variance reduction).

2.

Doubly-robust target
: Use
\tilde{r}_{t}=r_{t}^{\text{imm}}+m_{\theta}+\mathds{1}_{\{\text{matured}\}}(r_{t}^{\text{delay}}-m_{\theta})
. This achieves variance reduction when delays are pending (variance
=\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}
instead of
\sigma_{\text{imm}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{delay}}^{2}
), while remaining unbiased when the DCM is correct.

When to use which?
Model-imputed sampling is simpler to implement and generates complete synthetic transitions
(r^{\prime}_{t},s^{\prime}_{t+1})
, enabling model-based planning. Doubly-robust targets are preferred when variance reduction is critical and only the reward (not the next state) needs imputation. Our implementation uses model-imputed sampling for its simplicity and compatibility with standard DQN architectures.

10.3
Proposition: Strict Concavity of the MNL Log-Likelihood

Proposition 7

(Strict Concavity of the MNL Log-Likelihood)
.

Consider a multinomial logit (MNL) model with
K+1
discrete outcomes

\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
, where outcome
0
is the “base” or
“outside” option with utility normalized to zero. Suppose that

(A1)

No Degeneracy in Covariates and Probabilities
:
The covariates
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
(or their distribution)
provide sufficient variability so that the Hessian of the
log-likelihood (or its expected version) does not collapse to a
lower-rank matrix.
6
6

6

For example, in multinomial logit, one requires
that there be no perfect collinearity among the regressors
for different alternatives, and that each outcome can occur
with nonzero probability across the support of
\mathbf{x}
.

Then, the log-likelihood function

\ell(\theta)\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\Pr\{y_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n},\theta\}

is strictly concave in
\theta
. Consequently, there is a unique global maximizer

\hat{\theta}
that solves
\max_{\theta}\,\ell(\theta)
.

Proof.

1. Single-observation log-likelihood and gradient.

For a single observation
(\mathbf{x},y)
, the contribution to the log-likelihood is:

\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\;=\;\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x}\}.

Because
\beta_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, we have:

\Pr\{y=0\mid\mathbf{x}\}\;=\;\frac{1}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\beta_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x})},\quad\Pr\{y=m\mid\mathbf{x}\}\;=\;\frac{\exp(\beta_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\beta_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}\;\;\text{for }m=1,\dots,K.

Hence,

\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\;=\;\begin{cases}-\,\ln\!\Bigl(1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\beta_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\Bigr),&\quad\text{if }y=0,\\[10.0pt]
\beta_{y}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\;-\;\ln\!\Bigl(1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\beta_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\Bigr),&\quad\text{if }y\neq 0.\end{cases}

Define
p_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\Pr\{y=k\mid\mathbf{x}\}
for compactness.
A routine differentiation gives

\nabla_{\beta_{k}}\,\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}\;=\;\mathbf{x}\,\bigl[\mathbb{I}\{y=k\}\;-\;p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigr].

2. Second derivative (Hessian) for a single observation.

We now compute
\partial^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}/(\partial\beta_{k}\,\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top})
.
Focus on

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\,\Bigl(\mathbf{x}\,\bigl[\mathbb{I}\{y=k\}-p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigr]\Bigr)=\mathbf{x}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\bigl[\mathbb{I}\{y=k\}-p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigr].

Since
\mathbb{I}\{y=k\}
is constant w.r.t.
\beta_{\ell}
, the derivative depends only on
p_{k}(\mathbf{x})
.
Recall that

p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\frac{\exp(\beta_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(\beta_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{x})}.

It follows that

\frac{\partial p_{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\;=\;\begin{cases}p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,\bigl[\mathbf{x}\bigr],&\quad\text{if }\ell=k,\\[3.0pt]
-\,p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,p_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\,\bigl[\mathbf{x}\bigr],&\quad\text{if }\ell\neq k.\end{cases}

Equivalently, in a single formula one can write

\frac{\partial p_{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\;=\;p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,\Bigl[\delta_{k\ell}-p_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\Bigr]\;\mathbf{x}^{\top}.

Thus

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\bigl[-\,p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigr]\;=\;-\,\Bigl[p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\delta_{k\ell}-p_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathbf{x}.

Putting it all together, we get

\frac{\partial^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}}{\partial\beta_{k}\,\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\;=\;\mathbf{x}\,\Bigl[0-\frac{\partial p_{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}\Bigr]\;=\;-\,\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\,\Bigl[p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,\bigl(\delta_{k\ell}-p_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\Bigr].

By inspecting off-diagonal
(k\neq\ell)
and diagonal
(k=\ell)
, one recovers the
well-known structure:

\displaystyle\text{(off-diagonal)\quad}k\neq\ell:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}}{\partial\beta_{k}\,\partial\beta_{\ell}^{\top}}

\displaystyle=+\,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\,\bigl[p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\,p_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\bigr],

\displaystyle\text{(diagonal)\quad}k=\ell:

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\partial^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}}{\partial\beta_{k}\,\partial\beta_{k}^{\top}}

\displaystyle=-\,\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\,\bigl[p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(1-p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)\bigr].

3. Hessian for the full sample: concavity.

Collecting the blocks for
k,\ell\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, we obtain the Hessian for a *single* observation:

\nabla^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x},y)}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\;=\;-\,\Bigl[\mathrm{diag}\bigl(p(\mathbf{x})\bigr)-p(\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\Bigr]\;\otimes\;\bigl(\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bigr),

where
p(\mathbf{x})=\bigl(p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,p_{K}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)^{\top}
.
Summing over all
n=1,\dots,N
,

\nabla^{2}\ell(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\;=\;\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla^{2}\ell_{(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\;=\;-\,\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigl[\mathrm{diag}\bigl(p(\mathbf{x}_{n})\bigr)\;-\;p(\mathbf{x}_{n})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\Bigr]\;\otimes\;\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{n}\,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\top}\bigr).

Since each matrix
\mathrm{diag}\bigl(p(\mathbf{x}_{n})\bigr)-p(\mathbf{x}_{n})\,p(\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}

is positive semi-definite (for
p(\mathbf{x}_{n})
in the probability simplex)
and
\mathbf{x}_{n}\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\top}
is also positive semi-definite, the
overall sign in front is negative. This shows
\nabla^{2}\ell
is *negative semi-definite*,
hence
\ell
is concave in
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})
.

4. Strict concavity under identification conditions.

To remove the additive shift indeterminacy, we fix
\beta_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
Under the assumptions:

(i)

the design matrix has full column rank (i.e., no perfect collinearity), and

(ii)

each outcome
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
attains
p_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{n})>0
for some set of
n

(with positive measure),

one can show that the negative semi-definite Hessian above is *in fact negative-definite*
when restricted to the identified subspace (i.e., directions that do not alter
the baseline’s parameter nor violate the linear-independence condition).
In other words, for any nonzero
v
in the identified parameter subspace,

v^{\top}\Bigl[\nabla^{2}\ell(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})\Bigr]\,v<0.

Thus the log-likelihood is *strictly concave* (negative-definite) on the parameter space
modulo that identification choice. Consequently, there is a unique global maximizer
for
\ell(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K})
once
\beta_{0}=\mathbf{0}
is set.

∎

10.4
Proposition: Parameter Convergence of Recurrent MLE

Proposition 8

(Parameter Convergence of Recurrent MLE)
.

Let
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the true parameter vector governing the choice model. For each block
t=1,2,\dots
, define

\ell_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\,\ln\Pr\bigl(y_{t,i}\,\mid\,\mathbf{x}_{t,i},\theta\bigr),\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\theta}_{t}\;=\;\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\ell_{t}(\theta).

Suppose that the following assumptions hold:

(A2)

Large Samples:
Each block
t
has sample size
N_{t}\to\infty
almost surely as
t\to\infty
.

(A3)

Ergodicity or i.i.d. Blocks:
The sets
\{(\mathbf{x}_{t,i},y_{t,i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}}
either are i.i.d. across
t
or satisfy mixing conditions sufficient to invoke a strong law of large numbers.

Then it holds that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\|\hat{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star}\|\;=\;0\quad\text{almost surely.}

Proof.

Step 1. Define the population log-likelihood.

Let
\bigl(\mathbf{x},y\bigr)
be a generic observation drawn from the true data-generating distribution. Define the
population
or
expected
log-likelihood:

L(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,\ln\Pr\bigl(y\,\mid\,\mathbf{x},\theta\bigr)\bigr].

By Proposition
7
,
L(\theta)
is well-defined and continuous on the compact set
\Theta
. Moreover,
L(\theta)
is strictly concave in a neighborhood of
\theta^{\star}
and has a unique global maximizer at
\theta^{\star}
.

Step 2. Blockwise sample log-likelihood and convergence.

For block
t
, the sample log-likelihood is

\ell_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\,\ln\Pr\!\bigl(y_{t,i}\,\mid\,\mathbf{x}_{t,i},\theta\bigr).

Consider the
average
log-likelihood:

\frac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{1}{N_{t}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\,\ln\Pr\!\bigl(y_{t,i}\,\mid\,\mathbf{x}_{t,i},\theta\bigr).

Under Assumption on ergodicity or i.i.d. sampling and Assumption (
N_{t}\to\infty
, a strong law of large numbers argument implies that for each fixed
\theta\in\Theta
,

\frac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{a.s.}\;}L(\theta)\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

(28)

This gives
pointwise
convergence of the sample criterion to the population criterion.

Step 3. Uniform convergence.

We next show that the convergence in (
28
) is in fact
uniform
over
\theta\in\Theta
. Since
\Theta
is compact and
\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta\}
is assumed continuous (and bounded from above) in
\theta
, standard results in M-estimation guarantee:

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\Bigl|\,\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)-L(\theta)\Bigr|\;\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{a.s.}\;}0,\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Uniform convergence will be crucial to control the behavior of the sample maximizer
\hat{\theta}_{t}
.

Step 4. Consequences for the sample maximizer.

By definition,

\hat{\theta}_{t}\;=\;\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)\bigr\}.

Hence,

\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{t}\bigr)\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)\quad\text{for all }\theta\in\Theta.

By the uniform convergence result,

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\,\Bigl|\,\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}(\theta)-L(\theta)\Bigr|\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{a.s.}\;}0,\quad\text{thus}\quad\tfrac{1}{N_{t}}\,\ell_{t}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{t}\bigr)\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{a.s.}\;}L\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{t}\bigr).

But since
\hat{\theta}_{t}
depends on
t
, we need to show it converges to
\theta^{\star}
. Suppose for contradiction that
\hat{\theta}_{t}
did
not
approach
\theta^{\star}
. Because
\Theta
is compact, there is a subsequence
\hat{\theta}_{t_{k}}
converging to some
\theta_{\infty}\neq\theta^{\star}
. By the strict concavity and uniqueness result from Proposition
7
,

L(\theta^{\star})\;>\;L(\theta_{\infty}).

Meanwhile, uniform convergence implies

\lim_{k\to\infty}\,\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{N_{t_{k}}}\,\ell_{t_{k}}(\hat{\theta}_{t_{k}})-L(\hat{\theta}_{t_{k}})\bigr\}\;=\;0,

and similarly for any
\theta
. Thus, for sufficiently large
k
,

\tfrac{1}{N_{t_{k}}}\,\ell_{t_{k}}(\hat{\theta}_{t_{k}})\;<\;\tfrac{1}{N_{t_{k}}}\,\ell_{t_{k}}(\theta^{\star})-\delta

for some positive
\delta
(because
L(\theta^{\star})-L(\theta_{\infty})
remains strictly positive in the limit). This contradicts the definition of
\hat{\theta}_{t_{k}}
as the global maximizer over
\Theta
. Consequently, no such subsequence can converge to
\theta_{\infty}\neq\theta^{\star}
, implying

\hat{\theta}_{t}\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{a.s.}\;}\theta^{\star}.

That is,
\|\hat{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star}\|\to 0
almost surely.

Step 5. Concluding remarks.

We have shown that every sequence of maximizers
\{\hat{\theta}_{t}\}
converges almost surely to the unique maximizer
\theta^{\star}
of the population log-likelihood. This completes the proof of consistency of the recurrent MLE.
∎

Proposition: Full-Rank Fisher Information for DCM

Proposition 9

(Full-Rank Fisher Information for DCM)
.

Under the assumption (A1)-(A3),
Then, under these conditions, the Fisher information matrix

\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})
defined by

\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\bigl(-\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\bigr)\;\nabla_{\theta}\bigl(-\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\bigr)^{\top}\Bigr]

is
full rank
(i.e. positive definite).

Proof.

Step 1: Relate Fisher Information to the Expected Hessian.

Recall that the Fisher information matrix at
\theta^{\star}
for i.i.d. samples

\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}
has two well-known equivalent definitions:

\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\bigl(-\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\bigr)\;\nabla_{\theta}\bigl(-\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\bigr)^{\top}\Bigr]\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\bigl(-\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta\}\bigr)\bigm|_{\theta=\theta^{\star}}\Bigr].

The first expression shows it is the covariance (in parameter space) of the

score function
; the second expresses it as the
negative
of the
expected Hessian of the log-likelihood.

Step 2: Strict Concavity Implies Negative-Definite Hessian.

By Proposition
7
(or its population analogue),
the log-likelihood (or population log-likelihood) is
strictly concave

near
\theta^{\star}
. Concretely, let

L(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta\}\bigr].

Strict concavity around
\theta^{\star}
means that the Hessian

\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,L(\theta^{\star})

is
strictly negative definite
. Equivalently, for any nonzero vector

\mathbf{u}
,

\mathbf{u}^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,L(\theta^{\star})\,\mathbf{u}\;<\;0.

Since

\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,L(\theta^{\star})\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,\ln\Pr\{y\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\Bigr]\;=\;-\,\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star}),

we see that
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,L(\theta^{\star})=-\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})
.
Hence
\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\,L(\theta^{\star})
being
negative definite

exactly means
\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})
is
positive definite
.

∎

MNL Convergence Rate Analysis

The convergence properties of MNL maximum likelihood estimation are well-established in the econometrics literature. We state the key results here and refer to standard references for detailed proofs.

Theorem 10

(Convergence Rate of MNL)

\hat{\theta}_{N}\xrightarrow{p}\theta^{\star}

\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta}_{N}-\theta^{\star})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})^{-1})

Proof.

This is a classical result in discrete choice econometrics. The strict concavity of the MNL log-likelihood (established in Proposition
8
) ensures global convergence of the MLE. The
\sqrt{N}
-rate and asymptotic normality follow from standard M-estimation theory: a Taylor expansion of the score around
\theta^{\star}
, combined with the central limit theorem for the score function and convergence of the Hessian to the Fisher information. See
32
Chapter 3,
22
, and
3
Chapter 5 for complete proofs.

Theorem 11

(Convergence Rate of Predicted Choice Probabilities)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
10
, for each fixed covariate vector
\mathbf{x}
and alternative
k
:

\Bigl|\Pr\{y=k\mid\mathbf{x},\hat{\theta}_{N}\}-\Pr\{y=k\mid\mathbf{x},\theta^{\star}\}\Bigr|=O_{p}(N^{-1/2})

and

\sqrt{N}\bigl(p_{k}(\mathbf{x};\hat{\theta}_{N})-p_{k}(\mathbf{x};\theta^{\star})\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\mathbf{J}_{k}(\theta^{\star})\mathbf{I}(\theta^{\star})^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{k}(\theta^{\star})^{\top}\bigr)

where
\mathbf{J}_{k}(\theta^{\star})=\nabla_{\theta}p_{k}(\mathbf{x};\theta)|_{\theta=\theta^{\star}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times m}
is the Jacobian.

Proof.

Immediate by the Delta Method applied to the smooth mapping
\theta\mapsto p_{k}(\mathbf{x};\theta)
.
∎

t

), the DCM approximation error decays as

Delayed Feedback Analysis

This section analyzes the delayed feedback structure in our revenue management setting. The theoretical treatment of MDPs with delayed rewards has a substantial literature
[
16
]
, establishing foundations for asynchronous cost collection and delayed state observation.

12.1
Maturation Rate Lemma: Linking Delay Distribution to Sample Size Growth

A critical component of our theoretical framework is establishing that the number of
matured
booking outcomes observed by time
t
grows linearly with
t
. This maturation rate determines how quickly the DCM accumulates training data, which in turn governs the approximation error decay rate
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-1/2})
in Assumption A6.

Lemma 12

(Maturation Rate for Delayed Feedback)
.

Consider the delayed feedback model where:

(i)

Each customer arrival at time
s
generates a booking decision that matures (i.e., becomes observable) at time
s+D_{s}
, where
D_{s}
is the random delay for the customer arriving at time
s

(ii)

Delays
\{D_{s}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d. random variables with:

•

Finite support:
D_{s}\in\{0,1,\ldots,D_{\max}\}
for some
D_{\max}<\infty

•

Finite mean:
\mathbb{E}[D_{s}]=\mu_{D}<\infty

(iii)

Customer arrivals occur at a stationary rate: on average,
\lambda>0
customers arrive per time period

Define
N_{t}
as the number of matured observations by time
t
:

N_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{s+D_{s}\leq t\}=\#\{\text{customers arriving at }s\leq t\text{ whose decisions matured by time }t\}

(29)

Then:

(a)

Almost-sure linear growth
:
N_{t}/t\xrightarrow{a.s.}\lambda
as
t\to\infty

(b)

Concentration
: For any
\epsilon>0
and all
t\geq 2CD_{\max}/\epsilon
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{N_{t}}{t}-\lambda\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2D_{\max}}\right)

(30)

for some universal constant
C>0
. Note the quadratic dependence on
t
in the exponent: the concentration becomes exponentially tight as
t
grows, because only the
D_{\max}
most recent arrivals contribute randomness (all earlier arrivals have deterministically matured)

(c)

Reciprocal convergence
:
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}}}=\Theta(t^{-1/2})
almost surely

Proof.

We establish each part separately.

Part (a): Almost-sure linear growth.

Fix time
t
and decompose the matured count:

\displaystyle N_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{s+D_{s}\leq t\}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq t-s\}

(31)

The key observation is that since
D_{\max}<\infty
, any customer arriving at time
s\leq t-D_{\max}
has their decision matured by time
t
with probability 1 (since the maximum delay is
D_{\max}
). This yields:

N_{t}\geq\sum_{s=1}^{t-D_{\max}}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq D_{\max}\}=t-D_{\max}

(32)

and trivially
N_{t}\leq t
.

For the expected value:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(D_{s}\leq t-s)

(33)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{s=1}^{t-D_{\max}}1}_{=t-D_{\max}}+\underbrace{\sum_{s=t-D_{\max}+1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(D\leq t-s)}_{=\sum_{k=0}^{D_{\max}-1}\mathbb{P}(D\leq k)}

(34)

\displaystyle=t-D_{\max}+\sum_{k=0}^{D_{\max}-1}\mathbb{P}(D\leq k)

(35)

where the second sum is bounded by
D_{\max}
. Thus
\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]=t+O(D_{\max})
(for
\lambda=1
; the general case gives
\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]=\lambda t+O(D_{\max})
).

For almost-sure convergence, decompose
N_{t}=N_{t}^{\text{old}}+N_{t}^{\text{recent}}
where:

•

N_{t}^{\text{old}}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t-D_{\max}}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq t-s\}=t-D_{\max}
(deterministic, all old arrivals have matured)

•

N_{t}^{\text{recent}}:=\sum_{s=t-D_{\max}+1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq t-s\}
(at most
D_{\max}
terms)

Since
|N_{t}^{\text{recent}}|\leq D_{\max}
, we have:

\frac{N_{t}}{t}=\frac{t-D_{\max}+N_{t}^{\text{recent}}}{t}=1-\frac{D_{\max}}{t}+\frac{N_{t}^{\text{recent}}}{t}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\lambda

(36)

as
t\to\infty
(for
\lambda=1
arrival per period; the general case follows by rescaling time).

Part (b): Concentration.

We decompose the deviation into two parts. For the “old” arrivals (
s\leq t-D_{\max}
), the maturation is deterministic, so there is no randomness. For the “recent” arrivals (
s>t-D_{\max}
), define:

Z_{s}:=\mathds{1}\{D_{s}\leq t-s\}-\mathbb{P}(D_{s}\leq t-s)

(37)

as zero-mean random variables with
|Z_{s}|\leq 1
. Since there are at most
D_{\max}
such terms:

N_{t}-\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]=\sum_{s=t-D_{\max}+1}^{t}Z_{s}

(38)

By Hoeffding’s inequality applied to these
D_{\max}
independent bounded variables:

\mathbb{P}(|N_{t}-\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]|>\epsilon t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2(\epsilon t)^{2}}{D_{\max}}\right)

(39)

Since
|\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]-\lambda t|\leq C_{1}D_{\max}
for some constant
C_{1}
, we apply the triangle inequality. If
\left|\frac{N_{t}}{t}-\lambda\right|>\epsilon
, then either
\left|\frac{N_{t}-\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]}{t}\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
or
\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]-\lambda t}{t}\right|>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

The second event occurs only when
\frac{C_{1}D_{\max}}{t}>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, i.e., when
t<\frac{2C_{1}D_{\max}}{\epsilon}
.

For the first event, we use the Hoeffding bound with
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/2
:

\mathbb{P}\left(|N_{t}-\mathbb{E}[N_{t}]|>\frac{\epsilon t}{2}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2(\epsilon t/2)^{2}}{D_{\max}}\right)=2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2D_{\max}}\right)

(40)

For
t\geq\frac{2C_{1}D_{\max}}{\epsilon}
, the deterministic bias term satisfies
\frac{C_{1}D_{\max}}{t}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, so:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{N_{t}}{t}-\lambda\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}t^{2}}{2D_{\max}}\right)

(41)

which is the stated bound with
C=C_{1}
.

Part (c): Reciprocal convergence.

From part (a),
N_{t}=\lambda t+O(D_{\max})
almost surely. Therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{t}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda t+O(D_{\max})}}

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda t}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+O(D_{\max}/t)}}

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\cdot t^{-1/2}\cdot(1+O(D_{\max}/t))

(44)

\displaystyle=\Theta(t^{-1/2})

(45)

This establishes the desired rate.
∎

Remark 1 (Implication for DCM Convergence Rate).

Lemma
12
directly implies that the DCM parameter estimation error at time
t
satisfies:

\|\hat{\theta}_{N_{t}}-\theta^{\star}\|=O_{p}(N_{t}^{-1/2})=O_{p}(t^{-1/2})

(46)

This establishes the
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-1/2})
decay rate required in Assumption A6 for the Q-learning convergence analysis.

Remark 2 (Episodic Structure).

In the hotel revenue management application (Section 6 of main paper), each episode spans
H=14
days (booking window). Customers arrive throughout the episode, with delays
D_{s}\in\{0,1,\ldots,14\}
representing days until booking maturation. The episodic reset ensures that delays do not exceed the episode horizon, satisfying the finite support condition
D_{\max}=14<\infty
.

Remark 3 (Comparison to Fixed DCM Case).

In our empirical experiments (Section
15
), we use a
fixed
pre-calibrated DCM with
N_{0}=61,619
historical observations. In this case:

•

The DCM error
\varepsilon_{0}=O(N_{0}^{-1/2})\approx 0.004
remains constant

•

The Q-learning analysis applies with
\varepsilon_{t}=\varepsilon_{0}
(no decay)

•

Convergence rate becomes
\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O(t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log t}+\varepsilon_{0})

•

For large
N_{0}

\varepsilon_{0}

The maturation rate lemma justifies why

13
Q-Learning Convergence

This section establishes convergence of Q-learning under time-varying approximation error. Our analysis connects to the robust MDP literature
[
13
]
, where the DCM can be viewed as providing a nominal transition model with bounded uncertainty. The key insight is that Q-learning remains stable when the approximation error is bounded and decays appropriately.

13.1
Theorem: Nonstationary Q-learning with Shrinking Approximation Error and Rate

Theorem 13

(Nonstationary Q-learning with Shrinking Approximation Error and Rate)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,P,r,\gamma)
be a Markov decision process (MDP) with:

•

finite state space
S
,

•

finite action space
A
,

•

true transition kernel
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
,

•

bounded rewards
r(s,a)\in[0,R_{\max}]
for some
R_{\max}>0
,

•

discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

•

(Ergodicity) The MDP is ergodic, meaning that for any stationary policy, the induced Markov chain is irreducible and aperiodic.

•

(Exploration) The agent follows an
\epsilon
-greedy policy with a fixed
\epsilon>0
.

Let
Q^{\star}
be the unique optimal Q-function associated with
\mathcal{M}
, i.e., the unique fixed point of the Bellman operator

(TQ)(s,a)\;=\;r(s,a)\;+\;\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Suppose we run Q-learning in a nonstationary environment

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{t}\;=\;\bigl(S,\,A,\,\widetilde{P}_{t},\,\widetilde{r}_{t},\,\gamma\bigr),

where for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
,

\|\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot\mid s,a)-P(\cdot\mid s,a)\|_{1}\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{t},\quad\bigl|\widetilde{r}_{t}(s,a)-r(s,a)\bigr|\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{t},

with
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
(e.g.,
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-\beta})
for some
\beta>0
).

Let
n(s,a)
denote the number of times state-action pair
(s,a)
has been visited up to the current time. The asynchronous Q-learning update with per-pair stepsizes is:

Q_{t+1}(s_{t},a_{t})\;=\;Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\;+\;\alpha_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\Bigl[\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\;+\;\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})\;-\;Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\Bigr],

where
\alpha_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})=1/n(s_{t},a_{t})
, and
Q_{t+1}(s,a)=Q_{t}(s,a)
for all
(s,a)\neq(s_{t},a_{t})
. Here,
(s_{t+1},\widetilde{r}_{t})
are sampled from
\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t})
,
\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})
, respectively.

The per-pair stepsizes
\{\alpha_{k}(s,a)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
for each fixed
(s,a)
satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions:

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}(s,a)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}(s,a)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}<\infty,

and we assume each
(s,a)
is visited infinitely often according to an
\epsilon
-greedy exploration strategy.

Then the following two statements hold:

1.

Almost-sure convergence:
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}\;=\;0

with probability
1
.

2.

Finite-time rate with high probability:

If
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-\beta})
for some
\beta>0
, and under
\epsilon
-greedy exploration each state-action pair is visited
\Theta(t)
times by time
t
(so the per-pair stepsize
\alpha_{t}(s,a)=1/n(s,a)=O(1/t)
for frequently visited pairs), then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O\left(t^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(47)

In particular, if
\beta=1/2
, we get
\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O\left(t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\log(|S||A|t/\delta)}\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
13

Proof.

We provide a detailed proof in six steps:

1.

Analysis of the stationary Bellman operator and its contraction properties

2.

Characterization of the time-varying Bellman operators and their approximation error

3.

Formulation of the asynchronous Q-learning updates

4.

Analysis of the martingale difference noise

5.

Proof of almost-sure convergence using stochastic approximation theory

6.

Derivation of finite-time high-probability bounds

Step 1: Properties of the stationary Bellman operator.

For the
stationary
MDP
(S,A,P,r,\gamma)
, the Bellman optimality operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
is defined as:

(TQ)(s,a)\;=\;r(s,a)\;+\;\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

It is known that
T
has the following properties:

(a)

T
is a
\gamma
-contraction in the sup-norm, i.e., for any
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
:

\|TQ-TQ^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\gamma\,\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

(48)

(b)

By the Banach fixed-point theorem,
T
has a unique fixed point
Q^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
such that
Q^{\star}=TQ^{\star}
. This fixed point is the optimal Q-function for the MDP.

(c)

The fixed point
Q^{\star}
satisfies for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
:

Q^{\star}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\star}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

(49)

Step 2: Time-varying Bellman operators and their approximation error.

In the
nonstationary
environment
(S,A,\widetilde{P}_{t},\widetilde{r}_{t},\gamma)
, we define the time-varying Bellman operator
T_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
as:

(T_{t}Q)(s,a)\;=\;\widetilde{r}_{t}(s,a)\;+\;\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

Lemma 14

(Approximation Error of Time-varying Operators)
.

For any bounded
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
, define the reward error
\varepsilon_{r,t}:=|\widetilde{r}_{t}-r|_{\infty}
and transition error
\varepsilon_{P,t}:=\|\widetilde{P}_{t}-P\|_{1}
. Then:

\|T_{t}Q-TQ\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\varepsilon_{r,t}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P,t}

where
V_{\max}=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
is an upper bound on the maximum value function.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

For any
(s,a)\in S\times A
, we have:

\displaystyle|(T_{t}Q)(s,a)-(TQ)(s,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\widetilde{r}_{t}(s,a)-r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}|s,a)-P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\right)\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right|

(50)

\displaystyle\leq|\widetilde{r}_{t}(s,a)-r(s,a)|+\gamma\left|\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}|s,a)-P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\right)\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\right|

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{r,t}+\gamma\|Q\|_{\infty}\|\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot|s,a)-P(\cdot|s,a)\|_{1}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{r,t}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P,t}

(53)

The third inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality and the fact that
\|\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(\cdot,a^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq\|Q\|_{\infty}
. For any Q-function in an MDP with rewards bounded by
R_{\max}
, we have
\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq V_{\max}=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
.

Since this holds for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
, we have
\|T_{t}Q-TQ\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{r,t}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P,t}
.

Remark (Scaling interpretation):
When propagated through the Q-learning analysis with discount factor
\gamma
, the reward error contributes
O(\varepsilon_{r}/(1-\gamma))
while the transition error contributes
O(\varepsilon_{P}\cdot V_{\max}/(1-\gamma))=O(\varepsilon_{P}/(1-\gamma)^{2})
. This quadratic dependence on
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
for transition errors is consistent with standard simulation lemma bounds in the model-based RL literature.
∎

For notational convenience in subsequent analysis, we define the combined approximation error
\varepsilon_{t}:=\varepsilon_{r,t}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P,t}
and constant
C_{1}=1
, giving
\|T_{t}Q-TQ\|_{\infty}\leq C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}
. When tracking the separate scaling, we use
\varepsilon_{r,t}
and
\varepsilon_{P,t}
explicitly.

Since
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, we have
T_{t}\to T
in the operator norm, meaning the time-varying operators converge to the stationary operator.

Step 3: Asynchronous Q-learning updates.

The Q-learning algorithm updates one state-action pair at a time according to:

Q_{t+1}(s_{t},a_{t})=Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\alpha_{t}\Bigl[\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}\bigl(s_{t+1},a^{\prime}\bigr)\;-\;Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\Bigr],

where
(s_{t},a_{t})
is the state-action pair visited at time
t
, and
s_{t+1}
is the next state sampled according to
\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
. All other entries remain unchanged:
Q_{t+1}(s,a)=Q_{t}(s,a)
for
(s,a)\neq(s_{t},a_{t})
.

We can rewrite this update in vector form as:

Q_{t+1}\;=\;Q_{t}\;+\;\alpha_{t}\,e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}\,\Bigl[\bigl(T_{t}Q_{t}\bigr)\!\bigl(s_{t},a_{t}\bigr)\;-\;Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\;+\;\delta_{t}\Bigr],

where
e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}
is the standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
with 1 in the
(s_{t},a_{t})
component and 0 elsewhere, and
\delta_{t}
is the sampling noise defined as:

\delta_{t}\;:=\;\bigl[\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr]\;-\;\bigl[(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr].

Step 4: Martingale difference noise analysis.

We now analyze the noise term
\delta_{t}
in detail.

Lemma 15

(Martingale Difference Property)
.

The noise term
\delta_{t}
satisfies:

(a)

\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\bigl(\{(s_{u},a_{u},Q_{u}):u\leq t\}\bigr)
is the filtration representing the history of the algorithm up to time
t
.

(b)

|\delta_{t}|\leq 2V_{\max}
almost surely.

Proof of Lemma
15
.

(a) By definition:

\displaystyle\delta_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigl[\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr]-\bigl[(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})\bigr]

(54)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})-(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})

(55)

Given the history
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the Q-values
Q_{t}
are determined. The distribution of
s_{t+1}
is
\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\mathbb{E}[\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]-(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})

(56)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}|s_{t},a_{t})\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})

(57)

\displaystyle=(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})

(58)

\displaystyle=0

(59)

(b) Both terms
\widetilde{r}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{t}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime})
and
(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})
are bounded by
V_{\max}
since Q-values in an MDP with rewards bounded by
R_{\max}
are bounded by
V_{\max}=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
. Therefore,
|\delta_{t}|\leq 2V_{\max}
.
∎

Based on Lemma
15
,
\{\delta_{t}\}
forms a bounded martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
. This property will be crucial for applying concentration inequalities later.

Step 5: Almost-sure convergence via stochastic approximation theory.

We now prove the first part of the theorem:
Q_{t}\to Q^{\star}
almost surely as
t\to\infty
.

Define
\Delta_{t}:=Q_{t}-Q^{\star}
as the error at time
t
. We will analyze how this error evolves over time.

First, we decompose the update into a contraction term and error terms:

Lemma 16

(Error Decomposition)
.

For any time
t
, the error term
\Delta_{t}=Q_{t}-Q^{\star}
satisfies:

\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}\left[(TQ_{t}-TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}\right]

(60)

Proof of Lemma
16
.

Since
Q^{\star}=TQ^{\star}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=Q_{t+1}-Q^{\star}

(61)

\displaystyle=Q_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}[(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}]-Q^{\star}

(62)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}[(T_{t}Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}]

(63)

Now we add and subtract
(TQ_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}[(TQ_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-Q_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}]

(65)

Further, we add and subtract
(TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})=Q^{\star}(s_{t},a_{t})
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}[(TQ_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})-(TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}]

(67)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}[(TQ_{t}-TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}]

(68)

This gives us the desired decomposition.
∎

To establish the almost-sure convergence of
Q_{t}
to
Q^{\star}
, we will apply the asynchronous stochastic approximation theory developed in
[
33
]
. Specifically, we will use a simplified version of Theorem 3 from that paper.

Theorem 17
.

Consider an asynchronous stochastic approximation algorithm of the form:

x_{t+1}(i)=x_{t}(i)+\alpha_{t}(i)[h_{i}(x_{t})+w_{t}(i)],\quad i\in\{1,2,...,n\}

where:

•

Only one component
i
is updated at each time step

•

h=(h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{n})
is a
\gamma
-contraction mapping with fixed point
x^{*}

•

Step sizes
\alpha_{t}(i)
satisfy
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}(i)=\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}(i)<\infty

•

Each component
i
is updated infinitely often

•

w_{t}(i)
are martingale difference sequences with bounded variance

•

For every
i
and
t
,
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}(i)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}]=0
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the
\sigma
-field generated by the history of the algorithm up to time
t

Then
x_{t}
converges to
x^{*}
almost surely.

Now, we apply Theorem
17
to our Q-learning setting.

Proposition 18

(Almost-Sure Convergence)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
13
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=0\quad\text{with probability 1}

Proof of Proposition
18
.

We map our Q-learning setting to the framework of Theorem
17
:

•

The state space is
\mathbb{R}^{|S||A|}
, with
x_{t}=Q_{t}

•

The fixed point is
x^{*}=Q^{\star}

•

The mapping
h
corresponds to the component-wise application of
(TQ-Q)

•

The noise term
w_{t}(i)
corresponds to the sum of the martingale difference
\delta_{t}
and the approximation error
(T_{t}-T)Q_{t}

From Lemma
15
,
\delta_{t}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. While the approximation error
(T_{t}-T)Q_{t}
is deterministic given the history, it converges to zero as
t\to\infty
since
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0
. Therefore, the combined noise term eventually behaves like a martingale difference sequence.

To apply Theorem
17
, we need to show that the operator
T
induces a contraction mapping in our setting. From (
48
), we know that
T
is a
\gamma
-contraction. To express this in the component-wise form required, note that for any component
(s,a)
:

\displaystyle|(TQ-TQ^{\prime})(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\|TQ-TQ^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

(70)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|Q-Q^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

(71)

\displaystyle=\gamma\max_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}|Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q^{\prime}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|

(72)

Given our assumptions:

•

The step sizes
\alpha_{t}
satisfy the Robbins-Monro conditions

•

Each state-action pair is visited infinitely often

•

The time-varying operator
T_{t}
converges to
T
since
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0

Therefore, all conditions of Theorem
17
are satisfied (with the slight modification that our noise term has a vanishing deterministic component, which doesn’t affect the convergence argument). Consequently,
Q_{t}\to Q^{\star}
almost surely.
∎

Step 6: Finite-time high-probability bounds.

We now derive the finite-time high-probability bound stated in the second part of the theorem. This analysis is more intricate and requires tracking the error evolution precisely.

Let us define the weighted error sequence:

\Delta_{t+1}=(I-\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}^{\top})\Delta_{t}+\alpha_{t}e_{(s_{t},a_{t})}\left[(TQ_{t}-TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}\right]

For the component that gets updated, we have:

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}(s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t})\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\alpha_{t}\left[(TQ_{t}-TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})+((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})+\delta_{t}\right]

(73)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t})\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})+\alpha_{t}\left[\gamma\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\delta_{t}\right]

(74)

where we used the fact that
|(TQ_{t}-TQ^{\star})(s_{t},a_{t})|\leq\gamma\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
due to the contraction property, and
|((T_{t}-T)Q_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})|\leq C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}
from Lemma
14
.

Lemma 19

(Component-wise Dynamics)
.

For the state-action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})
updated at time
t
:

|\Delta_{t+1}(s_{t},a_{t})|\leq(1-\alpha_{t}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|+\alpha_{t}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\delta_{t}\right]

(75)

Proof of Lemma
19
.

Starting with our earlier inequality:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{t+1}(s_{t},a_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t})|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|+\alpha_{t}\left[\gamma\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\delta_{t}\right]

(76)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t})|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|+\alpha_{t}\gamma|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|+\alpha_{t}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\delta_{t}\right]

(77)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{t}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|+\alpha_{t}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{t}+\delta_{t}\right]

(78)

which gives us the desired inequality.
∎

For a sequence of updates, we need to track how the maximum error
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
evolves. We’ll use
\tau_{k}(s,a)
to denote the
k
-th time that the state-action pair
(s,a)
is updated.

Lemma 20

(Error Reduction Across Sweeps)
.

Let
\tau(t)=\min\{k>t:\forall(s,a)\in S\times A,\exists j\in\{t+1,\ldots,k\}\text{ such that }(s_{j},a_{j})=(s,a)\}
be the time required after
t
to visit all state-action pairs at least once. Then for any
t\geq 1
:

\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(1-\min_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t)}(1-\gamma)\right)\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+C_{1}\max_{t\leq i\leq\tau(t)}\varepsilon_{i}+M_{t}

(79)

where:

•

\tau_{1}(s,a,t)=\min\{k>t:(s_{k},a_{k})=(s,a)\}
is the first time after
t
that
(s,a)
is updated

•

M_{t}=\max_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\left|\sum_{i=t+1}^{\tau(t)}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\right|
is the maximum accumulated noise term

Proof.

We analyze how the max-norm error evolves as each state-action pair is updated. Let
(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})
be the state-action pair with maximum error at time
t
, i.e.,
|\Delta_{t}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|=\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
.

First, we recall from Lemma
19
that for any state-action pair
(s,a)
updated at time
i
:

|\Delta_{i+1}(s,a)|\leq(1-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|+\alpha_{i}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{i}+\delta_{i}\right]

(80)

We now track how the max-norm evolves through a complete sweep of updates. For each
(s,a)\in S\times A
, let
\tilde{\tau}(s,a)
denote the time after all state-action pairs have been updated at least once following the update of
(s,a)
. That is,
\tilde{\tau}(s,a)=\tau(\tau_{1}(s,a,t))
.

Case 1:
If after the complete sweep, the original maximizing component still has the maximum error:
\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}=|\Delta_{\tau(t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|
.

Then we have:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{\tau(t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|

(81)

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)+1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|\quad\text{(since no further updates to this component)}

(82)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|

(83)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}+\delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}\right]

(84)

Since
|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|\leq\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
(the error at this component cannot spontaneously increase without an update) and
\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
(the maximum error cannot increase without an update), the second term in brackets is non-positive. Therefore:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})|+\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}+\delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}]

(85)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}(1-\gamma))\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}+\delta_{\tau_{1}(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*},t)}]

(86)

Case 2:
If after the complete sweep, a different component
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\neq(s_{t}^{*},a_{t}^{*})
has the maximum error:
\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}=|\Delta_{\tau(t)}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|
.

Let
\tau_{1}=\tau_{1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},t)
be the time when
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
was updated. We have:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{\tau(t)}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|

(87)

\displaystyle=|\Delta_{\tau_{1}+1}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|\quad\text{(since no further updates to this component)}

(88)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}\right]

(89)

Since
|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})|\leq\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(90)

Now,
\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
(since max-norm can only decrease without updates), which gives:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(91)

Rigorous derivation of contraction factor.

We now provide a rigorous bound that avoids the need for a heuristic factor. The key insight is that the analysis in Cases 1 and 2 both yield contraction factors of the form
(1-\alpha(1-\gamma))
, but applied to different components and at different times within the sweep.

Key observation:
In both cases, the component
(s^{*},a^{*})
(Case 1) or
(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
(Case 2) that achieves the maximum error at time
\tau(t)
satisfies:

•

It was updated exactly once during the sweep (at some time
\tau_{1}\in\{t+1,\ldots,\tau(t)\}
)

•

At the time of its update, its error was bounded by
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
(since no component’s error increases spontaneously without an update)

•

After its update, no further modifications occur to this component

From the component-wise dynamics, at the update time
\tau_{1}
for the eventually-maximal component:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\tau_{1}+1}(\text{max-comp})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|

(92)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\underbrace{\gamma(\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|)}_{\geq 0,\text{ captures ``spillover'' from other components}}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(93)

Using
|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|\leq\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
and
\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\tau_{1}+1}(\text{max-comp})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma(\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|)+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(94)

In the worst case where
|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|=0
, we get:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\tau_{1}+1}(\text{max-comp})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma)+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma)\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(95)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-2\gamma))\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(96)

For
\gamma<1/2
, this gives direct contraction. For
\gamma\geq 1/2
, we use a refined argument based on the observation that the spillover term
\gamma(\|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|)
is only large when
|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|
is much smaller than the maximum—but then the baseline error to contract from is also smaller.

Refined bound for
\gamma\geq 1/2
:

Define
\rho:=|\Delta_{\tau_{1}}(\text{max-comp})|/\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}\in[0,1]
. Then:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\tau_{1}+1}(\text{max-comp})|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))\rho\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma(1-\rho)\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(97)

\displaystyle=\bigl[\rho-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma)\rho+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma(1-\rho)\bigr]\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(98)

\displaystyle=\bigl[\rho(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}})+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma\bigr]\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}[C_{1}\varepsilon_{\tau_{1}}+\delta_{\tau_{1}}]

(99)

The coefficient
\rho(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}})+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma
is maximized at
\rho=1
(since
\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\leq 1
), yielding:

\max_{\rho\in[0,1]}\bigl[\rho(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}})+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma\bigr]=(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}})+\alpha_{\tau_{1}}\gamma=1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma)

(100)

This recovers the original contraction factor
(1-\alpha_{\tau_{1}}(1-\gamma))
without any loss!

Therefore, combining both cases with the minimum step size across all components:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\tau(t)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\min_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t)}(1-\gamma)\right)\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}+C_{1}\max_{t\leq i\leq\tau(t)}\varepsilon_{i}+M_{t}

(101)

where
M_{t}=\max_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\left|\sum_{i=t+1}^{\tau(t)}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\right|
is the maximum accumulated noise.

Remark:
The refined argument above establishes that the full contraction factor
(1-\gamma)
is achievable without loss, yielding tighter convergence bounds than analyses that employ a conservative factor of
1/2
.
∎

Now, with the step size
\alpha_{t}=1/t
, we have:

Lemma 21

(Step Size Properties)
.

With
\alpha_{t}=1/t
:

(a)

\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma))\leq(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}

(b)

For any
i\leq t
:
\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{i+1}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}

Proof of Lemma
21
.

Using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for all
x\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t}e^{-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma)}

(102)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{i}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\ln(t+1)\right)

(105)

\displaystyle=(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}

(106)

Similarly, for part (b):

\displaystyle\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{j=i+1}^{t}\frac{1}{j}\right)

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\ln\left(\frac{t+1}{i+1}\right)\right)

(108)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{t+1}{i+1}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}

(109)

∎

To derive our finite-time bound, we need to control both the deterministic bias and the martingale noise.

Lemma 22

(Martingale Concentration via Freedman’s Inequality)
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}
be a martingale-difference sequence with respect to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}
, and suppose
|X_{i}|\leq b_{i}
almost surely. Let
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. Then for every
x>0
:

\Pr(|S_{n}|\geq x)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}/2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]+\frac{x}{3}\max_{i\leq n}b_{i}}\right)

Let’s apply Freedman’s inequality to bound the accumulated noise term.

Lemma 23

(Martingale Term Bound)
.

Define the weighted noise accumulation:

M_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}e_{(s_{i},a_{i})}\delta_{i}

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\|M_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq 2V_{\max}\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\frac{2V_{\max}}{3}\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)\max_{i\leq t}\alpha_{i}

Proof of Lemma
23
.

For each
(s,a)
, define:

M_{t}(s,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}

This is a martingale with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
. From Lemma
15
, we know
|\delta_{i}|\leq 2V_{\max}
, so each term
\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}
is bounded by
\alpha_{i}\cdot 2V_{\max}
.

Applying Lemma
22
and using the bound on conditional variance:

\mathbb{E}[(\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]\leq(2V_{\max})^{2}\alpha_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}

we get that with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{|S||A|}
:

\displaystyle|M_{t}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)(2V_{\max})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}}

(110)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2V_{\max}}{3}\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)\max_{i\leq t}\alpha_{i}

(111)

\displaystyle\leq 2V_{\max}\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\frac{2V_{\max}}{3}\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|}{\delta}\right)\max_{i\leq t}\alpha_{i}

(112)

By the union bound, the above holds for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
with probability at least
1-\delta
, which gives us the bound on
\|M_{t}\|_{\infty}
.
∎

For the weighted discounted noise term, we need a more sophisticated analysis.

Lemma 24

(Weighted Discounted Noise Analysis)
.

Define the weighted, discounted noise accumulation:

\tilde{M}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}e_{(s_{i},a_{i})}\delta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\|\tilde{M}_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq C\cdot t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}

for some constant
C>0
that depends only on
V_{\max}
and
\gamma
.

Proof.

The key challenge in analyzing
\tilde{M}_{t}
is that due to the discounting factors, it is not a standard martingale. We address this by using a decomposition approach.

Step 1: Decompose by state-action pairs.

First, we decompose
\tilde{M}_{t}
by state-action pairs:

\tilde{M}_{t}(s,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))

This gives us
|S||A|
separate weighted sums, one for each state-action pair. By the union bound, if we can bound each
|\tilde{M}_{t}(s,a)|
with probability
1-\frac{\delta}{|S||A|}
, then we can bound
\|\tilde{M}_{t}\|_{\infty}
with probability
1-\delta
.

Step 2: Time-splitting and doubling epochs.

To handle the time-dependent weights, we use a time-splitting technique with doubling epochs. Define epochs:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{1\}

(113)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{2^{k-1}+1,2^{k-1}+2,\ldots,2^{k}\}\quad\text{for }k\geq 1

(114)

For each epoch
\mathcal{T}_{k}
and state-action pair
(s,a)
, define:

\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))

Then:

\tilde{M}_{t}(s,a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor}\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)

Step 3: Analyze weights within each epoch.

For
i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
, we have
i\geq 2^{k-1}+1
. Using Lemma
21
:

\displaystyle\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{i+1}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}

(115)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{2^{k-1}+2}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}

(116)

For
i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
, the weights vary by at most a constant factor:

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))}{\prod_{j=i^{\prime}+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))}\leq\left(\frac{i^{\prime}+1}{i+1}\right)^{1-\gamma}\leq\left(\frac{2^{k}+1}{2^{k-1}+2}\right)^{1-\gamma}\leq 2^{1-\gamma}

(117)

for any
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
. This allows us to approximate the weights within an epoch by a constant.

Step 4: Define a modified martingale for each epoch.

For each epoch
\mathcal{T}_{k}
and state-action pair
(s,a)
, define:

W_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}

This is a martingale difference sum. Using Freedman’s inequality (Lemma
22
), with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2(|S||A|)(\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor+1)}
:

\displaystyle|W_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq 2V_{\max}\sqrt{2\ln\left(\frac{4(|S||A|)(\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor+1)}{\delta}\right)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\alpha_{i}^{2}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}}

(118)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2V_{\max}}{3}\ln\left(\frac{4(|S||A|)(\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor+1)}{\delta}\right)\max_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\alpha_{i}

(119)

Step 5: Relate the weighted sum to the martingale.

For each epoch
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, we can relate
\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)
to
W_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)
:

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{j}(1-\gamma))\cdot|W_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{2^{k-1}+2}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot|W_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

(121)

Step 6: Analyze the sum over all epochs.

By the union bound, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2|S||A|}
, for all epochs
\mathcal{T}_{k}
:

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{2^{k-1}+2}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\alpha_{i}^{2}}

(122)

where
C_{2}
is a constant. For
i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
,
\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{i}\leq\frac{1}{2^{k-1}+1}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\alpha_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\frac{1}{(2^{k-1}+1)^{2}}

(123)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{k}|}{(2^{k-1}+1)^{2}}

(124)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}-2^{k-1}}{(2^{k-1}+1)^{2}}

(125)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k-1}}{(2^{k-1}+1)^{2}}

(126)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}

(127)

Therefore:

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{t+1}{2^{k-1}+2}\right)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{k-1}}}

(128)

\displaystyle=C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot\frac{(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}}{(2^{k-1}+2)^{-(1-\gamma)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{k-1}}}

(129)

\displaystyle\approx C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot(2^{k-1})^{(1-\gamma)}\cdot(2^{k-1})^{-1/2}

(130)

\displaystyle=C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot(2^{k-1})^{(1-\gamma)-1/2}

(131)

Step 7: Sum over all epochs.

Finally, summing over all epochs:

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{t}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor}|\tilde{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(s,a)|

(132)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}t\rfloor}(2^{k-1})^{(1-\gamma)-1/2}

(133)

The exponent
(1-\gamma)-1/2
determines how the sum behaves. If
(1-\gamma)-1/2<0
, which happens when
\gamma>1/2
, the sum is dominated by the largest term (the last epoch), giving approximately
(t/2)^{(1-\gamma)-1/2}=O(t^{(1-\gamma)-1/2})
. For typical discount factors close to 1, we have
\gamma>1/2
, so:

\displaystyle|\tilde{M}_{t}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot(t+1)^{-(1-\gamma)}\cdot t^{(1-\gamma)-1/2}

(134)

\displaystyle=C_{3}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t\log_{2}(t)}{\delta}\right)}\cdot t^{-1/2}

(135)

By the union bound, this holds for all state-action pairs
(s,a)
with probability at least
1-\delta/2
. Since
\ln(t\log_{2}(t))=O(\ln(t))
, we get:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{M}_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq C\cdot t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{2|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}

(136)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
∎

Now we can derive our finite-time high-probability bound.

Proposition 25

(Finite-Time High-Probability Bound)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
13
, with
\alpha_{t}=1/t
and
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-\beta})
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
t\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O\left(t^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}\right)

Proof of Proposition
25
.

We’ll use the sequence of complete sweeps through the state-action space to analyze the convergence rate. First, we need to carefully characterize how the exploration policy affects the time required for complete sweeps.

Coverage time analysis:

Let’s define the following:

•

\tau(t)
: the time required after
t
to visit all state-action pairs at least once

•

\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
: a sequence where
t_{0}=1
and
t_{k+1}=\tau(t_{k})
for
k\geq 0

For an
\epsilon
-greedy policy with fixed
\epsilon>0
in an ergodic MDP, we can bound the expected time to visit all state-action pairs using results from Markov chain theory:

Lemma 26

(Coverage Time in Non-stationary MDPs)
.

Consider an MDP with state space
S
and action space
A
where the agent follows an
\epsilon
-greedy policy with fixed
\epsilon>0
. Let the original transition kernel be
P
, and let
\widetilde{P}_{t}
be a non-stationary transition kernel with
\|\widetilde{P}_{t}(\cdot|s,a)-P(\cdot|s,a)\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{t}
for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
, where
\varepsilon_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Assume the original MDP with kernel
P
is ergodic. Then there exists a time
T_{0}
such that for all
t\geq T_{0}
with
\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4|S|}
, the following holds:

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any
t\geq T_{0}
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2t^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\tau(t)-t\leq C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t/\delta)

(137)

where
\tau(t)
is the time required after
t
to visit all state-action pairs at least once, and
C_{4}
is a constant that depends on
\epsilon
and the spectral properties of the original MDP.

Proof of Lemma
26
.

The key challenge is that we’re operating in the non-stationary environment with transitions
\widetilde{P}_{t}
rather than
P
. We need to analyze how the approximation error
\varepsilon_{t}
affects the ergodicity properties.

First, we recall some facts from Markov chain theory:

•

For an ergodic Markov chain with transition matrix
P
and stationary distribution
\pi
, the mixing time is
t_{mix}=O(\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{2}}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon_{min}})
, where
\lambda_{2}
is the second largest eigenvalue and
\epsilon_{min}=\min_{s}\pi(s)
.

•

For an
\epsilon
-greedy policy in an ergodic MDP, we have
\epsilon_{min}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{|S||A|}
.

•

Perturbation theory for Markov chains tells us that if
\|P-P^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\delta
(entry-wise), then the stationary distributions
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
satisfy
\|\pi-\pi^{\prime}\|_{1}=O(\frac{\delta}{1-\lambda_{2}})
.

When
\varepsilon_{t}
is sufficiently small (specifically,
\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4|S|}
), the perturbed Markov chain with transitions determined by
\widetilde{P}_{t}
remains ergodic, and its stationary distribution
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\pi}_{t}-\pi\|_{1}=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{1-\lambda_{2}}\right)

(138)

This implies that for
t\geq T_{0}
, where
T_{0}
is chosen such that
\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4|S|}
for all
t\geq T_{0}
, we have:

\displaystyle\min_{(s,a)}\widetilde{\pi}_{t}(s,a)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2|S||A|}

(139)

Using standard results for the coupon collector problem in Markov chains, the time to visit all state-action pairs with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2t^{2}}
is bounded by:

\displaystyle C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t/\delta)

(140)

where
C_{4}
depends on
\epsilon
and the spectral properties (particularly
\lambda_{2}
) of the original MDP.
∎

Error evolution through sweeps:

From Lemma
20
, for any
k\geq 0
:

\|\Delta_{t_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\left(1-\min_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}(1-\gamma)\right)\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}+C_{1}\max_{t_{k}\leq i\leq t_{k+1}}\varepsilon_{i}+M_{t_{k}}

(141)

where
\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})=\min\{j>t_{k}:(s_{j},a_{j})=(s,a)\}
is the first time after
t_{k}
that state-action pair
(s,a)
is updated, and
M_{t_{k}}
represents the maximum accumulated noise term:

M_{t_{k}}=\max_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\left|\sum_{i=t_{k}+1}^{t_{k+1}}\mathds{1}\{(s_{i},a_{i})=(s,a)\}\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}\right|

(142)

From Lemma
26
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2t_{k}^{2}}
, the time required to visit all state-action pairs after
t_{k}
is bounded by:

\tau(t_{k})-t_{k}\leq C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)

(143)

This implies that for any state-action pair
(s,a)
:

\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})\leq t_{k}+C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)

(144)

With step size
\alpha_{t}=1/t
, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}

(145)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t_{k}+C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}

(146)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{t_{k}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}}}

(147)

For large
t_{k}
where
t_{k}\gg C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)
, this is approximately
\frac{1}{t_{k}}(1-O(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}}))
.

Define the contraction factor as
\rho_{k}=1-\min_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}(1-\gamma)
, consistent with Lemma
20
. With our bound on
\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=1-\min_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\alpha_{\tau_{1}(s,a,t_{k})}(1-\gamma)

(148)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}+C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}

(149)

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}\left(1-O\left(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}}\right)\right)

(150)

For large
t_{k}
, this is approximately
1-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}+O(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}^{2}})
.

Using the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\in[0,1]
, and focusing on the dominant term for large
t_{k}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}+O\left(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}^{2}}\right)\right)

(151)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}\right)\cdot\exp\left(O\left(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}^{2}}\right)\right)

(152)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}\right)\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}^{2}}\right)\right)

(153)

The second term approaches 1 quickly as
t_{k}
grows, so for large
t_{k}
,
\rho_{k}\approx\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}\right)\approx 1-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{k}}
.

Recursively applying Equation (
141
), after
k
complete sweeps:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\rho_{j}\right)\|\Delta_{t_{0}}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right)\left[C_{1}\max_{t_{j}\leq i\leq t_{j+1}}\varepsilon_{i}+M_{t_{j}}\right]

(154)

Let’s analyze each term:

1. Initial error decay:

\left(\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\rho_{j}\right)\|\Delta_{t_{0}}\|_{\infty}

Using our asymptotic bound on
\rho_{j}
:

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle\approx\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\gamma)}{t_{j}}\right)

(155)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{t_{j}}\right)

(156)

From Lemma
26
, we know that
t_{j+1}\approx t_{j}+C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{j}/\delta)
. For large
j
, this gives us
t_{j}=\Theta(j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta)}\right)

(157)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{j\cdot\log(|S||A|j/\delta)}\right)

(158)

This sum behaves asymptotically as
\Theta\left(\frac{\log\log k}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)
. Thus, the initial error decays as:

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\rho_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)\log\log k}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)\right)

(159)

\displaystyle=\left(\log k\right)^{-\Theta\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)}

(160)

2. Approximation error term:

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right)C_{1}\max_{t_{j}\leq i\leq t_{j+1}}\varepsilon_{i}

With
\varepsilon_{t}=O(t^{-\beta})
and
t_{j}=\Theta(j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))
, we have:

\displaystyle\max_{t_{j}\leq i\leq t_{j+1}}\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=O(t_{j}^{-\beta})

(161)

\displaystyle=O\left((j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))^{-\beta}\right)

(162)

The discounting factor
\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}
can be bounded as:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left(-(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{t_{i}}\right)

(163)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\Theta\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\log\frac{\log k}{\log j}\right)\right)

(164)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\log k}{\log j}\right)^{-\Theta\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)}

(165)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right)C_{1}\max_{t_{j}\leq i\leq t_{j+1}}\varepsilon_{i}

(166)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\log k}{\log j}\right)^{-\Theta\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)}\cdot O\left((j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))^{-\beta}\right)

(167)

For large
k
, this sum is dominated by the terms where
j
is close to
k
, giving:

\displaystyle=O\left(k^{-\beta}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-\beta}\right)

(169)

3. Martingale noise term:

\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right)M_{t_{j}}

From Lemma
23
, with high probability:

\displaystyle M_{t_{j}}

\displaystyle=O\left(t_{j}^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{j}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(170)

\displaystyle=O\left((j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{j}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(171)

Using our bound on the discounting factor:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{k-1}\rho_{i}\right)M_{t_{j}}

(172)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\log k}{\log j}\right)^{-\Theta\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}}\right)}\cdot O\left((j\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|j/\delta))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{j}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(173)

For large
k
, this sum is dominated by terms where
j
is close to
k
, giving:

\displaystyle=O\left(k^{-1/2}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(174)

Combining all terms:
Taking the dominant rate (slowest decaying term) among the approximation error and martingale noise terms:

For
\beta>1/2
, the martingale noise term dominates:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O\left(k^{-1/2}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(175)

For
\beta<1/2
, the approximation error term dominates:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O\left(k^{-\beta}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-\beta}\right)

(176)

We can combine these cases into:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O\left(k^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(177)

where
\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}
is 1 if
\beta\geq 1/2
and 0 otherwise.

Based on our relationship
t_{k}=\Theta(k\cdot|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))
, we can express this in terms of
t_{k}
:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O\left(\left(\frac{t_{k}}{|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta)}\right)^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot(|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|k/\delta))^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(178)

\displaystyle=O\left(t_{k}^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(179)

For any time
t\geq T_{0}
(where
T_{0}
ensures the bound on the coverage time). we can find
t_{k}
such that
t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1}
, and we need to bound
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}
in terms of
\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}
. From the Q-learning update and our error decomposition in Lemma
16
, for any state-action pair
(s,a)
updated at time
i
where
t_{k}\leq i<t
:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{i+1}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|+\alpha_{i}\left[\gamma(\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\infty}-|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|)+C_{1}\varepsilon_{i}+\delta_{i}\right]

(180)

If
\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\infty}=|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|
(i.e.,
(s,a)
has the maximum error at time
i
), then:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{i+1}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{i}(1-\gamma))|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|+\alpha_{i}\left[C_{1}\varepsilon_{i}+\delta_{i}\right]

(181)

If
\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\infty}>|\Delta_{i}(s,a)|
, then the error at
(s,a)
could potentially increase toward the maximum error.

Over the interval
[t_{k},t)
, with high probability, the martingale noise terms are bounded by:

\displaystyle\max_{t_{k}\leq i<t}|\delta_{i}|\leq O\left(\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{(t-t_{k})|S||A|}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(182)

Given that
t-t_{k}\leq C_{4}|S|^{2}|A|^{2}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)
and
\varepsilon_{i}=O(i^{-\beta})
, we can show that with high probability:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}+O\left(\frac{\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{t_{k}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S|^{3}|A|^{3}\log(|S||A|t_{k}/\delta)}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(183)

\displaystyle=\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}+O\left(t_{k}^{-1/2}\right)

(184)

This additional error term doesn’t affect the asymptotic rate in our final bound, as it decays faster than
t_{k}^{-1/2}
for large
t_{k}
. So it suffices to bound just
\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}
. For large
t_{k}
, we have
t=\Theta(t_{k})
, and therefore:

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{t_{k}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O\left(t_{k}^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t_{k}}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(185)

\displaystyle=O\left(t^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(186)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O\left(t^{-\min(\beta,1/2)}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)^{\mathds{1}\{\beta\geq 1/2\}/2}\right)

(187)

For
\beta=1/2
, this gives:

\displaystyle\|Q_{t}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}=O\left(t^{-1/2}\cdot\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}\right).

(188)

Corollary 27

(Fixed-DCM Finite-Time Bound)
.

When the DCM is pre-trained and held fixed (
\theta_{t}=\theta^{*}
for all
t
), define the reward approximation error
\varepsilon_{r}:=|\widetilde{r}_{\theta^{*}}-r|_{\infty}
and transition approximation error
\varepsilon_{P}:=\|\widetilde{P}_{\theta^{*}}-P\|_{1}
. Setting
\beta=0
in Proposition
25
, the Q-function satisfies with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{r}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma R_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}\right)

(189)

Proof.

With fixed DCM, the approximation errors
\varepsilon_{r}
and
\varepsilon_{P}
are constant (corresponding to
\beta=0
). From Lemma
14
, the one-step Bellman error satisfies:

\displaystyle\|T_{t}Q-TQ\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\widetilde{r}_{t}-r|_{\infty}+\gamma\|Q\|_{\infty}\|\widetilde{P}_{t}-P\|_{1}

(190)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{r}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}

(191)

where
V_{\max}=R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
. The contraction property of the Bellman operator then yields:

\displaystyle\|Q_{t}-Q^{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{r}+\gamma V_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}}{1-\gamma}+C_{2}t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}

(192)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{r}}{1-\gamma}+\frac{\gamma R_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+C_{2}t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{|S||A|t}{\delta}\right)}

(193)

The bound decomposes into:

•

Reward bias

\varepsilon_{r}/(1-\gamma)
: Error from approximate reward imputation, scaling linearly with effective horizon.

•

Transition bias

\gamma R_{\max}\varepsilon_{P}/(1-\gamma)^{2}
: Error from approximate transition dynamics. The quadratic scaling in
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
reflects that transition errors compound through the value function, consistent with simulation lemma bounds
[
17
]
.

•

Sampling noise

t^{-1/2}\sqrt{\ln(\cdot)}
: Stochastic approximation noise, vanishing with samples.

In our experimental setting, the DCM primarily imputes delayed rewards with high accuracy (
\varepsilon_{r}
small), making the transition bias term the dominant source of irreducible error.
∎

14
Extrapolation under Distributional Shift

This section provides the full proof of Proposition
30
(main paper), establishing when parametric models with structured inductive bias generalize better than model-free methods under distributional shift. Note that this is presented as a proposition with explicit caveats about the idealized comparison, acknowledging that neural network function approximators may have implicit inductive biases not captured by the worst-case analysis.

14.1
Lipschitz Property of MNL

We first verify that the MNL-based DCM satisfies the Lipschitz property (A7).

Lemma 28

(Lipschitz Property of Softmax)
.

The softmax function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\Delta^{K-1}
defined by
\sigma_{k}(z)=\exp(z_{k})/\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(z_{j})
is Lipschitz continuous:

\|\sigma(z)-\sigma(z^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

(194)

Proof.

We compute the
\ell_{\infty}\to\ell_{1}
operator norm of the softmax Jacobian and apply the mean value theorem.

Step 1: Jacobian structure.
The softmax Jacobian has entries:

J_{kj}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial z_{j}}=\sigma_{k}(\delta_{kj}-\sigma_{j})=\begin{cases}\sigma_{k}(1-\sigma_{k})&k=j\\
-\sigma_{k}\sigma_{j}&k\neq j\end{cases}

(195)

Step 2: Operator norm computation.
The
\ell_{\infty}\to\ell_{1}
induced operator norm is
\|J\|_{\infty\to 1}=\max_{j}\sum_{k}|J_{kj}|
(maximum absolute column sum). For column
j
:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}|J_{kj}|

\displaystyle=|J_{jj}|+\sum_{k\neq j}|J_{kj}|

(196)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}(1-\sigma_{j})+\sum_{k\neq j}\sigma_{k}\sigma_{j}

(197)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}(1-\sigma_{j})+\sigma_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}\sigma_{k}

(198)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}(1-\sigma_{j})+\sigma_{j}(1-\sigma_{j})

(199)

\displaystyle=2\sigma_{j}(1-\sigma_{j})

(200)

Since
x(1-x)\leq 1/4
for
x\in[0,1]
, we have
\sum_{k}|J_{kj}|\leq 1/2
for each column
j
. Therefore:

\|J(z)\|_{\infty\to 1}=\max_{j}\sum_{k}|J_{kj}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

(201)

Step 3: Mean value theorem for vector functions.
For any
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, the mean value theorem in integral form gives:

\sigma(z^{\prime})-\sigma(z)=\int_{0}^{1}J(z+t(z^{\prime}-z))\cdot(z^{\prime}-z)\,dt

(202)

Taking
\ell_{1}
norms and using the operator norm bound:

\displaystyle\|\sigma(z^{\prime})-\sigma(z)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|J(z+t(z^{\prime}-z))\|_{\infty\to 1}\cdot\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{\infty}\,dt

(203)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{\infty}\,dt

(204)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|z^{\prime}-z\|_{\infty}

(205)

This establishes the Lipschitz constant of
1/2
.
∎

Proposition 29

(Lipschitz DCM Reward)
.

Let the DCM be a multinomial logit model with utilities
V_{j}(\phi;\theta)=\theta_{j}^{\top}\phi
where
\|\theta_{j}\|\leq B_{\theta}
for all
j
. Then the expected reward function
m_{\theta}(\phi)=\sum_{j}P(j|\phi;\theta)\cdot r_{j}
satisfies:

|m_{\theta}(\phi)-m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})|\leq L_{m}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|

(206)

where
L_{m}=B_{\theta}R_{\max}
depends on the parameter bound
B_{\theta}
and maximum reward
R_{\max}
.

Proof.

Let
P_{j}(\phi)=P(j|\phi;\theta)=\sigma_{j}(V(\phi;\theta))
where
V(\phi;\theta)=(\theta_{1}^{\top}\phi,\ldots,\theta_{K}^{\top}\phi)
.

Step 1: Utility Lipschitz property.

For any alternative
j
:

|V_{j}(\phi;\theta)-V_{j}(\phi^{\prime};\theta)|=|\theta_{j}^{\top}(\phi-\phi^{\prime})|\leq\|\theta_{j}\|\cdot\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|\leq B_{\theta}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|

(207)

Step 2: Probability Lipschitz property.

By Lemma
28
:

\sum_{j}|P_{j}(\phi)-P_{j}(\phi^{\prime})|\leq\max_{j}|V_{j}(\phi;\theta)-V_{j}(\phi^{\prime};\theta)|\leq B_{\theta}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|

(208)

Step 3: Expected reward Lipschitz property.

\displaystyle|m_{\theta}(\phi)-m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j}r_{j}\cdot(P_{j}(\phi)-P_{j}(\phi^{\prime}))\right|

(209)

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\sum_{j}|P_{j}(\phi)-P_{j}(\phi^{\prime})|

(210)

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\cdot B_{\theta}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|

(211)

Setting
L_{m}=B_{\theta}R_{\max}
completes the proof. In practice, we may use a tighter bound accounting for the reward range
|r_{j}-\bar{r}|
rather than
R_{\max}
.
∎

14.2
Statement and Proof of Proposition
30

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}=\{\phi(s,a):(s,a)\in\text{supp}(\mu_{\text{train}})\}
denote the feature support during training, and define the
feature shift magnitude
:

\delta_{\phi}=\max_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{test}}}\min_{\phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|

(212)

Proposition 30

(Extrapolation under Distributional Shift)
.

Under distributional shift from
\mu_{\text{train}}
to
\mu_{\text{test}}
with feature shift magnitude
\delta_{\phi}
, and assuming the true reward function is Lipschitz with constant
L_{r}
. Let
\varepsilon_{\text{train}}=\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}}|m_{\theta}(\phi)-r(\phi)|
denote the supremum approximation error on the training distribution. Then:

1.

Parametric model (CA-DQN):
For all
(s,a)\in\text{supp}(\mu_{\text{test}})
:

|m_{\theta}(s,a)-r(s,a)|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{train}}+(L_{m}+L_{r})\cdot\delta_{\phi}

(213)

2.

Worst-case baseline:
In the absence of explicit parametric structure, standard generalization bounds yield:

|\hat{r}(s,a)-r(s,a)|\leq O(R_{\max})

(214)

for
(s,a)\notin\text{supp}(\mu_{\text{train}})
, without explicit dependence on
\delta_{\phi}
.

The parametric model has strictly lower worst-case error when
\delta_{\phi}<(R_{\max}-\varepsilon_{\text{train}})/(L_{m}+L_{r})
.

Caveat:
This comparison is idealized. Neural network function approximators (including MB-DQN) have implicit inductive biases that may provide some generalization beyond the training support, though these are harder to characterize theoretically. The proposition formalizes the intuition that
explicit
parametric structure (as in the DCM) provides
provable
extrapolation guarantees, while implicit biases offer no such guarantees.

14.3
Full Proof of Proposition
30

Full Proof of Proposition
30
.

We prove each part separately.

Part 1: Parametric Model Bound.

Let
\phi=\phi(s,a)
be any feature vector in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{test}}
. Define:

\phi^{\prime}=\argmin_{\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}}\|\phi-\psi\|

(215)

as the nearest training feature. By definition,
\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|\leq\delta_{\phi}
.

Decompose the error using the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle|m_{\theta}(\phi)-r(\phi)|

\displaystyle\leq|m_{\theta}(\phi)-m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})|+|m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})-r(\phi^{\prime})|+|r(\phi^{\prime})-r(\phi)|

(216)

Term 1:
By the Lipschitz property of
m_{\theta}
(Proposition
29
):

|m_{\theta}(\phi)-m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})|\leq L_{m}\|\phi-\phi^{\prime}\|\leq L_{m}\cdot\delta_{\phi}

(217)

Term 2:
Since
\phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}
, by definition of
\varepsilon_{\text{train}}=\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{train}}}|m_{\theta}(\phi)-r(\phi)|
:

|m_{\theta}(\phi^{\prime})-r(\phi^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{train}}

(218)

Term 3:
By the Lipschitz property of the true reward function:

|r(\phi^{\prime})-r(\phi)|\leq L_{r}\|\phi^{\prime}-\phi\|\leq L_{r}\cdot\delta_{\phi}

(219)

Combining:

|m_{\theta}(\phi)-r(\phi)|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{train}}+(L_{m}+L_{r})\cdot\delta_{\phi}

(220)

Part 2: Model-Free Method Bound.

For model-free methods like MB-DQN, the Q-network
Q_{\omega}(s,a)
is trained end-to-end on observed transitions
(s,a,r,s^{\prime})
from
\mu_{\text{train}}
. For novel state-action pairs
(s,a)\notin\text{supp}(\mu_{\text{train}})
, the network output depends entirely on
implicit generalization
from training data—there is no explicit reward model constraining the extrapolation.

Definition (Implicit reward estimate).
For model-free DQN with Q-network
Q_{\omega}
, define the implicit reward estimate via the Bellman residual:

\hat{r}_{\text{MF}}(s,a):=Q_{\omega}(s,a)-\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|s,a)}[V_{\omega}(s^{\prime})]

(221)

where
V_{\omega}(s^{\prime})=\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{\omega}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
.

Worst-case analysis.
For
(s,a)\notin\text{supp}(\mu_{\text{train}})
:

1.

The Q-network has received no gradient signal from
(s,a)
directly.

2.

Neural networks provide no
guaranteed
Lipschitz structure without explicit regularization.

3.

Thus the implicit reward estimate satisfies only the trivial bound:

|\hat{r}_{\text{MF}}(s,a)-r(s,a)|\leq R_{\max}

(222)

This bound is achieved when the network output is arbitrary on novel inputs. While neural networks often interpolate smoothly in practice, this behavior is not guaranteed—the extrapolation depends on network architecture, initialization, and training dynamics rather than domain structure.

Key distinction:
Choice-Assisted DQN’s reward model
m_{\theta}
has domain-specific structure (MNL choice probabilities) that
guarantees
Lipschitz extrapolation (Proposition
29
). MB-DQN lacks such structure.

Part 3: Comparison Condition.

The parametric model dominates when its worst-case error is smaller:

\varepsilon_{\text{train}}+(L_{m}+L_{r})\cdot\delta_{\phi}<R_{\max}

(223)

Rearranging:

\delta_{\phi}<\frac{R_{\max}-\varepsilon_{\text{train}}}{L_{m}+L_{r}}

(224)

This completes the proof.
∎

14.5
When Structural Assumptions Hold

The benefit of parametric models depends on whether structural assumptions remain valid under distributional shift.

Remark 6

(Domain Conditions for Proposition
30
)
.

The extrapolation bound relies on:

1.

Structural stability
: The functional form (e.g., MNL) remains valid under shift. Demand shifts (changing price sensitivity) preserve this; model misspecification violates it.

2.

Bounded shift magnitude
:
\delta_{\phi}
must satisfy the comparison condition. In our hotel experiments, demand shifts of
\pm 30\%
correspond to
\delta_{\phi}\approx 0.3
in normalized feature space.

3.

Lipschitz true reward
: This is satisfied when customer valuations change smoothly with prices, a standard economic assumption.

When these conditions fail (e.g., severe misspecification where
\varepsilon_{\text{train}}
becomes large), the parametric advantage diminishes—consistent with our empirical results in Table
5
.

Our distributional shift experiments (Table 2) involve parametric perturbations
within
the MNL family. To address ICML reviewer concerns about model misspecification, we conducted additional stress tests where the true customer behavior
violates

We introduce quadratic price sensitivity into the true data-generating process:

V_{j}^{\text{true}}=V_{j}^{\text{MNL}}+\beta_{2}(p_{j}-\bar{p})^{2}

(229)

where
V_{j}^{\text{MNL}}
is the standard linear utility,
\bar{p}
is the average price, and
\beta_{2}<0
creates non-linear price effects that the agent’s linear MNL cannot capture.

(

•

(

): Small quadratic effect

•

(
\beta_{2}=-0.0001
): Medium quadratic effect

•

(
\beta_{2}=-0.0002

15.9
Out-of-Family Stress Tests

The quadratic price sensitivity test (Section
15.8
) introduces parametric misspecification within a modified MNL framework. To test more severe
structural
misspecification where the true customer behavior violates the fundamental assumptions of MNL (e.g., IIA property, segment homogeneity), we conduct out-of-family (OOF) stress tests using simulators from entirely different model families.

15.9.1
OOF Simulator Designs

1. Nested Logit Simulator (IIA Violation):
The Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA) property is a core assumption of MNL that may not hold when alternatives are perceived as similar. We implement a nested logit model where similar room types (e.g., standard and deluxe) are grouped into nests with correlation parameter
\lambda=0.4
:

P(j|s,a)=\frac{\exp(V_{j}/\lambda_{k})}{\sum_{m\in B_{k}}\exp(V_{m}/\lambda_{k})}\cdot\frac{\left(\sum_{m\in B_{k}}\exp(V_{m}/\lambda_{k})\right)^{\lambda_{k}}}{\sum_{\ell}\left(\sum_{m\in B_{\ell}}\exp(V_{m}/\lambda_{\ell})\right)^{\lambda_{\ell}}}

(230)

where
B_{k}
denotes nest
k
and
\lambda_{k}
is the nest correlation parameter.

2. Bimodal Mixture Simulator (Heterogeneous Segments):
MNL assumes a representative agent with homogeneous preferences. In reality, customer populations often contain distinct segments with different price sensitivities. We implement a two-segment mixture:

P(j|s,a)=\pi_{1}\cdot P_{1}(j|s,a;\theta_{1})+\pi_{2}\cdot P_{2}(j|s,a;\theta_{2})

(231)

where
\pi_{1}=0.6,\pi_{2}=0.4
represent business and leisure traveler proportions, with
\theta_{1}
(business) having lower price sensitivity than
\theta_{2}
(leisure). The heterogeneity measure is 0.92.

3. Dynamic Mixture Simulator (Temporal Variation):
Customer segment proportions may vary over time (e.g., more business travelers on weekdays, more leisure travelers on weekends). We implement temporal variation:

\pi_{1}(t)=0.5+0.3\cdot\sin(2\pi t/T)

(232)

where
T
is the booking horizon. The heterogeneity measure is 0.57.

•

Seeds

•

Training
: 5 epochs
\times

•

Evaluation

•

: MB-DQN (delayed_dqn), CA-DQN (ca_dqn), CA-DR-DQN (ca_dr_dqn)

15.9.4
OOF Analysis

Key Finding:
MB-DQN (which waits for ground truth rewards) wins all three out-of-family stress tests. This is a striking contrast to the parameter shift experiments (Table 2 in main paper), where CA-DQN showed improvements in 4/10 scenarios.

Mechanism:
Under structural misspecification:

1.

The DCM’s structural assumptions (IIA, homogeneity, stationarity) are violated

2.

Imputed rewards
\hat{r}_{t}^{\text{delay}}
systematically deviate from true rewards

3.

Q-learning optimizes for the wrong objective function

4.

MB-DQN, by waiting for ground truth, avoids this bias

CA-DR-DQN Performance:
The doubly-robust correction does not consistently help under structural misspecification. In nested logit (IIA violation), CA-DR-DQN recovers slightly (-1.25% vs -2.34%). However, in bimodal and dynamic mixture settings, CA-DR-DQN performs worse than CA-DQN, suggesting the DR correction may amplify certain types of bias.

Implication for Practice:
The sharp contrast between parameter shifts (mixed results) and structural misspecification (consistent underperformance) establishes a critical boundary condition. Practitioners should:

•

Test for IIA violations using Hausman-McFadden or similar tests

•

Check for segment heterogeneity via latent class models

•

Monitor for temporal instability in choice parameters

•

Default to MB-DQN when structural assumptions are uncertain

16
Ethics and Responsible Deployment

16.1
Data Privacy and Protection

Our research uses anonymized hotel booking data under a data use agreement that prohibits:

•

Re-identification of individual customers

•

Sharing of raw data outside the research team

•

Commercial use beyond academic publication

All personally identifiable information (names, contact details, payment information) was removed before data transfer. Customer IDs were replaced with random hashes.

16.2
Algorithmic Fairness and Price Discrimination

Dynamic pricing algorithms raise legitimate concerns about discriminatory pricing. We address these through:

Design choices
: Our DCM uses only non-protected attributes: days-to-arrival, search history, booking history, and aggregate demand signals. We explicitly exclude demographic variables (age, gender, nationality) from features.

Fairness auditing
: We recommend organizations deploying similar systems conduct regular fairness audits comparing pricing outcomes across demographic groups.

Price caps
: The action space is bounded (
\pm
15% from base price), preventing extreme price discrimination.

16.3
Economic and Societal Impact

Revenue management algorithms affect consumer welfare:

Efficiency gains
: Optimal pricing can increase capacity utilization, benefiting both businesses (higher revenue) and consumers (more availability during off-peak periods).

Potential harms
: Aggressive yield management can make essential services less affordable for price-sensitive populations. Organizations should balance revenue optimization with accessibility.

Transparency
: We advocate for clear disclosure of algorithmic pricing to consumers, enabling informed purchasing decisions.

16.4
Deployment Guardrails

We recommend the following safeguards for production deployment:

1.

Human oversight
: Price recommendations should be reviewed by revenue managers before implementation, especially for large changes.

2.

Circuit breakers
: Automatic intervention when prices deviate significantly from historical norms.

3.

A/B testing
: Gradual rollout with control groups to measure actual impact.

4.

Monitoring
: Continuous tracking of fairness metrics and customer complaints.

17
Managerial Implications

17.1
Revenue Optimization Under Delayed Consumer Decisions

Revenue managers in industries with temporal gaps between price exposure and purchase completion can achieve meaningful improvements by implementing our framework. The 12.4% improvement under distributional shift (Table 2, main paper) represents substantial value, especially considering that typical RL deployments report 2-8% gains over ML baselines.

For a property generating $10M annually experiencing demand shifts, this translates to
\sim
$1.2M incremental revenue during shift periods.

17.2
Implementation Strategy

Organizations can implement this approach through phased rollout:

1.

Data Collection
(1-3 months): Gather historical data linking customer attributes, price exposure, and final purchase decisions with timestamps.

2.

Model Development
(2-4 weeks): Fit DCM via maximum likelihood; initialize Q-learning with conservative estimates.

3.

Shadow Deployment
(2-4 weeks): Run system in parallel without implementing recommendations.

4.

Limited Rollout
: Apply to subset of inventory while maintaining control groups.

5.

Full Implementation
: Expand coverage while continuing DCM refinement.

17.3
When to Use This Approach

Our analysis identifies conditions for beneficial embedding:

Use partial world models when
:

•

Distributional shift is expected (seasonal changes, new competitors)

•

Low-dimensional parametric structure is available (
d\ll|S\times A|
)

•

Delayed feedback is present (booking windows, subscription decisions)

Not beneficial when
:

•

Environment is stationary (no advantage over standard RL)

•

Real-time feedback is available (delays
\approx 0
)

•

Model class is severely misspecified

18
Limitations and Future Work

We acknowledge several limitations of our current work:

18.1
Gap Between Theory and Experiments

Fixed vs. Online DCM
: Our theoretical analysis covers the general case with online DCM updates (
\beta_{t}>0
), but all experiments use a fixed pre-trained DCM (
\beta_{t}=0
). This represents a
snapshot operational regime
where the DCM is calibrated from previous season data and held fixed during Q-learning deployment.

The fixed-DCM experiments validate the core insight that partial world models improve robustness under shift, but do not empirically demonstrate the two-timescale co-adaptation dynamics. An empirical evaluation of online DCM updates in a synthetic setting would strengthen the connection between theory and practice.

18.2
Model Misspecification and its Consequences

Our distributional shift experiments (Table 2) involve parametric perturbations
within the MNL family
. To address this limitation, we conducted a model misspecification stress test (Section
15.8
) where the true customer behavior violates MNL assumptions through quadratic price sensitivity.

Key finding:
Under misspecification, CA-DQN performs
worse
than MB-DQN (Table
9
). The imputation mechanism amplifies model errors rather than correcting for them. This establishes an important boundary condition: CA-DQN’s benefits require reasonably accurate choice modeling.

Remaining stress tests not conducted:

•

Structural changes
: Introduction of new alternatives or customer segments

•

Delay distribution changes
: More last-minute bookings or longer cancellation windows

18.3
Coupling Analysis Limitations

The theoretical analysis abstracts the feedback loop between Q-learning and DCM training through mixing assumptions (A3). In practice, the policy induced by Q-learning changes the data distribution for DCM calibration, which could violate i.i.d. assumptions. Our ergodicity assumption (sufficient exploration) partially addresses this, but a fully rigorous treatment of policy-dependent data would require more sophisticated analysis.

18.4
Relation to Existing Frameworks

Connection to Dyna-Q
: Our choice-model-assisted framework is conceptually similar to Dyna-style model-based RL
[
28
,
29
]
, where a learned model generates synthetic experience for value learning. The key distinction is our focus on
partial
models (DCM modeling only customer choice) rather than full environment dynamics, and our theoretical treatment of the coupling between model learning and value learning.

Terminology
: We use “model-imputed sampling” rather than “double sampling” to avoid confusion with double Q-learning
[
34
]
. The term “choice-model-assisted” emphasizes the role of the DCM as a domain-specific partial model, distinguishing our approach from general model-based RL with learned dynamics.
