HLA diversity is associated with TKI response and treatment‐free remission in chronic myeloid leukemia
Authors: Charles Toulemonde, Valérie Dubois, Christophe Bouvier, Alexandre Ridao, Marie‐Claire Archier, Marie Balsat, Sophie Ducastelle, Fiorenza Barraco, Gaelle Fossard, Julie Hildt, Julien Bollard, Mauricette Michallet, Hélène Labussière‐Wallet, Sandrine Hayette, Franck‐Emmanuel Nicolini, Vincent Alcazer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune surveillance is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of cancer treatment outcomes. However, the impact of Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) diversity in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) remains poorly understood and has been scarcely investigated. We retrospectively analyzed 367 CML patients with high‐resolution HLA typing to investigate the impact of HLA allele distribution and HLA evolutionary divergence (HED) on disease susceptibility, molecular response, and treatment‐free remission (TFR). Compared to 2832 healthy donors, CML patients exhibited significantly lower HED scores for all class I loci (A, B, C) and HLA‐DQB1 (FDR < 0.001), suggesting a narrower immunopeptidome repertoire at diagnosis. Specific alleles, such as HLA‐A*30:01 (OR [95% CI] = 2.08 [1.26–3.25]) and B*14:02 (OR = 1.90 [1.26–2.79]), were associated with increased CML risk (FDR < 0.01). Among 289 patients with clinical follow‐up, HLA‐DQB1*06:04 (aHR [95% CI] = 3.71 [1.57–8.77]) and DRB1*13:02 (aHR = 3.95 [1.77–8.81]) were associated with faster MR4 achievement in imatinib‐treated patients (FDR < 0.01), while B*44:02 (aHR = 4.83 [1.62–14.41]) predicted favorable response to dasatinib (FDR < 0.05). In the TFR cohort (n = 105), alleles A*26:01 (aHR = 3.47 [1.44–8.38]), A32:01 (aHR = 3.28 [1.52–7.09], FDR < 0.05), and B18:01 (aHR = 12.96 [3.59–46.77], FDR < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased relapse risk. Conversely, a higher HED score for HLA‐C was associated with improved TFR in dasatinib‐treated patients (P = 0.0067). These findings suggest that HLA genotype and class‐specific HED may influence CML susceptibility and outcomes and could inform TKI selection and discontinuation strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Since the rise of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), the prognosis of patients with chronic‐phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CP‐CML) has significantly improved. 1 However, these treatments are associated with significant adverse effects and socio‐economic costs. 2 Achieving a deep molecular response and a subsequent treatment‐free remission (TFR) are currently two major therapeutic goals for CP‐CML patients. 3 The two main factors associated with TFR are the duration of TKI treatment and the time spent in deep molecular remission prior to discontinuation, 4 , 5 , 6 and represent the basis of international guidelines regarding prerequisites for treatment cessation. 3 , 7 Apart from these treatment‐related factors, other parameters have been found to be associated with TFR success, such as e14a2 transcript, 8 very deep molecular response assessed by digital polymerase chain reaction (PCR), 9 or specific immune cell populations in peripheral blood, such as cytotoxic NK cells. 10 Still, only 35%–55% of patients reach a sustained 4.5‐log molecular response (MR4.5) after 5 years, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 and among those who attempt TKI cessation, about 50% will experience an early molecular relapse, requiring re‐initiation of therapy. 3 , 4 , 12 , 15 , 16 , 17

Hence, there remains a strong need to better predict the success of TFR, particularly from the time of diagnosis, to optimize therapeutic strategies and better understand the mechanisms underlying molecular relapse after TKI cessation.

Antitumor immunity plays an important role in the treatment response of CML, as evidenced by responses to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, donor lymphocyte infusions, and sensitivity to immunomodulatory agents. 18 Phenotypic and functional abnormalities in the immune response observed at the time of CML diagnosis seem to be restored under TKI therapy, with the reduction of tumoral burden and off‐target effects of TKIs on kinases involved in immune cell functions. 19 , 20 , 21 Initially suppressed antitumor cellular immunity appears to normalize through the persistent restoration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells), increased activity of natural killer (NK) cells, and decreased expression of Programmed Cell Death 1 (PD1), along with a reduction in regulatory T lymphocytes once deep molecular remission is achieved. 22 Immune surveillance restoration may have an effect on the elimination of residual leukemic stem cells (LSCs) by the restored recognition of tumor antigens on their surface. While the diversity of CML tumor epitopes has been the focus of several studies, few have focused on the impact of these epitopes' presentation by the human leukocyte antigen (HLA). Specific alleles have been suggested to be associated with CML development, 23 , 24 but very limited data exist regarding the impact of HLA on treatment response and TFR. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

To get a better insight into the link between interindividual HLA variability and immune response, the concept of HLA evolutionary divergence (HED) has shown promising predictive power in immunotherapy settings. Represented by a simple numeric value, the HED score provides an indirect quantitative evaluation of the immunopeptidome breadth using Grantham's formula. Chowell et al. were the first to report that greater class I HLA diversity evaluated by the HED leads to improved responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy in metastatic melanoma and non‐small cell lung cancer. 29 Similar results have been observed in other solid tumors, 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 as well as in hematologic oncology regarding responses to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplants in patients with acute myeloid and lymphoblastic leukemias. 34 , 35 , 36 To our knowledge, no studies have yet investigated the impact of HLA diversity in chronic‐phase CML.

In this study, we assessed the impact of HLA diversity on the development of CML, on molecular response to TKIs and TFR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and study design

Patients diagnosed with CML at the molecular biology laboratory of Hematology of the Centre Hospitalier Lyon Sud (CHLS) between January 2002 and March 2023, and with a high‐resolution 4‐digit molecular HLA typing available through the local branch of the Etablissement Français du sang (EFS), were retrieved and included in the global cohort. Patients in blast phase at diagnosis and those for whom clinical and biological follow‐up data were unavailable were excluded from the analyses regarding treatment response (follow‐up cohort) and treatment‐free remission (TFR cohort). A control cohort of healthy donor HLA genotypes was created using data from the EFS regional bone marrow donor registry (n = 2832). The two primary endpoints were time to achieve MR4 and molecular relapse‐free survival after treatment cessation (TFR).

Clinical and biological data

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from patient records, and biological data were extracted electronically from the local database. The molecular follow‐up of major BCR::ABL1 transcripts was performed by quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) using ABL1 as a control gene. Molecular responses were scored according to the last version of ELN recommendations. 37 A total of at least 10,000 and 32,000 copies of ABL1 was required to reach a sensitivity of at least 4 (MR4) and 4.5 (MR4.5) log, respectively. Criteria for TKI cessation were patients in chronic phase only, first‐line or second‐line treatment due to prior intolerance, at least 5 years of TKI treatment for imatinib (IMA) or 4 years for nilotinib (NIL) or dasatinib (DAS), and a deep molecular response (defined by MR4 or better) of at least 2 years. During the TFR period, BCR::ABL1 was quantified every month during the first year. Beyond 12 months, molecular follow‐up was performed every 2 months in the second year and every 3–6 months thereafter. 7 Molecular relapse after TKI discontinuation was defined as a loss of major molecular response (MMR, 0.1% international scale, IS) on one datapoint. The last TKI used before reaching MR4 or treatment cessation was considered for subgroup analysis according to treatment.

The study was approved by each center's local ethics committee (CNIL ID 24‐5206).

HLA typing and HED calculation

High‐resolution (4‐digit) molecular HLA typing was performed using Sanger sequencing (from 2002 to 2016) and next‐generation sequencing (NGS) after 2016 by the EFS. where i and j are the homologous amino acids at a given position in the alignment and c, p, and v their respective composition, polarity, and volumes. α, β, and γ represent constants. All these values can be retrieved from the original study. 38 The Grantham distance between HLA proteins was calculated as previously described. 29 Briefly, protein sequences corresponding to the peptide‐binding domain (exons 2 and 3 for class I and exon 2 for class II) of each HLA molecule referenced in the IMGT HLA database were extracted using Ensembl annotations. Divergences between each allele represented by the Grantham distance were calculated using a custom Perl script (https://sourceforge.net/projects/granthamdist/files/). Mean HED scores were computed as the mean divergence between the two copies of HLA‐A, ‐B, and ‐C for class I, and HLA‐DRB1, ‐DPB1, and ‐DQB1 for class II.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as count (%). The descriptive table function from the StatAid R package was used for descriptive analysis. 39

For HLA allele comparison, only alleles present at a frequency of ≥5% in at least one group were considered. Comparisons of allele distribution between the overall CML cohort and the healthy donor cohort were first performed using a chi‐squared test. An univariate logistic model was then run individually for each HLA allele, using the group as the Y variable. HED values were compared using Welch's T‐test.

Univariate and multivariate Cox models were computed using the survival R package. Missing values were not imputed. HLA*DPB1 alleles were not included in the cox analysis due to a high number of missing values. For non‐HLA variables, a multivariate model was built with a pre‐selected set of variables known to be associated with TKI response or TFR (age, sex, TKI, Transcript type, additional chromosomal abnormalities, Sokal/ELTS, and MR4 duration). For HLA variables, multivariate cox regression controlling for the significant non‐HLA variable associated with the outcome (sex for time to MR4 and MR4 duration for TFR) was performed. Final multivariate models were constructed using a backward selection approach.

P‐values were systematically adjusted using the Benjamini‐Hochberg correction (False Discovery Rate, FDR) when multiple testing was performed. A nonadjusted P‐value cutoff of 0.01 was considered for significance in the final multivariate model.

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software v4.2.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 1, 2002, and March 1, 2023, 1257 patients were diagnosed with CML at the CHLS laboratory, among whom 367 underwent high‐resolution HLA typing and were included in the overall cohort. Sixty‐two patients lacked clinical follow‐up data, and 17 were in blast phase at diagnosis. The remaining 289 patients formed the follow‐up cohort. Among them, 105 attempted treatment discontinuation and were included in the TFR cohort (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1A).

The overall cohort consisted mostly of males (59.13%), with a median age at diagnosis of 48 years (IQR: [37–55.5]) (Table 1). In the follow‐up cohort, the ELTS score was low in 62% of cases, intermediate in 30.74%, and high in 6.93%. Additional cytogenetic abnormalities were observed in 18.3% of patients at diagnosis. The transcripts were predominantly of the major type (96.89%). When transcript subtype typing was available (88.1% of cases), the e14a2 type was most common (73.1%), followed by e13a2 (11.9%). Baseline disease characteristics in the TFR cohort were similar to the rest of the follow‐up cohort.

The median [min–max] follow‐up duration was 10.52 years [0.50–22.80] in the follow‐up cohort and 14.2 years [5.83–22.80] in the TFR cohort. In the follow‐up cohort, the first‐line treatment was primarily imatinib (56.75%), followed by nilotinib (29.07%) and dasatinib (9.69%). First‐line treatment was discontinued in 71% of cases, with 18.7% stopping due to intolerance, 19.4% due to resistance, and 28.3% in attempts to achieve TFR. At the end of follow‐up, 18 patients (6.7%) in the follow‐up cohort had progressed to acute leukemia, 166 patients (57.4%) had never stopped TKI treatment, 59 (20.4%) had successfully discontinued TKI without relapse, and 46 (15.9%) had relapsed early, requiring treatment re‐initiation. In the TFR cohort, 79% attempted TFR after their first TKI, while 10.5% had to switch due to intolerance, and 9.5% due to suboptimal response or resistance. Overall, 56.2% of those attempting TFR maintained sustained major molecular response.

HLA distribution in CML patients compared to healthy donors

Complete high‐resolution HLA typing was available for 96,5%, 94,8%, 95,4%, 95,4%, 94,3%, and 35,9% of patients for HLA‐A, B, C, DRB1, DQB1, and DPB1, respectively. HLA allele frequencies comparison showed an overrepresentation of HLA‐A*30:01, B*14:02, and C*08:02 in the CML group, whereas an overrepresentation of HLA‐B*27:05 and C*01:02 was observed in the healthy donor group (Figure 1A). Univariate logistic regression revealed significant associations with CML for HLA‐A*30:01 (odds ratio [OR] 2.08, 95% confidence interval [CI] [1.26; 3.25], FDR = 0.0067), HLA‐B*14:02 (OR 1.90, 95% CI [1.26; 2.79], FDR = 0.0067), and HLA‐C*08:02 (OR 1.53, 95% CI [1.04; 2.19], FDR = 0.048) whereas HLA‐B*27:05 (OR 0.47 [0.22; 0.88], FDR = 0.048) and C*01:02 (OR 0.56 [0.31; 0.93], FDR = 0.048) were associated with a decreased risk of CML (Figure 1B).

Diversity analysis by the entropy measure using the Shannon and Simpson indexes did not reveal any significant differences between the two groups (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1B). Likewise, inferred KIR haplotypes from HLA typing showed no difference between the two groups (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1C).

Comparison of HED scores between the two populations revealed a significantly lower HLA evolutionary divergence in CML patients compared to healthy donors for all class I HLA types (A, B, C) and for HLA‐DQB1 (Figure 1B).

Subgroup analysis of HLA allele diversity and HED scores between blast‐phase (n = 17) and chronic‐phase (n = 286) CML patients revealed no statistically significant differences between these two populations, with only a trend toward a higher frequency of HLA DPB1*01:01 in the blast‐phase group (FDR = 0.058) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1D and S1E).

Association with TKI response

Association of HLA polymorphism and diversity with TKI response was then evaluated in the FOLLOW‐UP cohort (n = 286). Among non‐HLA variables, multivariate Cox analysis identified female sex as the only factor significantly associated with a faster achievement of MR4 (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.20 [1.61; 3.01], P < 0.001) (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A). No individual HLA alleles or HED scores were found significantly associated with time to MR4 across all patients (Figure 2A). Subgroup analyses revealed a significant association with time to MR4 for HLA‐ DQB1*06:04 (aHR 3.71 [1.57; 8.77], FDR = 0.0055) and HLA‐DRB1*13:02 (aHR 3.95 [1.77; 8.81], FDR = 0.0032) in imatinib‐treated (IMA) patients (n = 106), and HLA‐B*44:02 (aHR 4.83 [1.62; 14.41], FDR = 0.019) in dasatinib‐treated (DAS) patients (n = 53) (Figure 2A). No significant association was found for patients treated with nilotinib.

Cumulative incidence of MR4 according to the presence of a favorable HLA allele (HLA‐DQB1*06:04 or HLA‐DRB1*13:02 for IMA, HLA‐B*44:02 for DAS) confirmed a significant association with deep molecular response (Figure 2B,C).

Treatment‐free remission

The impact of HLA polymorphism and diversity on molecular relapse after TKI cessation was then evaluated in the TFR cohort (n = 105). MR4 duration before TKI cessation was the only non‐HLA variable associated with TFR in the multivariate cox screening (aHR 0.48 [0.24; 0.94] for time to molecular relapse after TKI cessation, P = 0.03; Supporting Information S1: Figure S2B). In the overall cohort, HLA alleles A*26:01 (aHR 3.47 [1.44; 8.38], FDR = 0.014) and A*32:01 (aHR 3.28 [1.52; 7.09], FDR = 0.012) were significantly associated with molecular relapse after TKI cessation (Figure 3A). Among IMA‐treated patients before TKI cessation (n = 43), HLA allele B*18:01 was significantly associated with earlier molecular relapse (aHR 12.96 [3.59; 46.77], FDR = 0.00046), with a trend for allele A*32:01 (aHR 3.76 [1.22; 11.55], FDR = 0.052). In the nilotinib group (NIL, n = 33), HLA alleles A*26:01 (aHR 12.1 [1.42; 103.04], FDR = 0.088) and A*32:01 (aHR 11.5 [1.19; 111.54], FDR = 0.088) also revealed a trend for an association with molecular relapse. For the DAS group, HED scores for HLA B and C were associated with a tendency for a higher chance of TFR success (aHR 0.72 [0.55; 0.95], FDR = 0.091 and aHR 0.77 [0.6; 0.98], FDR = 0.092), though not statistically significant after P‐value adjustment.

Kaplan–Meier estimation of treatment‐free survival according to the presence of an HLA allele associated with a higher risk of molecular relapse (A*26:01, A*32:01, or B*18:01) confirmed the association of these three alleles with a higher risk of molecular relapse after TKI cessation for the overall cohort (Figure 3B) and the IMA and NIL subgroups (Figure 3C,D). Similarly, patient stratification according to the median level of the HED C score correctly separated two groups with different chances of TFR success in the DAS group (Figure 3E), with the same tendency for HED B though not statistically significant (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

These results suggest that certain HLA alleles (HLA‐A*30:01, B*14:02, C*08:02, B*27:05, and C*01:02), as well as the peptidome diversity represented by the HED score, appear to influence CML development, with a notable restricted diversity in all the studied class I HLA and some class II HLA alleles in CML patients compared to healthy donors.

Very little data exists on the HLA phenotype of patients diagnosed with CML, and our results differ from those reported in the literature. In the late 1990s, the presence of the HLA‐B8 and HLA‐DR4 alleles was shown to be protective against the development of CML in a European population. 23 , 24 A meta‐analysis published in 2010 reported a significant risk association with the HLA‐A*02 allele and a significant protective effect with the HLA‐B*35 allele. 40 A more recent study in the Kazakh population found significantly higher frequencies of HLA‐A*11, HLA‐B*18, HLA‐B*41, HLA‐B*47, and HLA‐B*73 and lower frequencies of HLA‐A*01, HLA‐C*02, and HLA‐C*06 alleles in CML patients compared to healthy donors. 41 To our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the impact of high‐resolution HLA genotype with an NGS‐based 4‐digit typing in a European population and to explore the role of HLA diversity using the HED score in such a setting. The lower HLA diversity on HLA class I and class II DQ observed in this study aligns with previous results on other solid tumors, 42 supporting the importance of immune evasion as a hallmark of cancer, also implicated in the emergence of CML.

While no associations were found between HLA alleles and response to TKI in the follow‐up cohort, some specific alleles were associated with a favorable outcome depending on the TKI used (DQB1*06:04 and DRB1*13:02 for IMA and B*44:02 for DAS). However, the HED score was not associated with TKI response. Regarding TFR, HLA A*26:01 and A*32:01 were found to be associated with a higher risk of molecular relapse after TKI cessation, as was HLA B*18:01, but only in the IMA subgroup. Conversely, the HLA‐C HED score was associated with a higher chance of TFR following TKI discontinuation in the DAS group.

Few studies have examined the impact of HLA on the response to TKI and TFR. In one recent study in a cohort of 162 Ethiopian patients with CML, HLA alleles A*03:01/02, A*23:17:01, B*57:01/02/03, and HLA‐DRB4*01:01 were associated with a poor response to TKI, while HLA‐DRB4*01:03:01 was associated with a favorable outcome. These results are difficult to compare to ours due to differences in population ethnicity, disease baseline characteristics, and the definition of molecular responses. 26

Another study showed that patients with HLA‐Bw6 alleles (a phenotype unable to interact with the KIR ligand KIR3DL1) were more likely to achieve MR4. 27 The same group subsequently reported that the combination of KIR3DL1 and its cognate HLA‐Bw ligands was associated with TFR, with HLA‐Bw4 alleles (which bind KIR3DL1 more strongly) emerging as an independent factor associated with molecular relapse after TKI cessation. 43 These results underscore the role of the immune response and, more broadly, immunogenetic factors in disease control and TFR maintenance. However, both studies focused specifically on KIR ligands and were conducted in a Japanese population. Our study is, to our knowledge, the first to assess the impact of the HLA class I and II (DQB1 and DRB1) alleles on time to DMR and TFR in a European cohort.

Previous research has shown that specific CML tumoral epitopes were presented by HLA proteins, and preferentially HLA‐A*02, HLA‐A*03, HLA‐A*11, and HLA‐B*07. 44 Therefore, Ureshino et al. tested the impact of the presence of HLA‐A*02:01 or *24:02 or *11:01 on TFR and found a statistical association with prolonged TFR. 28 We could not confirm this association in our study, despite a sufficient representation of HLA‐A*02:01 and HLA‐A*11:01 alleles in our cohort. The low (<5%) frequency of the HLA‐A*24:02 phenotype in our cohort could explain the limited findings regarding this specific allele.

This study failed to reveal any strong association between HED score and treatment response. This result may be explained by HLA downregulation in residual leukemic stem cells, a known immune evasion mechanism 45 , 46 making the diversity of the immune peptidome less relevant once CML is established. Moreover, molecular response may be mainly driven by a direct therapeutic effect rather of the TKI itself than an immune‐mediated mechanism. However, the association found between HLA‐B and HLA‐C HED scores and TFR only in the DAS subgroup is quite intriguing and may relate to varying off‐target immunomodulatory effects of the TKIs themselves, each TKI modulating the immune system in its own way. 47 DAS in particular is known to have a broad kinase inhibition profile 48 and immunomodulation properties, including TCR signaling, as shown by its use in regulating CAR‐T cells. 49 Moreover, a study revealed that patients maintaining a TFR after DAS discontinuation display a significant increase in effector regulatory T‐cells (Treg) at 1 month after TKI discontinuation, together with a less exhausted phenotype of CD8+ T‐cells compared to patients presenting a molecular relapse, suggesting a role of immune response in TFR maintenance for DAS‐treated patients. 47 These results were not observed in the NIL group. Altogether, these data suggest a different profile of TFR for DAS‐treated patients that may partly rely on immune surveillance.

This study has strengths and limitations. The cohort size (286 patients, including over 100 TFR attempts) provides sufficient statistical power to analyze multiple HLA alleles across class I and II loci. However, multiple analyses may introduce a bias despite statistical adjustments. A validation cohort would be necessary to confirm these findings. In France, HLA typing is no longer routinely performed in CML patients at diagnosis, and dasatinib remains limited to second‐line therapy and beyond, resulting in a very small number of eligible patients. Collaborative international studies will thus be required to validate the prognostic relevance of HED scores, particularly in the context of TFR after dasatinib.

The bi‐centric nature of the study (both centers located in Lyon, France) and the young study population (HLA genotyping often linked to stem cell transplant eligibility) introduce selection biases and limit generalizability, notably to non‐European populations because of the HLA phenotype variability. Moreover, the high number of additional chromosomal abnormalities (around 19%) suggests a substantial proportion of patients with features of accelerated‐phase CML at diagnosis, which may have impaired their response to TKI therapy. However, this parameter was not statistically associated with time to MR4. This finding may be explained by the current clinical practice in France, where all patients diagnosed with accelerated‐phase CML are offered second‐generation TKIs since onset, significantly improving their outcomes to levels comparable to those of patients in the chronic phase. Nonetheless, the real‐life context and robust clinical and biological follow‐up reduce confounding, together with a proper and systematic adjustment of P‐values with the FDR method to preclude multiple testing bias.

The patient population is comparable to those described in other studies. Except for MR4 duration, other factors typically associated with TFR were not found—likely due to patients being treated in accordance with national recommendations (at least 5 years on TKI, including at least 2 years in MR4.5 or 3 years of MR4). 7 Some confounding remains, such as multiple treatment changes for intolerance, pregnancy, or protocol‐based cessation, dose modifications, or associated treatments (e.g., interferon‐α, cytarabine), which were not accounted for.

Finally, our findings may have clinical implications: the divergent outcomes observed according to TKI and HLA genotype suggest that HLA typing could help guide TKI selection at diagnosis to optimize the probability of response and successful TFR.

To conclude, this study is the first to explore the link between HLA diversity appreciated by the HED score and CML pathogeny, TKI response, and TFR. Certain HLA alleles are associated with response to TKI treatment and molecular relapse risk after treatment discontinuation, but these associations seem to be specific to the TKI used.

Although HED scores for all class I HLAs and DQB1 in class II HLAs appear to influence CML development, the HED score was not predictive of TKI response except for the HLA‐C alleles before DAS cessation for TFR. These results need to be confirmed in a large‐scale multicentric cohort.
