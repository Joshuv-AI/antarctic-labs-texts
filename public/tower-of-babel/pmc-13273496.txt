LLMs as emerging tools for understanding and managing bone metastasis and cancer-induced bone pain
Authors: Jingya Li, Yuhang Xie, Bingzhi Li, Rong He, Wanrong Meng, Jiajing Gong, Yi Fan, Longjiang Li, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArtificial intelligence (AI) chatbots powered by large language models (LLMs) show promise in human-like responses, yet their utility for complex biomedical issues remains uncertain. We evaluated four leading LLMs, including ChatGPT-5.2, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, Gemini 3.0 Pro, and DeepSeek-R1, on their performance in addressing queries related to bone metastasis (BM) and cancer-induced bone pain (CIBP). Using 18 structured questions derived from bibliometric analysis, we assessed responses across research, clinical, and patient perspectives. Qualitatively rating based on accuracy, readability, completeness, conciseness, and empathy demonstrated that LLMs efficiently provide intuitive information regarding BM and CIBP. However, due to limitations such as data bias, weak interpretability, and ambiguous accountability, these tools should be regarded as supportive assistants under human experts’ supervision. Although LLMs hold substantial promise for the medical field, it is essential to optimize model architecture and develop comprehensive evaluation standards for specialized domains.Subject areas: Medical informatics, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are advanced artificial intelligence (AI) systems trained on extensive text corpora to understand and generate human-like responses. Built on deep learning frameworks such as transformers, LLMs have demonstrated remarkable performance in natural language processing (NLP), not only offering knowledge synthesis and reasoning, but also acting as autonomous agents equipped with tool-use capabilities to perform complex tasks. These abilities are transforming productivity frameworks across business, education, and scientific research.

Nowadays, LLMs from different companies are evolving rapidly (Figure 1), characterized by a flourishing diversity of approaches and applications. OpenAI’s ChatGPT, launched in 2022, marked a major advancement in NLP.1 The latest release of GPT-5.2 claims substantial advancements in scientific and mathematical prowess, enabling significantly stronger holistic reasoning and abstract capabilities.2 Claude is another well-known LLM family released by Anthropic in 2023, with the latest released model of Claude 4.5, reportedly as an excellent coding model capable of maintaining focus and stability across extended, multi-step tasks. Google DeepMind unveiled its top version in 2025, Gemini 3.0 Pro, notably enhanced in reasoning power and factual consistency with built-in multimodal processing capabilities. Additionally, DeepSeek, an AI company founded in 2023, achieved an exceptional performance-to-cost ratio and an efficient engineering framework by utilizing a mixture of experts (MoE) architecture.3 The version DeepSeek-R1 utilizes a pure reinforcement learning framework to autonomously develop advanced emergent reasoning patterns, such as self-reflection and verification, thereby achieving superior performance on complex tasks without relying on human-annotated data.3 The progression of AI, from Alan Turing’s foundational work “computing machinery and intelligence” in 1950 to today’s advanced LLMs, underscores the remarkable advancements in this field.

Emerging evidence suggests that LLM chatbots play a competent role in biomedical fields.4,5,6,7 LLMs can assist in literature synthesis,8 new drug development,9,10 gene or protein function prediction,11 and even autonomous experimentation.12 Clinically, LLMs contribute to medical documentation,13,14 image reconstruction and diagnostic assistance,15 and report generation,16 while also showing the ability to almost meet the passing criteria for medical examinations, revolutionizing medical curricula and teaching methods.17,18 Beyond professional utility, LLMs are revolutionizing patient-centric care by bridging the health literacy gap, serving as personalized health instructors, thereby empowering patients to take a more active role in their own healthcare management.19,20

Bone metastasis (BM) and cancer-induced bone pain (CIBP) present a multifaceted challenge involving cellular mechanisms, diagnostics, and therapeutic strategies.21 As the third most common site for cancer metastasis—particularly from breast and prostate cancers (PCa), bone lesions affect over 1.5 million patients globally, highlighting the urgent need for researchers, healthcare professionals, and the general public to have rapid access to relevant information.22 BM, which leads to severe complications such as skeletal-related events (SREs) and CIBP, significantly impacts patients’ mobility, social functioning, and quality of life, diminishing patients’ treatment motivation and confidence.23 Consequently, addressing the burden of BM and CIBP requires a synergistic effort through the accelerated elucidation of pathogenic mechanisms for researchers, the optimized multimodal treatment strategies for clinicians, and the accessible education for patients and their families.

Although generative AI emerges as a transformative solution with the unique capability to synthesize vast amounts of biomedical data, navigate intricate treatment guidelines, and bridge the communication gap between medical professionals and patients, its reliability in specialized medical domains remains under scrutiny. Key challenges include outdated knowledge bases, training biases, potential hallucinations, and ethical concerns in medical contexts. To address these uncertainties, this study focuses on evaluating the performance of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek in answering a questions set regarding BM and CIBP designed based on bibliometric analysis (Data S1), which encompasses three crucial perspectives: (1) research progress for researchers, (2) diagnosis and clinical management for healthcare providers, and (3) daily care issues for patients and their families. Overall, we aim to systematically assess LLMs’ capacity to handle complex medical inquiries and explore the applicability of AI-generated content (AIGC) in this specialized domain.

Results

An unbiased bibliometric approach to analyze topics related to BM

BM and CIBP have attracted significant attention and have been extensively investigated due to their high prevalence and severe clinical consequences. Analysis of the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database through the bibliometric approach, which is similar to our previous work,24 revealed a sustained high volume of publications concerning BM and CIBP from 2020 to 2025, reflecting persistent academic interest in this domain (Figure 2A). Through overlay visualization of keyword co-occurrence (Figure 2B), we mapped the evolution of research frontiers and pinpointed the most prominent subtopics. The identified clusters naturally informed a balanced, tri-perspective framework encompassing foundational mechanisms (researchers), diagnostic and therapeutic strategies (clinicians), and patient-centered care issues. In light of this, we distilled these bibliometric hotspots into 18 specific prompts (Data S1). Crucially, we designed patient-centric questions for a dual assessment, aiming to evaluate whether LLMs could not only generate content that aligns with scientific and clinical frontiers but also deliver empathetic and understandable responses to the public.

Researchers’ queries concerning BM and CIBP

When tasked with basic research queries, all chatbots provided comprehensive responses similar to those of experienced researchers. We evaluate the key aspects of responses generated by LLMs (Figure 4).

GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek stood out, with accuracy scores of 4.41/5 and 3.84/5, respectively. In terms of readability, GPT-5.2 took a significant lead with a score of 4.81/5, followed by DeepSeek (3.58/5), while Gemini 3.0 (2.95/5) and Claude 4.5 (2.72/5) performed poorly. For completeness, GPT-5.2 was the best (4.55/5), followed by DeepSeek (3.71/5). Notably, GPT-5.2’s answers were the most concise, with an average score of 4.17/5. These findings highlight the progress of modern LLMs in supporting scientific research consultations.

GPT-5.2, DeepSeek, and Gemini 3.0 can effectively output core findings in research around BM and CIBP, such as the “seed and soil” hypothesis and the colonization-dormancy-reactivation process (Q1), the “CXCR4/CXCL12 axis” (Q1), and bone marrow-derived cells (Q3).25,26,27 For “CIBP” (Q2), GPT-5.2, DeepSeek, and Gemini 3.0 classified the mechanisms into peripheral sensitization and central sensitization,21 while Claude 4.5’s answer was more general. Regarding the “mechanism of bisphosphonates (BPs) in the treatment of BM” (Q6), only DeepSeek referred to the potential anti-tumor effects of BPs, indicating better completeness of its answers.

However, Claude 4.5’s answers were often overly general and lacked the depth required for in-depth understanding—for example, it only mentioned the basic definition of DTCs without elaborating on their mechanisms of action. Claude 4.5’s answers were also incomplete, clearly omitting key points such as the fact that BM of PCa is mostly osteoblastic lesions (Q4) or the regulatory role of EZH2 in tumor cells (Q5).28,29 In addition, Claude 4.5 had deficiencies in information summarization and integration, often providing templated responses—this was evident in its answers to “pre-metastatic niche in bone” (Q3). Furthermore, LLMs, apart from Claude 4.5, excelled at using tables for concise summaries and induction. Therefore, GPT-5.2, DeepSeek, and Gemini 3.0 provided structured insights and demonstrated excellent reasoning abilities, helping researchers quickly grasp foundational knowledge and keep pace with research progress in the fields of BM and CIBP.

GPT-5.2 (4.53/5) and DeepSeek (3.90/5) performed excellently in accuracy, with Gemini 3.0 following closely at 3.59/5, while Claude 4.5 scored lower at 3.23/5. In terms of readability, GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek took the lead with scores of 4.73/5 and 3.51/5, respectively. Gemini 3.0 scored 3.28/5, and Claude 4.5 performed poorly (2.75/5). Overall, GPT-5.2 demonstrates superior comprehensive capabilities, with DeepSeek and Gemini following closely behind.

Taking BM-related hypercalcemia (Q11) as an example, all LLMs demonstrated remarkable clinical accuracy by emphasizing the critical need for ionized calcium assessment and albumin correction.30 By accounting for hypoalbuminemia, a common confounder in these patients, models aligned perfectly with professional standards, proving their capacity to prioritize essential diagnostic precautions and support urgent clinical decision-making. Meanwhile, DeepSeek mentioned the grading criteria for hypercalcemia (Q11). When discussing osteoblastic (osteosclerotic) BM (Q9), only GPT-5.2 specifically identified the application of radiopharmaceuticals for castration-resistant PCa.31 Claude 4.5 failed to mention “Bone Scintigraphy” and “Radionuclide Therapy” in the test for bone tumors (Q7) and the cardiovascular effects in the manifestations of hypercalcemia (Q11).32,33 Gemini 3.0 did not propose the role of biopsy in the diagnosis of bone tumors, nor did it mention the possible occurrence of hypercalcemia (Q7). Impressively, all LLMs achieved human-level performance in answering a simulated United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) question (Q12), correctly identifying serum alkaline phosphatase as a diagnostic marker and explaining its significance in the management of BM of patients with PCa.34

Plus, clinicians often expect AI chatbots to provide detailed, advanced information. When distinguishing BM from degenerative bone diseases (Q10), recognizing specific signs, such as cortical bone destruction on MRIs, is crucial. In managing CIBP, doctors might seek treatment recommendations such as anti-NGF,35 which is linked to CIBP and may be explored in clinical trials. Therefore, LLMs serve as valuable tools for synthesizing professional information for non-specialist clinicians, aiding in specific diagnoses and personalized treatment plans.

Patients’ queries concerning BM and CIBP

Unlike academic communication between clinicians and LLMs, responses are expected to be more accessible and easier to understand in the context of patient consultations. Asked about the symptoms of BM, the timing, and the procedure for seeking medical advice (Q13, Q16, Q18), and the management or prevention of related complications or side effects (Q14, Q15, Q17), LLMs successfully delivered comprehensive information with a clear structure and process.

In terms of accuracy and readability, GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek performed excellently, scoring 4.08/5 and 3.97/5 for accuracy, and 4.50/5 and 3.82/5 for readability, respectively. Compared with other LLMs, DeepSeek’s answers were more complete (4.02/5). For conciseness, GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3.0 performed better, with scores of 3.93/5 and 3.46/5, respectively. GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek achieved the best performance in empathy, scoring 4.22/5 and 4.06/5, while Claude 4.5 performed the worst (2.71/5).

All LLMs emphasize the need to consult medical professionals for appropriate treatment plans. Among them, DeepSeek and Gemini 3.0 specifically include disclaimers such as “I am an AI assistant and not a medical professional.” Even though LLMs can provide accurate advice in some cases, they consistently emphasize the need to consult healthcare providers, even when answering questions such as “whether to supplement calcium and vitamin D” (Q18). In addition, we evaluated the empathetic capabilities of LLMs and their potential to provide humanistic care to patients, focusing on psychosocial support beyond factual medical information. All LLMs can offer emotional feedback, such as “I’m really sorry you’re going through this. That is a very understandable concern,” effectively alleviating patients' anxiety (Figure 6). Unfortunately, Claude 4.5 showed limitations in empathetic engagement, failing to consistently offer emotional support. Its responses were brief and rigid, resembling a formulaic, associative output style rather than natural communication.

Discussion

In this work, we explored the application of LLMs in BM and CIBP, including basic pathology, cellular and molecular mechanisms, clinical management, and the delivery of scientific and medical knowledge. Functioning as virtual assistants, LLMs mimic human conversations and reinforce their learning based on feedback and individual preferences. Unlike manual web searching, they synthesize information from multiple sources into concise summaries, offering significantly more relevant and informative responses.

To construct a comprehensive question set for evaluating LLMs, we employed a bibliometric analysis of publications from 2020 to 2025 to have scientific access to the authentic research landscape and clinical priorities reflected in recent literature. This methodology was chosen to ensure the breadth and representativeness of our prompts. By analyzing key co-occurrence, we objectively identified core research themes and emerging frontiers, such as tumor microenvironment, SREs, and recurrence, which helped mitigate potential bias from author-led topic selection. Moreover, this transparent and reproducible strategy offers a structured template for developing domain-specific queries in future studies, enhancing methodological consistency and comparability across different biomedical fields.

This study evaluated specific versions of mainstream LLMs available before early 2026. A key consideration in interpreting our findings is the rapid iteration of LLMs. The field’s dynamic nature means that model capabilities, knowledge bases, and potential failure modes evolve rapidly with frequent updates. Therefore, our results merely represent a snapshot of the performance of these contemporary versions. In other words, the generalizability of our quantitative ratings and qualitative observations to future versions is inherently limited. However, this temporal constraint does not diminish the validity of our comparative insights but underscores the necessity for continuous and longitudinal assessment in the future.

Regarding the interaction method, our work tests queries as single prompts in new chatbot sessions, without follow-up questions, to evaluate a single, initial response per query. Single prompt is a preferred method for the performance evaluation and practical application of LLMs, offering the advantages of task focus and low complexity, enabling precise adaptation to specific scenarios, and yielding results with strong interpretability and high evaluation stability.36,37 By contrast, interactive dialogues with multiple questions or user feedback may lead to significantly different answers, which are beneficial for LLM performance due to their ability to leverage contextual understanding and prompt analysis. However, we have to admit that a single response does not capture the complexity of LLMs’ outputs or their potential for iterative improvement across multiple interactions, which constitutes an essential direction for our future research.

This study selected five evaluation dimensions: accuracy, readability, completeness, conciseness, and empathy, derived from the specific biomedical demands of BM management and the diverse needs of its users. Accuracy critically evaluates the LLM’s ability to ground its responses in verifiable medical facts, minimize hallucinations, and consistently align with authoritative knowledge bases and established clinical evidence.38,39 The assessment of completeness tests the capacity for comprehensive information retrieval and synthesis across various sub-topics, ensuring all critical aspects are addressed without significant omissions or fragmented knowledge representation.40 Readability and conciseness optimize information delivery. The former requires LLMs to dynamically adapt their vocabulary and sentence structure to suit the target audience’s knowledge level, providing detailed explanations as needed or effectively simplifying complex medical jargon.41,42 The latter evaluates the LLM’s summarization capabilities in identifying and extracting core information, and eliminating verbosity without sacrificing essential detail or context.43 Empathy addresses the patient’s psychological burden.42 As a complication of malignant tumors, BM is often accompanied by pain and prognostic anxiety in patients, and empathetic expression can improve information acceptance and alleviate psychological pressure.44

To quantify these evaluation dimensions, we employed a Likert scale, a robust tool for subjective assessment in the medical field, which facilitates data aggregation and the final quantitative analysis of LLM performance.42 Its primary advantage lies in its ordinal nature, which allows for a nuanced differentiation of model capabilities across our five dimensions. This methodology aligns with numerous existing studies on LLM evaluation, ensuring comparability. In practice, our panel of eight raters found that this structured scoring system reduced ambiguity. It streamlined the judgment process, ultimately ensuring inter-rater consistency and detecting significant, granular differences in the performance of the evaluated models.45 Our findings indicate that all LLMs can deliver general information and assistance on BM and CIBP to researchers, clinicians, and the general public. The responses from GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.0, and DeepSeek are generally sufficient, albeit with minor omissions or inaccuracies, while Claude 4.5 Sonnet performed less satisfactorily (Table 2).

As for research, LLMs perform better in summarizing well-established concepts and textbook knowledge. Although LLMs can conduct online searches to ensure data currency, LLMs tend to prioritize established sources over cutting-edge, preliminary research, as conventional sources, rather than preliminary studies, shape their responses, potentially perpetuating outdated information or biases. For example, the prevailing view previously held that BM represented the final stage of tumor progression, whereas recent genomic studies indicate that the bone microenvironment can promote further metastasis of breast and PCa cells, leading to the formation of secondary metastatic lesions in multiple organs.46,47 LLMs cannot provide extended explanations regarding such an aspect of BM. A significant concern is that the dominance of established paradigms in LLM outputs may marginalize new research findings, potentially undervaluing important developments and impeding researchers' ability to acquire the latest advancements. Therefore, LLMs can effectively generate initial drafts of professional content, which then requires manual revisions and refinements by human experts.48 Addressing this requires a shift toward more diverse training data and active bias correction to ensure balanced representation of both established and emerging knowledge.

Currently, limited healthcare personnel and medical resources often result in an unmet clinical demand in developing countries. LLMs are poised to alleviate the clinical burden by automating labor-intensive tasks—from drafting records to supporting diagnostic reasoning and treatment planning. Moreover, LLMs also show potential in patient follow-up, including pain monitoring and rehabilitation tracking. In radiology, multimodal LLMs can democratize access to expert-level image interpretation, supporting non-specialists in detecting metastatic cancer.15 Moreover, medical professionals are obligated to educate patients, who should have a comprehensive grasp of their conditions and therapeutic modalities, thereby yielding better outcomes. For patients with BM, severe disease manifestations often impede their prompt understanding of diagnosis, treatment regimen, and prognosis. Here, LLMs offer a transformative solution. Training and fine-tuning LLMs with medical data could help patients better understand their condition, improving patient compliance and outcomes while significantly reducing the educational workload on medical professionals, creating a more sustainable healthcare ecosystem.

From a clinical standpoint, interpreting nuanced patient narratives or providing individualized care remains challenging for LLMs. The accuracy of these models is intrinsically tied to the quality of user input. However, a critical semantic discrepancy often exists between standardized clinical manifestations and the subjective, lay terminology patients use. Thus, AI chatbots may misinterpret patient descriptions, leading to incorrect judgments and responses. Consequently, reliance on professional medical consultation remains essential. Moreover, a lack of real-world knowledge and perspective hinders LLMs’ performance in complex clinical scenarios, logical reasoning, and ethical decision-making.

Another significant challenge with LLMs is their black-box nature, making it difficult to explain the basis for their decision-making and text generation. Interpretability is crucial in fields such as medicine and law, where logic and trust are paramount. Notably, the role of LLMs must be strictly defined as supportive rather than decisive. Given the potential for errors, unsupervised use is unacceptable. Continuous learning would help mitigate LLMs’ knowledge lag, but doing so raises computational costs.49 Improving the interpretability and controllability of LLMs is another way to gain users’ trust and, eventually, enhance LLMs’ utility and communication efficiency. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) enables professionals to fine-tune AI algorithms.50 Therefore, we advocate for a human-in-the-loop (HITL) approach to establish a continuous, bidirectional feedback cycle.51 Precisely, this expert interaction provides a rich source of feedback for fine-tuning, allowing the model to learn from its errors and continuously refine its output quality, which ensures that final decisions remain in human hands.

Last but not least, our study did not explicitly evaluate the potential impact of gender, racial, or regional biases within LLM responses. As LLMs are trained on vast datasets that may reflect healthcare disparities, there is a risk of systemic biases that exacerbate healthcare disparities.52 In our study, this is particularly critical given the gender-specific epidemiology of primary malignancies, such as breast cancer, and well-documented racial inequities in pain management and palliative care access. To address this, future applications must prioritize bias mitigation strategies, including the integration of multi-regional medical datasets. Additionally, employing specific expert verifications is another essential step to ensure AIGC provides equitable, unbiased support for global patient populations.

In conclusion, we explored LLMs’ performance in BM and CIBP in this study. It is imperative for us to fully acknowledge the complexity of the biomedical environment, diverse personnel requirements, and the continuous evolution of AI technologies. As these technologies advance rapidly, the perspective on LLMs should remain dynamic, requiring ongoing adaptation and recalibration.53 Evaluating LLMs requires collaboration among AI researchers, healthcare professionals, and regulatory bodies to ensure technical accuracy, ethical standards, and alignment with public concerns. In conclusion, while LLMs hold great promise in the medical field, establishing a robust evaluation framework is crucial to guarantee that their application aligns with medical professionals’ ethical standards and enables them to make precise, sustainable contributions to human health and medical advancement.

Limitations of the study

First, due to the hallucination phenomenon and the logical reasoning limitations inherent to current LLMs, their reliability in rigorous clinical decision-making has yet to be fully validated. Future studies should investigate domain-specific fine-tuning strategies and the deeper integration of multimodal data. Second, this study did not incorporate comprehensive, multi-source, heterogeneous, real-world data. This leaves the model’s generalizability and the security of the data in cross-scenario applications to be further explored. Third, algorithmic bias and limited mechanistic interpretability pose key challenges that require standardized medical AI evaluation frameworks and ethical regulatory systems to ensure fairness and rigor in clinical applications.

Method details

Bibliometric analysis and question preparation

The search terms were as follows: “TS = (“bone metastasis” OR “cancer-induced bone pain”) NOT TS = (osteocarcinoma) AND LANGUAGE = (English) AND DOCUMENT TYPES = (Article OR Review).” We extracted relevant papers published between 2020 and 2025 from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database. VOSviewer was used to visualize data, leading to the identification of key co-occurrences. To assess LLM’s capacity to handle complex medical inquiries, we formulated 18 questions based on the key topics in bibliometrics (Data S1), addressing three main aspects: the basic pathology of BM, management strategies by clinical practitioners, and common patient inquiries.

Prompts given to LLMs and data collection

A total of 18 prompts were given to the AI chatbots, including GPT-5.2, Claude 4.5 Sonnet (Claude 4.5), Gemini 3.0 Pro (Gemini 3.0), and DeepSeek to generate responses. All LLMs have been reset to their initial settings, with memory retention functions disabled, to ensure responses are free from external influences. Each question was posed in a new chat session to maintain response independence. To maintain objectivity, all responses were formatted uniformly as plain text, obscuring the source features, and presented in a randomized order. Data collection occurred from January 19 to January 21, 2026. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2023-071).

Evaluation and rating of answers

The evaluation focused on five distinct dimensions, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). Our scoring annotations were drafted independently by three qualified clinicians, and iterated until scoring methodologies converged, confirming the viability of the guidelines. The explanations of the dimensions are as follows.

(1)Accuracy: The response is factually correct and free of medical hallucinations.

Accuracy: The response is factually correct and free of medical hallucinations.

(2)Readability: The response is logically structured for the target audience.

Readability: The response is logically structured for the target audience.

(3)Completeness: The response covers all critical aspects without omitting necessary details.

Completeness: The response covers all critical aspects without omitting necessary details.

(4)Conciseness: The response avoids redundancy and extraneous details.

Conciseness: The response avoids redundancy and extraneous details.

(5)Empathy: The tone is supportive and human-centric, specifically weighted for patient-oriented inquiries.

Empathy: The tone is supportive and human-centric, specifically weighted for patient-oriented inquiries.

We assembled a multidisciplinary evaluation panel comprising eight senior practitioners from oncology, orthopedics, radiology, and nursing. All responses presented to raters were formatted uniformly as plain text, obscuring the source features, and presented in a randomized order for blinding. To ensure the objectivity and consistency of the subjective evaluations provided by the eight expert raters, ICC was calculated for each evaluation dimension to quantify the level of agreement among raters with a 95% confidence interval.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To provide a robust estimate of model performance and quantify the uncertainty of the scores, we employed a nonparametric bootstrap method, utilizing 1000 bootstrap samples to generate distributions for each set, with a 95% bootstrap percentile interval to report performance stability without assuming normality of the Likert scale data. Given the ordinal nature of the Likert scale data and the paired study design, we employed the Friedman test to determine statistical significance across the four LLMs. All analyses were performed using Python (3.14), and a p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.
