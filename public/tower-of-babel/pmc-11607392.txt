Geotechnical and geophysical investigations for infrastructure safety zones: a case study of the supporting ring road, Cairo, Egypt
Authors: Ahmed M. Saad, Mohamed A. H. Sakr, Mohamed Sh. A. Selim, Sherif A. Taalab, Hesham M. H. Zakaly, Sherif M. Aboueldahab, Ali E. Omar, Mahmoud Zayed, S. A. M. Issa, Hamdy A. Awad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to evaluate the suitability of subsurface layers for infrastructure development using geophysical and geotechnical studies. Six seismic refraction and six 2D geoelectic profiles were conducted in the study area to analyze the geotechnical characteristics of the subsoil in order to assess its suitability for construction projects. According to the geophysical investigation, there are two main geoelectric strata, each with a different lithology and thickness. The first layer has high resistivity values of more than 200 (Ωm) and its thickness is between 3.5 and 6.3 m. The second layer's resistivity ranges from 0.3 to 200 (Ωm). The findings indicate that the near-surface area of the study site is composed of two layers in addition to the surface layer, which consists of semi-consolidated wadi fill deposits of gravel, sand, and silt with rock fragments. The first layer comprises fractured limestone with clay intercalation, followed by a second layer of marly limestone, which transitions into marl and clay. Both compressional (P) and shear (S) waves were detected and analyzed. The shallow seismic refraction technique indicates that the velocity waves (Vp) in the first and second layers range from 133 to 770 and 790 to 3100 m/s respectively. Various engineering parameters for the second layer were determined, including elastic moduli (bulk modulus, Poisson’s ratio, rigidity modulus, and Young’s modulus), competence scales (stress ratio, concentration index, material index and density gradient), and bearing capacity (ultimate and allowable). The average Poisson ratio for the first and second layers is 0.235. The ultimate bearing capacity (Qu) of the first layer ranges from 379.1 to 1031.2 gm/cm2 and between 1040.8 and 1548.5 gm/cm2 of the second layer. Allowable bearing capacity (Qall) for the first layer is between 126.4 and 343.7 gm/cm2, and for the second layer, it is between 346.9 and 516.2 gm/cm2. According to the geotechnical investigation's findings, the range of liquid limit (LL) values is between 41.20 and 86.30%, the range of plastic limit (PL) values is between 18 and 35.25%, the range of plasticity index (PI) values is between 14.17 and 51.45%, and the range of free swelling values is between 60 and 170%. However, the study identified high-risk areas in the study area. These zones are located in the first and second layers represented by strongly swelling clay layers and strongly fractured limestone, which will have a major impact on the road and buildings. Therefore, this study recommends addressing these problems before any development to protect these areas from subsidence and collapse.

Introduction

Construction activities have significantly increased in response to population growth and economic development in Egypt and worldwide. The development of all countries depends on transportation infrastructure and construction projects1,2. To ensure project success, foundations must be robust, which necessitates improved soil qualities3. In particular, the occurrence of security incidents in urban areas such as natural disasters like hurricanes, earthquakes, nuclear leaks, tsunamis, explosions, and floods have led to significant economic losses and loss of life. These unpredictable events can pose various risks and damage to infrastructure. In the face of these challenges, urban infrastructure is often found to be vulnerable and fragile4. Urban infrastructure conditions vary greatly from city to city, and development is one of the most important issues. Unplanned and unregulated urban expansion in developing regions poses risks to urban areas due to increased vulnerability. As a result, examining these locations before development is critical to minimize potential dangers and their catastrophic impact. An investigation was conducted to evaluate the foundation layers and verify the suitability of the study area for sustainable development using both geotechnical and geophysical methods. Egypt's population has rapidly grown to over 106 million in recent years, occupying only about 5% of the country's territory. To accommodate this increase, the government has promoted the development of projects in desert areas outside the Delta and Nile Valley. Over the past two decades, numerous new cities, industrial zones, and tourist villages have been established as a result of these efforts5. In addition, the construction of the ring road is becoming a must to help solve the growing traffic jams. The Cairo ring road is expected to serve transport needs not only in Greater Cairo, but throughout Egypt, reduce traffic jams for transportation from the top to Lower Egypt and conversely eliminate the need to go through the heart of Cairo. Due to the importance of the ring road, many government projects have targeted it, comprising the development of the main road, the construction of new paved and parallel roads, petrol stations, power plants, underground water pipes, underground gas, underground electricity, telephone cable lines, and internet access, farmland restoration, and transmission facilities. There are numerous issues within the study area, most notably the presence of clay layers represented by Maadi Formation6, which have a significant impact on urbanization. As experience from various worldwide locations shows7,8, various clay characteristics can play a major role in determining geotechnical conditions. The swelling of the soil had a significant impact on the infrastructure and buildings in the study area, causing many buildings and roads to collapse and resulting in numerous cracks in the constructions. The second issue is represented by highly fractured limestone. Limestone cracks lead to serious damage to the local Infrastructure. Damage includes cracking and rupture of paved trunk roads, drinking water lines, tipping of electrical power and telephone lines, and cracking of adjacent buildings9. The soil swelling was treated with many industrial wastes and chemical additives such as marble powder to improve resistance, durability and reduce building risks3,10,11. Grout is key to increasing the strength of the bedrock of fractured limestone to the desired level. The most widely used grouting materials are cement, hot bitumen, asphalt, and clay5.

Many people shared their experiences with the study area and other aspects in their publications12–20. Yang Cao et al.4 civil infrastructure plays a crucial role in today's societies and urban areas, therefore it is essential that it has the ability to withstand and bounce back from both natural and man-made disasters. The importance of urban security and resilience has become a focal point in urban planning and development. Sultan21 identified geotechnical problems of Mokattam area using geophysical investigation. The results suggest that the subsurface strata is made up of alternating layers of limestone, marly limestone, clay, and dolomite limestone, with the clay unit varying in thickness from 10 to 40 m. Elgohary et al.5 the geotechnical features of the high plateau of the Mokattam region for the assessment of safe urban expansion using GIS technology have been integrated. The geotechnical investigation revealed several engineering problems encountered by civil engineers in Mokattam. These problems are characterized by poorly fractured limestone with large swelling layers of clay, whose value is greater than 100% and has a negative impact on the building. Saad15 used combined geophysical and engineering research to study superficial facial features in New Cairo city based on their petrophysical characteristics. The soil of New Cairo City is divided into four types: sandy soil, gravely soil (sand with gravels), clayey soil (silty clay), and rocky soil (sandstone, sandy limestone, and weathered basalt). The first and second types are suitable for direct foundation above them, with passing bearing limits ranging from (1.5 kg/cm2 to 3.0 kg/cm2). Because of its significant swelling property, which has a detrimental effect on buildings, the third kind is not permitted to guide the foundation. The rocky soil is suitable for a direct foundation for it. It also has a passable bearing limit between 3 and 5 kg/cm2. Tae and Gye22 used electromagnetic waves and elastic to get data about particulate materials. The first uses shear waves to study particle properties, specifically changes in effective stress during consolidation and particle micro-scale properties. The second application detected stratigraphy in layered soils, calculated void ratios, and mapped their spatial distribution using electromagnetic waves. Xiangwu23 used the piezoelectric sensor applications in geotechnical engineering, including soil characterization by wave velocity measurement. The goal of this research is to assess the stiffness of a wide range of soils, including gravel, sand, and clay, while also defining anisotropic properties of soil caused by an external load and measuring the strength of the base and foundation materials. Hong et al.24 employed the nonlinear ultrasonic approach to assess the concert's freezing and thawing damage. As a result, many engineering, geotechnical and geophysical studies priorities the exploration of such sites. To prevent having an impact on project prioritize, the geotechnical properties of earth materials (soils and rocks) and the assessment of various geological risks play an important part in the design of technical structures such as urban areas, bridges, and roads25. One of the most important tools of geophysical investigation is the shallow seismic refraction technique for the study of geological sequences in the vicinity of the surface and complex structures. Surface wave multichannel analysis has been shown to be useful for crack investigation and geotechnical characterization of soil near the surface26,27. Surface wave analysis can provide useful information on shear wave velocities, primary wave/shear ratio (Vp/Vs), and Poisson ratio, all of which are significant in understanding the characteristics of fracture-affected rocks.

In the present study, the main application of seismic refraction is delineating the subsurface layering and velocities. Geoseismic cross sections are constructed to reflect the number of layers that are penetrated by the seismic waves. Also, the type of lithology of each layer is determined according to the values of velocities of seismic waves through layers and the geologic structures, which may affect the shallow subsurface section. The study area focused on analyzing the shallow seismic refraction velocities (P-wave and S-wave) to provide insights into the shallow subsurface layers and assess the engineering properties of the shallow soil. This included determining parameters such as Poisson’s ratio, Young’s modulus, bulk density, material index, concentration index, density gradient, and stress ratio. In the last few years, electrical resistivity imaging has grown rapidly. This method can provide continuous subsurface coverage in two-dimensional space (2D) and therefore in three-dimensional space (3D). The use of 2D electrical tomography has increased in hydrogeological, geotechnical, and other surface investigations. The main benefit of this method is the ability to map areas with complex underground geology due to the presumption associated with the 2D resistivity model of the earth28. Sultan et al.29 carried out a geoelectric study in two locations in Greater Cairo, Egypt. In addition, Sultan et al.30 used geoelectric techniques to determine groundwater infiltration at the Hibis Temple in Kharaga, Egypt. Zayed and Nasr31 used the 2D geoelectric survey in the Mokattam Plateau to determine how fractured limestone, shale, and surface water leakage affected the landslides on the plateau scarb.

The fundamental objective of the present study is to determine the geotechnical parameters and soil bearing capacity of the foundation beds to identify risk zones for roads and constructions, using geotechnical, shallow seismic refraction, and 2D electrical resistivity studies.

Location of the study area

The research region encompasses a significant portion of the ring road connecting the new city of Cairo and El-Mokattam city. The area spans around 10 km and is distinguished by its varied and diverse natural features, encompassing friable sands, shales, and rocks. The study region is located between longitudes 31° 18′ 37.01″ E and 31° 23′ 58.66″ E, and latitudes 29° 59′ 11.73″ N and 30° 00′ 41.85″ N. The area was limited to the El Mosheer axis in the north and the El-Maadi Carrefour Tunnel in the southwest. Geological setting

The geological framework of the investigation area was contemplated by numerous authors; for example32–35. Figure 2 shows the geologic map of the study area (after EGSMA36. The study zone is dominated by sedimentary rocks varying in age from the Middle Eocene to the Upper Miocene6. Eocene Age: The Eocene succession is unconformable and lies on top of the Upper Cretaceous beds. The limestone of the Eocene is exposed in the form of high topographical features in the district of Cairo-Suez and represented by the rocks of the Middle and Upper Eocene in the study area. The Maadi Formation includes a portion of the study area from east to west (Fig. 2). The El Maadi Formation is a layer of dolomitic limestone, ranging from 45 to 60 m in thickness, which dominates the Mokattam area with its yellowish-brown color. This formation consists of a combination of shale, clayey marl, and limestone layers6. Oligocene Age: Within a portion of the study region, Oligocene sediments are predominantly represented by the Gebel Ahmer Formation (loose sand and gravel). Basalt flows have been observed at the top of the missing Oligocene material to the north of the research region35 (Fig. 2). According to Said6 all the Cairo-Suez basalts seem to belong to one phase of magmatic activity. The basalt flows overlie the Oligocene sands and gravels but underlie unconformably the marine Miocene sediments. The basalt in general is dark, vesicular in parts, and amygdaloidal in others. The mygdales are filled with calcite, chlorite, and chalcedony. It is, in part, weathered into small ball like structures36. There are no Oligocene sediments in the Helwan region south of the research area35. The thickness of Oligocene sediment significantly increases from east to west. In various locations, Miocene sediments that are not in conformity with Oligocene rocks are found on top; Miocene sediments cover much of the Cairo-Suez area and provide less evident topographical features than Eocene rocks. These can be found around the greater Cairo area. The Miocene sequence is characterized by two distinct units: the lower marine Miocene sediments and the upper non-marine Miocene sediments37,38: (A) Marine Miocene (Hommath Formation); which consists mainly of limestone, sandstone, and shale, is a marine Miocene rock formation. Over time and towards the east, the shale in this formation has become increasingly calcareous. (B) Non-Marine Miocene (Hagul Formation); this unit is exposed on both sides of the Cairo-Suez road with an average thickness of 40 m near Gebel El-Nassuri. It is frequently composed of white to grayish-white, coarse-grained quartz sands that are cross-bedded in some areas but unstratified in others. The sand section is made up of intercalated gravel beds with well-rounded flint pebbles connected with fossil wood. The Hagul Formation can be identified by its lighter color and finer grain size, distinguishing it from the Oligocene sediments. Mudstones are interpreted as middle to thick layers representing a deposition system of fluvial-fluviomarine origin39. The thickness of the clay layer in the north–south directions ranges from 10.3 to 10.9 m40. Material and methods

Geophysical field work

In this study, a 2D geoelectric survey was performed utilizing a Wenner array consisting of 25 electrodes coupled to a multi-core cable. The purpose of the survey was to identify the subsurface lithology in the area under investigation. The geoelectrical survey was conducted utilizing the ABEM-Terameter SAS 1000 resistivity meter, which utilizes the multi-functional direct current approach and enables automatic 2D geoelectric surveying. The geoelectrical survey in the research region involved the implementation of six 2D profiles utilizing the Wenner electrode array. The 2D geoelectric profiles were strategically placed throughout the surveyed route in order to provide a comprehensive representation of the underlying lithology (Fig. 3). The spacing between neighboring survey centers typically ranged from 500 to 600 m. The electrode spacing for the derived 2D resistivity profiles was 2 m, and each profile covers a total length of 48 m within the investigation zone. Following the field survey, the measured apparent resistivity was transformed into a comprehensible resistivity model section. The apparent-resistivity raw data was extracted from the resistivity meter using SAS 4000 Utilities program. The data file was subsequently imported into the 2D resistivity tomography program 'RES2DINV' for processing41. The initial stage in processing the apparent resistivity data involves handling the erroneous data points. Prior to reaching a conclusive interpretation, it is crucial to exclude any erroneous data points. The RES2DINV program is utilized to invert the apparent resistivity values and provide an inverse resistivity profile, enabling the identification of subsurface strata based on their true-resistivity values.Fig. 3Location map of 2D electric and shallow seismic profiles of the study area. The seismic refraction profiles were measured using a 12-channel signal improvement seismograph model (ES-1225 EG and G) from Geometrics. The seismic refraction method relies on measuring the velocity of seismic wave propagation via subsurface layers. The waves propagate in a direction parallel to the border, within the range of greater velocities (Vp). Their dependence is mostly determined by the elastic constants and the intensity of the medium. The refraction method is applicable for two, three, or many layers. In this study, we performed six seismic profiles to ascertain the seismic longitudinal Vp velocity and illustrate the subsurface model. The geophones were positioned at a fixed interval of 5 m, resulting in a profile length of 55 m and an investigation depth of 8 m. Each profile displays two images of P-waves, one in the forward direction and one in the reversed direction. The Vs waves can be calculated by employing the theoretical equation: Vs = Vp/1.7. The seismic refraction data in this investigation was analyzed utilizing the intercept time approach. This method is suggested for analyzing a subsurface that is composed of uniform and evenly distributed layers, which are separated by a horizontal or inclined interface. The purpose of this method is to calculate the velocities and thickness of the waves and generate a model of the ground.

This work includes sixteen open cuts and ten sections in the examination area gathered from various localities which covered all the investigation area, to study the geotechnical parameters of the foundation materials. The geologist first graded all samples on site and then were placed in plastic bags and transported to the laboratory for testing and categorization.

Geotechnical laboratory tests

The laboratory experiments were chosen to get the essential physical and mechanical parameters of soils for evaluating the various foundation beds for the investigation area. On representative soil samples, grain size analysis (ASTM D-42242), specific gravity (ASTM D-85443), and direct shear test (ASTM D-308044) for coarse-grained soil were all done. Moisture content (ASTM D-221645), free swell and Atterberg limit tests for fine-grained soil. Atterberg limits include the liquid and plastic of soils; these limits were applied according to (ASTM D-431846). The engineering performance of the rock was assessed through density and unconfined compressive strength tests (ASTM D-293847). All experiments were conducted in the engineering geology lab and soil mechanics and foundation lab at Azhar University. The X-ray diffraction analysis was performed using a Philips X-ray diffractometer 9 kind (PW 3710) and a Ni -filter cake radiation. The samples were performed at 40 kV and 40 mA from 2 to 50. In order to determine the mineral composition of the clay, three oriented particle assemblies were created using the pure clay fraction (< 2 µm) and subjected to X-ray analysis for the semi-quantitative identification of clay minerals. The preparation involved pipetting a clay suspension onto glass slides. The first slide was X-rayed in its initial untreated state. The second slide was X-rayed after being saturated with ethylene glycol (glycolate), and the third slide was X-rayed after being heated at 550 °C for 2 h, as described by Gibb48. This experiment was carried out at Egypt's National Research Centre. The data gathered consists of resistivity values for different layers, velocities of waves, and layer thicknesses. Furthermore, geotechnical data were utilized to evaluate the quality of soil and rock materials, as well as their geotechnical characteristics, which prove highly valuable in the construction of buildings, roads, and other civil engineering projects. In this study Arc GIS v. 10.5 was used to produce the series thematic map and plot the sample location, electric and seismic profile.

Results and discussion

Geophysical investigation

The geophysical survey includes six shallow seismic refraction and six geoelectric profiles (Fig. 3). The technique of geoelectric resistivity relies essentially on quantitative estimation of the conductivity properties of underground rock units, while the seismic refraction survey is applied to detect the wave velocity of the various types of layers, shallow fractured zones, and some modules as Young's Modulus and Poisson’s ratio.

Geoelectric survey interpretation

Geoelectric surveys are used extensively in structural construction to determine the depth of bedrock and the thickness of the affected area and the extent of the clay or shale. The geoelectric survey utilized the Wenner configuration method for 2D electrical imaging in the investigation area. The interpretation of 2D resistivity data has been done by using the computer programRES2DINV, ver 3.4, and it was explored a succession of two layers in addition to the surface layer, these layers from up to downward as follows: The surface layer consists of semi-consolidated wadi fill deposits of gravel, sand, and silt with rock fragments. It’s observed at profiles no. 1 and 5, with a thickness of up to 1.5 m and a moderate to relatively low resistivity ranging between 5 and 30 Ωm. It may also be present in the other profiles, but its thickness is less than half a meter, so it does not appear in the profile that starts at approximately half a meter. The first layer comprises fractured limestone with clay intercalation and has high resistivity values of more than 200 Ωm and a thickness ranging from 3.5 to 6.3 m. The second and last geoelectric layer has a low geoelectric resistivity ranging from 0.3 to 200 Ωm. Seismic refraction data interpretation

The seismic refraction approach, which involves the investigation of deformation propagation owing to sudden impact in defining media49 gives quick and reasonably inexpensive conclusions for foundation problems at these sites. Six seismic profiles are carried out to evaluate the foundation rock qualities and estimate the geotechnical parameters of the foundation beds. Seismic refraction measurements are applied principally to study the characteristics of subsurface layering. The interpretation of seismic refraction data aims to define the following: (A) geo-seismic cross section and that include P-wave velocity, depth, and thickness of subsurface layers. (B) Parameters resulted from equations based on Sultan et al.29 and that include the following: (1) Shear wave velocity and rock density of subsurface layers, (2) Material indices (Poisson’s ratio, material index, concentration index and stress ratio.

Geotechnical characteristics based on seismic interpretation

This study deals not only with determination of wave velocities and thickness, but also for assessment of soil rock material quality and geotechnical properties, which are very useful for buildings and other civil engineering purposes. The soil beneath our feet is unmatched in its diverse engineering and physical properties compared to any other construction material. These traits vary both horizontally and vertically, with significant differences often observed50,51. To assess the suitability of the subsurface for foundation, various shallow soil engineering parameters were computed. These included elastic moduli (such as bulk modulus, Poisson's ratio, Young's modulus and rigidity modulus), competence scales (including concentration index, material index, density gradient and stress ratio), and bearing capacity (both ultimate and allowable) Table 2. The integration of these factors is utilized in selecting the optimal site for construction projects. The calculation of these factors necessitates the values of P-wave velocity (VP), S-wave velocity (VS), and density (q). The P- and S-wave velocities are determined from the seismic refraction profiles collected, while the density is derived from these values. X-ray diffraction analysis

Mineralogical studies revealed that the studied clay samples consisted mostly of montmorillonite and kaolinite (Fig. 10). In the samples examined, montmorillonite predominates. In the clay specimens studied, kaolinite is the second most prevalent mineral. The mineral constitution of these clay soils, which ranges from relatively passive kaolinite to highly active montmorillonite, governs their technical behavior. Clay minerals are classified into two groups based on their crystalline structure: the two-layer structure (silica-gibbsite sheets in a 1:1 network), such as the kaolinite group, and the three-layer structure (2:1 structure, i.e., 2 silica: 1 gibbsite), such as the illite and montmorillonite groups. Tetrahedral silica layers [Si2O5]2 fused at the corners with octahedral alumina layers [Al2(OH)4]2+ determine the mesh structure of these groups. As such, the X-ray diffraction examination of the clay samples analyzed demonstrated that the significant minerals are montmorillonite. Montmorillonite is known for its high swelling capacity, with free swelling values ranging from 60 to 170%. This high expansiveness of the soil can lead to significant damage to structures built on it during construction. Conclusions and recommendations

The objective of this study is to assess the appropriateness of underground layers for the purpose of infrastructure development through the utilization of geophysical and geotechnical investigations. According to the findings, the area under investigation is covered by a top layer consisting of a combination of silt, sand, and gravel. This layer sits above fractured limestone with clay intercalation, which gradually transitions into bedrock of marl limestone. The geoelectric results identified a succession of two distinct layers in addition to the surface layer. The surface layer consists of semi-consolidated wadi fill deposits of gravel, sand, and silt with rock fragments. This layer was observed at profiles no. 1 and 5, with a thickness of up to 1.5 m and a moderate to relatively low resistivity ranging between 5 and 30 ohms. It may also be present in other profiles, but it does not appear prominently due to its smaller thickness, often below half a meter. The first subsurface layer comprises fractured limestone with clay intercalation, exhibiting high resistivity values exceeding 200 Ωm and a thickness ranging from 3.5 to 6.3 m. The second and final geoelectric layer has low resistivity values ranging from 0.3 to 200 Ωm and consists of marly limestone transitioning into marl and clay. The full thickness of this layer was not determined, as it extended beyond the surveyed depth.

The seismic results findings concluded that the research area's shallow subsurface consists of two main layers. The first layer's velocity (Vp) ranges from 133 to 770 m/s with a depth of 1.5 to 5.5 m, increasing in the northern portion of the region and decreasing in the southwestern portions. The second layer's Vp ranges between 790 and 3100 m/s, with values increasing in the southwestern parts and decreasing in the northeastern areas. The first layer consists of fractured limestone with clay intercalation. The second layer is composed of compacted marly limestone with marl and clay. The velocity variation is due to different rates of intercalation from shale, clay, marl, and surface fine sand and gravel. This variation aligns with the differences in the composition of the samples taken for the geotechnical study. Additionally, fractures and cracks in the limestone layer may significantly reduce its velocity. These findings highlight the complexity of the subsurface structure and the necessity for detailed geophysical surveys in geotechnical investigations.

According to geotechnical studies, the coarse soils in the study area are suitable for direct foundation above them because they are (SP) poorly graded sands, gravelly sands, and little to no fines according to USCS, and A-1-a, A-1-b, and A-3 according to AASHTO. While fine-grained soils are inorganic clays with intermediate and very high plasticity according to USCS and A-7-5 and A-7-6 according to AASHTO, they are highly expansive soils because the predominant clay type is montmorillonite, which is characterized by high plasticity. The geotechnical study described in this paper has revealed several engineering issues, such as poorly fractured limestone and the presence of large swelling layers of clay represented by the second layer, whose value exceeds 100% and has a negative impact on the building according to the Egyptian Code. Swelling soil can cause various types of damage, deformations, and failures in construction projects such as roads, shoulders, and foundations. The extent of these issues can range from minor to moderate to severe, depending on the degree of swelling. The UCS data obtained ranges from 150 to 473 kg/cm2, indicating that the limestone rocks are fractured and medium hardness according to the Egyptian code of 2001. The limestone in the research region is frequently porous and prone to chemical interactions with chloride and sulphate-rich water. Water may be stored in the rocky formation thanks to the pores, which creates an environment suitable for chemical reactions. Some of the components of soluble carbonate dissolve in stored water, leading to a continuous process of the creation of voids and the expansion of existing voids. Large pores, caverns, and pits are eventually created because of this process, which also causes the rock to become non-porous and remarkably weak. As a result, the strata above experience subsidence, bending, and fracture.
