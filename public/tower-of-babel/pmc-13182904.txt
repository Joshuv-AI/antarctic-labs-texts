Variation in time to payment in Medicaid managed care: evidence from national TAF data, 2022
Authors: Jane M Zhu, Thomas H A Meath, Nataniel Moreau, K John McConnell, Susan H Busch, Chima D Ndumele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely payment of Medicaid claims is critical for provider cash flow, administrative burden, and participation in managed care networks. Although federal and state prompt-pay standards apply to Medicaid managed care organizations (MCOs), national evidence on payment timeliness remains limited.MethodsUsing 2022 national Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System (TAF) claims data, we examined time to payment for professional and ancillary services inclusive of time associated with provider billing and subsequent payer reimbursement, billed to Medicaid MCOs in 39 states and the District of Columbia. We characterized claim-level payment distributions, service-specific patterns, and variation across MCOs and states. We estimated state and MCO fixed-effects models to assess the share of variation attributable to plan- vs state-level differences.ResultsMedian time to payment was 26 days (interquartile range, 32 days), with 90% of all professional claims paid within 128 days. Payment times were similar across most selected service types but varied across MCOs and states. While plans paid most claims within 30 days, 53 MCOs paid more than 25% of claims at or beyond 90 days, and 8 MCOs paid more than half of claims after 90 days.ConclusionsTime to payment can be substantial and in some cases, takes several months, with important implications for provider participation, oversight, and network stability.

In recent years, Medicaid payment rates have received growing interest from policymakers,1 with many states pursuing targeted fee increases to improve clinician participation and patient access. While a number of studies have examined the relationship between Medicaid payment rates and clinician participation,2-4 relatively less attention has been paid to the administrative processes by which clinicians are actually paid. For clinicians, nominal payment rates may matter less if reimbursement is delayed, unpredictable, or administratively burdensome. Emerging evidence suggests that payment delays, denials, and other billing frictions may meaningfully influence clinicians' willingness to participate in the Medicaid program, potentially independently of reimbursement rates.5-8

Federal and state policies establish “prompt pay” standards that apply to managed care organizations (MCOs), which now cover more than 70% of Medicaid enrollees. While there is some variation in state requirements, many states extend these federal rules for Medicaid fee-for-service to managed care, expecting MCOs to pay 90% of clean claims within 30 days and 99% within 90 days.9 These standards are intended to protect clinicians from excessive payment delays and ensure timely cash flow, but may be difficult to enforce in practice. States may rely on complaints, audits, and self-reported plan metrics rather than systematic monitoring.

Prior evidence suggests that Medicaid payment delays are common,3,10,11 and bear potentially important implications for health care costs, workforce participation, and access to care. Using 2013–2015 all-payer remittance data, Abe Dunn and colleagues found that Medicaid claims were paid more slowly and denied more frequently than claims in Medicare and private insurance.11 Notably, the authors found that the administrative “haggle” costs physicians face in collecting Medicaid payments may be as influential as reimbursement rates themselves in shaping participation decisions.11 However, empirical evidence on Medicaid payment timeliness remains limited, in part because remittance data are costly, proprietary, and difficult to access at a national scale.

As a result, little is known about how payment timeliness varies across Medicaid managed care plans and states, or the extent to which observed delays reflect state policy, MCO practices, or service complexity. In this study, we use 2022 national Medicaid administrative claims data to examine the distribution of time to payment for professional services in Medicaid managed care. We characterize variation across services, MCOs, and states, and assess the relative contribution of state- and plan-level factors. By leveraging national Medicaid claims data, this analysis provides new evidence on payment timeliness as a dimension of managed care performance, with direct relevance for oversight, provider participation, and access to care for Medicaid enrollees.

Methods

Study data and methods

Data

We used 2022 (Release 1) Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System Analytic Files (TAF) Other Service files (OT) to examine time to payment for services covered by Medicaid MCOs. The Other Service files contain claim-level records for physician, professional, and ancillary services paid by Medicaid, excluding inpatient and long-term care facility claims. These files include services billed using CPT and HCPCS codes, including evaluation and management visits, procedures, laboratory services, imaging, and durable medical equipment, and capture professional services delivered across care settings, including outpatient, emergency department, and inpatient settings when billed separately from facility claims.12 For each claim, the OT files include service dates, payment dates, and indicators of managed care enrollment, enabling measurement of time between claim submission and final payment.

State selection

We followed the TAF Analysis Reporting Checklist.13 Data quality for all 50 states and the District of Columbia (DC) was assessed using CMS Data Quality (DQ) Atlas criteria related to professional claims completeness, service use, procedure coding, and enrollment. Excluded states included those with “Unusable” or “High Concern” ratings for enrollment data or procedure codes and those with high rates of missing or invalid payment data (n = 2). We also excluded states with no comprehensive MCO enrollment or professional claims billed by an MCO (n = 9). A total of 39 states and the District of Columbia were included in the final analytic sample.

To identify Medicaid MCOs, we used a publicly available crosswalk of plan identifiers to parent MCOs using TAF plan IDs, state IDs, and ownership information.14 Counts of MCOs per state using the crosswalk are provided in Appendix Table S1 (To access the Appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online). MCO-level measures were created by collapsing claims to the plan parent level, weighting by claim count.

Claims were then restricted to professional claims billed for beneficiaries ages 18-64 who were enrolled in comprehensive managed care during the calendar year. We further excluded the following categories of claims: those for beneficiaries dually enrolled in Medicaid and Medicare; claims for services occurring outside of the beneficiary's Medicaid enrollment window; fee-for-service claims; claims with missing or invalid values for payment date (eg, payment date occurred before service end date); and claims in MCOs with fewer than 1000 professional claims in a year. The final analytic dataset included 549 million professional claims (see Appendix Table S2 for claims counts and construction) (To access the Appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).

To characterize service-specific variation, we identified a set of clinically recognizable “anchor services” representing common service categories (eg, imaging, procedures, office visits) and across a range of specialties. Detailed code definitions to define each service are shown in Appendix Table S3 (To access the Appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online).

Analysis

Our primary measure was the claim-level time to payment, defined as the difference (in days) between the date of service and the date of payment from an MCO to a provider. Descriptive analyses examined the distribution of time to payment across all professional claims; median time to payment for selected anchor services; variation across MCOs within and across states; and within-company variation for large national parent firms. MCO and service-level results were summarized using medians, interquartile ranges, and distributional plots.

Finally, we ran state and MCO fixed-effects models to quantify the share of variation attributable to state vs MCO differences. These models sought to isolate variation in time to payment attributable to unobserved characteristics associated with belonging to a particular state or MCO, without explicitly modeling those characteristics. MCOs are nested within states, so the MCO-fixed effects also implicitly account for unobserved state characteristics associated with time to payment. We assessed the R2 from each model as an approximation of the share of variation in time to payment explained by state- or MCO-specific effects. Each model included only fixed effect indicators and no additional exogenous covariates or controls.

Limitations

Several limitations are worth noting. First, our measure of time to payment captures the full interval between service delivery and reimbursement. Because Medicaid claims data capture only the interval between claim service date and final payment, they do not reflect the administrative “haggle costs” that may occur before a service is provided, such as prior authorization requirements, documentation requests, or resubmissions after informal denials. As a result, our measures may understate the full administrative burden and total time clinicians experience between service delivery and payment. In addition, as Medicaid claims do not contain claims remittance data, variation in time to payment captured in this analysis reflects both payer-side processing delays and provider-side billing practices. That is, this measure may partially reflect differences in provider billing timeliness or administrative capacity, in addition to payer-driven frictions.

Second, our analysis is limited to professional claims and does not include facility or pharmacy claims, which may follow different billing workflows and payment timelines.

Third, we rely on paid claims and therefore do not observe claims that were ultimately denied or abandoned, potentially underestimating the extent of payment challenges faced by clinicians. Fourth, although we apply extensive data quality checks and exclude states with incomplete or unreliable payment date information, variation in state reporting practices and MCO adjudication processes may still contribute to measurement differences across states. Finally, our analysis is descriptive and does not identify the specific contractual, operational, or regulatory factors driving variation in payment timeliness across MCOs and states.

Results

Nationally, at the claim level, time to payment for professional claims ranged from 0 to 787 days nationally (Figure 1). Mean and median time to payment at the claim level were 53 and 26 days, respectively (interquartile range [IQR], 32 days), with 90% of claims paid within 128 days (Figure 1). Median time to payment at the claim level was 26 days (IQR range, 15-47 days). Across anchor service types, median time to payment was similar, with selected common procedures and imaging services paid within 25-30 days (Figure 2). One exception was power wheelchairs, a category of durable medical equipment, which had a median time to payment of 41 days (IQR, 21-106 days).

Median time to payment also varied across Medicaid MCOs (Appendix Figure S1) (To access the Appendix, click on the Details tab of the article online). While most MCOs had median payment times concentrated within 35 days (75th percentile), a small number of MCOs exhibited substantially longer median payment times, extending beyond 90 days (Figure 3). In total, 53 MCOs had more than a quarter of claims paid after 90 days, and 8 MCOs exceeded 50% of claims paid after 90 days. Several MCOs simultaneously had large shares of claims paid between 31-89 days and after 90 days.

Within states, median time to payment differed across MCOs, with some states showing substantial dispersion across plans and others exhibiting tightly clustered MCO-level medians (Figure 4). States with among the largest MCO-level dispersion for time to payment included the District of Columbia (4 MCOs, IQR 203-270 days), Nebraska (3 MCOs, IQR 61-116 days), Minnesota (9 MCOs, IQR 41-67 days), and New York (14 MCOs, 27.5-46.5 days). Those with the narrowest interquartile range for median MCO time to payment included Utah (5 MCOs, 15 days), Hawaii (5 MCOs, IQR 16-17 days), and Louisiana (5 MCOs, IQR range 14-15 days), Indiana (5 MCOs, IQR 28-31 days), and New Hampshire (3 MCOs, IQR 29.5-32.5 days). Median time to payment among fee-for-service claims in each state differed from managed care but was not consistently shorter or longer. Service-mix-adjusted MCO means appear similar, with only minor differences in a small number of edge cases (eg, North Carolina, Appendix Figure S2).

Time to payment varied less substantially across MCO parent ownership groups, including the five largest national firms (Anthem, Centene, CVS Health, Molina, and United HealthCare) (Appendix Figures S3 and S4). Median time to payment across these parent firms narrowly ranged from 23 to 27 days, with more variation in the tails of the distributions. Across parent firms, similar shares of claims were paid between 31-89 days and after 90 days (Appendix Table S4).

In fixed effects models, state-level effects explained 8.9% of the variation in time to payment. Replacing state fixed effects with MCO fixed effects increased the adjusted R2 to 11.3%, indicating that MCO-level differences, net of state context, accounted for modest additional variation in payment timeliness.

Discussion

Using national Medicaid administrative claims data, we found substantial variation in time to payment for professional claims across Medicaid MCOs and states. Although most professional claims were paid within a relatively narrow time frame, a subset of claims underwent longer payment timelines beyond 90 days. These findings highlight payment timeliness as a distinct and under-examined dimension of Medicaid managed care performance. While prior research has focused on reimbursement rates,15-19 payment delays may independently affect clinicians' financial risk, cash flow, and administrative burden, and may therefore shape decisions about Medicaid participation.3,4,11 Understanding these factors is critically important to improve low rates of Medicaid participation nationally,20-23 particularly in specialties like psychiatry with high demand and critical access gaps.24

Our results suggest that, despite the existence of federal and state prompt-pay standards, clinicians' experiences with payment timeliness may differ depending on the MCO and state in which they practice. Our findings suggest that observed patterns in time to payment likely reflect a combination of service-level complexity, MCO administrative practices, and state-level regulatory environments. Longer payment timelines for certain services, like durable medical equipment, may be driven by greater documentation and coding requirements, or by more detailed review due to medical necessity requirements or coverage rules. Our data did not allow a window of observation into prior authorization, functional assessments, and claims denials and resubmissions upstream of final claim payment—all of which represent additional administrative frictions that delay payment for services.

We also observed substantial variation at the MCO- and state- levels. Importantly, plan-level variation in payment timeliness does not necessarily imply intentional delay or noncompliance. MCO-level variation could reflect differences in claims adjudication workflows or internal operational policies, as well as coverage policies and documentation requirements that may increase claims processing and review timelines. Nonetheless, a nontrivial subset of plans demonstrated large shares of claims paid after 90 days, with some exhibiting broad delays with high shares of claims paid between 31 and 89 days as well. These patterns suggest that for some MCOs, plan-level differences in claims processing practices, administrative capacity, or payment policies could be important factors contributing to delays. At the same time, within-state dispersion across MCOs suggests that state contracting arrangements, oversight, or enforcement mechanisms could constrain plan-level variation in some markets.

Importantly, our measure of time to payment should be interpreted as a system-level indicator of payment timeliness rather than a definitive MCO performance metric. Due in part to data limitations, this claims-based measure could reflect both payer-side processes (eg, claims adjudication, denials, and resubmissions) and potential provider-side factors (eg, billing timeliness and administrative capacity). As a result, differences across MCOs should not be interpreted as solely attributable to plan performance.

At the same time, the total elapsed time to payment remains a meaningful outcome, particularly in the context of cash flow and financial uncertainty among providers. Moreover, variation across MCOs within the same state- where providers operate under similar market and regulatory conditions—may still signal differences in administrative complexity, claims management practices, or plan-provider interactions. These behaviors could be modifiable by plan-specific requirements or supports even when originating from providers.

Finally, this analysis demonstrates the potential value of Medicaid administrative claims data for monitoring payment timeliness at scale. Prior empirical work has relied primarily on proprietary remittance data, which are costly and fragmented. These data are not routinely available for national analyses of Medicaid managed care. While claims-based measures do not capture the full administrative process surrounding reimbursement, they offer a feasible and transparent approach to assessing payment timelines across states and MCOs using existing data infrastructure. Used as a screening tool that identifies patterns of potentially burdensome payment timelines rather than as a precise measure of performance, these data provide potential opportunities for states to identify outlier plans, benchmark relative performance within states, and prioritize areas for further investigation or oversight.

These findings, while exploratory, have important policy relevance. As Medicaid continues to rely on managed care delivery systems, payment timeliness may warrant greater attention alongside payment rates, network adequacy, and access measures. Claims-based monitoring of time to payment could help states identify services or MCOs associated with prolonged payment timelines and inform oversight efforts to reduce administrative burden and support clinician participation in Medicaid.

Conclusion

Analyses of national Medicaid claims from 2022 demonstrated a mean and median time to payment of 53 and 26 days, respectively (IQR, 32 days). While 90% of claims paid within 128 days, a meaningful subset of MCOs exhibited prolonged payment delays. As Medicaid continues to rely on managed care delivery systems, greater attention to claims payment timeliness, alongside payment rates and network adequacy, may be warranted to support provider participation, administrative efficiency, and stable access to care for Medicaid enrollees.
