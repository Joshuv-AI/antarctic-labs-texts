Clinical complications and psychological distress of patients requiring dialysis during the Sudan conflict in Al-Qadarif State: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mowafag H Salih, Maha Qassim, Anfal Mahmoud, Gawahir Mutawali, Ladin Ali, Lana Shabat, Lina Mohamedahmed, Monzer Seedahmed, Eslam S Abdalla, Rayan Mohamed, Yosra AdamSabi, Omer A Mohammed, Saida Taha, Alrumaisa Alhassan, Doaa Mohammadat

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnd-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a growing global health problem with marked disparities in access to kidney replacement therapy, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Armed conflict disrupts health services and supply chains—leading to missed dialysis sessions, medication shortages, and increased mental-health burden among people requiring dialysis. Our objective was to assess the association between the ongoing conflict in Sudan and dialysis-related clinical complications and psychological distress among patients attending a haemodialysis centre in Al-Qadarif State.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study using a convenience sample of haemodialysis patients attending centres in Al-Qadarif. We collected sociodemographic and clinical data (displacement status, dialysis frequency/duration, comorbidities, self-reported dialysis interruptions), medication access, and a single-item measure of emotional impact. Anxiety was measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale. The association with binary clinical outcomes (complication/no complication) was estimated using multivariable logistic regression. The association with the GAD-7 score was estimated using multivariable linear regression.ResultsForced relocation and treatment disruption were common among displaced patients (94.8%). Nearly three-quarters of participants reported ≥ 1 medical complication and 84% reported negative effects on emotional well-being. Comorbidity was independently associated with higher odds of dialysis-related complications (adjusted OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.16–5.51, p = 0.019); age, interruption frequency, and interruption duration were not significantly associated with complications in this sample. Low-frequency dialysis was common (70% received two sessions/week; 26% received one session/week). Self-reported psychological distress was associated with age ≥ 40 (OR 3.59, p = 0.032) and difficulty obtaining medications (OR 2.41, p = 0.033). GAD-7 scores clustered in the minimal–mild range; lower anxiety scores were observed in men (B = − 1.13, p = 0.027) and married participants (B = − 1.77, p = 0.023); comorbidity was also associated with GAD-7 in adjusted models (B = 1.239, p = 0.032).ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional sample in a conflict setting, haemodialysis patients experienced a high prevalence of displacement, treatment disruption, and psychosocial burden. Comorbidity and medication access were salient correlates of clinical complications and distress. Given the observational design, causal inferences cannot be drawn; prospective and interventional studies are needed to evaluate mitigation strategies to preserve dialysis continuity and address mental-health needs during crises.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05055-0.Keywords: Conflict, Clinical complications, Dialysis, Psychological distress, Treatment disruption, Anxiety, Comorbidity, Sudan

Background

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a major global health problem associated with significant morbidity, premature mortality, and economic burden [1]. The number of people with ESKD worldwide is increasing, with a higher burden of the disease in low-income countries where access to care is severely limited. Effective management of ESKD requires access to qualified healthcare providers, dialysis facilities, and medications; in addition, it requires regular follow-up and adherence to specific nutritional requirements. Failure to adequately manage ESKD results in rapid deterioration of health and increased morbidity and mortality. During times of conflict, access to healthcare becomes increasingly challenging, significantly impacting health outcomes. Patients with ESKD experience interruptions in dialysis sessions and face obstacles in obtaining their essential medications. Whether displaced or remaining in conflict zones, they are at high risk of deterioration in their health status [2]. Studies of healthcare delivery during conflict have shown the challenges faced by persons living with ESKD. The Ukraine conflict, for example, resulted in significant infrastructure damage impacting health service delivery and complicating the management of ESKD [3].

Conflict not only disrupts access to essential healthcare for patients requiring dialysis, but also imposes a substantial mental health burden that affects every aspect of life. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a high prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder and depressive symptoms during conflict, with the burden greatest in conflict settings and more pronounced in low- and middle-income countries. Studies examining health-related quality of life, including mental health subscales, have also linked poor mental health with mortality and adverse events among haemodialysis patients [18–20]. Evidence from the ongoing conflicts in Ukraine and Palestine further highlights the impact of war on renal care, including infrastructure damage, missed or reduced dialysis sessions, medication shortages, and deterioration in emotional and mental well-being [4, 5].

In Sudan, the armed conflict that erupted on 15 April 2023 has resulted in over 11 attacks on healthcare facilities, with only one-third of hospitals operational in conflict zones [6]. Sudan is currently experiencing a huge displacement and humanitarian crisis, with over 11 million people displaced, and pushing millions of individuals to face critical conditions marked by severe shortages of food, medications, and basic life necessities [7]. It is in this setting that an estimated 8,000 patients suffering from ESKD seek a total of 70,000 dialysis sessions per month [8]. Those who are facing the consequences of the conflict have disrupted access to healthcare services.

Dialysis centres in Sudan were already under considerable strain before the conflict because of chronic underfunding of the health system. During the war, this situation worsened markedly, with around 80% of medical facilities in central and western Sudan rendered nonfunctional, while the displacement of dialysis patients to nearby states placed additional pressure on already overburdened health services [9–11]. In eastern Sudan, Al-Qadarif Dialysis Centre, one of the few and the largest functioning centres during the conflict, continued to operate beyond its capacity. Similar pressures were also reported in other states hosting displaced dialysis patients, where shortages of medical supplies further strained service delivery [12, 13]. Dialysis patients face the dual burden of maintaining uninterrupted treatment while also coping with the psychological distress caused by armed conflict. This study aims to examine clinical complications, psychological distress, including emotional well-being and anxiety, and the factors associated with these outcomes among patients at the Al-Qadarif Dialysis Centre during the conflict.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study between 25 of November and 15 of October 2024 at Al-Qadarif teaching hospital dialysis centre, in Al-Qadarif state. We assessed psychological distress ”Emotional well-being & anxiety”, and clinical complications among haemodialysis patients during the Sudan conflict. This State is primarily an agricultural region facing significant healthcare infrastructure challenges, worsened by socioeconomic issues and a large influx of displaced people due to the ongoing conflict in Sudan.

Study participants

All 207 haemodialysis patients registered at the dialysis centre was eligible for the study with the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: regular patients registered at this dialysis centre for haemodialysis treatment; patients needed to have been on dialysis before the starting of the conflict in Sudan [to compare pre and during conflict treatment to inform how conflict affected their renal replacement therapy and subsequently their mental health], and patients willing and able to consent to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria: non-regular haemodialysis patients at this dialysis centre, having started dialysis treatment after the conflict started, and patients refusing or unable to consent to participate in our study.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data was collected through structured interviews, guided by a comprehensive questionnaire that covered four primary areas: demographic information, clinical outcomes, dialysis treatment patterns, and the psychological burden of war. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic and then back-translated into English to check for internal consistency and ensure the clarity and cultural relevance of the English version (Additional file 1). The questionnaire was pilot tested on 5% of the study sample size.

Dependent/outcome variables

Clinical complications were defined as any new or worsening medical condition plausibly related to end-stage renal disease occurring during the study period (hyperkalaemia, anaemia, fluid overload/oedema, vascular access thrombosis/infection, documented arrhythmia, and dialysis-related hospitalization), this was assessed by self-reported question: “have you experienced any complications since the start of the war (hyperkalaemia, anaemia, fluid overload/oedema, etc.)?” If a patient answered yes to the question, they were designated as having a clinical complication.

The second main outcome of interest was psychological distress, which was assessed in two ways. First, we asked a general emotional well-being question: “is your mental/emotional well-being burdened by the conflict?” with responses being “yes or no.” Then we assessed the level of anxiety using the generalized anxiety disorder 7 (GAD-7) scale. GAD-7 is a scale used by health professionals to assess the severity of anxiety and as a tool to help diagnose generalized anxiety disorder. It consists of 7 questions to be answered by numbers from 0 to 3, anxiety severity was categorized into four ordinal groups: minimal (0–4), mild [5–9], moderate [10–14] and severe [15–21], which is most common validated tool in low- and middle-income countries and across different age groups [14–16].

Covariates

The main covariates of interest included factors related to dialysis access: duration of dialysis, frequency of sessions before and during conflict, interruption of dialysis sessions, and how many times interruption occurred. It also included assessment of social support asking if someone came with them to the dialysis sessions. Lastly, we assessed demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, including gender, age, education, economic status, living arrangements, health insurance status, acquiring medication [which due affordability or availability issues].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages; there were no missing data. Dependent variables and covariates were described using appropriate descriptive statistics. Associations between covariates and the primary outcomes (clinical complications since starting of conflict and psychological distress — anxiety level and mental/emotional well-being) were evaluated using cross-tabulations and Pearson’s chi-square tests (Additional file 2,3). All covariates that considered clinically relevant or showed significant value in Pearson’s test used for a regression analysis; logistic regression used for clinical complication and Emotional well-being as dependent variables, while a continues data of GAD-7 scores used for linear regression to adjust for other factors (failed to converge in logistic regression) (Additional file 4,5). The two regression models adjusted for; Age, Gender, Marital status, Displacement, interruption, frequency of dialysis/week, presence of comorbidities, insurance, difficulties in obtaining medications, patient have someone com with them to sessions.

Anxiety scores were categorized as 0–4 = minimal, 5–9 = mild, 10–14 = moderate, and 15–21 = severe, for descriptive analysis. To increase expected cell counts, sparse categories were combined, and some variables were recoded into binary indicators (yes/no). Examples of recoding include: interruption of dialysis sessions (any interruption → yes; no interruption → no); displacement (reported displaced → yes; no or skipped → no, as skipping was permitted only when not displaced); comorbidities (any reported comorbidity → yes; none reported → no); marital status (married vs. un-married); anxiety (collapsed for chi-square analyses into minimal vs. mild vs. moderate/severe). Age collapsed to binary variable(< 40 vs. > = 40) and the same for frequency of dialysis/week(1/week vs. > = 2/week), Both done in the regression analysis only. All main distributions are provided in the supplementary tables (Additional file 6).

For Pearson’s chi-square tests When more than 20% of expected cell counts were < 5 or any expected cell count was < 1, we reported the fisher-freeman-Halton exact test instead of the chi-square. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. While for the regression analysis Results are reported as adjusted odd ratio(OR) for logistic regression or unstandardised beta coefficient (B) for linear regression [to detect changes in the dependent variable for a one unit change in the covariate], with 95% confidence intervals and two-tailed p-values; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 30 (for Pearson’s chi-square tests) and version 27 (for regression analysis).

Use of AI

AI tool (ChatGPT) was used only for grammar correction and language polishing; all study design, data collection, analyses, interpretation, and final decisions on content were performed and approved by the authors.

Discussion

In this conflict-affected cohort, comorbidity was a strong predictor of complications among patients with ESKD (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.16–5.51, p = 0.02), consistent with the meta-analysis by Michael K. Sullivan [17]. Age was not a significant predictor in our study, which contrasts with reports linking older age to a higher risk of complications [18]. Dialysis interruption is not usually a direct cause of complications in stable settings; however, during war, prolonged disruption can become a major driver of adverse outcomes and may precipitate complications and psychological distress in haemodialysis patients [19–23]. In our analysis, however, neither the frequency nor the dialysis sessions interruptions were associated with complications. This may be explained by the fact that nearly half of our participants did not experience any interruption, and for most others the disruption may not have lasted long enough to produce a direct effect on complications in this study. In addition, most participants maintained the same weekly dialysis frequency during the conflict as before, although most were receiving only two sessions per week, which is below the recommended minimum of three sessions weekly. Still, some patients with favourable clinical profiles may safely receive twice-weekly dialysis [24–26], This is also supported by evidence showing that when countries at war secure adequate funding and international support to maintain access to dialysis services, lower rates of adverse outcomes have been observed, when patients received fewer dialysis sessions, as it seen in a study from Ukraine [27].

Our participants faced multiple, overlapping stressors. All factors linked to anxiety and depression in conflict-burdened chronic patients [22, 28, 29]. Nearly all reported that the conflict harmed their mental and emotional health; distress was significantly associated with age ≥ 40 (OR = 3.59, p = 0.03), a finding that contrasts with studies reporting greater psychological vulnerability in younger patients (< 40) [30, 31]. Difficulty obtaining medications was also associated with psychological distress (OR = 2.41, p = 0.03), this despite the high insurance coverage among our participants, which reflects the Sudanese national health insurance system; however, in practice, this coverage often fails to meet the actual needs of insured patients. The problem is particularly pronounced during war, when medication shortages further intensify access barriers [32]. These findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that cost-related and availability-related barriers to medication access increase psychological distress [33–35].

Despite these stressors, GAD-7 scores in our sample were predominantly minimal-to-mild. Lower anxiety scores were associated with male sex (B = − 1.13, p = 0.03), aligning with evidence that anxiety disorders are more prevalent in women [36], and also lower with being married (B = − 1.77, p = 0.02), consistent with longitudinal data showing better mental-health outcomes among married chronic-disease patients [37]. Comorbidity was also associated with anxiety (B = 1.24, p = 0.03), a relationship supported by a 2020 meta-analysis [38]. The surprising predominance of low GAD-7 scores may reflect individual and community resilience, adaptation to prolonged adversity or the protective factor of close social ties. Supporting this interpretation, a conflict-period study from Libya reported that more than 70% of students had low GAD-7 scores, while those living alone were significantly more likely to experience higher anxiety levels [39, 40]. As most of our respondents were married and typically accompanied by family members during dialysis sessions, the availability of emotional and practical support may partially explain the relatively low anxiety severity observed, stigma-related underreporting may also be a factor [41]. A Sudanese study examining the mental health of patients with chronic diseases during the conflict reported similar findings, with 75% of participants experiencing low anxiety level. In contrast, another Sudanese study conducted during the conflict found that 62.7% of dialysis patients had a markedly elevated anxiety level on the GAD-2. Because the GAD-2 is less specific than the GAD-7, it may capture broader psychological distress, which could partly explain the difference between the two studies [6, 42].

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling and the cross-sectional design restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, mortality data were not available from the centre, which limited our ability to fully capture the severity of the crisis among these patients. However, the Director of the National Centre for Kidney Diseases in Sudan reported that approximately 50% of haemodialysis patients died during the first year of the conflict, highlighting the heavy toll of the war on this vulnerable population [43]. Third, although this study focused on patients’ clinical complications and psychological distress, a comparison of dialysis centre infrastructure and service delivery before and after the conflict would have provided a clearer picture of the impact on dialysis care. In addition, the reliance on self-reported psychological data may have introduced recall bias. Finally, the use of an aggregated measure for medical complications, such as fluid overload, hypertension, and uraemia, limits a more detailed understanding of the specific problems patients experienced during the conflict.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of haemodialysis patients in Al-Qadarif State during active conflict, nearly three-quarters reported a medical complication, and the vast majority reported a negative burden on emotional well-being. Comorbidity was independently associated with greater odds of dialysis-related complications. Older age (≥ 40) and difficulty obtaining medications were associated with greater self-reported psychological distress, whereas overall GAD-7 scores clustered in the minimal–mild range; lower anxiety was observed among men and married participants. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and only as associations. The study’s cross-sectional design, convenience sampling, reliance on self-report, and aggregated outcome measures preclude causal inference and limit generalizability. Reporting and recall biases are possible, and the aggregation of diverse medical complications prevented analysis of condition-specific drivers or severity.
