Evaluation of immune protective efficacy of recombinant subunit vaccines based on Eimeria tenella EtAMA1 and EtSAG13
Authors: Shuying Zhu, Hui Wang, Yao Liu, Hanze Gao, Chenyang Lv, Shuning Kang, Xiaozhen Cui, Xiaoling Lv, Jianhui Li, Rui Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian coccidiosis is a severe parasitic disease caused by infection with species of the genus Eimeria, among which Eimeria tenella (E. tenella) is one of the most pathogenic. This study aimed to evaluate the protective efficacy of recombinant subunit vaccine candidates based on E. tenella apical membrane antigen 1 (EtAMA1) and surface antigen 13 (EtSAG13) against E. tenella infection in chickens. To this end, the EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 genes were cloned, the regions encoding the predicted signal peptides and transmembrane domains were removed, the sequences were optimized, and the resulting fragments were inserted into the pET-28a(+) vector. The recombinant subunit vaccine candidates rEtAMA1, rEtSAG13, and rEtAMA1-SAG13 were successfully constructed, and the corresponding recombinant proteins were expressed. Subsequently, animal experiments were conducted to evaluate the immunoprotective effects of the recombinant proteins at different doses (50 and 100 μg). The results showed that all three recombinant proteins were specifically recognized by sera from chickens infected with E. tenella and by polyclonal antibodies prepared in the laboratory. The group immunized with 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13 showed the greatest protective effect, with an anticoccidial index (ACI) of 175.48, indicating moderate anticoccidial efficacy. Histopathological analysis further revealed that cecal lesions were significantly alleviated in the immunized groups. Furthermore, serum levels of specific antibodies (IgG and IgY) and cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-17, and IFN-γ) were significantly higher in the immunized groups than in the unimmunized group (P < 0.05). In conclusion, the recombinant subunit vaccine candidate combining EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 provides effective immune protection against E. tenella infection and holds promise for the prevention and control of chicken coccidiosis.Keywords: E. tenella, EtAMA1, EtSAG13, Recombinant subunit vaccine, Immune protection

Introduction

Avian coccidiosis is a protozoan disease caused by infection with parasites of the genus Eimeria, which develop in the intestinal epithelial cells of chickens and are associated with high morbidity and, in severe cases, mortality. (El-Shall et al., 2022). Globally, this disease has been estimated to cause economic losses of up to USD 14 billion in the poultry industry (Blake et al., 2020; Chapman, 2009; Rahmani et al., 2024). Among the various Eimeria species, Eimeria tenella (E. tenella) is considered one of the most pathogenic, causing severe hemorrhage, diarrhea, and even death (Andreopoulou et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023). The life cycle of E. tenella is complex, divided into exogenous (sporogony) and endogenous (schizogony and gametogony) phases (Burrell et al., 2020; Olajide et al., 2022). Coccidial infection impairs nutrient absorption in the host, while the development and release of schizonts and gametocytes disrupt cellular structure and function (Memon et al., 2021). Following invasion by E. tenella, the schizogonic and gametogonic stages can induce hemorrhagic inflammation in the cecum, leading to emaciation and mortality in chickens (Daneshmand et al., 2021; Duan et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2023).

For a long time, the prevention and control of chicken coccidiosis have relied primarily on anticoccidial drugs (Chapman, 2009; Kadykalo et al., 2018). However, the long-term and extensive use of anticoccidial drugs has led to increasingly prominent drug resistance in coccidia (Chen et al., 2022b), and increasingly stringent restrictions on antibiotic use have necessitated the development of new control strategies (Fan et al., 2020). Although live vaccines provide effective immune protection, they have drawbacks, including potential reversion to virulence, the risk of pathogen shedding into the environment, and high production costs (Chen et al., 2024, 2022b). In this context, genetically engineered vaccines, particularly recombinant subunit vaccines, have attracted increasing attention because they offer distinct advantages, including high safety, good stability, and suitability for large-scale production (Juarez-Estrada et al., 2021).

The invasion of host cells by Eimeria spp. involves multiple secretory proteins, many of which exhibit good immunogenicity and represent promising targets for subunit vaccine development (Blake et al., 2011). Apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1), a member of the microneme protein family, plays a key role in parasite invasion of host cells and is widely distributed among apicomplexan parasites (Ferra et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2022). Studies have shown that EtAMA1 is specifically expressed at the sporozoite stage and, when used as a recombinant vaccine antigen, can induce protective immune responses (Liu et al., 2020). Surface antigen (SAG) family proteins play important roles in the adhesion to and invasion of host cells by coccidia and exhibit good immunogenicity (Britez et al., 2023). EtSAG13 is expressed at both the sporozoite and merozoite stages and can elicit Th1- and Th2-type immune responses in chickens (Kiang et al., 2024).

Given the complex life cycle of Eimeria, subunit vaccines based on a single antigen often provide limited anticoccidial protection. Therefore, combining antigens expressed at different developmental stages may help broaden antigenic coverage and induce more comprehensive immune protection (Huang et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020). Specifically, multivalent recombinant vaccines containing highly conserved Eimeria antigens have shown considerable promise in controlling mixed infections and addressing the limited efficacy of single-antigen formulations (Xu and Li, 2024). Research at the Royal Veterinary College, University of London, has shown that combining multiple antigens, such as AMA1, IMP1, and MIC3, can produce additive protective effects (Blake et al., 2017). Studies by the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences have also confirmed that recombinant coccidia expressing a single antigen provide limited immune protection, whereas co-immunization with multiple recombinant coccidia can significantly enhance immune protection (Chen et al., 2023).

On this basis, the present study selected two antigens, EtAMA1 and EtSAG13, which play important roles in parasite invasion of host cells, to construct single-antigen and dual-antigen recombinant subunit vaccine candidates. Their immunoprotective efficacy was systematically evaluated through animal immunization and challenge experiments to provide a theoretical basis for developing multi-antigen combination vaccines against chicken coccidiosis.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanxi Agricultural University, China (Approval No.: SXAU-EAW-2022C.YR.007015507).

Experimental animals, plasmids, strains, and parasites

One-day-old chicks were purchased from Kangmu Farm (Jinzhong, China) and raised under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions within isolators at the Experimental Animal Center of Shanxi Agricultural University. At 14 days of age, the chickens were transferred to thoroughly disinfected, coccidia-free cages. Two-month-old New Zealand white rabbits were purchased from Beijing Baierkangnate Experimental Rabbit Breeding Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained in rabbit cages at the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanxi Agricultural University. DH5α competent cells were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The pET-28a(+) expression vector was maintained in the Veterinary Pathology Laboratory of Shanxi Agricultural University. BL21(DE3) expression competent cells were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The E. tenella Shanxi virulent strain (Tsx) was preserved and provided by the Veterinary Pathology Laboratory of Shanxi Agricultural University. E. tenella oocysts were allowed to sporulate in 2.5% potassium dichromate solution under aerobic conditions at 28 °C until the sporulation rate exceeded 90%; they were then stored at 4 °C and passaged every 2 months.

Plasmid construction

Based on the EtAMA1 (Accession No.: JN032081) and EtSAG13 (Accession No.: AJ586543.1) gene sequences published in GenBank, specific primers incorporating BamHI and HindIII restriction sites were designed with Primer Premier 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft International, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. PCR amplification of the target genes was performed using E. tenella sporozoite cDNA as the template, according to the instructions for the TaKaRa Ex Premier DNA Polymerase kit (Takara, Beijing, China). The amplification program for EtAMA1 was as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 61 °C for 15 s, and extension at 68 °C for 50 s. The amplification program for EtSAG13 was as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 59 °C for 15 s, and extension at 68 °C for 30 s.

PCR products were purified, and the target fragments were recovered using the SanPrep Column DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China) and then cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector. Single colonies were selected, cultured for plasmid propagation, and subjected to sequencing. Plasmids from sequence-confirmed positive clones were extracted. The online tools SignalP 5.0 and TMHMM 2.0 were used to predict the signal peptides and transmembrane regions of the encoded EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 proteins. The EtAMA1-SAG13 tandem gene sequence was optimized by removing domains unfavorable for expression and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Protein expression and purification

The recombinant plasmids pET-28a-EtAMA1, pET-28a-EtSAG13, and pET-28a-EtAMA1-SAG13 were separately transformed into BL21(DE3) competent cells. Positive clones were selected and cultured for plasmid propagation and protein expression. When the bacterial culture reached an OD₆₀₀ of approximately 0.6, IPTG (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added to a final concentration of 1 mM, and protein expression was induced at 37 °C for 6 h. The bacterial pellets were resuspended and lysed by ultrasonication on ice, after which the soluble and insoluble fractions were collected. Protein purity and concentration were determined using 12% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; Boster, Wuhan, China) and the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), following the protein purification method described by Rosano (Rosano and Ceccarelli, 2014). Purified proteins were stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Preparation of anti-E. tenella positive serum and anti-recombinant protein polyclonal antibodies

Preparation of anti-E. tenella positive serum (Liu et al., 2022): Twenty 14-day-old SPF chicks were selected, and each was orally inoculated with 1 × 10⁴ sporulated E. tenella oocysts. Starting on day 3 post-infection, an additional oral inoculation with 5 × 10³ sporulated oocysts was administered every 3 days, for a total of four booster inoculations. Seven days after the final inoculation, blood was collected by cardiac puncture, and serum was separated and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of polyclonal antibodies against the recombinant proteins (Peng et al., 2017): Three 2-month-old New Zealand white rabbits were selected. Each rabbit was injected subcutaneously at multiple sites on the back with 200 μg of recombinant protein emulsified in Freund’s complete adjuvant (Bioss, Beijing, China). Booster immunizations were administered every 2 weeks for a total of three booster doses. Before each immunization, 30 μL of blood was collected from the ear vein, and serum was separated for antibody titer determination. Seven days after the final immunization, blood was collected by cardiac puncture, and serum was separated, heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min, and stored at −80 °C.

Evaluation of anticoccidial efficacy

Fourteen-day-old SPF chickens were randomly divided into eight groups (n = 20 per group) as follows: Group K: unimmunized and unchallenged group; Group G: unimmunized but challenged group; Group A50: rEtAMA1 (50 μg) group; Group A100: rEtAMA1 (100 μg) group; Group S50: rEtSAG13 (50 μg) group; Group S100: rEtSAG13 (100 μg) group; Group AS50: rEtAMA1-SAG13 (50 μg) group; and Group AS100: rEtAMA1-SAG13 (100 μg) group. Chickens in each immunized group were injected subcutaneously in the neck region with the corresponding recombinant protein emulsified with Montanide ISA 71 VG adjuvant (Seppic GmbH, Cologne, Germany) at 14 and 21 days of age. At 28 days of age, all groups except the unimmunized and unchallenged control group were orally challenged with 5 × 10⁴ sporulated E. tenella oocysts per chicken. Histopathological observation of cecal tissue

Cecal tissues were collected from chickens that died on day 5 post-challenge in the unimmunized but challenged group and from chickens in all other groups on day 7 post-challenge. The tissues were fixed in 10% formalin solution, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological changes in the cecum were observed under a light microscope.

Detection of serum antibodies and cytokines

Serum samples were collected before primary immunization, before booster immunization, and before challenge. Serum levels of IgG, IgY, IL-2, IL-6, IL-17, and IFN-γ were measured using ELISA kits (Jianglai Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Histograms and other graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism 5.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Each experiment was conducted in triplicate or with a higher number of replicates. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Gene cloning and plasmid construction

Using cDNA reverse-transcribed from total RNA of E. tenella sporozoites as the template, the EtAMA1 (1608 bp) and EtSAG13 (732 bp) gene fragments were successfully amplified (Fig. 1A and B). Signal peptides and transmembrane regions of the encoded EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 proteins were predicted using the online tools SignalP-5.0 and TMHMM 2.0. The results showed that the EtAMA1 signal peptide was located at residues 1-23 and its transmembrane region at residues 447–469, whereas the EtSAG13 signal peptide was located at residues 1–19 and its transmembrane region at residues 243–262 (Table 7). According to the experimental design, the coding sequences of the two genes were optimized, and the optimized fragments were inserted into the pET-28a(+) expression vector to construct the recombinant plasmid. Subsequently, the recombinant plasmid was confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion. The digestion results showed an insert band of approximately 1998 bp, which was consistent with the expected size (Fig. 1C).

Expression and identification of recombinant proteins

Using a His-tag mouse monoclonal antibody as the primary antibody, the purified recombinant proteins rEtAMA1, rEtSAG13, and rEtAMA1-SAG13 were subjected to Western blot analysis. The results showed that all three proteins specifically reacted with the antibody, confirming the successful expression of the recombinant proteins (Fig. 2A–C).

Identification of antigenicity and immunoreactivity of the recombinant proteins

Western blot analysis further showed that rEtAMA1, rEtSAG13, and rEtAMA1-SAG13 reacted with chicken anti-E. tenella positive serum, producing specific bands at approximately 58, 32, and 73 kDa, respectively (Fig. 3A–C). In contrast, no reaction was observed with chicken negative serum (Fig. 3D–F). In addition, these three recombinant proteins were recognized by rabbit polyclonal antibodies against the recombinant proteins (Fig. 3G–I) but showed no reaction with rabbit negative serum (Fig. 3J–L). These results indicate that the three recombinant proteins expressed in this study possess good antigenicity and immunoreactivity.

Evaluation of protective efficacy

The immunoprotective efficacy was evaluated using multiple parameters related to E. tenella infection. Compared with the K group, the G group and all immunized and challenged groups showed significantly lower average weight gain and RWG (P < 0.05). However, compared with the G group, all immunized groups showed significantly increased average weight gain, RWG, lesion reduction rate, and oocyst reduction rate, together with significantly decreased lesion scores and OPG (P < 0.05; Table 8, Table 9). Among the immunized groups, the AS50 group showed the most favorable overall protective performance. Notably, this group achieved an ACI of 175.48, indicating moderate anticoccidial efficacy. Among the tested doses, the AS50 group demonstrated better immunoprotective efficacy than the other immunized groups (Table 9).

Histopathological observation

Histopathological examination of cecal tissues was performed at 7 days post-challenge. The K group displayed intact cecal mucosal architecture and abundant intestinal glands, with no obvious pathological changes (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the G group showed severe lesions, including extensive epithelial necrosis and shedding, destruction of glandular architecture, numerous parasites at different developmental stages, dense inflammatory cell infiltration, and marked hemorrhage (Fig. 4B). The A50 group still exhibited disrupted cecal architecture, with extensive epithelial loss, numerous gametocytes and oocysts, and substantial inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4C). The A100, S50, S100, and AS100 groups showed largely preserved cecal architecture, with only focal epithelial shedding, mild epithelial degeneration, and a small number of parasites (Fig. 4D-G). Notably, the AS50 group exhibited the mildest lesions, with relatively intact cecal mucosal and glandular structures, minimal hemorrhage, and very few scattered oocysts, suggesting only mild pathological involvement (Fig. 4H). These findings indicate that immunization, particularly with 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13, markedly alleviated cecal pathology.

Analysis of immune response

Before booster immunization and before challenge, all immunized groups showed significantly higher IgG and IgY levels than the G group (P < 0.05), with the AS50 group showing the greatest increase; antibody titers before challenge were also significantly higher than those before booster immunization (P < 0.05; Figure 5A and B). Regarding cytokines, no significant differences in IL-2, IL-6, IL-17, or IFN-γ levels were observed among groups before primary immunization. However, before booster immunization, all immunized groups exhibited significantly elevated cytokine levels compared with pre-primary immunization levels (P < 0.05), and these levels further increased before challenge (P < 0.05), with the AS50 group consistently showing the most pronounced elevation (Fig. 5C–F).

Discussion

Avian coccidiosis is one of the major diseases that threaten poultry health and production. E. tenella primarily infects chickens 15–45 days of age and has been associated with morbidity rates of up to 83% and mortality rates of approximately 15–30%, making it one of the most pathogenic Eimeria species (El-Shall et al., 2022). Current control of avian coccidiosis relies primarily on anticoccidial drugs; however, these drugs have numerous drawbacks, necessitating the urgent development of safe and effective vaccines.

Sporozoites and merozoites are crucial for parasite invasion and development. EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 are expressed during both the sporozoite and merozoite stages (Britez et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2020). Therefore, the high expression of AMA1 and SAG proteins in sporozoites, merozoites, or both supports their potential as vaccine antigen candidates. In this study, prokaryotic expression plasmids for EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 were successfully constructed. The corresponding recombinant proteins were purified, and polyclonal antibodies against these recombinant proteins were prepared. Western blot analysis showed that all three recombinant proteins were recognized by sera from chickens infected with E. tenella and by laboratory-prepared polyclonal antibodies, confirming their antigenicity and immunoreactivity. This finding is consistent with a previous study on EtROP30, which suggested that prokaryotically expressed recombinant proteins can retain antigenic epitopes of the native protein (Bingxiang et al., 2022).

Wang et al. (2026) evaluated the protective efficacy of a recombinant gametocyte protein (rEnGAM82-T) and a multi-component fusion protein (rEnGAM82-T-22) at different doses. They found that the 100 μg dose of the single antigen rEnGAM82-T provided moderate protection, whereas the lower 50 μg dose of the fusion protein rEnGAM82-T-22 resulted in the greatest relative weight gain and overall anticoccidial efficacy. This study evaluated the immunoprotective efficacy of the EtAMA1-SAG13 recombinant subunit vaccine. Based on the above findings, doses of 50 and 100 μg were selected. The results showed that the 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13 group exhibited the most favorable protective efficacy, with relative weight gain of 89.48%, oocyst reduction of 79.36%, and an ACI of 175.48, indicating moderate anticoccidial efficacy.

The relatively favorable protection conferred by 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13, together with the anticoccidial effects observed in the other immunized and challenged groups, is consistent with recent studies evaluating novel recombinant proteins. For instance, vaccination with 50 μg of recombinant macrophage migration inhibitory factor (rEtMIF) similarly elicited significant cellular and humoral immune responses, highlighting the potential value of targeting key parasite proteins involved in invasion and immune evasion at optimized doses (Bai et al., 2025). Furthermore, whereas the present study used prokaryotically expressed recombinant proteins administered by subcutaneous injection, alternative delivery vehicles are also being actively explored. A whole-yeast-based oral vaccine expressing EtAMA1 and other antigens reduced parasite replication and cecal lesions (Soutter et al., 2022). Although the delivery route differed, both studies support the immunoprotective potential of AMA1-based vaccine strategies. Overall, these results indicate that the rEtAMA1-SAG13 subunit vaccine candidate can confer measurable immune protection against E. tenella infection.

The histopathological findings were consistent with previous reports showing numerous oocysts, severe hemorrhage in the cecal lamina propria, complete shedding of the mucosal epithelium, and edema in the muscular layer following E. tenella infection (Suprihati and Yunus, 2018). In the present study, E. tenella infection resulted in cecal structural damage, disruption of intestinal villi, shedding of cecal mucosal epithelial cells, capillary dilation with severe hemorrhage, and coccidial oocysts at various developmental stages in the upper mucosa, lamina propria, and intestinal contents. However, in the groups immunized with recombinant proteins, cecal lesions were alleviated, with the 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13 group showing the most intact cecal structure and the least damage.

Li et al. (2018) constructed an EtAMA1-expressing Lactobacillus vaccine, which induced high levels of IgG antibodies in immunized chickens. Studies have shown that the specific IgY antibody level in the group immunized with EmARM-β was significantly increased, and the antibody level after booster immunization was much higher than after the primary immunization (Chen et al., 2022a). It has also been demonstrated that specific IgG antibody levels were significantly increased after booster immunization in chickens immunized with a recombinant MIC2 subunit vaccine against E. tenella (Wei et al., 2025). The results showed that serum specific antibody levels were generally higher before the secondary immunization than before the primary immunization. Furthermore, before booster immunization and before challenge, serum IgG and IgY antibody levels in the immunized and challenged groups were higher than those in the non-immunized and non-challenged group and the non-immunized challenged group, indicating that the recombinant proteins effectively stimulated the production of high levels of serum antibodies. A related study reported significantly higher IgY, IFN-γ, and IL-17 levels in chickens inoculated with the pEGFP-N1-EtSAG4 eukaryotic expression vector than in the control group (Zhao et al., 2020). A study using a multi-epitope recombinant Toxoplasma gondii protein containing SAG1, AMA1, ROP2, and GRA4 showed that immunized mice exhibited stronger Th1-type immune responses, improved survival, and higher specific antibody levels than control mice. ELISA results from related studies have also demonstrated that nanoparticle-encapsulated E. tenella subunit vaccines can significantly increase serum levels of key cellular immune cytokines, including IL-6, IL-17, and IFN-γ, in chickens (Huang et al., 2021). In the present study, serum levels of IgG, IgY, IL-2, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-17 were measured by ELISA before primary immunization, before booster immunization, and before challenge. The results showed that IL-2, IFN-γ, IL-6, and IL-17 levels were significantly increased in all immunized and challenged groups at the tested doses (P < 0.05), with the most pronounced increases observed in the 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13 group. These findings suggest that immunization with the recombinant proteins elicited both cellular and humoral immune responses, with cellular immune responses being predominant. This pattern is consistent with previous reports.

Conclusion

In conclusion, prokaryotic expression vectors carrying E. tenella EtAMA1 and EtSAG13 were successfully constructed, and the purified recombinant proteins demonstrated good antigenicity and immunogenicity. Animal challenge experiments further showed that the 50 μg rEtAMA1-SAG13 immunization group achieved an ACI of 175.48, indicating moderate anticoccidial efficacy and superior protection compared with the single-antigen groups. These findings indicate that this multi-antigen recombinant subunit vaccine strategy may help reduce the pathological effects of E. tenella infection and provide a promising candidate approach for the prevention and control of avian coccidiosis.
