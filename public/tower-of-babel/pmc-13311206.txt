Bidens pilosa extract and bentonite, a phytogenic formulation, as a feed additive to improve diarrhea and gut microbiota in calves: Effects on feed use and regulation of gut microbiota
Authors: Yi-San Lee, Tien-Fen Kuo, Greta Yang, Yu-Chuan Liang, Wen-Chin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Gut health impacts host health and growth performance in livestock.•One phytogenic formulation, composed of the Bidens pilosa and bentonite (BPB), is a promising alternative to antibiotics in the livestock industry.•BPB modulates gut microbiota via increases bacteria associated with beneficial effects and decreases bacteria associated with harmful effects.

Introduction

Around 1.4 billion beef cattle and 264 million dairy cows are reared worldwide annually (Tona, 2022) with a global market value of USD 1.02 trillion (Schrobback et al., 2023). Calves are known as the future of cattle herds (Palczynski et al., 2022).

Gut health impacts host health and performance in cattle, as the digestive tract is crucial for food digestion and nutrient absorption, as well as for mucosal immunity against pathogens (Celi et al., 2017). Cattle have extraordinary richness and diversity of microbiota in their digestive tract (Jessop et al., 2024), the microbes of which contribute to food metabolism, detoxification, growth performance, milk production, and host defense against pathogens. Thus, the balance of gut microbiota supports disease control and better performance in cattle (Du et al., 2023a). Gut microbiota are present from the beginning of gut development in cattle and are gradually established in the calf’s early life. Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobia, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and a small number of Actinomycetes are first found in the guts of calves after birth. Proteobacteria gradually decline, and Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes increase in the calf gut (Du et al., 2023b). Escherichia coli, Clostridium, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can be detected in calves within 12 weeks after birth (Du et al., 2023b). Both beneficial and harmful bacteria are present in cattle guts. Among these, the Lactobacillus genus in particular may be beneficial to health. In contrast, E. coli, Clostridium perfringens and Salmonella enterica are harmful bacteria that cause calf diarrhea (Manzoor et al., 2018). Diarrhea is associated with microbial infections or nutritional changes. However, this disease leads to poor performance, high medical costs, and the possibility of death in calves (Manzoor et al., 2018).

Antibiotics are commonly used to treat calf scours. However, inappropriate use of antibiotics leads to disruption of gut microbiota, and antibiotic resistance and residue in cattle (Kisoo et al., 2023). As an alternative, supplementation with probiotics and probiotic metabolites promotes host health and milk production in cattle (Arshad et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024). However, exogenous probiotic and metabolite supplementation sometimes yields mixed outcomes due to strain specificity, retention time in the animal gut, and host status (Minh et al., 2023). Phytogenics, composed of plant extracts and/or compounds, are increasingly being considered as another option (Minh et al., 2023). It has been suggested that 1340 plants possess anti-bacterial activity (Vaou et al., 2021). Within this group, Bidens pilosa (Asteraceae) has been documented to treat gastrointestinal diseases, microbial infections, and other ailments in animals and humans (Bartolome et al., 2013). More recently, phytogenics, containing B. pilosa, have been demonstrated to promote health, growth performance and gut eubiosis in poultry (Chang et al., 2016; Minh et al., 2023), fish (Chen et al., 2024), and shrimp (Lee et al., 2024). A common mechanism of action reported in these studies was the regulation of endogenous probiotics and their metabolites. Furthermore, bentonite, a type of clay, is frequently used as an antidiarrheal agent to treat diarrhea in calves by removing intestinal water (Dzagurov et al., 2021). However, the effect of a combination of B. pilosa and bentonite on calf diarrhea has not been studied. In this study, we examined the effect and likely mechanism of a phytogenic formulation, BPB, comprising B. pilosa extract and bentonite, on diarrhea and gut microbiota in calves.

Materials and methods

Chemicals, reagents, bacteria, and bacterial media

Ampicillin (Amp) (Sigma-Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany), gentamycin (GEM) (Sigma-Aldrich), and a bovine immunoglobulin A (IgA) Elisa kit (Fortis, Boston, MA) were purchased. The phytogenic formulation, BPB, composed of B. pilosa extract and bentonite at a one-to-one ratio, was manufactured by Eubiotics Co. (Taipei, Taiwan) in accordance with good manufacturing practice guidelines. Briefly, B. pilosa was extracted with 10-fold volume of hot water for 2 h and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for quality control. After lyophilization, the plant material was pulverized and mixed with bentonite at a 1:1 ratio to form the phytogenic formulation, BPB. Quality control of the BPB was conducted as shown in Figure S1. The green powder that constitutes the extract has a particle size of 100–200 µm. Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB), de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium, Plate Count Agar (PCA) plates, Eosin Methylene Blue Agar (EMBA) plates, Salmonella Shigella Agar (SSA) plates, Reinforced Clostridial Agar (RCMA) plates, Campy Blood-Free Selective agar (modified CCDA) plates, Brilliance E. coli/coliform Selective Agar, and agar were purchased from Becton Dickinson (Sparks, MD). Lactobacillus reuteri (ATCC BAA-2837) was cultured under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 16 h. E. coli(ATCC 25922) was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth with shaking under facultative anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 16 h. Amp (10 µg/mL) and GEM (10 µg/disc) were used throughout this study unless indicated otherwise.

Animal study

Twenty-six 15-day-old calves (Holstein Friesian) were housed one animal per pen at Kao-Dah dairy farm (Kaohsiung, Taiwan), and randomly assigned into a control group (15 heads) and BPB group (11 heads). They were handled according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No 24–08–2277). The personnel assessing fecal scores and weighing were blinded. One observer recorded fecal patterns. The fecal patterns were subsequently scored by the other outcome assessor. The calves in each group, one calf per cage, were given access to water ad libitum throughout the experiments. The two groups were fed a standard creep diet (CTR) or a diet containing 0.5% BPB (2.5 L/meal, prewarmed at 37 °C), twice a day for 4 weeks. Feed equipment was sterilized with boiling water (100 °C) for 15 min weekly. The nutrient composition of the diet is shown in Table S1. Feeding records are indicated in Table S2. The initial body weight of calves was 48.82 ± 0.79 kg (for the control group) and 48.78 ± 0.92 kg (for the BPB group), respectively. The initial diet was 4 kg, at the age of 15 days to 28 days, and 4.5 kg, at the age of 15 days to 28 days, respectively. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as the ratio of feed consumption to body weight gain. The animals’ body weights were measured before and after the 4-week treatment, and their stools were inspected for diarrhea once a day from day 0 to day 28, and fecal scores were recorded. As published (Lee et al., 2024; Probo & Veronesi, 2022), fecal patterns and scores of each calf were determined based on a 4-level grading scale; 0 = normal stools (firm but not hard), 1 = soft stools (semi-formed or pasty), 2 = runny stools (spreads readily), and 3 = watery stools (splatters). Alternatively, fecal scores (%) were calculated using the following formula. Percentage (%) = 100% × (a summation of feces at each scale divided by the total feces). Fecal samples of all calves were further collected on day 0 and 28 for bacterial plating, metagenomic analysis and fecal IgA analysis. For plating, bacteria were diluted 1-fold, 10-fold, 1000-fold, and 100000-fold in the medium (three replicates) unless indicated otherwise.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing for gut microbiota profiling

Three out of the 15 control calves and three out of the 11 BPB-treated calves were randomly selected. Their stools were collected on day 0 and day 28 post-treatment. The 12 samples underwent DNA purification using PrestoTM Stool DNA Extraction Kit Quick Protocol (Geneaia Biotech, Taiwan) (Lee et al., 2024). After DNA quantification, bacterial DNA samples were used as templates to produce 16S-rRNA gene amplicons using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with 16S rRNA primers (Table S3). PCR conditions were denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min, 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 sec, 50 °C for 30 sec, and 72 °C for 30 sec, and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplicons were sequenced for 16S rRNA NGS (NovaSeq sequencing 6000, Illumina, San Diego, CA). The sequencing depth was 60,000 reads per sample. The 16S rRNA sequences were trimmed using Chimera Check and analyzed using the DADA2 pipeline Classifier (Quast et al., 2013) in conjunction with SILVA (v132) to infer amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Quast et al., 2013). For initial quality control, the R1 and R2 reads were trimmed for primer sequences, aligned in orientation, truncated at the low-quality end, and filtered with an expected error (EE) over 8 or containing any ambiguous base. The retained R1 and R2 reads were then used to learn the error model, and the denoise step was performed with pseudo-pooling. After merging paired reads and removing chimeras, the taxonomy of ASVs was identified using the SILVA database (v132) (Pascoal et al., 2024). ASVs without assigned species were additionally searched against the SILVA database using vsearch (Rognes et al., 2016) (–usearch_global) with 99% identity to obtain more species-level information. The phylogenetic relationships among ASVs were estimated using the R (v.4.5.2.1) and presented as rarefaction curves and the bacterial genus composition. The microbiota data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by non-parametric multiple comparison method (pairwise Dunn’s test with a 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05). The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used in multiple comparison analysis for controlling the FDR (P value adjustment).

Minimum activatory concentration test and minimum inhibitory concentration test

The minimum activatory concentration test (MAC) and the minimum inhibitory concentration test (MIC) of BPB for bacteria were measured in 5 to 8 replicates (Lee et al., 2024). For the MAC tests, L. reuteri (1 × 104 CFU/mL) was incubated with BPB (0.125–8 µg/mL) and 0.1% methanol (CTR) at 37 °C for 9 h. For the MIC tests, E. coli(1 × 104 CFU/mL) was incubated with BPB (50–1600 µg/mL), Amp (10 µg/mL), and 0.1% methanol at 37 °C for 16 h. Proliferation of bacteria was calculated according to the formula: bacterial growth (%) = ratio of optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the treatment group to that of the CTR group × 100.

Disc diffusion assays

To test antagonism between E. coliand L. reuteri, E. coliwas grown on LB agar plates at 37 °C for 16 h. One MRS agar disc (NC1), one paper disc soaked in water (150 µl, NC2), one paper disc infused with GEM (10 µg), and one MRS agar disc where L. reuteri (2 × 105 CFU) grew were positioned on the top of LB agar plates on which E. coliwas grown at 37 °C for a further 16 h. The diameter of the inhibition zone was imaged and measured with ImageJ software (v.1.53p). Three replicates of such assays were performed. Alternatively, the same disc diffusion assays were performed for E. coliin co-culture with each and a mixture of 5 SCFA, which had the same dosages as those in the stools of control and BPB-fed calves, were used.

Quantification of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) in calf stools using LC-MS/MS

The same stools of both calf groups on day 28 post-treatment (Fig. 3) were extracted with water, followed by incubation of 13C6–3NPH·HCl with the water extracts as published (Minh et al., 2023). The reaction products were characterized using the Acquity UPLC chromatography coupled to a Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer (Waters, Millford, MA) with an ESI source in negative mode.

Results

BPB reduces diarrhea and increases body weight in calves

First, fecal scores of 1-day-old calves fed the standard creep diet and the BPB diet composed of B. pilosa extract and bentonite, for 2 weeks were measured to determine a suitable dose of BPB for the field trial. Control calves, fed with a standard creep diet, had a higher fecal score than those calves fed with BPB at the indicated dosages (CTR vs.0.1%, 0.5% and 1% BPB, Figure S2A). Of note, reduction of fecal scores in BPB-treated calves was dose-dependent (Figure S2A). In addition, the individual components of BPB, the B. pilosa extract (BP) and bentonite (BT), reduced this score, respectively, to a lesser extent than BPB (Figure S2B). However, bentonite only produced a moderate reduction of fecal score in calves (BT, Figure S2B). In the interest of efficacy and cost-effectiveness, 0.5% BPB was selected for the remainder of the experiments.

Next, we examined diarrhea percentage, fecal scores, and fecal IgA in 15-day-old calves fed a creep diet (control group) or a diet supplemented with 0.5% BPB (BPB group) for 4 weeks. The control calves spontaneously developed diarrhea over time. By 19 days of age, 100% of control calves had scours (CTR, Fig. 1A). However, BPB-treated calves had a reduced percentage of scours (37%, BPB, Fig. 1A). Next, we measured fecal scores of the two groups of calves fed the creep diet and the diet containing 0.5% BPB at 0, 14 and 28 days post-treatment. The control calves had higher fecal scores than the BPB-treated calves (CTR vs.0.5% BPB, Fig. 1B). Of note, reduction of fecal scores in BPB-treated calves was dose-dependent (Fig. 1B). Finally, we assessed the levels of fecal IgA, an indicator of gut infection, in the two groups of calves fed the creep diet and the diet containing 0.5% BPB at 0 and 28 days post-treatment. The control and BPB-treated groups had levels of fecal IgA of 45.8 ng/ml and 44.7 ng/ml, respectively (CTR vs.BPB, 0 day, Fig. 1C). However, the control and BPB-treated groups had IgA levels of 105.4 ng/ml and 30.3 ng/ml, respectively, at 28 days post-treatment (CTR vs.BPB, 28 days, Fig. 1C). In parallel, we compared the average body weight of control calves and BPB-treated calves. BPB significantly increased body weight in calves by 8.2 kg after treatment for 4 weeks (Fig. 1D). Accordingly, BPB significantly reduced FCR (Fig. 1E).

Overall, we demonstrated that BPB significantly reduced diarrhea, fecal scores and fecal IgA, and increased body weight gain in calves.

BPB modulates the composition of bacteria in the feces of calves

To understand the mechanism through which BPB reduced calf diarrhea, we used different selective agar plates to analyze total bacterial counts and the abundance of gut bacteria in the feces of calves fed the standard creep diet and the diet containing 0.5% BPB, as described in Fig. 2. No difference in total count of fecal bacteria was seen in calves before BPB treatment using PCA plate assays (Day 0, Total bacteria, Fig. 2A). However, the total count of fecal bacteria in calves that received BPB treatment for 4 weeks was slightly lower than that of control calves (Day 28, Total bacteria, Fig. 2A). Of note, total bacteria in the feces of calves decreased over 4 weeks, from age 15 days to 43 days (Fig. 2A). Moreover, there was no difference in the number of fecal E.coli(on SSA plates), Campylobacter (on modified CCDA plates), Salmonella (on SSA plates), coliform (on EMBA plates), and Clostridium (on RCMA plates) in calves before BPB treatment (Day 0, Figs. 2B, 2C, 2D, 2E, and 2F). In remarkable contrast, there was a dramatic decrease in the number of fecal Campylobacter, E.coli, Salmonella, coliform, and Clostridium in calves after 4 weeks of BPB treatment (Day 28, Figs. 2B, 2C, 2D, 2E and 2F). Of note, BPB reduced E. coliand Campylobacter to a greater extent than the other pathogenic bacteria (Day 28, Figs. 2B–2F). In parallel, there was no difference in the number of fecal Lactobacillus in calves before BPB treatment (Lactobacillus, Day 0, Fig. 2G). Conversely, BPB significantly increased the number of fecal Lactobacillus in calves with 4-week treatment (Lactobacillus, Day 28, Fig. 2G).

To confirm the mechanism by which BPB altered gut microbiota in calves, we further analyzed the gut microbiota of the control and treatment groups before and after BPB treatment using 16S rRNA next-generation sequencing (NGS). An overview of calf gut microbiota in 4 experimental settings (Fig. 1) is summarized in Tables S4 and S5. The count of sequences (Table S4), amplicon sequence variants (ASVs, Table S4), and indices (Figs. 3A–3D) of bacteria in the stools of control calves and calves fed with 0.5% BPB were summarized. The number of sequences from 2 calf groups, aged 15 and 43 days, as shown by rarefaction curves, was sufficient for sequencing depth that revealed the major ASVs (Figure S3A). The gut microbiota of 15-day-old calves had less diversity than those of 43-day-old calves, as evidenced by the observed ASVs, Chao1, Shannon and Simpson diversity indices (CTR, Figs. 3A–3D). However, the gut microbiota of control calves (CTR) had similar diversity to that of BPB-treated calves (CTR vs.BPB, Figs. 3A–3D).

Furthermore, 8 phyla, 8 classes, 11 orders, 14 families, and 29 genera/subgenera were found in the samples. The details of the phylum, class, order, family, and genus level changes under BPB treatment are shown in Table S5 and Figures S3B-S3E. Notably, 17 genera/subgenera, which have been hypothesized to be associated with beneficial effects, Bifidobacterium (Chang et al., 2016), Ruminococcus (Chang et al., 2016), Ruminococcus_1 (Chang et al., 2016), Ruminococcus_2 (Chang et al., 2016), Faecalibacterium (Martin et al., 2023), Lactobacillus (Chang et al., 2016), Oscillospira (Yang et al., 2021), Blautia (Liu et al., 2021), Parabacteroides (Gervason et al., 2024), Prevotella_1 (Chiquette et al., 2012), Prevotella_2 (Chiquette et al., 2012), Prevotella_7 (Chiquette et al., 2012), Prevotella_9 (Chiquette et al., 2012), Prevotella (Chiquette et al., 2012), Succinivibrio (Bryant, 2015), Akkermansia (Thu Thuy Nguyen & Endres, 2022), and Coprococcus (Lu et al., 2024) were detected in the feces of calves (Genus, Table S5 and Fig. 3E).

In contrast, 9 genera/subgenera, which have been hypothesized to be associated with harmful effects, Turicibacter (Fujii et al., 2025), Clostridium (Chang et al., 2016), Clostridium_sensu_stricto_2 (Zhang et al., 2018), Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (Zhang et al., 2018), Fusobacterium (Zhang et al., 2018), Streptococcus (Zhang et al., 2018), Campylobacter (Chang et al., 2016), Bilophila [casue bacteriocemia (Kovacs et al., 2024), and Escherichia/Shigella (Chang et al., 2016) were identified from the calf guts (Genus, Table S5 and Fig. 3F). Of these, BPB increased the proportion of 9 bacterial genera/subgenera, which have been hypothesized to be associated with beneficial effects, Ruminococcus, Ruminococcus_1, Bacteroides, Oscillospira, Parabacteroides, Succinivibrio, Lactobacillus, Prevotella_1, Prevotella_7 and Coprococcus (Fig. 3E). Conversely, BPB decreased the proportion of 3 bacterial genera/subgenera, which have been hypothesized to be associated with harmful effects, Campylobacter, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, and Escherichia/Shigella (Fig. 3F).

Overall, our data indicated that BPB regulated the gut microbiota of calves by increasing 9 bacterial genera and reducing 3 bacterial genera.

BPB promotes the growth of L. reuteri, which antagonizes the growth of E. coli in the calf gut microbiota

To further explore the BPB-mediated regulation of gut microbiota in calves, i.e., the augmentation of bacteria with beneficial effects and reduction of potentially pathogenic bacteria, we used MALDI-TOF MS to identify Lactobacillus and E. coliin calf stools (Figure S4). Mass spectroscopy spectra of bacterial proteins were compared with entries in an in-house database using Biotyper 3.1 (Minh et al., 2023). The data revealed that L. reuteri and E. coliwere dominant in the feces of control calves (Figure S4).

We also tested the in vitro effect of BPB on L. reuteri and E. coligrowth. MAC experiments in MRS medium showed that compared to the control vehicle, BPB at 0.5 µg/mL promoted L. reuteri growth more significantly, in a dose-dependent manner (BPB, Fig. 4A). After that, we evaluated the in vitro effects of BPB on the growth of E. coli. The MIC experiments showed that in LB medium, 10 µg/mL Amp (positive control) significantly inhibited the growth of E. coli(Amp, Fig. 4B). However, BPB at 400 µg/mL and above inhibited the growth of E. colimore significantly in a dose-dependent manner (BPB, Fig. 4B). Therefore, BPB directly promoted the growth of bacteria associated with beneficial potential, which antagonized bacteria associated with pathogenic potential.

Next, we conducted antagonistic tests with L. reuteri (LR) and E. coli(EC) on LB agar plates using disc diffusion assays. As we predicted, the negative controls, MRS agar and LB, did not inhibit E. coli(see the lack of an inhibition zone, NC1 and NC2, Fig. 4C). Contrastingly, a disc infused with 10 µg GEM (positive control) inhibited E. coligrowth (see the inhibition zones, GEM, Fig. 4C). Likewise, a disc containing L. reuteri (LR) significantly inhibited E. coli, also shown by the corresponding inhibition zones (LR, Fig. 4C). L. reuteri exhibited slightly better inhibition of E. colithan 10 µg GEM (LR and GEM, Fig. 4C). Furthermore, disc diffusion assays indicated that the supernatant of L. reuteri inhibited the growth of E. colito a greater degree than 10 µg GEM (LRSN and GEM, Figure S5A). These results prompted us to identify active metabolites in the supernatant of L. reuteri using liquid chromatography-hyphenated tandem mass spectroscopy. Acetic acid (AA), lactic acid (LA), propionic acid (PA), 3‑hydroxy propionic acid (3-HPA), and butyric acid (BA) were identified from the supernatant of L. reuteri (LRSN, Figure S5B). These SCFA have been reported to function as antimicrobial metabolites in Lactobacillus (Lee et al., 2024; Minh et al., 2023).

Next, we measured these SCFA in the stools of the control and BPB-treated calves. BPB significantly upregulated LA, AA, PA, BA, and 3-HPA produced by gut microbiota in calves (Fig. 5A). The anti-bacterial potency of the 5 SCFA, alone or in combination, on E. coliat dosages that corresponded to their quantity in the stools of control and BPB-treated calves was assessed using disc diffusion assays on LB agar plates. No inhibition of E. coligrowth was observed in a disc filled with distilled water (NC, Fig. 5B). As anticipated, gentamycin, as a positive control, significantly inhibit its growth (PC, Fig. 5B). AA, PA, BA, and 3-HPA at the dosages corresponding to quantity in the control stools showed no inhibition of E. coligrowth (AA1, PA1, BA1, and 3-HPA1, Fig. 5B) whilst LA and a mixture of the 5 SCFA showed significant inhibition of E. coligrowth (LA1, and Mix1, Fig. 5B). In contrast, PA, BA, and 3-HPA at the dosages corresponding to quantity in the stools of BPB-fed calves showed no inhibition of E. coligrowth on LB agar plates (PA2, BA2, and 3-HPA2, Fig. 5B) whilst LA, AA, and a mixture of the 5 SCFA showed significant inhibition of E. coligrowth (LA2, AA2, and Mix2, Fig. 5B). Notably, LA (LA2), AA (AA2), and a mixture of the 5 SCFA (Mix2) had higher activities against E. colithan their amount present in the control stools.

In summary, BPB promoted the growth of bacteria associated with beneficial potential. Those bacteria and their antimicrobial compounds suppressed the growth of bacteria associated with pathogenic potential in the guts of calves (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Calves become future dairy and beef cattle herds (Palczynski et al., 2022). Calf scours are a common gastrointestinal disease in neonatal cattle. Although antibiotics and liquid fluid therapy have traditionally been used to mitigate scours, their misuse and abuse have undesirable consequences. Phytogenics, which have no adverse outcomes, are becoming viable alternatives to antibiotics and are now available for cattle (Kisoo et al., 2023). Eubiotics, including phytogenics, which can correct gut dysbiosis, may be a useful therapy for calf scours.

In this study, we demonstrated that a phytogenic formulation, BPB, had a unique function and novel mechanism in gut disease (diarrhea) and host health (as shown by body weight) in calves (Figs. 1A and 1D). The mode of action of BPB in calves involved the regulation of gut microbiota (Fig. 4). BPB, which consisted of B. pilosa and bentonite, was found to increase potentially beneficial bacteria and decrease the detrimental bacteria in the calf guts (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). In contrast, bentonite used alone seemed to have a moderate effect on diarrhea mitigation in calves (BT, Figure S2B).

BPB reduced diarrhea (Figure1 A) and fecal scores (Figures S2 and 1B) and increased body weight (Fig. 1D) in calves at dosages as low as 0.5%, proving its feasibility for use in the cattle industry. Furthermore, BPB was able to reduce diarrhea in neonatal calves 1 and 15 days of age. The effectiveness of such a low percentage (0.5%) of BPB might mean the supplement would have less impact on palatability and be relatively cheap if mass-produced for use in the calf diet.

In addition, due to the phytogenic complexity of its major constituent, B. pilosa, BPB may have additional functions in calves. So far over 40 distinct bioactivities have been documented for B. pilosa (Bartolome et al., 2013; Kuo et al., 2021), and we and other groups have characterized over 300 compounds from this plant (Kuo et al., 2021). Furthermore, in a previous report, we identified B. pilosa active compounds that promoted Lactobacillus growth, including linoleate, linolenate, and stearate (Lee et al., 2024). The same study also indicated that B. pilosa and its active compounds upregulated the production of antimicrobial metabolites, lactic acid, acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, and 3-hydroxypropionic acid in Lactobacillus (Lee et al., 2024). That study and other seminal publications (Bartolome et al., 2013; Kuo et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2024) explained the mechanism by which BPB exerts its eubiotic action on rectification of gut microbiota, leading to more bacteria associated with beneficial effects and their antimicrobial metabolites and fewer bacteria associated with harmful effects in the guts of calves (Fig. 5C). Indeed, unlike other plants with antimicrobial activities (Vaou et al., 2021), BPB and B. pilosa did not directly kill pathogens. Instead, BPB and B. pilosa altered the ratios of bacteria associated with beneficial effects and their metabolites relative to those associated with adverse effects. In addition to the eubiotic function, BPB can be speculated to boost immunity since B. pilosa has been reported to increase activation of macrophages (Chung et al., 2016; Mesa et al., 2023) as well as T cells (Chang et al., 2004, 2005; Chiang et al., 2007) in mice and humans.

In addition to its modulation of bacteria in calf guts, BPB’s ability to mitigate diarrhea was enhanced by its other major component, bentonite. Bentonite is known to remove intestinal water from the bowels of calves (Dzagurov et al., 2021).

Gut microbiota balance is crucial for animal health, disease eradication, reproduction and growth (Afzaal et al., 2022). However, dietary phytogenics, drugs, exogenous bacteria, immunity, host genetics, and stress cause gut dysbiosis (Karl et al., 2018). Therefore, the skewing of gut microbiota from dysbiosis towards eubiosis is a potential approach for the prevention and treatment of diseases in animal hosts (Scott et al., 2015; Welch et al., 2022). It is widely accepted that probiotics and related approaches (prebiotics, parabiotics, and postbiotics) are beneficial for host health and for decreasing disease in the host gut. Our data suggested that BPB may have advantages over probiotics and related approaches for increasing the abundance of endogenous bacteria associated with beneficial effects (but not exogenous bacteria associated with beneficial effects) in calf gut, resulting in a higher abundance of beneficial gut bacteria than achieved with exogenous probiotic supplementation. However, this supposition will require further validation.

This work has some limitations. First, this was a pilot study and lacked sufficient numbers of animals (44 calves) and fecal samples to draw strong conclusions about the impact of BPB on gut microbiota or calf performance. As this study was exploratory and preliminary, it will require further confirmation in a field trial with a large number of calves. Second, whether BPB is the root cause of the change in microbiota composition and improved performance remains to be elucidated. However, our data support the notion that BPB affects the gut microbiota, thereby mitigating disease and enhancing growth performance. Third, the evidence for BPB’s promotion of L. reuteri growth and its inhibition of E. coliprimarily came from in vitro experiments (MIC, MAC, inhibition zones, SCFA) as shown in Figures 4, 5, and S5. The in vitro evidence is consistent with in vivo data (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). However, the causal linkage between the in vitro and in vivo data remains to be ascertained. Finally, the number of probiotics and pathogens currently postulated by regulatory authorities (Cooper et al., 2024; Guarner et al., 2024; Marco et al., 2026) is limited and strict; moreover, probiotics (Guarner et al., 2024) and pathogens (Cooper et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2018) are precisely defined at the species and strain levels but not at the genus level. However, accumulating evidence from the literature and databases on bacterial function and mechanisms suggests that more and more bacteria are being recognized as having documentable beneficial and/or detrimental effects. As an additional point, the classification of bacterial genera is sometimes difficult because it is not linear; one species may act as a probiotic while others may act as pathogens within the same genus. Of the bacteria analyzed in this study, bacterial genera/subgenera (Bryant, 2015; Chang et al., 2016; Chiquette et al., 2012; Gervason et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2024; Martin et al., 2023; Thu Thuy Nguyen & Endres, 2022; Yang et al., 2021) such as Bifidobacterium, Ruminococcus, Ruminococcus_1, Ruminococcus_2, Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, Oscillospira, Blautia, Parabacteroides Prevotella_1, Prevotella_2, Prevotella_7, Prevotella_9, Prevotella, Succinivibrio, Akkermansia, and Coprococcus, have been reported to be associated with beneficial effects in humans or animals. In contrast, bacterial genera/subgenera (Chang et al., 2016; Fujii et al., 2025; Kovacs et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2018) such as Turicibacter, Clostridium, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_2, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, Campylobacter, Bilophila, and Escherichia/Shigella have been reported to be associated with harmful effects in humans or animals. Fourth, fecal secretory IgA is a multifunctional mucosal immune mediator. Two publications demonstrated that fecal IgA remained relatively stable in calves, aged 1 to 4 weeks, and increased over time (Burton et al., 2025). This increase was associated with mounted mucosal immunity to infections (Suzuki et al., 2025). Therefore, fecal IgA can serve as a biomarker of gut health in calves and other domestic ruminants. Consistent with the literature, in this study, a reduction of fecal IgA by the phytogenic formulation, BPB, might reflect decreased immune responses secondary to pathogen elimination or merely suppressed mucosal immune responses in calves. Both alternative interpretations should be acknowledged. Diarrhea in 15-day-old calves is strongly associated with milk-related management factors, including feeding frequency, volume per meal, temperature, hygiene of feeding equipment, and the composition of milk replacer used. The complete milk feeding records are provided in Table S2. Since the feeding management was similar in the control and BPB-treatment groups, differential milk management between groups was excluded as a confounding variable. Therefore, the observed reduction in calf diarrhea incidence (Fig. 1A) could be exclusively attributed to BPB supplementation.

Overall, our research may represent a new, unique eubiotic strategy to augment host growth, health, and defense in calves. Importantly, the use of BPB would not have the undesirable side effects of antibiotics, which is particularly attractive for rearing calves in the antibiotic-free era.

Conclusion

BPB can effectively reduce diarrhea and gut inflammation and promote body weight gain in calves. The mechanism of BPB involves regulating gut microbiota, i.e., upregulating bacteria associated with beneficial effects and downregulating bacteria associated with harmful effects in the calf gut. BPB-mediated increase in bacteria associated with beneficial potential and their metabolites suppressed pathogens in calf guts, benefiting calves’ gut health and growth performance. This study also demonstrated the feasibility of using phytogenics to modulate the gut microbiota, mitigating gut infections and increasing gut health and growth performance in calves and other food animals.
