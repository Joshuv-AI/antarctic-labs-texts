Slovak cross-cultural adaptation of the Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire
Authors: Łukasz Rypicz, Martin Bereta, Olexander Dobrovanov, Olga Fedorowicz, Mária Šupínová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlarm fatigue is a significant issue in intensive care settings, where frequent alarms from medical equipment may overwhelm healthcare workers. This can lead to desensitization, delayed responses, and risks to patient safety. There is a lack of validated tools for assessing alarm fatigue in Slovak healthcare environments. The Polish version of the AFAQ was translated and culturally adapted into Slovak following established international guidelines. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Background

Electromedical devices are equipped with alarm systems designed to alert staff to critical changes in patient status. Their main function is to alert the multiprofessional team to changes in the status of monitored physiological variables in patients, or even to their dysfunction [1]. Alarms contribute to improving patient safety, but they are also a major source of noise in intensive care units (ICUs). Alarms generate noise that can be hazardous to the workplace or interfere with patient recovery [2, 3]. Noise levels in most hospitals exceed World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations [2]. Alarm fatigue is a form of sensory overload resulting from repeated exposure to excessive alarm signals, which may lead to desensitization, delayed responses, and missed alarm interventions [3–6]. Nurses become desensitised and do not respond to alarms. As a result, they may miss serious alarms, which can lead to patient death [7]. According to Winters et al. (2018), there is no agreed valid method of measuring alarm fatigue and current methods are mostly indirect [5, 8]. Despite these concerns, the number of clinical alarm signals is increasing as more medical technologies are added to the clinical care environment [5, 9–10].

Despite the growing body of research on alarm fatigue, there is still no universally accepted or standardized method for its assessment [5, 8]. Existing approaches include indirect measures, such as alarm frequency analyses and staff response indicators, as well as questionnaire-based self-report instruments [5, 8]. One of the most commonly used tools is the Nurses’ Alarm Fatigue Questionnaire, originally developed for intensive care settings [11]. However, validated instruments adapted to different cultural and healthcare contexts remain limited, highlighting the need for further cross-cultural adaptations [5, 8].

In this context, the selection of the Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (AFAQ) as the reference instrument was based on its documented psychometric properties, previous cross-cultural adaptations, and the availability of a Polish version, which facilitates its adaptation to Slovak healthcare settings [12].

The aim of this study was to cross-culturally adapt the Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (AFAQ) into Slovak and to evaluate its psychometric properties in a Slovak clinical context.

Methods

The survey was conducted from June to July 2023 among a sample of 379 healthcare professionals from clinical units equipped with electromedical devices in healthcare facilities in Slovakia. The Slovak version of the AFAQ was administered using Google Forms. The electronic version retained identical item wording, item order, and five-point Likert response options as the source questionnaire, without skip logic, item randomization, or adaptive branching. All items were mandatory, which prevented missing data. The online format was pilot-tested together with the prefinal translated version to ensure clarity and usability. The survey was preceded by the approval of the bioethics committee and was conducted online using Google Forms. Links to the survey were distributed electronically through professional organizations and associations of healthcare workers, as well as through institutional communication channels and professional networks targeting healthcare personnel working with electromedical devices. Participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous. Because the survey was disseminated through multiple electronic distribution channels, the exact number of individuals who received or viewed the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a formal response rate could not be calculated. The study used the validated Polish version of the questionnaire [12], which retains the original item structure and response format and served as the reference language version for the Slovak cross-cultural adaptation.

Translation, adaptation, and modeling

The process of translation and cultural adaptation was carried out according to the international standards described by Beaton and colleagues and Wild and colleagues [13–14] to ensure semantic, conceptual, and operational equivalence between language versions. The whole process involved six stages: (1) initial translation, (2) synthesis of translations, (3) back translation, (4) expert committee evaluation, (5) testing the draft version, and (6) submission of the final version to the Coordinating Committee for the Evaluation of the Adaptation Process. The questionnaire was translated from the validated Polish version into Slovak following established cross-cultural adaptation procedures. Each translator received guidance from the research team to avoid metaphors and use simple, understandable wording. Both versions of the translation were then reviewed by the research team, who are fluent in Polish and Slovak and experienced in medical terminology and the context of the study. At this stage, the differences between the translations were discussed and a first Slovak version of the questionnaire was produced. The prepared version was then retranslated into Polish by an independent translator whose native language was Polish and who was not familiar with the original version of the instrument. The final version of the questionnaire was determined by a panel of experts with multidisciplinary qualifications and experience in health care management, nursing, medicine, public health and work ergonomics. In addition, these experts have experience in adapting research instruments, resulting in numerous scientific publications. The next step was to test the questionnaire on a group of 18 nurses who were asked to assess whether all the questions were understandable, unambiguous and clear. The test confirmed that the questionnaire had been prepared correctly and could be used in the study. None of the questions were questioned during the test and therefore all were included in the final version of the survey.

Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire

The source questionnaire for assessing alarm fatigue was published in 2017 by Ashrafi and colleagues [15]. For the purposes of the study, the Polish version [12] of the questionnaire was used due to the cultural convergence of the two countries and similarities in the functioning of the health care system. The Polish version is a validated cross-cultural adaptation of the original AFAQ and retains the same set of items and response format. It was therefore used as the reference language version for the Slovak translation in order to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence, in accordance with established cross-cultural adaptation guidelines [13, 14]. The tool consists of 20 items (Table 1), formulated as statements to which respondents referred by indicating the frequency of occurrence on a five-point Likert scale: always (5), usually (4), sometimes (3), rarely (2) and never (1). The questions were designed to capture medical personnel’s perceptions of alarm fatigue. The original Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (AFAQ) is available under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial license, allowing its use, translation, and cross-cultural adaptation for noncommercial research purposes with appropriate citation. Permission for the initial Polish adaptation was also obtained from the instrument developers.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed good clinical practice guidelines (World Medical Association, 2013). Participants received written information about the study as part of the introduction to the questionnaire, emphasising the voluntary and anonymous nature of their participation and ensuring confidentiality. By completing the questionnaire, participants implicitly agreed to take part in the research. The study protocol was approved by the Independent Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Wroclaw (approval number KB-156/2023).

Statistical analyses

The reliability of the scale was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for individual items. Ideally, Cronbach’s alpha (α) should fall within the recommended range of 0.60–0.90. The following thresholds for internal consistency were applied: α ≥ 0.9—excellent; 0.8 ≤ α < 0.9—good; 0.7 ≤ α < 0.8—acceptable; 0.6 ≤ α < 0.7—questionable; 0.5 ≤ α < 0.6—poor; and α < 0.5—unacceptable. Statistical analysis was conducted by a professional statistician using the R program, version 3.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing: Vienna, Austria) [16]. Each item was analyzed for floor and ceiling effects based on percentage distributions. Additionally, confirmatory factor analysis was carried out. Since the questionnaire items were expressed on an ordinal scale rather than a continuous one, the Diagonally Weighted Least Squares method was employed. Fit indices were calculated for the unidimensional (single-factor) structure of the scale.

The floor and ceiling effect

Floor effect is the % of respondents who chose the lowest scoring response to a question. Ceiling effect is the % of those who chose the highest scoring answer. A high floor or ceiling effect in a question indicates that the question may not be well matched to the population being analysed.

The results showed a high (more than 50%) floor effect in questions 2, 6, 10–12, 14 and 19, i.e. more than 50% of the ‘lowest scoring’ (indicating a lack of alarm fatigue) answers fall in these questions (Table 3). Several items demonstrated substantial floor effects, particularly items 2, 10–12, 14, and 19, where more than 60–70% of respondents selected the lowest response category. This finding suggests limited response variability and may indicate reduced sensitivity of these items in populations with relatively low levels of alarm fatigue. At the same time, these items were retained to preserve the conceptual scope and comparability of the original instrument. Future studies should evaluate whether item performance differs in clinical populations with higher alarm exposure or workload intensity.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The following measures are used to assess model fit: the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) and the Standardised Root Mean Residual (SRMR). A number of proposals for cut-off points for these measures can be found in the literature. The best known and most frequently cited is the work of Hu and Bentler, which provides the following guidelines: a model is a good fit according to Hu and Bentler when we have RMSEA < 0.06, CFI and TLI > 0.95 and SRMR < 0.08. However, they point out that meeting the conditions for all four measures is often too restrictive a requirement and propose a method they call the Two-Index Strategy. It says that a model is a good fit when SRMR < 0.09 and additionally one of the conditions CFI > 0.96, TLI > 0.96 or RMSEA < 0.06 occurs. As shown in Table 4, the initial one-factor model (Model I) did not meet the recommended fit criteria, particularly for the SRMR, CFI, and TLI indices. The modifications indicated by the so-called modification indices were therefore applied. In this case, they suggest introducing a correlation into the model between the following questions 4 and 5.

The chi-square test was statistically significant for both models, which is expected given the relatively large sample size. Therefore, model fit was evaluated using multiple complementary fit indices rather than relying solely on the chi-square statistic (Table 4).

As presented in Table 5, the standardized factor loadings in the final model ranged from 0.076 to 0.750. All items except item 9 demonstrated statistically significant factor loadings (p < 0.05; item 9: p = 0.191). Therefore, this item should be further examined in future validation studies. Because item 9 demonstrated a very weak and non-significant factor loading, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by re-running the CFA after excluding this item from the model (Model III, Table 4). The revised model showed only slight improvement in fit indices (CFI = 0.918; TLI = 0.907), while the overall factor structure remained comparable to the original modified model. Therefore, item 9 was retained in the final Slovak version of the AFAQ in order to preserve conceptual comparability with the original questionnaire and previous cross-cultural adaptations.

Assessment of the reliability of the tool

The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is 0.795. This indicates acceptable reliability of the scale - the results of using it are reproducible and not due to chance. An alpha greater than 0.7 is considered to be a reliable scale [17]. All items have positive discriminant validity (Table 6). This means that they correlate positively with the other items in the scale, which is a very desirable effect.

Tool results

The Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (AFAQ) assesses a respondent’s alarm fatigue. The questionnaire score is a number between 20 and 100 points, with higher numbers indicating greater alarm fatigue.

There are no standards for the AFAQ to say which scores indicate high and which indicate low alarm fatigue. However, it is possible to count the average score per question and interpret it according to the key for a single question, according to which (after reversing the scores in the relevant questions) 1 means that the feeling of tiredness never occurs, 2 means that it rarely occurs, 3 means that it sometimes occurs, 4 means that it often occurs and 5 means that it always occurs.

The average score of the AFAQ was 40.53, i.e. 2.03 points per question (rounding up to 2) (Table 7).

Discussion

According to recent literature, alarm fatigue remains a major patient safety concern and continues to represent a substantial challenge for healthcare professionals working in intensive care settings. Recent evidence also highlights the need for standardized definitions, valid measurement instruments, and multifaceted mitigation strategies to improve both patient safety and working conditions [2, 3, 5, 18–20].

The Slovak version of the study achieved satisfactory reliability of the tool, as measured by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.791. This result indicates acceptable internal consistency of the scale and confirms internal consistency of the scale. This result is comparable to that of the Polish adaptation of the tool, which obtained an α value of 0.88 [12], as well as to the results of other international studies indicating the effectiveness of the AFAQ in different cultural contexts. The authors of the original questionnaire obtained an α value of 0.806; the Slovak adaptation maintains the questionnaire’s internal consistency. Other authors’ experience with cultural adaptations of questionnaires for assessing alarm fatigue indicates similar reliability results for the tool: Italian α = 0.71 [21], Turkish α = 0.85 [22], and Iranian α = 0.95 [11].

The confirmatory factor analysis provided preliminary support for an approximately unidimensional structure of the Slovak version of the AFAQ. Although model fit improved after theoretically justified modification and additional sensitivity analyses, the obtained indices should still be interpreted cautiously, as several values remained below the most conservative recommended thresholds for optimal fit. Therefore, the present findings should be viewed as providing partial rather than definitive support for the proposed factorial structure. Overall, the obtained psychometric findings are broadly comparable to those reported in previous cross-cultural adaptations of the AFAQ. Nevertheless, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive confirmation that the Slovak AFAQ measures a single underlying construct, and additional validation studies are warranted to further examine its factorial structure.

The present study demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and provided preliminary evidence supporting selected structural properties of the Slovak AFAQ in the studied sample. However, additional forms of validity evidence, including convergent, discriminant, criterion, and test–retest validity, as well as confirmation of the questionnaire’s performance across more diverse healthcare populations and clinical settings, remain to be established in future studies.

The applicability of the Slovak version of the AFAQ beyond the studied population should be interpreted with caution. Although the survey included different healthcare professionals, the study sample consisted predominantly of nurses, and most respondents were employed in intensive care units, where alarm exposure and clinical workflows differ from those in other hospital settings. As a result, the present findings primarily support the validity and reliability of the Slovak AFAQ among nurses working in intensive care environments. Further studies involving more diverse professional groups and clinical contexts are warranted to confirm the broader applicability of the instrument. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as providing only preliminary validation of the Slovak AFAQ, and its use in broader clinical, research, or quality improvement settings should be interpreted with appropriate caution until additional psychometric evidence becomes available.

The results indicate that the overall level of alarm fatigue in the studied population was relatively low to moderate, as reflected by a mean AFAQ score of approximately 40 points on a scale ranging from 20 to 100. This finding suggests that, although alarm fatigue was present among the respondents, its severity was limited in this sample. Importantly, the primary aim of the present study was the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the AFAQ rather than a comprehensive assessment of the clinical consequences of alarm fatigue. Therefore, the interpretation of the results is restricted to the measurement properties of the instrument and the observed level of alarm fatigue within the studied population.

An additional issue requiring consideration is the substantial floor effect observed for several questionnaire items. In particular, items related to immediate reactions to alarms or alarm deactivation behaviors showed very low endorsement frequencies, resulting in limited response variability. Importantly, sensitivity analyses demonstrated that excluding item 9 resulted only in marginal improvement in model fit and internal consistency, supporting retention of the original item structure at this stage of cross-cultural adaptation. This may reduce the sensitivity of the instrument for detecting differences in alarm fatigue levels in populations with relatively low alarm burden, such as the present sample. At the same time, these items may become more informative in healthcare environments characterized by higher alarm exposure, workload intensity, or more frequent critical care interventions. Therefore, further studies should investigate the performance and discriminative ability of these items across different clinical settings and professional groups.

It should be noted that the lack of a uniform, validated tool for measuring alarm fatigue in many countries, including Slovakia, limits the effectiveness of prevention efforts and patient safety education. This study is a first step toward systematizing knowledge and establishing systemic measures for alarm management in clinical settings in the Central European region. Today, managing alarms in a hospital ward should be one of the elements of a safety culture — as it turns out, it affects the safety of both patients and medical staff. The human factor cannot be underestimated when it comes to building patient safety in the healthcare system [23–25].

Conclusion

The Slovak adaptation of the Alarm Fatigue Assessment Questionnaire (AFAQ) demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and preliminary evidence of structural validity in the studied sample. However, the factorial structure showed only partial support, and several items demonstrated limited psychometric performance. Therefore, the Slovak AFAQ should currently be considered a preliminary cross-cultural adaptation providing only preliminary validation of the instrument. Its application should presently be limited primarily to populations comparable to those included in this study, and further validation in more diverse healthcare populations and clinical settings is required before broader clinical or research use.

Study limitation

The present study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the use of an online survey may have resulted in the inclusion of participants who were more familiar with digital tools and potentially less burdened by professional duties, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Moreover, although the study included healthcare professionals from different units, the sample consisted predominantly of nurses working in intensive care settings. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalized to other professional groups or clinical contexts, such as emergency departments, operating rooms, or long-term care units.

In addition, several questionnaire items demonstrated limited psychometric performance, including low factor loadings and substantial floor effects. Although the overall internal consistency of the scale was acceptable, these findings suggest that certain items may have limited sensitivity or discriminative ability in the present sample. Further studies should therefore investigate the stability and performance of individual items across different healthcare settings and populations with varying levels of alarm exposure.

In addition, the psychometric evaluation focused primarily on internal consistency and confirmatory factor analysis based on an a priori structure. Although the results provided preliminary support for selected measurement properties of the Slovak AFAQ, other important forms of validity evidence, including convergent, discriminant, criterion, and test–retest validity, were not evaluated in the present study. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence supporting internal consistency and partial structural validity rather than full psychometric validation of the Slovak version of the instrument.

Finally, the study was conducted during a period of relative pandemic stability; however, previous experiences related to the COVID-19 pandemic may still have influenced participants’ perceptions of workload, stress, and alarm fatigue, which could not be fully controlled in the analysis.

Moreover, the substantial floor effects observed for several items may have reduced the sensitivity of the questionnaire to detect subtle variability in alarm fatigue levels within the present sample.
