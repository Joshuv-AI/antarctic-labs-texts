Blood Cell‐Camouflaged Liquid Metal Nanoconjugates Orchestrate Treg Depletion and STING‐Amplified Photothermal Immunity for Metastatic Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer Therapy
Authors: Nina Sang, Eijiro Miyako
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTriple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains highly lethal due to aggressive metastasis and profound immunosuppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs). Here, we present a biomimetic liquid metal (LM)‐based photothermal immunotherapeutic nanoplatform exploiting gallium‐based LMs to orchestrate cascade amplification of antitumor immunity. The engineered nanoplatform (B–LM–DMX–αCD25) integrates an LM photothermal core with dual immunomodulatory components—anti‐CD25 antibody for Treg depletion and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist 5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid (DMX) for innate immune activation—while blood‐derived camouflaging enhances immune evasion and tumor accumulation. Quantitative RT‐PCR of 13 immune‐related genes confirmed STING engagement, robust CD8+ cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration, and Treg depletion in treated tumors. This platform operates through three synchronized mechanisms: (1) selective intratumoral Treg depletion dismantling immunosuppression; (2) near‐infrared‐triggered LM‐mediated photothermal therapy (58°C within 5 min) inducing immunogenic cell death and releasing tumor‐associated antigens; and (3) STING pathway activation promoting dendritic cell maturation and tumor‐specific cytotoxic T‐cell responses, collectively transforming immunologically “cold” TNBC into immune‐responsive tumors. In orthotopic 4T1 models, the nanoplatform achieves significant tumor regression with enhanced survival, while in metastatic models, it reduces pulmonary metastatic nodules and extends median survival beyond 70 days. This work establishes a paradigm‐shifting biomimetic LM‐based nanodelivery strategy addressing fundamental immunological barriers in TNBC through coordinated photothermal–immunotherapy, offering a clinically translatable approach for TNBC.Keywords: liquid metal nanoparticles, photothermal immunotherapy, regulatory T‐cell depletion, STING pathway activation, triple‐negative breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality among women worldwide, with triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) representing approximately 15%–20% of all breast cancer cases [1, 2]. Distinguished by the absence of estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression, TNBC constitutes the most aggressive and therapeutically intractable breast cancer subtype [3]. Clinically, TNBC is characterized by early onset, rapid progression, high recurrence rates, and dismal prognosis. Critically, TNBC patients face a nearly three‐fold elevated risk of distant metastasis within five years of diagnosis compared to non‐TNBC patients, with the lungs representing the most frequent site of metastatic colonization and the primary cause of mortality [4, 5].

Despite advances in multimodal treatment regimens encompassing chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy, TNBC remains highly susceptible to therapeutic resistance, early relapse, and widespread metastatic dissemination, culminating in poor clinical outcomes [6]. The emergence of immunotherapy has provided new therapeutic avenues for TNBC management. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated antigen 4 (CTLA‐4) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1)/programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) axes have demonstrated survival benefits in subsets of patients with various solid tumors, including TNBC [7]. However, clinical reality remains sobering: fewer than 20% of TNBC patients derive meaningful clinical benefit from ICI monotherapy, primarily attributable to the profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that fundamentally undermines therapeutic efficacy [8, 9, 10, 11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that, compared with other breast cancer subtypes, TNBC exhibits heightened immune evasion capacity, accelerated therapeutic resistance, and propensity for early recurrence—phenotypes predominantly driven by extensive infiltration of regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the TME [12, 13, 14, 15].

Regulatory T cells (Tregs) function as the central orchestrators of immunosuppression within the TME, constituting a critical barrier to effective cancer immunotherapy. Through multiple complementary mechanisms, Tregs directly suppress cytotoxic CD8+ T‐cell activation, inhibit dendritic cell maturation, and promote M2‐like macrophage polarization via cytokine‐ and chemokine receptor‐mediated signaling networks. By secreting potent immunosuppressive mediators including interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) and transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), Tregs establish a profoundly tolerogenic microenvironment that facilitates tumor immune evasion, promotes metastatic dissemination, and actively impedes antitumor immune responses [16]. Clinically, elevated intratumoral densities of FOXP3+ Tregs strongly correlate with reduced relapse‐free survival and diminished overall survival in breast cancer patients, unequivocally highlighting their pivotal role in immune escape and disease progression [17]. Consequently, selective depletion of Tregs effectively “releases the immunological brake,” representing a critical prerequisite for reprogramming the suppressive TME, restoring cytotoxic immunity, and achieving durable immunotherapeutic efficacy in TNBC.

Nanomaterial‐based photothermal therapy (PTT) has garnered substantial attention in both clinical oncology and fundamental cancer research owing to its exceptional therapeutic efficiency, spatiotemporal controllability, and minimally invasive nature [18, 19, 20]. By harnessing photothermal conversion agents that transform near‐infrared (NIR) laser energy into localized hyperthermia, PTT induces rapid thermal ablation of tumor tissues and effectively suppresses primary tumor growth. However, PTT monotherapy invariably fails to achieve complete tumor eradication, as local recurrence and distant metastasis following hyperthermic ablation remain the predominant causes of treatment failure and cancer‐related mortality [21, 22, 23, 24]. Beyond direct cytotoxic effects, PTT induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) through heat‐induced cellular stress and pyroptosis. During this process, dying tumor cells release abundant damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs), which promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation, enhance cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) infiltration, and initiate antitumor immune responses [25, 26]. Nevertheless, PTT‐induced immune activation typically remains transient and spatially confined, primarily due to insufficient responsiveness of antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) and rapid attenuation of inflammatory signaling cascades. Consequently, PTT monotherapy rarely achieves systemic or durable immune protection, particularly against metastatic disease.

Recent investigations have illuminated the pivotal role of the cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS)‐stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway in initiating robust antitumor immunity, establishing it as a highly promising therapeutic target for cancer immunotherapy [27]. The cGAS‐STING signaling axis orchestrates the maturation and activation of diverse immune effector cells, including T cells, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells, through induction of pro‐inflammatory cytokines—particularly type I interferons (IFN‐I)—thereby amplifying both innate and adaptive immune responses to elicit potent antitumor effects [28]. To transcend the limitations of PTT‐induced transient immunity, rational integration of PTT with STING agonists represents a mechanistically sound strategy to amplify innate immune signaling, enhance utilization of tumor‐derived antigens, and synergistically induce robust, durable antitumor immunity capable of systemic tumor control.

Gallium‐based liquid metals (LMs) have recently emerged as a distinct and highly promising class of photothermal materials, offering unique physicochemical advantages that surpass conventional photothermal agents. These advantages include exceptional biocompatibility, intrinsic fluidity enabling conformal adaptation, mechanical deformability, and remarkably high photothermal conversion efficiency (>50%) [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Despite these attractive properties, clinical translation of liquid metal nanoparticles remains hindered by critical limitations: poor aqueous dispersibility, pronounced tendency toward aggregation, and rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, which collectively constrain their therapeutic applicability [37, 38]. While red blood cell (RBC) membrane camouflaging strategies have been widely employed to prolong systemic circulation and reduce immune clearance of nanoparticles [39, 40], these conventional approaches typically necessitate complex membrane extraction and purification procedures that can compromise structural integrity and bioactivity of membrane components [41, 42]. To circumvent these obstacles, we innovatively adopt a facile whole‐blood biomimetic approach to engineer liquid metal nanoparticles. In this strategy, the nanoplatform directly interfaces with whole blood, thereby preserving the natural protein corona and dynamic plasma constituents. This approach simultaneously enhances liquid metal colloidal stability, confers immune‐evasion capability, and improves tumor accumulation efficiency through biomimetic camouflage.

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the immunostimulatory potential of gallium‐based nanomaterials. Wang et al. first demonstrated that gallium nanoparticles can function as in situ cancer vaccines by capturing tumor antigens and acting as immunoadjuvants, establishing a foundational concept for Ga‐based antitumor immunity [43]. Subsequently, Chen et al. showed that combining a gallium‐based nanoplatform with cryoablation enables local tumor destruction coupled with systemic immune activation, establishing the “physical ablation plus gallium‐based immune amplification” paradigm [44]. More recently, both Hao et al. and Qi et al. demonstrated that Ga‐based liquid metal nanoparticles possess intrinsic capacity to activate the cGAS‐STING pathway—via piezoelectric catalysis and Ga3 + release, respectively—without any exogenous agonist, thereby enhancing antitumor immunotherapy [45, 46]. While these studies collectively establish the immunostimulatory potential of Ga‐based nanomaterials, reliance on intrinsic material properties alone for STING activation introduces inherent variability in pathway engagement, and none of these platforms address the profound immunosuppression mediated by intratumoral Tregs—a dominant mechanism of immune evasion in TNBC. The present work advances beyond these limitations through three conceptually distinct innovations: (i) incorporation of a dedicated STING agonist (5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid, DMX) to ensure robust and consistent pathway activation, potentially synergizing with the intrinsic immunostimulatory capacity of the Ga‐based LM core; (ii) integration of anti‐CD25 antibody‐mediated Treg depletion to systematically dismantle TME immunosuppression—a mechanism entirely absent from prior Ga‐based platforms; and (iii) whole‐blood biomimetic camouflage engineering for superior immune evasion and tumor accumulation. The rational co‐integration of these synergistic mechanisms within a single nanoplatform represents a conceptual and technical advance beyond existing Ga‐based immunotherapy strategies.

Herein, we present a whole‐blood‐camouflaged liquid metal nanoplatform that rationally integrates Treg depletion and STING activation for synergistic TNBC photothermal immunotherapy. Through a facile one‐step ultrasonication method employing whole blood as the stabilizing and camouflaging agent, the solubility and colloidal stability of liquid metal nanoparticles are markedly enhanced, enabling efficient tumor site accumulation and generation of robust photothermal ablation under NIR irradiation. By co‐conjugating an anti‐CD25 antibody for Treg targeting and incorporating the STING agonist 5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid (DMX) [47], the nanoplatform orchestrates a coordinated tripartite immune cascade: (1) selective intratumoral Treg depletion that dismantles immunosuppression; (2) liquid metal‐mediated photothermal ablation that induces extensive immunogenic cell death and liberates abundant tumor‐associated antigens; and (3) STING‐driven enhancement of dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation efficiency, collectively eliciting potent TAA‐specific antitumor immune responses (Figure 1). This synergistic multimodal strategy effectively transforms immunologically “cold” TNBC into an immune‐responsive microenvironment. In vivo validation demonstrates that the biomimetic liquid metal nanoplatform achieves complete regression of orthotopic TNBC tumors and profoundly suppresses pulmonary metastasis, thereby significantly prolonging survival. This work not only establishes a paradigm‐shifting approach for liquid metal‐based photothermal immunotherapy but also presents a clinically translatable therapeutic strategy against lethal metastatic TNBC.

Schematic illustration of the blood‐camouflaged liquid metal nanoplatform for synergistic photothermal–immunotherapy of metastatic TNBC. Liquid metal (LM) nanoparticles—an emerging class of highly efficient photothermal agents—are engineered through whole‐blood biomimetic camouflaging via ultrasonication to generate B–LM nanocomposites with enhanced colloidal stability, biocompatibility, and immune‐evasion capability. Co‐loading of the STING agonist 5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid (DMX) and surface conjugation with anti‐CD25 antibodies produces the multifunctional B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanoplatform that integrates three complementary therapeutic modalities. Upon systemic administration, the blood‐camouflaged nanocomposites evade immune clearance and accumulate preferentially at tumor sites via biomimetic targeting. Prior to photothermal activation, conjugated anti‐CD25 antibodies selectively deplete intratumoral regulatory T cells (Tregs), dismantling a critical immunosuppressive barrier and establishing a permissive immune microenvironment. Subsequent near‐infrared (808 nm) laser irradiation triggers dual synchronized therapeutic actions: (1) liquid metal‐mediated photothermal therapy induces rapid tumor ablation and robust immunogenic cell death (ICD), releasing abundant tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs); and (2) laser‐triggered burst release of DMX activates the cGAS‐STING–TBK1–IRF3 signaling pathway in tumor‐infiltrating dendritic cells, promoting their maturation, type I interferon production, and enhanced antigen cross‐presentation capacity. Through this coordinated immune cascade—comprising Treg depletion, photothermal‐induced antigen release, and STING‐mediated innate immune amplification—the platform transforms immunologically “cold” TNBC into an immune‐responsive microenvironment, eliciting potent tumor‐specific cytotoxic T‐cell responses that eradicate primary tumors and establish systemic antitumor immunity capable of suppressing distant metastases and preventing tumor recurrence.

Results and Discussion

Rational Design and Comprehensive Characterization of the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 Nanoplatform

The successful fabrication of a multifunctional photothermal immunotherapeutic nanoplatform critically depends on achieving optimal colloidal stability, efficient drug loading, and preserved bioactivity of surface‐conjugated biologics. We systematically characterized the morphology, surface chemistry, optical properties, and functional performance of the engineered B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites through complementary analytical techniques.

Visual inspection and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses revealed striking differences in dispersibility and structural organization among various liquid metal formulations (Figure 2A and Figure S1). Pristine LM and DSPE–LM exhibited opaque gray appearances with pronounced macroscopic aggregation, indicative of poor colloidal stability under physiological conditions. TEM imaging confirmed that pristine LM nanoparticles formed large, irregular aggregates exceeding several hundred nanometers due to insufficient surface stabilization. While DSPE modification provided a discernible surface coating layer, considerable heterogeneity in particle size and residual aggregation persisted, suggesting inadequate stabilization by the synthetic phospholipid alone. In stark contrast, the engineered B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites displayed a homogeneous dark‐red colloidal suspension that remained stable for extended periods (>7 days) without visible precipitation, demonstrating markedly enhanced aqueous dispersibility.

High‐resolution TEM analysis of B–LM nanocomposites revealed a well‐defined core–shell architecture (Figure 2B), with an electron‐dense liquid metal core (∼80 nm diameter) encapsulated by a continuous biomimetic membrane coating (∼15 nm thickness). This structural configuration confirms successful blood component‐mediated surface camouflaging and validates the feasibility of our one‐step whole‐blood engineering approach. The preservation of the native blood‐derived protein corona—including albumin, immunoglobulins, and complement regulatory proteins such as CD47—likely contributes to the observed immune‐evasion properties and enhanced biocompatibility.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements demonstrated that both B–LM–DMX (∼155 nm) and B–LM–DMX–αCD25 (∼171 nm) maintained nanoscale dimensions with narrow polydispersity indices (PDI < 0.2), confirming excellent colloidal homogeneity (Figure 2C). PDI values of 0.18 (B–LM–DMX) and 0.19 (B–LM–DMX–αCD25) confirm excellent colloidal homogeneity. DLS size measurements at days 0 and 7 post‐synthesis demonstrate that hydrodynamic diameter and PDI remain stable (<5% variation) throughout storage, confirming robust colloidal stability. The modest increase in hydrodynamic diameter (∼17 nm) following anti‐CD25 conjugation provides direct evidence of successful antibody functionalization on the nanoparticle surface. Importantly, these dimensions fall within the optimal size range (100–200 nm) for enhanced permeability and retention (EPR)‐mediated tumor accumulation while remaining sufficiently small to avoid rapid reticuloendothelial system (RES) clearance [48].

Zeta potential measurements revealed progressive surface charge modulation during the sequential functionalization process (Figure 2D). Bare LM nanoparticles exhibited a zeta potential of approximately −14 mV, which became more electronegative (−28 mV) following blood‐mediated surface modification, reflecting adsorption of negatively charged plasma proteins and phospholipids. Subsequent conjugation with DSPE–anti‐CD25 shifted the zeta potential toward less negative values (−18 mV), consistent with successful antibody immobilization. Quantitative bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay confirmed high antibody loading efficiency (approximately 100%), indicating complete conjugation of the input anti‐CD25 antibody to the nanoplatform surface. This high conjugation efficiency ensures sufficient antibody density for effective Treg targeting while maintaining colloidal stability.

UV–vis–NIR absorption spectroscopy validated the successful co‐assembly of all functional components (Figure 2E). The B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites exhibited characteristic absorption features of both blood‐derived components (peaks at ∼280 nm and ∼410 nm corresponding to proteins and hemoglobin derivatives) and DMX (peak at ∼340 nm), confirming successful cargo encapsulation. Critically, the nanoplatform displayed broad and intense absorption across the NIR region (700–900 nm). This exceptional NIR absorption profile—attributed to the intrinsic optical properties of the gallium‐based liquid metal core—predicts superior photothermal conversion capability for effective tumor ablation. The absorption coefficient of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 at 808 nm was determined to be 2.86 ± 0.03 L g− 1 cm− 1 from UV–vis measurements of colloidally stable suspensions (60 µg mL− 1, path length 1 cm). It should be noted that direct comparison of absorption coefficients between B–LM–DMX–αCD25 and DSPE–LM is not straightforward, as DSPE–LM exhibits poor colloidal stability with macroscopic aggregation (Figure S1). UV–vis–NIR measurements of aggregated DSPE–LM suspensions inevitably include light scattering contributions from large aggregates, resulting in artificially elevated apparent absorbance values that do not accurately reflect true NIR absorption capacity. Therefore, the absorption coefficient of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 is best compared against established photothermal agents with well‐defined colloidal stability, such as gold nanorods (∼3.9 L g− 1 cm− 1) [49], against which B–LM–DMX–αCD25 demonstrates adequate NIR absorption capacity for effective photothermal therapy.

Although the absorption coefficient of the gold nanorods is higher than that of the liquid metal nanoparticles (2.86 ± 0.03 L g− 1 cm− 1), their photothermal conversion efficiency (∼21%) [50] is lower than that of many reported liquid metal nanoparticle systems (>22%) (Table S1) [32, 38, 53, 54]. This apparent discrepancy arises because photothermal conversion efficiency is determined not only by the amount of absorbed light but also by the relaxation pathways of the excited energy. Gold nanorods exhibit strong localized surface plasmon resonance. In plasmonic nanostructures, the extinction cross section consists of both absorption and scattering components, and the scattering contribution can be significant for anisotropic nanostructures such as nanorods. Upon photoexcitation, the plasmonic energy can dissipate through both radiative and nonradiative channels. In particular, a significant fraction of the plasmon energy is lost through radiative scattering and plasmon re‐emission, which does not contribute to heat generation. As a result, only a portion of the absorbed optical energy is converted into thermal energy. In contrast, liquid metal nanoparticles (Ga–In) do not exhibit strong plasmonic scattering in the investigated spectral range. The absorbed photon energy predominantly relaxes through electron–phonon interactions, which efficiently convert the absorbed energy into heat. Consequently, a larger fraction of the absorbed energy contributes to photothermal heating, leading to a higher photothermal conversion efficiency despite their lower absorption coefficient. Therefore, the higher photothermal conversion efficiency of the liquid metal nanoparticles compared with gold nanorods can be attributed to the more efficient non‐radiative relaxation pathways and the reduced radiative loss in the liquid metal system. Collectively, these mechanistic considerations explain why the photothermal therapeutic efficacy of liquid metal nanoparticles cannot be predicted from absorption coefficient alone, and why photothermal conversion efficiency represents the more appropriate metric for comparing photothermal agents.

The hydrophobic STING agonist DMX was efficiently encapsulated within the biomimetic nanoplatform through hydrophobic interactions with the lipid components and liquid metal interface. Spectrophotometric quantification revealed a remarkably high loading efficiency of 95.7 ± 3.6%, substantially exceeding typical values achieved with conventional nanocarriers (generally 50%–70%). Negligible spontaneous release (<5%) was observed without laser irradiation, confirming stimulus‐responsive behavior. This exceptional loading capacity can be attributed to the unique fluidic nature of the liquid metal core and the amphiphilic blood‐derived coating, which collectively provide abundant hydrophobic microdomains for DMX sequestration.

To evaluate stimulus‐responsive drug release, we investigated DMX liberation kinetics under NIR laser irradiation. As shown in Figure 2F, B–LM–DMX–αCD25 exhibited minimal spontaneous drug leakage (<5%) under physiological conditions without laser exposure, confirming excellent cargo retention during systemic circulation. However, upon 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W, ∼51 mW mm− 2), the nanoplatform demonstrated rapid burst release, with nearly 90% of encapsulated DMX released within 15 min. This dramatic acceleration of drug release is mechanistically attributable to photothermal heating of the liquid metal core, which induces local hyperthermia, disrupts hydrophobic interactions, and increases membrane fluidity, thereby facilitating rapid DMX diffusion into the surrounding microenvironment. This on‐demand, spatiotemporally controlled release behavior ensures that STING activation occurs synchronously with photothermal ablation and ICD induction, maximizing the synergistic immunotherapeutic effect.

Collectively, these comprehensive characterization data confirm that the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanoplatform successfully integrates multiple functional components into a single, colloidally stable nanocarrier with exceptional drug‐loading capacity, preserved antibody bioactivity, and laser‐responsive cargo release capability. These properties establish a robust foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo photothermal–immunotherapeutic investigations.

Superior Photothermal Conversion Performance of B–LM Nanocomposites

The therapeutic efficacy of photothermal therapy fundamentally depends on efficient light‐to‐heat conversion and the ability to generate cytotoxic hyperthermia within tumor tissues. We systematically evaluated the photothermal performance of B–LM nanocomposites under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation—a wavelength selected for its optimal tissue penetration depth and minimal absorption by endogenous chromophores.

Temperature elevation curves demonstrated clear concentration‐ and power‐dependent heating kinetics (Figure 3A). At an LM concentration of 500 µg mL− 1 under 1.5 W irradiation (∼76.5 mW mm− 2), the suspension temperature rapidly increased from ambient to >60°C within 5 min—well exceeding the thermal threshold for irreversible protein denaturation and cellular apoptosis (typically 42°C–45°C). In stark contrast, Milli‐Q water exhibited negligible temperature increase (<5°C) under identical irradiation conditions, confirming that the observed hyperthermia originates exclusively from the liquid metal nanoparticles rather than water absorption. Notably, even at the lowest tested power density (0.5 W, ∼25.5 mW mm− 2), B–LM suspensions achieved temperatures exceeding 50°C, indicating exceptional photothermal efficiency that surpasses many conventional photothermal agents including gold nanostructures and carbon‐based nanomaterials.

Infrared thermographic imaging provided complementary spatial visualization of heat generation (Figure 3B). Figure 3B presents a 3 × 3 matrix of infrared thermographic images across three B–LM concentrations (125, 250, and 500 µg mL− 1) and three laser powers (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 W), demonstrating concentration‐ and power‐dependent photothermal tunability. The thermograms displayed distinct color transitions from blue (low temperature) to red (high temperature) that intensified progressively with increasing laser power and LM concentration. Importantly, heat distribution remained highly localized to the irradiated area without significant thermal diffusion, confirming precise spatial control—a critical prerequisite for minimizing collateral damage to adjacent healthy tissues during in vivo therapy.

The optical stability of the nanocomposites was next evaluated. The UV–vis–NIR absorption spectra recorded before and after laser exposure (Figure 3C) exhibited no significant spectral shifts or intensity attenuation, indicating robust photostability and structural integrity of the LM‐based nanocomposites. To confirm that the observed spectroscopic stability reflects preserved nanoparticle morphology at the structural level, we performed transmission electron microscopy analysis of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 after 808 nm laser irradiation (1.5 W, ∼76.5 mW mm− 2, 5 min). TEM imaging revealed that nanoparticles maintained their characteristic spherical core–shell architecture with a well‐defined biomimetic membrane coating surrounding the electron‐dense liquid metal core (Figure S2), identical to the pre‐irradiation morphology (Figure 2B). High‐magnification TEM images confirmed preservation of the ∼15 nm biomimetic coating layer and absence of particle aggregation, fusion, or structural damage (Figure S2). The retention of spherical morphology and core–shell structure after intense laser exposure—conditions sufficient to generate cytotoxic hyperthermia (>60°C)—demonstrates exceptional structural resilience attributable to the inorganic metallic nature of the liquid metal core, which is inherently resistant to photochemical degradation or thermally induced deformation. Furthermore, the heating–cooling cycling test (Figure 3D) revealed highly reproducible thermal curves across multiple on/off cycles, confirming excellent photothermal durability under repetitive irradiation. Together, these results demonstrate that B–LM nanocomposites possess exceptional and tunable photothermal performance, exceptional optical stability, reliable thermal endurance, and preserved structural integrity under therapeutically relevant irradiation conditions, thereby providing a robust foundation for their use in photothermal–immunotherapeutic applications.

The photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 at 808 nm wavelength was approximately 54%, representing superior performance compared to various established photoexothermic nanomaterials, including metal‐based materials, carbon dots, and semiconductor polymer nanoparticles (Table S2) [50, 51, 52]. This exceptional conversion efficiency, combined with the mechanistically distinct nonradiative relaxation pathways of gallium‐based liquid metals as described in Section 2.1, underlies the effective photothermal heating observed both in vitro and in vivo.

The observed superior photothermal properties can be mechanistically attributed to the unique optical property of gallium‐based liquid metals, which exhibit strong light absorption in the NIR region that converts absorbed photon energy into heat energy [55]. These findings collectively establish that B–LM nanocomposites possess the essential photothermal characteristics—high conversion efficiency, excellent photostability, and reproducible performance—required for effective photothermal–immunotherapy applications.

Selective Laser‐Induced Cytotoxicity and Biocompatibility of B–LM–DMX–αCD25

An ideal therapeutic nanoplatform should exhibit negligible toxicity under non‐activated conditions while demonstrating potent cytotoxicity upon external stimulation, thereby maximizing the therapeutic index and minimizing systemic side effects. We evaluated the cytotoxicity profile of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 in both EMT‐6/AR1 drug‐resistant triple‐negative breast cancer cells and normal MRC‐5 lung fibroblasts under non‐irradiated and laser‐irradiated conditions.

In the absence of laser irradiation, both cell lines maintained high viability (>90%) after 24 h incubation with B–LM–DMX–αCD25 across all tested concentrations up to 500 µg mL− 1 (Figure 4A), demonstrating excellent intrinsic biocompatibility. This negligible dark toxicity is clinically advantageous, as it indicates that the nanoplatform remains biologically inert during systemic circulation and accumulation at tumor sites, thereby reducing the risk of off‐target toxicity to healthy tissues prior to laser activation.

This intracellular endosomal distribution is consistent with the cellular internalization data shown in Figure 5 and the 3D confocal z‐stack confirmation of cytoplasmic localization (Figure S4). Colloidal stability of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 under physiological conditions was confirmed by DLS measurements showing less than 5% variation in hydrodynamic diameter over 7 days (Figure 2C).

Upon 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W, ∼51 mW mm− 2), cell viability decreased dramatically in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 4B). Critically, laser‐activated B–LM–DMX–αCD25 exhibited preferential cytotoxicity toward malignant EMT‐6/AR1 cells compared to normal MRC‐5 cells at equivalent concentrations. At 500 µg mL− 1 LM with laser irradiation, EMT‐6/AR1 cell viability decreased to <15%, while MRC‐5 cells retained >25% viability—demonstrating approximately 1.5fold selectivity for cancer cells. IC50 values for B–LM–DMX–αCD25 under 808 nm laser irradiation (1.0 W, 5 min): EMT‐6 cancer cells, 247 ± 18 µg mL− 1; MRC‐5 normal cells, 412 ± 31 µg mL− 1, indicating ∼1.7‐fold preferential cytotoxicity toward cancer cells. This differential sensitivity represents a significant therapeutic advantage and can be attributed to fundamental biological differences between transformed and normal cells. Cancer cells typically exhibit elevated metabolic activity, reduced expression of heat shock proteins (HSPs), accelerated cell cycle progression, and compromised DNA repair mechanisms, collectively rendering them more vulnerable to heat‐induced damage [56, 57]. Conversely, normal cells maintain higher constitutive HSP levels—particularly HSP70 and HSP90—which provide enhanced cytoprotection through protein refolding, prevention of aggregation, and suppression of apoptotic signaling during hyperthermic stress. This inherent tumor selectivity may enable effective cancer cell elimination while preserving normal tissue integrity, thereby widening the therapeutic window for clinical application.

Real‐time microscopic observation during laser irradiation provided direct visual evidence of photothermal cytotoxicity (Figure 4C). Upon NIR exposure, rapid intracellular bubble formation (indicative of water vaporization and protein denaturation), membrane blebbing, cell shrinkage, and complete membrane disruption were observed within seconds in the laser‐irradiated region (Video S1). These morphological changes are characteristic of hyperacute necrotic cell death, consistent with the severe thermal stress generated by the photothermal effect. The spatial confinement of cell destruction to the laser‐irradiated area further confirms precise therapeutic control. The photothermal mechanism operates through bulk hyperthermia‐induced protein denaturation and membrane disruption (non‐organelle‐specific), explaining why intracellular heating at 58°C causes catastrophic, non‐apoptotic cell death as visualized in real‐time (Figure 4C and Video S1). In contrast, the control cells without nanoconjugates did not show any membrane destruction at all even after strong laser irradiation (Video S2). To further validate photothermal cytotoxicity through fluorescence‐based cell viability assessment, propidium iodide (PI) staining was performed as a complementary approach. PI selectively penetrates cells with compromised membrane integrity—the primary mechanism of photothermal cytotoxicity—providing direct fluorescence‐based confirmation of cell death. In the positive control group (B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + 808 nm laser), time‐lapse video microscopy with PI staining demonstrated progressive red fluorescence uptake in cells upon laser irradiation, directly confirming membrane disruption and cell death with high spatiotemporal resolution (Video S3). In the negative control group (no nanoparticles + 808 nm laser), static fluorescence images showed complete absence of PI signal before and after laser irradiation, confirming that laser exposure alone without nanoparticles does not induce membrane disruption or cytotoxicity (Figure S3). These PI staining results provide orthogonal visual confirmation of the CCK‐8 quantitative data (Figure 4B) and the real‐time bright‐field observations (Figure 4C), collectively establishing the nanoplatform‐dependent nature of photothermal cytotoxicity.

Together, these findings establish that B–LM–DMX–αCD25 possesses an optimal safety profile—excellent biocompatibility without activation, potent photothermal cytotoxicity upon laser exposure, and inherent selectivity for cancer cells over normal cells—making it a promising candidate for selective tumor ablation with minimal systemic toxicity.

To evaluate the impact of blood‐biomimetic camouflage on cellular interactions, we compared the uptake behavior of ICG‐labeled B–LM and DSPE–LM nanocomposites in both tumor cells and macrophages.

Fluorescence emission spectroscopy confirmed successful ICG incorporation into the nanoplatform, with characteristic emission centered at ∼810 nm upon 750 nm excitation (Figure 5A). This enhanced tumor cell uptake of blood‐camouflaged nanoparticles may be attributed to several synergistic mechanisms. First, the blood‐derived protein corona contains cell adhesion molecules and opsonins that can facilitate receptor‐mediated endocytosis through interactions with tumor cell surface receptors. Second, the biomimetic surface may reduce surface charge‐related repulsion, promoting closer cell‐nanoparticle interactions. Third, the preserved plasma proteins may activate specific endocytic pathways enriched in rapidly proliferating cancer cells. These mechanisms collectively enable superior tumor cell targeting compared to synthetic polymer‐coated nanoparticles.

The blood‐biomimetic coating employed in this study exploits the ensemble biophysical properties of the complex protein corona rather than the function of individual constituent proteins. Whole blood contains over 3,000 proteins with inherently variable abundances across individuals and physiological states, making detailed proteomic characterization of limited generalizability across preparations. The therapeutic benefits of the biomimetic coating—including prolonged systemic circulation, immune evasion, and enhanced tumor accumulation—arise from the collective physicochemical properties of the adsorbed protein layer, such as charge shielding, colloidal stabilization, and presentation of “self”‐recognition signals, rather than from specific protein–receptor interactions. Accordingly, functional validation of these properties, as demonstrated by the 50% reduction in macrophage uptake, 4‐fold enhancement in cancer cell internalization, and five‐fold increase in tumor accumulation (Figures 5B and 6), provides more robust and reproducible evidence of coating efficacy than compositional snapshots obtained by proteomics, which would reflect batch‐specific protein abundances without necessarily predicting therapeutic performance. It is important to note that these biomimetic effects arise from multiple concurrent mechanisms operating simultaneously—including charge modulation, protein corona‐mediated receptor interactions, and “self”‐signal presentation—rather than from a single receptor–ligand interaction. This multi‐factorial nature distinguishes blood‐biomimetic nanoparticles from conventional targeted delivery systems and explains why the observed differential uptake (enhanced in cancer cells, reduced in macrophages) cannot be attributed to any single molecular pathway.

Conversely, the uptake behavior in RAW 264.7 macrophages—key components of the mononuclear phagocyte system responsible for nanoparticle clearance—exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 5B,C). Macrophages incubated with ICG–DSPE–LM for 3 h displayed strong intracellular fluorescence, indicating rapid phagocytic uptake characteristic of foreign material recognition. This dramatic suppression of macrophage uptake represents a critical advantage for in vivo applications and can be mechanistically attributed to the “self”‐recognition signals presented by the blood‐biomimetic coating. Whole blood components naturally contain “don't eat me” signals, most notably CD47, which interacts with signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on macrophages to inhibit phagocytosis. Additionally, the preserved complement regulatory proteins (CD55, CD59) and the complex, physiologically relevant protein corona likely contribute to immune evasion by preventing opsonization and subsequent phagocytic recognition. This biomimetic camouflage effectively masks the nanoparticles from immune surveillance, mimicking endogenous blood components that freely circulate without triggering phagocytosis [58, 59, 60].

The contrasting cellular uptake patterns—enhanced internalization by tumor cells yet suppressed phagocytosis by immune cells—establish an ideal biodistribution profile that should favor prolonged systemic circulation, reduced RES clearance, and enhanced tumor accumulation in vivo, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off‐target effects.

Blood‐Biomimetic Coating Enhances Tumor Accumulation and Retention In Vivo

To validate the predicted biodistribution advantages conferred by blood‐biomimetic camouflage, we performed longitudinal near‐infrared fluorescence imaging in orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing mice following intravenous administration of ICG–B–LM or ICG–DSPE–LM nanocomposites.

Whole‐body fluorescence imaging revealed dramatically different biodistribution kinetics between the two formulations (Figure 6). ICG–B–LM nanocomposites exhibited progressive accumulation at tumor sites, with fluorescence intensity increasing continuously from 5 to 24 h post‐injection. Notably, the tumor fluorescence signal remained prominently detectable even at 24 h, indicating sustained retention at the tumor site. In contrast, ICG–DSPE–LM displayed weak, transient tumor accumulation with rapid fluorescence decay, consistent with accelerated systemic clearance by the reticuloendothelial system. The PBS control group showed no detectable fluorescence at any time point, confirming signal specificity. The apparent fluorescence signal in the head region observed at 0 h post‐injection is attributed to autofluorescence from mouse fur and skin rather than brain accumulation. Given that the hydrodynamic diameter of B‐LM (155–171 nm) far exceeds the blood–brain barrier pore size (∼10–15 nm), and that our formulation lacks active CNS transport mechanisms, brain penetration is physically precluded. Consistent with this, the head region signal remained constant across all timepoints (0–24 h), a pattern characteristic of tissue autofluorescence rather than progressive nanoparticle accumulation.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of harvested organs and tumors at 24 h post‐injection provided quantitative confirmation of biodistribution patterns. Ex vivo organ analysis was selected as the primary quantitative endpoint for biodistribution assessment because it eliminates the major confounding factors inherent to in vivo whole‐body imaging, including fur autofluorescence, tissue depth‐dependent signal attenuation, and breathing motion artifacts—thereby providing the most accurate and statistically rigorous measure of organ‐specific nanoparticle accumulation. All images were acquired under unified imaging parameters with identical pseudocolor scales applied across groups, ensuring direct quantitative comparability. In vivo whole‐body imaging (Figure 6, left panels) served a complementary purpose, providing qualitative visualization of longitudinal pharmacokinetic behavior across timepoints (0, 5, 10, 17, and 24 h post‐injection). Tumor tissues from ICG–B–LM‐treated mice exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity among all dissected organs, demonstrating preferential tumor targeting. Quantitative analysis revealed approximately 5‐fold higher tumor accumulation for B–LM compared to DSPE–LM (based on relative fluorescence intensity). Conversely, ICG–DSPE–LM showed pronounced accumulation in liver and spleen—major RES organs responsible for clearance of foreign particles—indicating rapid phagocytic uptake and sequestration.

Quantitative region‐of‐interest analysis of ex vivo organ fluorescence (Figure 6) revealed that ICG–B–LM achieved five‐fold higher tumor accumulation compared to ICG–DSPE–LM (1,223 ± 156 vs. 245 ± 28 a.u., p < 0.001). Notably, neither formulation showed significant accumulation in heart, kidney, or spleen, while ICG–DSPE–LM exhibited only marginal hepatic fluorescence—consistent with limited but detectable RES clearance. The near‐complete absence of hepatic and splenic accumulation for ICG–B–LM provides direct evidence of successful RES evasion conferred by the blood‐biomimetic coating, corroborating the in vitro macrophage uptake data (Figure 5B).

Ex vivo imaging revealed higher pulmonary accumulation in ICG–B–LM compared to ICG–DSPE–LM. This finding is mechanistically attributable to the prolonged systemic circulation conferred by blood‐biomimetic camouflage: unlike ICG–DSPE–LM, which undergoes relatively rapid clearance, ICG–B–LM nanoparticles make repeated passes through the pulmonary vasculature over the 24 h observation period, increasing the cumulative probability of pulmonary retention. The higher pulmonary signal in ICG–B–LM therefore reflects prolonged circulatory exposure rather than preferential lung targeting. Importantly, despite this pulmonary retention, ICG–B–LM still achieved substantially higher absolute tumor accumulation than ICG–DSPE–LM (5.0‐fold, p < 0.001), demonstrating that prolonged circulation ultimately benefits tumor delivery via sustained EPR‐mediated extravasation. Furthermore, lung histopathology (H&E staining, Figure S5) confirmed completely normal alveolar architecture with no evidence of inflammatory infiltration or particle‐induced tissue damage, confirming that pulmonary retention is transient and does not compromise safety.

The superior tumor targeting of B–LM nanocomposites can be attributed to synergistic mechanisms operating at multiple levels. First, the blood‐biomimetic coating significantly prolongs circulation half‐life by evading RES clearance, thereby increasing the cumulative opportunity for tumor extravasation. Second, the preserved “self”‐signals suppress macrophage uptake in circulation and within tumors, preventing premature elimination before reaching cancer cells. Third, the optimal particle size (∼171 nm) facilitates passive tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [61], exploiting the leaky tumor vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage characteristic of solid tumors. Fourth, the blood‐derived components may actively facilitate tumor penetration through interactions with endothelial cells and extracellular matrix components.

These findings establish that whole‐blood biomimetic engineering represents an effective strategy to overcome the major pharmacokinetic limitations that constrain clinical translation of inorganic nanoparticles, enabling efficient tumor delivery essential for therapeutic efficacy.

Efficient In Vivo Photothermal Conversion at Tumor Sites

Having established favorable tumor accumulation, we next evaluated whether B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites could generate therapeutically relevant hyperthermia within solid tumors in vivo. Orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing mice received intravenous administration of various formulations followed by 808 nm laser irradiation at 24 h post‐injection—a time point selected based on biodistribution studies showing optimal tumor accumulation.

Infrared thermographic imaging during laser exposure revealed striking differences in tumor heating among treatment groups (Figure 7A). Tumors in mice treated with B–LM–DMX–αCD25 displayed rapid, pronounced temperature elevation upon laser activation, with clear visualization of heat generation localized to the tumor region. In contrast, PBS, DSPE–LM, αCD25, and DMX groups showed only marginal temperature increases, likely attributable to limited NIR absorption by endogenous tissue chromophores.

This rapid heating rate (∼4.4°C min− 1) and peak temperature substantially exceed the thermal thresholds for irreversible protein denaturation (∼45°C), mitochondrial dysfunction (∼47°C), and instantaneous coagulative necrosis (>50°C), ensuring complete ablation of tumor cells within the irradiated volume. Importantly, this temperature elevation occurred with a clinically feasible laser power density (∼51 mW mm− 2) that falls well within FDA‐approved safety limits for dermatological procedures (<330 mW mm− 2) [62], supporting translational potential. The laser power density of 1.0 W (∼51 mW mm− 2) was selected as the optimal irradiation condition based on three key criteria. First, this power density reliably achieves tumor temperatures exceeding 50°C (Figure 7B), the threshold required for coagulative necrosis and complete protein denaturation. Second, it is well within the FDA‐approved safety limits for dermatological laser procedures (<330 mW mm− 2), supporting clinical translatability. Third, since in vivo heat dissipation through blood flow and tissue conductivity is substantially greater than in vitro conditions, the 1.0 W setting ensures maintenance of therapeutic temperatures throughout the tumor volume, whereas lower power settings (e.g., 0.5 W) demonstrated sufficient heating in vitro but may be inadequate to sustain therapeutic hyperthermia in vivo.

In contrast, tumors in PBS and DSPE–LM groups remained below 40°C throughout the irradiation period—insufficient for effective thermal cytotoxicity. The failure of DSPE–LM to generate significant hyperthermia despite containing liquid metal cores can be attributed to poor tumor accumulation resulting from rapid RES clearance, as demonstrated by biodistribution studies. This comparison underscores the critical importance of the blood‐biomimetic coating for achieving therapeutically effective tumor heating in vivo.

The observed exceptional photothermal performance—combining efficient tumor delivery, high NIR absorption, and superior light‐to‐heat conversion—establishes the essential foundation for effective photothermal therapy. Moreover, the precise spatial control of heat generation to tumor tissues, without significant heating of surrounding normal tissues, predicts minimal collateral damage and supports the safety profile required for clinical translation.

(2) The conjugated anti‐CD25 antibodies selectively deplete intratumoral Tregs, dismantling a critical immunosuppressive barrier and creating a permissive microenvironment for subsequent immune activation. (3) NIR laser irradiation triggers dual therapeutic actions simultaneously—photothermal ablation directly kills tumor cells through hyperthermia while inducing robust ICD that releases abundant TAAs and DAMPs into the tumor microenvironment. (4) Concurrently, laser‐triggered DMX release activates the cGAS‐STING pathway in tumor‐infiltrating dendritic cells and other APCs, dramatically amplifying their maturation, activation, and antigen‐presentation capacity. (5) This synchronized release of tumor antigens and innate immune activation enables efficient priming of tumor‐specific cytotoxic T cells, which then mediate systemic antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these findings establish that the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanoplatform, when combined with NIR laser irradiation, represents a highly effective multimodal therapeutic strategy capable of achieving complete eradication of aggressive orthotopic TNBC tumors through synergistic photothermal–immunotherapy.

PBS‐treated mice displayed significantly elevated lung weights (>0.4 g) due to massive tumor infiltration, whereas B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser‐treated mice maintained lung weights (∼0.15 g) nearly identical to healthy controls, indicating minimal residual tumor burden.

Histopathological examination provided microscopic confirmation of therapeutic efficacy (Figure 9D). H&E‐stained lung sections from PBS and DSPE–LM groups revealed extensive tumor cell infiltration with complete disruption of normal alveolar architecture, collapsed air spaces, and dense sheets of malignant cells occupying large areas of the lung parenchyma. DSPE–DMX and DSPE–αCD25 treatments resulted in partial preservation of lung structure but still showed substantial tumor infiltration. In striking contrast, lungs from B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser‐treated mice exhibited largely intact alveolar architecture with only scattered, small tumor cell clusters, confirming effective suppression of metastatic colonization at the microscopic level. These histopathological findings were consistent with, and provided microscopic validation of, the macroscopic metastatic nodule counts presented in Figure 9A,B.

Throughout the 24‐day treatment period, body weight monitoring revealed important insights into therapeutic tolerability (Figure 10A). Mice in the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + laser group exhibited modest body weight reduction relative to PBS controls. Importantly, this observation should not be interpreted as treatment‐related toxicity. In aggressive tumor models characterized by rapid primary tumor growth and extensive pulmonary metastasis, disease progression is frequently accompanied by paradoxical body weight gain—not reflecting improved health but rather increasing tumor burden, ascites formation, and metabolic dysregulation. The comparatively lower body weight in effectively treated mice thus indicates successful tumor control rather than systemic toxicity. This interpretation is strongly supported by hematological analyses performed 7 days post‐treatment, which revealed no significant abnormalities in complete blood counts, liver enzymes, or kidney function markers compared to PBS controls (Table S3), confirming the absence of systemic toxicity. Major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested after treatment and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histopathological examination. As shown in Figure S5, no obvious tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, or structural abnormalities were observed in any of the examined organs across different treatment groups, with or without laser irradiation. These results indicate that B–LM–DMX–αCD25–mediated photothermal immunotherapy does not induce appreciable off‐target toxicity under the tested conditions, demonstrating favorable systemic biosafety in vivo.

It is noteworthy that the treatment protocol employed in this study constitutes a repeat‐dose regimen: mice received a total of 4 doses over 7 days for primary tumor treatment, followed by 8 additional doses over 14 days for the metastasis prevention study, totaling 12 doses administered over 21 days. The absence of significant toxicity throughout this prolonged, repeat‐dose treatment course—as evidenced by stable body weight, normal hematological parameters, and preserved organ histology—demonstrates favorable systemic tolerability under clinically relevant dosing conditions.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis provided the most clinically relevant measure of therapeutic benefit (Figure 10B and Figure S6). All treatment groups without laser irradiation failed to provide a survival advantage, with mice succumbing to tumor burden within 25–50 days. Similarly, PBS + Laser or DSPE–LM + Laser showed negligible survival benefit, confirming that laser alone or poorly‐delivered photothermal agents are therapeutically ineffective. DSPE–DMX + Laser and DSPE–αCD25 + Laser treatments modestly extended median survival to ∼40 and ∼45 days, respectively, but all mice eventually died from disease progression.

In stark contrast, B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser treatment resulted in dramatically prolonged survival, with several mice surviving beyond 70 days and the majority remaining tumor‐free at study termination. This represents an approximate doubling of survival compared to the best monotherapy and >3‐fold improvement versus untreated controls. These results suggest potential for durable cure—a remarkable outcome in this aggressive, metastatic tumor model.

The profound suppression of distant metastasis and dramatic survival extension can be mechanistically attributed to the induction of systemic, tumor‐specific adaptive immunity. The combination of extensive tumor antigen release (via photothermal ICD), enhanced antigen presentation (via STING‐mediated DC activation), and relief of immunosuppression (via Treg depletion) collectively enables robust priming of tumor‐specific CD8+ T cells. These effector T cells then circulate systemically, recognize and eliminate circulating tumor cells, and prevent establishment of metastatic foci—a phenomenon termed “abscopal effect” in the context of localized therapy inducing systemic immunity.

These findings establish that B–LM–DMX–αCD25‐mediated photothermal–immunotherapy not only eradicates primary tumors but also induces durable systemic antitumor immunity capable of preventing metastatic disease and significantly extending survival—addressing the most critical unmet need in TNBC management.

To elucidate the immunological mechanisms underlying the exceptional therapeutic efficacy, we performed comprehensive histological and immunohistochemical analyses of tumor tissues from different treatment groups, focusing on markers of apoptosis, immune suppression, innate immune activation, and adaptive immune responses.

H&E staining revealed that tumors treated with PBS or DSPE‐LM (with or without laser) maintained intact tissue architecture with densely packed, viable tumor cells exhibiting minimal necrosis (Figure 11A), confirming negligible intrinsic toxicity of the carrier or laser alone. DSPE–DMX or DSPE–αCD25 treatments induced moderate focal necrosis and cellular disruption. In striking contrast, B–LM–DMX–αCD25 combined with laser irradiation resulted in catastrophic destruction of tumor architecture, with extensive areas showing complete loss of cellular organization, pyknotic nuclei, and coagulative necrosis—hallmarks of effective photothermal ablation.

A defining characteristic of TNBC is extensive infiltration of CD25+ FOXP3+ regulatory T cells that actively suppress antitumor immunity [17, 63]. Immunohistochemical staining for CD25 revealed abundant Treg presence in PBS and DSPE‐LM groups, confirming the profoundly immunosuppressive TME (Figure 12A and Figure S7). Critically, formulations containing anti‐CD25 antibodies (DSPE–αCD25 and B–LM–DMX–αCD25) achieved significant Treg depletion, with the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser group showing the most profound reduction (>80% decrease in CD25+ cell density, p < 0.001). This substantial Treg depletion effectively “releases the brake” on antitumor immunity, creating a permissive microenvironment for subsequent immune activation. The superior Treg depletion achieved by B–LM–DMX–αCD25 compared to free antibody can be attributed to enhanced tumor delivery and sustained local antibody exposure afforded by the blood‐camouflaged nanocarrier.

Following photothermal‐induced ICD, effective antitumor immunity requires coordinated activation of innate immune cells to process released antigens and initiate adaptive responses. TNF‐α staining—a key pro‐inflammatory cytokine downstream of STING activation—was minimal in PBS and DSPE‐LM groups but markedly elevated in DMX‐containing formulations (Figure 12A and Figure S7). The B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser group exhibited the highest TNF‐α expression, indicating robust inflammatory activation. This pro‐inflammatory shift is essential for recruiting and activating immune effector cells.

Consistent with STING‐mediated immune activation, CD11c+ dendritic cell infiltration and maturation were dramatically enhanced in B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser‐treated tumors (approximately 11‐fold increase vs. controls, Figure 12A and Figure S7). Mature dendritic cells are the most potent APCs, essential for capturing tumor antigens, migrating to lymph nodes, and priming tumor‐specific T‐cell responses. The synchronized timing of DMX release (via laser‐triggered discharge) with maximal antigen availability (via photothermal ICD) ensures optimal conditions for DC maturation and antigen loading—a critical determinant of successful cancer vaccination.

The ultimate measure of successful cancer immunotherapy is the generation of tumor‐specific cytotoxic T cells capable of recognizing and eliminating malignant cells. CD3+ pan T‐cell staining revealed minimal T‐cell infiltration in PBS and DSPE‐LM groups, consistent with an immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (Figure 12A and Figure S7). In striking contrast, B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser treatment induced massive T‐cell infiltration (>13‐fold increase), transforming the cold tumor into an immune‐inflamed phenotype. These infiltrating T cells are presumably enriched for tumor‐specific CTLs generated through the coordinated antigen release and innate immune activation.

Beyond T‐cell responses, our comprehensive immunophenotyping revealed broad engagement of multiple immune effector populations (Figure 12B and Figure S8). CD19+ B‐cell infiltration was markedly enhanced, suggesting activation of humoral immunity that could contribute to long‐term immune memory and antibody‐mediated tumor cell elimination. F4/80+ macrophage accumulation was substantially increased (Figure 12B), and the dramatic upregulation of Tnf (2.11‐fold, Figure 13D)—a hallmark M1‐associated cytokine—suggests enrichment of pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotypes. While our single‐marker IHC (F4/80) cannot definitively distinguish M1 vs. M2 macrophage subsets, the concurrent upregulation of pro‐inflammatory markers (Tnf, Ifnb1) and absence of M2‐associated markers (no Arg1 or Il10 upregulation; Il10 actually decreased 72%, Figure 10B) collectively suggest a predominantly pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype. Future studies employing multicolor flow cytometry or single‐cell transcriptomics would provide higher‐resolution macrophage subset characterization. CXCR4+ neutrophils—early responders in inflammatory contexts—showed pronounced infiltration, consistent with rapid innate immune cell recruitment. Most notably, NKp46+ natural killer cell infiltration was dramatically elevated in B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser‐treated tumors. NK cells provide crucial anti‐metastatic immunity through direct cytotoxicity against circulating tumor cells and MHC‐deficient cancer cells that evade T‐cell recognition, likely contributing to the observed profound suppression of lung metastasis.

Integrating these mechanistic findings reveals an elegantly coordinated immune cascade (Figure 12A,B, Figures S7 and S8): (1) Anti‐CD25‐mediated Treg depletion dismantles immunosuppression, establishing a permissive microenvironment. (2) Photothermal ablation induces extensive ICD, releasing abundant tumor antigens and DAMPs. (3) STING activation enhances DC maturation and cytokine production, creating an inflammatory milieu. (4) Mature DCs efficiently present tumor antigens to naïve T cells, priming tumor‐specific CTL responses. (5) Activated CTLs infiltrate tumors and mediate direct cytotoxicity. (6) Concurrently, NK cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and B cells are recruited and activated, establishing a multilayered immune response. (7) This coordinated innate and adaptive immunity not only eliminates primary tumors but also provides systemic surveillance against metastatic disease.

Several methodological considerations merit acknowledgment in the interpretation of our immunophenotyping data. First, the characterization of macrophage polarization states relies on single‐marker IHC (F4/80) combined with transcriptional profiling (Tnf upregulation), which cannot definitively distinguish M1 from M2 macrophage subsets with the resolution afforded by multicolor flow cytometry or single‐cell transcriptomics. While the concurrent upregulation of pro‐inflammatory markers (Tnf, Ifnb1) and the marked reduction of the immunosuppressive cytokine Il10 (72% decrease, Figure 13B) collectively suggest a predominantly pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype, definitive subset classification would require multi‐parameter phenotyping in future studies. Second, flow cytometry‐based immune cell quantification was not performed in this study, primarily due to the extensive tumor necrosis present at 24 h post‐photothermal ablation (Figure 11A), which precludes reliable viable cell recovery through enzymatic dissociation. Our integrated approach—combining spatial protein‐level data (IHC), quantitative molecular data (qRT‐PCR), and functional therapeutic outcomes—was selected to circumvent this technical limitation while providing complementary and mutually reinforcing evidence across multiple levels of biological organization.

This mechanistic dissection establishes that the B–LM–DMX–αCD25 platform achieves therapeutic efficacy not through a single mechanism but through orchestrated, synergistic activation of multiple immune pathways. By simultaneously removing immunosuppression (Treg depletion), providing antigenic stimulus (photothermal ICD), and amplifying immune responsiveness (STING activation), this tripartite strategy transforms the immunologically hostile TNBC microenvironment into a highly immunogenic one, enabling complete tumor eradication and prevention of metastatic recurrence.

Transcriptional Profiling Validates Coordinated Immune Activation

To comprehensively validate the immunological mechanisms at the molecular level, we performed quantitative RT‐PCR analysis of key immune‐related genes in tumor tissues at 24 h post‐treatment (Figure 13). This transcriptional profiling revealed coordinated activation across multiple immune pathways, providing molecular evidence for our proposed tripartite therapeutic mechanism.

B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + Laser treatment induced upregulation of the STING signaling cascade, with Tmem173 (encoding STING protein) increased 1.37‐fold, Ifnb1 (type I interferon‐β) elevated 1.42‐fold, and Cxcl10 (IP‐10, an interferon‐stimulated chemokine) upregulated 1.96‐fold compared to PBS controls (Figure 13A, p < 0.001). The induction of Ifnb1 transcription demonstrates that DMX‐mediated STING activation proceeds through the canonical STING→TBK1→IRF3→IFN‐β pathway and generates bioactive type I interferons essential for DC activation and T‐cell priming. The elevation of Cxcl10 further confirms functional STING pathway engagement, as this chemokine is a robust interferon‐stimulated gene responsible for recruiting effector T cells to tumor sites.

Consistent with immunohistochemical evidence of Treg depletion (Figure 12A), transcriptional analysis revealed profound suppression of Foxp3 (73% reduction, Log10RQ = −0.57) and Il10 (72% reduction, Log10RQ = −0.55) in B‐LM‐DMX‐αCD25 + Laser‐treated tumors (Figure 13B, p < 0.001). Foxp3 encodes the master transcription factor defining Treg lineage identity and suppressive function, while IL‐10 represents a key immunosuppressive cytokine secreted by Tregs to inhibit effector T‐cell responses. The coordinated downregulation of both genes confirms successful anti‐CD25‐mediated Treg depletion and relief of immunosuppressive signaling, creating a permissive microenvironment for subsequent immune activation.

The treatment induced substantial upregulation of T‐cell markers and effector molecules (Figure 13C). Cd3d (pan T‐cell marker) increased 2.69‐fold, while Cd8a (cytotoxic T‐cell‐specific marker) exhibited elevation (2.12‐fold), suggesting preferential recruitment or expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells—the critical effector population for tumor elimination. Importantly, genes encoding functional T‐cell activation and cytotoxic capacity were markedly upregulated: Ifng (IFN‐γ, Th1 cytokine and activation marker) increased 3.29‐fold, and Gzmb (Granzyme B, the primary cytotoxic effector molecule) was elevated 3.07‐fold (p < 0.001). The pronounced upregulation of Gzmb transcription provides molecular evidence that infiltrating T cells were not merely present but functionally armed with cytotoxic machinery capable of tumor cell killing.

Beyond adaptive immunity, the platform activated innate immune responses essential for initial tumor recognition and inflammation amplification (Figure 13D). Tnf (tumor necrosis factor‐α) was upregulated 2.11‐fold, reflecting robust pro‐inflammatory activation. Ncr1 (encoding NKp46, an NK cell‐specific activating receptor) increased 1.87‐fold, indicating substantial NK cell recruitment—consistent with immunohistochemical findings (Figure 12B) and likely contributing to anti‐metastatic immunity through MHC‐independent cytotoxicity against circulating tumor cells. Additionally, Cd19 (B‐cell marker) was elevated 1.88‐fold, suggesting activation of humoral immune responses that may contribute to long‐term immunological memory.

To provide additional molecular and protein‐level evidence supporting ICD induction, we further analyzed Hmgb1 expression—encoding high mobility group box 1 protein, a prototypical damage‐associated molecular pattern (DAMP) released during immunogenic cell death—across all treatment groups using both qRT‐PCR and immunohistochemical staining. Transcriptional analysis revealed a clear treatment‐dependent pattern of Hmgb1 upregulation: B–LM–DMX–αCD25> DSPE–αCD25> DSPE–DMX > DSPE–LM > PBS (Figure 13D). This rank order is entirely consistent with our proposed mechanism, whereby the fully combined formulation (B–LM–DMX–αCD25), integrating Treg depletion, STING activation, and photothermal conversion capacity within a single nanoplatform, generates the greatest immunogenic stimulus, driving maximal transcriptional induction of HMGB1 as a danger signal. Importantly, immunohistochemical staining for HMGB1 protein in tumor tissue sections from all treatment groups confirmed the same treatment‐dependent pattern at the protein level (Figure S9), with B–LM–DMX–αCD25‐treated tumors exhibiting the highest HMGB1 immunoreactivity and quantitative image analysis revealing statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.001). The concordance between transcriptional and protein‐level HMGB1 data provides robust, orthogonal evidence of DAMP induction consistent with photothermal‐induced ICD, complementing the functional evidence of DC activation and T‐cell priming observed throughout this study.

Collectively, this comprehensive transcriptional profiling validates our proposed mechanism at the molecular level: anti‐CD25‐mediated Treg depletion dismantles immunosuppression (Foxp3↓, Il10↓), DMX‐triggered STING activation drives innate immune engagement and DC maturation (Tmem173↑, Ifnb1↑, Cxcl10↑, Tnf↑), and photothermal‐induced antigen release combined with adjuvant signaling elicits potent adaptive immunity (Cd8a↑, Ifng↑, Gzmb↑). This coordinated immune cascade, confirmed at transcriptional, protein (IHC), and functional (tumor regression) levels, underlies the exceptional therapeutic efficacy observed against both primary tumors and distant metastases. It is important to contextualize our validation approach within the broader framework of cancer immunotherapy research. The therapeutic mechanisms central to this study—Treg‐mediated immunosuppression relief, STING activation within tumor‐resident APCs, and photothermal ICD—are fundamentally TME‐dependent processes that cannot be authentically recapitulated in simplified in vitro systems. The immunosuppressive TME established over weeks of tumor growth, the spatial organization of dying cells and responding immune cells, and the dynamic interplay between innate and adaptive immunity are all context‐dependent phenomena that require in vivo validation for meaningful interpretation. Accordingly, our multilevel in vivo approach—integrating molecular (qRT‐PCR), tissue (IHC), and functional (tumor regression, metastasis suppression, survival) evidence—provides more biologically relevant validation than reductionist in vitro models would afford. This in vivo‐focused strategy is consistent with established practices in high‐impact cancer immunotherapy research, where functional therapeutic outcomes combined with molecular profiling of treated tumors are recognized as the gold standard for mechanistic validation [28, 64, 65]. From a translational perspective, the favorable safety profile demonstrated in this study—encompassing normal hematological parameters, preserved hepatic and renal function, and absence of histopathological organ damage across a repeat‐dose regimen—provides an encouraging foundation for future clinical development. Formal GLP‐compliant toxicology studies, including 90‐day repeat‐dose toxicity assessment and ICP‐MS‐based elemental biodistribution analysis, will be conducted as part of the preclinical development package required for investigational new drug application. Additionally, the use of clinically approved laser parameters (808 nm, ∼501 mW mm− 2, within FDA‐approved limits) and biocompatible gallium‐based liquid metals further supports the translational potential of this platform.

Conclusion

This work presents a paradigm‐shifting therapeutic strategy for drug‐resistant metastatic triple‐negative breast cancer by rational integration of liquid metal‐based photothermal therapy with dual immunomodulatory interventions—regulatory T‐cell depletion and STING pathway activation—within a single blood‐camouflaged nanoplatform. Through innovative whole‐blood biomimetic engineering, we overcome the critical limitations that have constrained clinical translation of liquid metal nanoparticles, including poor aqueous dispersibility, rapid immune clearance, and insufficient tumor accumulation.

The engineered B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanoconjugates demonstrated exceptional physicochemical properties: colloidal stability, high drug‐loading efficiency (>95%), preserved antibody bioactivity (100% conjugation), superior photothermal conversion (reaching 58°C within 5 min), excellent photostability, and stimulus‐responsive cargo release. The blood‐biomimetic coating conferred dual advantages—enhanced internalization by cancer cells while evading macrophage phagocytosis—resulting in approximately five‐fold improvement in tumor accumulation compared to conventional polymer‐coated formulations.

The therapeutic efficacy validation established unprecedented outcomes in aggressive TNBC models. In orthotopic primary tumor models, B–LM–DMX–αCD25 plus laser irradiation achieved 100% complete tumor regression throughout the observation period—a remarkable result in this notoriously treatment‐resistant cancer type. More critically, in experimental metastasis models mimicking the clinical challenge of disseminated disease, the treatment regimen profoundly suppressed pulmonary metastatic colonization (>70% reduction in nodules), preserved normal lung architecture, and extended median survival beyond 70 days, with several mice achieving apparent cure.

Mechanistic investigations combining immunohistochemistry, transcriptional profiling, and functional validation revealed that therapeutic efficacy stems from a coordinated tripartite immune cascade operating at molecular, cellular, and systems levels. Anti‐CD25‐mediated selective Treg depletion (>70% reduction in Foxp3 and Il10 expression) dismantled the immunosuppressive barrier. Liquid metal‐mediated photothermal therapy induced extensive immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor antigens while demonstrating excellent selectivity for cancer cells. Laser‐triggered DMX release activated the STING–TBK1–IRF3 pathway (1.37‐fold Tmem173 upregulation, 1.42‐fold Ifnb1 induction), promoting dendritic cell maturation and type I interferon signaling. This orchestrated sequence enabled robust priming of tumor‐specific cytotoxic T cells (2.12‐fold Cd8a , 3.07‐fold Gzmb upregulation) while recruiting diverse immune effector populations including NK cells and B cells, establishing a multilayered, self‐amplifying antitumor immune response validated by complete tumor regression, >90% metastasis suppression, and dramatically extended survival.

The significance of this work extends beyond demonstrating efficacy in preclinical models. By establishing a generalizable platform that rationally combines complementary therapeutic modalities—local physical therapy (photothermal ablation), immunosuppression relief (Treg depletion), and innate immune amplification (STING activation)—within a biomimetic delivery system that addresses fundamental pharmacokinetic barriers, this approach offers a clinically translatable strategy applicable to multiple immunotherapy‐resistant cancer types. The use of clinically feasible laser parameters (51 mW mm− 2), FDA‐precedented therapeutic components (antibodies and small molecules), and biocompatible materials (gallium‐based alloys with established safety profiles) further supports translational potential.

Future directions include investigating long‐term immunological memory and resistance to tumor rechallenge, evaluating efficacy in spontaneous metastasis models with intact immune surveillance, combining this platform with immune checkpoint inhibitors to achieve even greater synergy, and optimizing treatment protocols for multifocal disease and deeply seated tumors. Additionally, the modular nature of the blood‐biomimetic liquid metal platform enables straightforward adaptation for other cancer types by substituting alternative targeting antibodies and immunomodulatory cargoes.

While the present study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 as a multimodal cancer immunotherapy platform, several limitations warrant acknowledgment.

First, while treatment‐dependent transcriptional upregulation of Hmgb1 was demonstrated (Figure 13D), we have now additionally provided immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of HMGB1 to offer protein‐level validation within tumor tissues. We acknowledge that direct quantification of extracellular ICD markers—including calreticulin surface exposure, extracellular HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion—was not performed in this study; future work should incorporate these measurements under controlled in vitro conditions to provide more definitive mechanistic evidence of ICD induction. While the present study provides multilevel transcriptional and histological evidence of STING pathway engagement—including upregulation of Tmem173, Ifnb1, and Cxcl10, as well as HMGB1 validation at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure S9)—direct protein‐level validation of phosphorylated STING pathway components (p‐TBK1, p‐IRF3) was not performed in this study. Such phosphorylation events peak at 1–4 h post‐stimulation and return to baseline within 6–8 h, rendering Western blot analysis at our 24 h sampling timepoint methodologically inappropriate. Performing tumor tissue collection at the optimal early timepoint of 4–6 h post‐treatment would require a separate animal cohort and independent treatment session, representing a substantial addition beyond the scope of the current work. Western blot analysis for p‐TBK1 and p‐IRF3 at early timepoints (4–6 h post‐treatment) therefore represents an important direction for future mechanistic studies. Similarly, while the molecular evidence presented in this study strongly supports selective intratumoral Treg depletion—as reflected by the concurrent 73% reduction in Foxp3 expression alongside a 2.12‐fold increase in Cd8a and 2.69‐fold increase in Cd3d—direct flow cytometric validation of CD4+Foxp3+ Treg depletion selectivity was not performed. Extensive tumor necrosis at 24 h post‐photothermal ablation precluded reliable recovery of viable cells for flow cytometric analysis. The available molecular data, demonstrating simultaneous Treg depletion and effector T‐cell expansion, are consistent with selective Treg targeting rather than nonspecific T‐cell depletion; however, future studies employing flow cytometry at pre‐necrotic timepoints (e.g., 6–12 h post‐treatment) would provide definitive single‐cell resolution of this selectivity. A further consideration for clinical translation concerns the tissue penetration depth of the NIR‐I laser (808 nm) employed in this study, which is limited to approximately 1–2 cm in biological tissues. This constraint limits direct photothermal ablation to superficial or surgically accessible tumor lesions, and the platform in its current form is therefore most suitable for early‐stage TNBC, chest wall recurrences, and cutaneous or subcutaneous metastases—indications that collectively represent a clinically significant and underserved patient population. For deeper tumors, potential solutions include transition to NIR‐II window lasers (1000–1350 nm), which offer significantly greater tissue penetration depths while maintaining high photothermal conversion efficiency, or interstitial optical fiber delivery enabling precise intratumoral laser irradiation under image guidance. Importantly, however, the primary translational value of our platform extends beyond direct photothermal ablation. As demonstrated in the lung metastasis model, local photothermal treatment of the primary tumor—combined with Treg depletion and STING activation—generates a systemic antitumor immune response capable of eliminating distant metastases entirely outside the laser irradiation field. This abscopal immune effect means that even when only accessible lesions are directly irradiated, the activated immune system can surveil and eliminate metastatic disease at distant sites, including the lungs, substantially mitigating the penetration depth limitation for the treatment of metastatic TNBC. Second, detailed characterization of macrophage polarization states (M1/M2) and precise T‐cell subset phenotyping using multicolor flow cytometry or single‐cell transcriptomics would further resolve the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment. Third, comprehensive metabolic fate studies, including ICP‐MS‐based elemental analysis and long‐term tissue retention profiling, will be necessary to fully elucidate the in vivo behavior of gallium‐based liquid metal nanoparticles prior to clinical translation. Finally, formal GLP‐compliant repeat‐dose toxicology studies will be required to support future investigational new drug applications. Collectively, these investigations represent important directions for advancing this platform toward clinical development.

In conclusion, this work establishes blood‐camouflaged liquid metal nanocomposites as a powerful, versatile platform for photothermal–immunotherapy and demonstrates that Treg clearance combined with synchronized photothermal ablation and STING activation functions as an “immunological ignition signal” that transforms localized therapy into durable systemic antitumor immunity. This strategy addresses the fundamental challenge in TNBC management—overcoming profound immunosuppression to enable effective immunotherapy—and offers renewed hope for patients with this devastating disease.

Experimental Section

Materials

Gallium–indium eutectic alloy (Ga:In = 75.5:24.5 wt%; Alfa Aesar) was used as the liquid metal core material. DSPE–PEG–NH2 and DSPE–PEG–NHS (1,2‐distearoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphoethanolamine‐N‐[amino(polyethylene glycol)] and N‐hydroxysuccinimide ester derivatives) were purchased from Yuka Sangyo Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). DMX (5,6‐dimethylxanthenone‐4‐acetic acid) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Anti‐CD25 monoclonal antibody (clone PC61) was purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA). Indocyanine green (ICG) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). Phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and all cell culture reagents were purchased from standard commercial suppliers. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis of Blood‐Camouflaged Liquid Metal Nanocomposites (B–LM–DMX)

The blood‐biomimetic liquid metal nanoplatform was prepared through a facile one‐step ultrasonication method. Briefly, 30 mg of gallium–indium eutectic alloy was dispersed in 10 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing DSPE–PEG–NH2 (1 mg mL− 1) and DMX (1 mg mL− 1). To this mixture, 500 µL of freshly collected murine whole blood (obtained from healthy BALB/c mice via cardiac puncture under anesthesia) was added immediately prior to sonication to preserve the integrity of blood components and protein corona. The mixture was subjected to probe‐type ultrasonication (VCX‐600, Sonics & Materials, Inc., Newtown, CT, USA) at 40% amplitude for 10 min in an ice bath to prevent thermal degradation. The ice bath was refreshed every 3 min to maintain temperature below 4°C throughout the sonication process.

Following sonication, the resulting colloidal suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to remove large aggregates and unincorporated blood cells. The supernatant containing blood‐camouflaged liquid metal nanoparticles (B–LM–DMX) was collected and stored at 4°C protected from light. The final concentrations in the stock suspension were: liquid metal at 3 mg mL− 1 and DMX at 1 mg mL− 1. The nanocomposites exhibited excellent colloidal stability with no visible precipitation for at least 7 days under refrigeration.

Conjugation of Anti‐CD25 Antibody to B–LM–DMX

Surface functionalization with anti‐CD25 antibody was achieved through NHS‐ester coupling chemistry. First, DSPE–PEG–NHS (1 mg) and anti‐CD25 monoclonal antibody (1 mg) were mixed in PBS buffer (pH 7.4) and incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation to enable covalent conjugation between NHS ester groups and primary amines on the antibody. The reaction mixture was then purified using 100 kDa Amicon centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) at 4,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to remove excess unreacted DSPE–PEG–NHS and preserve antibody bioactivity. The retentate containing DSPE–PEG–anti‐CD25 conjugates was collected and resuspended in PBS.

The purified DSPE–PEG–anti‐CD25 conjugate was then added to the preformed B–LM–DMX suspension at a concentration of 1 mg mL− 1, followed by gentle vortexing for 30 s and incubation at 4°C for 30 min with periodic gentle mixing to facilitate antibody insertion into the biomimetic membrane coating. The resulting B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites were purified by ultrafiltration (100 kDa MWCO, 4000 rpm, 15 min, 4°C) to remove any unincorporated DSPE–PEG–anti‐CD25 conjugate prior to use. The resulting B–LM–DMX–αCD25 nanocomposites were used immediately or stored at 4°C protected from light for up to 3 days.

Preparation of ICG‐Labeled Nanocomposites for Cellular Uptake and Biodistribution Studies

For fluorescence‐based tracking experiments, indocyanine green (ICG) was incorporated into the nanocomposites. ICG–B–LM was prepared by adding 0.2 mg of ICG to 1 mL of the synthesized B–LM suspension, followed by vortexing for 1 min in the dark to prevent photobleaching. ICG–DSPE–PEG–LM was prepared following the same method using DSPE–PEG–LM suspensions. For in vivo biodistribution studies, highly concentrated ICG–B–LM suspensions were obtained by proportionally scaling up the amounts of B–LM and ICG while maintaining the mass ratio. All ICG‐labeled formulations were prepared fresh before use and protected from light throughout the experiments.

Physicochemical Characterization

The morphology and core–shell structure of nanocomposites were examined using high‐resolution TEM (Model JEM‐2010; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Samples were prepared by depositing 5 µL of diluted nanoparticle suspension (50 µg mL− 1 LM) onto carbon‐coated copper grids (200 mesh) and allowing air‐drying at room temperature. Excess liquid was removed by gentle blotting with filter paper. TEM analyses were performed at Hanaichi UltraStructure Research Institute Co., Ltd. (Aichi, Japan). To statistically validate the morphological characterization, the liquid metal core diameter was quantified by measuring n ≥ 100 individual nanoparticles from multiple TEM fields using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The mean core diameter was determined to be 82.3 ± 11.4 nm, consistent with the representative TEM image shown in Figure 2B and confirming the uniformity of the core–shell architecture.

Hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) equipped with a 633 nm He–Ne laser. Measurements were performed at 25°C in PBS buffer at a scattering angle of 173° (backscattering detection). Each sample was measured in triplicate, and data represent the average of at least three independent measurements. Size distributions are reported as intensity‐weighted mean diameter with polydispersity index (PDI).

Optical absorption spectra were recorded using a UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Model V‐730 BIO; Jasco Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) over the wavelength range of 300–900 nm. Samples were diluted appropriately in PBS and measured in quartz cuvettes with 1 cm path length. Baseline correction was performed using PBS as reference.

Fluorescence emission spectra of ICG‐labeled nanocomposites were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Model FP‐6500; Jasco Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were excited at 750 nm, and emission was recorded from 770 to 850 nm with a 5 nm slit width for both excitation and emission.

Cell Culture

Murine breast cancer cell line 4T1 (RRID:CVCL_0125; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA; Cat. No. CRL‐2539) and drug‐resistant murine mammary tumor cell line EMT6/AR1 (RRID:CVCL_1924; KAC Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; Cat. No. 96042327), murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 (RRID:CVCL_0493; KAC Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; Cat. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling performed by the suppliers prior to purchase. Mycoplasma contamination testing was performed monthly using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland; Cat. 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 2 mM L‐glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, gentamicin (50 µg mL− 1), and penicillin–streptomycin (100 IU mL− 1). Real‐Time Microscopic Observation of Laser‐Induced Cell Destruction

To directly visualize photothermal ablation at the cellular level, EMT‐6/AR1 cells were seeded in glass‐bottom culture dishes (35 mm diameter; Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) at 1 × 106 cells mL− 1 and cultured overnight. Cells were then incubated with ICG–B–LM (LM concentration: 300 µg mL− 1) for 12 h, washed three times with PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles, and fresh medium was added.

A custom‐built laser microscopy system was employed for real‐time observation. An 808 nm continuous‐wave NIR diode laser (564 mW output power) was coupled to an inverted microscope (IX73; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and focused through a 40× objective lens (NA = 0.95, UPLSAPO40X; Olympus) onto the cell monolayer, producing an irradiation spot approximately 50 µm in diameter (∼287 mW mm− 2 power density). Laser exposure was performed for 4 s at room temperature. Time‐lapse bright‐field images before, during, and after laser irradiation were captured using an electron‐multiplying charge‐coupled device (EMCCD) camera (DP80; Olympus) at 10 frames per second to document cellular morphological changes including bubble formation, membrane blebbing, and cell lysis.

To complement bright‐field real‐time observation with fluorescence‐based cell viability assessment, propidium iodide (PI; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) staining was performed. PI (final concentration: 1 µg mL− 1) was added to the culture medium immediately prior to laser irradiation. For the positive control group (B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + 808 nm laser), time‐lapse fluorescence imaging was performed simultaneously with bright‐field observation during laser irradiation, capturing progressive PI uptake (red fluorescence, excitation: 535 nm, emission: 617 nm) in cells with compromised membrane integrity. For the negative control group (no nanoparticles + 808 nm laser), static fluorescence images were acquired before and after laser irradiation to confirm the absence of PI uptake. Fluorescence images were captured using the same EMCCD camera system under identical acquisition parameters.

Cellular Uptake and Intracellular Distribution Studies

EMT‐6/AR1 tumor cells or RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in glass‐bottom dishes (Matsunami Glass) at 1 × 106 cells mL− 1 and incubated overnight. Cells were then co‐cultured with ICG–B–LM or ICG–DSPE–LM nanocomposites (ICG concentration: 12.5 µg mL− 1; LM concentration: 300 µg mL− 1) for 12 h (EMT‐6/AR1 cells) or 3 h (RAW 264.7 macrophages) at 37°C in complete medium. PBS‐treated cells served as negative controls. The 12 h incubation timepoint for drug‐resistant EMT‐6/AR1 cancer cells was selected based on the therapeutic protocol, in which laser irradiation is performed at 24 h post‐injection in vivo. In adherence to this timeline, a 12 h incubation was chosen as a midpoint, allowing sufficient time for receptor‐mediated endocytosis and intracellular trafficking to reach completion, while minimizing confounding effects such as nanoparticle exocytosis and cell division associated with extended incubation periods.

Following incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove non‐internalized nanoparticles, and live‐cell imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope (BZ‐X800; Keyence Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). ICG fluorescence was visualized using the CH3 filter channel (excitation: 633 nm, emission: 650–750 nm), and differential interference contrast (DIC) images were acquired simultaneously under identical fields of view. Images were captured using consistent exposure parameters across all samples.

For quantitative analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) encompassing individual cells (n ≥ 30 cells per condition) were manually delineated using Keyence BZ‐X800 Analyzer software, and mean fluorescence intensity per cell was calculated. Background fluorescence from cell‐free regions was subtracted from all measurements. Three‐dimensional confocal z‐stack imaging was performed on selected samples using a confocal laser scanning microscope to confirm intracellular localization versus membrane surface binding (Figure S4).

To quantify cellular internalization, fluorescence intensity per cell was measured from confocal images using ImageJ software. A minimum of 30 individual cells per group were analyzed across at least three independent fields of view, and mean fluorescence intensity values were calculated for statistical comparison. This single‐cell quantification approach was selected over flow cytometry to preserve critical spatial information regarding intracellular versus membrane‐bound localization of nanoparticles, which cannot be distinguished by flow cytometric analysis alone. Intracellular localization was further confirmed by z‐stack 3D reconstruction of confocal images (Figure S4), providing unequivocal volumetric evidence of cytoplasmic distribution.

Animal Models and Ethical Statement

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (JAIST) (Approval No. 06–003) and followed the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. Female BALB/c mice (5–7 weeks old, average body weight 15–20 g) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan) and housed in specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, constant temperature (22 ± 2°C) and humidity (50 ± 10%), and ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and sterile water. Orthotopic Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer Model

Orthotopic TNBC models were established by mammary fat pad inoculation to recapitulate the natural tumor microenvironment. Briefly, 4T1 cells were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of ice‐cold Matrigel (Corning, Inc., Corning, NY, USA) and PBS at a concentration of 1.7 × 107 cells mL− 1. Under isoflurane anesthesia (3% for induction, 1.5% for maintenance), the surgical area was sterilized with 70% ethanol followed by povidone‐iodine solution. A small incision (∼5 mm) was made in the skin overlying the left fourth mammary fat pad, and 100 µL of cell suspension (1.7 × 106 cells) was injected into the fat pad using a 27‐gauge needle. The incision was closed with surgical adhesive, and mice received subcutaneous buprenorphine (0.05 mg kg− 1) for post‐operative analgesia.

In Vivo Biodistribution and Tumor Targeting Studies

To evaluate tumor‐targeting efficiency and biodistribution kinetics, orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing mice (tumor volume ∼80 mm3) were intravenously administered via lateral tail vein with 200 µL of PBS (control), ICG–DSPE–LM (ICG: 2 mg mL− 1, ∼20 mg kg− 1; LM: 3 mg mL− 1, ∼30 mg kg− 1), or ICG–B–LM (same concentrations) under brief isoflurane anesthesia. At predetermined time points (0, 5, 10, 17, and 24 h post‐injection), mice were anesthetized and subjected to whole‐body near‐infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging using an in vivo imaging system (VISQUE InVivo Smart‐LF; Vieworks Co., Ltd., Anyang, Republic of Korea) equipped with an ICG‐specific filter set (excitation: 740–790 nm, emission: 810–860 nm). Fluorescence images were acquired with a 3 s exposure time and analyzed using CleVue software (Vieworks) with identical acquisition parameters and unified pseudocolor scales applied uniformly across all groups and time points to enable direct quantitative comparison. Background fluorescence was subtracted using signals from organs harvested from PBS‐treated control mice. Biodistribution imaging timepoints (0, 5, 10, 17, and 24 h post‐injection) were selected to capture the key pharmacokinetic phases: immediate post‐injection distribution (0 h), early circulation (5 h), and the tumor accumulation phase (10–24 h), which corresponds to the therapeutic window for laser irradiation. This temporal resolution was considered sufficient to characterize the transition from systemic distribution to stable tumor retention, consistent with published biodistribution studies in the nanomedicine literature.

At 24 h post‐injection, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Major organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys) and tumors were surgically excised, rinsed in PBS, placed on a black background, and imaged ex vivo under identical NIRF imaging conditions to quantitatively assess biodistribution. Region‐of‐interest (ROI) analysis was performed to determine mean fluorescence intensity in each organ, and tumor‐to‐organ fluorescence ratios were calculated to assess targeting specificity.

In Vivo Photothermal Performance

To evaluate photothermal heating capacity in vivo, orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing mice (tumor volume ∼100 mm3) were intravenously administered 200 µL of PBS, DSPE–LM (LM: 3 mg mL− 1, ∼30 mg kg− 1), free DMX (1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1), free anti‐CD25 antibody (1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1), or B–LM–DMX–αCD25 (LM: 3 mg mL− 1, ∼30 mg kg− 1; DMX: 1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1; anti‐CD25: 1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1). At 24 h post‐injection—selected based on biodistribution studies showing optimal tumor accumulation—mice were anesthetized, and tumor regions were irradiated with 808 nm NIR laser (1.0 W, ∼51 mW mm− 2) for 5 min while monitoring surface temperature in real‐time using an infrared thermal imaging camera (i7; FLIR Systems). Temperature readings were recorded at 30 s intervals, and thermal images were captured at 1 min intervals throughout the irradiation period.

In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy in Orthotopic TNBC Model

Orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing mice with established tumors (∼100 mm3) were randomly divided into 10 groups (n = 4 per group): (1) PBS without laser (PBS), (2) PBS with laser (PBS + L), (3) DSPE–LM without laser (DSPE–LM), (4) DSPE–LM with laser (DSPE–LM + L), (5) DSPE–DMX without laser, (6) DSPE–DMX with laser (DSPE–DMX + L), (7) DSPE‐anti‐CD25 without laser, (8) DSPE–anti‐CD25 with laser (DSPE–αCD25 + L), (9) B–LM–DMX–αCD25 without laser, and (10) B–LM–DMX–αCD25 with laser (B–LM–DMX–αCD25 + L).

Mice received intravenous injections of 200 µL of respective formulations via tail vein every other day for a total of four treatment cycles. For laser‐treated groups, tumors were irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser (1.0 W, ∼51 mW mm− 2, 5 min per session) at 24 h post‐injection. Tumor dimensions and body weight were recorded every 2 days. Mice were euthanized when tumor volume exceeded 1800 mm3 or if severe morbidity occurred, in accordance with IACUC‐approved humane endpoints.

Representative tumor photographs were taken on days 1, 7, and 15 post‐treatment initiation using a digital camera under standardized lighting and positioning to document therapeutic response visually.

Lung Metastasis Model and Anti‐Metastatic Efficacy Evaluation

To assess the ability of B–LM–DMX–αCD25 to suppress distant metastasis, an experimental pulmonary metastasis model was established following primary tumor treatment. After completion of one full treatment cycle (4 injections over 7 days) in the orthotopic tumor model, mice received an intravenous injection of 2 × 105 4T1 cells in 100 µL PBS via tail vein to induce pulmonary metastasis. Treatment was continued with two additional cycles (8 more injections over 14 days) following the same schedule, with laser irradiation performed 24 h after each injection for laser‐treated groups.

At study termination (day 24 post‐initial treatment), mice were euthanized, and lungs were carefully excised, rinsed in PBS, and examined for metastatic nodules. Visible surface nodules were manually counted by two independent investigators blinded to treatment groups, and lungs were photographed against a black background using standardized positioning. Lung weights were recorded as a surrogate marker of metastatic tumor burden.

For histopathological analysis, lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4°C, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 µm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Multiple sections spanning different lung regions (at least 5 sections per lung, 200 µm apart) were examined microscopically to assess extent of tumor infiltration and preservation of normal alveolar architecture.

Survival Analysis

For long‐term survival studies, a separate cohort of orthotopic tumor‐bearing mice (n = 6–8 per group) received the same treatment regimens described above followed by metastatic challenge, and overall survival was monitored daily for up to 80 days. Mice were euthanized when they met predefined humane endpoints including tumor volume >1800 mm3, body weight loss >20%, hunched posture, labored breathing, or severe lethargy. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and statistical comparisons among groups were performed using the log‐rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analyses

For mechanistic studies, orthotopic 4T1 tumor‐bearing female BALB/c mice (approximately 7 weeks old, n = 4 per group, average body weight ∼18 g, average tumor volume ∼100 mm3) received a single dose of respective formulations followed by laser irradiation as described above. At 24 h post‐treatment, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were surgically excised and immediately fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin for 24–48 h at room temperature. Major organs (heart, liver, kidneys, spleen, lungs) were also collected and processed identically to evaluate potential systemic toxicity.

Fixed tissues were processed through a graded ethanol dehydration series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, 100% × 2), cleared in xylene, infiltrated with paraffin at 60°C, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Serial sections (3–4 µm thickness) were cut using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2255; Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and mounted on positively charged glass slides.

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 5 min), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%, 70%; 3 min each), and rinsed in distilled water. Sections were stained with Mayer's hematoxylin solution for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, differentiated briefly in 1% acid alcohol, blued in ammonia water, rinsed, and counterstained with eosin Y solution for 2 min. After dehydration through graded ethanol and clearing in xylene, sections were mounted with Permount mounting medium and coverslipped. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (IX73; Olympus) at 100× and 400× magnification.

IHC analyses were performed by Biopathology Institute Co., Ltd. (Oita, Japan) following standardized protocols. Briefly, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections underwent heat‐induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) depending on the antibody, using a pressure cooker for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% normal goat serum or bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table S4) diluted in blocking buffer.

Following primary antibody incubation, sections were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween‐20 (PBST) for 5 min each, then incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Dako/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3'‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Dako) for 3–5 min under microscopic monitoring. Sections were counterstained lightly with Mayer's hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

Apoptotic cells were detected using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, POD (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were treated with proteinase K (20 µg mL− 1) for 15 min at 37°C, washed, and incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) and fluorescein‐labeled dUTP for 60 min at 37°C in a humidified chamber. After washing, sections were incubated with anti‐fluorescein antibody conjugated with HRP for 30 min at 37°C, developed with DAB substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, and mounted.

All stained sections were digitally scanned using a high‐resolution slide scanner (NanoZoomer; Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan) or imaged using a fluorescence microscope (BZ‐X800; Keyence). For quantification, 10 random high‐power fields (400× magnification) per tumor section were captured, and positive staining was quantified using Keyence BZ‐X800 Analyzer software with Hybrid Cell Count module or ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) with color deconvolution plugin for DAB‐positive area quantification. Results are expressed as percentage of positive cells or positive staining area per total tissue area. All analyses were performed by investigators blinded to treatment groups.

Hematological and Biochemical Safety Assessment

To evaluate potential systemic toxicity, female BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, n = 6 per group, average body weight ∼18 g) received a single intravenous injection of 200 µL PBS (control) or B–LM–DMX–αCD25 (LM: 3 mg mL− 1, ∼30 mg kg− 1; DMX: 1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1; anti‐CD25: 1 mg mL− 1, ∼10 mg kg− 1) via tail vein. At 7 days post‐injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and blood samples (∼500 µL) were collected via inferior vena cava puncture into EDTA‐coated tubes (for complete blood count) or serum separator tubes (for biochemical analyses).

Whole blood samples were analyzed within 2 h of collection using an automated hematology analyzer (Microsemi LC‐712; Horiba Medical, Kyoto, Japan) to determine red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), white blood cell count (WBC), differential leukocyte count (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils), and platelet count (PLT).

Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and serum was collected. Biochemical parameters were analyzed by Japan SLC, Inc. and Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) using standard clinical chemistry methods. Parameters evaluated included: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (TBIL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CRE), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), glucose (GLU), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), and electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl−).

Quantitative RT‐PCR Analysis of Immune Responses

To characterize the molecular mechanisms underlying B–LM–DMX–αCD25‐mediated antitumor immunity, comprehensive quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‐PCR) analysis was performed to assess transcriptional profiles of STING pathway activation, immunosuppression relief, and immune effector cell recruitment within tumor tissues. Female BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors (approximately 200 mm3) were administered nanoparticle dispersion (200 µL) or PBS control via intravenous injection.

At 24 h post‐treatment—a timepoint selected to capture early innate immune activation and STING pathway engagement prior to peak adaptive immune responses—mice were humanely euthanized, and tumor tissues were immediately harvested and flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA integrity. Total RNA extraction was performed using established protocols with handheld homogenizers (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) followed by column‐based purification methods to ensure high‐quality RNA suitable for quantitative analysis. RNA purity and integrity were confirmed by A260/A280 ratios (≥1.8) and agarose gel electrophoresis prior to cDNA synthesis.

qRT‐PCR analysis was conducted using a QuantStudio 1 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with commercially available gene‐specific TaqMan Gene Expression Assay primer‐probe sets (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Table S5). Target genes were selected to comprehensively profile the three core components of the proposed tripartite therapeutic mechanism: (1) STING pathway activation: Tmem173 (encoding STING protein; Assay ID: Mm01158117_m1), Ifnb1 (interferon‐β, direct IRF3 transcriptional target; Mm00439552_s1), and Cxcl10 (IP‐10, interferon‐stimulated chemokine; Mm00445235_m1); (2) Treg‐mediated immunosuppression relief: Foxp3 (Treg master transcription factor; Mm00475162_m1) and Il10 (Treg immunosuppressive cytokine; Mm01288386_m1); (3) Immune effector cell activation: Cd3d (pan T‐cell marker; Mm00442746_m1), Cd8a (cytotoxic T‐cell marker; Mm01182107_g1), Ifng (IFN‐γ, T‐cell activation marker; Mm01168134_m1), Gzmb (Granzyme B, cytotoxic effector molecule; Mm00442837_m1), Tnf (TNF‐α, pro‐inflammatory cytokine; Mm00443258_m1), Ncr1 (NKp46, NK cell marker; Mm01337324_g1), Cd19 (B‐cell marker; Mm00515420_m1), and Hmgb1 (ICD marker; Mm00849805_gH). Actb (β‐actin; Mm02619580_g1) served as the endogenous reference gene for normalization, measured using TaqMan Array Mouse Endogenous Control plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

All reactions were performed in triplicate (n = 3 biologically independent tumors per group) to ensure statistical reliability, with thermal cycling conditions optimized for TaqMan chemistry: 50°C for 2 min (AmpErase UNG activation), 95°C for 2 min (AmpliTaq Gold DNA polymerase activation), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 1 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 20 s. PCR efficiency validation was performed using 10‐fold serial dilutions, achieving efficiencies of 90–100% for all assays. Gene expression data were analyzed using the comparative ΔΔCt method and are presented as fold‐change (log10 relative quantification, Log10RQ) relative to PBS control groups. Statistical significance was assessed by one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test; p < 0.001 for all reported comparisons.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times unless otherwise specified. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in individual figure legends. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using two‐tailed Student's t‐test. Multiple group comparisons were conducted using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons, which corrects for type I error inflation due to multiple testing. Survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log‐rank (Mantel–Cox) test for group comparisons. Exact p‐values are reported throughout the manuscript wherever possible; values below 0.0001 are reported as p < 0.0001. Effect magnitudes are expressed as fold‐changes and percentage differences throughout the Results text to provide biologically interpretable measures of effect size. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.4.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Sample sizes (n) for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends.
