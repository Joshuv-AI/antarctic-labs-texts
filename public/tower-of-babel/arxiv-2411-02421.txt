Near-Optimal Quantum Algorithm for Finding the Longest Common Substring between Run-Length Encoded Strings
Tzu-Ching Lee, Han-Hsuan Lin

Abstract
We give a near-optimal quantum algorithm for the longest common substring (LCS) problem between two run-length encoded (RLE) strings, with the assumption that the prefix-sums of the run-lengths are given. Our algorithm costs 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 2 / 3 / d 1 / 6 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) ⋅ polylog ⁡ ( n ~ ) ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}{}}(n^{2/3}/d^{1/6-o(1)}\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(\tilde{n})) time, while the query lower bound for the problem is Ω ~ ​ ( n 2 / 3 / d 1 / 6 ) \tilde{\Omega{}}(n^{2/3}/d^{1/6}) , where n n and n ~ \tilde{n} are the encoded and decoded length of the inputs, respectively, and d d is the encoded length of the LCS. We justify the use of prefix-sum oracles for two reasons. First, we note that creating the prefix-sum oracle only incurs a constant overhead in the RLE compression. Second, we show that, without the oracles, there is a Ω ⁡ ( n / log 2 ​ n ) \Omega(n/\log^{2}n) lower bound on the quantum query complexity of finding the LCS given two RLE strings due to a reduction of 𝖯𝖠𝖱𝖨𝖳𝖸 \mathsf{PARITY} to the problem. With a small modification, our algorithm also solves the longest repeated substring problem for an RLE string.

1.2
Overview of the algorithm

Our algorithm is built on and modified from the LCS with threshold algorithm of
[
22
]
, which decides whether a common substring of length at least
d
exists in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}{}}(n^{2/3}/d^{1/6-o(1)})
.

As stated in the introduction, the main obstacle we face is the difference between encoded length and decoded length. To overcome this difficulty, we perform “binary searches” on both encoded length and decoded length with a nested search. The outer loop is a binary search on the decoded length of the answer
\tilde{d}\in[\tilde{n}]
. In each iteration of this binary search, we check whether a common substring of decode length at least
\tilde{d}
exists. The inner loop searches over encoded length
d=n/2,n/4,n/8,\dots
. In each iteration of the inner loop, we check whether a common substring with encoded length in
[d,2d]
and decoded length at least
\tilde{d}
exists. Running these two loops gives us an
\mathcal{O}(\log(n)\log(\tn))=\tilde{\mathcal{O}{}}(1)
overhead.

Note that there is a subtle issue in our inner loop search: unlike the original LCS problem, where having a common substring of length
d
guarantees that there is a common substring of length
d-1
, in our LCS between RLE problem, there might be no common substring of encoded length
d-1
and decoded length
\tilde{d}
, even though there is a common substring of encoded length
d
and decoded length
\tilde{d}
. Therefore, in our inner-loop search, we need to search over every possible encoded string length. We accomplish this by modifying the algorithms of
[
22
]
so that instead of just checking
d
, it will check the range
[d,2d]
and loops over
d=n/2,n/4,n/8,\dots
.

Similarly to
[
22
]
, in each iteration of our inner loop search, we run Ambainis’ element distinctness algorithm
[
2
]
on an anchor set of
A\$B
, where
A
and
B
are the input RLE strings of length
n
. Roughly speaking, a
d
-anchor set of a concatenated string
A\$B
is a subset of
A\$B
such that if a common substring of length
d
,
A[i_{1}:i_{1}+d-1]=B[i_{2}:i_{2}+d-1]
, exists, the respective copies of the common substring in
A
and
B
will be “anchored” at the same positions, meaning that there exists a shift
0\leq h\leq d
such that both
i_{1}+h
and
n+1+i_{2}+h
are in the anchor set. The
d
-anchored set has size roughly
n/d
. In each iteration of our inner loop search, we run a quantum walk on the elements of the
d
anchored set and check for a “collision”: a pair of anchored positions in
A
and
B
that can be extended backward and forward into a pair of common substrings with
encoded length in
[d,2d]
and decoded length at least
\tilde{d}
and elements of it can be computed in time
\sqrt{d}
by the construction in
[
22
]
. To check both conditions in the encoded length and decoded length, we have a delicate checking procedure: to check everything with encoded length
[d,2d]
, we search over all possible shifts of the anchor in
encoded
length;
To efficiently check for the decoded length, we store data about the lexicographically sorted
decoded
prefixes and suffixes of the stored anchors in the data structure.

r^{Q}
, we check whether the range
[\rho^{P}(k_{l^{P}})+1\ldots\rho^{P}(k_{r^{P}})-1]\times[\rho^{Q}(k_{l^{Q}})+1\ldots\rho^{Q}(k_{r^{Q}})-1]
is non-zero in the dynamic 2D range sum data structure.
If so, such a
k_{j^{\prime}}
exists.
Otherwise, we check at most
O(\log m)
blue
k
with
\rho^{P}(k)\in\{\rho^{P}(k_{l^{P}}),\rho^{P}(k_{r^{P}})\}
or
\rho^{Q}(k)\in\{\rho^{Q}(k_{l^{Q}}),\rho^{Q}(k_{r^{Q}})\}
explicitly whether the lexicographical ranking of its prefix and suffix are in
[l^{P},r^{P}]
and in
[l^{Q},r^{Q}]
, respectively.
The insertion and deletion to the 2D range sum data structure can be done in
\tilde{\mathcal{O}{}}(1)
time.
The checking algorithm uses
\tilde{\mathcal{O}{}}(1)
time and has
1/poly(m)
one-sided error.

∎

Remark 2
.

By dropping the red-blue constraint in the checking step, and receiving only one input string
S
of encoded length
n

theorem

3
to solve the Longest Repeated Substring problem.

4
Lower Bounds

In this section, we first show a query lower bound for
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}^{\mathsf{p}}
.
We then investigate the time complexity lower bound for calculating the length (encoded and decoded) of longest common substring from two RLE strings without access to prefix-sum oracles. And use the results to show the lower bound of finding an LCS from two RLE strings is
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n)
, which is our motivation and justification to introduce the prefix-sum oracle.

4.1
Lower Bound on
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}^{\mathsf{p}}

Lemma 7

(Lower Bound of
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}^{\mathsf{p}}
)
.

Any quantum oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}
requires at least
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n^{2/3}/d^{1/6})
queries to solve
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}^{\mathsf{p}}
, with probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

Note that for every string
A=a_{1}a_{2},\dots
, we can insert a special character
@
to create a string
A_{@}=a_{1}@a_{2}@\dots
, so that
A_{@}
cannot be RLE compressed. Also note that the length of LCS between any pair
(A_{@},B_{@})
is exactly twice the length of LCS between
(A,B)
, so any lower bound on LCS is also a lower bound on
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}^{\mathsf{p}}
. Prefix-sum oracle makes no difference since it can be calculated by multiplying the index of the runs by 2. Therefore, this lower bound follows Theorem 1.2 of
[
22
]
.
∎

4.2
Lower Bound on
\mathsf{DL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}

In this section we show how to reduce
\mathsf{PARITY}
to
\mathsf{DL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
, obtaining the following result.

Lemma 8

(Lower Bound of
\mathsf{DL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
)
.

Any quantum oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}
requires at least
\Omega(n)
queries to solve
\mathsf{DL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
, with probability at least
2/3
.

The main idea is to encode an
n
-bit binary string
B=B_{1}B_{2}\ldots B_{n}
as an RLE string
S_{B}
,
in which
R(S_{B}[i])=2+B_{i}
.
Then, using
\mathcal{A}
, we find the length of LCS of
S_{B}
with itself.
Here, what
\mathcal{A}
outputs is basically the decode length of
S_{B}
, i.e.
|\widetilde{S_{B}}|
.
From that, we can calculate the parity of
B
easily,
and thus
lemma

8
is proven.

Proof.

Given an
n
-bit binary string
B
, we can construct an RLE string
S_{B}
as:

S_{B}=\texttt{a}^{B_{1}+2}\texttt{b}^{B_{2}+2}\texttt{a}^{B_{3}+2}\texttt{b}^{B_{4}+2}\cdots\gamma^{B_{n}+2},

(6)

where
\gamma
is
a
if
n
is odd, otherwise it is
b
.

Then we assume the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
exists.
With
S_{B}
as the inputs, the output of
\mathcal{A}
, the decoded length of LCS between
S_{B}
and itself, is

\mathcal{A}(S_{B},S_{B})=|\tilde{S}_{B}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(B_{i}+2)=2n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}Bi,

(7)

which has the same parity as
\bigoplus_{i\in[n]}B_{i}
, i.e. the parity of
B
.
Therefore, by checking the lowest bit of
\mathcal{A}(S_{B},S_{B})
, we can solve
\mathsf{PARITY}
with no extra query.
Since solving
\mathsf{PARITY}
requires
\Omega(n)
queries, solving
\mathsf{DL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
needs at least the same number of queries.
∎

Appendix A
Lower Bounds on
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
and
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}

In this section, we show a
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n)
lower bound on both
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
(
lemma

9
) and
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}
(
corollary

1
).
More precisely, we reduce
\mathsf{PARITY}
to
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
, which is then reduced to
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}
.

Here is a high-level overview of the first reduction (from
\mathsf{PARITY}
to
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
).
We encode an
n
-bit binary string
B=B_{1}B_{2}\cdots B_{n}
into an RLE string
S_{B}
,
in a way similar to
eq.

6
in the proof of
lemma

8
.
We then assume an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
of query complexity
Q(\mathcal{A})
for
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
exists.
Using
\mathcal{A}
, we construct an algorithm to compare the decoded length of
S_{B}
, i.e.
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, with any
k>0
.
We then use binary search on
k
to find
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, invoking
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
times.
From
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, we calculate the parity of
B
without extra query.
Finally, since
\mathsf{PARITY}
has query lower bound
\Omega(n)
,
Q(\mathcal{A})
is at least
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n)
, getting
lemma

9
below.

Lemma 9

(Lower Bound of
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
)
.

Any quantum oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}
requires at least
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n)
queries to solve
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
, with probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

Given an
n
-bit binary string
B=B_{1}B_{2}B_{3}\ldots B_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
we can construct an RLE string

S_{B}=\texttt{a}^{2B_{1}+2}\texttt{b}^{2B_{2}+2}\texttt{a}^{2B_{3}+2}\texttt{b}^{2B_{4}+2}\ldots\gamma^{2B_{n}+2},

(8)

where
\gamma
is
a
if
n
is odd, otherwise it is
b
.

For every positive natural number
k
, we can also construct an RLE string, simply by repeating another character:
S_{k}=\texttt{c}^{k}
.
We then concatenate
S_{B}
and
S_{k}
together with different characters in the middle, getting

S_{B,\texttt{@},{k}}:=S_{B}\texttt{@}^{1}S_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{B,\texttt{\#},{k}}:=S_{B}\texttt{\#}^{1}S_{k}.

(9)

Let us check what we know about the LCS
\tilde{s}
between
\widetilde{S}_{B,\texttt{@},{k}}
and
\widetilde{S}_{B,\texttt{\#},{k}}
.
Firstly,
@
and
#
are not in
\tilde{s}
since none of them appears in
\widetilde{S}_{B,\texttt{@},{k}}
and
\widetilde{S}_{B,\texttt{\#},{k}}
at the same time.
Secondly,
\tilde{s}
is a substring of
\tilde{S}_{B}
or
\tilde{S}_{k}
, but not both,
because the character set of
\tilde{S}_{B}
,
\{\texttt{a},\texttt{b}\}
, and the one of
\tilde{S}_{k}
,
\{\texttt{c}\}
, do not intersect.
Finally,
\tilde{s}
is the “longest” common substring, so it is the longest one among
\tilde{S}_{B}
and
\tilde{S}_{k}
.

Now we assume the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
in
lemma

9
exists,
and it has query complexity
Q(\mathcal{A})
.

Additionally, the success probability of
\mathcal{A}
can be boosted from constant to high probability with an extra logarithmic factor on its query complexity (
lemma

5
).

With
S_{B,\texttt{@},{k}}
and
S_{B,\texttt{\#},{k}}
as inputs,
\mathcal{A}
outputs

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(S_{B,\texttt{@},{k}},S_{B,\texttt{\#},{k}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}|S_{k}|,&|\tilde{S}_{k}|>|\tilde{S}_{B}|\\
|S_{B}|\text{ or }|S_{k}|,&|\tilde{S}_{k}|=|\tilde{S}_{B}|\\
|S_{B}|,&|\tilde{S}_{k}|<|\tilde{S}_{B}|\end{cases}

(10)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&k>|\tilde{S}_{B}|\\
n\text{ or }1,&k=|\tilde{S}_{B}|\\
n,&k<|\tilde{S}_{B}|\end{cases}.

(11)

For a given
B
, we use
\mathcal{A}_{B}({}\cdot{})
as a shorthand for
\mathcal{A}(S_{B,\texttt{@},{\,\cdot\ }},S_{B,\texttt{\#},{\,\cdot\ }})
in the following text.
Note that when
k=|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, two answers (
n
and
1
) are possible,
and we only assume
\mathcal{A}
outputs one of them.
Thus,
\mathcal{A}_{B}(k)
is non-deterministic when
k=|\tilde{S}_{B}|
.
We will resolve this issue with a property of binary search later.

To find
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, we do a binary search on
k
to find a
k^{\prime}\in[2n,4n]
, such that
\mathcal{A}_{B}(k^{\prime}-1)=n
and
\mathcal{A}_{B}(k^{\prime})=1
.
8
8

8

The search range
[2n,4n]
comes from
2n\leq|\tilde{S}_{B}|=\sum_{i}(2B_{i}+2)\leq 4n
.

In the binary search,
\mathcal{A}_{B}
will not be called with the same
k
twice so it does not matter whether
\mathcal{A}_{B}
and the underlying
\mathcal{A}
are deterministic or not.
So from now on, we treat
\mathcal{A}_{B}
as if it were deterministic.

Since there are two possible outputs for
\mathcal{A}_{B}(k)
when
k=|\tilde{S}_{B}|

(the middle case in
eq.

11
),
each corresponds to a different result
k^{\prime}
for the binary search.
If
\mathcal{A}_{B}(|\tilde{S}_{B}|)
outputs
1
, we will get
k^{\prime}=|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, the desired result.
But if
\mathcal{A}_{B}(|\tilde{S}_{B}|)
outputs
n
, we will get
k^{\prime}=|\tilde{S}_{B}|+1
instead.
We can detect if the latter one is the case from the parity of
k^{\prime}
because
|\tilde{S}_{B}|=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(B_{i}+1)
is always even, and thus we can correct the result accordingly.

With
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
in hand, we then check if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2B_{i}+2\right)-n=\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{S}_{B}|-n

(12)

is odd or even to determine the parity of
B
.

Alternatively, we can XOR the lowest bit of
n
with the second-lowest bit of
k^{\prime}
, which is the same as the one of
|\tilde{S}_{B}|
, directly.
Then the result is the parity of
B
.
This allows us to avoid correcting
k^{\prime}
explicitly.

In total, we use
Q(\mathcal{A})\log^{2}n
queries to solve
\mathsf{PARITY}
.
The logarithmic factors come from boosting
\mathcal{A}
to high probability and the binary search.
Finally, solving
\mathsf{PARITY}
requires
\Omega(n)
queries so we have

Q(\mathcal{A})\log^{2}n\in\Omega(n)\implies Q(\mathcal{A})\in\Omega(n/\log^{2}n)\in\tilde{\Omega{}}(n),

(13)

and
lemma

9
follows.
∎

Furthermore, an algorithm solving
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}
outputs a triplet
(i_{A},i_{B},\ell)
, where

is the encoded length of LCS between the inputs, which is also the answer to
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}
.
I.e.
\mathsf{EL\text{-}LCS\text{-}RLE}

As a result,
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}
shares the same query lower bound with

.
This gives the corollary below.

Corollary 1

(Lower Bound of
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}
)
.

Any quantum oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}
requires at least
\tilde{\Omega{}}(n)
queries to solve
\mathsf{LCS\text{-}RLE}

.

corollary
