Reduced-crosstalk antennas for grating-lobe-free and wide-field-of-view integrated optical phased arrays
Authors: Henry Crawford-Eng, Andres Garcia Coleto, Benjamin M. Mazur, Daniel M. DeSantis, Tal Sneh, Jelena Notaros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrated optical phased arrays (OPAs) have emerged as a promising technology for many applications due to their ability to dynamically control free-space optical beams in a compact and non-mechanical manner. However, these integrated OPAs typically have a restricted field of view (FOV), limited by grating lobes caused by large antenna pitches that are typically necessary to reduce crosstalk between the antennas in the integrated OPA. In this work, we develop and experimentally demonstrate for the first time, to the best of our knowledge, a set of integrated grating-based antennas with significantly-reduced inter-antenna crosstalk that enable half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPAs with grating-lobe-free and wide-FOV functionality. First, we derive a generalized theoretical model to describe the coupling dynamics between lossy modes in a system and use this model to analyze the coupling between antennas. Next, we design and demonstrate a set of three integrated grating-based antennas with different propagation coefficients to enable reduced inter-antenna crosstalk, successfully measuring a significant reduction from 100% to 1% coupling. Finally, using these reduced-crosstalk antennas, we develop and demonstrate a half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPA, successfully demonstrating grating-lobe-free and wide-FOV functionality. This work facilitates new functionality for high-performance integrated OPAs.

Introduction

Integrated optical phased arrays (OPAs) enable manipulation and dynamic control of free-space optical beams in a compact form factor, at low costs, and in a non-mechanical way. As such, integrated OPAs have emerged as a promising technology for a variety of applications, including light-detection-and-ranging (LiDAR) sensors1–4, free-space optical data communications2,5, image projection and displays6,7, atomic quantum systems8, optical trapping9, optogenetics10, and 3D printing11.

In general, an integrated OPA consists of a photonic integrated circuit that distributes a coherent light source to a set of waveguide arms, encodes a phase signal onto each waveguide arm using integrated electro-optic modulators, and emits the phase front out of the surface of the chip using an array of integrated grating-based antennas. Typically, these antennas are spaced with a large antenna pitch, which leads to undesired grating lobes that restrict the field of view (FOV) and decrease the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the system. These grating lobes can be removed by placing the antennas at a half-wavelength pitch; however, tight antenna pitches result in strong evanescent coupling between antennas, which leads to inter-element crosstalk that degrades the quality of the emitted optical beam.

Prior works have used various methods to remove these grating lobes and increase the FOV of integrated OPAs. For example, integrated OPAs with aperiodic antenna spacings have been used to enable grating-lobe-free emission by creating a single optical beam by sampling a variety of spatial frequencies12–17; however, these aperiodic antenna spacings do not fully illuminate the aperture, redistributing the power in the main beam to many angles in the far field, leading to a decreased SNR. Another recent work enabled grating-lobe-free emission by optimizing the amplitude and phase of each individual antenna element in a half-wavelength-pitch OPA to mitigate the effect of the inter-element crosstalk18; however, this optimization technique modulates the amplitude of the array elements, leading to decreased SNR due to non-uniform illumination of the antenna aperture. As another example, integrated OPA-adjacent systems have utilized a distribution network that leads to a single diffractive grating to enable grating-lobe-free emission19; however, this approach does not utilize a true antenna array and, as such, is unable to generate complex beam patterns with the single slab-grating aperture (for example, the input wavefronts required for focused9,20,21 or holographic6 emission interfere in the slab grating, leading to undesired emission patterns). Lastly, integrated OPAs that utilize end-fire antennas have varied the propagation coefficients of neighboring waveguides to enable half-wavelength pitches while maintaining reduced crosstalk22–25; however, these end-fire-based approaches are limited to one-dimensional arrays with end-fire antennas that have a large divergence and no wavelength dispersion, requiring additional off-chip optics to confine the spot in the second dimension and restricting the beam steering to one dimension.

In summary, while there has been exploration into techniques for enabling grating-lobe-free emission from integrated OPAs, these approaches result in undesirable effects, such as reduced SNR or restricted beam-forming capabilities. Although techniques have been conceptually proposed to mitigate these effects26, thus far, an integrated OPA with half-wavelength-pitch antennas that maintains its beam quality and SNR has not been previously shown.

In this work, we develop and experimentally demonstrate for the first time, to the best of our knowledge, a set of integrated grating-based antennas with significantly-reduced inter-antenna crosstalk that enable half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPAs with grating-lobe-free and wide-FOV functionality. First, we derive a generalized theoretical model to describe the coupling dynamics between lossy modes in a system from first-principles coupled-mode theory and use this model to analyze the coupling between antennas. Next, we design three separate integrated grating-based antenna geometries with different propagation coefficients but similar emission characteristics to enable reduced inter-antenna crosstalk while maintaining optimal beam quality. We then fabricate and experimentally characterize these reduced-crosstalk antennas, successfully measuring significantly-reduced coupling on the order of only 1% for the reduced-crosstalk antennas compared to approximately 100% coupling for standard antennas. Finally, using these reduced-crosstalk antennas, we develop, fabricate, and experimentally demonstrate a half-wavelength-pitch reduced-crosstalk integrated OPA, successfully demonstrating grating-lobe-free functionality and active beam steering across 60°. This work facilitates new functionality for integrated OPAs, enabling high-performance systems that can achieve wide FOVs while maintaining high SNRs.

Results

Theoretical framework for lossy coupled-mode systems

In this section, we develop the theoretical framework to describe the coupling dynamics between lossy modes in a system, which we use in later sections to understand the coupling behavior of our reduced-crosstalk grating-based antennas. We modify the typical coupled-mode equations that describe lossless systems to account for the unique propagation losses of each mode. We then develop an analytical solution for the generalized lossy two-mode coupling problem. Finally, we constrain the solution to the case of a single input mode that allows for accurate parameter extraction from simulated and fabricated two-antenna systems.

In general, an integrated OPA consists of a photonic integrated circuit that distributes a coherent light source to a set of waveguide arms, encodes a phase signal onto each waveguide arm using integrated electro-optic modulators, and emits the phase front out of the surface of the chip using an array of integrated grating-based antennas to generate and non-mechanically steer a beam in the far field. The antennas used in these integrated OPAs generally consist of an integrated waveguide in which periodic perturbations are introduced to scatter the light traveling along the waveguide with a specific emission pattern out of the chip27. A tighter element pitch will increase the angular spacing between grating lobes and subsequently increase the FOV (Fig. 1c), with a half-wavelength pitch completely removing grating lobes from the emission pattern and enabling full 180-degree-FOV operation for the array-factor contribution to the emission pattern (Fig. 1b). However, when the antennas approach this tight of a spacing, strong evanescent coupling occurs between the antennas, which leads to inter-element crosstalk that degrades the quality of the emitted optical beam. This crosstalk is governed by the modal overlaps as well as the propagation coefficients and propagation losses of the modes in the antennas. Prior work has modeled the crosstalk in waveguide lattices and end-fire integrated OPAs22–25,28–30, but no work thus far has created a model to describe the coupling between grating-based antennas in an integrated OPA. Specifically, these prior works utilize standard coupled-mode theory that accurately describes the behavior of lossless coupled modes; however, we cannot use this standard theoretical model to describe modes with significant propagation losses (for example, coupled antenna modes in an integrated OPA). Instead, we must develop a new lossy-coupled-mode theory that accounts for the distinct propagation losses of each mode in the system.

To model these coupling dynamics between lossy modes in a system, we start with the typical coupled mode equations that describe lossless systems and modify them to account for the propagation losses of each mode. Design of the reduced-crosstalk antennas

In this section, we design three separate integrated grating-based antennas with different propagation coefficients that enable reduced-crosstalk functionality. We simulate and select antenna geometries that reduce neighboring antenna crosstalk while maintaining similar emission characteristics, including the scattering strengths, emission angles, and wavelength dispersions.

To enable wide-FOV integrated OPAs with high SNRs, we must reduce the coupling between neighboring antenna elements in the OPA apertures. To address this, we design three different integrated grating-based antenna geometries, each with a different propagation coefficient, that can then be tiled together in an alternating pattern into a larger lattice (as shown in Fig. 2a) to reduce inter-element crosstalk across an entire antenna-array system. To enable wide FOV functionality, we design these antennas so they can be placed with a tight 775-nm pitch, which is half of the 1550-nm design wavelength and, therefore, the maximum antenna pitch that results in grating-lobe-free emission in the array dimension. To reduce the crosstalk between antennas when placed at such a tight pitch, the propagation coefficients of the antennas must be sufficiently different. We achieve this by designing a set of three separate antennas with varying geometries, corresponding to antennas with widths of 550 nm, 600 nm and 650 nm. While the three antenna geometries need to have varying propagation coefficients to reduce crosstalk (i.e., sufficiently different widths to significantly reduce crosstalk), the antennas must still share the same emission characteristics to ensure optimal beam quality and efficiency.Fig. 2Design of reduced-crosstalk antennas.a Schematic of a section of a wide-FOV integrated OPA aperture, consisting of antennas with three alternating geometries to reduce crosstalk (not to scale). b Table listing the dimensions and simulated emission characteristics at the 1550-nm design wavelength for each of the three antenna geometries. c Simulated scattering strength versus inward perturbation for the 550-nm-wide (red), 600-nm-wide (green), and 650-nm-wide (blue) antenna geometries, with the target scattering strength overlaid (dashed line). d Simulated emission angle versus unperturbed length for the 550-nm-wide (red), 600-nm-wide (green), and 650-nm-wide (blue) antenna geometries, with the target emission angle overlaid (dashed line). e Simulated emission angle versus wavelength for the 550-nm-wide (red), 600-nm-wide (green), and 650-nm-wide (blue) antenna geometries.

First, we consider the scattering strength of the antennas. We ideally want uniform illumination of the antennas in the array dimension across the entire length of the aperture to generate a far-field beam with a high SNR. For this reason, we design each of the three antenna geometries to have the same scattering strength, such that each of the three antennas emits with the same emission pattern along the length of the antenna. To achieve this, for each antenna geometry, we first select a constant inward perturbed length of 300 nm. We then simulate the scattering strength as a function of the inward perturbation width using Lumerical FDTD (as shown in Fig. 2c), while keeping the emission angle constant for each simulation point by analytically calculating27 the appropriate unperturbed length for each inward perturbation width that maintains a constant emission angle of − 10.32° (see below for detailed discussion on the emission angle). Using this data, we then select a unique inward perturbation width (and corresponding unperturbed length) for each antenna geometry that corresponds to a scattering strength of 0.018 dB/µm, ensuring that 87% of the light will scatter by the end of the 500-µm-long antenna.

Second, all three antenna geometries must emit at the same angle to create a single beam in the antenna dimension of the integrated OPA. For longer antenna lengths, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the beams emitted by each antenna geometry decreases, requiring a more precise matching of emission angles. To avoid substantial beam degradation, the mismatch between the emitted angles of the three antenna geometries must be less than the FWHM of the emitted beams in the antenna dimension. To achieve this and confirm the validity of the analytical emission-angle calculation used above, for each antenna geometry, we simulate the emission angle as a function of the unperturbed length (near the analytically-calculated unperturbed length) at the design wavelength of 1550 nm using Lumerical FDTD (as shown in Fig. 2d). These simulations verify that the analytical calculation for the unperturbed lengths results in an emission angle close to the target angle of − 10.32° for all three antenna geometries. We then use the data to apply a small correction to the unperturbed length for each antenna geometry to perfectly match the emission angles (with only a negligible change in the scattering strength).

Third, we must verify that all three antenna geometries have very similar emission patterns in the array dimension (i.e., antenna element factors) to ensure that the emission from every antenna in the aperture can evenly interfere at all angles in the far field (a critical requirement to maintain a high system SNR). For each antenna geometry, we simulate the final antenna design using Lumerical FDTD and successfully confirm that all three antenna geometries have similar antenna element factors.

Finally, we must verify that all three antenna geometries emit at the same angle over a large span of wavelengths to ensure compatibility with wavelength-induced beam steering. Often, in integrated OPA systems, we achieve two-dimensional non-mechanical beam steering by varying the wavelength of light input into the system to steer the beam in the antenna dimension of the integrated OPA1,2,9. Simulation validation of the reduced-crosstalk antennas

In this section, we simulate and analyze the coupling between two of our reduced-crosstalk antennas (designed in Design of the Reduced-Crosstalk Antennas) versus the coupling between two standard antennas, verifying the reduced-crosstalk functionality of the antennas. We then validate our theoretical model for a lossy two-mode system (developed in Theoretical Framework for Lossy Coupled-Mode Systems) by fitting the model parameters to the simulated results.

To simulate the reduced-crosstalk functionality of the antennas, we utilize an antenna-coupling test structure (as shown in Fig. 3a) to measure the coupling between two standard antennas versus the coupling between two reduced-crosstalk antennas. For each test structure, we place two antennas at a 775-nm pitch, matching the antenna pitch required for grating-lobe-free operation when the antennas are used in an integrated OPA. We then place a source at the top input port of the test structure to excite a single antenna mode and place mode-expansion monitors at the bar and cross output ports to measure the power at the output of each antenna at the end of the antenna coupling region. Using Lumerical FDTD, we vary the antenna length (i.e., the length of the antenna coupling region) and record the resulting power transmitted in the cross and bar output ports. We then normalize the power in each port to the total power of the two ports to remove the radiative losses from the analysis and isolate the coupling effects. Experimental validation of the reduced-crosstalk antennas

In this section, we fabricate and experimentally characterize a suite of antenna-coupling test structures to quantify the coupling between two reduced-crosstalk antennas (designed in Design of the Reduced-Crosstalk Antennas) versus two standard antennas, experimentally validating the reduced-crosstalk functionality of the antennas.

We fabricate a suite of antenna-coupling test structures in the AIM Photonics (American Institute for Manufacturing Integrated Photonics) Base Active Process (see Methods section for details on the fabrication platform). Design and experimental demonstration of the wide-FOV integrated OPA

In this section, we develop, fabricate, and experimentally demonstrate a half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPA using our reduced-crosstalk antennas (designed in Design of the Reduced-Crosstalk Antennas), experimentally validating the grating-lobe-free and wide-FOV functionality of the system.

Figure 5a shows a simplified schematic of the wide-FOV integrated OPA. At the input, an inverse-taper edge coupler couples the light into a single-mode silicon waveguide. A splitter tree network then distributes the input power evenly into 16 waveguide arms using cascaded 1-to-2-waveguide splitter devices33. Each waveguide arm then routes through a separate thermo-optic phase shifter (provided in the AIM Photonics Active Process Design Kit (PDK)34,35); each of the phase shifters is controlled independently using a separate voltage control line, which enables arbitrary phase encoding for all elements in the OPA. Finally, for each waveguide arm, a taper adiabatically transitions the waveguide to one of the reduced-crosstalk antennas (designed in Design of the Reduced-Crosstalk Antennas), which then emits the light out of the chip. To enable a wide FOV for the OPA, we place the antennas with a tight 775-nm pitch, which is half of the 1550-nm design wavelength, and, to reduce the crosstalk between neighboring elements, we place the three different antenna geometries in an alternating pattern in the array (as shown in Fig. 2a) to ensure that all neighboring antennas have different propagation coefficients.Fig. 5Design and experimental setup for wide-FOV integrated OPA.a Simplified schematic of the wide-FOV integrated OPA. b Photograph of the wide-FOV integrated OPA emitting a single beam onto an infrared-detecting card. c Photograph of the experimental setup with a rotating microscope stage used to obtain the wide-FOV far-field emission profiles of the fabricated chip.

We fabricate the system alongside the aforementioned antenna-coupling test structures (described in Experimental Validation of the Reduced-Crosstalk Antennas) in the AIM Photonics Base Active Process (see Methods section for details on the fabrication platform). To enable independent active control of the phase shifters, we attach the fabricated chip to a PCB and wire bond the phase-shifter control pads to contacts on the PCB. We then connect the PCB contacts to a 16-channel programmable power supply controlled using a Python script.

To enable characterization of the wide-FOV functionality of the fabricated system, we build a custom rotating microscope setup capable of imaging the full far field of the chip (as shown in Fig. 5c). We couple light from a 1455-nm off-chip tunable laser into the input inverse-taper edge coupler. We then build a Fourier-plane-imaging microscope system consisting of a microscope objective, lens, and infrared camera1,7,27 and attach this microscope to a rotating stage. We can then adjust and scan the angle of this rotating stage to enable imaging of the full far field of the chip in the array dimension. We choose a microscope objective with a numerical aperture of 0.4 to image a large 47° FOV for each discrete angle setting, so that we can capture a large angular slice of the far field for any given setting (which is necessary to improve the calibration procedure described below).

To calibrate the system for a specific angle of emission, we perform a standard phase-calibration optimization procedure to both set the phases necessary for beam steering to the target angle and account for any phase errors accumulated from fabrication variations in the distribution network. Specifically, we rotate the microscope stage to center the captured microscope FOV at the target angle of emission. We keep the microscope at this rotation for the duration of the calibration, thus requiring a calibration routine that accounts for the emission both at angles imaged by the microscope and at angles outside of the limited FOV of the microscope. To address this, we use a multi-objective optimizer to select the voltages for each phase shifter that both minimize the mean-squared difference between the experimentally-measured and the target theoretically-calculated beam patterns in the array dimension and maximize the total power in the beam to ensure minimal emission at angles outside of the microscope FOV1,36. We then rotate the microscope stage to image the full far field at many discrete angles and stitch the captured images together to create a single wide-FOV image.

Note that the fabrication bias of the process introduces a non-random error between the angles of emission for the three antenna geometries. Therefore, we utilize the experimental input operating wavelength of 1455 nm (instead of the design wavelength of 1550 nm) to account for these antenna-emission-angle differences. We believe this difference in angle of emission is the result of a fabrication bias manifesting as over etching of the antenna widths in the system (an effect that we confirm plausible through simulations and have seen in prior systems fabricated in this process1). While we did apply a positive width offset to the antennas on the mask to account for this known fabrication bias, the pre-bias correction was insufficient, and, therefore, the system requires a shorter operating wavelength. In addition to this operating-wavelength shift, the small angular errors from this fabrication bias also create a low-power artifact at a 36° offset from the main beam, as shown in Fig. 6b. This wavelength shift and artifact are not fundamental limits to the performance of the system and are instead a result of the known fabrication bias. Since the integrated OPA performance is consistent over multiple chips, we believe this is not a random fabrication error and is instead a fabrication bias, which could be easily further corrected in future iterations of the system by applying an even larger pre-bias positive offset to the antenna widths.

Next, to demonstrate the grating-lobe-free functionality of the system, we use an infrared-detecting card to visualize the far-field emission of the calibrated system (as shown in Fig. 5b). We observe only a single beam formed in the entire 180° FOV, successfully confirming the grating-lobe-free operation.

To demonstrate the wide-FOV operation of the system, we use the phase-calibration procedure to steer the beam over many angles across a 60° range. For each desired angle, we first perform the aforementioned phase-calibration procedure at the target angle and then rotate the microscope stage to image the full far field at many discrete angles. We then stitch the captured images together and normalize to the maximum power measured to create a single wide-FOV image. We repeat this procedure for a variety of equally-spaced target angles across a 60° range (as shown in Fig. 6c), successfully confirming the wide-FOV operation of the system.

Beyond this 60° range, the wide-FOV array-factor contribution to the emission pattern of the system and the reduced-crosstalk functionality of the antennas still hold. However, the element-factor contribution to the emission pattern limits the power emitted at larger angles beyond this range. In future work, the angular spread of the element factor of the antennas could also be engineered to leverage the full capability of the reduced-crosstalk antennas and demonstrate active beam steering across an even wider range of angles.

Finally, to confirm that the system is still compatible with the standard wavelength-induced beam-steering technique utilized for integrated OPAs, we repeat this phase-calibration procedure for many input operating wavelengths to demonstrate wavelength-induced beam steering. For each wavelength, we perform the phase-calibration procedure and verify that the system still maintains a high SNR and grating-lobe-free performance in the array dimension. We then record the antenna-dimension angle of emission at each wavelength (as shown in Fig. 6d). The system successfully scans over wavelengths spanning 80 nm, corresponding to an antenna-dimension beam-steering range of approximately 15°.

Discussion

In this work, we developed and experimentally demonstrated a set of integrated grating-based antennas with significantly-reduced inter-antenna crosstalk that enable half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPAs with grating-lobe-free and wide-FOV functionality. First, we derived a generalized theoretical model to describe the coupling dynamics between lossy modes in a system from first-principles coupled-mode theory and used this model to analyze the coupling between antennas. Next, we designed three separate integrated grating-based antenna geometries with different propagation coefficients but similar emission characteristics to enable reduced inter-antenna crosstalk while maintaining optimal beam quality. We then fabricated and experimentally characterized these reduced-crosstalk antennas, successfully measuring significantly-reduced coupling on the order of only 1% for the reduced-crosstalk antennas compared to approximately 100% coupling for standard antennas. Finally, using these reduced-crosstalk antennas, we developed, fabricated, and experimentally demonstrated a half-wavelength-pitch reduced-crosstalk integrated OPA, successfully demonstrating grating-lobe-free functionality and active beam steering across 60°.

In the future, the angular-emission spread of the element factor of the reduced-crosstalk antennas could also be increased to enable active beam steering across an even wider range of angles within the full 180° FOV of the system; for example, narrower antenna widths could be utilized that would enable antenna emission profiles with a greater angular-emission spread, increasing the active beam-steering range of the system. Additionally, the reduced-crosstalk antenna designs could be further improved by introducing both uniform- and unidirectional-emission capabilities to the antennas, which would improve the beam quality in the antenna dimension by increasing the effective aperture size and remove blind spots in the array dimension due to substrate reflections, respectively27,37. Finally, the number of antennas in the wide-FOV integrated OPA could be increased to enable larger aperture sizes using conventional integrated-OPA scaling techniques; for example, future demonstrations could leverage electronic-photonic integration for scalable phase control to many antennas and leverage scalable phase-calibration algorithms to reduce the computational complexity required for beamforming4,38.

To the best of our knowledge, this work presents for the first time a set of integrated grating-based antennas with significantly-reduced inter-antenna crosstalk. By reducing the inter-antenna crosstalk, this development enables half-wavelength-pitch integrated OPAs that can achieve grating-lobe-free functionality and wide FOVs while maintaining high SNRs. Ultimately, this work facilitates new functionality for integrated OPAs, enabling high-performance systems for a variety of high-impact application areas.

Methods

The wide-FOV integrated OPA and the antenna-coupling test structures were fabricated in the AIM Photonics Base Active Process, a commercially-available multi-project-wafer service. The AIM Photonics Base Active Platform is a 300-mm-wafer silicon-photonics platform consisting of a silicon layer with several doping masks, two silicon-nitride layers, three metal layers for electrical routing and contact pads, two via layers and metal-to-silicon contacts, and a dicing trench for fiber coupling.
