Substance use patterns in a community sample of adult women in the United States: Alcohol, Cannabis, and other drug use
Authors: Shelly F Greenfield, Allen J Bailey, Laura C Seidman, Dawn E Sugarman, Kristin N Javaras, Laura A Payne, Victoria R Votaw, Nicole A Barbaro, R Kathryn McHugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsSubstance use is a significant public health problem among women in the United States, although limited data exist on patterns of use across different demographic groups. This cross-sectional survey study aimed to examine in adult women (1) patterns of substance use (i.e., alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, and prescription medication misuse) and (2) whether these patterns varied by age cohort.MethodsFrom July to September 2024, we conducted an online cross-sectional survey of women ages 18–70 in the community who reported substance use in the prior 30 days. Using latent class analysis, we analyzed data from 2296 women.ResultsOverall, heavy use was common particularly for alcohol and cannabis (approximately 18% and 22%, respectively). Heavy substance use patterns varied qualitatively with age. The prevalence of heavy alcohol use (≥16 days/month) increased with older age cohort, as did prescription medication misuse. Heavy cannabis use (≥ 16 days/month) decreased with older age cohort. In a latent class analysis of substance use patterns, four discrete classes emerged: (1) alcohol use with some cannabis (approximately 57% of the sample), (2) heavy cannabis with some alcohol (27%), (3) polysubstance use (10%), and (4) prescription medication misuse with some alcohol (6%).ConclusionsAmong adult women who reported recent substance use, more than half used more than one substance, and use patterns varied with age cohort. Consideration of polysubstance use, age cohort, and life stage is critical in designing effective substance use prevention, early intervention, and treatment for adult women.Keywords: Women, Female, Polysubstance, Substance use, Substance use disorders, Cannabis, Alcohol

Introduction

In the past three decades, the gender gap in the prevalence of substance use disorders (SUDs) in the United States has dramatically narrowed (Grucza et al., 2008; Keyes et al., 2008; Keyes et al., 2010; McHugh et al., 2021). This includes alcohol, opioids, and cannabis, with rising rates in women and girls across domains of race, ethnicity, and lifespan (McHugh et al., 2021; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2021). These trends are concerning, as women tend to have more severe substance-related medical and social consequences and most women do not seek treatment for substance use problems (Greenfield et al., 2010; Kay et al., 2010; National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2025).

There is a critical need to understand patterns of substance use in women to improve understanding of risk patterns across the lifespan. While studies often track use patterns for specific substances (e.g., alcohol, cannabis, opioids), the use of multiple substances (i.e., polysubstance use) is the norm among individuals who use substances (Rockhill et al., 2025), including among those with mild substance use severity (Bailey & McHugh, 2023). Currently, little is known about use patterns among community samples of women in the U.S., especially how these patterns may vary across the lifespan (Bailey & McHugh, 2023; Rockhill et al., 2025).

As part of a larger project to identify correlates of and potential risk factors for heavy substance use in women, this study aimed to elucidate substance use patterns in adult women who reported using substances in the past 30 days to answer the following questions:

1.What are the patterns of use of substances, including alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, and prescription medication misuse, in a community sample of adult women who have used substances in the past 30 days?

What are the patterns of use of substances, including alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, and prescription medication misuse, in a community sample of adult women who have used substances in the past 30 days?

2.Do these patterns of use vary across four age cohorts (18–30 years, 31–43, 44–56, and 57–70)?

Do these patterns of use vary across four age cohorts (18–30 years, 31–43, 44–56, and 57–70)?

Methods

Participants

Participants were community members recruited from online panels managed by Sago Health (Iselin, NJ) (Sago, 2024). Invitations were sent by Sago to individuals within the target age range. Those interested in participating were directed to click a link to a screening form to determine eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: 1) self-identified as female on Sago panel profile, 2) age 18–70 years, and 3) any reported substance use during the prior 30 days. To ensure adequate representation of both low- and high-frequency substance use, after the first 1495 respondents, the substance use inclusion criterion was changed to endorsement of at least 6 days in the past month in at least one of the four substance categories. After an additional 3073 respondents were screened, this inclusion criterion was reverted to the original for the remaining 337 respondents. There were no exclusion criteria.

Of the 4876 participants assessed for eligibility, 2405 (49.3%) were determined ineligible due to no substance use (n = 1385) and mild substance use only (n = 1020) in the second phase of survey recruitment (see Fig. 1). One hundred and seventy-four eligible participants were directed to the Information Sheet but chose not to participate. Of the remaining 2297 participants, one was identified as spam and removed from all analyses. A total of 2296 individuals participated in the survey.

Enrollment flow-chart.

Procedure

The study was determined to be exempt by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. After clicking the invitation link, potential participants completed four screening questions to assess their use of different substances (i.e., alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, and prescription medication misuse) over the prior 30 days. Eligible participants were directed to complete informed consent, and those who agreed to participate were directed to the survey. The survey was hosted on Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) (Harris et al., 2009; Harris et al., 2019). Data were collected between July and September 2024. Participants who completed the study were compensated 200 points (equivalent to $2) in their Sago account.

Measures

The survey included validated questionnaires to measure substance use and social and psychological characteristics. The following measures were used in these analyses.

Screener Questionnaire. The screener questionnaire asked about the frequency of substance use in the past 30 days, defined as any use of alcohol, cannabis, other drugs, and prescription medication misuse. Each screening question included four answer choices representing no use (“none”), mild use (“1–5 days”), moderate use (“6–15 days”), and heavy use (“16 or more days”).

Demographics and Social Determinants of Health. This instrument (designed for the study) assessed participant age, race, ethnicity, marital status, level of education, employment status, housing and financial statuses, household composition, substance use problems among family members, and other characteristics.

Tobacco, Alcohol, Prescription Medications, and Other Substances (TAPS) (McNeely et al., 2016). The TAPS-1 includes 4 items assessing use/misuse of tobacco, alcohol, prescription drugs, and other drugs (including marijuana) in the prior 12 months. Each substance or substance group is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “daily or almost daily.” TAPS-2 assesses substance use and related behaviors in the prior 3 months for tobacco cigarettes, alcohol, marijuana, cocaine, crack, and methamphetamine, heroin and fentanyl, misuse of prescription opiate pain relievers, misuse of medication for anxiety or sleep, misuse of medication for ADHD, and use of other illicit or recreational drugs.

Data analysis

First, we used descriptive statistics to characterize substance use behaviors overall and by age cohort. Next, latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted using the four response categories from the Screener Questionnaire assessing past 30-day frequency of alcohol, cannabis, other illicit substance use, and prescription medication misuse. LCA models were estimated in Mplus (Muthèn & Muthèn, 2017[1998]) utilizing a robust maximum likelihood estimation (“MLR” estimator in Mplus) that is appropriate for categorical indicators. A series of LCA models were fit (i.e., 2-class, 3-class, four-class…) and evaulated based on the following criteria: (1) theoretical interpretability, (2) Bayesian information criteria (BIC) with lower values indicating better model fit, (3) entropy, which is a metric that ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating higher demarcation between classes, and (4) the size of identifed classes. There were no missing data in variables used in LCA. Participants were assigned to latent classes based on their highest estimated class membership probability. Figures were generated using ggplot (Wickham, 2016).

Multionmial regressions were estimated, in R statistical software version 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2024) utilizing the VGAM package (Yee, 2010), to test the associations between age cohort and latent class membership. For the presented model, the youngest age cohort was used as the reference category. Models were also rerun with other age cohorts as the reference category to obtain all direct statistical comparisons.

In this sample, 72.3% identified as white and 18.7% as Black/African American and 41.4% percent were married. Among the 63.1% who had children, the mean number of children was 2.3 (SD = 1.2) and 42.6% had children living in the household. Substance use descriptives by age

Heavy Use Patterns of Specific Substances: All participants used at least one substance in the past 30 days, per study inclusion criteria. Over this period, 84.8% of participants had used alcohol at least one day, with 18.3% endorsing heavy use (i.e., ≥ 16 days). Forty-five percent used cannabis, with 22.6% endorsing heavy use; 19.1% endorsed prescription medication misuse, with 8.6% misusing on ≥16 days; and 11.2% endorsed using other substances, with 6.1% endorsing heavy use. These patterns of heavy use (≥16 days in past 30 days) varied by age cohort (see Fig. 2). Qualitatively, heavy alcohol use (≥ 16 days) was lowest in the youngest age cohort and increased with each succeeding age cohort. This contrasted with heavy cannabis use, which was highest in the youngest age cohort and decreased with age. Prescription medication misuse of 16 or more days per month was highest in the two oldest cohorts; heavy use of other drugs was highest in two middle-aged cohorts. Latent class analysis results

Table 3 presents fit statistics for 2–5 class Latent Class Analysis (LCA) models. The four-class solution was selected based on the combination of theoretical interpretability, excellent entropy, and the lowest BIC; Fig. 3 displays the probability of endorsing each substance use frequency category by latent class. This solution consisted of 4 latent classes as follows: (1) “Primary Alcohol” class (approximately 57% of total sample) characterized by alcohol use of varying frequencies, predominantly cannabis abstinence or low frequency cannabis use, abstinence from “other” illicit drugs, and predominantly low likelihood of prescription misuse; (2) “Heavy Cannabis with Alcohol” (27%) characterized by high likelihood of heavy cannabis use (i.e., 16+ days) and varying frequencies of alcohol use; and (3) “Polysubstance Use” class (10%) characterized by the highest probabilities of endorsing frequent “other” illicit substance use. This class also has a wide range of estimated frequency of alcohol and cannabis use, in addition to “other” illicit substance use. Therefore, the “Polysubstance” use class likely contains significant variability and captures several substance use combinations, and lastly (4) “Prescription Medication Misuse with Alcohol” class (6%), characterized by moderate probabilities of endorsing moderate to high frequency prescription misuse, in addition to some alcohol use. Discussion

As part of a larger project to identify correlates and potential risk factors for heavy substance use in women, this study aimed to elucidate substance use patterns in adult women in the community who reported past 30-day substance use and identify whether there are differences in use by age cohort. Results of latent class analyses demonstrated four main substance use patterns. The first two patterns focused on alcohol and cannabis use and represented the largest groups. More than half of the sample was in a class characterized predominantly by alcohol use, with a small likelihood of cannabis use, and just under one-third were in a class characterized by heavy cannabis use with some alcohol use. It is not surprising that the largest class had a predominant pattern primarily of alcohol, as alcohol is by far the most commonly used substance in the US (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). Members of this group show a wide range of alcohol use frequency, from infrequent alcohol use of 1–5 days per month to more frequent use of 16 or more days per month. This was accompanied by a small likelihood (<20%) of endorsing any cannabis use. This contrasts with the group with predominantly heavy cannabis and some alcohol use. Women in this group are characterized by higher likelihood of heavy cannabis use of 16 or more days per month. Women in both these classes of use patterns had a low likelihood of prescription medication misuse and a very low likelihood of other illicit drug use.

Consistent with population base rates, fewer participants were in the polysubstance use class with heavy use of other illicit drugs, as well as some alcohol and cannabis use, and misuse of prescription medications; and a small minority of the sample belonged to a class with predominant misuse of prescription medications. Of note, the prescription medication misuse group was characterized by a high likelihood of frequent (16 or more days per month) prescription medication misuse, with a moderate likelihood of alcohol use. These two use patterns are understudied in women (McHugh et al., 2018) but pose risks for morbidity and mortality, including risks of accidents, overdose, chronic illness development, and greater co-occurring psychiatric disorders (Brady et al., 1993; Greenfield et al., 2007; Park et al., 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2022; White, 2022).

Significantly, the latent class patterns varied with age. The proportion of each age cohort in the primary alcohol use class increased with age cohort. This contrasts with the class where heavy cannabis use and some alcohol predominate, which is greatest in the younger age cohorts (i.e., 18–30, 31–43) and decreases in prevalence progressively with each older cohort. The two younger age cohorts also have the highest likelihood of other illicit drug use and membership in the polysubstance use class. By contrast, while the overall prevalence of the prescription medication misuse with some alcohol use class is low, it is most prevalent in the two oldest age cohorts. This may be reflective of greater exposure to prescribed medications, such as benzodiazepines and opioids in older age cohorts (Innes et al., 2024; Rhee, 2019). This study extends the current literature by demonstrating in a sample of community participants that these patterns of use are distinct and vary with age.

This study's findings that those in the older age cohorts are more likely to belong to the class with predominant use of alcohol is consistent with previous epidemiologic studies (Breslow et al., 2017; Han et al., 2017). For example, results from one study showed that between 1997 and 2014, binge drinking increased in people 60 years and older, and especially among women (Breslow et al., 2017). In addition, analyses of data from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health from 2005 to 2014 demonstrated that females 50 to 64 years had a significant 44.4% relative increase (6.3% to 9.1%) in binge drinking while males demonstrated a non-significant 9.7% relative increase (19.6% to 21.5%) in this same period (Han et al., 2017). Heavy alcohol use in older women carries multiple health risks including cognitive impairment, falls, liver disease, and breast cancer (Office of the Surgeon General (US), 2025).

As the landscape of cannabis legislation has continued to evolve along with societal norms, understanding cannabis use in women across the lifespan is of utmost importance. In our sample, it is the two younger age cohorts that have the highest prevalence of cannabis use (56.1% and 52.9% of 18–30- and 31–43-year-olds, respectively). Even so, it is notable that 45.1% of the overall sample endorsed using cannabis, including 34.7% of the oldest age cohort. Of note, recent national data demonstrate that females aged 18–30 exceeded males in cannabis use prevalence for the first time (Miech et al., 2025; National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2024). This study adds to the existing literature by demonstrating that cannabis use in adult women in a community sample varies with age cohorts including mean days of use per month. Understanding these patterns is essential to understanding the potential risks of alcohol, cannabis, and other drug use across women's adult lifespan.

Older age cohorts of women were also more likely to belong to the prescription medication misuse class, with 7% of each of the two older adult cohorts belonging to this class. While medication misuse is known to vary in the population by a range of factors including age, gender, and other factors (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2020), the results of the present study extend findings of previous literature regarding substance use in older compared with younger women indicating a greater likelihood of prescription drug misuse combined with alcohol use in older women (Keyes, 2023; Schepis et al., 2022). Future research will be needed to parse differences among prescription drug types, as these vary across both age and gender (e.g., stimulants vs. benzodiazepines). By contrast, the two younger cohorts of women were more likely to use other illicit drugs and have polysubstance use across multiple substances compared with the two older age cohorts. Use of illicit drugs and polydrug use are reported in nationally representative epidemiologic data as being more common in adult men than women (Ellis et al., 2024; National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2020; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). This survey of adult women demonstrates that in a community sample of women who used substances in the past month, illicit substance use co-occurring with use of multiple other substances may be most relevant among women in younger age cohorts.

Finally, while the U.S. has seen a decrease in tobacco smoking nationwide over the last five decades (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025), in this study 25.8% of adult women smoked combustible tobacco in the prior 3 months, with middle-aged women (31–43 and 44–56 years) having the highest prevalence (31.6% and 28.1%, respectively). Of those who smoked tobacco, 49.0% smoked more than 10 cigarettes per day, and 65.3% smoked within 30 min of awakening. These data indicate that in adult women in the community, nicotine and tobacco use remain significant and ought to be assessed and treated. Yet, use of nicotine replacement therapy and medication for nicotine cessation remains underutilized overall (Nielsen et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2025).

This study has several limitations. This is a cross-sectional, online survey designed to characterize the substance use patterns of a community-based population of adult women in the U.S. who endorsed at least one day of use of any substance in the past month. The study is not meant to replicate national epidemiological surveys such as the National Survey on Drug Use and Health. Because participants were recruited from a non-probability convenience sample drawn from the Sago panel, results should be interpreted with respect to this specific cohort and may not be generalizable to the entire population. However, this survey study extends results from previous studies by examining use patterns in a diverse sample of women in the community who endorse any use across multiple substances and age cohorts and finds that use of multiple substances is common, and patterns of use vary with age among adult women. Another limitation is that cross-sectional surveys capture only a single point in time, and causality cannot be inferred from association.

Conclusion

These limitations notwithstanding, this study's findings extend the literature by showing that in this diverse national community sample of adult women with past-month substance use, there is substantial heterogeneity in patterns of use of alcohol, cannabis, other illicit drug use, and prescription medication misuse. More than half used more than one substance, and use patterns varied with age cohort. Many of these groups are understudied (e.g., older women with prescription drug misuse; younger women with heavy cannabis use; older women with heavy alcohol use), highlighting the importance of research specifically targeting these high-risk subgroups. Heavy use of substances is a risk for developing substance use disorders and women are also at risk for an accelerated course of substance problems including medical and psychiatric consequences. Clinician screening for women's use of multiple substances through the adult lifespan would provide a significant opportunity for earlier detection and intervention for problematic substance use. Research is also critical to elucidate the risks for heavy substance use, including polysubstance use, through women's lifespan to design the most effective substance use prevention, early intervention, and treatment for adult women.
