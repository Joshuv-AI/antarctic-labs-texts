On some classes of bivalent and trivalent planar graphs
Jorge Alencar, Jean-Guy Caputo, Leonardo de Lima, Arnaud Knippel

Abstract
A graph is called bivalent or trivalent if there exists an eigenvector of the graph Laplacian composed from { − 1 , 1 } \{-1,1\} or { − 1 , 0 , 1 } \{-1,0,1\} , respectively. These bivalent and trivalent eigenvectors are important for engineering applications, in particular for vibrating systems. In this article, we determine the structure of bivalent and trivalent graphs in the following planar graph families: trees, unicyclic, bicyclic, and cactus.



Keywords:

Laplacian matrix, Laplacian eigenvector, Bivalent graph, Trivalent graph.

Theorem 2.8

If the adjacent vertex

3
Trees and unicyclic graphs

In this section, we obtain a characterization of all bivalent and trivalent trees and unicyclic graphs.

Next result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2.8
for trees.

Theorem 3.1

(
Bivalent or trivalent tree
)
.

Bivalent and trivalent trees are obtained respectively
by connecting chains
P_{2}
(
[1,-1]^{T},\lambda=2
) or
star
S_{2k+1}
(
[0,-1,1,\ldots,-1,1]^{T},\lambda=1)
with equal links.

More precisely, bivalent trees are obtained by connecting
p\geq 1
chains
P_{2}
with
p-1
equal links without creating a cycle.
Trivalent trees are obtained by connecting
p\geq 1
stars with a soft center with
p-1
equal links without creating a cycle.

The proof is obvious because a tree has at least a leaf. Theorem
3.1
implies that bivalent trees have a perfect matching.

For unicyclic graphs, we then have the following.

Theorem 3.2

(
Bivalent or trivalent unicyclic graphs without equal links
)
.

Bivalent or trivalent unicyclic graphs without equal links are the cycles

•

Cycle
4k

[1,0,-1,0,1,0,-1,0,\dots,1,0,-1,0]^{T};

•

A consequence of the theorem on a graph with a leaf is that the unicyclic graph cannot
have a leaf because otherwise it would not have a cycle. Then the graph is reduced to a cycle, and
d_{i}=2
to every vertex

It is well-known that
\lambda\leq 4
for cycles.

Let
k
be the number of nodes valued
1
, which is equal to the number of nodes valued
-1
. The proof is based on Theorem
2.7
.

We first suppose that
G
is a trivalent cycle without equal links. Since there is at least one non-soft vertex
v
of degree 2, from Theorem
2.7
, we have
\lambda=d_{v}+\overline{d}_{v}\geq 2.
Then,
2\leq\lambda\leq 4.

If
\lambda=2
, then
\bar{d}_{j}=0
for every non-soft node
j
. From Theorem
2.7
(ii), the number of nodes valued -1 is also equal to
k.
It implies that the number of soft nodes equals
2k
since each node valued
1
or
-1
is linked to two soft nodes, and the cycle is
C_{4k}
.

If
\lambda=3,
we have
\bar{d}_{j}=1
for every non-soft node
j
. It implies that the number of soft nodes is equal to
k
since each non-soft node is linked to exactly one soft node, and then the cycle is
C_{3k}
.

If
\lambda=4,
we have
\bar{d}_{j}=2
for every non-soft node

, which implies that there are no soft nodes since every vertex has degree 2. So, there is no trivalent graph with this eigenvalue.

Now, suppose that
G
is a bivalent cycle. From Theorem
2.5
,
G
is regular bipartite with parts of size
k.
It implies that
G
is the cycle
2k,
and the eigenvalue
\lambda=4
is associated with eigenvector
[1,-1,\ldots,1,-1]^{T}.

The proof is complete.
∎

(

(i) Bivalent unicyclic graphs are composed of chains

The component with a cycle, as it has no equal links,

The bivalent or trivalent trees are described in Theorem

Multicyclic graphs

5.1
General results

We show below that a bivalent graph without equal links
can be decomposed in perfect matchings.

Theorem 5.1

(
Partition in perfect matchings
)
.

Let
G
be a
d
-regular bipartite graph, then the edges
can be partitioned in
d
perfect matchings.

Proof.

The theorem is trivially true for
d=0
(no edge)
or
d=1
(matching). For
d>1
, we use the fact that
a regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching. This is
a consequence of König’s matching theorem (see for example
Schjriver’s book
[
15
]
, corollary 16.2.b).
Taking out a perfect matching gives a
d-1
regular bipartite
graph, hence the conclusion.
∎

We note that two disjoint perfect matchings in
G
form a cycle cover of the graph (not necessarily
just one cycle).
If the two perfect matchings complete each other well, they may form
a Hamiltonian cycle.
Note that a regular bipartite graph is not necessarily Hamiltonian, as shown
in the counterexample in Figure
8
built from
three components
K_{3,3}\setminus P_{2}
and two additional
vertices
\alpha,\beta
linked to the vertices of degree 2.
This graph is regular and bipartite: the vertex set is

V=(A\cup\{\alpha\})\cup(B\cup\{\beta\})

.

The cyclomatic number
c
of a connected graph is defined as the number of independent cycles, and it can also be viewed as the minimum number of edges to be removed to get a tree. So,

m=n-1+c.

We now give a formula for the cyclomatic number
c
of regular bipartite graphs. This
will help finding the possible values of
c
.

Theorem 5.2

(
Regular bipartite multicyclic graphs
)
.

The cyclomatic number of regular bipartite graphs verifies

c={n\over 2}(d-2)+1,

(1)

where
d
is the degree.

Proof.

Assume
G
to be a connected regular bipartite multicyclic graph.
Then it’s number of edges
m
verifies

m=n-1+c,

where
c
is the cyclomatic number. We know that
n
is even so
if
d
is the degree of each vertex, we have

nd=2m.

From the two relations above, we get relation (
1
).
∎

Given this formula, one sees that not all values of
c
are
possible.

Corollary 5.3
.

The cyclomatic number
c
of regular bipartite graphs verifies

c-1\in\mathbf{Z}^{+}-\{k^{2},k\geq 2\}\cup\{k(k+1),k\geq 1\}.

The relation above imposes a constraint between the values of

n,d
and
c
. Let us consider the first few values of
d
.

For
d=0
, we have
n=1
and
c=0
so we have isolated vertices.

For
d=1
, we have
n=2
and
c=0
so the graph is
P_{2}
.

For
d=2
, the graph is a cycle and
c=1
.

Note also that
c=2
is not possible because

n/2
and
d-2
should be different non consecutive integers.
This is theorem
4.2
.

Similarly,
c=3
is not possible.
An example with
c=4
is
K_{3,3}
the complete bipartite graph
where
n=6
and
d=3
.

An infinite family of regular bipartite graphs is obtained
by taking an even cycle
C_{2k}
and adding to it

\ell

k,\ell\in\mathbf{N}^{*},~k\geq 2,~~\ell\leq n/2-2

These graphs are Hamiltonian.

An example is
d=4

and the graph is a
cycle
C_{2k}
with

matchings so
\lambda=8

In the following we consider a subclass of multicyclic planar graphs
called cactus graphs.
A
cactus graph
is
a connected graph in which any two cycles have at most one vertex in common. Such a graph is outerplanar: it can be represented with all vertices belonging
to the outer face.

5.2
Cactus bivalent graphs

First note

Theorem 5.4
.

[
Bivalent
cactus graphs without equal links
] If
G
is a cactus graph with more
than one cycle and without equal links, it cannot be bivalent.

Proof.

We show that it is not possible to build a finite d-regular cactus.

Consider a bivalent cactus graph without equal links, it is

d
regular. We choose an outer planar representation of the graph.
Take a face of this representation, it is a cycle without a chord.
Because the graph is connected and has at least two cycles, the
degree of each vertex is at least 3.
Take a vertex
u
of this cycle, it is connected to another vertex
v

outside the cycle. Vertex
v
has degree at least 3 and is connected
to vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
not belonging to the first cycle.
Otherwise the cactus hypothesis would be wrong.
Repeating the process for
v_{1}
we generate an infinite graph.
∎

Then we have

Theorem 5.5
.

[
Bivalent
cactus graphs
] A bivalent cactus graph, is either
formed by cycles of even length (
\lambda=4
) connected by
equal links or formed by chains
P_{2}
connected by
equal links (
\lambda=2
).

Proof.

Consider a cactus graph. Taking out equal links
we obtain
p
connected components.

Consider one of these connected components, it cannot have more
than one cycle because of Theorem
5.4
. Assume there is just one
cycle, then from Theorem
3.2
this component is just
a cycle
2k
(
\lambda=4
). If this component has no cycle then
it is just a chain
P_{2}
from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

5.3
Cactus trivalent graphs

As a consequence of Theorem
4.3
, we obtain all trivalent cactus graphs without equal links.

Theorem 5.6
.

[
Trivalent cactus
graphs without equal links
] Let
G
be a trivalent cactus graphs without
equal links, only three cases are possible:

•

\lambda=1,
then
G
is a chain star
S_{2k+1};

•

\lambda=2
, then
G
should be obtained by identifying soft nodes of cycles with length multiple of
4;

•

\lambda=3
, then
G
should be obtained by identifying soft nodes of cycles with length multiple of
3.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.8
, if
G
has a leaf then it should be a chain star
S_{2k+1}.
Now, consider that
G
does not have a leaf. By hypothesis,
G
cannot have a subgraph of the type
B_{3}(p,q,r)
; otherwise,
G
would have two cycles with
r-1
vertices in common. From Theorem
4.3
,
G
cannot have a path connecting two cycles since there is no trivalent bicyclic graph in
B_{2}(p,q,r).
So,
G
is composed of cycles identified by, at most, one vertex. Also, in light of the results of Theorem
4.3
,

G
is obtained by identifying cycles of lengths multiple to
3
or
4
. Notice that each pair of cycles should be identified by their soft nodes; otherwise, the degree of a vertex
i
of value
+1
(or
-1
) would increase while
\lambda
is fixed, and then
\lambda<d_{i}+\bar{d}_{i}.
The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

[
Trivalent
cactus graphs
] Trivalent cactus graph are

•

if
\lambda=1
, collection of stars

•

if
\lambda=2

P_{2}
and cycles

and zero vertices, all connected by equal links;

•

if
\lambda=3
, cycles
C_{3k}
intersecting at zero vertices
and zero vertices, all connected by equal links,

and extending with soft nodes.

Proof.

Consider a trivalent cactus graph. Taking out equal links
we obtain
p
connected components.
Consider one of these connected components, it falls into
the following three cases:

case 1
Isolated vertices. These vertices all have value 0,
otherwise the conditions for a trivalent graph are not satisfied,
see Theorem
2.7
.

case 2
Bivalent. If no cycle is present, the component is

P_{2}
(see Theorem
3.1
).

If a cycle is present, it is unique, and the component is reduced to
this cycle. From Theorem
5.4
, this cycle is

and
\lambda=4
.

case 3
Trivalent. Then from Theorems
3.3
and
5.6

the component is either

(a) a star
S_{2k+1}
with
0
in the center and an equal number
k
of vertices
+1
and
-1

\lambda=1
;

(b) a collection of cycles
C_{4k}
intersecting at zero vertices,
\lambda=2;

(c) a collection of cycles
C_{3k}
intersecting at zero vertices,
\lambda=3
.

Putting back the links, if there exists a cycle
C_{2k}
and
\lambda=4

(case 2) then all connected components should be even cycles

This rules out bivalent components consisting of a cycle

;
however bivalent
P_{2}
components are allowed for
\lambda=2
.

To conclude, there should be at least one trivalent component (case 3) connected to

.
∎
