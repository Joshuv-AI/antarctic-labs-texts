Early State Exclusion in 7-Qubit Spin Chains University of Hartford, West Hartford, CT ()
Mia G. Escobar, Valentin Garcia

Abstract
The existence of infinite families of N × N N\times N Jacobi matrices representing the Hamiltonians of quantum spin chains with and without early state exclusion (ESE) has been shown to exist for any even N ≥ 4 N\geq 4 . However, their existence for odd N ≥ 7 N\geq 7 has remained an open problem. In Section 3, we consider a chain of qubits experiencing nearest-neighbor interactions with environmental effects and present infinite families of 7 × 7 7\times 7 Jacobi matrices with and without ESE.

Introduction

Perfect state transfer (PST) in one-dimensional quantum spin chains is widely studied for its applications in quantum information processing. The study of PST was pioneered by Bose
[
1
]
, who analyzed chains with open boundary conditions governed by time-independent Hamiltonians. Subsequently, Kay
[
2
]
later showed that the Hamiltonian of a chain of
N+1
qubits experiencing nearest-neighbor couplings and environmental effects (see Figure
1
) has a matrix representation of the form of a (symmetric) Jacobi matrix
J
of order
N+1
. That is,

J=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}&&0\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&b_{N}\\
0&&b_{N}&a_{N+1}\end{bmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for $a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $b_{i}>0$.}

(1.1)

High fidelity quantum state transfer remains a central problem in quantum computation, with potential to transmit information across quantum systems more efficiently (see
[
3
]
). Here, we focus our attention in transporting states among linear chains from the first to last qubit via the time-evolution operator
e^{-iJt}
. Using the canonical basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
of
\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
with
\mathbf{e}_{k}
representing the excited state of the
(k+1)
th qubit and the convention
\mathbf{e}_{0}=[1,0,\dots,0]^{\top}
, we consider the dynamical evolution of
e^{-iJt}\mathbf{e}_{0}
. Under certain conditions on
J
, there exists some time
T>0
for which the probability of finding an excited state in the last qubit is one. This brings us to our first formal definition.

In some Jacobi matrices exhibiting PST, the probability amplitude of the first qubit has been observed to vanish before the first instance of PST. This phenomenon is referred to as early state exclusion.

Definition 1.3

Let

be a Jacobi matrix realizing PST at earliest time

\tau\in(0,T)
such that

\langle e^{-iJ\tau}\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=0,

then we say that

The authors of

2
Reconstructing Persymmetric Jacobi Matrices

The Hochstadt uniqueness theorem
[
6
]
states that any persymmetric Jacobi matrix
J
of order
N+1
can be uniquely reconstructed from its spectrum
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N}
. After introducing some background in orthogonal polynomials, we conclude this section by explicitly computing the entries of a
7\times 7
persymmetric Jacobi matrix
J
with a symmetric spectrum.

Let
p_{i}
be the characteristic polynomial of the
i
th principal submatrix of the persymmetric Jacobi matrix
J
defined in (
1.1
). Then the sequence of polynomials
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
satisfies the three-term recurrence relation

p_{i}(t)=(t-a_{i})p_{i-1}(t)-b_{i-1}^{2}p_{i-2}(t),

where
p_{0}\equiv 1
and
p_{-1}\equiv 0
. We define an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{w}
on the vector space of polynomials with real coefficients of degree at most
N+1
by

\langle f,g\rangle_{w}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}f(\lambda_{j})g(\lambda_{j})w_{j},

where the weights
w_{j}
are given by

w_{j}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{N}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|}.

Note that
w_{j}
is well-defined as
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
whenever
i\neq j
(see
[
7
]
). It follows from the recursion relation that
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
is an orthogonal sequence with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{w}
. Hence, the entries of
J
can be reconstructed via

a_{i}=\frac{\langle tp_{i-1},p_{i-1}\rangle_{w}}{\|p_{i-1}\|_{w}^{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptb_{i}=\frac{\|p_{i}\|_{w}}{\|p_{i-1}\|_{w}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{w}
denotes the norm induced by the inner product.

2.2
7\times 7
Persymmetric Jacobi Matrices with Symmetric Spectrum

For any positive real numbers
0<x<y<z
, the general form of a
7\times 7
persymmetric Jacobi matrix with symmetric spectrum
\{0,\pm x,\pm y,\pm z\}
is given by

J=\begin{bmatrix}0&b_{1}&&&&&0\\
b_{1}&0&b_{2}&&&\\
&b_{2}&0&b_{3}\\
&&b_{3}&0&b_{3}\\
&&&b_{3}&0&b_{2}\\
&&&&b_{2}&0&b_{1}\\
0&&&&&b_{1}&0\end{bmatrix},

where the off-diagonal elements are

b_{1}=\frac{xz}{\sqrt{x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2}}},\hskip 10.00002ptb_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{(y^{2}-x^{2})(z^{2}-y^{2})}{x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptb_{3}=\sqrt{\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2}}{2}}.

To compute the inner product in (
1.4
), we label the nonnegative eigenvalues of
J
as
\lambda_{0}=0
,
\lambda_{1}=x
,
\lambda_{2}=y
, and
\lambda_{3}=z
. Applying Sylvester’s formula yields

e^{-iJt}=\sum_{j=0}^{6}e^{-i\lambda_{j}t}J_{j},

where
\lambda_{k}=-\lambda_{6-k}
for
k=1,2,3
and
J_{j}
is the Frobenius covariant

J_{j}\equiv\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{6}\frac{J-\lambda_{j}I}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}

with
I
acting as the
7\times 7
identity matrix. We consider the inner products

\displaystyle\langle(J^{2}-x^{2}I)(J^{2}-y^{2}I)(J^{2}-z^{2}I)\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=\frac{(y^{2}-x^{2})(z^{2}-y^{2})}{2y^{2}(x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2})}

\displaystyle\langle(J^{2}-y^{2}I)(J^{2}-z^{2}I)(J^{2}\cos{xt}-xJ\sin{xt})\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=\frac{z^{2}(z^{2}-y^{2})\cos{xt}}{2(z^{2}-x^{2})(x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2})}

\displaystyle\langle(J^{2}-x^{2}I)(J^{2}-z^{2}I)(J^{2}\cos{yt}-yJ\sin{yt})\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=\frac{x^{2}z^{2}\cos{yt}}{2y^{2}(x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2})}

\displaystyle\langle(J^{2}-x^{2}I)(J^{2}-y^{2}I)(J^{2}\cos{zt}-yJ\sin{zt})\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=\frac{x^{2}(y^{2}-x^{2})\cos{zt}}{2(z^{2}-x^{2})(x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2})},

which by linearity yield

\displaystyle\langle e^{-iJt}\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2y^{2}(z^{2}-x^{2})(x^{2}-y^{2}+z^{2})}\Big((y^{2}-x^{2})(z^{2}-x^{2})(z^{2}-y^{2})

(2.5)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+y^{2}z^{2}(z^{2}-y^{2})\cos{xt}+x^{2}z^{2}(z^{2}-x^{2})\cos{yt}+x^{2}y^{2}(y^{2}-x^{2})\cos{zt}\Big).

We can express this equivalently as

A(t)=\langle e^{-iJt}\mathbf{e}_{0},\mathbf{e}_{0}\rangle=c_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}c_{k}\cos{\lambda_{k}t},

(2.6)

where the coefficients above are

c_{k}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{3}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{k}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}|}\Bigg/\sum_{j=0}^{3}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{3}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{j}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}|}.

Infinite Families of
7\times 7

7\times 7
Jacobi matrices without ESE, and an infinite family of
7\times 7
Jacobi matrices with ESE. We focus specifically on those with symmetric spectra.

on
\left(\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{2m}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},\pi-\frac{2\pi}{2m+1}\right)
, independently of
m
.

Finally, we show that
A(t)>0
for
t\in\left(\pi-\frac{2\pi}{2m+1},\pi\right)
.

A
and
B
for

Let
B(t)
be the
12
th order Taylor polynomial of
A(t)
centered at
t=\pi
; i.e.,

\displaystyle B(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{(6)}(\pi)}{6!}(t-\pi)^{6}+\frac{A^{(8)}(\pi)}{8!}(t-\pi)^{8}+\frac{A^{(10)}(\pi)}{10!}(t-\pi)^{10}+\frac{A^{(12)}(\pi)}{12!}(t-\pi)^{12}

\displaystyle=(t-\pi)^{6}\left(\frac{A^{(6)}(\pi)}{6!}+\frac{A^{(8)}(\pi)}{8!}(t-\pi)^{2}+\frac{A^{(10)}(\pi)}{10!}(t-\pi)^{4}+\frac{A^{(12)}(\pi)}{12!}(t-\pi)^{6}\right).

Since
A^{(12)}(\pi)<0
, it follows that
B(t)\leq A(t)
for every
0\leq t\leq\pi
. Define the polynomial

R(x)=\frac{A^{(6)}(\pi)}{6!}+\frac{A^{(8)}(\pi)}{8!}x+\frac{A^{(10)}(\pi)}{10!}x^{2}+\frac{A^{(12)}(\pi)}{12!}x^{3}

so that
B(t)=(t-\pi)^{6}R\big((t-\pi)^{2}\big)
.

We claim that
R(x)
has exactly one real root. To see why, notice that its derivative

R^{\prime}(x)=\frac{A^{(8)}(\pi)}{8!}+\frac{2A^{(10)}(\pi)}{10!}x+\frac{3A^{(12)}(\pi)}{12!}x^{2}

is a quadratic polynomial with a negative discriminant and a negative leading coefficient. Thus,
R(x)
is a strictly decreasing cubic polynomial with exactly one real root
x_{0}>0
since
A(0)=A^{(6)}(\pi)/6!>0
. This implies that
t_{0}=\pi-\sqrt{x_{0}}
is the only root of
B(t)
within the interval
(0,\pi)
. Now as

\displaystyle R\left(\left(\frac{2\pi}{2m+1}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{(6)}(\pi)}{6!}+\frac{(2\pi)^{2}A^{(8)}(\pi)}{8!(2m+1)^{2}}+\frac{(2\pi)^{4}A^{(10)}(\pi)}{10!(2m+1)^{4}}+\frac{(2\pi)^{6}A^{(12)}(\pi)}{12!(2m+1)^{6}}>0,

then it follows that
\big(\frac{2\pi}{2m+1}\big)^{2}<x_{0}
. Hence
t_{0}<\pi-\frac{2\pi}{2m+1}
, which means that
A(t)>0
for all
t\in(0,\pi)
. This completes the proof.

3.2
Infinite Family of
7\times 7
Jacobi Matrices with ESE

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer. Then the Jacobi matrix
J
realizing PST with symmetric spectrum

\{0,\pm(2m+1),\pm(2m+2),\pm(2m+3)\}

has ESE
2m
times.

Proof 3.7
.

Consider the polynomial
P
defined by
P(\cos{t})\equiv A(t)
. Then
P
has a root with multiplicity
3
at
x=-1
by Remark
3.1
. Since
\deg{P}=2m+3
, it follows from the fundamental theorem of algebra that
P
has at most
2m
roots in
(-1,1)
. Hence
J
has ESE at most
2m
times by the bijective correspondence between the roots of
A
over
(0,\pi)
and the roots of
P
over
(-1,1)
.

To show that
J
has ESE at least
2m
times, it suffices to check by Bolzano’s theorem that the sign of
A
changes at least
2m
times over
(0,\pi)
using the test points
t_{k}=\frac{k\pi}{2m+2}
for every integer
0\leq k\leq 2m-1
. Note that the explicit form of
A
can be obtained from (
2.5
) as

\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)}\big(2(4m+5)(4m+3)+(4m+5)(m+1)(2m+3)^{2}\cos{(2m+1)t}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+2(4m^{2}+8m+3)^{2}\cos{(2m+2)t}+(2m+1)^{2}(4m^{2}+7m+3)\cos{(2m+3)t}\big).

If
k
is even, then
k=2l
for some integer
0\leq l<m
and hence

A(t_{2l})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos\frac{l\pi}{m+1}\right)>0.

If instead
k
is odd, then
k=2l+1
for some integer
0\leq l<m
and so

\displaystyle A(t_{2l+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{8(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)}\Bigg(8m^{4}+32m^{3}+36m^{2}+8m-3

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+4(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)\cos\frac{(2l+1)\pi}{2m+2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-1}{8(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)}\Bigg(8m^{4}+32m^{3}+36m^{2}+8m-3

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-4(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)\cos\frac{3\pi}{2m+2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{-1}{8(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)}\Bigg(8m^{4}+32m^{3}+36m^{2}+8m-3

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-4(m+1)^{2}(2m^{2}+4m+3)\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{3\pi}{2m+2}\Big)^{2}+\frac{1}{24}\Big(\frac{3\pi}{2m+2}\Big)^{4}\right)\Bigg)<0.

Thus
A(t_{k})
alternates in parity as
k
alternates in parity, which implies that
A
has at least
2m
roots. Combining this with our last result shows that
J
has ESE exactly
2m
times.

3.3
Rescaling and Translation of Jacobi Matrices

It is important to note that both PST and ESE remain invariant under scaling and translation of Jacobi matrices. To see why, notice that (
1.3
) implies that
J
realizes PST if and only if for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
c>0
, the matrix
cJ+\lambda I
realizes PST. One may also extend the results from Theorem 1.1 in
[
5
]
to show that ESE also remains invariant under scaling and translation of Jacobi matrices. Thus we can generalize our results from Theorems
3.2
and
3.6
to encapsulate
centered
spectra of the form
\{\lambda+c\lambda_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
satisfying (
1.3
).

4
Observations and Future Work

Through numerical experiments (see Figures
6
-
8
), we observed that the ratios between the smallest positive eigenvalue of a
7\times 7
Jacobi matrix realizing PST and its other positive eigenvalues determined whether or not
J
experienced ESE. In particular, we arrived at the following conjecture.

Conjecture 4.1
.

Let
J
be a
7\times 7
Jacobi matrix with symmetric spectrum realizing PST. Then
J
does not have ESE if and only if its positive eigenvalues are integer multiples of the smallest positive eigenvalue.

We believe that the techniques used to prove Theorems
3.2
and
3.6
cannot be replicated here. More generally, we can extend this conjecture for an arbitrary Jacobi matrix of odd order with symmetric spectrum.
