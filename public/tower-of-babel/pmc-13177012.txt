Methodological Development and Theoretical Framework for the Isotopic Analysis of Mass Spectral Fragments of Phytol Using Orbitrap Mass Spectrometry
Authors: Merve Tomečková Öztoprak, Marcel T. J. van der Meer, Ellen C. Hopmans, Laura Villanueva, Stefan Schouten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent advances in Fourier Transform Orbitrap mass spectrometry have enabled precise position-specific isotope analysis (PSIA) of organic molecules at natural abundance by resolving isotopologues in molecular fragments. Here, we present a novel Orbitrap-based method to resolve intramolecular 13C isotopic patterns in a long-chain, apolar lipid at natural abundance. The method uses atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) with higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) for fragmentation, achieving analytical measurement precision of ∼3‰ across 26 fragment ions, measured simultaneously, using <1.2 μg of analyte within a 45 min analysis. We demonstrate the method on phytol, a ubiquitous long-chain, branched isoprenoid alcohol, mostly derived from the side chain of the chlorophyll molecule, and which is the biosynthetic precursor of phytane, a fossil lipid ubiquitously found in sediments. Our results reveal systematic differences in δ13C values of fragment ions indicative of intramolecular 13C heterogeneity within phytol from different biosynthetic origins. This approach provides a robust isotopic fingerprinting tool for tracing the metabolic and environmental history of phytol and other isoprenoid lipids.

Introduction

The development of gas chromatography combustion isotope ratio mass spectrometry (GC/C/IRMS) systems has enabled compound-specific isotope analysis (CSIA), representing a major methodological advance by allowing individual organic compounds to be isotopically characterized within complex mixtures. The analysis of the stable isotope composition of organic compounds has become a powerful tool across diverse scientific disciplines, offering critical insights into the origins and biogeochemical transformations of organic molecules. CSIA was initially developed for the study of lipid biomarkers (i.e., molecular fossils), thereby enabling reconstruction of biological sources, environmental conditions, and biogeochemical processes that prevailed at the time of their deposition (e.g., , ).

One biomarker lipid of major geochemical significance is phytane, which has been found globally in sediments as old as 1 Gyrs. Phytane is predominately derived from phytol, a ubiquitous alcohol which is esterified to chlorophyll pigments, but it can also originate from archaeol, a membrane lipid characteristic of archaea. Phytol and other isoprenoids are synthesized by two distinct and evolutionarily well conserved pathways, the methylerythritol-4-phosphate (MEP) and the mevalonate pathway (MVA). Archaea synthesize isoprenoids exclusively via the MVA pathway, whereas bacteria use the MEP pathway, and eukaryotes may possess both pathways (Supplemental Figure I). Consequently, the stable carbon isotopic composition of phytol and its diagenetic derivative phytane have been extensively used to infer biological sources and paleoenvironmental conditions. For example, Freeman et al. analyzed phytane in the Messel kerogen to infer its photosynthetic source. Furthermore, Witkowski et al. used the molecular average δ13C values of phytane to reconstruct marine primary productivity and atmospheric pCO2 concentrations throughout the Phanerozoic.

Nevertheless, CSIA often cannot unambiguously resolve contributions from distinct biosynthetic pathways or source organisms, or variability associated with carbon fixation mechanisms and environmental factors. This limitation arises because CSIA requires the conversion of molecules into simple gases, thereby averaging the isotopic composition across all positions in a compound. Isotopic fractionation effects, however, act at discrete carbon positions within a molecule. The stable carbon isotopic composition of organic molecules is determined by three key factors: 1) the isotopic properties of the carbon substrate from which they are derived, 2) fractionation effects associated with their synthesis, and 3) the relative partitioning of carbon pools during reversible and irreversible biosynthetic steps. Different biosynthetic pathways utilize distinct precursor pools and reaction sequences, which result in the preferential enrichment or depletion in the 13C isotope at specific carbon positions within a molecule. The intramolecular isotopic structure of organic compounds therefore encodes valuable information about both the biosynthetic origin and the chemical transformations a molecule has undergone (e.g.,). Resolving this information requires analytical approaches capable of resolving intramolecular isotopic variability.

Position-specific isotope analysis (PSIA) aims to address this limitation by resolving isotopic variation at individual positions within a molecule, thereby enhancing the diagnostic power of stable isotope measurements. To date, the only psia of phytol was performed by Schouten et al. In that study, chemical degradation techniques were used to show that the two terminal (C1 and C2) carbon atoms of phytol from a range of photosynthetic organisms were substantially enriched by >20‰ compared to the average δ13C of the remaining carbon atoms. This enrichment was attributed primarily to the strong fractionation effect at the C1 and possibly the C2 carbon atom associated with carbon pool partitioning during the formation of phytol and other isoprenoid derivatives from the geranylgeranyl diphosphate precursor. Isotope effects related to the MEP or MVA biosynthetic pathway origin were not conclusively observed due to the limited number of carbon atom positions analyzed using chemical degradation techniques.

Recently, new methodology for intramolecular isotope ratio analysis has become available using Fourier Transform Orbitrap mass spectrometry. , The exceptional resolving power of the Orbitrap allows for the detection of distinct isotopologues of molecules in nanomolar analyte concentrations. Several studies have already demonstrated the capacity of Orbitrap instruments to precisely (∼1‰) quantify position specific isotope ratios in amino acids at natural abundance (<10 nanomole) − and that Orbitrap analysis achieves a isotopic precision of ∼ 1‰, or better, for diverse organic and inorganic analytes and sample sizes in the nanomolar range. For instance, psia of alanine in the Murchison meteoroid elucidated mechanisms of its interstellar synthesis and allowed the construction of accurate predictive models of relative isotopologue abundances for related organic compounds of prebiotic significance.

To date, Orbitrap based intramolecular isotope ratio mass spectrometry (Orbitrap-IRMS) analysis techniques have mostly been applied to amino acids and oxyanions, , which are relatively small functional molecules composed of multiple elements, yielding diagnostic mass spectral fragments and distinct isotopologues. To the best of our knowledge, this technique has rarely been applied to study intramolecular isotope ratios of apolar, long chain aliphatic compounds (e.g. , ). Recently, we used gas chromatography combined with Orbitrap mass spectrometry to analyze the stable carbon isotopic composition of a single 85 m/z (C6H14) fragment of phytane (Tomečková Öztoprak et al, unpublished results). We found pronounced carbon isotopic differences between the 85 m/z fragment of phytane originating from cyanobacterial phytol (MEP pathway) and archaeol (MVA pathway). The isotopic difference between the 85 m/z fragment of these two phytane analytes was identical to the isotopic difference in their molecular average δ13C values, suggesting that the 85 m/z fragment was either derived from a mixture of all carbon atoms (i.e., scrambling of carbon positions through multiple fragmentations and condensations during electron ionization) or that the molecular average isotopic signal in phytol mostly reflects the strong enrichment in 13C of the terminal C1 and C2 carbon atoms, as speculated earlier by Schouten et al.

Here, we developed an analytical method to resolve intramolecular isotopic signatures in phytol by measuring isotope ratios of diagnostic mass fragments derived from singly substituted isotopologues of phytol via atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) coupled to multistage Orbitrap mass spectrometry. The singly substituted protonated molecular ion (“M+1”), comprised of all isotopologues of phytol substituted with either a 13C, 2H, or 17O, was mass selected as the precursor ion in the quadrupole and subsequently fragmented via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). Isotopologue abundances of the generated fragment ions were quantified and used to constrain position-specific isotopic signatures in phytol analytes derived from distinct biosynthetic origins. We systematically tested and optimized ionization source parameters (gas flows, temperature, voltages), solvent composition, analyte concentration and infusion duration to ensure signal stability and reproducibility, and determined analytical constraints including detection limits. Instrumental parameters of the mass spectrometer, including mass resolution, automatic gain control (AGC) target, and HCD energy were optimized to maximize ion yields and isotopic precision. We also developed methods for data processing and quality control, including corrections for intermittently missing ion beams across the acquisition. Finally, we demonstrate the presence of nonstochastic intramolecular isotope distributions in phytol and establish a reference framework for the comparative analysis of multifragment isotopologues of phytol, and other long chain biomarker compounds.

Materials and Methods

Samples

For our experiments, we used commercially available phytol (‘industrial phytol’; CAS 7541–49–3; ≥ 97% purity; Sigma-Aldrich). Additionally, phytol was prepared from commercially available pure chlorophyll a from the photosynthetic cyanobacterium Anacystis nidulans (CAS 479–61–8; Sigma-Aldrich) and frozen spinach (purchased from a local supermarket) as described below.

Extraction

All glassware and utensils used for lipid extraction and all following steps were precombusted or solvent-cleaned with dichloromethane (DCM) and methanol. Solvents of LC-MS grade or higher (CHROMASOLV, Honeywell, Seelze, Germany, or HiPerSolv Chromanorm, VWR International, Leuven, Belgium) were used for all extractions and following preparatory and analytical steps.

Chlorophyll was extracted from spinach by repeated ultrasonic solvent extraction in DCM:MeOH [2:1 (v:v)]. Dry total lipid extracts (TLE) from spinach, as well as the commercial cyanobacterial chlorophyll a, were saponified by refluxing with 1 N KOH in methanol (MeOH) for 2 h at 80 °C, releasing the phytol moiety from chlorophyll. After saponification, 2 mL of Milli-Q water was added to each sample and the neutral fraction was extracted by liquid–liquid extraction with n-hexane. The aqueous phase containing phytol was acidified with hydrochloric acid (pH < 2) and repeatedly extracted with DCM. The combined DCM extract was dried and separated into three fractions of increasing polarity via an activated aluminum oxide column, where phytol was collected in the final DCM:MeOH [1:1 (v:v)] fraction.

Isolation

Phytol-containing fractions from spinach were further purified via semipreparatory High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) following a method adapted from Smittenberg et al. (2002). Briefly, separation was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II UHPLC system equipped with XBridge BEH C18 OBD Prep column (5 μm, 130Å, 10 mm × 250 mm, Waters Corp). A maximum of 5 mg of material dissolved in 200ul MeOH was injected per preparatory run. Samples were eluted with a gradient of solvent A (H2O:MeOH [15:85 (v:v)]) to solvent B (isopropyl alcohol:MeOH [1:1 (v:v)]) at a flow rate of 3 mL/min. The elution gradient started with 5% B for 3 min. Subsequently, from minutes 3 to 12, %B was increased to 60%, followed by an increase to 81% B from 12 to 32 min. The column was washed with 100% B for 23 min and re-equilibrated to starting conditions over 20 min. The eluent was collected using a Foxy R1 Fraction Collector (Teledyne ISCO, Lincoln, NE, USA) in 1 min fractions which were subsequently analyzed for phytol content by flow injection analysis (FIA). Samples containing more then 5 mg of material were injected multiple times and combined in the same collection tubes.

Flow Injection Analysis

Phytol-containing fractions were identified via FIA using a Q Exactive plus quadrupole-Orbitrap HRMS with an Ion Max source (ThermoFisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with an APCI probe and connected to an Agilent 1290 UHPLC, which provided the probe with a total flow of 0.1 mL min–1 of 100% MeOH. The settings of the Orbitrap system are specified in Sections and Sections . Ten μL of each collected fraction was injected into a stainless-steel loop connected to a six-port switching valve. A total of eight phytol-containing fractions, which were identified by the presence of the mass 295.3 m/z [M-2+H]+ ion in MS mode, were combined and concentrated.

Quantification

Phytol samples were checked for purity and quantified via gas chromatography – flame ionization detection (GC-FID) as described in Witkowski et al. (2018). Briefly, the Agilent 6890N GC-FID was equipped with a CP Sil-5 fused silica capillary column (50 m × 0.32 mm, 0.12 μm film thickness) with Helium as the carrier gas. The GC was initially set to 70 °C, subsequently ramped up to 200 °C at a rate of 20 °C min–1, followed by a second temperature ramp of 4 °C min–1 to 320 °C where it was held for 10 min. Our target phytol peak eluded at ∼ 19 min. Total phytol abundances were quantified relative to our internal standard (2-nonadecanone) by coinjecting a subfraction of the sample, silylated using N,O-Bis­(trimethylsilyl)­trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) and pyridine, with 5ug of internal standard.

δ13C and δ2H Molecular Average Isotope Ratio Measurements

For molecular average stable isotope ratio analysis of phytol, subsamples were silylated using BSTFA and pyridine with known isotopic composition. Silylated phytol was analyzed using a Thermo Trace GC (1310) coupled to a Thermo Delta V IRMS via an Isolink II, equipped with a combustion or high temperature conversion reactor, and ConFlo IV. The GC was equipped with a CP Sil-5 fused silica capillary column (50 m × 0.32 mm, 0.12 μm film thickness) and operated using the same temperature program as the GC-FID. The measured δ13C values of silylated phytol samples, expressed relative to the V-PDB standard, were calculated by comparison to a CO2 reference gas (calibrated against NBS-19). Chromatographic performance of the GC-C-IRMS was checked daily using an in-house developed GC standard containing n-alkanes and fatty acids coinjected with two perdeuterated n-alkanes with a certified stable carbon isotope value (IAEA) to monitor the quality of the stable isotope results. For hydrogen isotope analysis using the high-temperature conversion reactor (GC-HTC-IRMS), Mix B (A. Schimmelmann, Indiana University) was analyzed daily to monitor the systems performance. Standard deviation of our measurement was determined using multiple injection of phytol and attained a value of ± 0.56‰ for δ13C and ± 1.3‰ for δ2H. Delta values of phytol were corrected for isotope values of carbon and hydrogen contributed by the TMS group added during silylation.

Orbitrap Analysis

Our analytical method was developed using a Q Exactive plus quadrupole-Orbitrap HRMS with an Ion Max source, equipped with a syringe pump and connected to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system, consisting of a binary pump, autosampler, column oven, degasser, and a stainless-steel pressure restrictor column to maintain system backpressure (all Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany). Components of the front-end module of the ion source, namely the ion shield, chamber and corona discharge needle (if applicable), were cleaned between sample runs, and the ion transfer tube was cleaned daily with MeOH. Additionally, an analytical blank of MeOH was run between each sample measurement for 15 min under identical instrument conditions. Finally, a heated cleaning sequence was run after every measurement day, during which the Orbitrap was set up with the APCI probe and supplied with a 100 μL min–1 flow of MeOH from the UHPLC. The Orbitrap was run for 60 min under the following conditions: sheath gas 22, auxiliary gas 4, sweep gas 0, probe temperature 500 °C, capillary temperature 400 °C, s-lens 50, discharge current 2.5 μA. Following this, the system was switched to standby mode and allowed to cool down. Mass calibration was performed every 48 h using a Thermo Scientific Pierce LTQ Velos ESI Positive Calibration Solution using the Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI) probe.

All sample measurements were acquired during two independent analytical sessions comprising multiple consecutive days under identical instrumental conditions. The industrial phytol sample (STD) was analyzed each measurement day, while cyanobacterial chlorophyll-a (CHLA) and spinach-derived phytol (SP) were measured in separate sessions. Analytical session one consisted of measurements acquired in the sequence STD/STD/CHLA/CHLA and STD/CHLA/CHLA/CHLA, while session two followed the sequence STD/SP, STD/SP, and STD/SP. Analytical blanks were run between acquisitions, and a heated cleaning sequence was performed after each measurement day (see above).

Analytical Method Development

Our analytical method was developed using the industrial phytol sample for all experiments described below. Analyte concentrations and flow rate were adjusted by modifying the syringe pump and eluent flow from an Agilent 1290 Infinity II HPLC system. Phytol was introduced using a 500 μL gastight syringe (1750 RN, Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) mounted in the syringe pump and connected via a T-split to the HPLC eluent line. We evaluated phytol concentration, solvent composition, mode of ionization, as well as source conditions and mass spectral settings to achieve stable ionization condition and obtain a wide range of fragments, which will be discussed in detail in the following sections. In addition to the thorough cleaning procedures as described in section , all sample measurements were preceded by MeOH blanks and brief cleaning runs where the ion source was heated to 400 °C for 15 min and allowed to cool down to operating temperatures. Solvent blanks were deemed acceptable when analyte concentrations were at least 3 orders of magnitude lower than those detected during analytical runs.

Ionization Mode

First, we evaluated the suitability of a HESI probe compared to an APCI for phytol. Previously described isotope ratio measurement applications for LC-Orbitrap MS introduced the analyte using a HESI probe, , which is reported to have favorable ionization efficiencies for various sizes of polar molecules. Phytol, however, is a relatively apolar molecule and similar apolar biomarker lipids have successfully been analyzed using APCI as the ionization method of choice. , Initially, industrial phytol was ionized using either a HESI or an APCI probe (both ThermoFisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). We evaluated total ion current (TIC) intensities, spray stability, and the distributions of molecular ions formed from the analyte in MS mode. Spray stability in the Orbitrap was assessed by monitoring the TIC and injection time (IT), which remain constant under stable ionization conditions (i.e., TIC < 5% SD). Unstable conditions were characterized by sudden and erratic drops of IT to zero, often accompanied by abrupt spikes or fluctuations in TIC.

For trials using the HESI probe, phytol was dissolved in hexane:isopropyl alcohol (IPA) [99:1 (v:v)] or MeOH:IPA [1:1 (v:v)] and infused though a syringe pump where samples were further diluted in-line with effluent from the LC system composed of various solvent mixtures that included different amounts of MeOH, IPA, formic acid, NH3 and H2O. The highest TIC was achieved for phytol dissolved in MeOH:IPA [1:1 (v:v)] infused at 5 μL min–1 with a coflow of 195 μL min–1 MeOH/H2O/formic acid/14.8 M NH3aq [85:15:0.12:0.04 (v:v)]. The MS mass spectrum showed multiple molecular ion adducts, in particular 295 m/z [M-2+H]+, 296 m/z [M-H2O+NH4]+, 312 m/z [M+NH4]+, and 319 m/z [M+Na+H]+ (Figure ). The relative intensities of these different adducts varied strongly between the different experiments, mostly depending on solvent choice, although [M+NH4]+, [M-H2O+NH4]+, [M+Na+H]+ and [M-2+H]+ ions were always present. Importantly, the singly substituted isotopologue peak of the [M-2+H]+ ion 296.303 m/z ([M-2+H]++1) is eluting closely to the major ion peak for 296.331 m/z [M+NH4]+. In any case, it was challenging to obtain a stable signal for any of the adducts over a prolonged (>10 min) time window. Since the accurate analysis of isotopologues requires several tens of minutes of acquisition time to obtain sufficient precision, stability of the target ion signal is considered of utmost importance.

Using the APCI probe, the highest ion yields and stable spray conditions for prolonged periods (>10 min) were obtained for phytol in MeOH infused at concentrations of >5 ng min–1 with the following probe settings: sheath gas (N2) 22, auxiliary gas (N2) 4, sweep gas (N2) 0, probe temperature 300 °C, capillary temperature 200 °C, S-lens 45, discharge current 2.5 μA. The most stable spray conditions were obtained when the probe was supplied with a total flow of 60 μL min–1 of eluent (5 μL min–1 syringe pump and 55 μL min–1 LC flow) for concentrations of phytol ranging from 0.5 μg mL–1 to 500 μg mL–1. Spray conditions were stable for >90 min of infusion. Achieved total ion intensities (∼1 × 10) with APCI were lower than for HESI (∼1.7e6) for similar concentrations, suggesting less efficient ionization. However, the MS mass spectrum obtained via APCI was dominated by the 295 m/z [M-2+H]+ ion of phytol, i.e. a proton adduct of phytol with an additional double bond, as well as its dehydration product at m/z 277 [M-4-H2O]+ and minor peaks for m/z 297 [M + H]+ and other proton adducts with mass 293 m/z [M-4+H]+ and 291 m/z [M-6+H]+. The absolute intensity of the 296.303 m/z [M-2+H]++1 ion is comparable to that of the same ion measured using the HESI source (see Figure ), indicating no disadvantage of APCI for M+1 isotopologue analysis. It is unclear why ionization resulted in the introduction of an additional double bond, but a similar effect has been described previously by Smittenberg et al. (2002) for APCI MS analysis of phytol. Furthermore, this phenomenon was also observed in additional experiments using phytanol, the hydrogenated derivative of phytol, and isophytol, as well as when phytol and isophytol were dissolved in various mixtures of MeOH, hexane, IPA and ethyl acetate (data not shown). Thus, it seems, that the introduction of a double bond is an inherent feature for APCI ionization of phytol and structurally similar compounds. Nevertheless, we do not consider this additional unsaturation to be of concern for our experiments, since we are primarily interested in the comparative analysis of the stable carbon isotopic properties. Ionization under identical conditions should therefore make analytical runs of phytol from discrete sources comparable. Thus, due to the high long-term signal stability, the predominance of a single molecular ion at 295 m/z, and the absence of closely eluting major ions interfering with its singly substituted isotopologues, APCI was selected as the preferred ionization method over HESI for M+1 isotopologue analysis.

Optimizing Settings of the Orbitrap for M+1 Experiments

After identifying [M-2+H]+ as the representative target ion for phytol, we explored its isotopic structure by measuring isotope ratios of mass fragments generated from the “M+1” isotopologues, i.e. the 296.3 m/z ion, which consists of all isotopically substituted versions of phytol that have an isotopic substitution with a cardinal mass increase of 1 anywhere in the molecule. To effectively exclude all other major ions and reduce noise, the M+1 isotopologues of [M-2+H]+, i.e. [M-2+H]++1, were isolated by setting the quadrupole to a mass window of 296.3 ± 0.5 amu. This results in only the 13C, 2H and 17O substituted isotopologues of [M-2+H]+ to be passed on to the HCD cell for fragmentation, along with an unknown contaminant which never exceeded 6% relative to the combined M+1 ion peak in any of our measurements (Figure ). Fragmentation resulted in the generation of 13C, 2H and 17O substituted, as well as unsubstituted isotopologues of mass fragments with carbon chain length of up to C14. The mass resolution of Orbitrap mass analyzer is inversely proportional to the square root of the m/z value and proportional to the acquisition time (Makarov, 2006). Detecting and differentiating ions very close in mass while maintaining a measurement time of <1 h per sample therefore requires us to balance the choice of mass resolution with the acquisition time required to observe enough ions for the accurate measurement of each isotopologue of interest. Comparison of phytol analytes ran at resolutions of 70.000, 140.000 and 280.000, respectively, shows that relative standard errors of <2‰ within a measurement time of 45 min was achieved for most ions generated by fragmentation with a resolution of ≥ 70.000 (Supplementary Figure II). The ion beams for 13C and 2H isotopologues of fragments up to C14H25 + (m/z 193.1956) are baseline separated at a resolution of 70.000, i.e. the required resolving power for 13C12C13 1H25 and C14 2H1H24 is ∼ 66.000.

One further consideration is that low abundance ion beams often fall below the background noise level of the instrument and are not observed in a substantial number of individual scans. These so-called “missing ion” scans, where one or more rare isotopologues for any given fragment is missing, need to be minimized in order to decrease the error of our isotope ratio calculations (cf.). To increase the signal-to-noise ratio of low intensity rare isotopologue peaks, the instrument was set to average observations of several microscans, or transients, into a single scan cycle. Comparison of 30 min phytol measurements performed under identical conditions using 1, 3, and 5 microscans per scan cycle, respectively, shows that the relative number of scans in which one isotopologues of any fragment ion is missing decreases from 20.4% for 1 microscan per cycle, to 12.4% for 3 microscans, to 8.8% for 5 microscans per scan cycle. To illustrate this, we can look at 2H isotopologues for the most abundant fragments C6H11 to C8H15. While 2H isotopologues of these fragments are frequently observed for 3 and 5 microscan measurements, they are completely missing for measurements with 1 microscan per scan cycle (Supplementary Table I). These frequently missing low abundance rare isotopologues in measurements with 1 microscan per scan cycle introduce a bias toward lower isotope ratios compared to measurements averaging 3 and 5 microscans. Due to the improved signal-to-noise ratio and more reliable detection of low-abundance isotopologues while still obtaining a sufficient number of scans within an acquisition time <60 min, we chose to set our instrument to 3 microscans per scan cycle for all further measurements.

The most important control on the number of ions observed in a single transient is the AGC target setting, which determines the number of ions that need to be accumulated in the C-trap prior to injection to the mass analyzer. While low AGC target settings increase the number of transients that can be observed within a time unit, abundant ion beams may quickly accumulate and fill the C-trap, introducing a bias against less abundant ion beams and decreasing isotope ratios. For instance, singly substituted 2H and 13C isotopologues of the abundant C7H11 fragment were compared in acquisitions with the AGC target set to values between 2e and 3e. The 2H isotopologue of the C7H11 fragment was observed in fewer scans with the AGC target set to 2e4 (26.7%) and 5e (82.6%) compared to all other AGC target settings (100%), while 13C isotopologues were consistently observed across all AGC target settings. In contrast, when the AGC target is set too high, ion to ion interactions, or so-called “space charge effects”, may occur. For instance, coalescence is a type of space charge effect where low abundance ion beams may shift in frequency of oscillation toward a closely adjacent more intense ion beam, broadening the peak and potentially biasing calculated isotope ratios. This effect is particularly pronounced for ion beams which are relatively abundant in the mass analyzer, whereas coalescence may be negligible for a pair of nearby masses which contribute a lower number of total ions in the same transient. To evaluate the potential impact of ion coalescence on mass accuracy, we examined the observed m/z values for isotopologues of all mass fragments of interest across a range of AGC target settings. No isotopologue exhibited a systematic shift in m/z exceeding ± 2 milli mass units (mmu) relative to the theoretical mass, demonstrating that coalescence was negligible.

An important measure for the stability and sensitivity of an acquisition is the TIC (total ion current), and IT (injection time per scan cycle). If the instrument is performing under AGC control, both IT and TIC should be stable throughout the measurement. We tested AGC target settings for phytol infused at a concentration of 25 ng/min, where maximum IT was set to 3000 ms, and observed that for AGC target settings of 1e6 and 3e the maximum IT of 3000 ms is reached before the AGC target. This results in the C-trap only being filled by ∼ 73% and ∼ 26% per scan cycle, respectively, before injection into the mass analyzer, which reduces the number of effective ion counts observed within the given measurement time. Furthermore, the standard deviation (SD) and relative standard error (RSE) of TIC and IT values are significantly higher for AGC target settings of 2e4, 2e5 and 5e (Supplementary Figure III), showing that signal stability as well as counting statistics deteriorate at ion loads that are too low or too high. Given low SD and RSE values of IT and TIC and consistent observation of 2H isotopologues, the instrument was set to an AGC target of 1e5 for all further measurements.

Using our optimized mass resolution, microscan and AGC target settings (Table ), we determined the limit of detection (LoD) and the limit of quantification (LoQ) by running triplicate measurements of a solvent blank and the industrial phytol sample infused at concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 5000 ng min–1. A solvent blank sample was run after cleaning the ion source between each sample measurement (see section ). The MS spectrum of our target ion [M-2+H]++1, 296.3 m/z for the blank sample only contained minor (ion intensities <3e2) ion beams for 13C isotopologues of C6H9, C6H11, C7H11, C7H13, C8H13, C8H15 and C9H15, which are also the most abundant 13C containing ions in the analyzed industrial phytol sample (see Supplementary Table II). We defined the limit of the blank (LoB) as the mean ion count of fragments in the blank +1.645* standard deviations, the LoD as the LoB + 1.645* standard deviations of the mean ion count of fragments in the 0.25 ng min–1 phytol infusion and LoQ as 3* LoD of fragments. Among the tested concentrations, infusions at a concentration of 25 ng min–1 provided the most stable signal and yielded the highest mean ion count across nearly all detected fragment ions (see Supplementary Figure VI). At the infusion rates of 2.5–25 ng min–1 ion counts were consistently above LoQ for nearly all fragments, while at concentrations above 250 ng min–1, ion counts began to plateau or decline for several fragments, suggesting ion suppression and reduced reproducibility.

Long-term measurement stability was tested by infusing phytol at a concentration of 25 ng min–1 for 6 h. The results demonstrated no significant difference in isotope ratio values between any time segment across our 6-h measurement (Supplementary Figure IV). Analytical reproducibility was tested and demonstrated by comparing the 13C substituted vs unsubstituted isotopologue ion beam ratio of fragments for 25 ng min–1 industrial phytol infused for 45 min (1.125 μg in total) in triplicates performed on the same day, within the same week and over the course of 12 months. The results showed that means of this ratio were not significantly different (ANOVA p > 0.05) between measurements repeated months apart (Supplementary Figure V), while minor but statistically significant differences were observed for triplicate measurements of low abundance short chain mass fragments performed on the same day (C5H9, t(2)=-2.64, p = 0.037).

Processing of Data Obtained by Orbitrap Analysis of Phytol

The first step in processing data from the raw data files generated by the Orbitrap MS is to extract all user defined masses of isotopologues of interest from the RAW files using the proprietary software IsoX. We extracted the isotopically unsubstituted (12C x 1H y or 12C x 1H y 16O; with x and y indicating the number of carbon and hydrogen atoms, respectively), the singly 13C substituted (13C12C x–1 1H y or 13C12C x–1 1H y 16O), the 2H substituted (12C x 2H1H y–1 or 12C x 2H1H y–1 16O) and 17O-subsitituted (12C x 1H y 17O) isotopologues of mass fragments in the MS spectrum of phytol ranging from carbon chain length 5 to 12 with a mass tolerance window of 2 mmu. For each defined isotopologue, IsoX extracts per scan cycle (average of 3 microscans, see above), among other values, the signal intensity, peak resolution, peak noise, measured exact mass, base peak intensity, total ion current and injection time. Subsequently, we processed the data using a custom R-script (https://github.com/IsoTrace/IsoFrag.Phytol), which utilizes selected functions of the IsoOrbi R package. Steps taken for data treatment and their reasoning are described below.

Calculation of Fractional Abundances of Isotopologues and Associated Errors

To compare the multifragment isotopologue measurements of the [M-2+H]++1 parent ion across different samples and acquisitions, the abundance of each substituted isotopologue (13C, 2H, or 17O) is expressed as a fraction of the total isotopologue population for that fragment (including both substituted and unsubstituted species) for each acquisition. This fractional abundance approach ,, directly captures meaningful differences in isotopologue distributions arising from position-specific isotope differences and variations in the molecular average isotopic composition of the phytol molecule.

To calculate fractional abundances of our experimentally observed isotopologues, we first calculated the number of ions per scan from the measured signal intensity values as NIO: 1 NIO=SN×Cnz×(RnR)0.5×μ0.5 where NIO is the number of ions observed in a scan, S is the ion intensity, N is the peak noise, Cn is an empirical constant measured by Makarov and Denisov relating the signal-to-noise ratio to ion counts, here taken to be 4.4 (cf.), z is the charge which is 1 in all cases here, Rn is the reference resolution of 120.000 at which Cn was determined, R is the mass resolution used (70.000; see above), and μ is the number of microscans used (i.e., 3; see above). For each scan j, the fractional abundance ρ of a fragment f = C x H y isotopologue was calculated as follows: 2 ρf,j13C=NIO(C13Cx−112Hy1)NIO(CxHy1)12+NIO(Cx13Cx−112Hy1)+NIO(CxHy2Hy−11)12 and: 3 ρf,j2H=NIO(Cx12Hy2Hy−11)NIO(CxHy1)12+NIO(Cx13Cx−112Hy1)+NIO(CxHy2Hy−11)12

The observed ρ (ρ obs ) value for an isotopologue i of a fragment in each acquisition was then calculated by averaging ρ j across all scans: 4 ρf,obsi=1N∑j=1Nρf,ji With N being the total number of scans. The acquisition error (σAE), was calculated as follows: 5 σAE=Stdev(ρf,ji)N×1ρf,obsi

The shot noise limit was estimated for each using the following equation, as described in 6 σSN=NBNA+NB1NA+1NB Where, NA is the total number of ion counts (N IO ) of the substituted isotopologue e.g. 13 C 1 12 C x ‑1 1 H y and NB is the total N IO of all other isotopologues e.g. 12 C x 1 H y +12 C x 2 H 1 1 H y –1. The σ SN is not a precise estimate (see discussion in M+1), but serves as an indicator for the theoretically lower limit of precision for Orbitrap analysis. Fragments where σAEσSN>2 were excluded as this likely indicated an issue with the measurement (see discussion in).

Treatment of Missing Ion Beams

A requirement for the calculation of fractional abundances of isotopologues is, besides the presence of the unsubstituted isotopologue, also the presence of the singly 13C or 2H substituted isotopologue and, in case of an oxygen-containing fragment, the 17O isotopologue. This is because fractional abundances are defined as the abundance of a substituted isotopologue versus all substituted and unsubstituted isotopologues (cf.)

For O-containing fragments, we note that we were unable to resolve the 17O-isotopologue from the 13C-isotopologue in any fragment given our measurement resolution set at 70,000. For example, separation of C4 13CH9O and C5H9 17O would require a minimum resolution of 100,080. Strictly speaking, the per-scan fractional abundance (ρ j ) of oxygen containing fragments therefore cumulatively represent both 13C and 17O isotopologues (ρ j 13C+17O ). However, the actual contribution of 17O isotopologues is expected to be negligible, because the natural abundance of 17O (0.0374 atom %) is only 3.4% of that of 13C (1.107 atom %) at natural isotope values, and oxygen occurs less frequently than carbon in any of our fragments. We can illustrate this using the example of the C5H9O fragment. Assuming natural abundance of 13C, 2H and 17O as 1.107%, 0.0155% and 0.0374% atom fractions respectively and that unresolved 17O and 13C ion beams combine additively, we can approximate ρj for C5H9O (see) as ρC5H9O2H≈9×0.000155(1+5×0.01107+9×0.000155+1×0.000374)=0.001320(≈0.13%) ρC5H9O17O≈1×0.000374(1+5×0.01107+9×0.000155+1×0.000374)=0.000354(≈0.04%)

The relative increase in 13C fractional abundance due to unresolved 17O is therefore only ρj17Oρj13C ≈ 0.0003540.052359 = 0.00676 (≈0.68%), which corresponds to a negligible increase in δ13C of <0.05‰. Furthermore, even a difference of 200‰ in δ17O between analytes, which far exceeds natural biological variation (typically <5‰), would produce an error of only ∼1.4‰ between apparent δ13C values. We therefore neglect the potential contribution of 17O in subsequent fractional abundance calculations.

In contrast, the contribution of 2H isotopologues is proportionally larger due to both the higher number of hydrogen atoms in fragments and the relatively higher natural abundance of 2H. For the C5H9O fragment, the 2H fractional abundance (∼0.13%) is approximately 3.7 times greater than that of 17O (∼0.04%), leading to a more significant bias on observed isotopologue distributions. Particularly, when the difference in molecular average δ2H values between samples becomes large, then errors in comparative analysis between become larger as well, i.e. a difference of 200‰ in δ2H values leads to an error of ∼1.7‰ in δ13C values. This requires explicitly accounting for 2H substitutions in our analysis, unlike the negligible 17O effect.

The percentage of scans with missing 2H isotopologues was highly variable relative to the unsubstituted isotopologue (see Supplementary Table II). For the analyzed industrial phytol sample, 2H isotopologues were missing in >10% of scans for a majority of fragments in all acquisitions. Since the absence of the 2H ion prohibits the calculation of true fractional abundances of 13C isotopologues (see above), Csernica and Eiler proposed several ways to correct for the absence of an ion beam which we will discuss in more detail below. For fragments where all isotopologues were observed, ρobs can simply be calculated from the measured scans, whereas in scans where for the substituted (13C, 2H, 17O) isotopologues of a fragment are missing ρ j -value is erroneously assigned to be 0 (ρobs,missing). To correct for this, we tested two approaches 1) by estimating ρ 2H values from experimental data and 2) from theoretical estimates.

For the experimental approach, ρ experimental of 2H isotopologues missing in >10% of scans were derived from other fragments where 2H isotopologues were detected in >90% of all scans. This approach requires the assumption that hydrogen and carbon have similar substitution probability for all fragments, which is incorrect (see below), but given the relatively low abundance of the 2H isotopologue in fragments and consequently the small correction term that we need to apply to our ρ obs 13 C values, this assumption can be used to provide a rough prediction of ρ 2H values for each fragment. We normalized ρ obs 2 H values of abundantly observable fragments by the number of atoms (n H ) and averaged the values across fragments. For instance, ρ experimental 2 H was calculated as 7 ρexperimental2H=∑1Fρ12HnH1+ρ22HnH2+...+ρF2HnHFF where F is the number of fragments for which reliable (>90% of scans present) ρ obs 2 H values were available. However, application of this approach showed that the number of fragments for which a reliable ρ obs 2 H could be obtained (i.e., fragments where 2H isotopologue peaks were detected in >90% of scans) was highly variable between samples, and for some sample runs this was not the case for any fragment. This would result in inconsistent adjustments of ρ 13C values (sometimes based on six reliably ρ obs 2 H , sometimes just on one, depending on the sample and quality of measurement), leading to increased experimental error.

We therefore decided to use a theoretical reconstruction approach based on the isotomics framework by Csernica and Eiler and refer to them for further details on the terminology and theoretical foundations. In this framework, fragment specific fractional abundances of isotopologues (ρ f , obs i ) are treated as competitive quantities within the isotopologue population of the precursor ion selected for fragmentation (C20H39O). Because all fragments in the MS experiment sample the same precursor isotopologue population (i.e., the M+1 ion), missing isotopologue contributions can be reconstructed provided that this precursor population can be sufficiently constrained.

For fragments with missing NIOf2H values (see eq ) in >10% of scans, measured ρ f 13 C values were corrected as 8 ρf,corr13C=(1−ρf,theory2H)×ρf,noH213C where ρ f,no 2 H 13C is the 13C fractional abundance calculated from scans missing the 2H isotopologue, and ρ f,theory 2H is a theoretically reconstructed estimate of the true 2H fractional abundance for a fragment based on molecular average isotope measurements.

To derive ρ f,theory 2H we first consider the fractional abundance of the hydrogen isotopologue ρ2 H of a fragment f = C x H y (O) z ignoring the contribution of the 17O (see above): 9 ρf2H=(nH−2)[C12H2]+[H2]APCInC[C13H1]+(nH−2)[C12H2]+[H2]APCI where e.g. [12C2H] is the isotope concentration of a singly substituted hydrogen atom in the fragment, n C and n H is the number of carbon and hydrogen atoms in the fragment and [2H] is the concentration of the singly substituted hydrogen atom from the proton added during APCI.

To relate the molecular average isotope measurements to the fractional abundances measured in the Orbitrap, two steps are required. First, we calculate the U 2H value, which is the concentration of 2H substituted fragment versus unsubstituted fragment in the Orbitrap. The U 2H is the ‘isotopologue-alternative’ to the use of ratio of 2H/1H (R 2H ) in conventional isotope measurements as this ratio is essentially different in concept, i.e. it is a ratio of isotopologues versus a ratio of elemental concentrations of hydrogen. This U 2H can be calculated from R 2H value, i.e. the actual elemental isotope ratio of the fragment, if we assume that the substituted atoms are randomly distributed across all isotopologues; (the stochastic assumption; cf. Csernica and Eiler). If so then U 2H ≈ R 2H . The isotope ratio of a fragment comprises both the R 2H values of the C-bonded hydrogen in the phytol backbone measured by HTC-IRMS, and the R 2H values for hydrogen introduced by the APCI: 10 U2H=[Cx12Hy]2[Cx12Hy]1≈RIRMS2H+RAPCI2H

For oxygen containing fragments R OH 2H should also be included in U 2H . Furthermore: 11 RIRMS2H=[H2][H1]=RVSMOW2H(1+δ2HIRMS1000) where for VSMOW (Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water) a value of 0.0001557530 was used. Note that since the hydrogen of the hydroxy group of phytol and the hydrogen introduced by the APCI were not measured we assume a δ2H of 0‰. Furthermore, we also assume absence of isotope fractionation during MS fragmentation, such that substituted and unsubstituted 2H isotopologues are transmitted proportionally from the whole molecule to the fragment.

The final step is to relate the fragment level U f 2H to the fractional abundance ρ f 2H of the 2H isotopologue in each fragment. This is done using the U M+1 value, which is a parameter that relates all singly substituted isotopologues to all unsubstituted isotopologues: 12 UM+1=[Cx13Hy1O16]+[Cx12Hy2O16]+[Cx12Hy1O17][CxHy1O16]12=Uf2Hρf,theory2H

This parameter can be determined experimentally by measuring singly substituted [M-2+H]++1 ion (m/z 296.3) and the unsubstituted [M-2+H]+ (m/z 295.3) of our precursor molecule C20H39O: 13 UobsM+1=NIO296.3m/zM+1NIO295.3m/zM

For reference, the U M+1 value can also be calculated using isotope ratios derived from independently measured molecular average values as 14 UIRMSM+1=nCUIRMS13C+(nH−2)UIRMS2H+U2H,OH+U2H,APCI+U17O where n C , n H and n O are the number of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in the precursor ion C20H39O. Two hydrogen counts are subtracted from n H to account for the hydrogen attached to the hydroxy group of phytol which is lost during silylation before measurement of the molecular average δ2H value and the proton added during APCI. U 13 C was calculated using the value of 0.011113 (i.e., Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite), U 17 O was calculated using the value of 0.0003799 (i.e., VSMOW). Differences between U obs M+1 and U IRMS M+1 values would primarily reflect mass fractionation effects associated with formation of the [M-2+H]+ ion of phytol (“instrumental fractionation“ c.f.), and to a lesser degree the error associated with our assumptions of δ2H = 0‰ for the hydrogen introduced by the APCI and the hydrogen in the hydroxy group of phytol and δ17O = 0‰.

Finally, for fragments in which the 2H isotopologue was missing in >10% of scans, the theoretical ρ f,theory 2H value was used to reconstruct the incomplete isotopologue population. For a fragment f = C x H y O z , ρ f,theory 2H was calculated using U IRMS 2H from eq as 15 ρf,theory2H=(nH−2)UIRMS2H+U2H,OH+U2H,APCIUM+1 And for a fragment C x H y as 16 ρf,theory2H=(nH−1)UIRMS2H+U2H,APCIUM+1

Patterns in ρ obs 13 C Values of Mass Spectral Fragments of Phytol

The M+1 experiments were performed by continuous infusion (25 ng/min infusions for 45 min, 1.125 μg total) of the industrial phytol standard, phytol from A. nidulans and spinach-derived phytol samples. Thirty-three distinct mass fragments were identified in the MS2 spectrum of the [M-2+H]++1 ion of our phytol analytes. A few fragments, in particular C8H17, C9H19, C9H17O, C11H21 and C12H23, had missing unsubstituted and/or 13C substituted isotopologues in more than 10% of scans and were therefore removed entirely from our data set (see representative example for industrial phytol, Supplementary Table II), leaving twenty-six mass fragments for analysis. Comparison of acquisition errors to the shot noise limits (Supplementary Table II and Supplementary Figure VII) showed that for all remaining fragments σAEσSN is <2, with values ranging from 1.49 to 1.70. Indicating that our measurements closely approached the theoretically best achievable measurement precision under the experimental constraints of M+1 analysis, and are consistent with previously reported ESI-Orbitrap-IRMS measurements. For these twenty-six fragments, ρ f , obs 13 C values where 2H isotopologues were missing in more than 10% of scans were corrected as described in section .

Plotting ρ f,corr 13C values against fragments in order of increasing chain length (Figure top) shows that ρ f,corr 13C values of all fragments consistently increase with increasing carbon chain length. This is expected in an M+1 experiment, as the longer the fragment carbon chain length becomes, the higher the chances are that the fragment will contain the 13C atom. However, differences are notable between fragments containing three unsaturations (general formula C x H x–5), fragments with two unsaturations (C x H x–3), and fragments with a single unsaturation (C x H x–1). The double bonds maybe 1) inherited from the precursor ion [M-2+H]++1, which already has 2 double bonds; 2) generated by HCD fragmentation-induced rearrangements, including dehydrogenation (e.g., from alkyl chains to form alkanes); or 3) originate from charge-remote fragmentation, where cleavage occurs sequentially along the hydrocarbon chain, distant from the charge at the functionalized end of phytol. We observe that ρ f,corr 13C values decrease as the degree of unsaturation increases among fragments with identical carbon chain lengths. Additionally, the relative differences in these shifts between fragments with different levels of unsaturation become more pronounced as the carbon chain length increases. The ρ f,corr 13C values of the few oxygen-containing fragments (C x H x–3O) generally lie between fragments of C x H x–3 and C x H x–5 of equivalent carbon chain length, with a tendency to be somewhat closer to the triunsaturated fragments.

Modeling the Intramolecular Isotopic Structure of Phytol from ρ f,corr 13C Values

To evaluate whether ρ f,corr 13C values indicate intramolecular heterogeneity within phytol, we compared our observed values to values predicted by theoretical models of phytol’s carbon isotopic structure. The purpose of this analysis is not to derive absolute position-specific δ13C values, but rather to assess whether the observed fragment-specific ρ f,corr 13C values are consistent with a heterogeneous or homogeneous intramolecular isotopic distribution.

We used a theoretical model that assumes a homogeneous 13C isotopic distribution across the entire phytol molecule based on the molecular average isotope values and the theoretical framework described in section . Under this model, all carbon and hydrogen atoms share the same substitution probability. For a fragment f = C x H y , the expected ρ f,homo 13C was calculated as 17 ρf,homo13C=nCUf13CUM+1 , where n C is the number of carbon atoms in f.

Then, we constructed a heterogeneous model (ρ f,het 13C ) by estimating position-specific 13C enrichments from ρ f,corr 13C values. This requires assigning carbon positions to each fragment based on plausible fragmentation pathways. However, the exact fragmentation pattern can only be resolved by analyzing synthesized phytol with known 13C-labels at specific positions, which was not available for this study. Therefore, assignments made here are necessarily speculative. The most straightforward assignments can be made for the oxygen-containing diunsaturated fragments, which must originate from part of the phytol molecule containing the hydroxy group at carbon position C1 and the double bond at C2 (see Figure for phytol carbon numbering). Similarly, monounsaturated fragments likely predominantly derive from the saturated tail part of the phytol molecule, starting from the carbon at position C16. The origins of triunsaturated and diunsaturated nonoxygenated fragments are more ambiguous; however, the similarity of ρ f,corr 13C values between triunsaturated and oxygen-containing fragments suggests a likely origin from the functionalized end, whereas distinct isotope patterns of diunsaturated nonoxygenated fragments indicate a different, plausibly tail-derived source.

We subsequently reconstruct the underlying isotopic structure of the industrial phytol standard using a position-specific extension of Mass Isotopomer Distribution Analysis (MIDA). A system of linear regressions was formulated in an “inheritance matrix”, where each fragment ρ f,corr 13C value was modeled as the sum of 13C incorporation at individual carbon positions within the fragment (see Supplementary Table III for fragment carbon position assignment). Solving this linear system gives estimates of relative position-specific 13C enrichments. Using the inheritance matrix, we reconstructed modeled ρ 13C values for each fragment (ρ f,het 13C ) by summing the position-specific enrichments of all carbon positions present in each fragment. This allows for direct comparison between modeled ρ f,het 13C and experimental ρ f,corr 13C values, where differences (i.e., residuals ε) identify potential incorrect assignments of fragment origin, which can be iteratively adjusted for, or systematic biases during fragmentation e.g. differential fragmentation and ionization efficiency among molecular regions. Some carbon positions cannot be resolved due to fragment size limitations (e.g., C1–C3) and were therefore treated as isotopically uniform.

Comparison of observed ρ f,corr 13C values with homogeneous and heterogeneous predictions (Figure ) shows that oxygen-containing diunsaturated and triunsaturated fragments are well approximated by both models, consistent with limited isotopic heterogeneity at the functionalized end. In contrast, monounsaturated and diunsaturated nonoxygenated fragments increasingly deviate from the homogeneous model with fragment size, while the heterogeneous model substantially reduces residuals. This pattern indicates pronounced intramolecular 13C heterogeneity localized in the alkyl tail of phytol. The 13Cρ values of individual carbon atoms inferred from the inheritance matrix reveal distinct isotopic patterns across the phytol structure (Figure ; Supplementary Table III), but we refrain from extensively discussing this because of its speculative nature. However, we do find a 13C enrichment of the carbon atoms at the functionalized (head) end of phytol relative to the mean of the remaining carbon positions of the molecule, which aligns with observations by Schouten et al., the only other experimental evidence for isotopic heterogeneity in phytol prior to this study. Importantly, while uncertainty in fragment–position assignments in our model precludes determination of position-specific carbon isotopic enrichments, the modeling approach applied here does suggest that nonrandom intramolecular 13C structure can be resolved from MS isotopologue data once fragmentation pathways can be rigorously quantified.

A Reference Framework for the Comparative Analysis of Intramolecular Fragments of Phytol

Although the above demonstrates that the analysis of observed fractional isotopologue abundances between fragments reveals heterogeneous 13C signatures in phytol, it is difficult to relate these findings to the more commonly used δ scale which are tied to accepted isotope standards. To enable meaningful comparisons between phytol from different sources, we established an intramolecular reference framework that expresses ρ f,corr 13C values on a relative δ scale.

For this, we first need to calculate U f 13C , which derives from eq for a fragment f = C x H y as 18 nCUf13C=ρf,corr13C×UobsM+1

Assuming stochastic distribution of 13C within each fragment, the mean carbon isotopic composition of a fragment f = C x H y can be calculated as 19 Uf13C®=Uf13CnC where n C denotes the number of carbon atoms in the fragment. Model calculations showed that the error in Uf13C® would exceed 1‰ only if the excess abundance of clumped 13C isotopes were >30‰, which are highly unlikely for natural samples.

To construct the intramolecular isotope reference framework, we followed the common approach of expressing isotope ratios of samples relative to a reference material measured under identical conditions. Fragment specific δ13C values were calculated relative to the corresponding fragment of the industrial phytol sample (STD): 20 δ13Csample−STD=(Uf,sample13C®Uf,STD13C®−1)×1000

Each sample acquisition was paired with the closest STD acquisition measured within <6 h of each other, rather than strict bracketing (see 2.7), as long-term stability tests suggest negligible intraday drift over this time window (Supplementary Figure IV). Analytical uncertainty of δ13Csample−STD was propagated from σ AE values of Uf13C® for both sample and STD, assuming independence of both measurements, as 21 σsample−STD=Uf,sample13C®Uf,STD13C®σAE,sample2+σAE,STD2×1000

Individual acquisition errors for most fragments ranged between ∼ 1–3‰ (Supplementary Table IV). Reproducibility of fragment-specific δ13Csample−STD values was evaluated from replicate acquisitions within and across analytical days. For CHLA, fragment specific standard deviations (SD) values were typically between 1 and 3‰ (median ≈ 3‰; Figure ), with monounsaturated fragments showing elevated variability (up to ∼ 12‰; Figure ). Replicate standard deviations of CHLA acquisitions were generally comparable to or moderately larger than acquisition errors, indicating that analytical precision accounts for a substantial fraction of total variability. In contrast, replicate SP values have greater variability for several fragments, with SD values between 5 and 15‰ and some fragments exceeding 20‰ (Figure ). Differences in reproducibility among fragment groups were also notable, where triunsaturated fragments exhibit consistently low SD values in both samples (<3‰) and monounsaturated fragments have considerably higher SD values (up to 12‰ CHLA, 30‰ SP). This may reflect differing sensitivities of fragments originating from different fragmentation pathways to fluctuations in ion-source conditions. Additionally, we observe that day to day variability is larger than within day variability, which demonstrates that δ13Csample−STD values are impacted by instrumental drift despite sample – standard referencing. Variance partitioning using a linear mixed effect model for SP showed that 13% of total variance was attributed to interday variability, 59% to fragment specific effects and 28% to a residual analytical error, with proportional contributions calculated as σ x 2/(σ date 2 + σ fragment 2 + σ residual 2). In contrast, for CHLA, only 4% of total variability can be attributed to interday variability, with 65% of variability was associated with fragment specific effects and 31% with residual analytical error. This confirms that, while day to day instrumental effects contribute measurably to δ13Csample−STD values of fragments between replicate acquisitions, fragment to fragment patterns remain consistent for each sample. Therefore, although individual fragment Csample−STD13 values show relatively large variability, differences in fragment to fragment patterns still reveal clear source-specific isotopic “fingerprints” for our two analytes (Figure and Supplementary Table IV).

In general, A.nidulans-derived phytol fragments are moderately depleted in 13C compared to the STD, with δ13Csample−STD values mostly ranging between 10‰ and −20‰, with a median value of - −1.8‰ across fragments. Spinach-derived phytol fragments have values mostly ranging from −4‰ to 67‰ and a median value of 21‰, with C4H9 and C4H7 having much lower values of ∼ −35‰ and ∼ −20‰. Isotope ratio trends are especially pronounced in fragment pairs where the addition or loss of a single carbon atom leads to opposite isotopic patterns between A.nidulans and spinach-derived analytes. For example, δ13Csample−STD values for A.nidulans-derived phytol fragments from C6H11O to C8H15O are increasing, while that of spinach derived phytol are decreasing with chain length. Similarly, fragments like C5H7 and C6H9, C5H9 and C6H11, C9H17 and C10H19, C9H15, C10H17 and C11H19, C12H21 and C13H23 show divergent values between A.nidulans and spinach-derived phytol. Regardless of the exact origin of these fragment isotopic differences, the magnitude and direction of fragment-to-fragment offsets differ systematically and significantly between analytes. Thus, these contrasting relative isotope ratio patterns point to differences in the underlying isotopic structure between the A.nidulans and spinach-derived phytol analytes. As MEP and MVA pathways have distinct enzymatic steps and precursor pools, isotopic fractionation at specific carbon positions differs, resulting in characteristic intramolecular δ13C signatures in phytol, which we may cause the distinct isotope patterning in the fragments of our A.nidulans (MEP) and spinach-derived (MEP/MVA) phytol analytes. These fingerprints may therefore ultimately serve as specific markers of biosynthetic origin for our analytes.

Conclusion

We developed an analytical method for investigating the intramolecular distribution of 13C in phytol by multifragment analysis of M+1 isotopologues by Orbitrap mass spectrometry. The method produces stable ion beams of collision-induced fragments derived from the M+1 isotopologue of phytol and achieves a measurement precision better than 3‰ for most fragments using less than 1.2 μg of analyte per measurement run and within an acquisition time of 45 min. Our data processing workflow corrects for missing low abundance hydrogen isotopologues for measurements of 13C substituted isotopologue abundances, ρ f , corr 13 C . Comparison of observed ρ f , corr 13 C values with homogeneous and heterogeneous isotope models suggests substantial isotopic heterogeneity within the phytol molecule. To facilitate comparison between samples, we introduce a reference framework that converts fractional abundances of 13C in mass fragments into δ-values relative to a phytol standard measured under identical conditions. Although, for some fragments, day to day variability between replicate acquisitions was much larger than intermeasurement errors, the relative fragment to fragment isotope patterns remained reproducible, enabling comparison of isotopic patterns of phytol from different biological origins. Using this approach, we observe that fragment pairs differing by a single carbon atom exhibit inverse δ13Csample−STD values between A.nidulans and spinach-derived phytol analytes, likely reflecting underlying intramolecular isotopic differences potentially caused by their distinct origins. Thus, this study demonstrates that Orbitrap-based multifragment 13C analysis can resolve characteristic isotopic fingerprints in long-chain apolar lipids at natural abundance, offering a promising tool for tracing the biosynthetic origin and environmental context of phytol and related compounds even at nanomolar (∼4 nmol phytol) abundances found in the environment.
