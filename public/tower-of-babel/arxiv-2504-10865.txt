Understanding the theoretical properties of projected Bellman equation, linear Q-learning, and approximate value iteration
Han-Dong Lim, Donghwan Lee

Abstract
In this paper, we study the theoretical properties of the projected Bellman equation (PBE) and two algorithms to solve this equation: linear Q-learning and approximate value iteration (AVI). We consider two sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution to PBE : strictly negatively row dominating diagonal (SNRDD) assumption and a condition motivated by the convergence of AVI. The SNRDD assumption also ensures the convergence of linear Q-learning, and its relationship with the convergence of AVI is examined. Lastly, several interesting observations on the solution of PBE are provided when using ϵ {\epsilon} -greedy policy.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved significant success, exemplified by the deep Q-network (DQN)
(
Mnih et al.,, 2015
)
. This success can be largely attributed to two algorithms: Q-learning
(
Watkins and Dayan,, 1992
)
and the approximate value iteration (AVI)
(
Bertsekas,, 2011
)
. Understanding the behavior of these algorithms has been a central focus of extensive research.

Q-learning, initially developed by
Watkins and Dayan, (1992)
in a tabular setup where
Q
-values are stored for every state-action pair, has since been the subject of considerable investigation. Both asymptotic and non-asymptotic analysis of the algorithm have been thoroughly explored in works such as
(
Borkar and Meyn,, 2000
;
Even-Dar and Mansour,, 2003
;
Lee and He, 2020b,
;
Chen et al.,, 2022
;
Lee,, 2024
)
, to list a few.

Moving beyond the tabular setup, function approximation is commonly used to address the problem of large state-action spaces in practical scenarios. Specifically, we focus on the simplest form of approximation: the linear function approximation scheme. However, introducing function approximation brings several challenges. In the case of Q-learning with linear function approximation—referred to as linear Q-learning—two major issues arise: 1) the existence of a solution to the projected Bellman equation (PBE) that the algorithm aims to solve, and 2) the stability of the algorithm. While recent works have explored these challenges
(
Melo and Ribeiro,, 2007
;
Meyn,, 2024
)
, there remains significant opportunity for further advancing our understanding in this area.

Meanwhile, value iteration is one of the simplest algorithms in RL when the model is known. By incorporating linear function approximation into the value iteration framework, the approximate value iteration (AVI) scheme has been widely used
Munos, (2007)
;
Mann and Mannor, (2014)
. AVI also seeks to solve the PBE as linear Q-learning does. Nonetheless, it is not well understood, when the AVI algorithm converges while linear Q-learning does not, and vice versa.

Overall, the theoretical understanding of PBE and its related algorithms, specifically Q-learning and AVI, are not well-understood. This motivates our study, and the purpose of this paper is to extend our knowledge on these subjects. The main contributions of this study are outlined in the following:

1.

A sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness of a solution to PBE:

•

We provide a thorough investigation of the existence and uniqueness of a solution to PBE under the assumption of a matrix having strictly negatively row dominating diagonals (SNRDD assumption), which is new in the literature. This assumption includes a wide class of settings: tabular and linear function approximation (with regularization). Moreover, our analysis considers various behavior and target policy scenarios including continuous or lispchitz policies.

•

A sufficient condition, derived from the AVI framework, is provided. We then explore its relationship to the SNRDD assumption, demonstrating that while the two are generally different, they can coincide under specific additional conditions.

2.

We provide a new convergence proofs for a family of Q-learning algorithms and AVI algorithm, respectively. Furthermore, we provide examples where AVI converges while linear Q-learning does not, and vice-versa.

•

The proof of Q-learning relies on ODE arguments based on contraction theory
(
Lohmiller and Slotine,, 1998
)
and the SNRDD assumption. This covers asynchronous tabular Q-learning, linear Q-learning with SNRDD assumption, and regularized Q-learning
(
Lim and Lee,, 2024
)
. To author’s best knowledge, SNRDD is the first assumption that can be applied to prove convergence of both linear and tabular Q-learning in a unified way using a fixed behavior policy. Regarding regularized Q-learning, the existing assumptions on positiveness and orthogonality of feature matrix are relaxed.

•

We provide an example showing that, even though the SNRDD assumption ensures the convergence of Q-learning and the existence of a solution to the PBE, the resulting solution may still lead to a sub-optimal policy.

•

The convergence of AVI follows from the condition that guarantees existence and uniqueness of a solution to PBE.

3.

Lastly, we provide two examples explaining the theoretical properties of solutions to PBE when
{\epsilon}
-greedy policy is used, which is not covered by previous analysis due to its discontinuity. The first example shows depending on the value of
{\epsilon}
, there is a chance of non-existence or multiplicity of the solution even though SNRDD condition is met. The second example shows a pathological phenomenon using
{\epsilon}
-greedy policy that increasing the parameter
{\epsilon}
can ensure a solution to PBE that yields an optimal policy, to which Q-learning cannot converge.

2.1
Markov decision process (MDP)

MDP consists of five tuples
({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},\gamma,{\mathcal{P}},r)

{\mathcal{S}}:=[|{\mathcal{S}}|]

-function, which is the

-function induced by the optimal policy

{\bm{R}}+\gamma{\bm{P}}{\bm{Q}}^{*}={\bm{Q}}^{*}

[{\bm{R}}]_{(s-1)|{\mathcal{A}}|+a}=\mathbb{E}\left[r(s,a,s^{\prime})\mid(s,a)\right]

is a vector such that
[{\bm{Q}}^{*}]_{(s-1)|{\mathcal{A}}|+a}=Q^{*}(s,a)
, where for

[{\bm{v}}]_{i}
denotes
i
-th element of
{\bm{v}}
, and

-th row and

-th column of

3.1

Let us introduce a condition that guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (

Definition 3.1

A matrix

For simplicity, we will call a matrix

is SNRDD if it satisfies Definition
3.1

(
Molchanov and Pyatnitskiy,, 1989
)
or analysis of fixed point problem
(
Davydov et al.,, 2024
)
, which is summarized in Appendix Section
9

{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\max_{i\in[p]}S_{i}({\bm{M}}_{{\bm{\theta}}})<0.

(4)

3.1

4

4.1
Stochastic approximation and ODE approach

Q-learning can be understood as a stochastic approximation scheme
(
Robbins and Monro,, 1951
)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{x}}_{k}+\alpha_{k}({\bm{A}}_{\sigma({\bm{x}}_{k})}{\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{b}}+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k+1}),\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

where

{\mathcal{M}}:=\{1,2,\dots,|{\mathcal{M}}|\}

{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}
is Martingale-difference sequence defined in Definition

\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}={\bm{A}}_{\sigma({\bm{x}}_{t})}{\bm{x}}_{t}+{\bm{b}},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},t\geq 0

(10)

where
\frac{d}{dt}{\bm{x}}_{t}=\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}

{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

and global asymptotic stability means that the solutions
{\bm{x}}_{t}

{\bm{x}}^{*}

{\bm{x}}_{0}

(
Borkar and Meyn,, 2000
)
provided in Appendix
11

Definition 4.1

(One-sided Lipschitz, Definition 3.2 in
Bullo, (2024)
)

For
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}

satisfies the following it is called one-sided Lipschitz with constant
b

[{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})]_{i}[{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}]_{i}\leq b\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|^{2}_{\infty}
where

and
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

If
{\bm{f}}
is one-sided Lipschitz with a negative constant, then every pair of trajectories of (
10

{\bm{x}}_{t}
and
{\bm{y}}_{t}

and
{\bm{y}}_{0}

\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{\infty}\to 0

(

Lemma 4.2

(Theorem 3.9 in
Bullo, (2024)

Suppose Definition

In fact, for a locally Lipschtiz function

In this section, we examine the case when
\epsilon

\epsilon

Change of number of solutions:
In this example, there is a critical value for
\epsilon

Emergence of solution that yields optimal policy but to which Q-learning cannot converge:
We provide an example showing that increasing
{\epsilon}

). Consider an MDP with

\displaystyle h({\bm{x}}):={\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}=\bm{0}.

) can be re-casted into a fixed point problem:
{\bm{x}}+\alpha h({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The study of fixed-point problems has been extensively explored in the literature, with foundational contributions from pioneering works such as
Brouwer, (1911)
and
Banach, (1922)
.

A common method for verifying the existence of a fixed point is to check if the matrix
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}
satisfies a specific condition. We focus on a matrix with a strictly negatively row-dominant diagonal introduced in Definition
3.1
:

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\max_{i\in[p]}S_{i}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}})<0,

(13)

where
S_{i}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}})
is defined in Definition
3.1
, and
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
will be will be formally defined later. This concept has been widely used in the literature of fixed point problem as well as in various system analyses
(
Molchanov and Pyatnitskiy,, 1989
;
Davydov et al.,, 2024
)
.

Now, let us present a simple result that follows from standard argument of Brouwer’s fixed point theorem given in Lemma
10.8
in Appendix
10
:

Lemma 9.1
.

Suppose (
13
) holds with
{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
. Furthermore, if the function
h
in (
12
) is continuous, then, a solution of (
12
) exists.

Proof.

For simplicity of the proof, denote
c:=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\max_{i\in[p]}S_{i}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}})
, which is a negative constant. Consider the following map :
H({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}+\alpha h({\bm{x}})
for small enough
\alpha
such that
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}}
. Then, we have,

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{I}}+\alpha{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq p}|1+\alpha[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}|+\alpha\sum_{j\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}\setminus\{i\}}|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq p}1+\alpha\left([{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}+\sum_{j\in\{1,2,\dots,p\}\setminus\{i\}}|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+c\alpha.

The second equality follows from the fact that
0<1+\alpha[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}
from the choice of
\alpha
. The last inequality follows from the definition of
S_{i}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}})
in Definition
3.1
.

Then, for
{\bm{x}}
such that
\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{|c|}
,

\displaystyle\left\|H({\bm{x}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq

\displaystyle\left\|({\bm{I}}+\alpha{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}){\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}+\alpha\left\|{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+c\alpha)\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}+\alpha\left\|{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{|c|}.

Therefore,
H
is a self-map. Moreover,
H
is a continuous function from the assumption. Therefore, by Brouwer’s fixed point theorem in Lemma
10.8
in the Appendix, the existence of a fixed point of the map
H
follows.
∎

Remark 9.2
.

Without continuity, characterizing the existence of a solution becomes challenging. An example where condition (
13
) is satisfied, yet no solution exists or multiple solutions arise, is provided in Section
6
.

If we assume a slightly more contingent assumption that
h
in (
12
) is a locally Lipschitz map (where the definition is given in Definition
10.1
in Appendix
10
), then we can guarantee a stronger result, i.e., the uniqueness of the solution, which is a result from
Davydov et al., (2024)
:

Lemma 9.3

(Lemma 24 in
Davydov et al., (2024)
)
.

Suppose
{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}
is a constant vector and
h
is a locally Lipschitz mapping, with condition (
13
) holding at all differentiable points of
h
. Then, there exists a unique point
{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}^{*}}{\bm{x}}^{*}+{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}^{*}}=\bm{0}_{p}
where
\bm{0}_{p}
is a zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Remark 9.4
.

The continuity of a function does not necessarily imply Lipschitzness. However, if a function is Lipschitz, it is necessarily continuous.

Remark 9.5
.

A locally Lipschitz function is differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher’s theorem in Lemma
10.2
in Appendix
10
.

Now, let us focus on a slightly different condition to study the solution of (
12
). For some
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
that is invertible, we can re-write the equation in (
12
) by

\displaystyle\tilde{h}({\bm{x}}):={\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}){\bm{x}}+{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{x}}=\bm{0}.

(14)

When
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}
,
{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}
, and
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
are constant matrices and vectors, i.e.,
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{A}}
,
{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{b}}
, and
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{C}}
for some
{\bm{A}},{\bm{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the system simplifies to a linear form. In this scenario, the reformulation in (
14
) is widely recognized as matrix splitting
(
Berman and Plemmons,, 1994
)
, a method extensively studied for analyzing the convergence of linear systems such as

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{C}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}+{\bm{C}}){\bm{x}}_{k},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

The convergence of these systems is determined by the spectral radius of
{\bm{C}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}
. However, when these matrices depend on
{\bm{x}}
, the spectral radius of each
{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}
becomes insufficient to ensure the existence of solutions or the stability of dynamical systems described by (
14
), specifically

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}_{k}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}_{k}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}_{k}}){\bm{x}}_{k}+{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}_{k}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}_{k}},\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Consequently, an alternative condition must be considered to address these challenges. In particular, we consider the following condition for some real number
c^{*}
:

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq c^{*}<1,\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}

(15)

for some set
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which will be clarified further.
Now, we have the result for existence of a solution of (
12
):

Lemma 9.6
.

Suppose (
15
) holds with
{\mathcal{D}}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
. If
\tilde{h}
in (
14
) is a continuous function, then there exists a solution of (
12
).

Proof.

For simplicity of the notation, let us denote
c=1-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}
, and consider
H({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}+\alpha\tilde{h}({\bm{x}})
where
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{1-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}}
. Now, we have the following bound

\displaystyle\left\|(1-\alpha){\bm{I}}+\alpha{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[p]}\left||(1-\alpha)+\alpha[{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})]_{i,i}|+\alpha\sum_{j\neq i}[[{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})]_{ii}]_{i,j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-\alpha+\alpha\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 1-\alpha c

(16)

where the last two inequalities follows from the choice of
\alpha
.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|H({\bm{x}})\right\|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\left\|\left((1-\alpha){\bm{I}}+\alpha{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right){\bm{x}}+\alpha{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(1-\alpha){\bm{I}}+\alpha{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}+\alpha\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\alpha c)\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}+\alpha\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}.

The last inequality follows from (
16
).

For
{\bm{x}}
such that
\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{c}
, we have
\left\|H({\bm{x}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}\right\|_{\infty}}{c}
. Therefore,
H
is a self-map, and a continuous function from the assumption. Now, applying Brouwer’s fixed point theorem in Lemma
10.8
in the Appendix, there exists a solution of (
12
).
∎

The uniqueness of the solution can be guaranteed with additional assumption of local lipshictzness of
\tilde{h}
:

Lemma 9.7
.

Suppose
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
and
{\bm{b}}_{{\bm{x}}}
are bounded constant matrix and vector, respectively. If
\tilde{h}
is a locally Lipschitz function and
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\tilde{h}}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}<1
where
{\mathcal{D}}_{\tilde{h}}
is the set of differentiable points of
\tilde{h}
, then a solution of (
12
) exists and is unique.

Proof.

For simplicity, let us denote
c:=\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\tilde{h}}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}<1
.

By a version of Lebourg’s mean value theorem in Lemma
10.6
in Appendix Section
10.1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})^{-1}{\bm{x}}-{\bm{C}}^{-1}_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{y}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{y}}})^{-1}{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq c\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}.

The Banach fixed point theorem in Lemma
10.9
in the Appendix ensures the uniqueness and existence of the solution.
∎

Remark 9.8
.

Suppose there exists a norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
such that
\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|<1
, which can be guaranteed if the joint spectral radius is smaller than one
(
Rota and Strang,, 1960
)
and
\{{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}):{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
is a finite set. Then, we can replace the infinity norm in (
15
) with this common norm. We refer to Definition
10.11
and Lemma
10.12
in the Appendix
10
for further details.

9.1
Discussion on the condition (
13
) and (
15
)

From the above discussion, we can see that the two important assumptions are conditions (
13
) and (
15
). Let us discuss the scenario when both conditions are satisfied.

Proposition 9.9
.

Suppose for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}
,
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
is a diagonal matrix such that
0<\sigma_{\min}<\lambda_{\min}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})
and
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})<\sigma_{\max}<\infty
for some positive constants
\sigma_{\min}
and
\sigma_{\max}
.

1.

Suppose (
13
) holds, and
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
has non-negative diagonal elements for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, then, (
15
) holds.

2.

If (
15
) holds, then (
13
) holds.

Proof.

From (
13
), for some
\kappa>0
, we have the following:

\displaystyle-\kappa>

\displaystyle[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}+\sum_{j\in[p]\setminus\{i\}}|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}+[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}+\sum_{j\in[p]\setminus\{i\}}|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}\left(-1+[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]^{-1}_{i,i}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|\right).

The first equality follows since
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
is a diagonal matrix. The last equality follows from the fact that the diagonal elements for
{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
are non-negative. As
[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}>0
, we have the following result:

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\kappa}{\sigma_{\max}}\leq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\left(1-\max_{i\in[p]}[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]^{-1}_{i,i}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|\right)=1-\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\left\|{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}^{-1}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}})\right\|_{\infty}.

This proves the first statement.

Now, let us prove the second statement. Note that from the condition (
15
), for some
\omega>0
,

\displaystyle-\omega>

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[p]}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|}{[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[p]}\frac{1}{[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|-[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[p]}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|-[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}\right).

The first inequality follows from the assumption in (
15
), and the last inequality follows from the condition
\max_{i\in[p]}[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}>\sigma_{\min}
.

Now, we can check that

\displaystyle-\sigma_{\min}\omega>\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|-[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}\geq\sum^{p}_{j=1}\sum^{p}_{j\in[p]\setminus\{i\}}|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}|+{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}.

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}
is a diagonal matrix.

Therefore, from the definition of
S_{i}(\cdot)
in Definition
3.1
, taking supremum and maximum over the above inequality, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\max_{i\in[p]}S_{i}({\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}})\leq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}}\max_{i\in[p]}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left|[{\bm{A}}_{{\bm{x}}}+{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,j}\right|-[{\bm{C}}_{{\bm{x}}}]_{i,i}\right)\leq-\sigma_{\min}w<0,

which proves the second statement.

∎

10.1
Fine properties of function

Definition 10.1

(Locally Lipschitz function
Clarke, (1981)
)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be locally Lipschitz, if for
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a constant
L
an
\delta
such that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{0}\right\|<\delta\Rightarrow\left\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|\leq L\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{0}\right\|

Lemma 10.2

(Rademacher’s theorem, page 810 in
Evans, (2018)
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
f
is a locally lispcthiz function, then
f
is differentiable almost everywhere.

Definition 10.3

(Generalized directional derivative)
.

The generalized directional derivative of the locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at the point
{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the direction
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{o}({\bm{u}};{\bm{v}})=\limsup_{{\bm{w}}\to{\bm{u}},t\to 0+}\frac{f({\bm{w}}+t{\bm{v}})-f({\bm{w}})}{t}

Definition 10.4

(Clarke subdifferential, page 54 in
Clarke, (1981)
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function. The Clarke subdifferential
\partial_{C}f(u)
of
f
at a point
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defind as the following:

\displaystyle\partial_{C}f({\bm{u}})=\left\{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{y}}\leq f^{o}({\bm{u}};{\bm{v}}),\;\forall{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}

When
f
is locally Lipschitz, then

\displaystyle\partial_{C}f({\bm{u}})=\mathrm{conv}\left\{\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}):\text{${\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{f}$ such that ${\bm{x}}_{i}\to{\bm{v}}$ and $\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})$ exists }\right\}

where
\mathrm{conv}(A)
denotes convex hull of a set
A
, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{f}
is the differentiable points of
f
and
{\bm{x}}_{i}
is a converging sequence to
{\bm{v}}
.

Lemma 10.5

(Lebourg’s mean value theorem, Theorem 2.4 in
Clarke et al., (2008)
)
.

Consider a locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists
{\bm{v}}\in\{t{\bm{x}}+(1-t){\bm{y}}:t\in[0,1]\}
such that

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{x}})=

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{\top}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})

where

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}\in\partial f_{C}({\bm{v}})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{conv}\left\{\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}):\text{${\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{f}$ such that ${\bm{x}}_{i}\to{\bm{v}}$ and $\lim_{i\to\infty}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})$ exists }\right\}.

{\mathcal{D}}_{f}
is the differentiable points of
f
and
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{f}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a converging sequence to
{\bm{v}}
.

Lemma 10.6
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a locally lipscthiz function and
\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq f_{\max}
for all the differentiable points
{\bm{x}}
for some positive real number
f_{\max}
. Then, the following holds:

\displaystyle\left\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq f_{\max}\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Consider
{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}f({\bm{x}})
for some
i\in[n]
. By Lebourg’s mean value theorem in Lemma
10.5
, we have, for a basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose
i
-th coordinate is one,

\displaystyle{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}(f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}}))={\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}})

(17)

for
{\bm{a}}_{i}\in\text{conv}\{\lim_{k\to\infty}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k})^{\top}{\bm{e}}_{i}:{\bm{x}}_{k}\to{\bm{v}},{\bm{x}}_{k}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{f}\}
where
{\bm{v}}\in\{t{\bm{x}}+(1-t){\bm{y}}:t\in[0,1]\}
. We can find such
{\bm{a}}_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, and we have

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{a}}_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}).

Taking the infinity norm on both sides, we get

\displaystyle\left\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|{\bm{a}}_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_{j}\hat{f}_{j}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|_{\infty}

(18)

where
\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_{j}=1
. For
j\in[q]
,
\lambda_{j}\geq 0
, and
\hat{f}_{j}=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{j}_{k})
for some sequence
\{{\bm{x}}^{j}_{k}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{f}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Note that we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{q}\lambda_{j}\hat{f}_{j}\right\|_{\infty}=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{q}{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{k}^{j})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}^{j}_{k})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=1}\lambda_{j}f_{\max}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\max}.

Applying this result to (
18
) yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma 10.7
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is locally Lipschitz if and only if
f
is Lipschitz on every compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

The necessity part is an immediate consequence of the definition of local Lipschitz continuity. We now establish the converse implication. Suppose the local Lipschitzness holds with some norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
and
f
is not Lipschitz on some compact set
K
. Then, there exists some
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in K
such that
\frac{\left\|f({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{y}})\right\|}{||{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}||}>C
for any
C\geq 0
. Therefore, there exist a sequence
\{({\bm{x}}_{n},{\bm{y}}_{n})\in K\times K\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
\frac{\left\|f({\bm{x}}_{n})-f({\bm{y}}_{n})\right\|}{||{\bm{x}}_{n}-{\bm{y}}_{n}||}\to\infty
. From the compactness of
K
, there exist a convergent subsequence
\{({\bm{x}}_{k_{n}},{\bm{y}}_{k_{n}})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converging to
(\tilde{{\bm{x}}},\tilde{{\bm{y}}})
. Moreover, as continuous function is bounded on compact set, we should have
||{\bm{x}}_{k_{n}}-{\bm{y}}_{k_{n}}||\to 0
. This contradicts the fact that
f
is locally Lipschitz at
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}
, and this proves the reverse direction.
∎

Lemma 10.8

(Brouwer’s fixed point theorem
(
Brouwer,, 1911
)
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{R}:=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|<R\}
be an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
centered at the origin and of radius
R
with some norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
. If
f:{\mathcal{B}}_{R}\to{\mathcal{B}}_{R}
is a continuous function, then,
f
has a fixed point, i.e., a solution of
f({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}
.

Lemma 10.9

(Banach fixed point theorem
(
Banach,, 1922
)
)
.

Consider a mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose there exists a norm

Now, let us briefly explain the concept of joint spectral radius
(

Definition 10.11

(Joint spectral radius
(
Rota and Strang,, 1960
)
)

Given a set of matrix

\displaystyle\rho({\bm{A}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{A}}_{m})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\max_{\sigma\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}^{k}}\left\|{\bm{A}}_{\sigma_{k}}\cdots{\bm{A}}_{\sigma_{2}}{\bm{A}}_{\sigma_{1}}\right\|^{1/k}.

(

)

Given a set of matrix
\{{\bm{A}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\}_{i=1}^{m}

\left\|\cdot\right\|
such that
\left\|{\bm{A}}_{i}\right\|<1
for all
i\in[m]
.

Lemma 10.13

(Gerschgorin circle theorem
(

)
)
.

Let
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and

\displaystyle\{z\in\mathbb{C}|:|z-[{\bm{A}}]_{i,i}|\leq R_{i}({\bm{A}})\},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

The eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
are in the union of Gerschgorin discs

\displaystyle G({\bm{A}})=\cup^{n}_{i=1}\{z\in\mathbb{C}|:|z-[{\bm{A}}]_{i,i}|\leq R_{i}({\bm{A}})\}.

A matrix

11
Stochastic approximation

Let us consider a stochastic approximation scheme
(
Robbins and Monro,, 1951
)
:

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{x}}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(f({\bm{x}}_{k})+{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}\right)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous function,
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}
is a Martingale difference sequence and
\alpha_{k}\in[0,1]
is the step-size.

Definition 11.1

(Martingale difference sequence)
.

Consider a sequence of random variables
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{0},{\bm{\epsilon}}_{1},\dots
and let
\sigma
-fields
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}=\sigma({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{\epsilon}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k})
. If
\mathbb{E}\left[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k+1}\middle|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[\left\|{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k+1}\right\|^{2}|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]<\infty
almost surely for the
\sigma
-fields
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
,
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k}
is called a Martingale difference sequence.

The ODE counterpart can characterize the stability of stochastic approximation scheme:

\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=f({\bm{x}}_{t}),\quad{\bm{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\geq 0.

Assumption 11.2
.

1. The mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally Lipschitz continuous, and there exists a function
f_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\lim_{c\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(c{\bm{x}})}{c}=f_{\infty}({\bm{x}}),\quad\forall{{\bm{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(19)

2. The origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium for the ODE
\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=f_{\infty}({\bm{x}}_{t})
.

3. There exists a globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the ODE
\dot{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=f({\bm{x}}_{t})
, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}_{t}\rightarrow{\bm{x}}^{*}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

4. The sequence
\{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k},{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
where
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
is sigma-algebra generated by
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i},k\geq i\}
, is a Martingale difference sequence. In addition , there exists a constant
C_{0}<\infty
such that for any initial
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[||{\bm{\epsilon}}_{k+1}||^{2}|{\mathcal{G}}_{k}]\leq C_{0}(1+||{\bm{x}}_{k}||^{2}),\forall{k}\geq 0
.

5. The step-sizes satisfies the Robbins-Monro condition
(
Robbins and Monro,, 1951
)
:

\displaystyle\sum\limits^{\infty}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}=\infty,\quad\sum\limits^{\infty}_{k=0}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty.

Lemma 11.3

(Borkar and Meyn Theorem,
Borkar and Meyn, (2000)
)
.

Suppose there exists unique
{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
f({\bm{x}}^{*})=\bm{0}
and Assumption
11.2
holds. Then,
{\bm{x}}_{k}\to{\bm{x}}^{*}
with probability one.

The third statement (one-sided Lipschitzness of regularized Q-learning) follows from the same logic of second statement.

15
Related Works on linear Q-learning

This section provides additional literature on linear Q-learning. Several studies have proposed variations of linear Q-learning.
Chen et al., (2023)
explored the use of target networks and truncation, while
Maei et al., (2010)
;
Devraj and Meyn, (2017)
;
Carvalho et al., (2020)
employed a two-time-scale approach to design a convergent Q-learning algorithm. Although these methods ensure boundedness or convergence, the exact points to which the algorithm converges remain not well understood. In a slightly different setting,
Lu et al., (2021)
explored a linear programming formulation of Q-learning under deterministic transitions. Furthermore,
Che et al., (2024)
examined Q-learning with a target network in an overparameterized regime, where the number of features exceeds the size of state-action space.

The set of realizable policy by the linear feature set
(
Lu et al.,, 2018
)
is defined as

\displaystyle\left\{\pi\in\Omega:\pi(s)=arg\max_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{\phi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\right\}.

The optimal policy
\pi^{*}
may not be in abaove set, and therefore, the solution of PBE might induce only sub-optimal policies.
