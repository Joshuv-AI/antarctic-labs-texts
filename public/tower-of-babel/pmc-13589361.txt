Improbable healing in endodontics: a case series of favorable outcomes despite adverse prognostic conditions
Authors: Ricardo Machado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndodontic prognosis is based on clinical and radiographic factors that estimate the likelihood of treatment success. Because periapical healing reflects complex interactions among microbial, host, anatomical, and treatment-related factors, individual outcomes may occasionally differ from conventional prognostic expectations. This paper aimed to describe and critically analyze favorable outcomes in teeth presenting conditions traditionally associated with reduced probabilities of treatment success.Case presentationThree clinical cases were described in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Case Reports in Endodontics (PRICE) 2020 guidelines. The cases presented adverse prognostic conditions, including substantial root resorption, inability to achieve apical patency or complete canal instrumentation, overextension of root filling material, absence of a definitive coronal restoration, and lack of recommended periodontal treatment. Despite these conditions, the teeth remained functional and asymptomatic and demonstrated favorable radiographic outcomes during longitudinal follow-up.ConclusionsThese cases illustrate that unfavorable prognostic conditions do not invariably result in treatment failure. The reported outcomes neither diminish the importance of established prognostic factors nor validate the treatment limitations encountered. Rather, they emphasize that prognosis is probabilistic and that unusual favorable outcomes should be interpreted cautiously as individual observations.

Background

Apical periodontitis is an inflammatory disease of the periradicular tissues that develops primarily as a consequence of microbial infection of the root canal system (RCS) and the host response to intracanal microorganisms and their products [1–3]. Accordingly, root canal therapy aims to control intracanal infection, prevent or resolve periapical disease, and maintain or re-establish periapical health while preserving the natural dentition [1, 4–18]. Although complete sanitization of the RCS is biologically unattainable because of its anatomical complexity and the inherent resilience of biofilm-associated microorganisms [2–8, 11, 13, 15, 18, 19], contemporary endodontic treatment remains one of the most predictable interventions in Dentistry, consistently achieving high success rates [20, 21]. Hence, periapical healing should not be regarded simply as the consequence of technically adequate treatment but rather as the result of a complex and dynamic biological process [7–9, 11, 15, 22–33].

Over the past decades, numerous studies have identified clinical and technical factors associated with endodontic prognosis, including anatomical complexity [5, 19], pulpal and periapical diagnosis [5, 10, 14, 21], microbial persistence or recurrence [1, 3, 7, 15, 17, 18], procedural complications and treatment adequacy [4, 5, 34], root resorption [35], missed canals [36], quality of coronal restoration [5, 37], and several patient-related variables [11, 31, 32]. These factors provide valuable information for estimating the likelihood of treatment success and are fundamental to clinical decision-making.

Despite substantial advances in the understanding of endodontic prognosis, the mechanisms underlying periapical healing remain insufficiently understood [1, 7, 17, 18, 38]. The interplay among key factors, including the virulence and composition of the residual microbiota [7, 11, 15], host immune response [11, 28, 31–33], genetic susceptibility [29], inflammatory regulation [25, 31], and tissue regenerative capacity [29, 30], introduces substantial biological variability, meaning that tissues exposed to apparently similar pathological conditions may respond quite differently despite receiving comparable treatment [33, 39]. Consequently, teeth presenting unfavorable prognostic factors may occasionally undergo complete tissue repair, whereas apparently favorable cases may fail despite technically appropriate therapy [17, 18, 40, 41]. These observations reinforce that current prognostic models provide probabilistic rather than deterministic estimates of treatment outcomes and cannot fully account for the variability observed in individual healing responses [4, 6, 42–44].

Although uncommon, favorable outcomes in teeth presenting substantial adverse prognostic conditions have been reported [45–48]. Such observations should not be interpreted as contradicting established prognostic principles, because factors associated with treatment outcome modify the probability of success rather than determine the response of every individual tooth. Exceptional outcomes cannot establish the biological mechanisms responsible for healing and may represent favorable events within the expected distribution of individual treatment responses. Nevertheless, carefully documented cases may provide clinically relevant examples of the variability inherent in endodontic outcomes and offer opportunities to examine the relationship between treatment-related factors and tissue repair critically [33, 39, 49–53].

The present case series describes three distinct clinical situations in which periapical and/or periodontal healing occurred despite conditions conventionally associated with a reduced probability of treatment success, including extensive root resorption, inability to achieve apical patency or complete instrumentation of the apical root canal third, overextension of root filling material, prolonged absence of definitive coronal restoration, and lack of recommended periodontal treatment. These outcomes are critically analyzed considering established prognostic evidence, the limitations and complications encountered during treatment, and plausible alternative explanations for the unexpectedly favorable healing observed. The expression “improbable healing” is used herein solely as a descriptive term and is not intended to represent a new diagnostic, biological, or prognostic concept.

Case Reports

The three clinical cases described below were performed by the author of this paper at the Centro de Especialidades Odontológicas de Navegantes, Santa Catarina, Brazil, between 2018 and 2020, and are reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Case Reports in Endodontics (PRICE) 2020 guidelines [54]. At follow-ups, treatments outcomes were assessed clinically and radiographically. A favorable clinical outcome was characterized by the absence of signs and symptoms associated with endodontic disease, whereas radiographic healing was assessed based on the resolution or substantial reduction of the pre-existing periapical radiolucency, together with evidence of re-establishment of the periapical bone architecture on conventional periapical radiographs.

Case 1

A 34-year-old Caucasian male was referred for evaluation of the right mandibular first molar, which had undergone endodontic treatment approximately seven years earlier. His medical history was non-contributory. During anamnesis, the patient reported that the tooth had previously received multiple restorative procedures. Several months after the last restoration, the patient reported marked discoloration of the entire crown and returned to the clinic for further evaluation. According to the patient, clinical and radiographic assessments at that time revealed pulpal necrosis, and root canal treatment was indicated. After finishing the endodontic therapy, the tooth was restored with an occlusodistal amalgam restoration. The patient did not attend follow-up appointments due to relocation.

At the initial visit, clinical examination revealed physiological probing depths, absence of response to cold (Endo-Ice, Coltene/Whaledent, Cuyahoga Falls, OH, USA), and electric pulp tests (C Pulse Pulp Tester, Foshan COXO Medical Instruments Co Ltd, Foshan City, China), and no tenderness to percussion or palpation. Radiographic analysis demonstrated inadequate root canal treatment, a significant resorptive process affecting the distal root, and a large periapical radiolucency associated with the mesial root. Based on these findings, the diagnoses were previously treated tooth and asymptomatic apical periodontitis, respectively [55] (Fig. 1A). Clinical decision-making considered the specific characteristics of the case together with the patient’s expectations and preferences. After detailed verbal and written explanations of the clinical findings and available treatment options, including no intervention, nonsurgical endodontic retreatment, surgical endodontic treatment, and extraction, nonsurgical retreatment was selected as the initial conservative approach to address the probable intracanal source of persistent disease. Surgical endodontic treatment or extraction remained potential alternatives if nonsurgical retreatment failed. Written informed consent was subsequently obtained, and treatment was scheduled for the following week.

At the first clinical appointment, local anesthesia was administered using 4% articaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (DFL, Rio de Janeiro, RJ, Brazil), followed by placing the rubber dam. The operative field was disinfected with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) (Biodinâmica, Ibiporã, PR, Brazil), the previously placed amalgam restoration was removed with a No. 1016 round bur, and the endodontic access was refined with an Endo-Z bur (KG Sorensen, Barueri, SP, Brazil). Three root canal orifices (mesiobuccal [MB], mesiolingual [ML], and distal [D]) were identified and pre-flared using Nos. 1 and 2 Largo drills (Dentsply-Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). Removal of the root canal filling material was performed using Nos. 1 and 2 Gates-Glidden drills and Hedström files (Dentsply-Maillefer). Working lengths (WLs) for the MB and D root canals were set close to the apical constriction (AC) [56, 57] – around 0.5 mm short of the 0.0 mm reading by using an electronic apex locator (Root ZX II, J. Morita, Kyoto, Japan). In the ML canal, apical patency could not be achieved [58, 59] despite multiple attempts using different clinical strategies; therefore, the working length (WL) was established at the maximum depth reached by the endodontic instruments (Fig. 1B).

Subsequently, K-Flexofile instruments (starting from No. 15) (Dentsply-Maillefer) were calibrated up to the WLs and used in ascending order to determine the clinical anatomical diameter (CAD) of each root canal [60], which corresponded to a No. 30 K-Flexofile (Dentsply-Maillefer) for the MB and ML canals, and to a No. 40 K-Flexofile (Dentsply-Maillefer) for the D canal. Reinstrumentation was performed using the ProTaper Universal system (Dentsply-Maillefer) within the established WLs. The F4 instrument was used as the finishing file for the MB and ML canals and the F5 instrument for the D canal; in the ML canal, instrumentation remained limited to the maximum depth previously reached because apical patency could not be achieved. Irrigation was performed with 2.5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Biodinâmica) at each instrument use or change using a 31-gauge NaviTip irrigation needle attached to a 5 mL plastic syringe (Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA), calibrated at 2 mm from the WLs, totaling around 40 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (Biodinâmica) per root canal. After chemomechanical preparation, the canals were flushed with 17% EDTA (Biodinâmica) for 3 min, followed by saline irrigation (Biodinâmica), and then dried with sterile F4 and F5 paper points (Dentsply-Maillefer). Subsequently, calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) paste (UltraCal XS, Ultradent) was placed and maintained for 14 days as intracanal dressing.

At the next visit, Ca(OH)2 paste was removed using the finishing files in conjunction with copious irrigation with 2.5% NaOCl (Biodinâmica). After drying the root canals with sterile paper points (Dentsply-Maillefer), the obturation limits of the D, MB and ML root canals were verified using a No. 50 K-file and F4 gutta-percha cones (Dentsply-Maillefer) (Fig. 1C, D). The D canal was obturated with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) (Angelus, Londrina, PR, Brazil). In contrast, the MB and ML canals were filled with gutta-percha cones (Dentsply-Maillefer) and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany) by the lateral condensation technique [61] (Fig. 1E).

Definitive restoration was performed using a light-cured composite resin (Filtek Z250, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) five days after completion of endodontic therapy. Clinical and radiographic follow-up at 3 years demonstrated absence of signs and symptoms, no evident radiographic progression of the resorptive defect in the D root, and complete radiographic resolution of the periapical radiolucency associated with the M root (Fig. 1F).

Case 2

A 34-year-old Caucasian female patient was referred for endodontic treatment of the left mandibular first molar. Her medical history was non-contributory. Clinical and radiographic examinations revealed the presence of a temporary restorative material of undocumented composition sealing a previously performed endodontic access, normal periodontal status, mild tenderness to percussion and palpation, no response to cold sensitivity (Endo-Ice, Coltene/Whaledent) or the electric pulp tests (C Pulse Pulp Tester, Foshan COXO Medical Instruments Co Ltd), and a periapical radiolucency associated with the M root (Fig. 2A). Hence, the pulpal and periapical diagnoses were previously initiated endodontic therapy and symptomatic apical periodontitis, respectively [55]. Thus, the endodontic treatment was proposed, and the patient was provided with a detailed verbal and written explanation of the procedure’s specific characteristics, risks, and limitations. Afterward, written informed consent was obtained.

Temporary restoration was placed using a glass ionomer-based restorative material (Fuji II LC, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and the patient was referred for final restoration. Clinical and radiographic follow-up at 5 years demonstrated absence of signs and symptoms and complete radiographic resolution of the periapical radiolucency previously associated with the M root (Fig. 2D).

Case 3

A 63-year-old Caucasian male patient was referred for endodontic treatment of the right mandibular first molar. His medical history was non-contributory. Clinical examinations revealed a physiological probing depth in all the sites except for the MB aspect (7 mm), the presence of a temporary material sealing a formerly performed endodontic access, no tenderness to percussion and palpation, and no response to cold sensitivity (Endo-Ice, Coltene/Whaledent) or the electric pulp tests (C Pulse Pulp Tester, Foshan COXO Medical Instruments Co Ltd). Radiographically, there was a periapical radiolucency associated with the M root (Fig. 3A). Hence, the pulpal and periapical diagnoses were asymptomatic apical periodontitis and previously initiated endodontic therapy, respectively [55]. Thus, the endodontic treatment was proposed, and the patient was provided with a detailed verbal and written explanation of the procedure’s specific characteristics, risks, and limitations. Afterward, written informed consent was obtained.

At first glance, immediate removal of the overextended material was planned. However, after discussing this undesirable event with the patient, he preferred to follow up the treatment. The patient was provided with post-operative instructions and referred to the general clinician for final restoration and periodontal analysis due to the presence of a 7 mm probing depth in the MB site. He was also advised to come back to the clinic in case of any pain or significant discomfort. The first follow-up was scheduled for 3 months, but the patient did not show up. New attempts were made, but the patient could not be located for 12 months. For this reason, the first follow-up examination was performed only 14 months later, when the patient reported that the definitive restoration had not been completed and that he had not undergone the recommended periodontal treatment. Nevertheless, no endodontic signs or symptoms were present, and radiographic examination demonstrated complete resolution of the periapical radiolucency previously associated with the M root (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

The three cases presented herein demonstrate favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes despite pathological and treatment-related conditions conventionally associated with a reduced probability of endodontic success. These observations should not be interpreted as evidence that such prognostic factors are unimportant, nor do they validate the technical limitations or complications encountered during treatment. Rather, they represent individual outcomes occurring within a clinical context in which prognosis is inherently probabilistic [33, 39, 49, 51–53, 63, 64]. Accordingly, the significance of these cases lies not in establishing a new healing concept but in critically examining why favorable outcomes may occasionally occur despite adverse prognostic conditions.

Case 1 involved a previously treated right mandibular first molar presenting with a substantial resorptive defect, a large periapical radiolucency, and technically inadequate previous root canal treatment. The radiographic appearance of the periapical lesion was relatively well defined and surrounded by a radiopaque border, a feature that may raise suspicion of a cystic lesion. However, the histopathological nature of the lesion was not established in this case; therefore, it cannot be classified as a radicular cyst based on the available radiographic findings alone [65, 66]. Nonsurgical retreatment was selected to address the probable intracanal source of persistent disease before considering more invasive approaches. During retreatment, apical patency could not be achieved in the ML canal [58, 59] despite multiple attempts. Failure to negotiate and adequately shape and disinfect the apical portion of an infected canal may negatively influence treatment and retreatment outcomes because residual microorganisms [1, 3, 7, 15, 17, 18], smear layer [67, 68] and previous root canal filling [69] might persist in inaccessible areas of the RCS which are in close proximity with the periradicular tissues. Nevertheless, clinical evidence regarding the influence of apical patency on treatment outcome remains inconclusive. In a randomized clinical trial involving necrotic teeth with apical periodontitis, Arslan et al. [70] reported comparable 12-month healing rates when apical patency was maintained or not maintained (85.0% and 86.4%, respectively). Conversely, a recent systematic review [71] suggested that maintaining apical patency may be associated with more favorable long-term outcomes, although the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. Importantly, apical patency represents only one component of chemomechanical preparation and does not directly quantify microbial reduction; therefore, failure to achieve patency should not be regarded as an absolute determinant of treatment failure. In the present case, radiographic resolution of the periapical radiolucency and absence of evident progression of the resorptive defect were observed after 3 years.

Case 2 presented a similar technical limitation. Despite repeated attempts, the apical portions of both M canals could not be negotiated, and instrumentation and obturation terminated approximately 4 mm short of the radiographic apex. The apical extent and technical quality of root canal treatment are recognized prognostic factors, particularly in teeth with preoperative apical periodontitis [4, 5, 14]. An uninstrumented apical canal segment may retain microorganisms and necrotic tissue capable of maintaining periapical inflammation [1, 2, 17]. From this perspective, the radiographic resolution observed at the 5-year follow-up represents an unexpectedly favorable outcome.

Although necessarily hypothetical, biologically plausible explanations may be proposed for the favorable outcomes observed in Cases 1 and 2. Chemomechanical preparation plays a major role in reducing the bacterial burden within the RCS [72, 73]. Despite the inability to achieve apical patency in the ML canal in Case 1 and to negotiate the apical third of the M canals in Case 2, canal enlargement was performed based on determination of the respective clinical anatomical diameters (CADs) [60], together with copious NaOCl irrigation and the use of calcium hydroxide as an intracanal dressing. These procedures may have sufficiently reduced the intracanal microbial content to create conditions compatible with tissue repair. Characteristics of the residual microbiota [3, 7, 15, 18, 38], the lesion [1, 17, 65, 66], host response [11, 31, 32], or other unmeasured variables may have contributed to the favorable results. However, this interpretation remains hypothetical because microbiological analyses were not performed.

Case 3 combined several distinct adverse circumstances. Root filling material was unintentionally overextended beyond the M root, the tooth presented an isolated 7-mm periodontal probing depth at baseline, the recommended periodontal treatment was not performed, and a definitive coronal restoration had not been placed by the 14-month follow-up. Extrusion of root filling materials is an undesirable procedural event because materials placed beyond the root canal space interact directly with periapical tissues and may influence postoperative inflammation and healing. Clinical outcome studies have associated overextension of root filling materials with less favorable periapical healing [14, 16]. Conversely, favorable outcomes have also been documented despite extruded gutta-percha and sealer, including cases followed for several decades, indicating that overextension does not invariably result in treatment failure [74]. The biological consequences of extrusion may depend on the material involved, the amount and location of extrusion, microbial contamination, host response, and the effectiveness of intracanal infection control [4]. Immediate intervention to remove the extruded material was considered in Case 3, but after discussion the patient elected clinical and radiographic monitoring. The subsequent absence of symptoms and radiographic resolution of the periapical lesion indicate that immediate intervention was not required during the period evaluated in this individual case.

The restorative and periodontal circumstances of Case 3 require similar caution. Adequate coronal restoration is important for preventing reinfection of the RCS and protecting the remaining tooth structure, and its quality has been associated with periapical healing and tooth survival [5, 22, 37]. Likewise, the isolated 7-mm probing depth warranted periodontal evaluation. Because the available record does not provide complete objective periodontal measurements at follow-up or document a definitive baseline periodontal diagnosis, the periodontal outcome should not be overinterpreted.

Collectively, the present cases emphasize the distinction between prognostic factors identified at the population level and outcomes observed in individual patients [33, 39, 49, 51–53, 63, 64]. Endodontic outcomes are influenced by the complex interplay of multiple factors, including anatomical complexity [5, 19], pulpal and periapical diagnosis [5, 10, 14, 21], microbial persistence or recurrence [1, 3, 7, 15, 17, 18], procedural complications and treatment adequacy [4, 5, 34], root resorption [35], missed canals [36], quality of the coronal restoration [5, 37], and several patient-related variables [11, 31, 32]. The biological mechanisms responsible for the favorable outcomes observed herein cannot be established from these cases. Although differences in microbial factors, host inflammatory responses, and regenerative capacity may have contributed [7, 11, 15, 25, 28–33], none of these variables was directly assessed. These cases may therefore simply represent favorable individual outcomes within the expected distribution of responses to endodontic treatment [1–4, 11, 14–18, 31].

Accordingly, the expression “improbable healing” is used solely as a descriptive designation for unexpectedly favorable outcomes occurring despite substantial adverse prognostic conditions. It is not proposed as a new biological, diagnostic, or prognostic entity, nor do these cases suggest that established technical standards should be relaxed.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these cases. First, this is a descriptive case series involving only three patients without a comparison group; consequently, causal relationships cannot be established, and the observations cannot be generalized. Second, the mechanisms responsible for healing were not investigated microbiologically, histologically, immunologically, or molecularly, making any biological explanation necessarily speculative. Third, evaluation of healing was based on clinical examination and two-dimensional periapical radiographs. CBCT was not used to confirm three-dimensional resolution of the lesions; therefore, residual periapical alterations not visible on conventional radiographs cannot be excluded. Fourth, the available radiographic documentation was not identical among the three cases and does not document every procedural stage. This is particularly relevant to the interpretation of WL and apical instrumentation in Cases 2 and 3. Finally, follow-up duration differed among the cases, and aspects of subsequent restorative and periodontal care occurred outside the direct control documented in the case records.

Future studies incorporating standardized three-dimensional imaging, microbiological assessment, host-response biomarkers, and larger prospective populations may help clarify why teeth with apparently similar prognostic profiles sometimes exhibit substantially different healing responses [33, 39].

Conclusion

The present case series describes favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes in three teeth presenting distinct pathological, technical, restorative, and periodontal conditions associated with a reduced probability of endodontic success. These observations neither diminish the importance of established prognostic factors nor validate the treatment limitations and complications encountered in the individual cases. Instead, they illustrate that population-derived prognostic factors estimate the likelihood of treatment success but cannot predict individual outcomes with certainty. The biological mechanisms responsible for the observed healing cannot be determined from these cases, and the possibility that they represent favorable exceptions must be acknowledged. Accordingly, the expression “improbable healing” should be understood solely as a descriptive characterization of these unusual clinical outcomes rather than as a distinct prognostic or biological concept.
