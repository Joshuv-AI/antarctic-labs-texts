Iatrogenic Enamel Lesions with Fixed Multi-bracket Treatment
Authors: Mukesh Kumar, Sommya Kumari, Sowmya Shree Guntipally, Ajay Chhabra, Ashok Kumar, Ratnesh Kumar, Ritesh Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Malocclusion is a serious oral health issue that requires orthodontic correction. Fixed orthodontic appliances are known for acquired enamel damage.Aims:To evaluate the incidence of iatrogenic white spots in subjects treated with fixed orthodontic therapy.Methods and Materials:This was a retrospective study. The study sample included the orthodontically treated subjects debonded during the study period. An imaging technique was followed to evaluate enamel white spots. Teeth debonded after orthodontic therapy were evaluated and compared with pretreatment records.Results:A majority of subjects were young adults. Maximum subjects were school-going children from the rural background. Maxillary incisors were the most affected tooth, whereas maxillary posteriors were the least affected with white spot lesions.Conclusions:Iatrogenic enamel damage is the prime concern for orthodontists. Good oral hygiene maintenance and demineralization prevention is the ideal strategy.

INTRODUCTION

Malocclusion affects the overall quality of life and is a significant global oral health concern. Orthodontic treatment is the gold standard as this refines structural, functional, and aesthetic appearance improving the social well-being of individuals.[123] The appliance is effective in aligning teeth but poses a challenge in oral hygiene maintenance.[245] Orthodontic aligners offer comparatively better oral hygiene maintenance but uphold saliva accumulation and bacterial colonization.[6]

The poorer oral hygiene with fixed orthodontic appliances/aligners results in varying amounts of iatrogenic enamel lesions in the form of white spots.[4] These acquired enamel defects are unique for their site, size, and shape.[7]

During orthodontic treatment, the cariogenic bacterial load is increased more compared to other pathogens, resulting in white spots on the enamel.[128] After debonding, this oral ecological disbalance is believed to revert without specific intervention, with a concurrent decrease in enamel white spots.[9]

The ecosystem of oral microbiota plays a tug-of-war between demineralization and remineralization on the enamel surface, and salivary minerals buffer the equilibrium. A balance shift toward gross mineral loss, results in clinically detectable non-cavitated enamel damage appearing as white spots.[9101112]

The white spot-affected enamel surfaces have altered mineral density, composition, and mechanical properties. The transmuted enamel surface intensely scatters light giving an unaesthetic appearance.[111] These acquired enamel defects are differentiated from caries by their existence both in dry and wet environments.[2] These enamel defects may transform into frank cavities.[14] This study aimed to evaluate the incidence of iatrogenic enamel white spot lesions in subjects treated with conventional fixed orthodontic appliances.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted from the archives of departmental records of the Department of Dentistry AIIMS.

Inclusion Criteria: All subjects with fully available records, treated at our institution with fixed orthodontic appliances during the study period.

Exclusion Criteria: Re-treatment subjects were excluded. No subjects were excluded on the basis of age and gender, or treatment mechanics.

Study sample: All orthodontic subjects debonded from February 2024 to May 2024 were included in the study. the total sample size of our study was 35.

Ethical Approval: The study was approved by the institutional research and ethics committee.

Evaluation of WSLs: The pre-and post-treatment intraoral images were used to evaluate the prevalence and incidence of enamel white spots. All the teeth from the second premolar to the second premolar, in both the arches, were evaluated. Molars were not included due to inadequately visibility. A total of 650 teeth available for bonding and clearly visible were compared.

All the pre-and post-treatment images were taken with the same camera in the Auto HDR Mode. Anterior segment photography was done directly while posterior segment was done indirectly. The photographic technique used for evaluation of white spots was modified version of - Gorelick L et al 1982” method.[7]

The photographic evaluation criteria was - 0= No evidence of white spot, 1=Mild white spot, 2=Significant white spot and, 3= White spots with caries.

The images were also evaluated for whether white spot lesions have improved or worsened.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated and Chi-square statistics were used. The P < 0.05 was considered significant and the confidence level was predetermined at 95%.

RESULTS

The pre-and post-treatment images of 16 male and 20 female subjects were evaluated. The subjects were using orthodontic brush along with fluoridated toothpaste.

Almost 75% of subjects were in the age group 11 to 20 years and 21 to 30 years. The maximum subjects were school-going children from the rural background.

The total number of teeth available for bonding after extraction planning affected the actual prevalence of enamel white spots although the difference was very small and not significant statistically.

Of the 650 teeth, 569 teeth were sound at pre-treatment. All the affected teeth with white spots were in grade 1 on pre-treatment. The number of teeth affected with grade 1 white spots increased to 102 at the end of treatment and 6 teeth in grade 2 [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Iatrogenic enamel white spots are the inevitable consequence of orthodontic treatment. A globally accepted management approach is demineralization prevention and reassuring remineralization. Early recognition and timely intervention facilitate cessation and reversal of the condition.[1314] The condition usually is reversible but may require intervention.[14]

Poor oral hygiene and low fluoride exposure have additive effects. The relationship between the duration of orthodontic treatment and the occurrence of enamel defects remained inconclusive.[9]

The reported prevalence of enamel white spots in orthodontic patients is between 2% and 96%.[15] Patient compliance is a major factor for this wide range of prevalence.[16] The current study observed a very low prevalence and incidence of WSL, probably due to better oral hygiene maintenance.

Maxillary incisors, especially lateral incisors are reported to be affected the most.[4] Our study observed similar results with incisor prevalence. A study reports on the incidence and prevalence amongst genders remained indecisive, with more male prevalence. Females are reported to care for better oral hygiene.[15] The effect of age on post-orthodontic enamel defects also has dissimilar study results.

Nanoparticle-modified orthodontic adhesives have shown promising antimicrobial properties, re-mineralizing capacity, and reducing frictional resistance. Antimicrobial coating may disrupt the microflora and promote resistant species.[17]

Topical fluoride is the most preferred and trusted re-mineralizing agent. Titanium tetrafluoride is preferred over sodium fluoride.[18]

A combined laser and topical fluoride therapy offers better results compared to laser or fluoride alone. Bioactive glasses are another remineralizing agent with a rejuvenating effect.[19]

CONCLUSION

Iatrogenic enamel damage is the prime concern for orthodontists. Good oral hygiene maintenance and demineralization prevention is the ideal strategy.
