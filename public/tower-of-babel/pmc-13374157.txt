Rare giant cell tumour of the left parieto-occipital skull in an 8-year-old female patient: a case report
Authors: Eyob Ayenew Engidaw, Esayas Adefris Tefera, Kenean Mesfin Bekele, Eyosias Mesfin Belayneh, Sharon Bayu Tibebe, Habib Abdela Seid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Giant cell tumor (GCT) of bone is a generally benign but locally aggressive neoplasm that most commonly affects the epiphysis of long bones in young adults, while involvement of the cranial bones is exceedingly rare, particularly in the pediatric population. Calvarial GCTs pose significant diagnostic challenges because their clinical and radiologic features overlap with other lytic skull lesions. We report a rare case of a pediatric giant cell tumor arising from the calvarium that presented with progressive scalp swelling and localized pain. Radiologic evaluation demonstrated an expansile osteolytic lesion involving the skull with cortical destruction. Surgical excision of the lesion was performed with adequate margins, followed by histopathological examination together with clinico-radiological correlation supported the diagnosis of giant cell tumor of bone in the absence of immunohistochemistry and molecular testing. Although immunohistochemistry tests were not available, diagnosis was established based on clinicopathological correlation. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery with no evidence of recurrence during follow-up. This case highlights the importance of considering giant cell tumor in the differential diagnosis of pediatric calvarial lesions despite its rarity. Early recognition and complete surgical excision remain critical for optimal outcomes and for preventing local recurrence. Reporting such uncommon presentations contributes to the limited literature on cranial GCTs in children and helps improve diagnostic awareness among clinicians.

Introduction

Giant cell tumor (GCT) of bone is a relatively uncommon primary bone neoplasm characterized by the presence of numerous osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells distributed within a background of mononuclear stromal cells. Although histologically benign, GCT is considered a locally aggressive tumor with a tendency for bone destruction and recurrence if not adequately treated. The tumor accounts for approximately 4–5% of all primary bone tumors and most frequently arises in the epiphyseal region of long bones, particularly around the knee joint, distal radius, and proximal humerus [1]. Giant cell tumor before skeletal maturity is exceptionally rare, as the tumor primarily affects skeletally mature individuals aged 20–40, representing less than 5% of all giant cell tumor cases reported in large series.

Cranial involvement is extremely rare and represents only a small fraction of reported GCT cases. Pediatric calvarial giant cell tumors are exceptionally uncommon, with only isolated case reports and small case series available in the literature. Most giant cell tumors occur after skeletal maturity, and cranial involvement accounts for less than 1% of all GCT cases.When the skull is affected, the tumor most commonly arises from bones formed through endochondral ossification, such as the sphenoid and temporal bones. In contrast, involvement of the calvarial bones formed through intramembranous ossification is particularly unusual, especially in children [2]. Because of this rarity, giant cell tumor of the skull is often not initially considered in the differential diagnosis of pediatric lytic skull lesions.

The clinical behavior of cranial GCT differs from that of lesions arising in long bones due to the confined intracranial environment and proximity to critical neurovascular structures. These tumors may present with localized swelling, pain, cosmetic deformity, or symptoms related to mass effect depending on their location and extent. Radiologically, they usually appear as expansile osteolytic lesions with cortical thinning or destruction, but these findings are not pathognomonic and may mimic other benign or malignant calvarial lesions [1].

Given the rarity of pediatric calvarial giant cell tumors and the potential diagnostic challenges they pose, documentation of individual cases remains important to enhance clinical awareness and guide management strategies. In this report, we present a rare case of a giant cell tumor arising from the calvarium in a child, highlighting its clinical presentation, diagnostic considerations, and surgical management.

Clinical presentation

An 8-year-old female school-age child presented to the pediatric clinic with a 1-year and 6-month history of a progressively enlarging mass on the left posterior side of her head, associated with mild headache and localized tenderness. There was no history of trauma or significant family history of bone tumors. On examination, she was clinically stable and in good general condition. A firm to hard consistency, non-mobile, and slightly tender mass measuring 10 cm × 12 cm was palpated over the left parieto-occipital region. Neurological examination revealed no deficits, and there were no signs of increased intracranial pressure. Other systemic examinations were otherwise unremarkable (Fig. 1).

Upon imaging, a computed tomography (CT) scan of the skull revealed a well-defined, lytic lesion measuring approximately 8.4 cm × 5.3 cm × 5.8 cm in size in the left parieto-occipital calvarium region expansile and soft tissue density mass with internal bony fragments. The mass had a compressive mass effect on the parietal and occipital lobes with sulci effacement and dural inward displacement. The mass infiltrated the overlying scalp and showed post-contrast avid heterogeneous enhancement. No calcification was noted.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the lesion's characteristics, showing a well-defined T1 and T2 hypointense mass with areas of hyperintensities in the left occipito-parietal calvarium. The expansile osteolytic appearance with cortical destruction correlates with osteoclastic bone resorption induced by neoplastic stromal cell RANKL expression, which is characteristic of giant cell tumor biology. The lesion was expansile and caused marked expansion of the outer table, and involved the scalp soft tissue. The mass also compressed adjacent brain parenchyma but showed no invasion. It had heterogeneous enhancement on post-contrast study with central non-enhancing areas. The mass showed no restriction on DWI and no blooming on SWI.

Radiologically, differential considerations included aneurysmal bone cyst, eosinophilic granuloma, fibrous dysplasia, telangiectatic osteosarcoma, and metastatic or infectious lesions. However, the absence of fluid–fluid levels, lack of aggressive periosteal reaction, and the lesion’s expansile osteolytic pattern favored giant cell tumor (Fig. 2a and b ).

Surgical intervention

The patient underwent surgical resection of the lesion under general anesthesia. A craniotomy was performed over the left parieto-occipital region to expose the tumor. Intraoperatively, the lesion appeared as an expansile calvarial mass with involvement of both the outer and inner tables and adherence to the underlying dura, but without clear invasion of the brain parenchyma.

The tumor was excised en bloc along with a rim of surrounding uninvolved calvarial bone, approximately 1 cm beyond the visible tumor margin, in order to achieve an R0 resection and reduce the risk of recurrence. Careful dissection was performed to separate the lesion from the adjacent dura while preserving the underlying neural structures. Particular care was required during dissection because of inward dural displacement and proximity to adjacent cortical structures. Preservation of the dura and avoidance of cortical injury were prioritized while obtaining negative bony margins. Hemostasis was achieved after complete removal of the mass.

Intraoperative challenges included careful dissection from the displaced dura and preservation of adjacent cortical structures while maintaining oncologic margins. Particular attention was given to minimizing blood loss and preventing dural injury because of the lesion’s proximity to intracranial structures.

Following tumor excision, the resulting calvarial defect was reconstructed with alloplastic implant-cranioplasty technique using polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) bone cement to restore skull integrity and provide protection to the underlying brain. Histopathological analysis

The excised tumor was sent for histopathological evaluation. Microscopic examination demonstrated evenly distributed multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells within a background of monomorphic oval to spindle-shaped stromal cells. No marked pleomorphism, atypical mitotic figures, malignant osteoid production, or necrosis were identified. Hemosiderin-laden macrophages and focal hemorrhagic areas were present. These findings favored giant cell tumor of bone. In resource-equipped settings, confirmation with H3.3 G34W immunostaining or molecular testing would further support the diagnosis. Giant cell-rich tumours of bone can be difficult to accurately diagnose, especially when only a limited tumour biopsy is available or when clinical and radiological information are ambiguous [3]. Some studies propose that the mutational status of the H3-3A gene should be evaluated using direct Sanger sequencing as the gold standard and IHC as a screening tool in the diagnostic algorithm of GCT [4]. As a resource limited setup, a tertiary hospital in a third world country, immunohistochemical and molecular confirmation (e.g., H3.3 G34W mutation-specific staining) was not available, the diagnosis of giant cell tumor of bone was supported by a combination of characteristic histomorphology, including uniformly distributed osteoclast-like giant cells within a mononuclear stromal background, absence of cytologic atypia or malignant osteoid, and concordant radiologic features. Furthermore, clinic-biochemical correlation excluded other giant cell-rich lesions such as brown tumor of hyperparathyroidism (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Histopathology slides showing evenly distributed multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells embedded within mononuclear stromal cells without significant atypia or malignant osteoid formation (H&E stain, × 100 and × 400)

Future incorporation of mutation-specific H3.3 G34W immunostaining and molecular sequencing techniques may further improve diagnostic precision, particularly in distinguishing giant cell tumor from morphologic mimics in resource-limited settings.

Postoperative course

The patient tolerated the procedure well and was monitored in the pediatric ward. Pain management was initiated, and the patient was started on physiotherapy to facilitate recovery. Surgical margins were histologically free of tumor. Follow-up imaging at 6 months post-surgery showed no evidence of recurrence. The patient remains on scheduled long-term radiologic surveillance because recurrence may occur several years after apparently complete excision. And the patient is planned for continued annual radiologic surveillance for at least 3–5 years.

Longer follow-up remains necessary to evaluate long-term recurrence-free survival.

Discussion

Giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) is a benign but locally aggressive primary bone tumor characterized by osteolytic bone destruction and a tendency for local recurrence. It most commonly arises in the epiphysis of long bones in skeletally mature individuals, particularly around the knee joint, distal radius, and proximal humerus [5, 6]. Cranial involvement is distinctly uncommon, accounting for a very small proportion of cases. When it occurs, it most frequently involves the sphenoid and temporal bones due to their endochondral origin. Involvement of calvarial bones such as the occipital bone is particularly rare, especially in pediatric patients, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [2]. This rarity often presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as expansile lytic lesions of the pediatric skull have a broad differential diagnosis and require careful clinicoradiological and histopathological correlation.

The present case highlights key diagnostic pitfalls: the unusual pediatric age group, rare calvarial location, and overlapping imaging features with both benign and malignant entities. In such settings, reliance solely on morphology without adjunctive testing may lead to diagnostic uncertainty, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary correlation.

Histologically, GCT is composed of three principal cellular components: multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells, mononuclear histiocytic cells, and neoplastic stromal cells, the latter representing the proliferative component of the tumor [7]. These stromal cells play a central role in tumor biology through expression of receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), which promotes recruitment and differentiation of osteoclast precursors into multinucleated giant cells responsible for bone resorption and the characteristic osteolytic appearance of the lesion [8]. Contemporary pathological and molecular studies support the neoplastic nature of the stromal cell population and highlight the complex biological behavior of these tumors which, despite their benign classification, may demonstrate aggressive local growth and occasional metastatic potential [1].

The clinical presentation of cranial GCT differs from that of lesions arising in long bones. While long-bone tumors often present with joint-related symptoms such as pain and limitation of movement, cranial lesions more commonly manifest as a progressively enlarging skull mass, localized pain, or neurological symptoms resulting from compression of adjacent intracranial structures. In pediatric patients, these tumors may be detected earlier because skull deformity or swelling becomes clinically apparent. Radiologically, cranial GCTs typically appear as expansile osteolytic lesions with cortical thinning or destruction and may extend into adjacent soft tissues. However, these imaging findings are not specific and overlap with several other conditions affecting the pediatric skull [1, 5].

The differential diagnosis of expansile lytic calvarial lesions in children includes both benign and malignant conditions. Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC) represents an important consideration, as it frequently occurs in young patients and presents as an expansile lesion with cortical thinning and characteristic fluid–fluid levels on imaging, rulled out in our patient due to absence of fluid–fluid levels & cystic architecture. Eosinophilic granuloma, the localized form of Langerhans cell histiocytosis, is another common pediatric skull lesion and typically appears as a solitary well-defined lytic defect with associated soft tissue swelling. It also usually demonstrates eosinophil-rich infiltrates and characteristic CD1a/Langerin positivity. Our patient lacks eosinophilic infiltrates and punched-out lesion pattern comomly associated with LCH. Metabolic lesions such as brown tumors of hyperparathyroidism may also demonstrate osteolytic destruction with multinucleated giant cells on histology; however, associated biochemical abnormalities such as elevated serum calcium and parathyroid hormone levels help establish the diagnosis, and the subject of this case lacks the clinical or biochemical evidence of elevated calcium or PTH. Malignant conditions, particularly telangiectatic osteosarcoma, should also be considered because they may present as aggressive osteolytic lesions with soft-tissue extension, but no atypia and malignant osteoid production was seen on histopathologic examinations on our patient, features osteosarcoma typically shows. Consequently, definitive diagnosis relies primarily on histopathological examination demonstrating the characteristic distribution of numerous osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells dispersed within a background of mononuclear stromal cells [9].

Immunohistochemical confirmation with markers such as SATB2, CD68, CD1a, and Langerin would have further strengthened diagnostic certainty, but in most cases, the pathologist can render an accurate diagnosis based on histopathologic features [10], however, these studies were not available locally and represent a limitation of this report.

Although classified as benign, GCT demonstrates unpredictable biological behavior. Local recurrence rates after treatment range from approximately 10% to 30%, largely depending on the surgical approach and adequacy of resection margins [11]. Furthermore, approximately 1–5% of patients develop pulmonary metastases despite the absence of malignant histological features [9]. This potential for recurrence and metastasis underscores the importance of achieving complete tumor removal and ensuring long-term surveillance.

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment for GCT. In long bones, intralesional curettage combined with local adjuvant therapy is often preferred in order to preserve joint function. However, treatment strategies differ in cranial lesions where preservation of skull integrity and prevention of intracranial extension are critical considerations. En bloc resection with negative margins is generally recommended when feasible, as it significantly reduces the risk of local recurrence compared with intralesional procedures [12]. Reconstruction of the resulting bone defect is often required to restore skull stability and protect the underlying brain.

In the present case, complete surgical excision was achieved with removal of the lesion along with a margin of uninvolved surrounding calvarial bone to ensure an R0 resection. The resulting calvarial defect was reconstructed using polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cranioplasty, which provides immediate structural stability and protection of intracranial contents. PMMA is widely used in cranial reconstruction because it is readily available, cost-effective, and capable of restoring the contour of the skull. Achieving tumor-free surgical margins is particularly important in cranial GCT because incomplete excision may predispose to local recurrence, which can be more difficult to manage in the confined intracranial environment [11]. Although six-month postoperative imaging showed no recurrence, giant cell tumors are known to recur even after apparently complete excision, often within the first 2–3 years. Continued serial radiologic surveillance is therefore necessary. The relatively short follow-up period is a limitation of this report.

Adjunctive therapies have been explored for unresectable or recurrent disease. Radiotherapy may be considered when complete surgical excision is not feasible due to proximity to critical neurovascular structures, although concerns remain regarding the potential risk of radiation-induced sarcomatous transformation. More recently, targeted therapies such as denosumab, a monoclonal antibody against RANKL, have shown promising results in controlling tumor progression by inhibiting osteoclast formation and activity [9]. Nevertheless, surgery continues to represent the primary curative modality for most localized cases.

Conclusion

Overall, cranial GCTB remains a rare clinical entity, particularly in the pediatric population. Because of its rarity and overlapping radiologic features with other skull lesions, accurate diagnosis requires careful correlation of clinical presentation, imaging findings, and histopathological examination. Early recognition, multidisciplinary diagnostic evaluation, and complete excision remain central to management, and prolonged follow-up is necessary given the known recurrence potential. This case was presented at an MDT session at our hospital, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration between neurosurgery, radiology, and pathology in achieving accurate diagnoses and optimal management.

This case contributes to the limited literature regarding pediatric calvarial giant cell tumors and highlights the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation in rare skull lesions. Additional reports and pooled analyses are needed to better define optimal diagnostic strategies, recurrence patterns, and long-term outcomes. Reporting rare pediatric calvarial GCTs may improve early recognition and guide future multidisciplinary management strategies.
