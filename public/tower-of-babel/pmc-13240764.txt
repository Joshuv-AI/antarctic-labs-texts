Triple impalement by fishing spears (‘teta’): Surgical decision-making and multisite management in a resource-limited setting — A case report
Authors: Saumik Sarkar, Saumitra Sarkar, Shuvro Saha, Avijit Dey, Shankar Kumar Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Penetrating injury is an important reason for hospital admission. In case of multiple penetrating injury early resuscitation, adequate examination and investigation with proper surgical management sequence can significantly reduce the risk of morbidity and mortality.A 34-year-old male from a border area of Sylhet, Bangladesh, presented to the emergency department 6 h after being assaulted with three iron fishing spears (“teta”). The patient had impalement injuries to the left cheek, left lumbar region, and left foot. On admission, he was conscious and hemodynamically stable, though with abdominal distension. Radiological investigations confirmed deep soft tissue and visceral penetration. An emergency laparotomy revealed perforations in the transverse colon and ileum, necessitating segmental repair and jejunostomy due to concerns of delayed presentation, bowel edema, and risk of anastomotic failure in a resource limited setting.All three foreign bodies were successfully removed surgically at Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital, Bangladesh. Postoperative recovery was uneventful. This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary surgical intervention, and resilience in managing complex penetrating injuries in a resource limited setting.

Introduction

Missile and non-missile weapons can cause penetrating injury. Penetrating trauma, especially from low-velocity, non-ballistic weapons such as “teta”, are rare and often underreported in modern surgical literature. Teta, a traditional instrument, is a long, metallic spear-like object with a long wooden or bamboo handle and it typically has sharp points, barbs, or toggling mechanisms to catch fish in shallow water [1] (Fig. 1). While urban trauma centers primarily manage firearm and sharp force injuries, rural and north-eastern areas still face assaults with traditional weapons, often during interpersonal or territorial disputes. The offending object is considered a foreign body and can cause damage to the organs and vascular structure in the abdomen. However, multiple site teta injury in a single patient is first documented as per our knowledge. Multiple-site impalement raises significant diagnostic, anesthetic, and surgical challenges [2], [3]. This case presents a unique survival following triple implement and successful surgical management, underscoring the importance of comprehensive trauma protocols in resource-limited settings. Case presentation

A 34-year-old male was referred from a primary care hospital to the Surgery Casualty Department at Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College Hospital, 6 h post-assault with three tetas (locally crafted iron spears) in different parts of body on his left side. The first impacted teta was entered through the left cheek projecting medially towards the mandible (Fig. 2), the second one found impacted in the left lumbar region suggestive of visceral injury (Fig. 3), and the final one caused through and through injury in the left foot between metatarsals (Fig. 4). During the initial assessment at the emergency room, the patient was conscious, oriented but anxious. His vitals were within normal limit. There was abdominal distension with mild tenderness, but no active bleeding or sign of shock. X-ray skull showed a metallic foreign body in the left cheek (Fig. 5). Abdominal X-ray revealed a retained metallic, spare with intestinal gas pattern and free air under diaphragm suggesting perforation (Fig. 6). Management

The patient resuscitated by using advanced trauma life support (ATLS) protocols and triaged quickly as there was abdominal penetrating injury. After doing emergency baseline investigations a multidisciplinary surgical team proceeded with staged management. Initially an emergency exploratory laparotomy under general anesthesia was planned at emergency operation theatre. The removal of lumbar teta, repair of transverse colon and ileal perforations with formation of loop jejunostomy was done. The loop jejunostomy was done due to concerns of delayed presentation, bowel edema, and risk of anastomotic failure in a resource limited setting. Then careful removal of cheek impacted teta by knockout technique with the help of neurosurgeons to avoid facial nerve and maxillary artery damage. Adequate wound debridement was ensured and then sutured. Finally, the impacted teta at foot was removed by knockout technique with preservation of planter arch and neuro vascular structures with the help of orthopedic surgeons [5]. Tetanus prophylaxis was ensured. The patient was managed with intravenous antibiotics (ceftriaxone + metronidazole), adequate analgesia, and local wound care. Jejunostomy functioned adequately from postoperative day 2, ambulation initiated on day 3, oral intake resumed on day 5. The patient was discharged on day 10 with clean wounds and follow-up plans. Discussion

In the emergency department, penetrating abdominal trauma is an important reason for hospital admission. Worldwide about 24% trauma patient have abdominal injuries [6]. Abdomen is the most injured area after the head and extremities [7], [8]. The incidence of penetrating abdominal injury various in different countries due to demographic characteristics, violent crime rates and firearm legislation. However, in most countries, the prime cause of penetrating abdominal injury is gunshot, stab injury, occupational injury, and accidental injury [9], [10]. Abdominal penetrating injury with extremity caused by teta is a rare occurrence. Largest teta injury series were reported by Chowdhury FH and Hague MR where affected patients were only four [11]. As this type of injury has low incidence, the management creates challenges for the surgeons during and after resuscitation room care. Moreover, this case exemplifies a rare and survivable example of multisite impalement from traditional spears. Literature on triple impalement with low-velocity weapons is exceedingly limited [11], [12]. However, impalement injuries have been historically discussed in trauma literature, mainly involving construction accidents or rebar injuries [12], [13]. In case of a retained non-missile penetrating injury, immediate life-threatening complications should be accessed and managed accordingly under the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocol, and there should be minimal manipulation before imaging to avoid converting a tamponade into hemorrhage. Imaging studies give a panoramic visualization of the extent of the injury. As teta is a metal foreign body which may be hooked or non-hooked, plane radiographs in two different planes are very important for visualization of the trajectory. Computed tomography (CT) scan may complicate the situation with making.artefacts. Prioritization of injuries is very crucial for multiple penetrating injuries. Abdominal injury was first managed as it contained the highest mortality risk A particularly important aspect of this case was the complexity of operative decision-making despite the patient initially presenting in a hemodynamically stable condition. Although primary repair or resection with anastomosis may be considered in selected penetrating bowel injuries, several intraoperative and contextual factors influenced our decision to perform a protective loop jejunostomy. The patient presented approximately 6 h after injury, with evidence of localized contamination and bowel edema around the perforation sites [14]. Skelhorne-Gross G et al. suggested that the treatment of penetrating abdominal trauma solely depends on patient's physiological condition and the magnitude of the injury [15]. Knockout technique was used for the removal of facial and foot teta with preservation of neurovascular structures. BM Harrington et al. suggested a knockout technique for the removal of retained foreign bodies in case of bone penetrating injuries [5]. In addition, multisite impalement carries the risk of sudden clinical deterioration due to occult vascular compromise, evolving peritonitis, or delayed septic complications. Therefore, the surgical strategy was guided not only by the anatomical injuries but also by principles of damage-control thinking aimed at minimizing postoperative morbidity and anastomotic failure in an uncertain physiological environment [16]. This case also highlights important differences between trauma management in resource-limited environments and high-resource trauma centers. In well-equipped centers with advanced perioperative monitoring, intensive care support, and early re-intervention capability, definitive bowel reconstruction may often be pursued more aggressively. However, in many low-resource settings, surgeons must adopt safer and more pragmatic strategies tailored to local realities. The fundamental principles of impalement injury management remain universal: the foreign body should be left in situ until operative control is achieved, removal should occur under direct visualization, and life-threatening injuries must be prioritized sequentially [17]. In our case, abdominal exploration and controlled extraction of the lumbar spear were prioritized before addressing facial and extremity injuries, reflecting the need for structured operative planning in multisite impalement trauma. The favorable outcome in this case demonstrates that adherence to these principles can achieve successful results even in resource-constrained environments. Finally, the synchronizing action of General surgeon, neurosurgeon, orthopedic surgeon with anesthesiologist allowed for safe removal and recovery. Such injuries necessitate high suspicion for hidden injuries, robust trauma protocols even in semi-urban centers, and emphasis on patient-centered recovery. The positive outcome here is a testament to coordinate emergency response and surgical acumen under pressure. A limitation of this report is the absence of advanced vascular imaging and limited postoperative intensive care resources, which influenced aspects of surgical decision-making.

Conclusion

Not only the rarity of combined teta injury but also the complex decision making at a resource limited setting makes this case clinically significant. In rural and border areas where unconventional weapons are still used, emergency preparedness and trauma training remain paramount.
