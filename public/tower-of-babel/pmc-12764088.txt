Effect of dexmedetomidine on postoperative mortality and prognosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Ziwei Xu, Di Wang, Huanan Sun, Wenhui Zhang, Siru Li, Naying Su, Yutao Chen, Chenxin Jiang, Congjie Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis meta-analysis aimed to systematically evaluate the impact of intraoperative dexmedetomidine (DEX) on postoperative mortality and clinical outcomes in surgical patients, addressing existing controversies in the literature.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) from PubMed, Embase and Cochrane Library (inception to October 8, 2024; PROSPERO: CRD42024583524). Included studies compared intraoperative DEX against controls (placebo/active comparators) in adults undergoing general anesthesia. Primary outcome was mortality; secondary outcomes included postoperative delirium (POD), ICU/hospital stay, mechanical ventilation duration, and safety endpoints. Risk ratios (RR) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were pooled using fixed/random-effects models.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 17 randomized controlled trials (RCTs). In cardiac surgery patients, DEX significantly reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.85; P = 0.02) and in-hospital mortality (RR 0.23, 95% CI 0.08–0.70; P = 0.01), but not 30-day mortality. In non-cardiac surgery patients, DEX did not significantly reduce all-cause, 30-day, or in-hospital mortality. DEX decreased the incidence of POD in both cardiac (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.46–0.82; P = 0.001) and non-cardiac surgery patients (RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.36–0.87; P = 0.01). For other outcomes, significant reductions in hospital stay, ICU stay, and mechanical ventilation duration were primarily observed in the cardiac surgery subgroup. Safety analysis indicated there was no significant difference in intraoperative bradycardia or hypotension between groups.ConclusionsDexmedetomidine use in cardiac surgery significantly reduces all-cause and in-hospital mortality, shortens hospital length of stay, decreases ICU stay duration, and reduces mechanical ventilation requirements. However, its impact on 30-day mortality is not significant. Additionally, the drug consistently demonstrates a preventive effect on postoperative delirium across different surgical types.Clinicians must carefully weigh the benefits of this medication against the potential risk of transient hemodynamic instability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03514-z.

Introduction

Globally, patients undergoing major surgeries, such as cardiac surgery or major abdominal procedures, who often have higher ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) physical status scores (e.g., ASA III-IV) and significant comorbidities, have a persistently high postoperative mortality rate, which not only causes immense patient suffering but also poses a severe challenge to the rational allocation and utilization of healthcare resources [1–4]. Despite advances in surgical and anesthetic techniques, the quest for pharmacological agents that can mitigate these risks and improve patient outcomes remains paramount. Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a highly selective alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonist, has emerged as a promising candidate due to its unique pharmacological profile offering sedation, analgesia, anxiolysis, and sympatholysis without significant respiratory depression [5, 6].

The theoretical rationale for DEX’s potential benefit in improving postoperative prognosis is compelling. Its sympatholytic effects may confer cardiovascular stability and reduce myocardial oxygen demand, potentially lowering the risk of perioperative cardiac events [7]. DEX also significantly reduces the occurrence of stress response in patients and maintains hemodynamic stability. Greis et al. [8] administered a loading dose of dexmedetomidine intravenously 10 min before anesthesia induction to adult laparoscopic patients. The medication was then maintained at a rate of 0.2–0.7 µg/kg/h until 10 min before the end of surgery. Compared with a fentanyl infusion, this approach resulted in significantly reduced morphine consumption within 24 h postoperatively. Blood glucose and serum cortisol levels at 4 h postoperatively indicated markedly diminished stress responses, while both early and late-stage visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores were lower. Furthermore, DEX has been associated with reduced the incidence of postoperative delirium (POD), a significant predictor of poor long-term outcomes and mortality, and potentially lower rates of acute kidney injury (AKI) in some patient cohorts [9–11]. The study by Zhang et al. [12] showed that controlling for low central venous pressure, combined with a DEX loading dose, could effectively reduce blood loss, improve liver and kidney function, and protect patients’ cognitive function in laparoscopic hepatectomy patients.

However, the impact of dexmedetomidine on the most critical endpoints – all-cause mortality and overall prognosis – remains controversial and inadequately defined by individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs). While some studies report positive trends or significant reductions in mortality [13]– [14], others show no benefit or even signal potential harm related to adverse effects like bradycardia or hypotension [15]– [16]. This inconsistency stems from several factors: the relatively low mortality rates in many surgical populations requiring very large sample sizes for adequate power; the frequent focus of individual trials on surrogate or secondary outcomes (e.g., pain scores, delirium incidence, length of stay); and variability in dosing regimens, patient populations (e.g., cardiac vs. non-cardiac surgery) and comparator groups. Consequently, clinicians lack definitive evidence to guide its use specifically for mortality and major morbidity risk reduction.

This meta-analysis, therefore, aims to rigorously evaluate the effect of intraoperative administration of DEX, compared to placebo or alternative sedatives, on the primary endpoint of mortality. By pooling data from RCTs, we seek to provide a more precise and reliable estimate of DEX’s true impact on these patient-centered outcomes, informing evidence-based clinical decision-making.

Methods

Search strategy

We executed a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus (inception to October 8, 2024) using predefined MeSH terms (e.g., ‘Dexmedetomidine’, ‘Survival rate’, ‘Surgery’) and free-text keywords. During the process where the three reviewers (Z.X., D.W. and H.S.) conducted independent literature searches and data extraction. Any disagreements were resolved by the corresponding author. The study was registered in PROSPERO with identifier CRD42024583524 on 08 October 2024.

Study selection

Using the PICO(Population/Intervention/Comparator/Outcome) criteria, we identified eligible studies as follows: the population consisted of adult and pediatric patients undergoing cardiac surgery, and adult patients undergoing noncardiac surgery; the intervention was the intraoperative administration of DEX; the comparator was other drugs or normal saline; and the outcome was the postoperative mortality using either the mortality or survival. Only RCTs published in English or Chinese were included for analysis. The following exclusion criteria were applied to studies: (1) were non-RCTs, observational studies, case reports, or reviews; (2) did not assess or report on mortality or survival rate; and (3) had been retracted.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of our analysis was mortality. Given the variability in how mortality was reported across trials, we stratified this outcome into three clinically relevant timeframes for analysis: (1) All-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring at any time point during the study’s follow-up period; (2) 30-day mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days postoperatively; and (3) In-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring during the index hospitalization for surgery. Secondary endpoints encompassed a mix of intraoperative safety parameters and postoperative outcomes. The intraoperative safety endpoints included bradycardia and hypotension. The postoperative outcomes included the duration of mechanical ventilation, the length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), the total hospital length of stay, the incidence of postoperative delirium (POD), diagnosed using validated tools such as the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) or its ICU-adapted version (CAM-ICU), and acute kidney injury (AKI), defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, typically as an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or a 1.5-fold increase from baseline within 7 days.

Data extraction

Data extraction was mainly conducted by two reviewers (Z.X. and D.W.) independently following a standardized protocol. In instances where uncertainty or inconsistency arose, clarification was sought through consultation with the corresponding authors. The data extracted encompassed several key elements: the first author’s name, year and country of publication, details of the experimental design, sample sizes for both the intervention and control groups, age range of the study participants, anesthesia modalities, types of surgery performed, medications administered in the control group, specifics of the DEX infusion strategy, mortality rates, and other pertinent outcomes.

Bias assessment

Two investigators (D.W. and H.S.) worked independently to identify risk of bias RCTs using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. Risk of bias was analyzed in all five categories, including (1) Random sequence generation (selection bias); (2) Allocation concealment (selection bias); (3) Blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias); (4) Blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias); (5) Incomplete outcome data (attrition bias); (6) Selective reporting (reporting bias); and (7) Other bias was reported.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using Review Manager (RevMan version 5.4.1) and STATA (version 12.0) Dichotomous data were analyzed using risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and categorized as low (I² < 50%), moderate (50% ≤ I² ≤ 75%), or high (I² >75%) [17]. A fixed-effect model was used for meta-analysis when I² < 50%. For studies exhibiting moderate or high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50%), a random-effects model was employed, and subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, with a significance threshold of p >0.05. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings.

Results

Search results

Consistent with our pre-established search strategy, our database queries initially returned 944 articles, with no further articles identified through manual searching of reference lists or trial registries. After the elimination of duplicates, 798 articles were retained. Following a review of titles and abstracts, 487 articles were excluded for their lack of alignment with the objectives of our meta-analysis. A detailed examination of the full texts of the remaining 311 articles resulted in the exclusion of an additional 289 articles, largely due to their noncompliance with the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 17 RCTs were deemed suitable for inclusion in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of trials

The present analysis included trials published from 2016 to 2024, involving 3013 participants [18–34]. All 17 studies were reported in English. Among the included studies, there were 1 experiment conducted in America, 1 in Australia, 1 in Belgium, 6 in China,3 in Egypt, 3 in Korea, 1 in Indonesia, and 1 in Russia. There was no restriction on the type of surgery or type of anesthesia used. DEX was administered to at least one group in each study. The comparator encompassed other drugs or normal saline. The primary outcome measure in our study was the mortality of patients undergoing surgery. It is worth noting that many articles have evaluated the mortality of patients at multiple time points after surgery. Thus, these studies were included as separate outcomes in the analysis. Meta-analysis for the studies

We performed pre-specified subgroup analyses to investigate the potential influence of surgical type (cardiac surgery vs. non-cardiac surgery) on the primary outcomes and on the key secondary outcome.

The all-cause mortality analysis included 5 trials (n = 1026) in cardiac surgery and 2 trials (n = 260) in non-cardiac surgery(Fig. 4).A significant reduction was observed in patients undergoing cardiac surgery (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.85; P = 0.02), whereas no significant benefit was detected in non-cardiac surgical patients (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.38–1.41; P = 0.34), although the test for subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.18). Similarly, the in-hospital mortality outcome was analyzed across 5 trials (Fig. 5). DEX was associated with a pronounced risk reduction among patients undergoing cardiac surgery (4 trials, n = 642; RR 0.23, 95% CI 0.08–0.70; P = 0.01). This effect was not evident in the non-cardiac surgery cohort, which included data from 1 trial (n = 201) (RR 0.51, 95% CI 0.05–5.48; P = 0.57). A total of 8 trials reported on 30-day mortality (Fig. 6). No significant between-group difference was found in either the cardiac surgery subgroup (5 trials, n = 964; RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.37–2.90) or the non-cardiac surgery subgroup (3 trials, n = 727; RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.06–2.86).

Regarding the incidence of POD, a beneficial effect of DEX was consistent across surgical types (Fig. 7). The analysis, which included 6 cardiac surgery trials (n = 1510) and 2 non-cardiac surgery trials (n = 635), showed significant risk reductions in both subgroups (Cardiac: RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.46–0.82, P = 0.001; Non-cardiac: RR 0.56, 95% CI 0.36–0.87, P = 0.01), with no significant subgroup interaction (P = 0.73).

Significant reductions in hospital length of stay were demonstrated in both subgroups (Fig. 8). The magnitude of the effect was more substantial in the 4 cardiac surgery trials (n = 595) (MD − 2.49 days, 95% CI − 3.43 to − 1.55) compared to the 4 non-cardiac surgery trials (n = 897) (MD − 1.37 days, 95% CI − 2.26 to − 0.48).

For ICU length of stay (Fig. 9), a significant reduction was noted exclusively in the cardiac surgery subgroup, derived from 5 trials (n = 813) (MD − 0.80 days, 95% CI − 1.38 to − 0.23; P = 0.006). In contrast, the synthesis of 3 non-cardiac surgery trials (n = 840) showed no significant effect (MD − 0.05 days, 95% CI − 0.34 to 0.24; P = 0.76). The test for subgroup differences was significant (P = 0.02), indicating a differential effect based on surgical type.

Similarly, the duration of mechanical ventilation was significantly shortened in patients from the 4 cardiac surgery trials (n = 820) (MD − 3.18 h, 95% CI − 4.76 to − 1.60; P < 0.0001). This finding was not replicated in the analysis of 2 non-cardiac surgery trials (n = 406) (MD − 0.30 h, 95% CI − 1.82 to 1.23; P = 0.70). The difference between these subgroups was statistically significant (P = 0.002) (Fig. 10).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

The funnel plots (Figs. 13, 14 and 15) and the results of Egger’s tests for All-Cause Mortality (P = 0.932), 30-Day Mortality (P = 0.883), In-Hospital Mortality (P = 0.094) pointed to no significant publication bias within the included studies. We also performed a sensitivity analysis (Figs. 16, 17 and 18) on the included studies to assess the influence of each individual study on the pooled effect size. This process involved sequentially removing one study at a time from the aggregate data. The exclusion of a specific study didn’t influence the overall results.

Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials, encompassing 3013 surgical patients.

Our principal findings demonstrate that DEX significantly reduces all-cause mortality, in-hospital mortality and the incidence of POD. Importantly, these benefits were achieved without a statistically significant increase in the risks of intraoperative bradycardia or hypotension, although point estimates suggested potential trends warranting clinical vigilance.

The finding of robust all-cause mortality and in-hospital mortality reduction stands in contrast to the neutral effect observed for 30-day mortality (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.34–2.38). This apparent paradox likely reflects the heterogeneity in mortality endpoint definitions across trials and the multifaceted nature of “all-cause mortality” capture. The significant reduction in all-cause mortality, coupled with in-hospital mortality, suggests that DEX may exert its most potent survival benefits during the immediate perioperative and early recovery period. This finding resolves a key controversy in the literature. While earlier meta-analyses reported inconsistent mortality effects [35, 36], our updated synthesis—incorporating more recent trials—provides compelling evidence for a significant all-cause mortality advantage. This discrepancy may be attributed to our stricter endpoint stratification (all-cause vs. 30-day vs. in-hospital), and inclusion of higher-quality RCTs published since prior analyses. Mechanistically, the protective effects of DEX during this high-risk phase likely stem from the convergence of several properties: its ability to provide hemodynamic stabilization through sedative, analgesic, and sympatholytic effects, thereby attenuating surgical stress responses [36]; direct cardio protection mediated via α2-receptor activation and modulation of the eNOS/NO pathway [37]; attenuation of ischemia-reperfusion injury achieved through mitochondrial KATP channel modulation [38]; suppression of pro-apoptotic pathways in vulnerable tissues [39]; and mitigation of the systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgery [16]. Furthermore, the significant reduction in POD incidence—itself an established predictor of mortality—likely contributes to these improved outcomes [40]. Standardization of mortality endpoints in future trials is essential to definitively elucidate this temporal pattern of benefit.

Regarding secondary outcomes, DEX demonstrated substantial benefits in both cardiac and non-cardiac surgery populations for POD reduction. The significant 40% reduction in POD incidence is a clinically salient finding, given its established association with increased long-term mortality, cognitive decline, and healthcare costs. DEX’s α2-adrenergic agonist properties – conferring sympatholytic, anxiolysis, analgesia with minimal respiratory depression, and a unique “cooperative sedation” profile – offer a plausible mechanistic explanation for its efficacy in preventing this devastating complication [41, 42]. This result strongly corroborates findings from previous meta-analyses focused specifically on neurocognitive outcomes [9, 10]. It must be acknowledged, however, that the cardiac surgery cohort included pediatric patients, a population in which the pathophysiology, risk factors, and clinical presentation of POD differ substantively from those in adults. This heterogeneity within the cardiac subgroup necessitates caution. Nonetheless, the consistency of the POD benefit highlights its clinical salience, given delirium’s established association with long-term mortality, cognitive decline, and increased healthcare costs. Furthermore, DEX was associated with reduced hospital length of stay (MD −2.02 days, 95% CI −2.60 to −1.45), ICU length of stay (MD −0.47 days, 95% CI −0.78 to −0.16), and duration of mechanical ventilation (MD −2.96 h, 95% CI −5.35 to −0.57). These reductions underscore DEX’s role in facilitating smoother, faster recovery trajectories and enhancing resource utilization, aligning with findings from several meta-analyses [42]. The lack of significant effect on kidney injury risk (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.36–1.09) is also noteworthy, suggesting renal safety within the studied dosing regimens.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis revealed that the mortality benefit was driven primarily by patients undergoing cardiac surgery, with significant reductions in both all-cause mortality (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18–0.85) and in-hospital mortality (RR 0.23, 95% CI 0.08–0.70). In contrast, no significant benefit was observed in non-cardiac surgical patients. This differential effect may reflect the unique physiological stress of cardiac surgery, which involves direct manipulation of the heart and cardiopulmonary bypass, potentially leading to a more pronounced adrenergic and inflammatory response that is particularly amenable to modulation by DEX’s sympatholytic effects. Similarly, reductions in ICU stay and mechanical ventilation duration were significant only in the cardiac surgery subgroup, suggesting that the perioperative context modulates the magnitude of DEX’s clinical benefits.

The safety profile observed is reassuring. While DEX is known to cause dose-dependent bradycardia and hypotension, our pooled analysis found no statistically significant increase in bradycardia (RR 1.54, 95% CI 0.92–2.58; P = 0.10) or hypotension (RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.93–1.70; P = 0.13) compared to controls. However, the point estimates indicate a potential for increased risk. In the study conducted by Helene Beloeil et al. [43], five instances of severe bradycardia were reported in the DEX group under the context of opioid-free anesthesia, and three cases of cardiac arrest were terminated prematurely. This suggests that caution should be exercised when administering DEX, emphasizing the need for appropriate patient selection (e.g., avoiding use in severe bradyarrhythmia or hypovolemia), careful hemodynamic monitoring, and dose titration, particularly during bolus administration.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, significant heterogeneity was present for some outcomes (e.g., ICU stay, ventilation duration), attributable to variations in surgery types, DEX dosing, and outcome definitions. Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings is influenced by two key factors: the included trials predominantly enrolled patients without contraindications to DEX, which may limit the applicability of the safety profile to a broader population; and the focus on specific surgical settings necessitates caution when extrapolating to all surgical contexts. Second, the follow-up duration was insufficient to assess long-term outcomes. Third, and crucially, the original trials were not powered for mortality or several other secondary outcomes we analyzed, meaning some results, particularly in the non-cardiac surgery subgroup, may be underpowered and should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, while we did not detect significant publication bias for primary outcomes, it cannot be ruled out for all endpoints.The results suggest that we should concentrate on specific populations and patients undergoing various surgical procedures. Differences in trial designs and medication regimens may affect outcomes, potentially indicating areas that require further investigation through randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to uncover potential benefits for targeted populations. Moreover, our meta-analysis encompassed a limited number of studies, especially those involving patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery, underscoring the necessity for such research in future endeavors.

Conclusions

Dexmedetomidine use in cardiac surgery significantly reduces all-cause and in-hospital mortality, shortens hospital length of stay, decreases ICU stay duration, and reduces mechanical ventilation requirements. However, its impact on 30-day mortality is not significant. Additionally, the drug consistently demonstrates a preventive effect on postoperative delirium across different surgical types.Clinicians must carefully weigh the benefits of this medication against the potential risk of transient hemodynamic instability.
