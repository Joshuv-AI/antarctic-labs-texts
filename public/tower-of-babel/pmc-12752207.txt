Parentification and the family system in female adolescents of the second year of high school
Authors: Mitra Soltanian, Somayeh Jalil, Azita Jaberi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParentification in female students can lead to numerous physical and psychological issues. However, the relationship between family systems and parentification has not yet been explored. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate this relationship in female adolescents.Materials and methodsThis cross-sectional study included 124 female high school students in their second year in Shiraz, Iran, selected through convenience sampling. The participants completed the Parentification Inventory and the Family System Test (FAST).FindingsThe results showed no significant relationship between the mean scores of family system and total parentification score in participants (p > 0.05), except for the ideal cohesion between father and adolescent (p = 0.009). There was a significant relationship between age and parentification (p < 0.001). An inverse and significant relationship was found between the ideal cohesion of father-adolescent and mother-adolescent, as well as between the real power of adolescent-father and adolescent-mother (p < 0.05). The mean scores of parentification, perceived fairness, emotional parenting, and instrumental parenting were higher in the 18-year-old age group compared to other age groups (p < 0.05).ConclusionWhile no significant relationship was found between family system and parentification in adolescents, those who take on parental responsibilities within the family tend to feel less cohesion with their mother and perceive the father’s power as low. Given the social and psychological risks faced by female adolescents, it is recommended to implement empowerment programs for these families and provide support for female adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06364-3.

Introduction

By definition, “child maltreatment is an act of commission (doing something) or omission (failing to do something) by a parent or caregiver that has caused or has the potential to cause harm to a child or young person” [1]. Previous studies have shown that individuals with secure attachments and appropriate interactions with parents, might cope more easily with daily problems. In other words, child maltreatment may contribute to psychological insecurity [2]. A combination of adverse childhood maltreatment and family dysfunction might lead to negative outcomes in subsequent life courses [3].

“Parentification” is a kind of child neglect and a part of child maltreatment that may have substantial effects on emotional, social and educational development, personality and identity, interpersonal relationships and adaptation of children [4, 5]. In 2015, out of every 1000 children, nearly 10 children had a history of parentification [6]. Since adolescent girls are exposed to more problems than other family members [7], parentification may cause more problems for them [6]. A recent review study showed a greater likelihood of victimization of emotional abuse for girls; as well [8]. Literature review also showed that several factors, such as gender, age appropriateness, cultural and family context, and individual child and adolescent characteristics, could determine the effect of parentification on the health and well-being of adolescents [9]. Thus, investigating the antecedents, predictors, and outcomes related to parentification during early adolescence is crucial due to the susceptibility to the emergence of health-related issues frequently observed in this age group [10].

According to the family system theory, as proposed by Minuchin in 1974, each family member has roles and responsibilities based on the division of power, and if one of the family members does not perform these roles, other members may be forced to play theirs [11, 12]. Researchers have also highlighted the significance of maintaining distinct boundaries between parents and children. Moreover, the development of a child and the formation of the family system are continuous, interdependent processes. In this context, the psychological makeup of the child is integral to the family’s structure [13]. Therefore, family structure has a very important effect on children and their physical and mental health [14].

A meta-analysis in Iran reported that the prevalence of child neglect was 40.945% [15], which means that child abuse in Iran is in critical condition [16]. Despite the importance of the family system and the prevalence of child maltreatment in Iran, research is limited on the most common types of parentification in various family circumstances. Additionally, it is unknown whether any particular family structure or function is related to these family structures. Hence, the factors that emerge parentification, such as chronic diseases, divorce, and parental addiction, which makes the family vulnerable and high risk, are relatively prevalent in Iran [17, 18]. Although the existing studies in Iran have mostly examined the family system and its relationship with variables such as self-efficacy, social adaptation, and behavioral problems [19], there are scarce studies about parentification and its relationship with the family system. On the other hand, in studies that have dealt with high-risk families, the concept of parentification as one of the destructive things affecting the psychological and social condition of adolescents has not been investigated. Therefore, the present study was conducted with the aim of investigating the relationship between perceived parentification and the family system in female adolescents.

Materials and methods

This research is a cross-sectional study conducted from December 2022 to November 2023. The participants were adolescent girls in their second year of high school in Shiraz, Iran. Inclusion criteria included parental consent for the adolescent’s participation, an age range of 15 to 18 years, enrollment in one of the three schools in District 4 of Shiraz, and no history of mental or physical illness in the parents that resulted in hospitalization during the study period. It is important to note that students from District 4 were selected because their families faced challenges such as addiction and divorce more frequently than those in other districts, making their children more vulnerable to parentification. Participants who missed more than 10% of the questions on the parentification questionnaire were excluded.

Convenient sampling was conducted by obtaining permission from university and high school authorities to distribute the parentification questionnaire via WhatsApp to adolescents and their parents. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the process of obtaining consent and distributing the questionnaire was done virtually through WhatsApp. School Deans were informed and asked parents to fill out consent forms and adolescents to complete the parentification questionnaire. Consent forms were sent via WhatsApp to parents and adolescents, and a link containing the questionnaire items was provided one by one on the PORSONLINE platform. The researcher had access to the participants’ responses through the provided link.

Once the COVID-19 quarantine ended and schools reopened, the researcher visited the schools to explain the family system test to participants who had completed the questionnaires. The family system test was administered in person. Additionally, the psychometric properties of the questionnaire, including test-retest reliability, were checked individually and in person.

The data gathered in the present study by the following tools:

Demographic information The questionnaire included adolescents’ age, field of education, and Grade point Average (GPA), the parents’ age, the parents’ education level, the number of family members, and if the adolescents and the parents have had any physical or mental diseases.

Parentification instrument (PI) This questionnaire was designed by Jurkovic and Thirkield (1998) consists of 30 items and 3 factors, i.e. instrumental parentification (10 items), emotional parentification (10 items), and perceived fairness (10 items). The items score based on a 5-point Likert scale that a higher score indicates a higher level of parentification. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.81 for instrumental parenting, 0.82 for emotional parenting, and 0.88 for perceived fairness [20]. The questionnaire was translated, and the validity and reliability of this tool have been measured in the present study. We used the backward-forward translation method, in which two independent native Persian speakers performed the translation of the original English PI (forward translation), and after discussing discrepancies, a consensus was adopted. Afterwards, two other independent and blinded translators performed backward translation. Then, an expert review group consisting of one pediatrician, one psychiatrist, one psychometrician, and the authors checked and confirmed the final version of the instrument. In qualitative face validity, 5 face-to-face interviews with adolescent girls were performed to clarify the items in terms of ambiguity. This way, the cross-cultural adaptation of the PI was assured.

According to experts’ opinions, all items had an importance coefficient higher than 1.5; content validity index (I-CVI and S-CVI) was higher than 0.9, and content validity ratio (CVR) showed that all items obtained Lawshe critical scores. In the current research, factor analysis was used for construct validity. In this method, the factor load was considered 0.4, and since the correlation between the factors was less than 0.3, the varimax rotation was used. The results showed that the 3-factor is a fit model based on the scree plot (Fig. 1) and factor loading after rotation. Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha of the entire questionnaire was 0.846, and in the test-retest distribution of the questionnaire, there were no significant differences between the mean scores in a two-week interval (p = 0.055), which acknowledged the internal consistency and stability.Fig. 1The scree plot of the factor analysis for parentification inventory

Family system test (FAST) This scale was prepared in 1986 by Gehring and Wheeler [21], which simultaneously measures cohesion and decision-making authority in family relationships in a symbolic way, which includes power and cohesion. This tool consists of a 45 × 45 cm check-like wooden board that has a total of 9 × 9 houses.

Additionally, there are wooden sculptures with a height of 6 cm in male and female forms, and cylindrical pieces of wood in the size of 1.5, 3 and 4.5 cm. The statues are placed on the screen based on the proximity of the families and the intimacy between the family members. The scoring is based on different dimensions of relationship, cohesion, power and situation (real or ideal) as follows: 4 scores related to cohesion in the real situation, 4 scores related to ideal cohesion, 4 points for power in real situation, and 4 points for power in ideal situation.

In the study of Feldman and Gehring (1988), the construct validity and the reliability of this scale has been confirmed. This tool was translated and used in the Jokar (1999) study [22]. Nevertheless, we also checked the reliability by using the test-retest method. The results depicted that the mean scores of 20 adolescent 16 to 18 years old girls, not involved in the present study, real and ideal power and cohesion with father, with mother, with siblings and between parents did not have a significant difference with the time interval of 2 weeks (p > 0.05). Also, in examining the construct validity of this tool, according to the opinions of the main creators of this tool, when the cohesion and power scores are independent of each other, that means, they measure two different constructs, which confirmed the construct validity of the tool.

Data analysis

SPSS software (version 25) was used for data analysis. In order to check the normal distribution in the continuous variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was done. The number and percentage, mean and standard deviation were the descriptive statistics. To assess the relationship between the variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficient test. Furthermore, to determine the difference between the studied variables based on the demographic variables, independent t-test and ANOVA were used with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study is the result of a research project approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (No. IR.SUMS.REC.1400.015) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants and their parents in the research completed the informed consent forms. The participants were informed that their participation in the research was completely voluntary. They were assured that their information will remain confidential with the researcher and will be used only for research purposes.

Discussion

The present study was conducted with the aim of investigating the relationship between parentification and the family system in female adolescents. The findings indicated that there was no significant relationship between the mean scores of family system and parentification total score in participants, except for the ideal cohesion of the father-adolescent. This could be due to the fact that parentification may not have been perceived negatively by adolescents. Instead, she may be regarded it as a beneficial experience, as explained by family system theory. Jurkovic (1999) described this as the perceived fairness of parentification and Hooper (2011) describes this as the perceived benefits of parentification [23, 24]. Theorists also believe that the destructive or beneficial nature of parentification and the effect of family relationships on it depends on the feelings one has about parentification.

Both clinicians and researchers assert that it is essential to understand how an individual perceives or evaluates their roles and circumstances within the family system, regardless of the actual duties carried out within the family [25]. For instance, if a child recognizes the benefits of caregiving roles, responsibilities, and relationships, and holds that perspective while believing in its cultural relevance or gaining a sense of self-worth, then these perceptions regarding their experiences can contribute to a favorable self-assessment [26].

Additionally, adolescents from collectivistic cultures, like Iran, are motivated to show other-focused emotions for which others are the primary referent, while those from individualistic cultures are encouraged to express self-centered emotions [27]. Therefore, parentified Iranian adolescent girls are inclined to hide their feelings and difficulties. Hence, this hypothesis should be examined in upcoming research.

However, there was an inverse relationship between the adolescent’s ideal cohesion with father and direct association with ideal cohesion with mother and emotional parentification shows that an adolescent who is parentified emotionally feels less cohesion with father and more cohesion with mother in an ideal situation. Also, the inverse relationship between instrumental parentification and real cohesion with the mother and the father’s real power indicates that adolescents who are forced to perform parental duties in the family feel less cohesion with their mother and consider the father’s power in the family to be low.

These findings are in line with family system theory in which the father’s power and cohesion with the mother are expected to be higher in order to minimize the instrumental abuse of the adolescent in the family. In other words, to prevent adolescent from being subjected to maltreatment or abuse, maintaining distinct boundaries between parents and children is important [13]. A review of family system and maltreatment theories also confirms that family relationships should be balanced in terms of cohesion and power, so that children feel secure in this environment and benefit from better physical, mental and social health [8].

The In this regard, literature review showed that an inappropriate and broken family system can harm the roles of parents and adolescents in the family. For example, Danaei et al. (2018) found out a significant and inverse relationship between family support and parentification, which means that if the family support increases, the parentification in the adolescents will decrease [28]. In another study in Sweden, the results showed that parentification can cause dysfunctional communication and the loss of cohesion and power in the families with substance abuse [29]. Another study also confirmed that in families where the family cohesion decreased, children were forced to play the role of parents [3]. These results were not consistent with the present study, which may be due to several reasons. The current research was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, so due to the quarantine, school closure and lockdown, and the need to be more present at home, the relationships between family members were strengthened because families were more in touch with each other than before. Another reason is recruiting only one gender in the present study, while in previous studies there were both genders. Different physical and psychological conditions in girls and boys and their different abilities and weaknesses can cause them to show different behavior and performance in different situations. Therefore, recruiting both boys and girls for future researches should be warranted. It is also suggested to carry out research in this field in the post-COVID-19 pandemic.

The present study also showed a relationship between age and scores of real cohesion between mother and adolescent, but there was no association between other real and ideal cohesion and power dimensions. Contrary to the our results, Jokar et al.‘s research showed that there was a significant and inverse relationship between participants’ age and the mean family system score, so that younger adolescents had a higher score [22]. The reason for this difference in the results could be due to the fact that in the present study only the girls was recruited, while in the aforementioned study, both male and female genders were recruited. Studies indicated a higher probability of experiencing child maltreatment victimization among girls. This may be due to that girls are more responsive and sensitive to social bonds than boys, including the family bonds [30] and girls oriented more toward family relationships and compliance [31].

The direct relationship between the ideal power of the adolescent-father and the ideal power of the adolescent-mother also showed that the adolescents in an ideal and desirable situation believe that both parents should have equal authority. It can be concluded that the improvement of relationships in the family will be formed in line with the presence of balanced authority in the family, especially when one of the parents, the father, has more authority. As authors stated, adolescents who had communication with their fathers, had great opportunity to find their own identity and looked for their own abilities [32]. Having good communications with attentive and intimate fathers can support the social growth of adolescents [33]. These adolescents have the ability to empathize, and to make adjustments [34].

The significant relationship between age and the total score of parentification and its sub-dimensions meant that reverse parenting, perceived fairness, emotional parenting and instrumental parenting in the 18-year-old age group was higher than other age groups, which is consistent with other studies [35]. Usually, parents expect the elder children to take over the responsibility of their younger siblings, which may lead to parentification in these children. It is noteworthy that the age at which adolescents become parentified is a significant factor in assessing future outcomes. Research indicates that a child who is parentified at a younger age may face more harmful consequences compared to a child who is parentified at an older age [9].

The present research is the preliminary study that investigated the relationship between parentification and family system in female adolescents; however, there are some limitations. High risk families were recruited in the present study, which were reported as the most common contributor to parentified experiences. So, results may have differed with a sample reporting other family socioeconomic status. Thus, more representative sampling that includes adolescents with various levels of childhood parentification experiences across several family circumstances in longitudinal studies is warranted. Also, the study only performed in female adolescents. Hence, the results might be different if both genders included. Moreover, we recruited the students of one district, so the findings cannot be generalized to all female students. Furthermore, the parents’ job wasn’t assessed, but it might impact the results. Therefore, it should be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

In total, the findings of the research indicated that the age factor is an important factor for female students’ parentification, which needs to be given special attention to this age group. Since female adolescents are exposed to many social and psychological risks, the implementation of empowerment programs for these families and maximum support for this population are suggested. It should be noted that factors that can make reverse parenting destructive or constructive can be numerous and require further mediated or longitudinal studies. These studies are needed to clarify and validate the distinct effects of childhood parentification in all dimensions, such as instrumental and emotional on health outcomes.
