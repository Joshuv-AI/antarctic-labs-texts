Measurement incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation
Francesco Albarelli, Dominic Branford, Jesús Rubio

Abstract
We present a comprehensive and pedagogical formulation of Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation, providing explicit conditions for achieving minimum quadratic losses. Within this framework, we analyse the role of measurement incompatibility and establish its quantitative effect on attainable precision. We achieve this by deriving upper bounds based on the pretty good measurement—a notion originally developed for hypothesis testing—combined with the evaluation of the Nagaoka–Hayashi lower bound. In general, we prove that, as in the many-copy regime of local estimation theory, incompatibility can at most double the minimum loss relative to the idealised scenario in which individually optimal measurements are assumed jointly implementable. This result implies that, in many practical situations, the latter may provide a sufficient and computationally efficient benchmark without solving the full optimisation problem. Our results, which we illustrate through a range of applications, including discrete quantum phase imaging, phase and dephasing estimation, and qubit sensing, provide analytical and numerical tools for assessing ultimate precision limits and the role of measurement incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter quantum metrology. We also provide an open-source package that implements all bounds discussed here, enabling practical evaluation and comparison across quantum metrological models.

It is instructive to briefly review the impact of such incompatibility effects on the development of local QET, before drawing a parallel with the Bayesian approach.
A multiparameter version of the quantum CRB was first derived by Helstrom in the dawn of the field, based on the notion of the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD)

In the following decades, further quantum generalisations of the CRB were developed, providing a better account of measurement incompatibility, and culminating in the discovery that the ultimate precision limit attainable with collective (entangling) measurements over many copies is given by the Holevo–Cramér–Rao bound (HCRB)

;

;

;

;

Despite significant advances in local QET, the role of measurement incompatibility in Bayesian estimation has received much less attention.
The general conditions that a Bayesian estimation strategy must satisfy to be optimal were already identified in the aforementioned early works
Holevo 1973a
;
Holevo 1973b
;
Helstrom and Kennedy 1974
;
Helstrom 1976
, including a discrete analogue for hypothesis testing
Yuen et al. 1970
;
Yuen et al. 1975
.
A generalisation to convex functions has also been proposed
Coutts et al. 2021
.

Helstrom 1976
provided examples of how these conditions can be used to verify whether a proposed strategy is indeed optimal.
Reversing this procedure—namely, using the conditions to derive the optimal strategy—is, however, exceedingly challenging, with only a limited number of problems where this can be done analytically.
For instance, this procedure can be carried out in the simple case of two hypotheses
Brody and Meister 1996
.

For single-parameter estimation, the fairly broad class of location-isomorphic estimation problems can be optimised exactly

and the solution can be shown to satisfy the corresponding optimality conditions.
This family includes, as a particular case, the optimisation of the standard mean square error—called mean square loss (MSL) in this work—a problem originally solved by Personick

While Personick made an early attempt to extend this reasoning to the estimation of two parameters

, closed-form solutions based on indirect measurements were subsequently obtained

, and a solution to the problem of estimating two displacement parameters encoded in Gaussian states with Gaussian priors is known

, a general and practical procedure for determining optimal solutions in the Bayesian estimation of multiple parameters has remained elusive for decades.

Only recently has a multiparameter bound, analogous to the Helstrom (SLD) CRB—and thus neglecting incompatibility effects—been derived
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
.
As motivated later, we propose referring to this as the symmetric posterior mean (SPM) bound.
Bayesian analogues of several other local bounds, including the NH bound, have since been established, incorporating measurement incompatibility to a certain extent

;
Zhang and Suzuki 2024
.
In addition, numerical algorithms for identifying optimal solutions are now under active investigation
Kaubruegger et al. 2023
;
Bavaresco et al. 2024
.

Continuing the parallel between local and Bayesian estimation, this work addresses a key question: how strongly does measurement incompatibility affect Bayesian multiparameter QET?
In other words, what is the maximal discrepancy between the achievable precision and the bound that neglect incompatibility?

Contributions.
—We show that, in analogy with local QET, the minimum MSL can be at most twice the SPM benchmark that is obtained when measurement incompatibility is ignored.
Key differences, however, are that this upper bound now holds at the single-shot level, but can also be trivial, i.e., larger than the MSL derived solely from
a priori
information.
This effect stems from the central role of prior information in Bayesian estimation, which, as illustrated through examples in quantum imaging, phase and dephasing estimation, and qubit sensing, may significantly modify the apparent incompatibility of the estimation protocol.
Consequently, measurement incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation is not only a relatively mild factor in the overall estimation precision, but it can also be concealed when the influence of the prior is strong.
We further derive a tighter upper bound that is guaranteed to be non-trivial, by combining measurements introduced in hypothesis testing
Belavkin 1975a
;
Belavkin 1975b
;
Holevo 1979
;
Hausladen and Wootters 1994
with optimal classical Bayes estimators
Jaynes 2003
.
Our upper bounds are complementary to existing lower bounds tighter than the SPM one, where the Bayesian NH bound
Suzuki 2024
represents the state of the art.
In addition, we derive a class of lower bounds based on generalised posterior mean operators beyond the symmetric version that, while less tight than the NH bound, may be of independent interest, since they are obtained via the theory of monotone metrics, analogously to established results in local QET
Hayashi 2017
.
An open-source code for the numerical evaluation of all relevant bounds is available on GitHub
mul 2025
.
Finally, beyond deriving bounds on the minimum MSL, we determine explicit conditions for the measurement strategies that achieve it.
Combined with the pedagogical and self-contained introduction to Bayesian multiparameter quantum metrology presented in this work, these results establish a solid foundation for further advances in the field.

II
Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation theory

II.1
Preliminaries

Let
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
denote a vector of unknown parameters and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Omega
vector hypotheses for its true value, both understood as
d{\times}1
column vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Here,
\Omega
represents a generic parameter space.
The information about
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
available prior to any measurement is encoded in a probability density
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, satisfying the non-negativity and normalisation conditions:

p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0,\quad\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})=1.

(1)

This
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is referred to as the
prior probability
.

To gather information about
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
via measurements, the vector hypothesis is encoded in the quantum state of a suitable physical system, such as an atomic ensemble or a Mach–Zehnder interferometer, denoted by
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
This is a positive semidefinite operator with unit trace:

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\succeq 0,\quad\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=1.

(2)

We focus on finite-dimensional quantum systems, and whilst we expect most results in Sec.
III
to hold more generally, we leave a more rigorous analysis for future work.

Once a state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is prepared, a measurement with outcome
x\in\chi
is performed, where
\chi
denotes the sampling space.
Each outcome is associated with an element
M(x)
of a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), which is positive semidefinite and resolves the identity:

M(x)\succeq 0,\quad\int_{\chi}\mathrm{d}x\,M(x)=\mathbb{I}.

(3)

For simplicity, in Sec.
IV
, we only consider examples with discrete outcomes, which is natural when focusing on finite-dimensional systems.
In such cases, continuous outcomes can be realised by performing finite measurements depending on a classical parameter
Chiribella et al. 2007
.
Conversely, discrete outcomes can be embedded in a continuous-outcome formulation by expressing
M(x)
as a sum of Dirac delta functions centered on the discrete outcome
n
,

M(x)=\sum_{n}M_{n}\,\delta(x-n),

(4)

associated with a POVM element
M_{n}
satisfying
M_{n}\succeq 0
and
\sum_{n}M_{n}=\mathbb{I}
.
The continuous-outcome formalism is particularly convenient for variational calculus, which is frequently employed in Bayesian optimisation
Personick 1971
;
Jaynes 2003
.

At this point, Bayesian inference offers a straightforward solution to the problem of inferring the value of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
by combining Bayes’ theorem with the Born rule.
Specifically, the prior probability
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is
updated
according to

p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mapsto p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x)\propto p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\Tr[M(x)\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})].

(5)

Here, the probability density
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x)
denotes the
posterior probability
while
\Tr[M(x)\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is identified as the
likelihood function
.
It represents the full state of knowledge about the values of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
available after observing the measurement outcome
x
.
In that sense, it could be already considered a complete solution of the problem.

Calculation of the posterior probabilities underpins all Bayesian measurement protocols, however more ergonomical theoretical constructions are generally required.
For instance, it is customary to identify a single vector value representing our best guess for the unknown
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
.
Intuitively, one possibility is taking the vector of parameters that maximises the posterior probability, which is particularly suitable in situations with a unimodal Gaussian profile.
That is,

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathrm{MAP}}(x)=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x).

(6)

This is the so-called maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimator
von derLinden et al. 2014
, which plays a role similar to that of the maximum likelihood estimator in local QET
Kay 1993
.

Furthermore, to select a state
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and a POVM element
M(x)
and guide
optimal experimental design, together with an estimator
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}(x)
for post-processing the data, one requires a figure of merit that quantifies estimation performance and serves as the optimisation criterion.
To that end, one typically introduces a loss function
1
1

1

This function is also often referred to as a cost, deviation, or error function.
The term
loss
is preferred because it conveys a general notion of estimation inaccuracy, encompassing statistical, systematic, and context-dependent sources.
In contrast,

and

typically emphasise purely statistical discrepancies, whereas

may suggest application-specific considerations more typical of engineering contexts.

l(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})
quantifying the inaccuracy that may arise by choosing an estimate
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}
should the hypothesis
\boldsymbol{\theta}
correspond to the true parameter values

.
An estimator, state, and POVM are then said to be optimal when they minimise the mean loss

\mathcal{L}\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}x\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\Tr[M(x)\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\,l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}(x),\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(7)

where, for simplicity, we omit explicit reference to the domains
\Omega
and
\chi
.
This convention will be followed henceforth.

This work evaluates measurement incompatibility within the Bayesian paradigm by establishing lower and upper bounds on a particular type of mean loss
\mathcal{L}
.
The remainder of this section addresses formal aspects of
\mathcal{L}
, as well as the notion of metrological optimality based on this figure of merit, that are necessary to derive and contextualise our main results in Sec.

II.2
Estimation performance

We first observe that Eq. (
7
) quantifies estimation performance by integrating over all hypothesis values in
\Omega
, whose contribution can vary from small to large, weighted by the prior probability

.
This is the key feature that makes the framework not only global but also Bayesian.
There exists a more conservative, non-Bayesian approach to global estimation: the minimax approach, which is based on the
frequentist

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}x\,\Tr[M(x)\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\,l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}(x),\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(8)

and seeks to minimise the worst-case value of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

over relevant regions of the parameter space

.
However, since the minimax criterion can be written as the supremum of Eq. (
7
) over all priors, our bounds in Sec.
III
are also relevant for minimax estimation.
Note that, at the other extreme, Eq. (
8

\boldsymbol{\theta}

;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015

We further comment that, in Bayesian estimation, the additional integral over
x
is required only when searching for optimal estimation protocols, as in our case, since this task precedes the recording of specific outcomes.
For data post-processing, estimation performance must instead be assessed using an outcome-dependent quantifier

, e.g.,

\mathcal{L}(x)\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x)\,l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}(x),\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(9)

Alternatively, one may employ credibility intervals, also based on the posterior
Jaynes and Kempthorne 1976
;
Boeyens et al. 2021
.
Both experimental error assessment and the minimax and local estimation approaches discussed above are beyond the scope of this work, and we therefore focus exclusively on Eq. (
7

So far we have not specified any loss function
l
.
The most prevalent choice is

l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{T}L\,(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(10)

where
L
is a positive semidefinite weight matrix, i.e.,
L\succeq 0
, and we assume it to be parameter-independent.
The resulting
\mathcal{L}
is the standard MSL, and this loss quantifier leads to the posterior mean (PM) as the optimal Bayes estimator

\tilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{opt},i}(x)=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x)\,\theta_{i}=\frac{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}}{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})}.

(11)

There is a widespread belief that the MSL applies universally, regardless of the specific system, measurement, or type of parameters of interest.
Strictly speaking, however, the MSL is only appropriate for location or shift parameters.
This is because the topology for such parameters corresponds to the real line, so that the corresponding loss function must be translation invariant

, i.e.,

l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\theta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma})=l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(12)

as Eq. (
10
) is.
2
2

2

While one can generally consider parameter-dependent weight matrices, these break the translation invariance of Eq. (
10
).

Here,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is an arbitrary constant vector.

In contrast, parameter types with different properties require alternative loss functions.
Cycle parameters, for instance, are estimated using trigonometric loss functions—phases in interferometry are the archetypical example
Helstrom 1976
;
Holevo 2011
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
.
Moreover, recent results show that scale parameters such as temperature or decay rate require logarithmic loss functions for consistent estimation

, while weight parameters such as probability of success or coherence parameters demand hyperbolic loss functions

Naive use of the MSL, while it may occasionally yield correct results—particularly when

is known to lie close to a reference point

—is physically unjustified and therefore discouraged unless there is no better choice.

Despite this, one can retain the mathematical simplicity of the MSL while respecting parameters’ more general topologies in certain scenarios.
Specifically, Refs.

;
Tsang 2022
demonstrated that many parameter types admit mean losses equivalent to the MSL, up to a mapping that, in the context of multiparameter estimation, can be expressed as

l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})=(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))^{T}L\,(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})-\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta})),

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{f}
is chosen according to symmetries of the problem

; see Ref.
Overton et al. 2024
for an experimental demonstration with cold

atoms.
Note that phases fall outside this framework

\pi/2

;
Rubio and Dunningham 2019
.

Our results in Sec.
III
are formulated in terms of the standard MSL for pedagogical reasons, but the extension to Eq. (

While a general multiparameter symmetry-informed treatment is deferred, Sec.

provides an illustration of how to use Eq. (

II.3
Optimal quantum strategies

We proceed by providing the conditions that minimise the MSL and tools to establish an operational notion of measurement incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation.
For a fixed measurement, the optimal classical estimator is the posterior mean (PM), already introduced in Eq. (
11
).
In the quantum setting, however, one must also minimise the MSL over all generalised measurements, thereby making the optimisation substantially more challenging.

17
).
This leads to the minimum mean loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}=\mathcal{L}[M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})],

(20)

i.e.,
\mathcal{L}
in Eq. (
14
) evaluated at the optimal POVM.

Eq. (
19
) constitutes our first result.
In general, determining the explicit form of
M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})
from Eq. (
19
) is a nontrivial task.
Nevertheless, these conditions provide a practical criterion for verifying whether a candidate POVM is truly optimal.
Note that employing a symmetry-informed yet still quadratic loss function [Eq. (
13
)] yields optimality conditions of the same structure, with the parameter space remapped according to the vector symmetry function
\boldsymbol{f}
.

II.4
Lower bounds from optimal single-parameter strategies

While exact computation of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
is generally difficult, it admits a lower bound
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
;
Sidhu and Kok 2020
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}\geq\lambda-L^{ij}\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{j}S_{i}]\eqqcolon\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}},

(21)

where
\{S_{i}\}
are the SPM operators, defined as the solutions to the Lyapunov equations

\bar{\rho}_{i}=\frac{S_{i}\rho_{0}+\rho_{0}S_{i}}{2}=E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{S}}\left[S_{i}\right].

(22)

Where, for later convenience, we introduce the anticommutator map (superoperator)

E_{\rho,\mathrm{S}}[X]\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\left(X\rho+\rho X\right),

(23)

which allows to compactly express the SPMs as
S_{i}=E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{S}}^{-1}[\bar{\rho}_{i}]
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}=\lambda-L^{ij}\Tr[\bar{\rho}_{j}E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{S}}^{-1}[\bar{\rho}_{i}]].

(24)

Here and in the rest of this work, we assume that this inverse exists, which is guaranteed for a full-rank
\rho_{0}
.
When this is not the case, one simply needs to work in the relevant subspace spanned by the parametric family
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which corresponds to the support of
\rho_{0}
.
3
3

3

This is possible because the support the operators
\{\bar{\rho}_{j}\}
is contained in the support of
\rho_{0}
, since
\braket{\psi|\rho_{0}|\psi}=0
implies
\braket{\psi|\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\psi}=0
almost everywhere (in the measure-theoretic sense), which in turn implies
\braket{\psi|\bar{\rho}_{j}|\psi}=0
.
This is markedly different from local QET, where the partial derivatives operators may have components also in the kernel of the state, and properties
of rank-deficient models are non-trivial.
For example, necessary and sufficient conditions for attainability of the SLD CRB are not fully characterized
Yang et al. 2019b
;
Horodecki et al. 2022
;
Conlon et al. 2024
;
Nurdin 2024
(except for pure states
Matsumoto 2002
;
Pezzè et al. 2017
)

We note that several names have been adopted in the recent literature for the operators
\{S_{i}\}
, mostly in analogy with the SLD operators that appear in local QET.
We propose instead the term SPM operators, by analogy with the optimal classical Bayes estimator for each parameter in Eq. (
11
).

Formally, the PM estimator is the component-wise ratio of the first-moment probability vector
\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}
to the average probability vector
\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
Intuitively, if
p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is formally replaced by a density operator
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, there are infinitely many inequivalent ways to “divide”
\bar{\rho}_{i}
by
\rho_{0}
. The SPMs correspond to the symmetric choice, where “multiplication by
\rho_{0}
” is implemented by the map
E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{S}}
.
Infinitely many other choices are possible and become inequivalent whenever
\bar{\rho}_{i}
and
\rho_{0}
do not commute, leading to different lower bounds; see App.
C
.
This intuitive reasoning was originally identified by Personick
Personick 1971
and is semantically consistent with local QET, in which the logarithmic derivative of a probability distribution admits inequivalent operator generalisations in the quantum setting
Hayashi 2017
.

Crucially, the SPM bound has the additional property of being attainable in single-parameter estimation by projecting onto the eigenbasis of the single SPM operator available in that case
Personick 1971
;
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
;
Rubio 2022
.
Consequently, upon performing the eigendecomposition

S_{i}=\sum_{n}\ket{s_{i,n}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{s_{i,n}}}s_{i,n}

(25)

for the
i
-th operator, where we assume a discrete eigenbasis for simplicity but without loss of generality,
the spectrum
\{s_{i,n}\}
defines the estimator (see Refs.
Personick 1971
;
Rubio 2022
for details, including a continuous-variable formulation)

s_{i,n}=\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,p(\theta|s_{i,n})\,\theta=\tilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{opt},i}(n),

(26)

which naturally corresponds to the posterior mean (PM) estimator for the
i
-th parameter.

While the PM estimators remain optimal in the multiparameter case
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
, the SPM bound is generally not attainable due to the non-commutativity of the SPM operators.
Nevertheless, it provides a valid lower bound on the minimum MSL and can be interpreted as the optimum obtained when incompatibility among the individually optimal measurements is neglected.
Accordingly, the next section introduces a notion of measurement incompatibility based on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
, establishing it as a result of Bayesian estimation theory analogous in fundamental significance to the Helstrom (SLD) CRB in local QET
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
.

III
Measurement incompatibility: definition and bounds

III.1
Incompatibility figure of merit

As we have just argued, the benchmark to evaluate the impact of measurement incompatibility is the lower bound on the MSL,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
, which is achievable only when all SPM operators share a common eigenbasis and thence are compatible observables.
Therefore, measurement incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter estimation can be quantified by comparing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
with the minimum MSL achievable by measurements,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
.

We introduce a figure of merit for measurement incompatibility of a given Bayesian multiparameter estimation problem as follows:

\mathcal{I}\coloneq\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}}-1\geq 0\,,

(27)

where the inequality follows from the SPM lower bound in Eq. (
21
).
A similar figure of merit has been studied also in the framework of local QET
Belliardo and Giovannetti 2021
.
In the following sections, we shall derive upper and lower bounds on
\mathcal{I}
by obtaining corresponding bounds on the unknown
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
.

III.2
Upper bounds

The incompatibility quantifier
\mathcal{I}
can be upper bounded by considering specific POVMs and estimators, since the corresponding MSL is, by definition, never smaller than the achievable minimum.

III.2.1
Pretty good measurement

Taking inspiration from hypothesis testing, we introduce the square root measurement, also known as the pretty good measurement (PGM)
Belavkin 1975a
;
Belavkin 1975b
;
Holevo 1979
;
Hausladen and Wootters 1994
:

M_{\mathrm{PGM}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\coloneq p(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(28)

This measurement is usually introduced for discriminating among discrete hypotheses, where the Barnum–Knill theorem guarantees the “pretty good” performance
Barnum and Knill 2002
.
Recently, this theorem has been extended to continuous hypotheses
Mishra et al. 2025
and applied to prove that the MSL of the PGM is within a factor two of the optimal
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
.
While this remarkable result establishes the usefulness of the PGM for parameter estimation, it does not shed light on the issue of measurement incompatibility, i.e., understanding the discrepancy between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
.
This will be the precise topic of the next subsection.
In the remainder of this subsection, we set the stage by deriving the expression for the MSL achieved by the PGM.

Before proceeding, it is important to emphasise that the PGM is a POVM with outcomes in the parameter space and therefore already incorporates a classical estimator, which may not be optimal.
Nonetheless, it yields simple expressions for the MSL that are easy to interpret and compare with the baseline provided by the SPM bound.
In Sec.
III.2.3
we will show how the PM estimator—the optimal choice—can achieve a better MSL, at the expense of having a more involved expression.

The MSL of the PGM can be computed from Eq. (
14
), by first evaluating the operators in Eqs. (
17
) as

\displaystyle M_{\text{PGM},i}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\rho}_{i}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(29a)

\displaystyle M_{\text{PGM},{ij}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\int\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\tilde{}\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\rho(\tilde{}\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\tilde{\theta}_{i}\tilde{\theta}_{j}\right]\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(29b)

Noticing that, from cyclicity of the trace,

L^{ij}\Tr[\rho_{0}\,M_{\text{PGM},{ji}}]=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}L\,\boldsymbol{\theta}=\lambda,

(30)

since
\rho_{0}
is cancelled by the two factors
\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and
\Tr[\rho(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})]=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}

\displaystyle=2\left(\lambda-L^{ij}\Tr\left[\bar{\rho}_{j}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\rho}_{i}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=2\left(\lambda-L^{ij}\Tr\left[\bar{\rho}_{j}E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{sq}}^{-1}[\bar{\rho}_{i}]\right]\right)\,.

In the second line, we have expressed the MSL in terms of the inverse of the map

E_{\rho,\mathrm{sq}}[X]\coloneq\sqrt{\rho}X\sqrt{\rho}.

(32)

This map, like
E_{\rho,S}
in Eq. (
23
), is one of the infinitely many inequivalent ways of introducing a superoperator representing “multiplication by
\rho
”.
This plurality is due to noncommutativity in quantum theory, and indeed for diagonal
\rho
and
X
, i.e., embedding classical probability into quantum objects, they all correspond to an element-wise multiplication of probability vectors.
Such maps are fundamental mathematical objects in the theory of monotone metrics
Petz 1996
;
Hayashi 2017
;
Scandi et al. 2025
.

III.2.2
Fundamental incompatibility upper bound

Crucially for us, a fundamental result in the theory of monotone metrics is that the inner product obtained from the inverse of
E_{\rho}^{S}
is the minimal one, implying the inequality

\Tr(XE_{\rho,\mathrm{S}}^{-1}\left[X\right])\leq\Tr(XE_{\rho,\mathrm{sq}}^{-1}\left[X\right])\,.

(33)

Notice that the two sides of the inequality are norms induced by different inner products between operators.
Since the weight matrix is symmetric and positive semidefinite, it admits a (non-unique) decomposition as a sum of rank-one outer products:
L=\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{k}^{T}
for some real vectors
\boldsymbol{v}_{k}
.
This, together with linearity of
E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{sq}}^{-1}
, allows to rewrite the second term of Eq. (
31
) as the sum of norms of the Hermitian operators
v_{k}^{i}\bar{\rho}_{i}
:

\begin{split}L^{ij}\Tr\left[\bar{\rho}_{j}E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{sq}}^{-1}[\bar{\rho}_{i}]\right]=\sum_{k}&\Tr\left[(v_{k}^{i}\bar{\rho}_{i})E_{\rho_{0},\mathrm{sq}}^{-1}[(v_{k}^{j}\bar{\rho}_{j})]\right].\end{split}

(34)

Plugging this identity into the second term of Eq. (
31
) and applying the inequality (
33
) to each summand, we prove the first inequality in the following chain

2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}},

(35)

where the second inequality holds by definition and the third is the SPM lower bound in Eq. (
21
), which neglects potential incompatibility effects.
Thus, we have succeeded in bounding the maximal increase in the MSL due to measurement incompatibility, which can equivalently be written as an upper bound on the incompatibility figure of merit:

0\leq\mathcal{I}\leq 1.

(36)

We remark that the upper bound in Eq. (
35
) is our second and one of our key results.
Interestingly, this mirrors a well-known result in local QET: the HCRB is at most twice the Helstrom (SLD) CRB
Tsang et al. 2020
;
Carollo et al. 2019
.
Notice, however, that the HCRB is in general attainable only asymptotically with collective measurements on multiple copies.
Hence, only the fundamental asymptotic impact of measurement incompatibility is limited by the upper bound on the HCRB
Belliardo and Giovannetti 2021
;
Das et al. 2025
.
On the other hand, our result is obtained in a Bayesian estimation setting, and holds for a single shot.

III.2.3
Pretty good measurement with optimal estimator

While generally valid, the bounds derived above can be trivial, i.e., less informative than the
a priori
MSL (cf. App.
A
):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}
, where

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}=\lambda-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\,L\,\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad\mu_{i}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}.

(37)

Such a behaviour may appear counter-intuitive, since measuring a system is supposed to increase the information about the parameters’ values.
The intuition is re-established if one realises that this happens only because of a suboptimal choice of the classical estimator in the construction of the PGM bound.
Similar situations may appear also in quantum state discrimination, i.e., for discrete hypotheses.
Indeed, it has even been shown that, for specific ensembles, guessing the hypothesis that maximises the prior probability is optimal and the probability of success cannot be improved upon by performing measurements on the system
Hunter 2003
;
Barnett and Croke 2009
.

Instead of viewing the PGM as a POVM already equipped with a built-in estimator—one not specifically tailored to our quadratic loss function—we now drop the interpretation of its outcomes as estimates.
For clarity, the POVM elements in Eq. (
28
) can be rewritten with a distinct label for the measurement outcomes as

M_{\mathrm{PGM}}(\boldsymbol{x})=p(\boldsymbol{x})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(38)

where each
\boldsymbol{x}
is still a vector in the hypothesis space
\Omega
, though it is not directly used as an estimator.
Instead, the measurement outcomes are processed through the optimal PM estimator in Eq. (
26
), which is constructed from the likelihood function
p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,M_{\mathrm{PGM}}(\boldsymbol{x})]
.
From a straightforward calculation, we obtain the MSL

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}\coloneq\lambda-L^{ij}\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,&p(\boldsymbol{x})\,\Tr\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\rho}_{j}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\\
&\cdot\Tr\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\rho}_{i}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right].\end{split}

(39)

Contrary to the standard PGM, this quantity is guaranteed to be smaller than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
, which can be seen explicitly by applying Jensen’s inequality to the second term of Eq. (
39
), since the function of
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is quadratic and convex.
Whilst for an arbitrary POVM with the same estimator—which is guaranteed to be smaller than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
—the MSL may be larger than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}
.
Note that, as discussed, for our choice of quadratic loss we have a formal guarantee that the PM is the optimal classical estimator, but there is numerical evidence that it also leads to a better fidelity than the standard PGM approach in state estimation
Quadeer 2024
.

Tightening the previous inequalities, by taking into account that they may be trivial, and adding this new tighter upper bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\min\!\left[2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min\!\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

(40)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\,.

The corresponding bounds on
\mathcal{I}
are

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}

(41)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\min\!\left[\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min\!\left[1,\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right],

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM^{*}}}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}
are defined as in Eq. (
27
).
The first three inequalities in Eq. (
40
) (the last three inequalities in Eq. (
41
)) constitute our third result.

III.3
Lower bounds

Having established an upper bound on the MSL to characterise the worst-case impact of measurement incompatibility, as well as a tighter version, our aim is now to tighten the lower bound in Eq. (
36
) for a given model. To this end, we seek lower bounds on the MSL that are tighter than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
in the presence of non-commuting SPM operators. In this section, we focus primarily on the tightest known lower bound on the MSL available in the literature, the Nagaoka–Hayashi (NH) bound,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}
, recently introduced in Bayesian QET by Suzuki
Suzuki 2024
.

III.3.1
Nagaoka–Hayashi bound

To derive this bound, one starts by noticing that any choice of POVM and estimators satisfies the following inequality
(
Suzuki 2024
, Lemma 1)
,

\sum_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes M_{ij}\succeq\sum_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes M_{i}M_{j}\,,

(42)

in terms of the first and second moment operators introduced in Eq. (
17
).
Note that an additional vector space of dimension
d
(number of parameters) has been introduced, but this is not a physical Hilbert space.
While we use the bra–ket notation,
\ket{k}
simply stands for the standard column basis vector
e_{k}
of
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
(we have also temporarily reintroduced an explicit summation for extra clarity).
The two sides of Eq. (
42
) are block matrices: the left-hand side can be interpreted as an operator-valued second moment matrix, while the right-hand side as an outer product of operator-valued first moment vectors.

Since the MSL in Eq. (
14
) is expressed only in terms of these operators, a lower bound is obtained by treating the Hermitian operators
M_{ij}=M_{ji}
and
M_{i}
as
independent
variables satisfying Eq. (
42
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}\coloneq\lambda+

\displaystyle\min_{\{M_{ij},M_{i}\}}L^{ij}\,\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{0}M_{ji}-2\bar{\rho}_{j}M_{i}]

(43)

\displaystyle\,\,\mathrm{subject\penalty\ to\penalty\ }\mathrm{Eq.\penalty\ }\eqref{eq:lemma1-Suzuki}\,.

This means that, for a given set of
M_{ij}
and
M_{i}
attaining the minimum, there is no guarantee that an underlying POVM
M(x)
exists from which they can be derived, according to Eqs. (
17
).
Consequently, the bound may be loose.
However, despite not
fully
capturing measurement incompatibility, the NH bound can be shown to be tighter than the SPM one.
This follows by noticing that Eq. (
42
) implies

L^{ij}\Tr[\rho_{0}M_{ji}]\geq L^{ij}\Tr[\rho_{0}M_{j}M_{i}],

(44)

which is a weaker constraint.
Applying this inequality to Eq. (
43
) one obtains a relaxation of the original problem that can be solved analytically to obtain
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
.

The bound
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}
formally mirrors the NH Cramér-Rao bound (NHCRB) in local QET
Conlon et al. 2021
, and analogously it can be evaluated by solving a semidefinite program (SDP)
Suzuki 2024
.
Notice, however, that the upper bound in Eq. (
35
) that holds in the Bayesian case has no direct counterpart in the local case, since the NHCRB can be much larger than twice the Helstrom (SLD) CRB, and this gap can grow with the dimension of the Hilbert space and number of parameters
Das et al. 2025
.

For two parameters, the optimisation over the second moment operators
M_{11}
,
M_{12}
and
M_{22}
can be solved exactly.
For the sake of clarity, but without loss of generality
4
4

4

Similarly to local QET, the scalar NH bound can be recast to an equivalent problem with
L=\mathbb{I}
and suitably redefined first moment operators
\bar{\rho}_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j}(\sqrt{L})^{ij}\bar{\rho}_{j}
.
The explicit expression for a general
weight matrix is also reported in Ref.
(
Suzuki 2024
, Eq. (20))
.
, we present this expression for an identity weight matrix:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}\overset{L=\mathbb{I}_{2}}{=}\lambda+\min_{M_{1},M_{2}}\{\Tr\left[\rho_{0}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})\right]\\
+\left\|\sqrt{\rho_{0}}[M_{1},M_{2}]\sqrt{\rho_{0}}\right\|_{1}-2\Tr\left[\bar{\rho}_{1}M_{1}+\bar{\rho}_{2}M_{2}\right]\}\,.\end{split}

(45)

where
\|A\|_{1}=\Tr[\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}]
denotes the trace norm, i.e., the sum of the singular values.
This expression can be derived from Eq. (
43
), following, e.g., Ref.
(
Conlon et al. 2021
, Supplementary Note 2)
.
This form is the one originally obtained by Nagaoka for local two-parameter estimation
Nagaoka 1989
.

Overall, we have the following inequalities:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\,.

(46)

In turn, this restricts the allowed range of the incompatibility figure of merit for a given model:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{NH}}\leq\mathcal{I}\leq 1\,,

(47)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{NH}}
is defined analogously to Eq. (
27
).
Moreover, by combining this NH bound on incompatibility with the tightest upper bound in Eq. (
40
), we obtain a tight range for the possible values of incompatibility in Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation:

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{NH}}\leq\mathcal{I}\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}.

(48)

This constitutes our fourth result.

III.3.2
Other lower bounds

We conclude this section by highlighting other lower bounds on the MSL which, although less tight—and mostly derived using techniques from local QET—can be easier to compute and may therefore remain useful in practice.

We first mention the Holevo bound, here denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}
, which is a Bayesian version of the HCRB
Suzuki 2024
;
Zhang and Suzuki 2024
.
It can be obtained by loosening the constraints in Eq. (
42
) taking the expectation value with respect to
\rho_{0}
, thereby obtaining a minimisation problem with an inequality constraint involving
d{\times}d
matrices.
The HCRB sits strictly between the SPM and NH bounds:
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
.
While in local QET the HCRB has a fundamental meaning, being asymptotically attainable, the Bayesian version does not seem to share the same significance.
5
5

5

Note that such a Bayesian Holevo-type bound is different from a so-called Bayesian CRBs obtained by averaging the HCRB over the prior
Gill 2008
, conjectured to be attainable in the large sample limit of Bayesian estimation
(
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
, Sec. 5.5)
, see the discussion in Sec.
V
.

The Bayesian Holevo bound may be computationally more efficient because, while it can be computed by solving an SDP as the NH bound, it involves positive semidefinite constraints in terms of matrices of smaller dimension
Suzuki 2024
.

Pursuing further the goal of identifying easily computable bounds, one may instead seek lower bounds that capture measurement incompatibility to some extent and can always be expressed in closed form, rather than indirectly as the outcome of an optimisation.
A whole class of such bounds exists, based on the idea of non-commutative PM operators, of which SPMs are just one example.
This is in full analogy with logarithmic derivative operators in local QET
(
Hayashi 2017
, Ch. 6)
.
In App.
C
, we derive this family of lower bounds in a unified manner, employing the theory of monotone metrics, and generalising the approach of Ref.
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
.
Here, we only mention the right posterior mean (RPM) bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPM}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\lambda-L^{ij}\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R},ji}+\|\sqrt{L}\Im[\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R}}]\sqrt{L}\|_{1},

(49a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R},ij}

\displaystyle\coloneq\Tr[\bar{\rho}_{i}\rho_{0}^{-1}\bar{\rho}_{j}],

(49b)

written in terms of the complex matrix
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R}}
.
Such a matrix is analogous to the right logarithmic derivative quantum Fisher information matrix in local QET.
This bound is less informative than the NH or Holevo ones, since
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPM}}

(
Zhang and Suzuki 2024
, Thm. 8)
.
However, it can be tighter or looser than
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
depending on the specific model.
For example, it is always less tight than the SPM bound for
d=1
, where the latter is attainable.
Conversely, Holevo has shown the RPM bound to be attainable for a multiparameter problem with non-commuting SPM operators: estimation of the average position and momentum of a Gaussian state with a Gaussian prior
Holevo 1976
.

As a means of summary, we combine the upper bounds in Eq. (
40
) with all the lower bounds discussed in this section to obtain the following hierarchy of bounds on the minimum MSL:

\displaystyle\min\!\left[2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min\!\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle\geq\max\!\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPM}}\right].

Correspondingly, the hierarchy of bounds on measurement incompatibility is

\displaystyle\max\!\left[0,\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{RPM}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{H}}

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{NH}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\min\!\left[\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}},\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min\!\left[1,\,\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}\right],

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{RPM}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{H}}
and are defined as in Eq. (
27
).

IV
Applications

In the following sections we evaluate the measurement incompatibility range in Eq. (
48
), as well as some of the less tight bounds derived, for a range of examples.

IV.1
Discrete quantum phase imaging

Consider a
(d{+}1)
-mode interferometer, where
d
hypotheses
\boldsymbol{\theta}
for a set of unknown phases
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
are encoded via the unitary operator
\mathrm{e}^{-iN^{j}\theta_{j}}
.
Here,
N^{j}
is the number operator of the
j
th mode, and the zeroth mode serves as a reference beam
Jarzyna and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2012
.
This configuration models discrete quantum imaging and has been extensively explored in both local and Bayesian frameworks
Macchiavello 2003
;
Humphreys et al. 2013
;
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
;
Albarelli et al. 2020
;
Gebhart et al. 2021
.
In this context, a common probe state is the generalised N00N state

\ket{\psi_{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d+\alpha^{2}}}\left(\alpha\ket{n_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ket{n_{i}}\right),

(52)

where
\ket{n_{i}}
represents a state with
n
photons in the
i
-th mode and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is free.
Consequently, the parameter-dependent state is constructed as

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathrm{e}^{-iN^{j}\theta_{j}}\ket{\psi_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{\psi_{0}}}\mathrm{e}^{iN^{j}\theta_{j}}.

(53)

To address this problem using the MSL, we restrict the hypothesis space
\Omega
so that the phases are sufficiently localised to behave as location parameters, but remaining within a global regime.
Specifically, we assume that,
a priori
,
\theta_{i}\in[-\pi/n,\pi/n]
, with
n\geq 4
, so that the largest prior width is
\pi/2
.
For this configuration—and choosing a normalised
L
with equal weights, i.e.,
L=\mathbb{I}/d
—the average state, SPM operators, and SPM bound are
Rubio and Dunningham 2019

\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{d+\alpha^{2}}\left(\alpha^{2}\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ket{n_{i}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{i}}}\right),

(54)

\bar{\rho}_{j}=\frac{-i\alpha}{n(d+\alpha^{2})}\left(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}-\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}\right),

(55)

S_{j}=\frac{-2i\alpha}{n(1+\alpha^{2})}\left(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}-\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}\right),

(56)

and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{(1+\alpha^{2})(d+\alpha^{2})}\right]

(57)

respectively.

Two observations are in order.
First, the Heisenberg scaling in Eq. (
57
) is achievable in a global setting only because the chosen prior width scales as
\mathord{\sim}1/n
.
Indeed, since all first moments of the prior vanish for this problem, the
a priori
MSL is given by (cf. Eq. (
37
))

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}=\lambda=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3n^{2}},

(58)

which already exhibits Heisenberg scaling; this is consistent with the observations in Refs.
Hall and Wiseman 2012
;
Giovannetti and Maccone 2012
;
Tsang 2012
;
Pezzè 2013
;
Rubio et al. 2018
.

Secondly, and more importantly for our purposes, the SPM operators do not commute:

[S_{j},S_{k}]=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}}(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{k}}}-\ket{n_{k}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}).

(59)

As such, the SPM bound is not tight, and we can therefore expect incompatibility to arise in this system.

To quantify the amount of incompatibility, we first need to calculate the bounds on the minimum MSL.
We start with the PGM bound, given in Eq. (
31
).
Since

\bar{\rho}_{j}\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{-i}{n\sqrt{d+\alpha^{2}}}\left(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}-\alpha\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}\right),

(60)

and so

\Tr[\bar{\rho}_{j}\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\bar{\rho}_{i}\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}]=\frac{2\alpha}{n^{2}(d+\alpha^{2})}\delta_{ji},

(61)

Eq. (
31
) yields

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{2\alpha}{d+\alpha^{2}}\right).

(62)

However, this upper bound is trivial, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}>\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
, and consequently so is
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
.
This follows from showing that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
is strictly positive by virtue of the inequality

\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}>\max_{\alpha}\frac{4\alpha}{d+\alpha^{2}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{d}},

(63)

valid for all
d
.

In view of this, we proceed to calculate the PGM bound enhanced by the PM estimator, given in Eq. (
39
), which is guaranteed to be smaller than or equal to the
a priori
MSL.
First, calculate

\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\bar{\rho}_{j}\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{i}{n}(\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}-\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}),

(64)

so that

\Tr[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\bar{\rho}_{j}\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}]=\frac{2\alpha\sin(n\theta_{j})}{n(d+\alpha^{2})}.

(65)

Since the following integrals hold:

\int\limits_{-\pi/n}^{\pi/n}\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}\,\sin(n\theta_{j})=0,\!\int\limits_{-\pi/n}^{\pi/n}\mathrm{d}\theta_{j}\,\sin^{2}(n\theta_{j})=\frac{\pi}{n},

(66)

Eq. (
39
) leads to

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\left(d+\alpha^{2}\right)^{2}}\right].

(67)

This is our tightest upper bound on the minimum MSL.

We next turn to the lower bounds.
We begin with the simplest tractable bound beyond the SPM bound, namely the RPM bound in Eq. (
49a
). Using

\bar{\rho}_{j}\,\rho_{0}^{-1}\,\bar{\rho}_{k}=\frac{1}{n^{2}(d+\alpha^{2})}\left(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{k}}}+\alpha^{2}\delta_{jk}\ket{n_{0}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{0}}}\right),

(68)

we obtain

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R},jk}=\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}(d+\alpha^{2})}\,\delta_{jk},

(69)

and therefore

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPM}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}-\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{d+\alpha^{2}}\right).

(70)

However, it is straightforward to verify that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RPM}}
, consistent with the fact that
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{R}}
in Eq. (
69
) is real-valued. Consequently, we shall instead evaluate the NH bound in Eq. (
43
) using the numerical algorithm of Ref.
mul 2025
to obtain a tighter lower bound on the minimum MSL.

.
This relatively low magnitude can initially be attributed to the proximity between
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
and

, evidenced by the dotted line.
That is, within the Bayesian framework, the prior information imposes a finite and natural upper bound on the MSL, thereby fundamentally limiting the maximum attainable relative precision gain.

[N^{j},N^{k}]=0
for all

—or from satisfying the weak commutation relation
Ragy et al. 2016

\Tr\left[\rho_{0}[S_{j},S_{k}]\right]=0,

(71)

which holds in this case since

\rho_{0}[S_{j},S_{k}]=\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{n^{2}(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}(d+\alpha^{2})}\bigl(\ket{n_{j}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{k}}}-\ket{n_{k}}\mkern-4.0mu{\bra{n_{j}}}\bigr).

(72)

Determining whether a weak commutation condition, of this or any other form, plays a role in Bayesian multiparameter estimation is an open question.

IV.2
Phase and dephasing estimation

While phase estimation serves as the archetypal problem in quantum estimation, the simultaneous estimation of phase and phase diffusion plays an analogous role in multiparameter quantum estimation.
The simplest single-qubit incarnation of this model exhibits measurement incompatibility in local QET
Vidrighin et al. 2014
, which however asymptotically disappears as many identical copies are measured collectively
Roccia et al. 2018
.
This corresponds to preparing identical and factorised probe states, whereas asymptotically large entangled probes generally allow to optimally estimate both parameters
Kobus and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2025
.
Nevertheless, we consider here independent identical copies collectively measured, since this is a paradigmatic setting for investigating measurement incompatibility
Vidrighin et al. 2014
;
Roccia et al. 2018
;
Yung et al. 2025
.

For an initial state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
, the single-qubit phase and phase-diffusion channels
6
6

6

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sigma_{z}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\,\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sigma_{z}},

(73a)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4\theta_{2}^{2}}}}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,\theta_{2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\frac{x}{2}\sigma_{z}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\,\mathrm{e}^{i\frac{x}{2}\sigma_{z}},

(73b)

The MSL is not suitable for this problem.

IV.3
Qubit planar tomography

We conclude by analysing a configuration that, unlike the previous two examples, displays an
a priori
MSL larger than twice the SPM bound—the quantity leading to the upper bound on measurement incompatibility in Eq. (
36
).
Before proceeding, we note that the purpose of this application is to illustrate specific mathematical properties of the theoretical bounds introduced, rather than to address a problem of physical relevance. For simplicity, we shall therefore use the MSL as a loss quantifier, even if this choice may not be fully justified by the symmetry arguments discussed in Secs.
II.2
and
IV.2
.

Consider the following family of single-qubit states:

\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}_{2}+\theta_{1}\sigma_{x}+\theta_{2}\sigma_{y}\right),

(84)

where the unknown parameters satisfy the constraint
\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}\leq 1
to ensure physicality.
This setting corresponds to states lying on the plane
z=0
of the Bloch sphere, hence the term
planar tomography
.
We assume independent, but not identical, prior distributions for the two parameters, i.e.,
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})=p_{1}(\theta_{1})\,p_{2}(\theta_{2})
, where
p_{1}(\theta_{1})
and
p_{2}(\theta_{2})
are supported on
[-W_{1},W_{1}]
and
[-W_{2},W_{2}]
, respectively.
Overall, we have a bivariate distribution over a rectangle contained inside the Bloch circle in the
x-y
plane, ensured by the condition
W_{1}^{2}+W_{2}^{2}\leq 1
.
Finally, we assume that the prior for each parameter has zero mean and a finite second moment:

\int\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\,p_{i}(\theta_{i})\,\theta_{i}=0,\quad v_{i}\coloneq\int\!\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\,p_{i}(\theta_{i})\,\theta_{i}^{2}\leq\infty.

(85)

Under these assumptions, and choosing
L=\mathbb{I}_{2}
, the
a priori
MSL is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}=\lambda=v_{1}+v_{2}\,,

(86)

while the zeroth and first state moments are

\rho_{0}=\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2},\quad\bar{\rho}_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{2}\sigma_{i},

(87)

where for simplicity we label Pauli matrices as
\sigma_{1}\equiv\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{2}\equiv\sigma_{y}
.
Given that
\rho_{0}
is proportional to the identity, the SPM operators are trivially obtained:

S_{i}=2\bar{\rho_{i}}=v_{i}\sigma_{i}.

(88)

Since they do not commute; this estimation problem is impacted by measurement incompatibility.

The SPM lower bound on the MSL can next be calculated as

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}&=\lambda-\sum_{i}\Tr\!\left[\rho_{0}S_{i}^{2}\right]\\
&=\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}\Tr\!\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]\\
&=v_{1}+v_{2}-\left(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(89)

The NH bound will be calculated numerically, as before.

Moving now to the upper bounds, in this scenario we have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}}=2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
, since
\rho_{0}
commutes with the first state moments, and the symmetric and square-root inner products are therefore identical.
Unlike in the two previous applications, here we find that the upper bound
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
is
not
trivial when

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}>2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\iff 2\left(v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}\right)>v_{1}+v_{2}.

(90)

Nevertheless, in order to have a tighter upper bound, we also calculate the PGM with the optimal estimator in Eq. (
39
).
Since here such a bound depends on the following quantity:

\Tr\left[\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\rho}_{i}\rho_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2}=\left(\frac{v_{i}}{2}\,\theta_{i}\,\Tr[\sigma_{i}^{2}]\right)^{2}\\
=v_{i}^{2}\theta_{i}^{2},

(91)

the integrals over
\theta_{i}
with respect to the prior in Eq. (
39
) yield a further multiplication by the second moments
v_{i}
, giving the final expression

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PGM}^{*}}=v_{1}+v_{2}-\left(v_{1}^{3}+v_{2}^{3}\right).

(92)

We now show a particular choice that satisfies the inequality (
90
).
Let each independent prior be based on a symmetric beta distribution rescaled and translated such that it is supported on the interval
[-W_{i},W_{i}]
, and having zero mean as required:

p_{i}(\theta_{i})=\frac{1}{2W_{i}}h_{\beta}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}}{2W_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(93)

where

h_{\beta}(z)=\frac{(1-z)^{\beta-1}z^{\beta-1}}{B(\beta,\beta)},

(94)

\beta>0
controls the shape,

\begin{split}B(z_{1},z_{2})&=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{z_{1}-1}(1-t)^{z_{2}-1}\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(z_{1})\,\Gamma(z_{2})}{\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})}\end{split}

(95)

is the beta function, and
\Gamma(z)
is the gamma function.
For such an
a priori
assignment, the variances are

v_{i}=\frac{W_{i}^{2}}{1+2\beta}.

(96)

Therefore, by appropriately adjusting the parameters
\{W_{i}\}
and
\beta
, one can find regions where the inequality (
90
) is satisfied.

, for various choices of the prior parameters
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
and
\alpha
.
The graphical conventions distinguishing curves follow those established in Sec.

.
The region of incompatibility values permitted by the bounds is shaded in Fig.

.
We conjecture, however, that for this class of models the NH bound is attainable, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NH}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
, in analogy with local QET
Nagaoka 1991
.

From these plots we observe that, when
\beta
is sufficiently small—roughly speaking, the prior distribution becomes more concentrated towards the edges—the upper bound
\mathcal{I}\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{PGM}}=1

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{prior}}
can exceed the value
1
.

W_{2}^{2}+W_{1}^{2}<1
.

V
Discussion

One important aspect that we have not addressed is the relationship between Bayesian QET and the bounds derived in local QET for the frequentist loss in Eq. (
8
).
In this regard, we recall a conjecture of Ref.
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
: under appropriate regularity conditions, the Bayesian MSL and the frequentist loss become asymptotically equivalent; that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}^{(n)}=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(97)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}^{(n)}
is the MSL associated with
n
copies of a given state,
\rho^{\otimes n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, while
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the HCRB of local QET.
7
7

7

Bounds in local QET are most commonly denoted by the letter
C
, which stands for CRB—for example,
C_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
for the HCRB.
However, for consistency with the rest of this work we retain the terminology “loss” and the symbol
\mathcal{L}
, since these quantities bound the frequentist loss
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
defined in Eq. (
8
).

This expression represents the minimum achievable frequentist loss in the asymptotic limit of large
n
.

Since in local QET it holds that
Tsang et al. 2020
;
Carollo et al. 2019

2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(98)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the Helstrom (SLD) CRB, our inequality in Eq. (
35
) is consistent with this asymptotic conjecture.
Notice that, by definition, the Bayesian MSL
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}^{(n)}
is obtained by minimising over POVMs acting collectively on
n
copies, dubbed “correlated measurements” in Ref.
Hayashi and Ouyang 2023
.

Despite increasing efforts in the experimental implementation of few-copy collective measurements
Roccia et al. 2018
;
Hou et al. 2018
;
Yuan et al. 2020
;
Conlon et al. 2023
;
Mi et al. 2025
;
Yung et al. 2025
, scaling them to large
n
remains challenging in practice.
Following this observation, recent work in local QET has addressed the minimum multiparameter frequentist loss achievable with single-copy measurements and classical feedback, referred to as “uncorrelated measurements” in Ref.
Hayashi and Ouyang 2023
.
This quantity corresponds to a minimisation of the weighted trace of the inverse Fisher information matrix (FIM) over all single-copy POVMs,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\min_{M(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\Tr[LF(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1}],

(99)

where
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the FIM obtained by applying a POVM to a single copy
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
This is often called the “most informative”
Nagaoka 1989
or “tight”
Hayashi and Ouyang 2023
bound.

Unlike the HCRB,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

can
be larger than
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{S}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

Das et al. 2025
;
Hayashi and Ouyang 2023
.
This appears to be in contrast with the Bayesian setting, where the inequality
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
holds at the single-copy level as well.
The resolution of this apparent tension comes from noticing that the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is still attainable only asymptotically, but restricting each copy to be measured independently, albeit adaptively with classical feedback from previous outcomes.
Therefore, we expect that imposing an analogous restriction in the Bayesian case should lead to a similar behaviour as the number of shots increases.
More concretely, we conjecture that, under suitable regularity conditions

\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}^{(n),\mathrm{uncorr}}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,,

(100)

where now
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}^{(n),\mathrm{uncorr}}
is defined as the MSL for the prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and family of states
\rho^{\otimes n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, but minimised over the uncorrelated measurement strategies defined above, instead of arbitrary collective (correlated) POVMs over all the copies.

We conclude by noting that hybrid figures of merit, incorporating methods from both local and global QET, have been introduced
Mukhopadhyay et al. 2025
;
Vasilyev et al. 2024
.
However, these approaches are mostly heuristic, and understanding the connections outlined in this discussion could clarify their exact range of applicability.

VI
Conclusions ad outlook

QET was initiated in the 1960s and 1970s, but only in recent years have several lines of research converged, leading to a deeper understanding of multiparameter aspects
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
;
Albarelli et al. 2020
;
Pezzè and Smerzi 2025
.
Building on this progress, in this work we have laid the foundations for a comprehensive theory of Bayesian multiparameter quantum estimation.
We have achieved this by reviewing, streamlining, and contextualising existing results, and by deriving a number of new ones, including: the explicit set of conditions (
19
) that the optimal POVM must satisfy under the MSL criterion; the upper bound
2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
on the minimum MSL in the first inequality of Eq. (
35
), showing that such a minimum can be at most twice the value of the bound when neglecting measurement incompatibility; the tighter upper bound based on the notion of PGM combined with the optimal PM estimator; the introduction of general PM operators and their corresponding matrix lower bounds via the monotonicity of inner products, presented in App.
C
; and finally, the demonstration that measurement incompatibility in a single-shot Bayesian setup is substantially affected by prior information, as illustrated through examples in qubit and optical configurations.

This work also opens several theoretical and experimental avenues for future research, a few of which we now highlight.
For example, apart from measurement incompatibility, in multiparameter quantum metrology—i.e., in the simultaneous estimation of several parameters encoded by a quantum channel—additional intrinsic precision trade-offs may arise from the choice of probe state and control strategy
Ragy et al. 2016
;
Górecki et al. 2020
;
Hayashi and Ouyang 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
, a phenomenon referred to as
probe incompatibility

Albarelli and Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2022
.
While this aspect has not, to the best of our knowledge, been examined in detail and in full generality within a Bayesian framework, numerical approaches for designing optimal multiparameter Bayesian estimation protocols
Kaubruegger et al. 2023
;
Bavaresco et al. 2024
inherently account for probe incompatibility effects.

In addition, while our analysis has focused on finite-dimensional
systems, continuous-variable (CV) platforms—particularly quantum optical ones
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2015
;
Polino et al. 2020
;
Pirandola et al. 2018
;
Barbieri 2022
—play a central role in metrology and sensing applications. It will therefore be important to rigorously extend the theoretical framework developed here to CV systems.
Given the rapid progress in learning theory for CV states
Wu et al. 2024
;
Mele et al. 2024
;
Bittel et al. 2025
;
Fanizza et al. 2025
, this direction appears especially promising.
In particular, the case of Gaussian states and priors
Wang et al. 2007
may allow for a more tractable analysis while remaining highly relevant for practical implementations.
In the single-parameter Bayesian setting, several specific results for Gaussian states are already known
Morelli et al. 2021
, and a general framework for multiparameter local QET with Gaussian states has recently been proposed
Chang et al. 2025
.

Another important problem is understanding the effect of incompatibility—measurement and other forms thereof—for parameters obeying symmetries other than translation invariance over the real line, i.e., beyond location parameters.
We have provided the groundwork to generalise the results of this study to any location-isomorphic estimation problem through the symmetry-informed mapping postulated in Eq. (
76
), concrete illustrations of which have been discussed both in Sec.
IV.2
and in Ref.
Overton et al. 2024
.
A more rigorous formulation should include the possibility of correlated priors and loss functions coupling different parameters, as well as the application of information geometry in a multidimensional parameter space
Boeyens et al. 2021
.
Moreover, a further extension to symmetries beyond location-isomorphic metrology is necessary to encompass phase parameters, which constitute a paradigmatic class requiring suitable trigonometric loss functions and priors
Holevo 2011
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański 2011
.

At a practical level, identifying optimal measurements and controls for even an individual shot in an adaptive, experimental protocol can be computationally difficult.
The upper bound
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}\leq 2\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
suggests that optimising the SPM affords a good compromise between optimality and tractability, compared to the exact but difficult minimum loss, Eq. (
20
).
This is due to its computational simplicity while also capturing much of the structure of the problem at hand, with a smaller SPM bound also meaning a smaller upperbound on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}
and so the worst-case impact of measurement-incompatibility is smaller in absolute terms.
In practice this sees the pseudo-gain
\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{j}S_{i}]
—i.e., the second term in the SPM lower bound (
21
); see App.
A
—become the optimiser of experimental control parameters for individual shots in an adaptive protocol, fulfilling the role of a global alternative to the weighted trace of the inverse quantum FIM.
Although such an approach will
not
generally lead to full optimality, we anticipate a substantial boost in quantum technological applications, in light of recent results from cold-atom experiments showing two-fold
Glatthard et al. 2022
and five-fold
Overton et al. 2024
precision enhancements obtained by employing the “classical” version of this quantifier, that is, the pseudo-gain computed using the optimal estimator and a fixed POVM.

Altogether, we expect the Bayesian multiparameter framework for QET introduced here, together with the insight into the impact of measurement incompatibility, to become a fundamental building block for benchmarking both theory and experiment in quantum metrology, guiding the design of practically relevant, information–optimal quantum sensors.

Appendix A
Prior loss and precision gain in Bayesian quantum estimation

Suppose we make a random guess of the unknown parameters based solely on the prior probability.
Let us denote the corresponding vector of estimates by
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}
, noting the absence of any outcome dependence.
The MSL in this case is given by

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{T}L\,(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(101)

which we may refer to as the
prior loss
.
Alternatively, one may obtain Eq. (
101
) by inserting a trivial POVM consisting of a single element, the identity operator, into the MSL in Eq. (
14
).

It is straightforward to verify that the estimator minimising Eq. (
101
) is the prior mean,

\boldsymbol{\mu}\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta},

(102)

which leads to the minimum prior loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\coloneq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu})

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}L\,\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\,L\,\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\,L\,\boldsymbol{\mu},

where
\lambda
was already defined in Eq. (
15
).
Such an optimal prior loss is nothing but
\tr[L\Sigma_{p}]
, where
\Sigma_{p}
is the covariance matrix of the multivariate prior probability
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and we have introduced a lowercase trace of
d{\times}d
matrices, distinct from the uppercase trace of operators acting on the physical Hilbert space of the system.

One may further show that
\mu_{i}=\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{i}]
, where
\rho_{0}
and
S_{i}
are defined in Sec.
II.3
.
This, together with Eq. (
102
), allows us to rewrite the SPM bound in Eq. (
21
) as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SPM}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}-\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{SPM}},

(104)

where

\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{SPM}}\coloneq L^{ij}(\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{j}S_{i}]-\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{j}]\,\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{i}]).

(105)

Written in this form, the SPM bound can be interpreted as the result of reducing the prior loss by the amount of precision gained through individually optimal measurements,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{SPM}}
.

Recent literature has referred to the quantity
L^{ij}\Tr[\rho_{0}S_{j}S_{i}]
as an alternative definition of precision gain when the SPM bound is used as an optimiser for control parameters
Overton et al. 2024
;
Boeyens et al. 2025
.
From a computational standpoint, this definition is often more convenient, and it has likewise been adopted in our code
mul 2025
.
Accordingly, we refer to this quantity as the
pseudo-gain
, following the discussion above.
Naturally, the gain and pseudo-gain coincide whenever the vector prior mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
vanishes.

Appendix B
Optimality conditions for the MSL

Let
M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})
denote the optimal POVM for a Bayesian quantum multiparameter estimation problem.
Following Holevo
Holevo 1973a
;
Holevo 1973b
;
Holevo 1976
and Helstrom
Helstrom and Kennedy 1974
;
Helstrom 1976
, a necessary and sufficient condition for such a POVM to exist is the existence of a Hermitian operator

\Upsilon=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}\,M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\,\varrho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}\,\varrho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\,M_{\mathrm{opt}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}),

(106)

with

\varrho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,l(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(107)

that satisfies

\varrho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})-\Upsilon\succeq 0

(108)

for all
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}
.
The minimum loss is then given by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{min}}=\Tr(\Upsilon)
.
A discrete version of this optimality condition exists for hypothesis testing
Yuen et al. 1970
;
Yuen et al. 1975
, and a generalisation to convex functions has recently been proposed
Coutts et al. 2021
.

We now derive the explicit form of these conditions for the MSL.
Using the notation introduced in Sec.
II.3
, Eq. (
107
) becomes

\varrho(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}})=\Lambda+L^{ij}\bigl(\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{i}\rho_{0}-2\tilde{\theta}_{j}\bar{\rho}_{i}\bigr),

(109)

where
\Tr(\Lambda)=\lambda
.
Accordingly, the operator in Eq. (
106
) takes the form

\Upsilon=\Lambda+L^{ij}\bigl(M_{\mathrm{opt},ji}\rho_{0}-2M_{\mathrm{opt},j}\bar{\rho}_{i}\bigr)=\Lambda+L^{ij}\bigl(\rho_{0}M_{\mathrm{opt},ji}-2\bar{\rho}_{j}M_{\mathrm{opt},i}\bigr).

(110)

By adding both expressions for
\Upsilon
and dividing by two, we obtain

\Upsilon=\Lambda+\tfrac{1}{2}L^{ij}\bigl(M_{\mathrm{opt},ji}\rho_{0}+\rho_{0}M_{\mathrm{opt},ji}-M_{\mathrm{opt},j}\bar{\rho}_{i}-\bar{\rho}_{j}M_{\mathrm{opt},i}\bigr).

(111)

Finally, inserting Eqs. (
109
) and (
111
) into Eq. (
108
), we obtain the conditions that any POVM optimal under the MSL criterion must satisfy:

L^{ij}[(\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{i}\mathbb{I}-M_{\mathrm{opt},ji})\rho_{0}+\rho_{0}(\tilde{\theta}_{j}\tilde{\theta}_{i}\mathbb{I}-M_{\mathrm{opt},ji})-2(\tilde{\theta}_{j}\mathbb{I}-M_{\mathrm{opt},j})\bar{\rho}_{i}-2\bar{\rho}_{j}(\tilde{\theta}_{i}\mathbb{I}-M_{\mathrm{opt},i})]\succeq 0,

(112)

for all
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\theta}}
, as stated in the main text.

Appendix C
Matrix lower bounds from monotone metrics

C.1
Brief overview of monotone metrics

In this subsection we follow
Hayashi 2017
, but see also Ref.
Scandi et al. 2025
for a recent survey on the topic of monotone metrics.
We define an inner product as follows:

\langle Y,\,X\rangle_{\rho}^{(e)}\;\coloneq\;\Tr\left[Y^{\dagger}\,E_{\rho}[X]\right],

(113)

where
E_{\rho}
is a linear map satisfying

\displaystyle\Tr\left[X^{\dagger}E_{\rho}[X]\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(114)

\displaystyle\Tr\left[Y^{\dagger}E_{\rho}[X]\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[E_{\rho}[Y]^{\dagger}\,X\right],

(115)

and

\displaystyle E_{\rho}\left[U^{\dagger}XU\right]

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}\,E_{\,U\rho U^{\dagger}}\!\left[X\right]\,U,

(116)

\displaystyle E_{\rho}\left[\mathbb{I}\right]

\displaystyle=\rho,

(117)

\displaystyle E_{\,\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}}\!\left[X\otimes X^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{\rho}\left[X\right]\otimes E_{\rho^{\prime}}\left[X^{\prime}\right].

(118)

The choice of the map
E_{\rho}
determines the inner product.
We will primarily work with a generic one; however, three notable examples are:

\displaystyle E_{\rho,\mathrm{S}}[X]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(X\rho+\rho X\right)

(119)

\displaystyle E_{\rho,\mathrm{R}}[X]

\displaystyle=X\rho

(120)

\displaystyle E_{\rho,\mathrm{sq}}[X]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\rho}X\sqrt{\rho},

(121)

which we will call “SLD”, “RLD” and “square root”, respectively.
As in the main text, we assume
\rho\succ 0
, so that we can introduce the dual inner product in terms of the inverse linear map
E_{\rho}^{-1}
without further regularisations:

\langle A,\,B\rangle_{\rho}^{(m)}\;=\;\Tr\left[E_{\rho}^{-1}[A]^{\dagger}B\right];

(122)

it is denoted by the superscript
(m)
, following Hayashi’s notation.

One of the crucial properties of such dual inner products is the monotonicity under the action of quantum channels, which makes them a good measure of information.
Let us consider a channel—a completely positive trace-preserving map—
\Lambda
, such that the output state is also full rank
\Lambda[\rho]>0
and we can state the main monotonicity property
(
Hayashi 2017
, Theorem 6.1)

\langle A,A\rangle_{\rho}^{(m)}\geq\langle\Lambda[A],\Lambda[A]\rangle_{\Lambda[\rho]}^{(m)}\,.

(123)

Moreover, another crucial property is that the norm induced by this class of inner products is minimal and maximal for the symmetric and right versions, respectively:

\langle A,A\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{S},(m)}\,\leq\langle A,A\rangle^{(m)}_{\rho}\,\leq\langle A,A\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{R},(m)}\,.

(124)

Eq. (
33
) in the main text follows from the first inequality.

C.2
Posterior mean operators

Introducing the MSL matrix, with elements

\Sigma_{ij}=\int\!\mathrm{d}x\,\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta},x)\,(\tilde{\theta}_{i}(x)-\theta_{i})(\tilde{\theta}_{j}(x)-\theta_{j})\,,

(125)

where
p(\boldsymbol{\theta},x)=p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the scalar MSL can be written as

\mathcal{L}=L^{ij}\Sigma_{ji}=\tr(L\Sigma)\,.

(126)

Classically, and thus also for quantum problems if the measurement is fixed, the optimal Bayes estimator that minimises the multiparameter MSL matrix is the PM:

\tilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{opt},i}(x)=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|x)\,\theta_{i}=\frac{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}}{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(127)

which was already defined in Eq. (
26
) of the main text.
Applying this estimator, the optimal MSL matrix has elements

\Sigma_{ij}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}\theta_{j}-\int\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\left[\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{i}\right]\left[\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{j}\right]}{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\,.

(128)

Following Ref.
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
, we now proceed to consider the scalar

\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{u}=u^{i}u^{j}\Sigma_{ij}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}-\int\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\left[\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]^{2}}{\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(129)

where
\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}=u^{i}\theta_{i}
is a linear combination of parameters.
For quantum systems, the outcome probability for a given value of the parameters is obtained from Born’s rule
p(x|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Tr\left[M(x)\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]
, which leads to
(
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
, Eq. (6))

\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{u}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}-\int\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\Tr\left[M(x)\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]^{2}}{\Tr\left[M(x)\rho_{0}\right]}\,,

(130)

where
\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}=u^{i}\bar{\rho}_{i}=\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}
.

A crucial observation from Ref.
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
is that the second term resembles the classical Fisher information and so it is possible to apply the Braunstein–Caves inequality
Braunstein and Caves 1994
and obtain a matrix SPM lower bound on
\Sigma
.
Here, we take a different route and use a more general family of inequalities which stem from the monotonicity of the inner products defined in the previous sections.
This allows us to derive a whole family of matrix lower bounds, similarly to the case of local QET.

For simplicity, we focus on a discrete set of measurement outcomes
x
, which can always be encoded on a register composed by an orthonormal basis of vectors
\{\ket{e_{x}}\}
.
We introduce the measurement channel

\kappa[\bullet]\coloneq\sum_{x}\Tr[M(x)\bullet]|e_{x}\rangle\langle e_{x}|\,;

(131)

this is an entanglement-breaking quantum channel that formally represents the map between quantum states and probability distributions (diagonal quantum states) implemented by the POVM with elements
M(x)
.
Applying this channel to
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{\boldsymbol{u}}
we obtain

\begin{split}\kappa[\rho_{0}]&=\sum_{x}\Tr\left[M(x)\rho_{0}\right]|e_{x}\rangle\langle e_{x}|,\\
\kappa[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}]&=\sum_{x}\Tr\left[M(x)\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]|e_{x}\rangle\langle e_{x}|.\end{split}

(132)

These operators are diagonal in the orthonormal basis
\{\ket{e_{x}}\}
and they commute with each other; consequently, any dual inner product defined in the previous section gives exactly the second term of Eq. (
130
):

\langle\kappa[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}],\kappa[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}]\rangle^{(m)}_{\kappa[\rho_{0}]}=\Tr\left[E_{\kappa[\rho_{0}]}^{-1}(\kappa[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}])^{\dagger}\kappa[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}]\right]=\sum_{x}\frac{\Tr\left[M(x)\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]^{2}}{\Tr\left[M(x)\rho_{0}\right]}.

(133)

Therefore, by the monotonicity property (
123
) we finally obtain that

\sum_{x}\frac{\Tr\left[M(x)\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]^{2}}{\Tr\left[M(x)\rho_{0}\right]}\leq\langle\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle^{(m)}_{\rho_{0}}=\langle L_{\boldsymbol{u}},L_{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{(e)},

(134)

where now
L_{\boldsymbol{u}}
is a generic PM operator obtained as

L_{\boldsymbol{u}}\coloneq E^{-1}_{\rho_{0}}[\bar{\rho}_{\boldsymbol{u}}]=u^{i}E^{-1}_{\rho_{0}}[\bar{\rho}_{i}]=u^{i}L_{i},

(135)

where we have also introduced PM operators for each original parameter, satisfying the equations
E_{\rho_{0}}(L_{i})=\bar{\rho}_{i}
.
Such PM operators are generalisation of the symmetric version introduced in the main text in terms of the map
E_{\rho,\mathrm{S}}
.
For example, they also include the operators introduced in Ref.
(
Zhang and Suzuki 2024
, Theorem 8)
, where they are called “
\lambda
logarithmic derivatives”.

From the inequality in Eq. (
134
), the lower bound for the scalar introduced before immediately follows:

\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\Sigma\boldsymbol{u}\geq\int\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\mathcal{K}\boldsymbol{u},

(136)

where we have introduced the matrix with elements

\mathcal{K}_{ij}\coloneq\langle\bar{\rho}_{i},\bar{\rho}_{j}\rangle^{(m)}_{\rho_{0}}=\langle L_{i},L_{j}\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{(e)}=\Tr[L_{i}^{\dagger}E_{\rho_{0}}[L_{j}]]\,.

(137)

By choosing the map
E_{\rho,\mathrm{S}}
, this matrix becomes the real-valued SPM matrix introduced in Ref.
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
.
However, for other choices, such as
E_{\rho,\mathrm{R}}
, this matrix is still Hermitian but complex-valued.
Regardless of the particular choice of monotone metric, since the scalar inequality holds for an arbitrary choice of
\boldsymbol{u}
, we obtain the family of matrix lower bounds on the MSL matrix

\Sigma\succeq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}-\mathcal{K}\;.

(138)

This is again a generalisation of the SPM matrix bound obtained in Ref.
Rubio and Dunningham 2020
.

C.3
Scalar bounds from matrix inequalities

We start from the matrix inequality in Eq. (
138
) and denote the right-hand side by the symbol
\mathcal{B}\coloneq\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}-\mathcal{K}
,
where
\mathcal{K}\geq 0
is a
complex-valued
Hermitian positive definite matrix.
We now want to show that also the imaginary part of
\mathcal{K}
sets constraints on the minimum MSL
\mathcal{L}
.
This fact is well-known
Holevo 2011
; for completeness, we provide a proof here, roughly following Ref.
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2020
.

First, let us rewrite the function to minimise, subject to the constraint (
138
), as the trace of a symmetric matrix
\Tr[L\Sigma]=\Tr[\sqrt{L}\Sigma\sqrt{L}]
.
From (
138
) we can obtain the two matrix inequalities

\sqrt{L}\,\Sigma\sqrt{L}\geq\sqrt{L}\left(\Re{\mathcal{B}}\pm i\Im{\mathcal{B}}\right)\sqrt{L},

(139)

where the two signs correspond to taking the transpose of the two sides of the original equation.
Now, this matrix inequality implies

\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\dagger}\sqrt{L}\left(\Sigma-\Re{\mathcal{B}}\right)\sqrt{L}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\geq\pm\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{\dagger}\left(i\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}\right)\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\,,

(140)

for any set of complex-valued vectors
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}
.
Since
i\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}
is Hermitian, we can choose its eigenbasis as the vectors
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
; noting the corresponding eigenvalues as
\lambda_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tr[L(\Sigma-\Re{\mathcal{B}})]\geq\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|=\tr[|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}|]=\|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}\|_{1},

(141)

where the absolute values come from choosing the
\pm
sign corresponding to a positive quantity.
The matrix
|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}|
is a Hermitian matrix with the same eigenbasis as
i\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}
, and with eigenvalues
|\lambda_{i}|
, while
\|A\|_{1}=\tr[\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}]
is the Schatten 1-norm (also known as trace norm or nuclear norm).
8
8

8

Note that
\tr{|A|}=\|A\|_{1}
only when
A
is normal, as it is the case here.

This inequality for the trace of the real part of a positive semidefinite complex matrix is known as the Belavkin–Grishanin inequality
Belavkin and Grishanin 1973
;
Tsang et al. 2020
.

The minimum can be achieved by setting

\Sigma=\Re{\mathcal{B}}+\sqrt{L^{-1}}|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}|\sqrt{L^{-1}}\,,

(142)

as seen from direct computation.

In conclusion, we have proven that

\min_{\Sigma\geq\mathcal{B}}\tr[L\Sigma]=\tr[L\Re{\mathcal{B}}]+\|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{B}}\sqrt{L}\|_{1}.

(143)

Substituting back the original right-hand side of the inequality (
138
), we get

\mathcal{L}\geq\lambda-\Tr[L\Re{\mathcal{K}}]+\|\sqrt{L}\Im{\mathcal{K}}\sqrt{L}\|_{1},

(144)

since
\lambda=\tr[L\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}]=\int\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}L\,\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\Im{\mathcal{B}}=-\Im{\mathcal{K}}
, given that the other matrix terms are real-valued by assumption.
In general, this bound is not necessarily tight, as it is not always possible to find a measurement and estimator that produce a MSL matrix as in (
142
).

We finally note that the bound in Ref.
(
Zhang and Suzuki 2024
, Theorem 8)
in terms of the
\lambda
logarithmic derivatives is also in the class of scalar bounds derived in this Appendix.
