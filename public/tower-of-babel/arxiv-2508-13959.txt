Adversarially robust quantum state learning and testing
Maryam Aliakbarpour Vladimir Braverman Rice University Johns Hopkins University maryama@rice.edu vova@cs.jhu.edu Nai-Hui Chia Yuhan Liu Rice University Rice University nc67@rice.edu yuhan-liu@rice.edu

Abstract
Quantum state learning is a fundamental problem in physics and computer science. As near-term quantum devices are error-prone, it is important to design error-resistant algorithms. Apart from device errors, other unexpected factors could also affect the algorithm, such as careless human read-out error, or even a malicious hacker deliberately altering the measurement results. Thus, we want our algorithm to work even in the worst case when things go against our favor. We consider the practical setting of single-copy measurements and propose the γ \gamma -adversarial corruption model where an imaginary adversary can arbitrarily change γ \gamma -fraction of the measurement outcomes. This is stronger than the γ \gamma -bounded SPAM noise model, where the post-measurement state changes by at most γ \gamma in trace distance. Under our stronger model of corruption, we design an algorithm using non-adaptive measurements that can learn an unknown rank- r r state up to O ~ ​ ( γ ​ r ) \tilde{O}(\gamma\sqrt{r}) in trace distance, provided that the number of copies is sufficiently large. We further prove an information-theoretic lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( γ ​ r ) \Omega(\gamma\sqrt{r}) for non-adaptive measurements, demonstrating the optimality of our algorithm. Our upper and lower bounds also hold for quantum state testing, where the goal is to test whether an unknown state is equal to a given state or far from it. Our results are intriguingly optimistic and pessimistic at the same time. For general states, the error is dimension-dependent and γ ​ d \gamma\sqrt{d} in the worst case, meaning that only corrupting a very small fraction ( 1 / d 1/\sqrt{d} ) of the outcomes could totally destroy any non-adaptive learning algorithm. However, for constant-rank states that are useful in many quantum algorithms, it is possible to achieve dimension-independent error, even in the worst-case adversarial setting.

Introduction

Learning properties of quantum states is a fundamental task in quantum computing and physics. In the canonical setting, we are given identical copies of an unknown state
\rho
and need to design measurements and algorithms to learn information about
\rho
. Commonly studied problems include tomography
[
KRT17
,
OW17
,
HHJ+17
,
CHL+23
,
ADLY25b
]
, where the goal is to learn the entire state description, and testing
[
BCL20
,
CLO22
,
LA24b
]
, where the goal is to test whether
\rho
satisfies some specific property.

While extensive research has been done on these problems, a common assumption in many works (e.g., all the aforementioned works) is that the
n
copies of the state are identical. This means that the copies are generated through identical and independent physical processes. They also assume that measurements are accurate and work exactly as described by their mathematical formulations.
However, neither assumption may be true in practice, as the state preparation and measurement device may be prone to unknown error, especially in the current NISQ era
[
Pre18
]
of quantum computing.

To this end, many works have considered robustness under state preparation and measurement (SPAM) error
[
FBaK21
,
YSHY23
,
SFMD+24
]
. A general formulation considered in
[
FBaK21
]
for single-copy measurements is that the state preparation and measurement lead to a total of
\gamma
error in trace distance of post-measurement states. They show that
\Theta(\gamma)
error is tight with sufficiently many samples.

We argue that SPAM noise may not be able to characterize all possible errors in the system.
Unexpected physical factors, such as an unstable power source or even a subway train passing beneath the laboratory, may cause unwanted effects to the device. There may also be human factors. Some may be benign, like
inadvertent errors in measurement reading. Others may be malicious, like a hacker with sufficient quantum knowledge who tries to cause as much damage as possible. When any of these occur, the SPAM error assumptions may be violated, and the algorithms proposed by previous works may fail to work.

One may argue that improved quantum error correction may eventually reduce all physical errors below an acceptable threshold, especially given the recent progress
[
Ac23
,
AABA+25
]
. However, as discussed, many factors are non-physical and thus cannot be eliminated by error correction.
Therefore, it is necessary to propose a stronger corruption model for quantum state learning that takes into account these unexpected factors and design algorithms that are robust in this more challenging setting. The main question is as follows,

Can we design quantum learning algorithms that still work even when things go against our favor?

We focus on single-copy tomography and quantum state testing, and describe a corruption model suitable for these problems.

1.1
Adversarial corruption model for single-copy tomography

There are
n
copies of
\rho

\mathcal{M}^{n}=(\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{n})

\mathcal{M}_{i}=\{M_{x}^{i}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}

Tomography.

The goal is to design a measurement scheme

{\hat{\rho}}:{\mathcal{X}}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}

\Pr\left[\,{\left\|{\hat{\rho}}({x}^{n})-\rho\right\|}_{1}\leq\varepsilon\,\right]\geq 0.99.

The probability is over the random seed

We also define a related problem of quantum state testing, which is useful to establish our lower bounds,

Testing.

Given a known target state
{\rho_{0}}

\rho={\rho_{0}}
or
{\left\|\rho-{\rho_{0}}\right\|}_{1}>\varepsilon
with probability at least 0.8.

It is a standard fact that tomography is a harder problem than quantum state testing in terms of computation,

Fact 1.1

Any tomography algorithm that learns an unknown state up to an accuracy of

\varepsilon
.

When the state is
\rho
, the distribution of
{x}_{i},i\geq 1
is determined by Born’s rule,

\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{i}(x)=\Tr[M_{x}^{i}\rho],

(1)

For

{x}^{t}
when the state is
\rho

Adversarial corruption.

We imagine that there is an adversary

Definition 1.2

(
\gamma
-adversarial corruption model)

For non-adaptive measurement schemes, the interaction between the adversary and the algorithm is as follows,

1.

Measurements are applied to all copies of

2.

The adversary
\mathcal{A}

\gamma

3.

The algorithm uses the corrupted outcomes
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
to learn about the state
\rho
.

The adversarial model is inspired by formulations in the robust statistical estimation literature. Many works
[

,

,

,

consider the case when

-fraction of the samples can be arbitrarily changed.

]
studied distributed estimation where a central server tries to learn the distribution from information-constrained messages sent by a group of users. In this setting, the adversary has the power to directly change the messages sent by the users. Beyond statistical estimation, there is also a vast literature on adversarial robust streaming algorithms for many fundamental problems, such as frequency estimation
[

In stark contrast to the abundance of adversarially robust classical algorithms, to our knowledge, there have been very few works, at least in quantum state learning, that consider a similar adversarial setting. Thus, we believe our work is an important step in understanding the adversarial robustness of quantum algorithms.

Adversarial corruption is stronger than SPAM noise.

We first review the
\gamma
-bounded SPAM model in
[
FBaK21
]
. Let
\gamma_{s}
be the error due to state preparation, where the actual state we measure is
\tilde{\rho}
that satisfies
{\left\|\tilde{\rho}-\rho\right\|}_{1}\leq\gamma_{s}
.

The measurement error is characterized by the maximum induced error in trace norm. For a measurement
\mathcal{M}
applied to a state
\rho
, we abuse the notation a bit by denoting the post-measurement state as
\mathcal{M}(\rho)
. Then

\sup_{\rho\text{ state}}{\left\|\mathcal{M}(\rho)-\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\rho)\right\|}_{1}\leq\gamma_{m}.

Here
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is the actual measurement that is applied. Note that
\tilde{\rho}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
could depend on measurements and differ for different copies. Bounded SPAM noise requires that
\gamma_{s}+\gamma_{m}\leq\gamma
.

In our formulation, it may seem at first glance that our model is incomparable or even weaker than SPAM noise, since we still assume all states and measurements are perfect. However, because bounded SPAM noise only changes each outcome distribution by at most
\gamma
total variation distance, the adversary can nearly simulate a
\gamma
-bounded SPAM noise by only corrupting at most
2\gamma
-fraction of the outcomes. See
Section

4.3
for formal proof.

This is similar to how in robust statistics, the strong contamination model where
\gamma
-fraction of the samples are adversarially changed, is stronger than the case when each sample is replaced by a sample from another distribution that is
\gamma
-far in total variation distance
[
DK23
, Section 1.2.3]
.

1.2
Results

We now state our results for robust quantum state learning under adversarial corruption.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\rho
be an unknown state with rank
r
. Under
\gamma
-corruption, there exists an algorithm using non-adaptive measurements that achieves an error of
\tilde{O}\left(\gamma\sqrt{r}\right)
, provided that the number of copies
n
is sufficiently large.

Thus, for constant rank states, we can achieve dimension-independent error that nearly matches the
\Omega(\gamma)
lower bound. Intuitively, this shows that prior knowledge of the structure of the state helps to achieve better performance under adversarial corruption.

However, it leaves the question of whether it is possible to achieve dimension-independent error for general states whose rank may be large. We answer this question negatively.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under
\gamma
-corruption, any quantum state testing algorithm using non-adaptive measurements must incur an error of
\varepsilon=\Omega(\gamma\sqrt{r})
. In particular when
r=d
, the lower bound is
\Omega(\gamma\sqrt{d})
.

Since tomography is harder than testing (
1.1
), the above error upper and lower bounds hold for both tomography and testing up to a constant of 2. This shows that our tomography algorithm is nearly optimal, at least when
n
is sufficiently large.

In contrast to bounded SPAM noise
[
FBaK21
]
, a dimension-dependent error is inevitable for adversarial corruption and can be as large as
\gamma\sqrt{d}
for full-rank states. Since trace distance is at most a constant, and
d
is exponential in the number of qubits in
\rho
, the adversary only needs to change
exponentially small
fraction of the outcomes to entirely ruin any learning algorithm using non-adaptive measurements.

Our lower bound also deviates from many works on robust mean estimation (with direct access to all potentially corrupted samples), where dimension-independent error
[
CDG19
,
ASZ21
]
can be achieved even in high dimensions. Thus, non-adaptive single-copy quantum state learning is more susceptible to adversarial attacks than classical distribution estimation.

Why is dimension-independent error inevitable?

The main reason is that single-copy measurements are inherently information-constrained: any measurement outcome we obtain only tells very limited information about the state. Thus, to fully take advantage of the limited information, the post-processing algorithm must be sensitive to small changes. High sensitivity generally implies less robustness. This is similar to distributed estimation under local differential privacy and communication constraints
[
ASZ21
]
, where a dimension-dependent error is also inevitable.

2
Technical overview

2.1
Lower bound

Under our strong corruption model, the adversary has the power to arbitrarily change a small fraction of the outcomes to make them appear as if they come from some other states that are far away. The key to proving a lower bound is then to quantify how much damage could be caused given a measurement scheme. To this end, we need (1) an information-theoretical framework that takes adversarial corruption into account, and (2) a mathematical tool that quantifies the weakness of any measurement under adversarial attack.

Information-theoretic framework under adversarial corruption

The general recipe to prove lower bounds is to find a reference state
\rho
and a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over states sufficiently far from
\rho
, say with trace distance at least
\varepsilon
. Then we argue that for all measurements, the distribution of measurement outcomes would be close. By a standard hypothesis testing argument, e.g., Le Cam’s method
[
LeC73
,
Yu97
]
, we can conclude that it is hard for any measurement to distinguish
\rho
and states that are
\varepsilon
-far. This establishes a
testing
lower bound with an error of
\varepsilon
, which also holds for tomography.

Under our corruption model, since the adversary can arbitrarily change a fraction of the outcomes, it gains the power to make the outcome distributions even closer, and thus cause more difficulty for the algorithm to distinguish between different states.
Then, we need to characterize how much closer the outcome distributions can be under adversarial corruption. This is achieved through the earth-mover distance, or a coupling argument.

To illustrate our argument, let us fix a set of measurements
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots\mathcal{M}_{n}
1
1

1

We assume that the adversary knows the shared random seed, so all measurements are fixed to the adversary
. We obtain outcomes
x^{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
when measuring the quantum states. When the state is
\rho
, we denote the outcome distribution as
\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{x^{n}}
. To successfully test between quantum states that are
\varepsilon
far, we need to solve the following hypothesis testing problem,

H_{0}:\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{x^{n}},\quad H_{1}:\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\,\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}^{x^{n}}\,\right].

How much fraction of the outcomes
x^{n}
one needs to change to make one distribution
H_{0}
look exactly like the other
H_{1}
? This can be characterized using coupling. A coupling
\Pi
between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
is a joint distribution
(X^{n},Y^{n})
such that each marginal is distributed according to
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, respectively. For any coupling
\Pi
, we can define

\mathbb{E}_{(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim\Pi}\left[\,\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},Y^{n}\right)\,\right],

(2)

which is the expected number of coordinates that
X^{n},Y^{n}
differ. We can view
\Pi
as a randomized mapping. Then an adversary can use
\Pi
as a strategy to change the outcomes. If the above distance is at most
\gamma
, then using
\Pi
, the adversary only changes
\gamma
-fraction of the outcomes
in expectation
to make outcomes from
H_{0}
look exactly as if they come from
H_{1}
.

One caveat is that we enforce a hard constraint on the number of outcomes that an adversary can change, so
\Pi
cannot be directly used. However, we can make small changes to the adversarial strategy to meet the hard constraint while still ensure the corrupted outcomes are close in total variation distance.

Usually, to prove the tighter lower bound for tomography, we need to design a more difficult decision problem via more advanced arguments such as Fano’s, Holevo
[
Hol73
]
, or Assouad
[
Ass83
,
ADLY25b
]
. However, since the power of the adversary is so strong, a testing lower bound suffices even for tomography. This can also be seen from bounded SPAM noise

[
RQK+21
]
and robust statistical estimation literature
[
DK23
,
ASZ21
]
.

Measurement information channel quantifies the weakness under adversarial attack

Having established a general framework, we just need to upper bound the minimum achievable expected coupling distance (
2
) for any measurement. The
measurement information channel

[
LA24a
]
is helpful for our purpose.

Definition 2.1
.

For a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}
, the measurement information channel
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{M_{x}}{\Tr[M_{x}]}\Tr[M_{x}\rho].

It has a matrix form of

{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\frac{|M_{x}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle M_{x}|}{\Tr[M_{x}]},

which satisfies
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\rangle=|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)\rangle\rangle
. We sometimes drop the subscript when the measurement is clear from context.

We instantiate our lower bound framework with
{\rho_{\text{mm}}}=\mathbb{I}_{d}/d
as the reference state and a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
parameterized by binary vectors in
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell})\{-1,1\}^{\ell}
,

\sigma_{z}:={\rho_{\text{mm}}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{d\ell}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}z_{i}V_{i},\quad\ell\sim\{-1,1\}^{\ell}.

Here
V_{i}
’s are trace-0 orthonomal Hermitian matrices,
\Tr[V_{i}]=0
and
\Tr[V_{i}V_{j}]=\mathds{1}_{\left\{i=j\right\}}
. There are many ways we can choose the matrices: one option is the (normalized) Pauli observables. We choose
\ell=d^{2}/2
, which roughly matches the dimension of full-rank states. Using random matrix theory, we can argue that the above state is a valid quantum state with overwhelming probability (at least
1-\exp(-d)
).

For our choice of
{\mathcal{D}}
, we can upper bound the minimum coupling distance in terms of the measurement information channel,

\min_{\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim\Pi}\left[\,\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},Y^{n}\right)\,\right]={O\left(\frac{n\varepsilon}{d}\sqrt{\sup_{\mathcal{M}}{\left\|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\|}_{1}}\right)}.

For all measurements,
{\left\|\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\|}_{1}\leq d

[
LA24a
]
. By our argument in
Section

2.1
,
n\gamma
cannot be larger than the right-hand-side, else learning would not be possible. This proves that
\varepsilon=\Omega(\gamma\sqrt{d})
.

To obtain a lower bound that depends on the rank
r
, we simply restrict the construction to an
r
-dimensional subspace. Our lower bound techniques have regularity conditions that only apply to
r\geq 200
. However, since a lower bound of
\gamma
hold for all states, we conclude that the error lower bound is
\Omega(\gamma\sqrt{r})
for all rank-
r
states.

2.2
Tomography algorithm

Reduction to covariance estimation

We first review the tomography algorithm in
[
GKKT20
]
, which reduces to estimating the (complex) covariance matrix of a vector-valued random variable. Thus to make it adversarially robust, it suffices to apply techniques in robust covariance estimation
[
KSS18
,
DK23
,
BDH+20
]
.

We apply the uniform POVM to all copies, which consist of all suitably normalized rank-1 projectors,

\mathcal{M}_{unif}=\{d|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|\}_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d}}.

Each
|{v}\rangle
is drawn uniformly from the Haar measure, i.e., a uniform distribution over the complex unit sphere.
We can confirm using (
5
) that
\int d|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|\mathrm{d}v=\mathbb{I}_{d}
and thus it is a valid POVM. When applying the measurement to
\rho
, by Born’s rule, we obtain a vector
|{v}\rangle
as an outcome which occurs with a probability density of

d\langle v|\rho|v\rangle\mathrm{d}v.

(3)

We denote this distribution as
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
. Again using (
5
), the expectation of
|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|
is directly related to the unknown state
\rho
,

\Sigma_{\rho}:=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|\,\right]=\int|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|\langle v|\rho|v\rangle\mathrm{d}v=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{d}+\rho}{d+1}.

(4)

Note that since
\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,|{v}\rangle\,\right]=0
by symmetry,
\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|\,\right]
is exactly the (complex) covariance matrix of the random variable
|{v}\rangle
. The algorithm then estimates
\rho
using the empirical covariance matrix,

\hat{\rho}=(d+1)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|{v_{i}}\rangle\langle{v_{i}}|-\mathbb{I}_{d}.

Robust covariance estimation in Hilbert-Schmidt/Frobenius distance

To achieve the desired
\gamma\sqrt{r}
error, we note that it suffices to obtain
\gamma
error in terms of Hilbert-Schmidt distance. To see this, let
\rho
be the unknown state and
\hat{\rho}
be our estimate, and assume
{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}\leq\gamma
. By Cauchy-Schwarz,

{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{1}\leq\sqrt{d}{\left\|\rho-\hat{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}\leq\gamma\sqrt{d}.

Thus, we recover the worst-case error bound. To show a better bound when
\rho
is low rank, it suffices to find the best rank-
r
approximation of
\hat{\rho}
, call it
\tilde{\rho}
. Then
\tilde{\rho}-\rho
has rank at most
2r
, and
{\left\|\rho-\tilde{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}\leq 2\gamma
by triangle inequality. Rank-
2r
Hermitian matrices only have at most
2r
non-zero eigenvalues. Thus, applying Cauchy Schwarz, we obtain the
O(\gamma\sqrt{r})
error as desired.

However, even though
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
is a sub-gaussian distribution, estimating the covariance with (relative) Frobenius distance is a challenging task. For general sub-gaussian distributions, dimension-dependent Frobenius error is unavoidable. Only a (relative) spectral error, i.e. operator norm distance, can be guaranteed
[
KSS18
,
DHPT24
]
, which is not sufficient for our purpose.

To our knowledge, only highly structured distributions can achieve dimension-independent Frobenius error. These include Gaussian
[
DK23
]
, affine transform of uniform spherical distribution, distributions with sub-gaussian marginals
[
BDH+20
]
, and Poincaré distributions
2
2

2

A distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Poincaré with parameter
\sigma
if for all differentiable functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\operatorname{Var}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}{\left[f(x)\right]}\leq\sigma^{2}\lVert\nabla f(x){\rVert}_{2}^{2}
.

[
BKR+24
, Lemma 9.7]
.

Although the outcomes
|{v}\rangle
solely lies on the unit sphere, the distribution has a complicated structure. We start with the simple case where
\rho=|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|
is a pure state. Then from (
3
), the distribution has density of
0
for vectors
|{v}\rangle
orthogonal to
\psi
, and highest density when
|\langle\psi|v\rangle|=1
.
Moreover, we can show that
\mathbb{E}_{|{\psi}\rangle\langle{\psi}|}\left[\,|\langle\psi|v\rangle|^{2}\,\right]\simeq 1/d^{2}
, so most of the probability mass should lie in some rings such that
|\langle\psi|v\rangle|\simeq 1/d
. When
\rho
is a mixed state, then the distribution would be a mixture of these rings.
It is unclear how to characterize this complicated distribution via affine transforms and/or projections of Gaussian or uniform spherical distributions.

Hypercontractivity of outcomes from the uniform POVM

The key to designing a robust tomography algorithm is to upper bound the high-order moments of
|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|
. We prove that the outcome distributions from the uniform POVM satisfies
hypercontractivity
,

Definition 2.2
.

For all even integers
h\geq 2
,
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
satisfies
C
-hypercontractivity, i.e., there exists constant
C>0
for all Hermitian matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

\left(\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{h}\,\right]\right)^{2}\leq\frac{(Ch)^{2h}}{d^{2h}}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h}.

Mathematically, one can view
\langle v|M|v\rangle
as a degree-2 polynomial in the real and imaginary parts of
|{v}\rangle
(which satisfies some algebraic structure defined by complex numbers). Physically, we can view the measurement outcome as a pure state
|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|
, and
\langle v|M|v\rangle
is the expectation value of an
observable

M
(in quantum mechanics, anything that can be observed about the state must be represented as a Hermitian matrix). Regardless of how one chooses to interpret
\langle v|M|v\rangle
, it should have well-bounded high-order moments. This is similar to hyper-contractivity proposed in
[
BDH+20
]
. We extend the notion to complex random vectors for complex covariance estimation.

To see how this property comes into play, we describe a highly inefficient algorithm and “prove” its guarantees using a slightly hand-wavy argument.

We fix some
h=2t
to be chosen later. Let
|{u_{1}}\rangle,\ldots,|{u_{n}}\rangle
be a set of clean samples. With a sufficiently large number of samples
n
, we can guarantee that with high probability, the empirical average is close to its true mean, so the finite samples also satisfy hyper-contractivity.

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle^{h}\leq\frac{(Ch)^{h}}{d^{h}}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h/2}.

With an even larger
n
(perhaps exponentially large), we can further guarantee that hypercontractivity holds for any subset of the samples
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=(1-\gamma)n
.

Let
|{v_{1}}\rangle,\ldots,|{v_{n}}\rangle
be the
\gamma
-corrupted, so
|{v_{i}}\rangle=|{u_{i}}\rangle
for at least
(1-\gamma)n
of the samples.
We can brute-force search for a subset
S
that satisfies hyper-contractivity. This may not be feasible since we need to verify for all Hermitian
M
, but suppose we are able to find such a subset. Then we output our estimate
\hat{\Sigma}
as the empirical covariance over the set
S
,
\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}|{v_{i}}\rangle\langle{v_{i}}|
.

Note that
n
is very large, so we can assume that the true covariance is roughly the empirical average of clean samples, i.e.
\Sigma_{\rho}\simeq\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|
. Then for all Hermitian
M
,

\displaystyle\Tr[M(\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho})]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}^{n}(\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle-\langle u_{i}|M|u_{i}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}(\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle-\langle u_{i}|M|u_{i}\rangle).

Raising to the power of
h=2t
and using Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Tr[M(\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho})]^{2t}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2t-1}\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle\right)^{2t}+2^{2t-1}\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}\langle u_{i}|M|u_{i}\rangle\right)^{2t}

Using Hölder’s inequality that for any measure
\mathrm{d}x
,
\int|fg|\mathrm{d}x\leq(\int|f|^{p}\mathrm{d}x)^{1/p}(\int|g|^{q}\mathrm{d}x)^{1/q}
for
1/p+1/q=1
, and that
\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}^{2}=\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle\right)^{2t}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq|{v_{i}}\rangle\right\}}^{2t}\right)^{2t-1}\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\langle v_{i}|M|v_{i}\rangle^{2t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(C^{\prime}\gamma)^{2t-1}t^{2t}}{d^{2t}}({\left\|M\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}+\Tr[M]^{2})^{t}.

Here we used that the potentially corrupted samples in
|S|
satisfies hypercontractivity, and that
\sum_{i\in S}\mathds{1}_{\left\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\neq v_{i}\right\}}\leq\gamma n
since the adversary can only change
\gamma
-fraction. We can bound the second term similarly. Therefore combining the two parts, we have for some constant
c
,

\Tr[M(\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho})]^{2t}\leq\frac{(c\gamma t)^{2t}}{\gamma d^{2t}}({\left\|M\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2}+\Tr[M]^{2})^{t}.

Since it holds
for all

M
, we can set
M=\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho}
. Note that
\Tr[M]=0
. Thus,

{\left\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{4t}\leq\frac{(c\gamma t)^{2t}}{\gamma d^{2t}}{\left\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{2t}\implies{\left\|\hat{\Sigma}-\Sigma_{\rho}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}\leq\frac{c\gamma t}{\gamma^{1/(2t)}d}.

Therefore, setting
t=O(\log(1/\gamma))
yields an error of
\tilde{O}\left(\gamma/d\right)
. Setting
\hat{\rho}=(d+1)\hat{\Sigma}-\mathbb{I}_{d}
, we conclude that the Frobenius error for estimating
\rho
is
\tilde{O}\left(\gamma\right)
as desired.

The major issue in our analysis is how to verify that hypercontractivity holds for all Hermitian matrices. To this end, we use the sum-of-squares technique
[
HKP+17
,
BK20
,
BDH+20
]
. As long as we can show that hypercontractivity can be written as a degree
O(h)
sum-of-squares polynomial in the matrix indeterminant
M
, then we can design a sum-of-squares algorithm to verify and “find” a subset
S
that satisfies hypercontractivity. The full algorithm description and proof are given in
Section

5
.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Quantum state and POVM

We use the Dirac notation
|{\psi}\rangle
to denote a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We use
|{j}\rangle
to denote the vector with 1 at the
j
th coordinate and 0 everywhere else.

\langle{\psi}|:=(|{\psi}\rangle)^{\dagger}
is a row vector.
\langle\psi|\phi\rangle
is the inner product of
|{\psi}\rangle
and
|{\phi}\rangle
. We denote the set of all
d\times d
Hermitian matrices by
{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
. A
d
-dimensional quantum system is described by a positive-semidefinite Hermitian matrix
\rho\in{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
with
\Tr[\rho]=1
. We assume
d=2^{{N}}
where
{N}
is the number of qubits in the system.

A special case is the maximally mixed state
{\rho_{\text{mm}}}:=\mathbb{I}_{d}/d
.

Measurements are formulated as
positive operator-valued measure
(POVM). Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be an outcome set. Then a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{x}\}_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}
, where
M_{x}
is p.s.d. and
\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}M_{x}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
. Let
X
be the outcome of measuring
\rho
with
\mathcal{M}
, then the probability observing
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
is given by the
Born’s rule
,

\Pr\left[\,X=x\,\right]=\Tr[\rho M_{x}].

This definition can be extended to an infinite outcome set.

3.2
Complex matrices and operators

Complex matrices

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. Inner product between
A
and
B
is defined as
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle:=\Tr[A^{\dagger}B]
. For Hermitian matrices
A,B
,
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\left\langle B,A\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus the subspace of Hermitian matrices
{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
is a
real
Hilbert space (i.e. the associated field is
\mathbb{R}
) with the same matrix inner product.

Let
\{|{j}\rangle\}_{j=0}^{d-1}
be the natural basis for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we define vectorization as
\text{vec}(|{i}\rangle\langle{j}|):=|{j}\rangle\otimes|{i}\rangle
. For convenience we denote
|A\rangle\rangle:=\text{vec}(A)
. Matrix inner product satisfies
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle=\left\langle\left\langle A|B\right\rangle\right\rangle
.

Linear superoperators

Let
\mathcal{N}:\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}\mapsto\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a linear operator over
\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. Every superoperator
\mathcal{N}
has a matrix representation
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}\times d^{2}}
that satisfies
|\mathcal{N}(X)\rangle\rangle={\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N})|X\rangle\rangle
for all matrices
X\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
.

Schatten norms

Let
\Lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\geq 0
be the
singular values
of a linear operator
A
, which can be a matrix or a superoperator. For Hermitian matrices, the singular values are the absolute values of the eigenvalues. Then for
p\geq 1
, the
Schatten
p
-norm
is defined as

\|A\|_{S_{p}}:=\|\Lambda\|_{p}
. The Schatten norms of a superoperator
\mathcal{N}
and its matrix representation
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N})
match exactly,
\|\mathcal{N}\|_{S_{p}}=\|{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{N})\|_{S_{p}}
. Some important examples are trace norm
{\left\|A\right\|}_{1}:=\|A\|_{S_{1}}
, Hilbert-Schmidt norm
{\left\|A\right\|}_{\text{HS}}:=\|A\|_{S_{2}}=\sqrt{\left\langle A,A\right\rangle}
, and operator norm
{\left\|A\right\|}_{\text{op}}:=\|A\|_{S_{\infty}}=\max_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}
.

3.3
Probability distances

Let
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
be distributions over a finite domain
\mathcal{X}
. The
total variation distance
is defined as

{{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\right)}:=\sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{p}(S)-\mathbf{q}(S))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\mathbf{p}(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)|.

The KL-divergence is

{\operatorname{d_{KL}}\left({\mathbf{p}\mid\mid\mathbf{q}}\right)}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{p}(x)\log\frac{\mathbf{p}(x)}{\mathbf{q}(x)}.

The chi-square divergence

{\operatorname{d}_{\chi^{2}}\left({\mathbf{p}\mid\mid\mathbf{q}}\right)}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{(\mathbf{p}(x)-\mathbf{q}(x))^{2}}{\mathbf{q}(x)}.

By Pinsker’s inequality and concavity of logarithm,

2{{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}}\right)}^{2}\leq{\operatorname{d_{KL}}\left({\mathbf{p}\mid\mid\mathbf{q}}\right)}\leq{\operatorname{d}_{\chi^{2}}\left({\mathbf{p}\mid\mid\mathbf{q}}\right)}.

We define
\ell_{p}
distance as
\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}{\rVert}_{p}:=\left(\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{|\mathbf{p}(x)-\mathbf{q}(x)|^{p}}\right)^{1/p}.

All the definitions above can be extended to general probability measures.

3.4
Haar measure

The analysis of testing and tomography algorithms requires computing Haar integrals. Let
\mathbb{S}^{d}
denote the complex unit sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We use
{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
to denote the Haar measure over
d\times d
unitary matrices. The Haar measure induces a unique unitarily invariant measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, which we denote as
\mathrm{d}v
.

Detailed exposition of the representation theoretic techniques is beyond the scope of this paper. We only present the necessary definitions and results.

Permutation and cycles

A permutation
\pi:[n]\mapsto[n]
is a bijection over
[n]
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the set of all permutations over
[n]
. Every permutation can be decomposed into cycles. For example, the permutation
\pi=(1,4,2,3)
has cycles

(1),(2,3,4).

We use
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
to denote the set of cycles in
\pi
. For each cycle
c\in\mathcal{C}(\pi)
, we use
|c|
to denote the length of the cycle. In the example above
|(2,3,4)|=3
and
|(1)|
=1.

Haar measure moments

Let
|{u}\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a unit vector drawn uniformly from the unit sphere. The
k
-th order moment
|{u}\rangle\langle{u}|^{\otimes k}
can be computed using Schur-Weyl duality,
[
GKKT20
, Eq.(14)]
.

\mathbb{E}_{|{u}\rangle\sim{\mathcal{U}_{d}}}\left[\,|{u}\rangle\langle{u}|^{\otimes k}\,\right]=\binom{d+k-1}{k}^{-1}P_{\text{Sym}^{(k)}}.

(5)

Here
P_{\text{Sym}^{(k)}}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}P_{\pi}
is the projection matrix onto the symmetric subspace of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes k}
, where
P_{\pi}
is a permutation operator defined as

P_{\pi}|{\psi_{1}}\rangle\otimes|{\psi_{k}}\rangle=|{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(1)}}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(k)}}\rangle.

Permutation operators are unitary with
P_{\pi}^{\dagger}=P_{\pi}^{-1}=P_{\pi^{-1}}
.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. For
\pi=(2,3,4,\ldots,k,1)
(which circular shifts all numbers by one place), we have

\Tr[P_{\pi}M_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{k}]=\Tr[M_{1}\cdots M_{k}].

(6)

Using (
5
), we can compute the
k
-th moment of
\langle u|M|u\rangle
for any Hermitian matrix
M
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{|{u}\rangle\sim{\mathcal{U}_{d}}}\left[\,\langle u|M|u\rangle^{k}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{|{u}\rangle\sim{\mathcal{U}_{d}}}\left[\,\Tr[|{u}\rangle\langle{u}|^{\otimes k}M^{\otimes k}]\,\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr[M^{\otimes k}\mathbb{E}_{|{u}\rangle\sim{\mathcal{U}_{d}}}\left[\,|{u}\rangle\langle{u}|^{\otimes k}\,\right]]

\displaystyle=\binom{d+k-1}{k}^{-1}\Tr\left[P_{\text{Sym}^{(k)}}M^{\otimes k}\right].

From (
6
), we have for each permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
,

\Tr[P_{\pi}M^{\otimes k}]=\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}(\pi)}\Tr[M^{|c|}].

See also
[
CLO22
, Equation (4)]
for reference. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{|{u}\rangle\sim{\mathcal{U}_{d}}}\left[\,\langle u|M|u\rangle^{k}\,\right]=\binom{d+k-1}{k}^{-1}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}(\pi)}\Tr[M^{|c|}].

(7)

3.5
Sum-of-squares and pseudo-distributions

The sum-of-squares method is a powerful tool for robust statistical estimation tasks. We only describe the necessary preliminaries needed in our tomography algorithm and refer the readers to
[
HKP+17
,
KSS18
,
BDH+20
]
for more details.

3.5.1
Pseudo-distributions

Pseudo-distributions are a generalization of probability distributions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set of vectors. A level-
\ell
(or degree-
\ell
) pseudo-distribution over
\mathcal{X}
has a “mass function”
D(x)
such that,

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}D(x)=1,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}D(x)f(x)^{2}\geq 0,

for all polynomial
f
such that
\deg(f)\leq\ell/2
.

For all function
f
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the pseudo-expectation as

\mathbb{E}_{D}\left[\,f(x)\,\right]:=\sum_{x}D(x)f(x).

Given a set of polynomial constraints
\mathcal{A}=\{f_{1}\geq 0,\ldots f_{m}\geq 0\}
, we say that
D
satisfies
\mathcal{A}
at degree
r
if for every
S\subseteq[m]
and every sum-of-squares polynomial
h
with
\deg(h)=\sum_{i\in S}\max\{\deg(f_{i}),r\}
,

\mathbb{E}_{D}\left[\,h(x)\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}(x)\,\right]\geq 0.

We say that
D
satisfies
\mathcal{A}
approximately at degree
r
if the above inequalities are satisfied up to an error of
2^{-d\ell}\lVert h{\rVert}_{2}\prod_{i\in S}\lVert f_{i}{\rVert}_{2}
, where
\lVert\cdot{\rVert}_{2}
is the 2-norm of the coefficients of each monomial.

Due to the existence of an efficient separation oracle for moment tensors of pseudo-distributions
[
Nes00
,
Las01
]
, we can efficiently find a level-
\ell
pseudo-distribution that approximately satisfies a given set of constraints
\mathcal{A}
in time
(d+m)^{O(\ell)}
. See
[
BDH+20
, Fact 3.9]
.

Pseudo-expectations satisfy the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

Fact 3.2
.

Let
f,g
be polynomials of degree at most
d
in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for any degree-
d
pseudo-distribution
D
,
\mathbb{E}_{D}\left[\,fg\,\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{D}\left[\,f^{2}\,\right]\mathbb{E}_{D}\left[\,g^{2}\,\right]
.

3.5.2
Sum-of-squares proofs

In this section, we define sum-of-squares proofs and introduce the necessary theoretical tools.

Definition 3.3

(Sum-of-squares (SoS) proof)
.

For polynomials
p,q
in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if there exists polynomials
s_{1},\ldots,s_{t}
of degree at most
k/2
such that

p-q=\sum_{i=1}^{t}s_{i}^{2},

we say that
p\geq q
has a degree-
k
sum-of-squares proof, denoted as
\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{k}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{p\geq q\}
.

Given axioms
\mathcal{A}=\{f_{i}=0\}_{i\in[m]}\cup\{g_{j}\geq 0\}_{j\in[n]}
where
f_{i},g_{i}
are polynomials, we say that there exists a degree-
k
proof of
p\geq q
modulo
\mathcal{A}
if there exists polynomials
a_{i},b_{j},s_{t}
such that
\deg(a_{i}f_{i})\leq k
,
\deg(b_{j}^{2}g_{j})\leq k
, and
\deg(s_{t}^{2})\leq k
for all
i,j,t
, and

p-q=\sum_{t}s_{t}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}f_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}^{2}g_{j},

which we denote as
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{k}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{p\geq q\}
.

We now introduce some inference rules for SoS proofs.

Fact 3.4

(SoS inference rules)
.

SoS proofs satisfy the following rules.

Addition

If
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{f\geq 0,g\geq 0\}
, then
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{f+g\geq 0\}
.

Multiplication

If
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{f\geq 0\}
and
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{10.05208pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-10.05208pt\raisebox{-7.75671pt}{\makebox[10.05208pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell^{\prime}}$}}}\hskip-10.05208pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[10.05208pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{g\geq 0\}
, then
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{19.53825pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-19.53825pt\raisebox{-7.75671pt}{\makebox[19.53825pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}$}}}\hskip-19.53825pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[19.53825pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\{fg\geq 0\}
.

Transitivity

If
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\ell}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\mathcal{B}
and

Note on complex vectors and matrices

In our problem, we mostly deal with complex vectors and Hermitian matrices. However, every complex vector

4.1
Robust testing lower bound via earth-mover distance

Suppose that the unknown state is
\rho
. The key insight is that if an adversary can change
\gamma
proportion of the outcomes to make them appear as if they come from another state
\varepsilon
-far from
\rho
, then we cannot hope to achieve better error than
\varepsilon
.

This intuition is formalized in
Section

4.1
. We first define earth-mover distance with Hamming distance as the metric,

Definition 4.5

Let
\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}
be two distributions over

(note that they may not necessarily be product distributions). Let
\pi(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})
be the set of couplings between the two distributions. The earth-mover distance is

\operatorname{d}_{\rm EM}\left(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}\right):=\min_{\Pi\in\pi(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim\Pi}\left[\,\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},Y^{n}\right)\,\right].

This is Kantorovich’s formulation of optimal transport with Hamming distance as the cost. The existence of the minimum is due to the lower-semicontinuity of Hamming distance
[
AGS08

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}

\varepsilon

Proof.

Consider a game played by an adversary and a player with access to a testing algorithm. With equal probability (1/2), the adversary either (1) samples
\sigma\sim\mathcal{D}
and sets all copies to be
\sigma
or (2) sets the
n
quantum states to
\rho
. In either case, the adversary can arbitrarily change
\gamma
-fraction of the outcomes. The player is asked to distinguish between the two cases using the potentially corrupted measurement outcomes.

Since
\Pr_{\sigma\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\,{\left\|\rho-\sigma\right\|}_{1}>\varepsilon\,\right]>0.9
, if the testing algorithm can test whether
\sigma=\rho
or
{\left\|\sigma-\rho\right\|}_{1}>\varepsilon
with probability at least 0.8, then the player should be able to guess correctly with probability at least

0.8\cdot\frac{1}{2}(1+0.9)=0.76.

We now design a strategy for the adversary to corrupt the measurement outcomes. By

4.1
, for all measurements
\mathcal{M}^{n}
, there exists a coupling
\Pi
such that

\mathbb{E}_{(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim\Pi}\left[\,\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},Y^{n}\right)\,\right]\leq\frac{\gamma n}{2},

and the marginals are
X^{n}\sim\mathbb{E}_{\sigma\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\,\mathbf{p}_{\sigma}^{x^{n}}\,\right],Y^{n}\sim\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{x^{n}}
. By Markov’s inequality,

\Pr_{(X^{n},Y^{n})\sim\Pi}\left[\,\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},Y^{n}\right)>\gamma n\,\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}.

We can view

as a randomized function of
X^{n}
, i.e.

. This function can be used to alter the outcomes and fool the player.

In particular, the adversary does not make any changes in case (2) when the state is
\rho

In case (1) when
\sigma
is sampled from
\mathcal{D}
and outcomes

are obtained from the measurements,
the adversary changes the outcomes to

if

, and keeps the original outcomes
X^{n}
otherwise. Equivalently, the adversary applies the following function to

Adv(X^{n})=\begin{cases}F(X^{n}),&\operatorname{d}_{\rm Ham}\left(X^{n},F(X^{n})\right)\leq\gamma n,\\
X^{n},&o.w.\end{cases}

The adversary changes at most
\gamma
-fraction of the outcomes in all scenarios.
Let

be the outcomes in case (2). Since

has the same distribution as

, we can upper bound the total variation distance.

Denote

as the event that

, which happens with probability at least

. For any random variable
X
, we use the shorthand
X|E
be the distribution of

conditioned on

. Then,

\displaystyle{{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({Adv(X^{n}),Y^{n}}\right)}

\displaystyle={{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({Adv(X^{n}),F(X^{n})}\right)}

\displaystyle={{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({Adv(X^{n})|E,F(X^{n})|E}\right)}\Pr\left[\,E\,\right]+{{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({{Adv(X^{n})|E^{c}},{F(X^{n})|E^{c}}}\right)}\Pr\left[\,E^{c}\,\right]

\displaystyle={{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({{F(X^{n})|E},{F(X^{n})|E}}\right)}\Pr\left[\,E\,\right]+{{d_{\operatorname{TV}}}\left({{X^{n}|E^{c}},{F(X^{n})|E^{c}}}\right)}\Pr\left[\,E^{c}\,\right]

The final step is because

4.3

To conclude the section, we give a formal proof that our adversarial corruption model is at least as strong as

Theorem 4.12

Given

\rho

\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{n}

Using the same argument as

Robust tomography algorithm

We restate the algorithm without robustness guarantees.

Algorithm 1

5.2
Hypercontractivity of uniform POVM outcomes

We first upper bound the covariance of

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
|{v}\rangle

5

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|^{\otimes 2}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\int d|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|^{\otimes 2}\langle v|\rho|v\rangle\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=d\Tr_{1}\left[\left(\int|{v}\rangle\langle{v}|^{\otimes 3}\mathrm{d}v\right)\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes 2}\right]

\displaystyle=d\binom{d+2}{3}\Tr_{1}\left[P_{\text{Sym}}^{(k)}\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes 2}\right]

This is because if two Hermitian matrices commute, then they can be diagonalized using the same unitary matrix. Thus we prove the lemma.

Corollary 5.6

(Certifiably bounded second moment of observables)

3\Tr[M]^{2}+3\Tr[M^{2}]-2\Tr[M]\Tr[M\rho]-2\Tr[M^{2}\rho]\geq 0.

Since it is a degree-2 homogeneous polynomial, it must be SoS. We consider the following matrix,

P=(3\Tr[M]^{2}+3\Tr[M^{2}])\mathbb{I}_{d}-2\Tr[M]M-2\Tr[M^{2}].

We just need to prove it is p.s.d, then
\Tr[P\rho]\geq 0
for all quantum state
\rho
. Let
M=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|
, then

\displaystyle P=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(3\Tr[M]^{2}+3\Tr[M^{2}]-2\Tr[M]\lambda_{i}-2\lambda_{i}^{2})|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|.

We further prove that each eigenvalue is non-negative.

\displaystyle 3\Tr[M]^{2}+3\Tr[M^{2}]-2\Tr[M]\lambda_{i}-2\lambda_{i}^{2}=2\Tr[M]^{2}+(\Tr[M]-\lambda_{i})^{2}+3\Tr[M^{2}]-3\lambda_{i}^{2}\geq 0.

In the final step, we used that
\Tr[M^{2}]=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}
. This completes the proof of the first upper bound.

Next we prove the variance bound, using (
4
),

\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle\,\right]=\frac{\Tr[M]+\Tr[M\rho]}{d+1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}{\left[\langle v|M|v\rangle\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{2}\,\right]-\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle\,\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+1)(\Tr[M^{2}]+2\Tr[M^{2}\rho]-\Tr[M\rho]^{2})-(\Tr[M]+\Tr[M\rho])^{2}}{(d+1)^{2}(d+2)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Tr[M^{2}]+2\Tr[M^{2}\rho]}{(d+1)(d+2)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\Tr[M^{2}]}{(d+1)(d+2)}.

The final inequality is due to
0\preceq\rho\preceq\mathbb{I}_{d}
, and thus
\Tr[M^{2}(\mathbb{I}_{d}-\rho)]\geq 0
, which is a degree-2 homogeneous polynomial in
M
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.7

(Certifiable hypercontractivity of uniform POVM)
.

For all integers
h\geq 2
,
{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)
satisfies
C
-hypercontractivity, i.e., there exists constant
C>0
for all Hermitian matrix
M
,

\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{h}\,\right]^{2}\leq\frac{(Ch)^{2h}}{d^{2h}}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h}\leq(\sqrt{2}Ch)^{2h}{\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{2}\,\right]}^{h}.

Moreover, there exists a degree
2h
sum-of-squares proof, i.e., RHS
-
LHS is a sum-of-squares polynomial in terms of the real and imaginary parts of
H
.

Proof.

First we give a sum-of-squares lower bound for the second moment,

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
M\in{\mathbb{H}_{d}}
be the indeterminate. There is a degree-2 SoS proof of

(d+1)(d+2)\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{2}\,\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2}).

Proof.

Recall the expression for the second moment of
\langle v|M|v\rangle
,

\displaystyle\quad(d+1)(d+2)\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{2}\,\right]-\frac{1}{2}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Tr[M]^{2}+\Tr[M^{2}]+4\Tr[M]\Tr[M\rho]+4\Tr[M^{2}\rho]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Tr[M^{2}]^{2}+\Tr[(\Tr[M]^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}+4\Tr[M]M+4M^{2})\rho]\right).

\Tr[M^{2}]
is already a degree-2 SoS. Thus it suffices to prove that
P:=\Tr[M]\mathbb{I}_{d}+4\Tr[M]M+4M^{2}
is p.s.d. Let
M=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|
. Then,

P=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\Tr[M]^{2}+4\Tr[M]\lambda_{i}+4\lambda_{i}^{2})|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\Tr[M]+2\lambda_{i})^{2}|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|.

Thus
P
is p.s.d., and
\Tr[P\rho]\geq 0
for all
M,\rho
. Note that
\Tr[P\rho]
is a degree-2 homogeneous polynomial in
M
, so it must be a sum-of-squares polynomial. The proof is complete.
∎

Then it suffices to obtain an SoS upper bound of the
h
-order moment. Using (
5
) ,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{h}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{d+h}{h+1}^{-1}\frac{1}{h!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h+1}}\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(d+1)\cdots(d+h)}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h+1}}\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right].

Using SoS almost triangle inequality,

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.66782pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.66782pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[12.66782pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2h}$}}}\hskip-12.66782pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.66782pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{(d+1)^{h}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{h}\,\right]^{2}\leq(h+1)!\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{h+1}}\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right]^{2}\right\}.

(15)

Thus it suffices to prove that
\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right]^{2}\leq(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h}
.
For permutations
\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
with only one cycle, by
(
6
)
and symmetry,

\Tr[P_{\tau}M^{\otimes k}\otimes\rho]=\Tr[M^{k}\rho].

We decompose
P_{\pi}
according to cycles. Let

be the cycle that contains the element
h+1
(which corresponds to
\rho

\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right]^{2}=\Tr\left[M^{|c_{h+1}|}\rho\right]^{2}\prod_{c\neq c_{h+1}}\Tr[M^{|c|}]^{2}.

We use the Lagrange’s identity, which is a sum-of-squares proof for Cauch-Schwarz. For all complex numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
,

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|a_{i}|^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|b_{i}|^{2}\right)-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\overline{b_{i}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}|a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i}|^{2}.

(16)

For all integers
k
,

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.40623pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.40623pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[12.40623pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2k}$}}}\hskip-12.40623pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.40623pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{\Tr\left[M^{2k}\right]\leq\Tr[M^{2}]^{k}\right\}.

Therefore,

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{29.48941pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-29.48941pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[29.48941pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2|c_{h+1}|}$}}}\hskip-29.48941pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[29.48941pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{\Tr\left[M^{|c_{h+1}|}\rho\right]^{2}\leq\Tr\left[M^{2|c_{h+1}|}\right]\Tr[\rho^{2}]\leq\Tr\left[M^{2|c_{h+1}|}\right]\leq\Tr[M^{2}]^{|c_{h+1}|}\right\}.

In the penultimate step we used that
\Tr[\rho^{2}]\leq 1
. In addition, for
|c|\geq 3

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{16.30981pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-16.30981pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[16.30981pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2|c|}$}}}\hskip-16.30981pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[16.30981pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{\Tr\left[M^{|c|}\right]^{2}\leq\Tr\left[M^{2|c|-2}\right]\Tr[M^{2}]\leq\Tr[M^{2}]^{|c|}\right\}.

For

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{16.30981pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-16.30981pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[16.30981pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2|c|}$}}}\hskip-16.30981pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[16.30981pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{Tr[M^{|c|}]\leq\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2}\right\}.

Combining all the parts using multiplication rule of SoS proof system, we have

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{43.61838pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-43.61838pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[43.61838pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2\sum_{c}|c|=2h}$}}}\hskip-43.61838pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[43.61838pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{\Tr\left[P_{\pi}(M^{\otimes h}\otimes\rho)\right]^{2}\leq(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h}\right\}.

Using (
15
) and the addition rule,

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{12.66782pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-12.66782pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[12.66782pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2h}$}}}\hskip-12.66782pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[12.66782pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M}$}}}$}}}\left\{(d+1)^{h}\mathbb{E}_{v\sim{\mathcal{D}}(\rho)}\left[\,\langle v|M|v\rangle^{h}\,\right]^{2}\leq((h+1)!)^{2}(\Tr[M^{2}]+\Tr[M]^{2})^{h}\right\}.

Applying

proves the theorem with

∎

(Certifiable hypercontractivity under sampling)

Lemma 5.10

(From

6
Robust state testing algorithm

The algorithm idea is to first randomly sample a basis measurement and apply discrete distribution testing to the measurement outcomes. If the testing algorithm is adversarially robust, then so would be our quantum state testing algorithm.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
ASZ21
, Theorem 2]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{q}
be a known distribution over a domain of size
k
. Given
n

\gamma
-corrupted samples
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
from a discrete distribution
\mathbf{p}
, there exists an algorithm

RobustDistrTesting
(
\mathbf{x},\gamma,\mathbf{q}
) that can test whether
\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}{\rVert}_{1}>\varepsilon
or
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{q}
with probability at least 0.9 and an accuracy of

\varepsilon={O\left(\frac{k^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n}}+\gamma+\sqrt{\frac{k\gamma}{n}}+\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{\gamma}\right)}.

We now describe the robust quantum state testing algorithm in
Algorithm

3
.

Algorithm 3

Input
:
n

\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
,

{\rho_{0}}

\gamma
.

Output

YES
if
\rho={\rho_{0}}

NO

{\left\|\rho-{\rho_{0}}\right\|}_{1}

U=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}]
.

Apply basis measurement
\mathcal{M}_{U}=\{|{u_{i}}\rangle\langle{u_{i}}|\}_{i=1}^{d}
to all copies.

Let
\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}
be the outcome distribution measuring

(resp.
\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}

Obtain
\gamma

RobustDistrTesting
(
\mathbf{x},\gamma,\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}
)

Theorem 6.2
.

Under
\gamma

n

\rho
,

can test if
\rho={\rho_{0}}

The key is to argue that when

Formally, we have the following lemma,

There exists a constant universal

. When

d

, with

\gamma

6.1
Proof of

We prove

is sampled uniformly from the Haar measure, and

is the corresponding basis measurement. When the state is

The idea is to use Hölder’s inequality,

\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{1}\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{\infty}.

Thus, it suffices to lower bound the

norm and upper bound the infinity norm. The lower bound on

norm was proven in prior works
[
CLO22
,
LA24a
]
.

Lemma 6.4
.

For
d\geq 200
, with probability at least 0.95 over a Haar random unitary
U
,

\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{2}\geq 0.07\frac{{\left\|\rho-{\rho_{0}}\right\|}_{\text{HS}}}{\sqrt{d}}.

We refer the readers to
[
CLO22
, Lemma 6.4]
or
[
LA24a

Next we upper bound the

\ell_{\infty}

norms and then choose the

that minimizes the upper bound.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\Delta=\rho-{\rho_{0}}
and
U
sampled uniformly from the Haar measure. For all even integers

,

\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\,\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{p}^{p}\,\right]\leq\frac{d}{2}\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{p}.

Proof.

Note that for
x=1,\ldots,d

\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}(x)-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}(x)=\langle u_{x}|\rho-{\rho_{0}}|u_{x}\rangle=\langle u_{x}|\Delta|u_{x}\rangle.

Thus using (
7

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\,(\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}(x)-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}(x))^{p}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\,\langle u_{x}|\Delta|u_{x}\rangle^{p}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{p}}\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}(\pi)}\Tr[\Delta^{|c|}]

Since
\Tr[\Delta=0]
, the product would be 0 if
\pi
has a cycle of size 1. Thus, we only have to sum over all
\pi
that is a derangement, i.e., there does not exist

such that

. For these permutations, all cycles must have length at least 2, and thus, by the monotinicity of Schatten norms,

would be

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\,(\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}(x)-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}(x))^{p}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\pi\text{ derangement}}\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}(\pi)}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{|c|}

\displaystyle=\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\pi\text{ derangement}}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{p}

\displaystyle=\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}\frac{1}{2}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{p}

In the final step, we used the fact that the number of derangements in

. By Markov’s inequality,

\Pr_{U}\left[\,\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{p}^{p}\geq 10d\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{-1}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}^{p}\,\right]\leq 0.05.

Thus, with probability at least 0.95,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{p}_{\rho}^{U}-\mathbf{p}_{{\rho_{0}}}^{U}{\rVert}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(10d)^{1/p}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}}{\binom{d+p-1}{p}^{1/p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p(10d)^{1/p}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}}{d+p-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\ln d\cdot 1.5e}{d}{\left\|\Delta\right\|}_{\text{HS}}.

The penultimate step follows by
{n\choose k}\geq(n/k)^{k}
. The final step is because
d^{1/\ln d}=e
and
d\geq 200
. Thus, we prove the lemma.
∎
