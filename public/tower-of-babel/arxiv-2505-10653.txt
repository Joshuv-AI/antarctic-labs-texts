On the Evaluation of Engineering Artificial General Intelligence
Sandeep Neema, Susmit Jha, Adam Nagel, Ethan Lew, Chandrasekar Sureshkumar, Aleksa Gordic, Chase Shimmin, Hieu Nguygen, Paul Eremenko

Abstract
We discuss the challenges and propose a framework for evaluating engineering artificial general intelligence ( eAGI ) agents. We consider eAGI as a specialization of artificial general intelligence (AGI), deemed capable of addressing a broad range of problems in the engineering of physical systems and associated controllers. We exclude software engineering for a tractable scoping of eAGI and expect dedicated software engineering AI agents to address the software implementation challenges. Similar to human engineers, eAGI agents should possess a unique blend of background knowledge (recall and retrieve) of facts and methods, demonstrate familiarity with tools and processes, exhibit deep understanding of industrial components and well-known design families, and be able to engage in creative problem solving (analyze and synthesize), transferring ideas acquired in one context to another . Given this broad mandate, evaluating and qualifying the performance of eAGI agents is a challenge in itself and, arguably, a critical enabler to developing eAGI agents. In this paper, we address this challenge by proposing an extensible evaluation framework that specializes and grounds Bloom’s taxonomy - a framework for evaluating human learning that has also been recently used for evaluating LLMs - in an engineering design context. Our proposed framework advances the state of the art in benchmarking and evaluation of AI agents in terms of the following: (a) developing a rich taxonomy of evaluation questions spanning from methodological knowledge to real-world design problems; (b) motivating a pluggable evaluation framework that can evaluate not only textual responses but also evaluate structured design artifacts such as CAD models and SysML models; and (c) outlining an automatable procedure to customize the evaluation benchmark to different engineering contexts.



1
Introduction

Engineering the physical world remains among the most cognitively and technically demanding human endeavors. Designing aircraft, electric vehicles, energy systems, robots, or climate control infrastructure requires not only a deep understanding of physical laws but also the ability to model, analyze, and optimize systems that are tightly coupled across disciplines. Unlike software engineering, where modularity and abstraction have enabled powerful automation, physical systems engineering remains largely manual, iterative, and expertise-driven. Engineers must reason across thermal, structural, electrical, and control domains; often with incomplete data, uncertain constraints, and evolving design goals.
To mitigate this complexity, the industry has embraced tools such as
Computer-Aided Engineering (CAE) and Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) frameworks. These tools simulate physical behavior, guide architecture decisions, and improve verification workflows. Yet their adoption is limited by steep learning curves and reliance on domain-specific heuristics. Even with these tools, design iteration remains time-consuming and error-prone, especially when novel architectures or trade-offs must be explored.

Recent advances in AI and deep learning, particularly in Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic AI frameworks, offer promising avenues to reduce this burden. Early applications of LLMs include code generation for simulation scripts, language interfaces for CAE tools, and documentation assistants. However, these tools essentially serve as procedural copilots. They improve interaction efficiency but do not exhibit the kind of domain reasoning, multi-physics modeling, or design iteration that is necessary for true engineering collaboration.

To realize transformative gains, role of AI in engineering must evolve beyond interface-level assistance and become an active, reasoning partner in the design process. This evolution requires a new class of AI agents –
eAGI
agents – that can engage in creative problem-solving, simulate physical systems, evaluate trade-offs, and produce artifacts such as structured designs, models, or control strategies.
eAGI
agents are not just retrieval systems; they must be capable of reasoning with constraints, synthesizing cross-domain knowledge, and adapting learned principles to novel problems.

A central challenge in developing such systems is the absence of robust, structured, and context-aware methods for evaluating engineering intelligence. Unlike general NLP benchmarks or vision tasks with fixed outputs, engineering problems are context dependent and often have no single “correct” answer. Performance must be assessed across different levels of reasoning and creativity, taking into account how well an
eAGI
agent understands the design context, satisfies system constraints, and explores the solution space across multiple domains of physics and engineering.

Engineering Artificial General Intelligence (
eAGI
)

eAGI
aims at
cognitive automation of what a human engineer does, that is,

eAGI
is a specialization of AGI designed to perform expert-level reasoning, analysis, and creative synthesis for physical systems engineering. Similar to human engineers,
eAGI
agents must combine factual and methodological knowledge with tool fluency, domain-specific modeling intuition, and adaptive, iterative, reasoning-guided design thinking.
An
eAGI
agent should exhibit the following characteristics:

•

Background knowledge and recall: It should retrieve relevant engineering laws, formulas, heuristics, and design rules when prompted.

•

Process and tool familiarity: It should understand workflows in CAE and MBSE environments and be able to interface with simulation pipelines or design models. It should be aware of available tools, understand their limitations, configuration requirements, and constraints, be able to generate valid inputs to these tools, and ingest their output.

•

Contextual understanding: It should recognize components, systems, and recurring patterns from industry-standard designs in a domain, such as heat exchangers or propeller assemblies.

•

Creative and adaptive reasoning: It should explore novel design solutions, adapt knowledge to unfamiliar systems, and optimize performance across competing objectives.

•

Collaborate and communicate: It should participate and collaborate with human engineers in design activities through communication that is natural, domain, and context-aware.

The goal of eAGI is cognitive automation, so we explicitly exclude surrogate modeling and approximating detailed simulation or analysis tools and instead expect eAGI to be able to call such tools or their machine learning accelerated surrogates. We expect eAGI to be able to distill key design drivers from requirements, postulate one or more solutions, and conduct first-order design sizing using tools when needed.
This multifaceted skillset differentiates
eAGI
from both narrow AI applications in engineering (e.g., automated meshing, parametric sweeps) and general-purpose LLMs (use in generative and predictive auto-completion of artifacts). While current LLMs excel at natural language understanding and code generation, they fall short in areas where structured physical modeling, precise constraints, qualitative and quantitative cross-domain causal reasoning is required.

Given the breadth of tasks expected of an
eAGI
agent,
evaluating its capabilities becomes a foundational challenge
. Existing benchmarks either test shallow factual knowledge (e.g., textbook questions) or rely on rigid, domain-specific problems with fixed outputs. Neither of these approaches captures the range of reasoning, modeling, and synthesis skills needed for effective engineering intelligence.
We propose a comprehensive evaluation framework for
eAGI
agents that:

1.

Specializes Bloom’s taxonomy of human learning outcomes for engineering contexts, providing a structured hierarchy of cognitive abilities from factual recall to reflective, cross-domain design.

2.

Introduces a rich taxonomy of evaluation questions—spanning from low-level methods to high-level system synthesis requiring end-to-end problem solving, that can be instantiated across multiple engineering problem domains.

3.

Supports pluggable and adaptive benchmark customization, where evaluations can be dynamically generated and tailored to different systems (e.g., eVTOL propulsion, HVAC design) and physics domains (e.g., thermal, electrical, structural).

Our framework is designed to evolve alongside the growing capabilities of
eAGI
offering a scalable and extensible methodology to quantify engineering competence. It lays the groundwork for systematic development through principled qualification and objective benchmarking of AI agents that aim to collaborate meaningfully in the engineering of the physical world.

AGI and General Intelligence Evaluation.

Evaluating Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) requires metrics that transcend domain-specific skillsets and capture core attributes of generalization, transfer, memory, planning, and agency. Proposed evaluation frameworks for AGI often rely on multi-task performance, real-world decision-making simulations, and generalization under uncertainty
[
13
]
. Cognitive architectures have been tested in lifelong learning scenarios
[
23
]
, where models must adapt and improve over time without forgetting past tasks. Others have proposed meta-evaluation setups, such as ARC-Challenge
[
5
]
tasks, that probe whether a system can reason or decompose long-horizon goals. Beyond standard task performance, AGI evaluation must also consider agency (planning, tool use), robustness (adversarial perturbation), and autonomy (long-term task completion with minimal intervention).
Morris et al.
[
20
]
propose a framework for evaluating progress toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) based on two key axes: performance (depth of capabilities) and generality (breadth across tasks). Analogous to levels of autonomous driving, their “Levels of AGI” taxonomy introduces a matrix that categorizes AI systems from Emerging to Competent to Expert to Virtuoso to Superhuman. The authors argue for operational definitions of AGI that prioritize capabilities over underlying mechanisms. They further distinguish between capability and autonomy, proposing a separate set of autonomy levels ranging from tool, consultant, collaborator, expert, and agent. This framework provides a structured approach to evaluating and benchmarking AGI systems along a continuum, rather than using binary thresholds.
Despite advances, no widely accepted standard currently exists for AGI evaluation, and many existing benchmarks fail to distinguish between memorized responses and genuinely novel reasoning. By focusing on the engineering domain where artifacts can be objectively evaluated, we present a principled approach to define different levels of
eAGI
and propose an
eAGI
evaluation framework.

Skill Progression Models.
Several cognitive development models have been proposed to describe how humans acquire domain expertise over time, particularly in complex professions like engineering, medicine, and law. Among these, the Dreyfus Model of Skill Acquisition
[
10
]
is one of the most widely cited, outlining a five-stage continuum from Novice to Expert, where practitioners shift from rule-based responses to context-sensitive, intuitive action. Similarly, models such as the Benner Model
[
1
]
(adapted from Dreyfus for clinical nursing), the Rasmussen Skill-Rule-Knowledge (SRK) framework
[
22
]
, and the Berliner Model
[
2
]
in pedagogy all emphasize the transition from declarative knowledge to procedural and tacit knowledge, relying heavily on experiential learning and situated practice. These models are effective for evaluating human professionals, but present limitations when applied to LLMs or agentic systems: they assume continuous exposure and feedback loops.
In contrast, Bloom’s Taxonomy
[
3
]
provides a hierarchical, task-oriented structure that is far more amenable to evaluating AI systems.
Unlike the Dreyfus model and its variants, which require subjective evaluation of behavior over time, Bloom’s framework lends itself to quantitative, benchmarkable assessments.
Therefore, for the purpose of evaluating
eAGI
Bloom’s Taxonomy offers a more practical, granular, and scalable foundation, enabling the construction of benchmarks that both reflect domain-relevant challenges and allow for comparisons across models and systems.

Bloom’s Taxonomy for Hierarchical Evaluation.

A promising approach to evaluating LLMs, particularly in specialized or safety-critical domains, draws from educational psychology frameworks such as Bloom’s Taxonomy. Originally designed to classify educational goals, Bloom’s Taxonomy has been adapted to measure LLM capabilities across cognitive levels, including knowledge recall, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation
[
3
]
. Recent benchmarks, such as SciKnowEval
[
27
]
and LawBench
[
11
]
have implicitly or explicitly incorporated hierarchical evaluation frameworks. For instance, SciKnowEval assesses memory, reasoning, and application skills in science domains; LawBench categorizes legal tasks into memorization, comprehension, and application. This hierarchical perspective enables more granular insights into model competence and aligns evaluation with human cognitive development. Adapting Bloom’s framework to engineering intelligence opens avenues for more structured assessments - ranging from accurate recall of facts and methods to the design and optimization of complex systems under constraints.

4
Engineering Cognition Levels Using Bloom’s Taxonomy

Engineering design (Figure
1
) is the process of translating requirements and constraints into realizable physical systems. This process involves selecting and configuring components and defining their topology, that is, how components are connected logically and arranged in space and function within the context of a system-level objective.
Engineering problems are often underspecified and open ended such that they do not have a single correct solution. Instead, they span a rich space of possible configurations, each governed by various qualitative and quantitative constraints.
These constraints can range from fundamental physical laws (like energy conservation or structural mechanics) and performance targets (such as thrust, efficiency, or reliability) to discrete choices (like selecting components and preferred reuse of existing subsystems), spatial arrangements (e.g., heat exchanger layouts, wiring harness routing), manufacturing limitations (e.g., tolerances), and adherence to design standards and regulations (including safety). The core challenge lies in creating a design that meets all requirements and performance objectives. This design must then be assessed for its feasibility, robustness, alignment with human and organizational preferences, and optimality under both real-world and simulated scenarios.

We identify three major dimensions to measure the complexity of engineering problems.

1.

Directionality:
The first dimension relates to whether the problem requires a forward analysis to evaluate performance and conformance to requirements, or whether it needs inverse reasoning and synthesis from requirements to design. Since multiple designs are feasible for the same set of requirements and most engineering evaluation tools only provide the forward evaluation of a design, the inverse direction of the synthesis is typically ill-posed and more challenging.

2.

Design Behavior:
The second dimension is the level of details of the behavior of the engineering design. Typical designs are inherently multiphysics, and design evaluation involves running dedicated tools for specialized tasks such as electrical analysis, thermal analysis, computational fluid dynamics, etc. Whether this analysis is focused on the static aspect of the design, such as structure, or whether it focuses on its dynamic behavior significantly changes the complexity of the engineering tasks. Typically, dynamic behavioral analysis is more challenging than static analysis of designs.

3.

Design Scope:
The third dimension is whether the synthesis or evaluation problem is open world or closed world. In the simpler closed-world setting, the space of design decisions is bounded; for example, the design could be partially specified as a template with some remaining details to be filled out or an existing design needs to be updated to meet a regulatory standard. Similarly, for evaluation and analysis tasks, the open-world setting would require the engineer to identify all the relevant performance metrics for a problem. For example, the vibration analysis of a design might depend on material choice, and an experienced engineer can identify what analysis is needed for a design. The open-world design and analysis problems are naturally more difficult than the bounded design and evaluation.

Level 1 (Remember) - Accurate Recall of Factual Information.
At this basic level, an
eAGI
agent must demonstrate reliable access to factual, domain-specific knowledge. This includes recalling definitions, equations, and engineering principles, accessing properties of standard components from model libraries, and citing regulatory standards or design codes. Tasks at this level resemble look-up or Q&A behavior but form the basis for more complex cognition. Accuracy and consistency are key metrics for evaluating the responses here.

Level 2 (Understand) - Semantic Understanding of Designs.
This level assesses the AI’s ability to interpret and explain the structure and function of a given design. Core capabilities include identifying components in a schematic or model, understanding the spatial and functional topology of components within a system, and demonstrating an understanding of causal behavior between a design choice and observed performance. This level ensures that the model does not just recognize parts but understands their roles, interactions, and design intent.

Level 3 (Apply) – Design Evaluation and Operational Reasoning.
An
eAGI
agent at this level is expected to apply learned engineering principles to assess or manipulate designs. Capabilities include predicting the performance of new or unseen designs, making component substitutions while maintaining system feasibility, and invoking external tools such as solvers, simulators, or parametric models to evaluate or update design properties.
These tasks require the integration of reasoning, simulation outputs, and procedural operations - a significant leap from static understanding.

Level 4 (Analyze) – Design In-filling and Error Diagnosis.
At this level, the system goes beyond evaluation to autonomously diagnose and resolve design issues. Typical tasks include completing partially specified designs using appropriate domain rules and constraints; detecting inconsistencies, errors, or violations in a given design; and proposing viable corrections or workarounds. This level embodies inverse reasoning from observed failures or incomplete designs back to underlying causes, mirroring human debugging in engineering workflows.

Level 5 (Create) – Design Synthesis from Requirements.
This level involves the creative synthesis of new designs, especially under novel or shifted constraints. It includes generating full system designs from a given specification, adapting existing designs to new performance goals or regulatory requirements, and exploring the design space to approach or improve upon the Pareto frontier for trade-offs like performance vs. cost or weight vs. strength.
This level requires balancing competing objectives and leveraging domain knowledge in a generative form beyond pattern completion or memorized design templates.

Level 6 (Reflection and Abstraction) – Meta-Reasoning and Critique.
At the highest level, an
eAGI
agent exhibits reflective and meta-cognitive abilities, including
critiquing design decisions,
inferring systemic implications,
deriving and explaining general principles from multiple specific cases, and identifying situations where existing tools may be insufficient.
eAGI
can recognize its own uncertainty, limitations, or biases in design evaluation or synthesis.
This level reflects expert-level design reasoning, including judgment, abstraction, and the ability to explain and defend decisions - hallmarks of expert human engineers.

These levels can be located along the three dimensions for measuring the complexity of engineering problems identified earlier. With respect to forward (analysis) vs reverse (synthesis), Levels 1-3 operate predominantly in the forward direction of evaluating known designs, and the higher levels (4–6) involve inverse reasoning.
Level 1 is focused on testing universal knowledge and does not entail queries about any specific design. Level 2 is limited to questions that can be answered from the static description of the design. The higher levels involve reasoning over static and dynamic behaviors of the design. The scope of the problem is closed-world for Levels 1-4 wherein the space of designs and the requirements are given. At Level 5, the synthesis problems have a given set of requirements, the design space is open-world, but subject to reuse or bounded modification constraints. Level 6 is the most competent cognitive level of
eAGI
which considers an open scope, where design objectives may be underspecified, constraints may conflict, and new performance metrics must be inferred or prioritized.

5
Evaluation Coverage, Completeness, and Sufficiency

The engineering cognition taxonomy discussed above provides an effective framing for organizing evaluation queries, however, a robust evaluation framework for
eAGI
must demonstrate comprehensive
coverage
across the full breadth of engineering domains,
completeness
in assessing the depth of cognitive capabilities, and
sufficiency
in ensuring that each evaluation scenario tests meaningful and domain-relevant competencies.

To achieve this, we complement the cognitive taxonomy discussed above with a secondary taxonomy that includes aspects of engineering domain coverage.
This secondary layer includes structured metadata tagging along physics-based and engineering-specific dimensions. These tags provide essential contextual and domain-specific information that augments the primary cognitive taxonomy and enables fine-grained filtering, targeted benchmarking, and adaptive evaluation generation. Each tag class supports modularity, domain transfer, and traceability across problem types. Key dimensions include:

•

System Type
: Specifies the application or system context within which the engineering problem is situated. This ensures real-world relevance and contextual grounding. Common types include:

–

eVTOL systems
: Electric propulsion, rotorcraft dynamics, flight control

–

HVAC systems
: Split vs. packaged systems, refrigerant flow, compressor staging

–

Aerospace/Spacecraft
: Radiators, attitude control, orbital mechanics

–

Energy Systems
: Microgrids, battery packs, solar integration

–

Robotics/Mechatronics
: Actuator sizing, kinematic control, onboard computing

Tagging by system type enables evaluation generation tailored to specific industries or product verticals.

•

Design Scope
: Captures the granularity or scope of the design or analysis task, enabling assessments at different abstraction levels:

–

Component-level
: Selecting or modeling individual parts like motors, heat exchangers, or valves

–

Subsystem-level
: Designing integrated modules like propulsion units, HVAC loops, or powertrains

–

System-level
: End-to-end design tasks involving architecture selection, performance trade-offs, and inter-domain reasoning

This stratification supports progressive evaluation complexity and modular benchmarking.

•

Domain
: Refers to the underlying physical or engineering science involved in the question. Examples include:

–

Thermal
: Heat transfer, cooling loops, thermodynamic cycles

–

Electrical
: Circuits, motors, battery models, signal integrity

–

Control
: Feedback loops, PID tuning, stability margins

–

Structural
: Stress-strain analysis, beam loading, factor of safety

–

Fluid/Airflow
: Propeller flow dynamics, duct pressure drops, fan selection

These tags allow evaluation sets to be curated by domain, ensuring a diverse and representative distribution across physical modeling spaces.

•

Modeling Requirements
: Indicates the analytical or simulation models needed to solve or validate the problem. This helps calibrate
eAGI
agents capabilities to physics reasoning depth:

–

Steady-state models
: Static conditions, fixed boundary values (e.g., airflow in a duct at a fixed RPM)

–

Transient models
: Time-varying behaviors (e.g., startup thermal lag, motor ramp-up)

–

Linear vs. Nonlinear models
: Governing equation complexity (e.g., Ohm’s Law vs. Navier-Stokes)

–

Multiphysics models
: Coupled domains (e.g., electro-thermal effects in battery systems)

This enables evaluations to be generated based on solver or simulator capabilities of a given
eAGI
system.

•

Applicable Standards
: Refers to engineering norms, certification guidelines, or industry regulations that may constrain or guide the design. This ensures practical realism and regulatory awareness. Common examples include:

–

AHRI (HVAC)
: Component certification and energy efficiency rating

–

ASME (Mechanical Systems)
: Pressure vessel design, code compliance

–

UL (Electrical)
: Fire safety, insulation, current limits

–

MIL-STD (Defense Systems)
: Shock resistance, environmental qualification

Standards tagging allows problem constraints to reflect real-world feasibility and industry-aligned requirements.

These metadata tags serve not only as classification tools but also as dynamic
control parameters
for adaptive test generation pipelines. By incorporating them into the generation and filtering logic, the system can synthesize and tailor evaluation questions based on domain-specific constraints, cognitive difficulty, or model fidelity requirements. This capability is essential for constructing benchmarks that reflect real-world challenges faced by engineering professionals and allow
eAGI
systems to be assessed in a principled, scalable manner.
For example, the metadata tags enable:

•

Targeted Sampling
: Queries such as
“generate 10 transient thermal problems for HVAC subsystems”
or
“produce control-system questions focused on drones with nonlinear dynamics”
can be executed by filtering across domain, system type, modeling requirement, and cognitive level.

•

Stratified Evaluation
: Evaluation sets can be balanced or stress-tested by enforcing controlled distributions across tags. For instance, a diagnostic benchmark may intentionally mix linear and nonlinear cases, or alternate between subsystem and full-system tasks to expose weaknesses in generalization.

•

Curriculum-Aligned Progression
: Evaluation sequences can be scaffolded to align with an
eAGI
’s developmental milestones or a human educational curriculum. A curriculum designer may start with steady-state component-level problems in the electrical domain (e.g.,
“compute resistance of a given circuit”
) and progressively introduce transient, multi-domain, and system-level design tasks (e.g.,
“optimize the energy consumption of a hybrid HVAC loop with thermal and control interactions”
).

This fine-grained controllability transforms the metadata layer into a powerful lever for shaping the evaluation landscape - not just retroactively tagging problems, but actively guiding their generation to match learning objectives, domain focus, or benchmarking goals.
This dual taxonomy – combining cognitive levels and domain-specific metadata – enables
exhaustive yet modular
question generation across a wide variety of engineering domains. Evaluations can be flexibly assembled, diversified, or scaled to meet specific objectives. For example, to benchmark a subsystem-level control strategy for HVAC under transient thermal loads, the evaluation will focus on relevant Apply- or Analyze-level questions filtered by tags such as
[HVAC, Subsystem, Thermal, Transient, Control]
.

Together, the integration of the primary (cognitive) taxonomy and the secondary (domain and metadata) tagging framework ensures that evaluation quality is upheld along three critical dimensions:

•

Coverage
spans the full breadth of engineering domains, system types, physics phenomena, and modeling regimes. This guarantees that evaluations are not biased toward a particular field (e.g., electrical vs. thermal) or abstraction level (e.g., component vs. system), and instead reflect the interdisciplinary nature of real-world engineering.

•

Completeness
ensures that the full cognitive spectrum, from factual recall and conceptual understanding to synthesis, diagnosis, and meta-reasoning, is systematically assessed. Every cognitive level is addressed across a diverse set of contexts, enabling evaluation of both isolated skills and integrative reasoning.

•

Sufficiency
is achieved by grounding each question in realistic engineering scenarios, supported by domain-specific constraints, physics-based models, and industry standards. Evaluation tasks are not merely academic, but they are structured to reflect challenges that would be encountered by human engineers in practical design, analysis, or troubleshooting situations.

By distributing these three qualities – coverage, completeness, and sufficiency – across cognitive levels, domain contexts, task types, modeling complexity, and design granularity, this framework supports the systematic construction of rigorous, balanced, and extensible evaluation banks. These evaluations can adapt to the evolving capabilities of
eAGI
systems and provide meaningful benchmarks for reasoning, generalization, and innovation across engineering disciplines.

6
Application to Propeller-Motor Matching Problem

To ground our approach to evaluating
eAGI
, we chose an electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft as a representative example. This choice is deliberate: the problem’s multi-physics nature and the complex dependencies of design decisions create a realistic and nuanced scenario well-suited for
eAGI
evaluation. Additionally, the general familiarity and broad accessibility of drone domain knowledge mean that even readers without specific expertise can readily grasp the evaluation queries, and facilitate a comparative analysis of performance across human engineers, general purpose LLMs, and customized LLMs.

Specifically, for this discussion we limit our scope to a propellor-motor sizing and matching problem for an evTOL aircraft. This design task involves selecting an appropriate combination of electric motor and propeller such that the overall propulsion system can generate sufficient thrust to lift and maneuver the vehicle, while maintaining efficiency, weight, thermal compliance, and structural robustness.
The design challenge spans multiple physical domains:

•

Electrical domain: Converting battery power to motor torque (motor voltage, current, Kv rating).

•

Mechanical domain: Transmitting torque as angular rotation to the propeller.

•

Aerodynamic domain: Thrust as a function of propeller pitch, airfoil geometry, diameter, and RPM.

The typical requirements might include hover and cruise thrust levels, battery and motor constraints (e.g., voltage, current), thermal limits, physical design space, and redundancy constraints.
Addressing these requirements involves understanding of a tractable but realistic multiphysics system, requires both static and dynamic behavior reasoning capabilities, and exploration over both open and closed-world design spaces. We present examples of question-answer aligned with each
eAGI
level with explanation about the corresponding cognitive level in this section. Levels 2-6 require a design and/or requirements with respect to which understanding, applying, analyzing designs are performed.

It’s important to understand that while we’ve used textual responses to expected questions in this document for ease of reading, real-world applications would generate structured artifacts and design representations compatible with engineering tools like CAD, SysML, and Modelica. Evaluating these outputs would then necessitate syntactic and semantic checks using engineering software. Accordingly, the evaluation framework need to be be highly pluggable and customizable, but a detailed discussion of these aspects is beyond the scope of this current explanation.

We use the following specific example design problem for questions and answers. We present one example for each cognition level below and provide more examples in Appendix
A
.

7
Automated Evaluation and Alignment with Human Expertise

A central challenge in design of an evaluation framework for evaluating
eAGI
agents is balancing
automation of scoring
with the need for
human-level understanding
in higher-order tasks. The framework needs to enable scalable, structured evaluation aligned with expert judgment, while automating as much of the evaluation process as possible. We consider these challenges and approaches in context of the cognition taxonomy discussed earlier.

Levels 1 to 3
(
Remember, Understand, Apply
) are largely objectively scorable. Responses can be evaluated through symbolic math solvers, reasoning engines, lookup tables, knowledge bases, and domain-specific simulation models (e.g., computing thrust or efficiency).
These levels typically involve deterministic or well-constrained answers, making full automation feasible.

Level 4
(
Analyze
) introduces diagnostic reasoning. Here, responses may include fault localization (e.g., “identify the broken component”) and design fixes (e.g., “suggest a redesign to resolve the issue”). While localization may yield multiple correct answers, the proposed fix can be validated by patching the system and simulating its behavior to confirm resolution.

Level 5
(
Create
) involves open-ended design synthesis. Automated scoring here focuses on partial correctness and feasibility, such as
verifying that design constraints are satisfied (e.g., power, weight, voltage), ensuring physical consistency across components (e.g., torque-speed compatibility), and comparing solutions against reference architectures or simulation baselines.
Simulation-based validation or optimization metrics (e.g., Pareto dominance) are used to assess creativity and effectiveness.

Level 6
(
Reflect
)
At Level 6, an
eAGI
demonstrates meta-cognitive capabilities by reflecting on its own reasoning processes, knowledge boundaries, modeling assumptions, and the limitations of its outputs. Rather than just evaluating the quality of designs or making trade-offs, the agent critically examines the soundness, reliability, and completeness of its own decisions and design workflows.
This level focuses on the agent’s capability to
identify gaps or flaws in its prior reasoning or simulations,
recognize when additional data, expertise, or validation is needed,
evaluate whether it has used appropriate methods and whether results are valid under non-ideal or out-of-distribution conditions, and
articulate the limitations of its knowledge, tools, or models.
These often require human-in-the-loop scoring to assess the open-world reasoning.
However, partial automation is viable through heuristics such as
scoring argument consistency with design goals, and augmenting LLMs used as a judge with additional information such as the expected knowledge or reasoning gaps in the eAGI.

By aligning scoring methods with the cognitive complexity of evaluation tasks, our proposed framework establishes a principled and scalable mechanism for assessing
eAGI
performance. Each tier of cognitive difficulty is matched with an appropriate evaluation strategy, ensuring that the assessment process remains both rigorous and computationally tractable. Specifically, the evaluation uses the following:

•

Objective scoring
is prioritized for lower cognitive levels (e.g.,
Remember
,
Understand
, and
Apply
). These tasks often involve deterministic answers, such as recalling equations, computing values, or applying rules, which can be graded automatically using symbolic evaluation, lookup tables, or numeric simulation. This enables fast, repeatable assessments at scale without requiring human involvement.

•

Simulation-augmented heuristics
are employed at intermediate levels (e.g.,
Analyze
and
Create
), where responses include failure localization, subsystem redesign, or design synthesis. While these answers may not be uniquely defined, their soundness can be verified by simulating the impact of the proposed fix or design. Partial credit can be awarded based on constraint satisfaction, consistency across subsystems, or proximity to optimal reference solutions.

•

Expert-in-the-loop review and agent as a judge
is reserved for high-level cognitive tasks (e.g.,
Reflect
) that require nuanced judgment, such as evaluating trade-offs, critiquing alternative designs, or reasoning about uncertainty and system limitations. These tasks often depend on implicit domain knowledge or creativity, where correctness is not easily reducible to a numeric score. In such cases, human evaluators provide qualitative assessment or validation based on domain expertise, and LLM agents have been recently demonstrated to effectively surrogate human evaluators in some settings
[
29
]
.

This tiered scoring framework enables automated, scalable, and repeatable benchmarking of
eAGI
capabilities.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we have discussed a novel evaluation framework for
eAGI
grounded in Bloom’s taxonomy and tailored to the complexities of engineering design. We introduced a six-level cognitive hierarchy specifically adapted for engineering tasks and demonstrated how it enables structured, scalable, and cognitively aligned question generation.

Our proposed approach combines reusable question templates, domain-specific design contexts, and physics-aware metadata tagging to generate diverse and rigorous evaluation queries. By anchoring assessments in real-world engineering reasoning from basic recall to advanced design synthesis and reflective critique, our framework enables principled benchmarking of
eAGI
agents across a wide range of physical systems and engineering domains.

This taxonomy-driven methodology not only ensures broad coverage and deep cognitive progression but also supports automated and semi-automated scoring through symbolic evaluation, simulation-based validation, expert-in-the-loop review, LLM-as-a-judge
[
28
]
, and Agent-as-a-judge
[
29
]
. Ultimately, this work provides a foundation for the systematic development, benchmarking, and comparison of AI systems capable of collaborating in complex engineering workflows.

While our framework offers a structured path toward scalable and cognitively grounded evaluation, several limitations remain, particularly at the higher end of the cognitive spectrum. These challenges point to key opportunities for future research:

•

Automation bottlenecks at advanced cognitive levels: Tasks involving open-ended design, innovation, or trade-off reasoning (e.g., Levels 5 and 6) still require expert judgment. Although simulation and constraint-checking can partially validate responses, fully automating the scoring of creativity, novelty, or rationale remains an unsolved challenge. LLM-as-a-judge and agent-as-a-judge partially address this challenge, but their effectiveness in the evaluation of
eAGI
has not been demonstrated yet.

•

Absence of common evaluative frameworks for subjective reasoning: Reflective responses (e.g., “Which design is better and why?”) lack shared semantic representations, making it difficult to ensure that automated scoring aligns with expert intuition or domain-specific reasoning practices. Learning reward models to capture human preferences can partially address this challenge.

•

Limited traceability of multi-step reasoning processes:
eAGI
responses to complex design tasks often involve multiple implicit reasoning steps. Without interpretable intermediate traces, it is difficult to assess whether solutions stem from principled reasoning or surface-level heuristics.

•

Sensitivity to domain-specific constraints and complex dynamics: Certain domains, such as structural reliability or control stability, exhibit highly nonlinear behaviors that complicate automated evaluation. Robust scoring in these contexts will require tighter coupling with high-fidelity simulation environments and formal verification techniques.

Improving automation, explainability, and fidelity in these areas will be essential for enabling robust, continuously improving benchmarks. As
eAGI
approach expert-level reasoning, the evaluation infrastructure must evolve to not only track their progress but also challenge them with the same rigor expected in professional engineering practice.

1.

Platform Dynamics and Mission Profile:

This simplification ignored how efficiency varies with dynamic factors such as changes in angle of attack, inflow velocity, RPM transients, and air density. In reality, propeller efficiency decreases significantly during unsteady conditions like rapid throttle changes or deceleration phases.

Steady-State Thrust Estimation:
My model was based on static or quasi-static thrust coefficients without accounting for unsteady aerodynamics. This means the effects of blade vortex interaction, delayed flow separation, or wake interference during acceleration or descent were not captured. I did not simulate the variation in inflow velocity at the propeller disc during vertical climb or descent. In these conditions, especially during descent (where propeller blades may enter their own wake—known as the vortex ring state), actual thrust production can drop significantly, which was not reflected in my simplified model.

4.

My model did not include aerodynamic feedback from the airframe or its influence on the local flow field around the propeller. In practice, airframe-induced turbulence or flow deflection can reduce effective thrust, particularly in complex maneuvers.
