Assessment of knowledge and behavioral risk factors of reproductive tract infections among married women of 15–49 years in rural areas of Haldwani block, Uttarakhand
Authors: Neha Verma, Sadhana Awasthi, Kumar Pankaj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:One of the most serious public health issues in India is reproductive tract infections (RTIs), which have a profound effect on the lives of men and women, as well as their families and communities.Objectives:To describe the sociodemographic traits, assess women’s RTI knowledge, and determine which behavioral risk factors are involved in female RTI.Methods:Using a 30-cluster sampling approach, a cross-sectional study design was employed to examine RTIs in 30 villages located in the Haldwani block of the district Nainital.Results:It was shown that just 7.6% of women knew enough about RTIs. Among the 450 women surveyed, the percentage of RTI cases was significantly lower for those who used the barrier method during menstruation (7.7%), had more than three children (25.7%), had a trained person deliver their baby in a hospital (24.1%), used condoms as a family planning method (5.6%), used sanitary pads during menstruation (25.6%), and maintained good personal hygiene (22.4%).Conclusion:The study revealed that the participants’ understanding of RTI was lacking.

Introduction

Reproductive health, as outlined by the World Health Organization (WHO), is a “condition of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, rather than only the absence of illness or disability in any area related to the reproductive system and its functions and processes”.[1]

Reproductive tract infections (RTIs) and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), sometimes known as STIs or RTIs, are major public health issues in India and are typically viewed as a “silent” pandemic that has a severe effect on individuals, families, and communities.[2]

RTIs are exceedingly widespread in resource-poor contexts like India, and they can have disastrous repercussions for the health and social well-being of women and their children.[3] RTIs are more common in women than in men because they are harder to diagnose and less likely to seek medical assistance.[4] When going through normal life events like menstruation, pregnancy, or delivery, women in the reproductive age group are more likely to contract RTI. In India, RTIs account for one-third of all deaths related to reproduction.[5] Untreated RTI can lead to a higher risk of HIV infection, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, persistent pelvic discomfort, and even death.[6]

Worldwide, STIs have a significant influence on sexual and reproductive health, according to the WHO. Every day, almost a million people get STIs. According to estimates, 357 million new cases of one of the four STIs—trichomoniasis (143 million), gonorrhea (78 million), syphilis (5.6 million), and chlamydia (131 million)—occur each year. The genital herpes virus (HSV) affects more than 500 million individuals worldwide. One of the most prevalent STIs, human papillomavirus, affects around 290 million women worldwide at any given moment.[7] About 37,269 cases of syphilis and 74,390 cases of gonorrhea were recorded in India in 2014.[8]

A global conference on the theme of “Prevent and reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and reproductive tract infections (RTIs), including HIV/AIDS, and provide treatment for STIs and their complications, such as infertility with special attention to increasing the ability of girls and women to protect themselves” was held in Cairo, Egypt, in September 1994.[9] The Indian government’s Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) Program was introduced on October 15, 1997, as a response to this meeting.[10]

Maternal and child health (MDGs 4 and 5) and HIV/AIDS prevention (MDG 6) were two areas where international strategies to achieve the Millennium Development Goals relied heavily on STI/RTI control.[11] Today, this strategy is still crucial for achieving SDG 5.6 and Goal 3 of the Sustainable Development Goals 5.[12]

The incidence of RTIs varies greatly across India, according to several studies conducted in different regions. These variations can be attributed to variances in sociodemographic profiles, access to healthcare, economic position, and cultural taboos. Furthermore, our study was designed to shed light on the sociodemographic profile, knowledge, and behavioral risk factors of RTIs among married reproductive women, as we have not been able to access any relevant research conducted in this area in the Kumaon region to date.

Aim

To assess the knowledge and behavioral risk factors of RTIs among married women of 15–49 years in rural areas of Haldwani block, Uttarakhand.

Objectives

To describe the sociodemographic traits, assess women’s RTI knowledge, and determine which behavioral risk factors are involved in female RTI.

Materials and Methods

The current investigation was a year-long, community-based cross-sectional study carried out in Uttarakhand state’s Haldwani block of the district Nainital from September 2016 to August 2017 [Figure 1].[13]

Map of Nainital district, Western Himalayas, India

Study subjects

The study’s participants were married women in the Haldwani block of the district Nainital in the state of Uttarakhand, aged 15–49. Excluded from the study were women who could not be reached after two visits, did not want to participate, had missed periods during the study, were widowed, divorced, separated, had undergone a hysterectomy, had a history of drug allergies, had recently taken antibiotics, had bleeding vaginally, and had systemic disease.

Sample size

Thirty-cluster sampling was used as the sample technique. The formula for an infinite population [n = 4 pq/d2] was used to determine the sample size. A permitted error of 5% and a prevalence of 23% of RTIs were assumed.[2] After applying a design effect of 1.5 and adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 450.

Sampling technique

Using the village as the main sampling unit, a two-stage sampling approach was employed. The tehsil provided a list of 252 villages or clusters that make up the Haldwani block. By employing the lottery process, 30 villages or clusters were selected at random. Simple random selection was used to choose 15 homes from each village or cluster. Every chosen household’s reproductive female member was interviewed. If the home included many eligible women, one of them was chosen at random using the lottery procedure. If the needed female could not be located on the initial visit, two more visits were conducted.

Data collection

After obtaining the participants’ informed verbal consent, data were gathered using a pretested, semi-structured, and predesigned questionnaire that covered topics such as sociodemographic profile, menstrual history, women’s menstrual and personal hygiene practices, obstetric history, and knowledge about RTI.

The assessment of women’s RTI knowledge was conducted utilizing five different factors. Women were considered to have adequate knowledge if their score was 4–5 and poor if their score was 0–1. In our study, each variable received one mark if the response was affirmative and zero if it was not.

The assessment of personal hygiene level was conducted using five factors. Women’s personal hygiene was considered good if their score was 4–5 and bad if it was 0–1. In our study, each variable received one mark if the response was affirmative and zero if it was not.

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel was used to code and input the quantitative data that were collected using a predesigned, pretested, semi-structured questionnaire. SPSS version 21 was used for analysis, and percentages were used to interpret the data in a descriptive manner. To determine the significance of the relationship between RTI and other factors, the chi square test was employed. The WHO’s Training Modules for the syndromic treatment of STIs[14] provided the definitions of syndromes.

Ethical clearance

Prior to beginning the study, institutional ethical committee approval was acquired from Government Medical College, Haldwani.

Discussion

Among women in the reproductive age range, RTIs are a prevalent yet often ignored health issue. A ”Culture of Silence” that prevails causes women to experience reproductive morbidities for extended periods of time, according to several research done worldwide, particularly in Asia.

The average age of the research participants was determined to be 30.1 ± 7.9 years. According to the current research, 32.2% of people in the reproductive age group had RTI overall which was similar to the study done by Yadav A et al.[15]

In line with the research by Sreelatha CY et al.,[16] we found that the age range of 15–29 years had a substantially higher prevalence of RTI, at 42.3%, followed by the 30–49 year group at 22.6%.

In this research, uneducated women had a considerably higher frequency of RTI (40.4%). The prevalence of illiterate women was reported to be 47.1%, 40.9%, 72.2%, 57.9%, and 35.7% in studies by Rasheed N et al.,[17] Sreelatha CY et al.,[16] Sharma S et al.,[18] Thekdi KP et al.,[19] Singh A et al.,[20] and Mani G et al.,[21] respectively. Studies have continuously demonstrated a high correlation between women’s education and improved reproductive health. Compared to uneducated women, educated women make far better use of healthcare services.

Compared to women living in nuclear families, who had a rate of 27.4% RTI, women living in joint families had 42.2% RTI. This might be a result of the influence that joint family homes have on women’s behavior and decision-making, particularly in regard to their own health. The prevalence of RTI in joint families was reported by Rasheed N et al.,[17] Saluja N et al.,[22] and Thekdi KP et al.[19] to be 37%, 56.2%, and 60.75%, respectively.

After examining how cases were distributed according to socioeconomic class, it was shown that social class V had the highest frequency of RTI (75%), followed by class IV with 37.6%. We may draw the conclusion that keeping a healthy life and lifestyle is highly dependent on one’s socioeconomic standing. It is evident from the prevalence’s negative relationship with socioeconomic class that living in better circumstances reduces one’s risk of infection.

When it came to their knowledge of RTIs, 78.6% of the women in our survey said yes, and their scores varied from 0 to 5. A mere 7.6% have “adequate knowledge” of RTIs. No woman had “adequate knowledge” regarding RTI, according to the study by GK Ratnaprabha et al.[23]; instead, 69% of women had “some knowledge” and 31% had “no knowledge.”

It was noted that women who engaged in sexual activity during their menstruation experienced higher rates of RTI (37.2%) than those who did not (31.7%). Kafle P et al.[24] had previously published a similar finding.

According to our research, women whose most recent birth was handled by an unskilled individual had a greater rate of RTI (39.3%). The results are in line with the research conducted by Sreelatha CY et al.[16] This might be the consequence of unsanitary delivery procedures, which frequently lead to illnesses and a lack of awareness of appropriate behavior.

In our study, women who used the barrier technique had a reduced prevalence of RTI (5.6%), while those who used the IUCD had a considerably greater frequency (57.8%). This supports the widely accepted theory that protection against RTI may be achieved by the use of contraceptive techniques such as condoms. Poor follow-up care following insertion and inadequate sterile conditions might be the cause of the increasing prevalence of Copper-T use in women.

Within our study, the percentage of women who used handmade cloth pads (42.4%) during menstruation had a substantially higher RTI (P < 0.05) compared to the 25.6% of women who used sanitary pads. Similar findings had been reported by Saluja N et al.,[22] Mani G et al.,[21] and Sreelatha CY et al.[16] in their research.

Women with inadequate personal hygiene had a considerably greater frequency of RTI (53.5%). A correlation between RTI and inadequate personal hygiene was also noted by Saluja N et al.,[22] Singh S et al.,[25] and Mani G et al.[21]

Conclusion

This study demonstrated a significant frequency of RTIs based on history and examination as well as little participant understanding of RTI. Both the inability to identify and treat the current instances properly as well as the inability to stop new cases from occurring might be the causes of this high burden. People who are not well-informed about RTIs and how to prevent them are more vulnerable to contracting the disease, and those who do not seek treatment effectively could prolong the infection’s duration. Raising public awareness and implementing curative measures like efficient, all-encompassing therapy are two potential strategies to lessen the health system’s failure in these areas.

Limitations

The diagnosis of RTIs/STIs is not confirmed by per speculum/per vaginal examination or laboratory studies due to impracticality and resource scarcity. However, we employed the usual syndromic case definition approach, which is a WHO-approved methodology for managing and diagnosing RTI cases. Laboratory studies and per speculum/per vaginal examination could be incorporated in later studies.

Relevance of the study

The study provides a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and needs of this population. The insights gained from such a study can drive effective public health strategies, improve healthcare delivery, and ultimately lead to better health outcomes for women in rural communities.
