Perceptions of parents regarding psychiatric medication use in children presenting to a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic in Karabük, Türkiye
Authors: Bari Ay, Umut Balatacı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParental attitudes toward psychotropic medication substantially influence treatment acceptance and adherence in child and adolescent psychiatry. Yet, data on these perceptions remain limited in Türkiye. The aim of this study is to assess parents’ perceptions and attitudes toward psychiatric medication use for their children presenting to a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic in Karabük, Türkiye.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of Karabük Training and Research Hospital in Karabük, Türkiye. Between April and June 2025, 170 parents of children aged 1–18 years presenting for their first psychiatric evaluation and meeting the inclusion criteria (first-time attendance and consent to participate) completed a sociodemographic form and a 15-item Psychotropic Medication Perception Questionnaire developed by the investigators. Children were evaluated with the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children – Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL). Categorical responses were compared by parental education level, history of psychiatric consultation, and personal history of psychiatric medication use using Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact test.ResultsMean parental age was 39.8 ± 8.55 years; 74.1% were mothers. 59% of parents held at least a high-school diploma. Higher education was significantly associated with correct identification of psychotropic drug classes (Item 1: p < 0.001), belief that higher doses are not necessarily more effective (Item 6: p = 0.026), and recognition that long-term use can prevent relapse (Item 15: p = 0.047). Parents who had previously consulted a psychiatrist (41.2%) demonstrated significantly less agreement that medications merely sedate (Item 2: p = 0.001), greater belief in their therapeutic efficacy (Item 4: p = 0.046), and less fear of side effects (Item 8: p = 0.004). Similarly, parents with prior personal use of psychiatric medication (33.5%) showed reduced concern about sedative effects (Item 2: p = 0.009), higher confidence in mechanism of action (Item 3: p = 0.044), and stronger trust in prescriber objectivity (Item 5: p < 0.001).ConclusionsParental education level, prior psychiatric consultation, and personal experience with psychotropic medications each play a key role in shaping accurate, trustful attitudes toward child psychiatric pharmacotherapy. Incorporating these factors into family-centered psychoeducation may enhance treatment engagement and outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07653-y.

Background

In recent years, there has been a marked increase in the diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric disorders among children and adolescents [1]. Mental health problems common in this age group—such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), Anxiety Disorders (ADs), and Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)—can severely impact developmental trajectories and social adjustment [2]. In this context, psychotropic medications are frequently chosen to control symptoms and enhance functioning [3]. However, because childhood and adolescence are periods of ongoing neurodevelopment, pharmacotherapy in these populations demands careful ethical and clinical consideration [4].

The limited number of controlled studies supporting psychopharmacological treatments in this age group compared to adults poses challenges for clinicians in the decision-making process [5]. Moreover, factors such as the long-term effects of medications, side effect profiles, parental perceptions of medication use, and children’s adherence to treatment are among the key variables influencing psychotropic drug use [6]. Nevertheless, numerous studies have shown that pharmacotherapy—particularly in diagnoses such as ADHD, MDD, and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)—has beneficial effects on behavioral, academic, and social functioning [7, 8]. For all these reasons, addressing the use of psychiatric medications in children and adolescents through a comprehensive lens that includes scientific, ethical, and sociocultural dimensions is of great importance for both clinical practice and health policy.

Parental attitudes and perceptions play a decisive role in the decision-making process regarding psychiatric medication use in children and adolescents. While many parents acknowledge the potential benefits of medication, they also express concerns about its long-term effects on their child’s developing brain and possible side effects [9]. Social stigma surrounding psychiatric disorders—particularly in cases like ADHD and MDD—further heightens parental hesitations toward pharmacological treatment [10]. Some families perceive medication as a way to “sedate” the child or “suppress their personality,” whereas others view it as a tool to improve the child’s functional capacity [11]. Parents’ knowledge about psychiatric drugs, their trust in the physician, and cultural beliefs are among the key factors shaping these perspectives [12].

Prior research has assessed parents’ knowledge and attitudes toward psychotropic medications using structured questionnaires in outpatient child psychiatry settings. For example, Lazaratou et al. developed a 20-item parent survey on opinions, knowledge, and attitudes regarding children’s psychotropic medication; subsequent work in different regions likewise used questionnaire-based approaches to examine parental acceptance, perceived risks/benefits, and contextual factors influencing decisions [13]. More recent studies have explored these themes in Oman and Turkey, and reviews highlight parental factors as central to pediatric medication adherence [14, 15]. Building on this literature, the present Psychotropic Medication Perception Form integrates knowledge (e.g., medication-class identification) with attitudinal domains (e.g., preventive benefits, sedation misconceptions, trust in prescribing) tailored to our clinical context.

Therefore, in child and adolescent psychiatry, effective treatment planning requires open communication with families, provision of scientific information, and empathetic handling of their concerns. In Türkiye, a prior study by Dikeç et al. used a descriptive phenomenological qualitative design in Manisa: semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with adolescents aged 12–18 years who had DSM-5 diagnoses and had been using psychotropic medication for ≥ 3 months, together with at least one parent. Using Colaizzi’s method, the authors identified themes on perceived benefits of medication alongside adherence barriers such as side-effect concerns, forgetfulness, “not feeling like oneself,” and fears of dependence [15]. By contrast, our study is a quantitative, cross-sectional investigation of 170 parents attending their child’s first visit in Karabük; the sample was not limited to families in which the child was already on medication. Diagnoses were established at the index visit with the K-SADS, and parental knowledge–attitude domains were assessed using a 15-item Psychotropic Medication Perception Form developed by the investigators. This study is an original investigation conducted in the province of Karabük, aiming to assess parental perceptions regarding psychiatric medication use for their children.

Methods

Study design

Ethical approval

for this study was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Karabük University (Decision No: 2025/2199, Date of Approval: 16/04/2025). All participating parents/legal guardians provided written informed consent. Children were not research participants; only limited, de-identified clinical information (e.g., age, sex, diagnostic category) was abstracted from medical records in accordance with the approved protocol; therefore, assent was not applicable. The study was designed as a cross-sectional research project and conducted at the Department of Child and Adolescent Mental Health and Diseases of Karabük Training and Research Hospital. Parents of children presenting for the first time to the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic were included in the study. Data collection began in April 2025, and participants were enrolled between April and June 2025. A total of 177 individuals participated in the study. However, 7 participants were excluded due to incomplete forms, and the final analysis was conducted with 170 participants. We did not systematically track contemporaneous media coverage; parental media exposure during April–June 2025 was not quantified. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Our sampling frame comprised all parents/legal guardians attending the first visit, regardless of whether psychotropic medication was indicated at that encounter, to capture baseline perceptions present at the point of initial engagement. Participants were biological/adoptive parents or legal guardians of children aged 1–18 years presenting for a first visit to the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic. Eligibility required decisional (mental) capacity of the parent, operationalized as the ability to (i) understand and paraphrase—via brief teach-back—the study purpose, procedures, risks/benefits, confidentiality, and voluntariness; (ii) appreciate the personal implications of participation; (iii) reason about participation; and (iv) communicate a stable choice. Capacity was verified with a short consent-comprehension checklist and orientation to person, place, and time. One parent per family was enrolled, and all participants provided written informed consent. Children were not enrolled as research participants; therefore, assent was not sought. Only limited, de-identified clinical information about the child (e.g., age, sex, diagnostic category) was abstracted per protocol.

Exclusions pertained to the parent (the study participant), not the child, and included: acute intoxication or delirium; disorientation; documented severe cognitive impairment or neurocognitive disorder; active psychosis/mania interfering with consent; insufficient Turkish proficiency to complete study instruments; or uncorrected sensory deficits precluding questionnaire completion. Parents declining consent were excluded. The child’s intellectual functioning or diagnosis did not by itself constitute an exclusion; however, visits requiring urgent clinical intervention that prevented questionnaire completion were deferred.

Diagnostic ascertainment

Diagnoses counted as ‘psychiatric disorders’ comprised DSM-5-TR categories: neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., ADHD, autism spectrum disorder), depressive disorders, bipolar and related disorders, anxiety disorders (including separation anxiety and generalized anxiety), obsessive–compulsive and related disorders, trauma- and stressor-related disorders (including PTSD and adjustment disorder), disruptive/impulse-control/conduct disorders (e.g., ODD, conduct disorder), tic disorders, elimination disorders, somatic symptom and related disorders, and sleep–wake disorders. Only presentations meeting full DSM-5-TR criteria at intake were coded as a diagnosis.

DSM-5-TR diagnoses were established at the index K-SADS interview. We counted as ‘psychiatric disorder’ only full-threshold diagnoses at intake. Presentations limited to subthreshold symptoms, rule-out/provisional impressions, or V/Z-code conditions (e.g., academic or parent–child relational problems, situational stress reactions without cross-setting impairment) were coded as ‘no diagnosis at intake’ and referred for monitoring.

Study tools

Schedule for affective disorders and schizophrenia for School-Age Children – Present and lifetime version (K-SADS-PL)

The K-SADS-PL was adapted and finalized by Kaufman et al. (1997) [16] based on the earlier K-SADS-P developed by Chambers et al. (1984) [17] for use in evaluating psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents and in research settings. This semi-structured interview is administered to both children and their parents and includes five diagnostic supplements covering affective disorders, psychotic disorders, anxiety disorders, behavioral disorders, substance use disorders, and other conditions. It also incorporates sub-diagnostic supplements and a general assessment scale for children. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Gökler et al. in 2004 [18].

Sociodemographic data form

This form, specifically prepared for the study, evaluates data regarding the participants and their children who applied to the clinic, including age, gender, family structure, educational status, and psychiatric history.

Psychotropic Medication Perception Form

The 15-item Psychotropic Medication Perception Form was developed by the researchers to assess parents’ knowledge and attitudes regarding psychotropic medication use in children and adolescents. Item generation was based on a comprehensive review of the relevant literature and previously published parent questionnaires on pediatric psychotropic medication knowledge and attitudes, supplemented with items tailored to our clinical context (e.g., recognition of medication classes, perceived preventive benefits, sedation-related misconceptions, and trust in prescribing). Content validity, clarity, and comprehensibility were evaluated by an expert panel consisting of three child and adolescent psychiatrists and one psychiatric nurse, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. A pilot test was then conducted with 12 parents attending their first visit at the same clinic to assess clarity, acceptability, completion time, and item flow. Minor linguistic revisions were made based on feedback, but no items were removed. The final form consists of 15 statements rated on a three-option scale (Yes / No / Undecided). Negatively worded items are reverse-coded so that higher total scores indicate more accurate and favorable perceptions of psychotropic medication use. In the present sample, the internal consistency of the total scale was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.81), and item–total correlation coefficients ranged from 0.24 to 0.58, supporting the homogeneity and reliability of the measure for use in this clinical context.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Pearson’s Chi-Square and Fisher’s Exact Test were used to compare categorical variables between groups. As specified a priori, analyses were descriptive and bivariate, summarizing central tendency and dispersion and examining simple associations. Given the ordinal nature of attitude variables and sample size considerations, no multivariable or multivariate models were fitted. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Among the parents included in the study, 126 were mothers (74.1%), 37 were fathers (21.8%), and 7 (4.1%) were other relatives. The mean age of parents was 39.82 ± 8.55 years (range: 22–70). Regarding educational status, 69 parents (40.6%) had education below high school level, while 101 (59.4%) had completed high school or higher.

In terms of perceived income level, 79 participants (46.5%) reported it as low, 55 (32.4%) as moderate, and 36 (21.2%) as high. The majority of participants (n = 151, 88.8%) resided in urban areas, while 19 (11.2%) lived in rural settings.

Forty-three parents (25.3%) reported regular use of a prescribed medication for any reason, whereas 127 (74.7%) stated that they were not on any regular medication.

Seventy parents (41.2%) had previously consulted a psychologist or psychiatrist at least once, while 100 (58.8%) had never done so. Additionally, 57 parents (33.5%) had used a psychiatric medication in the past for any reason, whereas 113 (66.5%) had never used psychiatric medication.

The responses given by participants to the questionnaire items were analyzed in relation to their sociodemographic characteristics. Specifically, the impact of variables such as participants’ education level, the presence of regular medication use, prior visits to a psychiatrist or psychologist, previous use of psychiatric medication, and the presence of psychiatric medication history in siblings of the child were examined in relation to questionnaire responses.

The associations between parental characteristics and perceptions regarding psychiatric medication use are summarized in Table 3. Parents with at least a high school education were more likely to correctly recognize that psychiatric drugs are classified into distinct categories (Item 1), to reject the belief that higher doses are necessarily more effective (Item 6), and to agree that long-term use may help prevent recurrence in some cases (Item 15) (p < 0.001, p = 0.026, and p = 0.047, respectively).

Previous contact with mental health services was associated with more favorable perceptions: parents with such contact were less likely to endorse the belief that psychiatric drugs only sedate and do not treat (Item 2), more likely to view these medications as therapeutic (Item 4), less likely to believe that psychiatrists prescribe unnecessarily high doses (Item 5), reported less fear of psychotropic drugs (Item 8), were less likely to think that early use inevitably leads to later dependence (Item 10), and were less likely to report being generally against medication use (Item 13).

Similarly, parents with a history of psychiatric medication use themselves showed more positive and informed attitudes. They were less likely to agree that psychiatric drugs only sedate (Item 2) or that early use causes later dependence (Item 10), more likely to acknowledge that these medications correct abnormal brain mechanisms (Item 3), less likely to believe that psychiatrists prescribe unnecessarily high doses (Item 5), and reported less fear of psychotropic drugs (Item 8).

No statistically significant associations were observed between perceptions and regular use of non-psychotropic medications or parental gender; therefore, detailed tables for these comparisons are not presented.

Discussion

This study was conducted in Karabük province to investigate the perceptions and attitudes of parents who visited Child and Adolescent Psychiatry outpatient clinics for their children’s treatment regarding the use of psychiatric medications. To our knowledge, there is no similar study in our country in terms of design.

Before interpreting attitudinal correlates, we note that at the index K-SADS assessment only 35.9% met full DSM-5-TR criteria. The modest point prevalence of diagnosed disorders in this referral cohort is consistent with an index-visit sampling frame, stringent full-threshold coding, and a high proportion of context-bound or subthreshold presentations seen in first assessments. Longitudinal follow-up often clarifies trajectories, and some ‘no diagnosis at intake’ cases may meet full criteria over time.

When the educational levels of the participating parents were analyzed in relation to their responses to the questionnaire, it was found that those with high school education or higher had more knowledge about subtypes of psychiatric drugs, believed that these medications should be prescribed according to the child’s age or the type of disorder, and that properly dosed and timed psychiatric drug use could be effective in treatment and prevent recurrence. This finding suggests that as education level increases, so does the level of knowledge about psychiatric drugs. In a study measuring parental attitudes toward psychiatric illnesses and psychotropic medication use, it was concluded that education level was significant—participants with higher education believed more strongly in completing the recommended duration of psychiatric drug treatment for its effectiveness and prevention of relapse compared to those with lower education [19]. Another study investigating parental perceptions of psychiatric medication use in children with psychiatric disorders also found that higher education levels were associated with more positive attitudes and more accurate responses, with the difference being statistically significant [13]. Some researchers have examined the influence of parental education on adherence to prescribed psychiatric medications for their children. In a study conducted in Oman, it was found that lower parental education levels were associated with a higher tendency to pursue alternative treatments for psychiatric conditions, which in turn delayed or prevented appropriate psychiatric care for their children [14].

In our study, whether having a chronic medical condition requiring regular non-psychiatric medication use affected parental attitudes toward psychiatric drugs was investigated. The statistical analysis showed that regular non-psychiatric medication use had no significant impact on their perceptions of psychiatric medication.

We also examined whether previous psychiatric consultations influenced parents’ attitudes toward psychiatric medication. Seventy participants had previously consulted a psychiatrist, while 100 had not. Parents who had previously seen a psychiatrist showed greater trust in the therapeutic efficacy of psychiatric drugs, believed that psychiatrists prescribed medications objectively and only when medically necessary, and were less fearful of psychiatric medications than those who had never consulted a psychiatrist. These differences were statistically significant. There is limited literature on this topic. One study found that mothers who had previously consulted a psychiatrist or had a close relative who had done so demonstrated better adherence to psychiatric medication for their children [20].

We further investigated the impact of parents’ personal psychiatric medication history on their perceptions of psychiatric drugs. Data from 57 parents with a history of psychiatric medication use were compared with those from 113 parents without such history. Those who had used psychiatric medications themselves showed greater confidence in the therapeutic properties of psychiatric drugs, more strongly believed that these medications target biological mechanisms underlying mental disorders, and had more trust in their psychiatrists’ prescribing practices. Conversely, parents with no history of psychiatric drug use expressed more fear of psychiatric medications compared to other drugs and were more likely to believe that early use could lead to dependency later in life. These group differences were statistically significant. These results suggest that prior positive experiences with psychiatric drugs may reduce stigma and negative perceptions. One study found that parents with previous experience using psychiatric medications themselves were more likely to approve of initiating such treatment for their children [21]. Another study highlighted that parental experiences shaped their views—if a parent had a positive experience with psychotropic drugs, they were more likely to accept medication for their child [22].

This study extends prior work [15] by examining baseline perceptions at the index visit among all parents, not only those whose children are already on or imminently eligible for medication. In doing so, it complements qualitative reports of families already engaged in pharmacotherapy and highlights locally salient misconceptions that can be addressed through early psychoeducation in routine care. Parental attitudes toward psychiatric medications play a critical role in treatment adherence and engagement in the child’s therapeutic process. Their beliefs are shaped by various factors, including knowledge, prior experience, cultural values, and environmental influences. While some parents see medication as a necessary and beneficial intervention, others resist due to concerns about side effects or misconceptions about dependency. Social stigma, in particular, contributes to hesitancy around psychiatric drug use. Thus, providing accurate scientific information, fostering collaborative communication between parents and psychiatrists, and involving parents actively in the treatment process are vital to ensuring medication efficacy.

General Turkish-language reporting during the study window (e.g., antidepressant utilization trends, drug availability, consumer interaction cautions) may have influenced baseline perceptions; however, we did not measure individual exposure [23, 24].

This study has several limitations. Not using a standardized and validated tool specifically designed to assess parental perceptions of psychiatric drugs is a limitation that may affect data interpretation. The relatively small sample size also limits generalizability. Future studies should include larger samples to better explore parental knowledge and concerns regarding psychotropic drugs. The single-center design limits the evaluation of regional differences in parental attitudes. Multicenter studies are recommended for broader and more accurate insights. Because media exposure was not assessed, unmeasured confounding by contemporaneous reporting cannot be excluded. We did not implement multivariate multiple regression to jointly model attitude outcomes. This decision reflected our prespecified analytic scope, the ordinal scaling of outcomes, and sample-to-parameter considerations. Future adequately powered studies designed for multivariate hypothesis testing should evaluate these relationships within a multivariate framework. A key limitation is that the survey instrument’s psychometric properties were not formally established in this study (e.g., factor structure, internal consistency, test–retest). As such, findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating; future research should undertake a full validation in larger, multi-site samples. We did not restrict the sample to parents of youth for whom psychotropic medication was indicated at the index visit. Future longitudinal studies should examine whether baseline attitudes predict subsequent prescribing decisions and adherence among medication-eligible cases.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of 170 parents attending a child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic, we found that higher parental education, prior psychiatric consultation, and personal experience with psychotropic medications each markedly improved parents’ understanding of, trust in, and willingness to adhere to their children’s psychiatric pharmacotherapy. Specifically, parents with education beyond high school more accurately identified medication classes and recognized the preventive benefits of long-term treatment; parents who had previously seen a psychiatrist were less likely to view medications as mere sedatives and more likely to trust their efficacy; and those with a history of personal psychotropic use reported greater confidence in medication effects and in how prescribing decisions are made. Conversely, lack of experience or consultation was associated with greater fear of side effects, concerns about dependency, and skepticism toward therapeutic benefits.

These findings underscore the critical need for targeted, family-centered psychoeducation—particularly for parents with lower education levels or no prior exposure to psychiatric care—to address misconceptions, reduce stigma, and foster collaborative decision-making. Integrating structured informational interventions into routine outpatient services may enhance treatment acceptance, adherence, and ultimately child outcomes. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to confirm these associations and to develop validated tools for assessing parental perceptions of pediatric psychopharmacology.
