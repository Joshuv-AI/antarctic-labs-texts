Removal or inhibition of transglutaminase 2 decreases cellular stress, supporting tissue preservation, and recovery after SCI
Authors: Mousumi Ghosh, Omar Elwardany, Xiaoqi Pan, Shannon Jacqueline Saigh, Damien D Pearse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransglutaminase 2 (TG2) is a multifunctional enzyme widely implicated in neuroinflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling after central nervous system (CNS) injury and diseases. In spinal cord injury (SCI), TG2 contributes significantly to pathology through ECM remodeling and glial scar formation. Here, we explored the intracellular roles of TG2 following severe spinal cord compression injury, utilizing TG2 knockout (KO) mice and pharmacological inhibition via cystamine. Genetic deletion or inhibition of TG2 significantly improved locomotor function and promoted enhanced preservation of white and gray matter post-injury. Mechanistically, TG2 KO reduced fibronectin deposition, chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan accumulation, and reactive gliosis. Importantly, imaging mass cytometry revealed that TG2 deletion attenuated cellular stress responses, including reductions in ER stress markers (GRP78), oxidative stress mediators (pNrf2), mitochondrial dysfunction indicators (BNIP3 and cytochrome c), and increased neuronal expression of survival-associated (pREL-NFκB). These results highlight a novel intracellular function for TG2 in exacerbating neurodegeneration through modulation of cellular stress pathways, offering promising therapeutic implications for SCI recovery.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Transglutaminase 2, Glial scar formation, Axonal regeneration, Cellular stress, Locomotor recovery, Neuroprotection, Clip compression injury, Imaging mass cytometry

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) remains a major unmet clinical challenge worldwide, often resulting in permanent neurological deficits and a reduced quality of life for patients [1, 2]. In the acute phase, mechanical trauma initiates hemorrhage and necrosis, which constitutes the “primary injury” [3, 4]. This is followed by “secondary injury,” involving a cascade of events that includes excitotoxicity, ionic imbalance, inflammation, demyelination, and apoptotic cell death [5]. While considerable progress has been made in acute care management of SCI, the subsequent processes of neuronal loss, tissue degeneration, abortive regeneration and scarring persist without available therapeutics, limiting the potential for restoration of lost function [6]. Accordingly, there is substantial interest in elucidating and modulating the molecular drivers of post‐SCI pathology to improve functional outcomes.

A hallmark of SCI pathology is the formation of glial and fibrotic tissue scarring, composed of astrocytes, microglia, fibroblasts and NG2 cells that produce growth inhibitory extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) and fibronectin (FN) [7–11]. An important enzyme in ECM remodeling is transglutaminase 2 (TG2), which catalyzes calcium‐dependent transamidation reactions [12, 13]. TG2 can crosslink proteins into highly stable complexes, thereby contributing to scar formation through the establishment of ECM that is resistant to degradation [14–16]. TG2 can crosslink both FN and CSPGs and has been shown by our group and others to colocalize with these ECMs in experimental models of Multiple Sclerosis (MS) [11, 17].

The cross-linking activity of TG2 has been a target for therapeutics in many diseases from celiac disease to neurological disorders and aggressive cancers. TG2 is proposed to exist in two distinct conformational states that control its activity: a closed, GTP-bound form and an open, calcium-bound form that actively mediates crosslinking [18]. Further, TG2 is a multifunctional enzyme, possessing also GTPase, isopeptidase, and protein disulfide isomerase activities, underscoring a diverse set of functions in cell signaling [19, 20]. TG2 can additionally act as scaffolding protein with ECM. In experimental MS, TG2 interactions with FN in blood vessels results in aggregations that then act to sequester immune cells at sites of perivascular lesions to play a role in disease pathology [11, 14, 15, 17, 21–28]. The deleterious actions of TG2 have also similarly been identified in neurodegenerative conditions including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Alzheimer’s (AD), Parkinson’s (PD), and Huntington’s (HD) [29–35] as well as in CNS trauma. Recent studies have shown that TG2 inhibition improves tissue preservation and recovery after traumatic or ischemic injuries [22, 36–39]. Further, genetic deletion of TG2 (tg2 KO) has also been employed to investigate its role in systemic and CNS pathologies [40–43].

Although the extracellular effects of TG2 are well characterized, emerging evidence shows that TG2 also translocates to the cytosol and mitochondria after CNS trauma, where it perturbs Ca2+ homoeostasis, drives protein aggregation and opens the mitochondrial permeability-transition pore, thereby linking biomechanical injury to intracellular stress and cell death [44–47]. In work by Elahi et al. (2021), it was demonstrated that selective deletion or genetic ablation of TG2 in astrocytes could enhance motor recovery after moderate contusion SCI by modulating astrocyte reactivity and upregulating genes involved in fatty acid metabolism that provided a protective action for neurons [38]. In peripheral conditions, TG2 KO can also decrease endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial function, partially through blocking the enzyme’s effects on calcium homeostasis and protein aggregation [44–46]. Whether these effects of TG2 ablation on the cell stress response occur in SCI are presently unknown, but any decrease in ER or mitochondrial dysfunction would be predicted to favor neural cell survival [48]. Thus, a critical knowledge gap remains: how do TG2’s dual roles of ECM cross-linking versus intracellular stress modulation synergize to influence the balance between degeneration and repair after SCI? Nevertheless, it is apparent that the molecular mechanisms by which TG2 activities contribute to SCI pathology remain incomplete, prompting further investigation.

In the present investigation, methods of TG2 genetic ablation or inhibition were performed in a severe compression SCI paradigm in mice, demonstrating that improved tissue preservation and function could be achieved when TG2 activities were abrogated. By integrating high-parameter imaging mass cytometry (IMC) as shown in Fig. 1, with conventional histology, we interrogated both ECM remodeling and intracellular stress signatures within the same tissue, thereby providing an unprecedented systems-level view of TG2’s multifaceted contribution to SCI pathogenesis. Ensuing histological and IMC methodologies were employed, revealing not only a profound antagonism of pathogenic ECM remodeling and reversal of axon growth inhibition in line with previous findings [38], but also a marked diminution of key molecular constituents of the cell stress response and the relief of suppressed protective pathways in injured neurons. This study demonstrates for the first time that TG2 knockout significantly reduces cellular stress responses, shifting the perspective on TG2 from being solely involved in astrogliosis and an ECM regulator to a key intracellular modulator of cellular stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, collectively, after SCI, opening new therapeutic avenues for SCI treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult, age matched (4–6 months old) TG2 KO and TG2 intact (TG2 +) mice (n = 10–13 per genotype, inclusive of attrition, Total n = 23) or wildtype C57BL6 mice (n = 6–8 per cohort, total n = 14) with a mix of animals from both sexes (The Jackson Laboratory) were used in this study to test the effects of TG2 inhibitors. Additionally, separate cohorts of C57BL6 mice (n = 3 per group) were used specifically to determine TG2 expression in the spinal cord under uninjured control conditions, as well as at 1 day and 1 week post- SCI. Animals were housed according to the National Institute of Health’s guide for the Care and Use of Animals, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Miami approved all animal procedures.

Generation of TG2 knockout (TG2 KO) mice and genotyping

Homozygous TG2 knockout mice were generated from established in-house breeding colonies backcrossed onto the C57BL/6 background for more than 12 generations. To generate homozygous TG2 KO mice, mice engineered with loxP sites flanking the targeted exon of the TG2 gene (The Jackson Laboratory) were crossed with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of a cmv promoter (The Jackson Laboratory). Genotyping was performed using PCR with allele-specific primers, and heterozygous and homozygous individuals were distinguished via agarose gel electrophoresis. TG2 KO was further confirmed by Western blot and immunohistochemistry, as previously described by our group [17]. All animals were housed under pathogen-free conditions at 22 °C ± 2 °C, with controlled humidity and a 12-h light/dark cycle, and were provided food and water ad libitum.

Induction of spinal cord injury using the clip compression model

In these studies, we employed a microvessel clip-compression injury (CCI) paradigm at the T8 mid-thoracic level. CCI delivers a consistent, sustained mechanical force (10 g for 10 s) that reproduces both the acute contusive impact and the prolonged tissue compression seen in many forms of human injuries (e.g., burst fractures, herniated discs) [49]. Unlike impactor-based spinal cord contusion models which generate a high‐speed strike followed by rapid rebound, clip compression provides a more reproducible degree of cord deformation and a defined period of ischemia, leading to secondary hemorrhage, edema, and necrosis that closely parallels clinical SCI pathology [50–52]. Moreover, the clip model yields a well‐demarcated lesion with robust glial scar formation and demyelination, facilitating histological and molecular comparisons between genotypes or treatment paradigms. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) intraperitoneally. Under aseptic condition and while in the prone position with a soft cylindrical pad under the lower chest and abdomen, a longitudinal dorsal mid-line skin incision (0.8–1 cm in length) was made. Paravertebral muscles were parted by blunt dissection followed by a full laminectomy and removal of parts of the pedicles of two consecutive vertebrae at the mid-thoracic region. The surgical procedure was performed to expose the spinal cord at the level of the 8th thoracic vertebra (T8 level). A micro-dissecting hook was used to gently free any attachment between the ventral dura and the posterior vertebral body at the target level. The microvascular clip with a closing force of 10 g (World Precision Instruments [WPI], Item No. 15911) was applied extradurally in a horizontally oriented manner, where its lower blade was gently passed—from one side to the other—ventral to the cord. Once the intended positions were achieved, the clip was allowed to gradually close inducing ventral and dorsal compression across the spinal cord and kept closed for 10 s before opening it to induce a severe compressive SCI. After completing the injury procedure, paravertebral muscles were approximated then the muscle sheath was sutured using 5–0 vicryl sutures, and the skin was closed with metallic skin clips (Michel clips). Mice were placed on a 37 °C heating pad until they recovered from anesthesia, and urinary bladders were manually expressed twice daily until reflexive voiding returned. Post-operative care, including the administration of antibiotics, analgesics, and fluids, was performed as described elsewhere [53]. Briefly, the mice were allowed to recover in a warmed cage, with readily accessible water and food. Gentamicin (5 mg/kg, intramuscular (i.m.); Abbott Laboratories, North Chicago, IL, USA) was immediately administered post-surgery and then daily, for seven days. The analgesic buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg sub-cutaneous (s.c.); Reckitt Benckiser, Richmond, VA, USA) was delivered post-surgery and repeated daily for three days. Post-SCI monitoring included twice-daily assessments for distress, weight loss (> 20%), dehydration, or self-mutilation, with mice exhibiting these signs excluded from analysis.

Evaluation of cystamine administration on SCI recovery

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice (6–8 per group) underwent severe CCI. Starting one day post-injury, the treatment group received daily intraperitoneal injections of cystamine (100 mg/kg in sterile distilled water) for two weeks. Cystamine, a TG2 inhibitor, targets the Cys230–Cys370–Cys371 redox triad and promotes a disulfide bond between Cys370 and Cys371, allosterically inhibiting TG2 activity [54]. The control group received no cystamine treatment. The dose 100 mg/kg/day of cystamine, for 15 days was selected based on prior dose–response studies in neurodegeneration models that showed that doses of up to 100 mg/kg were efficacious in conferring significant neuroprotective effects, without exhibiting adverse effects in Huntington’s and Parkinsonian mouse models [55, 56].

Functional assessments

To evaluate hind limb motor function after SCI, all animals underwent baseline behavioral evaluations prior to induction of injury. Following the injury, functional tests were administered at specified intervals as described below. All behavioral scoring was carried out by observers who were blinded to the experimental procedures performed on each animal.

Open-field BMS assessment

Behavioral evaluations were carried out by two blinded observers who were not involved in the surgeries or in administering inhibitors. Before the induction of SCI, each animal was pretrained to navigate the open field. Animals were allowed to move freely in the open field during each session. The baseline locomotor score was evaluated just before the surgery (pre-injury), at 24 h post-injury, and once weekly thereafter for a total of 8 weeks until the study endpoint as previously described [57]. Any animal showing a BMS score above 1 on day one was removed from the study. For data analysis, scores from both hind limbs were averaged per animal and locomotor scores were compared between transgenic or drug-vehicle cohorts.

Horizontal ladder test

The horizontal ladder test was performed at 4- and 8-weeks following SCI to evaluate comparative sensorimotor function [58] among mice. Mice were allowed to run on a 1-m metal grid runway with irregularly spaced gaps (0.5–2.5 cm) while their movements were recorded on video. Prior to SCI, all mice underwent a three-day training period. A paw slipping through a gap was recorded as a foot fault error [59, 60]. For each trial, the average number of footfalls was determined based on three trials per mouse. Footfalls were assessed by a blinded observer and quantified as total hindlimb faults averaged per run.

Histological evaluation and stereological analysis of lesion volume and preserved tissue

At 8 weeks post-injury, mice were anesthetized with an overdose of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (15 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally and subsequently transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Spinal cords were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in phosphate buffer for 48 h. A 2-cm segment of spinal cord spanning 10 mm rostral and 10 mm caudal to the injury epicenter was blocked, followed with embedding in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound for tissue sectioning using a cryostat. Spinal tissue was coronally sectioned into 20 series, with each section having a thickness of 20 μm. One series of sections was stained with hematoxylin, eosin, and Luxol fast blue to visualize gray and white matter as conducted previously [61]. These stained sections at 400‑μm intervals were subsequently analyzed for tissue preservation using computer-assisted microscopy and Neurolucida software (version 4.5, Micro Brightfield Bioscience, Colchester, VT, USA) as described previously [62]. Preserved tissue was identified by the absence of immune cell infiltration, intact myelin, and/or healthy-appearing neurons. Individual areas of preserved white and gray matter or lesioned tissue in each section were contoured and measured by a researcher blinded to the identity of the mouse using serial section manager for graphing as absolute or percentage measurements of total cord area for each section location [61].

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to detect and quantify target proteins of interest, as described previously [17, 63]. Cryosectioned tissues mounted on glass slides were washed in PBS, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval (IHC-Tek epitope retrieval solution, IHC World Woodstock, Ellicott City, MD, USA) [11] for 20 min. Sections were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min, followed by blocking in 2% BSA with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. This was followed by incubating the tissue sections overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies details of which are provided in Table 1. The following day, the sections were washed and then incubated with fluorescent-labeled secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher Scientific; Invitrogen) for two hours. After three washes in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, slides were mounted using Prolong Diamond Antifade (P36970, Invitrogen) with micro cover glass (48,404–453, VWR) and stored at 4 °C until image acquisition.

Image analysis

Immunofluorescent images were acquired using a high-resolution confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Fluoview FV 1000) from at least three randomly selected fields within the region of interest (ROI) in stained tissue sections. Pixel intensity was measured under uniform exposure settings. Antibody reactivity for each target protein was assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity, quantified using ImageJ software (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij). For presented images, the image sharpness and tonal range of the Tiff files were normalized (smart sharpen, 0.9 pixels) using Adobe Photoshop CS2 (Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). All profile counts or immunoreactivity measurements were conducted by individuals who were blinded to the experimental procedures performed on each animal. Hematoxylin, Eosin and Luxol fast blue-stained images were acquired with an Olympus BX51 microscope and Meander scan software (Micro Brightfield, Williston, VT).

Cryosectioned spinal cord tissue sections (~ 4800 µm caudal to the injury epicenter) that had retained significant tissue integrity and neuron presence following severe CCI were selected from TG2 intact and KO mice (n = 3 per group). After washing in PBS, the tissue sections underwent heat-mediated antigen retrieval using IHC-Tek solution for 20 min. The sections were subsequently permeabilized (0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, 30 min) followed with blocking in 2% BSA/0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Permeabilized tissue was incubated overnight at room temperature with a cocktail of metal-conjugated primary antibodies specific for microglia/macrophages (Iba1), astrocytes (GFAP), neurons (NeuN), oligodendrocytes (APC), endothelial cells (CD31), in combination with markers of oxidative stress (pNrf2[64], PRDX1[65]), cellular stress (GRP78[66], HSP70[67]), and mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis (CytoC [68]), inflammatory/survival response (immune cell pRel-NFκB[69–71], Bc-3 [72]) as well as other known binding partners of TG2 that could play a role in the induction of cell stress or apoptosis (P53[54], BINP3[73, 74], cleaved caspase 3[75], Bax[76], Noxa[74]), cell survival (Bcl-3[72], neuron pRel-NFκB[77]) as well as other potential interacting partners such as ubiquitin [78]. The following day, antibody treated sections were washed with Maxpar PBS and Milli-Q water and subsequently incubated for 1 h with intercalator-Ir (125 μM, Fluidigm) to label the DNA, then air-dried.

Data acquisition and analysis for IMC was performed using the Standard Biotools Hyperion™ Imaging System (CyTOF®) operated with CyTOF Software v7.1. Spinal cord tissue sections were stained with the panel of metal-tagged antibodies, enabling high-parameter analysis without the spectral overlap limitations of conventional immunohistochemistry. Following staining, selected regions of interest (ROIs), either a 1 µm diameter sized region corresponding to the laser spot or a region encompassing the entirety of the coronally sectioned spinal cord were ablated using a high-power laser. The resulting atom clouds were transferred to the time-of-flight (TOF) chamber, where they were ionized, filtered, and analyzed for metal ion abundance. These data were spatially mapped back to the original coordinates on the slide, generating high-dimensional, multiplexed images.

Post-acquisition, the acquired data files (. mcd format) were visualized using MCD Viewer v1.0.560.6 and exported as multi-page, 16-bit OME-TIFF files for downstream analysis. Image analysis was performed in Visiopharm® v2023.9.4.15595 × 64, an AI-driven platform for high-parameter spatial and phenotypic analysis. Automated nuclear segmentation and single-cell identification were carried out using a pretrained application (APP) within the software, and cell phenotypes were classified using the Phenoplex™ Guided Workflow.

Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. For comparisons involving two groups, an unpaired two-tailed t test was used; when three groups were compared, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Time-dependent data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA, followed by either Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post-hoc tests as appropriate. Behavioral assessments (BBB, and horizontal ladder test) were evaluated with two-way ANOVA and post-hoc Bonferroni correction. Differences were considered significant if the p values were ≤ 0.05.

Results

Robust induction of TG2 in astrocytes and neurons after clip compression spinal cord injury

We induced a severe spinal cord injury in C57BL/6 rodents by applying a vessel clip to the exposed spinal cord at thoracic level 8 (T8) (Fig. 2). A constant pressure was applied to the exposed spinal cord at thoracic level 8 (T8) in C57BL/6 mice using a 10 g microvascular clip for 10 s, resulting in a trauma. Representative operative images before (Fig. 2A–C) and after (Fig. 2E–G) clip-compression injury are shown in Fig. 2. This model allows for a reproducible injury severity to study secondary tissue damage and neuroinflammatory response and to test TG2 targeted therapeutic interventions post-injury, mimicking human SCI pathology, making it clinically relevant.

Following the induction of SCI, immunofluorescence analyses were performed to examine the cellular localization and temporal expression of TG2 in the injured spinal cord. Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, neurons, and microglia were identified using cell-specific markers: GFAP, CC1, NeuN, and Iba1, respectively, and were co-stained with a specific antibody against TG2. In naive controls, GFAP astrocytes exhibited minimal TG2 expression (Fig. 3A, B), however by 24 h post-SCI, astrocytes showed a significant induction of TG2 (Fig. 3C, D) that continued to intensify through 1-week post-injury (Fig. 3E, F). Similarly, APC/CC1 oligodendrocytes showed minimal TG2 expression in naive controls (Fig. 2G, H) with only a modest induction at 24 h or 1-week after SCI (Fig. 3I–L). Basal TG2 in NeuN-positive neurons was also almost undetectable in naive animals (Fig. 3M, N), however a marked induction of TG2 occurred at 24 h that was even more pronounced by 1-week post-SCI (Fig. 3O–R). In Iba1 + microglia, negligible TG2 expression was observed in naive controls (Fig. 3S, T) that only marginally increased at 24 h after SCI but significantly increased by 1-week (Fig. 3U–X). These results collectively indicate that TG2 basal expression is low in all studied neuronal and glial cell types with differential induction after SCI with more robust upregulation in neurons and astrocytes.

TG2 knockout improves motor recovery and tissue preservation after SCI

Motor function after severe thoracic clip-compression SCI was assessed over 8 weeks comparing TG2 intact and TG2 KO mice. SCI was associated with a profound loss of locomotor function in TG2 intact mice as measured using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) open-field score, with recovery plateauing at endpoint and animals showing only extensive ankle movements of the hindlimbs. TG2 KO mice showed a significant improvement in gross locomotor function compared to TG2 intact mice as measured by the BMS from 2 weeks post-SCI until endpoint in comparison (Fig. 4A; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001), with mice reaching a plateau in which plantar paw placement with occasional stepping was exhibited.

Foot placement and positioning were measured using the horizontal ladder test where footfall errors were quantified. At 4 weeks post-SCI both TG2 intact and knockout mice exhibited high error rates, dragging their hindlimbs across the ladder bars. TG2 intact and knockout mice showed some improvement in performance on the test 8 weeks after SCI though no statistically significant differences were observed between groups (Fig. 4B).

Histological analysis revealed that global TG2 knockout had significantly improved tissue integrity after SCI compared to TG2 intact controls. Quantitative evaluation of preserved tissue showed that TG2 KO mice had smaller lesion areas compared to TG2 intact controls throughout the adjacent segments to the injury epicenter rostral and caudal (Fig. 4C; ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). Assessment of white and gray matter preservation indicated that TG2 KO animals retained significantly more white matter tissue both rostral and caudal to the injury epicenter compared to TG2 intact mice (Fig. 4D; **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001) with similar improvements also obtained with the area of preserved gray matter after SCI in TG2 KO mice (Fig. 4E; *p < 0.05, ****p < 0.0001). Representative histological images from an animal in each group showing the extent of SCI rostral and caudal, illustrate visibly improved tissue preservation and reduced lesion size in TG2 KO compared to TG2 intact mice after SCI (Fig. 4F, G).

TG2 knockout attenuates fibronectin and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan deposition following SCI

To evaluate ECM deposition and inflammation after SCI, immunofluorescence staining was performed at 8-weeks post-injury in TG2 intact and KO mice (Fig. 5A–H). Immunoreactivity (IR) for fibronectin (FN), an ECM, and Iba1, to identify innate immune cells, were compared between groups. The robust FN IR in TG2 intact mice after SCI (Fig. 5A, B) in the lesion area was markedly attenuated in TG2 KO mice (Fig. 5C, D). Furthermore, the colocalization of FN (red) with Iba1-positive immune cells (green) observed in the lesion of TG2 intact mice (Fig. 5B) was almost absent in TG2 KO mice after SCI, indicating reduced immune cell-mediated ECM deposition (Fig. 5D). Growth inhibitory chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (CSPG) IR, measured by CS56 antibody immunostaining, exhibited strong deposition in the lesion of TG2 intact mice after SCI (Fig. 5E, F) but was significantly diminished in TG2 KO mice (Fig. 5G, H). Co-labeling with GFAP (astrocytic marker, red) showed significantly greater CSPG colocalization with astrocytic processes in TG2 intact mice after SCI (Fig. 5F–H). Quantification of immunoreactivity (F5g. 5I–J) confirmed these histological observations. Taken together, these results demonstrate that genetic ablation of TG2 significantly attenuates ECM deposition, inflammatory responses, and inhibitory scar formation following SCI, suggesting that TG2 plays a critical role in lesion pathology and glial scar formation after SCI.

To assess axon-dendrite integrity and growth, immunostaining for serotonin (5-HT), mitogen-activated protein-2 (MAP2) and growth-associated protein-43 (GAP-43) was performed and analyzed within the lesion of TG2 intact and KO mice at 8 weeks after SCI (Fig. 6A–F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that genetic KO of TG2 relieves the suppression of axon growth leading to significantly more fiber sprouting of 5HT and GAP-43 axons post-SCI.

Cell-specific expression and spatial distribution of TG2 following SCI

Imaging Mass Cytometry was employed to determine the cell-specific expression and spatial distribution of TG2 in the injured spinal cord two months following SCI. Representative IMC images of spinal cord tissue sections, taken at 4800 µm caudal from the injury epicenter, are shown in Fig. 7 for both TG2 intact and TG2 KO mice. Sections at this distance from the lesion center were selected because they contained a comparable number of preserved neurons in both genotypes, enabling the analysis of signaling molecules regulated by TG2 after SCI. In TG2 intact spinal cord sections, robust TG2 expression was detected throughout the gray matter, as indicated by the pronounced TG2 specific immunostaining (Fig. 7A). The clearly delineated gray matter region, outlined by dashed white lines, displayed intense and relatively uniform distribution of TG2 immunoreactivity. In contrast, TG2 KO sections exhibited a lack of definitive TG2 staining (background fluorescence only) (Fig. 7C), validating the specificity of the TG2 immunostaining approach. Further multiplexed IMC analysis provided insights into the cellular localization of TG2 by simultaneously staining markers specific for neurons (NeuN), oligodendrocytes (APC), astrocytes (GFAP), microglia/macrophages (Iba1), and endothelial cells (CD31) (Fig. 7B, D). Both TG2 intact and TG2 KO tissues demonstrated comparable distributions of these cellular markers at the selected region 4800 µm caudal to the lesion, indicating that the overall cellular composition remained unaltered by TG2 deletion. Quantitative analysis of cell-specific TG2 immunoreactivity (Fig. 7F) revealed robust expression across all analyzed cell types in the injured caudal spinal cord tissue from TG2 + mice, including microglia/macrophages (Iba1), astrocytes (GFAP), oligodendrocytes (APC), neurons (NeuN), and endothelial cells (CD31). Immunoreactivity intensity was consistent across cell populations, suggesting broad involvement of TG2 in various cell types post-injury. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, highlighting the reliability of these observations. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TG2 is robustly and broadly expressed across different cell populations within the injured spinal cord below the level of the lesion, underscoring its potential widespread role in responses to injury and SCI pathophysiology.

TG2 knockout modulates stress, apoptosis, and survival signaling post-SCI

IMC was employed to investigate the impact of TG2 KO on oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, apoptosis, and pro-survival signaling pathways in spinal cord tissue at 2 months following SCI. Representative IMC images were obtained from spinal cord sections located 4.8 mm caudal to the injury epicenter. Evaluation of oxidative and ER stress markers, phosphorylated-Nrf2 (pNrf2), GRP78, and HSP70, revealed significant differences between TG2 intact and TG2 KO animals (Fig. 8A–D). TG2 intact sections after SCI demonstrated robust expression of these stress markers, indicative of ongoing oxidative and ER stress within the injured spinal cord. Conversely, TG2 KO tissues exhibited notably reduced expression, suggesting that TG2 deletion attenuates stress-related signaling pathways. Higher-magnification views (Fig. 8C, D) further confirmed the reduction in these markers within the delineated injured gray matter region. Apoptotic signaling pathways were assessed using BNIP3 (red) and Cytochrome C (CytoC) markers. TG2 intact spinal cord sections (Fig. 8E, G) displayed pronounced expression of both apoptotic markers, whereas expression levels were substantially reduced in TG2 KO sections (Fig. 8F, H). These differences were particularly evident in high-magnification images, highlighting the potential role of TG2 in triggering apoptosis following injury. Further, pro-survival signaling, characterized by pREL-NFκB and apoptotic mediator phosphorylated ERK1/2 (pERK1/2), differed significantly between TG2 intact and TG2 KO samples (Fig. 8I–L). Enhanced expression of these pro-survival mediators was noted in TG2 KO tissues, particularly within the gray matter region, compared to the TG2 intact group. High-magnification images (Fig. 8K, L) illustrate this clearly, suggesting a shift toward prosurvival signaling in the absence of TG2. Conversely, quantitative analysis of target proteins involved in proapoptotic or cell death pathways such as cleaved caspase-3, Bax, Noxa, and P53 or oxidative stress such PRDX1 as well as those associated with cell survival, including Bcl-2 revealed no significant upregulation at the 2-month post-SCI time point, nor any notable differences between TG2 intact and TG2 KO mice, as shown in the box plots (Fig. 8M, N). Similarly, although Ubiquitin was significantly expressed in the injured spinal cords of both TG2 intact and TG2 KO mice, its expression levels did not differ significantly following TG2 removal (Fig. 8M, N). Collectively, these results indicate that deletion of TG2 modulates multiple signaling cascades following SCI, decreasing oxidative and ER stress responses and apoptosis and preventing the disruption of mitochondrial integrity while simultaneously promoting pro-survival and neuroprotective signaling. These findings support targeting TG2 as a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance neuroprotection and recovery after SCI.

To assess how TG2 influences intracellular stress signaling after SCI, IMC analyses were performed on spinal cord sections from TG2 KO and intact mice. Sections were chosen at 4800 µm caudal to the injury center where most neurons were preserved after SCI but would be subjected to ongoing cell stress. Markers analyzed included pNrf2, GRP78, HSP70, CytoC, and pREL-NFκB, all indicative of cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and survival signaling, respectively.

Cystamine improved functional recovery and tissue preservation after SCI

Open-field locomotor function, evaluated using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS), indicated significantly enhanced motor recovery in SCI, cystamine-treated mice compared to SCI, untreated control animals. Following SCI, both groups showed a severe impairment, with almost complete hindlimb paralysis (BMS scores ≈ 0 at week 1 post-injury). Beginning at week 3 post-SCI and continuing through week 8, cystamine-treated mice exhibited significantly higher BMS scores, indicating improved hindlimb movement and coordination compared to controls (**p < 0.0001; Fig. 14A). In the horizontal ladder test assessment, cystamine-treated animals displayed a trend for fewer footfall errors compared to controls at both 1- and 2-month post-injury (MPI), though differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 14B).

Analysis of the lesion area as a percentage of total spinal cord cross-sectional area at specific locations within and adjacent to the injury site demonstrated a trend for reduced lesion size in cystamine-treated mice across many sections distal to the injury epicenter, although differences between groups did not achieve statistical significance (Fig. 14C). Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence uncovering a previously unrecognized intracellular role for TG2 as a central mediator of secondary injury following SCI, an enzyme that has been traditionally associated with ECM remodeling, inflammation, and scar formation [16, 19, 24, 38, 79, 80]. In this study, we employed a clip compression model of SCI because it delivers a consistent, sustained injury that reliably produces secondary ischemia, hemorrhage, edema, and necrosis which are typical hallmarks of clinical human SCI pathology [50–52], without the rebound variability inherent to impactor‐based contusions. Moreover, the clip compression model yields a sharply demarcated lesion with robust glial scarring and demyelination, ideal for direct histological and molecular comparison across genotypes and treatment regimens. While the specific clip-compression model used does not permit readily graded severities of impact or replicate the transient oscillation of weight-drop models and therefore may underrepresent certain dynamic aspects of human blunt‐force trauma, it largely recapitulates the key pathophysiological features of clinical SCI [49].

Utilizing this clinically relevant SCI rodent compression model and advanced imaging techniques, we demonstrate that genetic deletion of TG2 profoundly enhances functional recovery, preserves tissue integrity, and modulates critical cell stress responses. Collectively, these results firmly establish TG2 as a pivotal determinant of SCI-associated pathology and position it as a promising therapeutic target to enhance CNS repair and regeneration.

Our findings demonstrate that global ablation of TG2 yields markedly improved outcomes after severe SCI. TG2 KO mice showed significantly improved locomotor recovery with higher open-field motor scores compared to TG2 intact control mice. These behavioral improvements were accompanied by pronounced histopathological benefits where TG2 KO spinal cords had greater tissue preservation and significantly reduced lesion volumes at chronic time points post injury, indicating attenuated secondary degeneration. Importantly, both white and gray matter were significantly preserved in the absence of TG2. These collective improvements of enhanced motor function, increased tissue sparing, and smaller lesions, highlight TG2 as a pivotal determinant of SCI associated pathogenesis. Although it must be noted that despite marked improvements in open‐field locomotion and histological preservation, TG2 deletion did not yield a statistically significant reduction in foot‐fault errors on the horizontal ladder test (Fig. 4B). This likely reflects the clip–compression model’s severity, which produces extensive sensorimotor circuit disruption that challenges fine paw placement. The assay also demands complex proprioceptive integration and precision stepping beyond gross hindlimb movement, creating a potential ceiling effect in severely injured mice. Thus, although TG2 KO clearly enhances tissue sparing and gross locomotor recovery, restoring the nuanced sensorimotor control assessed by the ladder test may require adjunct therapies, extended recovery intervals, or less severe injury paradigms. Our results are congruent with prior work showing that the removal of TG2, particularly in astrocytes, can promote neurite outgrowth [81] as well as results in post-SCI functional recovery and reduced glial scarring​ [38]. By confirming and extending these observations in a clinically relevant CCI model using TG2 KO mice, we underscore that TG2’s activity exacerbates SCI pathology. In TG2’s absence, the injured spinal cord has improved structural integrity and regenerative capacity as shown in Figs. 4, 5 and 6 suggesting that TG2 is a major contributor to the pathophysiology underlying CNS trauma. A key advance of our study is the use of high-dimensional IMC to map how TG2 ablation reshapes intracellular stress signaling in neurons and glia after SCI. IMC results revealed that lesions in TG2 intact mice exhibit robust up-regulation of phospho-Nrf2, GRP78/BiP and HSP70, which are well established hallmarks of oxidative and ER stress, whereas all three markers are markedly attenuated in TG2 KO spinal cord tissue. Accumulating evidence suggests that TG2 directly orchestrates these maladaptive responses by transamidating critical chaperone and redox-sensing proteins, thereby altering their stability and function. This interpretation aligns with previous studies that have demonstrated that TG2 cross-links and inactivates the master regulator heat-shock factor 1 (HSF1), impairing HSP70 induction and perpetuating ER stress under proteotoxic conditions [82, 83]. Thus, TG2 deletion appears to curtail the upstream triggers such as excess reactive oxygen species, Ca2 + dysregulation, protein aggregation, that normally force cells into a stress response after injury [84–87], sustaining Nrf2 activation and exacerbating oxidative damage. Although our findings are correlative, direct biochemical validation such as demonstrating TG2-HSF1 interactions by co-immunoprecipitation or measuring ROS levels in TG2 KO neurons will be needed. Nonetheless, these data support a model in which intracellular TG2 acts upstream of both ER- and oxidative-stress pathways to drive secondary injury after SCI.

In addition to dampening stress markers, TG2 KO preserved mitochondrial integrity. Cytochrome c, a pro-apoptotic mitochondrial protein that is normally released into the cytosol upon mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, was markedly reduced in the injured spinal cord tissue of TG2 KO mice relative to the controls with intact TG2. Injured spinal cord tissues from TG2 KO mice showed cytosolic cytochrome c release primarily in the gray matter region, an indicator of neuronal apoptosis, whereas TG2 KO spinal tissue exhibited significantly reduced expression levels. This suggests that TG2 contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptotic signaling after SCI. Additionally, our findings also demonstrates BNIP3, a proapoptotic factor that is known to contribute to mitochondrial impairment, was found to be upregulated in the ventral horn motor neurons of the injured spinal cord tissue in TG2 intact mice compared to lower expression in TG2 KO mice, further highlighting TG2’s critical role in mediating apoptotic signaling pathways following SCI. [88, 89]. Indeed, prior studies support a role for TG2 in facilitating apoptotic pathways [47] where TG2 activation can promote Bax-mediated mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and drive the release of cytochrome c and AIF (apoptosis-inducing factor), thereby activating caspase-dependent and caspase-independent cell death. Our data are in line with these findings where TG2 removal showed less cytochrome c release and presumably less downstream apoptotic pathway activation, correlating with the improved cell survival and tissue sparing we observed. Therefore, depleting TG2 helps cells retain mitochondrial stability under conditions of injury, shifting the balance away from apoptotic outcomes.

Conversely, pro-survival signaling after SCI was enhanced in the absence of TG2. IMC revealed elevated levels of pRel/NF-κB in TG2 KO mice. NF-κB is a transcriptional driver of cell survival and anti-apoptotic genes particularly in neurons in addition to being a well-known transcriptional regulator of inflammation in immune cells [70, 90, 91]. The increase in NF-κB phosphorylation we observed in TG2 KO neurons and astrocytes after SCI compared to TG2 intact controls indicates a more robust endogenous survival response when TG2 is removed. This finding resonates with in vitro studies of astrocytes, where silencing TG2 led to heightened NF-κB activity [92]. TG2 appears to interact with NF-κB signaling in complex ways; astrocytic TG2 KO was reported to increase NF-κB, whereas pharmacological inhibition of TG2’s transamidation activity had the opposite effect [92]. Nonetheless, the observations made in the present investigation suggest that SCI-induced TG2 restrains NF-κB-driven survival pathways in neurons and astrocytes. TG2 KO appears to lift this brake, promoting NF-κB phosphorylation and the expression of neuroprotective and anti-apoptotic factors. Further investigations are needed to clarify the precise signaling pathways mediating the interaction between TG2 and NFκB. Together, the combination of lower cellular stress and higher pro-survival signaling after SCI and TG2 KO suggests a more favorable molecular milieu for recovery. These changes likely underlie the improved histological and functional outcomes in TG2 KO mice, linking the absence of TG2 to enhanced resilience of both neurons and glia in the injured spinal cord as shown in Figs. 4 and 14.

Traditionally, TG2 has been characterized as an ECM remodeling enzyme in the context of CNS pathology. TG2’s calcium-dependent transamidase activity can cross-link matrix proteins including FN and collagen, stabilizing tissue scars. In the injured CNS, TG2 is enriched on the surface of reactive astrocytes and can mediate their adhesion and migration on FN matrices [15], contributing to the formation of the glial scar. Indeed, reductions in both FN and CSPG production, ECM associated with tissue scarring, after SCI was also observed in the present study in TG2 KO mice compared to TG2 intact controls. This extracellular role of TG2 has led to the view that it primarily hinders recovery by affecting cell-ECM interactions, tissue stiffness, and inhibitory scarring. Conversely, reducing TG2 activity was hypothesized to limit scar maturation and make the wound environment more permissive for regeneration [15]. Our findings in SCI agree with this assertion. We show additionally though that intracellular TG2 is an equally critical player if not more in the response towards the injury. A reduction in cellular pathology observed in TG2 KO mice comprising reduced stress and a decrease in proapoptotic mediators combined with an increase in pro-survival signals point to TG2’s role within cells as a pathological modulator of key injury response pathways. This represents a paradigm shift suggesting that TG2 is not merely a crosslinking “glue” in the extracellular space, but also a potent intracellular mediator contributing to secondary tissue damage after CNS injury.

Several mechanisms could explain how intracellular TG2 drives these detrimental processes following SCI. TG2 is a multifunctional protein that, beyond its crosslinking of structural proteins, can interact with signaling molecules and regulators of cell death. TG2’s enzymatic activity can cross-link transcription factors or inhibitor proteins, potentially sequestering them. It has been proposed that TG2 can cross-link IκBα (the inhibitor of NF-κB) or other signaling proteins, thereby altering NF-κB pathway dynamics [92]. TG2 can also act as a GTP-binding protein and may interfere with small G-protein signaling cascades related to cell survival​ [47]. Additionally, TG2’s crosslinking of cytosolic and nuclear proteins under stress conditions can lead to the formation of insoluble protein aggregates. While this might temporarily mitigate proteostatic stress by sequestering misfolded proteins, it can also impair cellular functions and trigger the unfolded protein response. In neurodegenerative disease models, excess TG2 activity contributes to toxic protein aggregate formation, whereas inhibiting TG2 can reduce protein aggregation and cell death [93]. Thus, in acute SCI, TG2 may exacerbate proteostasis failure possibly by crosslinking damaged proteins and overwhelming protein clearance mechanisms [94] that is known to be impaired after SCI [95]. This in turn would lead to an elevated HSP70/GRP78 response in the TG2 intact mice after SCI. Moreover, as discussed above, TG2 can facilitate mitochondrial dysfunction by promoting the Bax-mediated permeabilization of mitochondria [47]. Finally, TG2’s influence on pathways like HSF1-HSP70 [82] and NF-κB underscores its role as a stress response modulator. Collectively, our data reposition TG2 as a dual player within cells: while it has some normal physiological roles, during traumatic stress dysregulated levels of TG2 within the injured spinal cord alters the balance toward cell stress and death. Targeting TG2 could therefore ameliorate the extracellular inhibitory scar as well as alleviate detrimental stress responses at the intracellular level resulting in a two-pronged benefit for cytoprotection and neurorepair. Therefore, TG2 likely acts at multiple levels of the injury response: outside the cell by shaping the scar and mediating cell–matrix interactions, and intracellularly by inducing pro-apoptotic signaling. This multifaceted involvement helps explain why TG2 KO yields broad improvements. The injured CNS can be conceptualized as having a more favorable extracellular environment (less rigid, inhibitory scar) and a more resilient intracellular environment (less oxidative/ER stress, less apoptosis, more pro-survival signaling) when TG2 is absent. Future studies should further dissect which of these mechanisms is predominant in driving functional recovery via cell-type specific deletions of TG2 in neurons, oligodendrocytes, microglia, or fibroblasts, in addition to astrocytes. Nonetheless, our current results clearly establish TG2 as a central node in the pathological cascade of SCI, far beyond its classical role in ECM crosslinking.

While the improvements seen in TG2 KO mice after SCI are compelling, several caveats merit consideration. First, our use of a global germline TG2 KO means that developmental compensation or off-target effects could have had influences on the outcome. Mice lacking TG2 from birth might undergo developmental adaptations, for instance, compensation of TG2 removal via upregulating other transglutaminase family members or altering the baseline ECM composition that make their spinal cords inherently more injury resistant. We attempted to mitigate this concern by conducting injuries at young adult ages and comparing littermate controls, but subtle developmental differences cannot be entirely ruled out. Supporting the idea that the observed benefits are indeed due to acute loss of TG2 function (rather than developmental artifacts), our results from this study (Fig. 14) as well as earlier work by others showed that even acute pharmacological inhibition of TG2 after SCI can improve recovery [38]. Previous reports by Elahi et al. [38] involving treatment of SCI mice with the selective TG2 inhibitor VA4 showed an improved motor function similar to the functional improvement that is observed in this study with TG2 knockout mice, further supporting the detrimental role of TG2 inducing injury induced neuroinflammatory responses. Nevertheless, global knockout might mask cell-specific roles. For example, TG2 in astrocytes, microglia, neurons, and endothelial cells could each contribute differently to injury outcomes. Prior studies have highlighted astrocytic TG2 as a key driver of glial scar formation and locomotor impairment [81]. In our TG2 KO model, the beneficial effects observed following removal of TG2 are partly attributed to the absence of astrocytic TG2 as evident from reduced inhibitory scarring. However, TG2 deletion in other cell types such as microglia might also reduce inflammation or phagocytic dysfunction. Cell type-specific TG2 knockouts in future experiments will be valuable to pinpoint which cell populations within the injured spinal cord are most responsible for the beneficial effects of TG2 removal or inhibition. It is possible that astrocytic specific TG2 ablation yields most of the structural scar-related benefits, whereas neuronal TG2 removal might contribute more to neuronal survival and plasticity benefits.

Another consideration is the difference between murine and human TG2 biology. Mice and humans share TG2 as a conserved protein, but there are species differences in injury responses and scar formation in the CNS. Human astrocytes in demyelinating lesions express TG2 on their surface and use it for adhesion and migration on FN [15], implying that TG2 plays a role in human glial scar formation similar to mice. However, the extent to which TG2 drives cell death versus survival in human neurons and glia is not fully understood. Human TG2 is implicated in various pathologies (from celiac disease to neurodegeneration), and its regulation may differ and have a different feedback mechanism to counteract TG2 activity. Thus, while our data strongly support TG2 as a therapeutic target in mice, translation to humans should account for potential differences in TG2 regulation and the complexity of human SCI pathology. Moreover, TG2 is a multifunctional enzyme present in many tissues; complete inhibition of TG2 could have off-target consequences on wound healing, with TG2 being important in peripheral tissue repair and coagulation, since Factor XIII is a transglutaminase [96, 97]. These concerns highlight the importance of developing targeted strategies that focus on the injury site or specific cell types, to avoid systemic side effects.

Finally, we acknowledge that IMC, while powerful, provides mostly correlative data regarding signaling changes. For example, the observed increase in NF-κB activity in TG2 KO cells could be a direct effect of removing TG2’s interaction with the NF-κB pathway, or an indirect result of overall reduced oxidative stress. Similarly, lower HSP70 could mean reduced stress or a direct impairment of HSF1 activation due to loss of TG2’s chaperoning role [82]. Interestingly, in TG2‐intact spinal cords after SCI, we observed elevated levels of pERK1/2, especially in the ventral horn motor neurons whereas TG2 KO markedly reduced pERK expression. Although ERK1/2 is well known for its pro‐survival roles by phosphorylating and inactivating Bad and upregulating Bcl-2 family proteins, sustained or excessive ERK activation can paradoxically trigger apoptosis [98, 99]. Previous studies have reported that pERK signaling exerts a context‐dependent dual role in neuronal apoptosis, promoting survival under transient activation yet driving cell death when activation is sustained or aberrant [100]. Prolonged pERK1/2 signaling has been shown to phosphorylate the BH3-only protein Bim, converting it into a potent inducer of mitochondrial apoptosis, and chronic pERK activity has also been shown to upregulate death‐associated genes such as Fas ligand, promoting cell death [101]. Our findings indicate that TG2 functions upstream of ERK1/2; in TG2‐intact spinal cords, where the high pERK levels in TG2 intact mice may reflect a pathological, pro-apoptotic ERK response driven by TG2, whereas the reduction of pERK in TG2 KO mice likely represents attenuation of this detrimental signaling cascade, shifting the balance back toward neuronal survival after SCI. While our data strongly suggest TG2’s role upstream of pERK1/2, direct biochemical validation, such as TG2–pERK1/2 co‐immunoprecipitation, in vitro kinase assays, or measurement of upstream MAPKK activity, is required to conclusively confirm these signaling interactions. Similarly, future mechanistic studies by reintroducing TG2 selectively to test if it rescues the stress phenotype are needed to link TG2 to each specific pathway. Despite these limitations, converging evidence from our study and previous reports strongly supports that the beneficial effects observed with TG2 depletion are real and involve multiple mechanisms.

The clear improvements seen with TG2 removal open an exciting translational avenue: targeting TG2 as a therapeutic strategy for CNS injury. TG2, although an important target in neurodegenerative conditions, has not been a traditional focus for therapeutics in neurotrauma. Our results suggest that TG2 inhibitors could be highly beneficial after SCI by simultaneously mitigating glial scarring and promoting cell survival. One proof-of-concept shown here in SCI comes from the pharmacological inhibitor studies using the irreversible TG2 inhibitor cystamine. Cystamine and its active metabolite cysteamine have been used previously in studies to inhibit TG2 activity in vivo [102]. Notably, cystamine treatment showed neuroprotective effects in models of brain injury and neurodegeneration. In intracerebral hemorrhage, cystamine reduced brain edema, decreased neuronal death, and improved neurological function [93]. These benefits were attributed in part to TG2 inhibition, though cystamine’s effects are pleiotropic, since it can also inhibit caspase-3, raise glutathione levels, and induce heat shock proteins [93]. Likewise, in Huntington’s disease models, cystamine reduced protein aggregation and extended survival [93] consistent with blocking TG2’s crosslinking of mutant huntingtin protein. While cystamine itself has limitations due to off-target effects and the need for high doses, these studies illustrate that pharmacologically dampening TG2 activity is achievable and can confer neuroprotection. Building upon these findings with the newer small-molecule TG2 inhibitors that are under development with greater specificity and brain penetration is an important new avenue. Compounds, including NC9 and VA4, covalently lock TG2 in its open (inactive) conformation, effectively abolishing its transamidation activity. In astrocyte cultures, such inhibitors mirrored the effects of TG2 knockdown on improving cell survival under stress [92]. The TG2 inhibitor VA4 delivered after SCI has also been demonstrated to significantly enhance recovery in mice [38]. These results are encouraging, suggesting that drug-mediated TG2 suppression can recapitulate the genetic knockout’s benefits.

Translationally, there are several important future directions. First, optimizing TG2 inhibitor delivery to the injured spinal cord is crucial where using nanoparticles or intrathecal delivery to maximize local drug concentration while minimizing systemic exposure is anticipated to provide superior outcomes. Second, gene therapy approaches could be considered: an adeno-associated virus delivering a TG2-silencing RNA or CRISPR construct to the injury site might provide a targeted, long-term TG2 knockdown in injured cells. Such an approach could potentially be turned on transiently to aid acute repair without permanently altering other essential physiological functions of TG2. Given the physiological roles of TG2, careful consideration of safety is essential; thus, achieving precise spatial and temporal control of TG2 inhibition will be critical.

Despite these challenges, the outlook for the development of TG2-targeted therapies for CNS injury is highly promising. Our study shifts TG2 to the forefront as a therapeutic target that tackles both the extracellular and intracellular pathomechanisms associated with SCI. Moving forward, testing TG2 inhibitors in preclinical SCI models including larger animals and probing TG2’s involvement in human SCI pathology including analyzing post-mortem human spinal cord tissue for TG2 activity and correlating these changes with outcome will be critical next steps. In conclusion, by revealing TG2’s role as a critical regulator of the post-injury response, our work lays a foundation for translational efforts to neutralize TG2 and thereby foster a more permissive environment for CNS repair and functional recovery [38]. The prospect of using small molecules or gene therapies to suppress TG2 by turning off this “trigger” of cell stress and scarring represents a compelling new direction for improving recovery after SCI and possibly other forms of CNS trauma.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings significantly advance the understanding of TG2 as a critical intracellular mediator in the pathogenesis of SCI. Through genetic knockout and pharmacological inhibition using cystamine, we demonstrated that eliminating TG2 markedly improves functional recovery, reduces lesion volume, and enhances both white and gray matter preservation after severe spinal cord trauma in mice. Notably, the beneficial effects observed in TG2 knockout mice were associated with decreased ECM deposition, reduced reactive gliosis, increased neuronal plasticity, and a substantial reduction in key markers of cellular stress, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These effects were paralleled by enhanced activation of neuroprotective signaling pathways such as NF-κB. Our data therefore has shifted the traditional paradigm of TG2 from merely an ECM remodeling enzyme to a crucial regulator of intracellular stress responses, apoptotic signaling, and neuronal survival pathways following SCI. The translational implications of these findings are substantial, positioning TG2 as a promising therapeutic target. Future studies aimed at developing specific, clinically applicable TG2 inhibitors could provide novel therapeutic approaches capable of simultaneously mitigating scar formation and promoting neuronal survival, ultimately improving recovery and quality of life for individuals with spinal cord injury.
