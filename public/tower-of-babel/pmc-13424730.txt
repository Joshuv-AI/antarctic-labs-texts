A multi-domain qualitative study on factors influencing access to preventive hepatitis B care among rural population
Authors: Caixia Li, Mi Zhong, Gengyang Yuan, Shujuan Liu, Shuangshuang Wen, Xiaofei Tian, Dorothy Ngo Sheung Chan, Xia Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore barriers precluding hepatitis B virus (HBV) preventive care among the rural population.MethodsGuided by the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities framework, this qualitative study applied an interpretative phenomenological approach. Participants (n = 34) were recruited from three healthcare settings in southern China from May 2024 to January 2025. Data from semi-structured interviews were analyzed using interpretative phenomenological analysis.ResultsBarriers spanned four domains with 11 subthemes. 1) Behavioral: the last-resort paradigm in healthcare utilization, reliance on folk remedies, and intergenerational silence in familial HBV prevention; 2) Physical environment: remote residence from healthcare facilities and compound vulnerabilities in living and working environments; 3) Sociocultural: social marginalization stemming from HBV discrimination, fatalistic resignation towards health, and the myth of the hardy survivor; and 4) Healthcare system: misperceptions, underestimated needs, and access barriers related to labor, mobility, and misaligned healthcare insurance policy.ConclusionsThese barriers perpetuate late HBV diagnosis, low vaccine uptake, and poor treatment adherence among the rural population. This highlights the need for mobile health services to overcome geographic barriers; culturally appropriate interventions to dismantle misinformation, HBV discrimination, and myths; and policies to reduce financial burden and improve insurance coverage, thereby making HBV preventive care accessible for this marginalized group.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a significant global health challenge. It affected approximately 254 million people and caused 1.1 million deaths in 2022, ranking as the third leading cause of mortality from infectious diseases globally [1]. The HBV burden is disproportionately high among the rural population, where a combination of poor preventive awareness and limited healthcare access might heighten their susceptibility [2]. Globally, the rural population exhibits alarmingly high infection rates, with anti-hepatitis B core antibody markers indicating past or current exposure ranging from 51.8 % [3] to 90 % [4]. These rates are approximately 3 to 30 times higher than the general population average [4]. The current chronic infection rate, measured by hepatitis B surface antigen seroprevalence, ranges from 4.6 % [5] to 10.7 % [6] and remains substantially elevated compared to the global average of 3.3 % [1]. The rural population also experienced approximately three times higher odds of developing HBV-related liver cancer than their counterparts, incurring significant disease burden [7]. China has made remarkable achievements in HBV prevention and control, yet uneven prevention effects between urban and rural areas still persist. According to a meta-analysis of 231 million participants in China, hepatitis B surface antigen seroprevalence in the rural population was 5.1 %, significantly higher than in the urban population (3.4 %) [8].

However, HBV preventive care, including vaccination, screening, and antiviral treatment, remains significantly underutilized among the rural population. Despite the established efficacy of the HBV vaccine, only 3.0 % to 49.1 % of the rural population from Tanzania [6], Sierra Leone [9], and China [10] reported being vaccinated against HBV. Moreover, the failure to identify undiagnosed cases and link them to standard antiviral treatment remains another obstacle to HBV elimination in the rural population. The WHO estimated that in 2022, only 13.4 % of the 254 million people living with chronic HBV were diagnosed, and only 2.6 % were receiving treatment [11]. Data are scarce for the rural population, whereas underdiagnosis and undertreatment may be more severe in rural areas due to poor service availability [12]. This gap in HBV preventive care perpetuates ongoing viral transmission in the rural population and undermines global efforts toward hepatitis elimination.

Addressing the persistent gaps in HBV preventive care requires an in-depth investigation into the barriers faced by the rural population. Existing studies on this topic remain limited and often emphasize structural and supply-side obstacles, such as remoteness of clinics [13], stockouts of hepatitis B surface antigen rapid diagnostic tests [5], lack of electricity or refrigerators to store vaccines [14], and the high costs and unavailability of antiviral treatment [15]. However, these studies may overlook the complex interplay among multidomain barriers, including those related to sociocultural beliefs, informal healthcare pathways, and policy implementation gaps. This oversight may leave a gap in understanding the root causes of suboptimal HBV preventive care uptake in the rural population and result in contextually inadequate interventions [16]. Given the limited understanding of this phenomenon, a qualitative methodology is well-suited to further understanding the barriers to HBV preventive care among the rural population [17].

The National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities (NIMHD) research framework provides a robust theoretical foundation for this qualitative enquiry. Grounded in the socioecological model, the NIMHD framework systematically delineates health determinants across multiple domains: 1) biological, emphasizing biological vulnerabilities and mechanisms; 2) behavioral, including health behaviors and coping strategies; 3) physical environment, encompassing personal, household, and community environments; 4) sociocultural, addressing sociodemographic, cultural identity, social networks, and social norms; and 5) healthcare system, coving health knowledge, availability of services, and quality of care [18]. This multidimensional perspective has proven effective in guiding qualitative research on health disparities related to diverse health behaviors, such as cancer diagnosis [19]. It was thus adopted in this qualitative study to explore multidimensional barriers to HBV preventive care in the rural population. This study specifically focused on identifying modifiable barriers across the behavioral, physical, sociocultural, and healthcare system domains. The findings will contribute to the development of targeted strategies to improve HBV service delivery, enhance equity in healthcare access, and ultimately reduce the burden of HBV in the rural population.

Methods

Study design

Employing an interpretative phenomenological approach [20], this study aimed to yield a deeper understanding and theoretical constructs of the narrative, analytical, and synthetic perspectives on barriers to HBV preventive care among the rural population. The study report followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies [21].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted between May 2024 and January 2025 at two university-affiliated hospitals and one community health center located in Shenzhen and Guizhou, China. Eligible participants were adults aged 18–65 years from the rural population, specifically including two key groups: 1) rural residents, defined as individuals with a rural household registration residing in and deriving their primary livelihood from rural areas, and 2) rural-to-urban migrant workers, defined as individuals with a rural household registration and working in an urban center [22]. These two groups were combined due to their shared rural identity, which underpins similar sociocultural backgrounds and health beliefs. This approach therefore strengthens the generalizability of findings across the broader rural population. Individuals were excluded if they had a diagnosis of a malignant tumor, a severe mental disorder, or communication difficulties that could compromise the interview process. This study included those susceptible or chronically infected individuals to gain a comprehensive view of barriers across the HBV preventive care continuum. HBV infection status was confirmed based on electronic medical records, specifically the detection of hepatitis B surface antigen, or on prior diagnostic reports provided by the participants. Six trained nurses identified potentially eligible participants and informed them about the interviews. The researchers then contacted interested individuals to explain the study in detail, confirm eligibility, and schedule the interview. Written informed consent was obtained following oral consent.

Purposive sampling was conducted to ensure different perspectives. Snowball sampling was conducted by asking participants to refer to their contacts of interest after the interview. The study sample size, based on the principle of data saturation, was determined to be 32 participants. Two additional interviews were added to ensure no key insights were missed and to facilitate a thorough exploration of diverse perspectives.

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted. An interview guide (Appendix A) was designed using the NIMHD framework to explore multidomain barriers to HBV preventive care among the rural population. It comprised open-ended questions to encourage discussion and was refined after pilot testing with four participants. As the interview guide underwent only minor revisions by adding explanations of HBV terminology, the pilot data were deemed valuable and included in the analysis. Interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese or local dialects (Henan, Guizhou, and Chaoshan) with the assistance of one translator. The interviewers were the principal investigator (C. Li, an associate researcher with prior experience in qualitative research; 21 interviews) and a research assistant (G. Yuan, a master’s candidate trained in qualitative interviews; 13 interviews). The average interview duration was 29.2 min (17.1–53.1 min) and was conducted face-to-face in quiet spaces in the study settings (n = 33) or via telephone (n = 1) based on the participants’ convenience. Non-verbal cues were recorded using field notes. Participants self-reported demographic data to understand their living contexts.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (No. 2024-089-01). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were thoroughly informed about the interview process, their right to voluntary participation, and the use of digital recordings. They were also assured that no personal identifying information would be collected to obtain signed informed consent. Each participant was compensated with 50 CNY for their time. The principal researcher securely stored all recordings and transcripts and will be destroyed five years after study completion.

Data analysis and rigor

Interviews were verbatim transcribed and imported into NVivo software (version 12, QSR International Pty Ltd.) for analysis. The interpretative phenomenological analysis was selected because it can be used to explore the intricacies of individual experiences and their underlying meanings [23], facilitating a nuanced understanding of disparities in HBV prevention among the rural population. The procedure included: 1) repeatedly reading each transcript alongside audio-recordings; 2) generating initial descriptive, linguistic, and conceptual notes; 3) developing emergent themes from initial notes; 4) grouping emergent themes into superordinate categories, 5) iterating this process with subsequent transcripts to identify new themes; and 6) reorganizing the thematic structure within the NIMHD framework to theoretically explain barriers to HBV preventive care in rural population. To mitigate potential biases, the principal researcher (C. Li) coded the collected data between October 2024 and April 2025, and a research assistant (G. Yuan) verified the coding. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached among all co-authors. Five participants engaged in member checking and validated the themes and quotes. The principal researcher (C. Li) and one bilingual expert collaborated to translate the codes from Chinese into English. All the authors reached a consensus on the coding and translation.

Appendix C presents a thematic map of barriers to HBV preventive care among the rural population, including themes and key content. The following sections detail these barriers within the behavioral, physical, sociocultural, and healthcare system domains.

The behavior domain

The last-resort paradigm in healthcare utilization

A dominant theme in participants’ narratives was a last-resort paradigm for healthcare, which significantly precluded timely HBV screening, diagnosis, vaccination, and treatment. This paradigm was fundamentally anchored in participants’ definition of a sick state. Their guiding principle—“You only go to the hospital when you are sick”—was defined by a threshold of severe bodily compromise. In this state, fundamental functions like eating, mobility, and work were suspended. Adhering to this view, seven participants with HBV disregarded the need for treatment, interpreting their asymptomatic state as evidence of not being sick.

Compounding this paradigm was financial precarity. Participants’ livelihoods were tethered to unstable, low-income labor in agriculture or urban migrant jobs such as cleaning, security, and construction. With most monthly incomes below 5,000 CNY (approximately USD $702), healthcare expenditures were viewed as a threat to basic survival. For example, 10 participants with HBV were forced to delay essential treatment due to financial burden. For a similar reason, even when aware of their high-risk status, ten participants postponed HBV screening, missing opportunities for early diagnosis and treatment. Critically, three of them were not diagnosed until HBV had progressed to advanced stages, manifesting in overt complications, such as cirrhosis and bleeding from esophageal varices.“After he (his father) passed, I knew it was from HBV. For the next few years, my wife kept telling me to get checked, but I’d say, ‘Forget it.’ I’m the only one working, I’ve got three little kids and her to support, plus I have to pay rent. My whole month’s pay is gone by the end of it, so how can I get it (HBV) checked? So, I just kept putting it off, for two or three years … One night, I ate an apple, and right after, I threw up blood. I kept putting it off until this April, when I finally came back to get checked and found out that I have cirrhotic hepatitis B.” (P 27)

Reliance on folk remedies

Participants relied on folk remedies, thereby precluding adherence to formal antiviral therapies. For instance, three participants discontinued prescribed antiviral therapies and adopted entirely folk practices—ingesting Sanqi (Pseudoginseng) and procuring unregulated herbal compounds. These remedies, circulating through their social networks, were unvalidated and carried an inherent risk of hepatotoxicity. This was observed in two participants whose reliance on folk remedies did not yield healing but precipitated liver injury, necessitating hospitalization.“Someone took a little home remedy, and it cured their hepatitis B. So, I thought, why not me? I got the Sanqi myself, ground it all up, made these tiny little pellets, and laid them out in the sun to dry. I’d take them with honey for almost half a year. But this time … I ended up in the hospital, and they told me my transaminases were dangerously high.” (P 12)

The intergenerational silence in familial HBV prevention

Most participants exhibited profound inaction toward familial HBV risk, revealing intergenerational silence that perpetuates preventable infection and mortality from HBV. For 14 participants with a personal or family history of HBV, the threat of household clustering was often met not with vigilance but passivity. While a small cohort (n = 4) implemented preventive measures, such as maternal antiviral prophylaxis and regular family antibody screening with timely boosters, the majority remained in a state of “no screening, no vaccination,” leaving generations vulnerable. This form of intergenerational silence had its most severe manifestation in four families, where a family member’s diagnosis of HBV, and even its progression to HBV-related mortality, failed to promote them to seek screening or vaccination.“My brother … he knew (that he had hepatitis B). He’d known since he was seventeen, eighteen. He knew, but he just brushed it off. Then, in the New Year’s 2019, it caught up with him. They said it was late-stage cirrhosis. After that, he was just … in and out of this hospital for a month. Then, he was gone. And still, none of us went to get ourselves checked. We just … we didn’t do anything.” (P 30)

At the cognitive level, misconceptions and knowledge deficits created a foundation of inaction. For example, six participants revealed a critical knowledge deficit regarding the severity and transmissibility of HBV. Four participants misperceived HBV vaccine as a greater threat (e.g., developmental delays in children) than the virus itself, which led them to refuse HBV vaccination. Systemically, three participants reported that healthcare professionals failed to recommend family screening or vaccination after their diagnosis, leading them to believe such action was unnecessary. At the socio-structural level, financial constraints, logistical uncertainty, and low health literacy effectively diminished familial HBV prevention, as reported by four participants.

The physical environment domain

Remote residence from healthcare facilities

Participants described a local healthcare infrastructure that was unprepared to manage HBV, compelling them to seek care at distant county or city hospitals. For six participants, this meant undertaking arduous journeys of 20 to 50 km, often on personal motorcycles, consuming hours, and making a simple check-up an all-day affair. This geographic barrier persisted even upon urban migration. Twenty participants who moved to cities for work reported living in industrial parks and on the urban fringe, isolated from mainstream medical services. Hence, participants proposed deploying mobile health units for HBV screening and vaccination directly in their villages and workplaces, such as factories and construction sites, thereby bridging the gap created by physical isolation.“I went to Fujian for abalone farming, way out on the ocean. Just to get to land, we’re looking at a minimum 10-min boat ride. And once we’re ashore, we still need to travel for over an hour by car to reach the hospital. The whole journey is really inconvenient.” (P 27)

Compound vulnerabilities in living and working environments

Participant accounts reveal how intersecting vulnerabilities within the living and working environments produce and obscure HBV risk. Firstly, low-wage employment constrained participants (n = 7) to substandard housing characterized by overcrowding and poor sanitation. The most extreme case involved living in a workplace storage room. Within such compromised living environments, HBV became an abstraction, a distant concern eclipsed by the immediacy of survival.

A hazardous working environment compounded this vulnerability. Nine participants were rural migrant workers with high-risk occupations, including sanitation, hospital cleaning, and patient caregiving, which entailed regular exposure to blood, bodily fluids, and medical waste. However, a lack of occupational safety protocols was evident. Five participants explicitly stated that they received no training in preventing infectious diseases such as HBV and were left without access to post-exposure procedures.

The sociocultural environment domain

The social marginalization stemming from HBV discrimination

An HBV diagnosis often precipitates a form of social marginalization, marked by pervasive discrimination and enforced isolation. For instance, 16 participants articulated that public misunderstanding of HBV transmission routes cultivates a deep-seated discrimination that permeates all spheres of life. Despite seven participants knowing that HBV is not transmitted via digestive or respiratory routes, they deliberately avoided sharing meals and wore masks during conversations with patients with HBV.

The threat of social marginalization becomes a more immediate danger than the virus itself, leading participants to reject HBV screening to evade the marked status of a diagnosis. For example, three participants avoided clinical evaluation even after close household contact, believing that they would “feel like a cut below everyone else once diagnosed.” For those diagnosed, concealment became a strategy for survival, an attempt to preserve relationships and livelihoods they believe would be obliterated by disclosure.“The stares, the whispers—even their so-called ‘sympathy’ feels like a burden. So no, I would never let anyone know …” (P 30)

“The stares, the whispers—even their so-called ‘sympathy’ feels like a burden. So no, I would never let anyone know …” (P 30)

The fatalistic resignation towards health

A fatalistic resignation towards health pervaded the accounts of participants, constructing illness and death as events scripted by destiny and immune to human influence. It undermined participant HBV preventive behaviors. For example, six participants described HBV infection as a matter of fate, thereby denying the value of HBV vaccination. Their narratives included assertions such as, “You won’t get it if it’s not your fate.” Four participants with HBV, convinced that their life trajectory was fixed, exhibited poor adherence to antiviral therapy, resigning themselves to a state of “living day by day” in the face of what they saw as an unalterable end. This is not a strategy for psychological resilience. Rather, it is a narrative of surrender, a passive acceptance of fate in the face of adversities—financial precarity and inaccessible healthcare—that ultimately diminishes this population's motivation and behavior in combating HBV.“If I’m meant to die, I’ll die. It felt like a waste of time, chasing a treatment that I couldn’t afford. So, I did nothing. What’s the point? It’s all up to fate anyway …” (P 17)

The myth of the hardy survivor

Rooted in the view that “those who suffer are destined to live long,” some participants believed that hardship forges a robust constitution capable of resisting diseases. This belief, formed in the context of poverty and limited healthcare access, is substantiated by personal anecdotes. For instance, eight participants interpreted their survival in past health crises, including staying healthy during epidemics or a self-resolved breast nodule, as empirical proof of their suffering’s protective power. They directly linked a hard life to disease resistance and life resilience, internalizing it as a self-protective creed. Consequently, eight participants believed that they could resist HBV without testing or vaccination, as their suffering had already provided their immunity.“Honestly, I’ve hardly been to a doctor in my decades. It’s like there’s a rule for people like me with hard lives: we’ve already suffered enough, so life doesn’t send more trouble our way. We don’t get sick. Our bodies are forged strong, and we can withstand whatever comes. Take, for instance, when I had my baby, I found a lump in my breast, I just applied some herbal paste, and sure enough, it went away … I never overthought it. Why bother? So, when it comes to things like that vaccine (for HBV), well, I don’t see the point. My suffering has already been my shield.” (P 1)

The healthcare system domain

Misperceptions regarding HBV and preventive care

Participants’ HBV preventive care engagement was precluded by multiple misperceptions regarding the nature of the virus, its transmission, vaccination, screening, and subsequent clinical management. For instance, ten participants failed to recognize the liver as the primary target organ of HBV and were unaware of its clinical course, severity, and symptoms. Three participants attributed signs of HBV infection, such as jaundice, to benign conditions such as “overwork” or “internal heat,” leading to significant diagnostic delays. In addition, more than half of the participants incorrectly identified digestive or respiratory routes—such as sharing meals or close conversation—as primary modes of HBV transmission. This belief fostered a false sense of security, leading to preventive efforts that were narrowly focused on avoiding shared utensils and physical proximity, thereby directly undermining the perceived value of HBV vaccination. Specifically, fourteen participants lacked fundamental knowledge regarding the vaccine's function, the recommended schedule, and the necessity of post-vaccination antibody titer monitoring. Furthermore, five participants had never heard of the vaccine, mistakenly believing it was exclusively for children or that natural immunity could be acquired after exposure, thereby neglecting the need for HBV vaccination.“Most people know hepatitis B isn’t contagious. A lot of us in the village, that’s what we think. For instance, a guy in our area got tuberculosis—that’s contagious, but hepatitis B it’s not …” (P 27)

“Most people know hepatitis B isn’t contagious. A lot of us in the village, that’s what we think. For instance, a guy in our area got tuberculosis—that’s contagious, but hepatitis B it’s not …” (P 27)

Furthermore, a lack of knowledge regarding HBV screening and long-term disease management was evident. Many participants equated the absence of symptoms or a normal routine physical exam with being uninfected with HBV. This resulted in five high-risk participants with close contact history neglecting HBV screening.

Underestimated needs in HBV preventive care

Perception biases regarding the risk, severity, and treatability of HBV led participants to underestimate their needs for preventive care. For instance, seven participants with objective risk factors, such as a family history or close contact with HBV, nonetheless insisted that their personal risk was negligible and therefore saw no need for HBV screening or vaccination. This low-risk perception was fueled by misconceptions, including the beliefs that dietary habits (e.g., not eating meat) or blood type incompatibility could prevent infection, and that HBV was inherited rather than contagious.

Regarding the disease severity, six infected participants downplayed the consequences of HBV infection due to the absence of obvious symptoms or complications, thus diminishing the perceived need for proactive management. Finally, concerning treatment efficacy, some participants believed that HBV is incurable. This conviction negated their perceived value of early diagnosis and discouraged investment in costly treatment.“I was in Dongguan, found out that I had it (HBV), and went to a doctor asking for medicine. He looked at me and said, ‘I have the same thing.’ He told me straight up, ‘It can’t be cured.’ I just … let it go.” (P 14)

Multiple factors influencing access to HBV healthcare service

A combination of structural influencing factors, including temporal constraints, geographic mobility, and misaligned healthcare insurance policies, impedes participant access to HBV preventive care. The nature of participants’ labor often involves physically demanding, rigidly scheduled work, such as factory assembly-line work and truck driving. For instance, three participants with HBV missed recommended follow-up visits every three to six months due to a lack of time. In addition, twenty participants reported working as migrant laborers with unstable jobs, requiring frequent changes of workplace and residence. Among them, four explicitly stated that this mobility led them to interrupt HBV vaccination or antiviral treatment.“I’ve had the hepatitis B vaccine before, but in the last ten years, I got two shots on and off … And I never got my antibodies checked afterward. That’s because I was away working, often doing firefighting, so I was always on the move. Later, after I hurt my hand, I ran off to Dongguan for a few years, worked as a cobbler, then moved to electronics, did management, was a quality inspector for a few years … Sometimes I couldn’t make my appointment to get the shot on time because of work relocations …” (P 3)

Ultimately, a misaligned healthcare insurance policy was a significant obstacle. The New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, while the most common insurance for the rural population, imposes a significant burden through its approximately 400 CNY (approximately USD $56) annual premium. This leads approximately five participants to forgo the coverage entirely. For those insured, seeking care in migrant cities involves complex reimbursement procedures and lower coverage. Almost all participants admitted to avoiding HBV screening, vaccination, or treatment due to cost fears and lack of reimbursement.

Discussion

This qualitative study systematically explored factors influencing access to HBV healthcare service among the rural population from behavioral, physical, sociocultural, and healthcare system domains. At the behavioral domain, our findings identify a last-resort healthcare paradigm as a principal barrier to HBV prevention among the rural population. Characterized by the deferral of healthcare until the onset of intolerable symptoms, this paradigm is particularly detrimental for preventing HBV, a frequently asymptomatic infection whose effective management relies on proactive healthcare [24]. Aligned with a previous study [25], HBV diagnoses and treatment in this population are often delayed until advanced stages. While financial precarity is a key driver [26], our findings also indicate that this behavior is fundamentally anchored in a culturally defined sick-state threshold, where illness is recognized only when essential functions such as eating, mobility, and labor are suspended. Compounding this delay is a preference for folk remedies, particularly traditional herbal medicine, which is often used as a substitute for evidence-based antiviral treatment among this population. Prior quantitative studies from the Philippines and Sudan indicated that the reliance on such remedies for common ailments ranges from approximately 43.9 % [27] to 92.4 % [28] among rural and underserved populations. The underlying reasons are multifaceted: folk remedies are often accessible in rural settings, lower in cost, and socially validated through trusted personal networks [29]. However, without professional oversight, substituting these remedies for antiviral treatment may heighten the risk of adverse HBV-related disease progression. Most importantly, intergenerational silence represents a distinct behavioral barrier. A previous study from India also reported limited knowledge and awareness of HBV transmission and prevention, alongside a low HBV vaccination rate (9.23 %) among family contacts [30]. The prevalence of family-clustered HBV infection remains as high as 22.8 % in rural China [31]. This underscores a family-centered approach to preventing HBV.

At the physical environment domain, geographic remoteness and compounded living-working vulnerabilities are drivers of inequity in HBV preventive care among the rural population. These barriers persist even after urban migration, further disrupting the continuity of HBV preventive care, including completion of the three-dose vaccination schedule and adherence to long-term treatment regimens [12]. These findings align with previous literature reporting geographic and migration-related barriers that preclude HBV preventive care for rural and marginalized groups [12,13]. Hence, mobile health services and migration-resilient follow-up mechanisms are essential to enhance HBV prevention among the rural population.

At the sociocultural environment domain, HBV discrimination, fatalistic resignation towards health, and the myth of the hardy survivor collectively preclude HBV preventive care in the rural population. Specifically, HBV discrimination creates a scenario where the perceived threat of social exclusion from family, workplace, and community supersedes the perceived threat of the virus itself, leading to screening avoidance and diagnosis concealment. This finding is corroborated by studies in other settings, including Ghana [32] and the United States [33]. Furthermore, pervasive fatalistic resignation undermines the perceived efficacy of preventive behaviors, including HBV vaccination and treatment adherence. This fatalism might be rooted in the financial precarity and limited healthcare access experienced by the rural population, which engenders passivity and diminishes their self-efficacy in pursuing better health outcomes [34].

In the healthcare system, misperceptions and structural access barriers critically undermine HBV preventive care engagement among rural populations. First, profound misperceptions, including the beliefs that HBV is noncontagious, HBV vaccination is exclusively for children, and routine physical exams are equivalent to HBV screening, directly led to no or ineffective preventive behaviors [35]. Second, structural barriers, in particular geographic mobility and misaligned insurance policies, disrupt the continuity of HBV preventive care. To date, HBV-related annual costs exceed 92,900 CNY per capita [36], well above the participants’ annual income in this study. However, the unaffordability of the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme and the procedural complexities of reimbursement upon their migration further exacerbate this phenomenon. Previous research identified that rural residents face threefold higher odds of poor adherence to antiviral therapy than their counterparts, underscoring a systemic access challenge [37]. Consequently, ensuring free access to HBV services, including screening, vaccines, and antiviral drugs, and implementing migration-sensitive healthcare models, are essential to achieve equitable HBV control for the rural population.

This study has several limitations. First, it did not include insights from healthcare providers or policymakers, which may lead to an incomplete understanding of the systemic and operational barriers to HBV preventive care. Second, the findings may be susceptible to recall and social desirability biases, despite our efforts to encourage authentic responses. Third, recruiting participants primarily from clinical settings may underrepresent rural individuals with limited engagement with the healthcare system. Despite these limitations, the use of purposive and snowball sampling techniques, combined with a rigorous six-step interpretative phenomenological analysis, facilitated the collection of rich, in-depth data that captures the lived experiences of HBV preventive care among the rural population.

Conclusion

This study reveals multidomain barriers to HBV preventive care in the rural population. The study highlights the need for mobile health services to overcome geographic barriers, culturally appropriate interventions to dismantle misinformation, discrimination, and myths, and policies to reduce financial burden and improve insurance coverage, thereby making HBV preventive care feasible for this vulnerable population.
