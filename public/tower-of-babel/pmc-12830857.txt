Risk factors for spontaneous preterm birth are mediated through changes in cervical length
Authors: Hope M Wolf, Roberto Romero, Jerome F Strauss, Adi L Tarca, Sonia S Hassan, Bradley T Webb, Shawn J Latendresse, Timothy P York
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough a short cervix in the mid-trimester of pregnancy is one of the strongest predictors of preterm birth (i.e., parturition before 37 completed weeks), there is limited understanding of how the dynamics of cervical remodeling (i.e., changes in cervical length) leading up to labor and delivery can inform obstetrical risk.MethodsIn this study, latent growth curve analysis was applied to serial cervical length measurements across pregnancy (median per subject = 6; IQR = 3–8) to quantify characteristics of cervical change in a cohort of 5111 singleton pregnancies carried by Black American women.ResultsA conditional mediation model including nine common maternal risk factors of spontaneous preterm birth as exogenous predictors accounts for 26.5% of the variability in gestational duration. The collective evidence supports the conclusion that the effect of common maternal characteristics on spontaneous preterm birth is mediated through cervical length change. Specifically, previous preterm birth, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and previous abortion are shown to have unique influences on preterm birth risk that likely operate, in part, through linear changes in the cervix across pregnancy.ConclusionsThis study provides an account of the intermediary role of cervical dynamics in associations between known maternal risk factors and gestational age at delivery. Although these findings may not be generalizable to all populations, they have the potential to enhance screening efforts for women who are disproportionately affected by health disparities related to preterm birth.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Reproductive disorders

Introduction

Preterm birth is the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, and babies born prematurely are at higher risk for long-term health issues, such as intellectual, developmental, and physical disabilities1,2. There are few interventions available to reduce the risk of preterm birth and the recent U.S. Food and Drug Administration withdrawal of Makena (17-alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate), indicated to prevent recurrent preterm birth in singleton pregnancies, has left practitioners with even fewer options3. Continued research should focus on the patho-etiology of preterm birth in order to provide robust evidence for intervention points to reduce preterm birth risk4.

Cervical shortening is one of the first steps in the biomechanical processes leading to labor and delivery, and can precede labor by several weeks. A mid-trimester cervical length measurement by transvaginal ultrasound remains the best available technique for predicting5–20 and preventing preterm birth, when paired with the administration of vaginal progesterone or cerclage in singleton pregnancies with a short cervix20–42. The length of the cervix, defined as the distance between the internal and external os of the cervical canal, is easily measured by transvaginal ultrasonography over the course of pregnancy43,44. Estimates for the mean length of the cervix in the mid-trimester vary between 35 and 45 mm, depending on the population45–47, with cervical lengths shorter than 25 mm before 24 weeks meeting a clinical definition of short cervix30.

A short cervix in the mid-trimester is associated with a six-fold increase in the risk of preterm delivery6, and the earlier in pregnancy that the shortening occurs, the higher the risk for spontaneous preterm birth5–20. Despite promise as a diagnostic tool this measure is characterized by relatively low sensitivity11,48. Improvements in the predictive value and development of future interventions may be achieved by taking into account the dynamic nature of cervical change across gestation48–52 and consideration of maternal characteristics43,53–55.

Cervical shortening has been linked to several maternal characteristics that are also associated with an increased risk of preterm labor, including advanced maternal age46, BMI > 2556,57, higher parity53, Black race/ethnicity58, tobacco use59, and history of cervical trauma60,61. In a recent study by Gudicha et al. an adjustment of the threshold to define a short cervix in the mid-trimester or later in gestation to account for maternal characteristics resulted in a substantial improvement in the sensitivity for prediction of women at risk for spontaneous preterm delivery43. Thus, insight into the mechanism describing how maternal risk factors contribute to cervical shortening across pregnancy may provide an understanding of how these maternal risk factors also influence preterm birth61–63.

This study aims to elucidate the etiological relationships between common maternal risk factors for preterm birth and the physiological changes in cervical length occurring during pregnancy. A multi-stage analytic strategy is employed to: (1) characterize changes in cervical length during pregnancy in a cohort of 5111 singleton pregnancies; and (2) examine the extent to which cervical change trajectories mediate associations between maternal risk factors and gestational duration. Results show that changes in cervical length during pregnancy, in part, mediate the effects of maternal risk factors (i.e., representing maternal health, obstetric history, and substance use domains), providing insight into etiologic mechanisms predisposing to preterm birth.

Methods

Cohort description

This study included women enrolled in a prospective cohort of pregnant women at the Center for Advanced Obstetrical Care and Research (CAOCR) at Hutzel Women’s Hospital, Detroit, USA. The center was affiliated with Wayne State University and the Detroit Medical Center and part of the Pregnancy Research Branch (formerly Perinatology Research Branch) of The Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (National Institutes of Health, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services). Clinical data collection was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Wayne State University (#110605MP2F) and NICHD/NIH/DHHS (OH97-CH-N067). All study participants were enrolled between 2005 and 2017 and provided written informed consent.

From an initial cohort of 8226 pregnancies with serial cervical length measurements available, 5111 pregnancies were selected based on the following criteria: a singleton pregnancy, self-identification as Black/African American, and availability of relevant demographic and clinical characteristics. Early births were included for uninduced deliveries before 37 completed weeks of gestation, including births following both spontaneous preterm labor and preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). Women treated with progesterone or cerclage during the study pregnancy were excluded, as these interventions may alter the natural progression of cervical shortening.

Cervical length was measured in millimeters (mm) using a transvaginal 12–3 MHz ultrasound endocavitary probe by shearwave elastography64. Serial cervical length measurements were obtained between 8 and 40 weeks of gestation when patients were seen for prenatal care visits in the CAOCR clinic. Gestational age at delivery (GAD) was measured from the first day of a woman’s last menstrual period and confirmed by ultrasound. The inter-observer correlation for transvaginal cervical measurements in this cohort was 77%, similar to 76%43, based on 182 instances when a clinical and a research ultrasound evaluation were conducted on the same day.

Analytic strategy

Sonographic cervical length is typically measured in the midtrimester of pregnancy as part of routine prenatal care. The availability of multiple cervical length measurements within pregnancies presents an opportunity to understand how inter-individual differences in cervical change across pregnancy relate to known maternal risk factors for a short cervix and preterm birth. Maternal risk factors could influence different aspects of cervical length change. For example, a maternal risk factor could be associated with the initial length of the cervix at the beginning of pregnancy (e.g., prepregnancy BMI), while another risk factor could influence how rapidly the cervix length decreases around the third trimester in preparation for delivery (e.g., previous PTB). The latter decrease could be described by either a gradual linear or more rapid nonlinear change, or aspects of both. The combination of the initial value, a linear change, and nonlinear change values could describe a large range of cervical trajectories across pregnancy for different women. How differences in cervical length trajectories defined by these parameters relate to maternal risk factors leading to gestational duration was a goal of this study.

Characterizing cervical length trajectories

The analytic strategy proceeded by first estimating the initial cervical length and the linear and nonlinear change coefficients for each pregnancy using a technique called latent growth curve analysis (LGCA). LGCA is a highly flexible method based on structural equation modeling (SEM) principles that is optimized for assessing relationships among clustered variables. In the context of assessing trajectories based on longitudinal assessments of cervical length, increasingly complex curves can be developed (e.g., nonlinear functions) to account for the overall decline in cervical length preceding delivery, and the method attempts to estimate individual curves for each pregnancy, as a deviation from an average curve. In this analysis, the model intercepts were centered at 17–20 weeks so that the estimated value of the intercept coefficient would correspond to the midtrimester cervical length (MTCL), to aid in interpretability. Thus, cervical length trajectories for each pregnancy were characterized by individual estimates for the MTCL value (i.e., intercept) and the linear (i.e., slope) and nonlinear (i.e., quadratic) coefficients (Fig. 1). All modeling and data analyses were performed using Mplus (Version 8.3 for Linux) with cervical length data assumed missing at random65, employing robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR). Path coefficients and standard errors were calculated while accounting for the non-independence of observations due to complex sampling, as 13.6% of pregnancies (n = 693) involved multiple pregnancies from the same woman.

Parameterized latent growth curve analysis (LGCA) model.

The intercept was set at M5 (i.e., month 5, 17–20 weeks gestation) to allow for comparison with the typical clinical assessment of the mid-trimester transvaginal sonographic measurement.

Testing for mediation pathways

A mediation approach was then used to test whether selected maternal risk factors were temporally related to both the cervical change components (i.e., intercept, linear, and nonlinear components) and GAD. Representative variables from each of three broad domains of maternal characteristics commonly associated with risk for preterm birth and cervical length were included as candidate predictors: maternal health (maternal age, maternal depression, pre-pregnancy BMI), obstetric history (parity, history of preterm births, history of spontaneous and induced abortions), and substance use (alcohol use, smoking, and other substance use). Theoretically based mediational relationships were specified to provide estimates of the direct (i.e., maternal risk factor → GAD) and indirect (i.e., maternal risk factor → cervical change → GAD) influences on GAD. A stepwise analytic strategy was adopted to integrate the LGCA model of cervical length trajectories alongside a simultaneous assessment of all mediation pathways to address the expected covariance structure among risk factors and mediators, and to balance the evidence for causal effects against an evaluation of model assumptions66. Considering these challenges, two complimentary approaches based upon an intuitive SEM-specified products of coefficients method67–69 and alternatively by the causal inference framework70 were enlisted to evaluate relationships among the set of predictors, mediators, and outcome.

In the first method, conditional assessments were obtained by testing all maternal risk factors within the context of a single multiple mediation model containing 27 mediational pathways (9 risk factors × 3 cervical change components). This comprehensive model was designed to account for the expected correlational structure that existed among the maternal risk factors and between the cervical change components with the goal of estimating unique contributions of each mediation pathway (Fig. 2). This method also provides a flexible modeling framework allowing for the simultaneous estimation of cervical change trajectories along with the mediational relationships in the context of a larger SEM model conducted using Mplus (Version 8.3 for Linux)65 (Fig. 2). The second method applied the causal mediation framework described by Imai et al.70 and implemented in the mediation R package71. This approach has the advantage of enabling an evaluation of necessary assumptions to establish a valid causal role of the mediator of interest72. Unlike the multiple mediation approach, mediation pathways were estimated individually for each maternal risk factor and cervical change component combination, and all other non-focal maternal risk factors were included as model covariates. In all analyses, the temporal order of variables was established relative to the maternal risk factor, cervical length intermediate measure, and gestational duration outcome, supporting the strength of this longitudinal cohort to test mediation pathways66.

Full conditional mediation model with path coefficients.

The total effects of individual maternal health, obstetric history, and substance use variables on gestational age at delivery (GAD) are unconditional with respect to the I, L, and Q factors characterizing cervical change, and not, therefore, explicitly depicted.

Results

The filtered study cohort was composed of 4474 Black/African American women carrying 5111 singleton pregnancies (i.e., some women in the cohort had multiple pregnancies) of which 738 pregnancies (14.4%) were delivered preterm (Table 1). A mid-trimester cervical length was measured by transvaginal ultrasound between 18 and 24 weeks in 4022 pregnancies (78.7%) and of these, 177 (4.40%) pregnancies met a clinical definition of a short cervix.

Characterizing cervical length change across pregnancy

By way of LGCA, and standard practice for model selection within structural equation modeling73, a combination of complementary fit indices corresponding to no change, linear change, and nonlinear change models of cervical length across pregnancy were compared (Tables 2 and S1). The χ2 test of model fit for all three LGCA growth models exceeded significance thresholds for the specified degrees of freedom, yet this test alone was likely insufficient to suggest poor model fit, particularly in such a large sample. In contrast, standard thresholds for two sample size adjusted indices, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI >0.95)74 and the Root-Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA <0.06)73, suggested that an unconditional model comprised of both linear and nonlinear components unequivocally provided the best representation of cervical change across pregnancy within this cohort.

A set of significant parameter estimates resulting from the nonlinear change model indicated that, on average: (1) cervical length during the fifth month of gestation (i.e., at the model intercept, from 17 through 20 weeks) was approximately 39.8 millimeters; (2) the change in cervical length across that same 4-week period in gestation evidenced a modest decrease of just over one-quarter of a millimeter (μL = −0.266 mm); and (3) this rate of cervical shortening accelerated by one-third of a millimeter per month post intercept (μQ = −0.333 mm), beginning at the vertex of the trajectory, around the 19th week of pregnancy, prior to which a commensurate monthly deceleration was observed (Table 2). Significant intercorrelations among the cervical length components suggested that women with longer cervical measurements at 17–20 weeks gestation (i.e., intercept) display slower rates of cervical shortening in the midtrimester (r = 0.664), with cervical shortening accelerating towards the end of the pregnancy in concave quadratic trajectory of change (r = −0.530). For reference, the mean trajectory of clinical preterm birth classes was plotted over a background of the raw cervical length data to illustrate average differences in the overall functional forms of each trajectory compared to term births (Fig. 3).

Evidence for mediation pathways

The full, simultaneously estimated LGCA SEM, complete with standardized conditional path coefficients for the multiple mediation models is depicted in Fig. 2 and summarized in Table 3. Overall, this model accounts for more than one-quarter of the sample variability in GAD (R2 = 0.265; χ2(96) = 265.147, p < 0.001) and based on the robust fit statistics (RMSEA = 0.019; CFI = 0.980) positions a multiple mediation pathway model as a strong candidate to explain the relationship structure among maternal risk factors, cervical length mediators, and GAD. The collective evidence supporting mediation for each maternal risk factor and cervical length combination is summarized in Table 4. Across all indices, the most consistent evidence for mediation was found for the linear cervical change component mediating the effect of pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, previous PTB, and previous abortion on GAD. The interpretation of results will focus on these four maternal risk factors.

In Table 4, the ‘a’ path column corresponds to the significance of the univariate relationship between the maternal risk factor and the mediator accounting for all other maternal risk factors as covariates. These four maternal risk factors explained an average R2 of 0.014 (SE = 0.007) of the variation in linear change in cervical length and their standardized beta coefficients ranked highest relative to all nine maternal risk factors (Table S2). Pre-pregnancy BMI and parity were both positively related to the linear cervical change component indicating a less steep linear decline in cervical length across pregnancies when reporting greater values of these maternal risk factors, while previous PTB and previous abortion were negatively related corresponding to a steeper linear decline.

The ‘b’ path column corresponds to the significance of the relationship between the cervical mediators and the GAD outcome (Table 4). The average R2 for the linear cervical change across these risk factors was 0.058 (SE = 0.003), which also included the maternal risk factor by linear cervical change interaction term (Table S3). In all cases the linear cervical length component was positively associated with the GAD outcome, intuitively indicating a longer gestational duration was associated with a slower linear decline in cervical length.

The ACME column in Table 4 presents the significance level of an average causal mediation effect (ACME) corresponding to the estimates and 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals presented in Table S4. The ACME estimates of the mediating effect of the linear cervical change for pre-pregnancy BMI and parity were positive while estimates for previous PTB and previous abortion were negative, which are consistent with coefficient signs for the ‘a’ and ‘b’ paths reported. The ACME for the mediation of these maternal risk factors were all significant at the p < 0.001 level, and further ACME models including an interaction term to test the assumption of no exposure × mediator interaction did not alter the significance level of the mediation effects. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to test the assumption of no unmeasured confounders based on the correlation between the residuals (i.e., rho) of the regression equations for the mediator and outcome models66,72. While there is not an accepted threshold for determining sensitivity to confounding, the obtained rho values between 0.20–0.25 for these mediation models indicate the confounding necessary at which the estimated ACME values would be zero72,75.

The multiple mediation column in Table 4 summarizes the significance of the mediation effect for the full conditional model depicted in Fig. 2. Table 3 provides a summary of the total, direct, and indirect effects for each of the nine maternal risk factors in relation to GAD. The full model tested all mediational paths simultaneously to derive conditional estimates with which to test the assumption that mediation mechanisms were independent. In addition, the effect of all mediators (i.e., cervical length coefficients) could be combined for an assessment of the total indirect effect for each maternal risk factor (Table 3). In this model, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, previous PTB, and previous abortion were significant in both their total indirect effects and remained significant for the indirect effect mediated through the linear cervical change component, indicating these mediation mechanisms were not dependent on other mediation pathways in the model (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Longitudinal measurements of cervical length from a large cohort of pregnant women provided a unique opportunity to examine how cervical length trajectories across pregnancy were related to common maternal risk factors of preterm birth. Based on prior knowledge suspecting the mediating role of cervical length61 mediation analysis was used to test for causal mechanisms as an alternative method to disentangle relationships among exposures and outcomes compared to the usual approach of multiple variable regression whereby mediating variables are treated as covariates75,76. In this study, the effects of previous preterm birth, parity, pre-pregnancy BMI, and previous abortion on gestational duration were found to have consistent evidence for being mediated, in part, by linear changes in the cervix across pregnancy.

Serial cervical length measures across pregnancies were summarized using LGCA methods to derive subject-level nonlinear trajectories revealing associations with GAD. Mediational analyses provided insight on whether cervical length components accounted for the effect of maternal risk factors on GAD, not yet seen before in birth outcomes research. All analyses were performed on a large cohort of Black women who historically account for the highest rate of health disparities in preterm birth and perinatal outcomes in the United States77,78, have on average shorter mid-trimester cervical lengths than other racial/ethnic groups46,63,79,80, and are represented by a disproportionately high burden of maternal risk factors10,46,81.

The LGCA model, including the nonlinear component, provided the best fit to the data. Cervical length trajectories by the three specified preterm birth classes displayed more rapid decreases in cervical length during pregnancy, while term births were associated with a slight increase in cervical length before 25 weeks, followed by a gradual decrease in cervical length until delivery. Note that this may not reflect a physiological “lengthening” of the cervix, but rather changes in the structure, such as increased hydration or swelling, that produce slightly longer cervical length measurements than earlier in pregnancy82. The mean estimate for cervical length at each timepoint also begins to differ significantly between preterm birth classes (Fig. 3), around 16 weeks of gestation, which is consistent with the literature for using mid-trimester cervical length between 18 and 24 weeks to predict preterm birth30.

Within the context of this investigation, relationships among variables were evaluated from a large, prospective study whereby the maternal risk factors were assessed by medical records abstraction and longitudinally collected cervical length measures and gestational duration outcomes allowed for temporal precedence and causality of the mediators to be established. Mediation relationships were tested by pairing the approach de rigueur of causal mediation analysis70,71, alongside the more flexible conventional mediation analysis approach whereby cervical length change components could be estimated in a single model that conditioned on all 27 mediational relationships under consideration. Evidence for mediation, and thus suspected causal relationships, was then evaluated considering the methodological strength of each approach.

Nearly one-quarter (0.245 [0.185, 0.325], p < 0.001, Table S4) of the influence of a previous preterm birth on GAD was mediated through the linear cervical length change component as estimated by causal mediation analysis. Thus, the negative association between previous preterm birth and GAD (i.e., total effect) was comprised, in part by the estimated indirect effect, of the negative relationship between previous preterm birth and the linear cervical length change (Table S2), and the positive relationship between this change component and GAD (Table S3). This mediated effect was more pronounced after conditioning on all mediation paths specified in the SEM-based multiple mediation model (i.e., all combinations of maternal risk factors and cervical length change components), indicating an independent influence of a previous preterm birth on GAD through the cervix (Table 3). A mediational relationship was also seen through the MTCL and nonlinear cervical change components, but to a lesser extent (Table 3). These findings imply that the effect of a previous preterm birth on GAD was not only mediated through the cervix (i.e., possibly related to cervical tissue remodeling and/or damage) but may involve other pathways not related to cervical function (e.g., vascular, hormonal, psychological stress pathways).

The estimated effect of parity on GAD was largely mediated by the linear cervical change component as estimated by both the causal mediation analysis and multiple mediation model. In the multiple mediation model the indirect effect was slightly larger than the total effect and, since the direct effect had an opposite sign, this implied that the mediation effect in this case was statistically as large as the total effect. Thus, and since the direct effect was non-significant, nearly all of the parity effect on GAD was mediated by the linear change component. In other words, the protective influence of an increasing number of live births on GAD was found to be entirely transmitted to GAD by a slower decrease in linear change in cervical length.

The interpretation of the mediating roles for pre-pregnancy BMI and previous abortion was less straightforward since the indirect effect (i.e., mediation) for both models was significant, but the total effect of GAD on each maternal risk factor was non-significant. Since the total effect was non-significant there arises the obvious question of what was being mediated yet, there exists statistical explanations that should be considered83. For instance, as described by Kenny and Judd, the statistical power to test a mediation effect is in theory greater than the test for the total effect as a consequence of the increased sample variability of the total effect estimate84. This may be the case for the mediation of previous abortions on GAD through the linear cervical length component as the total effect of previous abortion on GAD would be comprised of both the uncertainty in the mediation path (a*b) and the direct path (c’) thus reducing statistical power in comparison to testing the mediation (i.e., indirect) effect only. An increasing number of previous abortions was related to a more rapid decrease in cervical length (Table S2). Interpretations of the mediational relationship of previous abortion described should consider this measure lumped both spontaneous and induced abortions together and did not record the gestational age at which the abortion occurred.

The non-significant total effect of pre-pregnancy BMI on GAD may require a different interpretation since this coefficient was close to zero and the mediation effect was substantial. This mediation scenario has been referred to as a suppression effect whereby the direct effect and the mediation effect have opposite signs serving to cancel each other out leaving questions in this case on why an influence on pre-pregnancy BMI on GAD was not observed85. Both low and high values of pre-pregnancy BMI have previously been associated with risk for preterm birth86. A suppression effect in this cohort may have emerged since the mean pre-pregnancy BMI tested fell within the overweight range resulting in an overall positive relationship between the observed pre-pregnancy BMI and the linear cervical length component corresponding to a slower decrease in cervical length across pregnancy. Thus, this pathway was likely counteracted by the negative relationship in this cohort between pre-pregnancy BMI and GAD. Although this is still a valid mediation effect, the observation of a suppression effect may be a function of this cohort having higher values of pre-pregnancy BMI. Regardless, the direct effect (c’) would indicate the influence of pre-pregnancy BMI on GAD if the linear cervical length component was not affected by pre-pregnancy BMI.

One potential study limitation was that the number and spacing of participant visits were influenced by the prenatal care needs specific to each pregnancy. This lack of standardization in scheduling may have affected study results in unknown ways. Not surprisingly, the number of participant observations was associated with error variance in LGCA model parameters. Yet, only minimal differences in mean error variance for I, S, and Q were observed, for instance, between term and preterm pregnancies (I: 9.22 vs. 9.39; S: 2.96 vs. 3.12; Q: 0.047 vs. 0.054). Parameterization of the LGCA model was predicated on the binning of cervical measurements into nine 4-week intervals, which would necessarily exclude the last bin (37–40 weeks), at a minimum, from preterm births. Although the absence of observations for preterm participants in the latter bins would not technically be considered missing, as they were not expected. Preterm birth status was not associated with the absence of a cervical length measurement when a study visit could be expected (Fig. S1). Another limitation of this study was the exclusion of subjects who received therapeutic interventions likely to influence cervical length trajectories, due to the absence of critical treatment metadata (e.g., initiation and cessation timing, dosage adjustments, and formulation type). Although this filtering approach may introduce selection bias, it was deemed methodologically appropriate given the low prevalence of treatment cases and the inability to model treatment as a time-varying covariate within our latent growth framework. Future studies with more granular treatment data are needed to explicitly quantify the impact of such interventions on cervical dynamics. Although the findings of this study may not be generalizable to women from other populations or genetic ancestry groups, they could improve screening for a population of women disproportionally affected by health disparities in preterm birth. Finally, mediation models can be used to assess potential causal mechanisms conceptually, but do not prove the proposed causal pathway.

Conclusions

The nuances of these statistical interpretations notwithstanding, the mediation relationships estimated can provide clinical researchers with insights on pursuing more detailed studies to investigate how these maternal risk factors contribute to pathological shortening and effacement of the cervix cervical as an important mechanism of preterm birth risk66. There were four key conclusions. (1) The collective evidence supports that the effect of common maternal characteristics on preterm birth risk was mediated through cervical length change. Specifically, previous preterm birth, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and previous abortion were shown to have unique influences on preterm birth risk that likely operate, in part, through linear changes in the cervix across pregnancy. (2) Cervical change across pregnancy, particularly linear change, was more informative than a single mid-trimester cervical length measurement as a mediator of the relationship between maternal characteristics and preterm birth risk. Furthermore, the magnitude of the mediated effect was observed to be greater in the linear change component relative to the intercept (e.g., MTCL) and the nonlinear change. This suggests that characterizing the overall linear change in cervical length, which can only be obtained from multiple measurements of cervical length across pregnancy, rather than relying on a single MTCL measure, will benefit further research aimed at understanding mechanisms describing the relationship between cervical dysfunction and preterm birth risk. In fact, none of the maternal risk factors were mediated exclusively through the intercept component. (3) While the estimated mediated effects were relatively large compared to the total effect values, there still exists the possibility of other explanatory mechanisms. For instance, these findings imply that the effect of a previous preterm birth on GAD was not only mediated through the cervix (i.e., possibly related to cervical tissue remodeling and/or damage) but may involve other pathways not related to cervical function (e.g., vascular, hormonal, psychological stress pathways). Furthermore, other features of the cervix besides a change in cervical length may also mediate the influence of maternal risk factors. For instance, cervical stiffness and elasticity measured by elastography87 could provide another candidate mechanism and remains an open area of investigation. (4) These results suggest that therapies targeting the maintenance of cervical integrity can mitigate the influence of previous maternal exposures. While the only current therapy recommended to treat women with a short cervix with or without a previous history of preterm birth is administration of vaginal progesterone41, future therapy development could investigate the mechanism as to how these maternal risk factors affect cervical change.
