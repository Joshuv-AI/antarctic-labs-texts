HERAKLES: Hierarchical Skill Compilation for Open-ended LLM Agents
Thomas Carta, Clément Romac, Loris Gaven, Pierre-Yves Oudeyer, Olivier Sigaud, Sylvain Lamprier

Abstract
Open-ended AI agents need to be able to learn efficiently goals of increasing complexity, abstraction and heterogeneity over their lifetime. Beyond sampling efficiently their own goals, autotelic agents specifically need to be able to keep the growing complexity of goals under control, limiting the associated growth in sample and computational complexity. To address this challenge, recent approaches have leveraged hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) and language, capitalizing on its compositional and combinatorial generalization capabilities to acquire temporally extended reusable behaviours. Existing approaches use expert defined spaces of subgoals over which they instantiate a hierarchy, and often assume pre-trained associated low-level policies. Such designs are inadequate in open-ended scenarios, where goal spaces naturally diversify across a broad spectrum of difficulties. We introduce Herakles , a framework that enables a two-level hierarchical autotelic agent to continuously compile mastered goals into the low-level policy, executed by a small, fast neural network, dynamically expanding the set of subgoals available to the high-level policy. We train a Large Language Model (LLM) to serve as the high-level controller, exploiting its strengths in goal decomposition and generalization to operate effectively over this evolving subgoal space. We evaluate Herakles in the open-ended Crafter environment and show that it scales effectively with goal complexity, improves sample efficiency through skill compilation, and enables the agent to adapt robustly to novel challenges over time.

2
Related Work

Neuroscience

The strong abilities of humans to learn new skills as open-ended agents have long been studied in psychology and neuroscience. From learning to crawl on the ground as infants to performing complex sensorimotor operations playing an instrument or cooking, humans continuously acquire increasingly complex sensorimotor skills during their lifetime.
Gottlieb and Oudeyer 2018
explain that this lifelong process is nurtured by curiosity and supported by effective learning of new skills.
Fitts and Posner 1967
proposed a phenomenological model in which a transition occurs from cognitively demanding tasks to more automatic actions. Tsay et al.
[
49
]
expands on this model and proposes three processes: reasoning, when the novice reasons to understand action–outcome relationships; refinement, for optimizing the sensorimotor part to achieve motor goals; and retrieval, when the agent infers the best sensorimotor skills to recall to reach a new goal. Riesenhuber et al.
[
44
]
have also found a hierarchical structure in the brain for life-long object classification and recognition. This capacity is also crucial for open-ended agents which are confronted to new artifacts (created or encountered) during their existence.

Cangelosi et al. 2010
advocates in the context of developmental robotics, in favor of hierarchical learning with language, since the latter has combinatorial properties and generalization properties that would help the former. Although it does not directly try to model the processes described in these previous papers, our work shares strong conceptual similarities with them. In fact, it uses an LLM as a high-level policy guiding a smaller neural network which acts as a low-level policy using only elementary actions. Our method proposed a way to continuously compile the skills learned by the hierarchic agent as a whole into the low-level policy. Thus, the action space on which the high-level policy can act expands, facilitating the exploration and learning process of more complex goals.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning and language

The design or learning of meaningful abstractions at different resolutions in space or time in order to learn tasks more efficiently
[

Nachum et al.
[
36

have shown that the mapping of the observation space to the goal space has a strong influence on the quality of the hierarchical agent. The linguistic space already has generalization and abstraction properties, which are key to exploiting the compositional, relational, and hierarchical structure of an environment
[
29
,
34
,
28
]
. In recent years, the abilities of LLMs to model natural language have been used in HRL setups. They model HL policies decomposing goals into skills treated by specialized LL policies
[
2
,
3
]
. Finally, to avoid the bottleneck of the linguistic description of the environment, used by LLMs, several articles use Visual Language Models (VLMs) to accept both visual and linguistic inputs
[

,
27

. All these methods based on LLMs and VLMs pre-train the LL policy while the HL policy is trained to use it
[

,

or kept frozen

Our method also relies on language to obtain a structured space of skills. We do not rely on an expert-defined skill space as we reuse previously mastered goals as skills for reaching new ones. In addition, our method does not pre-train the LL policy on a fix set of skills.

3.1
Problem statement

Let
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\mathcal{T},G,\Omega,O,R,\gamma)
be a goal-augmented Partially Observable Markov Decision Process, with
S
the state space,
A
the action space,
\mathcal{T}
the transition function,
G
the goal space,
\Omega
the observation space,
O:S\rightarrow\Omega
the observation function that maps states to observations,
R:S\times A\times G\rightarrow\{0;1\}
the sparse reward function and
\gamma\in[0,1]
the discount factor. Here,
G\in{\cal V}^{L_{i}}
is a textual space of at most
L_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
tokens in a given vocabulary
{\cal V}
. In our setting, we consider that
G
always includes instructions corresponding to each action. We also consider a prompting function
\phi:\Omega\times G\rightarrow{\cal V}^{L_{p}}
that transforms any (observation, goal) pair into a textual prompt of
L_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
tokens. Let the goal space
G
be compositional, which means that there exists pairs of goals
(g,g^{\prime})\in G^{2}
, such that
g^{\prime}\prec g
, meaning that the completion of
g^{\prime}
is a prerequisite for the completion of
g
. We assume goal spaces whose the set of precedence relations
g^{\prime}\prec g
defines a hierarchy. For pairs of goals
(g^{\prime},g)
that respect
g^{\prime}\prec g
, one can factor any trajectory
\tau_{g}=\{s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},...,s_{n}\}
that reaches
g
with a trajectory that reaches
g^{\prime}
such that
\tau_{g}=\tau_{g^{\prime}}\cup\{s_{k},a_{k},s_{k+1},a_{k+1},...,s_{n}\}
, with
k>0
. This structure is common for goal spaces generated by open-ended agents, as they tend to reuse previously reached goals to generate new ones
[
54
,
15
,
41
]
.

3.2
The hierarchical policy

Figure 1
:
HERAKLES
is an autotelic hierarchical RL agent which relies on two policies. An LLM-based high-level policy iteratively selects goals for a low-level policy which interacts with the environment through elementary actions to solve them. In particular, the high-level policy only selects goals among the ones with high success probability by learning to estimate the probability of success of the low-level policy on each goal. As
Herakles
masters a goal, the full trajectory (i.e. all elementary actions used to accomplish the sequence of goals produced by the high-level policy) is compiled and learned as a new single skill by the low-level policy. This new skill can then be directly used by the high-level policy.

To exploit the structure of
G
, we consider an HRL agent that can learn and operate at different levels of temporal abstraction. Our agent is modelled as a two-level policy, composed of an HL policy
\pi^{HL}:\phi(\Omega,G)\rightarrow\tilde{G}
that samples skills in a skill space
\tilde{G}\subseteq G
and an LL policy
\pi^{LL}:\Omega\times G\rightarrow A
that samples actions.
\pi^{HL}
operates on a long-term scale with goals
g\in G
that can be achieved by
\pi^{LL}
using several elementary actions
a\in A
. The skill
\tilde{g}\in\tilde{G}
sampled by
\pi^{HL}
is passed to
\pi^{LL}
that executes it (see Figure
1
).
\pi^{LL}
operates on a finer time scale compared to
\pi^{HL}
, allowing for a finer control of the agent trajectory using elementary actions
a\in A
. As
A\subset G
,
\pi^{HL}
can directly guide
\pi^{LL}
using elementary actions
a\in A
:
\forall(a,s)\in A\times S,\;\pi^{LL}(a|s,a)=1
. This property is especially useful when
\pi^{LL}
is learning the goal.

\pi^{HL}
must reach the goal within
N^{HL}
sampled skills and
\pi_{LL}
must execute the skill provided by
\pi^{HL}
within
N^{LL}
steps in the environment. The objective is to find the
(\pi^{HL*},\pi^{LL*})
pair that maximizes
\mathbb{E}_{g\sim G}[\sum_{k}^{T}\gamma^{k}r_{t+k+1}^{g}]
, where
r_{t}^{g}=R(s_{t},a_{t},g)
, the reward obtained at step
t
when the hierarchical agent try to complete the goal
g
.

3.3
Skill space generation

A crucial point of our method is how to construct
\tilde{G}_{k}
at each step
k
of the policy
\pi^{HL}
. Indeed, at the beginning of training,
\tilde{G}
is initialized with the set of elementary actions as basic skills. At that point,
\pi^{LL}
can only complete these elementary actions
A
. Adding skills where
\pi^{LL}
fails would only encourage
\pi^{HL}
to completely stop sampling such skills, which could be an issue later in the training. After
\pi^{LL}
masters a new goal
g
, it can be added to the skill space
\tilde{G}
. Thus, the optimal
\tilde{G}
evolves during training and depends on the state
s_{k}
of the environment. At each step
k
of
\pi^{HL}
, we construct
\tilde{G}_{k}
starting from an empty set and adding to it each goal
g\in G
with a probability:

p_{g}=\max(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{LL}(.|g),s_{k}\sim D_{\pi^{LL}}}[r^{g}|O(s_{k})],\varepsilon_{k}),

with
D_{\pi^{LL}}
the distribution of states generated by
\pi^{LL}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
an exploration term. Thus, if
\pi^{LL}
has a high probability of success for a goal for a given state
s_{k}
, the goal is added to
\tilde{G}_{k}
. As
\pi^{LL}
always successfully executes elementary actions
a\in A
, we have
\forall k,A\subseteq\tilde{G}_{k}
.

To measure the probability of success
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{LL}(.|g),s_{k}\sim D_{\pi^{LL}}}[r^{g}|O(s_{k})]
, we leverage the LLM used by
\pi_{HL}
by learning the parameters
\theta_{k}
of a competence estimator
C_{\theta_{k}}(s_{k},g)\approx\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{LL}(.|g),s_{k}\sim D_{\pi^{LL}}}[r^{g}|O(s_{k})]
, following a strategy similar to that of
[
18
]
. We pass in the LLM a prompt describing the pair
(s,g)\in S\times G
which produces a latent representation on top of its final decoder block for the last token. The representation is passed to a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that outputs the estimated success probability
C_{\theta_{k}}(s,g)
. We train both the
LLM and the MLP by minimizing the binary cross-entropy:
\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{((s,g),r)\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}}\left[BCE(r,C_{\theta_{t}}(s,g))\right]
, with
\mathcal{D}_{t}
a buffer containing the most recent
((s,g),r)
pairs. In practice, we train two separate versions of the same initial LLM using LoRA adapters
[
24
]
: one for
\pi_{HL}
and one for our competence estimator.

3.4
RL training of the HL and LL policies

Figure 2
:
Skill learning and compilation in
Herakles
: Given a goal
g
and a state
s_{0}
, the high-level policy generates its skill space
\tilde{G}_{0}
and samples one skill
\tilde{g}_{0}
. This skill is then sent to the low-level policy which performs
k
elementary actions in the environment to achieve
\tilde{g}_{0}
, leading to state
s_{k}
. Given
s_{k}
and
g
, the high-level policy then generates a new skill space, samples a new skill
g_{k}
and sends it to the low-level policy. This process repeats until
g
has been achieved or the limited number of high-level steps have been reached. This process produces both multiple low-level trajectories (i.e. one per skill sampled by the high-level) and a single high-level trajectory (i.e. the sequence of skills sampled). The high-level policy is trained on the high-level trajectory to improve skill selection. The low-level policy is trained on all the low-level trajectories, conditioned to reach the associated skill. Crucially, we perform
skill compilation
by also training the low-level policy on a trajectory that is the concatenation of all the low-level trajectories and that is conditioned to reach the high-level policy’s goal
g
. Doing so, our low-level policy progressively internalizes the sequence of elementary actions required to directly solve
g
.

Herakles
simultaneously learns
\pi^{HL}
and
\pi^{LL}
, respectively the HL and LL policies (see Figure
2
).
\pi^{HL}
is a pre-trained LLM, fine-tuned using RL. It samples skills from the set of skill
\tilde{G_{k}}
, the skill space constructed at each step (see Section
3.3
), using constrained decoding.
\pi^{LL}
is a small, not pre-trained, neural network also trained using RL.

\pi^{HL}
is trained with on-policy RL using trajectories
\{\tau^{HL}_{g}=\{(s_{0},\tilde{g}_{0},r^{g}_{0}),..,(s_{K},\tilde{g}_{K},r^{g}_{K})\}|g\in G,K\in[|0,N^{HL}|]\}
with
\tilde{g}_{K}\in\tilde{G}_{k}
the skill space.

As depicted in Figure
2
,
\pi^{LL}
is trained with off-policy RL using two types of trajectories:

\bullet
Those from when it acts to execute the skill
\tilde{g}\in\tilde{G}
given by
\pi^{HL}
,
\{\tau^{LL}_{(s_{0},\tilde{g})}=\{(s_{0},a_{0},r^{(s_{0},\tilde{g})}_{0}),..,(s_{T},a_{T},r^{(s_{0},\tilde{g})}_{T})\}|\tilde{g}\in\tilde{G},T\in[|0,N^{LL}|]\}
.

\bullet
Those which achieve the goals
g\in G
obtained by concatenating the low-level transitions for each skill selected by the HL policy
\{\tau^{LL}_{g}=\{(s_{0},a_{0},r^{g}_{0}),..,(s_{M},a_{M},r^{g}_{M}\}|g\in G\}
.

As
\pi^{HL}
masters a goal
g
, it is distilled into
\pi^{LL}
.
\pi^{HL}
can then use
\pi^{LL}
to reach

Experiments

4.1
Experimental setup

Environment description

We evaluate our method in Crafter
[
21
]
, a procedurally generated and partially observable Minecraft-like 2D environment that enables collecting and creating a set of artifacts organized along an achievement
tree (see Appendix
A.1
). Most of the goals require the agent to reuse discovered or generated artifacts from previous goals. For instance, to "make a wood pickaxe", the agent needs to have built a crafting table. Consequently, Crafter’s goal space is a good candidate for simulating goal spaces produced by open-ended agents
[
13
]
. In this work, we assume an open-ended goal generator already produced the considered goals, and focus on an efficient learning paradigm for achieving goals of various difficulty.

Similarly to
[
11
]
we modify the Crafter game in three ways. First, we convert the
‘do’
action that originally represents several types of actions (meaning
‘attack’
in front of a zombie but
‘eat’
in front of a plant) into distinct actions (such as
‘eat plant’
or
‘attack zombie’
). This disambiguates these elementary actions for LLMs. Second, we remove enemies such as zombies or skeletons. More details are given in Appendix
A.2
. Finally, we make the environment generate textual description of the scene. Thus, an observation
o\in\Omega
is a tuple made from visual and textual observations
o=(o_{v},o_{text})
(see Appendix
A.4
for more details).
\pi^{HL}
receives the linguistic description
o_{text}
and
\pi^{LL}
uses the visual observation
o_{v}
.

Goal Sampler

In Crafter, the agent is not assigned a single main goal but is instead encouraged to explore and master as many goals as possible. These goals are structured within an achievement tree (Appendix
A.1
). The difficulty of a goal
g\in G
is not fixed, but conditioned on the current state of the environment
s_{k}
. For example, the goal
’place table’
becomes substantially easier if the agent already possesses wood in its inventory.

To enable the agent to select a goal
g
conditionally to a state
s_{T}
at a time step

that maximizes the hierarchical policy competence across the large goal space
G
of Crafter, we draw inspiration from methods based on Learning Progress (LP). These approaches prioritize goals that are expected to yield the greatest future improvement through practice
[
38
]
. Specifically, we implement the MAGELLAN algorithm
[
18
]
, which equips LLM agents with the ability to predict their current competence and learning progress online. Moreover, MAGELLAN enhances LP estimation by leveraging semantic relationships between goals, thus facilitating generalization across structurally related skills. Implementation details of MAGELLAN within our framework are provided in Appendix
G
.

\pi^{HL}

. At each step of the high-level we sample a skill inside the current skill space using constrain decoding. For

4.2
Heterogeneous goal space

A characteristic of Crafter’s goal space is its heterogeneous level of difficulty and compositionality. Such goal spaces are typically difficult to learn, as the number of elementary actions to chain for solving goals explodes as the difficulty of goals grows.

To investigate how
Herakles
’ performance scales with the difficulty of goals, we measure the number of high-level steps necessary for an agent to master a goal depending on its difficulty. In this experiment, a goal is mastered when the agent obtains a success rate superior to
0.8

materials
,

{table, furnace}
and

object
are required to reach it. For instance, the goal
’place table’
that requires
2

wood
and
1

place

table
is considered easier than
’make wood pickaxe’
which requires
3

collect

wood
,
1

place

table
, and
1

craft

wood pickaxe
. We expand on our measure of the difficulty of goals in Appendix

.

Figure
4

4
goals of increasing difficulty. Only

5
Conclusion

We introduced
Herakles
, a hierarchical framework for open-ended autotelic agents in which high- and low-level policies are trained concurrently. As the agent learns to solve increasingly complex goals, mastered behaviours are compiled into the low-level policy as reusable skills. This dynamic expansion of the low-level skill space enables the high-level policy to effectively operate in heterogeneous and evolving goal spaces. Through extensive experiments in the Crafter environment, we demonstrated that
Herakles
(i) scales nearly linearly with goal difficulty, (ii) leverages compiled skills for efficient goal acquisition, and (iii) exhibits strong generalization to novel challenges.

Despite these promising results, some limitations remain. The high-level policy can only deal with textual goal descriptions, but could be extended to visual goals using Vision Language Models
[
33
]
. Besides, we would like to avoid the computational overhead of retraining the low-level policy on all previously learned skills. Finally, the challenge of compiling complex hierarchical behaviours into low-level skills warrants further investigation. Moreover, our current setting assumes access to a goal space produced by an open-ended agent. A promising direction for future work is integrating
Herakles
with goal generation methods
[
15
,
41
]
, and useful skills identification, to address increasingly complex and autonomous open-ended environments.

Environment detail

A.1
Achievement tree

We build upon the Crafter environment introduced by
Hafner 2022
, introducing several modifications to emphasize learning in a heterogeneous and compositional goal space. Specifically, our environment features an achievement tree structure that explicitly defines the prerequisite relationships among goals, thereby encouraging agents to discover and exploit goal dependencies. The environment comprises
11
distinct goals, categorized into three types: (i)
5
movement-related goals requiring navigation to specific locations; (ii)
3
collection-based goals that require prior construction of the appropriate tools —e.g., collecting stone and coal is only feasible after crafting the corresponding equipment; and (iii)
3
crafting goals, which are achievable only when the agent possesses a sufficient quantity of the required resources in its inventory. This design introduces structured complexity that challenges agents to plan and act over extended temporal horizons.

A.2
Modifications

We modify the original Crafter environment to isolate and emphasize the compositional aspects of goal-directed behaviour, removing elements primarily associated with survival dynamics. In particular, we eliminate hostile entities such as zombies and skeletons, as well as the need for the agent to eat or drink, thereby reducing perturbation-related distractions. To improve the agent’s navigational capabilities, we augment the goal space with
"go to
X
"
objectives. These are always feasible for
X\in{\text{tree, stone, coal}}
, but may not be realizable for
X\in{\text{table, furnace}}
depending on the current world state, introducing ambiguity that poses a challenge for the low-level success rate estimator (see Appendix
F
). To maintain computational efficiency, we limit the goal space to include up to the
"collect coal"
objective, which suffices to capture the dynamic nature of compositional task learning. Furthermore, we enrich the observation space by integrating a captioning module (see Appendix
A.4
). The hierarchical policy operates under temporal constraints: the high-level controller is allowed up to
64
steps (i.e., skill invocations), each of which may be executed by the low-level controller in up to
128
steps. To enable multi-goal episodes while ensuring bounded episode length, we cap the environment at
155
high-level steps before a reset occurs.

A.3

We introduce a principled method to quantify the difficulty of individual goals within our environment. The difficulty is defined with respect to an environment in its initial (reset) state, ensuring consistency across evaluations. To compute this metric, we decompose each successful trajectory into a sequence of intermediate subgoals, categorized as
"collect
X
"
,
"make
X
"

"place
X
"

"collect"

1

"make"
and
"place"

0.5

"go to wood"
and
"collect wood"

"chop"
action. Similarly,
"go to
X

A.4
Observations

The modified Crafter environment used in our experiments generates both a standard visual observation and a structured textual description. The visual observation consists of an RGB image of dimension
3\times 32\times 32
. The textual description acts as a sparse captioning of the visual input and is composed of multiple informative components, each providing structured context relevant to the agent’s decision-making. Specifically, the textual description includes:

•

A general sentence describing the overall nature of the game.

•

The agent’s current goal, along with the number of remaining high-level steps to achieve it.

•

The player’s current position in the environment.

•

A description of all visible elements in the field of view, each identified by type and relative position using cardinal directions (e.g., "a tree is three steps north"). Each type of element is mentioned only once, even if multiple instances are visible.

•

A description of the block directly in front of the player.

•

A list of items currently in the player’s inventory.

•

The positions of environment elements that have been placed by the player.

•

The available elementary actions.

•

If applicable, the list of admissible skills that can be invoked by the high-level policy.

•

The last action executed by the agent.

This detailed textual observation allows the high-level policy to operate with a semantically rich input space, facilitating more informed and context-aware decision-making. An example of such textual observation is given in Figure

HERAKLES implementation

Figure 10:
HERAKLES architecture.

To instantiate the hierarchical agent described in Section
3.2
, we employ a large language model (LLM)-backboned agent as the high-level policy (detailed in Appendix
D
) and a collection of lightweight neural networks as the low-level policy (see Appendix
E
). Each low-level network is specialized in executing a single skill, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting across the skill set. The Figure
10

In accordance with the formulation in Section

\forall(a,s)\in A\times S,;\pi^{LL}(a|s,a)=1
. Consequently, when the high-level policy selects a skill that corresponds to an elementary action—e.g.,

\tilde{G}

F
and
G

Each module—the high-level policy, the individual low-level networks, the success rate estimator, and MAGELLAN—is trained independently using separate Adam optimizers. Detailed hyper-parameter configurations for each optimizer are provided in Appendix

Appendix D
High-level policy

D.1
High-level policy architecture

The high-level policy architecture in
Herakles
builds upon GLAM
[
8
]
, which employs an actor-critic framework grounded in a large language model (LLM) backbone. The value function is implemented as a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with
1024
sigmoid-activated units, applied to the final hidden state of the decoder. Both the policy and the value head utilize shared LoRA adapters
[
24
]
, enabling parameter-efficient fine-tuning of the LLM.

In contrast to GLAM, skill selection in
Herakles
is achieved through constrained decoding over the set of admissible skills
\tilde{G}
. Instead of sampling tokens from the entire vocabulary, token generation is restricted to sequences that correspond to valid skills in
\tilde{G}
, ensuring both syntactic validity and semantic precision.

Training of the high-level policy is conducted using
poad

[
56
]
, an algorithm that adapts PPO to operate at the token level for language agents. The policy is updated every
2496
high-level transitions, using
poad
with
4
training epochs per update cycle. In addition to the
poad
loss we add a penalty term based on Kullback-Liebler divergence:

\beta_{KL}KL(\pi^{HL},\pi^{HL}_{original}),

with
\beta_{KL}
an hyper-parameter controlling for the importance of this penalty term. It ensures that the high-level policy does not drift too much from the original policy and retains some plasticity, allowing it to adapt to the apparition of new usable skills during training.

Low-level policy architecture.

The low-level policy in
HERAKLES
is designed to be lightweight compared to the high-level language model and operates directly on raw sensory input, specifically RGB observations from the environment. To extract features and generate elementary actions, we adopt the architecture proposed by
Moon et al. 2023
. This model employs a two-block ResNet
[
23
]
with channel dimensions
[64,128,128]
to encode visual information. The resulting feature map is flattened and passed through two fully connected layers of sizes
1024
and
128
, respectively. The final embedding is then fed into a sigmoid-activated output layer of dimension
16
to produce a probability distribution over the discrete space of elementary actions. A low-level policy architecture diagram is given in Figure
14
. Each low-level agent contains approximately
2
million parameters, which constitutes only
0.03\%
of the total parameter count of the high-level policy, ensuring computational efficiency.

E.2
Low-level training

We reimplement the Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) algorithm
[
39
]
to train the low-level policy in
HERAKLES
. AWR is selected for its simplicity and robustness, as well as its flexibility in integrating heterogeneous data sources into a unified replay buffer. Specifically, we leverage two types of transitions: (i) those collected from the low-level policy executing a given skill to enable continual skill refinement, and (ii) transitions sampled from successful trajectories of the hierarchical agent, used to compile successful behaviour into low-level policies.

This dual use of transitions allows for multiplicative relabeling, akin to the principle of hindsight experience replay (HER)
[
4
]
. For instance, a transition such as
"facing a tree and chopping wood"
can simultaneously serve to reinforce the skill
"collect wood"
and contribute to the higher-level objective
"place table"
. This mechanism enables the low-level agent to acquire new skills by imitating composite hierarchic behaviours, while also refining its competence on previously known skills.

We employ sparse binary rewards to signal the success of skill execution and goal achievement. In the case of failed trajectories—i.e., when a skill is not completed within the
128
-step budget—we do not assign a reward of zero. Instead, we use the value predicted by the critic as a proxy reward, effectively smoothing the discontinuity introduced by the fixed time limit and providing a more informative learning signal. We update a low-level policy for the skill
g
every time
2496
new transitions have been added to the corresponding buffer.

E.3
Ablation of the AWR buffer size

We perform an ablation study on the size of the replay buffer used to train the low-level policy with the AWR algorithm. As illustrated in Figure
15
, a larger buffer capacity, accommodating up to
100{,}000
transitions, consistently yields slightly improved performance compared to smaller buffers limited to
25{,}000
or
50{,}000
transitions. This improvement can be attributed to the increased diversity of experiences available during training, which enhances generalization across skills. Based on this observation, we adopt a buffer size of
100{,}000
transitions in all our experiments.

F.2
Training of the low-level success rate estimator

The low-level success rate estimator predicts the probability of successful skill execution given an observation. It is built upon an LLM backbone, from which we extract the final hidden state of the decoder. This representation is passed through two SiLU-activated hidden layers of size
1024

[
14
]
, followed by a sigmoid-activated output layer of dimension
1
, yielding a scalar success probability.

During execution, whenever the high-level policy selects a skill, we record a tuple
(\text{obs}_{\text{ll\_sr\_estimator}},\text{skill},\text{outcome})
, where
outcome
is a binary indicator of whether the skill was successfully executed by the low-level policy. To augment training data and enhance the estimator’s ability to interpret rich observations, we assume that starting from any of the initial
10\%
of states visited in a trajectory would not alter the success outcome. Accordingly, we collect
12=\lfloor 0.1\times 128\rfloor
such tuples per skill execution.

These transitions are aggregated during each high-level data collection cycle (consisting of
2496
high-level steps; see Appendix
D
) and stored in a buffer of size
3
. The estimator is updated every
256
new transitions using a binary cross-entropy loss and trained for a single update epoch per cycle. These two hyper-parameters were selected based on empirical performance.

F.3
Composition of the set of skills

In Section
3.3
, we define the probability of sampling a skill
g
into the admissible skill set
\tilde{G}
at step
k
as:

p_{g}=\max\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{LL}(\cdot|g),\,s_{k}\sim D_{\pi^{LL}}}\left[r^{g}\mid O(s_{k})\right],\varepsilon_{k}\right),

where
\varepsilon_{k}
is an exploration term. We empirically found that
\varepsilon_{k}
should reflect the update frequency of the low-level policy corresponding to skill
g
. The rationale is that a frequently updated low-level policy is more likely to have improved, thus increasing the probability that the associated skill will succeed and should be selected.

We therefore define the exploration term as:

\varepsilon_{k}=\min(\text{update\_frequency\_g},\,0.1),

where
update_frequency_g
denotes the number of times the low-level policy for skill
g
has been updated in the past five high-level data collection cycles. This dynamic exploration term outperforms a fixed value —such as the constant
\varepsilon_{k}=0.1
— in empirical evaluations.

Appendix G
Implementation of MAGELLAN

We reimplemented MAGELLAN from
[
18
]
to make our autotelic goal sampler prioritize goals with maximum Learning Progress (LP) from
G
. Our implementation slightly differs from the original one to match our framework.

First, the setup in MAGELLAN involved a single goal per episode. As a result, their goal space was the combination of all possible initial states and instructions (i.e., the Multi-Armed Bandit had one arm per initial state-instruction pair). Here, multiple goals can be solved within a single episode. Therefore, whenever a goal has to be sampled, MAGELLAN estimates the LP of each goal from
G
only for the current state. While this does not change much from a practical point of view, our bandit is now a contextual Multi-Armed Bandit (i.e., the set of arms never changes but the current state conditions the LP estimation).

Second, instead of storing a single entry per goal (i.e. the goal, the state it was sampled from, and its associated outcome) in MAGELLAN’s dataset (
\mathcal{D}
in their paper), we store multiple copies of the same goal-outcome pair with different states seen during the trajectory. In particular, we store the first
N^{HL}*0.1=6
states observed by the high-level policy during the trajectory. This provides richer information for a goal and helps foster generalization.

Then, as a possibly varying number of entries are added to the dataset for each goal (i.e. up to
6
), MAGELLAN’s update frequency is now calculated based on the number of entries added instead of goals sampled. We perform this update every
128
new entries. Moreover, instead of keeping a dataset of fixed size, we use a dataset of varying size storing the entries from the last
3
data collection phases of the high-level policy (i.e.
2496
high-level steps, see Appendix
D
).

For the buffer of weights (named
\mathcal{B}
in MAGELLAN), we only keep the last
3
success rate estimator copies. Finally, we use an exponential decay for
\epsilon
with a rate of
3.34
. All the other hyper-parameters not mentioned in the section are kept the same as in the original implementation of MAGELLAN.

Appendix H
Baselines details

H.1
FuN

We implemented FeUdal Networks (
FuN
) a hierarchical RL architecture with two components: a Manager and a Worker, both modeled as recurrent networks. The Manager operates at a lower temporal resolution, producing directional goals
g_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a learned latent state space. The Worker receives these goals and produces elementary actions at every time step.

Observations
x_{t}
are encoded into latent states
z_{t}
via a shared perceptual module. The Manager computes a latent embedding
s_{t}
, and generates
g_{t}
using a dilated LSTM that supports long-range dependencies. The Worker pools recent goals, maps them into a low-dimensional space via a projection
\phi
, and combines them with action embeddings
U_{t}
to produce the policy:

\pi_{t}=\text{SoftMax}(U_{t}\,\phi\left(\sum_{i=0}^{c-1}g_{t-i}\right))

The Manager is trained using a
transition policy gradient
to align
g_{t}
with advantageous latent transitions
s_{t+c}-s_{t}
, while the Worker is trained via intrinsic reward based on goal-following:

r^{I}_{t}=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^{c}\cos(s_{t}-s_{t-i},g_{t-i})

and optimizes a weighted sum of intrinsic and extrinsic rewards.

In the original
FuN
implementation, only environment observations were passed to the perceptual module, as the evaluated environments were not goal-conditioned. In our version, we concatenate an embedding of the goal to the output of the perceptual module. These goal embeddings are generated using the same language model employed for HERAKLES (Mistral 7B v0.3), enabling the agent to generalize to goals it has not encountered during training.
