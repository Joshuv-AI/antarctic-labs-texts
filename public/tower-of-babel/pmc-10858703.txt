Comparison of ultrasound-guided paravertebral block versus erector spinae plane block for postoperative analgesia after percutaneous nephrolithotomy – A randomised, double-blind, controlled study
Authors: Prajna Pandit Khot, Sameer N. Desai, Sushmitha P. Bale, Bhuvanesh N. Aradhya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Paravertebral block (PVB) is the regional anaesthesia of choice for percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). Erector spinae plane block (ESPB) is also effective for the same. This study aims to compare the analgesic efficacy and ease of performing PVB or ESPB for PCNL surgery.Methods:This study was conducted in 60 patients undergoing PCNL, who were randomised to Group P (n = 30; received ultrasound-guided [USG] PVB) and Group E (n = 30; received USG ESPB) after general anaesthesia. Blocks were administered at T10 level on the side of the surgery using 20 ml of 0.25% bupivacaine. The trachea was extubated at the end of surgery. The primary outcome was analgesia duration, and secondary outcomes were postoperative pain scores, analgesic consumption, ease of block performance, time taken to perform the block and complications between the two groups. Continuous variables were compared using an independent sample t-test, and categorical variables were analysed using Pearson's Chi-square test.Results:Demographic variables were comparable in both groups. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) time of first rescue analgesia in Group P and Group E were 16.6 (20.4)(95% confidence interval [CI]: 9.02–20.32) h and 16.3 (21.8) (95% CI: 8.17–24.51) h, respectively (P = 0.95). The postoperative pain scores and number of doses of analgesics used were comparable between the groups (P > 0.05). The time taken to perform PVB was much longer compared to the time taken to perform ESPB (P = 0.01).Conclusion:USG PVB and ESPB were equally effective for postoperative analgesia for PCNL surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is commonly accompanied by severe pain and discomfort postoperatively, which necessitates the administration of opioids.[12] General anaesthesia (GA) along with regional anaesthesia techniques such as paravertebral block (PVB), intercostal nerve block, erector spinae plane block (ESPB) and epidural block are used to provide postoperative analgesia for PCNL surgery.[34]

A meta-analysis showed that PVB effectively provides postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing renal surgery, especially PCNL surgery.[5] Recent studies have shown that ESPB also effectively provides adequate analgesia postoperatively after PCNL surgery.[146] A recent meta-analysis assessing the postoperative analgesic effects of PVB versus ESPB concluded that the postoperative analgesic efficacy of PVB and ESPB varies with the surgical site.[7] There are no studies directly comparing the efficacy of ESPB with PVB for analgesia following PCNL surgery.

The primary aim of this study was to compare the duration of analgesia following ultrasound-guided (USG) PVB and ESPB in patients undergoing PCNL surgery. The secondary objectives were postoperative pain scores, analgesic consumption, ease of block performance, time taken to perform the block and complications between the two groups.

METHODS

This randomised controlled study was performed in a tertiary care hospital from November 2019 to August 2021. After approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (vide approval number SDMIEC: 186:2019 dated 18 November 2019), the trial was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry-India (vide registration number CTRI/2021/01/030351, www.ctri.nic.in). The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki, 2013 and is reported as per Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Patients of the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I–III, aged 18–70 years, undergoing elective PCNL surgery were recruited. Patients with contraindications for local anaesthetics, severe psychiatric disorders, infection at the injection site and those with renal disorders were excluded from the study. Informed written consent was taken from all the patients for participation in the study and use of the patient data for research and educational purposes. All the patients underwent thorough preoperative evaluation.

Patients were randomly allocated to two groups using a computer-generated random number table. The allocation was concealed by utilising serially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Patients in Group E received USG ESPB, and patients in Group P received USG- PVB. The patients were blinded for group allocation as the blocks were administered under GA. Two independent anaesthesiologists performed the block in the operative room as per randomisation and were not involved in further patient management or data collection. Postoperative pain scores were assessed by a different anaesthesiologist who was not present in the operating room.

Intraoperative monitoring consisted of pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) monitoring and capnography, and the baseline parameters were noted. GA was induced with intravenous (IV) fentanyl 2μg/kg, propofol 2mg/kg and vecuronium 0.1mg/kg, and the airway was secured with an endotracheal tube. Anaesthesia was maintained with isoflurane in 50% nitrous oxide in oxygen to maintain a minimum alveolar concentration value between 1 and 1.3. All the patients received IV ondansetron 4mg after induction of GA and IV dexamethasone 4mg towards the end of surgery before extubation. After GA, the patients were positioned in lithotomy, and a ureteric stent was placed. Then, they were turned to the prone position. In the prone position, patients received either USG PVB or ESPB as per their allocation using a high-frequency linear probe (HFL 38) resonating at 6–13 MHz in the multi-beam mode (SonositeM turbo; FUJIFILM Sonosite, Inc., Bothell, WA, USA). Two anaesthesiologists performed all the blocks and at least 10 USG PVB and ESPB procedures each. For patients in Group P, the spinous process of the 10th thoracic vertebra was located under the USG linear probe. Under aseptic precautions, a 22-G short-bevelled needle was inserted under ultrasonographic guidance using the in-plane approach and was directed medially between the transverse process (TP) and the pleura. The needle traversed the superior costotransverse ligament and entered the paravertebral space, where 2 ml of normal saline was injected. The needle tip's position in the paravertebral space was confirmed, noting the pleura being pushed down. Then, 20 ml of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected into the paravertebral space under USG visualisation. For patients in Group E, the USG linear probe was placed parallel to the vertebral axis at the level of the 10th rib. A 22-G short-bevelled needle was inserted towards the trapezius, erector spinae and TP of the 10th thoracic vertebra using the in-plane technique in a cephalad to caudal direction. After the needle made contact with TP, the plane was confirmed by injecting 2ml of saline and depositing 20ml of 0.25% bupivacaine.

The ease of performing the block was graded by the anaesthesiologist performing the block using a 5-point Likert scale as follows:[8] very difficult = 1, difficult = 2, neutral = 3, easy = 4, very easy = 5. The total time taken to perform the block was considered as the time from scanning for the area with the ultrasound probe and needle insertion (time needed for asepsis, draping and the ultrasound probe preparation was excluded) till the deposition of local anaesthetics in the desired space.

After the block, surgical incision for PCNL was carried out about 10 min later. If any increase in either heart rate (HR) or NIBP >20% of baseline was observed, the block was considered ineffective, and IV 0.5μg/kg fentanyl was given. The total amount of IV fentanyl used was noted. After surgery, the trachea was extubated, and patients were monitored in the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) at 10 min, 30 min and 1 hfor HR, NIBP and pain scores. Later, the patients were shifted to the ward and assessed for pain at 2, 6, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h postoperatively. IV tramadol 50mg was given two times a day, started just before extubation, to all the patients to provide baseline postoperative analgesia. Postoperative pain scores were assessed using a numerical rating scale (NRS) from 0 to 10. Postoperative HR, blood pressure (BP) and pain scores were noted. Patients with a pain score of ≥3 were given IV paracetamol 1 g (up to three doses/day). If pain persisted after 30 min of paracetamol injection, then IV diclofenac 75 mg was given slowly (up to two doses/day). Analgesic doses were used in 48 h, and complications of the block were noted.

The study's primary outcome was the duration of analgesia, which was noted as time for the first dose of additional analgesic. Secondary objectives were postoperative pain scores and analgesic consumption, which were recorded up to 48 h postoperatively, ease of block performance and time taken to perform the block, which was noted during the block performance. An earlier study on PVB indicated that the mean (standard deviation [SD]) duration of analgesia following PVB for PCNL was 15 (3.9) h.[9] For a 20% change in duration of analgesia with 80% power and an α-error of 0.05, we calculated that 27 patients were needed in each group. Considering possible dropouts, we planned to include 30 patients in each group.

Data was entered and analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 20 for Windows (International Business Machines Corp, Chicago IL, USA) as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables (duration of analgesia, time taken to perform the block, duration of surgery, age, weight, height) were compared using an independent sample t-test and are presented as mean (SD) (95% confidence interval [CI]). Categorical variables (ease of block performance, complications, gender distribution) were analysed using Pearson's Chi-square test, and the data is presented as numbers and percentages. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant. The Kaplan–Meier Survival curve was used to assess the time for the first analgesic drug used.

RESULTS

A total of 60 patients were randomised for the study, and all of them completed the protocol and were included in the analysis [Figure 1]. The demographic parameters and duration of surgery were comparable between the two groups [Table 1]. The time of first analgesia and total number of doses of analgesics were similar between the two groups (P > 0.05) [Table 2]. The NRS scores compared between the groups up to 48h postoperative period were comparable (P > 0.05) [Figure 2]. Eight patients in Group P and nine in Group E did not need rescue analgesia up to 48 h postoperatively. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve for the two groups showed that the time of first analgesia for 50% of the patients (Group P and Group E with 23.9 (4.3) h and 23.8 (4.8) h, respectively, P = 0.88 by log-rank test for equality in survivor function) was comparable between the groups [Figure 3]. Block was effective in all the patients; hence, none needed intraoperative fentanyl supplementation.

Ease of performing the block, as rated by the anaesthesiologist, was significantly more difficult for PVB than ESPB [Table 2]. The time taken to perform the block was significantly more for PVB when compared to ESPB [Table 2]. There was no significant difference between the groups for haemodynamic parameters like HR and BP. None of the patients in either group had significant complications like pneumothorax.

DISCUSSION

We found that ESPB and PVB were equally effective for postoperative analgesia for patients undergoing PCNL surgery, but ESPB was much easier and quicker to perform than PVB.

Previous studies for USG PVB versus ESPB for postoperative analgesia in modified radical mastectomy noted comparable time for the first analgesic requirement.[1011] Previous studies comparing USG PVB for PCNL noted that the time for first rescue analgesia varied between 2 and 8.1 (5.1) h.[1213] Another study indicated that the time for first rescue analgesia in patients receiving PVB with plain 0.5% bupivacaine was 7.93 (1.44) h and with 0.5% bupivacaine and clonidine was 13.46 (1.38) h, compared to 4.46 (0.86) h when no block was given.[14] The time for first rescue analgesia requirement was 16.6 (20.4) h in our study in the PVB group, which is much higher compared to previous studies where PVB was used as an analgesic for PCNL. All the patients in our study received IV tramadol 50mg twice daily postoperatively for 48 h as a part of multimodal analgesia, which may explain the delayed requirement of first rescue analgesia and lower pain scores compared to the other studies.

Ramachandran et al.[4] compared ESPB to local anaesthetic infiltration of the incision site for postoperative analgesia following PCNL and noted analgesia of 8 h and 30 min, respectively. Kim et al.[15] in their case report, noted very low pain scores (visual analogue score <1) up to 36 h postoperatively and 43 mg of 24-h postoperative morphine consumption in a case of PCNL where ESPB was used to provide analgesia. Other randomised controlled studies have noted lower intraoperative fentanyl consumption, longer time to first use of patient-controlled analgesia, lower rescue morphine consumption over 24 h and higher patient satisfaction scores when ESPB was used along with GA compared to the control group.[1] Results of these studies concur with our findings, such as very low pain scores up to 48 h postoperatively, prolonged duration of analgesia and reduced analgesic requirement in the ESPB group.

Many authors opine that practitioners perceive PVB as a challenging technique with a steep learning curve and a risk of complications such as pneumothorax.[16] A French nationwide survey on anaesthesiologists suggested that technical factors like complexity, risk involved, and time consumption for PVB are the main barriers to the widespread use of PVB.[17]We realised that USG identification of PVB space is easier, but reaching the space is technically very challenging. Previous studies have concluded that the learning curve for mastering PVB is higher when compared to ESPB.[1819] Previous studies also have reported higher time needed for the performance of PVB when compared to ESPB (8.18 (2.42) min vs. 4.39 (1.2) min, respectively).[18] A meta-analysis of USG-guided PVB versus ESPB for various surgeries showed that block performance time to complete ESPB was shorter than for PVB (mean duration =4.05, 95% CI: 2.95 to 5.14), which was highly significant.[7]

The drawback of the present study could be that anaesthesiologists in our research had performed a minimum of 10 USG PVB and ESP blocks each, which may be adequate to learn ESPB but may not be enough for PVB, as previous studies have shown that PVB has a longer learning curve. We do not perform a high number of USG-guided PVB for analgesia routinely. Others opine that in centres where PVB is practised in high numbers, they find it easier to perform the block and have lower complication rates. Hence, subjective ease of performance can be related to the several procedures performed.

CONCLUSION

We conclude that both USG PVB and ESPB are equally effective and can be used as postoperative analgesia for PCNL surgery, but ESPB is much quicker and easier to perform when compared to PVB.
