Ultra-deep vs. standard swept-source optical coherence tomography for posterior vitreous detachment assessment: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Zhuangling Lin, Kai Gao, Rebiya Tuxun, Guanli Zhu, Yuan Ma, Yuan Liu, Baoyi Liu, Runping Duan, Zhuojun Xu, Yang Meng, Yaping Liu, Ziye Chen, Chin-Ling Tsai, Lan Jiang, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy of ultra-deep mode of swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) for evaluating posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) remains unknown. The study aimed to compare the ultra-deep mode (scan length: 26 mm, scan depth: 12 mm) and standard mode (scan length: 16 mm, scan depth: 3 mm) of SS-OCT in PVD assessment.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients diagnosed with epiretinal membrane or macular hole at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center between May 2023 and August 2024, encompassing both affected and contralateral fellow eyes. All eyes underwent two SS-OCT scanning modes (the ultra-deep mode and standard mode) with 18-line radial scans centered on the fovea. PVD staging consistency was compared between the two modes, as well as the visibility of the detached posterior hyaloid membrane (PHM) and Weiss ring in cases of complete PVD.ResultsA total of 178 eyes from 100 patients were analyzed. The inter-mode agreement for PVD staging was high (Kappa =0.981, P<0.001), with 100.0% agreement in diagnosing complete PVD. Among 107 eyes with complete PVD, detached PHM was detected in 98.1% using the ultra-deep mode versus 47.7% using the standard mode (P<0.001). The ultra-deep mode visualized completely detached PHM in more quadrants than the standard mode, particularly in the inferior quadrants (98.1% vs. 44.9%, P<0.001). Weiss ring was observed at a higher rate with the ultra-deep mode compared to the standard mode (15.9% vs. 3.7%, P=0.005).ConclusionsThe ultra-deep mode and standard mode of SS-OCT showed high consistency in PVD staging. The ultra-deep mode provided superior visualization of completely detached PHM, thus enhancing diagnostic certainty for complete PVD.

Introduction

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is an age-related process involving the separation of the posterior vitreous cortex (PVC) from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) (1). It arises as a consequence of vitreous liquefaction and the progressive weakening of vitreoretinal adhesion (1). PVD is associated with vitreoretinal interface (VRI) disorders, such as vitreomacular traction (2), macular hole (MH) (3), epiretinal membrane (ERM) (4) and retinal breaks (5). These conditions are associated with distinct stages of PVD.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has been demonstrated to be an effective examination for PVD, particularly in the identification of localized partial PVD (6). However, the greatest challenge for OCT is distinguishing complete PVD from completely attached vitreous, largely due to the limited depth of field of current clinical OCT devices (7). The imaging depth of spectral-domain OCT ranges from 1.5 to 2.5 mm. Swept-source OCT provides a scanning depth of 2.5–3.5 mm owing to a longer wavelength of 1,050–1,060 nm. Nevertheless, the vitreous chamber depth of a normal adult eye is approximately 15–17 mm (8,9). Therefore, it is difficult to confirm complete PVD when the posterior hyaloid membrane (PHM) was located far anterior to the retina. In previous studies, the presence of premacular bursa was considered as a completely attached vitreous, whereas the absence of both the premacular bursa and PHM was regarded as complete PVD (6,7,10). However, this criterion may occasionally lead to diagnostic errors if the detached PHM remains undetected because of the limited imaging depth of OCT.

Recently, swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) technology has achieved significant breakthroughs in imaging depth, extending up to 12 mm with high-resolution imaging. In this study, we compared the ultra-deep mode (scan length: 26 mm, scan depth: 12 mm) and standard mode (scan length: 16 mm, scan depth: 3 mm) of SS-OCT for the evaluation of PVD. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2765/rc).

Methods

Study population

The retrospective study consecutively included patients diagnosed with ERM or MH at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center from May 2023 to August 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center (No. 2023KYPJ347), with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective design and use of deidentified data.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 18–85 years with a diagnosis of ERM or MH, as well as their contralateral eyes. Exclusion criteria were: absence of SS-OCT images in either scan mode; concurrent retinal detachment; previous vitrectomy; and poor OCT image quality (signal strength index <6). Data on demographic characteristics, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and SS-OCT imaging (VG200S, SVision Imaging, Henan, China) were retrospectively collected from the medical records of all patients.

Image acquisition

The VG200S incorporates an eye-tracking system based on confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (cSLO) to reduce motion artifacts. It employs a swept-source laser with a central wavelength of approximately 1,050 nm and supports an acquisition rate of 200,000 A-scans per second. Besides, the device provides an axial resolution of 3.8 µm and a lateral resolution of 10 µm.

All eyes underwent SS-OCT imaging after dilation. All imaging was performed by an experienced ophthalmologist (Z.L.). Two scanning modes were performed for each eye, both consisting of 18-line radial scans centered on the fovea. The standard mode had a scanning length of 16 mm and a depth of 3 mm, and the ultra-deep mode had a scanning length of 26 mm and a depth of 12 mm. To maximize imaging depth of the vitreous, the retinal layer was positioned at the bottom of the image. The built-in software optimized OCT image brightness for clear vitreous visualization, with consistent adjustments applied across all images. The SS-OCT imaging procedures are shown in Figure 1A,1B.

PVD staging

PVD was graded according to the 5-stage classification proposed by Johnson as follows: stage 0, absence of PVD; stage 1, perifoveal PVD with residual vitreofoveal adhesion; stage 2, macular PVD with no vitreofoveal adhesion; stage 3, near-complete PVD with only vitreopapillary adhesion remaining; stage 4, complete PVD (1). In this study, complete PVD was defined as the presence of completely detached PHM in any of 18 B-scans, or the absence of both the premacular bursa and completely detached PHM across all 18 B-scans. To eliminate inter-mode grading bias, an experienced examiner first evaluated all images using standard mode, followed by evaluation of all images using ultra-deep mode.

Retinal segmentation and classification of complete PVD

To further assess and compare the visibility of detached PHM and Weiss ring in complete PVD between the two scanning modes, the retina was divided into four quadrants centered on the fovea: superonasal, superotemporal, inferonasal and inferotemporal quadrants (Figure 1C,1D). Eyes diagnosed with complete PVD were categorized into two groups. Group 1 included eyes with detectable completely detached PHM in both scanning modes, whereas Group 2 comprised eyes in which completely detached PHM was visible exclusively under the ultra-deep mode, but not the standard mode.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data as counts and percentages. The agreement in PVD staging between the two scanning modes was evaluated using Kappa correlation analysis. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the visibility of completely detached PHM and Weiss ring in cases of complete PVD between the two scanning modes. In the subgroup of highly myopic eyes with complete PVD, Fisher’s exact test was used to further compare the visibility of detached PHM. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The retrospective study included a total of 178 eyes from 100 patients (59 females, 41 males) with a mean age of 62.8±6.5 years (range, 46–78 years). Among the 178 eyes, 69 (38.8%) were diagnosed with ERM, 41 (23.0%) with MH, and 63 (35.4%) were normal. The remaining 5 eyes (2.8%) included 2 cases of retinoschisis, 2 cases of retinal vein occlusion, and 1 case of glaucoma. Of the 178 eyes, 162 (91.0%) were phakic, and 16 (9.0%) were pseudophakic. Twenty-two eyes (12.4%) had high myopia (spherical equivalent ≤−6 D). The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Ultra-deep versus standard SS-OCT mode in PVD grading

For each eye, PVD stages assessed using the ultra-deep and standard modes of SS-OCT were compared. Identical PVD stages were found in 176 of the 178 eyes (98.9%). The inter-mode agreement for PVD staging was high (Kappa =0.981, P<0.001) (shown in Table 2). Discrepancies in staging between the two modes were noted in 2 eyes (1.1%). In these two eyes, PVD was graded as stage 3 with the standard mode but as stage 0 with the ultra-deep mode (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study compared two scanning modes of SS-OCT for the evaluation of PVD status (ultra-deep mode: 26 mm length, 12 mm depth; standard mode: 16 mm length, 3 mm depth). Our results showed a high agreement between the two modes for PVD staging. In eyes with complete PVD, the ultra-deep mode provided better visualization of the completely detached PHM and Weiss ring. Furthermore, both scanning modes demonstrated that in eyes with complete PVD, the detached PHM was more readily detected in the inferior quadrants than in the superior quadrants.

The greatest challenge for OCT to assess PVD status is distinguishing completely attached vitreous from complete PVD (7), which is attributable to the limited scanning depth of conventional OCT that prevents full visualization of the entire vitreous body. On the one hand, in eyes with complete PVD, the detached PHM may be located far anterior to the retina. With a limited scanning depth (e.g., 3 mm), the detached PHM cannot be visualized regardless of scan length (e.g., 6 mm or 12 mm), making it impossible to distinguish complete PVD from a completely attached vitreous. Only with sufficient scanning depth can the detached PHM be clearly identified, thus enabling a definite diagnosis of complete PVD. On the other hand, in eyes with a completely attached vitreous, the premacular bursa may not be visualized regardless of scanning length (e.g., 6 mm or 12 mm). Only when the scanning depth is sufficient to confirm the absence of a detached PHM can we reliably diagnose a fully attached vitreous.

In a retrospective study of 157 eyes, Kraker et al. compared 6-mm and 16.5-mm SD-OCT scans (Heidelberg Spectralis) for PVD staging (11). The 6-mm scan covered only the macula, whereas the 16.5-mm scan encompassed both the optic disc and macula (11). Complete PVD was defined as the absence of both the premacular bursa and PHM with both modes (11). They reported a high inter-mode agreement in 87.9% (138/157) of the eyes (11). When using a 6mm-scan centered on the fovea, partial PVD may be misdiagnosed as complete PVD, particularly when the premacular bursa or PHM was not visible but the vitreous remains attached to the optic disc. Moon et al. demonstrated that increasing peripapillary scans around the optic disc enhanced the diagnostic capability of SD-OCT for PVD when the vitreous status could not be definitively determined in macular cross-sectional images (12). In our study, both scanning modes covered the optic disc and the macula but differed in imaging depth (3 vs. 12 mm). Owing to our comprehensive evaluation of 18 radial B-scan images per eye, the definition of complete PVD differed slightly from that reported by Kraker et al. (11). In our study, complete PVD was defined as either completely detached PHM visible on any B-scan, or the absence of both the premacular bursa and detached PHM across all 18 B-scans. Our results showed a high agreement (98.9%) between the two scanning modes for PVD staging.

In this study, the detection rate of detached PHM in eyes with complete PVD was 47.7% using the standard mode versus 98.1% with the ultra-deep mode. Although the two modes exhibited excellent concordance (98.9%) in PVD staging, the ultra-deep mode provided enhanced diagnostic certainty for complete PVD. This advantage was particularly evident in eyes with high myopia. Of the 22 highly myopic eyes included, 19 eyes (86.4%) were diagnosed with complete PVD using both modes. Detached PHM was detected in significantly more eyes with the ultra-deep mode than with the standard mode (94.7% vs. 15.8%). Besides, the ultra-deep mode provided superior visualization of Weiss rings in eyes with complete PVD. Conclusively, the wider field of view and longer scanning depth of the ultra-deep mode improved the detection of fully detached PHM and Weiss ring, thereby enhancing diagnostic confidence for complete PVD.

In this study, the ultra-deep mode allowed complete visualization of the premacular bursa in eyes with completely attached vitreous, whereas the standard mode sometimes failed to visualize the entire bursa. This limitation may lead to misinterpretation of a partially visible premacular bursa as a detached PHM, resulting in the misclassification of completely attached vitreous as complete PVD. Thus, the ultra-deep mode can reduce false-positive diagnoses of complete PVD.

We further analyzed the quadrant distribution characteristics of detached PHM in eyes with complete PVD. Both modes demonstrated a higher prevalence of detached PHM in the inferior quadrants than in the superior quadrants, which was potentially attributable to gravitational effects. Moreover, the ultra-deep mode allowed for detection of detached PHM in a greater number of quadrants.

Accurate PVD staging is of paramount clinical importance for the screening, diagnosis and management of vitreoretinal interface disorders. The ultra-deep mode of SS-OCT provides clear visualization of both the vitreous and retina, and improves the efficiency and reliability of complete PVD diagnosis, making it suitable for routine clinical application and outpatient screening. Moreover, in patients with ERM or MH, accurate preoperative PVD staging helps surgeons optimize intraoperative vitreous management. Additionally, PVD status serves as an effective predictor of anti-VEGF treatment response in diabetic macular edema, with complete PVD potentially associated with a more favorable response.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not compare the SS-OCT modes with slit-lamp biomicroscopy or B-scan ultrasonography for PVD assessment. Second, our retrospective study mainly included eyes with ERM, MH and normal eyes. Future prospective studies should enroll a broader spectrum of vitreoretinal interface diseases, such as retinal detachment, diabetic retinopathy and myopic retinopathy, to comprehensively evaluate the clinical utility of the ultra-deep SS-OCT for PVD diagnosis. Thirdly, this study did not evaluate inter-observer agreement for PVD staging. However, our previous study demonstrated excellent inter-observer agreement for PVD grading using the standard mode of SS-OCT (Kappa =0.985, P<0.001) (13). Thus, this limitation is unlikely to substantially influence the main conclusions.

Conclusions

In our study, the ultra-deep and standard modes demonstrated high consistency in PVD staging. However, the ultra-deep mode provided superior visualization of completely detached PHM, thereby improving diagnostic certainty and confidence for complete PVD. The ultra‑deep mode of SS-OCT offers potential added value in the screening, diagnosis and management of vitreoretinal interface disorders.
