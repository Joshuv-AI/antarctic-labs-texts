Cross-Distribution Diffusion Priors-Driven Iterative Reconstruction for Sparse-View CT
Haodong Li, Shuo Han, Haiyang Mao, Yu Shi, Changsheng Fang, Jianjia Zhang, Weiwen Wu, Hengyong Yu

Abstract
Sparse-View CT (SVCT) reconstruction enhances temporal resolution and reduces radiation dose, yet its clinical use is hindered by artifacts due to view reduction and domain shifts from scanner, protocol, or anatomical variations, leading to performance degradation in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. In this work, we propose a Cross-Distribution Diffusion Priors-Driven Iterative Reconstruction (CDPIR) framework to tackle the OOD problem in SVCT. CDPIR integrates cross-distribution diffusion priors, derived from a Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT), with model-based iterative reconstruction methods. Specifically, we train a SiT backbone, an extension of the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture, to establish a unified stochastic interpolant framework, leveraging Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) across multiple datasets. By randomly dropping the conditioning with a null embedding during training, the model learns both domain-specific and domain-invariant priors, enhancing generalizability. During sampling, the globally sensitive transformer-based diffusion model exploits the cross-distribution prior within the unified stochastic interpolant framework, enabling flexible and stable control over multi-distribution-to-noise interpolation paths and decoupled sampling strategies, thereby improving adaptation to OOD reconstruction. By alternating between data fidelity and sampling updates, our model achieves state-of-the-art performance with superior detail preservation in SVCT reconstructions. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CDPIR significantly outperforms existing approaches, particularly under OOD conditions, highlighting its robustness and potential clinical value in challenging imaging scenarios.



I
Introduction

Sparse-view computed tomography (SVCT) has emerged as an effective strategy to reduce radiation dose and accelerate image acquisition. By minimizing the number of required angular projections, SVCT enhances the efficiency of advanced CT systems, particularly in time-sensitive scenarios. The benefits of SVCT are especially pronounced in MediPix detector-based photon-counting CT (PCCT) for extremity imaging
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
, which enables functional and molecular imaging with novel contrast agents. Compared with conventional CT, PCCT acquires rich spectral information but typically suffers from long scan durations and substantial radiation exposure, especially in extremity applications where high spatial resolution is essential. SVCT directly alleviates these limitations by shortening acquisition time and reducing dose, making spectral imaging more practical for longitudinal studies, dynamic contrast-enhanced protocols, and motion-prone clinical settings. Nonetheless, sparse-view imaging introduces severe artifacts and the risk of losing fine structural details in reconstructed images. Consequently, developing robust reconstruction algorithms capable of recovering high-quality images from sparse-view measurements is critical to fully realize the clinical potential of SVCT and to enhance the practical utility of extremity PCCT systems.

Over the years, many methods have been developed to address SVCT reconstruction. Traditional model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) techniques
[
5
,
6
]
have been widely explored. They impose prior assumptions onto the solution space to reduce artifacts, but often struggle with preserving high-frequency details and require extensive tuning. Recently, deep learning has emerged as a powerful alternative. CNN-based methods like FBPConvNet
[
7
]
and RED-CNN
[
8
]
employ paired training data to learn image priors and directly map low-quality reconstructions to their high-quality counterparts. These methods have demonstrated remarkable performance improvements over traditional approaches, mainly due to their strong inductive biases and ability to learn hierarchical features. In addition, Transformer-based networks have recently shown promise in SVCT reconstruction by capturing long-range dependencies and global contexts
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. Although their high computational cost poses challenges for large CT images, efficient variants such as the Restoration Transformer (Restormer)
[
12
]
offer scalable designs to balance local detail preservation with global structural modeling, making them particularly attractive for sparse-view reconstruction.
More recently, the field has witnessed a paradigm shift with the introduction of generative models, particularly denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs)
[
13
]
and score-based generative models (SGMs)
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. These models are capable of sampling from complex image distributions and have shown a strong potential to solve inverse problems. Diffusion-based inverse solvers (DIS), such as DiffusionMBIR
[
17
]
and the Decomposed Diffusion Sampler (DDS)
[
18
]
, further integrate data consistency constraints into the generative process. By incorporating residual correction steps, these methods iteratively guide the sampling trajectory to improve fidelity with the measured projections while maintaining the powerful prior learned by diffusion models.

Despite recent advances, methods based on CNNs, Transformers, and diffusion models remain highly sensitive to distribution shifts between training and testing datasets, which can significantly degrade reconstruction performance.
For example, the AAPM Low-Dose CT Grand Challenge dataset
[
19
]
was acquired on Siemens SOMATOM scanners with abdominal protocols, whereas the Stanford COCA dataset
[
20
]
consists of coronary calcium and chest CT scans with different anatomical focus, acquisition protocols, and unspecified reconstruction parameters. Such variations create out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios where test data deviate from the training distribution.
CNN-based models, while benefiting from strong inductive biases such as translation invariance and local connectivity, inherently lack flexibility in modeling global or long-range dependencies. Their reliance on statistical correlations within the training data makes them particularly vulnerable to domain shifts, often leading to severe performance degradation in unseen clinical settings.
Although transformers and diffusion models are more expressive and capable of capturing richer global and generative priors, their generative priors remain tightly coupled to the training distribution, making them difficult to adapt when confronted with patient-specific anatomical variations or scanner-specific imaging characteristics. Recent efforts, such as Steerable Conditional Diffusion (SCD)
[
21
]
, address this limitation through test-time adaptation that steers diffusion sampling based solely on measurement consistency. Although effective across various imaging modalities, SCD is less suited for CT reconstruction, where changes in the projection matrix or acquisition geometry can significantly degrade performance.

These observations highlight two fundamental challenges in SVCT reconstruction (Fig.
1
):
①
Sparse-view artifacts
: severe streaking and loss of structural fidelity arising from undersampled projections; and
②
Cross-domain distribution differences
: scanner- and protocol-dependent variations that induce OOD scenarios.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a Cross-Distribution Diffusion Priors-Driven Iterative Reconstruction (CDPIR) framework. It is a domain-adaptive diffusion approach for OOD robust SVCT. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, CDPIR uses Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)
[
22
]
to disentangle and exploit two complementary types of priors. During training, each dataset is associated with a distinct conditional token. When the token is randomly dropped and replaced with a null input, the model is forced to learn both conditional and unconditional representations. Thus, CFG helps the diffusion model to establish a principled separation between domain-specific and domain-invariant features.
Domain-invariant features capture anatomy-level structures that remain stable across vendors and imaging protocols. As high-level features, they include global anatomical patterns and structural regularities that neutralize cross-domain distribution shifts and ensure robust generalization across heterogeneous datasets. In practice, these features arise from the unconditional null-token branch of the diffusion prior, providing consistent anatomy-aware guidance independent of scanner-specific variations.
Domain-specific features encode scanner- and protocol-aware information such as textures and edge sharpness. These features act as priors that filter aliasing artifacts caused by sparse-view sampling while restoring high-frequency details that are consistent with domain statistics. When combined with iterative updates and residual correction, domain-specific features further suppress sparse-view artifacts and enhance structural fidelity, yielding more stable and accurate reconstructions.
Hence, CDPIR integrates the strengths of diffusion generative priors and physics-informed iterative reconstruction, enabling artifact-free and generalizable performance across both in-distribution and OOD settings.

Beyond CFG, CDPIR incorporates two additional components. First, it employs a transformer-based generative backbone, the Scalable Interpolant Transformer (SiT)
[
23
]
, which provides global context modeling, scalability, and reduced inductive bias compared with convolutional architectures. Second, it embeds these priors into a residual-guided iterative reconstruction pipeline, built within the unified stochastic interpolant framework, to enforce physics-based data consistency. This iterative correction aligns reconstructions with measured projections, thereby reducing artifacts caused by projection noise, sampling sparsity, and geometric mismatches.
In summary, CDPIR presents a unified and scalable framework in which CFG disentangles domain-specific and domain-invariant priors, SiT enhances global generative modeling, and physics-informed iterative refinement ensures stable and accurate reconstructions.

II
Background and Preliminaries

II-A

VP-SDE

Variance Preserving Stochastic Differential Equation (VP-SDE) is a widely recognized diffusion modeling framework that defines the generative process as the reverse of a continuous-time noising process governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs).
For the VP-SDE, the reverse-time diffusion process is explicitly defined as:

d\mathbf{x}=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{t}\mathbf{x}-\omega_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})\right]dt+\sqrt{\omega_{t}}d\bar{\mathbf{w}},

(1)

where
\omega_{t}
is a time-dependent positive function to control the reverse process, the drift term consists of a deterministic term
-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{t}\mathbf{x}
to pull the data state toward zero and score function
-\omega_{t}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
to guide the denoising process by pushing the state toward regions of high probability, and the diffusion term
\sqrt{\omega_{t}}d\bar{\mathbf{w}}
introduces noise during reverse-time sampling to ensure consistency with the forward process.
This reverse SDE enables the reconstruction of clean data from a noisy prior by iteratively denoising samples. It can generate high-quality samples and effectively reconstruct data distributions.

II-B

Scalable Interpolant Diffusion Transformer (SiT)

The Scalable Interpolant Diffusion Transformer (SiT) adopt the VP-SDE reverse process, and it integrates a stochastic interpolant formulation into the Diffusion Transformer (DiT)
[
24
]
architecture. It offers a unified stochastic interpolant formulation to incorporate stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
[
25
]
, providing improved flexibility in the training and generative modeling process. The reverse diffusion process in SiT can be expressed as:

d\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{t}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt+\sqrt{\omega_{t}}\,d\bar{\mathbf{w}},

(2)

where
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the velocity field at time
t
to guide the deterministic part of the process,
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
is the score function at time
t
to represent the gradient of the log-probability density
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{t}(\mathbf{x})
. The definitions of the velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
is given as

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt},

(3)

where velocity field is the time derivative of
\mathbf{x}
in time
t
.
In practice, the score function
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)
is estimated from the velocity field
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)
within the stochastic interpolant framework. This relationship is given by

\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sigma_{t}^{-1}\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)-\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}\mathbf{x}}{\dot{\alpha}_{t}\sigma_{t}-\alpha_{t}\dot{\sigma}_{t}},

(4)

where
\sigma_{t}
is the standard deviation of the noise process at time
t
,
\alpha_{t}
is a time-dependent scaling parameter,
\dot{\alpha}_{t}
represents the time derivative of
\alpha_{t}
,
\dot{\sigma}_{t}
represents the time derivative of
\sigma_{t}
, and
\mathbf{x}
is the data state at time
t
. The detailed derivation of this relationship can be found in
[
23
]
.

Unlike score-based generative models (SGMs), which directly learn the score function, SiT learns the velocity fields that describe both the direction and rate of data evolution. This formulation provides more explicit control of the transition from noise to data and supports sampling via either the probability flow ODE or the reverse SDE. By leveraging continuous-time interpolants, SiT achieves more accurate modeling of the denoising trajectory, leading to improved reconstruction fidelity compared with the original DiT framework.

III
Method

The SOTA methods such as DDS, can infer clear images effectively when the testing data follows the same distribution as the training dataset. However, they face significant challenges for OOD reconstruction tasks. To address this issue, the CDPIR employs a diffusion transformer framework combined with a residual-guided, projection-based measurement constraint. This design effectively guides the transformer to reconstruct a high-quality CT image from the given input measurements.

III-A

Sparse-view Iterative Reconstruction

SVCT reconstruction is a linear inverse problem and can be mathematically formalized as:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\epsilon,

(5)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is vectorized CT image,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vectorized sinogram,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a system matrix, and
\epsilon
is the adding noise in this process.
n
and
m
represent the dimensions of the image and sinogram, respectively.
The object is to reconstruct
\mathbf{x}
from its measurement
\mathbf{y}
. Because typically
m<n
, the problem is ill-posed. In SVCT, the limited number of projections exacerbates this, resulting in artifacts, blurring, and noise.
To address this issue, traditional iterative CT reconstruction employs a residual-guided process.
In this paper, we employ an improved Adaptive Steepest Descent Projection Onto Convex Sets (ASD-POCS) method with an improved total variation(iTV)
[
26
]
to guide the diffusion model to generate high-quality images.

III-B

Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) in CDPIR

We adopt standard classifier-free guidance (CFG)
[
22
]
to enable controllable conditional generation without an external classifier.
Let
\theta
denote the learnable parameters of the diffusion network. Instead of computing gradients from a pretrained classifier
[
27
]
, CFG jointly trains
a conditional velocity predictor
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)
with conditioning signal
c
, where in this work the signal is represented as a token derived from the class label, and an unconditional predictor
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;\varnothing)
is obtained by randomly replacing
c
with a null token
\varnothing
during training, referred to as
null conditioning
.

At sampling time, we use the standard CFG difference form

\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)=\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;\varnothing)+\mu\big[\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;\varnothing)\big],

(6)

where
\mu\!\geq\!0
is the guidance scale and larger
\mu
strengthens conditioning.
For notational clarity, we define

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{inv}}(\mathbf{x},t)}_{\text{domain-invariant}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;\varnothing),

(7)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{spec}}(\mathbf{x},t;c)}_{\text{domain-specific}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;\varnothing).

(8)

Hence, Eq. (
6
) can be written compactly as

\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)=\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{inv}}(\mathbf{x},t)+\mu\,\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{spec}}(\mathbf{x},t;c)
.
This guided velocity is not directly applied to the sampler. Following the unified stochastic interpolant formulation, it is first mapped to a guided score:

\mathbf{s}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\,\frac{\alpha_{t}\,\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)-\dot{\alpha}_{t}\,\mathbf{x}}{\dot{\alpha}_{t}\,\sigma_{t}-\alpha_{t}\,\dot{\sigma}_{t}},

(9)

where
\alpha_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
are the interpolant coefficients, and
\dot{\alpha}_{t}
and
\dot{\sigma}_{t}
are the their respective time derivatives.
Then, sampling integrates the reverse-time SDE

d\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)\,dt+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\omega_{t}\,\mathbf{s}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)\,dt+\sqrt{\omega_{t}}\,d\bar{\mathbf{w}},

(10)

with diffusion schedule
\omega_{t}=\sigma_{t}
and reverse-time Wiener increment
d\bar{\mathbf{w}}
.
We set the guidance scale to
\mu=1
in all experiments, implying
\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)=\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t;c)
at inference.
The domain-invariant and domain-specific terminology is intended to interpret roles learned during training through null conditioning, rather than to indicate an explicit mixture at testing time.

Model Architecture

[

]
, where the classifier
c
organizes multiple datasets from different modalities, scanners, or pathologies. By randomly dropping
c
with
null
, the model learns both conditional and unconditional priors, which allows the diffusion transformer to disentangle two complementary representations.
The first representation is the domain-invariant feature
v_{inv}
, obtained from the null branch. As shown in Fig.

The second representation is the domain-specific feature

\mathbf{x}_{0}
obtained via reconstruction of measurement
\mathbf{y}
, the CT image is iteratively reconstructed through intermediate steps (
\mathbf{x}_{i}
) until the final reconstruction (
\mathbf{x}_{N}
) is achieved.
Each iteration combines a diffusion transformer denoiser and a data consistency module. The denoiser leverages cross-distribution priors with Cross-Distribution Stochastic Interpolation, providing a flexible interpolant that stabilizes sampling and improves robustness to domain shifts, while the data consistency module enforces fidelity to measured projections for accurate reconstructions. In this work, ASD-POCS is employed as the data consistency method and initialization method, enhancing the accuracy of the reconstruction process.

III-D

Residual-Guided Alternating Solver

This approach defines the minimization problem as follows

\min_{\mathbf{x}}J(\mathbf{x})+\lambda_{1}\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{1},\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\delta_{n},

(11)

where
J(\mathbf{x})
denotes the objective of the reverse diffusion process,
\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
is the Total Variation (TV) regularization,
\lambda_{1}
controls its weight, and
\delta_{n}
specifies the noise tolerance. This constraint enforces consistency with the measurement data while guiding the diffusion prior.

For tractability, Eq. (
11
) can be reformulated into an unconstrained problem:

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}J(\mathbf{x}),

(12)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
balance the data fidelity, TV, and diffusion prior. Introducing an auxiliary variable
\mathbf{r}
yields an equivalent constrained problem:

\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}J(\mathbf{r}),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{x}.

(13)

Eq.(
13
) can be solved by alternatively solving two sub-problems:

\mathbf{r}_{i+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{r}}\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{2}J(\mathbf{r}),

(14)

\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\nabla\mathbf{x}\|_{1}+\lambda_{3}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}_{i+1}\|_{2}^{2},

(15)

where
i
denotes the iteration step, and
\lambda_{3}
balances measurement fidelity with consistency to the diffusion-generated image
\mathbf{r}_{i+1}
.

Diffusion Generative Subproblem Eq.(
14
) optimizes the diffusion model’s objective function while trying to minimize the difference between
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. In our model, this problem is solved by Stochastic Euler-Maruyama Sampler based on the conditional VP-SDE diffusion transformer model Eq.(
10
). Specifically, it will compute the drift and diffusion components using the classifier-free guidance score
\mathbf{s}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}
and velocity
\mathbf{v}^{(\mu)}_{\theta}
.

To solve the data fidelity subproblem Eq. (
15
), we adopt the ASD-POCS framework with the improved total variation (iTV) minimization strategy
[
26
]
. Each outer iteration alternates between a data fidelity update and a TV regularization step, followed by an adaptive fusion scheme that balances the two.

Specifically, given the current estimate
\mathbf{x}^{p}
at the
p^{th}
iteration, we first obtain a data-consistent update
X_{\text{SART}}^{p+1}
using a SART-based projection step. Then,
Q
gradient descent steps are applied to reduce the TV norm, yielding a regularized image
X_{\text{TV}}^{p+1,Q}
. The final update is computed as a convex combination:

\mathbf{x}^{p+1}(\lambda_{TV})=(1-\lambda_{TV})X_{\text{SART}}^{p+1}+\lambda_{TV}X_{\text{TV}}^{p+1,Q},

(16)

where the weight
\lambda_{TV}\in(0,1]
is adaptively determined to ensure projection-domain consistency. Following
[
26
]
,
\lambda_{TV}
is chosen such that the residual error satisfies

\|A\mathbf{x}^{p+1}(\lambda_{TV})-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}=(1-w)\epsilon_{\text{SART}}^{p+1}+w\epsilon^{p},

(17)

with
w=0.8
a fixed weighting factor,
\epsilon_{\text{SART}}^{p+1}
the residual after the SART step, and
\epsilon^{p}
the residual from the previous iteration. This condition can be solved efficiently since the residual is quadratic in
\lambda_{TV}
.

This adaptive fusion ensures a progressive reduction of the TV norm while maintaining controlled data fidelity, offering more stable convergence than conventional ASD-POCS
[
28
]
. This enables better convergence behavior, fewer hyperparameters, and improved stability across different datasets. To further accelerate convergence, we employ an Ordered-Subset SART (OS-SART) strategy during the projection update, where
K
denotes the number of subsets in the algorithm.

IV
Experiment and Result

IV-A

Experimental Details and Parameters

For CDPIR, we adopt the SiT model in the Big configuration (see Table
III
), with a modification to remove the autoencoder in the original design. This change ensures that both training and inference are performed entirely in the image domain, rather than in the latent space as in SiT and DiT. Operating directly on image data allows more explicit manipulation, which can enhance reconstruction quality—particularly for SVCT tasks.

To train CDPIR, we employ a patch size of 2, yielding an overall model size of approximately 14 million parameters. The total number of class labels is set to 1,000. The classifier-free guidance (CFG) scale in our model is fixed at 1.0 to maintain a balance between the diffusion prior and data fidelity. Training hyperparameters follow those of DiT
[
24
]
, with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
and Adam optimizer parameters
\beta=(0.9,0.999)
.
During inference, we use 1,000 diffusion steps to balance reconstruction quality and computational efficiency. The CFG scale is kept consistent with training. For conditional input, the classifier label
c
encodes prior information, where
c=0
denotes AAPM and
c=1
denotes COCA.

For ASD-POCS, we set the number of outer iterations
P=10
, the number of total variation (TV) inner iterations
Q=1
, and the number of subsets
K=5
. To ensure stable initialization, the number of iterations in the initial stage is set to 30. ASD-POCS is implemented via the LEAP toolbox
[
29
]
following the iTV algorithm. To guarantee consistency between the data-consistency step and the reconstruction step, all CT operators in this paper, including projection and backprojection, are implemented within the LEAP framework. All training and inference experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40 GB of memory.

Dataset Preparation

For the simulation experiments, projection data for the AAPM, COCA, and XCAT datasets are numerically generated using CT system parameters derived from the GE clinical cardiac dataset described above using LEAP toolbox.
Sparse-view inputs are generated by uniformly downsampling the full-view projections. Specifically, for all three datasets (AAPM, COCA, and XCAT), 55 views are uniformly sampled from the original 984 views to simulate sparse-view acquisition conditions.

OOD Qualitative Analysis

We first train all models on the AAPM dataset and evaluate their performance on the XCAT phantom dataset. This simulates an OOD scenario where the anatomical structure and noise statistics differ from those seen during training. As shown in Fig.

The ROI zoom-ins highlight that CDPIR better preserves high-contrast edges (yellow arrows) and low-contrast soft-tissue textures (red arrows), while maintaining sharper structural boundaries and fewer streak artifacts. The difference images further confirm that CDPIR achieves reduced noise residuals and improved anatomical fidelity, underscoring its superior generalizability.

IV-D

Reconstruction on GE Clinical Cardiac Dataset

For GE Clinical Cardiac experiments, raw projections are available and sinograms are extracted. The ground truth CT images are obtained by reconstructing from the full-view sinograms.
We conduct a zero-shot evaluation without pretraining or fine-tuning on clinical data, reflecting real-world scenarios. Both diffusion models and our method are trained on AAPM, while sparse-view sinograms from the clinical cardiac dataset serve as the sole input. To simulate sparsity, we uniformly subsample 123 views from the original 984-view acquisition. For evaluation, we reconstruct the full 3D volume slice by slice. For visualization in Fig.
5
, we present the central slice as a representative example.

IV-E

Reconstruction on Extremity PCCT Dataset

To enable fan-beam reconstruction, we rebin the raw 4D helical cone-beam projections into 2D slices. Each slice contains 373 views, from which we uniformly subsample 74 views to simulate a sparse-view condition. For evaluation, we reconstruct all slices from the 8th energy bin. For visualization in Fig.
6
, we present one representative slice. Using this input, we compare our model with several SOTA diffusion and iterative baselines .

From the second row in Fig.
6
, which highlights two ROIs, one can see that our method not only eliminates sparse-view artifacts but also preserves intricate anatomical details in soft tissue and bone. This outperforms all other methods both qualitatively and quantitatively.
Importantly, this PCCT dataset presents a significant domain shift from AAPM, and can be viewed as a far-OOD task
[
32
]
. In such cases, U-Net-based diffusion models fail to generalize, often performing worse than traditional iterative methods. For example, MCG does not even converge to a stable reconstruction. In contrast, CDPIR achieves a substantial improvement of approximately +3.5 dB in PSNR and +0.05 in SSIM compared with ASD-POCS.

These results further validate the robustness and clinical applicability of CDPIR in handling highly sparse and distribution-shifted real-world datasets. The clinical value of PCCT is significant and it is achievable in the real world in the near future.

V-C

Sampling Efficiency with Limited Inference Steps

We also conduct CT reconstruction using a reduced number of sampling steps (specifically, 100, 200, 500, and 1000) to evaluate how rapidly the diffusion models converge in practice. The comparison is performed between DDS (a score-based diffusion method) and our proposed CDPIR framework using a representative OOD testing image from the COCA-XCAT setup.
As shown in Fig.
8
, DDS requires a high number of sampling steps (close to 1000) to reach acceptable reconstruction quality. In contrast, our velocity-based interpolant model CDPIR consistently achieves high-quality reconstructions even with as few as 200 sampling steps. This behavior underscores the superior sampling efficiency and stability of the interpolant-based stochastic process used in SiT.
The results demonstrate that CDPIR benefits from more stable convergence dynamics, owing to its velocity formulation and transformer-based backbone, which together reduce the dependency on fine-grained stepwise denoising. The ability to generate high-fidelity reconstructions in fewer steps significantly reduces computational cost and makes CDPIR more feasible for real-time clinical applications.

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

Our experiments demonstrate that the proposed model can effectively handle OOD scenarios.
Compared with traditional approaches and the SOTA diffusion baseline DDS, our method better preserves fine structures and reduces artifacts.
The superior generalizability of our framework can be attributed to its transformer-based architecture. Unlike U-Net diffusion models constrained by CNN inductive biases, transformers capture long-range dependencies, scale effectively with model size, and propagate correction signals across regions with varying reliability. This design not only improves reconstruction quality but also provides a pathway toward architectures with even lower inductive bias, such as Mamba
[
33
]
, which may further enhance flexibility and performance.

A central advantage of CDPIR is its ability to train across heterogeneous datasets, potentially spanning hundreds of imaging domains. By learning both robust and domain-specific features, CDPIR adapts to specialized tasks, such as cardiac or extremity imaging, while maintaining broad applicability. Its scalability enables unified checkpoints to generalize across datasets and acquisition regimes without task-specific retraining, positioning CDPIR as a promising foundation model for medical image reconstruction with broad deployment potential. Beyond medical imaging, CDPIR’s principles of combining generative priors with explicit data consistency could extend to other AI domains, including complex synthesis tasks in diverse modalities and general-purpose text applications
[
34
]
.

In summary, CDPIR integrates transformer-based diffusion denoising with explicit data consistency to deliver high-quality SVCT reconstruction. Patch-based positional embeddings enable localized corrections under fidelity constraints while maintaining global coherence, ensuring both fine detail recovery and structural integrity. The conditional diffusion transformer with classifier-free guidance supports multi-domain training, while the unified stochastic interpolant framework enhances adaptability to OOD scenarios. Extensive experiments on AAPM, XCAT, COCA, and real-world PCCT datasets confirm that CDPIR outperforms SOTA baselines such as DiffusionMBIR and DDS. By uniting local precision with global consistency, CDPIR advances the field of medical image reconstruction and paves the way for scalable, generalizable, and clinically deployable generative reconstruction models.
