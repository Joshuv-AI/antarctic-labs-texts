Combinatorial leaky probiotic for anticancer immunopotentiation and tumor eradication
Authors: Cheng-Hao Liu, Yi-Chung Pan, See-Khai Lim, Chung-Yuan Mou, Che-Ming Jack Hu, Kurt Yun Mou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryCombination therapies present a compelling therapeutic regimen against the immunosuppressive and heterogeneous microenvironment of solid tumors. However, incorporating separate therapeutic modalities in regimen designs can be encumbered by complex logistical, manufacturing, and pharmacokinetic considerations. Herein, we demonstrate a single-vector combinational anticancer therapy using an lpp gene knockout leaky probiotic for simultaneous secretion of immunotherapeutic and oncolytic effector molecules. Through fusion protein design and vector optimization, a Nissle1917 (EcN) bacteria vector is engineered to secrete Neoleukin-2/15 (Neo-2/15) cytokine-functionalized anti-PDL1 nanobody (aPDL1-Neo2/15) and anti-mesothelin-functionalized hemolysin E (HlyE-aMSLN). The multifunctional leaky probiotic enables synchronous immune activation and tumor-targeted cytolytic activity for effective tumor suppression, elevation of tumor immune cell infiltration, and establishment of anticancer immunological memory. lpp gene knockout is further shown to improve probiotic tolerability and intravenous applicability, offering a therapeutically viable approach for combination regimen development.Keywords: bacterial therapy, combinational cancer therapy, leaky probiotic, chimeric protein, oncolytic toxins, immune checkpoint inhibitors, anticancer immunity

Introduction

Combinatorial therapies are a widely employed strategy against the complex and evolving oncogenic mechanisms of solid tumors.1 Multidrug synergism has demonstrated effectiveness in enhancing tumor suppression, preventing drug resistance development, overcoming the immune suppressive effect of tumor microenvironment, and establishing durable anticancer immunological memory.2,3,4,5 However, integration of multiple therapeutic modalities in therapeutic regimens can be hampered by logistical, manufacturing, and patent barriers, which can make combination therapies difficult to implement in clinical practices.1 In addition, the varying pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of different drug types also complicate the design and evaluation of combination therapies.6 Recently, the emergence of bacteria and probiotics as therapeutic vectors offers new opportunities for treatment development.7,8 The diverse toolset in recombinant cloning technology offers the synthetic flexibility to generate vectors expressing a broad array of biologics. In addition, the tumor tropism of bacteria and their ability to proliferate in the hypoxic tumor microenvironment facilitates tumor-targeted biologics delivery. While existing anticancer bacterial vectors possess singular anticancer modalities,7,8,9 equipping bacteria with the capability to synchronously secrete multiple therapeutics can vastly expand the probiotic therapy landscape, enabling the development of multi-drug combinational vectors with streamlined treatment modality integration, manufacturing, and delivery.

A major design consideration for preparing therapeutic bacterial vector is the incorporation of a release mechanism that unleashes the expressed biologics from the bacterial body. Several gain-of-function strategies, including the addition of phage proteins for bacterial lysis or transmembrane channels for protein transport, have been demonstrated to facilitate drug release.10,11,12,13 However, these gain-of-function strategies can compete for the packaging capacity and translational machineries available for therapeutic modalities,14,15 and their expression may add to the safety and immunogenicity concerns associated with bacterial vectors. Toward engineering single-vector combinational probiotics, we herein employ a loss-of-function strategy that loosens bacteria’s outer membrane through the deletion of lpp gene (Δlpp), which encodes for the lipoprotein that covalently staples bacterial outer membranes.16 We show that Δlpp bacteria possess a leaky phenotype that enables direct release of expressed biologics, providing a flexible platform for combination therapy design. In addition, the loss-of-function Δlpp design confers the added benefit of enhancing vector tolerability as Lpp lipoprotein is associated with immune stimulation and reactogenic responses.17,18 Through the insertion of a plasmid with a single promoter, we demonstrate the preparation of multifunctional leaky bacteria that combine immune-stimulatory, immune checkpoint inhibitor, and tumor-targeted oncolytic components in a single vector for anticancer treatment.

To demonstrate the preparation of combinational leaky probiotic for anticancer treatment, we herein integrate immunostimulatory and oncolytic chimeric proteins toward inducing tumoricidal activity, modulating tumor microenvironment, and promoting anticancer adaptive immune responses. The immunotherapeutic chimeric protein comprises an interleukin 2 (IL-2)-variant cytokine19,20 and immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PDL1),21 whereas the oncolytic chimeric protein comprises mesothelin-targeting nanobody and hemolysin E.22 We show that the leaky bacteria enable the screening of component design, and the functionality of each therapeutic component is verified during the vector engineering process. The optimized leaky probiotic, denoted as combinatorial immunotherapeutic-oncolytic probiotic (CIOP) (Figure 1), demonstrates direct and synchronous release of the different effector functions, enabling synergistic antitumor activities (Figure 1). The loss-of-function Δlpp strategy is further shown to improve probiotic tolerability without influencing its tumor tropic characteristic, which makes CIOP amenable to intravenous administration. CIOP shows superiority over single-modality probiotic in treating aggressive tumor types, and it mounts favorable anticancer immune responses for tumor containment. The multifunctional vector offers a versatile approach for single-vector combinatorial treatment development.

Schematic of combinatorial immunotherapeutic-oncolytic probiotic for single-vector combinational anticancer treatment

A loss-of-function strategy for spontaneous protein release from bacterial vectors is achieved through lpp gene knockout, giving rise to a leaky probiotic. Therapeutic components are then incorporated into the leaky probiotic through the insertion of a plasmid with a single promoter.

Results

Leaky bacteria enable direct protein release for immunotherapeutic functionality optimization

Establishing the secretory toolset is a current focal point in bacterial therapies. In contrast to commonly adopted gain-of-function secreting modalities,10 an EcB vector was knocked out of its lpp gene prior to therapeutic functionalization (Figures 2A, S1A, and Table S1). With the aim of equipping CIOP with the capability to stimulate anticancer immunity and blocking immune checkpoints, we first examined three IL-2 variants, including a wild-type IL-2 protein, a directed-evolution-enhanced IL-2 superkine (Sk2),19 and a computationally designed Neoleukin-2/15 (Neo2/15).20 The cytokines were initially tethered to a YebF secretory carrier protein to facilitate transport to bacterial periplasm,23 and the expression and functionality of the IL-2 variants were assessed upon incorporation in either wild-type EcB or EcBΔlpp. As compared to the wild-type EcB using OD600 as a reference standard,24,25 a greater amount of secreted protein was observed for all three IL-2 variants by the EcBΔlpp (Figure 2B), highlighting robust protein release upon bacteria loosening in the absence of lipoprotein. Among the EcBΔlpp expressing the different IL-2 variants, the vector expressing Neo2/15 showed the highest protein secretion level, which could be attributed to high thermostability of Neo2/1521 as well as bacterial vectors’ general propensity for expressing stable proteins.26 Functional assessment by a T cell expansion assay with supernatants derived from the different EcBΔlpp vectors showed that whereas IL-2- and Sk2-expressing vectors had negligible effect on T cell viability, supernatants from equivalent Neo2/15 vector significantly elevated T cell viability (Figure 2C). The immunotherapeutic function of Neo2/15-secreting EcBΔlpp was further validated in mice bearing small (Figures 2D, 2E, and S1B) and large MC38 tumors (Figures 2F, 2G, and S1C). Effective tumor suppression by the Neoo2/15-secreting EcBΔlpp and observation of released cytokine within the tumors supported the leaky bacteria’s viability to release functional cytokines for immunotherapy (Figure S2). Encouragingly, treatment by Neo2/15-expressing EcBΔlpp established robust anticancer adaptive immune response (Figure S1D) and increased tumor infiltration of CD8+ T cells (Figures 2H and S1E–S1J), which cemented the selection of Neo2/15 as the cytokine of choice for CIOP development.

To bestow CIOP with the added functionality to tackle cancer’s immune-suppressive characteristics, we then explored fusion protein designs that incorporated Neo2/15 with nanobodies targeting PD-L1, CTLA4, or VSIG4, which represent immune checkpoints associated with CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, and macrophages, respectively.27,28 The cytokine-nanobody fusion protein design not only offers the prospect of synergistic immune stimulation but also obviates the need of YebF carrier protein as nanobodies can be shuttled directly to the bacterial periplasm.29 EcBΔlpp transfected with plasmids encoding for anti-PD-L1, anti-CTLA4, or anti-VSIG4 nanobody-based Neo-2/15 fusion proteins showed consistent expression of the corresponding proteins (Figures 2I and S1A). Upon validating the ligand binding specificity of the nanobody moieties and the T cell stimulatory function of the fusion cytokines (Figures S3A and S3B), we proceeded to examine the anticancer activity of the fusion protein-expressing bacteria using a Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) tumor model. As LLC is classified as a “cold” tumor with low T cell infiltration and high infiltration of myeloid-suppressive cells, the tumor enables efficacy discrimination of different immune checkpoint inhibitors for the different fusion cytokine-expressing EcBΔlpp. Compared to the CTLA4 and VSIG4 variants, EcBΔlpp expressing aPDL1-Neo-2/15 demonstrated suppression against the immunosuppressive tumors (Figures 2J, 2K, and S3C), thus justifying its selection for combinatorial probiotic development.

Leaky bacteria enable direct and synchronized release of immunomodulatory and oncolytic chimeric proteins

To incorporate immunotherapeutic bacterial vectors with oncolytic functionality, we envisioned that the incorporation of tumor-targeting cytolysins offers a compelling tumoricidal component that can minimize off-target toxicity. Therefore, we incorporated a mesothelin-targeted nanobody-hemolysin E fusion protein (HlyE-aMSLN) into the leaky bacteria (Figures 3A, S4A, and Table S2). We first demonstrated that against mesothelin-expressing tumors, vectors expressing HlyE-aMSLN led to enhanced HlyE binding and elevated cytolytic activity as compared to those expressing non-targeted HlyE of similar comparable levels (Figures 3B, S4B, and S4C). EcBΔlpp strains expressing HlyE-aMSLN also exhibited selective recognition and inhibition of cell growth against mesothelin-expressing mammalian cells. Fusion sequence containing aPDL1-Neo2/15 and HlyE variants were then transfected into EcBΔlpp for combinatorial leaky bacteria assessment (Figure 3A). Transfection of the fusion sequence led to the secretion of both aPDL1-Neo2/15 and HlyE-aMSLN from the leaky bacteria as confirmed by western blot, and the dual functionality incorporation showed comparable effector protein secretion as compared to single-functionality bacteria (Figures 3C and 3D). As compared to combinatorial bacteria secreting non-targeted HlyE, HlyE-aMSLN-secreting combinatorial bacteria exerted superior antigen-targeted killing (Figures 3E, 3F, S4D, S4E, S5A, and S5B). Synchronized secretion of immunomodulators and oncolytic toxins from EcBΔlpp also demonstrated that synergistic tumor suppression against MC38 cancer cells as single-component bacteria expressing either immunomodulators or oncolytic toxins exhibited more modest cancer cell inhibition (Figures 3G and S5C). The combinatorial anticancer effect of combinatorial EcBΔlpp also manifested upon examination against mesothelin-expressing CT26 cancer cells (CT26MSLN) (Figures 3H and S5D). Importantly, the presence of oncolytic toxins from the combinatorial EcBΔlpp did not compromise the functionality of the co-secreted immunomodulator in immune cell stimulation (Figure 3I). The lack of negatory effect toward HT2 T cell expansion in the presence of combinatorial EcBΔlpp could be attributed to the negligible binding of HlyE-aMSLN on non-mesothelin-expressing cells (Figure S5), which limited the toxin from reaching critical concentration on cellular surfaces for oligomeric pore formation.30 These findings corroborate the leaky bacteria design for therapeutics release and reinforce the value of combinatorial bacteria co-secreting immunotherapeutic and oncolytic functionalities for anticancer treatment.

Δlpp leaky bacteria design enhances probiotic safety for intravenous delivery

As treatment safety is a major consideration in bacterial therapy development given the immunostimulatory nature of bacterial vectors,8,31 we examined how the removal of lipoprotein through lpp gene knockout would affect probiotic tolerability, particularly for intravenous administration. We adopted a commonly used probiotic Escherichia coli strain Nissle 1917 (EcN)32 as the chassis for CIOP development, and the safety of wild-type EcN and EcNΔlpp was first compared (Figures 4A and S6A). At equivalent intravenous dosing of 5 × 106 colony-forming unit (CFU), wild-type EcN caused significant weight loss whereas EcNΔlpp exhibited improved tolerability with subdued weight loss by the host (Figure 4B). Organ dissection showed that lpp gene knockout significantly reduced the probiotics’ ability to reside in the spleen and kidney (Figure 4C), but bacterial quantification in tumors and liver showed no statistical difference between the wild-type and the Δlpp vector. Notably, EcN showed higher proliferative activity in the blood and serum as compared to EcNΔlpp (Figures 4D and S6B), which can be attributed to lipoprotein’s protective function in fending off complement system-mediated lysis.33 EcNΔlpp’s reduced survivability in the blood may further contribute to the vector’s higher intravenous tolerability. In another vector safety assessment with high-dose intravenous administration at 5 × 108 CFU, the safety enhancement conferred by lpp gene knockout was further corroborated as EcN administration led to 100% mortality whereas all mice survived the high-dose EcNΔlpp treatment (Figure 4E). Of note, examination of EcNΔlpp content on day 3 post administration showed significantly higher bacteria CFU in the tumor as compared to the spleen and liver (Figure 4F). This result highlights that EcNΔlpp retains the previously reported tumor tropism of EcN probiotic,34 and the hypoxic and immunosuppressive microenvironment of tumor favors the probiotic’s survival. Together, the results support the applicability of EcNΔlpp as a safer probiotic vector for anticancer therapeutics development.

The EcNΔlpp vector was then functionalized with anticancer therapeutics for CIOP development, and the safety of cytolysin-functionalized vectors was examined. In comparison to non-targeted HlyE-functionalized EcNΔlpp, HlyE-aMSLN-functionalized EcNΔlpp exhibited significantly improved tolerability upon intravenous administration (Figure 4G). Notably, EcNΔlpp was more tolerable for intravenous treatment as compared to EcNΔlpp. The encouraging safety profile of EcNΔlpp prompted us to incorporate both the immunotherapeutic and oncolytic components into the vector. The leaky functionality of the Δlpp vector enabled synchronized release of aPDL1-Neo2/15 and HlyE-aMSLN (Figures 4H, 4I, and S6C–S6N). In comparison to the combinatorial immunotherapeutic-oncolytic EcBΔlpp (CIOB), CIOP EcNΔlpp was well tolerated upon repeated, high intravenous dosing as mice exhibited 100% survival following an 8-day regimen with four 5 × 108 CFU injections (Figure 4J). We further performed a hemolysis test with the different bacterial vectors, which showed that the EcNΔlpp vector and CIOP had minimal hemolytic activity (Figure S6O). The non-hemolytic CIOP stands in stark contrast to HlyE-expressing EcBΔlpp, which induced significant hemolysis in the assessment. The discrepancy can be attributed to EcN vector’s lack of the HlyE gene that is constitutively expressed in EcB.35 Additionally, the anti-mesothelin nanobody functionalization introduces steric hindrance factor to HlyE, which could impede pore formation of the toxin on non-targeted cells. The hemolysis assay corroborates the CIOP’s safety profile for in vivo administration.

To further demonstrate the functionality of the expressed immunomodulatory and oncolytic chimeric proteins by CIOP, we examined the cellular binding, immune-stimulatory, and cytotoxic properties of proteins released from CIOP and other vectors. Through immunofluorescence analysis, we confirmed that the released therapeutic proteins showed expected differential binding to cancer cells and immune cells (Figure S7). In in vitro functional assessments, the supernatants of CIOP showed robust immune-stimulatory activity toward HT2 T cell expansion (Figure 4K). CIOP supernatants also exhibited potent suppressive effect against cancer cells, leading to effective inhibition of MC38 (Figure 4L). In addition, we observed that CIOP’s supernatant also exhibited robust inhibitory activity against non-mesothelin-expressing B16F10 and LLC cell lines (Figures 4M and 4N), which are naturally more susceptible to the cholesterol-dependent pore-forming action of HlyE owing to the high cholesterol content in their membranes.36 These findings validate CIOP’s ability to express and secrete both aPDL1-Neo2/15 and HlyE-aMSLN for combinatorial anticancer therapy.

Combinatorial immunotherapeutic and oncolytic functionalities by CIOP exert synergistic anticancer activities

To demonstrate the benefit of bacterial therapy integrating multiple therapeutic components in vivo, we compared CIOP to EcNΔlpp vectors expressing monovalent components upon treatment against different tumor types. We first adopted a mouse model with established MC38 tumors averaging 150 mm3 in size, which were intratumorally injected with PBS, EcNΔlpp vector, EcNΔlpp expressing anti-PDL1 nanobody (EcNΔlpp-aPDL1), EcNΔlpp expressing aPDL1-Neo2/15 (immunotherapeutic probiotic; IP), EcNΔlpp expressing HlyE-aMSLN (oncolytic probiotic; OP), and EcNΔlpp co-expressing aPDL1-Neo2/15 and HlyE-aMSLN (CIOP). While EcNΔlpp, EcNΔlpp-aPDL1, and IP induced modest tumor suppression, a single administration of OP and CIOP was able to significantly reduce the size of the tumors to the point where they were undetectable (Figures 5A–5C and S8). Encouraged by the robust oncolytic activity of HlyE-aMSLN-expressing probiotics, we then sought to evaluate the therapeutic benefit of the co-secreted immunotherapeutic function by administering OP and CIOP to large established MC38 tumors (>300 mm3). Following probiotic injection into the primary tumor, additional MC38 cancer cells were implanted at a distal site to examine the abscopal effect induced by the different vectors (Figure 5D). The large MC38 tumors showed pronounced differences between the OP and the CIOP as OP showed negligible suppressive effect whereas CIOP effectively contained the primary tumors (Figure 5E). In addition, CIOP treatment was more effective than OP at suppressing distal tumor growth (Figure 5F), indicating superiority of the CIOP vector at inducing anticancer immune responses. The therapeutic benefit conferred by the combinatorial functionalities of CIOP was also evident in a fast-growing, mesothelin-expressing CT26 tumor model, which showed that the dual anticancer functionalities enhanced the tumor-suppressive effect of the probiotic treatment (Figures 5G–5I and S9). In mice bearing highly aggressive B16F10 melanoma, we further showed that CIOP can achieve significant tumor suppression, rendering the tumors undetectable and inducing durable anticancer adaptive immunity following a single-dose treatment (Figures 5J–5M and S10). These results highlight the synergistic effect of CIOP toward inducing tumoricidal and immune-stimulatory activities for robust and durable tumor containment.

Intravenous CIOP treatment achieves tumor suppression and increases immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment

Given the high intravenous tolerability of the leaky probiotic, we examined the anticancer effect of combinatorial probiotics upon systemic administration. Compared to the EcNΔlpp vector control and monovalent EcNΔlpp, CIOP showed superior tumor-suppressive effect in MC38 tumor-bearing mice. (Figures 6A–6C and S11) Notably, one tumor-bearing mouse showed complete response following CIOP treatment (Figure 6C), a feat unattainable by immunotherapeutic or oncolytic EcNΔlpp. To investigate how CIOP treatment may influence the tumor microenvironment and immune cell infiltration, we treated another group of MC38-bearing mice for tumor dissection and analysis. Upon reaffirming the tumor-suppressive effect by the intravenous CIOP (Figures 6D and S12), the resected tumors were disassociated into single-cell suspensions for flow cytometric analysis. Compared to EcNΔlpp vector control, CIOP-treated tumors showed a significantly higher percentage of CD45+ cells. (Figure 6E) In-depth analysis of the immune cell composition revealed higher presence of CD8+ and CD4+ T lymphocytes in CIOP-treated tumors. (Figures 6F and 6G) In addition to T lymphocytes, CIOP also increased the number of MHCII-positive immune cells (Figures 6H–6J), indicating the presence of antigen-presenting cells such as CD19+ B cells, CD11c+ dendritic cells, and F4/80+ M1 macrophages.

To further demonstrate the therapeutic potential of CIOP, we adopted a large established LLC cold tumor model for intravenous CIOP treatment (Figures 6K, S13A, and S13B). While the tumors in the non-treated group grew at a rapid pace to >1,500 mm3 within 7 days for this aggressive tumor model, intravenous CIOP treatment effectively suppressed tumor growth by reducing the day 7 average tumor size to ∼700 mm3 (Figures 6L and 6M, S13C–S13E). Upon analysis of the resected tumors on day 15 following the initial treatment, we also observed that the CIOP-treated tumor had an elevated level of CD45+ tumor-infiltrating leukocytes. These findings corroborate the immune-stimulatory function mediated by the immunomodulator component in the CIOP, which can transform the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment to improve disease prognosis.37,38 The high-dose intravenous treatment afforded by the leaky probiotic further enhances the translational prospect of anticancer bacterial therapy.8

Discussion

Synthetic biotechnics facilitates numerous possibilities of bacterial engineering for cancer therapies, and gain-of-function secretion strategies have been the standard vector engineering approach. We demonstrate a loss-of-function strategy that increases bacteria leakiness for therapeutic protein release. The lpp gene knockout approach is applicable to different bacterial vectors and therapeutic cargoes, opening a versatile venue for bacterial therapy development.

In addition to generating leaky membranes for protein release, knocking out the lpp gene of CIOP also enhances vector tolerability and safety profile. The improved safety can be attributed to the link between lpp gene expression and capsular polysaccharide content,18 which is a major virulence factor associated with pathogenic bacteria.39,40,41 We show that probiotic with lpp gene knockout has reduced viability in the blood, which is presumably due to the deprivation of immune-evasive function associated with lipoprotein and capsular polysaccharide.33,42 Despite the reduced viability in the blood, EcNΔlpp remained detectable in tumors following intravenous administration, which indicates that the leaky probiotics are capable of proliferating in the hypoxic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment for tumor-tropic therapeutic delivery. As bacterial therapies are witnessing growing adoption in oncological43 and other diseases including metabolic disease,44,45 infectious disease,46 and inflammatory disease,47,48 the robust cargo-releasing attributes and improved safety profile afforded by the lpp gene knockout make the leaky probiotic approach highly desirable for emerging bacterial therapies.

The present work also shows the feasibility to combine multiple anticancer components into a single vector through the integration of bacterial vector engineering and chimeric fusion protein design (Figure S14). We show that the judicious design of tumor-targeted cytolytic protein and immunomodulating fusion proteins can lead to synergistic antitumor actions that enhance immune cell expansion while suppressing cancer cell growth. Tumor-targeting functionality is shown to enhance the effectiveness and safety of HlyE whereas immunomodulators secreted from CIOP enhance anticancer activity against large established tumors, induce abscopal effect, and increase immune cell infiltration in tumors. In contrast to conventional combinational anticancer therapy, the combinatorial probiotic offers the benefit of streamlined production and simplified dosing regimen, resolving the production, logistical, and pharmacological complexities behind combination therapies.

Limitations of the study

Due to the multiplicity of anticancer effect associated with two distinctive chimeric proteins and the bacterial vectors, there may be synergistic or antagonistic mechanisms between the different components of CIOP, which could benefit from further mechanistic interrogations. In addition, administration of the leaky probiotic may alter endogenous microbiota. Such effect was not accounted for in the study.

Experimental models and study participant details

Cell line

MC38 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum, NEAA (Gibco, 11140050), sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070), and HEPES (Gibco, 15630080). HEK293T, CT26, LLC, and B16F10 cells were incubated in DMEM with 10% FBS. HT2 cells were maintained in RPMI with 10% FBS. All mammalian cells were sustained at 37°C with a 5% incubator. E. coli BL21(DE3) (EcB) and E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) were cultured in 2xYT medium (5 g/L sodium chloride (Merck, 1.06404.1000), 10 g/L yeast extract (Bio-basic, G0961), and 16 g/L tryptone (Oxoid, LP0042B)) and were selected by kanamycin (50 μg/mL).

Animal model

C57BL/6J and Balb/c female mice 7 to 8 weeks old were provided by the National Laboratory Animal Center, NARLabs, Taiwan (http://www.nlac.org.tw/). All animal studies were conducted in specific pathogen-free conditions and by guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Usage Committee of Academia Sinica (Protocol # 20-11-1551). All experimental animals were housed in accordance with international and local animal welfare regulations. The housing environment was strictly controlled, maintaining a stable temperature of 22°C ± 2°C, humidity of 50% ± 10%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle (12:12 light-dark cycle). Food and water were provided ad libitum and were regularly replenished. The animals were housed with sufficient space for their species and number, in compliance with ethical guidelines. Additionally, their health was monitored daily by trained staff and veterinarians to ensure they remained in optimal health throughout the experiment.

Method details

Plasmids and nanobodies

The vector pET28a was utilized with either the T7 or Lac-Tac-Tac promoter to establish the processes of CIOB and CIOP. The following primers were employed to extract the YebF (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/EF648003.1) and HlyE (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/945745) gene from E. coli BL21(DE3) by referring to the previous study23 and the NCBI website: forward_YebF, 5′-ATGGGAAAAAGAGGGGCGTTTTTAG-3’; reverse_YebF, 5′-ACGCCGCTGATATTCCGCCATTC-3’; forward_HlyE, 5′- ACTGAAATCGTTGCAGATAAAACG-3’; reverse_HlyE, 5′- GACTTCAGGTACCTCAAAGAGTG-3’. Primer synthesis was utilized to construct the stII signal peptide at the N-terminus of ICI-Neo2/15, following the previous ref. 52. GeneArt (Thermofisher) services were employed to synthesize the nanobodies for aPDL1, aCTLA4, aVSIG4, and aMSLN, by referring to previous studies12,21,22,49 and the RCSB PDB website (https://www.rcsb.org/).

Lentiviral mesothelin expression

The mouse mesothelin gene was synthesized through reverse transcription and amplification of RNA extracts from MC38 cells using the primers: forward, 5′-GATGCAGAACAGAAAGCCTGC-3’; reverse, 5′-TCAGCTCAGCCTTAAAGCTGG-3’. The resulting mesothelin gene was in its mature (cleaved) form, including the native phosphatidylcholine signal peptide. The amplicon was cloned into a lentiviral transfer plasmid, with a myc-tag positioned upstream of the transgene. To generate a stable cell line overexpressing mesothelin, the lentiviral transduction method was employed. In brief, target cell lines were co-transfected with three plasmids: pCMV-dR8.91 (1.35 μg), pMD2-G (0.165 μg), and the transfer plasmid (1.5 μg). This was achieved by incubating the cells with a transfection mix consisting of 3 μg/mL polyethylenimine (PEI) in Opti-MEM (Gibco) for 30 min. After 16 h, the medium was replaced, and the cells were further incubated for 72 h to allow lentiviral particle production. The viral particles were harvested by filtering the supernatant through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The target cell line was then incubated with the viral particles in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL) for 16 h, followed by selection with puromycin (8 μg/mL) for 2 weeks. The expression of transgene is then validated using flow cytometry, with anti-Myc-AF488 (cell signaling, 2279) and anti-mesothelin nanobody (100 nM) coupled with anti-His-PE (Biolegend, 362603) antibodies.

Leaky bacteria/probiotics establishment (Δlpp)

The KIKO plasmid50 (addgene, 46763) was employed as a template for fusing the homologous arm (HA) of the vector with the upstream and downstream regions of the lpp gene. The upstream primers were designed as follows: forward, 5′-ACGAAAAAACGGCTCATCAG-3’; reverse, 5′-TATTAATACCCTCTAGATTGAGTTAATCTCC-3’. For the downstream primers, the following sequences were utilized: forward, 5′-TAGTACCTGTGAAGTGAAAAATGGC-3’; reverse, 5′-GCATGGTAATGGCGAATACC-3’. The PCR products were transformed via electroporation into the λ-red system51 (addgene: 62225)-expressing EcB and EcN. After the replacement of the endogenous lpp gene with an antibiotic-resistant gene, the resulting leaky EcB/EcN strains became dependent on the Flippase of the temperature-sensitive vector53 (addgene: 45978) to remove the antibiotic-resistant gene. The knock-out genotype of EcBΔlpp and EcNΔlpp was confirmed using the following pair of primers: forward, 5′-CTAAGATGAATCCGATGGAAGC-3’; reverse, 5′-GTCGATAAAGCGTTGTA TCGTC-3’.

Western blot

The supernatant from an overnight (16 h) bacterial culture was collected to determine the secretion level of therapeutic proteins. The supernatant obtained after centrifugation of the bacterial culture at 4500xg for 10 min was analyzed. The boiled mixture containing bacterial supernatant with sample buffer was loaded onto SDS-PAGE, and the signal on PVDF was detected. Anti-His-HRP (Biolegend, #362613) was used to detect the secretion level of YebF-IL2 variants, and ICI-Neo2/15, anti-Myc-HRP (Merck Millipore, #16–213) was applied for the detection of HlyE and HlyE-aMSLN secretion. The secretion level of therapeutic proteins from secreting bacteria/probiotics was measured using ImageJ. The bacterial concentrations of the overnight (16 h) culture were indicated by OD600.

Cell and bacteria viability assay

The evaluation of cell viability was conducted using the CCK-8 assay (Abbkine, KTA1020). The procedure was briefly outlined as follows: On the day of incubation, mammalian cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate at a density of 10%. Subsequently, the mammalian cells were incubated in a complete medium supplemented with filtered bacterial supernatant for three days. Following this, the treated mammalian cells were incubated with a culture medium containing a 10% mixture of CCK-8 reagent at 37°C for 1 h, and the viability signal was detected by measuring the optical density at 450 nm (OD450). Regarding the IL2 functional test, normalization of each group was performed using the mean of commercially IL2 (R&D, #202-IL-050, 100U/ml)-treated HT2. For the toxicity test, normalization of each group was carried out using the mean of the untreated cancer cell line.

We employed the Abcam Bacterial Viability Assay Kit (ab189818). Bacterial cultures were grown overnight and harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 x g for 10 min. The cells were resuspended in 1x Wash Buffer, followed by the addition of 1 μL Total Cell Stain and 1 μL Dead Cell Stain to 1 mL of bacterial suspension. A blank solution was prepared similarly with 1 mL 1x Wash Buffer and the stains. The samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After incubation, 200 μL of each sample and the blank were transferred into individual wells of a 96-well plate in triplicate. Fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader at Ex/Em 490nm/525nm and 536nm/617nm. The percentage of dead cells was calculated by dividing Reading 2 by Reading 1 and multiplying by 100.

Tumor inoculation, intratumoral and intravenous therapy, and cancer rechallenge

C57BL/6J and Balb/c mice were provided by the National Laboratory Animal Center, NARLabs, Taiwan (http://www.nlac.org.tw/). All animal studies were conducted in specific pathogen-free conditions and by guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Usage Committee of Academia Sinica (Protocol # 20-11-1551). Each mouse, aged 7 to 8 weeks with trimmed fur, was administered 5×105 cancer cells via subcutaneous injection. The tumor size was measured using the following calculation method: (width (mm) × length (mm))1.5 × 0.52. Tumor-bearing mice were subjected to either intratumoral injection of 109 CFU per 50μL or intravenous therapy with 5 × 106 CFU per 50μL. In the case of higher bacterial titer in intravenous administration, each tumor-bearing mouse was administered 5 × 108 CFU per 50μL. In intratumoral (i.t.) treatment, each mouse received an i.t. injection every six days. For intravenous (i.v.) injections, each mouse received i.v. injections every three days. Each batch of leaky bacteria/probiotics was washed twice with PBS before being delivered to tumor-bearing mice. The mouse weight on the first day of treatment was considered 100% to record the difference in weight after treatment. Tumor-bearing mice were considered deceased when the tumor size exceeded 2,000 mm3. After complete recovery over a month, the mice were administered 2.5×105 cancer cells via subcutaneous injection on the opposite side.

Immunofluorescence

The following flow antibodies and reagents were used to stain the tumor section from bacterial-treated, tumor-bearing mice: Anti-CD45-FITC (Biolegend, 103107), Anti-CD8-FITC (Biolegend, 100706), Rabbit anti-E. coli (Abcam, ab137967), and goat anti-rabbit-AF647 (Abcam, ab150079). Anti-His-APC (Biolegend, 362605) was employed for the detection of the Neo2/15 fusion protein in solid tumors. Hoechst33342 (Thermofisher, H3570) was applied for nuclear staining. Full tumor section images were captured using the Leica Thunder Imager system, while regular tumor section images were obtained through the confocal microscope LSM700. For the confocal image of MC38 staining, MC38 cells were mixed at a concentration of 5×105 in complete DMEM with 50% filtered supernatant from overnight (16 h) CIOB culture and incubated at room temperature or on ice for 1 h. Localization of fusion proteins binding on the MC38 was revealed using Anti-His-APC (Biolegend, 362605) and anti-Myc-PE (Cell signaling, 3739), while the nucleus of MC38 was indicated by DAPI (BD, 564907). Confocal images were obtained using the confocal microscope LSM700.

CFU detection

Colony-forming units (CFU) in treated mice were detected by administering 5 × 108 CFU of EcN or EcNΔlpp to each mouse through intravenous injection. After 2 h, the treated mice were sacrificed, and their spleens, livers, kidneys, and tumors were dissected. The liver was placed in sterile PBS and homogenized at full speed for 1 min on ice, while the spleen, kidney, and tumor were homogenized using a homogenizer (Medclub, PREP-CB6) at full speed for 1 min. The mixtures were then spread onto antibiotic-containing LB plates and incubated overnight (16 h). The colony numbers were counted, and the CFU in each mouse organ was calculated. Following the method described previously,42 mouse blood was co-incubated with EcN or EcNΔlpp at 37°C for 1 h. Approximately 200μL of whole mouse blood, serum, or PBS was mixed with 10μL of 0.5M EDTA in a 1.5mL tube before the experiment. The bacterial-blood mixture was spread on antibiotic-containing LB plates and incubated overnight (16 h). The colony numbers were counted, and the CFU of EcN or EcNΔlpp was calculated.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes analysis

Tumors from mice treated with leaky probiotics were taken and placed into serum-free RPMI (Gibco, 31800-022) containing 0.33 mg/ml collagenase IV (Sigma, C5138) with 0.06 mg/ml DNaseI (Cyrusbiosicence, 101-9003-98-9) on ice. They were minced with scissors as finely as possible, then transferred to a C tube (Miltenyibiotec, MAC-130-093-237) of GentleMACS (Miltenyibiotec, 130-096-427) and digested using the protocol 37C_m_TDK_1. The tumor suspension was passed through a 70μm mesh strainer and washed with PBS three times. Blocking was performed with anti-CD16/32 (Biolegend, 101301) for 5 min on ice. Each subset of immune cells was stained with the following dye/antibodies: Fixable Viability Dye (Thermofisher, 65–0865), anti-CD45-PerCP-Cy5.5 (Biolegend, 103132), anti-CD8-FITC (Biolegend, 100706), anti-CD4-APC (Biolegend, 100516), anti-MHCII-FITC (Biolegend, 107606), anti-CD19 (BD Pharmingen, 562956), anti-CD11c-AF594 (Biolegend, 117346), anti-F4/80-AF700 (Biolegend, 123130). The tumor suspension was then incubated with these antibodies on ice for 25 min and analyzed using the Attune flow cytometer (Thermofisher).

Sandwich ELISA

Reasonable concentrations of immune checkpoint proteins (PD-L1, Sino Biological, 50010-M02H; CTLA4, Sino Biological, 50503-M02H; VSIG4, Sino Biological, 50187-M03H) were applied to a 96-well ELISA plate at 4°C overnight (16 h), following the vendor’s suggestions. The centrifugal bacterial supernatant, collected using the same method as Western blot, was applied to the ELISA plate and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The fusion nanobodies that bind to the immune checkpoints on the ELISA plate were identified using anti-His-HRP (Biolegend, #362613) during a 1-h incubation at room temperature. The binding signal of anti-His-HRP was examined using TMB substrate (Seracare, 5120-0047) and quantified by measuring OD450.

Flow cytometry

Each group of 5 × 105 MC38 or HT2 cells in complete DMEM or RPMI was incubated with 10% or 50% filtered supernatant from overnight (16 h) bacterial culture on ice for 1 h. Treated MC38 and HT2 cells were stained with anti-His-APC (Biolegend, 362605) of aPDL1 or aPDL1-Neo2/15, while anti-Myc-PE (Cell Signaling, 3739) of HlyE-aMSLN on ice for 30 min. The binding percentage of the fusion protein on the surface of MC38 cancer cells and HT2 T cells was evaluated using the Attune flow cytometer (Thermofisher) and FlowJo software.

Hemolysis assay

Approximately 200μL of whole mouse blood was collected and mixed with 10μL of 0.5M EDTA in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The blood was washed three times with an equal volume of PBS. Subsequently, 20μL of the washed blood cells were mixed with 100 μL of a 50% supernatant from an overnight bacterial culture diluted in sterile double-distilled water (ddH₂O). This mixture was incubated at room temperature (25°C) for 5 min. Following incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 600xg for 3 min. From the supernatant, 100 μL was transferred to a 96-well plate. The hemolytic activity was assessed using ddH₂O as a positive control by measuring the optical density at 540 nm.
