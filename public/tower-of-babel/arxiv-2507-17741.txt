Abstract
Let M n M_{n} be an n × n n\times n random matrix with i.i.d. sparse discrete entries. In this paper, we develop a simple framework to solve the approximate Spielman-Teng theorem for M n M_{n} , which has the following form: There exist constants C , c > 0 C,c>0 such that for all η ≥ 0 \eta\geq 0 , P ​ ( s n ​ ( M n ) ≤ η ) ≲ n C ​ η + exp ⁡ ( − n c ) \textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right)\lesssim n^{C}\eta+\exp\left(-n^{c}\right) . As an application, we give an approximate Spielman-Teng theorem for M n M_{n} whose entries are μ − \mu- lazy random variables, extending previous work by Tao and Vu.

Introduction and Main Results

Let
A_{n}
be an
n\times n
real matrix with singular values
s_{1}(A_{n})\geq\cdots\geq s_{n}(A_{n}),
in particular, the largest and smallest singular values of
A_{n}
are defined to be

\displaystyle s_{1}(A_{n})=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\|A_{n}x\|_{2},\quad s_{n}(A_{n})=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\|A_{n}x\|_{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
denotes the
n-1
dimensional Euclidean sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In computer science, the condition number of
A_{n}
, i.e., the ratio of
s_{1}(A_{n})
and
s_{n}(A_{n})
, is an important indicator to measure the stability of a linear system
A_{n}x=b
to input perturbations. The larger the condition number, the more unstable the system is, and the error of the numerical solution will be magnified. Therefore, the condition number is widely used in many fields, such as algorithm stability analysis, numerical linear algebra, machine learning, and signal processing.

Bounding the condition number of
A_{n}
is an important problem in this field. The behavior of
s_{1}(A_{n})
is well understood under general assumptions on matrix entries
\xi
. In particular, Yin, Bai and Krishnaiah
[
17
]
proved that, when
\mathrm{E}\xi=0
and
\mathrm{E}|\xi|^{4}<\infty
, with high probability

\displaystyle s_{1}(A_{n})\sim\sqrt{n}.

Hence, the main problem in estimating the condition number’s magnitude is exploring the lower bound of
s_{n}(A_{n})
.

For Gaussian random matrices with i.i.d.
N(0,1)
entries, the magnitude of
s_{n}(A)
is of the order
1/\sqrt{n}
with high probability. This observation goes back to von Neumann
[
10
]
, and it was proved by Edelman
[
3
]
that

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(A_{n}\right)\leq\varepsilon n^{-1/2}\right)\sim\varepsilon.

For Rademacher random matrices with i.i.d.
\{\pm 1\}
entries, Spielman and Teng
[
14
]
conjectured in the 2002 ICM survey that the same tail estimate (up to an exponentially small correction) should hold for Rademacher random matrices. This conjecture has since become known as the Spielman-Teng conjecture. It has motivated a great deal of work on the smallest singular value of random matrices.

The breakthrough work of Rudelson
[
11
]
gave the first bound on the smallest singular value beyond the Gaussian case. He proved that if
A_{n}
has i.i.d. mean–zero, unit–variance subgaussian entries, then

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(A_{n}\right)\leq\varepsilon n^{-3/2}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon+n^{-3/2}.

Shortly thereafter, Tao and Vu
[
15
]
developed the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory, which, as an important application, gave a new bound on the
smallest singular value of Bernoulli random matrices with i.i.d.
0/1
entries: for any
A>0
, there exists
B>0
such that,

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\bigl(s_{n}(A_{n})\leq n^{-B}\bigr)\leq n^{-A}.

Based on the Littlewood-Offord problem, Rudelson and Vershynin
[
13
]
proved a refined result; they gave

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(A_{n}\right)\leq\varepsilon n^{-1/2}\right)\leq C\varepsilon+e^{-cn},

for all random matrices with i.i.d. mean–zero, unit–variance subgaussian entries. This perspective was developed further in the 2020 breakthrough work of Tikhomirov
[
16
]
, for Bernoulli random matrices, he gave more refined ”constants”,

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(A_{n}\right)\leq\varepsilon n^{-1/2}\right)\leq C\varepsilon+\left(\frac{1}{2}+o_{n}(1)\right)^{n}.

Recently, Ferber, Jain, Luh and Samotij
[
5
]
developed a new approach to the counting problem in the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory, and then Jain
[
8
]
gave a novel framework for finding probabilistic lower bounds on the smallest singular value of a discrete random matrix in a truly combinatorial setting.

Inspired by previous work, this paper develops the framework described above for the study of the smallest singular value of a sparse discrete random matrix.
For any
0\leq\mu\leq 1
, let
\eta^{\mu}\in\{-1,0,1\}
denote a random variable equal to 0 with probability
1-\mu
and
\pm 1
with probability
\mu/2
each. This type of random variable is known as
\mu-
lazy random variable. A
\mu-
lazy random matrix is a matrix whose entries are i.i.d.
\mu-
lazy random variables. When
\mu
is very small, the
\mu-
lazy random matrix is an essential example of sparse discrete random matrices. Sparse random matrices are important for the study of combinatorial random matrices.
We obtain the small ball probability estimate for a
\mu-
lazy random matrix, and our method can be easily extended to general sparse discrete random matrices. In addition, the inverse Littlewood-Offord theorem tells us that when linear combinations of random variables are highly concentrated, their coefficient vectors must have some additive structure. Tao and Vu
[
15
]
, in order to conveniently study the relationship between the concentration function and the vector structure of the random walks as described above, consider a fixed
\mu
that can take values in
[0,1]
. However, in sparse conditions, many vectors have very few nonzero entries. Thus, in this paper, we consider a model in which the value of
\mu
can tend to 0 as an extension of the study of Tao and Vu
[
15
]
. In the remainder of this paper,
\mu_{n}
means that
\mu
is no longer fixed but converges to
0
as
n
increases.

Specifically, we define the largest atom probability as follows.

For an integer vector
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we define its largest atom probability to be

\displaystyle\rho^{(\mu)}(\bm{w}):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\textbf{P}\left(\eta^{\mu}_{1}w_{1}+\cdots+\eta^{\mu}_{n}w_{n}=x\right),

where
\eta^{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\eta^{\mu}_{n}
are i.i.d.
\mu-
lazy random variables.

Let
Y_{\mu}(\bm{w}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}^{\mu}w_{i}
, then
Y_{\mu}(\bm{w})
is a class of discrete random walks in integer space
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which start at the origin and consist of
n
steps, where at the
i^{\text{th }}
step one moves backwards or forwards with magnitude
v_{i}
and probability
\mu/2
, and stays at rest with probability
1-\mu
.

Notice that the probability
\textbf{P}\left(\eta^{\mu}_{1}w_{1}+\cdots+\eta^{\mu}_{n}w_{n}=0\right)
is closely connected to the singularity probability of the matrix
M_{n}
, where
M_{n}
is defined as an
n\times n
random matrix with i.i.d.
\mu-
lazy entries. Since for any
\bm{w}=(w_{1},...,w_{n})
(which we interpret as a column
vector), the entries in the product
M_{n}\bm{w}
are independent copies of
Y_{\mu}(\bm{w})
. It follows that for such a row-independent matrix
M_{n}
, the study of its smallest singular value is an important application of the study of the inverse Littlewood-Offord theorem. Based on this, the paper obtained the following main results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M_{n}
denote an
n\times n
random matrix, each of whose entries is an i.i.d.
\mu_{n}-
lazy random variable. Then, for any
\eta\geq 2^{-n^{0.0001}}
,
\mu_{n}\geq n^{-0.45}
,

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right)\lesssim\frac{\eta n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}}}.

Remark 1.1
.

We have not tried to optimize the constant 0.0001 in the above theorem, and similarly, for
\mu_{n}
, which characterizes the sparsity of sparse random matrices, it can be made more refined using an approach similar to that of this paper to make it closer to (but not lower than)
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Since few nonzero elements exist for sparse random linear combinations, studying the famous inverse Littlewood–Offord theory is challenging. In this paper, we not only show that the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory under a sparse
\mu_{n}-
lazy distribution retains the core conclusion of ”high concentration
\Rightarrow
strong structure” but also reveal how sparsity
\mu_{n}
affects the small ball probability and the smallest singular value.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
first recalls some basic concepts and lists some concentration inequalities for classical random variables and extends them to general
\mu-
lazy random variables. Section
3
gives the proof of the main results, and Section
4

Throughout the paper, we use the notation

. We will also use the following asymptotic notation: If
a=\Omega(b)

b\lesssim a
or
a\gtrsim b
; If

b\gg a

In this section, we introduce some lemmas that will be used to prove our

First, we introduce an important notion defined by Rudelson and Vershynin. The least common denominator (LCD) of a nonzero vector

\displaystyle\operatorname{LCD}_{\gamma,\alpha}(\bm{a}):=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\operatorname{dist}\left(\theta\bm{a},\mathbb{Z}^{n}\right)<\min\left\{\gamma\|\theta\bm{a}\|_{2},\alpha\right\}\right\},

for some parameters

[

and
\mu_{n}\in(0,1]
be such that
\mu_{n}\geq C_{0}\frac{\log n}{n}
. Then there exist positive constants
C_{2.4},c_{2.4}
, depending on

, so that

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\left\|M_{n}\right\|\geq C_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{2.4}n\mu_{n}\right).

The last lemma shows that, with very high probability, the image of a fixed unit vector under
M_{n}
does not have norm
o(\sqrt{n\mu_{n}})
.

Lemma 2.5

(Lemma 4.1 in
[
6
]
)
.

Fix a unit vector
\bm{a}=\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, there exist positive absolute constants
C_{2.5}
and

such that

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\|M_{n}\bm{a}\|_{2}\leq C_{2.5}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{2.5}n\mu_{n}\right).

In Lemma

are
\mu_{n}^{-1}\gg o(\frac{n}{ln^{2}n})

\mu_{n}=\Omega(\frac{1}{n^{0.45}})

3
Proof of main result

In this section, we are devoted to proving our main result. Throughout this section, we will take
\mu_{n}=\Omega(\frac{1}{n^{0.45}})
,
\alpha:=n^{1/4}
,
C^{*}_{2.4}:=\max\{C_{2.4},1\}
and
\gamma:=C_{2.5}/\left(rC^{*}_{2.4}\right)
(where r is a constant for the purpose of
\gamma\leq 1/100
and
r\geq 100
). Moreover, we can observe that
\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right)\lesssim\frac{\eta n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}}}
holds trivially for
\eta\geq n^{-3/2}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}
, thus, we will henceforth assume that
2^{-n^{0.0001}}\leq\eta<n^{-3/2}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}
.

Based on the magnitude of the least common denominator (LCD) of the vectors compared to
n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\cdot\eta^{-1}
, we decompose the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
into
\Gamma^{1}(\eta)\cup\Gamma^{2}(\eta)
, where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{1}(\eta):=\left\{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\operatorname{LCD}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\bm{a})\geq n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\cdot\eta^{-1}\right\},

and
\Gamma^{2}(\eta):=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\backslash\Gamma^{1}(\eta)
, we have

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right)\leq\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)+\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{2}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right).

Therefore, Theorem
1.1
follows from the following two propositions and the union bound.

Proposition 3.1
.

\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)\lesssim\frac{\eta n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}}}+n\exp(-2\sqrt{n}\mu_{n})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{2}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)\lesssim\exp\left(-c_{3.2}n\mu_{n}\right)
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

For every
M_{n}
satisfying the above assumptions, since
M_{n}
and
M_{n}^{T}
have the same singular values, the event
s_{n}(M_{n})\leq\eta
implies the existence of a unit vector
\bm{a}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which

\displaystyle\|\bm{a}^{\prime}{}^{T}M_{n}\|_{2}\leq\eta.

For each fixed
M_{n}
, we define the unit vector
\bm{a}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the above condition to be
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{M_{n}}
. Since the matrix rows are independent of each other, this leads to a partition of the space of all the above types of matrices with the smallest singular value at most
\eta
. Then, by taking a union bound,

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)\leq n\textbf{P}\left(\exists\,\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\|M_{n}\bm{a}\|_{2}\leq\eta\wedge\|\bm{a}^{\prime}_{M_{n}}\|_{\infty}=|a^{\prime}_{n}|\right),

it suffices to show the following:

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\exists\,\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\|M_{n}\bm{a}\|_{2}\leq\eta\wedge\|\bm{a}^{\prime}_{M_{n}}\|_{\infty}=|a^{\prime}_{n}|\right)\lesssim\eta\sqrt{\frac{n}{\mu_{n}}}+e^{-2\sqrt{n}\mu_{n}}.

(3.1)

To establish this, reveal the first
n-1
rows
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}
. If there are some
\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta)
satisfies
\|M_{n}\bm{a}\|_{2}\leq\eta
, then based solely on
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}
one can select a vector
\bm{y}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta)
such that

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(X_{i}\cdot\bm{y})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\eta.

Thus, for any vector
\bm{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with
w_{n}^{\prime}\neq 0
, we can rewrite
X_{n}
with
X_{1},...,X_{n-1}
:

\displaystyle X_{n}=\frac{1}{w_{n}^{\prime}}\left(\bm{u}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}^{\prime}X_{i}\right),

where
\bm{u}:=\bm{w}^{\prime T}M_{n}
. Indeed, since
X_{n}\cdot\bm{y}
is irrelevant to how
\bm{w}^{\prime}
is chosen,
thus, for the event
\left\{s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right\}\wedge\left\{\left\|\bm{a}^{\prime}{}_{M_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}=\left|a_{n}^{\prime}\right|\right\}
to occur, we must necessarily have

\displaystyle\left|X_{n}\cdot\bm{y}\right|=\inf_{\bm{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},w_{n}^{\prime}\neq 0}\frac{1}{\left|w_{n}^{\prime}\right|}\left|\bm{u}\cdot\bm{y}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}w_{i}^{\prime}X_{i}\cdot\bm{y}\right|,

(3.2)

then, using the inequality Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the right-hand side of (
3.2
) and setting
\bm{w}^{\prime}=\bm{a}^{\prime}{}_{M_{n}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|X_{n}\cdot\bm{y}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left|a_{n}^{\prime}\right|}\left(\left\|\bm{a}_{M_{n}}^{\prime T}M_{n}\right\|_{2}\|\bm{y}\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{a}_{M_{n}}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(X_{i}\cdot\bm{y}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sqrt{n}\left(\|\bm{y}\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{a}_{M_{n}}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}\right)\leq 2\eta\sqrt{n},

where the second inequality is due to the assumption that event
\{s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\}\wedge\{\left\|\bm{a}^{\prime}_{M_{n}}\right\|_{\infty}=\left|a_{n}^{\prime}\right|\}
occurs. It follows, by definition, that the probability in (
3.1
) is bounded by
\mathcal{L}\left(X_{n}\cdot\bm{y},2\eta\sqrt{n}\right)
, and using Lemma
2.1
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(X_{n}\cdot\bm{y},2\eta\sqrt{n}\right)\lesssim\eta\sqrt{\frac{n}{\mu_{n}}}+\exp(-2\sqrt{n}\mu_{n}).

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2

3.1
Reduction to integer vectors

Since the vectors in
\Gamma^{2}(\eta)
are still essentially vectors on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, this creates difficulties for our following study. Here, we present the first key step, which is the efficient transfer from vectors on the unit sphere to integer vectors.

Proposition 3.3
.

With the notation above, we have

\displaystyle\textbf{P}(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{2}(\eta)

\displaystyle:\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{{-c_{2.4}}n\mu_{n}}+\textbf{P}

\displaystyle(\exists\bm{w}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\right)\cap\left[-2\eta^{-1}n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}},2\eta^{-1}n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\right]^{n}:

\displaystyle\left.\left\|M_{n}\bm{w}\right\|_{2}\leq\min\left\{4\gamma C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\|\bm{w}\|_{2},2C^{*}_{2.4}\alpha\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right\}\right).

Proof.

Let
\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{2}(\eta)
. Then, recall the definition of
\operatorname{LCD}
, there exists some
0<\operatorname{LCD}_{\alpha,\gamma}(\bm{a})\leq\theta\leq n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\eta^{-1}
and some
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that

\displaystyle\|\theta\bm{a}-\bm{w}\|_{2}\leq\min\{\gamma\theta,\alpha\}.

in particular,
\theta(1-\gamma)\leq\|\bm{w}\|_{2}\leq\theta(1+\gamma)
.

Note that, since
\theta(1\pm\gamma)\leq 2\theta
, it follows that each coordinate of
\bm{w}
is bounded in absolute value by
2\theta\leq 2\eta^{-1}n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{w}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\right)\cap\left[-2\eta^{-1}n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}},2\eta^{-1}n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}\right]^{n}.

If
\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta
, it follows from the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left\|M_{n}\bm{w}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|M_{n}(\bm{w}-\theta\bm{a})+M_{n}(\theta\bm{a})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|M_{n}\right\|\cdot\|\theta\bm{a}-\bm{w}\|_{2}+\theta\cdot\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\cdot\min\{\gamma\theta,\alpha\}+\theta\eta

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\cdot\min\{\gamma\theta,\alpha\},

where the second inequality is due to the bounds on the spectral norms of
M_{n}
given by Lemma
2.4
:

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\left\|M_{n}\right\|\geq C_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{2.4}n\mu_{n}\right),

and the last inequality follows since
\eta\ll\gamma\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}
and
\theta\eta\leq n^{3/4}\sqrt{\mu_{n}}=\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\alpha
. With the following simple case study, we can draw the desired conclusion.

Case I
:
\gamma\theta\leq\alpha
. In this case,
\bm{w}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|M_{n}\bm{w}\right\|_{2}\leq 2\gamma C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\theta\leq\min\left\{4\gamma C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\|\bm{w}\|_{2},2C^{*}_{2.4}\alpha\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right\},

where the last inequality uses
\theta/2\leq\theta(1-\gamma)\leq\|\bm{w}\|_{2}
and
\gamma\theta\leq\alpha
.

Case II
:
\gamma\theta>\alpha
. In this case,
\bm{w}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|M_{n}\bm{w}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2C^{*}_{2.4}\alpha\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\leq\min\left\{2C^{*}_{2.4}\gamma^{-1}\alpha\gamma\sqrt{n\mu_{n}},2C^{*}_{2.4}\alpha\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{4\gamma C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\|\bm{w}\|_{2},2C^{*}_{2.4}\alpha\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\right\},

where the last inequality uses

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}\|_{2}\geq\theta(1-\gamma)\geq\gamma^{-1}\cdot(\gamma\theta)/2\geq\gamma^{-1}\alpha/2.

∎

The following study is to partition the integer vectors according to the size of their support set, and interestingly, through subsequent proofs, we find that we need to use a sparser partition of the integer vectors to study the smallest singular value of sparse matrices.

3.2
Dealing with sparse integer vectors

Furthermore, we need to transfer the vector to a prime range, and for a sparse vector
\bm{w}
, we have a simpler union bound. The goal of this subsection is to prove the following lemma, which follows from Lemma
2.5
. Throughout this subsection and the next one,
p=2^{n^{0.001}}
is a prime. Note that

Lemma 3.1
.

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{w}\in\left(\mathbb{Z}^{n}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\right)\cap[-p,p]^{n},|\operatorname{supp}(\bm{w})|\leq n^{0.99}\mu_{n}:\left\|M_{n}\bm{w}\right\|_{2}\leq\right.

\displaystyle\left.4\gamma C^{*}_{2.4}\sqrt{n\mu_{n}}\|\bm{w}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2.5}n\mu_{n}}{2}\right).

Proof.

The number of vectors

\displaystyle\binom{n}{n^{0.99}\mu_{n}}(3p)^{n^{0.99}\mu_{n}}\ll 2^{n^{0.992}\mu_{n}}.

Therefore, the union bound gives the desired conclusion.
∎

\displaystyle\textbf{P}\left(s_{n}\left(M_{n}\right)\leq\eta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{1}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)+\textbf{P}\left(\exists\bm{a}\in\Gamma^{2}(\eta):\left\|M_{n}\bm{a}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\eta n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}}}+n\exp(-2\sqrt{n}\mu_{n})+\exp\left(-c_{3.2}n\mu_{n}\right)\lesssim\frac{\eta n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{n}}},

since
\eta\geq 2^{-n^{0.0001}}
, the last inequality holds for all sufficiently large n. This completes the proof.
∎

4
Discussion

In the related studies on the smallest singular value of random matrices, there are fewer studies on sparse random matrices. Like Rudelson and Vershynin, we use the key notion of LCD of a vector for a simple partition of the
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and introduce the counting method developed by Ferber, Jain, Luh and Samotij
[
5
]
to the study of sparse vectors. Then, we extend the study of fixed
\mu
in Tao and Vu
[
15
]
to
\mu_{n}
, which tends to zero.

In future work, there are two problems to study. The first problem is to refine the LCD to partition
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Litvak and Tikhomirov
[
9
]
, in solving the problem of the smallest singular value of sparse random matrices with entries 0/1, introduced the degree of unstructuredness (u-degree) to better deal with the problems posed by sparsity. Inspired by this, finding a more refined tool to replace LCD in the study of sparse matrices may lead to better estimates.

Another problem is the study of symmetric sparse matrices, where the challenge lies in the fact that we cannot directly use row independence to classify the study of random matrices as the study of random vectors. The recent studies of Campos, Jenssen, Michelen and Sahasrabudhe
[
2
]
on the smallest singular value of symmetric random matrices have given us some approaches which will help us in our future studies of symmetric sparse matrices.
