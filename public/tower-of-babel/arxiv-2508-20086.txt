Smart Contract Intent Detection with Pre-trained Programming Language Model Conference: Preprint; Aug 2025; arXiv
Youwei Huang, Jianwen Li, Sen Fang, Yao Li, Peng Yang, Bin Hu, Tao Zhang

Abstract
. Malicious intent in smart contract development can lead to substantial economic losses. SmartIntentNN is a deep learning model specifically designed to identify unsafe intents in smart contracts. This model integrates the Universal Sentence Encoder, a K-means clustering-based intent highlighting mechanism, and a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network for multi-label classification, achieving an F1 of 0.8633 in distinguishing ten different intent categories. In this study, we present an upgraded version of this model, SmartIntentNN2 (Smart Contract Intent Neural Network V2). A significant enhancement in V2 is the incorporation of a BERT-based pre-trained language model, which has been trained on a dataset of 16,000 real smart contracts using a Masked Language Modeling objective. SmartIntentNN2 retains the BiLSTM-based multi-label classification network. With an improved F1 of 0.927, V2 demonstrates enhanced performance compared to its predecessor, establishing itself as the state-of-the-art model for smart contract intent detection.



1.
Introduction

Smart contracts serve as the foundational infrastructure for decentralized application (DApp) development
(
szabo1996smart
;
antonopoulos2018mastering
;
ethereum2025smart
)
, operating on various blockchain platforms,
e.g.
, Ethereum
(
buterin2014next
;
wood2014ethereum
)
, Binance Smart Chain (BSC), Solana.
These contracts enable decentralized financial services and automate on-chain transactions, fostering a trustless execution environment.
However, the transparency and immutability of smart contracts also introduce significant risks.
Vulnerabilities, as well as deliberately embedded malicious intent, can lead to severe economic losses for DApp users.
While extensive research has been conducted on vulnerability detection in smart contracts
(
he2020smart
;
chu2023survey
;
chen2025chatgpt
)
, relatively little attention has been given to detecting the developer’s intent behind the code.

To address this gap,
SmartIntentNN
was introduced as a deep learning-based approach for detecting unsafe development intents in smart contracts
(
huang2022smartintentnn
;
huang2025deep
)
.
This model’s architecture consists of three core components: (1) the Universal Sentence Encoder (USE)
(
cer2018universal
)
to generate contextual embeddings of smart contract source code, (2) a K-means clustering-based intent highlighting module to emphasize intent-related features, and (3) a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network for multi-label classification of ten distinct categories of unsafe intent.
Evaluations on
10,000
real smart contracts demonstrated that
SmartIntentNN
achieved an F1 of 0.8633, with an accuracy of 0.9647, a precision of 0.8873, and a recall of 0.8406.

In this study, we introduce an enhanced version of this model,
SmartIntentNN2
(Smart Contract Intent Neural Network V2).
The primary improvement in V2 is the integration of a BERT-based pre-trained language model
(
devlin2018bert
;
liu2019roberta
)
, specifically CodeBERT
(
feng2020codebert
)
, replacing the USE for embedding generation.
The pre-trained model was initially trained on a corpus of
16,000
smart contracts using a Masked Language Modeling (MLM) objective to better capture the contextual semantics of smart contract code.
It was subsequently fine-tuned via transfer learning for intent detection, incorporating a BiLSTM-based
(
hochreiter1997long
;
graves2005framewise2
)
multi-label classification network.

SmartIntentNN2
was trained on
16,000
smart contracts and evaluated on an independent test set of
10,000
contracts.
The results demonstrate that our model outperforms both traditional baselines and its predecessor, achieving an accuracy of
0.9789
, a precision of
0.9395
, a recall of
0.9149
, and an F1 of
0.9270
.
These results establish
SmartIntentNN2
as the state-of-the-art (SoTA) model for smart contract intent detection.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We upgraded
SmartIntentNN
from V1 to V2, enhancing its performance to achieve an F1 of
0.927
.

•

We trained a BERT-family pre-trained model, SmartBERT, specifically for generating smart contract embeddings.

•

We have open-sourced all dataset, code, documentation, and models at
https://github.com/web3se-lab/web3-sekit
.

2.2.
Smart Contract Intent Detection

Detecting intent within smart contracts is crucial for safeguarding against potential threats.

SmartIntentNN

(
huang2022smartintentnn
)
is the pioneering model developed to address this challenge, employing a combination of advanced components to enhance detection accuracy.
At its core,
SmartIntentNN
integrates the Universal Sentence Encoder for capturing contextual code representations, a K-means clustering model for spotlighting intent indicators, and a BiLSTM network for categorizing intents.
Evaluated on 10,000 smart contracts, this model achieved an impressive F1 of 0.8633, significantly surpassing traditional deep learning baselines such as LSTM, CNN, and even some generative models like GPT.

2.3.
Pre-trained Language Models

Pre-trained language models have revolutionized natural language processing with models like BERT
(
devlin2018bert
)
, RoBERTa
(
liu2019roberta
)
, and CodeBERT
(
feng2020codebert
)
, which leverage large-scale self-supervised learning.
These models are designed to generate robust contextual representations that are adaptable to various tasks via transfer learning, markedly reducing the demand for labeled data.
In the realm of code analysis, CodeBERT stands out for its dual training on code and prose. Our work builds upon this by pre-training CodeBERT specifically on a substantial dataset of smart contracts, enhancing its ability to discern subtle semantic nuances pertinent to intent detection in smart contracts.
This adaptation is critical for achieving the advanced performance levels demonstrated by
SmartIntentNN2
.

3.
Model

SmartIntentNN2
comprises SmartBERT, which is a pre-trained model specifically designed for smart contracts, paired with a BiLSTM-based network for multi-label classification.
Figure
2
illustrates the complete model architecture of
SmartIntentNN2
.
The following subsections detail the training process of this pre-trained model and its utilization through transfer learning in downstream tasks for smart contract intent classification.

Figure 2.

Architecture of

(iii) A BiLSTM-based network is employed to perform multi-label classification on the smart contracts.

3.1.
Data Preparation

We curated a comprehensive dataset comprising over 40,000 smart contracts, each annotated for the presence or absence of ten distinct categories of unsafe intents, with individual contracts potentially exhibiting none, one, or multiple intent labels.
For model development, the dataset was divided into two subsets.
The first subset contains 16,000 contracts, which were used both for pre-training SmartBERT and for subsequent multi-label intent classification training.
The second subset consists of 10,000 contracts, serving exclusively as the evaluation set, consistent with the configuration used in V1.

Due to limitations imposed by maximum sequence length, it is impractical to input entire smart contract contexts into our model during both training and evaluation phases.
Consistent with our approach in V1, we extract code at the function level, with subsequent data processing also conducted at this granularity.
From the perspective of processing an entire smart contract, this approach effectively treats function-level code as sequential data for analysis.

3.2.
Pre-training for Smart Contracts

We trained
SmartBERT
, a pre-trained programming language model specifically tailored for smart contract analysis.
The model is initialized from CodeBERT, leveraging its proven capability in code understanding, and adapted to capture the unique syntactic and semantic patterns of smart contract programming.
The pre-training process is visually depicted in the upper part of Figure
2
.

Training Data and Preprocessing:

We curated 16,000 real-world smart contracts, covering a broad spectrum of functionalities and structures in decentralized applications.
The dataset was tokenized at the function level, rather than at the contract level, to prevent exceeding the maximum sequence length of 512 tokens and to enable finer-grained semantic representation of code.

Pre-training Objective:

We adopt the MLM objective to adapt the pre-trained
CodeBERT-base-mlm
model to smart contract code.
In each function-level sequence, 15% of tokens are randomly selected and replaced with the
[MASK]
token.
Given an input sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{T})
of length
T
, let
M\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
denote the masked positions where
|M|\approx 0.15T
.
The model predicts the original tokens
\{x_{i}\mid i\in M\}
from the masked context
\mathbf{x}_{\text{masked}}
.
The MLM loss is defined as:

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}=-\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i\in M}\log P_{\theta}(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\text{masked}})

where
P_{\theta}(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\text{masked}})
is the predicted token probability at position
i
, parameterized by model weights
\theta
.

Model Architecture and Training Setup:

Our backbone model is
CodeBERT-base-mlm
, which is initialized from
RoBERTa-base
and trained with MLM objective on source code.
We retain its 12-layer encoder architecture with hidden dimension
d=768
and 12 self-attention heads.
Fine-tuning is performed on smart contract code using the MLM objective.
Training runs for 20 epochs with a batch size of 64 on two NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, taking approximately 10 hours.
We utilize the
AdamW
optimizer with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-5}
.
Evaluation on a held-out set of 4,000 smart contracts is performed every 10,000 steps during pre-training.

Function-level Representation:

For each function with input sequence
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{T})
, SmartBERT produces contextualized token embeddings

\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
,
where
T
is the sequence length and
d=768
is the hidden dimension. We obtain a function embedding
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
via mean pooling:

(2)

\mathbf{f}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{t}

Empirically, mean pooling outperforms using the
[CLS]
token embedding for function representation.

Contract-level Representation:

Given a smart contract containing
N
functions, we obtain function embeddings
\{\mathbf{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
using Eq.
2
for each function.
These embeddings are stacked to form the contract-level representation matrix:

(3)

\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{1}\\
\mathbf{f}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}

where each row
\mathbf{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the
i
-th function embedding.
This matrix
\mathbf{X}
serves as input to the downstream multi-label classifier for intent detection.

Through this hierarchical representation learning, SmartBERT produces contract matrix
\mathbf{X}
for downstream multi-label classification.

3.3.
Multi-label Intent Classification

We formulate smart contract intent detection as a multi-label binary classification task with
C
target intents (
C=10
in our dataset).
Given a contract with
N
functions, the function embeddings
\{\mathbf{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
obtained from SmartBERT are organized into a fixed-length matrix through padding or truncation:

(4)

\mathbf{X}=\text{pad}\left(\{\mathbf{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}

where
L=256
is the maximum sequence length. Contracts with fewer than
L
functions are padded with zero vectors, while longer contracts are truncated.

The BiLSTM encoder processes the padded matrix to capture inter-function dependencies:

(5)

\mathbf{h}=\text{BiLSTM}\left(\mathbf{X}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2U}

where
U=128
is the hidden size per direction, and
\mathbf{h}
concatenates the final forward and backward hidden states. A masking mechanism ensures padded positions do not contribute to the computation.

The final classification layer applies dropout regularization and produces per-class probabilities:

(6)

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}\cdot\text{Dropout}(\mathbf{h})+\mathbf{b},\quad\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\sigma(\mathbf{z})\in(0,1)^{C}

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times 2U}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
, dropout rate is 0.5, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the element-wise sigmoid function.

Loss Function:

To address class imbalance and emphasize hard examples, we use the binary focal loss
(
lin2017focal
)
. For a single binary label
y\in\{0,1\}
with prediction
p=\hat{y}
, the focal loss is:

(7)

\mathrm{FL}(p,y)=-\alpha\,y(1-p)^{\gamma}\log(p)-(1-\alpha)(1-y)p^{\gamma}\log(1-p)

where
\gamma\geq 0
is the focusing parameter and
\alpha\in[0,1]
balances the importance of positive
vs.
negative samples.
The overall training loss over
M
contracts is the mean per-sample, per-class loss:

(8)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{FL}(p_{i,c},y_{i,c})

where
p_{i,c}
and
y_{i,c}
denote the predicted probability and ground-truth label for class
c
on sample
i
.
We implement this loss via TensorFlow’s
BinaryFocalCrossentropy

(
abadi2016tensorflow
;
smilkov2019tensorflow
)
.

Optimization and Training Regimen:

The classifier is optimized using Adam with the following hyperparameters during the primary training phase (complete data training):

•

learning rate
1\times 10^{-3}
;

•

batch size
S=200
;

•

number of chunks
B=80
, processing
B\times S=16,000
training samples per epoch;

•

epochs per chunk
E=100
;

•

dropout rate
p=0.5
.

A secondary class-balanced training phase uses a reduced learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
, smaller batch sizes, and per-class balanced sampling strategy to further alleviate class imbalance.
Specifically, we randomly sample 10 instances from each intent class to ensure balanced representation across all
C=10
intent categories during training.

Padding and Masking:

Each contract’s function sequence is padded to length
L
by appending zero vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote this operation as
\mathrm{pad}\left(\{\mathbf{f}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\right)
.
The Masking layer with
\texttt{mask\_value}=0.0
ensures padded timesteps do not contribute to recurrent computations or loss.

3.4.
Overall Model Summary

In summary,
SmartIntentNN2
integrates pre-training, hierarchical representation learning, and sequence modeling for robust intent detection in smart contracts.
The pipeline operates in three stages:
(i) function-level embeddings are derived from SmartBERT, a domain-adapted language model pre-trained with the MLM objective on 16,000 real-world contracts;
(ii) these embeddings are aggregated into a contract-level matrix with fixed-length padding and masking, ensuring consistent batch processing while preserving semantic granularity;
(iii) a BiLSTM-based encoder captures inter-function dependencies, followed by a sigmoid-based output layer that produces multi-label probabilities across all
C=10
intent categories.

Key design choices include the use of mean pooling for function embeddings, masking-aware sequence encoding, and binary focal loss for handling class imbalance.
Together, these components enhance robustness against sequence length variability and uneven label distribution.
Building on this architecture, we will evaluate
SmartIntentNN2
on a held-out set of 10,000 smart contracts to demonstrate its effectiveness in intent detection.

4.
Evaluation

In this section, we evaluate the performance of
SmartIntentNN2
using a set of widely-adopted metrics and compare it against representative baselines to assess its effectiveness in smart contract intent detection.

4.1.
Evaluation Metrics

To comprehensively assess the performance of
SmartIntentNN2
in the multi-label binary classification setting, we adopt accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 as evaluation metrics.
These are computed from the confusion matrix statistics for each intent category.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
denote the ground-truth label vector, and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in\{0,1\}^{C}
the binarized prediction vector obtained by thresholding the sigmoid outputs.
For the
c
-th category, we define:

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{TP}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[y_{n,c}=1\land\hat{y}_{n,c}=1\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{TN}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[y_{n,c}=0\land\hat{y}_{n,c}=0\right]

(11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FP}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[y_{n,c}=0\land\hat{y}_{n,c}=1\right]

(12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{FN}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\left[y_{n,c}=1\land\hat{y}_{n,c}=0\right]

where
N
is the number of evaluation samples,
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
is the indicator function, and
\mathrm{TP}_{c},\mathrm{TN}_{c},\mathrm{FP}_{c},\mathrm{FN}_{c}
denote the true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives for category
c
, respectively.

The per-class
accuracy
is defined as:

(13)

\mathrm{Accuracy}_{c}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{TN}_{c}}{\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{TN}_{c}+\mathrm{FP}_{c}+\mathrm{FN}_{c}}

which measures the proportion of correct predictions among all samples for class
c
.

The
precision
and
recall
for class
c
are given by:

(14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Precision}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{FP}_{c}}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Recall}_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{FN}_{c}}

where precision quantifies the proportion of predicted positives that are correct, and recall measures the proportion of actual positives that are successfully retrieved.

The
F1
is computed as the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

(16)

\mathrm{F1}_{c}=\frac{2\cdot\mathrm{Precision}_{c}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}_{c}}{\mathrm{Precision}_{c}+\mathrm{Recall}_{c}}

This metric provides a balanced measure that is particularly informative under class imbalance.

To aggregate performance across all
C
categories, we report both the
macro-average
and
micro-average
for each metric:

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Macro\text{-}Accuracy}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{Accuracy}_{c}

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Micro\text{-}Accuracy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{TN}_{c})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{TN}_{c}+\mathrm{FP}_{c}+\mathrm{FN}_{c})}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Macro\text{-}Precision}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{Precision}_{c}

(20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Micro\text{-}Precision}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{FP}_{c})}

(21)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Macro\text{-}Recall}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{Recall}_{c}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Micro\text{-}Recall}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{\sum_{c=1}^{C}(\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\mathrm{FN}_{c})}

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Macro\text{-}F1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{F1}_{c}

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Micro\text{-}F1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cdot\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{TP}_{c}}{2\cdot\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{TP}_{c}+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{FP}_{c}+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{FN}_{c}}

Macro-averaging treats all categories equally regardless of sample size, while micro-averaging aggregates the contributions of all categories, favoring classes with more instances.

Against conventional neural baselines,
SmartIntentNN2
delivers substantial relative gains.
For instance, it achieves a
23.37%

The performance gap is even more pronounced when compared to LLM-based baselines, where
SmartIntentNN2
demonstrates
75.5%

65.5%

This substantial performance gap is primarily attributable to the fact that LLMs are pre-trained on general-domain corpora and lack exposure to smart contract intent detection tasks, resulting in a zero-shot setting for this domain where their representations and reasoning capabilities are not sufficiently specialized for this specific task.

Furthermore, we conduct an ablation study in which SmartBERT is replaced by general-purpose pre-trained models, RoBERTa and CodeBERT, as the encoder for generating smart contract representations, while keeping all other architectural components and training configurations unchanged.
As reported in Table

, both ablation variants exhibit a noticeable decline in performance: the model with RoBERTa achieves a micro F1 of 0.8962, and with CodeBERT, 0.8906, both substantially lower than the 0.9279 attained by
SmartIntentNN2
with SmartBERT.
This result underscores the critical role of domain-adaptive pre-training, as SmartBERT’s exposure to large-scale smart contract corpora enables it to capture domain-specific syntactic and semantic patterns that are not well represented in general-purpose models.

SmartIntentNN2
with SmartBERT consistently outperforms all ablation baselines across all metrics.

We formulate and address the following research questions:

RQ1: Effectiveness of Domain-Specific Pre-training

How does SmartBERT’s domain-specific pre-training on smart contract code contribute to intent detection performance compared to general-purpose pre-trained models?

Motivation:

Discussing whether domain-specific pre-training provides meaningful improvements over general-purpose language models is crucial for justifying the computational overhead of training specialized models for smart contract analysis.

Approach:

We conduct ablation studies comparing SmartBERT (trained on 16,000 smart contracts) against CodeBERT and RoBERTa baselines using identical downstream architectures and training procedures.

Answer to RQ1:

As shown in Table
3
, SmartBERT consistently outperforms general-purpose models across all evaluation metrics.
In the second balanced training phase,
SmartIntentNN2
with SmartBERT achieves a micro-averaged F1 of
0.9279
, outperforming using CodeBERT (0.8906) and RoBERTa (0.8962).
The macro-averaged results further highlight the advantage of domain-specific pre-training: SmartBERT achieves
0.8626
macro F1 score compared to 0.7735 for CodeBERT and 0.8026 for RoBERTa.
This demonstrates that SmartBERT’s domain-specific pre-training enables better capture of smart contract-specific syntactic patterns and semantic nuances that are not well-represented in general programming language corpora.

RQ2: Impact of Two-Phase Training Strategy

What is the effectiveness of the two-phase training approach, and how does the secondary class-balanced training phase improve performance on imbalanced intent categories?

Motivation:

Smart contract intent detection suffers from severe class imbalance, with some intents (
e.g.
,
MaxSell
,
Honeypot
,
Rebase
) appearing in less than 1% of contracts (shown in Figure
5
).
Understanding the contribution of balanced sampling strategies is essential for handling real-world deployment scenarios.

Approach:

We compare the complete training results (Phase 1 only) with the balanced sample training results (Phase 1 + Phase 2) across different pre-trained models to quantify the impact of the secondary training phase.
The second phase employs class-balanced sampling with 10 instances per intent category and a reduced learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
.

Answer to RQ2:

As demonstrated in Table
3
, the two-phase training strategy consistently improves performance across all pre-trained model variants.
For
SmartIntentNN2
with SmartBERT, the macro-averaged F1-score improves from 0.7125 (complete training) to
0.8626
(balanced training), a
21.07%
relative improvement.
Similar patterns are observed for RoBERTa (0.6699 to 0.8026,
+19.8%
relative improvement) and CodeBERT (0.6852 to 0.7735,
+12.89%
relative improvement).
The balanced training phase yields substantial improvements in macro metrics, particularly in recall and F1-score.
This is because macro-averaged metrics compute per-class results and assign equal importance to each intent class, thereby highlighting gains in minority classes that may be underestimated by micro-averaged metrics.

RQ3: Architectural Enhancements in V2

What are the key architectural improvements in
SmartIntentNN2
over its predecessor, and how do these enhancements contribute to the observed performance gains?

Motivation:

Identifying the specific components responsible for performance improvements guides future research directions and provides insights into effective architectures for code intent detection.

Approach:

We systematically compare the architectural differences between V1 and V2, including the encoder, training strategy, and loss function optimizations.
We quantify the contribution of each component to the overall performance improvement.

Answer:

SmartIntentNN2
demonstrates a
7.48%
relative improvement in F1 over V1 (0.9279
vs.
0.8633).
The key enhancements include:
(1)
SmartBERT Integration
: Replacing USE with domain-adapted BERT embeddings provides richer contextual representations of smart contract functions, contributing to improved semantic understanding;
(2)
Two-Phase Training
: The class-balanced secondary training significantly improves minority class detection without sacrificing majority class performance;
(3)
Binary Focal Loss
: This loss function effectively handles class imbalance by emphasizing hard examples and down-weighting easy negatives.
The combination of these architectural improvements results in more robust and balanced performance across all intent categories, establishing
SmartIntentNN2
as the new SoTA for smart contract intent detection.

5.
Threats to Validity

This section discusses various threats to the validity of our study, covering both internal and external factors that could affect our results.
These include data labeling inaccuracies, model and hyperparameter optimization, data imbalance, dataset coverage, and adaptability to evolving attack patterns.

5.1.
Internal Validity

Ground-Truth Accuracy:

Although we employed both automated pattern matching and manual audits by domain auditors to label the dataset, inaccuracies in multi-label annotations may persist.
We conducted iterative cross-verification to reconcile inconsistent labels and performed additional reviews on randomly sampled subsets to validate annotation quality.
We have also open-sourced our dataset to facilitate further industrial and academic review.
Contracts deemed ambiguous or highly disputed were omitted from inclusion in both the training and evaluation sets.
Despite these measures, minor imperfections in labeling may still exist, affecting the internal validity of our findings.

Model Selection and Hyperparameters:

In our study, we selected
codebert-base-mlm
as the initialized model for training SmartBERT, acknowledging that the model’s architecture and parameter settings may impact subsequent outcomes.
To ensure robust performance, we conducted extensive ablation testing, including experiments with BERT and RoBERTa variants to fine-tune different versions of SmartBERT.
For the downstream classification task, we tested various models, such as LSTM, CNN, and basic feedforward neural networks, along with different hyperparameters, including LSTM units, dropout rates, and normalization layers.
From these numerous experiments, the current model configuration was selected.
However, it is important to note that this setup may not represent the absolute optimum, posing an internal threat.
Thus, it remains open for long-term optimization and refinement.

5.2.
External Validity

Figure 5.
Distribution of intent types in the dataset.

Data Imbalance:

As depicted in Figure
5
, certain intent categories, such as
Rebase
and
Honeypot
, comprise less than 1% in our dataset, reflecting their infrequent occurrence in real-world mainnet code.
Even though a class-balanced second training phase was employed using randomized sampling, this threat persists due to the inherent scarcity of these data samples.
This scarcity suggests that these intents are indeed less prevalent in actual development, thereby naturally reducing their risk profile.
We are committed to continually collecting smart contract data from public blockchain mainnets to incrementally include more of these underrepresented samples.
This ongoing effort will facilitate further training and optimization of our model to better account for these low-frequency intents.

Dataset Coverage:

Our dataset primarily comprises open-source smart contracts from public blockchains like Ethereum and BSC, mainly using Solidity code, although it includes a small number of Vyper contracts
(
buterin2018vyper
;
vyper2025latest
)
.
There is a lack of data from private and consortium blockchains, which poses a threat to the effectiveness of our method.
We are working on enhancing the dataset with more Vyper contract data, and for non-public and non-open-source data, we require authorization to access these.
Upon obtaining access, we can use these new datasets for expanded training to improve our model’s effectiveness.

Emerging Attacks:

As the development of smart contracts expands, new categories of malicious intents are likely to emerge, posing potential threats to our model’s detection capabilities.
To address this challenge, our neural network model is designed to be both extensible and retrainable.
We can swiftly update our dataset with new samples and conduct extensive training to adapt the model to these evolving threats.

SmartIntentNN2
also benefits from its modular architecture; SmartBERT, as a pre-trained model, remains unchanged and does not require retraining.
By focusing on modifying the downstream neural network layers and utilizing a small amount of new dataset for training, we can effectively identify these unsafe intents, maintaining robustness against emerging attack vectors.

6.
Related Work

We review prior studies related to our work along three directions: (i) pre-trained models for natural and programming languages, which provide foundations for smart contract analysis; (ii) smart contract vulnerability detection, focusing on external risks; and (iii) malicious behaviors and developer intents, reflecting internal risks.

6.1.
Pre-trained Models for NLP and PLP

Pre-trained language models have transformed both natural language processing (NLP) and program language processing (PLP), and their advances are directly relevant to our work.
In NLP, general-purpose models such as BERT
(
devlin2018bert
)
and RoBERTa
(
liu2019roberta
)
provide strong contextual embeddings for a wide range of downstream tasks.
In PLP, specialized models such as CodeBERT
(
feng2020codebert
)
, CodeT5
(
wang2021codet5
)
, CodeT5+
(
wang2023codet5+
)
, and CuBERT
(
kanade2020learning
)
extend pre-training techniques to source code, supporting tasks such as code search, code summarization, clone detection, and defect detection.
More recently, LLMs have been explored for smart contract analysis: Smart-LLaMA-DPO
(
yu2025smart
)
applies reinforcement learning to improve explainability in vulnerability detection, while SCALM
(
li2025scalm
)
leverages prompting and retrieval to identify bad practices.

Compared to these lines of work, our approach differs in three aspects.
First, unlike general-purpose models (
e.g.
, BERT, RoBERTa) and code-focused pre-trained models (
e.g.
, CodeT5, CodeBERT), we design a domain-specific pre-trained model for smart contracts, tailored to capture their unique semantics.
Second, in contrast to recent LLM-based approaches, our model adopts an encoder-only architecture that is lightweight and efficient, making it easier to integrate with neural layers such as BiLSTMs for specialized tasks.
Finally, rather than focusing on generic vulnerability detection or programming best practices, our model explicitly targets
unsafe developer intents
, providing representations optimized for intent-level security analysis in smart contracts.

Research in smart contract security has traditionally focused on detecting vulnerabilities as external risks,

, flaws unintentionally introduced during development.

, Oyente

More recent work like ÆGIS

With the rise of machine learning and large models, vulnerability detection has advanced substantially.
SaferSC
(

adopts LSTM-based sequential models, while ContractWard
(

Chen

Yu

Li

Despite these advances, the primary focus remains on vulnerabilities or coding practices.
Our work differs by targeting

Several studies investigate specific scams.

While these works reveal important threats, they typically target specific malicious behaviors such as scams or backdoors, rather than providing a systematic view of unsafe developer intents.
In contrast, our earlier work,
SmartIntentNN

(

)

Building on this line, we extend the focus from threat-specific detection to a broader framework of high-risk developer intents, enabling proactive warnings for both developers and users before malicious behaviors materialize.

7.
Conclusion

We propose
SmartIntentNN2
, an improved deep learning model for smart contract intent detection.
The key improvement is the use of a BERT-based pre-trained model trained on smart contract function-level code.
This model generates contextual embeddings that a BiLSTM multi-label classifier uses to detect unsafe intents.
Evaluation on 10,000 smart contracts shows that
SmartIntentNN2
achieves an F1 of
0.927
, outperforming previous methods and other baselines.
Our approach sets a new SoTA in smart contract intent detection.
