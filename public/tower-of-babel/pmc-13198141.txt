Rapid Evolution of Sine-Wave Electrocardiographic Morphology Preceding Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Ahmed Y.M. El-Medany, Shahid Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case SummaryWe describe the case of a 21-year-old man with severe traumatic brain injury requiring prolonged sedation and vasopressor support, subsequently developing a sudden broad-complex sinusoidal rhythm, and degenerating into electromechanical dissociation and cardiac arrest. A differential diagnosis is discussed.Take-Home MessagesSuspect propofol infusion syndrome in patients receiving high-dose propofol (>4 mg/kg/h) or infusions >48 hours and recognize acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hypertriglyceridemia as the principal early warnings, followed by a “Brugada-like” electrocardiogram pattern. Minimize exposure in neurotrauma and intensive care unit patients; once sinusoidal rhythms appear, toxicity is often refractory, and early cessation is the only effective strategy.

Case

A 21-year-old man was admitted to the intensive care unit after a moped collision resulting in severe traumatic brain injury. Initial blood gas analysis revealed a new metabolic acidosis (pH 7.3) and hyperkalemia (5.9 mmol/L). Discussion

The initial ECG (Figure 1A) demonstrates a regular, broad-complex tachycardia with a sinusoidal morphology. A key diagnostic feature is the presence of coved ST-segment elevations in the right precordial leads (V2-V3), mimicking a type 1 Brugada pattern and reflecting profound sodium channel blockade. Within 5 minutes (Figure 1B), there is rapid interval widening and further loss of QRS-T separation (V3), indicating severe intraventricular conduction delay. This progression culminates in a classic “sine wave” pattern (Figure 1C) (leads I, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, and V6), where the QRS complex fuses with the T-wave, signifying critical myocardial toxicity and secondary hyperkalemia. The final stage (Figure 1D) is a chaotic rhythm representing electromechanical dissociation.

Although acute refractory bradycardia is frequently described in PRIS, it is not a universal or obligatory feature.1,2 Early or evolving disease may present with malignant conduction abnormalities and ventricular arrhythmias before terminal bradyasystole, particularly in the presence of severe metabolic acidosis and hyperkalemia. Accordingly, the absence of initial bradycardia does not exclude the diagnosis in this case.

PRIS is a rare, often fatal complication of propofol infusion. Risk factors include prolonged (>48 hours) or high-dose (>4 mg/kg/h) infusions, young age, neurologic injury, and critical illness.1 This patient exhibited a “perfect storm” of these risk factors. The cumulative propofol dose exceeded the high-risk threshold of >240 mg/kg.2,3

Although the broad complex rhythm mimics ventricular tachycardia, the PRIS sinusoidal morphology often lacks distinct QRS-T separation (Figures 1A to 1D) and, combined with metabolic acidosis, points to a toxic-metabolic etiology. Hyperkalemia is a secondary feature of PRIS, not the primary diagnosis. Cushing's reflex causes bradycardia.

The pathophysiology involves dose-dependent mitochondrial uncoupling and inhibition of fatty acid oxidation, causing adenosine triphosphate depletion and cellular hypoxia.1, 2, 3 Neurologic injury increases propofol clearance via catecholamine surges, often necessitating higher doses,1,3 and the concurrent use of catecholamines (noradrenaline) acts in opposition to propofol on cardiac β-receptors, accelerating myocardial depression.1,3

The principal warning features of PRIS are biochemical—specifically metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, and hypertriglyceridemia.1, 2, 3 In this case, biochemistry revealed elevated lactate (7.7 mmol/L), creatine kinase (1,881 IU/L), and triglycerides (2.4 mmol/L) (Supplemental Table 1). These often precede the “Brugada-like” ECG pattern, which serves as an early warning before the terminal sine wave develops.

Management relies primarily on early recognition and immediate cessation of propofol. Although isolated case reports describe some success with hemofiltration and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, the mainstay of treatment remains discontinuation.1 In this case, although the infusion was stopped at the onset of the sinusoidal rhythm, the patient still died, demonstrating that once gross conduction abnormalities appear, myocardial toxicity is often irreversible.
