Asymptotically optimal reinforcement learning in Block Markov Decision Processes
Thomas van Vuren, Fiona Sloothaak, Maarten G. Wolf, Jaron Sanders

Abstract
The curse of dimensionality renders Reinforcement Learning (RL) impractical in many real-world settings with exponentially large state and action spaces. Yet, many environments exhibit exploitable structure that can accelerate learning. To formalize this idea, we study RL in Block Markov Decision Processes (BMDPs) . BMDPs model problems with large observation spaces, but where transition dynamics are fully determined by latent states. Recent advances in clustering methods have enabled the efficient recovery of this latent structure. However, a regret analysis that exploits these techniques to determine their impact on learning performance remained open. We are now addressing this gap by providing a regret analysis that explicitly leverages clustering, demonstrating that accurate latent state estimation can indeed effectively speed up learning. Concretely, this paper analyzes a two-phase RL algorithm for BMDPs that first learns the latent structure through random exploration and then switches to an optimism-guided strategy adapted to the uncovered structure. This algorithm achieves a regret that is O ⁡ ( T + n ) O(\sqrt{T}+n) on a large class of BMDPs susceptible to clustering. Here, T T denotes the number of time steps, n n is the cardinality of the observation space, and the Landau notation O ⁡ ( ⋅ ) O(\cdot) holds up to constants and polylogarithmic factors. This improves the best prior bound, O ⁡ ( T + n 2 ) O(\sqrt{T}+n^{2}) , especially when n n is large. Moreover, we prove that no algorithm can achieve lower regret uniformly on this same class of BMDPs . This establishes that, on this class, the algorithm achieves asymptotic optimality.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

The field of machine learning, and artificial intelligence more generally, has taken society by storm.
One of its key techniques,
Reinforcement Learning (RL)

[
38
]
, is applied everywhere.
It is, for example, applied to problems in finance, games, healthcare, industrial control, and robotics.

However, despite its promise,
RL
still faces numerous practical challenges.
One key difficulty is the curse of dimensionality
[
38
]
, which can make learning in problems with large state spaces intractable.
This challenge is particularly nasty because the state space size is often exponential in the number of system components.

These issues can be unavoidable if no additional structure is present in a system.
There namely exist
Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
with
n
states on which the expected regret after
T
time steps is at least
\Omega(\sqrt{nT})

[
34
]
.
When
n
is exponentially large, such regret can render
RL
impractical.

Fortunately, many real-world environments exhibit exploitable structure.
For instance, if different states share similar transition probabilities, then we could first learn a clustering of the state space.
This reduces the effective size of the state space, mitigating the impact of the curse of dimensionality.

1.2
State-space reduction in Block MDPs

In this paper, we conduct a regret analysis for an episodic learning strategy that exploits a state-space reduction technique tailored to
Block Markov Decision Processes (BMDPs)
.

BMDPs

[
29
]
are
MDPs
in which observations are drawn from a large set of contexts.
The transitions dynamics, however, are determined fully by just a few unobserved latent states.
Specifically, each context
x
is associated to a latent state
s=f(x)
, and the system moves from latent state
s
to latent state
s^{\prime}
according to a transition kernel
p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
.
The agent then observes only a context
y\in f^{-1}(s^{\prime})
drawn from the emission distribution
q(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})
.
The map
f
is called the latent state decoding function, and the presumed challenge is that it is hidden from the agent.

The structural assumptions just discussed are what make
BMDPs
interesting and different from generic
MDPs
.
The remaining parameters, including the reward and episode structure, are summarized in
Figure

1
.
Please see
Section

2
for details.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of an episodic
BMDP
.
Shown from bottom to top are the hidden latent state sequence
s_{k,h}
, the action sequence
a_{k,h}
, the observed context sequence
x_{k,h}
, and the reward sequence
r_{h}(x,a)
.
After
H
transitions, the environment resets, and the next episode starts from a random context drawn from
\mu
.
Each episode, the agent gets to choose and fix a policy.

Recently, a clustering technique developed for
Block Markov Chains (BMCs)

[
35
]
, inspired by techniques for (labeled)
Stochastic Block Models (SBMs)

[
1
,
45
]
, was generalized to
BMDPs

[
24
]
.
Specifically,
[
24
]
mathematically studies a technique that estimates the latent state decoding function efficiently based on data generated under a fixed policy.
This technique was then used in
[
24
]
to study the sample complexity of a reward-free
RL
algorithm.
A regret analysis when exploiting these techniques, however, remained open.
This gap is addressed in this manuscript.

In typical regret analyses, one studies not only specific algorithms that minimize regret but also the least amount of regret that
every
algorithm will acquire
[
8
]
.
The fact that some regret will always be accumulated is, ultimately, because exploration must be balanced with exploitation.

Most methods tackle this so-called exploration–exploitation trade-off using the optimism–in–the–face–of–uncertainty principle
[
38
]
.
It is implemented in
MDPs
by computing
Upper Confidence Bounds (UCBs)
on the estimated long-term reward following a visit to a state–action pair
[
6
,
10
,
46
]
.

UCBs
are added to the immediate reward during policy updates as an exploration bonus, encouraging visits to state–action pairs with uncertain value.
This reduces uncertainty, leading to smaller bonuses and less exploration overall.

Now, in
BMDPs
, we can leverage the block structure by aggregating related transitions to reduce the value estimates’ variances.
This yields tighter
UCBs
and smaller exploration bonuses, ultimately reducing regret.
But, the quality of those value estimates would then depend on how well the block structure is estimated.
Misclassified contexts induce biases and therefore complicate efficient exploration.

To avoid this source of approximation error, we adopt a two–phase approach by building on
[
24
]
.
Under their structural assumptions, clustering techniques efficiently recover the latent structure through random exploration.
This enables a sharp regret analysis, which we present in this paper.

1.3
Summary of results

1.3.1
Structural properties

We analyze the following subclasses of
BMDPs
:

Definition 1.1

(
\eta
-Reachability)
.

Let
\eta>1
be independent of
n
.
A
BMDP

\Phi
is
\eta
-reachable if it has the following properties:

(i)

\max_{a}\max_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}}\{p(s_{2}\mid s_{1},a)/p(s_{3}\mid s_{1},a),p(s_{1}\mid s_{2},a)/p(s_{1}\mid s_{3},a)\}\leq\eta
;

(ii)

\max_{s}\max_{x,y\in f^{-1}(s)}q(x\mid s)/q(y\mid s)\leq\eta
; and

(iii)

for all sufficiently large
n
,

\max_{s_{1},s_{2}}|f^{-1}(s_{1})|/|f^{-1}(s_{2})|\leq\eta

.

Definition 1.2

(
\mathcal{I}
-Identifiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{I}>0
be independent of
n
.
An
\eta
-reachable
BMDP

\Phi
is
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable if, for all sufficiently large
n
,
\min_{x}I_{\eta}(x;\Phi)\allowbreak\geq\allowbreak\mathcal{I}
; see (
15
) for
I_{\eta}
’s definition.

A brief explanation of
Definitions

1.1
and
1.2
is as follows.
The reachability property ensures that random exploration achieves coverage of the observation space.
The identifiability property ensures that it is information-theoretically possible to detect the latent states.
The parameter
I_{\eta}
can be interpreted as a measure of separation between latent states.
For more detailed discussion on
Definitions

1.1
and
1.2
, we refer to
Appendix

B
.

1.3.2
Regret lower bound

Our first result is a regret lower bound for
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable
BMDPs
.
To state it, let
n
denote the number of contexts,
S
the number of latent states,
A
the number of actions,
H
the episode horizon,
K
the number of episodes, and
T_{K}=KH
the total number of time steps.
We denote by
\textnormal{Reg}_{K}(\mathcal{L};\Phi)
the expected regret after
K
episodes of a learning algorithm
\mathcal{L}
on the
BMDP

\Phi
; see (
10
) for the precise definition.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that the following hold:

S=o(n),\quad S/4\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2},\quad H\geq 2,\quad\textnormal{and}\quad A\geq S/2.

(1)

There exists a constant
\mathfrak{d}>0
independent of
n
such that the following holds:
for any
\eta>1
independent of
n

and any learning algorithm
\mathcal{L}
,
there exists a
BMDP

\Phi
with initial context distribution
\mu=\mathrm{Unif}([n])

that is

\eta
-reachable
and
I_{\eta}:=\mathfrak{d}\eta^{-1}(\eta-1)^{2}/(\eta+1)^{3}
-identifiable.
If moreover
T_{K}=\Omega(n\vee A)
, then

\textnormal{Reg}_{K}(\mathcal{L};\Phi)=\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}}+n).

(2)

Theorem

1.3
identifies the two asymptotically leading regret contributions.
Firstly, the
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}})
term captures the cost of learning the latent state transition probabilities.
Secondly, the
\Omega(n)
term encapsulates the cost of learning the latent structure.

1.3.3
Two-phase algorithm and regret upper bound

Our second result establishes that an explicit two–phase algorithm achieves near-optimal worst-case regret in
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable
BMDPs
.

Algorithms

1
,
3
and
2
in
Section

4
contain the pseudocode.

The first phase consists of exploration happening uniformly at random.
Its duration is set by a threshold
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}
.
Based on the observations made, the latent structure is estimated using
[
24
, Algorithms 1 and 2]
.
The second phase uses an optimism–based strategy to balance exploration with exploitation while refining its transition probability estimates.
It adapts
[
6
, Algorithms 1 and 2]
, designed for
MDPs
without latent structure, by incorporating improved value estimates and smaller exploration bonuses that take advantage of the learned structure.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
T_{K}=\Omega(H^{4}S^{3}A)
,
A=n^{O(1)}
, and
SH=O(n)
.
Assume, moreover, that the
BMDP

\Phi
is
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable, and that
\mu=\mathrm{Unif}([n])
.
If
Algorithm

1
is run with threshold
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}=\omega(nA(S^{3}\vee\ln n))
, then for any
c>0
,

\textnormal{Reg}_{K}(\mathcal{L};\Phi)=\tilde{O}\bigl(H\sqrt{SAT_{K}}+nSH^{3}+T_{K}/n^{c}+\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}\bigr).

(3)

Here, the notation
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
suppresses polylogarithmic factors.

To facilitate comparison with
Theorem

1.3
, consider that (
3
) implies for
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}=nS^{3}A\ln^{2}n
and
T_{K}=n^{O(1)}
that

\textnormal{Reg}_{K}(\mathcal{L};\Phi)=\tilde{O}\bigl(H\sqrt{SAT_{K}}+n(S^{3}A+SH^{3})\bigr).

(4)

This result is optimal in the sense that it matches
Theorem

1.3
’s dependence on
n
and
T_{K}
up to polylogarithmic factors.

It is also instructive to compare
Theorem

1.3
to other regret bounds for
BMDPs
when clustering.
The one paper closest in spirit that we found is
[
7
]
.
There, the authors prove a regret bound for an online algorithm that also uses spectral methods.
When specialized to
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable
BMDPs
, their bound is only
\tilde{O}(DS\sqrt{AT}\allowbreak+\allowbreak n^{2}SA)
.
Here,
D
bounds the time required to move between two contexts, replacing
H
in their nonepisodic setting.
Their result, however, does apply also to other
BMDPs
, not just
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable ones.

Our result improves the regret’s dependence on the number of contexts from
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
to
\tilde{O}(n)
, at the cost of a greater dependence on
S
,
A
, and
H
.
This leads to a better bound for sufficiently large observation spaces, specifically when
n=\omega(S^{2}+H^{3}/A)
.
We achieve this by performing dedicated exploration, and incorporating the efficient latent state estimation algorithm from
[
24
]
.

In addition, (
4
) improves the leading-order regret for large
T_{K}
by
\sqrt{S}
.
This is significant because
[
7
]
assumes rewards depend solely on the latent state.
In this case it is unnecessary to learn the emission kernel, simplifying the analysis considerably.
Our improvement stems from leveraging the algorithms and proof techniques in
[
6
]
, rather than the earlier
[
23
]
, upon which
[
7
]
builds.

1.4
Summary of proof techniques

1.4.1
Proof of the regret lower bound

Theorem

1.3
’s proof is presented in
Section

3
.
We define a class of hard–to–learn
BMDPs
, adapted from
[
24
]
, and show that it includes instances inducing
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}}+n)
regret.

To establish the
\Omega(n)
contribution, we prove a new lower bound on the worst-case number of misclassifications of
any
latent state estimator on
BMDPs
in our class (see
Proposition

3.13
).
Existing lower bounds, such as
[
24
, Theorem 1]
and the closely related
[
35
, Theorem 1]
, do bound the number of misclassifications on any given
BMDP
, but only for algorithms satisfying a consistency condition.
Instead, our class is such that any algorithm violating this condition must perform poorly on at least one instance.
The construction is based on a construction used for random graph clustering
[
47
]
.

The proof for the
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}})
contribution leverages techniques developed for tabular
MDPs

[
11
,
36
]

These were in turn adapted from the literature on bandit learning
[
8
]
.
A direct application of
[
11
]
’s construction to the induced
MDP
on the latent states would lead to an
\Omega(\sqrt{HSAT_{K}})
lower bound for general
BMDPs
.
However, the resulting instances would not be
\eta
-reachable or
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable.
We therefore modify those constructions to comply with
Definitions

1.1
and
1.2
.
This leads to a slightly weaker but still
\Omega(\sqrt{T_{K}})
-type regret.

1.4.2
Proof of the regret upper bound

Theorem

1.4
’s proof is presented in
Section

5
.
We establish there that our algorithm’s first phase successfully recovers the latent structure.
We subsequently bound the regret incurred during the algorithm’s second phase.

For the first phase, we refine
[
24
]
’s analysis of
[
24
, Algorithms 1 and 2]
in two ways.
First, whereas
[
24
]
establishes exact latent state recovery with high probability as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we provide an explicit probability bound.
This is required for bounding the expected regret.
Second, we relax their assumptions, allowing
S
,
A
and
p
to depend on
n
.
Moreover,
Definition

1.2
’s condition is slightly weaker than its analogue in
[
24
]
; see
Appendix

B
for a comparison.
These relaxations are necessary, because we could only prove
Theorem

1.3
under these weaker assumptions.

For the second phase, we leverage that
Algorithm

1
has already recovered the latent structure exactly.
In this case, the exploration bonuses need not account for uncertainty about this structure.
This allows us to broadly follow the analysis of
[
6
]
.
The main new difficulty arises from the structure of the
BMDP
kernel, involving both a latent state transition kernel and emission probabilities.
Their estimators are statistically dependent, and a key part of our analysis is a decomposition that disentangles their contributions to the regret.

1.5
Related literature

Our work connects to the following three research directions.

1.5.1
Function approximation for block and low-rank
MDPs

RL
in
BMDPs
often uses function approximation.
This assumes that the decoding- or value function belongs to a known, well-behaved function class
\mathcal{F}
.
Prior work has used function approximation for
Probably Approximately Correct (PAC)
learning
[
29
,
26
,
9
,
12
,
49
]
, which bounds the time required to find a near-optimal policy; reward-free learning
[
49
,
31
]
, where the agent explores without observing rewards; and regret minimization
[
14
]
, which is the focus of this paper.
These methods have also been applied to more general models such as low-rank
MDPs

[
3
,
42
,
32
,
30
]
, which include
BMDPs
as special cases.

The use of function approximation is typically motivated by settings with infinite or continuous context spaces.
Function approximation is then necessary to generalize to unseen contexts.
By contrast, we consider
BMDPs
with finite context spaces and arbitrary decoding functions.
In this case,
[
24
]
notes that function approximation achieves the same sample complexity scaling in
n
as tabular methods.
In other words, these methods do not exploit the inherent block structure, but instead benefit from the restrictions imposed by the class
\mathcal{F}
.
We therefore also do not rely on function approximation or oracles for latent state estimation.

1.5.2
Latent state estimation and clustering in block models

Another line of research develops explicit algorithms for learning the latent structure from observations.
This includes
[
7
]
, which estimates the latent structure using a spectral method based on
[
4
]
.
However, as explained in
Section

1.3.3
, this leads to suboptimal regret in the worst case.
Instead, we build on
[
24
]
, whose algorithm uses a two-step approach: an initial spectral estimate is refined using a likelihood-based improvement step.
This approach was inspired by similar techniques developed for
BMCs

[
35
]
(but see also
[
13
,
48
,
50
,
43
]
), which, in turn, build on techniques for
SBMs

[
44
,
45
]
.

[
24
]
also shows that their algorithm is nearly optimal by deriving an information-theoretic lower bound.
This is done using change-of-measure arguments, likewise inspired by
[
35
,
44
,
45
]
.
As detailed in
Section

1.3
, our work adds to these developments in latent state estimation by proving a new lower bound on the number of misclassifications, and refining
[
24
]
’s analysis of their algorithm.

1.5.3
Regret minimization in
MDPs
with known structure

Recall that, after learning the latent structure, we adapt the optimism-based approach of
[
6
]
to minimize the regret while refining our transition probability estimates.
We use specialized kernel estimates and bonus terms that exploit the latent structure, leading to improved regret.
This approach has also been successfully applied to other types of structured
MDPs
.
For instance,
[
40
]
designs exploration bonuses for factored
MDPs
,
[
17
]
for Markovian bandits, and
[
28
]
for linear
MDPs
.
Similar two–phase approaches have also been considered in the context of bandit learning
[
18
,
19
,
25
]
.

Linear
MDPs
in particular include
BMDPs
(with known latent structure) as a special case.
One can therefore also use algorithms designed for linear
MDPs
after the initial structure-learning phase.
However, because linear
MDPs
are more general than
BMDPs
, these algorithms do not fully exploit the discrete structure of
BMDPs
.
They therefore do not achieve optimal regret on
BMDPs
, with the best regret bounds being
\tilde{O}(SA\sqrt{HT_{K}})

[
20
,
2
,
22
]
.
We exploit the additional structure of
BMDPs
to improve this by a factor of
\sqrt{SA}
in
Theorem

1.4
.

Model definitions

Elementary notation

For
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
[m]\vcentcolon=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.
Functions like
v:[m]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are identified with vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with components
v(x)
for
x\in[m]
relative to the standard basis.
For
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
\langle v,w\rangle
denote the standard inner product.
Finally, let
\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{X})
refer to the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{X}\subset[m]
.

2.1
Block Markov Decision Processes

BMDPs
are described by tuples
\Phi=(n,S,A,H,\allowbreak f,\allowbreak p,\allowbreak q,\mu,r)
.
Here,
n,S,A,H\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
denote the number of contexts, latent states, actions, and episode horizon, respectively.
Unique to a Block
MDP
is that there is a
decoding function

f:[n]\rightarrow[S]
that assigns contexts to latent states, and that the latent states determine the dynamics.
Specifically,
BMDPs
have a
latent state transition kernel

p:[S]\times[A]\times[S]\rightarrow[0,1]
and an
emission kernel

q:[S]\times[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
.
Here,
p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
is the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
given that the system is in state
s
when choosing action
a
, and
q(x\mid s)
the probability of observing context
x
given that the system is in latent state
s
.

The triple
(f,p,q)
specifies the
MDP
’s transition kernel
P:[n]\times[A]\times[n]\rightarrow[0,1]
completely.
Specifically,

P(y\mid x,a)\vcentcolon=p(f(y)\mid f(x),a)q(y\mid f(y))

(5)

is the probability of transitioning to context
y
given that the current context is
x
when choosing action
a
.
Note that the probability of moving to context
y
depends only on the current latent state and not the specific context.

Finally,
r=\{r_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
with
r_{h}:[n]\times[A]\rightarrow[0,1]
denotes a bounded, nonstationary and deterministic reward function that may depend on the context that is observed.

2.2
Policies

A
memoryless policy

\pi
is a collection
\pi=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
of mappings
\pi_{h}:[n]\times[A]\rightarrow[0,1]
where
\pi_{h}(a\mid x)
denotes the probability of selecting action
a
when observing context
x
at round
h
.
Memoryless policies do not depend on the sequence of contexts prior to the current context.
Because we may only consider memoryless policies, we will simply refer to them as policies.

Together, a
BMDP

\Phi
and a policy
\pi
define a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,\pi}
and a random variable
(x_{1},a_{1},\ldots,x_{H},a_{H},x_{H+1})
representing the sequence of contexts and actions satisfying
\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,\pi}[x_{1}=x]=\mu(x)
,
\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,\pi}[a_{h}=a\mid x_{h}=x]=\pi_{h}(a\mid x)
and
\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,\pi}[x_{h+1}=y\mid x_{h}=x,a_{h}=a]=P(y\mid x,a)
.

2.3
Value functions

For
h\in[H]
, define the Q-function
Q^{\pi}_{h}
and value function
V^{\pi}_{h}
of a policy
\pi
element-wise as

Q^{\pi}_{h}(x,a)\vcentcolon=\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\Phi,\pi}[r_{h}(x_{h^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}})\mid x_{h}=x,a_{h}=a]\quad\mathrm{and}\quad V^{\pi}_{h}(x)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}_{\Phi,\pi}[Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a_{h})]

(6)

for
x\in[n]
,
a\in[A]
.
These satisfy the following Bellman recursion:

Q^{\pi}_{h}(x,a)=r_{h}(x,a)+\sum_{y}P(y\mid x,a)V_{h+1}^{\pi}(y),\quad V_{h}^{\pi}(x)=\sum_{a}\pi_{h}(a\mid x)Q_{h}^{\pi}(x,a),\quad V_{H+1}^{\pi}(x)\vcentcolon=0.

(7)

When
n
,
S
,
A
and
H
are all finite, there always exists a policy
\pi^{*}=\{\pi_{h}^{*}\}_{h\in[H]}
that satisfies
V_{h}^{\pi^{*}}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}V^{\pi}_{h}(x)
for all
x\in[n]
and
h\in[H]
.
Such a policy can be explicitly constructed through backward value iteration using (
7
).
Namely, starting from the initial condition
V_{H+1}^{\pi^{*}}=0
, we can obtain an optimal policy and value function at round
h\in[H]
by setting

\pi_{h}^{*}(\,\cdot\mid x)=\mathrm{Unif}(\argmax_{a}Q_{h}^{\pi^{*}}(x,a))\quad\textnormal{and}\quad V_{h}^{\pi^{*}}(x)=\max_{a}Q_{h}^{\pi^{*}}(x,a).

(8)

2.4
Episodic learning algorithms and regret

An
episodic learning algorithm
selects a policy for each episode based on its previous observations and the known reward function.
Formally, such an algorithm is a collection
\mathcal{L}=\{\mathcal{L}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
of maps where for any
D_{k}\in(([n]\times[A])^{H}\times[n])^{k-1}
and reward function
r
,
\mathcal{L}_{k}(D_{k},r)
is a policy.

A
BMDP
and learning algorithm give rise to a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,\mathcal{L}}
along with a sequence of random variables
\mathcal{D}_{k}
for
k\geq 2
representing the contexts and actions during the first
k-1
episodes:

\mathcal{D}_{k}\vcentcolon=\otimes_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\mathcal{D}_{(k^{\prime})}\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\mathcal{D}_{(k^{\prime})}\vcentcolon=(x_{k^{\prime},1},a_{k^{\prime},1},\ldots,x_{k^{\prime},H},a_{k^{\prime},H},x_{k^{\prime},H+1}).

(9)

Then

3.1
Proof outline

To prove
Theorem

1.3
, we construct a hard–to–learn subclass
\Lambda^{*}
consisting of
\eta
-regular and
\mathcal{I}_{\eta}
-identifiable
BMDPs
.
We then lower bound the maximum regret over
BMDPs
in
\Lambda^{*}
.

The proof takes inspiration from
[
24
, Theorem 5]
.
It is, however, modified to
(i) comply with
Definitions

1.1
and
1.2
and
(ii) obtain a worst-case regret lower bound.
We distinguish three parts:

Part 1.

We define
\Lambda^{*}
and show that all
BMDPs
in
\Lambda^{*}
are
\eta
-regular and
\mathcal{I}_{\eta}
-identifiable.

Intuitively,
\Lambda^{*}
is constructed so that rewards are received only upon visiting a specific subset of latent states,
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{\lfloor S/2\rfloor+1,\ldots,S\}
, say.
In each latent state
s
, there is one action
a_{s}^{*}
that is
\varepsilon
more likely to transition to
\mathcal{S}_{1}
relative to the other actions.
Even if a context
x
is revealed to belong to
s
, the agent must still identify the optimal action
a_{s}^{*}
.
Conversely, even if
a_{s}^{*}
is known for each
s
, the agent must still infer which latent state
x
corresponds to.
By allowing
\varepsilon
to depend on whether
s
belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{1}
or
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}
, and tuning its value separately for each, we control which of these two learning challenges dominates.
This gives rise to the two terms
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}})
and
\Omega(n)
in (
2
).

Part 2.

The
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}})
term in (
2
) arises from having to learn the transition probabilities
even if
the latent structure is revealed.

To establish this, we analyze the induced
MDP
on the latent states.
Following
[
11
,
16
]
, we use a change–of–measure argument to first lower bound the number of times an algorithm selects suboptimal actions while visiting
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}
.
We then use the transition structure of the
BMDPs
in
\Lambda^{*}
to bound how often
\mathcal{S}_{1}^{\mathrm{c}}
is visited.
Specifying
\varepsilon=\Theta(\sqrt{A/T_{K}})
yields the
\Omega(\sqrt{AT_{K}})
contribution in (
2
).

Part 3.

The
\Omega(n)
term in (
2
) arises from having to learn the latent structure
even if
the optimal actions are revealed.

To formalize this, we fix a set of optimal actions
\{a_{s}^{*}\}_{s}
and consider the basic estimator that assign contexts
x
to latent states
s
whose action
a_{s}^{*}
are chosen most.
We then show that in
BMDPs
where
\{a_{s}^{*}\}_{s}
are indeed optimal, the regret is bounded from below by the number of contexts misclassified by this estimator.
Applying
Proposition

3.13
, which lower bounds the maximum number of misclassifications over
BMDPs
in
\Lambda^{*}
of
any
latent-state estimator, yields the
\Omega(n)
contribution when specifying
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
.

in (

Part 1: Bounding the failure probability

We begin by bounding
\mathbb{P}[\Omega^{f}]
by refining
[
24
]
’s analysis.

The first step is to address the gap between our notion of
\mathcal{I}
-identifiability and the corresponding condition in
[
24
]
.
As explained in
Section

B.2.2
,
[
24
]
requires an asymptotic bound on
\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}\min_{x}I_{\zeta}(x;\Phi)
, rather than
I_{\eta}(x;\Phi)
.
In
Section

D.1
, we show that for
\eta
-reachable
BMDPs
, these two conditions are equivalent:

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
\Phi
is
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable.
Then there exists a constant
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}>0
that is independent of
n
such that for all sufficiently large
n
,
\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}\min_{x}I_{\zeta}(x;\Phi)\geq\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.

Using
Lemma

5.1
, we can deduce the following corollary of
[
24
, Theorem 3]
:

Corollary 5.2
.

If
S
,
A
and
p
are independent of
n
,
\mu=\mathrm{Unif}([n])
, and
\Phi
is
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable, then there exist constants
C,\mathcal{I}^{\prime}>0
independent of
n
such that: if
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}=\omega(n)
, then
Algorithm

2
misclassifies at most
O(n\exp(-C(\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}/n)\mathcal{I}^{\prime}))
contexts with probability
1-o(1)
.

Consequently, under these assumptions,
\mathbb{P}[\Omega^{f}]=1-o(1)
if
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}=\omega(n\ln n)
.

This result is, however, not enough for our purposes.
We therefore refine it in three ways in
Section

D.2
.
First, we make the
1-o(1)
probability bound explicit by inspecting the proofs in
[
24
]
and keeping track of which events imply
\Omega^{f}
.
Second, we sharpen the resulting probability bound from
1-O(1/n)
by showing that it can be
1-O(1/n^{c})
for any
c>0
.
Third, we allow
S
,
A
, and
p
to depend on
n
and account for this in the asymptotics.
This gives the following result:

Proposition 5.3
.

Assume
\Phi
is
\eta
-reachable and
\mathcal{I}
-identifiable, and that
\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}=\omega(nA(S^{3}\vee\ln n))
,
\mu=\mathrm{Unif}([n])
,
A=n^{O(1)}
, and
H=O(n)
.
Then, for any
c>0
,
\mathbb{P}[\Omega^{f}]=1-O(1/n^{c})
.

Given
\Omega^{f}
, it is straightforward to show that
\Omega_{k}^{1},\ldots,\Omega_{k}^{4}
hold with probability
1-O(1/T_{k})
using Hoeffding and Bernstein’s inequalities.
Together with
Proposition

5.3
, this allows us to prove the following bound on
R_{1}
in
:

Proposition 5.4
.

Adopt the assumptions of
Proposition

5.3
, and let
k\in[K]
be such that
T_{k}=kH>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}
.
Then, for any
c>0
,

R_{1}=H\sum_{k\in[K]:T_{k}>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}}\mathbb{P}[(\Omega_{k})^{\mathrm{c}}]=O\biggl(\ln K+\frac{T_{K}}{n^{c}}\biggr)=\tilde{O}\biggl(1+\frac{T_{K}}{n^{c}}\biggr).

(47)

Part 2: Proving optimism

We next show that
R_{2}\leq 0
by proving that on
\Omega_{k}
,
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(x)-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h}(x)\geq 0
for all
h
and
x
.

Note from
Algorithm

3
that
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(x)
equals the optimal value function of a
BMDP

\bar{\Phi}_{k}
with kernel
\hat{P}_{k}
and reward function
1\wedge(r_{h}(x,a)+\mathbbm{1}\{h<H\}\hat{b}_{k}(\hat{f}^{\textnormal{alg}}(x),a))
by construction.
Here,
\hat{b}_{k}
is given by (
43

This characterization gives the following decomposition, proven in

Lemma 5.5
.

For
x\in[n]
,
h\in[H-1]
, and
k\in[K]
,

\begin{split}&\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(x)-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h}(x)\\
&\geq\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\Phi}_{k},\pi^{*}}\bigl[\hat{b}_{k}(\hat{f}^{\textnormal{alg}}(x_{h^{\prime}}),a_{h^{\prime}})+\bigl\langle\hat{P}_{k}(\,\cdot\mid x_{h^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}})-P(\,\cdot\mid x_{h^{\prime}},a_{h^{\prime}}),V^{\pi^{*}}_{h^{\prime}+1}\bigr\rangle\mid x_{h}=x\bigr].\end{split}

(48)

Here, the expectation is with respect to the sequence
(x_{1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{H},x_{H+1})
.

Lemma

5.5
implies that
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x)-V^{\pi^{*}}_{1}(x)\geq 0
whenever
|\langle\hat{P}_{k}(x,a)-P(x,a),V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\rangle|\leq\allowbreak\hat{b}_{k}(\hat{f}^{\textnormal{alg}}(x),a)
for all
x
,
a
, and
h
.
The following lemma verifies this condition on
\Omega_{k}
.
It is proven in
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For
x\in[n]
,
a\in[A]
,
h\in[H]
, and
k\in[K]
, on
\Omega_{k}
,

|\langle\hat{P}_{k}(\,\cdot\mid x,a)-P(\,\cdot\mid x,a),V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\rangle|\leq\hat{b}_{k}(f(x),a).

(49)

Combining
Lemmas

5.5
and
5.6
and using that
\hat{f}^{\textnormal{alg}}=f
on
\Omega^{f}
proves that
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(x)-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h}(x)\geq 0
on
\Omega_{k}
.
This in turn implies that
R_{2}\leq 0
on
\Omega_{k}
, establishing the following:

Proposition 5.7
.

For
k\in[K]
,
\Omega_{k}\subset\cap_{x,h}\{\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h}(x)\geq V^{\pi^{*}}_{h}(x)\}
.
Therefore,

R_{2}=\sum_{k\in[K]:T_{k}>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}}\mathbb{E}[(V^{\pi^{*}}_{1}(x_{k,1})-\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k,1}))\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}]\leq 0.

(50)

Part 3: Bounding the estimation error

To bound
R_{3}
, we fix an episode
k\in[K]
such that
T_{k}=kH>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}
.
We will now bound
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(V^{\pi^{*}}_{1}(x_{k,1})-\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k,1})\bigr)\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}\bigr]
for this episode.

Recall (
9
).
Following
[
6
]
, we decompose

\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k,1})-V^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k,1})=\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{b}_{k}(\hat{f}^{\textnormal{alg}}(x_{k,h}),a_{k,h})+E_{k,h}(x_{k,h},a_{k,h})+F_{k,h}(x_{k,h},a_{k,h})\mid\mathcal{D}_{k},x_{k,1}\bigr],

(51)

where

\begin{split}&E_{k,h}(x,a)\vcentcolon=\langle\hat{P}_{k}(\,\cdot\mid x,a)-P(\,\cdot\mid x,a),V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\rangle\quad\mathrm{and}\\
&F_{k,h}(x,a)\vcentcolon=\langle\hat{P}_{k}(\,\cdot\mid x,a)-P(\,\cdot\mid x,a),\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\rangle.\end{split}

(52)

A proof of (
51
) is included in
.
We now bound
E_{k,h}
and
F_{k,h}
.

While
E_{k,h}
is bounded on
\Omega_{k}
by
Lemma

5.6
, handling
F_{k,h}
is more challenging because
\hat{P}_{k}
and
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}
are dependent.

Proposition

5.8
below addresses this by relating
F_{k,h}
to the expected next-context value of
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}
.
This removes the dependence on
\hat{P}_{k}
and enables one to bound
F_{k,h}
whenever
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}
is close to
V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
L\vcentcolon=1+\ln(nHS^{2}AT_{K}^{2})
.
Then, for all
x
,
a
,
h\in[H-1]
, and
k\in[K]
, on
\Omega_{k}
,

\begin{split}F_{k,h}(x,a)\leq&\,(1/H)(2+1/H)\langle P(\,\cdot\mid x,a),\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\rangle\\
&+\frac{2H^{2}SL}{1\vee\hat{N}_{k}(f^{-1}(f(x)),a)}+\sum_{s}p(s\mid f(x),a)\frac{4nH^{2}L}{1\vee\hat{N}^{\mathrm{to}}_{k}(f^{-1}(s))}.\end{split}

(53)

contains the proof of
Proposition

5.8
.
It is similar to that of
[
6
, Lemma 3]
, which bounds
F_{k,h}
for tabular
MDPs
.
We cannot directly apply their argument, however, because
\hat{P}_{k}
’s elements are products of
\hat{p}_{k}
and
\hat{q}_{k}
.
Instead, we decompose
F_{k,h}
into separate contributions involving only
\hat{p}_{k}
or
\hat{q}_{k}
.
These can then be bounded using
[
6
]
’s argument, applied separately to
\hat{p}_{k}
and
\hat{q}_{k}
.

We now return to (
51
).
First, observe that
V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\geq V^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}
since
\pi^{*}
is optimal.
Thus
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi^{*}}_{h+1}\leq\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}
.
With
Proposition

5.8
, this implies that
F_{k,h}
is bounded by the expected next–context value of
\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}-V^{\pi_{k}}_{h+1}
.
This allows us to prove the following recursive bound in
:

Proposition 5.9
.

Write
s_{k,h}=f(x_{k,h})
.
For
h\in[H-1]
and
k\in[K]
such that
T_{k}=kH>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[

\displaystyle\bigl(\bar{V}_{h}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h})-V_{h}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h})\bigr)\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}\Bigr]

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\hat{B}_{k}(s_{k,h^{\prime}},a_{k,h^{\prime}},s_{k,h^{\prime}+1})+\frac{1}{H}\biggl(2+\frac{1}{H}\biggr)\bigl(\bar{V}_{h^{\prime}+1}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h^{\prime}+1})-V_{h^{\prime}+1}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h^{\prime}+1})\bigr)\biggr)\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}\biggr],

where

\hat{B}_{k}(s,a,s^{\prime})\vcentcolon=\sqrt{\frac{4H^{2}L}{1\vee\hat{N}_{k}(f^{-1}(s),a)}}+\frac{6H^{2}SL^{2}}{1\vee\hat{N}_{k}(f^{-1}(s),a)}+\sqrt{\frac{16H^{2}L}{1\vee\hat{N}^{\mathrm{to}}_{k}(f^{-1}(s^{\prime}))}}+\frac{4nH^{2}L}{1\vee\hat{N}^{\mathrm{to}}_{k}(f^{-1}(s^{\prime}))},

(55)

with
L=1+\ln(nHS^{2}AT_{K}^{2})
as in
Proposition

5.8
.

By using
Proposition

5.9
iteratively, we can obtain a bound that depends only on the
\hat{B}_{k}(s_{k,h},\allowbreak a_{k,h},\allowbreak s_{k,h+1})
.
The following lemma, proven in
, makes this precise:

Lemma 5.10
.

Consider two sequences
\{\Delta_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
,
\{B_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}
with
\Delta_{h},B_{h}\in\mathbb{R}
for
h\in[H]
, that satisfy
\Delta_{H}=0
and
\Delta_{h}\leq\sum_{h^{\prime}=h}^{H-1}(B_{h^{\prime}}+(1/H)(2+1/H)\Delta_{h^{\prime}})
for all
h\in[H-1]
.
Then
\Delta_{1}\leq e^{2}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}B_{h}
.

We will apply
Lemma

5.10
with
\Delta_{h}=\mathbb{E}[(\bar{V}_{h}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h})-V_{h}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,h}))\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}]
.

Lemma

5.10
’s assumptions are met for
B_{h}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{B}_{k}(s_{k,h},\allowbreak a_{k,h},\allowbreak s_{k,h+1})\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}]
by
Proposition

5.9
and because

by definition.
Together with
\hat{B}_{k}\geq 0
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}[(\bar{V}_{1}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,1})-V_{1}^{\pi_{k}}(x_{k,1}))\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}]\leq e^{2}\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}[\hat{B}_{k}(s_{k,h},a_{k,h},s_{k,h+1})].

(56)

Observe now that
\hat{B}_{k}(s,a,s^{\prime})
depends inversely on the visitation count of the triple
(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
Hence, once a triple is visited, its corresponding

dereases in subsequent episodes.

Proposition

5.9
was formulated precisely so that we can exploit this fact.
We do so in
, where we prove our final bound on

.
This result is better than its analogue for tabular

because the visitation count of a latent state increases more quickly than that of a single context.

Proposition 5.11
.

If
T_{K}=KH=\Omega(H^{4}S^{3}A)
, then

\begin{split}R_{3}=\sum_{k\in[K]:T_{k}>\Theta^{\textnormal{clust}}}\mathbb{E}[(\bar{V}^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k,1})-V^{\pi_{k}}_{1}(x_{k_{1}}))\mathbbm{1}\{\Omega_{k}\}]=\tilde{O}(H\sqrt{SAT_{K}}+nH^{3}).\end{split}

(57)

Finally — the proof of
Theorem

1.4
is done by noting that the assumptions of
Theorem

1.4
are the same as those of
Propositions

5.4
,
5.7
and

,

, and

in (

).

consists of the iterative improvement algorithm
[
24
, Algorithm 2]
, whose pseudocode we reproduce here in our notation.

Starting from some initial estimate
\hat{f}_{0}
, at each iteration
\ell=0,1,\ldots
it uses the current estimate decoding function
\hat{f}_{\ell}
to estimate the latent state transition probabilities from
\hat{N}
.
Note that it also estimates the backward transition probabilities

, i.e., the probability that action
a
was taken in latent state
s
given that we are currently in latent state
s^{\prime}
.
These estimates are then used to estimate for each context
x
, the log-likelihood of the observed data over each potential new assignment
\hat{f}_{\ell+1}(x)=s^{\prime}
.

The context
x
is assigned to the latent state

that maximizes this estimate log-likelihood.
This greedy, local likelihood maximization is repeated
\lceil\ln n\rceil
times, each time using the updated decoding to estimate a new log-likelihood function.
In
Algorithm

2
, the improvement algorithm is initialized with the decoding function
\hat{f}_{0}
returned by the spectral clustering algorithm.

Appendix B
On the structural properties

This section further explains
Definitions

1.2
and
1.1
and relates them to analogous properties used in the literature.

B.1
Explanation of the structural properties

B.1.1
Reachability

Definition

1.1
imposes a degree of regularity on the latent state transition probabilities, the emission probabilities, and the number of contexts per latent state.
Concretely, since
\sum_{s^{\prime}}p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\sum_{y\in f^{-1}(s)}q(y\mid s)=\sum_{s}|f^{-1}(s)|/n=1
,
Definition

1.1
implies that for sufficiently large
n
,

\frac{1}{\eta S}\leq p(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\leq\frac{\eta}{S},\quad\frac{1}{\eta|f^{-1}(s)|}\leq q(y\mid s)\leq\frac{\eta}{|f^{-1}(s)|},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{n}{\eta S}\leq|f^{-1}(s)|\leq\frac{\eta n}{S}

(59)

for
s,s^{\prime}\in[S]
,
y\in f^{-1}(s)
.
In turn, this implies that in expectation, and up to a constant factor, all contexts are observed equally often under any exploration policy.

B.1.2
Identifiability

Definition

1.2
imposes a minimum degree of separation between different latent states.
In particular, it ensures this separation remains bounded away from zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Concretely, the quantity
I_{\tilde{s}}(x;c,\Phi,\pi)
in (
13
) is the leading-order term in the expansion of the log-likelihood ratio of the context–action sequence between
\Phi
and the confusing
BMDP

\Psi
in
[
24
, Appendix D]
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
The parameter
c>0
controls the emission probability of
x
in
\tilde{s}
.
Thus, by ensuring that
\min_{x}I_{\eta}(x;\Phi)
remains bounded away from zero,
Definition

1.2
prevents two distinct latent states from having asymptotically indistinguishable transition and emission probabilities.

B.2
Relation to literature

Analogues of
Definitions

1.1
and
1.2
are common in the
BMDP
literature.
See, for example,
[
12
, Definition 2.1 and/or Assumption 3.2]
as well as
[
31
, Definition 6]
.

Our formulation is based on
[
24
]
, in order to leverage their results on latent state estimation.
In turn,
[
24
]
builds on related conditions from the block model literature, including
BMCs

[
35
]
and (labeled)
SBMs

[
1
,
45
,
15
]
.

B.2.1
Reachability

When
S
is independent of
n
, any
\eta
-reachable
BMDP
satisfies
[
24
, Assumptions 2 and 3]
.
Both
\eta
-reachability and
[
24
, Assumptions 2 and 3]
are stronger than the reachability conditions typically considered in the
BMDP
literature.
For example,
[
12
,
31
]
require that every state is reachable under
some
policy, whereas
\eta
-reachability enforces a uniform degree of reachability under
any
policy.

As highlighted in
Section

1.5.1
, this difference reflects distinct objectives.
Works such as
[

,
31
]
aim to design efficient exploration policies to learn the latent structure using function approximation.
By contrast, our focus is on analyzing explicit recovery algorithms with provable guarantees in the line of
[
35
,
24
]
.

B.2.2
Identifiability

\mathcal{I}
-identifiability is closely related to
[
24
, Theorem 3]
’s assumption
I(\Phi)>0
, where

I(\Phi)\vcentcolon=-\frac{n}{T_{k}}\ln\biggl(\frac{1}{2\eta Sn}\sum_{x}\exp\biggl(-\frac{T_{k}}{n}I(x;\Phi)\biggr)\biggr)

(60)

with

I(x;\Phi)\vcentcolon=\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow\infty}I_{\zeta}(x;\Phi)=\min_{\tilde{s}\neq f(x)}\inf_{c>0}I_{\tilde{s}}(x;c,\Phi,\pi_{U}).

(61)

When

and
S
,
A
and
p
are independent of

, one can show that the conditions

(62)

are equivalent.
In our framework, however,
S
,
A
, and
p
do depend on
n
.
In this case,
\min_{x}I(x;\Phi)=\Omega(1)
still implies
I(\Phi)=\Omega(1)
, but the converse need not hold.

Definition

1.2
therefore requires an analogue of the stronger condition
\min_{x}I(x;\Phi)=\Omega(1)
.

Definition

1.2
’s requirement, however, is slightly weaker than
\min_{x}I(x;\Phi)=\Omega(1)
because
I_{\eta}(x;\Phi)\geq I(x;\Phi)
; (compare the infima in (

) and (

)).

Section

D.1
contains a proof that that

1.2
nonetheless implies
\min_{x}I(x;\Phi)=\Omega(1)
, ensuring compatibility with
[
24
]
.

For further comparisons with other notions of identifiability in the
BMDP
literature, see
[
24
, Appendix D.3.5]
.
