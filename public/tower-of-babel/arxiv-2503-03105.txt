Positive Feedback: How a Synergy Between the Streaming Instability and Dust Coagulation Forms Planetesimals
Daniel Carrera, Jeonghoon Lim, Linn E.J. Eriksson, Wladimir Lyra, Jacob B. Simon

Abstract
Context. One of the most important open questions in planet formation is how dust grains in a protoplanetary disk manage to overcome growth barriers and form the ∼ \sim 100km planet building blocks that we call planetesimals. There appears to be a gap between the largest grains that can be produce by coagulation, and the smallest grains that are needed for the streaming instability (SI) to form planetesimals. Aims. Here we explore a novel hypothesis: That dust coagulation and the SI work in tandem. That they form a feedback loop where each one boosts the action of the other to bridge the gap between dust grains and planetesimals. Methods. We develop a semi-analytical model of dust concentration due to the SI, and an analytic model of how the SI affects the fragmentation and radial drift barriers. We then combine those to model our proposed feedback loop. Results. In the fragmentation-limited regime, we find a powerful synergy between the SI and dust growth that drastically increases both grain sizes and densities. We find that a midplane dust-to-gas ratio of ϵ ≥ 0.3 \epsilon\geq 0.3 is a sufficient condition for the feedback loop to reach the planetesimal-forming region for turbulence values 10 − 4 ≤ α ≤ 10 − 3 10^{-4}\leq\alpha\leq 10^{-3} and grain sizes 0.01 ≤ St ≤ 0.1 0.01\leq{\rm St}\leq 0.1 . In contrast, the drift-limited regime only shows grain growth, without significant dust accumulation. Planet formation in the drift-limited portion of the disk may require other processes (particle traps) to halt radial drift.



1
Introduction

An interesting feature of planet formation is that it is a complex, multi-scale, multi-physics problem. The micro-physics of dust grain dynamics and cohesion, the long-range gravitational torques from the disk, and the intermediate-range physics of turbulence and aerodynamic drag, are all key ingredients that must be studied and understood. By necessity, these processes are generally studied in isolation and we often struggle to understand the interactions between them.

In this work we explore the interaction between two of these processes: Grain growth by coagulation
(
Birnstiel et al., 2012
, e.g.,)
and the streaming instability
(
Youdin & Goodman, 2005
;
Johansen & Youdin, 2007
;
Youdin & Johansen, 2007
, SI,)
. We seek to understand how they combine to form the
\sim 100
km primordial bodies that we call planetesimals, which are thought to be the building blocks of terrestrial planets and giant planet cores
(
Kokubo & Ida, 1996
;
Kokubo & Ida, 2000
)
.

1.1
Streaming instability

The SI
(
Youdin & Goodman, 2005
)
is a convergence of radial drift among solid grains. It is perhaps the leading candidate for an explanation of how planetesimals form. While the details are complex, its qualitative behavior is simple enough: As dust grains feel gas drag, they also push back on the gas, and this feedback causes radially-drifting grains to converge into azimuthal filaments. If those filaments reach the Roche density

\rho_{\rm R}=\frac{9\Omega_{\rm K}^{2}}{4\pi G},

(1)

where
\Omega_{\rm K}
is the Keplerian frequency and
G
is the gravitational constant, then the particle cloud may collapse gravitationally.

Much of the interest in the SI is driven by evidence that seem to favor it. The size distribution of present-day asteroids and the cratering record of Vesta
(
Morbidelli et al., 2009
)
, along with the large number of equal-size Kuiper belt binaries
(
Nesvorný et al., 2010
;
Fraser et al., 2017
)
, suggests that planetesimals formed through the gravitational collapse of a dust cloud. Gravitational collapse is also a neat way of overcoming the growth barriers discussed in the previous section. One piece of evidence that points to the SI in particular is that the SI reproduces the obliquity and angular momentum distributions of trans-Neptunian binaries
(
Nesvorný et al., 2019
;
Nesvorný et al., 2021
)
as well as the densities of Kuiper-Belt Objects
(
Cañas et al., 2024
)
.

However, the major challenge facing the SI (and a reason to be cautious about it) is that the conditions needed to trigger the SI
(
Carrera et al., 2015
;
Yang et al., 2017
;
Li & Youdin, 2021
;
Lim et al., 2024a
;
Lim et al., 2024b
)
are inconsistent with dust growth and disk evolution models, as it requires fairly large grains and/or high column dust-to-gas ratio
Z
in order to form filaments dense enough to reach the Roche density. Importantly, most studies of the SI criterion rely on 2D models without external turbulence.
Lim et al. (2024a)
showed that even a modest degree of external turbulence can make the critical
Z
much higher than those turbulence-free 2D models suggest. In this paper we make the case that dust coagulation is the missing ingredient that allows the SI to work with realistic turbulence.

1.2
Interaction between the SI and dust growth

In this paper we explore a simple hypothesis: That the SI and dust growth boost each other, in a positive feedback. The SI leads to enhanced dust concentration, which reduces turbulence and radial drift. This, in turn, boosts grain growth, which then leads to a stronger, more efficient SI. If so, the combination of the SI and dust growth may be the key ingredient that makes planetesimal formation possible. Following a similar thought process,
Tominaga & Tanaka (2023)
showed that SI filaments promote dust growth, and
Ho et al. (2024)
showed that dust growth can trigger the SI. We posit that there is, in fact, a feedback loop between dust growth and the SI, so that the two processes boost each other far beyond what might appear from studying just one interaction or the other.

This paper is organized as follows: Most of this manuscript consists of the presentation of the model in section §
2
, where we derive the detailed relationships between the SI, mass loading, turbulence, and radial drift. In section §
3
we put these ingredients together to obtain our final results. We discuss in section §
4
, and conclude in section §
5
.

2
Model Ingredients

2.1
How the SI boosts mass loading

Perhaps the best-known feature of the SI, and the reason it has garnered so much attention, is its ability to collect solid grains into dense filaments. We repurpose the dataset of
Lim et al. (2024a)
, who conducted a large number of 3D simulations of the SI with external (i.e. forced) turbulence for
{\rm St}\in[0.01,0.1]
,
Z\in[0.015,0.4]
, and
\alpha\in[10^{-4},10^{-3}]
where
{\rm St}\equiv t_{\rm stop}\Omega_{\rm K}
is the grain’s Stokes number,
Z=\Sigma_{\rm p}/\Sigma_{\rm g}
is the column dust-to-gas ratio, and
\alpha
is the squared Mach number of the turbulence. We calculated the typical dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon
inside the SI filaments and fit a powerlaw. The details of this procedure are included in Appendix
A
, but the final result is that 55-80% of the particles inside an SI filament will experience a dust-to-gas ratio at least as high as

\epsilon_{\rm SI}\approx 1.12\left(\frac{Z}{0.01}\right)^{2.65}\left(\frac{{\rm St}}{0.1}\right)^{1.42}\left(\frac{\alpha}{10^{-4}}\right)^{-1.38}.

(2)

2.2
How mass loading boosts
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}

The approach to mass loading commonly used in the literature is to think of the gas-dust mixture as a single fluid, taking the limit
{\rm St}\rightarrow 0
so that the mixture represents a colloidal suspension. In this limit, one can think of the dust as contributing to the inertia of the fluid but not to its pressure
(
Chang & Oishi, 2010
;
Shi & Chiang, 2013
;
Laibe & Price, 2014
;
Lin & Youdin, 2017
;
Chen & Lin, 2018
)
. This can be expressed as an “effective” sound speed

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm g}c_{\rm s}^{2}=(\rho_{\rm g}+\rho_{\rm p})\tilde{c}_{\rm s}^{2}

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{\rm s}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\rm s}}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon}}

where
\tilde{c}_{\rm s}
is the effective sound speed of the colloid.

However, in this work we are explicitly interested in the case where
{\rm St}
may not be negligible, and the fluid might not be well represented by a colloid. Therefore, we take a different approach. Let us posit that turbulence is generated by a finite energy source that has to be partitioned between the gas and dust components

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm g}v_{\rm g}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm p}v_{\rm p}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm g}v_{\rm g}^{2}\left(1+\epsilon\frac{v_{\rm p}^{2}}{v_{\rm g}^{2}}\right)=\text{Const}

Where
v_{\rm g}
and
v_{\rm p}
are the root-mean-squared velocities of the gas and dust. Several authors
(
Voelk et al., 1980
;
Cuzzi et al., 1993
;
Schräpler & Henning, 2004
)
have shown that the random speed of solid grains in turbulence is
v_{\rm p}=v_{\rm g}/\sqrt{1+{\rm St}}
. Insert this into Equation
2.2
, and treating
\rho_{\rm g}
as constant we obtain

v_{\rm g}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{1+{\rm St}}\right)=\frac{2E}{\rho_{\rm g}}=\text{Const}

(3)

Let
v_{\rm g0}\equiv\sqrt{2E/\rho_{\rm g}}
be the reference gas velocity. This is the gas velocity when
\epsilon\ll 1
, and it roughly equals the initial gas velocity before dust begins to accumulate. Also let
\alpha\equiv v_{\rm g0}^{2}/c_{\rm s}^{2}
.

v_{\rm g}\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon}{1+{\rm St}}}=v_{\rm g0}=\sqrt{\alpha}c_{\rm s}

(4)

Following the literature, we define an effective sound speed
\tilde{c}_{\rm s}
such that
v_{\rm g}\equiv\sqrt{\alpha}\tilde{c}_{\rm s}

\tilde{c}_{\rm s}\equiv c_{\rm s}\sqrt{\frac{1+{\rm St}}{1+{\rm St}+\epsilon}}

(5)

Notice that, in the limit as
{\rm St}\rightarrow 0
gives the
\tilde{c}_{\rm s}
for a colloid.

2.2.1
Fragmentation Limit

This limit occurs when grain collision speed
v_{\rm coll}=v_{\rm g}\sqrt{3{\rm St}}
reaches the fragmentation speed
v_{\rm frag}
. We combine this with Equation
4
to obtain

\displaystyle{\rm St}_{\rm frag}=\frac{v_{\rm frag}^{2}}{3v_{\rm g}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm St}_{\rm x}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{1+{\rm St}}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;\;{\rm St}_{\rm x}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{v_{\rm frag}^{2}}{3\alpha c_{\rm s}^{2}}

(7)

{\rm St}_{\rm x}
is the definition of
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}
commonly found in the literature. Mass loading boosts the fragmentation barrier by a factor of
1+\epsilon/(1+{\rm St})
. Equation
6
is a quadratic in
{\rm St}
. After simplification, its solution can be written as

{\rm St}_{\rm frag}=\frac{{\rm St}_{\rm x}-1+\sqrt{({\rm St}_{\rm x}+1)^{2}+4\epsilon{\rm St}_{\rm x}}}{2}

(8)

Notice that
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}={\rm St}_{\rm x}
for
\epsilon=0
and
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}>{\rm St}_{\rm x}
for
\epsilon>0
. Other limits include
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}\approx(1+\epsilon){\rm St}_{\rm x}
for
\epsilon<1
and
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}\approx\sqrt{\epsilon{\rm St}_{\rm x}}
for
\epsilon\gg{\rm St}_{\rm x}+{\rm St}_{\rm x}^{-1}
. But for practical use, the best strategy is to use Equation
8
.

2.3
How mass loading boosts
{\rm St}_{\rm drift}

The radial drift barrier occurs when the particle drift rate matches its growth rate. Here, too, the SI can increase
{\rm St}_{\rm drift}
as higher
\epsilon
leads slower radial drift. The steady-state particle drift rate was derived by
Nakagawa et al. (1986)
. In the limit where
{\rm St}^{2}\ll 1
the drift speed is

v_{\rm drift}=\frac{-2\eta v_{\rm k}}{{\rm St}+(1+\epsilon)^{2}/{\rm St}}

(9)

For
\epsilon\ll 1
this matches the formula for
v_{\rm drift}
seen in the literature. But once gain, we want to consider the case where
\epsilon
is not small. The particle drift timescale is

t_{\rm drift}\equiv\frac{r}{v_{\rm drift}}=\frac{{\rm St}+(1+\epsilon)^{2}/{\rm St}}{-2\eta\Omega_{\rm K}}

(10)

Birnstiel et al. (2012)
derive the formula for the growth rate
t_{\rm grow}
. If we follow their derivation, but omit the final step, we find

t_{\rm grow}=\frac{1}{\epsilon\Omega_{\rm K}}\sqrt{\frac{{\rm St}}{\alpha}}.

(11)

Birnstiel et al. (2012)
’s final step is to apply the expression for
\epsilon
from sedimentation to the midplane. Since we are interested in the
\epsilon
due to the SI, Equation
11
is more appropriate for our needs. The radial drift barrier occurs when
t_{\rm grow}=t_{\rm drift}
. The result is a quartic equation in
{\rm St}
.

{\rm St}^{2}+\frac{2\eta}{\epsilon\sqrt{\alpha}}{\rm St}^{3/2}+(1+\epsilon)^{2}=0

(12)

This equation has an analytic solution but it is very complicated. To simplify it, we assume that
{\rm St}\ll(1+\epsilon)^{2}/{\rm St}
, so that Equation
10
simplifies to

t_{\rm drift}\approx\frac{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}{-2\eta\Omega_{\rm K}{\rm St}}.

(13)

With this simplification, we obtain the radial drift limit as

\displaystyle{\rm St}_{\rm drift}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\epsilon^{2}(1+\epsilon)^{4}\alpha}{4\eta^{2}}\right]^{1/3}=\left[{\rm St}_{\rm y}^{2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}\alpha}{Z^{2}}(1+\epsilon)^{4}\right]^{1/3}

(14)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;\;\;\;{\rm St}_{\rm y}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{-Z}{2\eta}.

(15)

{\rm St}_{\rm y}
is the definition of
{\rm St}_{\rm drift}
commonly found in the literature. To obtain that value, one has to assume
\epsilon\ll 1
as well as
\epsilon
from vertical sedimentation.

2.4
Feedback between dust growth and the SI

Finally, we combine all the ingredients. Starting from an initial
(Z,\alpha,{\rm St}_{\rm x})
or
(Z,\alpha,{\rm St}_{\rm y})
, we apply the following algorithm:

1.

Let
t_{\rm SI}=50\Omega_{\rm K}
be the filament growth timescale. In truth,
t_{\rm SI}
depends on
{\rm St}

(
Carrera et al., 2015
;
Yang et al., 2017
;
Li & Youdin, 2021
, e.g.,)
, but there is no published formula for
t_{\rm SI}
and
50\Omega_{\rm K}
is typical.

2.

Initialize
\epsilon
with the midplane dust-to-gas ratio due to vertical sedimentation with particle feedback (section §
2.4.1
below).

3.

Feedback Loop:

•

Update
t_{\rm coag}
using Equation
11
.

•

Update
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
using Equation
2
.

•

Let
\Delta t\coloneqq 0.1\min(t_{\rm coag},t_{\rm SI})
be the iteration timestep.

•

Let
{\rm St}_{\rm max}\coloneqq{\rm St}_{\rm frag}
or
{\rm St}_{\rm drift}
(Equations
8
and
14
).

•

Update
({\rm St},\epsilon)
at the same time

–

{\rm St}\coloneqq\min\left[{\rm St}_{\rm max},{\rm St}\cdot\exp(\Delta t/t_{\rm coag})\right]

–

\epsilon\coloneqq\min\left[\epsilon_{\rm SI},\epsilon\cdot\exp(\Delta t/t_{\rm SI})\right]

Treating
\{{\rm St}_{\rm x},{\rm St}_{\rm y}\}
as input parameters allows us to explore the problem without assuming one particular disk model. We also examine each growth barrier independently.

2.4.1
Dust scale height and sedimentation

We start with the
\epsilon
due to sedimentation and vertical diffusion (i.e. prior to the SI). The usual approach is to compute

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z\frac{H}{H_{\rm p}}

(16)

\displaystyle\text{for}\;\;H_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{{\rm St}+\alpha}}

(17)

where
H
and
H_{\rm p}
are the gas and particle scale heights, and
\rho_{\rm p}
is assumed to have a vertical Gaussian density distribution, as derived by
Youdin & Lithwick (2007)
. However,
Lim et al. (2024a)
showed that, due to mass loading, this expression is a poor predictor of
H_{\rm p}
, and that the density profile is not Gaussian. They found that

H_{\rm p}=\frac{H}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Pi}{5}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\rm St}}}

(18)

is a better predictor of the height of the particle layer. We adopt
\Pi=0.05
because that was the value used by
Lim et al. (2024a)
. Equation
18
was based on expression for the effective scale height (
\tilde{H}\equiv\tilde{c}_{\rm s}/\Omega
) for a colloid
(
Yang & Zhu, 2020
)
. Because we only use this formula once, before
({\rm St},\epsilon)
have had a chance to grow, it can retain the colloid approximation.

Despite the fact that
\rho_{\rm p}
might not be Gaussian, Equation
16
is still the best estimate for
\epsilon
that we are aware of. Combined with Equation
18
we get a quadratic expression for
\epsilon
with solution

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm init}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta+\sqrt{\zeta^{2}+4\zeta}}{2}

(19)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;\;\zeta

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle Z^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\Pi}{5}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\rm St}}\right]^{-1}

(20)

There is another root, but it corresponds to
\epsilon<0
.

We find that in every single case, the combination of the SI and coagulation can lead to a drastic increase in particle size. The exact behavior depends primarily on whether the grain sizes are limited by fragmentation or radial drift.

3.1
Fragmentation Limit

The SI and coagulation act together to cause a drastic increase in both
{\rm St}
and
\epsilon
. By trial and error we chose the smallest
Z
that allows each particle track to converge at around
2\times
the SI criterion. We find that those
Z
values are quite robust because very small increases in
Z
lead to much longer tracks. Had we chosen to stop at
1\times
or
3\times
the SI criterion, the reported
Z
values would have been within 2% of the ones reported here.

We find that, as long as
\epsilon
starts at
\geq 0.3
, the particle track will go well inside the planetesimal-formation region. If we insert the
\epsilon>0.3
criterion into Equation
19
, we obtain

Z>0.263\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Pi}{5}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\rm St}}}

(21)

For example, for
\alpha=10^{-4},{\rm St}=0.01
we get
Z>0.026
. But raise
\alpha
to
10^{-3}
and the critical
Z
increases to
Z>0.078
.

3.2
Drift Limit

This case is more subtle: The growth tracks for both barriers exhibit a “kink”. Initially the growth is mainly in
{\rm St}
with little change in
\epsilon
. At some point, the SI becomes more effective and
{\rm St}
and
\epsilon
start to grow together. For the fragmentation barrier the kink appears quickly, but for the radial drift barrier the kink occurs so late that it is outside the plotted region for all examples except those that start with
{\rm St}=0.01
. But if our semi-analytical fit for SI clumping extends beyond
{\rm St}=0.1
, that kink could still increase
\epsilon
in the drift-dominated disk.

4
Discussion

The origin of planetesimals is a decades-old problem in planet formation. Crossing the vast chasm between mm-cm size grains and 100 km planetesimals is, by far, the largest step needed to form a planet.

We have discovered a powerful feedback loop in which the SI and fragmentation-limited dust coagulation assist each other, to accomplish something that neither one seems able to do alone: form planetesimals under typical disk conditions. That said, it is important to ponder what happens when dust sizes are
not
limited by fragmentation:

4.1
Drift Barrier

In drift-limited regions there is also a feedback loop which can significantly increase
{\rm St}
. However, this is not accompanied by much dust concentration, and seems to require implausible
Z
. We speculate that planet formation in these regions requires dust traps to limit or halt radial drift.

4.2
Bouncing Barrier

Another possibly critical barrier may occur when grain collisions simply do not result in sticking
(
Zsom et al., 2010
)
. This barrier is less understood since it depends on grain properties like shape and porosity. If present, it could drastically alter the grain size distribution
(
Dominik & Dullemond, 2024
)
and lead to grains far smaller than
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}
, or it might simply be overcome by electrostatic charges
(
Jungmann & Wurm, 2021
)
.

With that in mind, it is worth mentioning that the bouncing barrier is similar to fragmentation in that it is a limit in
v_{\rm coll}
. Therefore, if present, the bouncing barrier must partake in the same feedback loop that we found for fragmentation. The growth tracks would look like the ones for
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}
, but probably starting at lower
{\rm St}
and higher
Z
to reach the planetesimal formation region.

5
Conclusions

We have shown that there is a powerful feedback loop in which the SI and dust coagulation boost each other. Each process creates the environment that the other one requires: The SI creates dust-dense regions with low turbulence and slow drift; those are the ideal conditions for dust coagulation to form larger grains, which make the SI more effective. We developed three ingredients:

1.

A semi-analytical estimate of the dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon
created by the SI (Equation
2
).

2.

An analytical expression for
{\rm St}_{\rm frag}
that models turbulence dampening due to dust feedback (Equation
8
).

3.

An analytical expression for
{\rm St}_{\rm drift}
that models the reduced radial drift due to dust feedback (Equation
14
).

In the fragmentation-limited regime, the combination of the SI and dust growth led to a drastic increase in both
{\rm St}
and
\epsilon
, as the two processes fed each other. A practical rule of thumb is that a midplane dust-to-gas ratio of
\epsilon\geq 0.3
is sufficient to reach the planetesimal-forming region across the full range of
({\rm St},\alpha)
that we explored. In other words, the positive feedback between the SI and dust growth seems to resolve a decades-old problem in planet formation, at least in the fragmentation-limited inner disk.

In the drift-limited regime, we saw a dramatic increase in
{\rm St}
, but it was not usually accompanied by a significant increase in
\epsilon
, at least for
{\rm St}\leq 0.1
; but if our semi-analytical fit for SI clumping extends to
{\rm St}>0.1
, it could still lead to significant increase in
\epsilon
in the drift-dominated disk. Otherwise, planetesimal formation in the outer disk may require dust traps to mitigate radial drift and increase
\epsilon
.

Appendix A
How the SI boosts mass loading

Perhaps the best-known feature of the SI, and the reason it has garnered so much attention, is its ability to collect solid grains into dense filaments. Here we repurpose the dataset of
Lim et al. (2024a)
, who conducted a large number of 3D simulations of the SI with external (i.e. forced) turbulence. We refer to that paper for details of their simulation setup. In brief, they conducted a systematic exploration of the SI for
{\rm St}\in[0.01,0.1]
,
Z\in[0.015,0.4]
, and
\alpha\in[10^{-4},10^{-3}]
where
{\rm St}\equiv t_{\rm stop}\Omega_{\rm K}
is the grain’s Stokes number,
Z=\Sigma_{\rm p}/\Sigma_{\rm g}
is the column dust-to-gas ratio, and
\alpha
is the squared Mach number of the turbulence.

Our goal is to estimate the typical dust-to-gas ratio
\epsilon=\rho_{\rm p}/\rho_{\rm g}
inside the SI particle filaments. First, we take the mean particle density along the azimuthal direction
\langle\rho_{\rm p}(z=0)\rangle_{y}
. This removes most of the stochastic noise. Let

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm g,mid}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{\rm g}(z=0)\rangle_{xy}

(22)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm p,mid}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{\rm p}(z=0)\rangle_{xy}

(23)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm p,max}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\rm max}[\langle\rho_{\rm p}(z=0)\rangle_{y}]

(24)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm p,dev}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm p,max}-\rho_{\rm p,mid}

(25)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm SI}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{\rm g,mid}}\left(\rho_{\rm p,mid}+\frac{\rho_{\rm p,dev}}{2}\right)

(26)

where
\rho_{\rm p,dev}
is the density deviation due to the SI. At ½-deviation,
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
sits near the middle of the range of
\epsilon
values inside a filament. Figure
2
shows
\rho_{\rm p}
and
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
for six snapshots in two simulations. We have reviewed other snapshots and other simulations, and the results are similar.
Qualitatively,
Equation
26
appears to be a reliable measure of the typical
\epsilon
inside an SI filament.
Quantitatively
, we found that most (
55-80
%) of the particles contained in the filament are in grid cells with
\epsilon>\epsilon_{\rm SI}
. When the simulation first reaches a saturated turbulent state, around
55-70
% of the particle mass is above
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
, and that value increases to
70-80
% before the filament reaches the Roche density. That makes
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
a conservative measure of the density environment experienced by particles inside the filament, and a good choice for exploring dust coagulation inside SI filaments.

To fit a semi-analytical formula for
\epsilon_{\rm SI}
, we looked for a power-law of the form
\epsilon_{\rm SI}\propto Z^{a}{\rm St}^{b}\alpha^{c}
. Writing
\log\epsilon_{\rm SI}=C+a\log Z+b\log{\rm St}+c\log\alpha
, we used Ordinary Least Squares to find the fit

\epsilon_{\rm SI}\approx 1.12\left(\frac{Z}{0.01}\right)^{2.65}\left(\frac{{\rm St}}{0.1}\right)^{1.42}\left(\frac{\alpha}{10^{-4}}\right)^{-1.38}.

(27)

SI filaments exhibit a significantly steeper dependence on all three parameters
(Z,{\rm St},\alpha)
than the familiar
\epsilon=Z\sqrt{{\rm St}/\alpha}
for dust sedimentation.
