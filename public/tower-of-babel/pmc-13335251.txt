Complementary effects of acid-fast staining, culture, and Xpert MTB/RIF® on different respiratory tract samples in a referral center
Authors: Siwei Gu, Xiaojing Cui, Chunlei Wang, Lintao Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate diagnostic performance of different combinations of acid-fast staining (AFS), Xpert MTB/RIF (Xpert), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) culture across sample types in pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) in a referral center.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study on all PTB patients with confirmed microbiological diagnosis in China-Japan Friendship Hospital from January 1st, 2017 to December 31st, 2021. Distribution and sensitivity of different testing strategies were compared for sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and biopsy tissues [transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB)/transbronchial biopsy (TBB)] using parallel AFS/Xpert/culture combinations.Results1,094 microbiologically diagnosed PTB patients were identified. For sputum samples, the most common testing strategy was parallel AFS/Xpert/culture (42.3%), achieving 89.5% (95%CI 85.7%-92.1%) sensitivity. BALF samples demonstrated similar performance with this combination [86.3% sensitivity, (95%CI 82.1%-89.7%)]. In biopsy tissues, the same triad yielded a sensitivity of 73.8% (95%CI 65.0%-81.5%). Xpert and culture showed partially non-overlapping results in all sample types. A subgroup analysis of sputum tested before bronchoscopy showed 56.5% (95%CI 50.2%-62.7%) sputum Xpert sensitivity and 66.7% (95%CI 58.2%-74.2%) culture sensitivity.ConclusionSimultaneous usage of Xpert, AFS and culture on the same specimen increases MTB testing sensitivity on sputum, BALF and TBB/TBLB, which is valuable for early diagnosis for pulmonary tuberculosis in high-burden settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13461-w.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Diagnosis, Pulmonary, Mycobacteria, Sensitivity, Optimization

Background

Tuberculosis, as a re-emerging infectious disease, returned to be the leading cause of death from single infectious pathogen worldwide. In 2023, global incidence of tuberculosis reached 10.8 million new cases, continuing the upward trend since 2020 [1]. World Health Organization’s (WHO) End TB Strategy aims for a 50% reduction in tuberculosis incidence compared to 2015, but only an 8.3% decline was observed in 2023. Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) patients remain the primary source of tuberculosis transmission. Early diagnosis of PTB and antituberculosis treatments are critical for minimizing disease spread [2]. Microbiological evidence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in respiratory specimens is essential for definitive PTB diagnosis and drug resistance profiling. However, the rate of microbiological diagnosis in PTB remain suboptimal. In China, only 67.2% of newly-diagnosed PTB were bacteriologically confirmed [3]. 93.1% of PTB patients were first encountered in general hospitals, and only 30.7% were diagnosed on-site from a nationwide survey in 2010 [4]. The median time of PTB diagnosis was 5 days after first medical contact from a citywide survey. Delayed diagnosis, defined as the time interval between first medical contact to PTB diagnosis being longer than 14 days, occurred in 38% of PTB patients [5]. Insufficient coverage and low sensitivity of microbiological tests undermine the efficient detection of PTB in general hospitals. Despite WHO recommendations, rapid molecular tests were applied to only 48% of newly diagnosed TB cases [1, 6].

Four main MTB detection methods are currently used in clinical practice: acid-fast staining, nucleic acid amplification tests such as GeneXpert MTB/RIF and loop-mediated isothermal amplification, and culture-based approaches. While acid-fast staining offers rapid screening, its low sensitivity and specificity limits diagnostic value, especially faced with non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) infection [7]. Nucleic acid amplification tests provide higher accuracy but require specialized equipment and training [8–10]. MTB culture, recognized as the gold standard for diagnosis, suffers from prolonged turnaround times, averaging over 10 days to positivity [11].

While referral centers or specialized PTB hospitals have access to advanced diagnostic tools and diverse sampling methods, they also confront a more complex patient population. Factors such as obesity and sputum-scarcity reduce the efficacy of microbiological tests, many patients also present with comorbidities like immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, structural lung disease, and coinfections that necessitate accurate PTB screening and diagnosis [12–14]. Furthermore, these facilities face higher burdens of drug-resistant tuberculosis, which require microbiological confirmation to guide treatment decisions [15].

Many studies have sought to optimize microbiological diagnosis of PTB. GeneXpert MTB/RIF, acid-fast staining and MTB culture on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid have demonstrated efficacy in sputum-scarce PTB [16, 17]. Other studies report that transbronchial lung biopsy samples are also eligible for mycobacterial culture [18]. Serial tests on sputum have also been proposed to enhance diagnostic sensitivity but is only proven ex vivo [19]. The possible synergistic effects of combined microbiological tests across sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and tissue samples has not yet been investigated in clinical setting. In the current study, we evaluate the diagnostic yield of different microbiological test combinations using sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and tissue samples in a tertiary referral center specializing in respiratory diseases.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study to evaluate and compare different MTB testing strategies. Respiratory tract samples (RTS), defined as sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), or transbronchial biopsy tissue, were collected from patients diagnosed with PTB. The study population was identified through a comprehensive review of medical and laboratory records at China-Japan Friendship Hospital between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021. In final analysis, we included those with confirmed (microbiological) diagnosis of PTB based on RTS.

Sample acquisition

Expectorated sputum was obtained directly from patients. For cases highly suspected of PTB, sputum induction was performed via inhalation of hypertonic saline solution. All sputum specimens were stored in non-sterile, sealed containers and collected prior to bronchoscopy. Bronchoalveolar lavage was conducted on affected pulmonary segments identified by prior CT scans. A minimum of 5 mL of BALF was retrieved for testing. Transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) and transbronchial bronchial biopsy (TBB) samples were collected using aspiration needles or forceps during bronchoscopy. Tissue specimens were immediately placed in 4–6 mL of sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution, homogenized, and prepared for microbiological analysis. All samples were stored at room temperature and tested within 24 h of collection.

Microbiological tests

Acid-fast staining (AFS) with Ziehl-Neelsen method were used on samples decontaminated by 4% NaOH [20]. We used GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Cepheid, CA, USA) (Xpert) and GeneXpert® System (Cepheid, CA, USA) to detect MTB DNA in sputum, BALF and tissue samples. Tests on sputum, BALF and homogenized tissue were carried out according to raw sputum procedure described in the manual [21]. BACTEC™ MGIT 960™ liquid medium culture system were used to recovery and identify MTB [22].

Data extraction

We retrospectively retrieved anonymized patient data from the laboratory information system and hospital information system including demographic characteristics, department of admission, sample type, microbiological test results, and interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results.

Diagnosis of PTB

Clinical diagnosis of PTB is based on clinical symptoms (cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, mild fever, night sweats, fatigue, or weight loss) and characteristic CT findings such as cavitation, bronchiectasis, patchy pneumonitis, fibrosis, or calcification [23]. A confirmed diagnosis requires microbiological evidence of MTB in conjunction with either clinical symptoms or radiographic abnormalities. In the study center, microbiological evidence was defined by detection of MTB via Xpert or culture. Positive AFS alone was insufficient for definitive diagnosis due to a high NTM isolation rate in our hospital. Eligible specimens for microbiological testing included sputum, BALF, TBLB/TBB tissue and pleural effusion or biopsy. Histopathological examination and immunodiagnostic tests for MTB infection, including the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) were used to provide supportive evidence for PTB diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

A statistical analysis plan was set before collection of study data. Categorical variables are presented as percentages (counts), with differences analyzed using chi-square tests. For continuous variables, normality was first assessed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables conforming to a normal distribution were described by mean ± standard deviation. Non-normal data were summarized as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile). McNemar’s test was used to compare detection rates across groups on the same population. P values for multiple comparisons were corrected with Bonferroni method. 95% confidence intervals for sensitivity were calculated with Agresti-Coull method. Analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 [24]. We considered two-tailed P < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Study Population

We identified 1,094 patients with PTB eligible for analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 57 years (34.00, 71.00) and 42.3% (n = 463) were female. 21.9% (n = 240) of cases were diagnosed in outpatient departments. 78.5% (n = 859) utilized sputum tests, and 46.7% of patients (n = 511) had BALF tested. Only 18.5% (n = 202) had MTB test results on TBLB or TBB. The most frequently used method across all sample types was AFS, followed by Xpert and MTB culture. A total of 431 patients had IGRA results available. Detailed demographic characteristics and sample type distribution are summarized in Table 1.

Microbiological testing strategies on sputum

Our analysis identified seven distinct sputum testing strategies across the study population, as illustrated in Fig. 2A. Among 859 patients with available results, the most frequent combination was simultaneous use of AFS, Xpert, and culture on a single sputum sample (42.3%, n = 363). This subgroup was selected to compare diagnostic performance across all strategies. The overlap of positive outcomes among these tests is visualized in Fig. 2B. We assessed the following testing approaches: AFS alone, Xpert alone, culture alone, AFS + Xpert, Xpert + culture, AFS + culture, and simultaneous use of all three tests. Detection rates for these strategies are presented in Fig. 2C. The parallel combination (a positive result in any single test constitutes a positive parallel result) of Xpert and culture, achieved the highest sensitivity at 89.5% (95%CI 85.7%-92.1%), outperforming both AFS+culture (corrected P < 0.025) and AFS+Xpert (corrected P < 0.025). Xpert and culture demonstrated similar individual sensitivity among single-test strategies [sensitivity 73.28% (95%CI 68.3%-77.4%) vs. 74.93% (95%CI 70.0%-78.9%), P > 0.05], but showing statistically significant superiority over standalone AFS results [sensitivity 28.10% (95%CI 23.7%-32.9%)] (P < 0.05 for both comparisons).

Effectiveness of microbiological testing strategies on BALF

Of the 511 patients undergoing microbiological testing on BALF, 63.0% (n = 322) underwent all three tests (Xpert, AFS, and culture) on BALF samples, 24.1% opted for Xpert + AFS alone (Fig. 3A). We analyzed test outcome distributions and detection rates across strategies with the subgroup where all three tests were conducted on the same sample (n = 322) (Fig. 3 B–C). Xpert demonstrated superior sensitivity compared to other single-test approaches, achieving 77.0% (95%CI 72.1%-81.3%) sensitivity on BALF, surpassing MTB culture and AFS (corrected P < 0.01). The combination of Xpert + culture yielded the highest overall detection rate [86.3% (95%CI 82.1%-89.7%), corrected P < 0.01] (Fig. 3C).

Performance of different microbiological testing strategies on tissue samples

We analyzed 202 patients with TBLB or TBB sample. On these samples, 107 patients (53.0%) were tested for MTB with AFS, Xpert and culture at the same time (Fig. 4A). AFS on homogenized tissue was positive in 8.4% of patients. The difference in sensitivity between Xpert [64.5% (95%CI 55.4%-73.2%)] and culture [sensitivity 46.7% (95%CI 37.2%-55.7%)] is not statiscally significant (P>0.05). For parallel tests, Xpert+culture+AFS resulted in a 73.8% (95%CI 65.0%-81.5%) detection rate (Fig. 4C). However, there were still 10 cases where AFS and Xpert were both negative but later confirmed by culture (Fig. 4B).

Importance of sputum microbiological testing before bronchoscopy in suspected PTB patients

We investigated the positive rates of pre-bronchoscopy sputum tests. In a subgroup analysis of 356 patients, Xpert testing was performed on sputum samples from 269 individuals, while sputum MTB culture was conducted in 196 patients. Xpert detected PTB in 56.5% (95%CI 50.2%-62.7%) of cases, compared to a detection rate of 66.7% (95%CI 58.2%-74.2%) for sputum culture.

Discussion

In a specialized referral center for respiratory diseases equipped with diverse PTB diagnostic tools, our microbiological positivity rate of 61.6% only levelled with findings from a nationwide survey in China [3]. With the complicated and atypical patient population as a contributing factor, it’s impeding to refine microbiologic testing strategy. The overall sensitivity of AFS in our center was less than 30%, comparable to that reported by other centers in the same region [25]. Therefore, we recommend against relying solely on AFS for PTB diagnosis, as this method also lacks specificity to distinguish between MTB and NTM, which is particularly critical in settings with high NTM prevalence (approximately 60 cases per year, 10% of total isolated mycobacteria strains). Although AFS may offer a cost advantage, its lower cost-effectiveness compared to Xpert offsets this benefit [26]. Xpert and culture demonstrated excellent sensitivity across all sample types analyzed. Although prior studies have speculated that Xpert could potentially replace culture for sputum analysis [27], our findings indicate that culture remains more sensitive in sputum samples, with partially non-overlapping results between the two methods. This suggests complementary roles rather than redundancy. Therefore, we recommend integrating Xpert into regular sputum screening, aligning with targets in United Nations declaration [28], meanwhile maintaining sputum culture as a parallel test. In the settings of a referral center, on sputum, we recommend at least test for Xpert and culture as this combination ensures high sensitivity and specificity within 24 hours’ turnaround time. Therefore, early anti-tuberculosis treatment and transmission prevention measures can be implemented. Results from mycobacterium culture further strengthens the diagnosis while provides access to phenotypic drug susceptibility testing. If there’s a high suspicion for PTB, AFS can also be conducted as a rapid screening approach.

The application of bronchoscopy enables access to high-quality respiratory samples for PTB diagnosis. Pre-bronchoscopy rapid sputum tests effectively excluded a significant proportion of PTB patients, suggesting routine screening could reduce nosocomial transmission, but requires cost-effectiveness analysis. Several reports also indicate tests on post-bronchoscopy sputum are more sensitive [29, 30]. As we do not routinely obtain post-bronchoscopy sputum samples in study center. Future research is needed to confirm the added value of post-bronchoscopy sputum to BALF and/or TBB/TBLB in diagnosing PTB. Xpert performed similarly with culture on BALF/TBB samples with sensitivity consistent with previous studies [16]. Taking the difficulty of sample obtaining into account, we recommend combined use of at least Xpert and culture on BALF and TBLB/TBB. Although low in sensitivity, a positive AFS can provide evidence for NTM infection if combined with negative results from Xpert and MTB culture. Homogenized tissue provides a valuable adjunct for PTB diagnosis in eligible lesions or when distinguishing PTB from malignancies/atypical pathogens, though standardized protocols need further validation.

IGRAs can reveal underlying MTB infection, although it is unable to distinguish between active PTB, inactive TB and latent tuberculosis infection. A negative IGRA can effectively rule out PTB with sensitivity higher than 90%, according to previous reports [31]. In our center, however, the positivity rate of IGRAs in active PTB was only 76.3% (329/431), similar to the 81.5% positivity in another referral center in China and 81.4% from another study in low-incidence countries [32, 33]. Suggesting negative IGR Advanced age and low peripheral lymphocyte counts are known risk factors for false-negative results [34]. As a center for solid organ transplant (mainly kidney and lung transplant) and myositis, the proportion of immunosuppressed patients is relatively high in our center. In our study, we also observed a positive correlation between age and false-negative IGRA [Logistics regression OR=1.01, 95%CI (1.00,1.03), P=0.008]. The relatively low sensitivity of IGRA again calls for rigorous microbiological screening for PTB in aged and immune-compromised patients.

Our study has several strengths. First, as a real-world design, it reflects current PTB diagnostic practices and demonstrates the added value of combined MTB testing in clinical settings. Our large sample size enabled robust analyses. Additionally, parallel use of MTB tests on diverse samples allowed evaluation of specimens otherwise hard to obtain in other centers. But there are several limitations. Retrospective data may introduce selection bias. And the absence of next-generation sequencing results in LIS could underestimate PTB microbiological diagnosis rates. Also, subgroup analyses for induced vs. expectorated sputum were not conducted due to incomplete records of sampling method. The sample size was insufficient for tissue subgroup because it was not originally included in statistical analysis plan but conducted out of potential clinical significance. Future prospective research is needed to determine optimal sample processing technique and MTB test strategy on TBLB/TBB tissue.

Conclusion

In the setting of a referral center for respiratory disease in a high tuberculosis burden region, utilizing parallel tests with AFS, Xpert and MTB culture on sputum, BALF and TBLB/TBB samples can further increase the microbiological positivity in PTB patients. It is also necessary to do sputum PTB screening before bronchoscopy.
