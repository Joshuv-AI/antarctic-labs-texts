Assessing community resilience during the first COVID-19 wave peak in a megacity in eastern China: a cross-sectional study using social media data
Authors: Lanyue Zhang, Shouchuang Zhang, Yuehui Wei, Danijela Gasevic, Stella Talic, Jiayi Weng, Jun Zhang, Larry Z Liu, Weiyan Jian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn the face of pandemics resulting from infectious disease, improving community resilience has become increasingly vital. China’s sudden exit from Zero-COVID policy in December 2022 triggered a surge in COVID-19 cases, compounded by medication shortages due to earlier restrictions, creating a public health crisis. This study assesses community resilience during the first post-Zero-COVID infection wave (8 December 2022 to 7 January 2023), focusing on adaptation mechanisms, resource mobilisation, protective behaviours and medicine access, using real-time social media data to capture these dynamics.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed all geotagged COVID-19-related posts on Sina Weibo—China’s largest public microblogging platform—collected from a megacity in eastern China with over 10 million residents, covering 8 December 2022 to 7 January 2023. Posts were obtained through a data purchase agreement with Sina Weibo and comprised publicly available content. Machine learning and natural language processing were applied to classify posts across four dimensions: content, responder, response type and time. Community resilience was assessed using the community-level response ratio, response speed and sentiment expressed in interactions related to medicine-seeking posts.Results26 973 posts were analysed, of which 12 152 (45.05%) were help-seeking. Among these help-seeking posts, 11 236 (92.46%) specifically sought COVID-19 medicine, of which 8495 (75.61%) of these medicine-seeking posts received community support. Over 4 weeks, community responses comprised >70% of all replies (the rest were from market-based responders, government and NGOs). More than half of the community responses occurred within an hour, and the emotional state at the community level was the most stable and consistently positive, indicating a high level of prompt community engagement.ConclusionCommunities in the sample area consistently exhibited prompt and proactive responses during the health crisis, with community responses accounting for the majority of interactions on medicine-seeking posts. The power of community mutual aid can significantly enhance responsiveness to public health emergencies. Such insights suggest that strengthening community resilience is crucial in designing more effective disaster response strategies.

Introduction

Community resilience refers to a community’s ability to proactively prepare for, effectively adapt to and efficiently recover from disasters.13 When disasters strike, help from governments and aid organisations may not be guaranteed or it may be delayed, making community resilience crucial to help communities adapt to and recover from disasters.2 3 Central to community resilience is the development of self-sufficiency, as communities and individuals often serve as the initial responders in critical situations.4 5 This self-reliance is enabled by community-led emergency planning and rapid response mechanisms (eg, resource mobilisation through social support systems), which require diversified resources, strengthened collaboration and improved communication channels.68 The COVID-19 pandemic underscored its alignment with public health, positioning resilience as critical for preparedness and recovery during infectious disease crises.912 By empowering communities to address immediate needs early in emergencies, resilience can reduce reliance on delayed institutional responses and promote civic participation; previous studies from prior outbreaks such as Ebola and Zika have likewise shown that community engagement can play an important complementary role when formal public health responses are delayed, limited or insufficient.101214

In October 2021, China imposed rules requiring brick-and-mortar pharmacies to register buyers as part of an effort to track possible infections and banned the online sale of fever, cough, antiviral and antibiotic drugs. This regulation made it more difficult for residents to acquire these medicines, leading to a reduced ability for individuals and pharmacies to stockpile and resulting in lower-than-usual storage levels.15 However, in the month following 7 December 2022, China gradually lifted almost all COVID-19 pandemic restrictions, leading to a rapid increase in infections.15 This surge, coupled with the reduced availability of medicines due to earlier restrictions, prompted China’s government to institute a temporary rationing system for essential medicines. In response, communities and individuals found it necessary to mobilise their resources and collaborate to address the healthcare challenges brought about by this crisis.

Assessing community resilience is a challenging task. Traditional quantitative methods such as Baseline Resilience Indicator for Communities and Community Disaster Resilience Index rely on secondary data such as census and national databases but often miss local community dynamics.16 17 Qualitative approaches like Communities Advancing Resilience Toolkit18 and Community-Based Resilience Analysis capture individual experiences but require significant time and resources, limiting the scalability and efficiency for large-scale assessments.1921 In contrast, social media platforms offer real-time observational data on adaptive behaviours during crises, enabling researchers to analyse community-led coordination, resource mobilisation and risk communication patterns.22 23 Empirical studies demonstrate their utility in disaster contexts whereby social media platforms such as X (former Twitter) and Facebook support surveillance, risk communication and resource distribution.2426 These studies reported that social media remains a reliable communication and coordination channel during disasters, effectively supporting information sharing and response efforts by mobilising and integrating community efforts.27 28 Previous studies have demonstrated that social media platforms can serve as effective tools for community engagement during infectious disease outbreaks; for example, Twitter was used for real-time disease surveillance and public sentiment monitoring during the 2009 H1N1 outbreak, and social media facilitated community-driven resource coordination during the Ebola outbreak in West Africa.29 30 A rapid evidence synthesis of community engagement for COVID-19 prevention and control also suggested that the distribution of essential and medical supplies should be considered beyond interpersonal communication and education interventions.13 26 To withstand and recover from public health emergencies, it is essential to combine individual-level responses and preparedness with a network of social resources and assistance systems in communities, while local residents are typically at the forefront of recovery and reconstruction efforts during the public health crisis.31 32

Weibo is the most popular Chinese public microblogging platform similar to X. It allows users to post short messages, photos and videos to share with others. By 2021, it had accumulated over 580 million active users.33 During the first infection wave peak after the end of the ‘Zero-COVID’ policy in China, Chinese residents shared on Weibo numerous experiences and demands related to the pandemic, including requests for specific medications, prevention supplies, emotional reassurance and practical advice regarding home-based care and recovery. The content of these posts carries a sense of originality and immediacy, offering a raw, unfiltered perspective of the resident’s response to the health crisis.34 35 Thus, these posts serve as real-world data that can be harnessed to delve into the outcomes of community resilience under such public health emergencies.

The aim of this study was to assess community resilience in response to public health crisis during the first infection wave peak after the end of China’s Zero-COVID policy in a megacity in eastern China, with a particular focus on how communities adapted, mobilised resources, engaged in protective behaviours and sought access to essential medicines through a social media platform. We analysed the community-level response ratio (the proportion of responses to medicine-seeking posts from the community-level among all stakeholders), immediacy of the responses (response speed) and sentiment (emotional response) expressed in these interactions as key indicators of community resilience.

Methods

Study design and data collection

The region selected for this study was a megacity in eastern China with over 10 million population, characterised by substantial internet penetration and with the significant rise in COVID-19 cases; it represented a suitable context for implementing our methodology of assessing community resilience via social media data. This cross-sectional study analysed Weibo posts in this city from 8 December 2022 to 7 January 2023 to assess community resilience during the first COVID-19 wave after China’s Zero-COVID policy ended. Data collection began 1 day post-policy relaxation (7 December 2022), capturing real-time help-seeking behaviours related to medication shortages. The data were collected from the Sina Big Data System under the data purchase agreement signed between the School of Public Health, Peking University and Sina Weibo. By setting the post location to the study area, all COVID-19-related Weibo posts with geotags were collected (figure 1). The data consist of publicly available posts and information shared or displayed by Weibo users on the platform. No sensitive or private information that has not been anonymised is included. The study protocols were approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Centre (IRB00001052-25119).

Study flowchart. AUC, area under the curve; BERT, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers; ETS, Error, Trend, Seasonal

Posts were targeted by setting search parameters to include ‘help’ or ‘help-seeking’ and references to COVID-19 medicines listed in China’s National Health Commission guidelines, along with terms related to COVID-19 status. Terms within each category (help-related, medicine-related, COVID-19-related) were connected by OR, whereas a post had to include at least one help-related term and at least one term from either medicine-related or COVID-19-related categories to be included in the results. Posts irrelevant to COVID-19, posts with duplicates and non-substantive content were excluded, whereas posts containing personal help-seeking requests in the comments section of others’ posts were included. Given that the original Weibo posts were in Chinese, our keyword search inherently involved semantic mapping across languages (online supplemental appendix table D1 and D2).

Coding procedures

To effectively analyse the collected data (Weibo posts), a standardised data encoding format was established to be tailored to the unique characteristics of the help-seeking posts generated during the period under consideration. Social media data are multi-dimensional.36 There is evidence that studies have categorised text content posted on social media to obtain more information.37 38 Previous studies have particularly focused on four dimensions—space, time, content and network—for extracting valuable information to enhance situational awareness and better disaster response.36 39 For our analysis, the ‘space’ dimension was considered constant, as our sample area was specifically focused on the target city. Data were encoded based on the remaining three dimensions: time, content and network. Altogether, these three dimensions were represented through 23 specific items. The coding analysis framework for Weibo posts is presented in online supplemental appendix table D3.

‘Time’ was divided into 4 weeks; ‘content’ was analysed thematically and emotionally, categorised into help-seeking, aids provision, official guidance and daily life recording.38 The ‘network’ dimension, usually defined as ‘information exchange behaviours of various agents’, was subdivided in this study into two distinct sub-dimensions: Network A (responders, those who comment: community-level members, government entities, market actors and non-governmental organisations (NGOs)) and Network B (response types, the nature or substance of the comments: tangible assistance or aid, information or advice, emotional response and other). The categorisation of responders enabled a more nuanced understanding of the response behaviours and interactions within the social media network (figure 1, online supplemental appendix table D3).

Text classification

Each Weibo post was binary-tagged (‘1’ for theme relevance, ‘0’ otherwise) to streamline analysis. To ensure coding reliability, two authors independently labelled 2000 posts selected via stratified random sampling based on the time period so that posts from different stages of the crisis were represented in the annotated sample. The coding was conducted according to the content and network dimensions presented in Table D3 in online supplemental material.37 38 Inter-coder consistency exceeded 90%, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.40 Finally, following established methodologies, two authors randomly selected 2000 posts from the datasets for reclassification.37 38 Once the data coding was completed and dimensions and categories identified, outcomes were systematically assessed.

Measurement of outcomes

The outcome measures are presented in online supplemental Appendix Table D4. We focused on the response ratio and speed of response at the community level when residents sought medical assistance through social media and used the accumulative sentiment score (ASS) to analyse the mental outlook expressed through the emotional response or sentiment during this process.41 Response speed was assessed using a 1-hour benchmark adapted from H7N9 outbreak research, in which 1 hour was identified as the minimum effective response time on social media.42 Response times were categorised into intervals.

Statistical analyses

Text classification training tasks were performed for the top 19 items (excluding time-related classifications, ie, items 20–23) using the BERT-based Chinese model in Python 3.8.43 A five-fold cross-validation with stratified sampling and early stopping was applied to optimise model performance.37 44 The optimised model was used then to categorise the remaining posts (more details in online supplemental Appendix B). Descriptive statistics (Stata 17.0) summarised post characteristics, whereas temporal trends in medicine-seeking behaviour were analysed using an exponential smoothing state space45 model in Python.4547 The summary of the workflow process for modelling is presented in figure 1.

Overall, 12 152 posts were identified as help-seeking (45.05% of total), 10 261 (38.04%) were related to aid provision, 4908 (18.20%) as involving the reposting of official guidance and 13 326 (49.41%) as relating to the recording of users’ daily life. Considering that individual posts can belong to multiple categories, such as a single post both seeking medication and offering suggestions, some totals exceed 100%.

Within the help-seeking category, 11 236 posts (representing 92.46% of all help-seeking posts) specifically requested medications, highlighting the acute demand for medicines during this period. Among the 10 261 aid provision posts, 5243 (>51.10%) involved medication donations, further emphasising the magnitude of health concerns related to access to medications amid the first infection wave peak after the end of the Zero-COVID-19 policy.

In terms of the distribution of posts over time, more than half of all posts (51.79%) were dated from the first week following China’s reopening. The number of posts was found to demonstrate a gradual decrease in subsequent weeks.

Medicine-seeking posts across different time periods

The analyses of changes in the number of medicine-seeking posts and the time-series–forecasted quantities were included to provide contextual information on the overall trajectory of medicine-seeking demand during the observation period. The results indicate that the actual number of posts is largely consistent with the ETS model predictions at most time points, suggesting that the model can accurately capture the changing patterns of the time-series data (online supplemental appendix figure C2). Overall, the number of medicine-seeking posts showed a gradually declining trend. Additionally, according to the model’s predictions, the number of medicine-seeking posts was expected to show a fluctuating downward trend for a subsequent period. Meanwhile, during the 4 weeks, the response rate of medicine-seeking posts showed an upward trend, from 89.26% in week 1 to 93.90% in week 4 (online supplemental appendix figure C3).

The analysis of responders to medicine-seeking posts across time periods (figure 2) indicates that the community consistently acted as the fastest and most crucial responder throughout the crisis, demonstrating a strong and sustained presence. Community responses accounted for >70% of the replies to all medicine-seeking posts in the first 2 weeks and >60% in the last 2 weeks. Market-based responders followed the pattern of that of community responders, while the pattern for governmental assistance followed a different pattern. In the initial 2 weeks, the number of posts receiving governmental aid was relatively limited, while there was a noticeable surge in governmental responses after week 2, implying an intensified governmental response as the crisis progressed. NGOs were absent in the response chain in week 1 after reopening, with no interactions with medicine-seeking posts recorded.

The emotional feedback at the community level exhibits the most positive pattern among all actors, maintaining the highest scores in both initial (ASS=0.38 in week 1) and final phases (ASS=0.11 in week 2). Discussion

The resilience of a community depends to a significant extent on the level of responses at the individual and neighbourhood levels.2 6 48 The results of this study indicate that during the first infection wave peak following the end of the Zero-COVID policy, >45.05% posts were help-seeking, and among these help-seeking posts, 92.46% specifically sought COVID-19 medicine whereas 75.61% of these medicine-seeking posts received community support or donations.

We observed an exceptionally high-frequency response among communities compared with what has been reported in previous studies, suggesting a highly active pattern of community interaction and support during this public health crisis, which reflects an important dimension of community resilience.2338 4951 The observed higher community response ratio in the target city, particularly for medicine-seeking posts, compared with existing evidence may be due to continuing policy development and improvement and health education. That is, compared with the early stages of the pandemic in 2020, by December 2022, the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China had gradually released the 9th edition of the ‘Diagnosis and Treatment Plan for COVID-19’ and published guidelines for home treatment medications and recovery.52 The gradual publishing of these policies has improved the public’s understanding of the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, the primary care system, which includes community health facilities and township health centres, has continuously promoted health education to residents during the pandemic.5355 The improvement in residents’ medical awareness may also contribute to a higher response rate observed in this study, particularly the higher frequency of responses to medical help posts.56 Moreover, since 2021, the Chinese government has continuously strengthened the development of community governance capacities and systems, harnessing public enthusiasm and mobilising citizen participation in community affairs while steadily improving integrated service provision at the community level.57 58 This aspect relates to longer-term community governance and local response capacity. As a result, residents have gained more confidence to cope with the infection wave, leading to a more active participation in community mutual aid, such as sharing medicines, reposting help-seeking requests and offering practical information or support through social media. We build on available evidence by providing novel data on community individual and stakeholder reaction speed on social media as an additional assessment indicator for assessing community resilience during the public health crisis under examination. At the community level, the proportion of replies within a 1-hour timeframe showed a general upward trend, indicating a rapid reaction speed, which is a key aspect of community resilience.5961 Community networks have often shown unique scalability and speed, as their decentralised structure can enable the simultaneous mobilisation of many individuals, supporting both rapid responses and high response volume. This broader perspective is also reflected in previous policy-oriented discussion highlighting the importance of community-led action in pandemic response across multiple settings.59 In contrast, institutional actors such as market-based companies, government departments and NGOs may have faced organisational and procedural constraints that affected the timeliness of their responses. Previous studies on Chinese government social media use have also suggested that hierarchical decision-making structures, content approval procedures and limited operational flexibility can reduce the speed and interactivity of online public communication62 63.

Our sentiment analysis results display positive and strong mental resilience at the community level in response to this emergent public health crisis. We also identified a phased complementarity in multi-agent emotional responses: communities emerged as emotional anchors during the initial outbreak surge in week 1, followed by market-based positive sentiment peaking in week 2. Subsequently, previously latent NGOs unexpectedly dominated week 3 responses. Positive emotions, particularly through social media, enhanced adaptive capacity by fostering individual and collective resilience via mutual encouragement. Communities demonstrated rapid mobilisation and localised action but required cross-sector collaboration to address resource constraints. Governments, NGOs and others complemented these efforts through policy, funding and technical infrastructures,26466 consistent with the WHO’s definition of emergency preparedness as multi-stakeholder capacity to prevent, respond to and recover from crises.67 Collaboration enabled resource and knowledge sharing,68 bridging gaps69 and building trust that strengthens long-term social capital for future crises.70

Our findings indicate no immediate response from NGOs at the onset of the infection wave peak. This is a point of concern. One reason would be that most NGOs in China have a high dependence on government funding and policy.71 Thus, when the government assumes extensive responsibility in responding to emergencies, it can inadvertently limit the role and effectiveness of NGOs in these situations. Notably, many NGOs often lack transparency, which can lead to a lack of public trust.72 Our analysis also indicated a delayed government response to the crisis. This is consistent with findings from previous studies which have shown that, in crisis, the general public often acts as initial responders before governmental intervention and uses social media for emergency communication before the government sectors.24 26 73

Although China’s government had proactively developed strategies for the impending wave peak of infections prior to the official termination of the Zero-COVID policy—including policies to enhance emergency medicines and medical equipment reserves—widespread shortages of medical supplies were still observed during the peak period.74 One potential explanation for this scenario relates to the inherent lag between policy decision-making and its effective implementation. Policy formulation inherently requires time to translate into operational outcomes, especially during exponentially evolving crises.75 76 However, crucially, the government responded swiftly to medication shortages by enacting temporary rationing policies: mandating split sales of bulk-packaged antipyretics in pharmacies and coordinating regulatory oversight of pharmaceutical manufacturers and distribution chains to mitigate procurement challenges.77 This quick response aligns with our findings, which show that sentiment score towards the government remained positive and exhibited the least volatility compared with other stakeholders.

Some limitations should be noted. First, not all online rescue activities during the given time would have been carried out on Weibo. Some may have been offered by government departments or stakeholders without Weibo accounts, whereas some were provided to individuals who received assistance but never posted about it, which are not represented in the dataset. Second, some individuals may have deleted their help-seeking posts after receiving assistance, possibly because of privacy concerns, stigma or a desire to close the issue. This may have led to an underestimation of help-seeking posts and related interactions in our dataset. Third, only one information source related to community resilience in the context of a health crisis was investigated. Other social media platforms should, therefore, also be considered in future research. Fourth, despite the observed high-frequency and rapid responses at the community level, we were unable to determine whether these actions originated from formally organised mutual assistance efforts within the community or self-initiated collaborative actions by residents. Finally, although this study captured short-term temporal patterns, its cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference or assessment of longer-term changes in community resilience. Longitudinal data would provide more robust insight into this dynamic process.

Conclusion

This study showed that during the first post-Zero-COVID infection wave, medicine-seeking posts on social media received frequent and timely community-level responses, reflecting an important dimension of community resilience during a rapidly evolving public health crisis. We recommend that governments worldwide adopt more proactive and anticipatory measures in response to potential public health emergencies. This includes conducting comprehensive advance assessments to better predict and prepare for potential crises, as well as ensuring adequate medical resource reserves. It is essential to leverage the strength of communities by fostering collaboration among multiple stakeholders, including local governments, health agencies, community members and NGOs. Governments could also consider establishing a dedicated cross-sector team, involving relevant sectors such as public health and emergency response, to monitor social media during public health emergencies and provide timely, coordinated and context-sensitive responses to public concerns. These strategies aim to bolster both the immediate effectiveness and long-term resilience of public health responses, preparing communities and governments alike for future challenges.
