Photodynamic therapy-induced anticandidal effects via reactive oxygen species generation from bisdemethoxycurcumin and potassium iodide
Authors: Monique Ravago, Waranuch Pitiphat, Noppawan Phumala Morales, Teerasak Damrongrungruang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) is a promising non-pharmacological approach for managing biofilm-associated fungal infections. In this study, we investigated the antifungal efficacy of a double-agent aPDT system combining bisdemethoxycurcumin (BDMC) as a photosensitizer and potassium iodide (KI) as a photodynamic enhancer against Candida albicans biofilms, with particular emphasis on the underlying reactive oxygen species (ROS)–mediated mechanisms. Mature C. albicans biofilms were treated with BDMC alone (20, 40, and 80 µM), KI alone (100 mM), or their combination, followed by dental blue LED irradiation (430 ± 10 nm LED, 200 mW/cm², 0–100 J/cm²). Biofilm viability was assessed, and ROS generation quantified using fluorescence-based assays and electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy. The contribution of specific ROS to antifungal activity was evaluated through Spearman correlation analyses. The combined BDMC–KI aPDT system produced significantly greater biofilm inactivation than single-agent treatments. Enhanced antifungal activity was strongly associated with increased singlet oxygen (1O₂) generation, while hydroxyl radicals contributed to a lesser extent. These findings support a double-mode photodynamic mechanism in which BDMC-derived ROS are chemically amplified by iodide, yielding longer-lived reactive iodine species and oxidative intermediates that enhance biofilm disruption. Overall, this study demonstrates that KI functions as an effective photodynamic potentiator rather than a photosensitizer, significantly augmenting BDMC-mediated aPDT against C. albicans biofilms. The mechanistic insights gained here support the further development of double-agent photodynamic strategies for the management of drug-resistant fungal biofilm infections. Clinical relevance: Bisdemethoxycurcumin+potassium iodide act as a photosensitizer in PDT to inhibit Candida albicans biofilms.

Introduction

Oral candidiasis remains a common and clinically significant fungal infection, particularly in immunocompromised individuals and patients with chronic oral conditions1,2. Its management is increasingly complicated by recurrent disease and the emergence of antifungal resistance, especially to azole agents such as fluconazole, which often exert fungistatic rather than fungicidal effects3–8. Biofilm formation by Candida albicans further exacerbates treatment failure by limiting drug penetration and enhancing tolerance to conventional antifungal therapies.

Antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) has emerged as a promising non-pharmacologic strategy for managing microbial biofilms. aPDT relies on the activation of a photosensitizer (PS) by visible light to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce oxidative damage to microbial cells and extracellular biofilm matrices9–11. Because ROS act locally and through multi-target mechanisms, aPDT is considered less prone to resistance development than traditional antifungal agents6.

Curcumin has attracted attention as a naturally derived PS due to its biocompatibility and ability to generate ROS upon photoactivation12,13. However, its clinical translation in PDT is limited by poor aqueous solubility, chemical and photochemical instability, and rapid degradation under physiological conditions14. To address these limitations, bisdemethoxycurcumin (BDMC), a structurally related curcuminoid lacking methoxy substituents, has been proposed as an alternative PS. BDMC exhibits improved photostability, enhanced resistance to hydrolytic degradation, and favorable membrane permeability, while retaining photodynamic activity under blue-light irradiation14–17. These properties make BDMC a more suitable candidate for biofilm-targeted PDT applications.

Beyond PS selection, strategies that amplify photodynamic efficacy are increasingly recognized as critical for effective biofilm eradication. Potassium iodide (KI), a water-soluble inorganic salt, has been shown to markedly potentiate antimicrobial photodynamic inactivation when used in combination with various PS18–24. Importantly, KI is not a PS itself; rather, it acts as a photodynamic enhancer by reacting with PS-generated ROS to form secondary reactive iodine species (RIS) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)25. These longer-lived oxidative species exhibit enhanced diffusivity and antimicrobial activity compared with short-lived ROS such as singlet oxygen (¹O₂) — or hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), enabling improved penetration into dense biofilm structures18,19,24,26.

Mechanistically, when light-activated PS generate ¹O₂ (Type II) or radical intermediates (Type I), iodide ions can be oxidized to peroxyiodide, which subsequently decomposes into molecular iodine (I₂/I₃⁻), iodine radicals, and H₂O₂. Together, these species exert oxidative and iodination damage to fungal membranes, proteins, nucleic acids, and extracellular matrix components. Importantly, RIS formation can proceed under both oxygen-rich and relatively hypoxic conditions, potentially preserving antimicrobial efficacy in mature biofilms where classical Type II PDT alone may be limited18,19,24.

Recent work has demonstrated that BDMC combined with KI exhibits enhanced antifungal activity against C. albicans biofilms24. However, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding the photochemical mechanisms underlying this synergy, the specific ROS/RIS profiles generated, and how photodynamic parameters such as energy density and power density influence biofilm inactivation. Moreover, systematic correlations between ROS generation and antifungal outcomes in fluconazole-resistant C. albicans biofilms have not been fully established.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the antifungal effects of BDMC combined with KI under aPDT against C. albicans biofilms in vitro. Specifically, we sought to (i) characterize the generation of exogenous ROS under BDMC–KI-mediated aPDT, (ii) examine the influence of irradiation parameters on photodynamic efficacy, and (iii) correlate ROS production with biofilm inhibition in a fluconazole-resistant C. albicans strain. By refining the mechanistic understanding and photodynamic conditions of BDMC–KI-aPDT, this work seeks to advance the rational development of enhanced, non-drug-based strategies for the management of resistant fungal biofilm infections.

Materials & methodology

C. albicans biofilm formation and development

The formation of C. albicans biofilm was conducted using a modified biofilm development assay based on protocols from Jin, et al., Gulati, et al., and Thein, et al. 27–29. The ATCC (CRM-10231) strain, a standard C. albicans isolated from a human, was used to assess the antimicrobial effectiveness of the protocol.

The C. albicans ATCC (CRM-10231) strain was first cultured on Sabouraud’s Dextrose Agar (SDA) (GM063, HiMedia) plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to allow for colony growth. Following incubation, several well-isolated colonies were gently scraped from the agar surface using a sterile loop and transferred into 10 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The resulting suspension was vortexed briefly to disperse the cells, then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 25 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed once with PBS and re-centrifuged under the same conditions. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS, and cell concentration was adjusted to 10⁷ cells/mL using a spectrophotometer (Varioskan, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to achieve an optical absorbance (OD) of 0.38 at 520 nm, as previously reported for optimal biofilm formation29.

Sterilized 18 mm × 18 mm glass coverslips were pre-weighed, labeled, and placed horizontally into 6-well tissue culture plates. Each well received 1 mL of the standardized C. albicans suspension and was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to facilitate initial cell adhesion directly onto the upper surface of the coverslips. After this adhesion phase, non-adherent cells were gently removed by rinsing each well with 2 mL of sterile PBS. Biofilm development was then initiated by adding 2 mL of a Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) medium supplemented with 50 mM glucose to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C on an orbital shaker at 75 rpm for 48 h. Post-incubation, wells were washed twice with sterile PBS to remove loosely bound cells. The coverslips bearing mature biofilms (on their upper surfaces) were carefully transferred to sterile petri dishes and allowed to air dry at room temperature for 15–20 min. Once fully dried, each coverslip was re-weighed to estimate the mass of the formed biofilm based on the difference from its initial weight. These biofilm-covered coverslips were then subjected to aPDT.

Photosensitizer preparation and treatment groups (Fig. 1)

BDMC powder (Sigma-Aldrich®, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in PBS containing 1% (v/v) methanol. To ensure complete solubilization, the BDMC stock solution was briefly incubated in a water bath at 70 °C for 5 min. Working concentrations of BDMC were prepared at 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 µM, selected based on previous studies demonstrating effective photodynamic activity of curcuminoids within this concentration range while maintaining minimal dark toxicity19,30,31.

KI was freshly prepared in PBS at a concentration of 100 mM, consistent with concentrations widely reported to potentiate aPDT through reactive oxygen and iodine species generation23,24. KI was used exclusively as a photodynamic enhancer and not as a PS.

Experimental treatment groups were categorized as single-agent (BDMC alone or KI alone) and double-agent (BDMC + KI) conditions. Nystatin (1:100,000 U in PBS) served as the positive antifungal control, while PBS alone served as the negative control. All treatment groups were evaluated under defined irradiation conditions, including: (i) blue LED irradiation at energy densities of 50 and 100 J/cm², (ii) dark controls without light exposure, and (iii) light-only controls (no BDMC or KI) exposed to 50, 100, 150, and 200 J/cm² to assess light-induced cytotoxicity. In total, these combinations yielded 31 experimental treatment groups (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Photodynamic therapy

A volume of 2 mL from each experimental treatment group was applied directly onto glass cover slips bearing mature C. albicans biofilms. All procedures were conducted under controlled environmental conditions in a dark room to prevent unintended light exposure, at room temperature (25 °C) and 55–60% relative humidity. Following application of the treatment solutions, biofilms were incubated for a 20-minute pre-irradiation period to allow adequate interaction of BDMC and/or KI with the biofilm matrix.

Subsequently, designated treatment groups were irradiated using a blue LED light source (Elipar™ DeepCure-L, 3 M, Singapore; average wavelength 430 nm) operated in continuous-wave mode. The light source was fixed at a distance of 65 mm from the samples, delivering a calibrated irradiance of 200 mW/cm², as verified using a power meter (PM160T-HP, Thorlabs Inc., Newton, NJ, USA). Irradiation times of 250 s and 500 s corresponded to energy densities of 50 and 100 J/cm², respectively. The LED device was mounted on a customized stabilization apparatus to ensure uniform illumination and reproducibility across experiments.

To evaluate photodynamic selectivity and light-dependent cytotoxicity, additional light cytotoxicity controls were performed. Biofilms were exposed to blue LED irradiation at energy densities of 50, 100, 150, and 200 J/cm² in the absence of BDMC and KI (light-only controls). Dark cytotoxicity controls were also included, in which biofilms were incubated with treatment solutions under identical conditions without light exposure. For all treatment and control groups, biofilm viability was assessed by colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration. Nystatin served as the positive antifungal control, while PBS was used as the negative control32.

The selection of irradiation energy densities (50–200 J/cm²) was intended to encompass and extend beyond fluence ranges commonly reported in antimicrobial photodynamic therapy studies against C. albicans and other microbial biofilms. Previous in vitro investigations have employed energy densities ranging from approximately 10 J/cm² to several hundred J/cm², depending on the PS or treatment agent, light source, and experimental configuration17,24,30. Notably, studies combining PS with KI have demonstrated significant antifungal activity at relatively low light doses when KI is present, while higher fluences have been associated with enhanced dose-dependent photodynamic effects33,34. Accordingly, the broad fluence range used in this study enabled evaluation of both sub-threshold and maximal photodynamic responses, as well as identification of light-dose–dependent effects under BDMC–KI-mediated aPDT conditions.

PS concentrations and incubation parameters were selected based on preliminary optimization experiments and were consistent with those reported in analogous studies, ensuring sufficient reactive oxygen and iodine species generation while minimizing undesired dark cytotoxicity.

Candida albicans drop plate assay

Following aPDT irradiation, residual C. albicans biofilm viability was quantified using the drop plate method35. Each 6-well plate was gently washed with 2 mL of sterile PBS to remove planktonic or loosely attached cells. The glass cover slips were then carefully transferred to sterile Petri dishes containing 1.2 mL of PBS.

To detach biofilm-associated cells from the cover slip surface, the Petri dishes were sealed with parafilm and subjected to ultrasonic treatment using an ultrasonic cleaner (Ultrasonic Steri-Cleaner, Sturdy, New Taipei City, Taiwan) for 15 min. This step facilitated mechanical disruption of the biofilm matrix without compromising cell viability. The resulting cell suspension was transferred to sterile test tubes, from which 1.1 mL was collected for further processing.

Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 5 min to pellet the fungal cells. Following centrifugation, 950 µL of the supernatant was carefully discarded, leaving approximately 150 µL of concentrated cell suspension. This suspension was briefly re-centrifuged to ensure uniform cell recovery. A ten-fold serial dilution was then prepared in sterile PBS to a final volume of 1 mL, yielding four dilution levels: undiluted, 10⁻¹, 10⁻², and 10⁻³.

For enumeration, five 10 µL aliquots from each dilution were spotted onto SDA plates, which were pre-sectioned into four quadrants. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h under aerobic conditions. Following incubation, discrete colonies were enumerated at the dilution level yielding countable numbers.

Microbial viability was expressed as CFU per milliliter and normalized to biofilm dry weight to account for differences in biomass between samples. Colony counts were converted to log₁₀ CFU per biofilm weight. Descriptive statistics, including median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range (IQR), were calculated for each treatment group based on replicate measurements.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)

Hydroxyl radical generation and overall ROS production were analyzed using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Magnettech ESR5000, Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Germany)36. Spin-trapping techniques were employed to enable indirect detection of short-lived radical species. Specifically, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used as a spin-trapping agent for ⋅OH, while 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) served as a redox-sensitive probe for total ROS activity.

For ⋅OH detection, DMPO was prepared as a 1 mM stock solution in distilled water and added to each reaction mixture to obtain a final concentration of 10 µM. Immediately following light irradiation, a 30 µL aliquot of the reaction mixture was rapidly transferred into a quartz capillary tube, and EPR spectra were recorded. ⋅OH formation was identified by the characteristic four-line EPR signal of the DMPO-OH adduct, exhibiting an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1.

Total ROS generation was evaluated using TEMPO. A TEMPO stock solution prepared in distilled water was added to the reaction mixture to achieve a final concentration of 10 µM. ROS activity was quantified by monitoring the decrease in TEMPO signal intensity over time, reflecting oxidation of the stable nitroxide radical by ROS generated during photodynamic treatment.

EPR measurements were conducted at 0, 3, and 5 min following irradiation. Spectra were recorded and analyzed using EPR Studio software version 1.90.0 (Magnettech, ESR 5000, Bruker, Germany). The treatment groups included single-agent and double-agent (BDMC ± KI) conditions exposed to energy densities of 0, 50, and 100 J/cm², as well as light-only control groups.

Fluorescence probe-mediated ROS quantification

Fluorescence-based assays were employed to quantify specific ROS generated during aPDT, enabling complementary assessment of Type I and Type II photochemical pathways.

Detection of Type I ROS (Hydroxyl Radicals)

⋅OH generation was evaluated using the fluorogenic probe 2-[6-(4′-aminophenoxy)-3 H-xanthen-3-on-9-yl]benzoic acid (APF). APF is selectively oxidized by highly ROS such as ⋅OH, producing a fluorescent signal proportional to ⋅OH formation. A 5 mM APF stock solution was prepared and diluted to a final working concentration of 10 µM using dimethylformamide (DMF) as the solvent.

A 10 µM curcumin solution, prepared from a 1 mM stock using 1% methanol (v/v) as the diluent, served as the positive control for Type I ROS generation. The negative control consisted of an APF probe alone in deionized water. Fluorescence measurements were conducted using a Varioskan™ multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 490 nm and 515 nm, respectively.

Detection of Type II ROS (Singlet Oxygen)

1O₂ generation was assessed using 9,10-dimethylanthracene (DMA), a well-established chemical probe that undergoes fluorescence quenching upon reaction with1 O₂. DMA powder was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 1 mM stock solution and subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 10 µM.

A 10 µM erythrosine solution was used as the positive control for1 O₂ generation, while a DMA probe alone in deionized water served as the negative control. Fluorescence measurements were obtained using excitation and emission wavelengths of 375 and 436 nm, respectively.

Fluorescence Measurement Protocol

All treatment groups were irradiated using a blue LED light source (λ ≈ 430 nm) prior to fluorescence analysis. For each assay, a 10 µL aliquot of the appropriate probe stock solution (APF or DMA) was added to 900 µL of the corresponding 10× treatment solution. Subsequently, 100 µL of the resulting mixture was dispensed into a 96-well microplate, with each treatment condition tested in triplicate.

Fluorescence intensity was recorded immediately after irradiation (baseline) and subsequently at 3 and 5 min post-irradiation to monitor ROS kinetics. The mean fluorescence intensity and corresponding SD were calculated for each experimental condition.

Blinding and awareness

This study employed an open-label experimental design. Blinding was not feasible because the experimental protocol required predefined treatment conditions involving distinct PS concentrations (BDMC: 20, 40, and 80 µM), KI (100 mM), and specific light energy densities (0, 50, and 100 J/cm²). These parameters necessarily defined group allocation during treatment administration, irradiation, and downstream analyses.

Given the in vitro nature of the study and the objective quantification of outcomes—including CFU enumeration and spectroscopic/fluorescence-based ROS measurements—the absence of blinding was not expected to introduce systematic bias. The open-label approach enabled precise control and direct evaluation of treatment-dependent effects on C. albicans biofilm viability and ROS generation under standardized experimental conditions.

Statistical analysis

Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated by comparing post-treatment C. albicans biofilm viability to baseline controls. Biofilm inhibition was expressed as log₁₀ CFU/mL normalized to biofilm dry weight. A reduction exceeding 3 log₁₀ (corresponding to > 99.9% reduction) was considered to meet the predefined therapeutic threshold, consistent with established antimicrobial efficacy criteria.

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the majority of datasets did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric statistical methods were applied. Descriptive statistics for microbial viability were reported as median values with SD and IQR. ROS data were expressed as mean ± SD.

Comparisons among multiple treatment groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. The Spearman rank correlation test was used to assess associations between biofilm inhibition and ROS generation, including ⋅OH and1 O₂. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.001.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 28.0.1.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data visualization and additional statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.1; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Effects of aPDT on Candida albicans Biofilms

aPDT produced a significant reduction in C. albicans biofilm viability, with treatment efficacy dependent on PS composition, agent concentration, and light energy density. Dark cytotoxicity controls demonstrated negligible antifungal activity across all BDMC concentrations tested, including higher concentrations (160 and 320 µM), indicating minimal inherent toxicity of BDMC in the absence of light (Fig. 2). Similarly, light-only controls exposed to energy densities ranging from 50 to 200 J/cm² resulted in minimal reductions in viable biofilm cells, confirming that neither light nor PS alone was sufficient to elicit antifungal effects.

BDMC-mediated aPDT exhibited a clear concentration-dependent response. Increasing BDMC concentrations resulted in progressively greater reductions in viable biofilm cells, particularly under higher irradiation conditions. At an energy density of 100 J/cm², BDMC-only aPDT at 80 µM achieved a mean reduction exceeding 3 log₁₀ CFU/mL relative to untreated controls (Fig. 2). Lower BDMC concentrations (20 and 40 µM) produced more modest reductions under identical irradiation conditions, indicating a dose–response relationship between BDMC concentration and antifungal efficacy.

The incorporation of KI significantly enhanced the antifungal effects of BDMC-mediated aPDT. Double-agent BDMC–KI aPDT consistently produced greater reductions in biofilm viability than single-agent treatments at corresponding BDMC concentrations and energy densities. Notably, combined BDMC–KI treatments irradiated at 100 J/cm² yielded the highest levels of biofilm inhibition. Four treatment groups—80 µM BDMC + KI, 40 µM BDMC + KI, 20 µM BDMC + KI, and 80 µM BDMC alone—achieved reductions exceeding 3 log₁₀ CFU/mL, meeting the predefined therapeutic threshold and demonstrating antifungal efficacy comparable to the nystatin positive control (Fig. 2).

At lower irradiation energy densities (50 J/cm²), antifungal activity was reduced across all treatment groups, even at higher BDMC concentrations, indicating the presence of an energy-dependent threshold for effective biofilm inactivation. Increasing the energy density beyond 100 J/cm² did not result in proportionally greater reductions in all groups, suggesting a plateau in photodynamic efficacy under the tested conditions.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that effective biofilm inactivation requires the combined presence of BDMC, KI, and sufficient light energy. The enhanced antifungal performance observed in double-agent BDMC–KI aPDT highlights the importance of agent synergy and optimized irradiation parameters in achieving clinically relevant levels of C. albicans biofilm reduction.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using DMPO as a spin-trapping agent was employed to assess ⋅OH generation following aPDT. Discussion

Overview of Double Agent aPDT Efficacy

The present study demonstrates that double-agent aPDT employing BDMC in combination with KI offers a mechanistically distinct and biologically advantageous strategy for targeting C. albicans biofilms. Biofilm-associated Candida exhibits heightened tolerance to conventional antifungal therapy due to restricted drug diffusion, altered metabolic states, and protective extracellular matrix components. In this context, the enhanced performance of BDMC–KI–aPDT reflects not merely additive effects but a coordinated photochemical interaction that extends beyond classical PS-mediated ROS generation.

Compared with earlier curcumin-based aPDT systems, which often achieved only moderate antifungal effects due to poor photostability, limited membrane penetration, and rapid degradation, BDMC provides improved physicochemical and photodynamic properties, including enhanced cellular uptake and sustained1 O₂ generation14–24,30. These attributes likely underpin the stronger biofilm-directed activity observed in the present study relative to conventional curcumin formulations reported previously37,38. Inclusion of KI introduces a secondary amplification pathway, whereby short-lived ROS generated by BDMC are converted into longer-lived and more diffusible RIS, thereby extending oxidative stress beyond the immediate irradiation zone18–21.

This dual oxidative profile—combining rapid1 O₂-mediated damage with sustained iodine-based reactivity—addresses a key limitation of traditional aPDT approaches, namely the restricted penetration depth of ROS within dense biofilm matrices. Previous work has shown that iodine-derived oxidants possess superior diffusivity and prolonged antimicrobial activity compared to ¹O₂ alone19,20,39. The present findings align with and extend this concept by demonstrating that BDMC–KI–aPDT can generate a multifaceted oxidative environment more effectively capable of compromising biofilm integrity than single-agent photodynamic systems.

Collectively, these results position BDMC–KI–aPDT as a functionally enhanced photodynamic platform that integrates optimized PS performance with chemical amplification of oxidative stress. This approach represents a meaningful advance over conventional aPDT strategies and provides a mechanistic basis for its further development as a non-pharmacologic intervention.

Novel mechanism of action of antimicrobial photodynamic therapy

While the fundamental photochemical principles of aPDT are well established, the present findings provide additional mechanistic insights into how BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT enhances microbial inactivation within biofilm environments. Upon blue light irradiation, BDMC functions as an efficient PS, predominantly engaging Type II photochemical pathways that generate ¹O₂, a highly reactive species capable of inducing lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and nucleic acid damage in fungal cells10,34,40–42. The strong correlation observed between ¹O₂ production and biofilm inhibition in this study supports the central role of ¹O₂ as the primary cytotoxic mediator of BDMC-based aPDT.

Proteomic analyses have demonstrated that antimicrobial photodynamic therapy induces widespread oxidative modification of microbial proteins, including enzymes involved in metabolism, stress response, and cell envelope integrity. This multi-target protein oxidation leads to irreversible cellular dysfunction and cell death, rather than inhibition of a single molecular pathway43.

The introduction of KI substantially modifies this photochemical landscape. The broader chemical repertoire introduced by KI confers multiple advantages such as iodination damage which extends beyond the capabilities of ROS alone18,19,23,24,26,44–47. Although KI does not absorb light or independently generate reactive species, it functions as a redox-active enhancer that amplifies photodynamic outcomes through secondary chemical reactions. KI influences the photochemical profile by selectively scavenging hydroxyl radicals, thereby channeling the oxidative stress response toward RIS and ¹O₂-mediated pathways. Specifically, I⁻ reacts with ¹O₂ and PS-derived radical intermediates to generate a suite of secondary oxidants collectively termed RIS, including I₂, I₃⁻, I⋅, I₂⋅⁻, and H₂O₂18–21,23,26. This shift is potentially advantageous in hypoxic or reductive microenvironments – conditions known to dampen hydroxyl radical generation – such as those within mature biofilms.

The use of selective radical scavengers to infer dominant oxidative pathways is well established in photocatalytic and photochemical systems, where quenching experiments have been employed to distinguish the relative contributions of hydroxyl radicals and ¹O₂ to reaction kinetics, supporting analogous mechanistic interpretations in photodynamic studies48.

The generation of RIS introduces several mechanistic advantages over ROS-only systems. Unlike ¹O₂, which is short-lived and spatially restricted, iodine-based oxidants and H₂O₂ exhibit greater chemical stability and diffusivity, enabling deeper penetration into the extracellular polymeric matrix of mature biofilms19,20,39. These species are known to irreversibly disrupt fungal cell wall integrity, oxidize thiol-containing enzymes, impair membrane-associated proteins, and compromise biofilm structural cohesion—mechanisms that extend beyond the localized effects of ¹O₂ alone.

Additionally, KI appears to modulate the overall oxidative profile by scavenging ⋅OH, thereby shifting redox activity toward ¹O₂- and RIS-dominated pathways48. Such modulation may be particularly beneficial within hypoxic or reductive microenvironments characteristic of dense biofilms, where ⋅OH formation is often limited or rapidly quenched49–52. The reduced detectability of ⋅OH in KI-containing groups observed in this study is consistent with this redox-shifting effect and further supports a mechanistic transition from short-lived radical damage to sustained oxidative and iodination stress. Such non-specific, multi-target oxidative damage is consistent with proteomic studies showing that aPDT simultaneously affects numerous essential proteins, thereby limiting the capacity of microorganisms to develop resistance through conventional adaptive mechanisms43.

Taken together, BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT orchestrates a multi-layered antimicrobial mechanism involving rapid ¹O₂–driven damage followed by prolonged oxidative and iodination effects mediated by RIS and H₂O₂. This coordinated photochemical interplay addresses key limitations of conventional single-agent aPDT systems—namely restricted ROS diffusion and transient cytotoxicity—and provides a mechanistic rationale for the enhanced efficacy observed against structurally resilient and drug-resistant fungal biofilms. These findings position BDMC–KI–aPDT as a mechanistically optimized photodynamic strategy with clear translational relevance17,19,24,30,33,53.

Mechanism of Action: Role of KI

KI plays a pivotal role in enhancing aPDT through a combination of chemical reactivity and redox modulation. Unlike conventional PS, KI does not absorb light or independently generate reactive species. Instead, it acts as a redox-active co-adjuvant, transforming primary ROS—mainly1 O₂ produced by BDMC—into secondary, longer-lived microbicidal agents.

Upon photoactivation of BDMC, the generated1 O₂ oxidizes I⁻ into RIS, including I₂, I₃⁻, and I⋅, I₂⋅⁻ radicals. These iodine-based species have been reported to possess higher chemical stability and diffusivity than1 O₂ or ⋅OH, allowing them to penetrate deeper into biofilm matrices and prolong antimicrobial effects19,23,26. Additionally, the reaction cascade may yield H₂O₂, either through direct ROS–KI interactions or via decomposition of peroxyiodide intermediates21.These species synergistically contribute to irreversible oxidative and iodination damage of fungal cell membranes, proteins, and extracellular matrix components.

Moreover, KI appears to influence the ROS landscape by scavenging short-lived ⋅OH, shifting the redox balance toward ¹O₂–dominated mechanisms. This may be particularly advantageous under hypoxic conditions where radical-mediated PDT efficacy is typically limited. Thus, KI enables a dual-action mechanism—extending the antimicrobial reach of BDMC-based PDT by generating a broader oxidative profile and improving biofilm-targeting precision.

Collectively, these actions distinguish KI-enhanced PDT from conventional single-PS systems and underscore its potential as a clinically translatable strategy, especially against drug-resistant Candida biofilms.

Pre-irradiation time, biofilm susceptibility and membrane pretreatment strategies

The interval between PS application and light exposure—commonly termed the pre-irradiation period—represents a critical determinant of aPDT efficacy, particularly in structured microbial communities such as biofilms. In the present study, a 20-minute pre-irradiation incubation was employed based on prior evidence demonstrating that this duration facilitates adequate PS diffusion, cellular association, and intracellular localization within Candida biofilms54–56. This incubation window allows BDMC to traverse the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) and associate with fungal cell membranes prior to photoactivation, thereby enhancing photochemical efficiency upon irradiation.

The importance of pre-irradiation timing is accentuated in mature biofilms, which exhibit dense EPS networks, reduced metabolic activity, and altered cell wall architecture that collectively limit PS penetration compared with planktonic cells55,56. Planktonic Candida cells typically display rapid PS uptake and minimal dependence on incubation time, whereas biofilm-embedded cells require prolonged exposure to overcome diffusion barriers and ensure sufficient intracellular or membrane-proximal PS localization. Consequently, inadequate pre-irradiation may result in suboptimal excitation of the PS within deeper biofilm layers, thereby limiting ROS generation at critical target sites.

Beyond incubation time alone, chemical pretreatment strategies aimed at enhancing membrane permeability and EPS porosity have been explored to further improve photodynamic outcomes. One such approach involves the use of tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris) buffer, which has been reported to partially destabilize microbial cell walls and weaken ionic cross-linking within biofilm matrices57,58. Tris can alter surface charge interactions, disrupt divalent cation bridges within the EPS, and increase membrane permeability, thereby facilitating improved penetration of PS and other antimicrobial agents in both bacterial and fungal biofilms.

Previous studies have demonstrated that Tris-based pretreatment enhances the uptake of PS and potentiates aPDT efficacy by increasing intracellular access and improving ROS proximity to vital cellular targets57,58. Such permeability-modulating strategies may be particularly relevant for recalcitrant biofilms, where diffusion constraints rather than intrinsic photochemical limitations become the primary barrier to efficacy.

Although Tris pretreatment was not incorporated into the present experimental design, its reported ability to enhance PS accessibility highlights a potential avenue for further optimization of BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT. Future investigations integrating membrane- or matrix-disrupting agents with optimized pre-irradiation timing could help reduce required light doses, improve spatial ROS delivery within biofilms, and further enhance therapeutic selectivity against biofilm-embedded fungal cells.

Concentration-Dependent Response and ROS Profiles

A clear dose–response relationship was observed in this study: increasing BDMC concentrations led to enhanced antifungal efficacy, with elevated1 O₂ production confirmed by both fluorescence-based probes and electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy. This is consistent with prior findings using curcuminoids, where higher concentrations yielded more robust photochemical responses and improved microbial inactivation efficacy14,37.

Interestingly, although ⋅OH levels also increased with BDMC concentration, their contribution to antifungal outcomes appeared diminished in groups co-treated with KI. This is likely due to the well-documented radical-scavenging effect of iodide ions, which tend to neutralize highly reactive short-lived species like ⋅OH, thereby favoring more stable ROS/RIS pathways18,49,59.

Fluorescence measurements further revealed that 80 µM BDMC generated ⋅OH quantities comparable to those of 10 µM conventional curcumin under higher energy densities60. This underscores BDMC’s high photochemical reactivity and potential as a next-generation PS, particularly when combined with KI.

Compared to classical PS such as methylene blue (MB) and toluidine blue O (TBO), BDMC exhibits a unique ROS generation profile. MB primarily produces1 O₂ via Type II reactions, and its photodynamic efficiency has been reported to be energy-density dependent, with optimal performance at moderate light doses61. TBO, similarly, generates ¹O₂ but often requires higher light intensities or longer incubation times for significant biofilm penetration. Notably, both dyes lack the ability to produce the broader oxidative cascade observed in BDMC–KI-mediated aPDT, which includes RIS and H₂O₂ as additional antimicrobial agents20,26.

Thus, BDMC–KI–aPDT not only rivals the ROS yield of established PSs like MB and TBO, but also expands the photodynamic spectrum through iodide-mediated secondary reactions. This multifaceted ROS profile enhances fungal inactivation and supports its potential as a superior alternative, particularly against resistant biofilms62.

Impact of energy and power densities

Energy density and power density are central determinants of PDT efficacy, particularly in biofilm-associated infections where both light penetration and reactive species availability impose significant constraints. In the present study, a pronounced threshold effect was observed, whereby increasing the energy density from 50 to 100 J/cm² resulted in markedly enhanced C. albicans biofilm inactivation under BDMC–KI–aPDT conditions. Similar threshold-dependent behavior has been reported for commonly used PS such as MB and TBO, where higher fluences improved microbial killing by increasing PS excitation and downstream ROS production20,26,38,45,62–64.

However, the mechanistic basis for this threshold phenomenon is unlikely to be attributable to ROS yield alone. At lower energy densities, insufficient photon delivery may limit excitation of a critical fraction of BDMC molecules, thereby constraining ROS generation even when PS concentrations are adequate62,65. Additionally, dense fungal biofilms present substantial optical and physical barriers that attenuate light transmission and restrict the diffusion of short-lived ROS, resulting in spatially heterogeneous photochemical activity across biofilm depth9,66. As a consequence, subthreshold energy inputs may preferentially affect superficial biofilm layers while leaving deeper, metabolically altered cells largely unaffected65.

Power density (irradiance) further modulated photodynamic outcomes in this study. At irradiances below 200 mW/cm², neither ¹O₂ nor ⋅OH were reliably detected, whereas higher irradiances facilitated consistent ROS generation, particularly when combined with sufficient total energy delivery62. This observation is consistent with prior reports demonstrating that increased irradiance enhances triplet-state PS formation and ¹O₂ quantum yield in MB- and TBO-mediated PDT systems41,62. Nevertheless, excessive irradiance has also been associated with photobleaching and premature PS deactivation, underscoring the need for balanced optimization rather than maximal light intensity.

Taken together, the observed threshold effect reflects a multifactorial interplay between PS excitation efficiency, ROS generation kinetics, and biofilm structural resistance to both light and reactive species diffusion. Importantly, the BDMC–KI system appears particularly sensitive to these parameters. The inclusion of KI extends the oxidative landscape beyond short-lived ROS by generating longer-lived, diffusible iodine species, which may partially mitigate diffusion limitations inherent to biofilm matrices. As such, appropriate tuning of both energy density and power density is essential to fully exploit the therapeutic potential of BDMC–KI–aPDT and achieve effective photodynamic disruption of resilient fungal biofilms.

Dominant role of singlet oxygen

Among the ROS generated during antimicrobial photodynamic therapy, ¹O₂ emerged as the principal mediator of immediate antifungal activity in this study. Double-agent BDMC–KI–aPDT produced significantly higher ¹O₂ signals than single-agent configurations, and this increase was directly associated with enhanced C. albicans biofilm inactivation. Quantitative analysis revealed that treatment groups generating less than approximately 60% of the maximal ¹O₂ signal consistently exhibited inferior antifungal outcomes, underscoring the dose-dependent relationship between ¹O₂ production and microbial killing.

These findings align with extensive photodynamic literature identifying ¹O₂ as the dominant cytotoxic species responsible for membrane lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and nucleic acid damage in microbial cells. They also reaffirm the central importance of light activation, as non-irradiated PS conditions produced negligible ROS and no measurable antifungal effect. Together, these observations demonstrate that effective BDMC-based aPDT relies on precise light–PS interaction rather than PS concentration alone.

While ¹O₂ plays a critical role in initiating rapid, light-dependent fungal inactivation, its extremely short lifetime and limited diffusion distance constrain its ability to penetrate deeply into mature biofilm structures or sustain antimicrobial activity beyond the irradiation period. These inherent limitations suggest that additional oxidative mechanisms are required to explain the prolonged and enhanced antifungal effects observed with BDMC–KI treatment, which are addressed through the contribution of iodine-derived reactive species in the subsequent section.

ROS interpretation and detection limitations

BDMC alone can generate both ¹O₂ and ⋅OH; yet, combining BDMC with KI complicates ROS detection. While ¹O₂ generation under optimal conditions (80 µM BDMC + KI + 100 J/cm²) correlated strongly with fungal inactivation (r = 0.85), detection of ⋅OH became unreliable — presumably due to KI’s radical-scavenging activity, which converts ⋅OH into non-radical species, hindering measurement by EPR or fluorescence49–52. This highlights a limitation in common detection methods and suggests that double-agent PDT may produce a broader, more complex oxidative/iodination species profile than assessed by conventional assays.

In this context, the identified optimal treatment—80 µM BDMC + 100 mM KI at 100 J/cm²—produced > 3 log₁₀ CFU reduction and was strongly correlated (r = 0.85) with increased ¹O₂ production. EPR and fluorescence probe assays (TEMPO and DMA) confirmed significant1 O₂ generation under these condition34,42,67–70, affirming its primary role in microbial inactivation.

Power density also influenced ROS yields. A low power density (200 mW/cm²) failed to yield detectable ⋅OH, whereas higher power (800 mW/cm²) in combination with elevated energy densities (120–240 J/cm²) enabled robust ROS detection and improved antifungal efficacy71–73. These observations indicate that BDMC–KI–aPDT may demand higher photonic inputs than conventional PS-only systems; yet, the broader oxidative profile justifies this requirement, particularly for resilient biofilms10,34,41,61,72–78.

1O₂ emerged as the most critical ROS in this study, consistent with existing literature that links high1 O₂ quantum yields with greater microbial inactivation10,34,40–42. Double agent systems (B-KI-aPDT) consistently produced stronger ¹O₂ signals and superior fungal inhibition compared to single-agent setups, highlighting the advantage of combining BDMC and KI. Moreover, treatment groups producing < 60% ¹O₂ showed < 3 log₁₀ reductions, reinforcing the mechanistic significance of 1O₂ in this setting.

Notably, increasing PS concentration or light dose alone does not guarantee improved antifungal activity. Effective aPDT hinges on optimal PS-light interactions, as evidenced by our fluorescence and EPR data, which showed that non-irradiated PS produced negligible ROS14,33. This finding reaffirms the essential role of light activation in aPDT efficacy.

Iodine species and long-term efficacy

The incorporation of KI introduces an additional oxidative dimension to BDMC-mediated aPDT that extends beyond the immediate, light-dependent effects of ¹O₂. Upon irradiation, ¹O₂ and other primary ROS generated by photoactivated BDMC oxidize iodide ions to form RIS, including I₂, I₃⁻, I⋅, I₂⋅⁻, and H₂O₂. These iodine-derived oxidants have been reported to possess longer lifetimes and greater diffusibility than primary ROS, enabling them to more effectively traverse biofilm matrices and interact with fungal cells beyond the immediate vicinity of the PS.

Although the present study did not directly quantify RIS formation, the enhanced antifungal efficacy observed in BDMC–KI groups—despite comparable ¹O₂ thresholds—supports a contributory role for secondary iodine-based oxidants. Previous studies have demonstrated that iodine species can irreversibly disrupt fungal cell wall components, oxidize thiol-containing enzymes, and destabilize extracellular polymeric substances, thereby weakening biofilm structural integrity and increasing susceptibility to oxidative damage.

RIS generation offers a mechanistic explanation for how antimicrobial effects may extend spatially within dense biofilms, where the short diffusion distance of ¹O₂ alone may be insufficient. However, it must be emphasized that the duration and quantitative contribution of RIS following irradiation were not assessed in this work. Direct measurement of iodine species—using approaches such as UV–visible spectroscopy, iodometric assays, or Raman analysis—would be necessary to distinguish transient oxidative events from potentially prolonged antimicrobial effects.

Additionally, long-term treatment durability and biofilm recolonization were not evaluated. Factors such as BDMC photostability, aqueous solubility, and retention within biofilm matrices may influence sustained efficacy, particularly under in vivo conditions. Future studies incorporating temporal RIS profiling, post-treatment regrowth assessment, and advanced delivery systems (e.g., nanoparticle-based formulations) will be essential to fully define the translational potential of BDMC–KI-mediated aPDT.

Therapeutic implications against drug-resistant Candida Strains

The clinical relevance of the present findings is strengthened by the use of a fluconazole-resistant C. albicans strain (ATCC CRM-10231), a phenotype frequently associated with persistent oral and mucosal candidiasis. Resistance in such strains is often compounded by biofilm formation, which limits antifungal penetration, alters metabolic activity, and promotes stress-adaptive responses that reduce susceptibility to conventional fungistatic agents.

The observed efficacy of BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT against this resistant strain suggests that the photodynamic mechanism operates independently of classical antifungal targets such as ergosterol biosynthesis or efflux pump regulation. Instead, microbial inactivation is driven by oxidative and iodination-based damage to fundamental cellular structures, including membranes, proteins, and extracellular biofilm components. This non-specific, multi-target mode of action reduces the likelihood of cross-resistance with existing antifungal drugs and supports the potential utility of aPDT in treatment-refractory infections.

Importantly, the enhanced ROS/RIS profile generated by the BDMC–KI system may partially address diffusion limitations characteristic of mature biofilms. While ¹O₂ acts locally and transiently, secondary iodine-derived oxidants and hydrogen peroxide possess greater diffusibility, enabling oxidative stress to extend beyond the immediate vicinity of the PS. Such spatial expansion of antimicrobial activity is particularly relevant in resistant biofilms, where limited drug penetration is a dominant failure mechanism.

Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that the present study did not directly assess resistance reversal, post-treatment regrowth, or long-term suppression of viable biofilm populations. As such, the findings support mechanistic plausibility rather than definitive clinical superiority over antifungal agents. Future investigations incorporating resistant clinical isolates, mixed-species biofilms, and longitudinal regrowth models will be critical to determine whether BDMC–KI–aPDT can provide durable control of drug-resistant candidiasis.

Taken together, these results position BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT as a promising adjunctive or alternative strategy for managing antifungal-resistant Candida biofilms, particularly in localized infections where light delivery is feasible and conventional therapies are limited.

Future perspectives: toward clinical translation of double-agent aPDT

While the present study demonstrates the mechanistic and antifungal advantages of BDMC–KI–mediated aPDT in vitro, several steps are required to support clinical translation. Foremost, direct quantification of RIS and related oxidative by-products (e.g., H₂O₂, iodinated oxidants) will be essential to validate the proposed RIS-mediated contribution beyond ¹O₂–driven effects. Advanced analytical and imaging approaches capable of resolving intracellular ROS/RIS dynamics within biofilms would further clarify spatial and temporal aspects of oxidative stress during treatment.

Future investigations should also extend beyond mono-species laboratory biofilms to include mixed-species and clinically derived biofilms, which more accurately reflect oral and mucosal infection environments. Such models will be critical for evaluating whether the enhanced diffusibility and persistence of BDMC–KI–derived oxidative species can consistently overcome biofilm-associated diffusion barriers under clinically relevant conditions.

From a translational perspective, optimization of delivery strategies represents a key priority. Formulation approaches such as nanoparticle encapsulation, hydrogel or mucoadhesive carriers, and controlled-release systems may improve photosensitizer retention, KI co-localization, and light-mediated activation at infection sites. Parallel evaluation of cytocompatibility using relevant mammalian cell models, followed by in vivo validation in established candidiasis models, will be necessary to define therapeutic windows and safety profiles.

Collectively, these future directions position BDMC–KI–based aPDT as a promising, mechanism-driven platform rather than a fixed protocol. By integrating optimized photochemistry, delivery systems, and biologically relevant infection models, this double-agent approach may ultimately contribute to the development of clinically viable photodynamic strategies for managing drug-resistant and biofilm-associated fungal infections.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dual-agent antimicrobial photodynamic therapy combining bisdemethoxycurcumin (BDMC) and potassium iodide (KI) effectively inactivates Candida albicans biofilms in vitro. The enhanced antifungal efficacy is strongly associated with increased singlet oxygen (¹O₂) production and a broadened oxidative profile, resulting from KI-mediated conversion of primary reactive oxygen species into longer-lived reactive iodine species and hydrogen peroxide.

The BDMC–KI system provides a mechanistically robust, non-pharmacologic approach that operates independently of conventional antifungal targets, offering potential advantages in the management of drug-resistant and biofilm-associated candida infections. Its localized, light-activated mechanism and multi-target oxidative action may help overcome key limitations of existing antifungal therapies.

Further studies focusing on delivery optimization, comprehensive safety evaluation, and in vivo validation are required to support clinical translation. Collectively, these findings establish a strong mechanistic and therapeutic foundation for the development of BDMC–KI–mediated antimicrobial photodynamic therapy as a next-generation strategy for fungal biofilm control.
