Attractive and Repulsive Perceptual Biases Naturally Emerge in Generative Adversarial Inference
Hyun-Jun Jeon, Hansol Choi, Oh-Sang Kwon

Abstract
Human perceptual estimates exhibit a striking reversal in bias depending on uncertainty: they shift toward prior expectations under high sensory uncertainty, but away from them when internal noise is dominant. While Bayesian inference combined with efficient coding can explain this dual bias, existing models rely on handcrafted priors or fixed encoders, offering no account of how such representations and inferences could emerge through learning. We introduce a Generative Adversarial Inference (GAI) network that simultaneously learns sensory representations and inference strategies directly from data, without assuming explicit likelihoods or priors. Through joint reconstruction and adversarial training, the model learns a representation that approximates an efficient code consistent with information-theoretic predictions. Trained on Gabor stimuli with varying signal-to-noise ratios, GAI spontaneously reproduces the full transition from prior attraction to repulsion, and recovers the Fisher information profile predicted by efficient coding theory. It also captures the characteristic bias reversal observed in human perception more robustly than supervised or variational alternatives. These results show that a single adversarially trained network can jointly acquire an efficient sensory code and support Bayesian-consistent behavior, providing a neurally plausible, end-to-end account of perceptual bias that unifies normative theory and deep learning.



1
Introduction

Human perceptual judgments often exhibit systematic biases, offering critical insights into how the brain encodes and interprets sensory information. Understanding these biases is not only essential for explaining perceptual illusions and individual differences, but also for revealing the underlying principles that govern neural computation and learning. A well-known phenomenon is the tendency for perceptual estimates to regress toward statistically frequent or prototypical values—a pattern often described as a central tendency bias. However, repulsive biases have also been widely observed, where judgments are systematically shifted away from such central values. While this diversity may seem puzzling at first glance, decades of research have converged on a surprisingly parsimonious explanation.

Bayesian inference provides a normative framework for understanding perceptual estimates under uncertainty. Because sensory signals are inherently noisy and incomplete, the brain is thought to infer the most likely causes of sensory inputs by integrating them with prior expectations
(
Von Helmholtz 1867
;
Knill and Pouget 2004
;
Ernst and Banks 2002
)
. Importantly, before such inference can take place, the input itself must be encoded—mapped from the high-dimensional world into a constrained neural representation. Under the efficient coding hypothesis
(
Attneave 1954
;
Barlow 1961
;
Linsker 1988
)
, this encoding process is shaped by environmental statistics: more neural resources are allocated to frequently encountered stimuli, effectively warping the sensory space. As a result, the internal noise acting in this distorted space induces systematic biases in perception. A series of studies
(
Wei and Stocker 2015
;
Hahn and Wei 2024
)
have shown that the interaction between efficient encoding and Bayesian decoding can not only account for central tendency biases, but also predict a reversal toward repulsive biases away from the prior.

Despite their normative clarity, symbolic observer models face two fundamental limitations. First, they offer little explanation for how perceptual experiences—rich, continuous, and lifelike—emerge from abstract numerical estimates. Human perception is not a sequence of point estimates, but a structured, dynamic reconstruction of sensory reality
(
Clark 2013
)
. Second, these models rarely address how priors and likelihoods are learned, or under what conditions complex perceptual behaviors—such as the reversal from attraction to repulsion—naturally emerge. From a connectionist perspective, these questions invite a complementary approach: can a system learn both perceptual structure and inference dynamics from data alone, without hand-crafted probabilistic components? Recent work has shown that neural networks can exhibit sensory warping as emergent phenomena under specific training conditions (e.g.,
(
Benjamin et al. 2022
)
). However, no existing model demonstrates the full attraction-to-repulsion transition within a single unified system. To understand when and why such transitions arise—and what computational mechanisms support them—we need models in which efficient representation and adaptive inference emerge jointly through learning.

To address this gap, we present Generative Adversarial Inference (GAI), a learned system that reproduces the full transition from attraction to repulsion as an emergent behavior. Trained end-to-end on Gabor stimuli with varying uncertainty, GAI spontaneously develops both efficient internal representations and an implicit inference strategy that mirrors human perceptual biases. Perceptual estimates are obtained by decoding this latent space with a simple
L_{p}
loss. Together, these results demonstrate that a single learned system can naturally give rise to both efficient encoding and Bayesian-like inference—bridging normative and connectionist perspectives on perception.

Contributions.

This work makes the following contributions:

•

We introduce a generative adversarial inference (GAI) framework that explains both central-tendency and repulsive perceptual biases as emergent properties of a learned system—without any hand-crafted priors or likelihoods.

•

We uncover a computational mechanism whereby the interplay of efficient latent representations, internal noise, and decoding loss gives rise to human-like biases. The model recovers the predicted Fisher information profile and produces structured distortions under subtle perturbations.

•

Going beyond models that merely reproduce bias patterns or point estimates, GAI generates percepts that are rich, continuous, and lifelike—providing a structured reconstruction of sensory reality that aligns more closely with human perceptual experience.

2
Background & Related Work

2.1
Efficient Bayesian account of bias reversals

While Bayesian models often predict attraction toward prior expectations under uncertainty, empirical data reveal a more nuanced picture. In particular, human perceptual estimates can shift either toward or away from high-probability stimuli depending on the source of uncertainty. When external noise dominates—such as with low-contrast or degraded inputs—estimates tend to regress toward the mean of the stimulus distribution, producing the well-known central-tendency bias. In contrast, when internal noise is the primary source of uncertainty—arising from memory delays, brief exposures, or attentional limitations—biases often reverse direction, repelling away from prior expectations. This pattern has been observed across modalities: in orientation perception
(
De Gardelle et al. 2010
;
Wei and Stocker 2015
)
, size-weight estimation
(
Brayanov and Smith 2010
)
, and motion direction judgments
(
Lennie 1971
)
.

Wei and Stocker proposed a formal model that explains this reversal by integrating efficient coding principles into a Bayesian observer framework
(
Wei and Stocker 2015
)
. Under the assumption that sensory systems allocate coding resources in proportion to stimulus frequency—thereby maximizing mutual information—the model links the prior distribution
p(\theta)
to the Fisher information
J(\theta)
of the internal representation via the relation
\sqrt{J(\theta)}\ \propto p(\theta)
. Fisher information, in turn, sets a lower bound on discrimination thresholds: the just-noticeable difference
D(\theta)
scales inversely with the square root of information,
D(\theta)\propto 1/\sqrt{J(\theta)}
. Thus, more frequent stimuli are encoded with higher fidelity and discriminated more precisely. Combined with a noise model and an
L_{2}
loss function, this formulation leads to likelihood asymmetries that vary with the type of uncertainty:
external noise leads to attraction toward the prior, whereas internal noise leads to repulsion away from it
. This efficient Bayesian observer model offers a principled, quantitative account of perceptual bias reversal as a function of uncertainty structure. However, how such an encoding-decoding architecture might emerge through learning—and whether both attraction and repulsion can arise naturally in a single learned system—remains an open question.

2.2
Emergent perceptual representations in deep neural networks

A growing body of research has shown that deep neural networks can spontaneously give rise to internal representations that mirror perceptual structures observed in the brain. For example, recurrent networks trained on physical inference tasks develop latent dynamics that encode object velocity
(
Rajalingham et al. 2022
)
; convolutional neural networks trained for viewpoint-invariant recognition exhibit mirror-symmetric tuning reminiscent of cells in the primate visual cortex
(
Farzmahdi et al. 2024
)
; and networks trained on numerical classification tasks develop numerosity-tuned units that follow Weber-Fechner scaling
(
Nasr et al. 2019
)
. These findings suggest that task-driven learning alone, without any hand-crafted inductive structure, can give rise to domain-specific perceptual representations with notable biological parallels.

Other recent work has examined whether such emergent representations can also exhibit the hallmark features of efficient coding. One study
(
Benjamin et al. 2022
)
showed that gradient-based training alone can induce efficient neural codes for visual features like orientation and color hue, as quantified by Fisher information. Specifically, during training, the representational fidelity across stimulus values came to reflect the statistical prevalence of those features in the input—a signature predicted by efficient coding theory. However, this efficiency was observed under restricted conditions: the study used a fully connected multilayer perceptron trained on a reconstruction task, and the emergent coding structure degraded once the network fully converged. Without an architectural bottleneck or regularization pressure, the network eventually learned to reproduce all input features uniformly, flattening the Fisher information profile. These results highlight that while gradient learning can give rise to efficient-like codes, the persistence and generality of such representations depend critically on structural constraints—leaving open the question of how efficient and inference-compatible codes might jointly emerge in more flexible architectures.

2.3
Limitations of prior work and our approach

To sustain efficient coding throughout learning, biologically plausible architectures often impose structural constraints on the latent space. Autoencoders implement this through a low-dimensional bottleneck, forcing the network to compress input variability
(
Rumelhart et al. 1986
)
. However, their reliance on a point-estimate reconstruction loss means that, under complex or multimodal priors, the optimal output may interpolate across modes—yielding ambiguous or counterintuitive reconstructions that diverge from perceptual experience. This limitation stems not from empirical failure, but from the inherent bias of the loss function toward mean solutions.

Variational autoencoders (VAEs) improve upon this by introducing an explicit prior distribution-matching term that shapes the latent space during training
(
Kingma and Welling 2013
)
. While this approach enables sampling and regularization, it typically assumes simple distributions such as Gaussians for analytical tractability, which can limit expressiveness in certain settings. An alternative, adversarial autoencoders (AAEs), replace this term with a discriminator trained to align the marginal latent distribution with a chosen prior
(
Makhzani et al. 2015
)
. Although AAEs allow greater flexibility, they do not enforce alignment at the joint level between inputs and latents. This limitation applies broadly to many adversarial frameworks, where the encoder and generator are trained separately.

These structural limitations point to a deeper gap: the lack of a unified mechanism for learning both efficient representations and biologically plausible inference. The generative adversarial brain hypothesis
(
Gershman 2019
)
conceptually proposes that inference may arise from a bidirectional negotiation between bottom-up sensory signals and top-down generative predictions—a process reminiscent of adversarial dynamics. This idea resonates with predictive coding theories of the brain
(
Rao and Ballard 1999
;
Friston 2010
)
, where perception emerges from recursive interactions between encoding and generation. Yet, despite these proposals, no existing model demonstrates the central-to-repulsive transition observed in human behavior as a function of noise structure, nor do they jointly achieve efficient representation and implicit, uncertainty-sensitive inference within a single, end-to-end trained system.

To address this gap, we introduce the Generative Adversarial Inference (GAI) model: a network that integrates three key principles—efficient sensory encoding, implicit prior and likelihood structure, and uncertainty-dependent bias—within a unified learning framework. GAI uses a denoising encoder–decoder architecture trained adversarially to align latent representations with a fixed prior distribution. When trained end-to-end on parametrically controlled stimuli, the model develops an internal representational structure that reflects stimulus statistics via its Fisher information profile. It further captures the reversal from central-tendency to repulsive biases as input noise levels vary—reproducing the key signature of human perceptual inference.

3
Generative Adversarial Inference (GAI)

We design a learnable encoder–generator–discriminator architecture that instantiates the core principles of the efficient Bayesian observer model, trained end-to-end with a unified objective. GAI comprises three interacting modules—an encoder
E
, a generator
G
, and a discriminator
D
—that together (i) shape the latent space so its local Fisher information mirrors the empirical stimulus distribution and (ii) implicitly learn a posterior mapping from observations to latent codes without any explicit likelihood specification. Through this joint optimization, GAI naturally reproduces both the central-tendency attraction under high uncertainty and the repulsive bias at high reliability, providing a unified, data-driven model of representation and inference.

3.1
Joint objective

The key idea is to embed the efficient-coding/Bayesian observer hypothesis into a three-player adversarial game consisting of an encoder
E
, a generator
G
, and a discriminator
D
. We optimize

\min_{E,G}\;\max_{D}\;\mathcal{L}_{\rm adv}(E,G,D)\;+\;\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm recon}(E,G)\,,

where

\mathcal{L}_{\rm adv}=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm data}}\bigl[D\bigl(x,\,E(x)\bigr)\bigr]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}\bigl[D\bigl(G(z),\,z\bigr)\bigr]\;+\;\gamma\;\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x},\hat{z}}\bigl[\bigl(\lVert\nabla D(\hat{x},\hat{z})\rVert_{2}-1\bigr)^{2}\bigr],

\mathcal{L}_{\rm recon}=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\rm data}}\bigl\lVert x-G\bigl(E(x)\bigr)\bigr\rVert_{1}.

Here,
D
is trained to maximize the Wasserstein-GP objective, with
\gamma
weighting the gradient-penalty term over interpolates
(\hat{x},\hat{z})
, while
E,G
jointly minimize the sum of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm adv}
and the
L_{1}
reconstruction cost. This setup (i) sculpts a latent code whose local Fisher information mirrors stimulus statistics, and (ii) yields an implicit posterior mapping for Bayesian-style decoding without an explicit likelihood.

3.2
Architecture and training details

Model Architecture.

Our system is composed of three modules: an encoder
E
, a generator
G
, and a discriminator
D
, trained in an adversarial inference framework. Both
E
and
G
are fully convolutional networks that map between 1-channel
32\times 32
Gabor images and latent codes
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\text{5}\times 1\times 1}
. The encoder
E
consists of five convolutional layers with increasing feature width and stride-based downsampling, while the generator
G
uses a symmetric architecture with transposed convolutions to upsample. The discriminator
D
jointly evaluates image-latent pairs via separate convolutional branches for
x
and
z
, followed by a joint discriminator head. Architectural details are provided in the Appendix.

Training Details.

We train all models using the Adam optimizer with
\beta_{1}=0.5
,
\beta_{2}=0.9
, and learning rates with
\{1.0\}\times 10^{-4}
. Each training run lasts for 20,000 iterations with batch size 64. The discriminator
D
is updated for 3 steps per iteration of the encoder-generator pair
(E,G)
, following a standard alternating optimization scheme. The latent vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{\text{5}\times 1\times 1}
is sampled from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}(-\sqrt{3},\sqrt{3})
to match unit variance. We use a dataset of 20,000 synthetically generated 1-channel Gabor patches, with bimodal orientation prior and variable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), implemented via convex mixing between clean signal and Gaussian noise.

3.3
Data Generation and Evaluation Protocol

We generate synthetic
32\times 32
grayscale Gabor patches with orientations
\theta
sampled from a bimodal wrapped Cauchy distribution centered at
0^{\circ}
and
90^{\circ}
, reflecting the prevalence of cardinal orientations in natural scenes. Each patch is corrupted by additive Gaussian noise, blended with the signal using a per-sample weighting factor
w_{\text{SNR}}\sim 1-\sqrt{1-u}
, where
u\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
, producing a continuous range of effective signal-to-noise ratios.

Each training epoch consists of 20,000 samples, and models are trained over 20,000 iterations using mini-batches of 64. We reserve a fixed held-out batch of noisy–clean pairs for qualitative evaluation.

4
Results

4.1
Central-tendency bias emerges under stimulus uncertainty

Figure
2
A shows reconstructed orientations from our model across three levels of input uncertainty, parameterized by
w_{\text{SNR}}
. When the input is clean (
w_{\text{SNR}}=1.0
), reconstructions closely follow the identity line, indicating accurate recovery. As the input becomes noisier (
w_{\text{SNR}}=0.2
and
0.0
), reconstructions become increasingly biased toward the cardinal axes (
0^{\circ},\pm 90^{\circ}
), forming a bimodal distribution regardless of true orientation. This pattern reveals a classic central-tendency bias: low-SNR stimuli are perceptually attracted toward prior peaks.

To interpret this effect in Bayesian terms, we simulated a symbolic Bayesian observer under varying levels of posterior precision
\kappa
, generating samples from the posterior for each
\theta
(Figure
2
B). As
\kappa
decreases—corresponding to higher uncertainty—the posteriors become broader and their samples exhibit similar repulsion from obliques and attraction to cardinals, qualitatively matching our model.

We quantified this match by fitting
\kappa
for each noise level such that the Bayesian posterior samples align with our model’s reconstruction distribution. For each
w_{\mathrm{SNR}}
, we minimized the negative log-likelihood of model reconstructions under a Bayesian posterior combining the learned prior and a von Mises likelihood,
p(m\mid\theta)\propto\exp(\kappa\cos(\theta-m))
. We first performed a coarse grid search over
\kappa
(log-spaced from
10^{-1}
to
10^{4}
), followed by local refinement using Brent’s method
(
Brent 2013
)
implemented in
scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar
. The resulting precision values increase monotonically with
w_{\mathrm{SNR}}
(Figure
2
D), confirming that our model implicitly modulates posterior precision in response to input uncertainty, and that the emergent bias patterns correspond to probabilistic inference shaped by a learned prior.

4.2
Repulsive bias emerges from latent noise and decision criteria

To examine how internal variability interacts with the model’s geometry, we artificially injected isotropic Gaussian noise into the latent space along the learned orientation manifold. This manifold, shown in Figure
3
A, allocates disproportionate representational space to cardinal orientations—wide spacing near
0^{\circ}
and
\pm 90^{\circ}
, and compressed spacing elsewhere—reflecting an implicit efficient code.

We sampled latent codes near a fixed orientation (e.g.,
27^{\circ}
) by adding zero-mean Gaussian noise within the tangent plane of the manifold (Figure
3
B), and decoded each sample through the generator. The resulting orientation estimates fan out from the stimulus direction, forming a skewed distribution with mass repelled from the cardinals (Figure
3
C). The expectation of these decoded estimates defines the model’s final judgement.

As shown in Figure
3
D, increasing latent noise variance systematically amplifies this repulsive bias: the mean estimate shifts away from the nearest cardinal orientation. In parallel, increasing the decision-rule exponent
p
in the loss function (i.e., computing the
L_{p}
mean of decoded samples) steepens the bias profile and brings the zero-crossings closer to the cardinals (Figure
3
E). These results demonstrate that both latent dispersion and the shape of the decision rule jointly determine the magnitude of perceptual bias.

4.3
Latent Fisher information reflects efficient coding of stimulus statistics

To test whether the model’s internal representation adheres to efficient coding principles, we examined how the encoder allocates representational precision across orientation. Specifically, we computed the Fisher information
I(\theta)
from the Jacobian of the encoder’s latent representation with respect to stimulus orientation. As shown in Figure
4
A, the square root of Fisher information
\sqrt{I(\theta)}
exhibits clear peaks at cardinal orientations (
0^{\circ},\pm 90^{\circ}
).

These peaks closely align with the empirical stimulus prior used during training, which was bimodal and concentrated near the cardinals. Figure
4
B overlays the estimated
\sqrt{I(\theta)}
with a wrapped-Cauchy fit to the training orientation histogram, confirming the efficient-coding prediction that precision tracks stimulus prevalence.

Importantly, this increased precision does not reflect a collapse of variability: Figure
4
C shows that reconstructions from high-precision regions remain diverse, preserving fine-grained stimulus distinctions. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the model learns to encode common orientations with greater fidelity, as predicted by resource-constrained efficient coding theories.

5
Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that a single generative adversarial inference (GAI) network can spontaneously recover the full spectrum of orientation bias phenomena that have challenged vision science for over a decade. Without any hand-engineered priors or explicit likelihood functions, the model naturally exhibits (i) attraction toward cardinal axes when sensory evidence is weak, (ii) a reversal to repulsion under strong evidence, and (iii) a latent Fisher information profile that mirrors the empirical stimulus distribution—an information-theoretic signature of efficient coding (Figure
4
).

This unified account bridges normative theory and learned representation: both the direction and magnitude of perceptual bias emerge as a consequence of data-driven encoding and decoding, not as assumptions baked into the model. In doing so, our framework overcomes a key limitation of symbolic Bayesian observer models, which reproduce the attraction–repulsion transition only by assuming the very priors and likelihood asymmetries they aim to explain. By contrast, our model *learns* those structures from data, and reveals how computational constraints on inference and representation naturally give rise to the biases observed in human perception.

5.1
From Norms to Networks: A Unified Generative Mechanism

Efficient coding theory predicts that more neural precision is allocated to frequently encountered stimuli—a principle supported by both neurophysiology and psychophysics. While recent work shows that gradient descent can induce such codes in deep networks
(
Benjamin et al. 2022
)
, it leaves open how these representations produce behavioral biases. Our Generative Adversarial Inference (GAI) model bridges this gap: its encoder learns efficient codes through data-driven training, and when paired with an adversarial generator-discriminator and decoded via an
L_{p}
loss, the system exhibits both attraction and repulsion biases as emergent properties. This instantiates the "generative-adversarial brain" hypothesis
(
Gershman 2019
)
in a form that is computationally grounded and directly testable with psychophysical data.

5.2
Computational insights into perceptual anomalies

Beyond modeling perceptual bias, the GAI framework offers a unified account of both normal and pathological perception. For example, the vividness of peripheral vision—despite reduced sensory resolution—may reflect generative inference, where noisy inputs are encoded into compressed latent codes and decoded via priors learned from environmental statistics
(
Gershman 2019
)
. The same architecture may also help explain distortions such as hallucinations or delusions. In our model, such extreme biases can arise from corrupted encoder or generator weights, or from malformed samples drawn from internal noise. These disruptions can shift decoded estimates toward implausible interpretations, even when inputs remain within a plausible range. This suggests that accurate and aberrant perception may both result from the same underlying inference machinery—differing only in the integrity of its components and the fidelity of its uncertainty handling.

5.3
Limitations and Pathways Forward

Our current model is trained on 1D Gabor stimuli, with an orientation manifold that emerges through learning rather than explicit circular constraints. Extending the framework to richer domains such as motion, color, or natural images will test the generality of the latent-noise-loss-power interaction. While posterior precision
\kappa
scales robustly with input SNR across seeds, a larger ensemble is needed to quantify variability. Lastly, our comparison to human data remains qualitative; incorporating empirical bias curves as external validation is a key goal for future work.

6
Broader Impact

This work shows how systematic perceptual biases can emerge from the geometry of learned latent representations and simple decoding rules. Such mechanisms likely operate not only in biological systems but also in generative AI and robot perception models that rely on similar encoder-decoder architectures.

A key implication is that different training environments can shape different latent geometries, leading to diverging interpretations of the same input. In the near future, robots trained under heterogeneous sensory priors may struggle to coordinate or communicate effectively, as their internal estimates of "what is seen" may systematically differ. Understanding how bias arises from internal noise and representational asymmetry can thus inform the design of more transparent, robust, and interoperable AI systems—especially in multi-agent settings.

7
Conclusion

We presented a generative inference model that unifies efficient coding and Bayesian reasoning within a single learned system. Without hand-specified priors or likelihoods, the model reproduces hallmark features of human perception: central-tendency attraction, bias reversal under certainty, and representational precision shaped by stimulus statistics.

These results demonstrate that perceptual biases can emerge not from explicit assumptions, but from the geometry of learned representations and the structure of inference itself. This perspective reframes biases not as failures, but as signatures of rational behavior under resource constraints.

Beyond modeling human perception, our framework provides a blueprint for analyzing and designing artificial systems—where similar biases may shape how generative models perceive, misperceive, and interact with the world.
