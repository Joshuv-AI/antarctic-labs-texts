The effects of using water flosser versus interdental brush on gingival health in patients with mandibular bonded retainer - a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zahra Masood, Mubassar Fida, Rashna Hoshang Sukhia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimEffective interdental cleaning is essential for maintaining periodontal health in patients with fixed orthodontic retainers. This study compared the efficacy of a water flosser and an interdental brush in improving periodontal health over six months.MethodsSeventy participants were randomized into water flosser (n = 35) and interdental brush (n = 35) groups. Periodontal indices including plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), bleeding index (BI), calculus index (CI), clinical attachment level (CAL), probing depth (PD), and marginal recession (MR) were assessed at baseline (T0), three months (T1), and six months (T2). Mann-Whitney U tests compared groups at each time point. Repeated measures ANOVA evaluated time, group, and interaction effects. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) determined the influence of demographic and clinical variables on outcomes.ResultsAt baseline, no significant differences were found between groups (p > 0.05). By T1 and T2, the water flosser group showed significantly lower PI, GI, CI (p < 0.001). Significant time × group interactions favored the water flosser for these parameters, while CAL and PD showed no significant between-group differences. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) revealed superior soft-tissue outcomes with water flossers and identified male gender and thin gingival biotype as predictors of poorer periodontal outcomes.ConclusionWater flossers offer superior improvement in soft-tissue periodontal indices compared to interdental brushes in fixed retainer patients. Age did not influence periodontal outcomes, while male gender was associated with higher plaque and gingival inflammation scores.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrial.Gov; Registration number: NCT07054203; June 25, 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08233-6.Keywords: Water Flosser, Interdental Brush, Periodontal Health, Bonded Retainer

Introduction

Retention is a critical phase of orthodontic therapy aimed at preserving the results achieved during active treatment. Following the removal of orthodontic appliances, a variety of retention strategies have been employed, including both removable and fixed appliances [1 Since their introduction in the 1970s, bonded fixed retainers, particularly in the mandibular anterior region, have gained substantial popularity due to their minimal reliance on patient compliance, discreet appearance, and their capacity to provide long term stability [2 Recent international surveys have shown that fixed retainers remain the most common retention method for the mandibular anterior region, reflecting clinician preference and perceived effectiveness for maintaining alignment during retention [3, 4].

Despite their benefits, fixed retainers have practical limitations, including technique sensitivity, increased chairside time, and bond failures. Concerns have also been raised about possible periodontal effects however, the available evidence for long-term periodontal harm (including compared with removable retainers) remains limited and inconsistent [5]. Fixed retainers have been shown to act as plaque retentive factors, increasing the risk of gingivitis, calculus accumulation, marginal recession (MR), and probing depth (PD) over time [6]. Moreover, because thin gingival biotype is considered more susceptible to recession under inflammatory or mechanical challenge, marginal recession is clinically relevant when evaluating oral hygiene strategies in the retention phase [7]. The bonding of retainers to multiple teeth often limits access for interdental cleaning tools, thereby impairing oral hygiene maintenance and increasing susceptibility to periodontal inflammation. While manual toothbrushes remain effective for cleaning buccal, lingual, and occlusal surfaces, they often fall short in maintaining interproximal hygiene [8]. A wide range of adjunctive tools, including dental floss, wooden sticks, rubber tip stimulators, interdental brushes, and single tufted brushes, have been developed to address this gap. Although dental floss has traditionally been recommended for interdental cleaning, contemporary evidence suggests that interdental brushes are frequently more effective in reducing interdental plaque and gingival inflammation when sufficient embrasure space is present; flossing remains a useful alternative for tight contacts, but it is technique-sensitive and frequently limited by patient adherence [9].

Water flossers offer a technology-assisted approach that utilizes pulsating water streams to dislodge debris and biofilm in hard-to-reach areas. Their ease of use, comfort, and ability to improve patient compliance make them an attractive alternative, especially for individuals with fixed orthodontic appliances [10]. Despite the growing body of evidence on adjunctive oral hygiene devices, the literature remains virtually silent on their application in individuals with bonded mandibular retainers, a patient group uniquely challenged by restricted interdental access and heightened plaque retention [11]. To bridge this critical knowledge gap, we conducted a randomized controlled trial directly comparing a water flosser with an interdental brush to enhance gingival health among adult patients with mandibular bonded retainer. By employing a comprehensive set of oral hygiene and periodontal parameters including plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), bleeding on probing (BOP), and calculus index (CI), this study delivers clinically applicable evidence intended to guide personalized oral hygiene strategies during the retention phase.

Materials & methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [12]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Review Committee (ERC) under the reference number (2023-8799-26054). The trial was registered in the Clinicaltrial.gov database under the registration number (registration number: NCT07054203; June 25, 2025). All participants were informed about the nature and purpose of the study and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study design

A parallel-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted at the department of Orthodontics, Aga Khan University Hospital Karachi, Pakistan over a 12-month period (July 2024 to July 2025). Seventy participants with mandibular bonded retainers were recruited and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the water flosser group (n = 35) or the interdental brush group (n = 35). Given the nature of the interventions, participant blinding was not possible, however, outcome assessments were performed by a calibrated examiner blinded to group allocation.

Participants

Participants were recruited on the day of appliance removal, prior to placement of a mandibular bonded retainer. All participants received a standardized mandibular fixed retainer fabricated from 0.0175-inch multistrand stainless steel wire, bonded to the lingual surfaces of the mandibular anterior teeth. Eligibility was restricted to adults aged 18 to 40 years who had completed active fixed orthodontic treatment and were scheduled to receive a mandibular bonded retainer; this age range was selected to avoid adolescent growth-related variability and to reduce age-associated periodontal and systemic confounding. Systemically healthy status was defined as no self-reported systemic disease and no regular long-term medication use, confirmed through a standardized medical history form. Good oral-hygiene compliance was defined as brushing twice daily and willingness to follow oral-hygiene instructions, assessed using a structured history. Participants were excluded if baseline screening examination revealed clinical features suggestive of periodontitis, such as periodontal pocketing with attachment loss and/or radiographic bone loss, as determined by the examiner. Medication-related exclusions were determined by clinician judgement for drugs known to influence gingival inflammation or gingival enlargement. Skeletal classification was documented at baseline to contextualize craniofacial morphology. Although skeletal pattern is not an outcome variable, it may be associated with dentoalveolar compensation and incisor inclination, which can influence anterior alignment and the local geometry around a bonded retainer; this could plausibly affect plaque stagnation and interdental access. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Fig. 1).

Randomization & allocation

A total of 70 participants who met the eligibility criteria were randomly assigned to one of the two intervention groups with a fixed block size of 10 in a 1:1 ratio. The allocation sequence was generated using a computer-based blocked randomization tool (Sealed Envelope Ltd., London, UK) [13]. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent researcher not involved in the participant assessment or intervention. The envelopes were opened only after baseline data collection, ensuring that neither recruitment nor baseline assessment could be influenced by prior knowledge of the upcoming group allocation, thereby minimizing selection bias. Given the visible and functional differences between the interventions, participant blinding was not feasible, however, outcome assessments were conducted by a calibrated examiner blinded to group allocation to minimize measurement bias.

Intervention

Following baseline periodontal assessment (T0), all participants received full-mouth professional scaling as part of standard care to standardize oral conditions prior to initiation of the assigned oral hygiene intervention. Participants were randomly allocated to one of two intervention arms: the Water Flosser group or the Interdental Brush group. Those in the Water Flosser group were provided with a portable oral irrigator (Shenzhen Feiyu Bo trading co., ltd., Shenzhen, China) featuring three operating modes, soft, normal, and pulse (Fig. 2). The water flosser reservoir capacity was 200 mL. Participants were instructed to fill the reservoir once per session and to use the entire reservoir for irrigation; therefore, approximately 200 mL of water was used per use. Participants were instructed to use the device once daily in the evening in normal mode, with tip 1 and irrigating all surfaces of mandibular anterior teeth, after routine toothbrushing and using plain lukewarm water for six months. We standardized device readiness by providing units fully charged at baseline and instructing participants to recharge regularly throughout the study. Detailed instructions on proper usage including nozzle positioning and angulation were delivered verbally. Written instructions (manufacturer leaflet) were also provided for home reference. Compliance was assessed at each follow-up visit through participant self-reporting.

Water flosser

Participants in the Interdental Brush group received Dandis Interdental Brushes (Foshan Seeddent technology Co., LTD., China) with a brush diameter of 0.7 mm, selected to fit the typical interdental spacing in the mandibular anterior region. These brushes featured soft bristles and ergonomically angled handles to facilitate effective plaque removal around the bonded mandibular retainer. Participants were instructed to use the brush once daily in the evening following routine toothbrushing. For standardization, interdental brushes were inserted from the facial aspect only and moved gently back and forth across each interdental space using approximately 3–4 in-and-out strokes per site. Participants were instructed not to insert the brush from the lingual aspect, not to angle the brush apically or toward the gingival margin, and not to attempt subgingival insertion beneath the bonded retainer to minimize tissue trauma and maintain a consistent cleaning technique across participant. Usage instructions were provided both verbally and in written form, and adherence was monitored through self-reported compliance over the six-month period.

Standardized toothbrushing instructions were provided to all participants, including the use of fluoridated toothpaste, a soft-bristle toothbrush, and a vertical brushing technique. However, the specific brand or type of toothbrush and toothpaste, as well as dietary habits and other behaviors that could influence plaque accumulation, were not standardized or objectively monitored during the study period.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was the assessment of gingival health using bleeding on probing (BOP) index. Secondary outcomes included plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), calculus index (CI), probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), and marginal recession (MR) measured at three time points: baseline (T0), three months (T1), and six months (T2) on the permanent mandibular anterior teeth. Measurement was taken at six specific sites per tooth: mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, and distolingual.

Marginal recession was assessed to examine potential differences in soft-tissue response related to gingival biotypes. The influence of gingival biotype on all clinical parameters was also evaluated. Gingival biotype was determined at baseline using the probe visibility method, in which a periodontal probe was inserted into the gingival sulcus at the mid-facial aspect of the maxillary central incisors. The gingiva was classified as thin when the probe was visible through the tissue and thick when the probe was not visible. This assessment was included because a thin gingival phenotype has been associated with greater susceptibility to soft-tissue changes, including marginal recession, under mechanical and inflammatory challenge; this is clinically relevant in orthodontic patients [14].

All indices were recorded by a single calibrated examiner to ensure consistency.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated with Openepi software by using the findings of Lyle et al. [22] who reported the mean values of BOP at baseline T0 for proximal surfaces at four weeks as (0.16 ± 0.046 mm) and for marginal lingual surfaces at four weeks interval T1 as (0.13 ± 0.043 mm). Assuming a correlation of 0.5, keeping α as 0.05, the power of study as 80% and confidence level at 95%. The calculated sample size was 70 N (35 in each group).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between the two intervention groups at each time point. For intra-group comparisons across the three time points, repeated measures ANOVA was utilized. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Effect sizes were expressed as generalized eta squared (η²₍G₎). For each parameter, both main effects (time, group) and interaction effects (time × group) were examined. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure were used to assess the influence of demographic and clinical variables (age, gender, skeletal class, gingival biotype, and intervention group) on periodontal outcomes over time. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Mann-Whitney U test

At baseline (T0), no statistically significant differences were observed between the water flosser and interdental brush groups across all assessed parameters, confirming initial group equivalence (p > 0.05). By three months (T1), the water flosser group exhibited significantly lower PI, BI, CI, and GI compared to the interdental brush group (p < 0.001 for all). These differences remained consistent and statistically significant at six months (T2), reflecting sustained improvements in soft-tissue periodontal health among water flosser users. Clinically, the magnitude of reduction across plaque and gingival inflammation measures indicates meaningful improvement in oral hygiene status over follow-up. In contrast, CAL and MR scores remained low and comparable between groups at all time points (p > 0.05), and PD-threshold values were minimal, indicating periodontal stability with no evidence of deterioration over the six-month period. Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis

Across all models, age did not significantly predict any periodontal outcome, although a marginal trend toward reduced plaque levels with increasing age was observed (β = -0.0025, p = 0.089). Gender emerged as a significant predictor for several soft-tissue indices. Male participants showed significantly higher values for PI (β = 0.0454, p = 0.0492), BI (β = 0.0508, p = 0.031), and GI (β = 0.0589, p = 0.0073), suggesting a poorer inflammatory profile compared to females. No gender-related differences were found for CAL, MR, or PD. Skeletal class did not demonstrate a significant association with any periodontal index indicating limited influence of sagittal malocclusion on soft-tissue outcomes in this cohort. Gingival biotype was significantly associated only with clinical attachment level (CAL), participants with a thin biotype demonstrated greater attachment loss than those with a thick biotype (β = 0.0574, p = 0.014).

Participants in the water flosser group demonstrated significantly better outcomes across nearly all periodontal parameters. Compared to the interdental brush group, the water flosser group had significantly lower scores in PI (β = -0.1825, p < 0.001), BI (β = -0.1849, p < 0.001), GI (β = -0.1802, p < 0.001), CI (β = -0.1831, p < 0.001), and MR (β = -0.041, p = 0.013). However, no significant group differences were observed for CAL (β = 0.0058, p = 0.781) or PD (β = -0.0005, p = 0.979), indicating that the intervention improved plaque control and gingival inflammatory outcomes, while CAL and PD remained stable over the study duration. Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the comparative effectiveness of two widely utilized interdental cleaning modalities: water flossers, which employ pulsating hydrodynamic action, and interdental brushes, which rely on direct mechanical contact in patients with bonded orthodontic retainers [23]. Both interventions resulted in statistically and clinically significant improvements in periodontal parameters over six months. However, water flossers yielded superior outcomes across multiple indices, including PI, BI, GI, and CI underscoring their enhanced efficacy in managing soft tissue periodontal health in anatomically constrained sites. Because professional scaling was performed immediately after baseline assessment, differences in calculus index scores during follow-up should be interpreted as variations in calculus re-accumulation rather than direct mechanical removal by the hygiene devices. Measures reflecting deeper periodontal stability e.g., CAL, PD-threshold, and MR remained largely unchanged, which was expected in a periodontally stable cohort over a six-month hygiene intervention.

These findings are consistent with prior controlled studies. A randomized trial in adults with moderate gingivitis demonstrated greater reductions in BOP and GI with water flossers compared to interdental brushes, however, while their assessment was limited to a four-week period, our six-month follow-up demonstrates that this advantage is sustained over a longer timeframe [24]. Similarly, orthodontic cohorts have shown improved plaque and bleeding control with oral irrigators over conventional flossing [25]. A randomized crossover trial by Gänzer et al. [26] in adolescent orthodontic patients found no significant difference in cleaning efficacy between an oral irrigator with microburst technology and dental floss, although both improved hygiene indices with proper instruction. However, differences in device mechanism and study population limit direct comparability with pulsating water flossers used in adult post-orthodontic patients with bonded retainers. In contrast, the present study demonstrated superior outcomes with a pulsating water flosser compared with interdental brushes. This finding is consistent with recent evidence showing that pulsating oral irrigation can produce greater reductions in bleeding and gingival inflammation than interdental brushes, supporting improved control of interproximal plaque and gingival inflammation with reduced technique sensitivity [24].

Age did not significantly influence periodontal outcomes in this study, including CAL and PD. While age is traditionally associated with increased periodontal risk, our findings align with recent trials showing that the short-term efficacy of oral irrigators is largely age independent. A randomized study in orthodontic patients found comparable improvements across age groups, and reviews have similarly reported uniform benefits of water flossers regardless of age. This suggests that in periodontally stable cohorts, particularly younger individuals, age may not modulate short term responses to nonsurgical plaque control interventions [27].

In the present study, male participants demonstrated significantly higher scores in PI, GI, BI, and CI compared to females. These findings suggest that, across all time points, male patients exhibited poorer soft-tissue periodontal health. This is consistent with existing epidemiological literature, which has consistently shown that men are more likely to engage in suboptimal oral hygiene practices and are at higher risk for periodontal disease progression [28]. Nonetheless, recent evidence indicates that the periodontal benefits of adjunctive devices such as water flossers are consistent across sexes [29]. Our findings imply that while baseline disparities exist, adjunctive interventions can mitigate gender-based differences, offering equivalent therapeutic outcomes over short durations. These insights highlight the importance of behavioral reinforcement in male patients and may guide future efforts in tailored oral hygiene education.

Skeletal malocclusion, specifically Class II versus Class I, did not significantly affect periodontal indices. This suggests that sagittal skeletal relationships do not independently influence periodontal health or the efficacy of adjunctive cleaning modalities. A recent cross-sectional study using the Index of Complexity, Outcome, and Need similarly reported no significant association between malocclusion severity and periodontal outcomes [30]. Although case-based literature involving surgically managed skeletal discrepancies suggests possible periodontal improvement with combined therapy, such contexts differ markedly from our nonsurgical, periodontally stable cohort [31]. Accordingly, skeletal class may have limited relevance in short term hygiene interventions in the absence of active disease. These findings are important for orthodontic-periodontal collaboration and suggest that routine hygiene outcomes may not necessitate additional periodontal interventions based on skeletal diagnosis alone.

Gingival biotype was significantly associated with clinical attachment level but not with other periodontal indices. Participants with thin biotypes exhibited greater attachment loss, in line with prior studies indicating susceptibility of thin tissues to recession and breakdown [32]. However, plaque, bleeding, inflammation, and other soft tissue parameters improved uniformly across biotypes. This suggests that while tissue thickness may modulate structural outcomes such as CAL, inflammatory indices respond similarly to hygiene interventions irrespective of phenotype. These findings are consistent with longitudinal studies showing no difference in soft tissue healing between phenotypes following orthodontic treatment, although long term susceptibility to structural degradation may remain greater in thin biotypes. Furthermore, spectrophotometric analysis has demonstrated that inflamed gingiva in thin phenotypes appears redder, though resolution of inflammation remains similar across biotypes, reinforcing the idea that visual assessment must be interpreted cautiously [33].

This trial benefits from a robust randomized design, repeated measures analysis, and the use of multiple validated clinical indices to provide a comprehensive evaluation of periodontal changes. Homogeneity at baseline and standardized hygiene protocols minimize confounding, enhancing internal validity. Importantly, the focus on a clinically relevant yet understudied population individuals with bonded mandibular retainers adds practical significance, given their heightened risk of plaque retention in interproximal regions. The generalizability of our findings is further strengthened by the relatively long six-month follow-up duration, the inclusion of a demographically diverse sample, and adherence to strict inclusion criteria, which ensured baseline periodontal comparability.

Despite the use of randomization, residual baseline differences between groups cannot be entirely excluded. Periodontal outcomes are influenced by factors such as sex, gingival biotype, and baseline oral hygiene status, and any imbalance in these characteristics may have affected the comparative response to the interventions [7]. This may partly explain the relatively lower effectiveness observed for interdental brushes and should be considered when interpreting the results. Future trials may benefit from stratified randomization or adjustment for key baseline variables to further reduce this potential source of bias. Nonetheless, certain limitations warrant consideration. The six-month duration, while adequate for detecting short term clinical changes, does not capture long term attachment preservation or relapse. Additionally, the absence of microbiological profiling and patient reported outcomes such as device usability and satisfaction limits insight into behavioral adherence and subjective experience. Moreover, although standardized oral hygiene instructions were provided, self-reported compliance was not objectively validated, which may introduce performance bias. Although participants received standardized oral hygiene instructions, including soft-bristle and vertical brushing techniques, toothbrushing behavior was not formally standardized or monitored during the study period. Therefore, any minor variation in MR or CAL cannot be attributed specifically to the interdental cleaning devices. Importantly, neither intervention demonstrated clinically meaningful deterioration in MR or CAL over the six-month follow-up, consistent with periodontal safety. Published evidence further supports the safety of water flossing on gingival and epithelial tissues across pressure settings [34]. The use of a standardized interdental brush size was intended to reduce inter-participant variability and improve internal validity; however, individualized sizing may yield greater clinical benefit and should be explored in future studies. Future studies should incorporate longer follow ups, microbial assessments, and quality of life measures to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of adjunctive interdental devices.

The findings of this study suggest that water flossers may provide superior improvements in soft tissue periodontal health compared to interdental brushes in patients with bonded orthodontic retainers. Clinically, the water flosser group demonstrated a sustained reduction in plaque- and bleeding-related indices from baseline through six months, consistent with meaningful improvement in day-to-day periodontal health in an anatomically constrained setting. Given the technique sensitivity of conventional interdental cleaning around bonded retainers, the relative ease of use and access afforded by water flossing supports its consideration as an adjunct within routine oral hygiene protocols, particularly for individuals with limited manual dexterity or restricted interdental access. The six-month follow-up, the specific focus on retainer patients, and the comprehensive evaluation of multiple clinical indices provide meaningful insights to inform adjunctive oral hygiene recommendations.

Conclusions

Water flossers demonstrated superior efficacy over interdental brushes in improving soft-tissue periodontal health among patients with bonded retainers. Age did not influence periodontal outcomes, whereas male gender was associated with higher plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation. Thin gingival biotype correlated with greater clinical attachment loss.
