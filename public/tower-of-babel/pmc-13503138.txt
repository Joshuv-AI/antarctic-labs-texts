Diosgenin enhances transplanted human oligodendrocyte precursor cell-mediated myelination in preterm white matter injury by inhibiting matrix metalloproteinase-9–driven TGF-β/Smad2/3 signaling
Authors: Qingwei Lai, Xiaoyu Yao, Linkang Bai, Xinyu Cao, Yangxin Zhao, Chunyu Wang, Zuo Luan, Zhaowei Feng, Ruiqin Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreterm white matter injury (PWMI) is a major cause of long-term motor and cognitive disability, and there are no therapies that directly promote oligodendrocyte-lineage maturation. Here, we identify the natural steroid sapogenin diosgenin as a potent pro-myelinating modulator for transplanted human oligodendrocyte precursor cells (hOPCs) using a hypoxia–ischemia mouse model of PWMI, together with in vitro microglia–OPC co-culture systems. Diosgenin crosses the blood–brain barrier and promotes the differentiation of both transplanted hOPCs and endogenous OPCs into myelinating oligodendrocytes, thereby accelerating remyelination and improving neurological function. Mechanistic investigations reveal that diosgenin inhibits MMP-9/2 activity. This suppression blunts TGFβ–Smad2/3 signaling in microglia and OPCs, which in turn mitigates microglia activation and upregulates myelin-associated proteins. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized MMP-9–TGFβ–Smad2/3 axis controlling hOPC-mediated myelin repair, and establish diosgenin as a promising adjunct therapeutic agent for cell-based strategies against PWMI and other white matter disorders.Keywords: Diosgenin, Preterm white matter injury (PWMI), Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), Myelination, TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling

Introduction

Preterm white matter injury (PWMI) is a major risk factor for severe long-term neurodevelopmental disabilities, including cerebral palsy, motor deficits, and/or cognitive impairments that can persist into young adulthood [1]. Hypoxia-ischemia (HI), inflammation, and numerous other poorly defined factors contribute to the progression of PWMI [2]. During myelination, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) require an exceptionally high energy supply to differentiate into myelinating oligodendrocytes and synthesize myelin lipids. Even a mild ischemic episode can cause significant damage to OPCs due to their low tolerance for energy failure, which explains why these cells are highly vulnerable to HI and inflammatory insults [3]. It is therefore hypothesized that the depletion of OPCs or the subsequent failure of their differentiation into mature myelinating oligodendrocytes leads to hypomyelination in PWMI [4].

Although therapeutic hypothermia is established as a standard treatment to improve brain injury in term infants with perinatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), fewer than 20% of infants benefit from this intervention, and the majority continue to experience long-term disabilities [5,6]. Growing evidence suggests that cell-based therapies represent a promising alternative for neonatal WMI by supplying trophic support and replacing lost cells. Studies using HI-induced neonatal WMI rodent models have demonstrated the potential of OPC transplantation as a cell replacement strategy [[7], [8], [9]]. However, the clinical translation of OPC transplantation remains challenging due to the inefficient differentiation of OPCs into mature oligodendrocytes within the inflammatory microenvironment following WMI [10,11]. Our previous study revealed that the chemical drug ZJU-37 effectively promotes the differentiation of exogenous human OPCs (hOPCs) into oligodendrocytes, leading to improved myelination and cognitive function in a rat model of PWMI [10]. Therefore, enhancing the differentiation capacity of transplanted OPCs is crucial for advancing cell-based therapies for WMI.

Diosgenin, a steroidal sapogenin, has demonstrated considerable potential for the treatment of various disorders, including nervous system diseases, cancer, diabetes, arthritis, asthma, and cardiovascular conditions [[12], [13], [14]]. It has also exhibited promising pharmacological effects in models of Alzheimer’s disease, epilepsy, depression, and cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Notably, diosgenin has been shown to strongly promote OPC differentiation, reduce neuroinflammation and axonal degeneration, and enhance myelin recovery in a mouse model of demyelination [[19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, our recent studies revealed that diosgenin treatment alleviated epileptic seizures in both pentylenetetrazole-kindled and pilocarpine-induced epilepsy mouse models by promoting myelination [18,22]. Accumulating evidence supports the ability of diosgenin to facilitate OPC maturation and myelin repair in the brain. However, it remains unclear whether diosgenin enhances the differentiation and myelination of transplanted human OPCs in a PWMI animal model.

Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ1) signaling is essential for myelination and myelin integrity. Its disruption between microglia and oligodendrocytes compromises myelin structure [23]. In preterm infants, elevated TGFβ1 in cerebrospinal fluid after hemorrhagic hydrocephalus correlates with worsened pathology and outcomes. Inhibiting TGFβ1 signaling reduces disease severity in demyelination models [24]. Perinatal HI rat models show increased TGFβ1 level, and blocking the TGFβ1 type I receptor improves neurological function and preserves hippocampal integrity [[25], [26], [27]]. In vitro, TGFβ1 dose-dependently inhibits OPC maturation, while its inhibition promotes in vivo myelination [16]. These findings support targeting TGFβ1 as a therapeutic strategy for PWMI. Notably, diosgenin has been shown to inhibit TGFβ1 signaling and suppress epithelial-mesenchymal transition in a model of pulmonary fibrosis [28], suggesting that it may also enhance myelination and mitigate white matter injury in PWMI by inhibiting TGFβ1. Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) serves as a key activator of TGFβ signaling by cleaving the Latent-Associated Peptide (LAP) complex and extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby releasing active TGFβ cytokine. Herein, mechanistically, we aim to investigate whether diosgenin inhibits TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling through targeted suppression of MMP-9 activity, thereby upregulating myelin-associated protein expression and promoting OPC differentiation.

Methods

Regents and materials

Restriction enzymes were obtained from Thermo Scientific. The DNA Gel Extraction Kit and Plasmid Miniprep Kit were sourced from Shanghai Sangon Biotech. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12), Knockout Serum Replacement (KSR), GlutaMAX, non-essential amino acids (NEAA), β-mercaptoethanol, and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) were purchased from Gibco (Life Technologies Corporation, Grand Island, NY, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). The pLV-Flag vector and lentiviral helper plasmids were supplied by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. GlutaMAX, Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, puromycin and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA), epidermal growth factor (EGF), neurotrophin 3 (NT3), triiodothyronine (T3), and B27 were obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Induction of human OPCs from HESC-H9 cells

The HESC-H9 line was obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. In this study, H9 cell differentiation was performed following the workflow illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1A as previously reported [29,30]. Briefly, to obtain embryonic bodies (EBs), Olig2-overexpressing H9 colonies were trypsinized into single-cell suspensions. Cells were first cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1 μmol/L puromycin, 1% bFGF, and 20% KSR for 4 days, followed by an additional 9 days of culture in differentiation medium (DIM) containing 10 ng/mL bFGF to facilitate EB formation.

On day 14, harvested EBs were dissociated using TrypLE reagent to generate neural precursor cells (NPCs). The dissociated single cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL bFGF, 1% EGF, and 1% B27 for 14 days. On day 28, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM/F12 containing 10 ng/mL bFGF, puromycin, and 1% B27, and NPCs were maintained under this condition for 20 days to induce the generation of pre-OPCs.

To further promote pre-OPC differentiation, cells were cultured from day 48 in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL puromycin, 10 ng/mL bFGF, 10 ng/mL PDGF-AA, 1% T3, and 1% NT3 until day 55. Subsequently, N2, 5% FBS and T3 were used to induce the terminal differentiation of pre-OPCs into mature oligodendrocytes.

Treatment of hOPCs with rhMMP-9 and MMP-9 inhibitor

hOPCs were treated with recombinant active human MMP-9 (rhMMP-9, 30 ng/mL, PF024-5UG, Merck) and MMP-9 inhibitor SB-3CT (10 μM, HY-12354, MedChemExpress) or DMSO in equal concentrations (0.3%) as vehicle control.

BV2 microglia culture and treatment

BV2 microglial cells (obtained from the National Biological Resource Centre, Manesar, Haryana, India) were cultured following previously described methods [31]. To evaluate the effect of diosgenin on MMP-9 expression in vitro, BV2 cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; S1732, Beyotime) at various concentrations (10, 100, 500, and 1000 ng/mL) to stimulate MMP-9 production. SB-3CT (10 μM) was used as a positive control. A schematic of this experimental design is presented in Fig. 7B.

Diosgenin suppresses MMP-9 activity in vitro. (A) MMP-9 levels in the culture supernatant of BV2 microglia were measured using ELISA, and cytotoxicity was examined using LDH assays after stimulation with LPS at various concentrations (10, 100, 500, and 1000 ng/mL). (B–D) Administration of diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT significantly reduced relative MMP-9 activity in the supernatant of BV2 cells compared to the group treated with 100 ng/mL LPS. (E–G) Relative MMP-9 activity was decreased in the supernatant of OPCs following treatment with diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT compared to the rMMP-9-only group. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 4 per group; ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Hypoxia-ischemia induced PWMI mouse model

The neonatal HI injury model was established in P3 mouse pups according to our previously validated protocol [32]. Briefly, cryoanesthesia was induced by placing pups on an ice bed for 5.25 ± 0.38 min, with continuous temperature monitoring via a subcutaneous microprobe inserted in the interscapular region. Throughout the procedure, surface and core temperatures were maintained at 8.2 ± 0.3 °C and 22.5 ± 0.7 °C, respectively. Under microscopic guidance, the right common carotid artery was isolated and permanently ligated using an 8-0 nylon suture. After surgery, pups were gradually rewarmed at a controlled rate of 0.5 °C/min using a feedback-regulated heating system until normothermia (36.5 ± 0.2 °C) was achieved. Following a 2-h recovery period with the dam, pups were exposed to hypoxia in a temperature-controlled chamber (36.0 ± 0.5 °C) infused with 8% O2/92% N2 for 2 h. After an additional 60-min maternal reunion, selected pups received sevoflurane intervention: 3.2% sevoflurane (MAC 1.0 in neonates) delivered in 30% O2/70% N2 carrier gas for 2 h via a calibrated vaporizer. All pups remained with their dams until weaning. Humane endpoints were strictly observed in accordance with the 3R principles to minimize distress. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Ethical Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No. 202105T012; Date: 12 May 2021).

hOPCs transplantation and diosgenin treatment

OPCs prepared for transplantation were harvested on days 42–45 of the differentiation protocol. The purified cell population exhibited high expression of OPC markers, with a PDGFRα (CD140a)-positive rate of 92.19%, an NG2-positive rate of 78.3%, and an early pre-OPC marker A2B5-positive rate of 85.56%. The proportion of TRA-1-60+/TRA-1-81+ undifferentiated cells were below 0.1 % in our final graft-ready pre-OPC population, consistent with widely accepted pre-clinical benchmarks for hPSC-derived cell products.

Seven days after HI injury, model mice were anesthetized using 3% isoflurane (Shandong Keyuan Pharmaceutical Co., Jinan, China) for induction and maintained under 1.5–2% isoflurane for the duration of the procedure. The animals were then fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A suspension of 1 × 105 cells in 2 μL of sterile Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) was transplanted into each HI mouse. The injection site was positioned 1.0 mm posterior to the bregma, 0.6 mm lateral to the lambdoid suture on the left side, and at a depth of 1.8 mm. The injection was performed at a rate of 0.2 μL/min, and the needle was left in place for an additional 10 min before withdrawal. Sham group animals received an equivalent volume of sterile HBSS alone. For immunosuppression, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of cyclosporine A (5 mg/kg,Sigma) starting three days prior to cell transplantation and continuing for four weeks. Thereafter, cyclosporine A was administered via drinking water at a concentration of 20 μg/mL until perfusion. The cyclosporine dosage was appropriately reduced or temporarily discontinued if signs of toxicity were observed, such as sustained weight loss, eyelid redness, or nasal lesions. According to our previous study [22], diosgenin was administered orally at doses of 40 mg/kg or 80 mg/kg following OPC transplantation. The control group received an equivalent volume of saline.

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry

Mouse brain tissue (30 mg) from Sham + Dio (80 mg/kg), HI + Dio (40 mg/kg), and HI + Dio (80 mg/kg) groups was homogenized in 50 μL of ddH2O by sonication for 30 min. Each sample was then mixed with 200 μL of methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant (200 μL) was evaporated to dryness, reconstituted in 40 μL of methanol, and centrifuged again under the same conditions. Finally, 30 μL of the resulting supernatant was collected for analysis. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) was performed using a Waters Acquity Class I UPLC system connected to a Waters XEVO TQD mass spectrometer. Separation was achieved on a Waters Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1 % formic acid and (B) methanol with 0.1 % formic acid, delivered in a linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min.

AAV-MMP9 treatment

After HI injury, model mice (P7) were placed under hypothermic anesthesia and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus for targeted intrafascicular injection. Using the bregma as a reference point, the coordinates for the corpus callosum were determined based on a developmental mouse brain atlas (typically approximately 1.0 mm posterior, 0.5 mm lateral, and 1.8 mm deep to the bregma). An AAV-MMP9 viral suspension (titer ≥1 × 1012 vg/mL) was slowly infused at a rate of 50 nL/min using a Hamilton microsyringe, with an injection volume of 100–200 nL. The needle was left in place for 5 min before gradual withdrawal. After the procedure, the pups were warmed for recovery, rubbed with maternal bedding to minimize foreign odor, and returned to the home cage, where they were routinely housed until the desired time points for subsequent analysis.

rhTGFβ1 treatment

Following HI injury, P7 model mice received intracerebroventricular (ICV) injections of recombinant human TGFβ1 (rhTGFβ1; BioLegend) under induced hypothermia with stereomicroscopic guidance. Using the bregma as the anatomical landmark, the rhTGFβ1injection site was determined at coordinates 1.0 mm posterior and 1.0 mm lateral to the midline. A Hamilton microsyringe was inserted perpendicularly to a depth of 2.0 mm, and a solution of TGFβ1 reconstituted in sterile artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF)-calculated to deliver a dose of 4 μg/kg and 8 μg/kg in a volume of 1–2 μL was infused slowly. The needle was retained in place for 1 min before gradual withdrawal. This procedure was repeated every other day for two weeks. Following each injection, pups were promptly transferred to a 37 °C heating pad for recovery and gently rubbed with soiled bedding from the dam to minimize human scent before being returned to the home cage. All manipulations were carried out under strict aseptic conditions. A vehicle-injected control group, administered sterile aCSF alone, was included to account for non-specific effects.

Open field test

The open field test was conducted 11 weeks after OPC transplantation according to previous study [33]. The total distance traveled, number of entries into the center zone, and time spent in the center zone were analyzed.

Morris water maze test

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Morris water maze (MWM) paradigm according to previously described [34].

Immunohistochemistry and immunocytochemistry staining

For Immunohistochemistry staining, following deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (1%, 50 mg/kg), mice were subjected to transcardial perfusion with 0.9% saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4, 4 °C) at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The brains were aseptically removed and immersion-fixed in fresh 4% PFA/PB solution for 12 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution prepared in 0.01 M PBS until they sank to the bottom. The brains were then embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Leica) and coronally sectioned into 20 μm-thick slices using a cryostat microtome. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated for 2 h with secondary antibodies. The specificity of immunostaining was verified by omitting the primary antibody. Finally, the slides were mounted using Mowiol containing DAPI (Sigma, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

For immunocytofluorescence, cells were fixed in 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton™ X-100, and blocked with 10% donkey serum for 1 h. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Negative control samples received PBS instead of primary antibodies. Following this, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. All images were acquired using a Zeiss Axioskop 40 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

The primary antibodies includes Flag-tag (Rabbit, 1:500, Abcam), SOX2 (mouse, 1:500, proteintech), Nkx2.2 (Mouse, 1:500, Abcam), A2B5 (Rabbit, 1:500, Abcam), Platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRα, Rabbit, 1:100, Santa Cruz), Olig2 (Goat, 1:100, Santa Cruz), O4 (Mouse,1:200, Abcam), Myelin basic protein (MBP, Mouse, 1:1000, Covance Inc.), MBP (mouse, 1:100, Santa Cruz), Cyclic nucleotide 3′ phosphodiesterase (CNP, Rabbit,1:100, Santa Cruz), Myelin-associated glycoprotein (MAG, mouse, 1:100, Santa Cruz), STEM121(mouse monoclonal antibody specific for human cytoplasmic marker, 1:500, Takara Bio USA, Inc.), SMI-32 (mouse, 1:1000, Covance), Iba-1(mouse, 1:100, Santa Cruz), PDGFRα (mouse, 1:100, Abbkine). The secondary antibodies used were: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor ®555 conjugated, goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor ®555 (1:500, Invitrogen), Rhodamine conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (H + L), FITC conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), FITC conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:200, Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc.).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

To evaluate the ultrastructure of the myelin sheath, ultra-thin sections (60–70 nm thick) of the corpus callosum were prepared as previously described [34]. The g-ratio (defined as the ratio of the inner axonal diameter to the total outer fiber diameter, d/D) and the number of myelinated axons were quantified.

Western blot

The animals were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane (oxygen flow rate: 1 L/min; Shandong Keyuan Pharmaceutical Co., Jinan, China) for 3 min. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Protein extraction and membrane transfer were performed according to previously described methods [34]. The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-MBP (1:500, rabbit, proteintech), anti-TIMP-1 (1:500, mouse, sc-21734, Santa Cruz), anti-MMP-9 (1:500, mouse, sc-21733, Santa Cruz), p-Smad2/3(1:1000,Rabbit,80427-2-RR,proteintech), Smad 2/3 (1:2000,Rabbit,12570-1-AP,proteintech), p21 (1:1000,Rabbit,2947,Cell Signaling Technology), Cyclin D1 (1:1000,Rabbit,2978,Cell Signaling Technology), ID4 (1:1000,Rabbit,21803-1-AP,proteintech), MYRF (1:500,Rabbit,PA5-113555,Thermo Fisher Scientific). After washing, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies Dylight 800 (1:500,Goat,A23910,A23920,Abbkine) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey infrared scanner (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA), and band densities were quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.52).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were conducted using the AutoDock 9 software suite. The crystal structures of the target proteins (MMP-9: PDB ID 1l6j; MMP-2: PDB ID 1qib) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Protein preparation was performed with AutoDock Tools 9.0, which included removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of Kollman united atom charges. The three-dimensional structure of the ligand (diosgenin, Dio) was constructed and energy-minimized using Avogadro software to obtain the lowest energy conformation. Gasteiger partial charges were assigned to the ligand, and its rotatable bonds were defined using AutoDock Tools. A grid box for docking was generated with the center coordinates set at (MMP-9: x = 36.940, y = 39.126, z = 34.943; MMP-2: x = 72.872, y = 27.271, z = 20.779). The dimensions were set to 50 × 50 × 50 grid points with a default spacing of 0.375 Å, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the active binding site. Docking calculations were performed using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm (LGA) with 100 independent runs to ensure sufficient conformational sampling. All resulting poses were clustered according to their predicted binding free energy (ΔG), and the conformation with the most favorable binding energy from the largest cluster was selected as the representative binding mode for further analysis.

Cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate cytotoxicity, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was measured using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The protein levels of TGF-β1 in mouse brain tissue, as well as TGF-β1 and MMP-9 in BV2 cell supernatant, were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (mouse TGFβ1 ELISA Kit, BES0038K, BIOESN; mouse MMP-9 ELISA Kit, Bsk12013, BIOSS) according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Briefly, mice were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS to eliminate blood contamination. Whole brains were then dissected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Tissue samples were homogenized in 10 vol (w/v) of RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) using a motorized homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected, avoiding the lipid layer. No acid activation step was performed to ensure specific detection of active TGF-β1. Optical density (OD) values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using an Epoch™ 2 Microplate Reader. Sample concentrations were determined by interpolation from the standard curve.

Conditioned medium transfer assay

Conditioned medium (CM) harvested from differently treated BV2 cells, as previously described in our work [31], was applied to hESC-derived OPCs. Following CM stimulation, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect PDGFRα and MBP expression. In parallel, the protein levels of TGFβRII and phosphorylated Smad2/3 were examined via Western blot.

Gelatin zymography

MMP-9 activity in brain homogenates and conditioned media from BV2 and OPC cultures was assessed using gelatinase zymography as previously described [35,36]. Briefly, Samples containing 15 μg protein were diluted in 5 × sample buffer to 1 × and loaded onto a 7.5% acrylamide gel copolymerized with 0.1% gelatin. Electrophoresis was performed at 110 V for 2 h at 4 °C. Gels were then washed twice for 30 min in washing buffer (2.5% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 μM ZnCl2) with agitation, followed by incubation in reaction buffer (1.0% Triton X-100, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 μM ZnCl2) for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, gels were stained with Coomassie blue for 1 h and destained (40% methanol, 10% acetic acid, 50% water) until clear bands were visible. Gel images were acquired using a LI-COR Odyssey scanner. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) based on lysis area density.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.4.1, with data presented as mean ± SEM. Parametric comparisons (normally distributed data) employed one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni/Dunnett T3 post hoc correction, while nonparametric datasets used Kruskal-Wallis rank sum tests. Group differences in the escape latency in the MWM training task were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

Results

Diosgenin enhanced myelination of transplanted hOPCs in a PWMI model

To obtain hOPCs from H9 cells, we induced differentiation into the oligodendrocytic lineage according to the protocol outlined in Supplementary Fig. 1A. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that SOX2-positive H9 cells successfully differentiated into OPCs, which expressed markers including Nkx2.2, A2B5, Olig2, and PDGFRα. Pre-OPCs at a later stage were positive for O4 and Olig2, and ultimately, mature oligodendrocyte markers MBP and CNP were detected (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These results indicate that Olig2-overexpressing OPCs derived from H9 cells could differentiate into mature oligodendrocytes in vitro.

To investigate the effects of diosgenin on the differentiation and myelination of transplanted hOPCs, we injected Flag-labeled hOPCs into the corpus callosum of a PWMI mouse model with or without diosgenin treatment, and assessed cell survival, differentiation, and cognitive function according to the protocol in Fig. 1A. Seven days after transplantation, immunofluorescence staining showed that Flag+ exogenous OPCs were dispersed within the corpus callosum near the injection site (Fig. 1B). The number of Flag+/PDGFRα+ OPCs did not differ significantly among the hOPCs-only, hOPCs +40 mg/kg diosgenin, and hOPCs +80 mg/kg diosgenin groups (Fig. 1B–D). After 12 weeks of HI, the MBP fluorescence intensity in the ipsilateral corpus callosum of PWMI rats was reduced compared to the contralateral side (Fig. 1E). Compared with mice transplanted with hOPCs alone, diosgenin treatment (at both 40 and 80 mg/kg) increased both the number of Flag+/MAG+ cells (Fig. 1F and G), the percentage of STEM121+/MBP+ cells among MBP+ cells and total MFI of MBP (Fig. 1H–J). These results suggest that the transplanted hOPCs were capable of forming myelin sheaths and that diosgenin enhanced this function.

Compared with mice receiving either hOPC transplantation or single diosgenin treatment, combined treatment with diosgenin significantly increased the positive area of MBP (Fig. 2A, D), while reducing SMI-32 positive area in the corpus callosum (Fig. 2B, E). Ultrastructural analysis of myelinated axons via TEM revealed a decrease in g-ratio in the hOPCs + diosgenin group compared to the hOPCs-only or diosgenin-only group (Fig. 2C, F). Moreover, the density of myelinated axons per mm2 was higher in animals that received combined hOPCs and diosgenin treatment than in those receiving hOPCs alone (Fig. 2G). Western blot analysis confirmed that the protein level of MBP was consistent with the immunofluorescence findings (Fig. 2H and I).

Diosgenin enhanced the efficacy of hOPC transplantation in improving cognitive functions in PWMI mice

To evaluate whether diosgenin combined with hOPCs transplantation ameliorates anxiety-like behavior and cognitive deficits in PWMI mice, we conducted open field and MWM tests. Compared to the vehicle group, both the hOPCs-alone and hOPCs + diosgenin groups showed significant increases in total travel distance, number of entries into the center zone, and time spent in the center zone (Fig. 3A–D). Moreover, treatment with 80 mg/kg diosgenin further enhanced these parameters relative to the hOPCs-alone or diosgenin-alone groups (Fig. 3C and D). The MWM test was used to assess learning and memory function. While no differences in escape latency were observed on day 1 among the groups, mice treated with hOPCs + diosgenin exhibited significantly shorter escape latencies on day 5 during the acquisition phase compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 3E, G). During the probe trial on day 6, when the platform was removed, mice in the hOPCs +80 mg/kg diosgenin group spent significantly more time in the target quadrant and crossed the former platform location more frequently than those in the hOPCs-alone or diosgenin-alone groups (Fig. 3F, H, I).

Diosgenin targets MMP-9 to inhibit its activity

Western blot analysis was used to assess changes in the protein levels of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) and MMP-9 following HI. The results showed that TIMP-1 expression was significantly up-regulated at 1 and 4 days after modeling, but decreased to a level comparable to that of the Sham group by 7 and 14 days (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast, MMP-9 expression in the HI group was markedly increased at all time points compared to the Sham group (Fig. 4A, C). To explore the interaction patterns between diosgenin and key MMP family members, we conducted molecular docking studies. The results show that diosgenin fits well into the active pockets of both MMP-9 and MMP-2, forming stable complexes (Fig. 4D and E). Binding free energy calculations indicate stronger affinity of diosgenin for MMP-9 (ΔG = −7.3 kcal/mol) and MMP-2 (ΔG = −8.1 kcal/mol). Analysis of binding modes revealed that diosgenin forms extensive hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds with key active-site residues (e.g., His211 in MMP-2 and Ser412 in MMP-9), which may explain its stable binding. These results provide a theoretical basis for diosgenin as a potential selective inhibitor of MMP-2 and MMP-9. We examined the effects of diosgenin on MMP-9 and MMP-2 protein levels. The results showed that neither diosgenin nor SB-3CT altered the expression of MMP-9 or MMP-2 (Fig. 4F and G). We further assessed MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity using gelatin zymography. Compared with the Sham group, vehicle-treated mice exhibited significantly increased activity of both MMP-2 and MMP-9. Treatment with diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT significantly reduced the relative activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9 compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 4H and I).

(H–J) Gelatin zymography was used to assess the inhibitory effects of diosgenin on MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 4 per group; ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

To determine whether diosgenin crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and interacts with MMP9, we measured its concentration in the corpus callosum using total ion chromatography and UPLC-MS/MS. The retention time for diosgenin in standard and sample solutions was consistently observed at 3.2 min, with no interfering peaks detected (SFig. 2A–D). A solvent standard was used to establish linearity, and the calibration curve for diosgenin was linear over the range of 100–5000 ng/mL. UPLC-MS/MS analysis further revealed that in HI mice treated with 40 mg/kg diosgenin or 80 mg/kg diosgenin, the concentrations of diosgenin in the corpus callosum reached 2374.63 ng/g and 2646.34 ng/g, respectively, whereas in sham-operated mice the level was only 254.66 ng/g (SFig. 2E). These results indicate that under pathological conditions, diosgenin can penetrate the BBB and extravasate into the corpus callosum.

Diosgenin promotes myelination via MMP-9-mediated inhibition of TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling

The concentration of TGF-β1 protein was measured using ELISA. Compared with the sham group, vehicle-treated mice showed a significant increase in TGF-β1 levels. Treatment with diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT markedly reduced TGF-β1 levels compared to the vehicle group (Fig. 5A). TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling was activated, as indicated by an increase in p-Smad2/3. Consequently, the target protein p21 was upregulated, while cyclin D1 was downregulated, suggesting disruption of the cell cycle (Fig. 5B–E). Meanwhile, ID4-a transcription factor that inhibits OPC differentiation was significantly upregulated, whereas myelin regulatory factor (MYRF), a key transcription factor that promotes OPC differentiation and oligodendrocyte maturation, was notably downregulated (Fig. 5B, F–G).

Diosgenin inhibits TGFβ1–Smad2/3 signaling in HI mice. (A) The protein concentration of TGFβ1 was measured by ELISA after HI and diosgenin treatment. (B–G) Western blot analysis and quantification of phosphorylated Smad2/3 (p-Smad2/3), cell cycle regulators (p21 and cyclin D1), and key transcription factors (ID4 and MYRF) for OPC differentiation. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 4 per group; ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

To investigate whether MMP-9 mediates the inhibitory effects of diosgenin on TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling, we overexpressed MMP-9 in diosgenin-treated HI mice. Compared with diosgenin-treated mice overexpressing AAV-NC, MMP-9 overexpression (Fig. 6C and D) led to a decrease in both MBP MFI (Fig. 6A and B) and protein levels (Fig. 6C, E). Additionally, TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling was inhibited, as shown by upregulation of p-ALK5 and p-Smad (Fig. 6F and G).

To further confirm that diosgenin impairs myelination via suppression of TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling, we treated HI mice receiving diosgenin with recombinant human TGF-β1 (rhTGF-β1). The results demonstrated that TGF-β1 administration decreased MBP MFI (Fig. 6H and I) and protein expression (Fig. 6 J–K), while increasing levels of p-ALK5 and p-Smad (Fig. 6J, L–M).

Diosgenin suppresses MMP-9 activity in LPS-induced microglia and recombinant MMP-9-treated OPCs

To confirm the direct effect of diosgenin on MMP-9 activity, BV2 microglial cells were stimulated with LPS at various concentrations (10, 100, 500, and 1000 ng/mL) to induce MMP-9 production. Based on the levels of MMP-9 in the culture supernatant and cytotoxicity assessments (Fig. 7A), a concentration of 100 ng/mL LPS was selected for subsequent experiments. Compared with the control group, LPS treatment significantly increased MMP-9 activity in BV2 cells. However, administration of diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT markedly reduced the relative MMP-9 activity compared to the LPS-treated group (Fig. 7B–D). Similarly, in OPCs treated with recombinant MMP-9 (rMMP-9), the relative MMP-9 activity was also decreased following treatment with diosgenin (40 and 80 mg/kg) or SB-3CT compared to the rMMP-9-only group (Fig. 7E–G).

Diosgenin inhibits TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling in OPCs and microglia both in vivo and in vitro

To further clarify the cell-type-specific activation of the TGF-β-Smad2/3 signaling pathway, we performed immunofluorescence double staining for PDGFRα/p-Smad2/3 and Iba-1/p-Smad2/3 in brain sections. The results showed that diosgenin treatment significantly reduced the number of PDGFRα/p-Smad2/3 and Iba-1/p-Smad2/3 double-positive cells (Fig. 8A and B). These in situ observations directly confirm the inhibitory effect of diosgenin on TGF-β-Smad2/3 signaling in both OPCs and microglia.

We further investigated the regulatory effects of diosgenin on LPS-induced BV2 microglial activation, hESC-derived OPC differentiation, and the associated changes in TGF-β-Smad2/3 signaling. We found that diosgenin markedly suppressed LPS-triggered BV2 activation, accompanied by reduced Iba-1 expression (Fig. 8C and D). Moreover, diosgenin decreased the secretion of TGF-β1 in the culture supernatant (Fig. 8E) and downregulated the protein levels of TGFβRII and p-Smad2/3 in BV2 cells (Fig. 8F–H).

Subsequently, hESC-derived OPCs were treated with CM collected from differentially stimulated BV2 cells. LPS-primed CM significantly inhibited OPC differentiation, as evidenced by a decreased number of MBP-positive cells. In contrast, CM from LPS plus diosgenin-treated BV2 cells effectively rescued the LPS-mediated suppression of OPC maturation (Fig. 8I and J). Consistently, the inhibitory effects of diosgenin were accompanied by reduced expression of TGFβRII and p-Smad2/3 in CM-treated hESC-OPCs (Fig. 8K–M).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that diosgenin significantly enhanced the myelination capacity of transplanted hOPCs in a PWMI model, improves functional recovery, and acts through a novel mechanism involving the direct inhibition of MMP-9 activity and subsequent modulation of the TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling pathway (Fig. 9). Unlike previous studies that have predominantly characterized diosgenin as a neuroprotective agent with broad anti-inflammatory properties and demonstrated its capacity to promote OPC differentiation via estrogen receptor-mediated ERK1/2 activation to accelerate remyelination [14,15], our study identifies a previously unreported mechanism specifically targeting MMP-9 in the context of hOPC transplantation for PWMI.

Diosgenin promotes myelination and functional recovery in PWMI via inhibition of MMP-9 and TGFβ–Smad2/3 signaling.

Human OPCs were engrafted into neonatal shiverer mice, a model of congenital myelin deficiency, these cells successfully myelinated the host brains, substantially increased survival, and did not form tumors [37]. Our previous studies demonstrated that transplanted hOPCs could differentiate into mature oligodendrocytes in the brain of PWMI rats and fetal goat model, improving their cognitive functions [38,39]. In this study, we found that diosgenin treatment did not alter the survival of transplanted hOPCs, but significantly facilitated their maturation. Consistent with this finding, diosgenin increased the number of mature oligodendrocytes and improved myelination, ultimately elevating the density of myelinated axons. We further demonstrated that diosgenin exerts dual beneficial effects by regulating both grafted hOPC maturation and endogenous remyelination. Additionally, we verified the protective effect of diosgenin on axonal integrity via detection of SMI32 expression. These structural improvements were paralleled by significant functional benefits. Mice receiving combined hOPC transplantation and diosgenin treatment exhibited markedly reduced anxiety-like behavior and enhanced spatial learning and memory. This data aligns with and extends our earlier studies showing that hOPCs transplantation alone confers limited myelination and functional recovery [10]; however, combinatorial strategy using a small-molecule adjuvant represents a significant advance toward enhancing regenerative efficacy.

At the molecular level, we identified MMP-9 as a key target of diosgenin. Following HI injury, MMP-9 expression was persistently elevated, while its endogenous inhibitor, TIMP-1, was only transiently upregulated. This observation aligns with previous studies that have implicated MMP-9 as a detrimental factor in WMI [40]. Importantly, diosgenin potently inhibited the enzymatic activity of both MMP-9 and MMP-2, as demonstrated by gelatin zymography. This inhibitory profile mirrors that of the established MMP inhibitor SB-3CT and was consistently reproduced across both LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia and recombinant MMP-9-treated OPCs, affirming a direct mode of action. These findings not only reinforce the pathological role of MMP-9 in white matter damage but also position diosgenin as a promising therapeutic agent capable of selectively modulating extracellular protease activity without affecting gene expression. By directly curtailing MMP-9 hyperactivity, diosgenin helps restore the proteolytic balance in the injury microenvironment, thereby potentially preserving integral components of the extracellular matrix and facilitating regenerative processes such as remyelination.

We further elucidated a downstream pathway through which MMP-9 inhibition promotes myelination. HI injury led to the activation of TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling [27], upregulating p21 and downregulating cyclin D1, which disrupts the cell cycle. Concurrently, the expression of ID4, an inhibitor of OPC differentiation [41], was increased, while MYRF, a transcription factor necessary for oligodendrocyte maturation [42], was decreased. Diosgenin treatment effectively reversed these changes. Most importantly, we demonstrated that the promyelinating effects of diosgenin are mechanistically dependent on MMP-9 inhibition and the suppression of TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling. Overexpression of MMP-9 in diosgenin-treated mice abolished the therapeutic benefits, reducing MBP expression and reactivating the TGFβ-Smad2/3 pathway. Conversely, supplementing with rhTGF-β1 similarly counteracted the effects of diosgenin, confirming that the compound acts through this specific axis. This regulatory axis diverges from previously described mechanisms involving BDNF or Wnt signaling in remyelination [43,44], highlighting a novel drug gable pathway for future therapeutic development.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study primarily relied on a rodent PWMI model; further validation in large animals or human organoids would strengthen clinical relevance. Second, while molecular docking suggests selectivity for MMP-2/9, off-target effects of diosgenin cannot be fully ruled out. Proteomic or transcriptomic analyses in future work could provide a system-level understanding of its mechanism. Third, long-term safety and immunogenicity of combined diosgenin and hOPC treatment require extended observation. Finally, the precise molecular link between MMP-9 inhibition and TGFβ-Smad2/3 suppression warrants further investigation-in particular, whether MMP-9 directly cleaves components of the TGFβ pathway or acts through intermediate mediators.

In summary, the present study demonstrates that diosgenin efficiently enhances hOPC transplantation-mediated myelination repair and cognitive functional restoration in PWMI mice. Mechanistically, diosgenin exerts protective effects by suppressing MMP-9 activity and mitigating excessive activation of the TGFβ-Smad2/3 signaling pathway. Moreover, diosgenin modulates microglial activation within injured white matter, establishing a favorable cellular context to facilitate the maturation and integration of transplanted hOPCs as well as endogenous oligodendrocyte populations. Collectively, our findings uncover a novel MMP-9-dependent mechanism through which diosgenin boosts the therapeutic efficacy of hOPC transplantation. This work highlights the potential of diosgenin as a promising adjunct therapeutic strategy to facilitate white matter injury repair.
