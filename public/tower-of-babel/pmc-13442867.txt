Effects of septic shock vasopressors on the fitness of Escherichia coli
Authors: Prabakar Vaittinada Ayar, Antoine Bridier-Nahmias, Pradeebane Vaittinada Ayar, Alice Blet, Paul-Louis Woerther, Erick Denamur, Feriel Azibani, Hervé Jacquier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNorepinephrine is a catecholamine used as a first-line vasopressor in the management of sepsis. Nevertheless, norepinephrine has been shown to increase fitness and virulence of Escherichia coli, the most predominant facultative anaerobic commensal bacterium in the vertebrate intestinal microbiota and one of the bacteria most frequently implicated in sepsis. Despite high genetic diversity within E. coli species, these effects have been studied on a limited number of archetypal strains. Moreover, the fitness effect of alternative vasopressors, angiotensin II and vasopressin, has never been studied in E. coli. Here, we aimed to explore the effect of norepinephrine, vasopressin and angiotensin II on the fitness of E. coli in stressful media mimicking body compartments (serum-SAPI and urine), using a collection of 21 strains representative of the phylogenetic diversity of the species, and E. coli 536, an urosepsis model strain. We showed that norepinephrine increased the fitness of E. coli, regardless of genetic background, virulence or site of isolation, both in serum-SAPI and urine media. Unlike norepinephrine, angiotensin II and vasopressin display low or no effects on E. coli fitness. Our results bring insights on in vitro effects of norepinephrine, vasopressin and angiotensin II on E. coli, crucial for optimizing sepsis management and guiding future in vivo studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52743-w.Keywords: Fitness, Escherichia coli, Vasopressors, Norepinephrine, Vasopressin, Angiotensin IISubject terms: Microbiology, Physiology

Introduction

Sepsis is a condition characterized by life-threatening organ failure resulting from an inappropriate immune response to an infection. It is a severe syndrome that causes millions of deaths worldwide1, and requires optimal management that combines both symptomatic and etiologic treatments. The most common organ dysfunction during sepsis is circulatory failure, resulting in low blood pressure. To maintain blood pressure ensuring organ perfusion, vasopressor drugs are often required1. Norepinephrine (NE), is a catecholamine recommended as first-line therapy by the latest Surviving Sepsis Campaign2. Prolonged use of NE has deleterious effects. At the vascular system level, NE can reduce microcirculation, leading to multiorgan dysfunction3–5, including cardiotoxicity6,7. In addition, NE is thought to contribute to the immunosuppression state associated with sepsis, by limiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and increasing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines8,9. In this context, alternative non-catecholaminergic vasopressors, such as vasopressin (VASO)10 or angiotensin II (AngII)11 have been proposed as potential options to mitigate these adverse effects.

Escherichia coli is the predominant facultative anaerobic bacterial species in the vertebrate gut microbiota and is also associated with intra- and extra-intestinal infections12,13. Genetic studies of E. coli have revealed a species structured into phylogenetic groups13,14: some groups are associated with intestinal commensalism (A and B1), while others are more frequently associated with extra-intestinal infections (B2 and D)13. Thus, in sepsis, the genetic background of E. coli strains may differ according to the portal of entry, group A strains being most involved in biliary infections, whereas B2 strains are more generally associated with urinary tract infections13,15. Furthermore, there is a great diversity within E. coli species concerning the presence of virulence factors, such as adhesins, fimbriae, toxins, or different iron acquisition systems13.

Virulence factor-encoding genes can be found in clusters in particular regions of bacterial chromosomes known as pathogenicity-associated islands (PAIs). The presence of these PAIs is often associated with the pathogenicity of the strain16. For example, the E. coli strain 536, which causes urosepsis, has been extensively studied as a model of virulence and contains seven PAIs, the involvement of which has been explored17. NE has been described to increase the virulence of some E. coli strains, particularly in nutrient-poor environments18–23. These studies have been performed on E. coli archetypal model strains that do not represent the diversity of the E. coli species. The molecular mechanisms underlying these effects are likely multifactorial. Nonetheless, due to its catechol structure, NE may function as a siderophore, chelating environmental iron and being recognized by specific bacterial iron uptake systems24. Accordingly, Burton et al. proposed that NE’s impact on E. coli is linked to enterobactin, a highly conserved catecholate siderophore in this species25. Other catechol-mediated iron capture systems, such as salmochelin and yersiniabactin, inconsistently present in E. coli isolates, were not evaluated. Furthermore, the effect of other vasopressors such as VASO or AngII on the fitness of E. coli have never been investigated.

In this study, the effect of NE on the fitness of E. coli was assessed on a collection representative of the phylogenetic diversity of E. coli, to evaluate if the effect of NE on the fitness of E. coli is different according to the different genetic backgrounds. We then explored the role of two iron uptake systems in the fitness changes associated with NE. Last, the effect of AngII and VASO on the fitness of E. coli was assessed on the same collection of E. coli, to determine if alternatives to NE in the management of sepsis present effects on E. coli fitness.

Methods

Bacterial strains

Strains from the NILS collection

The “Natural Isolates with Low Subcultures” (NILS) collection consists of 82 strains representative of the phylogenetic diversity of the E. coli species26. Genomes were previously sequenced and annotated27. Briefly, genome typing was performed with in silico methods including phylogrouping28, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST)(according to the Warwick University scheme)29, serotyping (O: H serotype combinations)30, and identification of virulence factors31. The in vivo virulence was also assessed in a well-calibrated mouse model of sepsis27,32. Among the strains of this collection, we selected a representative panel of 21 isolates based on the following criteria: collection site, phylogroup, genotype (based on serotype and sequence typing) and virulence (based on animal model mortality). Isogenic mutants of the model strain E. coli 536

The E. coli strain 536 was isolated in a case of acute pyelonephritis33. This strain belongs to the B2 phylogenetic group, harbors seven PAIs, and is often used as a model strain of virulence17,33. This strain was acquired by our laboratory in 1998 from Jörg Hacker who isolated it and then stored at − 80 °C with glycerol. The genes associated with these PAIs are described in Table S1. Some of them harbor catechol-mediated iron capture systems, such as PAI III (harboring salmochelin operon) and PAI IV (harboring yersiniabactin operon). In a previous study, single- and multiple-PAI deletion mutants of E. coli 536 were constructed17. In our study, the wild-type (WT) E. coli 536 strain, single deletion mutants of PAI III (E. coli 536 ΔPAI III) and PAI IV (E. coli 536 ΔPAI IV) were studied (Table S1).

Culture media

Three different culture media were used: a nutrient-rich culture medium, lysogeny broth (LB) (Thermo Scientific), and a medium mimicking blood (serum-SAPI) as well as a synthetic urine. Serum-SAPI was developed by Lyte and Ernst18 in 1992. This is a highly stressful bacteriostatic medium that exposes bacteria to conditions similar to those inside a host, such as iron limitation, limited availability of nutrients, and immune defence proteins such as antibodies and complement (see Supplementary material)34. Synthetic Urine BIOIVT® was used to mimic a urinary compartment35,36. For the 21 strains from the NILS collection, the effect of NE, AngII and VASO was assessed in LB, serum-SAPI and synthetic urine. For the isogenic mutants of E. coli 536, the effect of NE was assessed on LB, serum-SAPI, but not on synthetic urine.

Vasopressors

NE, AngII and VASO were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France). For all strains tested, the three vasopressor agents were used at therapeutic concentrations: 10− 6 M for NE18,37–40, 10− 7 M for AngII41–44, and 10− 9 M for VASO45,46. The rationale for tested concentrations of vasopressors is available in Table S2. We also explored the dose effect for NE. We exposed the model strain E. coli 536 in LB and in serum-SAPI to a serial dilution of NE ranging from 5.10− 3 to 5.10− 12 mol/L.

Maximum Growth Rate (MGR) determination

The MGR has been used as a proxy of fitness, and was determined as previously described47. Briefly, strains were grown in LB in deep well plates for 16 h with shaking at 280 rpm at 37 °C to obtain bacterial growth in the stationary phase. Cultures were then diluted 1:10,000 for LB and 1:200 for synthetic urine and serum-SAPI media. Growth curves were performed at 37 °C in 96-flat bottom well microtiter plates containing 200 µL or medium supplemented with various concentrations of vasopressor agents, using a Tecan infinite 96-well plate reader. Each condition was performed in triplicate in each microplate, and experiments were performed in triplicate from independent cultures. The MGR (in s − 1) was determined as the maximum value of the derivative of logOD600, using R software with the Tecantools package, as previously described48. Relative increase or decrease of MGR was expressed in percentage.

Statistics

The normality of the data was verified using the Shapiro Wilk test. The medians of the MGR were compared using the t-test for normally distributed continuous variables or Mann–Whitney test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. No transformation or normalization was necessary.

To determine whether the impact of NE on MGR differs between the E. coli 536 WT, E. coli 536 ΔPAI III and E. coli 536 ΔPAI IV strains, the following approach was performed. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of two categorical independent variables, the treatment condition (TREAT: presence or absence of norepinephrine) and the PAI category (STRAIN: E. coli 536 WT vs. ΔPAI III vs. ΔPAI IV), on the MGR. The two-way ANOVA assesses the main effect of each independent variable, as well as the interaction effect between treatment and ΔPAI interval49. The ANOVA tests the null hypothesis, which states that samples in all groups are drawn from populations with the same mean values. The analysis relies on F-statistics that indicate whether observed differences between groups are statistically significant. The two-way ANOVA only indicates if there are at least two groups with different means but not which ones. To find them, post-hoc pairwise student comparison tests, with the Benjamini-Yekutieli p-value adjustment50 method, are performed using the pairwise.t.test R function.

The same statistical approach was used to determine whether the impact of NE on MGR differs between E. coli in the NILS panel according to the presence of the salmochelin and yersiniabactin operons.

Analyses were performed using R® software (R Development Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of norepinephrine on the fitness of E. coli

The MGR was determined for the 21 selected strains from the NILS collection and the archetypal model strain E. coli 536 under various conditions based on the medium (LB, serum-SAPI, and synthetic urine). In the presence of NE at therapeutic concentrations (10− 6 mol/L), the panel of selected strains and E. coli 536 exhibited a slight reduced MGR (P < 0.001) in LB (Fig. 1). The average decrease in MGR for the entire panel was 4.3% [95% CI (-8.9; 0.6)]. Conversely, all strains had an increased MGR (P < 0.001) in the presence of NE under stress conditions (serum-SAPI and synthetic urine) (Fig. 1). The increase in MGR over the panel was 54.3% [95% CI (27.3; 81.3)] in serum-SAPI and 177.3% [IC 95% (-121.9; 476.4)] in synthetic urine.

Effect of norepinephrine (10− 6 M) on the MGR of 21 strains of E. coli from the NILS collection as well as of the E. coli 536 strain, in LB, serum-SAPI, and synthetic urine. Outliers are represented as individual points. MGR: maximal growth rate (in s − 1), NE: Norepinephrine; significant p-value < 0.05 using the t-test.

To go further, we assessed the dose effect of NE on the MGR of E. coli 536 in LB and serum-SAPI (Figure S1 and S2). We used a serial dilution ranging from 10− 3 to 10− 12 mol/L, covering both therapeutic and physiological concentrations (respectively 10− 6 mol/L and 10− 9 mol/L). We observed a significant decrease in the MGR until 10− 9 mol/L in LB (P < 0.05), and a significant increase until 10− 6 mol/L in serum-SAPI (P < 0.05).

Table 1 shows the relative effect of NE on the MGR of each of the 21 strains of E. coli from the NILS collection, and E. coli 536. We showed that most isolates displayed a significant slight decrease of MGR in LB, and significant increase of MGR in serum-SAPI and synthetic urine. Overall, we observed a significant impact of NE on the MGR of E. coli strains encompassing the diversity of the species, whatever the genetic background, virulence and site of isolation.

Role of iron capture systems in relative changes in MGR induced by NE

To go further, we investigated the role of salmochelin and yersiniabactin, two catechol-mediated iron capture systems, in MGR changes induced by NE (Table 1; Fig. 2). Comparisons were made between three groups of strains: those that harbored neither of these operons (S-/Y-, n = 8), those that harbored only the yersiniabactin operon (S-/Y+, n = 8) and those that harbored both operons (S+/Y+, n = 4). The analysis did not take into account the S+/Y- category due to the presence of a single S+/Y- strain in the NILS panel. MGR did not differ significantly among the S-/Y-, S-/Y+, and S+/Y+ strains in LB or serum-SAPI in the presence of NE (Fig. 2).

The comparison was between S-/Y- (n = 8), S-/Y+ (n = 8) and S+/Y+ (n = 4) in LB (left plot) and serum-SAPI (right plot). Outliers are represented as individual points. However, to control for the extensive genetic diversity among these isolates, which could confound the observed effects, we studied the effect of NE in LB and serum-SAPI media on the growth of isogenic E. coli 536 strains deleted for PAI III and PAI IV, which respectively carry the genes encoding salmochelin and yersiniabactin. In LB, MGR differed significantly between ΔPAI III (P < 0.001) and ΔPAI IV (P < 0.01) compared to WT in the presence of NE (Fig. 3, left plot). In serum-SAPI, MGR did not differ significantly between ΔPAI III and ΔPAI IV compared to WT in the presence of NE (Fig. 3, right plot).

Effect of AngII and VASO on the fitness of E. coli

In the presence of AngII at therapeutic concentrations (10− 7 mol/L), the panel of selected strains and E. coli 536 exhibited a slight increased MGR (P < 0.05) in LB (Fig. 4A). The average increase in MGR across the panel was 2.2% [95% CI (-1.7; 6.1)]. AngII had no effect on the MGR of E. coli in LB for most strains when studied independently (Table 1). There was no significant effect of AngII on the MGR of E. coli in serum-SAPI for all tested strains (Fig. 4A; Table 1). In the presence of VASO at therapeutic concentrations (10− 9 mol/L), no effect was observed for all E. coli strains in LB or in serum-SAPI (Fig. 4B). For 3/21 strains of the NILS collection, an increase in MGR was observed in SAPI (Table 1), but was not associated with a given genetic background.

Effect of Angiotensin II (AngII)(10− 7 M) (A) and Vasopressin (VASO)(10− 9 M) (B) on the MGR of 21 strains of E. coli from the NILS collection and E. coli 536 WT. Outliers are represented as individual points. MGR: maximal growth rate (in s − 1); significant p-value < 0.05 using the t-test.

Noteworthy, the growth of E. coli in synthetic urine was extremely low compared to that in LB or serum-SAPI (Fig. 1). The addition of NE allowed a major increase in MGR, whereas the growth stayed very low in the presence of AngII and VASO, and MGR was often impossible to calculate. Hence, the impact of AngII and VASO on the MGR of E. coli is not presented here.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of NE, AngII, and VASO on the fitness of E. coli in nutrient-rich medium (LB) and in two stressful environments mimicking body compartments, serum-SAPI and synthetic urine, using a collection of 21 strains representative of the species diversity. In the presence of NE, we observed a slight decrease in fitness in LB medium and an increase in serum-SAPI medium. This latter medium is particularly relevant because it is competitive for bacterial survival closer to physiological conditions in a stressed vascular compartment34. In vitro, NE displays an increase in the growth and production of Shiga-like toxins by entero-haemorrhagic E. coli20,51,52 or the expression of the K99 pilus adhesin in enterotoxigenic E. coli21 under stressful conditions. The molecular determinants underlying these effects are probably due to several interdependent mechanisms53,54. Notably, the QseBC two-component system is sensitive to NE in enterohemorrhagic E. coli, with pleiotropic effects, including upregulation of flagellar, motility, virulence, metabolic, and stress response gene22,55,56. Because of its catechol core, NE may also act as a siderophore recognized by certain iron uptake systems. Hence, Burton et al. showed that the effects of NE on E. coli involves the iron capture systems of enterobactin, a highly conserved catecholate siderophore in E. coli25. In our study, we investigated whether the presence of the two other catecholate-dependent iron uptake systems, salmochelin and yersiniabactin, could affect fitness in the presence of NE. The presence of salmochelin and yersiniabactin operons was not associated with fitness change in the strains of the NILS panel in LB and serum-SAPI. To control for genetic background variability that could confound the results, we assessed the effect of NE on isogenic E. coli 536 mutants lacking PAI III or PAI IV, which harbor the genes encoding salmochelin and yersiniabactin, respectively. Fitness increase observed for E. coli 536 ΔPAI III and ΔPAI IV compared to E. coli 536 in LB with NE suggests that salmochelin and yersiniabactin may be involved in fitness decrease. The catechol-mediated uptake system could be involved in the accumulation of excess iron in the cell in nutrient-rich medium, which is known to be toxic57.

In synthetic urine, NE increased fitness for almost all our strains. This medium is of particular interest, as urinary tract infections are a leading cause of E. coli bacteremia. We found no link between collection site or genetic background and the impact of NE on fitness: strains isolated from synthetic urine or strains from phylogroups B2 and D, more frequently involved in urosepsis, did not show increased fitness compared with other strains.

While the effects of NE on the physiology of E. coli had extensively been studied in the literature, these studies have generally focused on model strains. Using a representative strain panel of the species, we showed that the increased fitness concerns all the strains tested, regardless of their genetic background, suggesting this effect is probably transposable to the vast majority of strains in the species. Additionally, the effect of AngII and VASO on E. coli fitness had never been studied. Unlike NE, AngII and VASO have little or no effect on E. coli fitness in stressful environments that mimic the compartments of organisms.

Our work has limitations. Firstly, additional controls could have been used. As mentioned above, Burton et al. demonstrated that the effects of NE on E. coli were associated with the enterobactin iron capture system, which is a highly conserved catecholate siderophore in E. coli25. E. coli mutants lacking the enterobactin and TonB used by these authors could have been useful as controls under our experimental conditions. Secondly, we did not use E. coli 536 that had been specifically deleted for the salmochelin and yersiniabactin operons. The observed effects in E. coli 536 ΔPAI III and ΔPAI IV in LB with NE could therefore be due to other genes present in these PAIs. Thirdly, NE, VASO and AngII have short in vivo half-lives, due to their rapid clearance via enzymatic and cellular reuptake mechanisms58,59. In our model, we did not investigate whether such systems in E. coli exist and could influence the concentration of these vasopressor agents in the supernatant. Lastly, our study has been limited to an in vitro approach. Nevertheless, the effects of VASO and AngII on E. coli fitness have never been explored before, and our results showed little or no fitness effect of these vasopressor agents.

Concerning in vivo effects, Lyte and Bailey showed an increase in E. coli population in the intestinal lumen in a mouse model exposed to a sudden increase in NE60. The effect of NE on the virulence of E. coli strain was also demonstrated in another model of infection in rabbit22. Thus, some authors suggest that the high release of catecholamines in the early phase of sepsis may act as a mediator of bacterial virulence24. Interestingly, NE infusion increases bacterial dissemination and immunoparalysis in a mouse model of polymicrobial sepsis9, suggesting that therapeutic use of NE could have effects on bacterial virulence, whereas VASO had no such deleterious effects in the same model. These studies demonstrate that the effects of NE also occur in vivo. The impact of VASO and AngII on bacterial virulence in vivo is still poorly explored and requires further investigation.

Conclusions

Vasopressors are essential in the management of sepsis, but the knowledge on their intrinsic effects on the fitness of E. coli remains incomplete. Unlike norepinephrine, non-catecholaminergic vasopressors, VASO and AngII have very few effects on E. coli fitness regardless of their genetic background, their site of isolation or their virulence. Our results bring insights on in vitro effects of vasopressors, necessary for the understanding of in vivo effects in animal models and human studies.
