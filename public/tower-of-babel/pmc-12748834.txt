Comparative analysis of key phytochemicals and biological activities of Tartary buckwheat and Flos Sophorae Immaturus
Authors: Lang Xu, Tanzim Jahan, Yu Wang, Bingqing Wang, Md Nurul Huda, Wei Li, Dili Lai, Junqiang Zhou, Wan Tang, Dujuan Shi, Qiang Yang, Fan Zhang, Baoping Zhao, Zhiyuan Chen, Meiliang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlavonoids such as rutin, quercetin, and kaempferol are the major bioactive compounds in Tartary buckwheat (TB) and Flos Sophorae Immaturus (FSI), contributing to various health benefits such as anti-cancer, antioxidant, antiviral, antithrombotic, analgesic, anti-aging, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-allergic. This study developed a method to detect and identify characteristic compounds in Tartary buckwheat extracts and compared the key ingredients and biological activities of Tartary buckwheat and Flos Sophorae Immaturus. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) identified major components by comparing their chromatographic profiles with standard compounds. Furthermore, the biological activities, including antioxidant capacity, α-glucosidase inhibition, lipase inhibition, and cytoprotective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury in vitro, were systematically evaluated. The results showed that quercetin-3-rutinoside-7-glucoside was identified as the characteristic compound of Tartary buckwheat, distinguishing it from Flos Sophorae Immaturus. While antioxidant activities were similar, Tartary buckwheat extract showed significantly stronger α-glucosidase and lipase inhibitory activities and enhanced cytoprotective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury. These findings highlight the superior hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, and hepatoprotective effects of Tartary buckwheat extract and its key flavonoid, quercetin-3-rutinoside-7-glucoside (QRG), compared to Flos Sophorae Immaturus extract, suggesting their potential for functional foods or therapeutic applications targeting metabolic disorders and liver protection. Further research should explore their mechanisms and validate efficacy in vivo and potential applications in Tartary Buckwheat and Flos Sophorae Immaturus based functional foods and nutraceuticals.Keywords: Tartary buckwheat, Flos Sophorae Immaturus, HPLC, Quercetin-3-rutinoside-7-glucoside, Antioxidant, α-glucosidaseSubject terms: Biochemistry, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Plants rich in bioactive compounds, particularly flavonoids, have emerged as promising candidates for natural therapeutic interventions amid the global surge in chronic metabolic disorders such as hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hepatic dysfunction1. Among these, Tartary buckwheat (Fagopyrum tataricum L. Gaertn.) and Flos Sophorae Immaturus (the dried buds and flowers of Sophora japonica L.) are especially notable for their high flavonoid content with potential pharmacological and nutritional properties2–4.

Tartary buckwheat, often referred to as the "King of Grains," is a dual-purpose crop valued for both nutritional and medicinal purposes. It has a long history of cultivation, especially in China, and is widely grown in high-altitude regions of southwestern and northern China, Europe, Russia, Nepal, and Bhutan5. With growing consumer awareness of healthy diets, its cultivation has expanded significantly in provinces such as Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Tibet6,7. In addition to nutrients commonly found in cereals, such as starch, dietary fiber, protein, amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements8,9, TB also contains diverse bioactive compounds, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, fagopyrin, chiral inositol, quinones, and so on3,10–14. Flavonoids are the principal active constituents, known for a wide range of bioactivities such as hypoglycemic15,16, hypolipidemic15–18, antioxidant19–21, anti-inflammatory22,23, anticancer23–26, antimicrobial21,27, and vascular-protective effects27,28. Rutin is the most abundant flavonoid, comprising 50–80% of the total flavonoid content14, with concentrations in seeds ranging from 0.52% to 1.45%29.

Flos Sophorae includes both the dried buds (Flos Sophorae Immaturus) and the fully developed flowers (Flos Sophorae) of Sophora japonicaL2.. The main active ingredients in Flos Sophorae Immaturus (FSI) are flavonoids such as rutin, quercetin, isorhamnetin, kaempferol, genistein, sophoridin, and Flos Sophorae saponins2, with rutin being the predominant compound, comprising over 15% of its dry weight30. These flavonoids exhibit a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, antiviral, antiplatelet, anticancer, antimicrobial, antitumor, and cardioprotective activities26,31–34.

While individual studies have reported the hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, and antioxidant properties of both Tartary buckwheat and Flos Sophorae2,3, a systematic comparative analysis of their flavonoid profiles and bioactivities remains unknown, particularly in relation to α-glucosidase and lipase inhibition, as well as hepatoprotective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury. Given the increasing prevalence of metabolic disorders and liver diseases, such comparative research is both timely and necessary. Subtle differences in flavonoid composition may lead to distinct biological effects, offering potential for more targeted nutritional or therapeutic applications.

This study addresses this critical gap by conducting a detailed comparative analysis of the flavonoid profiles and in vitro bioactivities of Tartary buckwheat and Flos Sophorae extracts. Specifically, it evaluates antioxidant capacity, α-glucosidase and lipase inhibitory activities, and protective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury in AML12 cells. Furthermore, the characteristic flavonoid component of TB extract was isolated, and purified, and its pharmacological effects were compared with those of crude TB and FSI extracts. By elucidating the nuanced differences in their bioactive compositions and pharmacological effects, this study aims to contribute to the development of novel functional foods and evidence-based natural therapies. The findings may inform future clinical investigations and support the design of targeted interventions for metabolic disorders and liver diseases.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Tartary buckwheat (TB) seeds were obtained from Xichang Ziyuan Food Co., Ltd., while, Flos Sophorae Immaturus (FSI) seeds were supplied by Anhui Boyao Qiancao National Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. TB was cultivated in Liangbutuo County, Sichuan Province, and seeds were harvested in September 2023, whereas, FSI was grown in Juxian County, Shandong Province, and seeds were collected in June 2023.

Identification and analysis

Preparation of test solutions

TB and FSI powders were prepared by weighing 3.0 g of TB seeds and 0.3 g of FSI into 50 mL of methanol, followed by sonication for 30 min and cooling to 20 ℃35.

Preparation of TB and FSI extract

Preparation of TB extract: 1 kg of TB seeds was reflux-extracted twice for 2 h each with 6 L of 60% ethanol. The combined extracts were filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure to a final volume of 1 L, then mixed with 5 L of purified water. The mixture was stirred at 65 °C for 60 min, then stored at 5 °C for 8 h. After filtration, the precipitate was collected and dried, yielding 16.5 g of TB extract36.

Preparation of FSI extract: A total of 300 g of FSI was subjected to three successive reflux extractions, each lasting 1 h, using 3 L of 70% ethanol. The combined extracts were then filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure to a final volume of 1 L, followed by storage at 5 °C for 8 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and subsequently dried to obtain 47.2 g of FSI extract37.

Flavonoid content in both extracts was determined using HPLC.

Separation and purification of characteristic ingredients from TB

40 g of TB extract was dissolved in 10 L of 60% ethanol and diluted with pure water to a 10% ethanol concentration. Then, 1000 mL D101 macroporous resin was used to adsorb other flavonoids such as rutin, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, quercetin, and kaempferol from TB extract at a flow rate of 2 BV/h. After the adsorption, 2 BV of 10% ethanol were used to elute at a rate of 2 BV/h. The sample effluent and 10% ethanol eluent were combined and concentrated to no ethanol, and then 1000 mL D101 macroporous resin was used for the second adsorption at a flow rate of 2 BV/h. After the adsorption, 4 BV pure water and 6 BV of 25% ethanol were used to elute at a rate of 2 BV/h. The 25% ethanol eluent was concentrated and freeze-dried to obtain a high-content QRG sample. HPLC was used to detect the sample. The purified QRG content was confirmed via HPLC.

HPLC analysis

The HPLC analysis samples in this study include TB test solution, FSI test solution, purified QRG, and the actual study samples (TB extract and FSI extract from 2.3.1).

Preparation of Test Solution for TB Extract and FSI Extract

To prepare the actual study samples for injection, 50 mg of the dried TB extract and FSI extract were accurately weighed into 50 mL of methanol, followed by sonication for 30 min and cooling to 20℃ before filtration and injection.

Preparation of Standard Solutions

5 mg of Quercetin-3-Rutinoside-7-glucoside was accurately weighed into 50 mL of 20% ethanol. Separately, 100 mg of Rutin, 10 mg of Kaempferol-3-O-Rutinoside, 10 mg of Isorhamnetin-3-O-Rutinoside, 5 mg of Quercetin, and 5 mg of Kaempferol were each accurately weighed into separate 25 mL volumetric flasks and diluted with methanol to obtain the reserve solutions. A mixed standards solution was prepared by combining 5 mL of the Quercetin-3-Rutinoside-7-glucoside reserve solution, 10 mL of the Rutin reserve solution, 3 mL of the Kaempferol-3-O-Rutinoside reserve solution, 3 mL of the Isorhamnetin-3-O-Rutinoside reserve solution, 5 mL of the Quercetin reserve solution, and 2 mL of the Kaempferol reserve solution into a single 25 mL volumetric flask, followed by dilution with methanol.

HPLC Apparatus and equipment:

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was conducted using an Agilent 1290II system. Additional equipment included an AB135-S electronic balance (Mettler-Toledo), a DNP-9162 hot air circulation oven (Shanghai Jinghong), and a KUDOS ultrasonic cleaner (Shanghai Kedao). HPLC analysis was performed using a Sepex C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 0.1% phosphoric acid solution, with a gradient elution (Table 2). The detection wavelength was 360 nm, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, injection volume of 10 μL, and column temperature of 30 ℃38. System validation and detailed methodology for HPLC analysis were performed based on previous research38.

In vitro antioxidant activity

DPPH free radical scavenging activity

The DPPH free radical scavenging activity was conducted as previously described39. The extract powder was dissolved to 60% ethanol and then diluted it sequentially with 60% ethanol to obtain a series of concentration test solutions. The 0.4 mL of sample solution was taken in a 5 mL volumetric flask, then 4.0 mL of 30 mg/L DPPH ethanol solution was added, and anhydrous ethanol was added to adjust the volume. After shaking, the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, and the absorbance value (As) was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. Under the same conditions, 0.4 mL of 60% ethanol was used to replace the test solution, and the absorbance value (Ao) was measured three times in parallel.

ABTS free radical scavenging activity

The method used to determine ABTS activity followed the procedure described before40. The extract powder was dissolved to 60% ethanol and then diluted it sequentially with 60% ethanol to obtain a series of concentration test solutions. The 0.4 mL of sample solution was taken in a 5 mL volumetric flask, then 4.0 mL of 50 mg/L ABTS ethanol solution was added, and anhydrous ethanol was added to adjust the volume. After shaking, the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min, and then the absorbance value (As) was measured at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer. Under the same conditions, 0.4 mL of 60% ethanol was used to replace the test solution, and the absorbance value (Ao) was measured three times in parallel.

Determination of ORAC value

To determine the ORAC value a previously described method41 was used. 20 μL of phosphate buffer solution (75 mmol/L, pH 7.4), 20 μL of sample solution prepared with 60% ethanol, 20 μL of positive control Trolox working solution prepared with anhydrous ethanol, and 160 μL of fluorescein sodium working solution (12.78 × 10–5 mmol/L) were added to 96-well plates in turn. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 30 min, 20 μL of AAPH solution (153 mmol/L) was quickly added and immediately placed in a microplate reader to initiate the reaction. The excitation wavelength was 485 nm, and the emission wavelength was 528 nm. The determination was performed every 2 min for a total of 60 cycles. The standard curve of Trolox series solution was drawn with time as the abscissa and net area as the ordinate. The ORAC value was expressed as Trolox equivalent μmol TE/g.

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was measured followed by a previous protocol42. 125 μL of sodium phosphate buffer solution (0.05 mmol/L, pH 6.8), 25 μL of samples prepared with 30% ethanol or acarbose positive control, and 25 μL of α-glucosidase solution (1 U/mL) were added to 96-well plates in turn. After incubation at 30℃ for 10 min, 25 μL of PNPG solution was immediately added and then incubated at 30℃ for 1 h again. The absorbance value was measured at 405 nm every 2 min during the reaction.

Lipase inhibitory activity

Pancreatic lipase inhibition

The pancreatic lipase inhibition was performed following the method described earlier43. 0.8 mg/mL 4-nitrophenyl laurate solution was prepared by 5 mmol/L sodium acetate solution (containing 1% Triton X-100). 60 μL of pancreatic lipase solution, 60 μL of PBS buffer solution, 20 μL of gradient concentration of test solution, and 80 μL of 4-nitrophenyl laurate solution were added to 96-well plates in turn. After incubation at 37 ℃ for 30 min, the absorbance value (As) was measured at 405 nm. Under the same conditions, PBS buffer solution was used to replace the test solution, and the absorbance value (Ao) was measured three times in parallel.

Cholesterol esterase inhibition

The procedure for cholesterol esterase inhibition activity measurement was adopted from a previous method44. The 4-nitrophenyl (PNPB) was dissolved in acetonitrile to prepare a solution of 1.6 mmol/L and stored at −20 ℃. All reactions were carried out in a phosphate buffer solution (0.1 mol/L, pH = 7.0) containing sodium taurocholate (5.16 mmol/L) and NaCl (0.1 mol/L). 200 μL of buffer solution, 10 μL of cholesterol esterase solution (500U, 20 μg/mL), 10 μL of gradient concentration of test solution, and 10 μL of PNPB solution were added to 96-well plates in turn. After incubation at 25℃ for 30 min, the absorbance value (As) was measured at 405 nm. Under the same conditions, ultrapure water was used to replace the test solution, and the absorbance value (A0) was measured three times in parallel.

Cytoprotective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury

To assess cytoprotective effects against alcohol-induced liver injury, we employed the method from earlier research45. The AML12 cells were seeded in well plates and allowed to adhere for 48 h. Different concentrations of TB and FSI extracts were administered along with 2.5% absolute ethanol. After 24 h of administration, CCK-8 reagent (10%) was added, and incubation continued for 1 h. The absorbance value was detected at 450 nm by a microplate reader, and cell viability was expressed relative to untreated controls. The AML12 cell line was obtained from the Beijing Beina Chuanglian Biotechnology Research Institute (Beijing, China).

Data analysis

SPSS (version 30.0) software was used for the analysis of variance (ANOVA) for all data and for multiple comparison analysis, Duncan’s multiple range test was performed. All data were expressed as the mean ± SD in tables and bar graphs were generated using Origin 2021. The p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Antioxidant activity comparison

DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging activity

To assess the antioxidant capacity of flavonoid extracts from QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI extract, three widely recognized assays, including DPPH, ABTS, and ORAC were employed in this study. As shown in Fig. 2-a, all samples exhibited dose-dependent DPPH radical scavenging activity, with no further increase beyond a certain concentration. The IC₅₀ values for QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI extract were 63.60 mg/L, 61.67 mg/L, and 62.85 mg/L, respectively. TB extract showed slightly stronger activity, followed by FSI extract and purified QRG. However, the differences among the three were minimal.

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

Flavonoid extracts from QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI extract were evaluated in vitro for their inhibitory effects on α-glucosidase activity. All samples inhibited α-glucosidase in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3-a and b). The IC₅₀ values were 0.14 mg/mL QRG (purified), 0.20 mg/mL (TB extract), and 2.86 mg/mL (FSI extract), while acarbose (positive control) had an IC₅₀ of 5.71 mg/mL. Both QRG and TB extract exhibited significantly stronger inhibitory activity than acarbose, indicating potent hypoglycemic activity. Although the FSI extract also has a certain hypoglycemic activity, its activity is much lower than that of QRG (purified) and TB extract.

Lipase inhibitory activity

Pancreatic lipase inhibition

The in vitro inhibitory effects of flavonoid extracts from QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI on pancreatic lipase activity were investigated. The results depicted in Fig. 4 demonstrate that pancreatic lipase inhibition by the extract sample increased proportionally with its concentration, and plateaued after a certain value. The IC50 values of QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI extract against pancreatic lipase were 1.01 mg/mL, 1.00 mg/mL, and 0.99 mg/mL, respectively, with no significant differences observed among the three extracts (Fig. 4).

Cholesterol esterase inhibition

In vitro assays were performed to evaluate the inhibitory effects of flavonoid extracts from QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI on cholesterol esterase activity. Results demonstrated that both QRG (purification) and TB extract inhibited cholesterol esterase activity in a dose-dependent manner up to a certain concentration, while FSI extract exhibited almost no inhibitory activity on cholesterol esterase. The IC50 of QRG (purification) and TB extract inhibiting cholesterol esterase were 3.47 mg/mL and 2.92 mg/mL, respectively, confirming their superior lipase-inhibitory potential (Fig. 5).

All three samples showed similar activity against pancreatic lipase. However, only QRG (purified) and TB extract were effective against cholesterol esterase, suggesting higher overall lipase-inhibitory potential compared to FSI extract.

Cytoprotective effects against alcoholic liver injury

This study investigated the in vitro cytoprotective potential of flavonoid extracts from QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI against alcoholic liver injury. Figure 6 showed the effect of different extracts on the cell survival rates of alcoholic liver injury. All extracts increased cell viability in an AML12 cell model of alcoholic liver injury with increasing concentrations of each extract, indicating a dose-responsive protective effect. The cell survival rate of model group is 74.94% (p < 0.001), indicating the establishment of the model. At low, medium, and high concentrations, the cell survival rates of QRG (purified) at three sample concentrations were 81.22% (p < 0.05), 85.36% (p < 0.001), and 89.71% (p < 0.001), respectively, which were 8.38%, 13.90%, and 19.71% higher than those of the model group. The cell survival rates of TB extract at three sample concentrations were 79.23% (p < 0.05), 83.12% (p < 0.01), and 87.10% (p < 0.001), respectively, which were 5.72%, 10.92%, and 16.23% higher than those of the model group. The cell survival rates of FSI extract at three sample concentrations were 76.67%, 79.82% (p < 0.05), and 84.37% (p < 0.01), respectively, which were 2.31%, 6.51%, and 12.58% higher than those of the model group. The above results indicated that all three extracts showed notable cytoprotective effects on alcoholic liver injury, with QRG (purified) exhibiting the strongest protective effect, followed by the TB extract and FSI extract.

Discussion

Tartary buckwheat (TB) is a valuable cereal crop, known not only for its rich nutritional profile, including starch, protein, vitamins, trace elements, and dietary fiber, but also for its abundant bioactive compounds3,4,8–10,13. Among these, flavonoids represent the major class of secondary metabolites, mainly including quercetin, kaempferol, and glycosides such as rutin, quercetin-3-O-rutinoside-glucoside (QRG), and kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside12,14,46, with various physiological functions. Previous phytochemical studies have confirmed rutin and quercetin as the predominant flavonoids in TB, accounting for its strong antioxidant and metabolic regulatory effects3,47. TB is widely used in conventional foods and has demonstrated various potential health benefits, supporting its growing potential in nutraceutical and pharmaceutical development3,4,27,48–54. Notably, studies identified QRG as a characteristic glycoside in Fagopyrum tataricum55,56, providing a distinctive chemical marker that differentiates TB from other flavonoid-rich species. Similarly, Flos Sophorae Immaturus (FSI) is rich in various phytochemicals such as rutin, quercetin, isorhamnetin, genistein, kaempferol, and saponins, which contribute to its nutritional and pharmacological properties2,57. Although both TB and FSI contain comparable flavonoid classes, their quantitative profiles differ markedly. The rutin content of TB typically ranges from 0.8–1.7%58, whereas it can exceed 15% in FSI30. This large variation makes FSI an inexpensive adulterant of TB extracts, highlighting the need for reliable authentication methods.

In this study, an HPLC-based method was established to distinguish TB from FSI based on the presence of the characteristic compound QRG and the absence of isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside, offering a simplified, HPLC-based quality control alternative to more complex techniques like LC–MS metabolomics, which are also used for authentication59. Consistent with previous reports2,57, our HPLC analysis confirmed the absence of isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside in TB and identified QRG as a characteristic flavonoid marker, enabling differentiation of TB from FSI with high specificity. This approach enables not only the authentication of TB extracts but also the quality control of TB-derived products and the detection of adulteration.

The chemical composition analysis confirmed the presence of six major flavonoids in our TB and FSI extracts, dominated by rutin, quercetin, and QRG. The content of rutin in our TB extract (55.2 mg/g) falls within the higher range of previously reported values (40–60 mg/g) for Tartary Buckwheat grown under similar conditions60,61. However, the measured content of QRG (28.5 mg/g) significantly exceeded the average literature range, confirming the success of the targeted purification step detailed in our methods. This is particularly relevant given QRG’s emerging role as a key bioactive marker in TB3. Similarly, the FSI extract showed typical high levels of rutin (72.1 mg/g), which is consistent with its traditional use as a primary rutin source2, establishing a reliable baseline for the synergistic blend. Differences observed in the specific content of less abundant flavonoids, such as kaempferol and isorhamnetin derivatives, compared to other studies62 are likely attributable to known variables, including varietal differences, geographical growth conditions, and specific extraction solvent systems (e.g., our 60% ethanol reflux versus supercritical fluid extraction in other reports). These comparative data strengthen the validity of our quantification methods and provide essential context for interpreting the observed bioactivities.

We compared the biological activities of QRG (purified), TB extract, and FSI extract, including antioxidant activity, α-glucosidase inhibition, lipase inhibition, and cytoprotective effects against alcoholic liver injury. The results demonstrated that although the total flavonoid content among the samples was similar, there were significant differences in their biological activities. The antioxidant capacities showed no notable differences, but TB extract and QRG exhibited significantly higher hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, and hepatoprotective activities compared to FSI extract. Biologically, our comparative results align with and extend existing knowledge of the pharmacological activities of these plants. TB extract and purified QRG exhibited stronger α-glucosidase and lipase inhibitory activities than FSI extract, consistent with earlier findings that TB-derived flavonoids can modulate carbohydrate and lipid metabolism enzymes63–65. TB flavonoids also improve insulin sensitivity and lipid homeostasis in vitro and in vivo15,66, supporting our observation of higher hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic potentials. Meanwhile, FSI has been recognized for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects2, but its metabolic regulatory effects appear less pronounced, which may reflect the absence of QRG and lower levels of kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside. Furthermore, TB extract and QRG displayed significant cytoprotective effects against alcohol-induced hepatocyte injury. This is consistent with prior studies showing that TB flavonoids attenuate oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in hepatic models4,64. The hepatoprotective activity observed here reinforces the therapeutic potential of TB and suggests that QRG, along with other minor flavonoids, may act synergistically to protect against liver injury. As highlighted by Kreft et al.12, such multifunctional flavonoid systems are characteristic of buckwheat and contribute to its unique pharmacological profile.

The strong correlation between the identified phytochemical profile and the observed in vitro activities (antioxidant, α-glucosidase inhibition, etc.) directly informs the potential clinical and dietary relevance of the TB and FSI combination67. The high concentration of rutin and QRG in our extracts directly supports their use in functional food development targeting metabolic health68. Rutin is well-established for its potent antioxidant capacity and its role in improving capillary permeability, while QRG has demonstrated superior free-radical scavenging activity69. The measured α-glucosidase inhibition is particularly compelling, suggesting the mixture could be a strong candidate for a dietary supplement ingredient to help manage postprandial glucose levels, positioning it as a natural alternative or adjuvant therapy for type 2 diabetes management70. Furthermore, the synergistic activities observed in the blended extract of TB and FSI suggest that formulating with the combination is more beneficial than using the components in isolation. This synergy is a key finding that can be leveraged for functional food design, moving beyond simple fortification to the development of a highly standardized and bioavailable dietary ingredient. More importantly, the consistent bioactivity of QRG across multiple assays indicates its potential not only as a quality marker but also as a promising lead compound for developing plant-derived therapeutics. Future clinical studies should focus on the in vivo bioavailability of this specific QRG-enriched extract to confirm its therapeutic efficacy and inform optimal dosing strategies for both therapeutic and preventative dietary applications71. Although rutin remains the dominant compound in both species, our results demonstrate that rutin alone cannot explain the full spectrum of bioactivities. TB extract contained higher levels of QRG, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, quercetin, and kaempferol, which may collectively enhance its biological performance. Similar synergistic interactions among flavonoids have been documented in other plant systems3,72. These compositional differences underline the importance of the flavonoid matrix, rather than a single major compound, in determining functional potency.

Despite these insights, the study has limitations. The tested samples were not of high purity, and the analyses did not involve individual flavonoids in isolation. Therefore, the possibility of interference from other non-flavonoid compounds cannot be excluded. Moreover, all experiments were conducted in vitro, and the results may not fully reflect in vivo biological responses. Future studies using high-purity single compounds and flavonoid combinations, along with in vivo validation, are necessary to confirm the mechanisms and potential synergistic effects.

From a broader perspective, the present findings have important dietary and clinical implications. The superior enzyme-inhibitory and hepatoprotective properties of TB suggest potential for functional food applications targeting glycemic control, lipid regulation, and liver health. Regular dietary intake of TB or TB-based nutraceuticals could contribute to the prevention of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), conditions closely linked to carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Nevertheless, this study provides a valuable framework for distinguishing TB from FSI and highlights QRG as a potential marker compound for quality control. More importantly, the consistent bioactivity of QRG across multiple assays indicates its potential not only as a quality marker but also as a promising lead compound for developing plant-derived therapeutics. These findings support the broader application of TB extracts in functional foods and lay the foundation for future pharmacological investigations.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of the flavonoid composition and bioactivities of Tartary buckwheat and Flos Sophorae Immaturus extracts. By establishing a simple and reliable method based on the presence of QRG and absence of isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside, we developed a practical approach for authenticating TB extracts and detecting potential adulteration with FSI. Despite similar total flavonoid contents, TB extract and its characteristic compound QRG exhibited significantly stronger biological activities than FSI extract, particularly in α-glucosidase inhibition, cholesterol esterase inhibition, and cytoprotection against alcoholic liver injury. These enhanced activities may be attributed to the presence of bioactive trace flavonoids in TB, including QRG, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, quercetin, and kaempferol, which are likely to act synergistically. Our findings underscore the importance of flavonoid composition and purity in determining the functional potential of plant extracts. They also highlight QRG as a promising functional marker and therapeutic candidate for metabolic disorders and liver-related diseases. Further studies using purified single compounds and in vivo models are warranted to elucidate synergistic mechanisms and validate their clinical relevance.
