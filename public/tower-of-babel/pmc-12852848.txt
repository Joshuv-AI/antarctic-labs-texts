Leveraging engineered mitochondria through intercellular communication network for accelerated transport and delivery
Authors: Yiwei Peng, Datong Gao, Yiliang Yang, Yitian Du, Zhenzhen Yang, Jiajia Li, Meng Lin, Yanxia Zhou, Xinru Li, Xianrong Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inspired by the non-transmembrane transfer of mitochondria in cell-to-cell communications, herein, we report an original exploration to accelerate mitochondrial intercellular transport, and its application to exogenous cargo delivery. We discover that deliberate PINK1-targeted mitophagy downregulation elevates mitochondrial transit capacity via multifaceted drivers—morphological adaptation, metabolic reprogramming, and respiratory enhancement. Capitalizing on this, we engineer high-speed mitochondrial vehicles for photosensitizer hitchhiking, with spatiotemporal tracking elucidating its dynamic intercellular transit and physiological impacts. Through mitochondria’s communication network—tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), the mitochondria-photosensitizer cotransporter achieves reinforced intercellular delivery, thereby inducing deep tumor penetration and enhanced photodynamic killing. Our work establishes a transformative mitochondria-hitchhiking platform for overcoming biological barriers in drug delivery and provides mechanistic insights into manipulating intercellular organelle transport for therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Effective delivery of therapeutics into tumors is the prerequisite for efficient and low-toxic treatment of malignant tumors. However, the abnormal tumor microenvironment—including dense stromal tissue, high interstitial fluid pressure, low blood perfusion, abnormal angiogenesis, and hypoxia—greatly hinders the diffusion distance and extent of therapeutics, forming a ‘blind zone’ in deep tumor where drugs cannot reach1. Currently, multi-functional or ‘smart’ synthetic drug delivery systems aimed at overcoming these obstacles are active and effective, but complex and rigorous, and unable to provide a comprehensive solution to all problems2,3. It has been reported that the delivery efficiency of cancer nanomedicine is less than 1%4, and the limited effect is not conducive to clinical expansion5–7.

In contrast, the cell-to-cell transport of substances and signals needed by tumor cells themselves occurs ‘unobstructed’ within tumors. In 2006, Spees et al. first demonstrated that cell-to-cell transfer of mitochondria from mesenchymal stem cells to mitochondria-depleted A549 cells could rescue aerobic respiration8. Studies have shown that mitochondria carry out intercellular transport in a specific manner to improve mitochondrial respiration, restore cell function, and enhance tumor survival9–12. Intercellular mitochondrial transfer depends on biological information communication between donor cells and recipient cells and is mediated by diverse cellular structures, mainly extracellular vesicles (EVs), tunneling nanotubes (TNTs), and gap junctions13. EVs and gap junctions mediate mitochondrial migration between close and adjacent cells, respectively, while TNTs mediate long-distance directional mitochondrial transport14. Rustom et al. first reported TNTs as a new mechanism of intercellular communication in 2004 and termed them ‘Nanotubular highways for intercellular organelle transport'11. Although TNTs are considered to be the major intercellular mitochondrial delivery pathway in solid tumors15–18, the fundamental mechanisms governing TNT-mediated mitochondrial transport remain poorly understood.

Changes in cellular energy and metabolism, as well as differences in cell status drive the transport of mitochondria between cells15. Mitochondria precisely regulate themselves through cycles of fusion, fission, biogenesis, mitophagy, and intercellular transport—collectively referred to as ‘mitochondrial dynamics’—to adapt to various physiological states. They frequently alter their morphology or position in response to diverse stressors and energy requirements19,20. However, harnessing mitochondria movement (whether or not carrying cargo) and further enabling it to transport exogenous substances have rarely been explored, highlighting a gap in the understanding of tumor endogenous transport mechanisms that limits their potential to deliver exogenous substances.

Building on these foundations, this study uncovered that suppression of PINK1-mediated mitophagy initiated a compensatory cellular response—engaging cellular homeostatic recalibration and rescue programs. This adaptive rewiring concomitantly potentiated mitochondrial transit velocity/extent through TNTs and mobilized mitophagy-evaded mitochondria as autonomous bio-carriers. Capitalizing on this mechanism, we engineered a ‘mitochondrial autonomous drive’ (MAD) system by spatiotemporally coupling the mitochondria-targeting photodynamic agent IR780 to form ‘passenger-vehicle’ co-transporters. Within solid tumors, MAD orchestrated mitochondrial transport network expansion and high-speed motility through cellular homeostasis and oxidative stress modulation, enabling deep IR780 penetration. Consequently, MAD-mediated photodynamic therapy achieved potent tumor eradication in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, we pioneered a mitophagy-modulated platform leveraging engineered mitochondria for non-transmembrane drug delivery, providing transformative solutions for overcoming penetration barriers in oncology.

Results

Downregulated mitophagy enhances mitochondrial intercellular transport via TNTs

In order to maintain homeostasis, cells undergo autophagy or excretion of mitochondria that have detached from fused filamentous mitochondria during physiological processes. Inhibition of mitophagy may conversely lead to increased mitochondrial transcellular export. PINK1/Parkin pathway is one of the most classic mitophagy pathways, so firstly we verified the effect of the PINK1/Parkin pathway on mitochondrial transport by silencing PINK1.

After 24 h culture in the confocal dish, a communication relationship was established between B16F10 cells. Compared with nonsense siRNA control (NC), the fluorescence of mitochondria was significantly enhanced (Fig. 1a), accompanied by a 4.2-fold increase in the number of TNTs, long, thin, membranous structures extending from cells after mitophagy inhibition by siPINK1. Even more fascinating, the images (Fig. 1a) clearly showed that there were mitochondria transferring in the TNTs structure (green mitochondria were present in the red tubular TNT structure). Afterwards, we designed a donor-recipient cell co-culture system to explore mitochondrial communications between cells after siPINK1 treatment (Fig. 1b). Recipient cells were stained with a living cell tracker CMFDA (green), while the mitochondria of donor cells were stained with MitoTracker (red), respectively. Donor and recipient cells were treated by nonsense siRNA containing medium (abbreviate as NC), or treated by siPINK1 containing medium, respectively. Then, donor cells and recipient cells were co-incubated, and laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM) was performed to count the number of MitoTracker red spots that were transferred from donor cells in the green recipient cells in four cases, i.e., NC (donor) to NC (recipient), NC (donor) to siPINK1 (recipient), siPINK1 (donor) to NC (recipient) and siPINK1 (donor) to siPINK1 (recipient) (Fig. 1b, c). Images in Fig. 1c showed that almost all green cells contained red mitochondrial signals, indicating that the transfer of mitochondria between cells was ubiquitous. Among them, the red mitochondria of donor cells in recipient cells were significantly increased after siPINK1 treatment (siPINK1 to NC, NC to siPINK1 and siPINK1 to siPINK1) (Fig. 1d). Notably, the mitochondrial transport increased in siPINK1 (donor) to NC (recipient) and NC (donor) to siPINK1 (recipient) may reflect two different mitochondrial transport mechanisms, namely, cells with metabolic changes send dysregulated mitochondria to normal cells to maintain their own quality control, while normal cells transfer healthy mitochondria to unstable cells for rescue21–23. In a separate experiment, conversely, activation of mitophagy using Urolithin A24 resulted in a further reduction in mitochondrial transfer (Supplementary Fig. S1, P < 0.0001). This demonstrated that enhanced mitophagy induced compensatory attenuation of the alternative mitochondrial disposal pathway—mitochondrial extrusion—thereby decreasing mitochondrial transfer, in accordance with our hypothesis. Mitochondria traversed between multiple cells (Donor with Donor, Donor with Recipient, Recipient with Recipient cells, respectively) to form a network through TNTs (Fig. 1e), and TNTs acted as a communication bridge between donor and recipient cells, as observed from 3D confocal microscopy image (Fig. 1f). These results highlighted that inhibiting mitophagy enhanced mitochondrial transfer by increasing the number of mitochondria and TNTs.

Blockade of mitophagy arouses transport driving forces

The above research suggests that blockade of mitophagy in the tumor opens up the extensive TNTs transport channels. Replacing siRNA with CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to knock out PINK1 could modify the short-term inhibition and unpredictable off-target effects of siRNA25,26. The sgRNA targeting PINK1 gene was cloned into pX330 backbone to construct a CRISPR/Cas9 gene knockout plasmid. Then the plasmid was combined with cationic polymer fluorinated highly branched poly(β-amino ester) to form the plasmid delivery nanocomplex27,28 (named FP, the details were presented in Supplementary Methods and Results and Supplementary Tables 1–2). As a result, the gene editing efficiency was as high as 41.6% after treatment with this CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system (Fig. 2a, b), with a weak expression in PINK1 protein (about 35.7% reduction) compared with the negative control plasmid (Fig. 2c). Taken together, this CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system could induce indels at the targeted PINK1 loci (Supplementary Figs. S2–7) and thus efficiently disrupt the expression of PINK1. The Keima and mitochondria co-localization marker plasmid (mKeima-Red-Mito-7)29,30 was performed to evaluate mitophagy after PINK1 gene editing (Fig. 2d). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of cells transfected with PINK1 plasmid revealed 2457 upregulated genes and 1699 downregulated genes (including PINK1, Fig. 2f). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that descending genes were strongly correlated with glycolysis and mitophagy (Fig. 2g). The biological process analysis in GO functional enrichment analysis showed that the transcription-related capabilities were decreased (Fig. 2h). Further analysis of the differentially expressed genes found that genes related to transcription and translation were downregulated, which may be related to the DNA mismatch caused by CRISPR/Cas9 system and the subsequent decrease in PINK1 transcription and translation. The decrease in mRNA related to glycolysis may indicate an increase in aerobic respiration caused by an increase in mitochondria (Fig. 2i). Protein interaction network analysis revealed that the reduced mitophagy-related genes formed a network relationship centered on PINK1 (Fig. 2j), which also underlined the rationality of our inhibition of mitophagy by knocking out PINK1.

Mitochondria are the energy source of cells, and mitochondrial dynamics are closely related to the energy supply within cells31. Mitochondria respiratory capacity reflects metabolic function. To verify that PINK1 knockout improved cell metabolism, respiratory capacity of mitochondria was investigated. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was recorded to monitor oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in real time, and inhibitors of mitochondrial respiration were added successively: oligomycin, which inhibits ATP production in mitochondria; FCCP, an ionophore that uncouples mitochondrial respiration leading to maximal oxygen consumption; and antimycin A & rotenone, inhibitors of electron transport complexes I and III, respectively, which completely block mitochondrial OXPHOS. As Fig. 2k showed, the entire OCR curve was elevated after PINK1 gene editing treatment, but still maintained the same sawtooth shape as the control group. According to statistics, the basal respiration, maximum respiration, and spare respiration capacity of cells were significantly improved by PINK1 blockade (2.1-, 4.1-, 5.0-fold versus control), showing a stronger metabolic activity without inducing hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. S8). Increased cellular metabolism is a powerful driving force behind mitochondrial intercellular transport15,32. This suggested that PINK1 knockout can globally enhance the mitochondrial OXPHOS pathway, and the enhanced metabolic phenotype supported the cell-to-cell transfer of mitochondria.

Inhibition of mitophagy increases mitochondrial motility and bidirectional transport

Mitochondrial morphology and motility at the subcellular level provide more direct visualization of their movement dynamics and behavioral tendencies. From the 3D imaging and reconstruction of mitochondria by structured illumination microscopy (SIM), we found that knockout of PINK1 increased the round mitochondria (the aspect ratio of each mitochondrion in 1.0 ≤ L/W < 1.5), and decreased the tubular mitochondria (the aspect ratio of each mitochondrion in 2.0 ≤ L/W < 5.0) (Fig. 3a). As we know, mitochondria can fuse or fission from the mitochondrial network as the cell state changes. Previous experiments have shown that decreased PINK1 expression can induce increased mitochondrial migration along TNTs (Fig. 1). Given the increased transport driving forces (Fig. 2), we speculate that the moving speed of mitochondria increases. So, in the following, we explored the speed and pattern of mitochondrial movements within and between cells (in TNTs) under different treatments, respectively.

Continuous imaging for 3 min at 5 s intervals with SIM and tracking of mitochondrial trajectories (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2) were performed to study the alterations on mitochondrial motility in individual cells. The speed-position diagram of mitochondria migration paths in the cytosol (Fig. 3b) and the displacements normalized into the X–Y coordinates (Fig. 3c) underlined that the number of high-speed mitochondria increased, as well as the mean track speed (1.3-fold versus control) and track length (1.7-fold versus control) increased under the blockade of PINK1.

Next, we conducted an in-depth and intuitive visualization study on the movement of mitochondria between cells via TNTs. First, mitochondria and TNTs (F-actin) were marked with pAcGFP1-Mito and LifeAct-mCherry plasmid, respectively, and long-term SIM images of a fixed field of view for up to 20 min were captured (Supplementary Movies 3 and 4). The movement of mitochondria along TNTs was rendered and tracked (Fig. 3d, e), visually demonstrating that the treated mitochondria were generally faster in cell-to-cell transfer, with more high-speed mitochondria shuttling through TNTs. Similarly, mitochondria in cells with PINK1 knockout had faster trajectories (2.0-fold versus control) and longer displacements (1.6-fold versus control) in TNTs when both directions normalized to X-Y coordinates (Fig. 3e), reflecting their enhanced motility. Ultimately, long-term imaging captured complete visuals of a mitochondrion transporting from one cell to another in the FP group, which directly proved the intercellular transfer of mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. S9, Supplementary Movie 5).

Then we labeled the mitochondria of PINK1 knockout and untreated cells with green and red, respectively, and co-cultured them to observe the mitochondrial movement pattern between the two individual cells connected with TNT. The results intuitively showed that the exchange of mitochondria between the two cells was mutual (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Movie 6), which was consistent with our previous explanation of the bidirectional mitochondrial transport mechanism, the ‘maintaining homeostasis’ and ‘rescue’. Excitingly, the movement trajectories of the PINK1 knockout cells were faster (1.3-fold versus control) and longer (1.8-fold versus control) than those of untreated cells (Fig. 3g), which may indicate that mitochondria in cells with reduced PINK1 expression are more inclined to move between cells and transfer from donor cells to recipient cells.

Cargo IR780 hitches a ride on mitochondria for efficient transport along TNTs

Based on the previous studies that decrease in mitophagy induced an increase in mitochondrial cell-to-cell transport, we designed and constructed a ‘mitochondrial autonomous drive’ (MAD) system, containing FP and FIS (integrating SPION and IR780) nanoparticles, to exploit accelerated mitochondrial motility for cargo delivery. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticle (SPION) is an excellent but short-lived oxidative stress generator at subtoxic concentrations34. Skillfully, when SPION was applied under low-mitophagy condition, as the significantly increased DCFH-DA-stained ROS displayed (Supplementary Fig. S10), the oxidative stress was effectively accumulated to provide more sufficient and long-lasting fuel for mitochondria movements, which was not achieved by SPION alone. IR780 has a natural property of attaching to mitochondria, which is attributed to its highly electropositive and lipophilic nature35, and has been widely used in medical fields such as mitochondrial imaging36 and photodynamic tumor therapy37. So IR780 was selected as a model cargo for delivery, in the form of nanoparticles.

The nanoparticle containing SPION and IR780 (named FIS) was prepared with ∼80 nm diameter and ∼50 mV zeta potential (Supplementary Figs. S11–13, the detail in preparation and characteristics of FIS was presented in Supplementary Methods and Results). IR780 exhibited much higher overlay with mitochondria than lysosomes in the B16F10 cells according to Pearson’s coefficients analysis (Supplementary Fig. S14), emphasizing that the IR780 in MAD was highly accumulated in mitochondria.

The transfer capacity of mitochondria (red fluorescence) and hitchhiker IR780 (magenta fluorescence) from donor cells to recipient cells was investigated employing the donor-recipient system described previously (Fig. 1b). FIS alone served as control. Mitochondrial migration was enhanced when MAD-treated cells served as donor cells (MAD to FIS, MAD to MAD) (Fig. 4c), which was consistent with previous findings (Fig. 1c, d). Excitingly, IR780’s migration was simultaneously enhanced, and the groups of MAD treated donor cells showed the strongest migration of IR780 hitchhiking on mitochondria, exhibiting a stronger outward transport capacity. Conversely, as demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. S15, recipient cells cultured in conditioned medium exhibited no detectable MitoTracker or IR780 staining, indicating that the fluorescent signals in recipient cells can be attributed to mitochondrial transfer rather than dye crossover contamination. Typical pharmacological inhibitors—cytochalasin D (restraining actin polymerization and thus TNT formation38), L-778123 (inhibiting farnesyltransferase and geranylgeranyltransferase type 1 related to nanotube-mediated intercellular communication39) and dynasore (a non-competitive GTPase inhibitor40) blocked the transfer of mitochondria and IR780 from donor cells to recipient cells (Fig. 4d, P < 0.0001 compared with MAD group). These results emphasized that under the driving forces caused by MAD system, shed mitochondria employed TNTs to radiate to surrounding cells under the purpose of maintaining homeostasis, and cargo IR780 followed to migrate to other cells; at the same time, rescue signals were sent to obtain mitochondria and IR780 from other healthy cells.

Further, we showed the synchronous movement of mitochondria and IR780. Through 20 min long confocal fluorescence imaging (Supplementary Movie 7), we observed that IR780 and mitochondria were transported together along TNTs (Fig. 4e), representing that IR780 can hitchhike on mitochondria to transfer from one cell to another without crossing membrane. The 3D rendering of mitochondria and IR780 moving in TNTs (Fig. 4f) clearly revealed the spatial positional relationship among TNTs, mitochondria, and IR780, indicating that IR780 and mitochondria shuttled synchronously inside the TNTs formed between cells in a mutually fused manner.

To demonstrate MAD’s versatility in delivery, we used 10-N-nonylacridine orange bromide (NAO), which employed ΔΨm-independent cardiolipin-specific binding41. NAO showed excellent mitochondrial colocalization (PC = 0.90, Supplementary Fig. S16a) and transmission efficiency comparable to IR780 (Supplementary Fig. S16b), confirming MAD’s broad applicability. Virtually any attachable molecule/peptide—through linkage to triphenylphosphonium (TPP) or mitochondrial targeting peptides (MTP)—could be mitochondria-targeted using our MAD system. In contrast, ICG (hydrophilic/sulfonated) and IR780 (lipophilic/ammonium-iodide) shared heptamethine NIR cores but diverged in localization: IR780 showed mitochondrial accumulation (PC = 0.83) versus ICG’s diffuse distribution (PC = 0.08). Correspondingly, ICG exhibited minimal transfer in MAD (Supplementary Fig. S16b), confirming that mitochondrial loading was essential for intercellular delivery.

Key molecules mediating mitochondrial transport along TNTs deliver a reliable, direct window into the mitochondrion-dependent transport behavior of cargo IR780 within cells. M-sec (TNFAIP2) protein functions as a key regulator of membrane nanotube formation by interacting with the Ral-exocyst pathway42. Knockdown of M-sec protein expression by M-sec siRNA suppressed TNT formation, leading to a decrease in both IR780 and mitochondrial trafficking (Fig. 4g). M-sec was found colocalized with F-actin, while IR780 colocalized with mitochondria (Fig. 4h) in MAD treated cells. These results suggested that the intercellular transmission of IR780, like mitochondria, depends on the formation of intercellular TNTs.

Miro-1 (RHOT1) is a single-pass type IV membrane protein located in the outer mitochondrial membrane, and is involved in mitochondrial trafficking and control of anterograde transport and subcellular distribution of mitochondria43. Knockdown of Miro-1 protein also resulted in decreased intercellular transport of IR780 and mitochondria (Fig. 4i). The colocalization of Miro-1 and IR780 indicated that the intercellular trafficking of IR780 was closely related to Miro-1-mediated active mitochondrial transport (Fig. 4j). In summary, the dependence of cargo IR780 on key proteins in mitochondrial intercellular transport also confirmed its hitchhiking behavior on mitochondria.

We anticipated that MAD with NIR would lead to better phototherapy effects, and meanwhile destroy the rescue road of mitochondria transferring, bringing a ‘utilize first and then eliminate’ effect.

Following, we sought to dissect the reasons for the different therapeutic effects of these regimens, with a particular focus on the mechanistic changes in vivo associated with mitochondrial trafficking. Compared with control, immunofluorescence results of all treatment groups showed that both M-sec and Miro-1 proteins increased in the tumor tissue (Fig. 7i, j), indicating an increase in TNTs and an increase in mitochondrial movement along TNTs. In Supplementary Fig. S23, additional tumor immunofluorescence analyses revealed upregulated KIF5B (microtubule motor for mitochondrial anterograde transport45) and MYO19 (mitochondrial-specific myosin regulator46), confirming coordinated enhancement of mitochondrial trafficking machinery.

After treatment with PINK1 plasmid in group ② (FP), the protein expression of PINK1 decreased further after each administration cycle (Fig. 7k). And in Fig. 7l, the reduction of PINK1 in group ② and ④ inhibited the phosphorylation of Parkin, further inhibited the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-Ⅱ, and reduced the consumption of the substrate p62, whereas this mitophagy process (Fig. 7m) did not change in the absence of FP treatment (group ① and ③). It should be noted that only the PINK1/Parkin pathway is the main pathway of mitophagy19, while the LC3-I/Ⅱ and p62 pathways are common to all autophagy47, and p62 is also related to the apoptosis signaling pathway48, so their changes were not as typical as Parkin phosphorylation. For example, the higher level of p62 in group ③ than in group ② may be related to apoptosis caused by phototherapy. These results highlighted the changes in mitophagy-related pathways in tumors. MAD inhibited the mitophagy pathway49 (Fig. 7m), especially inhibited PINK1 expression and the downstream Parkin phosphorylation, thereby causing MAD to break the homeostasis of cells and promote the movement of cargo in mitochondria between cells to reach the inside of tumor to exert its effects.

Discussion

The difficulty of drug penetration and retention in tumors has long plagued cancer treatment. Currently, mitochondria are a hot topic in the field of tumor treatment50,51. Most studies focus on tumor nutrient deficiency caused by mitochondrial metabolic disorders52 and tumor death caused by mitochondrial ROS bursts53. We believe that mitochondria are highly autonomous organelles, and their unique transport mode between cells without transmembrane deserves further physiology and pathology understanding and application. Here, we speculate that exogenous substances (such as drugs, photosensitizers, imaging agents, etc.) can be carried on mitochondria for non-transmembrane migration and delivery. Therefore, we focused on the mitochondrial dynamics cycle and finally designed the MAD system.

We first demonstrated the benefits of mitophagy in promoting mitochondrial transport, and discovered a facilitating role for SPION in this process. Specifically, we found that decreased mitophagy can improve mitochondrial motility and donor-receptor transporters by increasing the driving forces of mitochondrial movement and the number of freely moving detached mitochondria. Therefore, we constructed a ‘mitochondrial autonomous drive’ (MAD) system. After treatment with MAD, mitochondria showed a strong tendency to move, including an increase in round mitochondria, faster movement speed, and extended travel distance, induced by the increased metabolic and oxidative stress levels. In fact, the mitochondria moved twice as fast in TNTs.

In the presence of cargo, we verified that TNT is the primary conduit for intercellular mitochondrial transfer, and proved the spatiotemporal collaborative behavior of IR780 hitching a ride on mitochondria. The key proteins (M-sec and Miro-1) involved in the mitochondrial movement along TNTs illustrated the dependence of cargo on mitochondria and their spatial relationships. More intuitively, the results of various long-term dynamic observations of mitochondria and IR780 directly revealed the increase in the bidirectional transfer speed of mitochondria between cells and the synchronous transport of IR780 with mitochondria by non-invasive fluorescence microscopy and corresponding motion tracking. In fact, intercellular transport of mitochondria is ubiquitous. MAD not only utilizes this original mode of transportation in tumor cells, but also transforms tumor cells into factories that continuously launch mitochondrial Trojan horses to the surrounding. This is directly demonstrated by the explosive increase in the number of cellular TNTs under MAD treatment, and the disappearance of TNTs accompanied by the death of cells after NIR irradiation revealed that the switch of drug transport in mitochondria can be controlled by the MAD system. Today, precision medicine urgently needs controllable drug delivery systems with stimulus response or targeted delivery capabilities. Taking the phototherapy capabilities of IR780 as an example, MAD brings deeper tumor penetration and higher NIR response capabilities to IR780. In the treatment of tumor-bearing C57BL/6 J mice, the complete MAD system brought 43% complete cure, which was much higher than other groups. These results verified the potential of MAD in the field of tumor treatment and laid the foundation for on-demand drug delivery with the help of mitochondria. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to provide a detailed exploration of the relationship between mitophagy and mitochondrial intercellular movement along TNTs, as well as the mechanism of controllable, self-driving mitochondrial motility applied to deep drug penetration and tumor elimination.

Interestingly, our study exploited two oppositely directed modes of mitochondrial intercellular transport, namely, the export of mitochondria by donor cells to maintain cell homeostasis under metabolically altered conditions, and the rescue mechanism of recipient cells to launch healthy mitochondria to donor cells. This suggests that we may regulate the direction of mitochondrial intercellular transport by constructing different donor-recipient cell states. In addition, tumor cells can obtain mitochondria from different cell types in the tumor microenvironment, including mesenchymal stem cells/stromal cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, T cells, macrophages, astrocytes and other cell types, and these different mitochondrial transfer axes may have different effects on recipient cancer cells, mostly related to tumor growth and resistance to treatment23. This inspires us that it is reasonable to adopt a ‘utilize first and then eliminate’ strategy for mitochondrial intercellular transport, and destroying it after drug delivery can effectively prevent the self-rescue behavior of tumors.

The application scope of this mitochondrial self-driving delivery and therapy system is far beyond the field of tumor treatment. The transfer of mitochondria between cells occurs in many types of cells and is closely related to cell metabolism, cancer, immune response regulation, tissue homeostasis maintenance, and wound healing23. The first clinical application involving artificially forced mitochondrial transfer was oocyte transplantation, and donor mitochondrial DNA was detected in children born using this procedure54. In addition, there are clinical trials in which patients with large-scale mtDNA deletion syndromes have their autologous hematopoietic stem cells loaded with healthy maternal mitochondria prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation55. These studies have shown the great application prospects of intercellular mitochondrial transfer. Nevertheless, the dilemma that most current studies focus on in vitro experiments reminds us that it is crucial to have a deep understanding of the molecular mechanisms behind the intercellular mitochondrial transfer axis. Leveraging mitochondria for migration and delivery through non-transmembrane pathway could bypass the intercellular barrier to achieve deep penetration and exert a powerful tumor therapeutic effect, which may have far-reaching potential in medicine. Looking forward, further related work is still needed to realize in vivo experiments and even clinical applications of drugs loaded on mitochondria. Optimizing future MAD iterations could include functionalizing the delivery carriers with tumor-homing ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides) to promote enhanced and more specific tumor accumulation, thereby potentially mitigating off-target effects and associated long-term cellular and systemic consequences. We believe that MAD system enables a controllable intracellular drug delivery approach for precision medicine.

Cell culture

B16F10 melanoma cells were originated from American Type Culture Collection and were cultured in DMEM medium with FBS (10%), penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (0.1 mg/mL). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Donor cells and recipient cells system

B16F10 cells (2 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded and divided into two experimental groups: donor cells (D) and recipient cells (R). siPINK1 (50 nM)/FP (Plasmid: 1 µg/mL) were given in D-siPINK1/FP and R-siPINK1/FP group for 48 h or 72 h, respectively. Then the donor cells were treated with FIS (3.0 µg/mL for IR780) for 4 h and labeled by MitoTracker and Hoechst 33342, while recipient cells were stained with CMFDA and Hoechst 33342. Donor cells were transferred and co-incubated with recipient cells for 4 h. Laser scanning confocal microscope (LAS X 3.7, LSCM, TCS SP8 STED, Leica, Germany) was performed to observe the intercellular communication between donor and recipient cells. Recipient cells were segmented using Cellpose (https://www.cellpose.org), followed by processing of the resultant masks in ImageJ (1.54 f) to define cellular boundaries. Individual recipient cells were isolated by subtracting these masks from raw images. Subsequent ‘Analyze Particles’ analysis (with size thresholding and ‘Add to Manager’ enabled) generated discrete regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to individual cells. Mitochondrial and IR780 fluorescence channels were then imported, converted to 8-bit, thresholded at a fixed value across all experimental groups, and converted to binary single-point representations. These puncta were normalized to unity (=1) using the Divide function, enabling quantification via the Measure tool which recorded RawIntDen values—representing the summed intensity of all pixels within each recipient cell ROI—to determine counts of mitochondrial and IR780 puncta per cell. During quantification, RawIntDen values were normalized against recipient cell area to account for size-dependent variability, thereby yielding mitochondrial/IR780 puncta density per cell as the definitive metric for cargo delivery efficiency to recipient cells.

For inhibition experiments, donor and recipient cells were cultured respectively in DMEM with FP containing different inhibitors, cytochalasin D (0.5 µM), L-778123 (10 μM) and dynasore (80 μM) for 24 h. Then donor and recipient cells were treated with FIS and stained as the same steps as above, and co-incubated in DMEM with inhibitors for 4 h, respectively, followed by imaging.

For M-sec and Miro-1 knockdown experiments, donor and recipient cells were cultured respectively in DMEM with FP, and siM-sec/siMiro-1 (100 nM) were transfected into both cells via PEI25k for 48 h. Then donor and recipient cells were treated with FIS and stained as the same steps as above, followed by imaging.

Bleedthrough control for MitoTracker Deep Red and IR780, and cellular autofluorescence control following mitophagy modulation, are provided in Supplementary Figs. S24 and S25, respectively.

Western blot for protein detection

B16F10 cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (including PMSF) and protein concentration was unified with Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit. The protein samples were boiled, separated by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred with PVDF membrane. Next, membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk (5% BSA for phosphorylated proteins), and then primary antibody and secondary antibody were incubated in sequence. Finally, Immobilon Western HRP Substrate (Millipore, USA) was added and a gel image processing system (Amersham Imager 600, GE, USA) was used to analyze the gray value of the target band. Protein expression was quantified as the intensity ratio of target protein bands relative to loading control bands. Complete uncropped western blot data were shown in Supplementary Figs. S26–29.

Structure illumination microscopy (SIM) for super-resolution fluorescent imaging

To analyze mitochondrial morphology, Multi-SIM system (NanoInsights-Tech) with a Nikon ×100, 1.49-NA oil objective and imaging system were used for 3D-SIM imaging. After FP treated for 72 h and pAcGFP1-Mito transfected for 48 h, cells were seeded onto confocal glass-bottomed dishes 18–24 h prior to imaging. 3D-SIM images were acquired with 488 nm laser. Z-stack performance was set as 0.27 μm/slice per field. Cells were maintained in 37 °C and 5% CO2 during imaging. Multi-SIM images were analyzed using ImageJ. Following threshold optimization, shape descriptors were enabled in ‘Set Measurements’ (with Limit to threshold selected). ‘Analyze Particles’ was then executed, with the Aspect Ratio (‘AR’) column recording the major/minor axis ratio (‘L/W’) as a metric of morphological elongation.

To observe real-time mitochondrial movement, Polar-SIM system (Airy Technology) with a Nikon ×100, 1.49-NA oil objective and imaging system were used for 2D-SIM imaging. After FP treated for 72 h and pAcGFP1-Mito/ LifeAct-mCherry/ pDsRed2-Mito transfected for 48 h, cells were seeded onto confocal glass-bottomed dishes 18–24 h prior to imaging. 2D-SIM images were acquired with 488 nm and 561 nm laser for 36 loops in movement within cells and 120/ 30 loops for movement between cells. Cells were maintained in 37 °C and 5% CO2 during imaging. ND processing in NIS-Elements (Nikon, Japan) was used to correct the jitter of long-term images. Polar-SIM images were analyzed by Imaris software (9.0.0). Surface rendering of mitochondria was generated in consecutive images. Tracking project was used to depict traces of single mitochondrion. Through autoregressive algorithm, tracks with >10 s duration for movement within cells and >20 s duration for movement between cells were analyzed and dynamic parameters were calculated. Quantitative motility parameters—‘Track Speed Mean’ and ‘Track Displacement Length’—were acquired in Imaris (‘Statistics’ panel) and subjected to statistical analysis.

Cellular metabolic assays

Cellular OXPHOS were assessed with Seahorse XF Pro extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Briefly, B16F10 cells were cultured as 2 × 104 cells/well in a XFe96 cell culture microplate. Prior to metabolic measurements, supernatant was removed and replaced with Seahorse assay medium. The cells were then incubated in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h. Oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and R&A (0.5 μM) were injected sequentially. Finally, Hoechst33342 reagent was injected to quantify the cell number using a high-content analyzer (CellVoyager CV8000, Japan) for normalization. The parameters in the assay were analyzed in Seahorse Analytics (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Flow cytometry for in vitro detection

Cell suspension was prepared in PBS after incubating. For samples that required staining, DCFH-DA (10 μM), MitoSOX (5 μM) or Rhod-2 (5 μM) were added to the cells for 20 min, followed by washing with PBS and passing through a 200-mesh cell sieve. Samples were examined using Cytoflex flow cytometer (CytExpert 2.5, Beckman Coulter, USA). All data were processed using FlowJo software (V10). Details of the gating strategy were provided in Supplementary Figs. S30–33. Typically, 10,000 live cell-gated events were acquired for analysis.

RNA-seq for gene expression

RNA sequencing and analysis were performed by Novogene. Specifically, sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The raw data were filtered to obtain clean data, and Q20, Q30 and GC content of the clean data were calculated. The reference genome was indexed using HISAT2 v2.0.5, and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5. FeatureCounts (1.5.0-p3) was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene. The FPKM of each gene was then calculated based on the length of the gene and the number of reads mapped to the gene. Differential expression analysis between two comparison combinations was performed using edgeR (3.22.5). Genes with FDR <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 were identified as differentially expressed genes. The DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov) was used to analyze the GO and KEGG datasets. The STRING database (http://string-db.org) was used for functional protein association network analysis of differentially expressed genes.

Intracellular ROS detection

B16F10 cells (2 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the cells were incubated with FP for 72 h and FIS for 4 h, respectively. The nontreated cells were used as control. Then, DCFH-DA (10 μM) was added at 37 °C for 20 min, and the cells were irradiated (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 20 s) after washing. Finally, cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (5 μg/mL) and observed by CLSM (ZEN 3.9, LSM880, Zeiss, Germany). All images were processed in ImageJ following a standardized workflow: raw data underwent 8-bit conversion followed by application of identical channel-specific thresholds across experimental groups before intensity measurement. Per-cell ROS generation was quantified as the ratio of ROS channel integrated density (IntDen) to nuclear channel IntDen, establishing a normalized metric for robust intergroup comparative analysis; this protocol was systematically applied to all subsequent analogous experiments to ensure methodological consistency.

Calcein-AM/PI staining

B16F10 cells (2 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Then the cells were incubated with FP for 72 h and FIS for 4 h, respectively. Then, the cells were irradiated (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 20 s) and stained by Calcein-AM (2.0 μM) and PI (4.5 μM) for 20 min, and then observed by CLSM (STELLARIS 8 STED, Leica, Germany), respectively. Viability quantification was performed using the dead/live cell ratio derived from PI channel IntDen normalized to calcein channel IntDen.

3D B16F10 tumor spheroid model for penetration

A three-dimensional cell spheroid model was established using a 96-well plate. 20 g/L agarose dissolved at 80 °C was added to each well, and 3 × 103 B16F10 cells were inoculated and cultured until the diameter of the cell spheroid was about 100 μm. The cell spheroids were treated with FP for 72 h and FIS for 4 h, then gently rinsed with PBS and fixed with 4% (m/v) paraformaldehyde. Finally, the cell spheroids were scanned layer by layer along the Z-axis to observe the penetration of tumor cell spheroids at different depths using CLSM (TCS SP8 STED, Leica, Germany). IR780 distribution was subsequently quantified by measuring layer-specific mean fluorescence intensity across all optical sections in ImageJ.

B16F10 solid tumor penetration in vivo

B16F10 cells were inoculated into the right flank of C57BL/6 J mouse at 1 × 106 cells each. On the 10th day, tumor volume reached about 200–300 mm3. Mice were randomly divided into 2 groups: FIS and MAD, with one mouse per group for the representative experiment presented. After FP (10 μg plasmid for 20 g mice) was given peritumorally for 72 h and FIS (12 μg IR780 for 20 g mice) was given for 4 h, the tumor was removed and fixed with tissue fixative. After dehydration with 30% sucrose, the tumor was embedded in OCT embedding medium. Then the tumor tissue was sliced into frozen sections using a freezing microtome (Leica CM3050S, Germany), stained with WGA and Hoechst33342, and observed with CLSM (STELLARIS 8 STED, Leica, Germany).

Anti-cancer therapy in mice bearing B16F10 solid tumor

B16F10 cells were inoculated into the right flank of C57BL/6 J mouse at 1 × 106 cells each. On the 8th day, tumor volume reached about 100 mm3. Mice were randomly divided into 6 groups: ① Control (n = 6), ② FP (n = 6), ③ FIS + NIR (+) (n = 6), ④ MAD + NIR (+) (n = 6), ⑤ FIS + NIR (++) (n = 7) and ⑥ MAD + NIR (++) (n = 7). Animals were treated peritumorally according to schedule. NIR was irradiated with 808 nm laser at 1.0 W/cm2 for 5 min. Tumors were measured every other day using a vernier caliper, and tumor volume (mm3) was calculated as (long diameter × short diameter2)/2. Total body weight was routinely measured as a measure of any gross toxicity. When the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3, body weight loss surpassed 15% of the initial weight, or any other signs of distress were observed, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia in accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center.

When the tumor grew to the 12th day, two mice in each of groups ① and ② were euthanized and the tumors were isolated for western blot experiments; on the 19th day, mice in groups ①, ②, ③ and ④ were euthanized as shown in Supplementary Fig. S17 for H&E staining, Ki67 staining, western blot, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry and blood routine analysis; on the 26th day, the remaining mice in groups ①, ②, ③ and ④ were euthanized for western blot experiments; the survival of mice in groups ⑤ and ⑥ were observed until 60 days. Immunofluorescence data were quantified by normalizing target protein IntDen to nuclear channel IntDen. Western blot expression levels were calculated as target protein band intensity relative to loading control (GAPDH) band intensity, with both analyses performed using ImageJ.

Hematoxylin-eosin, Ki67, and immunofluorescence staining

Tumor tissues and major organs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and prepared into paraffin sections and frozen sections for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analysis, respectively. Immunohistochemistry was performed for H&E and Ki67 staining. Immunofluorescence was used for TUNEL assay and the detection of protein expression. All frozen sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, blocked with 2% BSA, and incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, secondary antibody (AF488) was added for 1 h and DAPI was added for 5 min at RT. H&E and Ki67 images were taken using a digital pathology slide scanner (Nano Zoomer, Hamamatsu, Japan). Immunofluorescence results were observed using CLSM (STELLARIS 8 STED, Leica, Germany). Quantitative analysis involved normalization of target protein IntDen to nuclear IntDen in ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as the mean or mean ± standard deviation (SD). SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26) and GraphPad Prism 8 were used for statistical analysis. Comparison between two groups was analyzed by two-tailed unpaired t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s/Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, or repeated-measure ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was set as a significant difference. All experiments were repeated independently with similar results for the reproducibility of the results.
