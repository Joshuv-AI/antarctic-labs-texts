Real-time visualization of collagen assembly uncovers metastable properties in hierarchical organization
Authors: Bohuan Fang, Dan Zeng, Zhiguang Duan, Bei Gao, Tong Zhu, Xiao He, Liming Xu, Xinglan Liu, Qixin He, Luobin Huang, Huangchen Shen, Zhaoting Yuan, Siqi Li, Yawen Jiang, Yuxi Zhang, Yinghui Feng, Chuanxi Zhang, Hao Wei, John Z H Zhang, Grant J Jensen, Daidi Fan, Lujia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollagen, a central component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), precisely regulates tissue mechanical properties and biological functions through hierarchical assembly, playing a vital role in maintaining homeostasis. However, the molecular mechanism of assembly remains poorly understood, limiting insights into tissue remodeling, aging, and ECM-related diseases. Here, we employ time-resolved cryo-electron microscopy to resolve two critical hierarchical intermediates in fibrillar collagen assembly, proposing the 3D collagen assembly pathway. We identify a metastable triple-helical conformation as the fundamental assembly unit, whose structural lability propagates through the assembly cascade, rendering the process sensitive to microenvironmental perturbations. Through hierarchical assembly, metastable intermediates achieve enhanced structural stability, culminating in the formation of stable collagen that retains its integrity under physiological conditions. In contrast, structural defects in intermediates lead to aberrant assembly and disruption of ECM integrity. Functional assays reveal that intermediates lacking D-band retain biological activity. Our findings redefine the fibrillar collagen assembly as a hierarchical, time-resolved cascade driven by metastable intermediates and propose the fundamental F-Z-F rules. The metastable triple helix provides a structural basis for hierarchical assembly and suggests a potential link between abnormal fibrillar collagen assembly and aging-related matrix dysfunction, offering valuable insights into collagen assembly and its role in aging diseases.Subject terms: Protein folding, Structural biology, Cell adhesion

Introduction

Collagen, the most abundant protein in the human body (accounting for approximately 30% of the total body protein), forms the core structural scaffold of the extracellular matrix (ECM) through its highly ordered hierarchical assembly1–3.It provides essential mechanical support to tissues and regulates cellular behavior, playing a central role in key physiological processes, including tissue development, ECM homeostasis, and regenerative repair4–7. Based on their supramolecular organization, collagen in ECM can be broadly categorized into fibrillar (types I, II, III,V, XI) and non-fibrillar (types IV, VII, IX, and XII)8. The former confer tensile strength to tissues, whereas the latter are vital for basement membrane stability and cell signaling mediation9,10. Type I collagen, the major fibrillar collagen in the ECM, plays a critical role in ECM integrity through its synthesis, degradation, and remodeling11,12.Disruptions in these processes can directly lead to ECM structural disorganization, impairing cellular mechanoreception and contributing to various pathological conditions, including skin laxity, chronic wound healing defects, organ fibrosis, and cardiovascular stiffening7,13–15. These collagen-associated disorders not only compromise tissue function but also contribute significantly to age-related morbidity and mortality16. Therefore, deciphering the molecular mechanisms of collagen assembly is crucial for unraveling ECM homeostasis and the pathogenesis of collagen-associated diseases.

Static structural studies of assembly have demonstrated that collagen fibrils exhibit a characteristic pattern of alternating light and dark repeating bands (known as D-bands) under an electron microscopy17. Cross-sectional analyses reveal well-ordered filaments that are approximately 1.5–2.0 nm in diameter18. Fiber diffraction analyses further identified the filament as collagen’s fundamental substructure, named triple helix19. Based on the D-periodicity pattern, Smith subsequently proposed the classic mathematical model, named microfibril, another substructure hierarchically superior to the triple helix20. Thereafter, various fiber diffraction models incorporating 2-, 3-, and 8-strand configurations were developed to depict the microfibril21–26. A major breakthrough was made when Orgel applied synchrotron radiation and single-crystal X-ray diffraction to collagen, leading to the first electron density map of collagen and the establishment of a 5-strand microfibril model27,28. The triple helix and microfibril, definitively characterized by static structural biology, were identified as fundamental substructures of collagen, have become the foundational elements in modern collagen research. However, whether these substructures exist in the same form during the dynamic, in vivo assembly processes, and how they are assembled at the molecular level, remains unknown.

In vitro, collagen extracts self-assemble into fibrils that exhibit characteristic D-banding pattern, serving as a foundational model for studying collagen dynamics29. Monitoring changes in light scattering intensity has established that collagen assembly follows a three-phase process: an initial lag phase, followed by rapid growth, and a final plateau phase30. The growth and plateau phases predominantly consist of fibrils with heterogeneous diameters31,32. In contrast, studies focusing on the lag phase remain extremely limited. Robert et al. were the first to capture intermediates in the earliest stages of collagen assembly using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), establishing the staged nature of the lag phase33. More recently, advances in high-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM) have enabled real-time visualization of dynamic assembly process with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution34,35. Nevertheless, the transient character and limited reproducibility of the natural collagen lag phase result that current studies largely provide only partial morphological snapshots of intermediates, leaving the molecular-level details of dynamic assembly unresolved31,36,37. Recombinant collagen, produced through genetic engineering, closely mimics the structural properties of natural collagen while offering superior production control and reproducibility38–40. Using this system, Lesley et al. successfully isolated a 4-nm-diameter intermediate with polydisperse length distributions that structurally resembled natural substructure microfibrils41 and provided the first structural parameters associated with dynamic assembly, including inter-triple-helical spacing and intra-assembly distances. However, the freeze-drying pretreatment introduced sample heterogeneity, which reduced diffraction resolution and limited the structural insights, constrained by the limitations of the Smith 5-strand microfibril model. Consequently, real-time structural characterization of the dynamic assembly process of collagen remains elusive, and the hierarchical pathway by which collagen assembles from single chains into mature fibers is poorly understood.

In this study, we utilized a post-translational modification (PTM)-free, simplified, and controllable model (CF-1552) whose assembly rate is markedly slower than that of natural collagen, thereby enabling a controllable recapitulation of the natural lag phase. By systematically integrating the higher-order assembly characteristics (growth and plateau phases) of natural collagen, we successfully proposed the complete collagen assembly pathway (Fig. 1, Supplementary Video 1). Using time-resolved cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we resolved two critical assembly intermediates at structural detail, thereby uncovering the fundamental mechanism of collagen assembly. Our structural analysis revealed that the transition from a stable triple-helical conformation to a metastable, structurally relaxed state is essential for enabling hierarchical organization. These metastable structural intermediates endow fibrillar collagen assembly with unique kinetic fragility, heightening its sensitivity to environmental perturbations and predisposing it to premature termination or pathological crosslinking. Notably, their metastability progressively diminishes as assembly proceeds. Beyond deepening the understanding of the evolution of collagen assembly, our findings suggest that the assembly pathway may inherently encode regulatory information relevant to mechanotransduction and aging. This provides a conceptual framework for exploring how extracellular matrix influences physiological regulation and may shifting the focus from conventional “cell-signaling” modulation strategies toward innovative approached centered on “matrix-structure” remodeling.

Collagen assembly: A 3D LEGO-like hierarchical pathway from triple helix to fiber.

Collagen undergoes a hierarchical assembly driven by metastable intermediates, proceeding through stepwise stabilization. Assembly proceeds through three stages: (I) 1D elongation—single chains →triple helix→spindlefibril (microfibiril)→axialfibril; (II) 2D lateral growth—axialfibrils assemble laterally, forming the fibrils with characteristic D-period; (III) 3D thickening—fibrils accrete layers to form mature fibers for tissue integration. Notably, axialfibril formation may exhibit concentration-dependent, transitioning from end-to-end assembly at low concentrations to a staggered lateral arrangement at high concentrations.

Results

Model protein-recombinant collagen CF-1552

Natural collagen exhibits instantaneous assembly and hierarchical complexity, making it challenging to capture and analyze the dynamic intermediate structures34,42,43. We construct a simplified model to study fibrillar collagen assembly under controlled conditions. Collagen consists of repeating, highly conserved Gly–X–Y triplets44, and based on this, we selected a segment of natural collagen for assembly studies. Gly–Pro–Hyp (G-P-O), the most abundant G-X-Y triplet, is crucial for triple-helix stability45. However, G-P-O-rich sequences have limited assembly capability46 and require modifications for higher-level assembly47,48. Moreover, natural collagen sequence alternates between G-P-O and non-G-P-O regions, the true assembly potential likely lies in the non-G-P-O sequences47,49. Therefore, we excluded G-P-O fragments and focused on the role of non-G-P-O segments in hierarchical assembly. Additionally, we employed an E. coli expression system to avoid complex post-translational modifications (PTMs), thereby maximizing the controllability of the assembly process50,51. The Gly-Ala-Pro (G-A-P) rich region plays a crucial role in forming the collagen triple helix and facilitating higher-level assembly52. We analyzed the sequence of type I collagen (NCBI: NP 000079.2) and identified a G-A-P-enriched region at positions 713-727(GAPGAPGSQGAPGLQ), which is highly conserved across multiple species. This 52-tandem repeat sequence has also been extensively studied53,54. Based on these findings, we selected this repeat fragment and named it CF-1552. Preliminary experiments further confirmed that CF-1552 exhibits self-assembly capability (Supplementary Fig. 1), with its assembled products closely resembling natural collagen assembly intermediates33–35,55. These features make CF-1552 a PTM-free, simplified and controllable model for probing the dynamic collagen assembly process.

The regulation of CF-1552 assembly

To obtain well-structured CF-1552 assemblies, we conducted a comprehensive biophysical analysis of temperature, pH, and ionic strength effects on the self-assembly process. Structural evolution was monitored by negative-stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM), revealing distinct assembly intermediates and their morphological transitions at nanometer resolution.

For temperature, we monitored transmittance and physical state transitions across a controlled temperature gradient (4−37 °C). At 4 °C, assemblies exhibited low transmittance (10%) and formed milky gels (Fig. 2A, 4 °C). TEM analysis revealed the assemblies comprised of 1.5-nm diameter filaments (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), a dimension characteristic of the triple helix substructure17, thereby confirming that discrete triple helices arise during dynamic assembly. Upon warming to 10 °C, transmittance sharply increased to 70%, with assemblies becoming transparent (Fig. 2A, 10 °C). Concomitantly, triple helices assembled into short, nonuniform spindle-shaped structures (Fig. 2B 10 °C), indicating the onset of higher-order hierarchical assembly. Further temperature increased (18–37 °C), yielded >90% transmittance (Fig. 2A, 18–37 °C). At 18 °C, assemblies adopted longer, denser, and more uniform morphologies (Fig. 2B 18 °C), suggesting enhanced assembly capacity. However, at 25–37 °C, structures progressively shortened and dispersed (Fig. 2B 25–37 °C), reflecting thermal disruption of assembly. These transitions aligned with previous studies on natural collagen56. Specifically, the assembly capacity is promoted up to an optimal temperature, beyond which triple helix stability is compromised and impaired assembly.

To elucidate pH effects on CF-1552 assembly, we monitored zeta potential across a pH gradient (3–9) (Fig. 2C). At pH 3, the zeta potential was positive, indicating the molecules carried a positive charge. TEM analysis revealed the assemblies were sparse, short with poor assembly competence (Fig. 2D pH 3). When the pH increased to 4–5, approaching the isoelectric point (pI=4.2), the zeta potential neared zero and assemblies became longer, and more abundant (Fig. 2D pH 4 and pH 5), demonstrating enhanced assembly capabilities. As the pH increased to near-neutral or slightly alkaline conditions (pH 6–9), the zeta potential became negative, indicating that molecules carried a negative charge. Under these conditions, the assemblies were shorter, less concentrated (Fig. 2D pH 6 and pH 7, Supplementary Fig. 2C pH 8 and 2D pH 9) and displayed reduced assembly capabilities. These findings are consistent with the behavior of natural collagen57. Specifically, a pH proximity to pI promoted assembly, whereas acidic or alkaline conditions weakened assembly capabilities owing to electrostatic repulsion. Notably, in strongly acidic environments significantly disrupted triple helix formation, likely due to the higher proton concentration in acidic environments hindered hydrogen bond formation.

In addition to temperature and pH, ionic strength plays a critical role in regulating the assembly. A schematic diagram illustrating the impact of salt ions on assembly is presented (Fig. 2E). Under salt-free conditions (0 mM), TEM analysis revealed the assemblies tended to be long rods at a high density and were prone to aggregation (Fig. 2F 0 mM). Increasing the ionic strength to 50–300 mM did not significantly alter assembly morphology but led to a gradual reduction in assembly density (Fig. 2F 50 mM, 150 mM and 300 mM). At 500 mM, axial growth was inhibited, resulting in shorter assemblies with reduced uniformity (Fig. 2F 500 mM) and markedly weakened assembly capability. These results indicated that the ionic-strength regulation of CF-1552 aligned with that of natural collagen58, where low ionic strength enhanced high assembly and excessive salt disrupts supramolecular organization. However, owing to sequence differences, natural collagen contains partially charged amino acids, and a specific ionic strength is required to shield the surface charges of these amino acids effectively.

These results demonstrate that CF-1552 is capable of forming filamentous assemblies and that its assembly process is exquisitely regulated by temperature, pH, and ionic strength, exhibiting dynamic behaviors closely resembling those of natural collagen. Crucially, our study offered compelling evidence supporting the role of the triple helix as an independent structural module in fibrillar collagen assembly, addressing a long-standing debate in collagen biology. Concurrently, triple helices displayed remarkable stability at 4 °C, indicative of a cold-induced conformational locking that may restrict molecular flexibility and hinder higher assembly.

Metastable triple helix drives hierarchical self assembly

Following definition of CF-1552 assembly parameters and successful generation of the fully assembled product, we conducted structural analyses benchmarked against bovine Achilles tendon collagen (BAT) extracts as a natural collagen reference. All samples were processed using identical protocols prior to characterization (Fig. 3G). To elucidate the structural basis of CF-1552, we first analyzed the circular dichroism (CD) spectra (Fig. 3A). As expected, BAT assemblies exhibited a characteristic triple-helical CD spectrum, with peak minima at approximately 198 nm and maxima around 220 nm. In contrast, CF-1552 assemblies displayed an atypical spectrum, with negative absorbance at around 220 nm, a signature indicative of a random coil56. This appeared contradictory to our assembly regulation experiments (Fig. 2B2) demonstrating CF-1552 form well-defined, regular nanostructures under 18 °C. Given the temperature-dependent CD absorption characteristics of natural fibrillar collagen57, we further analyzed CF-1552 assemblies at 4 °C, 10 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C. At lower temperatures (4 °C and 10 °C), we observed positive absorption at 220 nm, which gradually diminished with increasing temperature (Fig. 3A), resembling the temperature-responsive spectral behavior of BAT (Supplementary Fig. 3), strongly suggesting that CF-1552 assemblies adopted a triple-helical conformation. Interestingly, CF-1552 retained assembly competence even under conditions where negative absorption at 220 nm was observed (Fig. 2B3-B5). Notably, assemblies formed at 18 °C displayed superior assembly capacity than those at 4 °C and 10 °C, where positive absorption was observed. Therefore, we proposed that negative absorption at 220 nm may not random coil, but instead a more relaxed triple-helical conformation. This conformation is likely driven by thermally induced weakening of hydrogen bonding and interchain interactions. Collectively, these findings suggested that a tightly wound triple helix predominates at lower temperature and a moderately relaxed conformation represented a thermodynamically favorable state that facilitated higher-order assembly.

To confirm that CF-1552 assemblies adopt a triple helix rather than random coils, we measured thermal melting (Tm) defined as the transition point at which the triple helix unravels into a random coil58. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to determine the Tm. Immediately after dissolution (0 h), no Tm or assemblies were detected (Supplementary Fig. 4), confirming the absence of organized structure. After 24 h of assembly, a clear thermal transition emerged at 39 °C (Fig. 3B), demonstrating the formation of stable and ordered triple-helical structures. This finding conclusively established that the negative CD absorbance at 220 nm represented a triple helical conformation rather than random coils. Comparative analysis with BAT assemblies (Tm = 42.5 °C) revealed slightly lower thermal stability for CF-1552. We attributed this difference to BAT’s high G-P-O triplet content, which formed two hydrogen bonds per unit: the canonical Gly-X bond plus a unique Hyp-mediated water-bridged interaction59. These supplementary interactions promoted tighter chain packing and significantly elevated thermal stability60,61. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that CF-1552 assembles into triple-helical structures resembling natural fibrillar collagen, albeit in a more relaxed conformational state.

We further employed Raman and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to comprehensively characterize the secondary structures of CF-1552 and BAT assemblies. Raman spectra of both assemblies showed pronounced absorption in the 200-600 cm⁻¹ region (Fig. 3C), a characteristic fingerprint of triple helical structure62,63, confirming that CF-1552 maintained a triple helical foundation. Further analysis of the 200–1750 cm⁻¹ region, encompassing random coils, β-sheets, and α-helices64, revealed similar vibrational profiles between CF-1552 and BAT assemblies. Notably, both displayed similar absorption peaks within the 1600–1680 cm⁻¹ region, indicative of analogous hydrogen-bonding patterns and secondary structure content. FTIR spectra further supported these findings, with both assemblies displaying characteristic amide I (1600–1700 cm⁻¹), amide II (1500–1600 cm⁻¹), and amide III (1200–1300 cm⁻¹) bands indicative of collagen (Fig. 3D). To quantify secondary structure content, we performed Fourier self-deconvolution and Gaussian curve fitting of the amide I region (Fig. 3E, F)65,66. Both assemblies exhibited comparable β-sheet content (Supplementary Table 1), while CF-1552 showed a slightly lower polyproline-II (PP-II) helix content than BAT, consistent with its lower CD signal intensity (Fig. 3A), a known correlate of PP-II content67. Notably, CF-1552 assemblies exhibited a higher proportion of β-turn structure, likely resulting from PP-II to β-turn conversion during assembly57. We postulated that the more relaxed conformational state of CF-1552 triple helices may facilitate this transition. To assess triple-helical integrity, we calculated the AIII/A1450 ratio, a validated indicator of collagen folding68. CF-1552 assemblies yielded a ratio of 0.994, slightly lower than BAT assemblies (1.026) but significantly higher than denatured collagen (0.60)69, indicating substantial, albeit marginally reduced, triple-helical preservation.

Collectively, our analyses demonstrated that CF-1552 self-assembled into an ordered architecture that preserved both the triple-helical framework and key structural features of natural fibrillar collagen. Crucially, the characteristic CD absorption of the triple helix at 220 nm decreases as the temperature rises. At low temperature, molecular vibrations decrease, and the structure tends to adopt a more compact triple-helix conformation, which may lead to the formation of more hydrogen bonds, thereby increasing the absorption at 220 nm70, resulting in a positive value in the ensemble CD spectrum. As the temperature increases, the vibrational mobility is enhanced, structural stability decreases, the number of hydrogen bonds reduces, and some triple helices gradually disassemble, causing the absorption in the ensemble CD spectrum to decrease, and even negative values. However, this does not imply that the triple helix completely disappears, but rather that they are fewer and looser. The above results suggest that the triple helix exists in a dynamic equilibrium between assembly and disassembly, characterized by reversibility, fragility, and uneven looseness, which we define as a metastable triple helix. This structure is regulated by factors such as sequence composition, temperature, ionic strength, and pH, and possesses assembly potential, retaining typical triple helix features such as a stable Tm value and characteristic absorption bands in infrared and Raman spectra. Therefore, the metastable triple helix can be confirmed by CD signatures comprising a negative band at ~198 nm and a 220 nm feature that may cross zero (from weakly positive to slightly negative), along with morphological evidence of ordered assembly and consistent typical triple-helix spectroscopic markers in infrared and Raman spectra. This framework establishes a structural foundation for fibrillar collagen assembly and provides benchmarks for assessing collagen assembly competence.

CF-1552 recapitulates the lag phase characteristic of fibrillar collagen assembly

After structural characterization, dynamic assembly behavior was investigated. We recorded real-time turbidity measurements and utilized atomic force microscopy (AFM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to characterize the morphology of the CF-1552 and BAT assemblies. BAT assembly exhibited a typical “S”-shaped curve (Fig. 4A). From 0 to 0.25 h, the absorbance was low, representing the lag phase. During this phase, the assemblies primarily consisted of small fibrils with a D-banding feature (width ~90 nm, height ~15 nm) (Fig. 4B 0.25 h; Fig. 4C). From 0.25 to 1 h, the absorbance rapidly increased, marking the growth phase. During this stage, small fibrils underwent lateral interactions, merging to form larger D-banded structures (width ~150 nm, height ~24 nm) (Fig. 4B 0.5 h, Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 5). The remarkable lateral expansion indicated that lateral assembly primarily drove the turbidity rise. From 1-2 h, the absorbance plateaued, signifying the plateau phase. Compared to the assemblies observed early in this phase (width ~210 nm, height ~29 nm) (Fig. 4B 1h, Fig. 4C), those observed later (width ~223 nm, height ~44 nm) showed continued vertical growth (Fig. 4B 2h, Fig. 4C), highlighting that vertical assembly dominated during this phase.

Compared to the BAT, the CF-1552 assemblies were smaller in size, with a less pronounced but still “S”-like turbidity profile (Fig. 4D). From 0 to 0.5 h, the absorbance was low, corresponding to the “lag phase”. During this phase, numerous small aggregates predominantly formed as short spindle-shaped products (90%) with length of ~180 nm, width of ~25 nm, and height of ~1.5 nm, which we termed “spindlefibril” (Fig. 4E 0.5 h, Fig. 4F). Subsequently, the spindlefibrils were observed to assemble axially through their “tips” with a staggered overlap of ~50% length (indicated by arrows in Fig. 4E 6 h, 12 h, 24 h). These assemblies elongated markedly, forming rod-like structures (~95%), which we designated as “axialfibril”. From 1 to 6 h, the turbidity slightly increased, marking the transition to the “growth phase”, during which the predominant species were 2–3 spindlefibrils aggregates (~90%) with dimensions of ~300 nm in length, ~25 nm in width, and ~2.5 nm in height (Fig. 4E 6 h, Fig. 3F). From 12 to 24 h, the turbidity stabilized, indicating the “plateau phase”. During this period, axialfibrils continued elongating, forming higher-order aggregates comprising 5–6 spindlefibrils (~70%) with average dimensions of ~500 nm in length, ~25 nm in width, and ~2.5 nm in height (Fig. 4E 12 h, Fig. 4F). After 24 h, the length increased to ~940 nm, with some regions exceeding 1 μm, suggesting occasional incorporation of seven or more spindlefibrils (80%) (Fig. 4E 24 h, Fig. 4F). It is noteworthy that minor populations of intermediates with different aggregation degrees were present throughout the assembly process, possibly due to local concentration fluctuations71. Overall, the assembly pattern of CF-1552, initially forming spindle-shaped intermediate (spindlefibril) followed by their axial fusion into elongated fibrils (axialfibril), closely mirrors the lag phase observed in natural fibrillar collagen assembly33,34. This suggests that CF-1552 effectively recapitulates the early stages of natural fibrillar collagen assembly.

Based on turbidity curves, we assessed the assembly kinetics of two proteins (Supplementary Fig. 6). Results show that the CF-1552 assembly rate (k = 4.20 × 10⁻³ min⁻¹) is significantly slower than that of BAT (k = 6.25 × 10⁻² min⁻¹), providing theoretical support for CF-1552’s ability to recapitulate the assembly process at a controlled, slower rate. Through dynamic visualization, we identified at least two structurally distinct intermediates (spindlefibirl, axialfbiril) bridging the transition from triple helices to mature D-banded fibrils. By integrating the BAT assembly process (growth and plateau phases), we have visualized a complete collagen assembly pathway and proposed a 3D layer-by-layer assembly model. Specifically, the lag phase involves one-dimensional elongation, where triple helices rapid aggregate into spindlefibrils, which then axially to form axialfibrils. The growth phase is characterized by two-dimensional lateral growth, during which axialfibrils align laterally to generate D-banded fibrils. In the plateau phase, collagen entered a three-dimensional thickening stage, with vertical stacking and compaction of fibrils leading to a significant increase in fibril height. Together, these findings define a spatiotemporally resolved roadmap of fibrillar collagen assembly, revealing distinct structural transitions across each phase.

Real-time reconstruction reveals structural intermediates in fibrillar collagen assembly

To further elucidate the dynamic assembly of fibrillar collagen at molecular resolution, we pioneered the application of time-resolved cryo-EM to capture transient intermediates formed during CF-1552 assembly. Through assembly regulation, we successfully captured two time-resolved intermediates (Fig. 5A) and obtained electron density maps: the spindlefibril (Fig. 5B, top, purple) and the axialfibril (Fig. 5B, bottom, pink), with overall resolutions of 8.60 Å and 9.80 Å, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10). The internal structure of the intermediates was clearly resolved. The spindlefibril, comprised five strands arranged in two layers with 2-3 strand configuration, forming a compressed, trapezoidal cross-section. In contrast, the axialfibril, the later aggregate, consisted of ten strands stacked in four layers with a 2-3-3-2 strand configuration. The hierarchical multistrand stacking pattern observed in these intermediates aligned with static diffraction data from natural collagen, which may challenge for fiber diffraction studies and has led to the proposal of various microfibril models.

Leveraging the triple-helical architecture of CF-1552, we utilized homology modeling to construct a triple helix model based on sequence conservation (Fig. 6A). The model exhibited an excellent fit to the cryo-EM single-strand density map, confirming these strands as triple helix with a diameter of approximately 10.2 Å, an interchain spacing of about 8.8 Å, and a chain offset of roughly 0.8 Å. Based on the stacking pattern derived from the density map and the offset distance between triple helices obtained from Steered Molecular Dynamics (SMD), initial intermediate models were constructed. Through iterative refinement combining the model with the higher-order assembly maps, we determined the architectures of both spindlefibril and axialfibril (Fig. 5B). The spindlefibril was comprised of five triple helices stacked laterally, yielding a structure approximately 40 Å wide and approximately 30 Å high. The center-to-center distance between adjacent triple helix was approximately 12–14 Å, with each strand offset by approximately 35 Å. The pentameric organization closely matched Smith’s microfibril model20, recapitulating the predicted number of strands, packing geometry, and cross-sectional dimensions. In contrast, the axialfibril consisted of ten triple helices, organized in four layers with a width of approximately 50 Å and a height of 40 Å. The lateral spacing between adjacent triple helix ranged from 12–17 Å.

Using time-resolved cryo-EM, we resolved two key hierarchical intermediates-spindlefibril and axialfibril, confirming the 5-strand microfibril as a fundamental assembly unit and delineating the post-triple-helix pathway (Fig. 5C). The axialfibril and spindlefibril have similar widths, but the axialfibril exhibits greater height. Dynamic assembly visualization showed that the axialfibril formed through the lateral misalignment of spindlefibrils (Fig. 4E). This process, combined with the intermediate stacking patterns observed in the cryo-EM data, further confirms the spatiotemporal nature of these intermediates. Specifically, the growth of the axialfibril occurs axially through interactions between the three triple helix planes of two spindlefibrils.

Discrete molecular interactions orchestrate fibrillar collagen intermediate formation

Homology modeling and molecular dynamics simulations have significantly advanced the understanding of the atomic-level mechanisms underlying collagen72–75. To further investigate the hierarchical collagen assembly process, we used molecular dynamics simulations to explore the key molecular interactions driving the formation of intermediates. A triple helix model was first constructed via homology modeling, and its energy evolution during simulation was analyzed (Fig. 6A). The results showed a decrease in Gibbs free energy (ΔG = −4.475 kJ/mol) and activation enthalpy (ΔH = −5.630 kJ/mol) (Supplementary Table 2), indicating spontaneous triple helix formation. Free energy landscape analysis revealed a distinct energy basin, from which the lowest-energy conformation was obtained for further structural investigation (Fig. 6B, C). Interchain contact analysis of lowest-energy conformation showed symmetric interchain residue packing (Fig. 6D), indicating a regular and uniform triple helix76. To identify the dominant interactions facilitating helix formation, we generated an interaction-type diagram, which revealed that hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds were the primary driving forces (Fig. 6E). To track the dynamic evolution of these forces during assembly, we analyzed the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) and the number of hydrogen bonds across the simulation trajectory. The SASA decreased from 1480 Å² to 1400 Å², reflecting enhanced hydrophobic burial (Supplementary Fig. 14A), while the number of hydrogen bonds increased from 700 to 780, indicating strengthened interchain stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 14B). Together, these data showed that triple helix formation is synergistically driven by hydrophobic collapse and extensive hydrogen bonding, which together establish a stable structural basis for higher-order fibrillar assembly.

To further probe the amino acid-level determinants of intermediate formation, we analyzed the bonding patterns within a representative 15-residue repeat of the triple helix. Alanine residues occupying the X position of the canonical G–X–Y motif consistently formed hydrogen bonds, while leucine residues engaged in regular hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 6F, left), underscoring the critical role of X-position residues in triple helix stabilization. Given the importance of hydrogen bonding in regulating triple helix architecture, we compared the interchain packing of CF-1552 to that of natural collagen (PDB: 3HQV, ~10% G–P–O) and the classical (G–P–O)₉ helix (PDB: 3B0S). The interchain spacing of CF-1552 (8.8 Å) and natural collagen (7.0 Å) was significantly more relaxed than that of the densely packed (G–P–O)₉ model (5.3 Å, Supplementary Fig. 15), suggesting that enhanced hydrogen bonding, as in the G–P–O-rich motif, can promote tighter packing in triple helices. For higher-order intermediates, assembled from stacked triple helices, we analyzed the interfacial interactions between neighboring helices. In spindlefibril, hydrophobic interactions among Ala-Ala, Pro-Pro, and Ala-Pro residues mediated lateral packing between adjacent helices (Fig. 6F, middle). For axialfibrils, formed by the interaction of two spindlefibrils, we analyzed the interfacial interactions between their contact surfaces. The results revealed that Pro residues from one spindlefibril formed hydrophobic contacts with Leu or Pro residues on the adjacent one, therefore facilitating axial assembly (Fig. 6F, right). Together, these results demonstrated that hydrophobic packing dominated force governing hierarchical assembly from triple helix to fibrillar intermediates.

Our results revealed that distinct molecular interactions drive different stages of hierarchical collagen assembly, highlighting the importance of dissecting the specific forces governing triple helix formation. In addition to the well-reported role of hydrogen bonding70,77, hydrophobic interactions play an equally critical role, particularly in the transition to higher-order structures. Specifically, interchain hydrogen bonding regulates the spatial arrangement and conformational rigidity of the triple helix, establishing its foundational stability. Meanwhile, hydrophobic interactions serve as the primary driving force for promoting higher-level assembly beyond the triple helix. This insight further supports the findings that temperature modulates the balance between hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, generating metastable triple helices that subsequently trigger higher-level assembly (Fig. 3). It also offers a molecular explanation for previous findings that hydrophobic interactions influence the lag phase of collagen assembly78.

Fibrillar collagen assembly intermediates lacking D-band retain biological function

Collagen intermediates with D-band periodicity are known for their mechanical and physiological functions79. In this study, we identified several distinct non-D-band intermediates during fibrillar collagen assembly. To assess their biological relevance, we performed functional evaluation using L929 mouse fibroblasts as a model system. Cell behavior on axialfibril was compared to natural fibrillar collagen (BAT), which exhibits D-band periodicity, across concentrations from 0.25 to 4 mg/mL, with untreated cells as the control. Additionally, the assembly ability of CF-1552 at physiological temperature (37 °C) is limited (Fig. 3B). Thus, we assessed the stability of spindlefibrils during the culture process and the results showed that spindlefibrils retained robust stability over 24 and 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Cell proliferation is a fundamental biological process involved in wound healing. To assess this, we first examined cell viability using the CCK-8 assay and assessed morphological integrity via acridine orange–ethidium bromide (AO/EB) staining. Results revealed both treated samples exhibited biocompatibility and significantly promoted cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent enhancement (Fig. 7A). At lower concentrations (0.25–1 mg/mL), cell viability was notably enhanced, showing a strong positive correlation with concentration. However, at 4 mg/mL, the proliferation rate declined, likely due to osmotic stress induced by excessive protein concentrations80. AO/EB staining further confirmed the biocompatibility results. Morphological analysis showed that most cells in both control and treated groups retained typical spindle shapes with minimal signs of apoptosis or necrosis (Fig. 7B).

Cell migration plays a crucial role in tissue repair and regeneration. To evaluate the influence of treated samples on cell migration, a scratch wound assay was performed. The results demonstrated that BAT and spindlefibrils exhibited enhanced migration compared to control (Fig. 7C, D). 24 h, both BAT and axialfibril significantly promoted cell migration, with BAT showing superior effectiveness. After 48 h, the migration abilities of BAT and axialfibril became comparable, though BAT still performed better, likely due to precipitation of BAT over time, as larger assemblies tend to have poor solubility30. Additionally, similar to cell proliferation results, cell migration also followed a concentration-dependent trend, peaking at 1 mg/mL and declining at 4 mg/m, further highlighting the influence of collagen concentration on cell activity.

Our findings reveal that fibrillar collagen intermediates lacking canonical D-band periodicity retain biological activity, which likely stems from their preserved triple-helical architecture and complementary surface chemistry. Furthermore, spindlefibrils with hierarchical structure can stably exist at physiological temperature, indicating that assembly enhances intermediate stability and suggesting that collagen assembly transitions from a metastable to a stable state. These significant findings highlight the potential of hierarchical intermediates, which offer enhanced stability, physiological functionality, and structural assembly capabilities, as a promising approach for spatiotemporal disease treatment. For example, in chronic wound healing, incorporating hierarchical intermediates could facilitate early-stage wound healing by modulating cellular responses, and subsequently function as structural scaffolds that integrate with endogenous fibrillar collagen to enhance matrix deposition and tissue regeneration.

Discussion

Research on collagen-related diseases has significantly advanced the understanding of the assembly processes of different collagen types. As a core structural protein of the basement membrane, type IV collagen forms key network nodes by linking C-terminal NC1 hexamers with N-terminal 7S tetramers, thereby creating a highly ordered two-dimensional network51,81. High-resolution structural analysis of the NC1 region has provided molecular insights into how environmental factors influence the assembly of type IV collagen82–84. These findings offer critical insights into collagen’s physiological functions and the disruptions that can occur at the atomic scale. In contrast, type I collagen, the most extensively studied fibrillar collagen type, still has significant gaps in understanding its assembly mechanism—especially the early stages. Robert et al. first proposed a mode for the early stages of natural hierarchical assembly33, suggesting that triple helices initially formed spindle-shaped intermediates, which were thought to correspond to the microfibril substructure within the static structure of collagen fibers, based on their morphological characteristics. These intermediates then underwent axial alignment to form subfibrils. Although a recent study using HF-AFM has observed behavior consistent with the assembly pathway proposed by Robert et al.34, the early stages of natural fibrillar collagen assembly remain uncertain due to its instantaneous nature and poor reproducibility in vitro31,36,37. In particularly, the existence of the spindle-shaped intermediate remains controversial35 and direct structural information on dynamic intermediates is lacking. In this study, time-resolved cryo-electron microscopy was employed to provide direct structural evidence of the dynamic transition from the triple helix to fibril. We identified two sequential intermediates—spindlefibril and axialfibril—that bridged the lag phase between triple helix and fibril(video data: 00:23-02:25). This pathway—triple helix, spindle-shaped intermediate, and filamentous intermediate—is analogous to the assembly model proposed by Robert, suggesting that CF-1552 recapitulates the early stages of natural fibrillar collagen assembly. Structural analysis reveals that spindle-shaped intermediate, composed of five stacked triple helices, aligns with the static microfibril model20,28, providing authentic existence of microfibril intermediate during the dynamic assembly process. Additionally, we observed that axial growth was modulated by lateral interactions between adjacent spindlefibrils, a process distinct from the classical monolayer axial assembly model proposed by Robert under low concentration conditions33. Intriguingly, our lateral contacts resembled the high-concentration “anomalous” features reported in Robert’s study, which now appear to reflect physiologically relevant lateral interaction modes. Base on this, we propose a concentration-dependent dual-pathway assembly model: at low concentrations, end-to-end associations predominate, whereas at higher concentrations, lateral interactions drive rapid assembly. This dual-pathway mechanism provides a unified framework for understanding the transient and dynamic nature of fibrillar collagen assembly.

CF-1552 recapitulates the natural fibrillar collagen assembly pathway and the morphology of its intermediates. However, subtle structural deviations in the intermediates were observed. Static diffraction data reveal that the spindle-shaped intermediate (microfibril) in natural collagen consists of five triple helices, each exhibiting a regular offset of 67 nm (one D-period)28,85. In the axial direction, the microfibrils were connected head-to-tail through an overlap region of approximately 30 nm (0.46 D), while maintaining a gap region of about 36 nm (0.54 D). This strict periodic overlap-gap geometry generates periodic modulation in electron density, resulting in the characteristic 67 nm D-band pattern17,27,77,86. In contrast, the spindle-shaped intermediate of CF-1552 (spindlefibril) exhibits a significantly smaller offset of ~ 3.5 nm, which fails to support D-band formation. Moreover, the lateral packing transition from spindlefibrils to axialfibrils in CF-1552 follows a 2:3:3:2 pattern, deviating from the canonical 2:3:2:3 arrangement observed in natural fibrillar collagen87. This deviation may impede further lateral propagation and halt fibril thickening. D-band formation and higher-level lateral assembly is a complex process influenced by precise spatial alignment. Previous studies have shown that physiological post-translational modifications (PTMs), local amino acid properties, terminal peptides regulate spatial organization, including triple helix misalignment, microfibril axial overlap, and fibril lateral assembly during fibrillogenesis55,88–91. However, for the CF-1552 model, the simplification of sequence complexity has led to the absence of positional determinants, which likely hinders the precise spatial registration required for D-band formation and further hierarchical assembly, ultimately preventing the simulation of higher-level assembly (growth and plateau phases). Therefore, the spindlefibril and axialfibril observed here represent intermediates assembled under PTM-free conditions. Their stacking pattern is similar to that of natural collagen, but there are structural differences in the details. Nevertheless, as a pioneering and highly controllable model system, CF-1552 faithfully recapitulates the core features of the early stages of natural fibrillar collagen assembly. It provides a unique platform for systematically investigating how sequence-encoded information governs hierarchical organization, offering valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of fibrillar collagen assembly.

Collagen assembly is highly sensitive to environmental cues and is acutely sensitive to temperature92,93. Our study reveal that low temperature stabilizes triple helices but impede higher-order assembly, while elevated trigger hierarchical organization (Fig. 2B). Cryo-EM and molecular dynamics analyses reveal that thermal modulation of hydrogen-bond networks governs interchain spacing, giving rise to a metastable triple helix with “optimal looseness”, a previously unrecognized assembly-competent intermediate. The formation mechanism of the metastable triple helix is in alignment with previous studies that link the number of hydrogen bonds to the stability and quality of the triple helix70. The confirmation of the metastable triple helix may reconcile a long-standing paradox. For nearly half a century, the triple helix is thought to be stable at physiological temperatures94. This paradigm was challenged by Leikina et al., who demonstrated that body temperature exceeds the melting temperature (Tm) of collagen, suggesting that under physiological conditions, triple helices may not remain fully folded and could exist predominantly in random coil conformations95. Our findings clarify that the in vivo triple helix exists not as a fully folded state or random coil conformation but as a dynamic, partially relaxed metastable state poised for supramolecular assembly. Up on reaching a critical threshold of flexibility, hierarchical assembly is triggered. Notably, the hierarchical assembly markedly enhances the stability of metastable triple helix, allowing the assemblies to remain intact at physiological temperatures, confirming by assembly stability evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 16). Conversely, unassembled helices are targeted for proteolysis, enabling ECM quality control.

Collagen-based materials are widely used in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine due to their excellent biocompatibility and tunable mechanical properties. However, current collagen designs are largely restricted to triple helix and simple D-banded stacking structures79,96–99, suffering from low assembly efficiency, poor supramolecular stability, and suboptimal biomechanical performance100. Moreover, their functional mechanisms are predominantly limited to modulating cell signaling pathways, lacking the capacity for microenvironmental responsiveness or autonomous hierarchical organization. A common issue in current designs is the overuse of G-P-O triplets to enforce triple-helix formation across the entire sequence. However, our findings suggest that the metastable triple helix, characterized by an ideal looseness, serves as a possible effective assembly unit. Excessive incorporation of G-P-O is likely to over-tighten the triple helix, eroding the structural plasticity required for hierarchical assembly and limiting the capacity for higher-order organization101,102. These findings also provide a mechanistic rationale for the intermittent distribution of G-P-O motifs in natural fibrillar collagen Specifically, G-P-O rich segments primarily stabilize the structure, while interspersed non-G-P-O motifs facilitate supramolecular assembly. More broadly, the limited understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing collagen self-assembly has severely constrained the rational design of collagen-based materials. To address this, we propose the “F-Z-F” assembly rules. It describes collagen assembly a stepwise, LEGO-like assembly process in which individual chains first assemble into a triple helix, which then assembly into microfibril(spindlefibril), subfibril (axialfibril), fibril and ultimately mature collagen fiber. Crucially, this hierarchical pathway through a series of metastable intermediate whose formation is regulated by parameters such as amino acid composition, temperature, and pH. These metastable states exhibit flexible boundaries, endowing the assembly process with both structural specificity and environmental adaptability. In this study, we preliminarily propose three fundamental principles of fibrillar collagen assembly: 1) cooperative hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions drive single chains folding into triple helical protomers 2) thermally induced conformational transitions within triple helices promote their lateral association into five-stranded microfibril (spindlefibril). 3) interfacial hydrophobic packing among microfibril (spindlefibril) facilitates the emergence of higher-order architectures. Additionally, for the smallest assembly unit—the metastable triple helix, we characterized its structure by an uneven distribution of hydrogen bonds. The number of hydrogen bonds and the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) serve as quality control parameters. Future studies will require more metastable triple helices generated from different sequences to determine the free energy threshold and structural boundaries of the metastable state.

Understanding fibrillar collagen hierarchical assembly is essential for comprehending the mechanical behavior of ECM. The metastable triple helix identified in our study may provide the structural basis for collagen’s unique mechanical properties. This structure, characterized by a sparse interchain hydrogen-bond network and larger molecular radial spacing, significantly enhances flexibility, making it more prone to reversible deformation under external forces, an important feature for higher-order assembly34. Moreover, the metastable triple helix offers a perspective on the mechanical evolution of fibrillar collagen. The assembly process transitions from a “metastable” to a “stable” state, with mechanical properties evolving from high toughness and reconfigurability to high strength and low toughness. As the initial assembly unit, the metastable triple helix may buffer deformation and dissipate energy during the early strain phase, leading to unique hierarchical deformation behaviors, including the uncoiling of helical twisted molecules, axial molecular stretching, and molecular unwinding under extreme deformation74,103. Mechanical experiments on large collagen fibers have been reported104,105, but data on hierarchical intermediates remain limited, mainly due to their transient nature and the challenges in capturing them. Current measurements are primarily focused on the vertical direction35, leaving axial mechanics poorly understood. Future research should leverage the CF-1552 model to capture various intermediates and combine MEMS (Microelectromechanical Systems) technology106 with multiscale molecular dynamics for cross-scale mechanical analysis (from nanometers to micrometers). This approach will provide deeper insights into atomic-scale mechanical mechanisms and elucidate hierarchical linking during physiological processes. Additionally, constructing a “sequence feature–mechanical response” map will facilitate the precise design of biomimetic collagen with natural toughness, bridging biomechanics and collagen research.

Dynamic remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) has been proposed as the thirteenth hallmark of aging8, placing collagen among potential targets in aging biology. Whereas many studies have emphasized signaling-driven, largely static measures of collagen synthesis and degradation107–111, pathway cross-talk can generate unintended effects112,113.In parallel, aged tissues exhibit altered collagen architectures114, and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) have been reported to impair collagen assembly115, suggesting that assembly dysregulation may contribute to tissue aging. In this context, the metastable triple helix identified here may serve as the structural foundation for fibrillar collagen’s involvement in aging. Age-associated microenvironmental changes such as pH, temperature, and oxidative stress116–118, may perturb the “looseness-tension” balance of the metastable triple helix and bias assembly trajectories. AGE-mediated crosslinking may further increase rigidity of the metastable state119 and the resulting matrix, potentially limiting productive assembly and reducing accessibility to collagenolytic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)120,121, thereby favoring collagen accumulation and stiffening. Moreover, reduced fibroblast-mediated collagen secretion with age107may lower local collagen concentration and possibly promote low-concentration, monolayer-like assembly modes, slowing collagen turnover. Future studies combining real-time structural readouts with controlled perturbation assays will be required to test and quantify these couplings between collagen assembly and aging. Recent advances in AI-driven collagen protein design have made the precise engineering of collagen triple helices possible122. In the future, integrating AI to explore the natural collagen sequence space for motifs with ‘F-Z-F’ assembly properties may open another pathway for the design of programmable biomimetic collagens. These biomimetic collagens can respond to microenvironmental cues and support ECM remodeling, offering significant potential for regenerative strategies in aging-related contexts. We anticipate that future advancements in aging biology and collagen biomimetic design will shift collagen-based anti-aging interventions from passive supplementation to active structural regeneration. This will open an avenue for regenerative medicine and interventions in aging-related diseases, providing fresh perspectives for defining anti-aging strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study uses a PTM-free, simplified, and controllable model to recapitulate the lag phase while also incorporating the growth and plateau phases of natural fibrillar collagen, thereby achieving the preliminary visualization of the collagen assembly process and providing insights into the molecular mechanisms. However, several limitations remain that could be addressed in future research. Firstly, the current CF-1552 model lacks the complexity to achieve the D-periodic banding patterns. Future work will combine AI-guided sequence-structure prediction to identify additional regulatory sequence fragments (e.g., PTMs, electrostatic regions) from natural collagen and gradually integrate them into the model to uncover deeper assembly mechanisms. Secondly, collagen’s structural flexibility challenge high-resolution structure determination28,47. While combined modeling strategies have mitigated uncertainty from low-resolution maps, achieving atomic-level insights requires higher-resolution data. We will optimize cryo-EM data acquisition and reconstruction, and explore combining cryo-EM tomography with other in situ techniques to achieve atomic resolution breakthroughs. Thirdly, the F-Z-F rules serve as a foundational fibrillar collagen assembly framework, still in the preliminary stage, covering early hierarchical assembly behaviors. Future work will leverage CF-1552 as a tractable baseline to investigate additional regulatory variables, progressively refine the model with molecular dynamic simulations and experimental validation, uncover more molecular mechanisms, and progressively optimize the rule set while benchmarking its generalizability using multiple collagen types and more physiologically relevant conditions. Finally, the assembly process needs in vivo validation. Future studies will track collagen deposition, orientation, and fibrosis in animal models of wound healing, using fluorescence/spectral labeling and second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging, and assess co-assembly with endogenous collagen to further validate the proposed assembly pathways and intermediates.

Methods

In vitro regulation of CF-1552 assembly

The lyophilized CF-1552 (Giant Biogene Holding Company) was stored at 4 °C before being used. For experimental preparation, the lyophilized sample was dissolved in 10 mM PBS buffer. The concentration did not affect the assembly pattern (Supplementary Fig. 17). At a concentration of 3 mg/mL, the assembly products displayed a uniform distribution, facilitating electron microscopy observation. Therefore, all subsequent tests in this study were conducted at a concentration of 3 mg/mL. The assembly behavior of CF-1552 was systematically evaluated under varying assembly conditions, including temperature, pH, and salt concentration. The assembly temperatures were tested at 4 °C, 10 °C, 18 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C. The pH gradient was set at 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9. NaCl concentrations were varied at 0 mM, 50 mM, 150 mM, 300 mM, and 500 mM. A single-factor experimental design was employed, where one parameter was varied at a time while keeping the others constant. The assembly of CF-1552 was initially conducted under baseline conditions: 18 °C, pH 7, and 150 mM NaCl, with an assembly time of 24 h.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

Negative staining transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to characterize the microscopic morphology of the assemblies. A 300-mesh carbon-supported grid was cleaned using a Gloqube® Plus glow discharge system. Subsequently, 6 μL of the assemblies was applied to the grid and allowed to absorb for 150 s before blotting dry. The grid was then subjected to three washing cycles, each involving the addition of 3 μL of ultrapure water, a 30 s incubation, and blotting to dry. For staining, the grid was pre-stained with 4 μL of 3% uranyl acetate for 30 s, followed by blotting. TEM imaging was performed using a Talos F200C G2 electron microscope at room temperature, with a magnification of 57,000× and a defocus range of −3 μm to −4 μm. TEM Imaging & Analysis software v4.19 accessed the images to determine the morphology of assemblies.

Light transmittance

The light transmittance of assemblies at different temperatures were measured using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer (10 mM PBS, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7) with a concentration of 3 mg/mL. The resulting solution was incubated in a water bath at 4 °C, 10 °C, 18 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C for 24 h. The light transmittance was measured at 313 nm.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of assemblies at different pH was measured by the Zetasizer Nano-ZS90. The assembly buffer was titrated with 1.0 M hydrochloric acid and 1.0 M sodium hydroxide to adjust the pH to the desired values. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer at varying pH values of 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 with a concentration of 3 mg/mL. Then the zeta potentials were recorded.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

The circular dichroism (CD) data of assemblies was acquired on a Jasco J-815 spectropolarimeter equipped with a temperature controller. Collagen I extracts from bovine achilles tendons (BAT, provided by Hebeikanglide Company) served as the control. CF-1552 and BAT were each dissolved in assembly buffer at a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL. CF-1552 incubated for 24 h and BAT for 2 h at 4 °C, 10 °C, 18 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, or 37 °C. Prior to CD measurements, all assemblies were diluted to 1 mg/mL using pre-equilibrated assembly buffer corresponding to the respective incubation temperatures. CD spectra were collected in 1 mm path-length quartz cuvettes with a 2 nm bandwidth over a wavelength range of 190–250 nm at a scan speed of 200 nm/min. Each measurement was performed in triplicate.

Differential scanning calorimetry

The thermal properties of the assemblies were analyzed using a NANO DSC differential scanning calorimeter. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 18 °C for 24 h, while BAT was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Assembly buffer was used for the baseline scan at least three times. DSC scans were conducted at a scanning rate of 1 °C/min between 20 °C to 80 °C, with a constant pressure of 3 atm. The resulting data were processed and analyzed using NanoAnalyze software.

Raman spectroscopy analysis

Raman spectra analysis of assemblies were recorded using a LabRam spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm laser. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 18 °C for 24 h, while BAT was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Prior to analysis, the resulting assemblies were lyophilized. Spectra were obtained from three randomly selected points on each sample surface using a 100× objective lens (Olympus, NA 0.8), with an exposure time of 20 s and two accumulations.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis of assemblies was recorded by a Nicolet iS10 FTIR spectrometer. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 18 °C for 24 h, while BAT was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Prior to analysis, the resulting assemblies were lyophilized. FTIR spectra were recorded under 64 scans over a wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Further, the resultant spectra were transformed by Fourier Self-deconvolution (FSD) in OMNIC software. The Amide I band (1700–1600 cm−1) was analyzed for secondary structure content using Gaussian fitting and second-order derivative analysis in Origin 2019b. The peak bands within the ranges of 1600–1642 cm⁻¹, 1645–1657 cm⁻¹, and 1660–1690 cm⁻¹ were assigned to β-sheets, polyproline II (PPII) helices, and β-turns, respectively.

Turbidimetric measurement

Turbidity measurements were conducted using a MULTISKAN Sky spectrophotometer. CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL, and 200 μL of the solution was aliquoted into a 96-well plate and incubated at 18 °C for 24 h. BAT was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL, and 200 μL of the solution was aliquoted into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Assembly buffer served as the blank control. Absorbance at 313 nm was recorded every 30 s.

Atomic force microscopy analysis

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) analysis was performed using a MultiMode®8 Atomic Force Microscope at room temperature in ScanAsyst mode. The scan frequency was set to 0.977 Hz and the scan size was 4 μm. BAT was dissolved in assembly buffer with a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Assemblies formed at 0.25 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h were analyzed. Similarly, CF-1552 was dissolved in assembly buffer at a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and incubated at 18 °C, with assemblies formed at 0.5 h, 1 h, 12 h, and 24 h analyzed. For AFM imaging, 4 μL of the assemblies at each time point was deposited onto a freshly cleaved mica surface (KAl₂(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂, 1.2 cm × 1.2 cm) and allowed to adsorb for 5 min. The samples were then washed twice with 20 μL of ultrapure water, blotted dry using filter paper, and air-dried with an air bulb. The AFM data were processed using Nanoscope Analysis 1.9 software, with second-order flattening applied to remove background effects and to obtain the three-dimensional morphological characteristics of the assemblies.

Time-resolving Cryo-EM data collection and structure reconstruction

EM grids for time-resolving Cryo-EM structural studies were prepared using a self-assembled collagen solution. Assemblies were captured at specific time points: 0.5 h for spindlefibril and 24 h for axialfibril. A 3 μl aliquot of assemblies was applied onto glow-discharged Quantifoil holey grids (R1.2/1.3, Au, 300 mesh) and plunge-frozen using a Vitrobot Mark IV plunge freezer. The sample preparation parameters were set as follows: blot force −10, blot time 1.5 s, wait time 60 s, drain time 0.5 s, blot total 1, humidity 100% and temperature 18 °C. Grids were imaged and data were collected using a Glacios 200 kV electron microscope equipped with a Gatan K2 Summit direct electron detector in linear mode. The pixel size was set to 1.2 Å/pixel, with defocus values ranging from −1.5 μm to −2.8 μm. A total electron dose of 40 e/Å² was applied. Using EPU 3.4.0 software, high-quality images were acquired, totaling 1491 for spindlefibril and 1634 for axialfibril.

The data processing workflow for single particle cryo-EM is illustrated in Supplymentary Fig. S7 for the spindlefibirl and Supplymentary Fig. S8 for the axialfibirl. Raw images were preprocessed using CryoSPARC v4.3.1. Patch Motion Correction was applied to correct stage drift and anisotropic motion, followed by Patch CTF Estimation to determine the CTF. Low-quality images were discarded during this step. For spindlefibril, initial particles were picked using the template-free Filament Tracer and subjected to 2D classification. Multiple rounds of 2D classification were performed to remove obvious “junk” particles. The highest-quality classes were selected for ab initio reconstruction. The best class was then subjected to template-free 3D classification, followed by Helical Refinement. The final density map of the spindlefibril was achieved. For axialfibril, initial particles were picked from 10 images using the template-free Filament Tracer, generating 2D class averages. These 2D results served as templates for further particle picking with Filament Tracer, followed by 2D classification and ab initio reconstruction. After multiple iterations, the highest-quality particles were selected for Helical Refinement. Following multiple rounds of ab initio reconstruction and Helical Refinement, a density map of the axialfibril was obtained. Based on the number of triple helix strands, helix spacing, and axial offset distances identified from the map and molecular dynamics simulations, we developed Python scripts (code provided in the Supplementary Information) that enable precise control over the spatial positioning of the triple helix to generate the spindlefibril and axialfibril models. The models were manually fitted into the Cryo-EM density maps using UCSF ChimeraX 1.6.1 and subsequently refined using PHENIX 1.21.1-5286. Iterative cycles of manual adjustment in COOT 0.9.8.5 and refinement in PHENIX were performed to optimize the models. Ultimately, atomic models matching the density maps were obtained. Figures were generated using UCSF ChimeraX 1.6.1. For Cryo-EM data collection, refinement, and validation statistics, refer to Supplementary Table 3.

Structural modeling and molecular dynamics simulation

The initial model of the triple helix was constructed using the natural collagen triple helix (PDB:3hqv) as a template through ChimeraX 1.6.1 software. All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2020.5, under constant temperature and pressure conditions with periodic boundary conditions. The GROMOS96 54a7 united-atom force field and the SPC/E water model were applied. During the MD simulations, all bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, with an integration step size of 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method. The non-bonded interaction cutoff was set to 10 Å, and the interactions were updated every 10 steps. The V-rescale temperature coupling method was used to control the simulation temperature at 303 K, and the Parrinello-Rahman method was used to control the pressure at 1 bar. First, energy minimization was performed on the two systems using the steepest descent method to eliminate any close atomic contacts. Then, NVT and NPT equilibration simulations were carried out for 1 ns at 303 K. Finally, a 10 ns MD simulation was performed, saving the configurations every 10 ps. The simulation results were visualized using GROMACS embedded tools, ConAn and VMD.

Steered molecular dynamics

SMD simulation was performed using GROMACS 2020.5 with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field and the TIP3P water model. The system, consisting of two parallel triple helices (Helix A and Helix B), was solvated neutralized, and simulated under periodic boundary conditions. After energy minimization, the system was equilibrated under NVT (291 K, 1 ns) and NPT (1 bar, 100 ns) conditions with gradual release of backbone position restraints. SMD was performed by coupling the center of mass of Helix B to a dummy atom moving at constant speed along the z-axis, with elastic network restraints applied to each chain. No atoms were fixed during the SMD process. Each trajectory was simulated for 250 ns with 10 independent repeats for statistical reliability, recording force and inter-group distance every 10 ps. Force–displacement curves were used to calculate mechanical work and potential energy changes. To assess the relationship between axial offset and system stability, representative conformations were extracted for umbrella sampling, with 2 ns equilibration and 10 ns sampling per window. The weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) was used to compute the free energy curve (PMF–Δz), quantifying the relationship between axial displacement and system stability.
