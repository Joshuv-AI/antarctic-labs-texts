When Cars Have Stereotypes: Auditing Demographic Bias in Objects from Text-to-Image Models
Dasol Choi, Jihwan Lee, Minjae Lee, Minsuk Kahng

Abstract
While prior research on text-to-image generation has predominantly focused on biases in human depictions, we investigate a more subtle yet pervasive phenomenon: demographic bias in generated objects (e.g., cars). We introduce SODA ( S tereotyped O bject D iagnostic A udit), a novel framework for systematically measuring such biases. Our approach compares visual attributes of objects generated with demographic cues (e.g., “for young people”) to those from neutral prompts, across 2,700 images produced by three state-of-the-art models (GPT Image-1, Imagen 4, and Stable Diffusion) in five object categories. Through a comprehensive analysis, we uncover strong associations between specific demographic groups and visual attributes, such as recurring color patterns prompted by gender or ethnicity cues. These patterns reflect and reinforce not only well-known stereotypes but also more subtle and unintuitive biases. We also observe that some models generate less diverse outputs, which in turn amplifies the visual disparities compared to neutral prompts. Our proposed auditing framework offers a practical approach for testing, revealing how stereotypes still remain embedded in today’s generative models. We see this as an essential step toward more systematic and responsible AI development.

Text-to-image generation models, such as OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Imagen, and Stable Diffusion, have achieved remarkable success

.
They are transforming how people create content for marketing, product design, entertainment, and more. As these systems become increasingly integrated into professional workflows
(
Turchi et al. 2023
;
Hanafy 2023
;
Shelby, Rismani, and Rostamzadeh 2024
)
, ensuring their outputs are fair and unbiased has become as a pressing concern for researchers and practitioners
(

;
Raza et al. 2025
;
Bengio, Mindermann, and Privitera 2025
;
Vice et al. 2025
)
.

Most existing research on demographic bias in text-to-image generation has focused on images that directly depict people—examining how models generate humans of different demographics
(
Bianchi et al. 2023
;
Naik and Nushi 2023
;
Seshadri, Singh, and Elazar 2023
;
Barve et al. 2025
)
. These studies revealed significant disparities in occupational representation, skin tone, and gender portrayal when humans are explicitly included in generated images.

However, demographic bias can appear more subtly in images of non-human objects, where the same type of object may be styled or configured differently based on demographic cues in the prompt.
Unlike bias in images that depict people, this implicit form of bias has received little attention, yet it can quietly embed stereotypes into products, marketing materials, and visual content when users generate objects for real-world use
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
.
For example, a prompt like “shirt for women” may yield images of pink or pastel-colored shirts, while “shirt for men” may result in darker tones. This reinforces the stereotype that women prefer such colors, even though many women may want to wear darker clothing. As a result, these biased outputs can influence real-world product offerings, leading to more pink shirts for women and fewer options in darker colors. Such patterns not only limit consumer choice but also reinforce gender norms in society.

To address this challenge, we introduce
SODA

We focus on bias arising when demographic cues in prompts, such as gender, age, or ethnicity, lead to consistent differences in visual attributes like color, shape, or style, extracted using vision-language models.

Our analysis uncovers a wide range of stereotypes, some expected, others surprising and unintuitive, demonstrating how demographic cues can lead to biased object appearances.

Related Work

Text-to-image (T2I) models are known to reproduce and amplify societal stereotypes in human depictions. These biases manifest in multiple ways: occupational prompts show skewed gender and racial representations, with high-status professions defaulting to white male figures while caregiving roles generate female representations
(
Bianchi et al. 2023
;
Seshadri, Singh, and Elazar 2023
;
Wu, Nakashima, and Garcia 2024
;
Wan et al. 2024
)
. Even neutral prompts like “a person” predominantly generate light-skinned Western male figures, systematically underrepresenting women and non-Western ethnicities
(
Steed and Caliskan 2021
;
Cho, Zala, and Bansal 2023
;
Ghosh and Caliskan 2023
)
.

To measure such effects, evaluation frameworks have been proposed: T2IAT
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
adapts the Implicit Association Test to generated imagery, while TIBET
(
Chinchure et al. 2024
)
introduces a prompt‑adaptive, counterfactual pipeline that surfaces relevant bias axes and explanations beyond fixed taxonomies.

Beyond human depictions, recent studies reveal bias in non‑human visual elements through different mechanisms. OpenBias identifies unintended object attribute biases in neutral prompts, where specific brands, colors, or styles emerge without explicit specification
(
D’Incà et al. 2024
)
. Other analyses reveal asymmetric co‑occurrence patterns between demographics and objects in existing datasets
(
Mannering 2023
)
, and show that seemingly neutral object prompts systematically default to culturally Western/American design styles
(
Naik and Nushi 2023
)
. In multimodal VLMs, benchmarks such as VisoGender and GenderBias‑VL demonstrate that demographic biases permeate contextual objects, scenes, and tasks like pronoun resolution and VQA
(
Hall et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2024
)
.

Building on these directions, we present SODA, which targets a distinct form of bias:
demographic-driven object bias
—how explicit demographic cues in prompts systematically alter the visual attributes of generated objects. Unlike prior work that identifies unexpected biases in neutral prompts or analyzes existing dataset patterns, our approach employs controlled baseline-vs-demographic comparisons to measure how demographic targeting influences object design characteristics across multiple models.

Controlled Prompt Generation

Prompt Structure Design

We design a two-level prompt structure to isolate demographic influence on object design across five object categories: cars, laptops, backpacks, cups, and teddy bears.

•

Base Prompts
: Prompts specify only the object without demographic information (e.g., “car, one product only, no people”).

•

Demographic-Conditioned Prompts
: Prompts specify the object and additionally include explicit demographic conditions (e.g., “car for women, one product only, no people”).

This controlled setup enables direct comparison between objects generated from the base prompts without demographic cues and those generated with demographic specifications across three widely studied dimensions of potential bias: age, gender, and ethnicity.

Object-Specific Attributes
: We used GPT-4o to capture unique visual patterns specific to certain objects or models. For each model-object combination, we sampled 18 representative images: 2 from base prompts and 2 samples from each of the 8 demographic groups (3 age + 2 gender + 3 ethnicity). We then provided these sample images to GPT-4o with prompts asking it to identify 4 distinguishable visual attributes that could differentiate between demographic conditions for that specific object type. This process yielded 3 product-specific attributes and 1 background-related attribute per model-object combination. For instance, cars are characterized by body_type, headlight_design, wheel_design, and background_lighting (detailed attribute taxonomies provided in Appendix). All GPT-4o operations used temperature=0 to ensure deterministic and reproducible results.

Phase 2: Attribute Assignment

In the second phase, we assign the identified visual attribute values to all 2,700 generated images using GPT-4o. We employ structured prompts that instruct GPT-4o to analyze each image and return classifications in a predefined JSON format (detailed in Appendix). For example, for a car image, GPT-4o identifies whether the body_type is “sedan,” “SUV,” or “hatchback” based on what it observes in that specific image.

Validation Process

We validated our attribute extraction process in two steps: (1) Human verification across all 15 model-object combinations found 818 out of 820 attributes suitable (99.8%), with two exceptions (one parsing error and one wheel design not visible in front-facing car images). (2) A stratified sample of 270 images achieved 95.4% agreement between automated extraction and human annotation (2,061 out of 2,161), with 43 classified as incorrect (2.0%) and 58 as ambiguous (2.7%), mostly due to visual ambiguity or attribute inapplicability (detailed methodology in Appendix). This human validation was conducted by an author not involved in the attribute extraction or framework design process, to ensure independence.

Statistical Bias Measurement

We employ three metrics to quantify demographic bias across different analytical dimensions:

1) Base vs. Demographic Divergence Score (BDS):
To measure how much demographic targeting (e.g., “ethnicity = Asian”) shifts the distribution of visual attributes away from a supposedly
neutral
base prompt, we compute Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence between base and demographic-conditioned distributions :

\text{BDS}(g)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\text{JS}(P_{\text{base}}^{(a)},P_{g}^{(a)})

(1)

where

A
is the set of visual attributes, and
P_{\text{base}}^{(a)}
,
P_{g}^{(a)}
represent attribute distributions under base and demographic groups.

2) Cross-Demographic Disparity Score (CDS):
To quantify distribution difference across demographic groups within each dimension
G
(e.g., ethnicity), we compute the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence between all group pairs:

\text{CDS}(G)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{G}|}\sum_{(g_{i},g_{j})\in\mathcal{P}_{G}}\text{JS}(P_{g_{i}}^{(a)},P_{g_{j}}^{(a)})

(2)

where
G
is a demographic dimension,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is the set of all unordered pairs of demographic groups in
G
, and
P_{g}^{(a)}
denotes the attribute distribution for group
g\in G
.

3) Visual Attribute Concentration Score (VAC):
This entropy-based score quantifies how concentrated attribute distributions become under a specific prompt:

\text{VAC}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}1-\frac{H(P^{(a)})}{H_{\text{max}}}

(3)

where
H(P^{(a)})
is the Shannon entropy of the value distribution for attribute
a
and
H_{\text{max}}
is the maximum possible entropy (under a uniform distribution).
We can compute an VAC score for each model-object combination by averaging over all visual attributes
A

We evaluate three state-of-the-art text-to-image models representing different architectural approaches: GPT Image-1, Imagen 4, and Stable Diffusion (SDXL)

OpenAI 2025

Within-Group Concentration: Do Models Generate Diverse or Stereotypical Outputs?

Generative models are often understood as probabilistic systems that reflect a range of possibilities in the world. However, when demographic cues consistently lead to fixed outputs, this assumption breaks down. If a model always generates the same output (e.g., red cars for women), it may reinforce dominant stereotypes.

To examine this, we measured the Visual Attribute Concentration Score (VAC), which quantifies how concentrated attribute values become for each model–object–demographic combination. A score of 1.0 indicates extreme concentration, where all 20 generated images share the same attribute value.

Table
4
reveals large differences across models. Imagen shows the most extreme concentration (average VAC: 0.662) with 83 cases reaching the maximum possible score (i.e., 1.0), which means all 20 images share the same attribute value (e.g., red cars).
These highly concentrated generations signal deterministic behavior driven by demographic prompts.
As shown in Table
5
.
Imagen consistently generates red cars for women, charcoal gray for men, and white cars for White demographics. Similarly, GPT produces chocolate brown teddy bears for Black and sippy cups for the elderly while producing handle-free cups with vibrant colors for young adults.

Such patterns go beyond statistical tendencies, revealing how models reproduce social biases through fixed design choices. This is especially concerning given the expectation that generative systems would sample diverse possibilities, not amplify dominant association. When that association reflects a stereotype, repeated generation only reinforces it.

Beyond these fully deterministic cases, we also observe cases with high VAC scores. For instance, GPT’s default car is a sedan, but it shifts to hatchbacks for young adults and SUVs for Black. Appendix provides more examples.

Qualitative Analysis of Bias Across Models

In addition to the analysis based on the three quantitative metrics, we conducted a qualitative investigation of the generated images to examine how different models manifest bias.
Using a custom web-based image exploration interface (Appendix), we manually inspected all 2,700 images and identified several interesting patterns unique to each model.

GPT consistently embeds demographic-related text directly onto objects
, creating explicit visual markers that reinforce targeting assumptions, as illustrated in Figure
5
left. For instance, laptop screens display demographic identifiers: Asian-targeted laptops show Chinese characters and Japanese kana script, while ethnicity-specific generation includes national flags (Korean and Taiwanese flags visible in screens). Similarly, cups generated for Black demographics contain explicit text like “Black is Beautiful.”
demonstrating how GPT transforms demographic prompts into literal visual text rather than implicit design choices.

Imagen exhibits recurring structural patterns linked to specific demographic groups
, often materializing in object geometry or configuration (Figure
5
right).
Notably, 5 out of 20 Black-targeted cars feature both gull-wing doors open, evoking stylized or exaggerated design cues. Young adult backpacks show a striking pattern: 12 out of 20 are depicted already open with laptops inside, potentially encoding assumptions of digital readiness. These patterns extend to elderly demographics as well—all 20 elderly-targeted car images show at least one door entirely missing, reflecting architectural simplification, while 10 out of 20 elderly-targeted backpacks appear with their front compartments unzipped. These consistent structural choices across object categories indicate that Imagen’s demographic bias may be encoded not just through textures or colors, but through fixed physical arrangements triggered by social cues.

Discussion and Limitations

Illusion of diversity in Diffusion.

While our analysis reveals that Diffusion demonstrates the highest diversity among the three models we tested, these lower bias scores may not indicate superior fairness.
Our inspection of all 900 Diffusion-generated images revealed constraint violations in 12.44% of cases (112 images), including multiple objects or people despite explicit “no people” instructions, as shown in Figure
6
. This reduced prompt adherence, while resulting in more demographically balanced outputs, may reflect limited instruction following rather than intentional debiasing.

Risks of bias in object generation.

The pervasive demographic bias in object generation poses significant risks for commercial applications in marketing and product design. When AI-generated content unconsciously perpetuates stereotypes at scale, it can systematically embed and amplify societal biases. These biases have tangible consequences: marketing teams using AI-generated product catalogs may inadvertently limit consumer choice through stereotypical associations.

Limitations of our work.

Despite the strengths of our analysis, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, automated attribute extraction via GPT-4o may introduce occasional classification errors, while we observed strong alignment between quantitative metrics and human verification. Second, our minimal prompt design—while effective for isolating demographic effects—may underrepresent the complexity of real-world usage, where richer prompts could surface more severe or subtle bias patterns. Third, while the three models and five object types cover widely used models and scenarios, our results should not be overgeneralized to all possible cases.
Even with these limitations, our study provides a crucial first step toward quantifying object-level demographic bias in generative models.

Conclusion

We introduced SODA (Stereotyped Object Diagnostic Audit), the first systematic framework for measuring demographic bias in AI-generated objects. Our analysis of 2,700 images across three models reveals pervasive stereotyping, where demographic cues consistently alter object appearances.
In addition, we observed extremely skewed distributions, such as 100% red cars for women, and hidden biases in “neutral” prompts, which often align with middle-aged and white.
We hope SODA serves as a stepping stone toward more socially responsible AI by making hidden biases in visual outputs more visible and measurable.

Appendix D
Human Validation Methodology

We validated our automated pipeline through human evaluation of attribute discovery appropriateness and classification accuracy.
An author not involved in the attribute extraction or framework design process performed evaluation.

Attribute Discovery Validation

First, the annotator evaluated all 820 discovered attributes across 15 model-object combinations using standardized guidelines. Attributes were marked as “appropriate,” “inappropriate,” or “ambiguous.” Human evaluation achieved 99.8% appropriateness (818/820), with two exceptions: one parsing error and one wheel design not visible in front-facing car images.

Attribute Classification Validation

In addition, we labeled the assignment of attribute values for a stratified sample of 270 images (2,161 attribute classifications) covering all models, objects, and demographic conditions. The annotator classified attributes using the same taxonomies as automated analysis and marked as “appropriate,” “inappropriate,” or “ambiguous.”

Automated GPT-4o extraction achieved 95.4% agreement with human annotation (2,061/2,161). The remaining cases included 43 incorrect (2.0%) and 58 ambiguous (2.7%) classifications, with patterns shown below:

Ambiguous cases (2.7%) primarily occurred when features were partially visible, lighting conditions affected assessment, or categorical boundaries were unclear (e.g., distinguishing between “modern” and “contemporary” design styles). These cases reflect inherent visual interpretation challenges rather than systematic extraction errors. Misclassification rates were consistent across demographic groups (1.8-2.1%), confirming no systematic bias affecting specific groups.

Appendix E
Baseline-to-Demographic Value Shifts

While perfect segregation cases (score = 1.0) represent the most extreme form of demographic bias, we also observe systematic shifts in visual attributes when comparing baseline generation to demographic-targeted prompts. These shifts reveal how models systematically alter design choices based on demographic conditioning, even when they don’t achieve complete deterministic concentration.

Table
8
presents comprehensive examples of these baseline-to-demographic transitions across all models. The shifts demonstrate three distinct patterns of bias manifestation: (1)
Color transitions
, where baseline neutral colors shift to demographically stereotypical colors (e.g., tan teddy bears becoming chocolate brown for Black demographics), (2)
Functional design changes
, where object functionality adapts to perceived demographic needs (e.g., standard cups becoming sippy cups for elderly users), and (3)
Categorical design shifts
, where entire design categories change based on demographic assumptions (e.g., sedans becoming SUVs for Black demographics).

Notably, these shifts often reflect established societal stereotypes but can also reveal more subtle or unexpected biases embedded in training data. For instance, GPT’s systematic transition from metal to plastic laptop materials for elderly demographics may reflect assumptions about technological sophistication, while Imagen’s shift from standard handles to no handles for young adults in cup design suggests age-based assumptions about drinking preferences.

The consistency of these shifts across multiple demographic-attribute combinations indicates that the observed patterns are not random variations but systematic bias expressions deeply embedded in model training processes.
