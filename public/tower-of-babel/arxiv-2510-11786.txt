I
Introduction

In several quantum algorithms, we possess some information regarding the input initial state. This is especially true while comparing to the classical solver, as the initial state
\ket{b}
is prepared from a classical vector
b\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. If such initial state information, particularly the spectral measure, is obtained beforehand, then we refer to such problems by state-aware formalism. Some examples include quantum linear system algorithms (QLSAs) such as HHL, eigenvalue estimation, quantum walks from a localized source, and spectral filtering problems
Harrow_2009
;
Venegas_Andraca_2012
;
Kempe_2003
.

In the state-aware formalism, the effective dynamics are captured by Krylov space rather than the full Hilbert space. Krylov methods have been extensively used in classical computation theory
10007764
;
krylov1931numerical
. In fact, the conjugate gradient technique, which belongs to the family of Krylov methods, is one of the most powerful techniques used in machine learning algorithms
hestenes1952methods
. Recently, Krylov methods have attracted interest in the quantum domain in the context of quantum chaos and various complexity measures
M_ck_2022
;
Nandy_2025
;
rabinovici2025krylovcomplexity
;
Balasubramanian_2022
;
Adhikari_2023
;
Adhikari_2024
. One open problem is that if there exists any relationship between these complexity measures and quantum computational complexity. In this manuscript, we show that, indeed, there exists an exact relationship between the Krylov space dimension and the quantum query complexity.

The problem statement goes as follows. In several quantum algorithms, the goal is to prepare a state of the form

|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle=f(H)\,|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(1)

where
H
is a Hermitian operator accessible via oracle or block-encoding queries,

f
is a target function (e.g., exponential, inverse, or filter),
and
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is a given initial state. Conventional analyses are state-oblivious and neglect the actual spectral support of the initial state, which leads to overly pessimistic bounds. However, repeated applications of
H
on the initial state generate the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{m}(H,|\psi_{0}\rangle)=\mathrm{span}\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,H|\psi_{0}\rangle,\ldots,H^{m-1}|\psi_{0}\rangle\},

within which all states of the form
f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle
reside.
A quantum circuit making
q
oracle queries to
H
can prepare only states

p(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle
where
p
is a polynomial of degree at most
q
; thus, we show that the query count and the Krylov depth are equivalent notions. More precisely, we show that the given full knowledge of the spectral measure
\mu_{H,\psi_{0}}
, the minimal query complexity equals the optimal polynomial approximation degree of
f
, up to some approximation error
\epsilon
. Such optimal polynomials can be implemented via the Quantum Singular Value Transformation (QSVT) framework
Gily_n_2019
;
Martyn_2021
.
Therefore, our work unifies quantum query models, orthogonal polynomial theory, and Krylov compression
into a single coherent framework
Koornwinder_2013
;
M_ck_2022
;
Balasubramanian_2022
.

II
Krylov Compression, Lanczos Recurrence, and Favard Polynomials

Let us consider a quantum system with a time-independent Hermitian Hamiltonian
H
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
The time evolution of a state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is given by a Schrödinger equation:

i\partial_{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=H\ket{\psi(t)}

(2)

with solution
|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle
. The solution has a power series expansion as:

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}\ket{\psi_{n}},\quad\ket{\psi_{n}}=H^{n}\ket{\psi_{0}}.

(3)

Thus, the entire time evolution is built from repeated applications of
H
to the starting state. Applying the Gram–Schmidt procedure to the sequence
\{|\psi_{n}\rangle\}=H^{n}\ket{\psi_{0}}
, produces an ordered, orthonormal basis

\mathcal{K}=\{|K_{n}\rangle:n=0,1,2,\dots\},\qquad|K_{0}\rangle=|\psi_{0}\rangle.

(4)

The basis
\mathcal{K}
is also referred to as the Krylov basis, and the dimension of the Krylov basis denoted by
m=\dim\mathcal{K}(H,|\psi_{0}\rangle)
is called the Krylov dimension. Depending on the Hamiltonian and the choice of initial state, the Krylov dimension may be much smaller than the full Hilbert space dimension, since linear dependencies can arise among the vectors
H^{n}|\psi_{0}\rangle
. In this case, the dynamics of
|\psi(t)\rangle
are effectively confined to this reduced subspace. If
m
is finite, all evolution remains in an
m
-dimensional space; otherwise
m=\infty
.

Lanczos Algorithm

Starting from
\ket{K_{0}}=\ket{\psi_{0}}
, such an orthonormal basis (and Krylov space) can be constructed recursively via the Lanczos algorithm:

\ket{A_{n+1}}=\left(H-a_{n}\right)\ket{K_{n}}-b_{n}\ket{K_{n-1}}

(5)

where

\ket{K_{n}}=b_{n}^{-1}\ket{A_{n}}
, and
b_{0}=0
. The
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are called Lanczos coefficients

a_{n}=\bra{K_{n}}H\ket{K_{n}},\qquad b_{n}=\sqrt{\langle A_{n}|A_{n}\rangle}.

(6)

This recursion implies

H\ket{K_{n}}=a_{n}\ket{K_{n}}+b_{n+1}\ket{K_{n+1}}+b_{n}\ket{K_{n-1}}.

(7)

Thus, in the Krylov basis, the Hamiltonian is a tridiagonal matrix with Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
on the diagonal and
b_{n}
on the off-diagonal.

J=\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&b_{1}&&&\\
b_{1}&a_{1}&b_{2}&&\\
&b_{2}&a_{2}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&b_{m-1}\\
&&&b_{m-1}&a_{m-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

The matrix
J
is commonly referred to as a Jacobi or Hessenberg matrix; here we will refer to it as the Krylov matrix for convenience.

This representation is equivalent to a one-dimensional tight-binding Hamiltonian or quantum walk model, with
a_{n}
acting as on-site potentials and
b_{n}
as nearest-neighbor hopping amplitudes. In this sense, the Lanczos algorithm maps the dynamics of a general Hamiltonian onto an effective one-dimensional chain, greatly simplifying its study. In the Krylov basis, the state evolves as

|\psi(t)\rangle\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\psi_{n}(t)\,|K_{n}\rangle,

(9)

with amplitudes obeying

i\,\dot{\psi}_{n}(t)\;=\;b_{n}\,\psi_{n-1}(t)+a_{n}\,\psi_{n}(t)+b_{n+1}\,\psi_{n+1}(t),

(10)

subject to
\psi_{-1}(t)=\psi_{m}(t)=0
and
\psi_{0}(0)=1
. The mean position
1
1

1

This quantity is also called by Krylov complexity in the literature, but we will refer to it as a mean position to avoid confusion with other notions of complexity.

C(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}n\,|\psi_{n}(t)|^{2}

(11)

quantifies the spread of the wavefunction on the Krylov chain.

Spectral measure

The Lanczos coefficients can also be obtained recursively, via the method outlined in
Balasubramanian_2022
, from the moments of the Hamiltonian in the initial state:

M_{k}\;=\;\langle\psi_{0}|H^{k}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle K_{0}|H^{k}|K_{0}\rangle

(12)

The moment itself can also be obtained from the survival amplitude, which is the amplitude that the state at time
t
is the same as at the initial state:

S(t)=\langle\psi(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle\psi(t)|K_{0}\rangle=\psi_{0}(t)^{*}

(13)

Survival amplitude is then just the moment-generating function for the Hamiltonian of the initial state:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}S(t)\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\langle\psi(0)|\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}e^{iHt}\ket{\psi(0)}\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\bra{K_{0}}(iH)^{n}\ket{K_{0}}=M_{k}.

(14)

This is particularly appealing because the moment or survival amplitude method works even for the infinite-dimensional system, and can provide Lanczos coefficients and the corresponding Krylov basis.

In fact, several key dynamical quantities, including Lanczos coefficients, can directly be inferred from the spectral measure itself.
The pair
(H,|\psi_{0}\rangle)
admits a unique probability measure
\mu
supported on
\mathrm{spec}(H)
such that

\langle\psi_{0}|f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle\;=\;\int f(\lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda),

(15)

for all bounded functions
f
. For example, the moments are

M_{k}\;=\;\langle\psi_{0}|H^{k}|\psi_{0}\rangle\;=\;\int\lambda^{k}\,d\mu(\lambda)

(16)

One can see the measure
\mu
as a quantity that tells which eigenvalues of
H
matter for the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
, and by how much. The survival amplitude turns out to be just the Fourier transform of
\mu
:

S(t)=\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\int e^{-i\lambda t}\,d\mu(x).

(17)

with Taylor expansion that shows explicitly how the first few moments control the short-time dynamics

S(t)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-it)^{k}}{k!}\,M_{k}.

(18)

The Laplace transformation of the measure
\mu
gives the classical partition function:

Z(\beta)=\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-\beta H}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\int e^{-\beta x}\,d\mu(x).

while the Stieltjes transformation gives the Green’s function:

G(z)=\langle\psi_{0}|(z-H)^{-1}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\int\frac{1}{z-\lambda}\,d\mu(\lambda).

(19)

The continued fraction of
G(z)
also encodes the Lanczos coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
as:

G(z)=\cfrac{1}{z-a_{0}-\cfrac{b_{1}^{2}}{z-a_{1}-\cfrac{b_{2}^{2}}{z-a_{2}-\cdots}}}.

(20)

Favard’s polynomials

The Lanczos recursion coefficients
(a_{n},b_{n})
define a three-term recurrence relation for a sequence
of polynomials
\{P_{n}(\lambda)\}
:

\lambda P_{n}(\lambda)=b_{n+1}P_{n+1}(\lambda)+a_{n}P_{n}(\lambda)+b_{n}P_{n-1}(\lambda).

(21)

By Favard’s theorem, there exists a unique positive measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
such that the polynomials
\{P_{n}\}
are orthogonal with respect to
\mu
:

\int P_{m}(\lambda)P_{n}(\lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda)=h_{n}\delta_{mn},\qquad h_{n}>0.

(22)

This measure is precisely the spectral measure
\mu
associated
with the pair
(H,|\psi_{0}\rangle)
introduced in Eq.
15
.
Via the spectral theorem, one can replace
\lambda
with
H
in Eq.
21
, and obtain:

HP_{n}(H)\;=\;b_{n+1}P_{n+1}(H)\;+\;a_{n}P_{n}(H)\;+\;b_{n}P_{n-1}(H).

(23)

Acting with both sides on
\ket{K_{0}}
, and using
\ket{K_{n}}=P_{n}(H)\ket{K_{0}}
(orthogonal to all lower Krylov vectors), one obtains:

H\ket{K_{n}}\;=\;b_{n+1}\ket{K_{n+1}}\;+\;a_{n}\ket{K_{n}}\;+\;b_{n}\ket{K_{n-1}}

(24)

which is equivalent to the Krylov/Lanczos recurrence Eq.
7
. Therefore, the Lanczos coefficients
(a_{n},b_{n})
, Krylov matrix
J
, the orthogonal polynomials
\{P_{n}(\lambda)\}
(From Favard’s theorem) and the spectral measure
\mu
, are several equivalent ways of encoding the same structure.

Furthermore, this orthogonal polynomial structure guarantees optimality of approximation in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
Any square-integrable function
f
admits an expansion

f(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}P_{n}(\lambda),\quad c_{n}=\int f(\lambda)P_{n}(\lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda).

(25)

The coefficients
c_{n}
can be computed via the Gaussian quadrature method, such that the integrals get replaced by summation. But, since it is a classical procedure, we will not discuss it further.

The degree-
d
truncation

p_{d}(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n}P_{n}(\lambda)

(26)

is the orthogonal projection of
f
onto the space of polynomials of degree at most
d
.
The residual
f-p_{d}
is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree at most
d
, so for any other polynomial
q
with
\deg q\leq d
,

\displaystyle\|f-q\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f-p_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}+\|p_{d}-q\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle\geq\;\|f-p_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}.

Thus
p_{d}
is the unique best degree-
d
approximation to
f
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

III
Operational picture

In this section, we will show how Krylov compression from original full Hilbert space to effective Krylov space captures the necessary physics about the action of
H
on
\ket{\psi_{0}}
. More precisely, the dynamics of
(H,\ket{\psi_{0}})
are exactly reproduced by
(J,\ket{e_{1}})
. To see this, let us introduce an isometry

V:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathcal{H},\qquad V\ket{e_{j}}=\ket{k_{j}}\quad(1\leq j\leq m),

(28)

where
\{\ket{e_{j}}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and
m
is the Krylov dimension for the pair
(H,\ket{\psi_{0}})
. Mathematically,
V^{\dagger}V=I_{m}
,
P:=VV^{\dagger}
is the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{K}_{m}
and the Krylov compression of
H
to
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is given by the Krylov matrix
J
as

J\;:=\;V^{\dagger}HV\;\in\;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.

(29)

Operationally, in the state-aware formalism,
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is the smallest Hilbert space containing all physically relevant information about the problem.
If
m\ll\dim\mathcal{H}
, the number of qubits needed to reproduce the same physics is reduced from
\lceil\log_{2}\dim\mathcal{H}\rceil
to
\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
. A canonical example of this phenomenon is the glued trees problem of quantum walk where the dynamics is mapped from full Hilbert space to Krylov space of polynomial size. This exponential compression leads to quantum advantage in such quantum walk algorithms
Childs_2003
.

III.1
Intertwining relation

A crucial mathematical reason why this compressions works is the following polynomial intertwining relation

p(H)V=Vp(J),\qquad\text{for all polynomials }p.

(30)

This follows from
H^{n}V=VJ^{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. To see this, we can use the principle of induction. The case
n=0
is trivial. For
n=1
,

\displaystyle(HV)\ket{e_{j}}

\displaystyle=H\ket{k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ket{k_{i}}\braket{k_{i}|H|k_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\ket{k_{i}}\,(V^{\dagger}HV)_{ij}

\displaystyle=(VJ)\ket{e_{j}}.

(31)

Thus
HV=VJ
, and the general step follows from
H^{n+1}V=H(H^{n}V)=(HV)J^{n}=(VJ)J^{n}=VJ^{n+1}
.
By linearity,
p(H)V=Vp(J)
for any polynomial
p
.
Furthermore, by polynomial approximation, this extends further to any function
f

that can be uniformly approximated by polynomials on
\mathrm{spec}(H)
:

f(H)V=Vf(J).

(32)

Applied to the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
, this yields

f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle=f(H)V|e_{1}\rangle=Vf(J)|e_{1}\rangle.

(33)

Thus, while
H
and
J
are not globally equivalent, their action on the Krylov subspace
generated by
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is exactly the same. Consequently, any computation that
applies
f(H)
to
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can be carried out by applying
f(J)
to
|e_{1}\rangle

in the reduced
m
-dimensional space and mapping back with the isometry
V
.

III.2
Ampltitudes and Correlators

By applying the polynomial intertwining relation termwise to the power series

e^{-iHt}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}H^{n}
, one obtains

e^{-iHt}V=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}H^{n}V=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-it)^{n}}{n!}VJ^{n}=Ve^{-iJt}.

(34)

In particular, if
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\|\psi_{0}\|\,V\ket{e_{1}}
, then the time-evolved state
obeys

e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi_{0}}=Ve^{-iJt}\ket{e_{1}}.

(35)

This ensures that Schrödinger evolution of states starting in
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is exactly reproduced by evolution in compressed space
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Similarly, for any observable
A
with compression
\tilde{A}=V^{\dagger}AV
, the Heisenberg evolution satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}|A(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bra{e_{1}}V^{\dagger}e^{iHt}Ae^{-iHt}V\ket{e_{1}}

(36)

\displaystyle=\bra{e_{1}}e^{iJt}(V^{\dagger}AV)e^{-iJt}\ket{e_{1}}

(37)

\displaystyle=\langle e_{1}|\,e^{iJt}\,\tilde{A}\,e^{-iJt}\,|e_{1}\rangle

(38)

More generally, for any
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
and times
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
,

\braket{\psi_{0}|A_{1}(t_{1})\cdots A_{r}(t_{r})|\psi_{0}}\;=\;\bra{e_{1}}\prod_{s=1}^{r}\Big(e^{iJt_{s}}\,\tilde{A}_{s}\,e^{-iJt_{s}}\Big)\ket{e_{1}}.

(39)

For example, two-time correlators satisfy

\braket{\psi_{0}|A(t)B(s)|\psi_{0}}\;=\;\bra{e_{1}}e^{iJt}\tilde{A}\,e^{-iJ(t-s)}\tilde{B}\,e^{-iJs}\ket{e_{1}}.

(40)

Therefore, all the relevant amplitudes and correlators can be computed in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
without loss of information.

Overall, this implies that one could instead compute the correlators using the compressed tri-diagonal Hamiltonian
J
, which is just a one-dimensional tight-binding Hamiltonian or a one-dimensional quantum walk problem. Starting with the standard basis
\ket{e_{0}}
, and computing the correlators of the operator
\tilde{A}=V^{\dagger}AV
would give the same result as for the actual Hamiltonian
H
and the state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
.

IV
State-aware optimal query complexity: construction

In this section, we study the query complexity of preparing, within some
\epsilon
-approximation, a state proportional to

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{out}}}=f(H)\,\ket{\psi_{0}}

(41)

where
H
is a Hermitian operator (or its block-encoding) accessible only
through queries to the corresponding unitary

U=e^{iH}\quad\text{or}\quad U_{\mathrm{block}}=\begin{pmatrix}H&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

The query complexity,
q_{\text{min}}(\epsilon)
, is defined as the total number of such queries made by the algorithm
2
2

2

There exist other query models, such as the power query model used in Shor’s period finding, but that falls outside the scope of our analysis.
. The query complexity depends on the Hamiltonian
H
, the target function
f
, the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
, and the polynomial approximation
p
of
f
.
We define the state-aware degree functional as

\displaystyle n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon)\;:=\;\min\bigl\{\,n:

\displaystyle\exists\,p\ \text{with}\ \deg p\leq n,

(43)

\displaystyle\|f-p\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\varepsilon\bigr\}.

where,

\displaystyle\|f-p\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int|f(x)-p(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(x)

(44)

\displaystyle=\bigl\|(f(H)-p(H))\ket{\psi_{0}}\bigr\|^{2}.

It gives us the information-theoretic lower bound for the minimum number of queries,
q_{\min}
, required as

q_{\min}(\varepsilon)\;\geq\;n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon).

(45)

Among all such polynomials
p
, the error is minimized precisely by
p_{d}
, from equation
26
, as follows:

\displaystyle\|f-p_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\|f-p\|_{L^{2}(\mu)},

(46)

\displaystyle\forall\,p\text{ with }\deg p\leq d.

One can implement
p_{d}
with the QSVT framework (after rescaling), thus achieving the bound exactly with just
d
queries. Hence

q_{\min}(\varepsilon)\;=\;n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon).

(47)

These results can be summarized in a single theorem as follows:

Theorem 1

(Krylov–Favard Query Duality)
.

Let
H
be a Hermitian operator and
|\psi_{0}\rangle
an initial state with
spectral measure
\mu_{H,\psi_{0}}
. Construct the Krylov basis

|K_{n}\rangle=P_{n}(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle
via the Favard–Lanczos recurrence

\lambda P_{n}(\lambda)=b_{n+1}P_{n+1}(\lambda)+a_{n}P_{n}(\lambda)+b_{n}P_{n-1}(\lambda),\quad P_{0}(\lambda)=1.

For any bounded function
f
, its expansion in this intrinsic orthogonal
basis is

f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}|K_{n}\rangle,\qquad c_{n}=\int f(\lambda)P_{n}(\lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda).

Let
p_{d}(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n}|K_{n}\rangle
be the
degree-
d
truncation. Then the minimal number of oracle queries required
to prepare
f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle
within error
\varepsilon
equals the
smallest integer
d
such that

\|f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle-p_{d}(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle\|\leq\varepsilon.

Equivalently,

q_{\min}(\varepsilon)=n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon),

where
n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon)
is the minimal Favard degree satisfying the
above condition.

To validate this, we can follow the same argument as in Favard polynomials in the Equation
27
. The action of
f(H)
on
\ket{K_{0}}
gives:

\displaystyle f(H)\ket{K_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}P_{n}(H)\ket{K_{0}}

(48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\ket{K_{n}},

where we used
\ket{K_{n}}\;=\;P_{n}(H)\ket{K_{0}}
. The action of
p_{d}(H)
gives:

\displaystyle p_{d}(H)\ket{K_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{d}c_{n}\ket{K_{n}}

(49)

\displaystyle=c_{0}\ket{K_{0}}+c_{1}\ket{K_{1}}+\dots+c_{n}\ket{K_{n}}.

Since
\bigl(f(H)-p_{d}(H)\bigr)\ket{K_{0}}=\sum_{n>d}c_{n}\ket{K_{n}}
, one obtains:

\displaystyle\bigl\|\,(f(H)-p_{d}(H))\ket{K_{0}}\,\bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f-p_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}

(50)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n>d}|c_{n}|^{2}.

Compared with arbitrary polynomial
p(\lambda)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{n}P_{n}(\lambda)
with
\deg p\leq d
,

(f(H)-q(H))\ket{K_{0}}=\sum_{n=0}^{d}(c_{n}-a_{n})\ket{K_{n}}+\sum_{n>d}c_{n}\ket{K_{n}},

the minimum is attained when
a_{n}=c_{n}
for all
n\leq d
, i.e. when
q=p_{d}
. The minimum error of degree-d truncation is given by
\sum_{n>d}|c_{n}|^{2}
:

\displaystyle n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\min\bigl\{\,d:\ \|f-p_{d}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\leq\varepsilon\,\bigr\}

(51)

\displaystyle=\min\biggl\{\,d:\ \sum_{n>d}|c_{n}|^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\,\biggr\}.

.

4:

Obtain spectral measure

Implement

using QSVT with

.

Output state
\propto f(H)|\psi_{0}\rangle
within error

.

In particular, if the Krylov dimension

\mu
has
m

equals the minimal interpolation degree of

, the query complexity is thus
m-1
. If the Krylov dimension is infinite, which can happen if the Hilbert dimension itself is infinite, then
\mu
has infinite support, and one has to use approximations. In that case, query complexity equals
n_{\mu}(f,\varepsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is the
L^{2}(\mu)
approximation error. For example, for the time-evolution operator
f(\lambda)=e^{-i\lambda t}
, the Lanczos construction provides the optimal degree-
d
polynomial approximation to
e^{-iHt}|\psi_{0}\rangle
within the Krylov subspace.

IV.1
Example: State-aware HHL

We now illustrate the state-aware formalism in the context of the quantum
linear systems problem (HHL algorithm). Take
f(x)=1/x
and

\mu=\mu_{A,b}
, the spectral measure of
(A,b)
with occupied support

S_{b}=\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. The algorithm needs to act only on eigenmodes
populated by
\ket{b}
. The optimal query count equals the minimal
L^{2}(\mu)
degree
required to approximate
1/x
on
S_{b}
,

q_{\mathrm{opt}}=n_{\mu}(1/x,\varepsilon).

(52)

If the occupied support lies in a continuous interval

S_{b}\subseteq[\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}},\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{eff}}]
,
then approximation theory yields

q_{\mathrm{opt}}=\Theta\!\big(\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}\log(1/\varepsilon)\big),\quad\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}=\lambda_{\max}^{\mathrm{eff}}/\lambda_{\min}^{\mathrm{eff}}.

(53)

These effective endpoints can be substantially better than the global
condition number if
\ket{b}
has little overlap on small eigenvalues.

IV.2
Generalization to a Family of Initial States

So far, we have focused on a single fixed initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
. In many applications, however, one wishes to process not just one input but a family of possible states

\mathcal{S}=\{\ket{\psi^{(1)}},\ket{\psi^{(2)}},\dots,\ket{\psi^{(r)}}\}

using the same Hamiltonian
H
. The relevant subspace in this setting is the joint Krylov space

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{fam}}(H,\mathcal{S})\;=\;\mathrm{span}\Bigl(\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{K}(H,\ket{\psi^{(j)}})\Bigr),

(54)

where each
\mathcal{K}(H,\ket{\psi^{(j)}})
is the cyclic Krylov subspace generated from
\ket{\psi^{(j)}}
. Let
m_{\mathrm{fam}}:=\dim\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{fam}}(H,\mathcal{S})
. Clearly
m_{\mathrm{fam}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}
where
m_{j}=\dim\mathcal{K}(H,\ket{\psi^{(j)}})
, and in practice
m_{\mathrm{fam}}
can be much smaller whenever the individual subspaces overlap. Compressing
H
to this joint space yields the
m_{\mathrm{fam}}\times m_{\mathrm{fam}}
Jacobi surrogate
J_{\mathrm{fam}}
that exactly reproduces the dynamics of
(H,\mathcal{S})
.

In this setting the optimal query complexity is determined by the family-aware degree functional, defined analogously to (
43
) but with respect to the joint spectral measure supported on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{fam}}
. For measures with finite support (i.e., when
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{fam}}
closes after
m_{\mathrm{fam}}
steps), one has

Q_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{fam}}\;=\;m_{\mathrm{fam}}-1,

(55)

matching the single-state case. For infinite support, the minimal query count is instead given by

Q_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\mathrm{fam}}\;=\;n_{\mu_{\mathrm{fam}}}(f,\varepsilon),

(56)

the smallest degree-
n
polynomial that approximates
f
to accuracy
\varepsilon
in
L^{2}(\mu_{\mathrm{fam}})
, where
\mu_{\mathrm{fam}}
is the family spectral measure.

This shows the distinction from the worst-case setting. In the input-agnostic case, query complexity is governed by the degree of the minimax (Chebyshev) polynomial approximating
f
on
\mathrm{spec}(H)
, which typically scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
regardless of the input state. By contrast, the state-aware family formalism replaces this worst-case degree with the joint Krylov dimension
m_{\mathrm{fam}}-1
(or more generally the family-aware degree functional), which depends only on the occupied spectral support of the family. When
\mathcal{S}
overlaps only a restricted or highly correlated part of the spectrum, this can be orders of magnitude smaller than the worst-case scenario.

V
Discussions and Conclusion

We have shown that in the state-aware setting, quantum simulation reduces exactly to evolution in the Krylov subspace generated by
(H,\ket{\psi_{0}})
, with the Jacobi matrix
J
providing the minimal effective Hamiltonian. For the finite support case, and a generic function
f
, the Krylov dimension
K_{D}
sets the optimal query complexity
K_{D}-1
, while for the infinite support case, the optimal polynomial approximation follows from the Krylov/Favard approximations. This framework applies to any
f(H)
, including algorithms like HHL, and yields potentially large savings over worst-case methods. Extensions to Christoffel filtering, non-Hermitian settings, and quantum–classical advantage boundaries offer promising directions for future work, which are as follows.

Orthogonal Polynomials theory

Our work reveals a deep connection between the theory of orthogonal polynomials and quantum complexity. Techniques from the theory of orthogonal polynomials—such as spectral transformations (e.g., Christoffel filtering), classical inequalities, and results like Bochner’s theorem—can be leveraged to further reduce query complexity. Moreover, the underlying mathematics of the QSVT formalism exhibits striking parallels with supersymmetric quantum mechanics, suggesting fruitful directions for future study. Extensions from single-variable to multivariate settings, for instance, through Jack polynomials, offer another promising avenue for generalization.

Quantum-classical advantage boundary

So far, we have focused on the quantum query complexity. However, if the quantum query complexity is polynomial, while the classical methods would require superpolynomial or even exponential queries, the quantum-classical boundary can be defined. A similar approach has already been used in the context of quantum versus classical walks.

Coherent noise erros

In Krylov-based algorithms, the spectral measure
\mu_{H,\psi_{0}}
, determined entirely by the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, fixes the orthonormal polynomial sequence
\{p_{n}\}
and the recurrence coefficients
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})
that govern the Krylov dynamics. Consequently, even a small coherent perturbation to
|\psi_{0}\rangle
can significantly alter this measure, modifying the optimal polynomial approximation problem faced by the algorithm. Coherent implementation errors
H\mapsto H+\delta H
induce inhomogeneous perturbations
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\mapsto(\alpha_{n}+\delta\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}+\delta\beta_{n})
; sufficiently irregular variations act as disorder on the Krylov chain and can trigger Anderson localization, whereby amplitudes fail to reach large
n
and quantities such as
C(t)
saturate prematurely. This leads to a distinct, “silent” failure mode in which the circuit continues to operate, but computation stalls in low-
n
sectors. Mitigation of such errors seems extremely crucial as well for a functioning quantum computation.

Benchmarks and experimental validation.

Using the state-aware methods, one can also develop benchmark suites where the spectral measure
\mu
is analytically known or efficiently learnable—such as gapped lattice systems, or sparse graph Hamiltonians—to enable systematic end-to-end validation of Krylov-based pipelines. This can be implemented on both NISQ and fault-tolerant simulators to confirm that the realized query counts follow the theoretically predicted
K_{D}-1
under realistic conditions of noise, disorder, and calibration drift.
