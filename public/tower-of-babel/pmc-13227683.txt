Epidemiology and clinical characteristics of intestinal parasites among patients of a medical center in central Taiwan: a retrospective study of Blastocystis infection
Authors: Ju-Ying Chien, Li-Lian Chao, Chien-Ming Shih, Chia-Hui Shen, Sung-Yuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal parasitic infections remain among the most common infectious diseases worldwide, posing significant public health challenges. Blastocystis spp., in particular, has emerged as the most prevalent microorganism detected in human stool examinations in recent years. Accumulating evidence suggests a potential association between specific Blastocystis subtypes and gastrointestinal disturbances. This study aimed to assess the current epidemiological profile of intestinal parasitic infections in Taiwan and to explore potential associations between parasitic infections and chronic diseases.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using 1498 parasite-positive stool reports from 1265 patients who underwent stool examinations at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (TCVGH) between January 2012 and December 2023. Stool specimens were examined using the merthiolate-iodine-formaldehyde (MIF) method, as recommended by the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control. Demographic and clinical data were collected to evaluate infection patterns, stool specimen positive rate, and species distribution across different patient subgroups.ResultsDuring the 12-year surveillance period, the average stool specimen positivity rate was 0.9%, ranging from 0.46 to 1.54%. The majority of infected individuals (81.43%) were local Taiwanese residents. Young adult males (aged 18–64 years) had a higher risk of infection. Blastocystis spp. represented the predominant parasite, accounting for 87.19% of all identified cases, followed by Giardia lamblia (9.16%). Background

Intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) are distributed worldwide and are transmitted primarily through the fecal-oral route or by skin penetration. These infections are particularly prevalent in subtropical and tropical regions and encompass both helminthic and protozoan diseases, with amoebiasis, ascariasis, hookworm infection, and trichuriasis among the most common parasitic infections globally, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Expert Committee report in 1987 [1].

Blastocystis spp., an enteric protozoan, has increasingly attracted attention in recent decades as one of the most frequently detected microorganisms in stool examinations worldwide. Reported prevalence rates vary substantially among countries, ranging from 7 to 50% in industrialized nations and from 55 to 100% in developing regions [2]. Despite its global distribution, the pathogenicity and modes of transmission of Blastocystis spp. remain incompletely understood. Most infected individuals exhibit no apparent gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, although sporadic cases of severe illness have been documented [3]. In recent years, numerous studies have indicated possible associations between Blastocystis subtypes, the gut microbiota, and gastrointestinal disorders such as irritable bowel disease (IBD), suggesting a more complex host–parasite interaction than previously recognized.

In Taiwan, the prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections reached 78.3% in the 1920s based on surveys conducted during the Japanese colonial period [4]. The infection rate subsequently declined markedly-from approximately 70% in the 1950s to 2% in the 1990s-following a series of public health interventions and urbanization.

A renewed increase in research activity was observed in the 1990s, coinciding with the arrival of foreign migrant workers in Taiwan, with many studies focusing on this population. High positivity rates for intestinal parasites were reported in stool screenings among migrant workers, and Blastocystis spp. gradually became the most frequently identified organism in these studies, consistent with global trends [5–7].

Although parasitic diseases have largely faded from public discourse in Taiwan since industrialization, sporadic infections continue to occur. The growing number of migrant workers, together with climate change [8], has raised concerns regarding the potential re-emergence of intestinal parasitic infections. At present, only amoebiasis and toxocariasis are included in the government’s official disease surveillance system [9].

To assess the current epidemiological situation, species distribution, and potential associations between intestinal parasitic infections and chronic diseases, we retrospectively analyzed patients who visited a medical center in central Taiwan between 2012 and 2023. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to evaluate intestinal parasitic infections among Taiwanese residents following the liberalization of the domestic labor market in 1992. The findings are expected to contribute to the understanding of the contemporary epidemiology of parasitic diseases in Taiwan.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study employed a retrospective observational design to investigate the prevalence and characteristics of intestinal parasitic infections based on stool specimen analysis. The study was conducted at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (TCVGH), Taiwan, and approved by the Institutional Review Board of TCVGH (Approval No. CE25203A).

Data collection and definition

We retrospectively analyzed stool specimens that tested positive for intestinal parasites over a 12-year period, from January 2012 to December 2023. In the medical records, these stool samples were obtained from patients in the emergency department, outpatient departments, and hospital wards. The positive specimens were confirmed using the Merthiolate-Iodine-Formaldehyde (MIF) concentration technique and microscopic examination, as recommended by the Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (CDC) [7]. All associated patient data were extracted from the electronic medical records at TCVGH.

The following data were collected for analysis: patient demographics, preexisting comorbidities, and identified parasite species. Individuals were classified as Taiwanese or expatriates (expats) based on national identification numbers. To account for recurrent cases, repeat infection was defined as a positive parasitic stool specimen occurring more than 30 days after a previous positive result. Co-infection was defined as the presence of two or more parasitic species within the same stool specimen. The stool specimen positivity rate was defined as the number of parasite-positive samples per year divided by the total number of stool microscopy examinations performed annually.

Age groups were categorized as follows: children (≤ 17 years), adults (18–64 years), and the elderly (≥ 65 years). Figure 1 and Table 1 illustrate the annual distribution and demographic characteristics of the included cases.

Stool examination procedure

Stool samples were collected using the S-Y Feces Examination Apparatus (SYFEA) for both ova and occult blood (OB) testing. For expatriate patients undergoing stool parasite examination, specimens were transported to the laboratory within 30 min of passage. For general stool microscopy, samples were delivered within four hours. All examinations were completed within 30 min of receipt in the laboratory.

Intestinal parasite detection was performed using the MIF concentration method. A 7.5 mL solution of merthiolate-formaldehyde and 0.5 mL of iodine were mixed in a bottle, which was then filled with the stool sample. The mixture was filtered through the SYFEA apparatus, then ether was added. The filtered solution was then centrifuged at 500 × g for 2 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the sediment was examined microscopically to identify parasitic ova, cysts, or trophozoites [10].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics were performed to compare characteristics between Taiwanese and expatriate patients, and between individuals undergoing routine health examinations versus those who did not. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages.

Comparative analysis of continuous data was conducted using Student’s t-test. For categorical variables, the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was applied, depending on sample size and distribution. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Stool positive rate of intestinal parasitic infections

A total of 1498 stool specimens were collected from 1265 individuals who underwent microscopic stool examination at TCVGH in Taiwan between 2012 and 2023. Over this 12-year period, the median annual number of parasite-positive specimens was 117.5, ranging from 80 to 197 specimens. The overall average stool specimen positivity rate was 0.9%, with yearly rates ranging from 0.46% to 1.54%. These rates reflect the proportion of parasite-positive specimens among those tested and should not be interpreted as prevalence in the general population. Most of the specimens originated from individuals undergoing routine health examinations, either in inpatient or outpatient settings (Table 1).

Demographic characteristics of infected individuals

Among the 1265 individuals who tested positive for intestinal parasites, 1019 (80.56%) cases were detected from stool samples collected during health examinations (Table 2). Taiwanese nationals comprised the majority of the infected group (81.43%). In the health examination group, the gender ratio was evenly distributed, whereas in the symptomatic (non-health exam) group, males were predominant, accounting for 67.88% of cases. Among Taiwanese individuals, infections were more common among males (61.26%), while females comprised the majority of the expatriate group (78.72%). In terms of age distribution, the largest proportion of cases was among individuals aged 18 to 64, followed by those aged 65 and above. Infection cases occurred throughout all four seasons, indicating no significant seasonal pattern.

Repeat infections and comorbidity associations

An analysis of reinfection frequency is presented in Fig. 1. Of the 1265 individuals with confirmed infections, 158 had multiple positive stool specimens recorded. After excluding consecutive collections that may not represent distinct episodes, 139 individuals remained, with non-consecutive repeat-positive results separated by intervals of several months or years. Supplementary Table S1 provides additional data on comorbidities and infection frequency. The most frequently recurring parasites in this subgroup were Blastocystis spp. and Giardia lamblia. Notably, co-infection with multiple parasite species was more prevalent among individuals with repeat infections (3.59%) than among those with single episodes (1.42%). However, no statistically significant associations were identified between repeat infection and factors such as gender, age, parasite species, or presence of chronic disease.

Common parasite species by population group

As shown in Table 3, Blastocystis spp. was the predominant intestinal parasite identified, accounting for 1103 of the 1265 cases (87.2%), followed by Giardia lamblia (n = 116, 9.2%). Entamoeba histolytica/dispar was detected in only eight cases. Other parasites, including Enterobius vermicularis, Strongyloides stercoralis, Trichuris trichiura, hookworms, and Cryptosporidium spp., were rarely encountered, with case numbers for each species remaining below 15. Among Taiwanese individuals, the top three parasites were Blastocystis spp. (85.24%), G. lamblia (10.97%), and Entamoeba coli (1.55%). In the expatriate population, Blastocystis spp. was even more dominant (95.74%), followed by Endolimax nana (3.82%) and Entamoeba coli (2.13%). A total of 18 cases (1.42%) involved co-infection with two parasite species. The most common co-infection pattern was Blastocystis spp. in combination with Endolimax nana, and Blastocystis spp. was involved in all co-infected cases.

Discussion

This retrospective cross-sectional study examined the composition of intestinal parasites and the demographic characteristics of individuals who underwent stool examinations in central Taiwan between 2012 and 2023. A total of 1498 stool specimens tested positive, representing 1265 individuals infected with intestinal parasites. Among them, 139 individuals experienced repeated infections. Local Taiwanese accounted for 81.43% of the infected cases. Most of the parasite-positive specimens (80.55%) were collected during routine health examinations. In terms of nationality and gender, Taiwanese males constituted the majority of positive cases (61.26%), a trend also observed among symptomatic visits. In contrast, foreign females formed the majority of infected cases in the expatriate group. This finding is likely related to the higher number of foreign women coming to Taiwan for marriage, work, or study, as reported by the National Immigration Agency in 2021 (52.66% female vs. 47.34% male) [11]. Most infected individuals were adults aged 18–64 years, with over 97% of cases in both local and foreign groups falling within this age range. Among those undergoing health examinations, adults represented the majority of positive cases. However, in the symptomatic group (non-health examination), elderly individuals were the most susceptible. Review the previous research on adults and children, possible contributing factors include immunocompromised status [12], inadequate awareness of sanitation practices, and increased contact with foreign caregivers [13]. These factors warrant further investigation. Notably, the occurrence of intestinal parasitic infections did not vary significantly by season, regardless of patient nationality or whether the stool specimens came from symptomatic individuals or those undergoing routine checks. In summary, our data reflect the current state of intestinal parasitic infections in Taiwan, with adult males comprising the majority of infected individuals.

This study also revealed a shift in the composition of intestinal parasites in Taiwan. In the 1990s, the infection rate of intestinal parasites was approximately 2%, with species such as Clonorchis sinensis and blood filarial worms prevalent in the local area [4]. While helminth infections have decreased, Enterobius vermicularis has maintained an average prevalence of 1.5% in recent years. In certain counties, the infection rate among elementary school children reached 14.48% in 2005 [14]. Interestingly, the number of Entamoeba histolytica cases observed in our study was lower than the estimated prevalence in central Taiwan (approximately 30 per 1,000,000 people annually) [9]. Although we could not determine the exact prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections, the overall positive rate for stool specimens declined over the study period. This decline may be attributed to ongoing urbanization, improvements in sanitation policies, and the accessibility provided by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI), which together have interrupted fecal-oral transmission routes. However, the further decrease in positive cases over the last five years may be due in part to reduced international travel and behavioral restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, the dominant type of parasites has shifted from helminths to protozoa, with protozoa being the most common cause of both single and double infections. Blastocystis spp. was the most frequently detected parasite, accounting for 87.19% of all positive specimens.

Blastocystis spp. is likely the most common intestinal parasite globally [15]. Although its pathogenicity remains uncertain, several studies have suggested associations with various clinical conditions, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), IBD, and alterations in gut microbiota composition [16–18]. Some reports have also discussed its potential impact on child development and cognitive function [18, 19]. Historical data from Taiwan indicate that Blastocystis spp. was already prevalent among migrant workers in 1999, with a reported positive rate of 3.4% [20]. In the current study, Blastocystis spp. accounted for 85.24% of infections in the Taiwanese group and 95.74% in the foreign group. All observed co-infections involved Blastocystis spp., with the most common combination being Blastocystis spp. and Endolimax nana. Prior research has indicated that this combination may be associated with bowel inflammation [21]. In our study, among the 878 Blastocystis spp.-Positive Taiwanese patients, no cases of IBD were recorded, and only seven cases of IBS were identified (Fig. 2). However, 41 patients were concurrently diagnosed with colitis, yielding a coexistence rate of 4.7%. This rate is notably higher than the general prevalence of ulcerative colitis in Taiwan (12.8 per 100,000 in 2015) [22]. It is regrettable that our study used retrospective data and that no further molecular analyses were performed for Blastocystis spp. subtypes. We cannot precisely assess the relationship between subtypes and colitis, and it remains unclear whether these colitis cases will progress to IBD, suggesting the need for continued monitoring.

The coincidence infection and comorbidities of Blastocystis spp

The low incidence of pathogenic parasites among expatriates may be attributable to Taiwan's mandatory stool screening policy for foreigners. Given the potential pathogenicity of Blastocystis spp., we emphasize the need for comprehensive surveys to determine its true prevalence and to investigate possible associations with chronic diseases. To identify potential risk factors for intestinal parasitic infections, we classified cases into two groups: those with a single infection and those with repeated infections (Supplementary Table S1). Among the repeated infection group, Taiwanese individuals comprised 89.21% of cases, and young adults (aged 18–64) accounted for 92.08%. There were no significant differences in gender, season, or prevalence of major chronic diseases between the two groups. Notably, Blastocystis spp. and Giardia lamblia were the most commonly recurring parasites. Previous studies have suggested that these species may colonize the human intestine for extended periods [23, 24]. External factors that could influence the susceptibility of young Taiwanese adults—such as travel history, occupational exposure, or residential environment—should be further investigated.

Severe immunocompromised status may amplify the clinical impact of intestinal parasites. Nineteen deceased patients in our cohort presented with abdominal pain or diarrhea during hospitalization; 10 of them received metronidazole treatment. Of these, 17 were reported with Blastocystis spp., while the remaining two had coexisted with Giardia lamblia and Strongyloides stercoralis, respectively. Causes of death included acute respiratory failure, septic shock, or terminal-stage cancer. Although the fatality rate due to intestinal parasites in Taiwan is extremely low, their symptoms may be exacerbated in immunocompromised individuals. Prior research has indicated that the pathogenicity of Blastocystis spp. may vary by subtype. In Asia, the most common subtypes are ST1 and ST3, with ST1 being associated with IBS and alterations in gastrointestinal microbiota. Based on this, we speculate that Blastocystis spp. has a relatively low potential to cause opportunistic infections in the general population.

This study has several limitations. First, although Taichung Municipality has a population of approximately 2.8 million and includes three medical centers and 61 regional and local hospitals, our data were collected from only one medical center, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, only positive stool specimens were included to indicate intestinal parasitic infection; histopathological diagnoses from surgical biopsies were not considered. Third, we analyzed stool specimens from symptomatic patients and individuals undergoing health examinations, but not all clinical orders were fulfilled, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our dataset. Fourth, Enterobius vermicularis screenings in elementary schools are conducted by local education bureaus and authorized hospitals. Only severe cases are referred to medical centers, and some mildly symptomatic children receive medication without stool analysis. These factors may contribute to underreporting. Fifth, our dataset lacked molecular characterization of Blastocystis spp., and subtype variation is known to influence host interactions and clinical relevance. This limitation may affect the interpretation of our findings. Sixth, the diagnosis based on the MIF examination relies on the morphological characteristics of organisms. This method does not allow differentiation of Blastocystis subtypes, and its performance is dependent on operator expertise and light microscopy. It may underestimate mixed infections and cannot distinguish colonization from clinically relevant infection. Lastly, as a retrospective study, it is limited by the availability of baseline information such as occupation, travel history, and residential status, which may have influenced both infection rates and our findings.

Conclusions

This study contributes to the understanding of the status of intestinal parasitic infections in Taiwan. Young adult males were found to be at higher risk of infection; however, the underlying causes remain under investigation. Analysis of the composition of intestinal parasites revealed that Blastocystis spp. plays a leading role in both infection and colonization. We recommend conducting a comprehensive survey to better understand the prevalence, molecular characteristics, and pathophysiological mechanisms of Blastocystis spp. before definitive conclusions about this protozoan can be drawn.
