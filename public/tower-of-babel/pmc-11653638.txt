A systematic review of whether the number of linguistic errors in medical interpretation is associated with the use of professional vs ad hoc interpreters
Authors: Iben Gad Lauridsen, Morten Deleuran Terkildsen, Lisbeth Uhrskov Sørensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical consultations depend on a shared linguistic understanding between the patient and physician. When language concordance is not possible, interpretation is required. Prior studies have revealed that professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) results in patients reporting higher satisfaction and a better understanding of things the physician explained. Despite this, language-discordance often results in using family and/or friends for ad hoc interpretation. This systematic review examines the linguistic aspect of medical interpretation by assessing the number of linguistic errors made and their relation to professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) or in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI). PIPI was defined as people employed as interpreters, but with no specific requirements for education or experience. This systematic review examines studies comparing the number of errors when using PIPI and IPAHI. We performed a PICO-criteria-based search in five scientific databases. We screened English and Danish studies published between 1995 and October 2024. Furthermore, we screened references from, and citations of the included articles. We used the appropriate Cochrane Tool for risk of bias assessment. We identified six studies using a PICO search and one additional study by snowballing. The included studies revealed critical methodological differences, and consequently a statistical synthesis of results was not conducted. We found indications that the number of interpreting errors was significantly lower when using PIPI than family members for IPAHI. Interpreting error rates were not significantly lower when comparing PIPI to the use of medical staff without interpretation training for IPAHI. Generally, we found that the difference between PIPI and IPAHI tended to be more prominent when dealing with more severe diagnoses, e.g., incurable cancer. The methodological differences between included studies and the risk of bias within included studies limit the conclusions drawn in this review. Also, no other kinds of interpretation than PIPI and IPAHI were considered, and the recommendations are solely based on accuracy. Considering these limitations and the fact that no other systematic reviews within this highly specific topic exist, this review resulted in the following recommendations: 1) Professional in-person interpretation should be the first choice in language-discordant medical consultations. 2) If professional interpretation is not possible, using medical staff without interpretation training should be chosen before interpretation by family or friends. 3) All consultation participants should keep sentences short and straightforward, as this is related to a lower risk of omissions in interpretation.Keywords: Interpretation, Translation, Interpreting errors, Communication barriers, Limited English proficiency, Health care

Introduction

Patient satisfaction is closely related to understanding the physician. Thus, language concordance (having a shared language) leads to a better understanding of physician explanations and instructions and a stronger feeling of being treated with respect than consultations defined by language discordance [1]. Furthermore, when consulted by a language-concordant physician, patients are generally more likely to ask questions and initiate unprompted speech than in language-discordant consultations [2, 3]. Also, language-discordant patients receive care with a lower chance of hospital admittance [4] and a higher risk of transfer to the intensive care unit within 24 h of admittance [4]. In a world with increasing migration [5] and a subsequent increase in non-native speakers in health care, the prevalence of language-discordant consultations is rising, resulting in an increasing need for interpretation.

Using interpreters to overcome language discordance may influence the quality of care and clinical outcomes. Studies comparing language-discordant patients without any interpretation and language-discordant patients with professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) demonstrate how such interpretation may critically improve access to care interventions [6], shorten the length of hospital stays [7], and lower the risk of readmissions [7]. When interpreters are used, it is well documented that the use of PIPI is rated better than the other, often prevalent, method of in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI), i.e. using a person not employed as an interpreter such as a family member, friend, nurse or social worker as an interpreter, in regards to both patient satisfaction [8–11], understanding of things that were explained by the physician [10], and in willingness among patients to report psychological symptoms [12].

For both forms of interpreting (PIPI and IPAHI), the accuracy of the interpretation is a natural predictor of other outcomes. However, recent studies indicate that interpretations often suffer from inaccuracies [13] and that both patients and physicians often articulate significantly more statements than are interpreted (in PIPI consultations) [14]. Such obstacles have prompted previous reviews within the field of medical interpretation to call for additional research concerning language use services in general [15] and specifically different methods of interpretation [16], this includes modality (e.g. in-person, remote) and type (e.g. professional, ad hoc).

A recently published review by Heath et al. [17] found that professional interpretation resulted in the highest satisfaction and best overall communication. However, no reviews have explicitly touched upon the accuracy of interpretation and the relation to the severity of diagnosis or ad hoc interpretation by individuals with different types of preparation. Heath et al. [17] include four studies [18–21] also included in this review. However, we have found three additional studies [22–24] regarding errors in medical interpretation. Adding these three extra studies to a field limited by a significant lack of research increases the study pool by 75% and, at the same time, broadens the included languages significantly, as one of the additional studies is English/Bengali [23] and another is Japanese/Portuguese [24]. In addition, we try to answer Heath et al.‘s [17] call for future research to distinguish between relational interpreters (e.g. family, friends, other acquaintances) and other kinds of ad hoc interpretation (e.g. strangers from the waiting area, nurses, social workers). Therefore, this review may further help fill existing research gaps on accuracy of interpretation and add to the findings of Heath et al. [17] among others. We aim to assess the collective studies regarding the accuracy of interpretation in a clinical setting when using PIPI vs. IPAHI. We evaluate studies from different countries, in different languages, and provide insights into the effects of type of IPAHI on interpretation accuracy. We do so to crucially guide further development of health care services for patients experiencing language barriers.

Methods

Search strategy

This review is based on the STROBE [25, 26] and PRISMA [27, 28] criteria and checklists. STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology) and PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) were developed to improve reporting and conform to a standard format within research. The first author conducted a systematic literature search using (1) The databases PubMed [29], Web of Science [30], Embase [31], Scopus [32] and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) [33], (2) The references of eligible articles found in the initial search, and (3) A Scopus search of articles citing one of the included articles. Whenever the first author could not firmly establish the eligibility of an article, she would discuss such issues with the third author. The first author conducted an initial PICO-criteria-based search in February 2021 in PubMed. The PICO-method is used to find search-terms based on the following categories: Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison and Outcome. This approach led to the identification of the following relevant MeSH terms: Medical Errors OR Quality of Health Care, AND Communication Barriers OR Multilingualism, AND Translations OR Translating. The search was updated several times throughout our research. We conducted the final search in the included databases on October 7th, 2024. This search generated 1187 articles (Fig. 1).

Eligibility – inclusion and exclusion

When searching the separate databases, filters were applied, if possible, to exclude articles based on (1) Lack of abstract, (2) Full text not available, (3) Language other than Danish or English, (4) Articles published before 1995 and/or (5) Study of other species than humans. These filters excluded 164 articles; the remaining 1023 were manually deduplicated by the first author, resulting in 862 articles.

These remaining 862 articles were screened through title and/or abstract, and 40 papers were either screened based on the full text or thoroughly read. Only articles within the following criteria were included: (1) The study design had to be cross-sectional or randomized controlled trials, either as observations of real medical encounters or scripted consultations. (2) The intervention had to be professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) vs. in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI), and (3) The outcome had to be the number of interpretation errors.

All included studies were based on transcribed audiotaped consultations. All included studies compared linguistic errors for PIPI vs. IPAHI, but some also evaluated the use of professional remote consecutive interpretation, professional remote simultaneous interpretation, and/or no interpretation. These interpretation modalities are not included in this review. Additionally, results concerning interpretation speed, and the clinical significance of linguistic errors were not included.

The process mentioned above resulted in the inclusion of six articles. Screening references of these 6 articles yielded one extra article. Afterward, a search was made in Scopus on October 11th, 2024, to identify articles citing any of the seven included articles [18–24]. This process identified another 636 articles, which were deduplicated and screened. None of these were eligible for inclusion. The first author read one of the excluded studies [34] found through snowballing very thoroughly as it is a study of communication in language-discordant consultations comparing the use of PIPI and IPAHI. This study compared the number of sentences uttered by the clinician, interpreter, and patient within different conversation categories. Still, the study did not address if the interpretation of each sentence was linguistically correct, and the study outcome did thus not fall within the aim of this review. As the study had many similarities with other included studies, the first author screened the 60 references of this study [34]. This process yielded zero new studies eligible for inclusion. These 60 references are not shown in the flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

We applied the STROBE checklist for cross-sectional studies [25, 26] to evaluate the reporting quality of included studies.

For bias assessment, we used “The Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I)” [36] tool and “The Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions” [37]. All the included articles were systematically analyzed by the first author using the ROBINS-I template [38]. The ROBINS-I tool focuses on bias assessment within the following domains: Pre-Intervention (Confounding and Selection bias), At-Intervention (Information bias), and Post-Intervention (Confounding, Selection bias, Information bias and Reporting bias). As recommended by the handbook, we rated the overall risk of bias for an article according to the highest rating within a single domain. The risk of bias within domains is rated as Low (comparable to randomized trial), Moderate (sound, but not comparable to a randomized trial), Serious (important problems) or Critical (too problematic to provide evidence regarding the intervention). This means that studies with some important problems within a single domain (e.g. confounding), but otherwise sound within all other domains, will still end up with an overall bias assessment of serious. The entire process mentioned above was subsequently repeated by the first and third author, discussing assessments within every domain for every article. An agreement was reached whenever doubt arose. (Table 3).

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Study designs were all cross-sectional, either as observations of real medical encounters or scripted consultations (Table 1). The actual/real medical encounters were all conducted in outpatient clinical settings, where professionals asked patients to participate upon arrival at the medical facility. The patients were assigned the interpretation type that would have been used regardless of the studies. The scripted consultation studies were generated by creating bilingual scripts containing comparable, standardized medical content. Researchers gave the script to the physician and the patient but not the interpreter. These scripts were then acted out several times with different types of interpretation and different interpreters. Having the doctor and patient act out the script with the interpreter knowing nothing about its contents beforehand resulted in situations mimicking real out-patient consultations. As a measure of consultation length, four of the seven studies [20–23] stated the number of conversation segments (CS) defined as an identifiable delineated unit of continuous speech. All included studies were audiotaped, 6/7 were conducted with English speaking doctors [18–23] and 1/7 was conducted with Japanese speaking doctors [24]. The recordings were transcribed, and all segments were translated into the language of the doctor (English or Japanese). In 6/7 instances this was done by bilingual researchers, transcriptionists or a medical linguist. The remaining article [20] did not provide a description of the translators’ qualifications but used the same translators for all transcripts. A team of error analysts consisting of two to four authors, bilingual coders, linguists, physicians, and/or behavioral scientists then analyzed the monolingual transcripts.

The team conducted error analysis within all the included studies by highlighting all dissimilarities in the monolingual transcripts and individually assessing them. All included studies had an individual definition of errors, but the main aim of all studies was to evaluate whether the essence of the statement was preserved and passed on. Error analysis was based on conversation segments (CS) [18, 20–24] or the entire consultation as one [19], using either a categorical “in-accurate/accurate” classification [18, 20, 22–24] or a dimensional classification “the number of errors” [19, 21]. The precision of error analysis within each study was assessed by comparing the work of two independent coders. Disagreements ranged from 0 to 5% in 3 studies [19, 22, 23] to 10% in one study [18]. Hamai et al. [24]. used a different method to evaluate precision and states an agreement by kappa coefficient of 0.955 and 0.908. The remaining two studies [20, 21] did not state the degree of disagreement in error analysis.

One of the included studies [22] found that an 11-year-old girl did the linguistically best interpretation among the people conducting IPAHI. She interpreted as directly as possible without inserting herself into the conversation.

Several of the included studies also evaluated the type of errors, with one specific error dominating the studies. Errors of omission were quantified differently across studies but constituted 31% of all errors in one study [18]. Three other studies quantified omissions divided in PIPI and IPAHI. When using PIPI 40% [24], 42% [19] and 63% [20] of errors were identified as omissions. For IPAHI 51% [24], 46% [19] and 61% [20] of errors were identified as omissions. Also, two studies [21, 22] found that the longer and more complex the sentences were, the higher the risk of misinterpretation. Conversation segments (CS) with the interpretation quality score “Good” had a mean word count of 9.2, whereas CS with the interpretation quality score “Poor” had a mean word count of 27.1 [22]. One study [21] found a higher risk of medical errors of moderate or greater clinical significance in two instances. These were when CS contained more than one concept (OR 2.76), and professional interpreters had less than 1 year of interpretation experience (OR 2.78).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodology of included studies was generally well-described, although many had the same issues. The reporting of bias and possible confounders was scarce. Most of the studies did not sufficiently report how the patients and physicians were chosen to participate or give any characteristics of these participants. None of the studies reported the number of consultations intended to be included before the start of inclusion, and none provided sample size calculations.

Only one study states that the patients and physicians were blinded to the intervention and outcome [19]. In the remaining studies, the patients and physicians were either not blinded, or the study did not report whether the participants were blinded. (Table 3).

The five studies that ended with a serious overall risk of bias were all classified as such based on the domain Pre-intervention Confounding [18–20, 22, 24]. These studies did not use an analysis method that controlled for confounding domains. Also, not all studies collected any data within possible pre-intervention confounding domains. Pre-intervention confounding could be related to, e.g., patient education, English knowledge, and/or cultural background.

Discussion

Main results

The main findings of this review can be summarized as follows. Four of the 7 included studies [18, 20, 23, 24] identified significantly lower numbers of interpreting errors when using PIPI compared to IPAHI. Furthermore, two of the included studies [21, 22] revealed that: shorter sentences and only one message within a statement tended to lower the risk of interpretation errors.

In addition to this, our analysis (Table 2) indicates that:

In cases of severe diagnosis, a lower risk of interpreting errors was observed when using professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) compared to in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI).

In cases of severe diagnosis, a lower risk of interpreting errors was observed when using professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) compared to in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI).

When using in-person ad hoc interpretation (IPAHI), the use of medical staff without interpreter training tended to give a lower risk of interpreting errors compared to the use of family and friends.

Professional in-person interpretation is preferred, especially when dealing with severe diagnosis

Previous reviews within the medical interpretation field recommend that professional interpreters be favored over ad hoc interpretation [16, 17, 39, 40]. Considering this, it is surprising that only 57% of the included studies in this review found significantly lower error rates with PIPI than IPAHI [18, 20, 23, 24]. However, this review only evaluates linguistic errors. Other reviews assess several other elements, e.g., confidentiality, clinical importance of the errors, or patient preferences. At the same time, a lack of evidence for PIPI is not the same as evidence against PIPI. In addition to this recommendation, we add that using PIPI is especially crucial in consultations dealing with severe diagnoses, as our review indicates that studies conducted within specialties with a higher degree of severe diagnoses (oncology [18, 23] and internal medicine [20]) did majorly fall within the significant category (Table 2). In contrast, the non-significant studies were generally conducted in specialties with a lower degree of severe diagnoses (primary care [21, 22] and ED/outpatient pediatrics [19, 22]) (Table 2). At the same time, one could argue that the possible errors might have a more severe clinical effect when already dealing with a severe diagnosis. This adds to our argument for the recommendation of PIPI. As the severity of a specific case can be challenging to predict, the recommendation to use PIPI in severe cases leads us to recommend PIPI in all cases.

When ad hoc interpretation is necessary, medical staff is preferred

Within the category of IPAHI, this review indicates that the background of people conducting IPAHI could be of importance (Table 2). This insight aligns with Heath et al.’s [17] call for future research to distinguish relational interpreters from other kinds of ad hoc interpretation. Although the studies included in this review did not make this separation either, we found a higher risk of errors when family or friends [18, 20, 23, 24] were used for IPAHI as opposed to hospital employees without interpretation training [21, 22], e.g., nurses or social workers (Table 2). We speculate that the difference between using family/friends as opposed to medical staff without interpretation training might be attributed to medical knowledge or experience, just as experience was shown to be essential for professional interpreters [21]. Also, when IPAHI is used, special attention is required regarding potential privacy issues, as previous research found that patients report concerns about confidentiality when using IPAHI [41, 42], as well as experiences of family interpreters trying to control the consultation [42].

Children should not be used for ad hoc interpretation

One included study [22] indicates that using children for IPAHI might yield a more precise interpretation. However, using children for IPAHI may not be without its challenges. For example, Laws et al. [22] found that the physician did not trust the accuracy of the child’s interpretation. More urgently, using children as interpreters may bring up critical ethical issues. As highlighted in a case report [43] a 10-year-old girl acted as an interpreter in the medical care of her dying younger brother. She developed severe PTSD and pervasive refusal syndrome (PRS) to the extent that she was admitted to long-lasting treatment at a child psychiatric ward. PRS is a severe and rare child psychiatric disorder [44], in this case, thought to have been triggered by the immense involvement in her brother’s medical care. In addition to this, previous research has found a higher degree of depressive symptoms among children who expressed negative feelings concerning the act of interpreting [45, 46]. Also, Hua et al. [47] found that more frequent interpretation by the child was associated with a higher degree of parent-child conflict. We strongly recommend against using children in interpretation, as it is associated with the risk of unwanted consequences to the child [43, 45–47], and although the risk of errors was low, the study did find a higher rate of clinically significant errors [22].

Beware the risk of omissions and the complexity of sentences

Previously, Sleptsova et al. [48] have found an overall percentage of omission of 42%, but with a higher level of omission when interpreting physician utterances than patient utterances. We found high percentages of omissions (31–63% of all errors) within 4 of the included studies [18–20, 24]. Two of the included studies [21, 22] evaluated the complexity and length of sentences. They found that conversation segments should be short and only contain one message to optimize interpretation. The causes hereof were not evaluated, but we hypothesize that the cause could be using consecutive interpretation. When interpreting consecutively, the interpreter must remember what was said and then provide a rendition afterward. Naturally, as the conversation segments grow longer, so does the risk of partially forgetting what was said. A possible way to amend this could be improving note-taking skills among interpreters. We encourage future research into ways to amend this.

Education and experience improve the linguistic accuracy of professional interpretation

As seen in Table 2, our results indicate that the background of people conducting PIPI is essential, as the tendency to have education within the field was higher within the significant studies. Also, included studies indicate that education [19] and experience [21] improve the quality of interpretation. This finding aligns with previous research [16] recommending that professional interpreters should be educated within the field. In addition, we argue that supervision and apprenticeship should also be included in the training of interpreters, as the risk of medical errors is related to the extent of experience [21]. With supervision and apprenticeship, it is possible to achieve experience among newly educated interpreters without risking a lower quality of interpretation.

Strengths and limitations

This review is based on a thorough literature search and subsequent snowballing, making it reasonable to assume that all relevant studies concerning the aim of this study are included. Despite this, only seven studies were identified, and within these studies, considerable methodological heterogeneity is evident. Although we believe to have captured all relevant peer-reviewed literature in this review there is a general risk of publication bias within both this review and the international research community, since most researchers exclude both grey literature, unpublished studies and sources beyond the writers’ own languages and English.

Risk of bias

Notably, our risk of bias analysis resulted in five of seven studies being labeled with a serious overall risk of bias [18–20, 22, 24]. The serious risk of bias pertained to the Pre-Intervention Confounding domain for all five studies and the fact that the studies did not control for this through analysis. The possible Pre-Intervention Confounding domains we identified were the patients’ educational level, English knowledge, and cultural background. For these domains to significantly impact the conclusions drawn in the studies, they must be predictive of both the intervention and outcome. We argue that the effect of these possible confounders on outcome is low as the patients’ English knowledge, cultural background, and educational level should not influence the degree of errors in the interpretation. On the other hand, it could be argued that the domains mentioned above could affect the length, complexity and idiomatic nature of the patients’ speech, which could influence the number of errors. Also, there could be confounders that we did not find, and thus did not evaluate. Additionally, possible co-interventions were not evaluated.

The patients and physicians in six of the seven studies [18, 20–24] were either not blinded to the intervention and outcome or lacked such information entirely. This condition should not directly influence the quality of interpretation, as the interpreters would always be interested in delivering the best interpretation possible. However, this could affect the results if patients or physicians made their sentences longer or more complicated, depending on the type of interpretation. If this were the case, we speculate that physicians and patients would talk in shorter and less complex sentences when using IPAHI, which is associated with a lower risk of error [21, 22] and if this were to occur, thereby would result in a linguistically more accurate interpretation. Thus, a hypothetical effect on the results would presumably move the significance toward the null.

Country and language

Most studies were performed in the USA [19–23] and with Spanish-speaking patients [19–22]. It is noteworthy that the three studies [18, 23, 24] conducted with non-Spanish speaking patients all showed a significant difference, whereas 3 out of 4 studies [19, 21, 22] with Spanish-speaking patients did not show a significant difference. We speculate that this might be associated with the widespread knowledge and use of Spanish in the USA. In line with this, the difference between PIPI and IPAHI might be higher the less common the interpreted language is in a specific country. Another explanation could be that the other interpreted languages are further away from the target language linguistically speaking and thus more difficult to interpret accurately. As the number of studies regarding interpretation errors is low, especially in languages other than English/Spanish, we propose that generalizing the results for other languages must be done cautiously.

Data processing and analysis

All included studies were based on data that have been through several steps of analysis. At every stage of data processing, some could potentially be lost, altered, or added, which could influence the results of the studies. Using a transcribed audio recording results in losing all body language, facial expressions, and intonation. The translations of the audio recordings were done by different individuals with different backgrounds, and thus, there might be differences in the translation quality of the recordings. These translations have been the foundation of error analysis. Since the error analysts are also different individuals with different backgrounds, this constitutes another layer of varying quality, although all studies used at least two error analysts. In addition, the error analysts were only known to be blinded to the interpretation mode in three of the seven included studies [20, 21, 23]. All the above-mentioned give rise to possible doubt about our findings, but it is hard to imagine a better way of evaluating the linguistic aspect of interpreted consultations. At the same time, all studies had several different professionals involved in the data analysis process, and the results were therefore not dependent on one individual. Also, all studies were evaluated to have a low to moderate risk of bias in all the post-intervention domains (Table 3). We suggest future researchers to thoroughly research different methodologies before planning studies within the field. Also, we suggest other researchers conduct further evaluations of different methods to advance standardization.

Time gap between data generation and publication

Data translation, analysis, and processing of the audiotaped consultations have comprised a time-consuming workload, resulting in a gap of up to 10–12 years between data collection and the publication date [19, 20]. Despite this time gap, we argue that the results are still very relevant, as the methods of interpretation are still the same, and the technology used throughout the data analysis has not changed significantly over the years.

Conflicts of interest within included studies

Conflict of interest or a lack thereof were not mentioned in three out of seven studies [20, 22, 23]. Three studies declared no potential conflicts of interest [18, 19, 24]. In contrast, one study declared conflicts of interest [21] as two authors are co-owners of a company providing tech-based solutions for remote simultaneous medical interpretation. However, this specific interpretation type was not included in this review, nor did any analyzed interpretation modalities use tech-based solutions.

Information about participants

In general, the data provided about both professional interpreters and physicians were limited. Only two studies mentioned years of experience among professional interpreters [21, 24]. Another study used years of experience in their data analysis but did not provide the specific numbers in the article [19]. Two studies [19, 22] did not mention how many physicians participated in the study. The remaining studies used 1 [23], 4 [21], 10 [18], 14 [20] and 30 [24] different physicians. This paper aims to evaluate communication, and as communication is an interaction between people, it is safe to assume that it depends on the individual participants. When some of the studies use a few different physicians or do not mention the number of physicians, differences between the use of PIPI and IPAHI could be based on the individual physician and not the type of interpreter. We assume that the communication style and skills of the individual physician do not change from consultation to consultation. Based on this assumption, the following is evident:

When only one physician is used, there should be no difference between the types of interpreters because of the physician.

Clinical implications

Interpretation is associated with the outcome of consultation

Despite the positive impact of using interpreters in language-discordant consultations, data indicate that interpreters are in some settings only used in about half [4, 49] of the consultations where patients report a preference for a non-English language, but with large variation depending on language. Across different languages Blay et al. [50] found that the highest percentage of interpreter provision for patients identified as needing interpretation was 30.9% (Arabic), the degree of interpreter provision was far lower for several other languages. Previous studies have found that the use of interpreters in language-discordant consultations is essential to both satisfaction [8–11], understanding [10], and quality of treatment [4, 6, 7]. At the same time, a study by Rocque et al. [51] reported that unsatisfactory communication was associated with feeling less motivated to follow the treatment plan. In addition, the need for interpretation is underlined by a qualitative study by Maria Dahm [52] where it was found that non-native English speakers in Australia tended not to ask for clarification of words and medical terms they did not understand. In line with previous studies, we recommend using interpretation in language-discordant consultations.

In cases of severe diagnoses, professional in-person interpretation (PIPI) is preferred

The need for PIPI is evident as a study of ethnic minority patients found that the use of ad hoc interpreters limited the topics discussed, like mental health and intimate information [51]. An association was also found between the use of ad hoc interpreters and patients feeling embarrassed, guilty, and uncomfortable [51]. Based on our findings, we argue that using PIPI is preferred over IPAHI in all consultations and may be especially important when dealing with severe diagnoses. If PIPI is not available in a consultation regarding severe diagnoses, one should consider if the consultation could be moved to another time to ensure the use of PIPI. Also, healthcare professionals report that the use of family for IPAHI in cancer care is associated with increased difficulty in obtaining informed consent and discussing end-of-life care and that, in some cases, family members withhold the diagnosis in an attempt to maintain hope, especially when severe [53]. However, further studies are needed to clarify if and to what extent the severity of the diagnosis impacts the interpretation process.

When in need of ad hoc interpretation, medical staff without interpretation training is preferred

Using qualified interpreters for PIPI should be the primary choice in health care. In cases where the use of PIPI is not possible, one should be attentive as to who conducts IPAHI. If possible, medical staff should be chosen before relatives. However, with no professional interpreter available, using relatives for IPAHI is preferred for all practical purposes over no interpreter. Also, the use of children for IPAHI is strongly discouraged as it puts the child at risk of severe psychological consequences [43], a higher degree of parent-child conflict [47], depressive symptoms [45, 46], and feelings of anxiety or helplessness [54].

When speaking, use shorter sentences and statements only containing one message

Sleptsova et al. [55] have previously recommended agreeing upon a basic set of rules for interpretation and clarifying the interpreter’s role before every medical encounter. Our findings support this insight, and we add that short sentences containing only one concept result in a lower risk of errors [21]. Moreover, it indicates that the interpretation quality depends on the patient, physician, and interpreter. Our research proposes that all participants in interpreted consultations should communicate through short sentences with only one aim for each sentence. And each sentence should be followed by interpretation before initiating the next sentence.

Conclusion

We recommend that interpretation is always used in medical consultations with a language barrier. Our results may indicate that the number of linguistic errors was lower when using professional in-person interpretation in consultations regarding severe diagnoses, e.g., cancer. Generally, we recommend that when interpretation is necessary, professional in-person interpretation should be the primary choice, a secondary choice should be medical staff without interpretation training, and a third choice should be relatives. If possible, the use of children should be avoided.

Future directions

Generally, the focus in a clinical setting should be on choosing professional interpreters with education and training, as this is related to significantly lower error rates [19]. We have found substantial linguistic errors even with PIPI, and with this in mind, we cautiously suggest that future basic research could focus more on the use of technological advances. Among these the use of machine interpretation or AI. The use of technology could in the future be advantageous in logistics and potentially in promoting cost reduction, and hopefully in the diminished use of children as interpreters. However, such suggestions come with a proviso that AI improves significantly first, as the current state of AI interpretation shows that it is not without its challenges. Current studies report far too many hazards, these include (1) low accuracy [56], (2) concerns regarding different accents and interpretations between non-English languages [57], and (3) large gaps in interpretation quality based on language, both written [58] and spoken [59]. Also, currently AI-interpretation quality is inferior to professional human interpretation [60]. Generally, it is recommended that accuracy needs to improve before AI should be considered for use in a clinical interpretation setting [60, 61]. Therefore, before venturing into studies of AI-interpretation, we recommend reading (1) the Interpreting SAFE AI Task Force Guidance on AI and Interpreting Services [62] and (2) the recently published guidance paper by the American National Council on Interpreting in Health Care (NCIHC) suggesting questions to ask internally and externally when considering implementing AI [63].
