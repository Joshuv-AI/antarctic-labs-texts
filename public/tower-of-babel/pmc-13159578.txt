Using randomization to compare AI and expert-generated formative assessment questions in medical education
Authors: Akshat Kumar, Katie Stinson, Litao Wang, Elizabeth Nugent, Yongsu Lee, Mark Hormann, LaTanya Jones Love, Toufeeq Ahmed Syed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAI-generated content is being used across the education spectrum and is beneficial for creating complex multiple-choice questions, such as those used in medical education. However, evaluating AI-generated content is challenging, and existing testing and evaluation methods are falling short. This study uses randomization to compare medical students’ performance on and subjective evaluation of AI versus expert-generated questions. We hypothesized that there would be no difference in student performance or subjective evaluation of AI vs expert-generated questions.MethodsWe designed a single-center, randomized study where medical student participants received either one AI- or expert-generated question per day over four weeks.ResultsOur study showed that participants had similar perceptions of AI versus expert-generated questions (p = 0.18), with no significant difference between the distributions of the proportion of correct responses. The cumulative proportion of questions answered correctly over 28 days was consistent across the two question sets. However, participants rated 53% of AI-generated questions as very easy or easy compared with only 31% of the expert-generated questions.DiscussionRandomization was crucial to show that AI-generated questions were nearly imperceptible from expert-generated questions, demonstrating the need for additional evaluation methods to compare AI- and expert-generated medical education content.Keywords: Generative AI, medical education, formative assessment, multiple-choice questions, randomized study

Introduction

The evolution of generative AI has increased more rapidly than existing testing and evaluation methodologies, creating challenges with testing generative AI content [1]. Additionally, most studies focus on evaluating the reliability of the AI rather than its usability or the comparison to human-generated content [2].

Generative AI is being used to develop educational assessment content, particularly multiple-choice questions (MCQs), but it lacks comparison to expert-generated content. MCQs are widely used in medical school assessments, and using generative AI to create the questions is more efficient and cost-saving than using expert-generated questions. Few prior studies have tested the efficacy of AI-generated questions, yielding mixed results [3].

This study addresses the gaps in comparing AI-generated content to expert-generated content by examining answering patterns of randomised AI- and expert-generated questions over a 28-day period, and hypothesises that there will be no statistically significant difference in participants’ perception and performance.

Methods

We designed a single-centre, randomised study in which participants received either an AI- or expert-generated question over 28 days following the Reproductive Systems course in March 2024. Participants were second-year medical students in the 4-year MD programme at McGovern Medical School, UTHealth Houston. Questions were delivered to students using QuizToo [4], which provides spaced micro-learning (daily question delivery) [5,6]. Participation was voluntary, and the study was approved by the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects [UTHSC‐H] under protocol HSC-SBMI-23-1137. All participants were provided a letter of information about the study and asked to consent to participation. Participation included reviewing and acknowledging a Letter of Information, completing pre- and post-study surveys, enroling in the QuizToo Module, and answering at least one question over the study period.

In January 2024, we created two sets of questions based on the Reproductive Systems course: 1) AI-generated questions created via OpenAI, ChatGPT-4, and 2) expert-generated questions. The AI-generated questions were reviewed for accuracy and approved or rejected by the Reproductive Systems Course Director; the accepted AI-generated questions were not edited. QuizToo was configured to randomly divide the cohort into two groups; every day, one group received an AI-generated question while the other received an expert-generated question. QuizToo re-randomised groups daily; both participants and study facilitators were blinded to the question types each participant received. After each question, participants received immediate feedback on whether their response was correct. Participants were prompted to provide feedback for each question, rating the difficulty of the question (Likert Scale) and whether they believed it was AI-generated or expert-generated.

We collected the following data over 28 days: 1) accuracy—we compared participants’ answers in AI-generated versus expert-generated questions and measured whether the accuracy fluctuated throughout the study; 2) guess if the question was AI- or expert-generated—we asked participants to guess if they thought each question was expert-generated or AI-generated; 3) quality and perception of difficulty—we asked participants to rate each question using a Likert Scale of Difficulty; and 4) perception of generative AI in education—we asked participants to complete a pre-survey and a post-survey regarding their perceptions of generative AI. The survey questions were adapted from Yilmazet and colleagues (2023) and Triana and colleagues (2021) and can be viewed in the supplement [7,8].

Accuracy

Figure 1A’s histograms with overlaid kernel density curves show that responses to questions were distributed across a range of accuracy levels, with a noticeable concentration of students performing at moderate levels (around 0.5 to 0.7). Since these distributions may not necessarily have equal variance and spread, we used the Wilcoxon’s Rank Sum Test to assess if the two distributions were statistically significantly different. Overall, the AI-generated questions were of similar quality as the expert-generated questions based on our statistical analysis (p = 0.18). In Figure 1B and C, each coloured line represents the cumulative performance trend of a single participant across the question sets over time. Neither graph shows a drastic increase nor decrease in trend between the question sets, indicating that participants did not learn question-answering patterns over time.

Guessing

Participants’ responses if they thought questions were AI-generated or expert-generated were tabulated, and it was demonstrated that they correctly guessed AI-generated questions 44.4% of the time and correctly guessed expert-generated questions 47.7% of the time (Figure 2 and Table 2). Although the participants correctly guessed the expert-generated questions slightly more often, the difference is not substantial enough to indicate that they could correctly distinguish between the two. This data suggests that participants could not differentiate between AI- versus expert-generated questions.

Difficulty

Participants’ responses on the perceived difficulty of each question were tabulated, which showed that they rated 53% of the AI-generated questions as very easy or easy, while they rated 31% of the expert-generated questions as very easy or easy. This indicates that the AI-generated questions selected for this study were perceived as easier than the expert-generated questions (Figure 3 and Table 3).

Lessons learned and future direction

Randomising the AI- and expert-generated questions was crucial for accurately determining similarities and differences between the two question sets. This study shows great promise for using generative AI to create formative assessment questions for medical students, with some limitations. This study’s cohort was small and from a single institution. Future studies should include a larger sample of at least 400 participants across multiple medical schools to discover if generative AI can be widely adopted [9].

Additionally, before we shift medical school content generation to AI, we must determine, without bias, if it is equivalent to or better than expert-generated content. This study demonstrates the need for additional evaluation methods when testing the efficacy of AI-generated content compared to expert-generated content.

Generative AI has quickly become instrumental in many use cases across the educational landscape. By using randomisation in our study, the data support that generative AI can be leveraged to create formative assessment questions in medical education.
