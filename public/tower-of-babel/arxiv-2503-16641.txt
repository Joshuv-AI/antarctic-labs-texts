Type C K K –stanley symmetric functions and Kraśkiewicz–Hecke insertion Thanks: August 24, 2026
Joshua Arroyo, Zachary Hamaker, Graham Hawkes, Jianping Pan

Abstract
. We study Type C K K –Stanley symmetric functions, which are K K –theoretic extensions of the Type C Stanley symmetric functions. They are indexed by signed permutations and can be used to enumerate reduced words via their expansion into Schur Q Q -functions, which are indexed by strict partitions. A combinatorial description of the Schur Q Q - coefficients is given by Kraśkiewicz insertion. Similarly, their K K –Stanley analogues are conjectured to expand positively into G ​ Q GQ ’s, which are K K –theory representatives for the Lagrangian Grassmannian introduced by Ikeda and Naruse also indexed by strict partitions. We introduce a K K –theoretic analogue of Kraśkiewicz insertion, which can be used to enumerate 0–Hecke expressions for signed permutations and gives a conjectural combinatorial rule for computing this G ​ Q GQ expansion. We show the Type C K K –Stanleys for certain fully commutative signed permutations are skew G ​ Q GQ ’s. Combined with a Pfaffian formula of Anderson’s, this allows us to prove Lewis and Marberg’s conjecture that G ​ Q GQ ’s of (skew) rectangle shape are G ​ Q GQ ’s of trapezoid shape. Combined with our previous conjecture, this also gives an explicit combinatorial description of the skew G ​ Q GQ expansion into G ​ Q GQ ’s. As a consequence, we obtain a conjecture for the product of two G ​ Q GQ functions where one has trapezoid shape.



1.
Introduction

Although this paper is combinatorial in methods and results, our underlying objective is to understand geometric properties of the isotropic or Lagrangian Grassmannian.
Beginning in the 19th century with work of Schubert, mathematicians have investigated enumerative properties of the intersections of curves and hypersurfaces.
The modern approach to calculating these quantities is to interpret them by computing the cup product for the cohomology ring of an appropriate projective variety.
In this guise, such computations are fundamentally combinatorial in nature.
For example, Schubert’s computations are encoded in the cohomology of the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
, the space of
k
–planes in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, whose cup product is computed by the Littlewood–Richardson rule for multiplying Schur functions (see e.g.
[
26
]
for a textbook treatment).
Similarly, the cup product in cohomology for the orthogonal and Lagrangian Grassmannians is computed by multiplying Schur
P
- and Schur
Q
- functions
[
18
,
29
]
.

A major line of active research is to understand combinatorially more exotic cohomology theories such as
K
–theory, which encodes finer data about the boundaries of intersections.
For
K
–theory, products in the Grassmannian were first computed combinatorially by Buch
[
5
]
, and later extended to the orthogonal Grassmannian
[
10
,
8
]
.
For the Lagrangian Grassmannian,
K
–theory has proved far more difficult to understand combinatorially, with the only progress being Buch and Ravikumar’s Pieri rule
[
7
]
.
We offer a new pathway towards understanding such products based on Type C
K
–Stanley symmetric functions.

1.1.
Stanley symmetric functions and Kraśkiewicz–Hecke insertion

The Stanley symmetric functions

Introduced by Stanley to enumerate reduced words

For

\overline{n}=-n

Let

Here, the

of

of

The

See Figure

We say the strict partition
\mu
is

\mu_{i}\leq\lambda_{i}

.
Here, we refer to

2.1.
Signed Permutations

A
signed permutation

w
is a permutation of the elements
[\overline{n}]\cup[n]
such that
w(i)=-w(\overline{i})
.
For example,
v=\overline{1}\overline{3}2\overline{2}31
is a signed permutation.
By antisymmetry,
w
is determined by
w([n])
.
When writing a signed permutation, we frequently omit
w([\overline{n}])
; for instance
v=\overline{2}31
.
Signed permutations with composition form a group
W_{n}
, which is the Coxeter group of Type B/C.
Viewed as a Coxeter group, the generators of
W_{n}
are
s_{0},s_{1},\ldots s_{n-1}
where
s_{0}=(\overline{1},1)
swaps positions
\overline{1}
and
1
and
s_{i}=(\overline{i{+}1},\overline{i})(i,i{+}1)
simultaneously swaps positions
i
and
i+1
and positions
\overline{i}
and
\overline{i+1}
for
i>0
.
These generators satisfy the relations

(1)

Self-inverse:
s_{i}^{2}=1
for
i=0,1,\dots,n-1
;

(2)

Commutation:
s_{i}s_{j}=s_{i}s_{j}
if
|i-j|\geq 2
;

(3)

Braid Relation:
s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}=s_{i+1}s_{i}s_{i+1}
if
i>0
;

(4)

Long Braid Relation:
s_{1}s_{0}s_{1}s_{0}=s_{0}s_{1}s_{0}s_{1}
.

The
length
of
w\in W_{n}
is the minimum number of generators needed to express
w
, denoted
\ell(w)
.
A
reduced expression
for
w
is an expression of the form

w=s_{a_{1}}\cdot s_{a_{2}}\cdot\ldots\cdot s_{a_{p}}

where
p=\ell(w)
.
The associated word
(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
is a
reduced word
for
w
.
The Matsumoto–Tits theorem says that the set of reduced words for a given
w
is connected by the commutation, braid and long braid relations.
For example, the reduced words of
w=\overline{2}31
are
(1,0,2)
and
(1,2,0)
.

The
0–Hecke monoid

(W_{n},\circ)
is the monoid on signed permutations obtained by replacing the Self-inverse relation with the Idempotent Relation
s_{i}\circ s_{i}=s_{i}
.
A
0–Hecke expression
for
w\in W_{n}
is an expression of the form

w=s_{a_{1}}\circ s_{a_{2}}\circ\ldots\circ s_{a_{p}}

with associated
Hecke word

(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{p})
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{p}(w)
be the set of Hecke words for
w
with
p

\mathcal{H}(w)=\cup_{p\geq 0}\mathcal{H}_{p}(w)
.
For example, with

we have
\mathcal{H}_{3}(w)=\{(1,0,2),(1,2,0)\}

\mathcal{H}_{4}(w)=\{(1,0,0,2),(1,0,2,0),(1,0,2,2),(1,1,0,2),(1,2,0,0),(1,2,0,2),(1,2,2,0)\}.

Note
\mathcal{H}_{\ell(w)}(w)
is the set of reduced words for
w
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}(w)
is empty when
m<\ell(w)
.
The
peak set
of a word

\mathsf{Peak}((a_{1},\dots,a_{p}))=\{i\in[p]:a_{i-1}<a_{i}>a_{i+1}\}

By construction

–Idempotent Relation and the Coxeter relations:

\displaystyle u_{i}^{2}=\beta u_{i},

\displaystyle u_{1}u_{0}u_{1}u_{0}=u_{0}u_{1}u_{0}u_{1},

\displaystyle u_{i}u_{i+1}u_{i}=u_{i+1}u_{i}u_{i+1}\ (i>0),

\displaystyle u_{i}u_{j}=u_{j}u_{i}\ (|i-j|>1).

Define

(3.1)

G^{C}_{n}(\textbf{x})=\prod_{i\geqslant 1}(1+x_{i}u_{n-1})(1+x_{i}u_{n-2})\dots(1+x_{i}u_{1})(1+x_{i}u_{0})(1+x_{i}u_{0})(1+x_{i}u_{1})\dots(1+x_{i}u_{n-1}),

where the product is computed beginning with the terms containing

.
For
w\in W_{n}
, the
type

K-Stanley symmetric function
of

is the coefficient of

in

, which we denote

.
These symmetric functions (with
\beta=-1
) are introduced in
[

]
in order to define Type C double Grothendieck polynomials and are stable under the natural inclusion
W_{n}\hookrightarrow W_{n+1}
.
Note that

is not homogeneous; its lowest degree homogeneous component is the
Type C Stanley symmetric function

.
It is not obvious, but

is symmetric for each

.
In fact, it can be expressed in terms of

–functions.

Proposition 3.1
.

For

G^{C}_{w}\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta][GQ^{(\beta)}_{\lambda}:\lambda
strict
]
.

Proof.

This is essentially
[

is a symmetric function closely related to the

that we do not define.

[

is an element of

∎

A
unimodal factorization
of the word
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
in the alphabet

is the biword
(\textbf{a},\textbf{i})
where

\textbf{i}=i_{1}\preceq i_{2}\preceq\dots\preceq i_{p}

with

for all

implies

when

and
a_{k}<a_{k+1}
when

Such biwords are also known as compatible sequences.
Our nomenclature comes from the following depiction of the unimodal factorization

: group consecutive subwords of

whose corresponding entry in

have the same absolute value using parentheses, indicating the transition from negative to positive values in each parenthesized subword with

.
For example, the unimodal factorization

\left(\textbf{a}=(0,1,2,3,0,1,4,2,3,3,0,1,2,5,0,1),\quad\textbf{i}=(1,1,1,\overline{2},2,2,\overline{4},\overline{4},4,\overline{5},5,5,\overline{6},6,6)\right)

is depicted as

(|\;012)(3\;|\;01)()(42\;|\;3)(3\;|\>012)(5\;|\;01).

Let
\mathcal{U}(w)
be the set of unimodal factorizations whose word is in
\mathcal{H}(w)
and
\mathcal{U}_{p}(w)
be the subset whose word is in

.
By interpreting the definition of

in terms of (

Proposition 3.2

(
[
20
, Prop. 17]
)
.

For

G^{C}_{w}(\textbf{x})=\sum_{p\geq 0}\sum_{(\textbf{a},\textbf{i})\in\mathcal{U}_{p}(w)}\beta^{p-\ell(w)}x^{\textbf{i}}\quad\mbox{where}\quad x^{\textbf{i}}=x_{|i_{1}|}\dots x_{|i_{p}|}.

Proof.

A non-zero monomial in
G^{C}_{n}(\textbf{x})
is of the form

x_{j_{1}}u_{a_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdots x_{j_{p}}u_{a_{p}}.

Construct the biword
(\textbf{a},\textbf{i})
from this monomial with
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
and
\textbf{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{p})
where

i_{k}=\begin{cases}\overline{j_{k}}&x_{i_{k}}u_{a_{k}}\ \mbox{is in the descending part of the}\ j_{k}th\ \mbox{term in}\ G^{C}_{n}(\textbf{x}),\\
j_{k}.&\mbox{else}\end{cases}

The reader can confirm that
(\textbf{a},\textbf{i})\in\mathcal{U}_{p}(w)
where

, and that all such terms arise in this way.
∎

As a corollary, we see Type

.

For

, the coefficient of

is

Proof.

This follows Proposition

: each monomial

corresponds to a unimodal factorization

where

and each

can be

or

independent of other choices.
∎

Using the unimodal factorization characterization of Type C

Proposition 3.4

Let

Proof.

Consider the

where the

are greater than

and

This term can be split into two parenthesizations

(b_{1}>\dots>b_{i}\mid b_{i+1}<\dots<b_{m})\quad\mbox{and}\quad(c_{1}>\dots>c_{j}\mid<c_{j+1}<\dots<c_{\ell}).

Applying this procedure to all parenthesizations of

and

3.2.

In this section, we study top fully commutative elements of

There is a transparent bijection between shifted standard tableaux of strict shape
\lambda/\mu

We extend this bijection to the map

We say

(0,1,0,1)
and

Fully commutative elements for Coxeter groups have been studied extensively, with Stembridge

A key feature of fully commutative

of

We are especially interested in signed permutations

(1,0,1)

As shown in

Definition 4.1

A sequence

For shifted tableau with a unimodal row

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}(R)

Remark 4.2
.

Often, unimodal sequences are first increasing, then decreasing.
However, the previous observation demonstrates that working with sequences that first decrease, then increase is more natural in our context.

The goal of this section is to construct a bijection from words in the alphabet

to pairs of tableaux

strict decomposition tableau

This algorithm generalizes Kraśkiewicz insertion, introduced in

We are now prepared to introduce our insertion algorithm, beginning with the row insertion rule.

|b|\in(|y|,|q|)
, by the definition of
(R3)
would give
b=a^{\prime}
, a contradiction.

Case 2.2:
Suppose
a^{\prime}\in R^{\downarrow}
and
b\not\in R^{\downarrow}
, then we know the left insertion of
a^{\prime}
into
R^{\downarrow}
will trigger
(L3)
.
By the definition of
(R3)
every element between
a^{\prime}\in R^{\downarrow}
and
a\in R^{\uparrow}
must be less than or equal to
|y|
, as otherwise a different element would be left inserted.
Combining this observation with the fact that
|y|<|b|<|a^{\prime}|
, and the definition of
(L3)
shows that
b
is strictly to the right of
a^{\prime}
in
S
.
Suppose
b\in S^{\uparrow}
, then the element above
b
must be greater than or equal to
z
(defined at the start of this case).
However, as
|b|<|a^{\prime}|\leq|z|
we would have had
a^{\prime}
witnessing
b
less than the element above it before insertion, so the tableau wouldn’t be a SDT.
Therefore,
b\in S^{\downarrow}
.
Let
x
be the entry above
b
in
R
.
Since
b
is to the right of
a^{\prime}
and to the left of
y
, we see
x
is between
a^{\prime}\in R^{\downarrow}
and
a\in R^{\uparrow}
.
Therefore
|x|\leq|y|<|b|
so
y
witnesses
b<x
in
P
, a contradiction.

We conclude that the right bumping path of our insertion is weakly decreasing.

Left bumping path:
The argument is similar, but not identical to the previous case.
If
b^{\prime}=\infty
, which is the
(L1)
case, then the result is vacuously true.
Otherwise the left bumping path will be weakly decreasing if
|b^{\prime}|\geq|x|
or if
x\in S^{\uparrow}
, where
x
is the element below
a^{\prime}
after
a^{\prime}
was left inserted into
R
.
For the sake of contradiction, we suppose that
|b^{\prime}|<|x|
with
x\in S^{\downarrow}
.
By the definition of right insertion we have
|b|<|b^{\prime}|
, so
|b|<|x|
as well. Next we discuss the two cases and find contradiction in each of them.

Case 1:

Suppose
b
was bumped by
a^{\prime}
using
(L1)
. Since
b^{\prime}
is left inserted into
S
we must have either
b
or
b^{\prime}
in
S^{\uparrow}
.
If
b
or
b^{\prime}
is to the left of
x
, then
x\in S^{\downarrow}
as
|b|<|b^{\prime}|<|x|
which contradicts our assumption that
x\in S^{\uparrow}
.
If
b
or
b^{\prime}
is to the right of
x
, then we have
x<b
in
P
witnessed by
b
or
b^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction.

Case 2:

Suppose
a^{\prime}
is left inserted using
(L2)
, hence
a^{\prime}\in R^{\downarrow}
.
If
b^{\prime}\in S
, since
|b^{\prime}|\in(|b|,|x|)
the insertion of
a^{\prime}
by
(L2)
would bump
b^{\prime}
instead of
b
, a contradiction.
Necessarily, when

is right inserted to
S
it triggers
(R3)
, outputting
b^{\prime}
. Therefore we

and
b^{\prime}
is in the row below
S
in
P
.

Let
z
be the element in
S
above
b^{\prime}
, and note
z
is to the right of
b
by the definition of
(R3)
, hence also to the right of
x
.
Since
x\in S^{\downarrow}
, we must be in case (I) of
(L2)
. Suppose
|z|<|x|
, then we have
|r_{j+1}|<|b|<|z|<|x|
and
A\neq\emptyset
, which will cause
(L3)
to output a
|z|
instead of
|b|
. Therefore we must have
|z|\geq|x|
.

However now we have
b^{\prime}<z
witnessed by
x
, hence
P
is not a SDT. This is a contradiction, which completes our proof.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Right insertion outputs a greater number than the input and left insertion outputs a smaller number than the input.

Proof.

The result is clearly true for (R1), (R2), and (L1).
Let
R=r_{1},\ldots r_{\ell}
be the row we are inserting into and
S=s_{2}\ldots s_{k}
the row below
R
.
We first consider (R3).
Let
a
be the element right inserted and
b
be the output from this right insertion.
By the definition of (R3)
a
must already in be in the increasing part of the row, say
r_{i}=a
in
R^{\uparrow}
.
Further, we have
b\in(s_{i},r_{i+1})
or
b=r_{i+1}
, which is greater than
r_{i}=a
as
r_{i}
is in
R^{\uparrow}
.
Therefore if
a\geq b
then
b\in(s_{i},r_{i+1})
so
r_{i}=a\geq b>s_{i}
, hence
b
witnesses
r_{i}
over
s_{i}
.
Therefore, we have
a<b
.

Next we consider the case (L2).
Let
c
be the element left inserted and
d
be the output from this left insertion.
By definition of (L2)
c
must already in be in the decreasing part of the row, say
r_{j}=c
in
R^{\downarrow}
.
Further, by our last case we know that
c
cannot be the dip of
R
.
In case (II) we either have
d=r_{j+1}<r_{j}=c
or we have
d=s_{j+1}
.
In (b) of the latter case, either
d=s_{j+1}<r_{j+1}<r_{j}=c
or
d=s_{j+1}<r_{j}=c
.
Now for case (I) suppose that
c\leq d
.
If
s_{j}\in S^{\downarrow}
, case (I), then we have
d<s_{j}
or
d=r_{j+1}
.
The latter contradicts
c\leq d
as
c
is not the dip, so
d<s_{j}
.
However, if
d<s_{j}
then
r_{j}=a\leq b<s_{j}
meaning
b
witnesses
r_{j}
over
s_{j}
.
Therefore, we have that
c>d
.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

Right insertion can never output the dip.

Proof.

Right insertion always ends with the input being in the increasing part of the row, thus as the output is greater than the input we cannot output the dip.
∎

We are now prepared to show that row insertion is well-defined.

7.3.

In

Finding the

7.4.

One consequence of Conjecture
1.3
is the weaker statement:

.

For

, we have

This statement likely has a geometric proof.
Assuming this weaker conjecture, we suspect that (

As a heuristic for why this should be possible, we compare our situation to the expansion of Type A

]
