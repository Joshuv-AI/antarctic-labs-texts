Hemostatic and Wound Healing Nonisocyanate Polyhydroxyurethanes (NIPHUs) Dressings from Soybean Oil: In Vivo Characterization
Authors: Maria Paula Morales-Gonzalez, Manuel F. Valero, Luis Eduardo Díaz, Nallely Álvarez-Santos, Adriana Montserrat Espinosa-González, José Del Carmen Benítez-Flores, José Guillermo Avila-Acevedo, Ana María García-Bores
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound healing is a dynamic process that consists of four stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Some alterations in this process can lead to fatal adverse effects such as bleeding or chronic wound formation. Thus, the use of appropriate dressings is necessary to allow for hemostasis and healing to take place properly. Nonisocyanate polyhydroxyurethanes (NIPHUs) have emerged as a more sustainable alternative for biomaterials. Thus, the aim was to evaluate the wound healing capacity of four new NIPHUs with different ratios of amines used. The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR), in vivo hemostatic capability in the mouse tail model, skin irritation, and wound healing assays were performed. Results showed new formulations of NIPHUs with different WVTRs (95.6 g/m2/day to 593.3 g/m2/day). The amount of blood loss was lower when NIPHU 50/50 was applied than in wounds without any treatment. NIPHUs that were nonirritating dressings were used in the healing process assays. On day 14, all of the wounds were closed, including controls. The healed zone in animals treated with any NIPHUs was smaller and thinner, while an elongated and thick scab was still observed in the healed skin of the control groups (wounds without any treatment and wounds treated with a commercial patch). In accordance with the macroscopic observations, in all treatments, the histologic observations showed that the healing zone was epithelialized, and below it was occupied by residual granulation tissue. In NIPHU 100/0, NIPHU 67/33, and controls, no hypodermis was identified, but in the case of NIPHU 50/50, below this epithelium, several regions could be recognized: connective tissue with active fibroblasts was observed, and there were some newly formed adipocytes, neoformation of pilosebaceous units, and the muscle layer was complete. In conclusion, as a preliminary study, NIPHU 50/50 exhibited hemostatic activity and allowed the wounds to be in the remodeling phase in 14 days, making it suitable for use as a dressing.

Introduction

Wound healing is a complex and dynamic process that normally occurs in four stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. When a wound is created, healing begins with hemostasis, as it is the body’s response to bleeding from damaged blood vessels. , Partial closure of the wound occurs in the first few hours, when clot formation takes place, activating the inflammatory cascade.

Nevertheless, in some cases, uncontrolled bleeding leads to hemorrhages, which account for approximately 90% of military deaths worldwide and between 26% and 40% of civilian deaths. Therefore, adequate control of hemorrhages can reduce mortality rates, as fatal hemorrhages occur within 5 to 10 min after the injury. To prevent this, the process of hemostasis is important, although in many cases, this stage tends to be overlooked given its immediacy. However, it can determine the entire subsequent healing process.

Following the hemostasis phase, during the inflammatory stage, the defense against pathogenic wound invasion occurs, which promotes the infiltration of neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes to the wound site.

Within 3 to 4 days of a wound injury, the proliferative phase takes place and is characterized by the re-epithelialization and granulation tissue formation processes. In the re-epithelialization process, fibroblasts and endothelial cells migrate to the wound site, secreting cytokines and growth factors, leading to ECM deposition and the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis). As well, both fibroblasts and keratinocytes undergo epithelial–mesenchymal transition which allows them to become more mobile and migrate to the wound site to regenerate the epidermal layer. , Parallel to this process, the secreted cytokines contribute to the development of the granulation tissue, which is composed of fibroblasts, blood vessels, macrophages, type I collagen, fibronectin, glycoprotein, hyaluronic acid, and myofibroblasts. The myofibroblasts are important to secrete α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) that allows the start of the contraction of the wound and thus the remodeling phase. This last phase, also named the maturation, takes up to one year, and contraction of the wound and collagen deposition occur. During this process, the granulation tissue is converted into scar tissue, and the collagen type III, which was more abundant in the wound, is replaced by collagen type I, generating an increase in the tensile strength of the scar. ,

Due to these processes, materials that help with wound closure and healing are necessary. Wound dressings are a type of material that covers the wound to protect it from damage and promote healing. To do this, dressings must have specific characteristics that support hemostasis and ensure the proper progression of the entire wound healing process. Therefore, the ideal dressing should have hemostatic capacity so that hemostasis can be achieved and bleeding can be stopped, high mechanical performance (compared to the mechanical performance of the skin), antibacterial properties to prevent the wound from becoming chronic and unable to close, adequate breathability to maintain a moist environment, an adequate pH for cell development, and biocompatibility with the different types of cells present (blood cells, inflammatory cells, and endothelial cells). Currently, many of the dressings used tend to fail in terms of adhesion to the tissue formed, low breathability, and low flexibility, among others. Therefore, the search for new and better dressings remains of great interest to global health care systems.

Polyurethanes (PUs) for use as wound dressings have varied, some of which are currently commercially available. One example is the Tegaderm film dressing, which is an adhesive polyurethane film that has been recommended for use in wound healing and catheter coverage for several years. Similarly, PUs from renewable polyols, such as vegetable oils, have been created. From castor oil, Rezaei Hosseinabadi et al. (2020) used two different polyols, castor oil with CAPA 7201A (a copolymer of polycaprolactone and poly­(tetramethylene ether glycol) (PTMEG)) and hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI), to obtain a PU with greater hydrophilicity and better interaction with fibroblasts, with a water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) suitable for low to moderate exudates (285 g/m2/day). However, it showed a moderate inflammatory response in healing, but no necrosis effects were seen.

For their part, Gholami and Yeganeh (2020) have researched various PUs using soybean oil, one of which incorporates quaternary ammonium. They generated materials with two polyols, one from carbonated soybean oil and the other from castor oil with isophorone diisocyanate, demonstrating the ability of these materials to generate suitable dressings with good cytocompatibility and effective antimicrobial activity; a WVTR of 390 g–2 day–1, indicates good progress in wound healing, showing that the tensile strength of the regenerated skin reaches approximately 80% of normal healthy skin by day 21.

However, one limitation of PUs is the source of the monomers used. This type of material is generated by the reaction of polyols with isocyanates, which are derived from petrochemical sources. The production process generates various highly toxic compounds, and the degradation products have also been found to be potentially carcinogenic.

Therefore, the search for materials with the good properties of PUs but derived from renewable sources has been carried out, and nonisocyanate polyhydroxyurethane (NIPHUs) emerged as a solution. In this case, these materials are synthesized from renewable monomers such as vegetable oils, where carbonates can be generated and then, through an aminolysis reaction with diamines, can form the urethane group.

The NIPHUs have been found to have mechanical properties like those of PUs, but with greater hydrophobicity, which is an advantage for some applications, such as dressings. They also have greater thermal stability, water resistance, and solvent resistance compared to conventional PUs, as well as self-repairing properties, shape memory, and reprocessability, which PUs do not have. Furthermore, their importance for biomedical applications is beginning to be recognized, taking advantage of the generation of hydroxyl groups adjacent to the urethane groups of NIPHUs, which allow their functionalization with different antibacterial compounds and structures that give them greater biocompatibility.

Some research has been conducted on NIPHUs as wound-healing dressings. Gholami et al. (2021) prepared NIPHUs from soybean oil and tetraethylenepentamine and treated them with epichlorohydrin to produce cross-linked networks containing cationic azetidinium groups, which provided better antibacterial activity. These dressings showed no cytotoxicity to fibroblasts and a WVTR between 390 and 710 g/m2/day suggested good performance as wound-healing dressings, preserving the moist environment in wounds with low to moderate exudate.

However, few in vivo research have been conducted. For example, Ge et al. (2024) generated NIPHU nanofiber membranes from ethylene carbonate (EC), 1,6-hexanediamine (1,6-HDA), and polyethylene glycol 1000 (PEG1000) with the incorporation of glycolic acid (GA) and neopentyl glycol (NPG) to prepare LPGD and curcumin to enhance the healing effect of the dressings. The nanofiber membranes effectively prevented the formation of chronic wounds and promoted the growth of granulation tissue without the need to replace the dressing throughout the healing process. Also, Quan et al. (2025) generated lignin-based NIPHU sponges and incorporated copper peroxide (CuO2) into them to create a pH-sensitive wound dressing. These dressings performed well in promoting wound healing. However, this foam has limitations such as long-term stability under simulated physiological conditions, degradation rate, and possible toxicity that may result from the release of copper ions.

Therefore, the aim was to evaluate the performance of biobased NIPHUs obtained from soybean oil in a murine model as candidates for wound dressing with hemostatic capability.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Nonisocyanate Polyhydroxyurethanes (NIPHUs)

The NIPHUs were obtained as published previously in Morales-González et al. (2024). Briefly, the carbonate soybean oil (CSBO) was heated to 60 °C. Then, the amines 1,4-butadiamine (putrescine) (BDA) and 1,3-cyclohexanobis­(methylamine) (CHM) were added in different proportions in a 1:2 mol ratio of cyclocarbonate to amine. The mixture was left under agitation at 60 °C for 5 min. Then, the mixture was transferred to a mold and left to cure at 90 °C for 12 h. Four NIPHUs were obtained; the molar percentages of BDA and CHM were 100/0, 75/25, 67/33, and 50/50, respectively.

Water Vapor Transmission Rate (WVTR)

The moisture permeability of the NIPHUs was determined by the WVTR according to ASTM E96/E96 M and ISO 2528:2017 by the rate of change in the mass of water. NIPHUs were cut (with consistent area and thickness) and placed in glass vials containing distilled water (20 mL), and then the vials were sealed and placed in an incubator at 37 °C and a relative humidity of 70%. The WVTR was calculated using the following equation: 1 WVTR(g/m2/day)=Wi−WtA×t where Wi corresponds to the initial weight, Wt is the final weight, A is the area of the cup mouth (m2), and t is the time (days). A blank correction was done according to the ASTM to compensate for variability in test conditions due to temperature or barometric pressure fluctuations.

Skin Irritation, Hemostatic, and Wound Healing Assays

CD-1 et/et mice (Mus musculus) aged 5–7 weeks were employed for skin irritation, hemostatic capability, and wound healing assays. The mice were kept in a climate-controlled environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle and had access to food and water ad libitum in the FES Iztacala Bioterium, UNAM. All animals were acclimatized for 1 week before starting the experiment. The Bioethical Committee/FES Iztacala, UNAM, approved all animal protocols (CE/FESI/092024/1809, 9, 4, 2024).

NIPHUs’ Skin Irritation Test

Three healthy female mice (CD1 et/et) were used for the skin irritation study, following the OECD guideline for testing chemicals: 404. The NIPHUs were placed on the backs of the mice and left in contact with the skin. The dorsal zone was observed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after the application of the NIPHUs, and it was photographed for signs of erythema and edema.

The scoring criteria are as follows: no erythema, mild erythema (barely visible), clear erythema, moderate erythema, severe erythema, or even the formation of scabs, scored 0–4 points; no edema, mild edema (barely visible), clear edema (clear swelling edge), moderate edema (swelling about 1 mm), severe edema (swelling over 1 mm), scored 0–4 points.

In Vivo Hemostatic Capability in the Mouse Tail Model

A standardization was performed in which the cut site was controlled, and the bleeding time was measured. Based on the times, the assay was set to 3 min. If the bleeding hadn’t stopped after 3 min, a hemostatic agent was applied (Barmazal, Sabra).

The in vivo hemostatic capability of the NIPHU 50/50 was tested by employing a mouse-tail model, following Ye et al.’s (2024) protocol with some modifications. Male CD-1 et/et mice were anesthetized via inhalation (with 99.9% isoflurane (100 μL/mouse), their tails were cut at half of the tail length (tail length = 89.06 ± 0.41 mm and diameter = 10.75 ± 0.5), and the wound was allowed to bleed freely for 60 s before any intervention to ensure a consistent baseline blood loss. Subsequently, the NIPHU 50/50 was applied to the incision site without compression for 3 min. Then, the blood loss was weighed. The assay was performed with randomization and blinding.

Wound Healing Assay

Previously, a standardization was performed to ensure control over incision geometry and the measurement procedure.

Male mice were anesthetized with 99.9% isoflurane (100 μL/mouse), and incisional wounds of 1 ± 0.136 cm were made through the skin in the dorsal zone. − Animals were distributed into six experimental groups as follows: 1) Blank: mice with normal skin (without wound), 2) mice with wounds without any treatment, 3) mice with wounds treated with Tegaderm film (3M, 1624W), 4) mice with wounds treated with NIPHU 50/50, 5) mice with wounds treated with NIPHU 67/33, and 6) mice with wounds treated with NIPHU 100/0. All the material was placed over the lesion area, and in the case of group 2, the wounds were left open. The incision area was photographed, and the wound length was measured every 2 days.

Mice were sacrificed by asphyxiation in a CO2 chamber at 14 days posttreatment, following the AVMA guidelines for euthanasia, and skin samples from the lesion site were obtained and processed for histological evaluation.

Histological Analysis

Once the mice were sacrificed, skin samples were taken from the wound area and the adjacent normal skin (4 cm2). The skin samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h and dehydrated in an alcohol series (70%, 80%, 90%, 90%, 96%, 100% I, and 100% II). Transverse cuts were made in the wound area of the skin samples, and then the samples were embedded in paraffin.

At least 20 histological sections, each 5 μm thick, were obtained from each paraffin-embedded specimen and arranged on glass slides. Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed.

Staining with Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE)

Deparaffinization with xylol, hydration in a series of alcohol (100%, 96%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), rinsing with water, and staining with Harris hematoxylin were performed. Subsequently, differentiation was performed with 1% acidic alcohol, the sections were blued using ammonia-water, and cytoplasmic staining was carried out with eosin. Finally, dehydration was made with 96%, 100% I, and 100% II ethyl alcohol, followed by clarification with xylol. Finally, the slides were mounted with Entellan.

Staining with Masson’s Trichrome

Deparaffinization with xylol, hydration in a series of alcohol (100%, 96%, 96%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), rinsing with water, and staining with Weigert’s hematoxylin were performed. Then staining was performed with Biebrich’s scarlet solution, aniline blue, and 1% acetic acid. Finally, dehydration (ethyl alcohol 96%, 100% I, and 100% II) and clarification with xylol were performed. The slides were mounted with Entellan.

The slides were examined under a Leica light microscope, and representative areas were photographed. Photomicrographs were taken using the LAS EZ program by Leica. The histological architecture of the wound was compared among the three groups.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed at least three independent times. Data were reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Mechanical data (Table ) and size of healing zone data (Figure B) were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance to determine statistical significance. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. The Tukey multiple comparisons test was performed to determine significant differences between pairs.

Results

The main function of the skin is to act as a barrier that protects against external damage and bacterial invasion. Also, the skin regulates water and permeates oxygen and carbon dioxide, which affects thermoregulation and immune function. In skin wounds, an appropriate moist environment regulates the activity of cells and enzymes, which are important to the wound healing process; thus, maintaining a moist environment around the wound area is a key factor for ideal dressings.

In a previous publication, four NIPHUs from soybean oil were synthesized, by mixing the type of amine between BDA and CHM, and the physicochemical and mechanical properties were analyzed. In this study, the WVTR of NIPHUs was calculated after 14 days of incubation and ranged from 95.6 g/m2/day to 593.3 g/m2/day (Table ).

Throughout the skin irritation test, the skin in contact with NIPHU 75/25 showed redness compared to intact skin at 2 h. However, at 24 h, the redness disappeared. Thus, mild erythema (barely visible) was attributed to this NIPHU. For the other NIPHUs (NIPHU 100/0, NIPHU 67/33, and NIPHU 50/50), the observations indicated the absence of erythema, as there were no indications of any redness (Figure ). None of the NIPHUs presented signs of edema during the evaluation. Because of the mild erythema seen in NIPHU75/25 at 2 h, this material was discarded for the wound healing assays.

Preliminary study of hemostatic activity was carried out in a mouse tail model, evaluating the NIPHU 50/50 in comparison to the control without the use of any material or substances (Figure A). The amount of blood loss was lower when the NIPHU 50/50 was applied for three minutes than in wounds without any treatment (Figure B). After applying NIPHU 50/50, the hemostasis time was around 31.33 ± 3.05 s, whereas in controls, no hemostasis or bleeding control was observed after 180 s.

Following hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling occur in wound healing, during which the wound is closed, and the restoration of the skin’s structure follows. The closure of the wound is seen in the reduction of the wound lengths in different groups of animals over 14 days (Figure ). By day 14, all the wounds were closed. Approximately 10 days after the incision, the wounds closed in all the groups; however, macroscopically different sizes of the healing zones could be observed (Figure ). All groups showed a healing zone after 14 days of treatment, possibly due to the thinness of the skin in that area. The healed zone in animals treated with any NIPHUs was smaller and thinner, while an elongated and thick scab was still observed in the healed skin of the control groups. NIPHU 50/50 showed a slightly smaller healing zone length compared with NIPHU 100/0 and NIPHU 67/33, but it was like the wound with no treatment and wounds treated with a commercial material (Figure ).

Wound closure over time with the NIPHUs, wounds without any treatment, and commercial dressing.

The healing process throughout the 14 days of treatment progressed differently in each of the groups, as shown in Figure . On the fourth day, it can be observed that in the wounds that did not receive treatment, a scab formed, and the shape of the healing zone was more circular; those wounds that had some material placed on them appeared to be thinner, and the healing zone was moister and without the formation of a scab. This suggests that the healing process developed differently in each group. After day 8, the scab disappeared and the healing zone became thinner and larger.

Histological analysis was performed to assess wound healing processes and to evaluate the structure of the skin after the treatment of an injury with NIPHUs. The description given for each of the groups corresponded to the central area of the healing zone, which was the most recently closed area; however, the healing zone, the edges (of the healing zone), and the adjacent normal skin were considered for comparison.

In accordance with the macroscopic findings observed in the wound closure trials, the histological characteristics of the skin in the scar zone differed among groups and from those of the normal skin (Figure A).

The scar zone did not exactly correspond to that of normal skin in most treatments. For the group with the wound without any treatment, the healing zone is wide, with an approximate width of 0.70 ± 0.17 mm (Figure G). It is epithelialized, and below it, an area of advanced granulation tissue can be recognized. At the edge of the healing zone, there are neoformation pilosebaceous units and connective tissue of the adjacent dermis. No hypodermis is identified (Figure B).

In the wounds treated with Tegaderm, a commercial patch, the healing zone is also wide, with an approximate width of 0.86 ± 0.36 mm (Figure G). The healing zone is epithelialized, and below it, the residual granulation tissue is present; there is also an inflammatory infiltrate assessed as residual. The healing edge has advanced granulation tissue, and no hypodermis is identified (Figure C).

In the case of the three NIPHUs, different widths of the healing zone were observed (Figure G). NIPHU 100/0 was approximately 1.07 ± 0.06 mm (Figure D), NIPHU 67/33 was approximately 0.94 ± 0.31 mm (Figure E), and NIPHU 50/50 was approximately 0.27 ± 0.29 mm (Figure F).

Also, differences in the structure of the skin were seen for NIPHUs 100/0 and 67/33. Although the healing zone is epithelialized, it was hyperplastic, and the cell population of the subepithelial zone has fibroblasts and a residual neutrophilic population (Figure D). For NIPHU 67/33 beneath the epithelium, there is advanced granulation tissue preserving erythrocytes, and no myofibroblasts are observed (Figure E). However, these groups on the edges of the healing zone’s hypodermis in formation are identified.

In the case of NIPHU 50/50, the healing zone is much narrower. It is also epithelialized, and below this epithelium several regions can be recognized. Connective tissue with active fibroblasts is observed and has some neo-formed adipocytes. The healing edge has newly formed follicles with pilosebaceous units, and the muscle layer is complete (Figure F).

Deep observation of the skin structure in all of the groups showed differences after the treatments, allowing them to understand the stage of wound healing that each one was.

Epithelium

In all groups, the area is completely epithelialized; however, different layers of keratinocytes were seen. The normal skin of the mice presents an epidermis formed by a flat stratified epithelium of 5 to 6 layers in which well-differentiated strata with a well-defined basement membrane can be distinguished (Figure A). In the case of the wound with any treatment, in some specimens, the epidermis-equivalent epithelium consisted of 6–7 layers of flattened cells (Figure B); in other specimens, the incipient formation of strata is observed, which are not identified as typical of the epidermis. And in the case of commercial dressing, no differentiated strata are recognized; all cells are flat, forming an epithelium of 8–9 layers (Figure C).

Regarding the NIPHU treatments, for NIPHU 100/0 and NIPHU 67/33, a stratified epithelial layer has formed, consisting of 10–12 layers (Figure D,E). Mitotic activity of keratinocytes is observed, but this epithelium has no papillary formation. In the case of NIPHU 50/50, the epithelium is observed with well-defined strata, including the stratum corneum, with approximately 7 layers and papillary formations can be observed, similar to the normal skin appearance (Figure F).

Dermis

Normal skin presents the papillary and reticular dermis, which are distinguished by the arrangement of collagen, with the collagen being more compact in the reticular dermis than in the papillary dermis. Some blood vessels and abundant pilosebaceous units in the active growth phase were identified. The cell population is represented by abundant, active, and large fibroblasts. Occasionally, some neutrophils could be identified (Figure A).

In the case of the wound without any treatment and the commercial patch, the healing area of the tissue underlying the epithelium does not correspond to normal dermis. Several subregions were identified: one is located immediately below the epithelium, and another is located further down. These layers are clearly distinguishable because the upper layer is rich in myofibroblasts and active fibroblasts, as well as empty residual blood vessels (corresponding to advanced granulation tissue). Masson’s stain is positive for collagen throughout the healing zone, appearing as thin fibers. However, no fibrillar organization is recognized (Figure B, C). The lower layer has no myofibroblasts; fibroblasts are abundant, and there is a mild to moderate population of neutrophils.

Similarly, NIPHU 100/0 and NIPHU 67/33 presented a subepithelial zone different from that of normal skin. In these cases, high cellularity, such as fibroblast and neutrophil infiltration, is seen. Granulation tissue is present in many cases with vessels containing erythrocytes (Figure D,E). For the subepithelial area in NIPHU 50/50, the cell population consists of some small myofibroblasts and active fibroblasts; some neoformation of pilosebaceous units can be seen. A cell population with a predominance of active fibroblasts and eosinophilic collagen fibers with a wavy trajectory and a papillary and reticular dermis is forming (Figure F).

Discussion

The WVTR in normal skin is approximately 250 g/m2/day at 35 °C; however, water loss can increase in wounds to 5000 g/m2/day. Depending on the type of wound, this value may change. According to the literature, adequate WVTR values are close to 840 g/m2/day. However, values up to 2000–2500 g/m2/day can create environments suitable for wound healing. In the case of WVTR lower than 300 g/m2/day (too low), it may lead to maceration of the skin in the wound area, and values higher than 2500 g/m2/day (very high), dehydration of the wound area may occur. Nevertheless, these dressings could be useful for wounds with high exudate.

For the NIPHUs obtained, the WVTR values differed among them, with very low values for NIPHUs 67/33 and NIPHU 100/0 (less than 115 g/m2/day) compared to NIPHU 50/50 (Table ). Given this, NIPHU 50/50 would be within the expected vapor rate range for a dressing (between 300 and 2500 g/m2/day), even though it is not the ideal value for the dressing. Nevertheless, this is related to wound behavior in mice over 14 days. NIPHUs with a lower WVTR presented moist wounds until days 8 to 12 of treatment (Figure A), while wounds treated with NIPHU 50/50 remained with an adequate balance.

Differences and variability in the WVTR values among the NIPHUs synthesized can be related to the differences in the composition of the material and, thus, the differences in their physical properties. In a previous publication, it was seen that the contact angle and water absorption change according to the inclusion of CHM in the backbone of the NIPHUs, and these changes are related to the differences in the WVTR values. In polymeric membranes, permeability depends on the hydrophilicity of the material, which influences vapor sorption and diffusion. NIPHU 50/50 presented a more hydrophilic behavior, and thus, the WVTR increased.

This variability observed in WVTR can be attributed to local differences in the distribution of hydrophilic domains and in the organization of the polymer network, a phenomenon known in segmented polyurethanes. NIPHUs contain abundant hydroxyl groups and urethane bonds capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water molecules, promoting local phenomena of differential moisture absorption and diffusion.

Further histological analysis shows that the presence of this more moist environment in wounds treated with NIPHU 67/33 and NIPHU 100/0 led to inadequate re-epithelialization, resulting in epidermal hyperplasia (Figure D–F and Figure D–F) and even skin maceration (whitish or grayish-white appearance on days 8 to 12 for NIPHU 67/33); the same phenomenon seen in wounds treated with the commercial dressing, which created a more humid environment (by not allowing water vapor exchange), leading to delayed healing and, in this case, a much thinner epithelium compared to intact skin (Figure C and Figure C). These phenomena also had an impact on the other layers with inflammation observed after 14 days in some of these treatments. Untreated wounds, on the other hand, showed clot formation (Figure A), which, in contrast to the others, was related to the dehydration of the wound area due to much greater water loss.

Similar studies have found that Tegaderm has a moderate WVTR, usually between 794 g/m2/24 h and 846 g/m2/24 h, values close to those reported in the literature as suitable for dressings. However, it has been observed that this type of film has no absorption capacity. This causes the film to generate the adverse effects mentioned above, such as the formation of blisters under the film due to excess exudate accumulating under the dressing, maintaining a moist environment, as can be seen in the wounds treated with this material (Figure A),

Dressings created with prepared CO-based polyurethane membranes with the addition of glycidyltriethylammonium chloride (GTEAC) presented a WVTR of 53 ± 32 g/m2/day for NPU0% and 154 ± 10 g/m2/day for NPU50%. The authors observed that the addition of GTEAC improved WVTR; however, it is too low for an optimal dressing. PU dressings created from a combination of carbonated soybean oil and castor oil with isophorone diisocyanate, together with the incorporation of quaternary ammonium, showed a WVTR of 390 g/m2/day, suitable for application in wounds with low exudation, and which generated good progress in wound healing, showing that the tensile strength of the regenerated skin reached approximately 80% of normal healthy skin on day 21 after application. Similarly, dressings made with castor oil with CAPA 7201A (a copolymer of polycaprolactone and poly­(tetramethylene ether glycol) (PTMEG)) and hexamethylene diisocyanate (HDI) showed hydrophilic behavior (contact angle of 70°) with a WVTR of 260 ± 20 g/m2/day, which improved interaction with fibroblasts.

In vivo, hemostasis occurs in two stages: in the primary hemostasis, the von Willebrand factor for platelet adhesion is released at the wound site; and then, conversion of prothrombin to thrombin and fibrinogen for clot formation occurs in the secondary hemostasis. For hemostatic activity, the mouse-tail test was performed, finding that, after 3 min, the untreated wound had not stopped bleeding, and there were no signs of hemostasis, while with NIPHU 50/50, the bleeding had stopped at 31 s, and blood loss was significantly lower (Figure B). This preliminary analysis demonstrates the hemostatic activity of NIPHU 50/50 and its potential use in wound healing.

One possible cause of the hemostatic activity of biomaterials is related to their water absorption capacity, which causes the dressing to absorb water from the blood, concentrating the platelets and coagulation factors related to the coagulation cascade and helping to speed up clot formation. NIPHUs with a more hydrophilic character may be associated with better hemostatic activity as they could improve the interaction of the material with water, the main component of blood. In the case of NIPHU 50/50, the contact angle is 63.7, giving it a hydrophilic surface that facilitates its interaction with water, compared to NIPHU 100/0 and NIPHU 67/33, with contact angles of 101.4 and 83.1, respectively, which makes them more hydrophobic, and in the wound healing test, more bleeding was seen with them.

Similar results were found in the study by Ge et al. (2024), where nanofiber membranes were generated from copolyester with isocyanate-free polyurethane (NIPU) and polyglycolic acid with a dihydroxy structure (LPGD, synthesized from glycolic acid and neopentyl glycol). They found that NI-LPGD5 membranes had a hemostatic effect, with much less blood loss in vivo than the controls, and this membrane had a contact angle of 38, showing that the addition of LPGD increases the hydrophilicity of the material. Also, Niu et al. (2024) synthesized asymmetric sponge dressings with oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA) and cross-linked with ε-polylysine (EPL), finding that, in the tail amputation model, blood loss was 63 mg, significantly lower than that shown in the blank and unmodified PU, with a hemostasis time of 104 s, compared to the 33 s shown with the NIPHU 50/50. This is also related to WVTR. As NIPHUs have more hydrophilic behavior, the WVTR also increases, creating a better environment for blood cells and then fibroblasts and keratinocytes.

Thus, the hemostatic activity of NIPHU 50/50 is plausibly explained by the contact angle–WVTR correlation. However, further experimental evidence, such as in vitro platelet adhesion or activation assays, is needed to confirm the mechanism of the hemostatic activity of NIPHU 50/50.

Once the clot has formed and the bleeding or hemorrhage has stopped, the inflammation stage begins, during which vasodilation occurs, and the body responds to damaged cells or the invasion of pathogens with the infiltration of inflammatory cells, neutrophil granulocytes, and monocytes. Toward the end of this stage, fibroblasts can be observed in the reticular dermis, which will help the transition to the proliferative phase. In this phase, there will be migration and proliferation of fibroblasts toward the papillary dermis, contributing to re-epithelialization, and favoring restoration of the epidermal barrier, as well as the activation, migration, and proliferation of keratinocytes from the edges of the wound and skin appendages, sweat glands, and hair follicles, along with the degradation of the temporary extracellular matrix. By the end of the proliferative stage, modulation will occur in the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, and keratinocytes will multiply and differentiate to restore the stratified layers of the epidermis. Finally, in the remodeling phase, tissue maturation occurs, with the transformation of type III collagen into type I collagen and the reorientation and interconnection of collagen to give the tissue greater tensile strength. There is also a decrease in the vascularization and cellularity of the tissue. This phase can last up to a year.

The evaluated NIPHUs showed a better progression in healing (structure of the restored skin) in all mice of the group compared to the healing process in the group with untreated wounds. However, the application of NIPHUs generates differences in healing processes. For NIPHU 100/0 and NIPHU 67/33, histologically, after 14 days, neutrophils and inflammatory cells are still visible in the area below the formed epithelium (Figure ). As mentioned, this is related to differences in the WVTR of the NIPHUs, creating a humid environment that favors tissue maceration processes in the healing area and the development of epidermal hyperplasia. However, migration of some fibroblasts under the epithelium and myofibroblasts can be seen, which is why the mice in these groups can be placed in the proliferative healing stage (Figure ).

On the other hand, for the NIPHU 50/50 group, skin quality is better compared to that of the other NIPHU treatments, even with the commercial biomaterial. The epidermis and dermis are well differentiated. Three layers were observed in the epidermis. The dermis showed well-defined collagen fibers, young pilosebaceous units, and a fibroblast cell population. Young fibers were observed in the muscle layers. All of this indicates that in these mice, the process advanced to a transitional stage between late proliferation and possible remodeling after 14 days of experimentation (Figure ).

At each stage of healing, there are macroscopic signs of healing, and these signs characterize each stage. During the proliferation phase, the scab detaches, a new epidermis develops (which begins to differentiate by day 12), granulation tissue forms, and the inflammatory response subsides. During the remodeling stage (which can begin as early as day 12) of the healing process, macroscopic evidence includes differentiation of the newly formed epidermis, a decrease in cellular response, an increase in elastic fiber content, and maturation of collagen fibers.

In the case of NIPHU foams and the addition of 100 mM copper peroxide, the skin on day 21 is almost indistinguishable from normal tissue, with the presence of newly generated hair follicles and blood vessels, as well as a fully connected epidermis. Similarly, with NI-LPGD5 nanofiber membranes, it was observed that the inclusion of LPGD5 increased the healing effect present in NIPHU due to its hydrophilicity, increasing the thickness of the granulation tissue and the migration and proliferation of epithelial cells.

Therefore, it is evident that the hemostatic action of NIPHU 50/50 aids in the development of the wound healing process. The skin has structures similar to those of normal skin, with a formed and stratified epithelium, in the dermis, the beginning of collagen organization and maturation, the formation of new pilosebaceous units, and incipient restoration of the muscle layer are observed 14 days after the wound. However, further quantitative measurements are needed to confirm this.

Conclusions

Ideal wound dressings must strike a balance among mechanical, physicochemical, and biological properties to help the healing process. In addition, they should be hemostatic materials that generate clots efficiently, which will affect the subsequent development of healing and the quality of the regenerated skin. NIPHU 50/50 was found to be a hemostatic biomaterial, generating clots in less time and with less blood loss, which helped the entire healing process develop properly. Re-epithelialization with this material occurs in 14 days and generates an epithelium like normal skin with its papillary appearance. Likewise, the dermis region has two zones, the papillary and the reticular, with organized collagen tending to mature with the generation of new pilosebaceous units, in comparison with the other NIPHUs and commercial dressings. The creation of NIPHUs from vegetable oils by a greener synthesis route generates dressings capable of allowing wound healing to progress within the expected time frame, providing a more sustainable biomaterial.
