1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a von Neumann algebra with a faithful, normal tracial state
\tau
. The pioneering work of Voiculescu in
[
12
]
showed it is possible to understand the limits of empirical spectral distributions of independent Gaussian random matrices as the laws of freely independent random variables. While there are powerful and varied techniques in determining the limit laws of Hermitian random matrices, there have been few techniques and examples for determining the limit laws of non-Hermitian (i.e. non-normal) random matrices. Two notable examples have been the Circular Law (finally proven in complete generality in
[
11
]
) and the Single Ring Theorem in
[
5
]
(which has been recently generalized in
[
7
]
).

Before proving the proving the convergence, we must first determine what the limit of the random matrix model should even be. Of the random matrix models that are considered, typically there is already a natural limit operator of the random matrix model that comes from free probability. To any operator in a tracial von Neumann algebra, there is an associated complex Borel probability measure, the
Brown measure
. The Brown measure of the natural limit operator is the candidate limit law of empirical spectral distributions of the random matrices. There are counterexamples to this (see
[
8
, Chapter 11, Exercise 15]
for a simple counterexample), but typically this is true (see
[
9
]
for a precise statement of this). In the case of the Circular Law and the Single Ring Theorem, the limit law is indeed the Brown measure of the limit operator.

Having identified the tentative limit law, then we can ask what techniques are available in proving the convergence. The main method was first introduced in
[
4
]
as a method of proving the Circular Law and involves a “Hermitization” of the problem, i.e. translating the problem of the convergence of the non-Hermitian random matrices to the convergence of associated Hermitian random matrices. The convergence of these associated Hermitian random matrices is well-understood, but the trade-off is that one must bound the minimum singular values of the random matrices from below. The Hermitization method was used to great effect in the proofs of the Circular Law and the Single Ring Theorem, and obtaining the appropriate minimum singular value estimates was the critical step in both of the proofs.

In this paper, we will use this method and prove that the limit of the empirical spectral distributions of a random matrix model
X_{n}
is the Brown measure of the appropriate limit operator.

Now, we define the matrix model
X_{n}
that is considered in this paper:

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Brown measure

In this subsection, we recall the definition of the Brown measure of an operator
X\in(M,\tau)
and the relevant properties.

First, we recall the definition of the Fuglede-Kadison determinant, first introduced in
[
3
]
:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
x\in(M,\tau)
. Let
\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}
be the spectral measure of
\left\lvert x\right\rvert=(x^{*}x)^{1/2}
. Then, the
Fuglede-Kadison determinant
of
x
,
\Delta(x)
, is given by:

(4)

\Delta(x)=\exp\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}(t)\right]\,.

When
x=X_{n}\in(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\frac{1}{n}\text{{tr}})
, then
\Delta(X_{n})=\left\lvert\det(X_{n})\right\rvert^{1/n}
. Thus, the Fuglede-Kadison determinant is a generalization of the normalized, positive determinant of a complex matrix.

By applying the functional calculus to a decreasing sequence of continuous functions on
[0,\infty)
converging to
\log t
, we have:

(5)

\log\Delta(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}}(t)\,.

In practice, it is more convenient to compute the right-hand side to compute
\Delta(X)
.

Then, the Brown measure is the following distribution:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
x\in(M,\tau)
. The
Brown measure
of
x
is defined as:

(6)

\mu_{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\nabla^{2}\log\Delta(z-x)\,.

In
[
2
]
, some properties of the Brown measure are proven (see also
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
6
]
for more exposition on some of the basic results). In particular, the Brown measure of
x
is a probability measure supported on the spectrum of
x
. When
x
is normal, then the Brown measure is the spectral measure. When
x
is a random matrix, then the Brown measure is the empirical spectral distribution.

Further, the Riesz representation for the subharmonic function
L(z)=\log\Delta(z-x)
is given by:

(7)

\log\Delta(z-x)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert\,d\mu_{x}(w)\,,

and the Brown measure is the unique complex Borel measure to satisfy this equation.

2.3.
Hermitization method

In this subsection, we review the Hermitization method and give an outline of the steps of the proof of Theorem
5.1
. We refer to
[
10
]
and
[
5
]
for the justification of facts stated without proof.

For the Hermitization method, we consider the logarithmic potential of compactly supported, positive measure on
\mathbb{C}
:

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mu
be a compactly supported, positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Then, the
logarithmic potential
of
\mu
,
L_{\mu}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, is given by:

(27)

L_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\log\left\lvert z-w\right\rvert\,d\mu(w)\,.

Hence, if
\mu
is the Brown measure of
x\in(M,\tau)
, then

(28)

L_{\mu}(z)=\log\Delta(z-x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log t\,d\mu_{\left\lvert z-x\right\rvert^{2}}(t)\,.

In particular, this identity holds when
x=X_{n}
is a random matrix, where the Brown measure
\mu=\mu_{n}
is the empirical spectral distribution of
X_{n}
.

Since
\mu
is compactly supported, then
L_{\mu}\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure). When
\mu=\mu_{n}
is the empirical spectral distribution of a random matrix, this follows from the fact that
\log\left\lvert\cdot-w\right\rvert\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
for any
w\in\mathbb{C}
. In general,
L_{\mu}\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
follows from the fact that
\log\left\lvert\cdot-w\right\rvert\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{C})
uniformly for
w
in a compact set.

In particular,
L_{\mu}
is a well-defined distribution and since
\frac{1}{2\pi}\nabla^{2}\log\left\lvert\cdot-w\right\rvert=\delta_{w}
, then

(29)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\nabla^{2}L_{\mu}=\mu\,,

so we can recover
\mu
from
L_{\mu}
.

In the context of Theorem
5.1
, let
\mu_{n}
be the empirical spectral distribution of the random matrices
X_{n}
and let
\mu
be the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
. Then, if we can prove the limit:

(30)

L_{\mu_{n}}(z)\to L_{\mu}(z)\,,

then we should be able to take distributional Laplacians and conclude that
\mu_{n}\to\mu
.

The following Proposition
[
10
, Theorem 2.8.3]
makes this precise:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mu_{n}
be a sequence of random probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
and suppose that
\mu
is a deterministic probability measure on
\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
\mu_{n},\mu
are almost surely supported on some compact set. Suppose for Lebesgue almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
L_{\mu_{n}}(z)\to L_{\mu}(z)
almost surely (resp. in probability). Then,
\mu_{n}
converges almost surely (resp. in probability) in the vague topology.

As in our case, the boundedness hypothesis in Proposition
2.7
is typically not an issue.

We highlight some notation that we will use for the rest of the paper:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X_{n}
be the random matrix model from Definition
1.1
, and suppose that the law of
P_{n}
converges to the law of
p
and the law of
Q_{n}
converges to the law of
q
, where
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are freely independent. Then, define the following quantities:

•

Let
\bm{H_{z}(X_{n})}=(z-X_{n})^{*}(z-X_{n})
.

•

Let
\bm{\nu_{n,z}}
be the spectral measure of
H_{z}(X_{n})
.

•

Let
\bm{H_{z}(X)}=(z-X)^{*}(z-X)
.

•

Let
\bm{\nu_{z}}
be the spectral measure of
H_{z}(X)
.

•

Let
\bm{\mu_{n}}
be empirical spectral distribution of
X_{n}
.

•

Let
\bm{\mu}
be the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
.

Thus, to prove Theorem
5.1
, it suffices to prove the convergence of the following integrals for almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
almost surely:

(31)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{n,z}(x)\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{z}(x)\,.

In general, the strength of the convergence of these integrals (i.e. whether the convergence is in probability or almost surely) determines the strength of the convergence of
\mu_{n}
to
\mu
.

The main difficulty in justifying the convergence of the logarithmic integrals is the fact that
\log x
is not continuous at
0
:
\log x\to-\infty
as
x\to 0^{-}
. So, the logarithmic integrals may not converge if the measures
\nu_{n,z}
have too much mass near
0
. Note that the measure
\nu_{n,z}
is supported on
[\sigma_{\min}(z-X_{n})^{2},\left\lVert z-X_{n}\right\rVert]
, so in order to prove the convergence of the logarithmic integrals, it suffices bound the minimum singular value from below.

In practice, this is the most difficult part of these convergence arguments. For our
X_{n}
, we use the algebraic properties of projections and the geometric properties of the support of the
X_{n}
.

To summarize, in order to prove Theorem
5.1
, we need to complete the following steps:

(1)

Show that for Lebesgue almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the spectral measures
\nu_{n,z}
converge to
\nu_{z}
almost surely in the vague topology.

(2)

Bound the minimum singular value of
z-X_{n}
from below to justify the convergence

(32)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{n,z}(x)\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{z}(x)

for Lebesgue almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
almost surely.

We will prove these steps exclusively in the case where
p
and
q
have
2
atoms (resp.
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
have
2
atoms), even if the results can be generalized for the case when
p
or
q
is constant (resp.
P_{n}
or
Q_{n}
is constant). The exceptional cases will be handled in the final proof of Theorem
5.1
.

In the case

and

Definition 2.9

If

\displaystyle\bm{\mu_{p}}

\displaystyle=a\delta_{\alpha}+(1-a)\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\bm{\mu_{q}}

\displaystyle=b\delta_{\beta}+(1-b)\delta_{\beta^{\prime}}\,,

.

Recall that

\displaystyle=b_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}+(1-b_{n})\delta_{\beta_{n}^{\prime}}\,.

(resp.

\displaystyle\mu_{q^{\prime}}

5.
Proofs of Convergence and Converse

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
5.1
and then deduce Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the random matrix model
X_{n}=P_{n}+iQ_{n}
, where
P_{n},Q_{n}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are independently Haar-rotated Hermitian matrices with distributions with at most
2
atoms. Suppose that the law of
P_{n}
converges to the law of
p
and the law of
Q_{n}
converges to the law of
q
, where
p,q\in(M,\tau)
are freely independent. Then, the empirical spectral distribution of

converges almost surely in the vague topology to the Brown measure of
X=p+iq
.

Proof.

Recall from the discussion after the statement of Theorem
5.1
in the Introduction that
\mu_{p}
and
\mu_{q}
have at most
2
points in their supports. First, we consider the case where
p
and
q
have
2
atoms, i.e.
(a,b)\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\neq\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta\neq\beta^{\prime}
. Recall from the discussion after Definition
2.9
that we may assume that
\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
,
\beta_{n}\to\beta
,
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}\to\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta_{n}^{\prime}\to\beta^{\prime}
,
a_{n}\to a
, and
b_{n}\to b

Recall that we need to complete the following steps to complete the proof of Theorem
5.1
when
p
and
q
have
2
atoms:

(1)

Show that for Lebesgue almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the spectral measures
\nu_{n,z}
converge to
\nu_{z}
almost surely in the vague topology.

(2)

Bound the minimum singular value of
z-X_{n}
from below to justify the convergence

(85)

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{n,z}(x)\to\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{z}(x)

for Lebesgue almost every
z\in\mathbb{C}
almost surely.

The first step was completed in Corollary
3.2
.

For the second step, it suffices to prove the convergence of the logarithmic integrals for
z\not\in H\cap R
, where
H
and

are the hyperbola and rectangle associated with

. Fix a

. Let

and

be the hyperbola and rectangle associated with

.

From the triangle inequality, for arbitrary

, the following inequality holds:

(86)

\text{{dist}}(z,H\cap R)\leq\text{{dist}}(z,H_{n}\cap R_{n})+\sup_{w\in H_{n}\cap R_{n}}\text{{dist}}(w,H\cap R)\,.

Hence,

(87)

\text{{dist}}(z,H_{n}\cap R_{n})\geq\text{{dist}}(z,H\cap R)-\sup_{w\in H_{n}\cap R_{n}}\text{{dist}}(w,H\cap R)\,.

As

,
\sup_{w\in H_{n}\cap R_{n}}\text{{dist}}(w,H\cap R)\to 0
. This can be seen by using the parameterization of
H_{n}\cap R_{n}
,

in
[
13

and comparing the points on
H_{n}\cap R_{n}
,
H\cap R
with the same
\theta
. Since
z\not\in H\cap R
, then
\text{{dist}}(z,H\cap R)>0
. So, for
n
sufficiently large,
\text{{dist}}(z,H_{n}\cap R_{n})>\delta
for some

.

Since
\left\lVert X_{n}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert P_{n}\right\rVert+\left\lVert Q_{n}\right\rVert=\max(\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n}^{\prime})+\max(\beta_{n},\beta_{n}^{\prime})
and the sequences all converge, then the

are uniformly bounded. Hence, the

are uniformly bounded.

Combining these two facts and Theorem
4.8
, then for

sufficiently large,
\sigma_{\min}(z-X_{n})>\delta
almost surely, for some

. As

are uniformly bounded in

is almost surely supported on

By applying the convergence

\nu_{z}
is also supported on

(88)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{n,z}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,d\nu_{n,z}(x)\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,d\nu_{z}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log x\,d\nu_{z}(x)\,.

Next, we consider the case where both

as

. The same result applies for

and
q

. Let

is larger than

. If
\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n}^{\prime}\in U
and

\mu_{n}
is supported on

tends to
\delta_{p+iq}
. In the other case, suppose that

a_{n}=\tau(\chi_{\{\alpha_{n}\}}(P_{n}))>1-\epsilon
.

When
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})\neq(\alpha_{n}^{\prime},\beta_{n}^{\prime})
, we claim the following equalities of (generalized) eigenspaces:

(89)

V_{\alpha_{n}+i\beta_{n}}(X_{n})=E_{\alpha_{n}+i\beta_{n}}(X_{n})=E_{\alpha_{n}}(P_{n})\cap E_{\beta_{n}}(Q_{n})\,.

The first equality follows from Proposition
4.6

. For the second equality, the non-trivial containment is:

,
\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1
. Then,

(90)

\alpha_{n}+i\beta_{n}=\langle X_{n}v,v\rangle=\langle P_{n}v,v\rangle+i\langle Q_{n}v,v\rangle\,.

As
P_{n},Q_{n}
are Hermitian, then
\langle P_{n}v,v\rangle=\alpha_{n}
and
\langle Q_{n}v,v\rangle=\beta_{n}
. As

are Hermitian and have up to

, as desired.

Thus, from the parallelogram law,
