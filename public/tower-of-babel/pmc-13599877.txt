Exploring educational approaches and strategies for developing evidence synthesis skills among pharmacy students: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Kapu Haritha, Veekshitha S Shetty, Rajalakshmi Rajendran, Sonal Sekhar M, Vijayanarayana Kunhikatta, B Unnikrishnan, Sohil Khan, Tiffany K Gunning, Eugene Athan, Sajal K Saha, Girish Thunga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDeveloping competency for evidence synthesis and translation of evidence into healthcare practice is critical for evidence-based patient care. Pharmacy professionals have a substantial role in evidence generation, synthesis and translation across their professional practice in industry, academia and healthcare. This scoping review aims to explore educational approaches and strategies for building evidence synthesis skills among pharmacy students.Method and analysisThis scoping review protocol will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines and will be conducted using the Arksey and O’Malley framework, supplemented by recommendations from the Joanna Briggs Institute. PubMed, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL Ultimate and Web of Science biomedical databases will be searched for literature from 2010. Two reviewers will screen the literature and extract data independently. Data related to the type of educational approaches being adopted to develop evidence synthesis skills, specific concepts delivered and reported effectiveness and feasibility factors will be extracted. The quantitative data will be compiled and summarised using descriptive statistics and will be presented using percentages, frequency, mean, median and SD. Qualitative evidence will be narratively described.This review will contribute to the development of an educational framework to develop evidence synthesis skills among pharmacy students. This review will also provide key insights for policymakers and regulatory authorities in the educational sector to strengthen existing curriculum and educational frameworks, to promote long-term sustainability of the acquired knowledge and skills that underpin evidence synthesis.Ethics and disseminationThe current review does not require ethical approval. The data will be disseminated at national or international conferences and will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberThis scoping review has been registered in the Open Science Framework with registration DOI:10.17605/OSF.IO/ZQVGYKeywords: Pharmacists; EDUCATION & TRAINING (see Medical Education & Training); Education, Medical

Introduction

The term evidence synthesis refers to a systematic and rigorous process of identifying and consolidating readily available information on a specific research question.1–4 Due to a substantial rise in literature load, data resources and the emergence of numerous inconsistencies across the published evidence, identifying relevant and reliable information that supports effective healthcare decisions is often challenging for healthcare professionals.5 6 To ensure effective healthcare delivery and promote patient-centred care, all healthcare providers must align with the advancements in the field and critically appraise the literature to identify optimal and cost-effective treatment options.7–9 Therefore, insufficient evidence synthesis skills among healthcare practitioners hinder the effective application of evidence in practice. This, in turn, leads to suboptimal clinical decision-making due to the underuse of up-to-date information resources, ultimately contributing to increased healthcare burden.5 6 This burden significantly highlights the need for targeted development of evidence synthesis skills among health professionals, including pharmacists.10

The practising pharmacists frequently serve as the first, and at times the only, healthcare personnel approached for self-medication-related concerns and clinical queries. Appropriate and effective utilisation of pharmacists’ expertise in providing self-care can significantly contribute to the safe, cost-effective and personalised clinical care at the patient level, thereby reducing the burden on the broader healthcare system.10 Given their crucial role in healthcare delivery, pharmacists must be able to identify, analyse and interpret the research information to facilitate high-quality patient care and informed decision-making in clinical practice.11 To ensure optimal pharmaceutical care, all pharmacists need to upgrade their therapeutic knowledge, medication usage and ability to resolve drug-related queries through constantly exploring the scientific research evidence. This continuous learning approach not only enhances professional competencies but also enables building trust among the patients.11 12

Considering the growing importance of evidence synthesis in healthcare, academic institutions have increased their expectations for promoting evidence synthesis skills through scholarly productivity.13–15 Thus, supporting pharmacy students to develop competency in evidence synthesis not only supports attainment of clinical and scholarly expectations but also fosters interest in conducting quality research, while enhancing their competitiveness for academic roles, postgraduation pathways, fellowships, residency programmes and even industrial positions that rely on the ability to summarise and interpret relevant evidence.15 16 Furthermore, strengthening pharmacy students’ competency in evidence synthesis may enhance their ability to conduct clinical research, evaluate the quality of evidence and their capacity to translate evidence into effective pharmaceutical care and policy development.17

Efforts are being continued to develop various training modalities to deliver evidence synthesis skills among healthcare students. These training initiatives are usually delivered either as a short-term course, workshop, seminar or extended training programme under comprehensive topics including research competency, research education, evidence-based practice (EBP) or evidence-based medicine, and aim to enhance literature searching skills while promoting real-world application.18–20 Despite the availability of various training programmes and competing interests towards scholarly works, students continue to report limited confidence in evidence generation and translation, resulting in a gap between knowledge and practice.6 21

Hence, it is essential to explore the available methods, content and scope for translating evidence synthesis skills into practice. Multiple studies reported various training approaches for developing evidence synthesis skills among various healthcare professionals.22–25 A recent study published by Whitney R and Wisniewski CS focused on implementing and evaluating a game-based learning approach to improve literature searching skills among pharmacy students through a drug information course, which statistically demonstrated a significant improvement in student performance, with average scores increasing from 79.9% to 90.6% from 2021 to 2023, respectively. However, the credits were allocated based on task completion rather than the quality of the search strategies developed.26 Apart from game-based learning, researchers have explored hands-on tutorial sessions, librarian-assisted instructional approaches, workshops and information literacy programmes integrated within the curriculum to enhance literature searching skills.22 27–29

Besides evidence searching skills, to enhance competence in evidence synthesis, there is a need for the ability to critically appraise and interpret the evidence retrieved. The journal club activities are adopted and used as an effective strategy to enhance critical appraisal skills among pharmacy students as well as other healthcare students, demonstrating positive outcomes by improving students’ ability to address drug-related queries and confidence.30 31 Additionally, periodic practical sessions with theoretical lectures are also widely used to improve critical appraisal skills.32 Subsequently, academic/scientific writing is considered one of the foundational components of evidence synthesis, which facilitates documentation and communication of research findings to the scientific community.33 Academic writing skills are being improved through various approaches, such as workshops, inquiry-based writing as an instructional model and gamification among healthcare students.34 35

Based on the preliminary literature search, it was identified that authors have comprehensively explored available information focused on either a single component of evidence synthesis skills at a time, including literature searching, critical appraisal and academic writing, or focused on evidence-based practice skills among broader healthcare professionals.22 36–39 However, there is limited evidence available on systematically conducted reviews that comprehensively assessed the approaches and strategies to improve evidence synthesis skills of pharmacy students. Hence, this review will comprehensively identify the diverse educational approaches and strategies that are being implemented and evaluated to inform the development of an integrated educational model framework that promotes evidence synthesis skill development among pharmacy graduates.

Objectives

Primary objective

To explore the types of educational approaches and strategies for developing evidence synthesis skills among pharmacy students.

Secondary objective

To summarise the factors contributing to the effectiveness and feasibility of the identified educational approaches and strategies.

Methods and analysis

This study will be conducted according to the revised guidelines of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) to sequentially explore the available educational techniques focused on enhancing evidence synthesis skills.40 This protocol is designed as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines, the planned scoping review will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guideline, and a checklist has been provided as an online supplemental file 1.41 42 This protocol has been registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry with a registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/ZQVGY. The scoping review has not yet commenced. Formal screening is anticipated to begin in June 2026.

Stage 2: performing a systematic literature search

This review will explore available literature from January 2010 to the present and will be updated during submission of the full review. The start point of data collection was set at 2010 due to a substantial paradigm shift in training methods adopted within healthcare education, marked by a transition from traditional lecture-based passive learning to active learning approaches (eg, Competency-based education, simulation-based learning). Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly accelerated the digitalisation of the educational system, leading to online and blended modes of teaching approaches. Furthermore, the adopted timeframe during the search will facilitate reviewing the most recent and relevant evidence on the given topic. Considering studies published before 2010 might be of limited relevance, as the findings of this review are intended to inform the design and development of advanced and innovative educational approaches, aligning with the rapid advancements in the pharmacy profession towards sustainable outcomes.39 44 45

Search strategy will be developed through a systematic literature search conducted across five databases, PubMed, CINAHL Ultimate, SCOPUS, EMBASE and Web of Science, ensuring extensive coverage of relevant studies. A supplementary search will be performed manually in web-based search engines, including Google and Google Scholar, using the PCC terms to identify relevant references that may not be retrieved through database search. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the search, both backward (referring to the bibliography of a ‘seed’ article) and forward (referring to citations of a ‘seed’ article) citation tracking will be adopted for relevant primary and secondary research (eg, reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses and meta-synthesis) articles through the Citation Chaser tool as per JBI recommendation.40 46 However, the secondary research articles will not be directly considered for data synthesis, as they may result in duplication of the evidence and limit access to intervention-level information.

In addition to published literature, online registries such as PROSPERO, OSF and official platforms, including the WHO International Clinical Trial Registry Portal (WHO ICTRP), will be explored to identify ongoing studies.47–49 Searching the WHO ICTRP serves as a centralised platform that aggregates clinical trial records from multiple primary registries from different countries worldwide. This provides a comprehensive source of global clinical trial information through a single search interface, facilitating efficient access to information on relevant clinical trials and helping to minimise potential bias during the search process. Although registered protocols and ongoing clinical trials will not be included in the data synthesis of this scoping review, as they do not represent empirical findings, searching scientific registries may provide valuable insights into current research direction and emerging trends in the field of pharmacy education and the evidence synthesis domain. The search strings will be tailored according to the databases and directed by the PCC framework to increase the retrieval of relevant results. Established controlled vocabulary thesauri, such as MeSH (PubMed, Scopus), Emtree (Embase), CINAHL Subject Headings (CINAHL Complete), along with natural language keywords, authors’ keywords and keywords plus (Web of Science), will be used to identify synonyms of the key search terms. Free-text search will be adopted for the terms that are not found in the controlled thesaurus, using specific fields including title, abstract and keywords search filters. Boolean operators ‘OR’, ‘AND’ and ‘NOT’ will be used to combine the terms, resulting in either broadening or narrowing of the search and finally resulting in search query development. Truncation search will be employed in case of altered spellings and terms with multiple endings as per the requirement. Terms such as ‘education’ OR ‘training’ OR ‘teaching’ will be used to represent the content, ‘evidence synthesis skills’, ‘evidence synthesis techniques’, ‘research question development’ OR ‘literature searching’ OR ‘critical appraisal’ OR ‘report writing’ OR ‘medical writing’ OR ‘scientific writing’ OR ‘academic writing’ will be employed to address the context and ‘pharmacy students’ to represent the population in this review. A preliminary search will be performed among selected databases, and the search strategy will be tested against 5–10 highly relevant articles identified through a hand-searching method.50 This will ensure that relevant articles are captured according to pre-defined study criteria (table 2). The Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) 2015 checklist will also be employed to further evaluate and ensure that the developed search strategy significantly reflects the research question.51 A precise and complete search strategy adopted across the databases has been provided in online supplemental file 2.

Stage 3: selecting relevant studies

All the references obtained from the selected databases will be imported into Covidence Software and subjected to an automatic deduplication process to eliminate any duplicate records to ensure consideration of a unique data set for the screening process.52 Article selection will be as per the study criteria (table 2). Studies that meet the inclusion criteria will be retained, and the remaining will be excluded with a clearly documented reason. Data screening will be conducted by two independent reviewers (KH, VSS) who will be provided with brief instructions on the study criteria before initiating the process. Any changes made regarding the number of reviewers involved in the data screening will be clearly documented and justified.40

A two-step manual approach will be adopted for data screening. First, studies will be screened based on the title and abstract of the article (first-pass screening). Second, full-text screening will be performed (second-pass screening), where the reviewers thoroughly go through the complete text of the article to decide on study selection. Only articles with a decision of included/maybe included/unclear in the first-pass screening will be considered for full-text screening. In cases where an article is published in a language other than English, the English-language abstract, if available, will be reviewed to assess its relevance. If the abstract seems relevant and aligns with the inclusion criteria, the complete text will be translated using Microsoft Translator. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the translation process, a pilot translation of the title and abstract will first be conducted and compared with the available English version of the abstract.

For the article without an available English-language abstract, key sections, including the title, objectives and conclusions, will initially be translated and assessed for relevance against the eligibility criteria. Based on this assessment, a decision will be made regarding the inclusion or exclusion of the study. If the article is found potentially eligible, the complete text will be translated. The accuracy and reliability will be assessed based on a triangulating translation approach, where similar textual information will be translated using two or more translation tools and compared for their similarity.53 Following confirmation of translation accuracy, the translated article will be considered for full-text screening.54

Inter-rater reliability will be assessed after completion of the first pass screening by calculating Cohen’s Kappa Coefficient (k) using the formula κ=(P₀−Pₑ)/(1−Pₑ), where the actual observed proportion of agreement (P₀) among the raters will be compared with the expected proportion of agreement (Pₑ). The results will be interpreted and reported as per the criteria recommended by McHugh ML.55 A third reviewer (GT) will be consulted to resolve any further disagreements, ensuring transparency and an unbiased data screening process. A PRISMA flowchart for scoping reviews will be provided, illustrating the total number of records explored, records considered for screening, extraction and final analysis.40

Stage 4: extracting the data

This study uses a standard data extraction template provided by JBI for scoping reviews, which will be further tailored to better suit the context of this review.56 The extraction sheet will be prepared in Microsoft Excel sheet by KH and subsequently reviewed and validated by GT and SKS. Two reviewers (KH and RR) will independently extract data from the included studies. Extracted information will include publication year, country, study design and sample demographics, along with information on training programme, components of evidence synthesis, delivery method, duration, assessment timepoint and factors influencing the effectiveness and feasibility of the implemented educational programme. Furthermore, information on methods of outcome assessment will be extracted to illustrate enhancements in knowledge, skills and competency.

Before the commencement of data extraction, the reviewers (KH, RR) will undergo training to ensure consistency in the application of the extraction criteria. A pilot data extraction will be performed for a small set of included articles to assess the subsequent reliability between the reviewers. Inter-rater agreement for the pilot extraction will be evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa statistics, with a coefficient (k) value >0.70 considered representative of a strong level of agreement.55 56 Any disagreements identified during the pilot phase will be resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer (GT), and necessary modifications will be made to the extraction sheet before proceeding with the full data extraction process.

Once the pilot extraction phase is completed, KH and RR will independently extract the data in parallel with periodic evaluation by supervisory individuals (GT, SKS) to maintain accuracy. The process will strictly adhere to the pre-defined extraction criteria to maintain uniformity, transparency and replicability. The two data extraction sheets will be compared, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussions and consultation with a third reviewer (GT) if necessary.

Stage 5: compiling, summarising and reporting the outcomes

The data obtained through the extraction process will be compiled, summarised and reported narratively along with basic numerical statistics. The obtained data regarding educational approaches will be compiled and mapped as per the predefined evidence synthesis skill components, such as research question development, literature searching, study selection, critical appraisal (risk of bias assessment), data analysis and interpretation of results and final report writing. Additionally, information on the mode of delivery (online, offline or blended), teaching approach (lecture-based and/or practice-based), duration of training programme (short-term, moderate and long-term), categories of outcome measures documented (self-reported, observer-reported or performance-based) and feasibility indicators will be charted. All the quantitative evidence from the included studies will be summarised using basic numerical statistics, where the data will be presented in terms of frequency counts, percentages, ranges, mean, median or SD as appropriate to the nature of the obtained data. The qualitative evidence identified relevant to the research question representing student experiences (eg, perception, attitude, confidence, motivation), challenges faced during the implementation, facilitators promoting successful implementation of the educational approaches and reported recommendations to be incorporated in the future educational approaches related to evidence synthesis will be mapped, summarised and narratively described in terms of patterns, concepts and unexplored gaps reported in the literature. The summarised results will be represented in the form of text, graphs, figures or tables to enhance clarity and easy interpretation of the outcomes. The existing literature gaps will be explored and discussed in the final report.

Discussion

The proposed scoping review will explore various educational strategies, approaches or teaching techniques adopted to develop and evaluate the evidence synthesis skills among pharmacy students. Reflecting on the wider applicability and importance of evidence synthesis skills in various domains, including healthcare, academia, research, industry and the regulatory system, multiple educational interventions have been implemented focusing on diverse healthcare populations.22 57 58 Designing and executing such skill-based training programmes alongside the conventional academic curriculum, delivered via independent workshops, practical sessions or short courses, significantly promotes domain-specific professional competencies among students, thereby bridging the gap between individual capabilities and organisational expectations in terms of both job readiness and theoretical grounding.59 60 This is mainly because such programmes are purposefully structured to align with rapidly evolving industrial demands, whereas embedding them directly within the formal academic curriculum could be extremely challenging due to the rigid and bureaucratic nature of curriculum refinement processes.61 Nevertheless, these skill-based programmes must extend their focus beyond immediate short-term skill enhancement towards ensuring persistent retention and sustainability of acquired knowledge and skills so that students can effectively translate the knowledge and skills gained in real-world professional contexts.62

The efficiency of educational interventions is considerably influenced by various factors, including duration, format, mode and content being delivered, particularly for specialised topics, such as systematic literature search, critical appraisal of evidence and scientific writing. These programmes are often provided as part of extended learning via short-term courses with a duration ranging from a few hours to days.58 63 64 This is mainly due to the flexible nature and reduced resource mandate for short-term courses.65 66 However, studies have also reported significant drawbacks associated with such training programmes, along with non-longitudinal training models, resulting in challenges in estimating long-term skill retention, either due to the accelerated nature of the programmes or lack of follow-up assessments within the same population.65 This creates a need for specialised educational approaches to significantly overcome constrained time limits to deliver the concepts and provide scope for both knowledge and practical skills development to meet organisational expectations.22 58 63 64

A cross-sectional survey conducted in the year 2024 by Ratajczak P and his team among pharmacy professionals, including pharmacists, pharmacy technicians and pharmacy students undergoing professional training through internships in Poland, revealed that only one-third of the total population (n=313) surveyed had been through formal training to identify literature evidence and translate it to practice. Furthermore, the study also concluded that there is a need for training programmes to promote translation of evidence to practice.12 Additionally, Chandran et al reported that, despite demonstrated positive attitude towards application of evidence to practice among pharmacy students and practitioners, inadequate skills in evidence synthesis resulted in nearly 70% reporting a lack of confidence in applying evidence in patient care.6 To conclude, delivering the concepts of evidence synthesis in simple techniques could significantly contribute towards better EBP, especially among pharmacy students.6 67

Hence, this scoping review attempts to facilitate understanding of limitations within the current practices, along with potential challenges and facilitators involved, to establish a robust skill-based education system. The study also provides insights for improving methods to develop various skills and evaluate their long-term sustainability. Valuable information will be provided for key stakeholders, including regulatory authorities and policymakers involved in curriculum development, highlighting the need for strengthening existing educational frameworks, promoting effective translation of evidence to practice and meeting stakeholders’ needs. Additionally, the gaps identified might serve as a foundation to guide future skill improvement programmes addressing the limitations identified in this scoping review. In line with JBI recommendations for scoping reviews, the included studies were not subjected to critical appraisal, given that the review’s purpose is to map and explore available evidence rather than assess methodological quality.40

Despite these contributions, the study has certain limitations. First, the study excluded non-empirical evidence, such as expert recommendations, educational frameworks and methodological studies related to the evidence synthesis domain. Second, the study did not consider guidelines, policies or reports published by government or non-government organisations.

Ethics and dissemination

The following scoping review does not require ethical approval as no human/animal data, biological samples, chemical or hazardous procedures will be collected and considered for analysis. The outcomes of this review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and will be presented at a national/international conference or seminar.
