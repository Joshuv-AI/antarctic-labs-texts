Negations are powerful even in small depth
Bruno Cavalar, Théo Borém Fabris, Partha Mukhopadhyay, Srikanth Srinivasan, Amir Yehudayoff

Abstract
We study the power of negation in the Boolean and algebraic settings and show the following results. 1. We construct a family of polynomials P n P_{n} in n n variables, all of whose monomials have positive coefficients, such that P n P_{n} can be computed by a depth three circuit of polynomial size but any monotone circuit computing it has size 2 Ω ⁡ ( n ) 2^{\Omega(n)} . This is the strongest possible separation result between monotone and non-monotone arithmetic computations and improves upon all earlier results, including the seminal work of Valiant (1980) and more recently by Chattopadhyay, Datta, and Mukhopadhyay (2021). We then boot-strap this result to prove strong monotone separations for polynomials of constant degree, which solves an open problem from the survey of Shpilka and Yehudayoff (2010). 2. By moving to the Boolean setting, we can prove superpolynomial monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds for specific Boolean functions, which imply that all the powers of certain monotone polynomials cannot be computed by polynomially sized monotone arithmetic circuits. This leads to a new kind of monotone vs.​ non-monotone separation in the arithmetic setting. 3. We then define a collection of problems with linear-algebraic nature, which are similar to span programs, and prove monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds for them. In particular, this gives the strongest known monotone lower bounds for functions in uniform (non-monotone) NC 2 \NC^{2} . Our construction also leads to an explicit matroid that defines a monotone function that is difficult to compute, which solves an open problem by Jukna and Seiwert (2020) in the context of the relative powers of greedy and pure dynamic programming algorithms. Our monotone arithmetic and Boolean circuit lower bounds are based on known techniques, such as reduction from monotone arithmetic complexity to multipartition communication complexity and the approximation method for proving lower bounds for monotone Boolean circuits, but we overcome several new challenges in order to obtain efficient upper bounds using low-depth circuits.

Monotone Boolean lower bounds.

We now discuss the monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds in this paper. Recall from above that there is a fixed matrix
r\times n

M
and we want to prove a lower bound for the Boolean function
f_{M}:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
which decides if a given subset of the columns of
M
is full-rank or not.

To prove such a lower bound, we turn to the approximation technique of
Razborov
[
58
]
which has seen many refinements over the
years
[
4
,
59
,
16
,
14
]
.
Based on these works,
we abstract
out the core of the
approximation method as applied to monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds
into a general and clean statement (Theorem
3.3
) below.
Informally, the statement says that to prove a monotone circuit lower bound
for a Boolean function
f
, it suffices to devise two distributions

\mathcal{D}_{0}
and
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, supported on the
0
s and
1
s of
f

respectively, that satisfy two conditions:

•

(Spreadness of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
): The probability that any
w
variables are simultaneously set to
1
under
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is at most
q^{-w}
(for as large a
q
and
w
as possible).

•

(Robust Sunflowers for
\mathcal{D}_{0}
): Any
w
-DNF of size

s^{k}
contains a subset that is a
robust sunflower
with
respect to
\mathcal{D}_{0}
. Informally, this is a subset of the
terms that can be
replaced by a single term
without changing the DNF much on inputs
from
\mathcal{D}_{0}.

The lower bound obtained from these two conditions is roughly
(q/s)^{w}.
At a high-level, the spreadness of
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
says that it is supported on strings of low weight, and the sunflower property of
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
says that it is supported on strings of high weight.
By the monotonicity of
f
,
these weight are “opposite of what they should be”; the 1s of
f
should be higher than the 0s of
f
.

To use this technique for
f_{M},
we need to define the distributions which in turn depend on the choice of
M.
The cleanest example is that of a random
r\times n
matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
where
r\approx n^{\varepsilon}
. Standard calculations show that such a matrix satisfies two linear algebraic properties:

•

P1: Most subsets of
r
columns are linearly independent.

•

P0:
All
subsets of columns of cardinality
r^{\prime}=\tilde{\Omega}(r)
are linearly independent.

P1 allows us to easily obtain a spread
\mathcal{D}_{1}
with as low a Hamming weight as possible: simply choose a random subset of size
r
and set these bits to
1
. This is a spread distribution with spreadness parameter
q\approx(n/r).

Choosing the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{0}
is slightly trickier: To sample a large number of columns that are linearly
dependent
, we choose a random vector
u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
and pick all the columns that are orthogonal to
u.
This distribution has
uniform
marginals (and thus relatively high Hamming weight) and is also
r^{\prime}
-wise independent by property P0 above. This last property allows us to prove the robust sunflower property for
\mathcal{D}_{0}
by replacing
\mathcal{D}_{0}
by the uniform distribution, since low-width
w\approx\sqrt{r^{\prime}}
DNFs cannot distinguish between the two
[
9
,
65
]
except with error
\exp(-\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{r^{\prime}}))
. Recent bounds on robust sunflower lemmas with respect to product distributions
[
5
,
11
,
54
]
can now be used to argue the desired property with the parameter
s\approx\sqrt{r^{\prime}}
.
Optimizing the parameters leads to choosing
r
as
r\approx n^{2/3}

which leads to a lower bound of
\approx\exp(n^{1/3}).

Interestingly, this is the first
time Bazzi’s theorem is employed
to show monotone circuit lower bounds.

We note that Bazzi’s theorem is the only overhead in our lower bound,
otherwise we could have matched the best known monotone circut lower
bound for an
\mathbf{NP}
function (
\approx\exp(n^{1/2})

[
16
]
).
Nonetheless, our reduction to the robust sunflower lemma via Bazzi
is more efficient than the reduction from matching-sunflowers
via “blocky” families of

]

which gives us a lower bound of

for an

(theirs being

On the other hand,
their upper bound is better (the function is in

which makes the results arguably incomparable.

To obtain explicit matrices with the above properties,
we use constructions of binary linear codes with sufficiently high distances and dual distances.
By translating properties P0 and P1 into the coding theory language, we can obtain P0 by proving that the dual distance of the code is at least
r^{\prime}
, and P1 by proving that its distance is sufficiently large.

Finally, to prove the separation between the arithmetic and Boolean models, we need to argue the above lower bound for a
real
matrix
M
. A natural strategy is to repeat the above argument, say, for random matrices where each column is an independent sign vector
v\in\{-1,1\}^{r}
. However this leads to a problem with defining the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{0}
as above, since the point thus sampled is unlikely to have a high Hamming weight
(by standard anti-concentration arguments
[
44
,
26
]
, the chance that two random sign vectors are orthogonal is small), and thus
\mathcal{D}_{0}
has marginals that are small.

We need to have many real vectors so that many of their subsets are linearly dependent.
How to achieve this?

The main idea is to choose a sparse matrix.
We sample each column of
M
to be a random Boolean vector of low Hamming weight. This now gives a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{0}
that has higher marginals and satisfy a weak form of bounded independence. Unfortunately, we are unable to apply this weak form in conjunction with the strongest robust sunflower lemmas.
We are, however, able to use it along with the classical sunflower lemma
[
25
]
and thus prove a superpolynomial lower bound
à la
Razborov
[
58
]
(instead of an exponential lower bound).

Hamming balls and subspaces.

On the way to establishing the spreadness property for
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, we prove a technical result about intersection patterns of subspaces and the Boolean cube, which we believe is independently interesting. Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field and

be a subspace of

of dimension

(see e.g.

We prove that the number of points of the Boolean cube and Hamming weight at most

These bounds are all easily seen to be tight for a vector space

generated by

standard basis vectors.

(any other vector can be chosen by a proper change of basis).
There is a linear-size monotone span program computing the functions from
[
30
,
14
]
.
Using standard tricks, it is not hard to compute
f_{M}
with a monotone span
program as well.

Interestingly, the function
f_{M}
can also be computed by a seemingly simpler
monotone model which we
name

monotone rank programs
.
Just like a monotone span program, a monotone rank program is a matrix

A
with a labelling of the rows.
The difference is that a monotone rank program accepts an input
x
iff
A_{x}
is
full-rank.
Such models have been studied in the context of linear secret sharing
schemes
[
50
]

and have been called “non-redundant” as there are no dependencies
in
A_{x}
for any minimally accepting
x
.

A related model called
monotone dependency programs

accepts
x
iff
A_{x}
is linearly dependent.
This was shown to be exponentially weaker than monotone span programs
in
[
53
]
.
It remains an open problem to determine whether monotone rank programs
are weaker
than monotone span programs.

Monotone complexity of matching over planar graphs.

There are two parts for proving a separation in computational complexity. We have two computational classes
A
and
B
,
and we need to come up with a problem
P
that belongs to
B
but does not belongs to
A
.
In our context,
A
is a monotone class and
B
is a non-monotone class.
We have used techniques from communication complexity
[
57
,
41
]
and approximation methods
[
58
]
to prove lower bounds against
A
.
To prove that
P
belongs to
B
, we have used either the power of depth-3 circuits with negations, or the power of linear algebra.

There is also a different path for proving separations. In his
work
[
68
]
, Valiant used the fact the number of perfect
matchings
in a planar graphs is given by the Pfaffian
[
43
]
so we can
reduce it to computing the determinant polynomial.
Again, the upper bound comes from linear algebra.
Valiant proved that (for some planar graphs) the Pfaffian can be computed
by a non-monotone arithmetic circuit of polynomial size; more
precisely, by an algebraic branching program of polynomial size. But we
are interested in separating Boolean monotone circuits and arithmetic
non-monotone circuits. We know that the upper bound holds, but we do not
know if deciding the perfect matching problem is hard for planar graphs for
monotone Boolean circuits.

Constant-depth separations.

Theorem

1.1
shows a polynomial computable by depth-3 arithmetic
circuits
requiring maximal monotone arithmetic complexity, improving previous
separations of this kind
[
19
]
.
In the Boolean setting,
a line of
works
[
1
,
52
,
55
,
31
,
8
,
21
,
30
,
15
,
14
]

has either directly or indirectly
studied the relative power of constant-depth and monotone circuits,
recently
discovering
a monotone function
computable by constant-depth circuits
which requires
superpolynomial size monotone circuits
[
14
]
.
It remains an open question whether there exists a monotone function
computable by some constant-depth circuit model
(in fact, even
O(\log n)
-depth)
requiring exponential-size monotone circuits.
Is there a matrix
M
such that
f_{M}
can exhibit this separation?

This type of question is also interesting from the point of view algebraic
separations.
Is there a polynomial
P
computable by constant-depth or even
logarithmic-depth arithmetic circuits and such that
f_{P}

requires superpolynomial or even exponential size monotone Boolean circuits?
This looks plausible in light of several nontrivial algorithms
which have recently been implemented with constant-depth arithmetic
circuits
[
6
]
.

Other questions.

There are also more specific questions about the techniques we used to obtain our results:

•

For all

For every

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left(\left(\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}a_{u}a_{v}\right)^{2}-2\left(\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}a_{u}a_{v}\right)+1\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i,(a_{(i-1)n/k+1},\dotsc,a_{in/k})}

\displaystyle=Q_{2}-2Q_{1}+Q_{0}.

First,

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n/k}}x_{i,a}\right).

Second,

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{u,v\}\in E(G)}\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n}}a_{u}a_{v}\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i,(a_{(i-1)n/k+1},\dotsc,a_{in/k})}

\displaystyle Q_{1,uv}

\displaystyle Q_{1,uv}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n/k}}a_{j_{u}}a_{j_{v}}x_{i_{u},a}\right)\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]\setminus\{i_{u}\}}\left(\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n/k}}x_{i,a}\right).

Third,

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{u,v\},\{r,s\}\in E(G)}Q_{2,uv,rs}

2.2
Monotone arithmetic complexity and communication complexity

Our monotone arithmetic circuit lower bound in based on communication complexity. This connection was utilized in several works
[
56
,
40
,
63
]
.
We start by defining the
multipartition rectangle number
of a Boolean function and prove (for completeness) how it is related to the monotone arithmetic complexity of a polynomial.

Let
X
be a finite set. We say that
R\subseteq\mathcal{P}(X)
is a
rectangle

if there is a partition
\{Y,Z\}
of
X
such that

R=\{\,S\cup T\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}S\in\mathcal{Y},T\in\mathcal{Z}\}

for some
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(Y)
and
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(Z)
.
We say that a rectangle is
balanced
if
|Y|,|Z|\in[1/3,2/3)\cdot|X|
.

For every
a\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, let

\supp(a)\vcentcolon=\{\,i\in[n]\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}a_{i}=1\}

and for
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
, let

\supp(S)\vcentcolon=\{\,\supp(a)\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}a\in S\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([n]).

The
multipartition rectangle number

\mpcC(f)
of
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is the minimum number
r
such that there is a family
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in[r]}
of balanced
rectangles (with respect to
[n]
) such that

\supp(f^{-1}(1))=\bigcup_{i\in[r]}R_{i}.

The connection between arithmetic complexity and rectangle numbers is established as follows.
Let
X\vcentcolon=\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m}\}
and let
\mathcal{X}\vcentcolon=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
be a partition of
X
to
n
parts.
We say that a polynomial
P
is
\mathcal{X}
-set-multilinear
if every monomial of
P
has exactly one element of
X_{i}
for every
i\in[n]
.
We say that a pair
(g,h)
of polynomials is a
monotone pair
(with respect to
\mathcal{X}
)
if there is a partition
\{Y,Z\}
of
X
such that
\mathcal{X}
is a refinement of
\{Y,Z\}

and
g\in\mathbb{R}[Y]
and
h\in\mathbb{R}[Z]

and
g
and
h
are monotone polynomials.
We say that a nonnegative pair
(g,h)
is
balanced
if
|\mathcal{X}_{Y}|,|\mathcal{X}_{Z}|\in[1/3,2/3)\cdot|\mathcal{X}|

where for every
W\subseteq X
,

\mathcal{X}_{W}\vcentcolon=\{\,B\in\mathcal{X}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}B\subseteq W\}.

In words, the number of parts of
\mathcal{X}
in each of
Y,Z
is balanced.

The following structural lemma
was proved in
[
56
]
for general (not necessarily set-multilinear) monotone circuits and
in
[
70
]
for ordered polynomials. The proof for the set-multilinear case below is basically the same.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
P
is a monotone
\mathcal{X}
-set-multilinear polynomial
computed by a monotone circuit of size
s

then there is a family of balanced monotone pairs
\{(g_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in[s]}
such that

P=\sum_{i\in[s]}g_{i}h_{i},

and each product
g_{i}h_{i}
only contains monomials from
P
.

Proof sketch.

In a nutshell, the proof uses that monotone circuits for set-multilinear polynomials are syntactically set-multilinear, and the lemma follows by induction on the number of edges
by locating a single product gate that computes a balanced pair.
∎

Now,
let
X
be the set of
2n
variables
X\vcentcolon=\{x_{1,0},x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{n,0},x_{n,1}\}

and
\mathcal{X}\vcentcolon=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
be its partition with
X_{i}\vcentcolon=\{x_{i,0},x_{i,1}\}
.
For any
\mathcal{X}
-set-multilinear monomial
m\vcentcolon=\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,a_{i}}
, let

\Assignment(m)\vcentcolon=\{\,i\in[n]\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}a_{i}=1\}.

For a
\mathcal{X}
-set-multilinear polynomial
P
, let

\Assignment(P)\vcentcolon=\{\,\Assignment(m)\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m\in\supp(P)\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([n]).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and
let
P
be a monotone
\mathcal{X}
-set-multilinear polynomial.
If
\supp(f^{-1}(1))=\Assignment(P)
, then

\monoC(P)\geq\mpcC(f).

Proof.

Let
C
be a monotone circuit of size
s
computing
P
.
By Lemma
2.3
, we know that there is a family
\{(g_{i},h_{i})\}_{i\in[s]}
of balanced monotone pairs such that

P=\sum_{i\in[s]}g_{i}h_{i}.

This decomposition of
P
implies the following decomposition of its support

\supp(P)=\bigcup_{i\in[s]}\{\,m_{1}m_{2}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m_{1}\in\supp(g_{i}),m_{2}\in\supp(h_{i})\}.

Thus, we get the following decomposition

\Assignment(P)=\bigcup_{i\in[s]}\{\,\Assignment(m_{1})\cup\Assignment(m_{2})\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m_{1}\in\supp(g_{i}),m_{2}\in\supp(h_{i})\},

which, by hypothesis, implies

\supp(f^{-1}(1))=\bigcup_{i\in[s]}\{\,\Assignment(m_{1})\cup\Assignment(m_{2})\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m_{1}\in\supp(g_{i}),m_{2}\in\supp(h_{i})\}.

The final observation is that for any balanced monotone pair
(g,h)
,
the set

R\vcentcolon=\{\,\Assignment(m_{1})\cup\Assignment(m_{2})\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}m_{1}\in\supp(g),m_{2}\in\supp(h)\}

is a balanced rectangle of
[n]
. Together with the decomposition above, this concludes the proof of the lemma.

∎

2.3
A multipartition communication complexity lower bound

In this section, we prove the multipartition communication complexity lower bounds, which in turn implies the monotone circuit lower bound.
The problem we consider is defined over a graph
G
with vertex-set
[n]
.
For a subset
U
of the vertices of
G
,
we denote by
G[U]
the induced graph of
G
on
U
.
Denote by
f_{G}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
the function defined by

f_{G}(a)=1\Longleftrightarrow e(G[\supp(a)])\neq 1.

Note that the support of the polynomial
P_{G}
is the same as
f_{G}^{-1}(1)
.

Our lower bounds hold as long as
G
is an
expander graph
, since this is required by Lemma 9 from
[
63
]
, which we restated as Lemma
2.7
.
For concreteness, the definition of expander graph used by
[
63
]
is that
G
is a
d
-regular graph with the second largest eigenvalue of its adjacency matrix at most
d^{0.75}
.
Our main lemma is the following.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
G
is an expander graph with vertex-set
[n]
, then

\mpcC(f_{G})\geq 2^{\Omega(n)}.

The main ingredient in the lower bound is the following corruption-like lemma.
It follows from a communication complexity perspective of a result by Kaibel and Weltge
[
41
]
; see Lemma 5.10 in Roughgarden’s lecture notes
[
60
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
2\ell
-variate

\mathrm{DISJ}^{\neq 1}_{\ell}
as

\mathrm{DISJ}^{\neq 1}_{\ell}(b_{1}\ldots,b_{\ell},c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell})=1\Leftrightarrow|\supp((b_{1},\dotsc,b_{\ell}))\cap\supp((c_{1},\dotsc,c_{\ell}))|\neq 1.

Let

and

.
For every
i\in[\ell]
, choose independently and uniformly at random an
element
(b_{i},b_{\ell+i})
of the set
\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}
,
and let

be the corresponding random assignment over
2\ell
-variables.
If
R
is a rectangle of
[2\ell]
with partition

\{\{1,\dotsc,\ell\},\{\ell+1,\dotsc,2\ell\}\}
such that

\Pr_{b}[b\in R]<2^{-\ell/2}.

Proof.

As the proof of Lemma 5.10 in the notes
[
60
]
works for any unique-disjointness 1-rectangle, this lemma follows from the fact that any different-from-one disjointness 1-rectangle is also a unique-disjointness 1-rectangle.
∎

Next, we need to define a “hard distribution” on
f_{G}^{-1}(1)
such that the measure of all balanced
1

f_{G}^{-1}(1)
of is small. While the function
f_{G}
bears some similarity to the different-from-1 disjointness function, this does not immediately follow from the lemma above, because we do not a priori know the partition we need to deal with.

Consider the following algorithm, as defined in
[
63
]
,
to sample a random matching from
G
:
for
m\in[n]
given as input,

1.

Set
M\leftarrow\varnothing
.

2.

For
i=1,2,\ldots,m
, do the following:

(a)

Remove all vertices from
G
that are at distance at most
2
from any vertex in
M
. Let
G_{i}
be the resulting graph.

(b)

Choose a uniformly random edge
e_{i}
from
E(G_{i})
, and add it to
M
.

3.

Output
M
.

Now given a matching
M
, we use the following procedure to define a random input

to

:

1.

For each

.

2.

For each

element
(a_{u},a_{v})
from the

.

Note that

by our choice of

.
Hence, Lemma
2.5
immediately follows from the claim that for every balanced rectangle

\Pr_{a}[\supp(a)\in R]\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

So it remains to prove this inequality.

The useful properties of the random input

rely on the following properties of the random matching

,
which were stated in Lemma 9 from
[

]
.

Lemma 2.7

If

is an expander with vertex-set

,

for

By Lemma

\{\,u_{i}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}i\in S\}\cup\{\,v_{i-s}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}i\in T\}\in R\subseteq\supp(f^{-1}(1)),

which
implies that

, where we have identified

and
T
with their corresponding indicator vectors.

Thus,

is a rectangle satisfying the hypothesis of Lemma

, so we can apply this lemma to obtain that

\Pr_{a}[b\in R_{E}]\leq 2^{-\Omega(s)}=2^{-\Omega(n)},

which implies that

\displaystyle\Pr_{M,a}[a\in R\ |\ M\cap E_{G}(A,B)=E]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{M,a}[b\in R_{E}\ |\ M\cap E_{G}(A,B)=E]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{a}[b\in R_{E}]\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\Pr_{M,a}[a\in R]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{M,a}[a\in R,|M\cap E_{G}(A,B)|<s^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\;+\sum_{E\subseteq E(G):|E|\geq s^{\prime}}\Pr_{M,a}[a\in R\ |\ M\cap E_{G}(A,B)=E]\cdot\Pr_{M}[M\cap E_{G}(A,B)=E]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\Omega(n)}.

Monotone arithmetic circuit lower bounds

Let us now prove our monotone arithmetic circuit lower bounds.

(Theorem

)
.

If

thus, by Lemmas

and

∎

Corollary 2.9

(Theorem

)
.

Let

and

.

3
A criterion for monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds

The goal here is to describe a criterion for proving monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds for a function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
.
The criterion is based on the existence of distributions

{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying some properties.
We start with two important definitions.

Definition 3.1

(Sunflower)
.

For
S\subseteq[n]
,
let
t_{S}
denote the function

t_{S}(x)\coloneqq\bigwedge_{i\in S}x_{i}

where by convention
t_{\varnothing}\equiv 1
.

We say that a family of sets
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({[n]})

is a
({\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon)
-sunflower

if
\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\rvert\geq 2

and

\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\exists S\in{\mathcal{S}}:t_{S\setminus K}(x)=1\right]>1-\varepsilon,

(4)

where
K\vcentcolon=\bigcap_{S\in{\mathcal{S}}}S
.
The family
{\mathcal{S}}
is called
\ell
-uniform if
|S|=\ell
for every
S\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Let

r({\mathcal{D}},\ell,\varepsilon)
be the minimum integer
r
such that
every
\ell
-uniform family of sets of size at least
r^{\ell}

contains a
({\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon)
-sunflower (if no such
r
exists
then it is
\infty
).

Definition 3.2

(Spread)
.

For an integer
t
and
q>0
,
a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}

is
t
-wise
q
-spread
if,
for every
A\subseteq[n]
such that
\lvert A\rvert\leq t
,

\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[t_{A}(x)=1]\leq q^{-\lvert A\rvert}.

We show that a monotone circuit lower bound for a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}

follows immediately from a spreadness bound on a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
of
accepting inputs,
and a sunflower bound on a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
of rejecting inputs.

Theorem 3.3

(A lower bound criterion)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a monotone function.
Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in(0,1)
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{0},{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
be distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that

{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
is
t
-wise
q
-spread, and let

\alpha\vcentcolon=\min\left\{\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[f(x)=0],\;\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[f(x)=1]\right\}.

Let
w\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w\leq t/2
, and
define

r_{w}\vcentcolon=\max_{\ell\in[2w]}r({\mathcal{D}}_{0},\ell,\alpha n^{-3w}).

If
8r_{w}\leq q\leq r_{w}n
,
then any Boolean monotone circuit computing
f
has size at least

\left(\frac{c\alpha q}{r_{w}}\right)^{w}

where
c
is a universal positive constant.

We prove Theorem
3.3
using the approximation method of

[

]
,
generalising the “tailored sunflower” approach
of recent works
[

,
13
,
14
]

which began with
[

]
.
Certain sunflower criterions have previously appeared

[

,
13
]
.
Our presentation of the method is similar to
[

]
,
with the difference that we consider arbitrary distributions.
The following is the main lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a monotone function.
Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in(0,1)
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{0},{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
be distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that

{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
is
t
-wise
q
-spread and

\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{i})\subseteq f^{-1}(i)
for every
i\in\{0,1\}
.
Let
w\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w\leq t/2
, and
define

r_{w}\vcentcolon=\max_{\ell\in[2w]}r({\mathcal{D}}_{0},\ell,n^{-3w}).

If
8r_{w}\leq q\leq r_{w}n
,
then any Boolean monotone circuit computing
f
has size at least

\left(\frac{cq}{r_{w}}\right)^{w}

where
c
is a universal positive constant.

Before proving our main lemma, let us use it to prove Theorem
3.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

For every
b\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}}[f(x)=b]\geq\alpha>0
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{*}
be the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{b}

conditioned on the event

.
First, the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{*}
is
t
-wise
(\alpha q)
-spread because for every

\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{*}}\left[t_{A}(x)=1\right]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}\left[t_{A}(x)=1\right]\leq(\alpha q)^{-\lvert A\rvert}.

Second, every
({\mathcal{D}}_{0},\alpha\varepsilon)
-sunflower

is a

({\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{*},\varepsilon)
-sunflower because

\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{*}}\left[\forall S\in{\mathcal{S}}\;\;t_{S\setminus K}(x)=0\right]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\Pr_{x\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}\left[\forall S\in{\mathcal{S}}\;\;t_{S\setminus K}(x)=0\right]<\frac{\varepsilon\alpha}{\alpha}=\varepsilon,

.
The result now follows by

.
∎

For the rest of this section, we prove Lemma

.
Let

be as in the assumptions of the lemma.
We denote by
{\mathcal{D}}
the distribution

{\mathcal{D}}\vcentcolon=({\mathcal{D}}_{0}+{\mathcal{D}}_{1})/2.

Our goal is to approximate a given monotone circuit by a monotone DNF formula

for a given family of sets

We say that
F_{{\mathcal{S}}}
is
r
-small

for every

We say that

has

.
Finally, we say that

is a

The approximation method now proceeds in the following two steps.

Claim 3.5

There is a universal constant

(w,r_{w})

For every

Before proving the two claims, we prove the main lemma.

Proof of
Lemma

.

The proof uses the two claims above.
When

r_{w}<\infty
.
Assume (towards a contradiction) that

can be computed by a monotone circuit of size
\leq(cq/2r_{w}))^{w}

As

(w,q/8)

Proof of

.

Let

be a
(w,\delta q)

If

Otherwise, note that

As

Proof of

.

For convenience,

and let

Let

We will construct a

-DNF for

Every input variable is already a

-DNF.
As we naively

In order to maintain smallness of our DNF, we approximate the naive

The subsequent claim

Algorithm 1

\mathsf{p\ell uck}({\mathcal{S}})

1:

4:

Note that Line 2 of the algorithm is always possible by the choice of

Claim 3.7

If

has width

-DNF with

Proof.

Since the core

{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}
and

,
the size of

-small family.
Therefore, as

has width

-DNF.

(5)

(6)

4
Boolean separation

Recall that, given a matrix

over a field

,
the Boolean function

has full rank.
In this section, we only consider
{\mathbb{F}}\vcentcolon={\mathbb{F}}_{2}

in order to

.

4.1
Nonmonotone low-depth circuit upper bounds

Let us first prove the upper bound part of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 4.1

(Upper bound)
.

For any matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
,
the function

is computed by a Boolean circuit of size
\poly(m)
and depth

.

Proof.

On an input

corresponding to the indicator vector of a set

,
we need to compute the rank of the matrix
M[S]
.
This is known to be computed by a Boolean circuit of size
\poly(m)
and depth
O(\log m)^{2}

[
3
,
49
]
.
∎

4.2
Monotone Boolean circuit lower bounds via well-behaved codes

Let us start by defining a special kind of linear binary codes, which are explicitly constructed in Section
4.3
.
We will only use the abstract properties of these codes
to define probability distributions that allow us to apply
Theorem

3.3
and obtain lower bounds for monotone circuits computing functions related to these codes.

Definition 4.2
.

For
M\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
,
we define the
(linear) code
\mathcal{C}_{M}
of
M
as

\mathcal{C}_{M}\vcentcolon=\{\,M^{\top}w\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}w\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m},

and define the
dual code
\mathcal{D}_{M}
of

as

\mathcal{D}_{M}\vcentcolon=\{\,w\in\mathbb{F}^{m}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}Mw=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}.

We say that a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
is a

if there is a rank
n
matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}
, called a
generator matrix
of
\mathcal{C}
, such that

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{G}.

For any linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
,
we say that a rank
m-n
matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}^{(m-n)\times n}
is a
parity check matrix
of
\mathcal{C}
if

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}_{H},

and, in this case,
we define the
dual code
\mathcal{C}^{*}
of
\mathcal{C}
as

\mathcal{C}^{*}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{C}_{H},

which is independent of the choice of a parity check matrix
H
.
We define the
distance
of a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
as

d(\mathcal{C})\vcentcolon=\min_{x\in\mathcal{C},x\neq 0}\lvert\{\,i\in[m]\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x_{i}\neq 0\}\rvert,

and its
dual distance
as
d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\vcentcolon=d(\mathcal{C}^{*})
.
For every
d,t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
we say that
\mathcal{C}
is
(d,t)
-well-behaved
if

d(\mathcal{C})>d
and
d^{*}(\mathcal{C})>t
.

The main theorem of this section is the following.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
m\vcentcolon=m(n)\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
d\vcentcolon=d(n)\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
t\vcentcolon=t(n)\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
be any
(d,t)
-well-behaved linear code with generator matrix

.
If

,
then the monotone complexity of

is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{d}{n\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{t}/(b\log m)},

where

is a sufficiently large positive constant.

In Section
4.3
, we are going to use this theorem with

m\approx n^{3/2}\log n,\text{ and }d\approx m-n,\text{ and }t\approx n.

The rest of this section is devoted to prove this theorem.
Henceforth we assume that
{\mathcal{C}}
and
M
are as in the assumption of the
theorem.
Let us denote by
M_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
the
i
-th column of
M
for every
i\in[m]
.
We first prove a few properties related to
the rank of submatrices of
M
.
Some of them are standard facts from coding theory, but we add the proof here for completeness.

Lemma 4.4

(Theorem 10 from Chapter 1 of
[
46
]
)
.

For every
S\subseteq[m]
with
|S|\leq t
,
the set of columns of
M
indexed by
S
is linearly independent.

Proof.

Let
S
be a subset of
[m]
such that the set of columns of
M
indexed by
S
is linearly dependent.
Then there is a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
such that
\supp(v)\subseteq S
, and

\sum_{i\in[m]}v_{i}M_{i}=0.

Thus,
Mv=0
, and
v\in\mathcal{D}_{M}
.
Note that
\mathcal{D}_{M}=\mathcal{C}^{*}
,
since, for any matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}^{(m-n)\times m}
such that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{D}_{H}
,
we have that

\mathcal{C}^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{H}\text{ and }HM^{\top}=0.

By the definition of the distance of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
and the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is
(d,t)
-well-behaved,
we get that

|S|\geq|\supp(v)|\geq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})>t.\qed

Lemma 4.5
.

For any subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
of dimension
D<n
,
and for
u
being a uniform random column of
M
,
we have

\Pr_{u}\left[u\in V\right]\leq 1-d/m=\vcentcolon\Delta.

Proof.

As
V
has dimension
D\leq n-1
,
there is a nonzero vector
c\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that, for every
v\in V
, we have
v^{\top}c=0
.
Let
C\subseteq[m]
be the set of the columns of
M
that are in
V
, and let

w\vcentcolon=M^{\top}c\in\mathcal{C}_{M}=\mathcal{C}.

Note that, for every
i\in C
, we have
w_{i}=M_{i}^{\top}c=0
.
Thus, by the definition of the distance of
\mathcal{C}
and the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is
(d,t)
-well-behaved, we get that

m-|C|\geq|\supp(w)|\geq d(\mathcal{C})>d.

and, consequently,

\Pr_{u}\left[u\in V\right]\leq\frac{|C|}{m}\leq 1-\frac{d}{m}.\qed

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
N\vcentcolon=N(m)\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\leq m
, and
for a random set
S
distributed uniformly in
\binom{[m]}{N}
,
we have

\Pr_{S}\left[\text{$M[S]$ is not full rank}\right]\leq 2^{n}\Delta^{N}.

Proof.

Let
\linspan(S)\vcentcolon=\linspan(\{\,M_{i}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}i\in S\})
, where
M_{i}
is the column of
M
indexed by
i\in[m]
.
Note that the rank of
M[S]
is at most
n-1
if and only if
there is a subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
of dimension
n-1
such that
\linspan(S)\subseteq V
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\Pr_{S}\left[\text{$M[S]$ is not full rank}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{S}\left[\exists V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\text{ s.t. }\dim(V)=n-1\text{ and }\linspan(S)\subseteq V\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}_{2},\dim(V)=n-1}\Pr_{S}\left[S\subseteq V\right].

By Proposition 1.7.2 in Stanley
[
64
]
, the number of
n-1
dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is

\frac{(2^{n}-1)(2^{n}-2)\cdots(2^{n}-2^{n-2})}{(2^{n-1}-1)(2^{n-1}-2)\cdots(2^{n-1}-2^{n-2})}\leq 2^{n}-1.

For any
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
V
of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, let
C_{V}\subseteq[m]
be the set of columns of
M
that are contained in
V
.
By Lemma
4.5
, we know that
c\vcentcolon=|C_{V}|\leq\Delta m
, which implies that

\displaystyle\Pr_{S}\left[S\subseteq V\right]\leq\Pr_{S}\left[S\subseteq C_{V}\right]=\frac{\binom{c}{N}}{\binom{m}{N}}\leq\left(\frac{c}{m}\right)^{N}\leq\Delta^{N}.

Therefore,

\Pr_{S}\left[\text{$M[S]$ is not full rank}\right]\leq 2^{n}\Delta^{N}.\qed

Now our goal is to apply
Theorem

3.3
to prove a lower bound for
f_{M}
.
So we first define two probability distributions that, intuitively, are hard to distinguish for monotone Boolean circuits of small size.

Definition 4.7
.

We define the
distribution
a\sim D_{1}
by sampling a uniformly
random
a\in\{0,1\}^{m}
of Hamming weight

N\vcentcolon=n\lceil m/d\rceil.

(7)

We define the
distribution
a\sim D_{0}
by sampling a uniformly
random
u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and, for every
j\in[m]
, we set

a_{j}\vcentcolon=1
if
\langle M[j],\mathopen{}u\rangle=0

and

a_{j}\vcentcolon=0
otherwise, where the inner product is taken over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Note that, by the choice of
N
and the assumption
2n\leq d
, we have
N<m
and

1-2^{n}(1-d/m)^{N}\geq 1-2^{n}e^{-Nd/m}\geq 1-2^{n}2^{-n/\ln 2}\geq 1/2.

Thus, by Lemma
4.6
,
we have

\Pr_{a\sim D_{1}}\left[f_{M}(a)=1\right]\geq 1/2=\vcentcolon\alpha.

Moreover,
the point
a
sampled from
D_{0}
is an element of

f_{M}^{-1}(0)
as long as
u\neq 0
,
so

\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[f_{M}(a)=0\right]=1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\geq\alpha.

Spreadness of
D_{1}
.

To apply
Theorem

3.3
, we need to show that
D_{1}

is “spread”.

Lemma 4.8

(Spreadness of
D_{1}
)
.

The distribution
D_{1}
is
N
-wise
(m/N)
-spread.

Proof.

Let
T
be any subset of
[m]
of size
k\leq N
.
The number of
N
-sized sets of columns of
M
which contain the set of columns
indexed by
T
is
\binom{m-k}{N-k}
. Thus,

\Pr_{a\sim D_{1}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in T}a_{i}=1\right]\leq\frac{\binom{m-k}{N-k}}{\binom{m}{N}}.

Simplifying the above, we get

\Pr_{a\sim D_{1}}\left[\bigwedge_{i\in T}a_{i}=1\right]\leq\left(\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(N-i)}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(m-i)}\right)\leq\left(\frac{N}{m}\right)^{k},

using the fact that
\frac{N-i}{m-i}\leq\frac{N}{m}
for
i\leq k-1
.
∎

Sunflower bound for
D_{0}
.

The second step is to show a sunflower bound for
(D_{0},\varepsilon)
-sunflowers.
We first prove that
D_{0}
is
t
-wise independent.

Lemma 4.9

(Independence of
D_{0}
)
.

The distribution
D_{0}
has uniform marginals and is
t
-wise independent.

Proof.

Fix any set
S\vcentcolon=\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{t}\}
of
[m]
. Also fix a vector

b\vcentcolon=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{t})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}.

Then

\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[a\mathord{\restriction}_{S}=b\right]=\Pr_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[(\langle u,\mathopen{}M[j_{1}]\rangle=b_{1})\wedge\ldots\wedge(\langle u,\mathopen{}M[j_{t}]\rangle=b_{t})\right]=\Pr_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[u^{\top}M[S]=b\right].

Using the rank-nullity theorem, the above probability is the same as

\Pr_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[u^{\top}M[S]=b\right]=\frac{|\ker(M[S])|}{2^{n}}=\frac{1}{2^{t}},

where the last equality follows from the fact that the rank of
M[S]
is
t
(Lemma
4.4
).
As the columns of
M
are nonzero (as
t>0
), we can prove that the marginals are uniform: for every
\ell\in[t]
,

\Pr_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\left[\langle u,\mathopen{}M[j_{\ell}]\rangle=0\right]=\frac{1}{2}.\qed

Another important ingredient in our proof is Bazzi’s
theorem
[
9
]
, which has been further improved by work of
Tal
[
65
]
.
This shows that small DNFs have almost the same behaviour on
k
-wise independent
distributions as they have over truly uniform distributions.

Theorem 4.10

(Fooling DNFs with independence: Theorem 7.1
[
65
]
)
.

Let
F
be a DNF with
N
terms and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Then there exists
T\leq O(\log N\cdot\log(N/\varepsilon))
such that,
for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
that is
T
-wise independent with uniform marginals, we have

\left|\Pr_{a\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\left[F(a)=0\right]-\Pr_{a\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[F(a)=0\right]\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Finally, to show our sunflower lemma,
we combine Bazzi’s theorem with the optimal bounds for “robust
sunflowers”
recently obtained
(see, e.g.,
[
54
, Lemma 2]
,
[
11
]
).

Robust sunflowers
are
(\mathsf{Uniform},\varepsilon)
-sunflowers,
where
\mathsf{Uniform}
denotes the uniform distribution.
Recall the definition of “sunflower bound”
r(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
from

definition

3.1
.

Lemma 4.11

(Robust Sunflower Lemma: Lemma 2
[
54
]
)
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon\leq 1/2
,
we have

r(\mathsf{Uniform},k,\varepsilon)=O(\log(k/\varepsilon))
.

We can now show a sunflower lemma for
D_{0}
.
Recall that
m
is the number of input bits of the function
f_{M}
.

Lemma 4.12

(Sunflower lemma for
t
-wise independent distributions)
.

There exists a constant
b\geq 1
,
such that, for

w\vcentcolon=\sqrt{t}/(b\log m)

and every

k\leq 2w
,
we have

r(D_{0},k,m^{-4w})=O(w\log m).

Proof.

Let
w\vcentcolon=\sqrt{t}/(b\log m)
for some constant
b
to be defined
later, and set
\varepsilon\vcentcolon=m^{-4w}
.
We will show

r(D_{0},k,\varepsilon)\leq r(\mathsf{Uniform},k,\varepsilon/2).

Indeed, suppose that
{\mathcal{S}}
is an
\varepsilon/2
-robust sunflower with core

K
.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{K}=\{\,S\setminus K\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}S\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
.
Note that
F_{{\mathcal{S}}_{K}}
has width at most
k
and at most
\binom{m}{\leq k}\leq m^{k}
terms.

We now wish to apply

Theorem

4.10
on
F_{{\mathcal{S}}_{K}}
with the

t
-wise independent distribution
D_{0}
and
approximation parameter
\varepsilon/2
.

Theorem

4.10
can only be applied when

t\geq D\log(\lvert{\mathcal{S}}_{K}\rvert)\log(\lvert{\mathcal{S}}_{K}\rvert/(\varepsilon/2)),

for some constant
D>0
given by
Theorem

4.10
.
Now note that

D\log(\lvert{\mathcal{S}}_{K}\rvert)\log(\lvert{\mathcal{S}}_{K}\rvert/(\varepsilon/2))\leq O(w^{2}(\log m)^{2})=\frac{1}{b^{2}}O(t)\leq t

for large enough
b
.
Thus, we can apply
Theorem

4.10
and obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{x\sim D_{0}}[\exists S\in{\mathcal{S}}:t_{S\setminus K}(x)=1]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{x\sim D_{0}}[F_{{\mathcal{S}}_{K}}(x)=1]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[F_{{\mathcal{S}}_{K}}(x)=1]-\varepsilon/2

\displaystyle=\Pr_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[\exists S\in{\mathcal{S}}:t_{S\setminus K}(x)=1]-\varepsilon/2

\displaystyle>1-\varepsilon.

Thus, the set
{\mathcal{S}}
is a
(D_{0},\varepsilon)
-sunflower.
The result now follows from
Lemma

4.11
by observing that

r(\mathsf{Uniform},k,\varepsilon/2)\leq O(\log(k/\varepsilon))\leq O(w\log m).\qed

Wrapping up.

We can now apply
Theorem

3.3
, finishing the proof.
Note that the distributions
D_{1}
and
D_{0}

defined in
definition

4.7

satisfy the following properties:

1.

We have

\Pr_{x\sim D_{i}}[f_{M}(x)=i]\geq\alpha

for every
i\in\{0,1\}
;

2.

D_{1}
is
N
-wise
(m/N)
-spread (
Lemma

4.8
);

3.

For
w\vcentcolon=\sqrt{t}/(b\log m)

where
b
is a sufficiently large universal constant (Lemma
4.12
),
we have
r(D_{0},k,\alpha m^{-3w})\leq r(D_{0},k,m^{-4w})\leq O(w\log m)

for every
k\leq 2w
and sufficiently large
n
.

Therefore, by
Theorem

3.3
and the choice of
N
(Equation
7
),
we obtain that the monotone complexity of
f_{M}
is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{m}{Nw\log m}\right)^{w}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{nw\log m}\right)^{w}=\Omega\left(\frac{d}{n\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{t}/(b\log m)}.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
4.3
.

4.3
Explicit constructions of well-behaved codes

We now construct an explicit well-behaved binary code
\mathcal{C}
for which we can apply the argument from Section
4.2
to prove exponential lower bounds for monotone circuits computing a boolean function in uniform-
\NC^{2}
.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
n,m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q\vcentcolon=2^{\ell}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
be the linear code generated by a
matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
.
Then there is a linear binary code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{s}
generated by a matrix

M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times s}
for
r\vcentcolon=\ell n
and
s\vcentcolon=\ell m
such that

d(\mathcal{L})\leq d(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d(\mathcal{L})\text{ and }d^{*}(\mathcal{L})\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d^{*}(\mathcal{L}).

Furthermore, if each entry of the generator matrix
G
for
\mathcal{L}
can be computed in time
\poly(n,m,\ell)
,
then we can compute all the entries of the generator matrix
M
for
\mathcal{C}
in time
\poly(n,m,\ell)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}\vcentcolon=\{b_{1},\dotsc,b_{\ell}\}
be a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as an

\ell
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
be the map
from elements
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
to their coordinate vector with respect to the basis
\mathcal{B}
:
that is, for every
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, let
\phi(v)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
be the unique vector such that

v=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}\phi(v)_{i}b_{i}.

Note that
\phi
is a linear isomorphism between
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
.
Let
\gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
be the following map: for every
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,

\gamma(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})\vcentcolon=(\phi(v_{1}),\dotsc,\phi(v_{m})).

Again note that
\gamma
is a linear isomorphism between
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
; in particular:

\gamma(0)=0\text{ and $\gamma$ is a linear bijection}.

Let

\mathcal{C}\vcentcolon=\{\,\gamma(w)\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}w\in\mathcal{L}\},

and let
g_{1},\dotsc,g_{n}\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
be the rows of
G
.
Note that, for every
w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
,

G^{\top}w=\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}g_{i}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[\ell]}\phi(w_{i})_{j}b_{j}g_{i},

hence

\gamma(G^{\top}w)=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[\ell]}\phi(w_{i})_{j}\gamma(b_{j}g_{i}).

Let
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times s}
, for
r\vcentcolon=\ell n
and
s\vcentcolon=\ell m
,
be the matrix with the vectors
\gamma(b_{j}g_{i})
as rows, that is,

M\vcentcolon=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[\ell]}e_{(i-1)\ell+j}\gamma(b_{j}g_{i})^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(b_{1}g_{1})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma(b_{n}g_{\ell})^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
e_{1},\dotsc,e_{n\ell}
are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\ell}
.
As
\phi
is a linear isomorphism, we can prove that

\mathcal{C}=\{\,M^{\top}w\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}w\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\ell}\}.

Now let us prove the claimed distance bounds for
\mathcal{C}
.
First note that if
v\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies
v=\gamma(w)=(\phi(w_{1}),\dotsc,\phi(w_{m}))
for some
w\in\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
,
then, as
\phi(x)=0
iff
x=0
, we have

|\supp(w)|\leq|\supp(v)|,

which implies that
d(\mathcal{L})\leq d(\mathcal{C})
,
and
|\supp(v)|\leq\ell|\supp(w)|
, which implies that

d(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d(\mathcal{L}).

In order to prove the bound on the dual distance of
\mathcal{C}
, we will use the following characterization of the dual distance of codes:

Proposition 4.14

(Theorem 8 from Chapter 5 of
[
46
]
)
.

We say that a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
is
t
-wise independent if,
for a uniformly chosen
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\vcentcolon=x\sim\Unif(S)
and
for all
I\subseteq[m]
with
|I|\leq t
,
the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
are independent and uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Then a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
is
t
-wise independent if and only if
t\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})-1
.

Now let us prove that
d^{*}(\mathcal{L})\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})
.
For any
t\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{L})-1
, we know that
\mathcal{L}
is
t
-wise independent over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by Proposition
4.14
.
This implies that,
for a uniformly chosen
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\vcentcolon=x\sim\Unif(\mathcal{L})
and
for
I\subseteq[m]
with
|I|\leq t
,
the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
are independent and uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Let

(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{\ell m})\vcentcolon=y\sim\Unif(\mathcal{C}).

As
x_{i}
for any
i\in I
is uniformly over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and as
\phi
is a bijection from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
,
we get that
(y_{i,1},\dotsc,y_{i,\ell})\vcentcolon=\phi(x_{i})
are independent random variables and uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Thus, any subset
J\subseteq I\times[\ell]
of the random variables
(y_{i,p})_{i\in I,p\in[\ell]}
with
|J|\leq t
satisfies that

(y_{j})_{j\in J}
are independent random variables and uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Hence,
\mathcal{C}
is
t
-wise independent, and, by Proposition
4.14
,

d^{*}(\mathcal{L})\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C}).

Now suppose that

d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\geq\ell d^{*}(\mathcal{L})+1.

By Proposition
4.14
, we get that
\mathcal{C}
is
(\ell d^{*}(\mathcal{L}))
-wise independent.
Again using the fact
\phi
is a bijection from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
,
we can prove that,
for a uniformly chosen
(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\vcentcolon=x\sim\Unif(\mathcal{L})
and
for
I\subseteq[m]
with
|I|\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{L})
,
the variables
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
are independent and uniformly distributed over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
By Proposition
4.14
,
we obtain that
d^{*}(\mathcal{L})+1\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{L})
,
which is a contradiction.
Therefore,

d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d^{*}(\mathcal{L}).

Now let us argue about the explicitness of our construction.
Note that:

•

Element representations (via a basis for
\mathbb{F}_{q}
) and operations over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}

can be performed in time
\poly(\ell)

[
61
]
.

•

For any given
(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})\vcentcolon=v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
,
the element
\gamma(v)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
can be uniformly computed in time
\poly(m,\ell)

as it is just the concatenation of
\phi(v_{1}),\dotsc,\phi(v_{m})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell}
and

\phi(v_{i})
is the representation of
v_{i}
as an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

•

The generator matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell n\times\ell m}
(Equation
8
)
for
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
can be uniformly computed in time
\poly(n,m,\ell)
.
∎

Corollary 4.15
.

For every
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
with
m>n
and for
\ell\vcentcolon=\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil
,
there is a binary code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
such that
we can explicitly construct
a generator matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell n\times\ell m}
for
\mathcal{C}

and

m-n\leq d(\mathcal{C})\leq 2\ell(m-n)\text{ and }n\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\leq 2\ell n.

Proof.

For
q\vcentcolon=2^{\ell}
, let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
be a Reed-Solomon code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times m}
. The following facts are standard results in coding theory (e.g., see Chapter 10 of
[
46
]
):

•

Each entry of the matrix
G
can be constructed in time
\poly(n,m,\ell)
.

•

The distance of
\mathcal{L}
is

d(\mathcal{L})=m-n+1.

•

The dual code of
\mathcal{L}
has distance

d^{*}(\mathcal{L})=m-(m-n)+1=n+1.

By Lemma
4.13
,
there is a binary code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
such that
we can explicitly construct
a generator matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell n\times\ell m}
for
\mathcal{C}
,
and

m-n\leq d(\mathcal{L})\leq d(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d(\mathcal{L})\leq 2\ell(m-n),\text{ and }n\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{L})\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\leq\ell d^{*}(\mathcal{L})\leq 2\ell n.\qed

Theorem 4.16

(Theorem
1.5
)
.

For every sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
for

m\vcentcolon=\lceil n^{3/2}(\log n)^{2}\rceil\text{ and }\ell\vcentcolon=\lceil\log_{2}m\rceil,

there is an
\ell m
-variate monotone Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{\ell m}\to\{0,1\}
such that the following hold:

1.

f
can be computed by a uniform Boolean circuit of size polynomial in
n

and depth
O(\log\ell m)^{2}
.

2.

Any monotone Boolean circuit computing
f
has size at least
2^{(\ell m)^{1/3-o(1)}}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
4.15
,
there is a binary code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell m}
such that
we can explicitly construct
a generator matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\ell n\times\ell m}
for
\mathcal{C}

and

m-n\leq d(\mathcal{C})\leq 2\ell(m-n)\text{ and }n\leq d^{*}(\mathcal{C})\leq 2\ell n.

(9)

Let

f\vcentcolon=f_{M}
be the
\ell m
-variate Boolean function corresponding to
M
.
By Lemma
4.1
, the function
f

can be computed by uniform Boolean circuit of size
\poly(\ell m)
and depth
O(\log\ell m)^{2}
.
Let

Note that, for sufficiently large

,

d\geq 2n\ell.

By Theorem
4.3
, we obtain that
the monotone complexity

of

is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\ell nw\log(\ell m)}\right)^{w},

for

.
By Equation

\Omega(m^{1/3}/(\log m)^{2})\leq w\leq O(n^{1/2}/(\log m)^{1/2}).

Therefore,

S^{+}(f)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\ell nw\log(\ell m)}\right)^{w}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{m}{n^{3/2}(\log m)^{3/2}}\right)^{w}\geq\Omega(\log m)^{w/2}\geq 2^{\Omega(m^{1/3}\log\log m/(\log m)^{2})}.\qed

This section uses some ideas from Section

5.1
Nonmonotone low-depth circuit upper bounds

Let us first prove the upper bound part of Theorem
1.4
.

Lemma 5.1

(Upper bound)
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
the polynomial
P_{A}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
, as defined in Equation
2
,
is computed by an arithmetic circuit of size
\poly(m)
and depth
O(\log m)^{2}.

Proof.

Let

I_{X}\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}[X]^{m\times m}

be the
m
-dimensional identity matrix with diagonal elements replaced by the variables

x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
.
By the Cauchy-Binet formula, we have

\det((AI_{X})A^{T})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[m]:\\
|S|=n\end{subarray}}\det((AI_{X})[[n],S])\det(A^{T}[S,[n]])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[m]:\\
|S|=n\end{subarray}}\det((AI_{X})[[n],S])\det(A[S]),

and, by the block structure of
I_{X}
and the multiplicative property of the determinant,

\det((AI_{X})[[n],S])=\det(A[[n],S]I_{X}[S,S])=\det(A[[n],S])\det(I_{X}[S,S])=\det(A[S])\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}.

Hence,

\det((AI_{X})A^{T})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[m]:\\
|S|=n\end{subarray}}\left(\det(A[S])\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)\det(A[S])=P_{A}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m}).

By efficient computation of the determinant of a symbolic matrix
[
12
,
47
]
, we obtain that the polynomial

P_{A}=\det(AI_{X}A^{T})
can be computed by an arithmetic circuit of size
\poly(m)
and depth
O((\log m)^{2})
.
∎

5.2
Choice of a well-behaved
\mathbb{R}
-matrix

Let
k\vcentcolon=k(n)\in\mathbb{N}
be a growing function, which will be specified later, such that
k\leq n^{0.1}
.
Let

m\vcentcolon=n^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pts\vcentcolon=200k^{2}.

(10)

Let
M_{n}
be an
n\times m
random matrix obtained by sampling each column

M_{n}[i]
of
M_{n}
independently and uniformly at random from the set of
vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
of Hamming weight at most
s
.

For technical reasons, it is nicer to sample the matrix using points of Hamming weight
at most

s
, though the following lemma shows that this is not very different from sampling points of weight close to
s.

Lemma 5.2
.

With probability at least
1-1/n,
every column of
M_{n}
has Hamming weight at least
s/2.

Proof.

Note that
s=200k^{2}=O(n^{0.2})
.
We note that
\binom{n}{i}\leq n^{i}
for
i\in[n]
, and use the bound on
s
to conclude

\binom{n}{\leq s}\geq\binom{n}{s}\geq\left(\frac{n}{s}\right)^{s}\geq n^{0.7s}.

Putting things together, the probability that any fixed column
M_{n}[j]
has Hamming weight less than
s/2
is at most

\sum_{i=0}^{s/2}\frac{\binom{n}{i}}{\binom{n}{\leq s}}\leq\frac{(s/2+1)\cdot n^{s/2}}{n^{0.7s}}\leq\frac{n}{n^{0.2s}}.

Union bounding over
m=n^{2}
columns and using the fact that
s\geq 200
,
the probability that there is a column of Hamming weight at most
s/2
is at most
1/n
.
∎

To construct our hard distributions, the following lemma will be important.

Lemma 5.3

(Full rank lemma)
.

If
S
is distributed uniformly in
{[m]\choose 10n\log n}
and independently of
M_{n}
, then

\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\Pr_{S}\left[\text{$M_{n}[S]$ is full rank}\right]\geq 1/10\right]\geq 1/10.

Proof.

The idea is to carry out an argument analogous to the solution to the coupon-collector’s problem to show that for each
S\in\binom{[m]}{10n\log n}
, we have

\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\text{$M_{n}[S]$ is full rank}\right]\geq\frac{4}{5}.

(11)

The claim of the lemma then follows from Equation
11
by the following averaging argument.
Define the random variables

X\vcentcolon=\Pr_{S\in\binom{[m]}{n}}\left[\text{$M_{n}[S]$ is full rank}\right],\text{ and }Y\vcentcolon=1-X.

Thus

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}[X]&=\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{S\subseteq[m]:|S|=n}\mathbbm{1}_{\rank(M_{n}[S])=n}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\binom{m}{n}}\sum_{S\subseteq[m]:|S|=n}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\rank(M_{n}[S])=n\right]\geq\frac{4}{5},\end{split}

and
\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}[Y]\leq\frac{1}{5}
.
By Markov’s inequality, we get

1-\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[X\geq\frac{1}{10}\right]=\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[Y\geq\frac{9}{10}\right]\leq\frac{10\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}[Y]}{9}\leq\frac{2}{9},

which implies that

\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[X\geq\frac{1}{10}\right]\geq\frac{7}{9}>\frac{1}{10}.

Now let us prove the probability bound in Equation
11
.
Assume, without loss of generality, that
S=\{1,\ldots,10n\log n\}
.
Assume that we choose an infinite sequence of vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of vectors independently and uniformly at random
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq s}
.
For each
d\leq n
, let
X_{d}
denote the smallest
r
such that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
span a subspace of dimension
d
.
It suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}X_{n}\leq 2n\log n
, because Equation
11
then follows by Markov’s inequality,
since we can interpret the definition of
M_{n}
as choosing the vectors
v_{1},\cdots,v_{10n\log n}
as
M_{n}
’s columns.
For
X_{0}\vcentcolon=0
, we have that
X_{n}\vcentcolon=\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}X_{d+1}-X_{d}
, which implies that

\mathbb{E}X_{n}=\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[X_{d+1}-X_{d}].

To compute
\mathbb{E}[X_{d+1}-X_{d}]
, we condition on the value
X_{d}=r
and the vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
which span a vector space
V
of dimension
d.
Let
p_{d}
denote the probability that a uniformly random
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq s}
lies outside
V
. By the subspace lemma (Lemma
5.4
) proved below, we have

1-p_{d}\leq\frac{\binom{d}{\leq s}}{\binom{n}{\leq s}}\leq\frac{d+1}{n+1}

where the latter inequality follows from the following simple binomial estimate:

\binom{d}{\leq s}=1+d+\sum_{i=2}^{s}\binom{d}{i}\leq 1+d+\sum_{i=2}^{s}\frac{d+1}{n+1}\cdot\binom{n}{i}=\frac{d+1}{n+1}\cdot\binom{n}{\leq s}.

It follows that
X_{d+1}-X_{d}
(conditioned on
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
) has a geometric distribution with success probability
p_{d}\geq(n-d)/(n+1)
and thus has expectation at most
(n+1)/(n-d)
.
Hence

\mathbb{E}X_{n}\leq\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}\frac{n+1}{n-d}\leq 2n\log n.

We have thus shown the desired upper bound on
\mathbb{E}X_{n}
, which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The above proof used the following lemma, which show that a uniformly randomly chosen
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}_{s}
cannot lie in any proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with high probability.

Lemma 5.4

(Subspace lemma)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field and let
V
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
of dimension
d
, and
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq s}
be the set of all binary strings of Hamming weight at most
s
. Then,

\lvert V\cap\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq s}\lvert\leq\binom{d}{\leq s}.

We note that the above lemma is tight, as witnessed by a subspace
V
generated by any
d
standard basis vectors.

Proof.

As
V
has dimension
d
,
there is a basis
B
of
V
such that
there is a set
R\in\binom{[n]}{d}
indexing the elements of
B
as

B=\{\,b^{(r)}\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}r\in R\}

such that, for every
r\in R
and
v\in B
,

\begin{cases}v_{r}=1&\text{if $v=b^{(r)}$, and}\\
v_{r}=0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that we can find such a basis via Gaussian elimination over any arbitrary basis of
V
,
as the set
R
above corresponds to the rows of the pivot element of the matrix in column echelon form.
For every
v\in X\vcentcolon=V\cap\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq s}
, let
\alpha_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{B}
be the unique vector such that

v=\sum_{b\in B}\alpha_{v,b}b
.
So, for every
r\in R
,

v_{r}=\sum_{b\in B}\alpha_{v,b}b_{r}=\alpha_{v,b^{(r)}},

which implies that
\supp(v)\cap R
uniquely determines
\alpha_{v}
(as
v_{r}\in\{0,1\}
),
and, consequently, uniquely determines
v
.
As
|\supp(v)\cap R|\leq|\supp(v)|\leq s
for every
v\in X
, we get that

|X|\leq\left\lvert\binom{|R|}{\leq s}\right\rvert=\binom{d}{\leq s}.\qed

We also need the following lemma to show that, with high probability,
most columns in an arbitrary small tuple of columns of
M_{n}
have a small
fraction of their support intersecting the union of the support of their
preceding columns.

Definition 5.5
.

For
\tau\vcentcolon=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{t})
a tuple of distinct elements of
[m]
,
we say that
i_{j}
is
c
-contained w.r.t.
\tau
if
the set
\supp(M_{n}[i_{j}])
has at least
c
elements in common with
\bigcup_{p<j}\supp(M_{n}[i_{p}])
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
S_{\tau}
be the following (random) set

S_{\tau}\vcentcolon=\{\,j\in[t]\nonscript\>|\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\text{$i_{j}$ is $10k$-contained w.r.t. $\tau$}\}.

Then, for any positive integer
t\leq n^{0.1}
, we have

\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\text{$\exists\ \tau$ such that $|S_{\tau}|\geq t/2k$}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

Let
r\vcentcolon=\lceil t/2k\rceil
and
\kappa\vcentcolon=10k
.
By the union bound over the choices of
\tau\in[m]^{t}
and
S\in\binom{[t]}{r}
, we get that

\displaystyle\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\text{$\exists\ \tau$ such that $|S_{\tau}|\geq t/2k$}\right]\leq m^{t}\cdot\binom{t}{r}\max_{\tau,S}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[S\subseteq S_{\tau}\right].

Now we are going to find an upper bound for
\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[S\subseteq S_{\tau}\right]
for every
\tau=\vcentcolon(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{t})
and
S\in\binom{[t]}{r}
.
Let
\{s_{1},\dotsc,s_{r}\}\vcentcolon=S
such that
s_{1}<\dotsc<s_{r}
, and,
for every
j\in[r]
, let

T_{j}\vcentcolon=\cup_{p<s_{j}}\supp(M_{n}[i_{p}]).

First note that
|T_{j}|\leq ts
, and

\displaystyle\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[S\subseteq S_{\tau}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\cap_{j=1}^{r}\{|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T_{j}|\geq\kappa\}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T_{j}|\geq\kappa\bigg|\cap_{i=1}^{j-1}\{|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{i}}])\cap T_{i}|\geq\kappa\}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T_{j}|\geq\kappa\right],

where the last equality follows from the independence between choices for the columns of
M_{n}
.
For every
j\in[r]
, we have, again by independence of columns,

\displaystyle\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T_{j}|\geq\kappa\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\subseteq[n],\kappa\leq|T|\leq ts}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T|\geq\kappa\right]\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[T_{j}=T\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{T\subseteq[n],\kappa\leq|T|\leq ts}\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T|\geq\kappa\right].

For every
T\subseteq[n]
such that
\kappa\leq|T|\leq ts
,
we get

\displaystyle\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[|\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\cap T|\geq\kappa\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\exists R\in\binom{T}{\kappa}\text{ s.t. }R\subseteq\supp(M_{n}[i_{s_{j}}])\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\binom{|T|}{\kappa}\binom{n-\kappa}{\leq(s-\kappa)}}{\binom{n}{\leq s}}\leq\frac{\binom{|T|}{\kappa}\cdot(s+1)\cdot\binom{n-\kappa}{s-\kappa}}{\binom{n}{s}}

\displaystyle\leq(s+1)(tse/\kappa)^{\kappa}(2s/n)^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-0.5\kappa}=n^{-5k}.

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[S\subseteq S_{\tau}\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-5kr}\leq n^{-2.5t}

and, as a consequence,

\Pr_{M_{n}}\left[\text{$\exists\ \tau$ such that $|S_{\tau}|\geq t/2k$}\right]\leq\binom{t}{r}\cdot m^{t}n^{-2.5t}=\binom{t}{r}\cdot n^{-0.5t}\leq 1/n.\qed

We now collect in the following definition the properties of a matrix that we need for the proof of our lower bound.

.

We say that a matrix

is

if the following properties hold:

Every column

is
10k
-contained w.r.t.

In particular, if

, there are no

such that

is
10k
-contained w.r.t.
\tau
.

By Lemma
5.2
, Lemma

and Lemma

]
,

\Pr_{B}\left[|B-\mathbb{E}B|>\delta\mathbb{E}B\right]\leq 2e^{-\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}B/3},

and, for
\delta\vcentcolon=\sqrt{18/r}
,

\Pr_{B}\left[|B-r/3|>\delta r/3\right]\leq 2e^{-2}\leq 1/2.

Hence,

\sum_{U\subseteq R,||U|-r/3|\leq\delta r/3}\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[u^{-1}(0)=U\right]\geq 1/2.

Now we just need to find good estimates for
\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[Y_{v}=p\right]
for
v\vcentcolon=r-w
with
w\in[r]
satisfying

|w-r/3|\leq\sqrt{2r}.

In order to obtain these estimates, we will use standard inequalities to deal with binomial coefficients.
First note that

\displaystyle\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[Y_{v}=p\right]=\binom{v}{(v+p)/2}2^{-v}.

By using that
1-x\geq e^{-x/(1-x)}
for every
x\leq 1
, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{v}{(v+p)/2}}{\binom{v}{v/2}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{p/2-1}\frac{v/2-i}{v/2+p/2-i}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{v/2-p/2}{v/2}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(e^{-\frac{p}{v}\frac{1}{1-p/v}}\right)^{p/2}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\frac{p^{2}}{2(v-p)}},

and, using that
\binom{v}{v/2}=\Theta(2^{v}/\sqrt{v})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[Y_{v}=p\right]

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\frac{p^{2}}{2(v-p)}}\binom{v}{v/2}2^{-v}

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{p^{2}}{2(v-p)}}}{\sqrt{v}}\right).

As
p\in[-10k,10k]
and

200k^{2}=s\geq v\geq 2r/3-\sqrt{18r}\geq r/3\geq\frac{1}{3}((s/2)-10k)=\frac{1}{3}(200k^{2}-10k)\geq 60k^{2}

for sufficiently large
k
, we get

\displaystyle\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[Y_{v}=p\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{200k^{2}}{2\cdot(60k^{2}-10k)}}}{\sqrt{k^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Omega\left(1/k\right).

Therefore, we obtain

\Pr_{a\sim D_{0}}\left[a_{i_{j}}=1\ |\ a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{j-1}}\right]\geq\Omega\left(1/k\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\Omega(1/k).\qed

Our main combinatorial tool is the classical sunflower lemma
[
25
]
(see also
[
39
, Section 6.1]
).
Recall that a sunflower is a collection of sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{r}
such that
the pairwise intersections
S_{i}\cap S_{j}
are all the same.
The improved bounds of
[
5
]
and subsequent works will not make any
substantial difference in our bounds.

(Sunflower lemma)
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is a family of sets of size at most
\ell\in[k]

such that

\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\rvert\geq\ell!(r-1)^{\ell}
,

{\mathcal{S}}

contains a sunflower with
r
sets.

We now prove our
D_{0}
-sunflower bound.

(12)

For every

Let

It suffices to show that, for

Let

{\mathcal{F}}

.
In the remaining terms, all of their indices are not

\tau
.
Removing the corrupted terms yields a
a subfamily

with

-contained w.r.t.

\tau
.
Let
