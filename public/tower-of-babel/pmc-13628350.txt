Predictors of treatment response to semaglutide for weight management
Authors: Ploutarchos Tzoulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence of weekly, injectable glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists has revolutionized the obesity management landscape. Numerous studies have shown high efficacy as well as great variability in weight loss, highlighting the need for this mini review, summarizing current evidence about predictors of semaglutide-related weight loss. The first well-known determinant of semaglutide-induced weight loss is the presence of type 2 DM which is associated with significantly lower body weight reduction. The second predictor is sex, with females exhibiting a greater weight loss than males by a mean 6%. In addition, higher maintenance dose of 7.2 mg is superior to standard 2.4 mg dose in weight reduction by a mean 3%. There is no clear association of age and baseline body mass index with the magnitude of semaglutide-driven weight loss, except for the possibility of adolescence enhancing and overweight reducing this treatment effect. The potential impact of genetic polymorphisms of individual treatment response to semaglutide remains incompletely understood, limited so far to a modest effect of specific variants in GLP1 receptor gene. Another potential predictor of response could be obesity phenotype, with preliminary data supporting greater effectiveness of semaglutide in individuals with ‘‘hungry gut’’ phenotype. Type of lifestyle modification does not seem to have a significant effect on the variability of semaglutide-driven weight loss. Further studies are needed to expand knowledge about predictors of response to semaglutide, paving the way for validated predictive models in order to guide clinical decision-making and provision of personalized obesity care.

Introduction

Over the last three decades, the worldwide prevalence of obesity has more than doubled, rendering the obesity epidemic one of the most high-burden, high-cost public health issues across the globe (1). The emergence of highly efficacious glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and their widespread use in clinical practice have led to a decline in the obesity rates (2). In December 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) issued a global call towards a complete transformation of the approach to people living with obesity, suggesting a central role for modern pharmacotherapy combined with intensive lifestyle modification (3). This paradigm shift in obesity care brings new challenges since current drug capacity can cover less than 10% of people with obesity, posing a significant financial burden on healthcare systems and potentially exacerbating health disparities (1). Until incremental expansion of capacity, funding and access is reached, an equitable, transparent evidence-based framework is urgently needed to enable fair prioritisation of people living with obesity for prescription of GLP-1 agonists (3) and to ensure that these medications will reach those who need them most and would benefit most (1).

Several randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have demonstrated the great efficacy of injectable weekly semaglutide for weight management, with a mean weight loss of 15-16%, but they have also reported great variability in the magnitude of weight reduction, including at the one end of the spectrum 10-13% of participants not reaching the 5% weight reduction threshold and at the other end of the spectrum 32-38% achieving more than 20% weight loss (4, 5). This significant heterogeneity in medication-induced weight loss also applies to tirzepatide, a dual agonist of GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) (6). For these reasons, there has been great interest in identifying predictors of treatment effect, guiding personalized clinical decision-making. In view of the limited evidence base about predicting response to tirzepatide, this mini review will focus on summarizing scientific literature about predictors of response to semaglutide.

Methods

We undertook a search via PubMed and the Cochrane Library until 28th May 2026 of all publications, including the key words ‘GLP-1 agonists’, ‘glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists’, ‘incretin mimetics’, ‘semaglutide’, ‘tirzepatide’, ‘antiobesity medications’, ‘pharmacotherapy for obesity’, and ‘medications for weight loss’. Data from various types of studies, ranging from real-world observations to RCTs and meta-analyses, were taken into consideration. The primary endpoint chosen for the assessment of treatment response and its relationship with different parameters was the total percentage weight loss over the duration of the study period. However, studies using different primary outcomes, such as percentage of participants reaching a specific threshold of weight reduction, were also considered.

Predictors of response to semaglutide

Glycaemic status

Semaglutide therapy leads to significantly lower weight loss in individuals with type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM) compared to those without, as evidenced by a mean 68-week reduction in bodyweight of 9.6% (7) versus 14.9% (4), respectively. Interestingly, it remains unclear whether this association extends across the continuum of glucose levels to the presence of prediabetes. On the one hand, in STEP 1 trial extension, those with prediabetes had numerically lower decrease in body weight of 15.7% versus 19.7% in those with normoglycemia (8), but on the other hand, 4-year weight loss in semaglutide-treated participants in the SELECT trial was similar in those with prediabetes and normoglycemia (9). In addition, STEP 10 trial, evaluating semaglutide in people with obesity and prediabetes, showed a mean weight reduction of 13.9% (10), similar with that reported in studies including people with obesity and either normoglycemia or prediabetes (4). Taking into account all these data, the impact of prediabetes at baseline on the magnitude of weight loss has not been clarified yet. It’s worth mentioning that confounding may be introduced because of indirect comparison between studies with different populations and, also, that prediabetes is an umbrella term covering a spectrum, including impaired fasting glycemia, impaired glucose tolerance and their combination. Amongst semaglutide-treated patients with type 2 DM, higher baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), longer duration of DM, concomitant insulin administration and larger number of antidiabetic medications have been associated with inferior weight loss (11).

Therefore, the presence or absence of type 2 DM has been the most studied predictor of semaglutide-related weight loss, with individuals with type 2 DM achieving significantly lower weight reduction than those without. Studies are needed to evaluate whether baseline HbA1c or fasting glucose or markers of insulin resistance, such as Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), are associated with the extent of semaglutide-induced weight loss across the continuum of normoglycemia and prediabetes.

Sex

There seems to be sex-specific differences in the efficacy of semaglutide for the treatment of obesity, with STEP studies reporting a mean weight reduction of 14.0-16.2% in women and 8.0-9.3% in men (12), accounting for a greater weight loss by a mean 6% among females than among males (13). Two recent meta-analyses of trials have also shown a greater weight loss in women than men for five different GLP-1 agonists, highlighting sex as a major differentiator (14).

A growing body of evidence supports sex differences in response to GLP-1 analogues, highlighting the key role of oestrogens (15). Multiple preclinical studies have shown (i) the synergistic interaction of GLP-1 and its analogues with oestrogens, (ii) that oestrogens enhance the effect of GLP-1 and its suppressive effect on food-motivated behaviour, (iii) that the exogenous addition or removal of oestrogens influences the effects of GLP-1 analogues on feeding behaviour, and (iv) that the presence of oestrogens leads to activation of additional brain areas and downstream signals which otherwise remain unaffected (15). The enhanced response of women might also be explained to some extent by exposure difference due to lower starting weight in females than males. Moreover, women exhibit slower gastric emptying compared to males, while a significant proportion of men develop excess adiposity-related hypogonadotropic hypogonadism which may render obesity resistant to treatment (12). Data are still lacking about the treatment response to semaglutide in women before and after menopause as well as the impact of Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT) and any other treatment affecting reproductive hormones on the weight loss outcomes. In total, both preclinical and clinical data confirm the key contribution of gonadal steroids to the enhanced response of females to incretins (15), leading to greater weight loss in females than males.

Maintenance dose

Two recently published RCTs, STEP UP and STEP UP T2D trials, undertaking head-to-head comparison of different maintenance semaglutide doses in terms of weight loss outcomes, have reported superior weight loss with 7.2 mg semaglutide dose compared to standard 2.4 mg maintenance dose by a mean 3% in people with obesity, without and with type 2 DM (16, 17). These findings suggest that semaglutide-induced weight loss is dose-dependent, while the rates of discontinuation and severe adverse events was similar with the exception for more frequent occurrence of dysesthesia in those receiving semaglutide at 7.2 mg dose (16, 17).

Age and baseline BMI

A systematic review has not determined any significant effect of age and baseline body mass index (BMI) on treatment response to GLP-1 analogues (14). However, an analysis of SELECT trial, a 4-year cardiovascular outcomes trial of semaglutide-treated people with excess weight, reported a linear relationship between age group and degree of weight reduction, with younger age being associated with greater weight loss (9). Of note, the actual observed difference was small, with mean weight loss percentage of 9.3% in people under the age of 55 years compared to 7.5% in individuals aged above 75 years old (9). Another interesting observation was that people with baseline BMI < 30 kg/m2 achieved a mean percentage weight loss of 7.5% compared to 9.2% in participants with BMI > 40 kg/m2, while weight loss was similar across different classes of obesity. The reason behind this observation might be that individuals with overweight are closer to their ‘‘set point’’ where the weight loss reaches a plateau, but this is a hypothesis awaiting to be tested (9). Examining the efficacy of semaglutide as antiobesity medication (AOM) across different age groups, semaglutide-treated adolescents achieved a significant placebo-extracted reduction in body weight, 5% greater than adults (18). At the other end of the age spectrum, a pooled analysis of SUSTAIN 1–5 trials has showed a similar weight loss in older semaglutide-treated patients with type 2 DM with those younger than 65 years (19). A meta-analysis of five studies assessing GLP-1 agonists in patients with obesity found that people older than 65 years old experience similar weight loss with younger individuals (20).

In conclusion, the effect of age and BMI subgroup on treatment effect of semaglutide is limited, if any, with two possible exceptions. Firstly, adolescence may enhance its treatment effect (18), but this pronounced effect needs to be reproduced in large-scale studies. In this case, it remains to be determined whether this association extends to those younger than 30 years old. Second, people with overweight may experience smaller percentage of body weight reduction than those with obesity.

Genetic factors

The glycaemic response to GLP-1 agonists is associated with the presence of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), especially in the GLP1 receptor gene (21). Until recently, pharmacogenomic studies had failed to identify consistent genome-wide associations for incretin-induced weight loss (22, 23). However, a recent large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified a significant association of a missense variant rs10305420 in GLP1R gene with enhanced efficacy of both semaglutide and tirzepatide, as evidenced by a mean additional weight loss of 0.76 kg (24). This association was statistically significant, albeit of questionable clinical significance due to the small effect size.

The potential contribution of genetic factors to individual treatment response remains incompletely understood, highlighting the need for future studies evaluating the predictive utility of genetic factors (24).

Obesity phenotypes

A phenotype-guided treatment approach of people with obesity has been proposed, with preliminary data supporting that individuals with ‘‘hungry gut’’ phenotype, characterized by abnormal satiety and evaluated by the rate of gastric emptying and the duration of fullness after a meal, are the best responders to GLP-1 receptor agonists (25). However, application of obesity phenotyping in clinical practice can be cumbersome and time-consuming, with the availability of required tools being limited to research settings. For this reason, a machine-learning- assisted genetic risk score (GRS) was developed to predict high or low CTS (calories to satiation), the number of calories needed for a person to reach satiation, with those having low CTS being classified as ‘‘hungry gut’’ (26). Preliminary data have shown significantly greater weight loss outcomes in individuals with a GRS indicative of low CTS compared to those with high CTS, for both liraglutide (26) and semaglutide. Based on these data, MyPhenome test is currently available in the United States as a machine-learning, genetic-based test. However, large-scale prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the reproducibility of preliminary findings, its generalizability to different populations, its predictive power for treatment response to different AOMs, and its cost-effectiveness.

A subgroup of people with obesity is characterized by higher levels of anxiety, emotional eating, cravings and reward-seeking behaviour, classified as having the ‘‘emotional hunger’’ phenotype (25). Our hypothesis which remains to be tested is that these emotional traits may to some extent counteract the physiological action of increased GLP-1 levels, resulting in diminished efficacy of incretin mimetics (25). On the one hand, small studies have demonstrated positive effect of both semaglutide and tirzepatide on emotional eating, cravings, and other abnormal eating behavioural patterns (27, 28), while real-world data support that semaglutide leads to significant reduction of ‘‘food noise’’, defined as a constant, obsessive and intrusive stream of food-related thoughts which is distinct of physiological sense of hunger. On the other hand, an observational study of people with type 2 DM treated with GLP-1 agonist showed sustained suppression of external eating scores in response to external cues, but less pronounced effect on emotional eating scores, indicating emotional eating as a potential predictor of reduced responsiveness to GLP-1 agonists (29). In total, the efficacy of semaglutide in people with ‘‘emotional hunger’’ phenotype warrants further study.

Type of lifestyle modification

Among semaglutide-treated individuals, the addition of intensive behavioural therapy combined with low-calorie, meal-replacement diet (5) did not lead to greater long-term weight loss compared to less intensive lifestyle intervention (4), indicating a neutral effect of highly intensive lifestyle interventions on the magnitude of overall weight loss (5).Data are scarce about the effect of different medical nutrition therapies and physical exercise on the magnitude of weight loss in semaglutide-treated individuals. So far, studies of pharmacotherapy have focused on the efficacy and safety of AOMs as an adjunct to standard lifestyle measures. In light of paucity of data, future studies are needed to evaluate the impact of different dietary and exercise regimens not only on the efficacy and tolerability of AOMs, but also on body composition and sustainability of weight loss after drug discontinuation.

The key emerging role of dietary intervention is that of minimizing loss of skeletal muscle and optimizing body composition combined with tackling weight regain after treatment discontinuation (30). In a similar manner, physical exercise has indisputable efficacy for preventing or, at least, reducing weight regain following semaglutide discontinuation (31), while resistance exercise training is widely recommended to maintain muscle mass and function (32, 33).

Psychiatric disease

The reciprocal link between mental health illness and excess adiposity raises the question whether the presence of psychiatric disease affects treatment response to GLP-1 analogues. Two small RCTs of antipsychotic-treated patients with schizophrenia and obesity have demonstrated great efficacy of semaglutide for weight loss (34, 35). However, both studies had small sample size, limited duration 30–38 weeks and lower than commonly used maintenance doses of 1.0-2.0 mg. Therefore, the semaglutide-induced weight loss outcomes in patients with schizophrenia receiving antipsychotic medications are not comparable to the findings of other studies. As far as active major depressive disorder (MDD) is concerned, STEP trials have excluded patients with active MDD or other severe psychiatric condition (36). A post hoc subgroup analysis of STEP trials has demonstrated that the use of antidepressants medications for other indications than depression does not alter the treatment response to semaglutide (36). The only data supporting a possibly limited semaglutide efficacy in people with active major depressive disorder (MDD) were reported in a small, non-controlled, real-world, retrospective study with several potential confounders, such as questionable compliance or early discontinuation for various reasons other patients (37). Therefore, the efficacy of GLP-1 analogues in people with obesity and active depression remains unknown and warrants evaluation in prospective studies.

Discussion

The main determinants of the magnitude of semaglutide-induced weight loss are the presence or absence of type 2 DM and sex. Twelve-month semaglutide treatment at the 2.4 mg weekly maintenance dose leads to a mean weight loss of 14-18% in females and 8-11% in males without type 2 DM, while the mean body weight reduction is diminished in people with type 2 DM, to 7.5% and 4.6% in women and men, respectively (12, 13).

At present, a ‘‘trial and error’’ approach is followed in prescribing pharmacotherapy for obesity, with the early treatment response at various time points, such as week 8, week 16 (8, 38), and week 24 (39) predicting individual weight loss trajectories (38, 39). Entering the era of precision medicine, a model was recently validated to predict the glucose-lowering effect of various drug classes in patients with type 2 DM (40). However, the lack of robust evidence basis does not currently allow the development of a similar multivariable model for the prediction of treatment response to AOMs, as summarized in Table 1. Nowadays, only three predictors of treatment response to semaglutide have been established; (i) presence or absence of type 2 DM, (ii) sex, and (iii) maintenance dose (1 mg or 2.4 mg or 7.2 mg) characterized by a clear dose-response relationship, with higher semaglutide doses leading to greater amounts of weight loss (41, 16). Age and BMI, across a continuum, do not make a significant contribution to treatment effect. However, upon confirmation of preliminary data, younger age and overweight are potential enhancer and reducer of semaglutide response, respectively. The role of genetic polymorphisms hasn’t been fully understood yet, with the contribution of variants to GLP1 receptor gene being modest at best, currently excluding them from a prediction model. The obesity phenotype is also emerging as a possible determinant of the AOM selection in the future. At present, scarce data support the use of a genetic risk score, according to which those with low ‘‘calories to satiation’’ score exhibit exaggerated response to semaglutide, with these findings requiring confirmation in large-scale prospective studies. In addition, studies are required to evaluate the effect of the type of medical nutrition therapy or physical exercise prescription on the magnitude of weight loss in people receiving AOMs. Finally, the presence of schizophrenia plays a neutral role on the effect to semaglutide, while the likely impact of active major depression remains be studied. Future model predictors would incorporate clinical and genetic data, with potential response enhancers being younger age, ‘‘hungry gut’’ phenotype, and presence of variant in GLP1R gene, while the role of obesity phenotyping for provision of individualised obesity care remains to be determined.

In line with semaglutide, tirzepatide is also characterized by great variability in treatment effect, with 9% of tirzepatide-treated individuals not achieving a clinically meaningful weight loss in contrast to one third of people exceeding 25% weight loss (6). People with type 2 DM had a mean weight loss of 11.6% (42), significantly lower than that of 20.9% in those without (6), while females achieved greater weight loss than males by approximately 6% (13). A novel observation of a retrospective cohort study was the association of concurrent HRT with an additional 5% weight loss in postmenopausal women receiving tirzepatide, suggesting a potential synergistic effect of exogenous oestrogens and tirzepatide on weight loss outcomes.

In a rapidly evolving landscape of obesity pharmacotherapy, the armamentarium of gut hormones-based therapies in 2027 is expected to include the following classes of drugs: (i) injectable GLP-1 agonists, weekly semaglutide at a maintenance dose of either 2.4 mg or 7.2 mg and daily liraglutide, (ii) oral GLP-1 agonists, daily semaglutide and daily orforglipron, (iii) dual GLP1-GIP agonists, such as weekly injectable tirzepatide, (iv) dual GLP1-amylin agonist, named CagriSema, a weekly injectable combination of semaglutide and cagrilintide, and (v) triple GLP1-GIP-glucagon agonists, such as weekly injectable retatrutide (43). This new era of multiple effective pharmacotherapies with different mechanisms of action, routes of administration, efficacy-safety profiles, cost-effectiveness and different effects on various obesity-related complications highlights the need to develop multivariable models to predict treatment response to AOMs, enabling provision of personalized obesity care in day-to-day care (44).

Following the new care paradigm of personalized management in type 2 DM, the time has come to move beyond one-size-fits-all approach towards precision medicine for obesity. The great inter-person variability in the treatment effect of AOMs highlights the need for studies examining the impact of various demographic, metabolic, behavioural, and genetic factors on the treatment response to pharmacotherapy in people with obesity (44). Therefore, precision medicine initiatives, integrating data from genetics, epigenetics and metabolomics as well as encompassing demographic, environmental and psychological factors, are currently in progress in order to stratify obesity into different phenotypes with distinct biological basis, risk for future complications and treatment approach (45, 46). Despite significant scientific breakthroughs in the field of obesity, evidence to date does not support yet the application of genetics, nutrigenomics, pharmacogenetics, microbiomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics on guiding the therapeutic strategy for each person with obesity (47). Finally, the rapidly increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare suggests a possible future role of AI-driven models in provision of personalized obesity care.

In conclusion, there is a great heterogeneity in the individual response to semaglutide, while our predictive ability remains limited. Current evidence is inadequate to enable development of a prediction model since only two factors, glycaemic status and gender, have a significant effect on treatment response for a given dose of a medication. Future studies are urgently warranted to explore the impact of various biomarkers on treatment response, paving the way for validated predictive models and algorithms in order to guide clinical decision-making (45, 48) and enable clinicians to provide the right treatment for the right patient at the right time (45).
