Islamic reasoning and the use of prohibited medicines among Muslim patients: a qualitative study
Authors: Zachariah Nazar, Bilal Ali, Paul Rutter, Nicola Barnes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMuslim patients may avoid medicines containing ingredients prohibited by their faith (haram), such as alcohol, gelatine, or porcine derivatives. While Islamic law permits exceptions based on necessity (darura) or biotransformation (istihala), the way these principles influence medication adherence and shape patient–healthcare provider (HCP) interaction is underexplored.AimTo explore how Muslims apply Islamic reasoning to medication adherence decisions involving medicines containing haram ingredients.MethodThirteen ethnically diverse Muslim adults were purposively sampled for semi-structured interviews. A prior scoping review and mosque-based public and patient involvement (PPI) informed the interview guide. Interview data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA), with themes constructed inductively. Data interpretation was guided by the Necessity–Concerns Framework (NCF) and locus of control (LOC) theory. Rigour was supported through member checking, peer debriefing and reflexive journalling.ResultsFour themes emerged: (1) Halal as worldview: the halal–haram spectrum functioned as a moral lens for daily behaviour and intake, extending to medication use. (2) Motivations for consumption: halal was linked to perceived health and spiritual benefit, while haram signalled impurity and harm. (3) Minor illness or major disease: darura and istihala were applied flexibly in chronic or life-threatening illness, whereas participants avoided prohibited medicines for minor conditions, favouring complementary remedies. Practical workarounds included switching dosage forms or opening gelatine capsules and discarding the shell to minimise religious harm. Decisions were shaped by perceived severity, symptom burden, financial considerations, and the extent to which HCPs were perceived as trustworthy, culturally competent, and responsive to religious disclosure, all of which influenced adherence. (4) Personalised care: participants valued shared decision-making, transparent disclosure of religiously relevant excipients, and a reasonable degree of religious literacy among HCPs; scepticism about halal certification underscored the need for clearer labelling and guidance.ConclusionIslamic reasoning influenced how participants engaged with medicines deemed haram. Supporting adherence requires pharmacy practice that incorporates religious literacy and responds to concerns about transparent labelling and faith-sensitive communication. These steps will strengthen patient-centred care by aligning religious and ethical reasoning with treatment decisions, fostering trust, enhancing adherence, and supporting more equitable care for Muslim patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11096-025-02046-3.Keywords: Cultural competency, Medication adherence, Patient preference, Pharmaceutical preparations, Islamic

Introduction

Medication adherence plays a crucial role in determining health outcomes. Non-adherence is associated with avoidable hospital admissions, increased morbidity and mortality, and higher healthcare costs [1–3]. While psychological and behavioural drivers of adherence have been widely researched, religio-cultural factors have received much less attention [4]. Yet these dimensions can shape how patients interpret healthcare advice, reconcile it with their religious beliefs, and decide whether to adhere to prescribed treatments [5–7].

In many religious contexts, observances such as fasting and dietary rules interact directly with medication use. Across other Abrahamic traditions, analogous religion-based constraints exist; for example, in Judaism, non-kosher substances may be permitted for medical purposes when administered in non-edible forms (e.g. injections) or where oral forms are presented in a non-edible manner, with such questions managed via rabbinic guidance; by contrast, Jehovah’s Witnesses may decline transfused blood on doctrinal grounds, prompting “bloodless” strategies and explicit shared decision-making [8–10].

Within the Muslim community, this includes adherence to Islamic dietetic principles, which categorise substances as permissible (halal), doubtful (mashbooh), or forbidden (haram) [11]. A recent scoping review conducted by the authors found that across the included studies patients reported avoiding medicines containing porcine-derived gelatine, alcohol, or other uncertain excipients and may switch brands or use complementary remedies [12–14]. Uncertainty about excipient sources and inconsistent guidance on transformation (istihala) and necessity (darura) can lead to moral discomfort and, at times, delays or interruptions to treatment [15, 16]. Views are heterogeneous and shaped by engagement with non-binding Islamic legal opinions (fatwas), personal religiosity, trust in healthcare providers (HCPs), clarity of labelling, and context (e.g., vaccination; Ramadan fasting) [17, 18]. In this paper, ‘Islamic law’ refers to the religious legal tradition derived from recognised scriptural and interpretive sources and applied through non-binding juristic opinions, with legitimate variation across schools and contexts [19, 20]. Key Islamic terms are defined in Online Resource 1 (S1: Glossary of Islamic terms).

Some Muslim patients follow religious rulings strictly, while others show more flexibility—either by seeking guidance from scholars or relying on dispensations such as biotransformation (istihala) and necessity (darura) [21, 22]. With istihala, for example, a substance like alcohol converting into vinegar is regarded by some scholars as a sufficient transformation to change its religious status [23]. Although organisations such as the Islamic Organisation for Medical Sciences (IOMS) and the World Health Organisation (WHO) have supported such allowances, interpretation still varies across Islamic legal traditions [24–26]. Acceptance of such dispensations often depends on a person’s level of religious observance, interpretive position, and the extent of trust placed in the consulted scholars or authorities [27, 28].

Likewise, darura permits the use of otherwise impermissible substances in cases where no halal substitute is available and the treatment is considered necessary to prevent significant harm [29]. Though recognised by many fatwa councils, including the European Council for Fatwa and Research (ECFR), patient-level awareness and understanding remain limited [30, 31]. Conflicting fatwas around porcine-derived ingredients, which intensified during the COVID-19 pandemic, amplified pre-existing debates on istihala and contributed to patient confusion and hesitancy around vaccines and other pharmaceutical interventions [32].

Despite growing recognition that religion can influence adherence, the available literature remains limited and is dominated by cross-sectional, context-specific surveys. Few studies have examined how Islamic legal principles, particularly istihala and darura, are interpreted and applied by Muslim patients in real-world medication decisions within routine pharmacy care [14]. This study addresses that gap by analysing patients’ moral reasoning and outlining implications for culturally competent pharmacy practice and shared decision-making with HCPs.

Aim

The aim of the study was to explore how Muslim patients applied Islamic reasoning to medication adherence decisions involving medicines containing haram ingredients.

Method

Study design

This qualitative exploratory study used semi-structured in-depth interviews to examine Muslim patients’ reasoning regarding the use of medicines considered haram. It was informed by a critical realist ontology and a constructivist epistemology, recognising that participant experiences and decisions are shaped by cultural, religious, and social contexts, even if an objective reality exists [33, 34]. Thematic analysis was carried out using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive approach [35], allowing themes to be developed across participant narratives. Reporting followed the COREQ guidelines for transparency in qualitative research [36].

Theoretical framework

Behavioural theories helped explore decision-making processes, while Islamic legal principles contextualised how participants approached prohibited medicines. The Necessity Concerns Framework (NCF) explored how participants balanced medication necessity with concerns about prohibited ingredients [37]. Locus of control (LOC) theory supported the analysis of how participants attributed responsibility for health outcomes, whether to divine will or personal agency [38]. Islamic legal concepts, particularly darura and istihala, were drawn on alongside the behavioural frameworks to understand how participants navigated concerns about haram medicines [39, 40]. To situate the study and refine the interview guide, a prior scoping review, incorporating a targeted review of fatwa council publications on darura and istihala and published clinical case summaries on haram excipients, was used to inform the interview schedule and underpin the analysis (Online Resource 1).

Interview guide development

A semi-structured in-depth interview guide was developed through a three-phase process informed by Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) focus groups in South Wales and then piloted with two members of the target population [41]. The interview guide covered five broad areas: how participants saw their religious identity, their awareness of halal and haram, personal experiences with medication, interpretations of Islamic rulings, and how these shaped adherence. After piloting, minor refinements were made to improve question clarity, phrasing, and sequencing. The final interview guide is provided in Online Resource 1 (S3: Interview schedule).

Participant selection and setting

Purposive non-probability sampling was used to recruit Muslim adults (aged 18 years and over) in the UK who could participate in English and had recent experience with medicines they considered haram. Eligibility was based on participants reflecting on the religious permissibility of such medications, as outlined in the participation information. Recruitment was conducted across South Wales between July 2021 and January 2022 through mosque announcements, gatekeeper referrals (e.g. imams), and mosque-affiliated social media.

Sampling followed the information-power principle, with adequacy judged against the study aim, sample specificity, use of theory and interview richness [42]. Accordingly, a sample of 13 was considered appropriate. In line with reflexive thematic analysis (RTA), the study did not seek data saturation but focused on contextually situated theme development [43]. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered. The sample reflects practical constraints: lower mosque attendance by women and preferences for same-gender interviewer-participant matching. Eight people declined because of timing, COVID-19-related personal circumstances, or the interviewer’s gender.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted by the first author (BA) via Zoom between July 2021 and January 2022. The researcher had no prior relationship with the participants. This online format facilitated participation, especially when gender-related or religious considerations (such as prayer times) applied. Participants gave written informed consent in advance, which was reconfirmed verbally at the start of each session. All interviews were video-recorded with permission, transcribed verbatim, anonymised, and managed in NVivo 12 (QSR International).

Demographic information (participants’ age, gender, ethnicity and sect affiliation) was gathered during the interviews, which lasted 60–80 min. Field notes were taken during and immediately after each session. Following all interviews, eight of the thirteen participants completed member checking, reviewing theme summaries to confirm these reflected their perspectives.

Data analysis

Verbatim transcripts were analysed using RTA as per Braun and Clarke [35], with themes developed inductively through repeated and ongoing engagement with the data. This facilitated a detailed account of how participants understood the role of religious belief in medication adherence. BA conducted data familiarisation and open coding in NVivo 12. A reflexive journal and analytic memos supported interpretive decisions and tracked positionality. Two co-authors (ZN, NB) independently coded a subset of transcripts to enrich the reflexive process. This was not to establish inter-coder reliability, but to support collaborative reflexivity by surfacing alternative perspectives and interrogating early assumptions. Debriefing sessions supported the refinement of the thematic framework.

Codes were organised into clusters and refined through iterative review to develop candidate themes. Earlier transcripts were revisited to ensure consistency and thematic integrity. Feedback from face-to-face member checking allowed the final themes to be refined and validated. An audit trail, including codebook iterations, a decision log, and thematic maps, is provided in Online Resource 1 (Tables S1–S2) and Online Resource 2 (Figs. S1–S3).

Reflexivity

The first author (BA) is a practising Muslim and UK-registered pharmacist with training in Islamic studies and experience working with Muslim patients. This background informed his interpretive lens; reflexive bracketing and regular team debriefs were used to surface alternative readings and minimise projection. Reflexivity was supported by a positionality memo (Online Resource 1), analytic memos, and a reflexive journal maintained throughout the study.

Ethics approval

The Science and Health Faculty Ethics Committee at the University of Portsmouth granted ethical approval on 8 April 2021 (Reference: SHFEC 2020–098). All participants provided informed consent prior to participating.

Results

Participant characteristics

Thirteen adults, ranging from 22 to 72 years old (mean: 43), participated in the study, including ten men and three women. They represented a range of ethnic heritages—including South Asian, Arab-European, Black British, and White British—and diverse sect affiliations: ten identified as Sunni, and three as Shia. Three were converts to Islam, while the remainder were raised in Muslim households. Occupations included social care, pharmacology, engineering, and community liaison; two participants were retired, and one was unemployed (Table 1).

Theme 1 Halal as worldview: a moral lens for medicine use

Participants conceptualised halal as more than just a binary of dietetic classifications. It encompassed a comprehensive worldview that guided all aspects of life. They articulated the pervasive nature of the halal–haram spectrum, which functioned as a lens through which behavioural decisions and social interactions were judged morally justified and religiously compatible.

Thus, halal was described not only in terms of religious obligation but as a way of life—a moral compass through which the world was interpreted and which informed daily routines and priorities. This worldview was often spoken of in practical terms, emphasising that intentions behind mundane actions can be reframed as acts of worship. In this sense, halal operated as a comprehensive moral frame that extended to medicines as well as food.

“Whether you’re in the kitchen or driving… everything we do is ibadah (worship) as long as we have intention.” (P11 Male).

Fatalism anchored this worldview. Participants framed adversity, personal setbacks and illness as part of divine will.

“Everything that has happened had to happen… the whole way of life of Islam, as I understand it, is meant to be.” (P2 Male).

Participants also linked this worldview directly to ingestion and spiritual accountability, explicitly including medicines within its scope:

“Within our belief system… what we ingest, food, drink, medication, supplements… can make the difference between your next salah (prayer) being accepted by God or not, and so it’s a very important matter.” (P10, Female).

This framing positioned medicines on the same religious plane as food, informing how participants evaluated permissibility and dispensation. Participants also contrasted Islamic reasoning with culturally inherited halal–haram binaries, recognising a “doubtful” category that requires consideration.

“You talked about haram… halal… but you also talked about doubtful… In our communities, it’s just haram or halal… there is no middle up for discussion… that’s the problem.” (P9, Male).

This emphasis on careful evaluation was accompanied by a visible commitment to halal as identity in everyday life. Halal thus functioned not only as a dietetic code but also as a lived expression of faith that was interwoven with participants’ sense of self and identity. Maintaining halal dietary practices reinforced group boundaries and difference within the culturally diverse setting of Welsh society.

Theme 2 Motivations for consumption: purity, harm, and semantic framing

Participants framed the classifications of halal in dietary practices not only as a framework of religious permissibility, but also in terms of perceived health and spiritual benefits. They clearly distinguished between their approach to food and their experience of illness. Food was treated as a matter of individual control and preference, reinforcing the expectation of strict halal observance. In contrast, illness was seen as divinely ordained, allowing for greater flexibility in applying halal–haram regulations. Intent and context altered religious categorisation.

“When you’re ill… God wants ease not difficulty.” (P10 Female)

Conversely, haram categories such as pork and alcohol were associated with impurity, harm, and moral repugnance. These sentiments were often expressed through visceral distaste.

“Pork… makes me feel sick just thinking about it… I feel there’s some kind of scientific justification for it.”(P12 Male)

“If you're saying ethanol then okay but as soon as someone mentions wine I feel weird, you know, like if I had chocolate that had liquor I'm going to vomit it out.”(P7 Female)

These accounts align with the Islamic legal concept of khabith (impure), which includes both physical and moral contamination. Alcohol was likewise linked to social harms.

“The reason why alcohol is not allowed is because you get drunk… and you do antisocial behaviour.” (P9 Male).

The notion of tayyib, translated as ‘wholesome’ or ‘pure,’ was invoked as an aspirational benchmark—a ‘halal plus’. While halal represented a ‘theological minimum’ for the permissibility of consumption, tayyib encompassed hygiene, nutrition, ethical sourcing, animal welfare, and environmental sustainability.

“I always try to go somewhere that’s five stars… more tayyib.” (P2 Male)

These accounts positioned consumption as a moral act shaped by Islamic legal tradition and everyday ethics.

Theme 3 Minor illness or major disease: applying necessity and biotransformation

Illness severity, as perceived by participants, shaped decisions surrounding medicine use. The demarcation of illness into minor illness or major disease helped justify whether or not the haram content of medicine was considered acceptable. In chronic or life-threatening cases, participants invoked the principle of darura, often framing acceptance of haram medicines not only as permissible but as religiously required.

“Taking haram medicines… the bottom line for me is that extreme situation… Do I eat pork or do I die? I live (laughs).” (P12 Male)

“If the situation could really bring my sugar level down. I would consider taking it even if it contains something that’s normally haram.” (P6 Male)

Some participants viewed refusing essential treatment as a religious transgression, noting that in serious illness the rule of necessity could set aside dietetic restrictions. This was not automatic. They were dependent on critical factors: sincere intention (niyyah), the availability of religiously acceptable alternatives, and endorsement by an HCP, which provided not only clinical but also religious reassurance.

“If I was really unwell and the GP advised me to take it, I would take it… three four weeks out of action from work or personal activity is not good.” (P13 Male).

Medications containing haram substances were avoided when dealing with minor or self-limiting conditions. In such instances, treatment was frequently postponed. Many chose to let the illness pass naturally or to rely on TAIM or CAM.

“Firstly they can contain like alcohol and gelatine, so I use things like honey and black seed like the prophet taught us.” (P7 Female)

Preventive treatments, such as the intranasal flu vaccine, were also commonly declined—particularly for healthy children-where haram ingredients were involved and the intervention was not perceived as essential:

“Why would I? It has pork… I don’t think every child needs a flu vaccine if they’re healthy…” (P10 Female)

However, a minority took a more lenient position, indicating that despite haram ingredients, they would take the medicine even for minor illnesses. Though rare, these negative cases reflected the gamut of interpretations participants expressed.

“I won't feel guilty having some Covonia® because I got a really bad chesty cough.” (P4 Male).

Several participants described pragmatic strategies to maintain adherence while minimising religious compromise, such as sourcing alternative formulations or removing the internal contents of gelatine-based capsules.

“When it comes to capsules, I know it's got gelatine, so what I do is I open up and have the powder.” (P8 Male).

Participants’ illness classification was shaped by chronicity, financial burden of the illness, symptom severity, and the level of trust they placed in their HCP, shaping adherence behaviour. Thus, chronic conditions or recurrent infections were deemed justifiable under the rule of darura, and when alternatives were unavailable. Conversely, for transient illnesses with symptoms that did not limit activities of daily living or attendance at work, many preferred to avoid non-halal medicines altogether.

Theme 4 Personalised care: faith-sensitive shared decision-making

Participants consistently desired care that recognised and respected their religious concerns. Personalised care was described as treatment that was both clinically and holistically appropriate. Disclosure of ingredients in religious contexts was seen as a matter of ethical responsibility and a legal right.

“It’s a right for everybody to know what they’re taking.” (P11 Male)

Lack of disclosure contributed to mistrust of HCPs and, in some cases, led to non-adherence or changing providers.

“If I found… it had a haram ingredient, I’d definitely just stop taking the medication, and even probably go to another doctor.” (P10 Female)

Participants valued being actively involved in shared decision-making and were more inclined to adhere to haram medications in cases of necessity when HCPs initiated the conversation and demonstrated understanding.

“Yeah, I think it should be discussed… I’d definitely be more inclined to take it if you involved me.” (P7 Female)

There was an expectation for HCPs to possess basic knowledge of Islamic dietary laws and religious sensitivities. Many participants viewed this as an essential component of cultural competence.

“To know a few words and what they mean—halal, haram, tayyib.” (P2 Male)

Where religious considerations were absent, it led to inappropriate care or missed opportunities, particularly during significant periods such as Ramadan fasting. Thus, participants advocated for the integration of religious literacy in both undergraduate pharmacy and medical training as well as continuing professional development (CPD) initiatives.

“It needs to be implemented in training… the pharmacy syllabus or doctors… because we don’t really talk about that topic.” (P9 Male)

Several participants also highlighted inconsistencies in care, such as being offered halal food in hospitals while lacking transparency about medicine ingredients, undermining NHS commitments to equity and inclusion.

“In hospital you’re offered halal food… but when it came to medicine that’s not an option.” (P8 Male)

In addition to calls for transparent excipient disclosure, participants questioned the reliability of halal certification schemes, noting variability between standards and, at times, expressing greater trust in vegetarian or vegan symbols, perceived as more strictly regulated.

“I personally find ‘suitable for vegetarian’ or the vegan sign more reliable than the halal sign sometimes, because halal is not regulated as strictly as ‘suitable for vegetarian’ or ‘vegan,’ legally I mean.” (P8, Male).

For participants, culturally competent care was not simply about language or social customs. It meant recognising their religious identity and moral autonomy as part of the shared decision-making process. Trust was built not only from professional knowledge but also from showing awareness of what mattered most to them.

“Imagine… the doctor asks about how the medicine… can clash with your religion… My respect for them would grow.”(P7 Female)

Discussion

Statement of key findings

This study highlights that Muslim patients’ adherence decisions about religiously prohibited medicines are shaped by principles of Islamic law, personal justifications, the severity of an illness, and trust placed in HCPs. Participants did not apply halal–haram categories rigidly. Case-by-case decisions were made, particularly during serious illness. Islamic principles such as darura and istihala were used to justify temporary dispensations, often reached through personal religious reasoning or consultation with religious authorities [21–23]. Three key concerns emerged: ambiguous labelling, limited transparency about excipient origins, and scepticism towards halal certification standards [45, 46].

Participants valued shared decision-making in which religious values and concerns were acknowledged and alternatives were explored. Care that showed cultural competency and was supported by religious dietetic literacy helped mitigate moral anxiety and engender trust. However, participants reported that Islamic concepts were rarely, if at all, addressed in pharmacy consultations. These findings are set against the backdrop of published fatwa guidance and NHS policy commitments to equitable, culturally responsive care [26, 47–50].

To meet Muslim patients’ ethical and religio-cultural needs, pharmacy teams should be supported to engage with these concerns through structured prompts in electronic health records (EHRs) that flag dietary or religious requirements at the point of prescribing and dispensing. In addition, transparent labelling should go beyond generic ingredient lists to include clear, plain-language disclosure of excipient sources, alongside faith-sensitive communication [51]. Given the globalisation of pharmacy education, embedding religious literacy competencies is relevant across transnational curricula and practice settings [52].

Interpretation and implications

Prior work shows that Muslim patients’ adherence decisions are shaped by perceived illness severity, trust in HCPs, and clarity about excipient sources, with dispensations applied case-by-case [5, 53–55]. The findings are consistent with these patterns and extend the literature by centring Muslim patients’ moral reasoning in routine UK pharmacy care (rather than context-specific scenarios) and by demonstrating how juristic principles are operationalised in day-to-day decisions, pointing to practical levers such as more transparent excipient disclosure and faith-sensitive counselling [16, 22].

This study illustrates how Muslim patients’ adherence decisions about prohibited ingredients were shaped by pharmacological considerations and by an ethical and theological framework rooted in Islamic law. Halal was described as extending beyond dietetic rules to a worldview that encompassed identity, moral decision-making, and daily behaviour. Participants flexibly invoked principles of darura and istihala to justify using prohibited medicines when clinically necessary, consistent with the NCF and juristic maxims that prioritise preservation of life and health [39, 40, 56, 57].

Practical decision-making incorporated formal fatwa guidance with lay religious reasoning informed by experience, trust in HCPs, and community views [21–23]. This aligns with previous findings that Muslim patients draw on doctrinal and contextual cues in health-related decisions [27].

Fatalism, often linked with an external LOC, was reinterpreted by some participants as tawakkul (active trust in the divine) through informed and responsible decision-making. This reflects the view that religious determinism can express agency rather than passivity [58]. Instead of rejecting treatment, participants sought alignment between divine will and medical necessity.

This moral logic also extended into non-adherence, with participants commonly employed CAM and TAIM treatments, such as black seed oil and honey, not as resistance to care but as religiously endorsed alternatives [5, 44].

Comfort with religiously prohibited ingredients was influenced by semantic framing. Greater distaste and guilt were evoked when ingredients were described using terms such as ‘pork’ or ‘wine’ rather than their pharmacological equivalents, like ‘ethanol’ or ‘pancreatin’ [45, 59, 60]. Switching dosage forms and opening gelatine capsules (discarding the shell and ingesting only the contents) were used to minimise perceived religious harm while maintaining adherence. This indicates a need for more explicit excipient labelling and routine guidance on suitable alternatives [61, 62].

Participants expressed pronounced scepticism about halal certification, noting variability between standards and favouring independent checks instead of accepting labels at face value [63, 64]. At the same time, halal practices reinforced religious identity and drew symbolic boundaries that signalled religio-cultural commitment in secular settings [65–67].

In pharmaceutical care, shared decision-making was central; participants regarded disclosure of religiously prohibited ingredients as integral to trust and patient autonomy [68]. They did not expect exhaustive theological knowledge but a “reasonable degree” of religious literacy, including awareness of halal, haram, tayyib, and available alternatives, echoing calls for structured prompts and training in cultural competence [51].

Participants also warned against ecological fallacies. They emphasised intra-Muslim diversity and rejected monolithic assumptions about practice or belief. Instead, they advocated for culturally sensitive, reflexive care that recognises interpretive diversity and religiously aligned interventions [27, 69]. Thus, these findings suggest that, when situated within patients’ moral reasoning, Islamic frameworks can enhance rather than obstruct ethical and inclusive pharmacy practice.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first UK-based qualitative study to examine how Muslim patients apply Islamic frameworks to adherence decisions about medicine containing prohibited ingredients. A key strength is its depth of engagement, achieved through RTA, which captured participants’ interpretive processes and lived experience. Methodological rigour was enhanced by member checking, peer debriefing, journalling, and NVivo to support transparent theme development.

However, some limitations are acknowledged. The small sample size (n = 13) and mosque-based recruitment may have skewed the sample toward more religiously engaged participants. Men were over-represented, which may limit insight into gendered aspects of decision-making [70]. Conducting interviews in English likely excluded some ethnic or linguistic minority participants. The researcher’s positionality as a practising Muslim and pharmacy professional helped build rapport and deepen theological insight, though it may also have shaped interpretive emphasis. This was mitigated through reflexivity and peer debriefing during analysis. In line with RTA, only interview data were analysed. A targeted review of fatwa publications and clinical case material informed the study framing but was not coded as data. Future studies may include document analysis and consultation observations for triangulation.

Transferability depends on local regulation and sources of religious guidance. Within the UK, pharmaceutical excipient disclosure varies, and multiple sources of religious guidance exist; consequently, patients’ choices differ [16, 54, 71]. In Muslim-majority countries such as Malaysia and Saudi Arabia, Islamic jurisprudence can formally inform health-related policy (e.g., via national fatwa councils), so the transferability of these findings may differ [48, 49, 72, 73].

Conclusion

This study contributes to growing evidence in clinical pharmacy literature that religious contexts are significant in how Muslim patients engage with healthcare advice. Adherence was not only guided by behavioural or cognitive factors, but also influenced by moral reasoning grounded in Islamic legal principles. These decisions were shaped through personal decision-making, religious consultation, and dialogue with HCPs. Culturally competent care should therefore include religious literacy, transparent labelling, and faith-sensitive communication, ensuring that pharmacy practice supports ethical reasoning, facilitates adherence, and helps reduce health inequalities for religious minority populations. This study offers important insight into how Islamic beliefs shape medication adherence. Future work should explore the perspectives of women and of Muslims whose medication decisions do not explicitly invoke halal/haram classifications through broader recruitment strategies to inform the development of meaningful, faith-sensitive pharmacy guidance. Longitudinal designs are also needed to examine how religious beliefs and adherence evolve. Finally, collaboration between Islamic scholars, pharmacists and ethicists could produce models grounded in doctrine and lived experience, and inform the design of tailored, religio-culturally sensitive interventions.
