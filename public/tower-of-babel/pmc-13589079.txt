Artificial intelligence in clinical trials—state of the evidence, gaps, and next steps
Authors: Antonis A. Armoundas, Constantine Tarabanis, Joseph Loscalzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryArtificial intelligence (AI) affects clinical trials in two distinct but overlapping ways: as the intervention under evaluation and as infrastructure supporting trial design, recruitment, monitoring, endpoint assessment, analysis, and reporting. In this manuscript, we define AI-as-intervention as AI whose output is itself part of the assigned clinical intervention being evaluated for its effect on participant care or outcomes, and AI-for-trial-operations as AI used to support trial design, conduct, or analysis without itself constituting the treatment under study. This is an important distinction because AI-as-intervention generally requires prospective clinical evaluation with prespecified estimands, prospectively governed model behaviour, and protocol-level oversight, whereas AI-for-trial-operations is often judged by workflow accuracy, impact of the decision, transportability, and safety under real-world constraints, although some uses, such as endpoint support or inferential modelling, may also require similarly explicit change control and oversight. Using this distinction as an analytical framework rather than as a division into separate parts, we examine how AI can improve each phase of the clinical-trial lifecycle, what evidence currently supports these applications, what limitations constrain their validity and transportability, and what methodological and governance safeguards are required. We synthesise evidence across safety, efficacy, operational risk prediction, network medicine, digital health technologies, retrieval-augmented generation, and agentic workflows, while aligning the discussion to ICH E6(R3), ICH E9(R1), and emerging structured-protocol standards.FundingAAA was partly supported by the Institute of Precision Medicine (17UNPG33840017) from the AHA, the RICBAC Foundation, and 10.13039/100000002NIH grants R01HL173935-01, 1 R01 HL161008-01.

Introduction

Once considered an emerging technology with limited application, artificial intelligence (AI) is now discussed across nearly every component of clinical development.1, 2, 3 Yet, the literature often conflates AI-as-intervention (for example, a diagnostic or decision-support algorithm randomised against standard care) with AI-for-trial-operations (for example, modelling of feasibility and eligibility, adjudication support, or centralised monitoring). Those categories overlap but do not share the same burden of evidence: when AI is the intervention under study, the relevant standard is prospective clinical evaluation, including explicit estimands, prospectively governed model behaviour, and protocol-level oversight; when AI supports trial operations, the central questions are more often operational, including workflow performance, impact of the decision, transportability, and safety under real-world constraints, although trial uses that influence endpoint support, extrapolation, or other inferentially relevant functions may also require pre-specified model behaviour, change control, and comparable oversight.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 This distinction becomes especially important in event-driven trials, acute-care enrolment, and settings with low event rates or rapidly evolving standard of care, where apparently strong model performance may not translate into faster or better trials.10 Accordingly, AI-as-intervention and AI-for-trial-operations are used throughout this manuscript as role-based categories rather than as two separate domains: the relevant classification depends on whether the model output forms part of the assigned clinical intervention or supports the design, conduct, or analysis of the trial.

Three processes make AI particularly relevant for clinical trials. First, mechanism: network-medicine approaches that map disease processes and mechanistic pathways can link multi-omics, phenotypes, and exposures to define rational stratification, mechanistic endpoints, and biological enrichment (selection or prioritisation of participants who are more likely to contribute useful trial information).11,12 Second, measurement: digital-health technologies and digitally derived endpoints can provide high-frequency longitudinal measurement of how patients function, feel, and respond between visits, but only if metadata, validation plans, and adjudication pathways are explicit.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Third, efficiency: large language models (LLMs)- and natural language processing (NLP)-based systems can parse eligibility criteria, retrieve protocol information, extract evidence, and support matching, especially when multimodal inputs are paired with retrieval or agentic coordination rather than a single prompt-response model.21, 22, 23, 24 These three pillars, mechanism, measurement, and efficiency, are best understood as complementary, not interchangeable.

This manuscript examines AI across the clinical-trial lifecycle with two linked aims: to synthesise the evidence supporting its use and to translate that evidence into practical methodological and governance guidance. Specifically, we address four questions. First, how can AI improve trial design, recruitment, conduct, endpoint assessment, analysis, and deployment? Second, what prospective, retrospective, and operational evidence currently supports these applications? Third, what limitations constrain decision impact, transportability, fairness, and inferential validity? Fourth, what methodological, regulatory, and governance safeguards are required before AI can influence trial conduct or interpretation? The clinical-trial lifecycle provides the manuscript's primary organisational structure, whereas mechanism, measurement, and efficiency serve as intersecting, interpretive lenses. The distinction between AI-as-intervention and AI-for-trial-operations is applied across this structure according to the role played by the model in each use case. Search strategy

In this opinion-based narrative review, we identified relevant peer-reviewed articles, selected preprints, regulatory and policy documents, and reports of real-world implementation through targeted searches focused on the topics addressed in the manuscript. References were selected based on their relevance to the clinical-trial lifecycle, methodological or regulatory importance, and contribution to the current evidence on the use of artificial intelligence in clinical trials.

Guidelines, oversight and best practices for AI in clinical trials

High-quality AI trials may follow familiar principles, but the governing framework depends on whether AI is being tested as the intervention or used to support trial operations. CONSORT-AI8 and SPIRIT-AI4 are most directly relevant when AI itself is randomised or prospectively evaluated as part of care delivery, whereas TRIPOD-AI25 and PROBAST-AI9 are especially relevant when prediction models are used for enrichment, prescreening, endpoint support, or risk forecasting (Table 1). The final ICH E6(R3) Good Clinical Practice guideline7 refocuses critical-to-quality factors, risk-based quality management, and documented oversight; in practice, this means that AI tools used in design, recruitment, adjudication, or monitoring should be tied to explicit trial processes, predefined responsibilities, and auditable outputs rather than treated as generic software additions (Table 1). CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI are included here to define the evidentiary and reporting standards applicable when AI forms part of the intervention under evaluation; the purpose of this manuscript is not to reproduce those guidelines, but to place them within the broader framework governing how AI is evaluated and used across the clinical-trial lifecycle.

In addition, AI-relevant governance should be anchored to ICH E9(R1) estimands and sensitivity analysis when models influence heterogeneous-treatment-effect analyses, adaptive decisions, or external controls, and to ICH M11 when protocol logic is being made more computable and interoperable.10,26 We also note that ICH M15 is relevant when AI- or model-based methods contribute directly to trial design, extrapolation, endpoint support, simulation, or regulatory interpretation because it provides a harmonised framework for the planning, evaluation, documentation, and communication of model-informed drug development evidence (Table 1).27

For trial operations, the key question is no longer whether a model performs well, but how risk is assessed, controlled, and monitored over time: whether the model is locked or adaptive; what the update cadence and recalibration triggers are; how version control is documented; what is written into the protocol, the statistical analysis plan (SAP), or the monitoring plan; and how the Data and Safety Monitoring Boards (DSMBs) or safety committees will interpret model-assisted recommendations.6,7,9,10,26,28, 29, 30

However, common governance requirements apply across the application domains. Regardless of whether AI is used for recruitment, endpoint support, monitoring, inferential analysis, or retrieval-enabled workflow assistance, credible use in trials depends on five core elements: first, pre-specification of the context of use, task definition, and any decision thresholds or action rules; second, documented provenance, audit trails, version control, and where relevant change-control procedures; third, explicit human accountability, including who can review, override, pause, or retire the tool; fourth, clear roles for DSMBs, endpoint committees, or equivalent oversight bodies when model outputs influence safety, adjudication, interim interpretation, or other relevant decisions; and fifth, lifecycle monitoring for drift, subgroup performance, failure modes, and the need for recalibration or rollback. In later sections we apply these same principles in specific contexts, but they can also be understood as components of this single governance framework.

Accountability in AI-driven clinical decisions

Accountability in AI-driven clinical decisions should be made explicit. When AI informs screening, matching, monitoring, adjudication, or escalation, the person responsible for each decision should be identifiable at each step, together with the committee, sponsor, investigator, or vendor role that governs model selection, deployment, updating, and error review.1,2,31, 32, 33, 34 In practice, this means that model outputs should be treated as decision support rather than autonomous decisions, with documented provenance, version control, prespecified action thresholds, and clear human override pathways.2 Accountability also requires that trial documents specify who reviews the alerts, who can pause or retire the tool, how disagreements between model output and clinical judgement are resolved, and how these decisions are recorded for audit and regulatory review.32 Without such role clarity and responsibility can become diffused across investigators, institutions, and technology vendors, weakening both participant protection and the interpretability of trial decisions.

Regulatory differences and emerging global frameworks

In the United States, the FDA's current approach to AI-enabled medical devices is comparatively product- and lifecycle-specific, with recent guidance emphasising total product lifecycle management, premarket documentation, and predetermined change control for post-market model modification.29,30 By contrast, the European Medicines Agency's (EMA's) paper addresses AI across the broader medicinal-product lifecycle and situates its use within EU medicines law, data-protection requirements, and the wider European legal framework.35 The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency similarly regulates software and AI as medical devices through a structured change-control framework, but has also paired guidance with the AI Airlock sandbox to explore evidentiary and implementation questions for Artificial Intelligence as a Medical Device in practice.36, 37, 38 Emerging global frameworks are more principle-based than jurisdiction-specific: the International Medical Device Regulators Forum finalised the Good Machine Learning Practice principles in 2025,39 WHO guidance emphasises ethics, human oversight, transparency, and equity,40 and FDA and EMA have jointly articulated good AI practice principles for drug development.41 Accordingly, trials should align each AI tool with the regulatory framework most appropriate to its intended use and should specify the corresponding requirements for documentation, change control, monitoring, and oversight.

Ethical and legal challenges

Ethical and legal questions should be treated as core design issues rather than downstream implementation concerns. Even when AI is used only to support trial operations, rather than as the intervention under study, it can still influence who is screened, invited, enrolled, monitored, escalated, or adjudicated.31 That application creates obligations around transparency, accountability, privacy, fairness, and human oversight.33 In practice, the key issues are not only whether participants should be informed that AI is being used, but also whether the system's role is sufficiently defined to permit audit, challenge, and correction when errors occur. These concerns become more acute when retrieval-enabled or agentic systems are introduced into regulated workflows, because responsibility may be blurred across sponsors, investigators, vendors, and institutions.32 Accordingly, legally and ethically acceptable deployment requires explicit assignment of decision rights, documentation of provenance and versioning, predefined human override pathways, and prospective monitoring for subgroup harms or unjustified exclusions.31 Efficiency gains are ethically meaningful only when they are achieved without weakening participant protections, obscuring accountability, or introducing inequitable access to trial opportunities.31

Current evidence across the trial lifecycle

AI can contribute at each phase of the clinical-trial lifecycle. During design, it can support feasibility assessment, accrual and duration forecasting, site selection, enrichment, and mechanistically informed endpoint selection. During recruitment, it can translate eligibility criteria into computable logic and prioritise potentially eligible participants. During trial conduct, it can identify operational anomalies, protocol deviations, missingness patterns, and emerging safety signals. During endpoint assessment, it can derive digital measures and support event adjudication. During analysis, it can examine treatment-effect heterogeneity, external comparisons, and evidence synthesis. During deployment and follow-up, it can support drift detection, recalibration, version control, and governed updating. Whether a particular system constitutes AI-as-intervention or AI-for-trial-operations depends on how its output is used, rather than on the underlying technology alone. Protocol design, feasibility and early risk prediction

AI can quantify feasibility risks before the first enrolled participant by predicting accrual, duration, and probability of early termination based on the features of the trial, epidemiology of the disease, and historical site-performance.42, 43, 44, 45, 46 These tools are most credible when they are used to inform evaluation under alternative assumptions, for example, whether an event-driven study is underpowered because endpoint rates are lower than assumed, or whether an acute-care trial is unlikely to recruit within the window demanded by a time-sensitive consent or due to workflow volatility.10,42, 43, 44, 45, 46 What such models cannot do is eliminate required observation periods: if a study depends on slow event accrual, long follow-up, or exposure-dependent safety observation, AI can improve forecasting and enrichment but it cannot eliminate those temporal constraints. Machine learning (ML) frameworks have also been developed to forecast site-level recruitment performance using historical site metrics and real-world patient distributions, supporting data-driven site prioritisation.44 Uncertainty-aware deep learning models have been introduced that generate probabilistic enrolment and duration estimates, providing interval-based forecasts rather than point estimates.29 AI-based feasibility models can be further extended through digital twins47 by generating patient-specific counterfactual trajectories, effectively providing n-of-1 simulations that test protocol variations and enrichment strategies before a site is activated.48 Overall, prospective decision-impact remains to be shown, as a scoping review including 142 studies noted that most models remain retrospective and restricted to individual risk domains.3

In practice, these tools are best used for scenario planning and site prioritisation rather than as substitutes for the temporal constraints imposed by event accrual, follow-up, and safety concerns.

Recruitment, eligibility and representativeness

NLP and LLM tools can convert free-text eligibility criteria into digital logic and match patients to trials.21, 22, 23, 24 In practice, however, performance must be interpreted operationally. For example, the randomised evaluation of AI-assisted heart-failure prescreening showed that retrieval-enabled LLM workflows can reduce both missed eligibility and coordinator workload, but only when it is coupled to clinician review, site-specific workflow integration, and clear error-mitigation steps.49 These details matter most in acute or unplanned care settings, where messy documentation, evolving medication lists, and compressed recruitment windows make even strong retrospective matching performance difficult to operationalise prospectively.

Criteria2Query and its successors parse eligibility into Structured Query Language. Recent systems such as TrialMatchAI use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to process structured and unstructured data, retrieve relevant trials via hybrid search, re-rank candidates, and apply chain-of-thought reasoning for criterion-level assessments. PRISM similarly uses LLMs to interpret patient records and eligibility criteria for semantic clinical trial matching on real-world oncology data.20, 21, 22, 23 Real-world evaluation of TrialMatchAI showed that 92% of oncology patients appeared somewhere in the first 20 trial recommendations suggested by the model and that expert assessment confirmed >90% accuracy.21 A randomised evaluation of a RAG-enabled GPT-4 prescreening system for heart failure trials demonstrated that AI-assisted eligibility review achieved accuracy comparable to experienced coordinators, reduced false-negative screening errors, and increased prescreening throughput by more than twofold while maintaining transparent, citation-linked reasoning.29,33 Site-selection and accrual-prediction models also help prioritise sites and allocate outreach resources, with early studies reporting performance gains against historical standards.25,26 Fairness remains a key concern; demographic representation metrics should be tracked at screening and randomisation, and non-safety-critical criteria should be adaptively relaxed under human supervision.

In practice, these systems are most useful as clinician-supervised prescreening tools, particularly when the performance is evaluated by missed eligibility, workload, and subgroup fairness.

Trial conduct, risk-based monitoring and pharmacovigilance

Centralised, risk-based monitoring is now mainstream in guidance, and AI can enhance signal detection in operational data (queries, missingness, protocol deviations) and clinical safety data.33,50, 51, 52 Adoption of risk-based quality management remains uneven, but statistical monitoring is increasingly used. Operational implementation is also occurring outside academic research settings. Novartis developed the Nerve Live platform, including its SENSE interface, to integrate historical and current operational data and apply predictive analytics to clinical-development oversight.53 AI-assisted centralised monitoring is also available in contract research organisation workflows. IQVIA's risk-based monitoring model combines remote centralised monitoring with targeted analytics,54 while Medidata Detect uses automated statistical algorithms and machine learning to identify patient-, site-, and study-level risks and anomalies.55 Separately, Worldwide Clinical Trials has announced enterprise implementation of Medidata AI across the clinical-trial lifecycle.56 These examples demonstrate implementation at organisational scale, although company- and vendor-reported experience should not be interpreted as independent evidence that these platforms improve participant safety, data quality, or trial duration. Beyond these centralised-monitoring applications, AI is also being used in pharmacovigilance; NLP and multimodal models extract adverse events from electronic health records and registries, and resources such as CT-ADE provide benchmarking datasets. CT-ADE offers a large-scale, evaluation-ready benchmark built directly from clinical-trial results, and includes baseline evaluations using LLMs, which demonstrated that incorporating treatment and population context substantially improves adverse drug event prediction performance over chemical-structure-only models.34 Additional resources such as Side Effect Resource (SIDER) and FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), when linked with trial data, could provide AI-ready standardised adverse-event annotations that strengthen signal evaluation, particularly for class effects.35 Pre-specifying adverse-event ontologies, logging of model versions and thresholds, and embedding human review for severe outcomes could ensure these systems remain auditable and safe for deployment in AI-driven pharmacovigilance workflows.

The important trial question is not only whether a safety model detects an adverse-event pattern, but how quickly the signal becomes clinically actionable, who reviews it, and whether the alert reduces harm or merely increases query volume. This is why adverse-event signal latency, decision thresholds, human review for serious events, and escalation pathways should be pre-specified rather than discovered ad hoc after deployment.7,51,52 Linking pooled trial safety data with pharmacovigilance resources can strengthen signal evaluation and class-effect reasoning, but those linkages require careful provenance, shared terminologies, and awareness that post-market reporting systems capture different populations and incentives that differ from those in randomised trials.34,35

In practice, deployment should prioritise predefined thresholds, rapid human review, and auditable escalation pathways.

Endpoints: digital health technologies-derived measures, digital surrogates, and automated event adjudication

Digitally derived endpoints, such as passive gait speed, respiratory patterns, arrhythmic burden, or step-level fatigue, can reduce participant burden and increase measurement density, but their value depends on endpoint specification rather than sensor novelty.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Here, endpoint specification means that the endpoint (for example, of a clinical concept, a derivation from raw or digital data, an assessment, a threshold, the handling of missing or poor-quality data, etc) is prespecified and clinically interpretable. Without this level of definition, ambiguous endpoints do not become more valid when measured digitally; rather, the uncertainty is shifted from coordinators or endpoint committees to the algorithm itself. For that reason, digital health technology (DHT)-based measures should be embedded in prespecified endpoint definitions, validation plans, and exception-handling workflows that explain when physician adjudication remains necessary.13,16,20,57,58 This is especially relevant in cardiovascular trials, where AI-assisted adjudication of heart-failure hospitalisations has already shown that NLP pipelines can reduce manual review while preserving agreement with clinician committees, yet still benefit from explicit clinical-endpoint-committee rules, source-document standards, and audit trails.57,58

More recent approaches that combine NLP with LLMs may improve workflow flexibility and portability across multicenter datasets, but claims of generalisability, reproducibility, and interpretability require task-specific validation against adjudicated reference standards.20, 21, 22, 23, 24 In high-consequence trial settings, performance evaluation should prospectively specify the endpoint definition, reference labels, prompt/model/tool versions, and handling of uncertain or missing cases, and should assess not only aggregate discrimination but also calibration or confidence levels where relevant, subgroup performance, transportability across sites, temporal robustness, and clinically important failure modes.32 On this basis, LLM-enabled adjudication may complement digital endpoints by supporting more consistent and scalable event classification, but only when these systems remain limited in scope, auditable, and subject to predefined human review pathways.

In practice, DHT- and LLM-enabled endpoint workflows are most credible when embedded in pre-specified endpoint definitions, validation plans, and exception-handling rules.

Analysis: heterogeneous treatment effects, external controls, and evidence synthesis

AI helps uncover heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs) using interpretable models, but complete and transparent reporting (TRIPOD-AI, PROBAST-AI) is essential.59,60 A recent scoping review of 32 randomised trials found that causal-forest and related ML algorithms are now the most commonly applied methods for HTE estimation in RCTs and that analysts increasingly use effect-modelling frameworks to derive individualised treatment effects.59 In one example, a causal-tree analysis of the DECAAF II randomised trial explored a potential age-based differential benefit from fibrosis-guided ablation, illustrating how causal ML can help generate structured, hypothesis-generating insights about possible treatment–response heterogeneity, even when intention-to-treat results are null.

Any AI-assisted subgrouping or individualised-effect analysis should be mapped to an ICH E9(R1) estimand, including how intercurrent events, such as treatment switching, discontinuation, rescue therapy, or background polytherapy, are handled analytically and interpreted clinically.10 The same methodological requirements apply to external control arms: pre-specification, data provenance, confounding control, overlap diagnostics, calibration, and sensitivity analyses are not optional regulatory additions; they determine whether an external comparison is scientifically interpretable.61, 62, 63, 64 Moreover, HTE modelling raises conventional statistical concerns that deserve fuller treatment than the AI literature often gives them, including multiplicity, false discovery, alpha control, SAP integration, and DSMB oversight when model outputs could affect adaptive decisions.10,59,60

In practice, AI-based HTE and external-control methods should be used as trial-methodologic tools only when they are tied to explicit estimands, sensitivity analyses, and pre-specified governance.

Continuous learning and post-deployment monitoring

For AI-enabled interventions and operational tools, the protocol should state whether the model will remain locked, undergo periodic recalibration, or be updated adaptively under predefined conditions.6,7,9,28, 29, 30 The lifecycle plan should identify who authorises changes, how versions are documented, what shadow-mode evaluation is required, and what drift thresholds, rollback triggers, and freeze periods apply around interim analyses and database lock.31,32 These requirements apply to both open and proprietary systems; the central question is whether the model version influencing each trial decision remains traceable and governed.

These governance questions are especially important when open academic algorithms are embedded into regulated workflows or when proprietary tools are used under contracts that obscure retraining data and change logs.31,32 In both cases, traceability and accountability matter more than whether the code is open or closed. Selective, human-in-the-loop alerting architectures illustrate how modest declines in specificity can be surfaced over weeks and converted into incremental clinical value, rather than remaining hidden until formal revalidation.20 In practice, these systems require predefined update rules, drift monitoring, and version freezes around key trial milestones.

Taken together, these examples define the current state of the evidence: AI is strongest where it improves efficiency or supports structured measurement tasks, and less mature where stronger mechanistic claims, broader transportability, or prospective decisions are required. Evidence gaps and implementation limitations

General limitations

The principal constraints in many trials are not computational. Endpoint latency, low event rates, mandated follow-up, minimum safety exposure, supply-chain logistics, contracting, and regulator assurance cannot be shortened simply because screening or modelling is faster. AI may help trials improve trial initiation, recruitment, adjudication, and interim analysis from interim data, but it does not exempt investigators from waiting for biological effects, exposure time, and clinically meaningful outcomes to occur. That distinction should temper overly broad claims that AI will make all trials dramatically shorter.

A second limitation is evidentiary; most published models remain retrospective, single-domain, and evaluated on convenience datasets that do not capture the institutional bottlenecks of live trials: delayed data access, incomplete source data, manual contract amendments, coordinator staffing limits, or cross-site heterogeneity in documentation.3,42, 43, 44, 45, 46,49 As a result, transportability is often assumed rather than demonstrated, and reported gains in accuracy do not necessarily translate into faster enrolment, fewer protocol deviations, or better safety oversight.

Data quality, multimodality, and metadata

A third limitation concerns multimodality and metadata; the potential of combining EHR data, omics, imaging, wearables, and protocol text is real, but these data streams differ in missingness, cadence, adjudication pathway, and regulatory status.11,13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Without explicit provenance, synchronisation rules, and context-aware handling of unstructured data, multimodal AI can increase model complexity while decreasing interpretability and reproducibility.

Data quality requires explicit evaluation because many apparent AI-mediated gains in trials are inseparable from the quality of the underlying data stream. Trial data differ not only in completeness, but also in how outcomes are defined, when measurements are taken, how missingness arises, and whether labels are adjudicated consistently across sites.14,17, 18, 19 These problems are amplified when routinely collected data are combined with prospectively collected trial data, since timing, coding practices, and clinical context may differ substantially between them. For multimodal systems, additional vulnerabilities include clock misalignment across devices and information systems, device or firmware changes, inconsistent preprocessing, site-specific documentation practices, and label noise in unstructured text. Strengthening data quality, therefore, requires prespecified provenance, harmonised metadata, explicit handling of missingness and drift, adjudication standards for key labels, and continuous verification that the inputs remain appropriate for the intended trial use.

A fourth limitation is methodological integration; many AI papers mention adaptive designs, external controls, or personalised effects without fully engaging estimands, intercurrent events, multiplicity, calibration, sensitivity analyses, or the governance role of DSMBs and endpoint committees.10,59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64 These are essential methodological requirements; they are the mechanisms by which a trial protects inference from operational complexity.

Finally, lifecycle governance for continuously learning systems remains immature.31,32 Predetermined change-control plans, version freezes around interim analyses, drift monitoring, rollback policies, and documentation standards are inconsistently reported, especially when open academic tools are transferred into institutional or sponsor-operated workflows.31,32 That gap is particularly important if agentic or retrieval-enabled systems are introduced into regulated trial operations.

Algorithmic bias and health inequities

Algorithmic bias and health inequities require explicit consideration because AI used for trial operations can influence screening, invitation, enrolment, monitoring, and ultimately be represented in the evidence base, even when AI is not the intervention under study.1,2,65,66 Bias can enter through unrepresentative training data, differential missingness, site-specific documentation practices, language and access barriers, and multimodal data streams that vary in quality and interoperability across subgroups.1,13,16,65 If prescreening, matching, or adjudication tools have lower sensitivity in underrepresented populations, the result is not only uneven model performance but also inequitable access to trial participation and a less generalisable evidence base.1,65,66 For that reason, fairness should be monitored prospectively at each step of screening, invitation, consent, randomisation, and outcome ascertainment, with prespecified subgroup performance reporting, denominator tracking, and predefined remediation when missed-eligibility or screening-failure patterns concentrate in a particular subgroup.65,66 Legally and ethically, such safeguards align with expectations for transparency, accountability, human oversight, and lifecycle monitoring under good-clinical-practice and emerging AI-governance frameworks, particularly when AI influences access to trial opportunity or downstream safety review.65,66

Data ownership and informed consent for AI-assisted trials

Data ownership and informed consent require explicit consideration in AI-assisted trials.33 When AI systems are used to support screening, matching, monitoring, adjudication, or evidence synthesis, investigators should specify who controls access to the underlying data, who can reuse derived outputs, whether third-party vendors or retrieval systems receive trial-related information, and whether model outputs become part of the trial record or source documentation.33 Consent language should also be proportionate to the role and risk of the AI tool: participants should be told whether AI is being used in recruitment, eligibility assessment, monitoring, or endpoint support; what categories of data are being processed; whether external data sources are queried; and what human oversight remains in place.31 Where secondary use, model refinement, or future reuse of data is contemplated, the governance framework should distinguish legal ownership from stewardship and access rights, while ensuring privacy protections, audit trails, and contractual controls remain aligned with good-clinical-practice and emerging AI-governance expectations.33 These issues are material governance considerations: they affect participant autonomy, institutional accountability, and the legitimacy of AI-assisted trial decisions.1,32,67

Agentic AI in clinical trials: evidence, validation challenges, and governance

Agentic AI refers to systems that plan, retrieve data, and revise outputs over multiple steps rather than producing a single static response. Unlike static predictive models, these systems can orchestrate multiple sub-components, LLMs, domain-specific predictors, simulators, and databases, with human oversight. For these reasons, its role in trials is evaluated by the same criteria applied throughout this manuscript: whether its evidence base is prospective or retrospective, whether it is influencing trial operations or acting more directly on care, and what that implies for protocol-level governance, change control, logging, human override, and committee oversight. In clinical trials, the evidence base for such systems remains narrow. It is therefore more accurate to describe agentic AI as an emerging architectural pattern than as a mature, deployed standard of practice.

Performance validation is particularly challenging for agentic systems because failure may arise not only in the final output, but also in intermediate steps such as retrieval, normalisation, ranking, etc. For that reason, evaluation in trial settings should occur at both individual component and end-to-end levels, with prespecified task definitions, locked or version-controlled prompts and tools, adjudicated reference cases, subgroup and site-specific analyses, and explicit assessment of retrieval fidelity, tool-use reliability, consistency, logging completeness, and the safety of escalation and override pathways.32 Generic benchmark performance is insufficient for high-regulatory-impact uses; what matters is whether the system performs reliably under the documented variability, missingness, temporal drift, and institutional constraints of real trial workflows.32 Until such evidence is available, agentic systems should be best used as limited-scope decision-support tools in shadow mode or under direct human oversight rather than as autonomous components of enrolment, adjudication, monitoring, or safety decision-making.32

The most credible near-term link between agentic AI and multimodality is not autonomy but coordinated handling of heterogeneous inputs: structured EHR fields, notes, protocol text, imaging summaries, omics features, and safety logs that must be retrieved, normalised, and reasoned over in a traceable sequence.21,64,68, 69, 70 This can reduce loss of context compared with single-prompt systems, but the current supporting literature is still dominated by prototypes, arXiv papers,61, 62, 63, 64,68, 69, 70, 71 and narrow task evaluations. While specific model names may quickly become outdated, retrieved and structured protocol logic can improve protocol interpretation, patient-trial matching, and evidence assembly, which is grounded in transparent and auditable data sources.21,26

Accordingly, the near-term role for agentic AI in trials is best framed as constrained decision-support systems: tools that draft eligibility logic, summarise protocol deviations, prioritise safety review, or assemble evidence for human adjudicators and monitors. These are valuable uses, but they are not equivalent to letting an autonomous system determine enrolment, adapt a preplanned threshold used to decide whether a trial or trial arm is unlikely to succeed and might reasonably be stopped early, or change an endpoint definition. Trial-specific governance should therefore focus on task thresholds, provenance, human override, and what decision rights remain with investigators, committees, and sponsors.

Governance and practical guardrails

In clinical trials, generative AI introduces risks beyond those of conventional locked models, including non-deterministic outputs, prompt- and retrieval-dependence, opaque provenance, and more frequent multidimensional updates, such that apparently similar inputs may yield different recommendations or evidentiary summaries over time. Accordingly, it should be confined to well-defined, auditable tasks and used only with prespecified operating limits, structured logging of inputs/prompts/versions, human oversight for high-impact actions, and lifecycle change-control and drift monitoring before it influences enrolment, adjudication, monitoring, or safety-related decisions.32

Agentic systems increase the risks for safety and transparency because they can connect multiple actions. To be acceptable in trials, they should have predefined scope, controlled access, authenticated logs, role-based approval for relevant actions, and prospective evaluation plans that include trial-methodologic oversight beyond generic software-safety principles. In practice, any system that could influence enrolment, adjudication, interim interpretation, or protocol deviation management should be tied to the protocol, SAP, monitoring plan, and DSMB or endpoint-committee responsibilities before deployment.

Structured, computable protocol content as envisioned in ICH M11 can make these data flows more interoperable across sponsors, sites, and regulators.26•Patient privacy Patient privacy deserves explicit discussion in relation to federated learning. Federated learning can support multi-site model development or validation without centralising raw patient data, making it attractive for trials conducted across institutions with legal or governance barriers to data pooling.72,73 However, privacy is not preserved by default: model updates can still leak information, so additional safeguards such as secure aggregation (allows a central server to compute only the aggregate model update across sites, without revealing each site's individual contribution) or differential privacy (limits what can be learnt about any one individual by adding controlled noise to data, model updates, or outputs), may be needed. Although federated learning may reduce the need to centralise raw patient data, privacy alone does not make a model suitable for trial use. Conclusions

AI can improve clinical trials, but only when it is embedded in the trial methodology. The field is best supported in computable eligibility, prescreening, adjudication support, and certain forms of risk-based monitoring; it is less developed where causal interpretation, estimands, lifecycle governance, and real-world implementation under institutional constraints are most demanding. The next phase should therefore emphasise prospective studies that evaluate the impact of decisions, interoperable protocol logic, explicit change-control policies, and trial-specific oversight for retrieval-enabled and agentic systems. AI may accelerate learning, but trustworthy trials still depend on careful design, human accountability, and time for outcomes to emerge.Outstanding questions1.How should prospective studies determine whether AI improves decision impact across trial design, recruitment, monitoring, endpoint assessment, and analysis, rather than merely improving predictive accuracy?2.Which study designs, reference standards, and outcome measures are required to establish the transportability, calibration, workflow benefit, safety, and cost-effectiveness of AI across sites, populations, and time?3.How should protocols and statistical analysis plans govern locked, periodically recalibrated, and adaptively updated AI systems, including version control, drift thresholds, shadow-mode evaluation, rollback criteria, and model freezes around interim analyses and database lock?4.How can fairness and representativeness be measured and protected prospectively across screening, invitation, enrolment, monitoring, and outcome ascertainment, particularly when data quality, documentation, and access differ across population subgroups?5.What prospective evidence and governance standards are required before multimodal and agentic AI systems can progress from bounded, human-supervised decision support to broader roles in trial conduct without compromising accountability, participant safety, or inferential validity?
