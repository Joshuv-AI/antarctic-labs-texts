Partial Redundancy in Saturation
Márton Hajdu (✉), Laura Kovács, Andrei Voronkov

Abstract
Redundancy elimination is one of the crucial ingredients of efficient saturation-based proof search. We improve redundancy elimination by introducing a new notion of redundancy, based on partial clauses and redundancy formulas , which is more powerful than the standard notion: there are both clauses and inferences that are redundant when we use our notions and not redundant when we use standard notions. In a way, our notion blurs the distinction between redundancy at the level of inferences and redundancy at the level of clauses. We present a superposition calculus PaRC on partial clauses. Our calculus is refutationally complete and is strong enough to capture some standard restrictions of the superposition calculus. We discuss the implementation of the calculus in the theorem prover Vampire . Our experiments show the power of the new approach: we were able to solve 24 TPTP problems not previously solved by any prover, including previous versions of Vampire .

Introduction

Modern theorem provers for first-order logic with equality use variants of the
superposition calculus

[
37
,
28
,
6
]
.
A key ingredient in superposition reasoning is the use of
simplification orders
on terms and clauses.
Efficiency is achieved by using a powerful concept of
redundancy
.
For example, it is known that a clause that is a logical consequence of smaller clauses in the search space can be removed from the search space. While checking redundancy is undecidable, there are special cases, such as demodulation, which are easy to check and are widely used.
The efficiency of superposition is evidenced by the practical performance of saturation-based provers, including E
[
38
]
,
Vampire

[
24
]
, and iProver
[
22
]
.

Standard completeness arguments of superposition calculi with redundancy are done by studying the behavior of the set of ground instances of clauses in the search space
[
28
]
.
When all ground instances are redundant, the clause becomes redundant.
In this paper, we propose a powerful generalization of standard redundancy, called
partial redundancy
.
In a nutshell, the idea is the following.
Each superposition inference produces a clause that makes some set of ground instances in the search space redundant. Since redundant ground instances remain redundant during saturation, some clauses become eventually redundant in the standard sense, while others that have some redundant ground instances, become
partially redundant
.

To capture this idea, we introduce the concept of
redundancy formula
, which can be attached to a clause
C
and express the fact that all ground instances of
C
that satisfy a redundancy formula
R
are redundant. A
partial clause

C\setminus R
is a clause with a redundancy formula attached to it. Our technique allows one to reduce the search space in the following two ways; neither of these steps can be done using the standard methods:

1.

We can change redundancy formulas to weaker ones to make a clause eventually redundant. For example, we can start with a clause
C
, change it to
C\setminus R_{1}
, then to
C\setminus R_{1}\lor R_{2}
, etc. If at any step we have a redundancy formula
R_{1}\lor\ldots\lor R_{n}
, which is a tautology, the clause
C
is redundant.

2.

Suppose that we apply an inference to a partial clause
C\setminus R
, which requires an application of a substitution
\sigma
to
C
. If
R\sigma
is a tautology, then all instances of this inference are redundant and the inference does not have to be performed at all.

Motivating example.

Let us illustrate our work with the following example. Consider binary function symbols
f
and
h
, a unary function symbol
g
, and constants
a
and
b
. Let
\succ
denote a Knuth-Bendix simplification order
[
21
]
with constant weight function and precedence
f\gg g\gg h
. Let
C
be the clause
f(g(y),y)\approx g(h(x,y))
. The sides of the equation in
C
are incomparable w.r.t.
\succ
, so standard superposition reasoning has to consider both orientations of this equation. For example, for an arbitrary substitution
\sigma
that grounds
C
, superposition orients the equation in
C
right-to-left only if
x\sigma\succ y\sigma
holds. Now, consider the clause
h(z,u)\approx h(u,z)
and the following inference:

h(z,u)\approx h(u,z)

f(g(y),y)\approx g(h(x,y))

f(g(y),y)\approx g(h(y,x))

We can easily show that in the presence of
h(z,u)\approx h(u,z)
and
f(g(y),y)\approx g(h(y,x))
, the right-to-left orientation of the equation in
C
is not needed anymore, since all instances
C\sigma
s.t.
x\sigma\succ y\sigma
are now redundant. In this case, we replace
C
with the partial clause
C\setminus x\succ y
in the search space. As the right-to-left orientation of the equation in
C
is not needed anymore (item 2 above), we can simply remove the right-hand side of this equation from all term indices, avoiding potentially thousands of unnecessary inferences.

A binary relation

We use the standard definition of a

Many first-order theorem provers work with clauses

3
The Partial Redundancy Calculus

We introduce the notion of
partial redundancy
and describe a general calculus, called PaRC, based on it. Soundness and completeness of PaRC are proved in Sections
4
–
5
.

Let us first define the key notions of redundancy formulas and partial clauses.
We assume to have a finite signature
\Sigma
consisting of function symbols and having at least one constant. As usual, the
term algebra
over
\Sigma
, denoted by
T(\Sigma)
, is the algebra with the universe consisting of the ground terms built from symbols in
\Sigma
and having
\Sigma
as the signature, such that the interpretation of every ground term
t
in
T(\Sigma)
is
t
itself. We will consider
extensions of
T(\Sigma)
by a finite set
\mathcal{R}
of predicate symbols
, each one having a fixed interpretation. We will denote these extensions by
T_{\mathcal{R}}(\Sigma)
and have their interpretations as
redundancy relations
.

Let us assume that we have a simplification order
\succ
on terms. We consider
\succ
to be a redundancy relation and assume that
\mathcal{R}
contains the symbol
\succ
interpreted as this simplification order. In general, we can also consider redundancy relations different from orderings. For the moment, we will leave the choice of suitable redundancy relations open. In a way,
T_{\mathcal{R}}(\Sigma)
is a Herbrand interpretation, with the difference that relations in it are not taken from clauses.

Definition 1
(Redundancy Formula and Partial Clause)

A
redundancy formula

R
is an arbitrary first-order formula of
T_{\mathcal{R}}(\Sigma)
.
A
partial clause
is an expression
C\setminus R
, where
C
is a clause and
R
a redundancy formula
whose free variables occur in
C
. If
\sigma
is a substitution and
R
a redundancy formula, we write
\sigma\vDash R
if every ground instance of
R\sigma
is true in
T_{\mathcal{R}}(\Sigma)
. We write
\sigma\nvDash R
if
\sigma\vDash R
does not hold.

❏

An example of a redundancy formula is
f(x)\succ y\lor y=x
. Note that we can use equality
=
in redundancy formulas as in this example.
In the sequel, we denote redundancy formulas by
R
, and partial clauses by
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
, possibly with indices. We sometimes write
C
instead of
C\setminus\bot
.
Logically, a partial clause
C\setminus R
is the same as
C
. The intuitive semantics of partial clauses comes from their use in saturation: when a partial clause
C\setminus R
occurs in a search space, we know that all ground instances of
C
that satisfy
R
are redundant in this search space.

Let us introduce some abbreviations for redundancy formulas. Let
s
,
t
and

be terms. We will write

for

;

is an equality literal, we write
(s\mathrel{\dot{\approx}}t)\succ l
for

and
(s\mathrel{\dot{\approx}}t)\succeq l
for

is a clause

, we write

for
L_{1}\succ l\lor\ldots\lor L_{n}\succ l
, and

for

We assume a literal selection function satisfying the standard condition on

4
Saturation with PaRC

This section explains how saturation algorithms work on partial clauses and concludes completeness of PaRC. We assume a fixed
signature
\Sigma
.

Definition 2
(Redundant Clause)

A ground clause
D
is
redundant w.r.t. a set of clauses

S
,
if there are clauses
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\in S
s.t.
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\vDash D
and
D\succ C_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
❏

Let
C
be a clause. Then
C^{*}
denotes the set of all ground instances of
C
. For a set
S
of clauses, the set
S^{*}
is defined as
\bigcup_{C\in S}C^{*}
.

Definition 3
(Saturation)

A
saturation

\mathbb{S}
is a sequence of sets of partial clauses
S_{0},S_{1},\ldots,
such that
S_{0}
is a set of partial clauses of the form
C\setminus\bot
, and for each
i
, we have one of the following cases:

1.

S_{i+1}
is obtained by adding to
S_{i}
the conclusion of a PaRC inference with premises in
S_{i}
. We then say that we
apply this inference at the step

S_{i+1}
.

2.

S_{i+1}
is obtained from
S_{i}
by changing a partial clause
C\setminus R_{1}
to
C\setminus R_{1}\lor R_{2}
, such that for every substitution
\theta
grounding for
C
, if
\theta\vDash R_{2}
, then
C\theta
is redundant w.r.t.
S^{*}_{i}
.

We call clauses in
S_{0}
the
initial clauses
of this saturation. We also refer to steps which apply an inference as
inference steps
and steps that update redundancy formulas as
redundancy steps
.
❏

To prove completeness of PaRC, we introduce notions of fairness and persistent ground clauses, which are modifications of similar notions used in completeness proofs of superposition calculi
[
28
]
. In the sequel, we assume that
\mathbb{S}
is a saturation
S_{0},S_{1},\ldots
and denote by
S_{\omega}
the set
\bigcup_{i\geq 0}S_{i}
. Let
C\setminus R
be a partial clause and
S
a set of partial clauses. We define

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(C\setminus R)^{\circledast}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textsf{def}}}{{=}}&\{C\theta\mid C\theta\text{ is ground and }\theta\nvDash R\};\\[4.30554pt]
S^{\circledast}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textsf{def}}}{{=}}&\bigcup_{\mathcal{C}\in S}\mathcal{C}^{\circledast}.\end{array}

Definition 4
(Limit and Persistent Clause)

The
limit
of a saturation
\mathbb{S}
is the set of ground clauses
\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\bigcap_{j\geq i}S^{\circledast}_{j}
. We call a ground clause
C

persistent
if
C
belongs to the limit.
❏

It is not hard to argue that a ground clause
C
is persistent if and only if there exists
i\geq 0
such that for all
j\geq i
we have
C\in S^{\circledast}_{j}
.

Definition 5
(Fair Saturation)

A saturation
\mathbb{S}
is
fair
if it has the following property.
Suppose that

1.

D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
are persistent ground clauses;

2.

there exists an inference
C_{1}\setminus R_{1},\ldots,C_{n}\setminus R_{n}\to C\sigma
and a substitution
\rho
such that for all
i=1,\ldots,n
we have that
D_{i}=C_{i}\sigma\rho
.

Then
\mathbb{S}
contains a step
i
such that
C\sigma\setminus\bot\in S_{i}
.
❏

Note that our notion of fairness is not a standard one: it reflects the fact that the redundancy formula attached to a clause
C
may change when we apply redundancy steps.
Indeed, we can have a partial clause
C\setminus R_{1}
that can be replaced by
C\setminus R_{1}\lor R_{2}
, then replaced by
C\setminus R_{1}\lor R_{2}\lor R_{3}
, and so on.
Our notion of fairness requires that we apply the rule to
some
of these partial clauses, but not necessarily to
C\setminus R_{1}
.

Our goal is to prove soundness and completeness of the PaRC calculus.

Theorem 4.1
(Completeness)

Let
\mathbb{S}
be a fair saturation
S_{0},S_{1},\ldots
. Then,
S_{0}
is unsatisfiable if and only if the empty partial clause
\Box\setminus\bot
occurs in
S_{i}
for some
i
.

We will not give a full proof of Theorem
4.1
, as it repeats standard completeness proof arguments. Instead, we give the sketch of the proof and consider a few representative cases in detail (Section
5
), to illustrate how we deal with redundancy formulas and a slightly non-standard notion of fairness.

Definition 8
(Admissible Redundancy Formula)

is
admissible for

\sigma

\sigma\vDash R

Implementation and Evaluation

Implementation.

We implemented a variant of saturation in the
Vampire
prover
[
24
]
to demonstrate the applicability of the PaRC calculus. Our implementation uses a standard saturation algorithm, and partial clauses as follows:

1.

At the beginning of saturation, every clause
C
is initialized as
C\setminus\bot
.

2.

We perform PaRC inferences instead of standard superposition inferences, and as usual, skip inferences whose side conditions are violated.

3.

If a PaRC inference is performed, we apply a redundancy step similar to demodulation steps (
Section

6
) and add a redundancy formula to the main premise of the inference.

We note that the added redundancy formulas might be slightly different from the one used in demodulation steps, corresponding to various demodulation variants used in
Vampire
, controlled by options.

Fully checking PaRC side conditions from Figure
1
and handling redundancy formulas can be expensive. Our implementation uses several sufficient conditions to check these. Namely, we always checks side conditions (1)–(6) of
Sup
inferences, side condition (1) of
EqRes
inferences and side conditions (1)–(4) of
EqFac
inferences. Additionally, when adding redundancy formulas to partial clauses, we simplify these redundancy formulas, similarly as in the examples of
Section

6
.

We introduce the following Boolean options in
Vampire
to control PaRC:

1.

The option
-crc
toggles checking
all
side conditions of the PaRC inferences, as well as attaching redundancy formulas to clauses when they contain tautological ordering constraints.

2.

The option
-croc
depends on
-crc
being enabled, and allows adding redundancy formulas with non-tautological ordering constraints.

3.

The option
-crs
depends on
-croc
being enabled, and toggles a more expensive case analysis on non-tautological ordering constraints, as in
Examples

2
and
3
.

Related work

Model construction
[
5
]
is utilized in the majority of refutational completeness proofs for superposition-based calculi. Other notable approaches include forcing
[
32
]
, semantic trees
[
20
]
, and using closures for basic superposition
[
8
]
and closure redundancy
[
17
]
. Model construction has been used for proving completeness of higher-order superposition
[
10
]
, and it has several formalizations
[
16
,
33
]
. Standard redundancy and static and dynamic completeness have been in detail discussed in
[
7
,
40
]
.
Our work proves static and dynamic completeness of PaRC at the same time, using a slightly non-standard notion of fairness and a derivation process that is based on redundancy formulas. Using redundancy formulas circumvents the need to separate redundancy for clauses and inferences, simplifying some reasoning in the proof.

Constraints in first-order reasoning are mainly used to characterize subsets of ground instances of non-ground clauses
[
18
]
. Unification constraints serve various purposes, from restricting the search space by implementing basicness restrictions
[
8
,
30
,
26
,
20
]
or dealing with infinitary unifiers
[
11
,
10
]
, to separating first-order and theory reasoning
[
34
]
. Ordering constraints also enforce and strengthen ordering rewriting
[
31
]
and are implicitly used in ground joinability and reducibility
[
17
,
1
,
25
]
.
Our redundancy formulas in PaRC contain predicates similar to unification and ordering constraints. PaRC differs from other constrained systems in that redundancy formulas are not inherited and they are compatible with standard redundancy elimination. This is due to the semantics of redundancy formulas, as they correspond to clauses on the ground level that are known to be redundant.
Various algorithms and specialized data structures have been proposed to solve ordering constraints
[
12
,
23
,
29
,
13
,
27
,
36
]
. Common axiomatizations, such as associativity-commutativity (AC), enjoy more specialized handling
[
14
,
1
]
.

More sophisticated restrictions on superposition inferences are special cases of connectedness
[
3
]
and ground connectedness
[
17
]
. These include, for example, blocking
[
4
]
, compositeness
[
19
]
and general superposition
[
41
,
42
]
.
Our framework generalizes such case analysis-based redundancy detection approaches since (partial) case analyses can be expressed as redundancy formulas. We note that our partial redundancy method is not limited to unification and ordering predicates.

9
Conclusion

We introduce partial redundancy to restrict quantified equational reasoning while retaining refutational completeness and full simplifications, resulting in the PaRC calculus. Our framework yields an alternative characterization of standard redundancy notions, using redundancy formulas, and opens up a new research direction in redundancy criteria. We implemented a variant of the PaRC calculus in the superposition theorem prover
Vampire
. Our evaluation demonstrates that partial redundancy is efficient and complements current techniques. Future work includes finding new ways to apply our framework within first-order reasoning and beyond, such as theories and higher-order logic. Developing tighter integration in saturation and designing more efficient data structures that fit our constraint solving purposes better is also a future work.
