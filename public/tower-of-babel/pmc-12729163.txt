Breakage of an epidural catheter in the psoas muscle; case report
Authors: Sandra Elias, Dareen Jammoul, Samantha Succar, Charbel Gerges, Kenny Younes, Maher Abdessater, Ramy ToumaSawaya, Micheline Bou Khalil, Vanda Yazbeck Karam, Nathalie Bteich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rupture of an epidural catheter is an exceedingly uncommon complication during patient repositioning.Case presentationThis report presents an unprecedented event of an epidural catheter rupture at the level of the psoas muscle that was managed by conservative measures. The breakage occurred during an urgent catheter withdrawal, before the completion of the procedure, due to a vagal response in a patient enduring labor.ConclusionsConventionally, surgical removal of retained fragments is reserved for symptomatic patients. Patient education and proper follow-up are indicated in asymptomatic cases.

Implication statement

“This case report highlights the rare complication of epidural catheter rupture at the psoas muscle during labor. It emphasizes the importance of careful management, conservative treatment for asymptomatic patients, and the need for proper follow-up to prevent complications in similar cases.”

Introduction

Epidural anesthesia is a commonly employed method for surgical anesthesia, pain relief during childbirth, and management of chronic pain [12]. The utility of epidural block has vastly improved with the implementation of epidural catheters, which allows for the prolongation of neuraxial blockades up until the postoperative period [23]. Despite the general safety and effectiveness of epidural catheters, rare complications, including catheter breakage, can still occur [19]. Given the limited number of cases documented in the literature, there is a continuous debate regarding the decision to retain or extract fragments of indwelling catheters [15]. We present a case of an epidural catheter rupture, and the subsequent management strategy adopted.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old, 67 kg, multiparous woman was admitted at 38 weeks and 5 days of gestation for induction of labor by normal vaginal delivery. Her past medical history was unremarkable; she had no previous surgical history and no documented allergies. Her obstetric history consisted of two former uncomplicated normal vaginal deliveries.

She was scheduled for epidural anesthesia upon a cervical dilatation of 1 centimeter. A skilled anesthesiologist was called, the patient was informed about the procedure, and consent was obtained.

Under aseptic conditions, the L2–L3 level was identified while the patient was in the sitting position. Local anesthetic was infiltrated into the skin. Using a Braun epidural set and a loss of resistance technique, an 18 gauze Tuohy needle was inserted to access the epidural space, which was identified by the loss of resistance felt at 5 cm from the skin. As per standard practice, the catheter was intentionally advanced to compensate for potential outward displacement during Tuohy needle withdrawal. Following withdrawal of the needle, the catheter would have been carefully pulled back to the designated depth mark to ensure optimal positioning within the epidural space.

While threading the catheter and upon reaching the 15 cm depth mark (as indicated by the catheter markings externally), the patient experienced a vasovagal episode manifested by sudden nausea and presyncope. The patient was instantly placed in the left lateral position. Given the patient’s presyncope and hypotension, anesthesiologist considerations concluded that it would be unsafe to leave the catheter in situ, as further manipulation could have exacerbated hemodynamic instability. In addition, removing only the needle could have inadvertently displaced the catheter, pulling it back to less than the recommended length within the epidural space and compromising placement integrity. Thus, the Tuohy needle and catheter were withdrawn simultaneously to eliminate any ongoing mechanical stimulation of the epidural structures contributing to the reflex.

Immediately after eliminating the stimulus, the patient was repositioned supine as part of conservative management, both to optimize venous return and to prepare for the potential development of severe bradycardia or, in the worst case, asystole. The anesthesiologist promptly called for assistance with hemodynamic stabilization (IV fluid rapid administration, obtaining vital signs, and preparing vasopressors and antimuscarinic agents if ultimately required). Following stabilization, the catheter was inspected, and it was discovered that the tip was not intact.

The situation was explained to the patient, and another epidural catheter insertion was attempted. For the second attempt, the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position, with adequate pre-hydration with IV fluids, continuous blood pressure/HR monitoring, and constant verbal reassurance to keep her calm. The second epidural was successfully placed at L3-L4 level using the same technique as before, loss of resistance was felt again at 5 cm from the skin, and the catheter was threaded slowly without resistance, while the patient was under consistent close observation for any recurrence of vagal symptoms.

Patient delivered pain free. After delivery, catheter removal was performed in the same position as it was introduced, with gentle traction, low-force technique, and immediate inspection of the catheter to confirm integrity. Upon exit from the operating room, a CT scan was done, and a neurosurgeon was consulted. A 1.08 cm linear hyperdensity was identified at the level of the L2 vertebral body, oriented supero-anteriorly and embedded 1.5 cm into the medial aspect of the right psoas muscle, consistent with the catheter tip, with surrounding marked intramuscular emphysema within the right psoas and right paravertebral muscles and soft tissues (Fig. 1).

The neurosurgeon explained to the patient and her family that as long as she was asymptomatic, no surgical intervention to remove the catheter tip was needed. Appropriate follow up was done. The patient recovered post-delivery without any symptoms, was discharged home with advice about alarming symptoms (back pain, weakness, numbness, incontinence), and was instructed to return for further imaging should she become symptomatic.

Upon further follow-up over many months post-operatively, the patient had no complaints regarding the catheter tip. The insertion site was assessed by the operating surgeon and both residents and attending anesthesiologists in the clinics. The patient was asked thoroughly about red flag symptoms such as back pain, weakness, numbness, incontinence, redness, swelling, discharge, etc., which she denied. Both the patient and her newborn were doing well during the follow-ups. The patient was satisfied with the care she received at the medical center and with the explanation and diagnostic tests done to relieve her fears and anxiety regarding the incident.

Discussion

The literature review indicates that there are few case reports of epidural catheter breakage, with the majority of incidents occurring during the removal of the catheter. However, We attributed the event to catheter insertion because the vasovagal symptoms appeared immediately after threading, without other precipitating factors. Patients with lower baseline pain tolerance may have a reduced threshold for discomfort during interventional pain management procedures, predisposing them to vasovagal reactions [10]. In our case, the direct temporal association between catheter advancement and symptom onset strongly supports catheter manipulation as the most plausible trigger. Furthermore, the likely mechanism that triggered vasovagal episode onset was mechanical stimulation provoking afferent signals, triggering increased vagal tone, with procedural anxiety and discomfort possibly compounding the response.

We present a brief overview of the most common causes of epidural catheter rupture, ways to prevent it, and the recommended management strategies.

During the insertion of an epidural catheter, the most common causes of breakage are inserting it to an extensive length and advancing against resistance. The catheter is susceptible to shearing when attempts are made to withdraw it through the Tuohy needle [14]. Throughout removal, rupture can occur when excessive force is applied against a kinked, knotted, or entrapped catheter. The vertebral arches and processes, lumbar fascia, ligamentum flavum, posterior vertebral joints, and the lumbar nerve roots have all been identified as potential sites for the catheter to become caught during withdrawal [22].

The presence of well-trained personnel during the procedure is of vast importance [7]. Catheters should be checked for manufacturing defects; an ideal catheter should have adequate stretching capacity [1]. It is advised not to leave more than 5 cm of the catheter length in the epidural space since excessive insertion may cause coiling, knotting, or entrapment of the catheter [7]. Low-force traction during withdrawal is advised [7]; applying force against resistance may result in rupture. If the resistance is encountered in the presence of the needle, both are removed as a single unit [23], and if during withdrawal, the procedure is halted, and the tissue is allowed to relax for 15–30 min before resuming [5]. Injecting a saline bolus slowly through the catheter can help release it from any tissue entanglements in the surrounding area [16]. Proper patient positioning can lead to higher rates of successful removal, as the lateral decubitus position or utilizing the same position as during insertion potentially reduces the force required during removal [9, 13, 17]. In our case report, the catheter and Tuohy needle were withdrawn simultaneously in the lateral position after being introduced in sitting position.

It is imperative for all patients who have a residual fragment of an epidural catheter to undergo appropriate imaging in order to determine its exact location. Variable results have been observed when utilizing radiographs, CT scans, MRIs, and ultrasounds for detecting ruptured catheters [23]. Computed tomography was able to detect the catheter tip lodged in the psoas muscle in our case.

Epidural catheter fragments are typically inert and are not expected to elicit a foreign body reaction [14]. A multidisciplinary team consisting of anesthesia, neurosurgery and/or orthopedics should collaborate in order to make a well-informed decision between surgical removal and conservative treatment [20]. This collaboration is crucial for evaluating the risks and benefits associated with each treatment option. The patient and their family must be provided with a thorough follow-up plan if the decision is made to leave the retained epidural catheter in place. This plan should cover the symptoms of catheter-related complications and advice to seek immediate medical attention if they experience any [20]. Surgery is indicated in complicated cases such as development of infection, CSF leakage due to intrathecal placement or migration [22], radicular pain due to nerve entrapment [2, 8, 9, 21], and spinal stenosis due to reactive scar tissue formation [18, 24].

Due to the rarity of occurrence, considered to be between 0.002 and 0.4% [4, 6, 11], evidence-based guidelines on optimal management remain unclear. Since most retained catheter fragments are inert and asymptomatic, surgical removal is not necessary, and instead conservative management is more often called for. This usually includes long term monitoring and consistent follow-up as fragment migration or natural immune response may lead to complications that arise after a period of latency, such as granuloma formation, fibrosis, and nerve irritation. While asymptomatic, no treatment is required, but adequate documentation and efficient, transparent physician-patient counselling are essential for proper follow-up [3].

Catheter fragment removal surgery is usually only kept for symptomatic cases that involve neurological deterioration, inflammatory responses, persistent cerebrospinal fluid leakage, external protrusion, or need for anticoagulant therapy initiation. The technique of removal depends on many patient-based, such as age, body mass index, degree of inflammatory response and anatomical variation, and catheter-based factors, such as size, location and depth. Most commonly, endoscopic approaches are favored due to their efficacy and tissue preservation capabilities. However, in cases of large fragments, or close proximity to sensitive and essential structures, such as nerves or large vessels, limited dissection or even an open technique may be warranted [5, 18, 21, 24].

This study brings forth the case of breakage of an epidural catheter due to patient movement during a pre-syncope episode. While uncommon, it is important to consider the possibility of hardware malfunctions that can cause injury and harm to patients. Furthermore, this study shows the importance of proper patient follow-up and education, even when no surgical or medical management is prompted. The simple course of the case at hand may be a limitation as more case studies may be required to fully comprehend the management process in complicated cases.

However, this case report has several limitations. First, the report includes a single case with an uncomplicated course. While this is the more common presentation of an epidural catheter retention, more exposure to cases may offer a deeper understanding of possible symptoms and their severities. Second, this study focused on the short-term follow-up of the patient. She still presents regularly for checkups with her primary care physician and gynecologist, who in turn consistently monitor for any symptoms or signs of complication due to the retained fragment, and has not experienced any worrisome symptom by the time of completion of this case report. The short-term follow-up of the case does however limit generalizability and further studying of similar cases may help in formulating cohesive and structured guidelines for such occurrences.

Conclusion

The breakage of an epidural catheter is a rare event, yet it causes significant distress to both the patient and the anesthesiologist. Epidural catheter insertion and removal should be performed by skilled personnel and executed as smoothly as possible. The cornerstones of conservative management for the asymptomatic patient are education and follow-up. Cases with symptomatic presentations require a multidisciplinary decision for surgical intervention.
