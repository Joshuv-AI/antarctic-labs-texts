Non-Asymptotic Analysis of Classical Spectrum Estimators for L L -mixing Time-series Data with Estimated Means
Yuping Zheng, Andrew Lamperski

Abstract
Spectral estimation is an important tool in time series analysis, with applications including economics, astronomy, and climatology. The asymptotic theory for non-parametric estimation is well-known but the development of non-asymptotic theory is still ongoing. Our recent work obtained the first non-asymptotic error bounds on the Bartlett and Welch methods with restrictive assumptions. In this work, we derive non-asymptotic error bounds for both Bartlett and Welch estimators for L L -mixing time-series data with unknown means, and the results cover the special case with known zero means. The class of L L -mixing processes contains common models in time series analysis, including autoregressive processes and measurements of geometrically ergodic Markov chains. Our new error bounds are of O ⁡ ( 1 k ) O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}) , where k k is the number of data segments used in the algorithm. Such bounds are the tightest among the existing work on non-asymptotic analysis of classical spectrum estimators with or without zero-mean assumptions.

Introduction

Spectral estimation is widely used in signal processing and control systems. The central objective of spectrum estimation is to estimate the power spectral density of a signal, given a finite data record. Spectrum estimators can be primarily classified into parametric and non-parametric methods
[
20
]
.
Parametric methods include ARMA and state space models. Fitting parametric model requires knowledge or assumptions about the process, such as autoregressive order.
Non-parametric methods include periodograms, the Blackman-Tukey method, the Bartlett method, and the Welch method. Non-parametric approaches can be more robust when little is known about the target system, since only the definition of power spectral density is used
[
20
]
.

The asymptotic analysis of spectral estimation has reached a substantial level of maturity (see, e.g.
[
3
,
2
,
20
,
16
,
24
]
).
Non-asymptotic theory aims to remove the assumption of asymptotically many data samples, but is less developed. For parametric estimators,
non-asymptotic analysis has been widely studied. In particular, the non-asymptotic results for autoregressive models have been established since the early 2000s
[
12
]
. In addition, in dynamic system identification, non-asymptotic error bounds are presented in
[
13
,
19
,
18
,
21
,
15
]
. In contrast, non-asymptotic analysis for non-parametric estimators is less developed. Existing non-asymptotic analysis of non-parametric spectral estimators includes the work
[
9
]
on smoothed periodograms, variations on Blackman-Tukey estimators in
[

]
, and
[

[

]
, which gives a framework for deriving error bounds for a family of estimators, under rather restrictive assumptions, and
[

-mixing data.

Similar to our previous paper,

The class of

Other related work uses the theory of

Time dependencies have also been described by other mixing conditions (see, e.g.

Our contribution is to obtain non-asymptotic error bounds for Bartlett and Welch estimators for
L
-mixing time-series data with unknown means for both batch and online algorithms.
This work extends the results in
[

In particular, these are the tightest error bounds for the Bartlett and Welch algorithms, whether the mean is known or unknown.
As is standard

The online method iteratively uses segments of data to estimate the mean and the power spectral density, and is suitable for both streaming data and large data sets.
This work represents a stepping stone for the non-asymptotic analysis of non-parametric spectral estimation for other less ideal time-dependent data.

This work unifies and substantially extends the preliminary frameworks introduced in our previous conference paper

II-B

Problem and Algorithm

Given a stationary discrete-time stochastic process
\mathbf{y}[k]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the mean, autocovariance sequence, and power spectral density are

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}[i]]

\displaystyle R[k]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{y}[i+k]-\mu)(\mathbf{y}[i]-\mu)^{\top}]

\displaystyle\Phi(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-j2\pi sk}R[k]

where
s\in[-1/2,1/2]
.

Assuming the true mean
\mu
is unknown, we want to analyze two non-parametric spectrum estimators, the Bartlett and the Welch estimators. Both methods rely on decomposing the data into segments of
M
data points and working with Fourier transform estimates. For streamlined notation, the sample mean and Fourier transform estimates for the
i
th data segment are denoted by:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\mathbf{y}[iK+k]

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}w_{k}(s)\mathbf{y}[iK+k].

The Bartlett method has
K=M
and
w_{k}(s)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}e^{-j2\pi ks}
, while the Welch method has
K\leq M
and uses
w_{k}(s)=\frac{v_{k}}{\|v\|_{2}}e^{-j2\pi ks}
for some window vector
0\neq v\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Note that when
K<M
, as in the Welch method, the data segments overlap. In both the Bartlett and Welch estimators,
w(s)
is a Euclidean unit vector for all
s
:
\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}|w_{k}(s)|^{2}=1
. In general, we will assume that
K\leq M
. (If
K>M
, then the estimators will lose data.)

The running average of the data segment sample means is given by:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}.

Note that

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)]=\sum_{i=0}^{M-1}h(s)\mu
, where

h(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}w_{k}(s).

Set
\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}-h(s)\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}.

The proposed algorithms are the following:

•

Batch Algorithm:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)-h(s)\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\right)\left(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(s)-h(s)\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\right)^{\star},

(1)

•

Online Algorithm:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{k}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k+1}(s)

\displaystyle=\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)+\alpha_{k}\left(\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}(s)^{\star}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}(s)\right),

where
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{k+1}
.

The batch algorithm, in particular, reduces to the standard presentations of the Bartlett and Welch algorithms from
[
20
]
, after subtracting the mean from the data. The online algorithm provides real-time responsiveness and memory efficiency. Also, when the mean is known and zero, the batch and online algorithms reduce to the batch-form estimators and the iterative algorithm respectively in
[
27
]
.

Our convergence results cover the general cases of dataset with both unknown mean and known mean and the special case of zero-mean dataset.
When the mean is known, it is equivalent to say that the perfect mean estimate exists.
Then we can always subtract such known mean value from the data to obtain a zero-mean dataset. This is the main motivation that many existing works simply assume the data is zero-mean
[
27
,
14
]
.

II-C

Background on the Class of
L
-mixing Processes

In this work, we assume that
\mathbf{y}[k]
is an
L
-mixing data sequence. In this subsection, we present some background on the class of
L
-mixing processes. We start with the classical definitions in continuous
time and describe how they change for discrete time.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an increasing family of
\sigma
-algebras.
Let
\mathcal{F}^{+}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+})_{t\geq 0}
be a decreasing family of
\sigma
-algebras such that for all
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}
are independent,

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\mathcal{F}_{0}^{+}
for all
t\leq 0
,
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\sigma\left\{\bigcup_{\epsilon>0}\mathcal{F}_{t+\epsilon}\right\}
.
A continuous-time stochastic process
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
is called
L
-mixing
with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{+})
if

•

\mathbf{y}_{t}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0

•

M_{q}(\mathbf{y}):=\sup_{t\geq 0}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}\|_{L_{q}}<\infty
for all
q\geq 1

•

\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y}):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})d\tau<\infty
for all
q\geq 1
, where
\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})=\sup_{t\geq\tau}\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-\tau}^{+}]\|_{L_{q}}
.

The number,
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
characterizes the speed at which dependencies decay over time.

Now, we introduce the definition of discrete-time
L
-mixing process with some mild modification from the continuous-time definition. Let
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
and
\mathcal{F}^{+}=(\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+})_{k\geq 0}
be monotone increasing and decreasing sequences of
\sigma
-algebras, respectively, such that
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{+}
are independent for all
k\geq 0
.
Then the discrete-time process
\mathbf{y}_{k}
is
L
-mixing with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{+})
if the continuous-time process defined by
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}
is
L
-mixing with respect to the continuous-time
\sigma
-algebras defined by
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{+}=\mathcal{F}_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{+}
.

The moment bounds remains the same as in continuous time, but the discrete-time counterpart of
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
is defined by
\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{q}(k,\mathbf{y})
.
Note that

\gamma_{q}(\tau,\mathbf{y})=\max\{\gamma_{q}(\lfloor\tau\rfloor,\mathbf{y}),\gamma_{q}(\lfloor\tau\rfloor+1,\mathbf{y})\}
, which implies that

\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})\leq\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})\leq 2\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y}).

In particular,
\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})
is finite if and only if
\Gamma_{q}(\mathbf{y})
is finite.

Lemma 3

If
\mathbf{y}_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
is a zero-mean
L
-mixing process with respect to
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{+})
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Y}
is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable with

, then for any

and
q\geq 1

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]\right|\leq 2\gamma_{p}(t-s,\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{z}\|_{L_{q}}.

Proof

We follow the same steps as the proof in
[
11
]
, but replace multiplication with
an inner product. The details are given below:

Let
\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}^{+}\right]
.
Decompose as following:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}+\left(\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\right),\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\langle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}\right]\rangle+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]

where the third equality uses the fact that
\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}

\mathcal{F}_{s}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\right]=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\right].

Therefore, taking the absolute value and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\mathbf{y}_{t},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\right]\right\|\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}\right]\right\|+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\langle\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+},\mathbf{z}\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\|_{L_{p}}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{L_{q}}+\|\mathbf{y}_{t}-\mathbf{y}_{t,s}^{+}\|_{L_{p}}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{L_{q}}

where for the second inequality the first term uses the monotonicity of
L_{p}
norm and the second term uses Hölder’s inequality. Therefore, using the definitions of
\gamma_{p}(t-s,\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq 2(\lfloor\frac{M-1}{K}\rfloor+1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})+(\lfloor\frac{M-1}{K}\rfloor+1)\Gamma_{d,2q}(\mathbf{y}).

Furthermore, bounding
M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})
is a direct result of Proposition 3 in
[
27
]
by replacing
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
by
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
there. This gives

\displaystyle M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})^{2}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq 6M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\Gamma_{d,4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}).

Then, plugging the bounds of
M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})
and
\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})
completes the proof.
∎

V-B

Proof of Lemma
2

The estimate of mean,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
, is updated via running average, i.e.
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
for all
k\geq 1
. Recall that
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\mathbf{y}[iK+j]
and so the total amount of data that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
takes average over is
N=(k-1)K+M
. We can rewrite the running average as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}=\frac{1}{kM}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}p[l]\mathbf{y}[l]
, where
p[l]
counts the number of times that
\mathbf{y}[l]
appears in the summation.

Note that the last data in
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
is
\mathbf{y}[iK+M-1]
and the first data in
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i+a}
is
\mathbf{y}[(i+a)K]
. If
\mathbf{y}[\ell]
appears in the sums for both
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i}
and
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{i+a}
, for
a>0
, then
we must have
iK+M-1\geq\ell\geq(i+a)K
, i.e.
a\leq\frac{M-1}{K}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{y}[l]
shows up in the summands of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
at most
\lfloor a\rfloor+1
times, i.e.
p[l]\leq\lfloor\frac{M-1}{K}\rfloor+1
.

Therefore, we have the following:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}-\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\bar{\mathbf{y}}[i]-\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Mk}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}p[l]\left(\mathbf{y}[l]-\mu\right).

Taking the
L_{2q}
-norm and applying Theorem
2
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}-\mu\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{Mk}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}p[l]\left(\mathbf{y}[l]-\mu\right)\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2(2q-1)M_{2q}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)\Gamma_{d,2q}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)}\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 120.0pt\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\frac{\left(\lfloor\frac{M-1}{K}\rfloor+1\right)^{2}}{(Mk)^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2(2q-1)M_{2q}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)\Gamma_{d,2q}(\mathbf{y}-\mu)}\frac{\lfloor\frac{M-1}{K}\rfloor+1}{\sqrt{Mk}}.

where the last inequality uses:
K\leq M\Rightarrow N\leq kM
.

Then applying the bound from (
16
) and the definition of
\Gamma_{d,q}(\mathbf{y})
completes the proof.
∎

VI
Proofs of Main Results

VI-A

Proof of Theorem
1

From the construction of the batch algorithm (
1
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}-\bar{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{\star}-\bar{\Phi}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left((\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)^{\star}-\bar{\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{\star}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)^{\star}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{\star}

where the second equality comes from adding and subtracting
h\mu
from
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}
and then expanding the terms.

Now, we bound the
L_{2q}
-norm of the four terms in (
19
) separately.

For the first term, Corollary
2
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left((\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)^{\star}-\bar{\Phi}\right)\right\|_{L_{2q}}

(20)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2(2q-1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{*}-\bar{\Phi})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}-\bar{\Phi})}\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{k^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{(2q-1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}.

Note that similar to (
16
), applying the triangle inequality and then Jensen’s inequality gives
M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}-\bar{\Phi})\leq 2M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})
. Besides,
\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}-\bar{\Phi})=\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})
holds directly from the definition of the
L
-mixing property.

The second and third terms can be bounded in the same way:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{\star}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

(21)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu\|_{L_{4q}}\|h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\|_{L_{4q}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\sqrt{M}c_{2q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})c_{2q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}

where the first inequality uses the absolute homogeneity of
L_{q}
-norm, the triangle inequality followed by the fact that for any vectors
\|xy^{\star}\|_{F}=\|x\|_{2}\|y\|_{2}
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
The third inequality uses Lemma
2
.
Note that
|h|=\sqrt{M}
from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Similarly, the fourth term is bounded as:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k})^{\star}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\|h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}\|^{2}_{L_{4q}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}Mc_{2q}^{2}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{k}\leq c_{2q}^{2}\frac{1}{k}.

Therefore, combining the bounds in (
20
), (
21
), and (
22
) gives

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}-\bar{\Phi}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(4\sqrt{(2q-1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\star})}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\left.+2M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})c_{2q}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+c_{2q}^{2}\frac{1}{k}.

Then, plugging the constant bounds from Lemma
1
followed by the monotonicity of
L_{q}
-norm completes the proof.
∎

VI-B

Proof of Theorem
2

The proof largely remains the same as that of Theorem
1
and only varies in the parts containing the time-varying mean estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}
.

From the construction of the online algorithm (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}-\bar{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{*}-\bar{\Phi})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left((\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)^{*}-\bar{\Phi}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})^{*}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)^{*}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})^{*}

(23)

where the second equality comes from plugging

\Delta\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k}-h\mu+h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{k}.

Similarly, we bound the
L_{2q}
-norm of the four terms in (
23
) separately. The bound of the first term in (
23
) remains exactly the same. The processes of getting the bounds on the remaining three terms are slightly different.

The second and third term share the same bound:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu)(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})^{\star}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}-h\mu\|_{L_{4q}}\|h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\|_{L_{4q}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k}\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{0}-h\bar{\mu}\|_{L_{4q}}\|h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}\|_{L_{4q}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})c_{2q}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}+\frac{1}{k}M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\sqrt{M}\|\mu\|_{L_{4q}}

\displaystyle\leq 3M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})c_{2q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})M_{4q}(\mathbf{y})\frac{\sqrt{M}}{k}

where the last two inequalities use
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{0}=0
and
\|\mu\|_{L_{4q}}=M_{4q}(\mathbf{y})
and the Riemann sum bound:
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}dt\leq 1+2\sqrt{k}\leq 3\sqrt{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.

Similarly, the fourth term is bounded as below:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(h\mu-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})(h\bar{\mu}-h\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})^{*}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2q}^{2}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{i}+\frac{1}{k}M\|\mu\|_{L_{4q}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{2q}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+M_{4q}(\mathbf{y})^{2}\frac{M}{k}

(25)

where the last inequality uses the following approximations:
\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{i}=1+\int_{1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{t}dt\leq 1+\log(k-1)
,
\log(k-1)\leq\log k\leq\sqrt{k}
for all
k\geq 2
and
1\leq\sqrt{k}
for all
k\geq 1
.

Therefore, combining the bounds in (
20
), (
24
), and (
25
) gives

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{k}-\bar{\Phi}\right\|_{L_{2q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(4\sqrt{(2q-1)M_{2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{*})\Gamma_{d,2q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{*})}+6M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})c_{2q}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt\left.+2c_{2q}^{2}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}+\left(2M_{4q}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})M_{4q}(\mathbf{y})+M_{4q}(\mathbf{y})^{2}\right)\frac{M}{k}.

Then, plugging the constant bounds from Lemma
1
followed by the monotonicity of
L_{q}
-norm completes the proof.
∎

VIII
Conclusion and Future Work

This work derives non-asymptotic error bounds for Bartlett and Welch estimators for
L
-mixing data with unknown means using batch and online algorithms. High probability error bounds are also obtained, and we have simulated a finite Markov chain to verify the theory. Our error bounds are
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}})
, where
k
is the number of data segments used in the algorithm, which are tighter than the results obtained in
[
14
]
and
[
27
]
.

One future direction is to obtain tighter error bounds, possibly by conducting a frequency-dependent analysis.
Furthermore, different choices of step size may improve the algorithm performance under the stationary assumption, and may also allow analysis in non-stationary settings.
Such work would benefit the analysis of complex dynamics where the presence of nonstationary time-series data is unavoidable.
