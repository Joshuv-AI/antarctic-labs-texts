Spectral properties of unions of intervals and groups of local translations
Bryan Ducasse, Dorin Ervin Dutkay, Colby Fernandez

Abstract
. In connection to the Fuglede conjecture, and to Fuglede’s original work [ Fug74 ] , we study one-parameter unitary groups associated to self-adjoint extensions of the differential operator D ​ f = 1 2 ​ π ​ i ​ f ′ Df=\frac{1}{2\pi i}f^{\prime} on a union of finite intervals. We present a formula for such unitary groups and we use it to discover some geometric properties of such sets in ℝ \mathbb{R} which admit orthogonal bases of exponential functions (also called spectral sets).

47E05,42A16

1.
Introduction

In 1958 Irving Segal posed the following problem to Bent Fuglede: Let
\Omega
be an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the partial differential operators
D_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,D_{d}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}
defined on the space of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable, compactly supported functions on
\Omega
, denoted by
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. With commutation being understood in the sense of commuting spectral measures, under what conditions do
these partial differential operators admit commuting (unbounded) self-adjoint extensions
H_{1},\dots,H_{d}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
?

In his 1974 seminal paper
[
Fug74
]
, Fuglede offered the following answer to Segal’s question, for
connected domains

\Omega
, of
finite measure
and which are Nikodym domains, meaning that the Poincaré inequality is satisfied:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
Fug74
, Theorem I]
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite measure, open and connected Nikodym region. Denote by

e_{\lambda}(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda\cdot x},\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(a)

Let
H=(H_{1},\dots,H_{d})
denote a commuting family (if any) of self-adjoint extensions
H_{j}
of the differential operators
D_{j}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
j=1,\dots,n
. Then
H
has a discrete spectrum, each point
\lambda\in\sigma(H)
being a simple eigenvalue for
H
with the eigenspace
\mathbb{C}e_{\lambda}
, and hence
(e_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\sigma(H)}
is an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the spectrum
\sigma(H)
and the point spectrum
\sigma_{p}(H)
are equal and

\sigma(H)=\sigma_{p}(H)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:e_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{D}(H)\}.

\mathscr{D}(H)
denotes the intersection of the domains of the operators
H_{j}
:
\mathscr{D}(H):=\cap_{j}\mathscr{D}(H_{j})
.

(b)

Conversely, let
\Lambda
denote a subset (if any) of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(e_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a unique commuting family
H=(H_{1},\dots,H_{d})
of self-adjoint extensions
H_{j}
of
D_{j}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
with the property that
\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}\subset\mathscr{D}(H)
, or equivalently that
\Lambda=\sigma(H)
.

Definition 1.2
.

A subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is called
spectral
if there exists a set
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that the family of exponential functions

\{e_{\lambda}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In this case, we say that
\Lambda
is a
spectrum
for
\Omega
.

We say that a set
\Omega

tiles

\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations if there exists a set
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\{\Omega+t:t\in\mathcal{T}\}
is a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
up to measure zero. We also call
\mathcal{T}
a
tiling set
for
\Omega
and we say that
\Omega

tiles by

\mathcal{T}
.

In these terms, when
\Omega
is connected, of finite measure and a Nikodym domain, there exist commuting self-adjoint extensions of the partial differential operators
\{D_{j}\}
if and only if
\Omega
is spectral. Since this condition is a bit vague, Fuglede proposed his famous conjecture:

Conjecture 1.3
.

[Fuglede’s Conjecture]

A subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of finite Lebesgue measure is spectral if and only if it tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by translations.

The conjecture was proved to be false in dimensions 3 and higher
[
Tao04
,
FMM06
]
, but it is true for convex domains
[
LM22
]
.

In this paper we focus on dimension one, on finite unions of finite intervals. Throughout the paper
\Omega
will denote such a union:

(1.1)

\Omega=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}),\mbox{ where }-\infty<\alpha_{1}<\beta_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\beta_{2}<\dots\alpha_{n}<\beta_{n}<\infty.

The self-adjoint extensions of the differential operator
D=D_{1}
on
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
were determined in
[
DJ15
,
DJ23
,
CDJ25
]
; detailed analyses can be found for unions of two finite intervals in
[
JPT12
,
JPT13
]
, for finite unions of finite intervals in
[
DJ15
,
DJ23
,
CDJ25
]
, for finite unions of intervals the form

\Omega=(-\infty,\beta_{1})\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})\cup(\alpha_{n+1},\infty)

in
[
JPT15
]
.

We recall some of the main results in
[
DJ15
,
DJ23
]
. Define the maximal domain

\mathscr{D}=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):\mbox{ The weak/distributional derivative $f^{\prime}$ is in $L^{2}(\Omega)$}\}.

It can also be described as

\mathscr{D}=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):f\mbox{ absolutely continuous on each interval and }f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}.

We use the same notation
D
for the differential operator on
\mathscr{D}
,
Df=\frac{1}{2\pi i}f^{\prime}
,
f\in\mathscr{D}
.

For an absolutely continuous function
f\in\mathscr{D}
, the side limits
f(\alpha_{i}+)
and
f(\beta_{i}-)
are well defined, and we denote them by
f(\alpha_{i})
and
f(\beta_{i})
, respectively,
i=1,\dots,n
.
We also use the notation

f(\vec{\alpha})=(f(\alpha_{1}),\dots,f(\alpha_{n}))
, and similarly for
f(\vec{\beta})
.

For
\vec{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
denote by
E(\vec{z})
, the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with entries
e^{2\pi iz_{1}}
,
e^{2\pi iz_{2}}
,
\dots,e^{2\pi iz_{n}}
.

The self-adjoint extensions
H
of the differential operator
D
are specified by a certain
n\times n
“boundary” matrix
B
which relates the boundary values of the functions in the domain by
Bf(\vec{\alpha})=f(\vec{\beta})
.

Theorem 1.4
.

A self-adjoint extension
H
of the maximal operator
D
on
\Omega
is uniquely determined by an
n\times n
unitary matrix
B
(which we call the boundary matrix associated to
H
), through the condition

\mathscr{D}(H)=\{f\in\mathscr{D}:Bf(\vec{\alpha})=f(\vec{\beta})\}=:\mathscr{D}_{B}.

Conversely, any unitary
n\times n
matrix
B
determines a self-adjoint extension
H
of
D
with domain
\mathscr{D}(H)
as above. We denote
H=D_{B}
.

The spectral measure of
D_{B}
is atomic, supported on the spectrum

\Lambda:=\sigma(D_{B})=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\det(I-E(\lambda\vec{\beta})^{-1}BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha}))=0\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

which is a discrete unbounded set. For an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma(D_{B})
, the eigenspace has dimension at most
n
, and it consists of functions of the form

(1.2)

f(x)=e^{2\pi i\lambda x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})}(x),\mbox{ where }c=(c_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\mbox{, and }BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})c=E(\lambda\vec{\beta})c.

Next, given the self-adjoint extension
D_{B}
with boundary matrix
B
, one can define the
associated unitary group

\{U(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}=\{U_{B}(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
by

(1.3)

U(t)=\exp(2\pi itD_{B}),\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

This unitary group acts as translation inside the intervals (see point (ii) in the next theorem), and once it reaches a boundary point, it splits with probabilities defined by the matrix
B
(point (iii) in the next theorem).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
D_{B}
be a self-adjoint extension of the differential operator
D
, with boundary matrix
B
. Let

U(t)=\exp{(2\pi itD_{B})},\quad(t\in\mathbb{R}),

be the associated one-parameter unitary group.

(i)

The domain
\mathscr{D}_{B}
is invariant for
U(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e., if
f\in\mathscr{D}
with
Bf(\vec{\alpha})=f(\vec{\beta})
, then
U(t)f\in\mathscr{D}
with
B(U(t)f)(\vec{\alpha})=(U(t)f)(\vec{\beta})
.

(ii)

Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\cap((\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})-t)\neq\emptyset
. Then, for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

(1.4)

(U(t)f)(x)=f(x+t),\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\cap((\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})-t).

In particular,

(1.5)

(U(\beta_{i}-x)f)(x)=f(\beta_{i}),\,(U(\alpha_{i}-x)f)(x)=f(\alpha_{i}),\mbox{ for }f\in\mathscr{D}_{B},x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}).

(iii)

For
f\in\mathscr{D}_{B}
, if
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and
t>\beta_{i}-x
, then

(1.6)

\left[U(t)f\right](x)=\pi_{i}\left(B\left[U(t-(\beta_{i}-x))f\right](\vec{\alpha})\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{i,k}[U(t-(\beta_{i}-x))f](\alpha_{i}).

Here
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denotes the projection onto the
i
-th component, i.e.,
\pi(x_{1},x_{2}\dots,x_{n})=x_{i}
.

The next question is when is a union of intervals spectral? The condition is that the constants
c_{i}
that define the eigenvectors in (
1.2
) are all equal, so that the eigenvectors of
D
in the domain
\mathscr{D}_{B}
are exponential functions.

Theorem 1.6
.

The union of intervals
\Omega
is spectral if and only if there exists an
n\times n
unitary matrix
B
with the property that for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the equation
BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})c=E(\lambda\vec{\beta})c
,
c\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
has either only the trivial solution
c=0
, or only constant solutions of the form
c=a(1,1,\dots,1)
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
. In this case a spectrum
\Lambda
of
\Omega
is the spectrum of the self-adjoint extension
D_{B}
associated to the boundary matrix
B
, as in Theorem
1.4
.

If
\Lambda
is a spectrum for
\Omega
, then the matrix
B
is uniquely determined and well-defined by the conditions

(1.7)

Be_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha})=e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta}),\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in\Lambda.

Moreover

(1.8)

\textup{span}\{e_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha}):\lambda\in\Lambda\}=\textup{span}\{e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta}):\lambda\in\Lambda\}=\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Conversely, if the boundary matrix
B
has the specified property then a spectrum for
\Omega
is given by

(1.9)

\Lambda=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:Be_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha})=e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta})\}.

One of the nice properties of the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
, that we will use in this paper, is that, when the set
\Omega
is spectral, the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
acts as translations not only inside intervals, but also when it jumps from one interval to another. More precisely, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

(U(t)f)(x)=f(x+t)\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\cap(\Omega-t).

In other words
(U(t)f)(x)=f(x+t)
when both
x
and
x+t
are in
\Omega
. We call such a unitary group, a
group of local translations
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Assume that
\Omega
is spectral with spectrum
\Lambda
. Then the unitary group
U=U_{\Lambda}
associated to
\Lambda
is a unitary group of local translations.
Conversely, if there exists a strongly continuous unitary group of local translations
(U(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
for
\Omega
, then
\Omega
is spectral.

Throughout the paper
\Omega
is the union of intervals in (
1.1
). We denote by
l_{i}=\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}
, the length of the interval
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
of
\Omega
,
1\leq i\leq n
. We denote by
\mathfrak{m}
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

If a unitary boundary matrix
B
is given,
D_{B}
represents the self-adjoint extension of
D
with domain
\mathscr{D}_{B}
, as in Theorem
1.4
and
\{U(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the unitary group associated to it as in (
1.3
).
\Lambda
denotes the spectrum of
D_{B}
as in Theorem
1.4
.

In the case when
\Omega
is spectral,
\Lambda
will denote its spectrum, and
B
is the unitary boundary matrix associated to it as in Theorem
1.6
.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we answer a question posed in
[
JPT13
, Section 6 - “Questions”]
and we present an explicit formula for the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
(Theorem
2.5
). In Section 3, we study the case when
\Omega
is a spectral set and we use the formula for
\{U(t)\}
to determine some geometric properties of
\Omega
. The central result of Section 3 is Theorem
3.1
, which states (among other things), that, for spectral sets, the gaps between the intervals of
\Omega
have to be sums of lengths of the intervals of
\Omega
.

In Section
4
, we consider a restriction that was suggested by Segal to Fuglede: what if the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
is multiplicative (Definition
4.1
)? Fuglede showed in
[
Fug74
]
, that (even in higher dimension) when
\Omega
is connected and spectral and the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
is multiplicative, then
\Omega
has spectrum a lattice and it also tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a lattice. We show here that similar statements hold for
\Omega
a union of intervals:
\{U(t)\}
is multiplicative if and only if
B
is a permutation matrix (Theorem
4.5
); in the case when
\Omega
is spectral and
\{U(t)\}
is multiplicative, the permutation is a full cycle,
\Omega
tiles with
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)\mathbb{Z}
, and one can read from the permutation, how the set
\Omega
is translation congruent to an interval with the same spectrum (Theorem
4.7
). At the end of Section
4
we prove similar results, when the multiplicative condition is replaced by the Forelli condition (Definition
4.10
).

2.
The formula for
U(t)

We begin with a simple proposition which shows how our elements are modified when one applies the reflection
t\to-t
. This will help us reduce problems to the case
t
positive, as we will describe a little later in Remark
2.2
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
D_{B}
and
\{U_{B}(t)\}
be the self-adjoint extension and the unitary group associated to the set
\Omega
and the boundary matrix
B
, and let
D_{B^{*}}
and
\{U_{B^{*}}(t)\}
be the self-adjoint extension and the unitary group associated to the set
-\Omega
and the boundary matrix
B^{*}
. Let
J:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(-\Omega)
, be the unitary operator

Jf(x)=f(-x),\quad(f\in L^{2}(\Omega),x\in-\Omega).

(i)

JD_{B}=-D_{B^{*}}J
and
J\mathscr{D}(D_{B})=\mathscr{D}(D_{B^{*}})
.

(ii)

U_{B^{*}}(t)=JU_{B}(-t)J^{*}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

\{U_{B}(t)\}
is a group of local translations if and only if
\{U_{B^{*}}(t)\}
is.

Proof.

It is clear that
J
is unitary and
J^{*}f(x)=f(-x)
for
f\in L^{2}(-\Omega)
,
x\in\Omega
.

For (i), a function
f\in L^{2}(-\Omega)
is absolutely continuous with derivative in
L^{2}(-\Omega)
if and only if
J^{*}f
has the same properties relative to
\Omega
. We check the boundary conditions:
B^{*}f(-\vec{\beta})=f(-\vec{\alpha})
iff
B^{*}J^{*}f(\vec{\beta})=J^{*}f(\vec{\alpha})
iff
BJ^{*}f(\vec{\alpha})=J^{*}f(\vec{\beta})
. Thus
\mathscr{D}(D_{B^{*}})=J\mathscr{D}(D_{B})
.

For
f\in\mathscr{D}(D_{B})
,
x\in-\Omega

D_{B^{*}}Jf(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}(f(-x))^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}f^{\prime}(-x)=-JD_{B}f(x),

and (i) follows.

For (ii), since
D_{B^{*}}=J(-D_{B})J^{*}
, we obtain, by functional calculus,

U_{B^{*}}(t)=\exp(2\pi itD_{B^{*}})=J\exp(-2\pi itD_{B})J^{*}=JU_{B}(-t)J^{*}.

For (iii), suppose
\{U_{B}(t)\}
is a group of local translations and let
x\in-\Omega
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x+t\in-\Omega
and let
f\in L^{2}(-\Omega)
. Then
-x
and
-x-t
are in
\Omega
so
U_{B}(-t)J^{*}f(-x)=J^{*}f(-x-t)
; this can be rewritten as
J^{*}U_{B^{*}}(t)f(-x)=J^{*}f(-x-t)
so
U_{B^{*}}(t)f(x)=f(x+t)
. Thus
\{U_{B^{*}}(t)\}
is a group of local translations. The converse is analogous.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Proposition
2.1
can be used to deduce results for
U_{B}(t)
with
t
negative, once we have the results for
U_{B}(t)
with
t
positive. The idea is to take
t
negative and apply the results for the set
-\Omega
, the boundary matrix
B^{*}
and
-t
; then we obtain some results or formulas for
U_{B^{*}}(-t)
; but, with Proposition
2.1
(ii), we obtain results/formulas for
U_{B}(t)
.

In the case when all the lengths of the intervals of
\Omega
are the same, we can also say a bit more about the self-adjoint extension
D_{B}
, and about the matrix
B
when
\Omega
is spectral. We include this in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose the intervals of
\Omega
have the same length
l_{k}={\ell}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Let
B
be a unitary boundary matrix and let
D_{B}
be the self-adjoint extension of
D
associated to the matrix
B
.

(i)

A point
\lambda
is in the spectrum
\sigma(D_{B})
of
D_{B}
if and only if
e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}
is in the spectrum
\sigma(B)
of
B
. Therefore, if
\sigma(B)=\{e^{2\pi i\theta_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi i\theta_{n}}\}
then

(2.1)

\Lambda:=\sigma(D_{B})=\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\left\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}\right\}+\mathbb{Z}\right).

(ii)

For a vector
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}=E(\lambda\vec{\beta})\vec{c}
if and only if
E(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}
is an eigenvector for
B
with eigenvalue
e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}
.

(iii)

Assume in addition that
B
is spectral. Then all the eigenvalues of
B
have multiplicity one and, if
e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is an eigenvalue for
B
, then its eigenvector (unique up to a multiplicative constant) is
(e^{2\pi i\lambda\alpha_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi i\lambda\alpha_{n}})^{T}
.

Proof.

Since the intervals have equal length
{\ell}
, we have
\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k}+{\ell}
, for all
1\leq k\leq n
. By Theorem
1.4
, we have that
\lambda\in\sigma(D_{B})
if and only if there is a nonzero vector
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}=E(\lambda\vec{\beta})\vec{c}
. But
E(\lambda\vec{\beta})=e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}E(\lambda\vec{\alpha})
, therefore we can rewrite the condition as
BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}=e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}E(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}
. This means that
E(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}
should be an eigenvector for
B
with eigenvalue
e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}
. This proves (i) and (ii).

Assume now, in addition, that
B
is spectral. Let
\vec{x}\neq 0
be an eigenvector for
B
with eigenvalue
e^{2\pi i\lambda{\ell}}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, using (ii),
\vec{c}:=E(-\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{x}
satisfies the equation
BE(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}=E(\lambda\vec{\beta})\vec{c}
. Since
\Omega
is spectral, with Theorem
1.6
,
\vec{c}
has to have equal entries
c
. Therefore
x=E(\lambda\vec{\alpha})\vec{c}=c\left(e^{2\pi i\lambda\alpha_{1}},\dots,e^{2\pi i\lambda\alpha_{n}}\right)
.
∎

Next, we will present the formula for the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
associated to the union of intervals
\Omega
and the unitary boundary matrix
B
. Before that, we want to give the reader some intuition. Imagine a point
x
in
\Omega
, say in the interval
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, at time
t=0
; we denote
i_{1}=i
. It has to travel for
T>0
seconds total. It starts to move to the right, inside the interval
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, after
t
seconds it is at the point
x+t
, until it reaches the endpoint
\beta_{i}
(if
T>\beta_{i}-x
); at that moment,
\beta_{i}-x
seconds have past. Once it goes trough
\beta_{i}
it splits into
n
points, one in each interval
i_{2}
,
1\leq i_{2}\leq n
, at the left-endpoint
\alpha_{i_{2}}
, with a weight
b_{i_{1},i_{2}}
; the numbers
|b_{i_{1},i_{2}}|^{2}
,
1\leq i_{2}\leq n
can be thought of as probabilities for the point at
\beta_{i_{1}}
to jump into the point at
\alpha_{i_{2}}
, since

\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}|b_{i_{1},i_{2}}|^{2}=1.

Now each point continues the journey from
\alpha_{i_{2}}
. There are
T-(\beta_{i}-x)
seconds left until time expires. If
T-(\beta_{i}-x)
is less than the length of the interval
l_{i_{2}}=\beta_{i_{2}}-\alpha_{i_{2}}
, then the point will stop at the point
\alpha_{i_{2}}+T-(\beta_{i}-x)
, and we denote
T-(\beta_{i}-x)=r(x,t,i_{1}i_{2})
, and we have
0\leq r(x,t,i_{2})<l_{i_{2}}
. It will have weight
b_{i_{1},i_{2}}
.

If
T-(\beta_{i}-x)\geq l_{i_{2}}
, then the point reaches
\beta_{i_{2}}
, after an extra
l_{i_{2}}
seconds and it splits again into
n
points at
\alpha_{i_{3}}
,
1\leq i_{3}\leq n
, with weights
b_{i_{2},i_{3}}
, so the combined weight will be
b_{i_{1},i_{2}}b_{i_{2},i_{3}}
. At the moment of the second split
(\beta_{i}-x)+l_{i_{2}}
seconds have passed, and so
T-((\beta_{i}-x)+l_{i_{2}})
seconds are remaining.

The process continues until the time expires. Thus, one has to split the interval of time
T
into pieces: the first piece is
\beta_{i}-x
required for the point to get out of the interval

With this intuition, we construct the following definition:

[

Theorem 2.5
.

Proof.

First, consider

. Let

Assume the formula is true for any

Again, take

We know the formula works for

Suppose first that
\epsilon<\beta_{i}-x

For the second case,

In this section we focus on the case when our union of intervals

[U(t)f](x)=f(x+t)
when both

and

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that the set
\Omega

(i)

All gaps
\alpha_{j}-\beta_{i}>0

\Omega

(ii)

Take
t

\Omega

\operatorname*{End}(x,t,\omega)=e\end{subarray}}b_{\omega}f(e).

We prove first that

and (i) follows.

Now take an endpoint

\omega=i_{1}\dots i_{m}
is an admissible path in
\mathcal{P}_{x,t}
with

, then

,

, and

2\epsilon=t=\epsilon+l_{i_{2}}+\dots+l_{i_{m-1}}+\epsilon.

This implies that

∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral. Suppose one of the gaps,
\alpha_{i+1}-\beta_{i}
is equal to the minimal length
l_{\operatorname*{min}}=\min_{k}l_{k}
.

(i)

The following relation holds:

(3.4)

\sum_{j:l_{j}=l_{\operatorname*{min}}}b_{i,j}b_{j,i+1}=1.

(ii)

b_{i,j}=0

Let

This shows that, if

If

3.2

Take now,

-t=-\epsilon-(l_{\operatorname*{min}}+\epsilon),

for a path for

\operatorname*{End}(x,t,\omega_{0})=\beta_{j}-(l_{\operatorname*{min}}+\epsilon)

If

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral and has at least two intervals. Then
|b_{k,k}|\neq 1
for all
1\leq k\leq n
.

Proof.

Suppose
|b_{i,i}|=1
for some
1\leq i\leq n
. Then, since each row and column of
B
has norm one, it follows that
b_{i,k}=0
for all
1\leq k\leq n
with
k\neq i
.

Take now
j\neq i
,
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and

such that

. With (
3.1
),

\sum\left\{b_{\omega}:\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{x,t},\operatorname*{End}(x,t,\omega)=x+t\right\}=1.

However, if

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral and
b_{i,j}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{0}}
for some
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

The spectrum
\Lambda
of
\Omega
is contained in
\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}-\beta_{i}}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{0})
.

(ii)

The sets
\{\Omega+k(\alpha_{j}-\beta_{i})\mathbb{Z}:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint.

(iii)

Let
E
be a subset of
\Omega
of positive measure and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let

\tilde{\Omega}=(\Omega\setminus E)\cup(E+k(\alpha_{j}-\beta_{i})).

Then
\tilde{\Omega}
is spectral with the same spectrum
\Lambda
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.6
, if
\lambda\in\Lambda
, then

(3.5)

Be_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha})=e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta}).

Since
|b_{i,j}|=1
and
B
is unitary, we have
b_{i,k}=0
for all
k\neq j
. Then, considering the
i
-th component in (
3.5
):
b_{i,j}e_{\lambda}(\alpha_{j})=e_{\lambda}(\beta_{i})
, which means that
\theta_{0}+\lambda(\alpha_{j}-\beta_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}
and (i) follows.

For (ii) we use a lemma:

Lemma 3.6
.

If a set
\Omega
is spectral with a spectrum
\Lambda
contained in
\frac{1}{a}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{0})
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
, and
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, then the sets

\{\Omega+ka:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
are disjoint .

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\neq 0
, the set
E=\Omega\cap(\Omega+ka)
has positive Lebesgue measure. Then
E\subset\Omega
and
E-ak\subset\Omega
. Define the function
f=e^{-2\pi ik\theta_{0}}\chi_{E}-\chi_{E-ak}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. For any
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we have

\left\langle f\,,\,e_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\lambda x}\,dx=\int_{E}e^{-2\pi i(k\theta_{0}+\lambda x)}\,dx-\int_{E-ak}e^{-2\pi i\lambda x}\,dx

=\int_{E}e^{-2\pi i(k\theta_{0}+\lambda x)}\,dx-\int_{E-ak}e^{-2\pi i(k\theta_{0}+\lambda(x+ak))}\,dx\,(\mbox{since }(\lambda a+\theta_{0})k\in\mathbb{Z}))

=\int_{E}e^{-2\pi i(k\theta_{0}+\lambda x)}\,dx-\int_{E}e^{-2\pi i(k\theta_{0}+\lambda y)}\,dy=0.\,(\mbox{with the substitution }y=x+ak).

Thus
\left\langle f\,,\,e_{\lambda}\right\rangle=0
for all
\lambda
in the spectrum
\Lambda
; but this implies that
f=0
, a contradiction.

∎

With (i) and Lemma
3.6
, we obtain (ii).

For (iii), we use another lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a spectral set with spectrum

contained in

Of course, we can assume that

Define the operator

\mathcal{T}f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x),&x\in\Omega\setminus E,\\
e^{-2\pi ik\theta_{0}}f(x-ka),&x\in E+ka.\end{array}\right.

It is clear that the operator is unitary. Also, for all

∎

With Lemma

4.
Multiplicative unitary groups

In this section we will impose some extra restrictions on the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
and see how this affects the geometry of
\Omega
and the boundary matrix
B
. The first one, the multiplicative condition, was proposed by Segal to Fuglede, in order to obtain a more precise form of Fuglede’s result Theorem
1.1
. Fuglede proved in his seminal paper
[
Fug74
]
that (in any dimension) if a connected set
\Omega
is spectral and the associated unitary group satisfies the multiplicative condition, then
\Omega
is a fundamental domain for a lattice and the spectrum of
\Omega
is the dual lattice. We will prove that if our union of intervals is spectral and the unitary group is multiplicative, then
\Omega
tiles with
L\mathbb{Z}
, where
L=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)
,
\Omega
has spectrum
\frac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}
, and we obtain detailed information about the matrix
B
and the geometry of
\Omega
.

Definition 4.1
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that
U(t)
is
multiplicative
if for all
f,g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
fg\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

U(t)(fg)=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g.

We also define the set

\mathscr{T}_{B}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:U(t)\mbox{ is multiplicative}\}.

If
\mathscr{T}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
then we say that the unitary group
\{U(t)\}
is
multiplicative
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
U(t)
is multiplicative if and only if for any
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
U(t)fg=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g
.

Proof.

The direct implication is clear. For the converse, let
f,g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
fg\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let

f_{n}=f\chi_{\{x\in\Omega:|f(x)|\leq n\}},\quad g_{n}=g\chi_{\{x\in\Omega:|g(x)|\leq n\}},\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Then
\lim f_{n}=f
pointwise and
|f_{n}-f|^{2}\leq|f|^{2}
, therefore, by the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem,
\lim f_{n}=f
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The same is true for
\{g_{n}\}
.

Also,
\lim f_{n}g_{n}=fg
pointwise and
|f_{n}g_{n}-fg|^{2}\leq|2fg|^{2}
, and with the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem,
\lim f_{n}g_{n}=fg
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Then,
U(t)(f_{n}g_{n})
converges to
U(t)(fg)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. On the other hand,
U(t)f_{n}
converges to
U(t)f
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and similarly for
U(t)g_{n}
. Therefore, replacing the sequence by a subsequence, we get that
\lim U(t)f_{n}=U(t)f
and
\lim U(t)g_{n}=U(t)g
pointwise, and so

\lim U(t)(f_{n}g_{n})=\lim U(t)f_{n}\cdot U(t)g_{n}=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g\mbox{ pointwise a.e.}.

Thus
U(t)fg=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g.

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

The set
\mathscr{T}_{B}
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
, so
\mathscr{T}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathscr{T}_{B}=p\mathbb{Z}
for some
p\geq 0
.

Proof.

\mathscr{T}_{B}
is nonempty because
U(0)=I_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
so
0\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
. We use Lemma
4.2
.
Let
t,s\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
. Thus, for all
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

U(s+t)(fg)=U(s)[U(t)(fg)]=U(s)[U(t)f\cdot U(t)g]=U(s)U(t)f\cdot U(s)U(t)g=U(s+t)f\cdot U(s+t)g.

This means that
t+s\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
. Note that, by Theorem
2.5
, if
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
then
U(s)h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then
U(-t)f,U(-t)g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then

U(t)[U(-t)f\cdot U(-t)g]=U(t)U(-t)f\cdot U(t)U(-t)g=fg,

therefore
U(-t)f\cdot U(-t)g=U(-t)(fg)
. This shows that
-t\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
and
\mathscr{T}_{B}
is a group.

To see that
\mathscr{T}_{B}
is closed, let
\{t_{n}\}
be a sequence in
\mathscr{T}_{B}
with
t_{n}\to t
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\{U(t)\}
is strongly continuous, we know that, for
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

U(t)(fg)=\lim U(t_{n})(fg)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Also
\lim U(t_{n})(fg)=\lim U(t_{n})f\cdot U(t_{n})g=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Therefore
U(t)(fg)=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g
and
t\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
.

∎

Definition 4.4
.

For a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
– the symmetric group on
n
symbols – we denote by
P^{\sigma}
the permutation matrix obtained from the identity matrix
I_{n}
by permuting the columns according to the permutation
\sigma
,

P^{\sigma}_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&,\mbox{ if }\sigma(i)=j,\\
0&,\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Theorem 4.5
.

The set
\mathscr{T}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
if and only if
B
is a permutation matrix.

Proof.

Suppose that
B=P^{\sigma}
for some permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
. For
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and for almost every
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and
0<t<l_{\operatorname*{min}}=\min_{k}l_{k}
, by Corollary
2.6
, we have that

[U(t)f](x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x+t),&\mbox{ if }(\beta_{i}-x)>t,\\
f(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)),&\mbox{ if }(\beta_{i}-x)<t,\end{array}\right.

Then, for all
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and for a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
,

[U(t)](fg)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}f(x+t)g(x+t),&\mbox{ if }(\beta_{i}-x)>t,\\
f(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))g(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)),&\mbox{ if }(\beta_{i}-x)<t,\end{array}\right.

=U(t)f\cdot U(t)g.

Thus
U(t)
is multiplicative for
0<t<l_{\operatorname*{min}}
. With Proposition
4.3
, we get that
\mathscr{T}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
.

Conversely, assume that
\mathscr{T}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
0<t<l_{\operatorname*{min}}
and
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then for a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
t-(\beta_{i}-x)<l_{\operatorname*{min}}
, by Corollary
2.6
, we have

[U(t)(fg)](x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))g(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)).

Since
U(t)
is multiplicative we must also have that

[U(t)(fg)](x)=[U(t)f](x)\cdot[U(t)g](x)

=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{i,j}g(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))

=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{n}b_{i,j}b_{i,j^{\prime}}f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))g(\alpha_{j^{\prime}}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)).

For each
1\leq j_{0}\leq n
we can choose
f_{j_{0}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
f_{j_{0}}(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=\delta_{j,j_{0}}
for
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
t-(\beta_{i}-x)<l_{\operatorname*{min}}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. Using
f=f_{j_{0}}
,
g=f_{j_{0}^{\prime}}
, for some
j_{0}\neq j_{0}^{\prime}
in
\{1,\dots,n\}
, in the two formulas above, we obtain

b_{i,j_{0}}b_{i,j_{0}^{\prime}}=[U(t)(f_{j_{0}}f_{j_{0}^{\prime}})](x)=0.

This means that there can be at most one index
1\leq j_{0}\leq n
with
b_{i,j}\neq 0
. If we use
f=g=f_{j_{0}}
in the two formulas above we obtain

b_{i,j_{0}}=[U(t)(f_{j_{0}}^{2})](x)=(b_{i,j_{0}})^{2},

implying that
b_{i,j_{0}}=1
. We denote by
\sigma(i):=j_{0}
.

Suppose now that for
i\neq i^{\prime}
we have
\sigma(i)=\sigma(i^{\prime})=j
. This means that
b_{i,j}=b_{i^{\prime},j}=1
. This is impossible since
B
is unitary and so the
j
-th column has norm 1. Thus
\sigma
is a permutation and
B=P^{\sigma}
.
∎

Definition 4.6
.

Let
L>0
. Two measurable subsets
A
and
B
are said to be
translation congruent modulo

L\mathbb{Z}
, if there exist measurable partitions
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
A
and
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
B
, with
I
at most countable, and integers
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
A_{i}+k_{i}=B_{i}
up to measure zero.

Theorem 4.7
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral and
\{U(t)\}
is multiplicative.

(i)

The matrix
B=P^{\sigma}
where
P
is a cycle of length
n
.

(ii)

Let
L:=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)=l_{1}+\dots+l_{n}
. The spectrum
\Lambda=\frac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}
and
\Omega
tiles with tiling set
L\mathbb{Z}
.

(iii)

Translate the intervals of
\Omega
as follows: move the interval
(\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\beta_{\sigma(1)})
at the end of
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
, that is

(\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\beta_{\sigma(1)})+(\beta_{1}-\alpha_{\sigma(1)})=(\beta_{1},\alpha_{1}+l_{1});

move the interval
(\alpha_{\sigma^{2}(1)},\beta_{\sigma^{2}(1)})
at the end of the previous one, that is

(\alpha_{\sigma^{2}(1)},\beta_{\sigma^{2}(1)})+(\beta_{1}-\alpha_{\sigma(1)})+(\beta_{\sigma(1)}-\alpha_{\sigma^{2}(1)})=(\alpha_{1}+l_{1},\alpha_{1}+l_{1}+l_{2});

and so on, at the end move the interval
(\alpha_{\sigma^{n-1}(1)},\beta_{\sigma^{n-1}(1)})
at the end of the previous one, that is

(\alpha_{\sigma^{n-1}(1)},\beta_{\sigma^{n-1}(1)})+(\beta_{1}-\alpha_{\sigma(1)})+(\beta_{\sigma(1)}-\alpha_{\sigma^{2}(1)})+\dots+(\beta_{\sigma^{n-2}(1)}-\alpha_{\sigma^{n-1}(1)})

=(\alpha_{1}+l_{1}+l_{2}+\dots+l_{n-1},\alpha_{1}+l_{1}+l_{2}+\dots+l_{n}).

The union is
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+L)
(except for finitely many endpoints). All the translations in the previous moves are in
L\mathbb{Z}
. They show how
\Omega
is translation congruent to
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+L)
modulo
L\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We know from Theorem
4.5
that
B=P^{\sigma}
for some permutation
\sigma
. We just have to show that
\sigma
is a cycle of length
n
.

Consider
i<j
in
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and
t>0
so that
x+t\in(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})
. With Theorem
3.1
, we know that

\sum\{b_{\omega}:\omega\in\mathcal{P}_{x,t},\operatorname*{End}(x,t,\omega)=x+t\}=1.

Thus, there is a path
\omega=i_{1}\dots i_{m}
with
i_{1}=i
,
i_{m}=j
and
b_{\omega}=b_{i_{1},i_{2}}\dots b_{i_{m-1},i_{m}}\neq 0
. But then
i_{k}=\sigma(i_{k-1})
for all
2\leq k\leq m
, and therefore
j=\sigma^{m-1}(i)
. Thus all the points belong to the same cycle of
\sigma
, hence
\sigma
is a cycle of length
n
. This proves (i).

Consider now

\Lambda^{*}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in\Lambda\}.

Clearly,
\Lambda^{*}
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.

Note that for any
1\leq i\leq n
, since
b_{i,\sigma(i)}=1
, with Proposition
3.5
(i), we have that
\Lambda
is contained in
\frac{1}{\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i}}\mathbb{Z}
. This implies that
\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i}
is in
\Lambda^{*}
. This shows that all the translations that appear in (iii) are in
\Lambda^{*}
.

Since
\Lambda^{*}
is a nontrivial closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
,
\Lambda^{*}=a\mathbb{Z}
for some
a>0
. Then
\lambda a\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
so
\Lambda
is a subset of
\frac{1}{a}\mathbb{Z}
. Using Lemma
3.7
at each step in (iii), we obtain that after each move, we have a set with the same spectrum
\Lambda
as
\Omega
, and therefore the final set which is the interval
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+L)
has the same spectrum
\Lambda
.

The spectra of an interval of length
L
are
\frac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}+\lambda_{0}
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. However, since

P^{\sigma}(e_{0}(\vec{\alpha}))=e_{0}(\vec{\beta})
, we get with Theorem
1.6
that
0\in\Lambda
. Thus
\Lambda=\frac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}
. From the previous argument, all the translations in (iii) are in
\Lambda^{*}=L\mathbb{Z}
and so
\Omega
is translation congruent to
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+L)
modulo
L\mathbb{Z}
, which implies that
\Omega
tiles by
L\mathbb{Z}
, a fact that can also be obtained from Fuglede’s original paper
[
Fug74
]
: a set has spectrum a lattice if and only if it tiles with the dual lattice.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Suppose all the intervals of
\Omega
have the same length
l_{k}={\ell}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
and that
\mathscr{T}_{B}\neq\{0\}
. If
t_{0}\in\mathscr{T}_{B}
,
t_{0}>0
and
p\geq 1
is an integer such that
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}\leq p\ell
, then
B^{p}
is a permutation matrix.

Proof.

We replicate the proof of Theorem
4.5
using Corollary
2.7
instead. Let
f,g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
For a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
such that
(p-1){\ell}<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p{\ell}
, using Corollary
2.7
, and setting
r(x)=t-(\beta_{i}-x)-(p-1){\ell}
, we have

[U(t_{0})(fg)](x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[B^{p}]_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+r(x))g(\alpha_{j}+r(x)).

Since
U(t_{0})
is multiplicative, we must also have that

[U(t_{0})(fg)](x)=[U(t_{0})f](x)\cdot[U(t_{0})g](x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[B^{p}]_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+r(x))\cdot\sum_{j=1}[B^{p}]_{i,j}g(\alpha_{j}+r(x)).

For each
1\leq j_{0}\leq n
, chose
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that for a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p\ell
, we have
f(\alpha_{j}+r(x))=\delta_{j,j_{0}}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. Use
f=f_{j_{0}}
,
g=f_{j_{0}^{\prime}}
in the two formulas above and we get

[B^{p}]_{i,j_{0}}[B^{p}]_{i,j_{0}^{\prime}}=[U(t)(f_{j_{0}}f_{j_{0}^{\prime}})](x)=0,

for all
j_{0}\neq j_{0}^{\prime}
. Hence, there can be at most one index
1\leq j_{0}:=\sigma(i)\leq n
such that
[B^{p}]_{i,j_{0}}\neq 0
. Since
B
is unitary, so is
B^{p}
, and therefore, there is exactly one index such that
|[B^{p}]_{i,\sigma(i)}|=1
and
[B^{p}]_{i,j}=0
for all
j\neq\sigma(i)
. Next, observe that if we take
j_{0}=j_{0}^{\prime}=\sigma(i)
, we get

[B^{p}]_{i,\sigma(i)}=[U(t_{0})(f_{j_{0}}^{2})](x)=[B^{p}]_{i,\sigma(i)}^{2},

implying that
[B^{p}]_{i,\sigma(i)}=1
. If
\sigma(i)=\sigma(i^{\prime})=j
, then
[B^{p}]_{i,j}=[B^{p}]_{i^{\prime},j}=1
, which contradicts the fact that
B^{p}
is unitary. Thus
\sigma
is a permutation and
B
is a permutation matrix.

∎

Remark 4.9
.

Assume that
\Omega
has equal length intervals, has measure 1, and that the spectrum
\Lambda
is rational,
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Q}
. (It is an open question whether any such spectral set of measure 1 has a rational spectrum). We know from
[
BM11
,
Kol12
,
IK13
]
, that the spectrum has to be periodic and the period
p
is a positive integer. This will imply that

\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\}+p\mathbb{Z},

for some rational numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}
. Then all the rational numbers in
\Lambda
have a common denominator, which we denote by
D
. With Proposition
2.3
(i), we get that the eigenvalues of
B
have to be of the form
\lambda_{B}=e^{2\pi i\lambda\ell}
; since
\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)=1
, we have that
\ell=1/n
. Then
\lambda_{B}^{Dn}=1
, for all eigenvalues
\lambda_{B}
of
B
. With the Spectral Theorem for the unitary matrix
B
, we get that
B^{Dn}=I_{n}
.

Conversely, in the same situation – when
\Omega
is spectral, of measure 1, and with equal length intervals – if
\mathscr{T}_{B}\neq\{0\}
, then with Theorem
4.8
,
B^{p}
is a permutation matrix. Then
B^{pk}=I_{n}
for some integer
k\geq 0
(
k
a common multiple of the lengths of the cycles of the permutation). Then the eigenvalues of
B
are roots of order
pk
of unity, and, with Proposition
2.3
(i), we get that
\Lambda
is rational.

In what follows we consider the Forelli condition on
\{U(t)\}
, and we will see that we will obtain similar results under this restriction as for the multiplicative case. The Forelli condition was introduced in this context by Jorgensen in
[
Jor82
]
, where it is proved (in any dimension) that a connected spectral set with the Forelli condition has to be a fundamental domain for a lattice.

Definition 4.10
.

For
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, let
M_{f}
be the multiplication operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
M_{g}f=gf
, for all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Let

\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{g}:g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\}.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we say that
U(t)
satisfies the
Forelli condition
if

U(t)\mathcal{M}U(t)^{*}=\mathcal{M}.

In this case
U(t)
induces an automorphism on
\mathcal{M}
, which we denote by
\gamma_{t}
:

U(t)M_{f}U(t)^{*}=M_{\gamma_{t}(f)},\quad(f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)).

Let

\mathscr{F}_{B}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:U(t)\mbox{ satisfies the Forelli condition}\}.

We say that
\{U(t)\}
satisfies the Forelli condition if
\mathscr{F}_{B}=\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

\mathscr{F}_{B}
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
and
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then

U(t_{1}+t_{2})M_{g}U(t_{1}+t_{2})^{*}=U(t_{1})U(t_{2})M_{g}U(t_{2})^{*}U(t_{1})^{*}=U(t_{1})M_{\gamma_{t_{2}}(g)}U(t_{1})^{*}=M_{\gamma_{t_{1}}(\gamma_{t_{2}}(g))}.

This means that
t_{1}+t_{2}\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
.

If
t\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
, then
U(t)\mathcal{M}U(t)^{*}=\mathcal{M}
, which implies that
\gamma_{t}
is indeed onto and one-to-one, so it has an inverse, and if
g=\gamma_{t}(f)
, for some
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then

U(-t)M_{g}U(t)=M_{f}=M_{\gamma_{t}^{-1}(g)}.

In particular,
-t\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
and therefore
\mathscr{F}_{B}
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.

We prove that
\mathscr{F}_{B}
is closed. Let
t_{n}\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
with
t_{n}\to t
. Let
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Since
\{U(t)\}
is strongly convergent,

M_{\gamma_{t_{n}}(g)}=U(t_{n})M_{g}U(t_{n})^{*}
converges strongly to
U(t)M_{g}U(t)^{*}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is a von Neumann algebra, it is closed in the strong operator topology and therefore,
U(t)M_{g}U(t)^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
. This proves
U(t)\mathcal{M}U(t)^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
. Repeating the argument for
U(-t)
, we obtain the reverse inclusion and therefore
U(-t)
satisfies the Forelli condition, so
t\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
and
\mathscr{F}_{B}
is closed.
∎

Definition 4.12
.

We say that the unitary matrix
B
is a
weighted permutation matrix
if there exists a permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
|b_{i,\sigma(i)}|=1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
(which implies that
b_{i,j}=0
for all
j\neq\sigma(i)
).

Theorem 4.13
.

\{U(t)\}
satisfies the Forelli condition if and only if
B
is a weighted permutation matrix.

Proof.

Assume that
B
is a weighted permutation matrix with permutation
\sigma
. Let
0<t<l_{\operatorname*{min}}=\min_{k}l_{k}
. For
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
define

\gamma_{t}(g)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}g(x+t),&\mbox{if }x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}-t)\mbox{ for some $i$};\\
b_{i,\sigma(i)}g(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)),&\mbox{if }x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})\mbox{ for some $i$}.\end{array}\right.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We check that
U(t)M_{g}f=M_{\gamma_{t}(g)}U(t)f
. Let
x\in\Omega
. For a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}-t)
we have

U(t)M_{g}f(x)=g(x+t)f(x+t)=M_{\gamma_{t}(g)}U(t)f(x)

For a.e.
x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})
, by Corollary
2.6
, we have

U(t)M_{g}f(x)=b_{i,\sigma(i)}g(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))f(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=M_{\gamma_{t}(g)}U(t)f(x).

Thus
U(t)M_{f}U(t)^{*}=M_{\gamma_{t}(g)}
, and therefore
t\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
. Since
\mathscr{F}_{B}
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
, it is either a lattice or
\mathbb{R}
, so
\mathscr{F}_{B}
has to be
\mathbb{R}
.

For the converse, assume that
\{U(t)\}
satisfies the Forelli condition. Let
0<t<l_{\operatorname*{min}}
. Let
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. With Corollary
2.6
, since
U(t)M_{g}f=M_{\gamma_{t}(g)}U(t)f
, we have for almost every
x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})
,

(4.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))g(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=\gamma_{t}(g)(x)\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x)).

We prove that there can not be two indices
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
with
b_{i,j_{1}},b_{i,j_{2}}\neq 0
. If not, then pick
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that for
x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})
,
g(\alpha_{j_{1}}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=1
and
g(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=0
for
j\neq j_{1}
. Pick also
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that for
x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})
,

f(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=\overline{b}_{i,j}\left(\overline{g}(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))-\overline{\gamma_{t}(g)(x)}\right),\quad(1\leq j\leq n).

Plugging these into (
4.1
) we obtain for a.e.
x\in(\beta_{i}-t,\beta_{i})
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|b_{i,j}|^{2}\left|g(\alpha_{j}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))-\gamma_{t}(g)(x)\right|^{2}=0.

Since
b_{i,j_{1}},b_{i,j_{2}}\neq 0
, we must have

\gamma_{t}(g)(x)=g(\alpha_{j_{1}}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=1\mbox{ and }\gamma_{t}(g)(x)=g(\alpha_{j_{2}}+t-(\beta_{i}-x))=0,

which is a contradiction.

Thus, there is only one index
j
such that
b_{i,j}\neq 0
and we denote it by
\sigma(i)
. Since
B
is unitary, we must have
|b_{i,\sigma(i)}|=1
. To prove that
\sigma
is a permutation, we use the same argument as at the end of the proof of Theorem
4.5
.

∎

Theorem 4.14
.

Suppose
\Omega
is spectral and it satisfies the Forelli condition. Let
L:=\mathfrak{m}(\Omega)=l_{1}+\dots+l_{n}
. Then:

(i)

B
is a weighted permutation matrix for some cycle
\sigma
of length
n
. There exists
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.2)

b_{i,\sigma(i)}=e^{2\pi i\frac{\theta_{0}}{L}(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})},\quad(1\leq i\leq n).

(ii)

All the numbers
\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i}
are in
L\mathbb{Z}
.

(iii)

\Lambda=\frac{1}{L}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{0})
and
\Omega
tiles by
L\mathbb{Z}
.

(iv)

The statements in Theorem
4.7
(iii) hold.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.13
,
B
is a weighted permutation matrix for some permutation
\sigma
. The proof that
\sigma
is a cycle of length
n
is the same as the proof of Theorem
4.7
(i).

Let
b_{i,\sigma(i)}=e^{2\pi i\theta_{i}}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda
we have
Be_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha})=e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta})
and therefore,
b_{i,\sigma(i)}e^{2\pi i\lambda\alpha_{\sigma(i)}}=e^{2\pi i\lambda\beta_{i}}
, and
e^{2\pi i(\lambda(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})+\theta_{i})}=1
. Therefore

\Lambda\subseteq\frac{1}{\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i}}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{i}).

We perform the translations from Theorem
4.7
(iii). Using Lemma
3.7
, after each translation the new set has the same the spectrum
\Lambda
. At the end, the interval
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}+L)
has spectrum
\Lambda
. But all the spectra of an interval are known, and we must have some number
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\Lambda=\frac{1}{L}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{0}).

In particular
\Omega
also has the spectrum
\frac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}
and hence it tiles by
L\mathbb{Z}
.

We use again the boundary conditions
Be_{\lambda}(\vec{\alpha})=e_{\lambda}(\vec{\beta})
, for all
\lambda\in\frac{1}{L}(\mathbb{Z}-\theta_{0})
and we obtain

b_{i,\sigma(i)}e^{2\pi i\frac{k-\theta_{0}}{L}\alpha_{\sigma(i)}}=e^{2\pi i\frac{k-\theta_{0}}{\beta}_{i}}\quad(1\leq i\leq n,k\in\mathbb{Z}).

Then

b_{i,\sigma(i)}e^{-2\pi i\frac{\theta_{0}}{L}(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})}=e^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{L}(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})}.

The left-hand side is independent of
k
, therefore the right-hand side should be as well. Plug in
k=0
and
k=1
and obtain
e^{-2\pi i\frac{1}{L}(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})}=1
, which means that
\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i}\in L\mathbb{Z}
. Then

b_{i,\sigma(i)}=e^{2\pi i\frac{\theta_{0}}{L}(\alpha_{\sigma(i)}-\beta_{i})}.

∎

Theorem 4.15
.

Suppose all the intervals of
\Omega
have the same length
l_{k}={\ell}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
and that
\mathscr{F}_{B}\neq\{0\}
. If
t_{0}\in\mathscr{F}_{B}
,
t_{0}>0
and
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}\leq p\ell
, then
B^{p}
is a weighted permutation matrix.

Proof.

Let
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We have the Forelli condition
U(t_{0})M_{g}f=U_{\gamma_{t_{0}}(g)}U(t_{0})f
. Then, with Corollary
2.7
, for a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
, with
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p\ell
, setting
r(x)=t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)-(p-1)\ell
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}[B^{p}]_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+r)g(\alpha_{j}+r(x))=\gamma_{t_{0}}(x)\sum_{j=1}^{n}[B^{p}]_{i,j}f(\alpha_{j}+r(x)).

Suppose there are two indices
j_{1},j_{2}
such that
[B^{p}]_{i,j_{1}},[B^{p}]_{i,j_{2}}\neq 0
. Pick
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
g(\alpha_{j_{1}}+r(x))=1
and
g(\alpha_{j}+r(x))=0
for
j\neq j_{1}
, for
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p\ell
. Pick also
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that for
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p\ell
,

f(\alpha_{j}+r(x))=\overline{[B^{p}]_{i,j}}\left(\overline{g}(\alpha_{j}+r(x))-\overline{\gamma_{t_{0}}(x)}\right),\quad(1\leq j\leq n).

Plug these into the relation above to obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|[B^{p}]_{i,j}|^{2}\left|g(\alpha_{j}+r(x))-\gamma_{t_{0}}(g)(x)\right|^{2}=0.

Since
[B^{p}]_{i,j_{1}},[B^{p}]_{i,j_{2}}\neq 0
, we obtain for a.e.
x\in(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
with
(p-1)\ell<t_{0}-(\beta_{i}-x)<p\ell
,

\gamma_{t_{0}}(g)(x)=g(\alpha_{j_{1}}+r(x))=1\mbox{ and }\gamma_{t_{0}}(g)(x)=g(\alpha_{j_{2}}+r(x))=0,

which is the contradiction. The rest is as at the end of the proof of Theorem
4.13
.
∎
