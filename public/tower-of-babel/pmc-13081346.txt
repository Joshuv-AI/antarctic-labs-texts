Benefits of physical exercises in chronic kidney disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yanqin Chen, Song Ren, Xiaoxiu Yao, Mengjiao Li, Guisen Li, Yunlin Feng, Xiaoling Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical exercise has been proven to be beneficial for chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients; however, there is a lack of a comprehensive summary of existing literature, especially the results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). We had conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the benefits of physical exercise in chronic kidney disease. Outcomes were classified into all-cause mortality, cardiovascular, pulmonary, and renal outcomes, inflammatory indices, laboratory tests, and physical indices. The risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane criteria. Relative risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for categorical variables, and weighted mean difference or standardized mean differences with 95% CIs for continuous variables were pooled using a random-effects model. Publication bias was assessed using Funnel plot analysis and Egger’s Test. At last, 82 studies were included, and the results of the meta-analysis show that the physical exercises can significantly reduce all-cause mortality, particularly in dialysis dependent patients. Cardiovascular function, reflected by 6-minute walk test and blood pressure and pulmonary function, reflected by peak oxygen uptake, forced expiratory volume in 1 second, and forced vital capacity, all improved after physical exercise interventions. Physical exercises helped to increase the dialysis adequacy and preserve the residual renal function. C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 levels decreased, and hemoglobin and albumin levels increased upon physical exercise interventions. The fat mass decreased after physical exercise interventions in non-dialysis patients. We concluded that physical exercise is beneficial for CKD patients, independent of the modes or locations of exercise.

Introduction

The global incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) has been rising rapidly [1]. As the disease advances, it adversely impacts the function of multiple organs, including the cardiovascular, pulmonary, hematological, and musculoskeletal systems [2]. Despite recent advancements in CKD treatment, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists [3], the progression of renal function decline often remains unavoidable [4]. Furthermore, medical treatment options are generally constrained by the extent of the residual renal function. Upon reaching the dialysis stage, patients typically experience a marked decrease in quality of life and a significant increase in mortality risk [5].

Physical exercise has been demonstrated to be beneficial for CKD patients [6–8]. One significant advantage of physical exercise therapy for CKD is its broad applicability, without substantial limitation, provided the patient is capable of movement. The physical exercises can take various forms, including aerobic exercise, resistance training, and a combination of both. Numerous studies in the literature support the benefits of physical exercise for CKD patients. Physical exercise therapies can effectively enhance cardiovascular and pulmonary function, improve quality of life, and reduce the risk of adverse events and all-cause mortality in CKD patients [9]. Intradialytic aerobic exercise not only improves cardiovascular function in hemodialysis patients, but also significantly increases dialysis adequacy reflected by Kt/V and urea reduction rate (URR) [10]. However, there is a notable lack of comprehensive summaries of existing literature on this topic, particularly concerning results from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which are regarded as the highest level of evidence in evidence-based medicine.

Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to summarize the benefits of physical exercises in the CKD population from RCT studies, providing evidence for the exertion for exercise prescription and individual management protocol to improve the quality of life of CKD patients.

Materials and methods

Data sources and searches

A systematic search was conducted for eligible studies published up to February 9th, 2026 in Embase via Ovid, MEDLINE via PubMed, and Cochrane Central Library via Ovid according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [11]. The search used text terms relevant to physical exercise, chronic kidney disease, and randomized controlled trial (eTable 1). No restrictions were imposed on publication date or study population. The systematic review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (Identifier# CRD42024520822).

Study selection

RCTs that had assessed the effects of any physical exercise in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and were reported in English were eligible for this study. No restriction was applied to the mode of physical exercises, which can be aerobic exercise, resistance training, or a combination of the two. The exercises could be conducted either at the medical center or at home.

The screening was conducted by two reviewers (CYQ and RS) independently following a standardized approach. The titles and abstracts of all retrieved records from the database search were carefully examined. Duplicates, reviews, pediatric studies, case reports, study protocols, letters, guidelines, editorials, commentaries, non-human studies, conference abstracts lacking sufficient information, non-RCT studies, secondary analysis or pooled analysis of RCT trials, and studies irrelevant to physical exercise were excluded. In addition, the RCT studies which used exercise interventions as control were excluded. The reference lists of included studies or important reviews after full text screening were also manually reviewed to identify any potentially relevant studies. Any discrepancy was adjudicated by a third reviewer (FYL).

Outcomes

This systematic review included a comprehensive list of outcomes that evaluated the effects of physical exercise in CKD patients. Briefly, these outcomes were classified into the following seven groups: 1) all-cause mortality, reported as death rate in individual studies; 2) cardiovascular outcomes, including 6-min walk test (6MWT) results, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); 3) pulmonary outcomes, including peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1), and forced vital capacity (FVC); 4) inflammatory indices, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6); 5) renal outcomes, including dialysis adequacy reflected by Kt/V and URR for dialysis dependent patients and residual renal function reflected by serum creatinine (Cr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) for non-dialysis dependent patients; 6) laboratory tests including hemoglobulin (HGB) and albumin (ALB); and 7) physical indices, including body mass index (BMI) and fat mass from body component analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (CYQ and RS) independently extracted data from included studies and compiled them into a shared document. Any discrepancy was resolved by the third reviewer (FYL). The extracted data included author, year of publication, nation or region, sample size, characteristics of the included population, dialysis status, types of physical exercises in the interventional and control groups, follow up time, and the outcomes.

Critical appraisal

The risk of bias of included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers (CYQ and RS), using the 7-item criteria in the RevMan analysis software based on the “Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions”[12]. The certainty of evidence for all-cause mortality was assessed using GRADEpro GDT (Guideline Development Tool) developed by the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) Working Group [13]. Any discrepancy was resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data analysis and synthesis were performed using Stata (version 17.0; Stata Corporation, TX, USA) and Review Manager (RevMan 5.35) software.

Relative risk (RR) ratio with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for categorical variables, and weighted mean difference (WMD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% CIs for continuous variables were pooled using a random-effects model. For the changes of values following interventions from baseline, the mean and standard deviation (SD) that had been reported in the studies were directly used for statistical analysis, otherwise, they were calculated by validated statistical formulas [14]. Subgroup analysis was conducted based on the dialysis status of the patients as well as the modes and locations of physical exercises. I2 statistic was used to determine heterogeneity among included studies, and the pooled results of individual outcomes were deemed having low, moderate, and high statistical heterogeneity if I2 was <25%, between 26% and 75%, and >75%, respectively [15]. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots and the Egger Test. A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) statement

All authors are from one country. The author group is gender balanced and consists of junior, mid-career and senior researchers from different disciplines. Our study population originated from published RCTs and included both male and female subjects. The findings are generalizable to CKD populations.

Results

Search findings

Overall, 418 records were initially identified through literature searching and after removing duplicates. 249 records were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts, and 169 publications were further excluded after the full text review. Finally, 82 RCTs were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Figure 1).

Abbreviations: RCT, randomized controlled trial.

Study characteristics

The included 82 RCT studies encompassed 4,192 patients. Of these, 56 and 26 studies evaluated the effect of physical exercises in dialysis dependent patients and non-dialysis dependent patients, respectively. Among the 56 studies conducted in dialysis dependent populations, 3 studies only included peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, 50 studies only included hemodialysis (HD) patients, and 3 included both PD and HD patients. 60.9% (50/82) of the studies had been registered. The follow-up duration varied substantially between 4 weeks and 5 years. All studies that reported the location of physical exercises were conducted in dialysis dependent patients. A detailed summary of the included studies is shown in eTable 2.

Effects of physical exercises on all-cause mortality

A total of 14 studies reported the effects of physical exercises on all-cause mortality, including 10 studies in dialysis dependent patients [16–25] and 4 studies in non-dialysis dependent patients [9,26–28]. The pooled results indicated that physical exercises significantly reduced the risk of all-cause mortality in the whole population by 46% (RR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.31, 0.93; I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.026) (Figure 2); however, the subgroup analysis based on the dialysis status indicates that the reduction of all-cause mortality by physical exercises was only significant in dialysis dependent patients (RR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.22, 0.89; I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.022) (eFigure 1). The subgroup analyses show there was no significant difference in the pairwise comparisons among aerobic exercise, resistance training, or a combination of the two (eFigure 2) or between different follow-up durations (eFigure 3).

Note: The pooled risk of all-cause mortality reduced by 46% upon interventions of physical exercises.

Abbreviations: CI, confidence interval; RR, relative risk.

Effects of physical exercises on cardiovascular outcomes

A total of 46 studies reported the effects of physical exercises on cardiovascular outcomes, including 32 studies for 6MWT results [2,5,10,20,22,24,29–54], 20 studies for SBP[9,21,33,35,39–41,50,53–64], and 21 studies for DBP[9,10,21,33,35,40,41,50,53–65], respectively. Physical exercises significantly increased the 6MWT results in CKD patients in comparison to the control group, although the heterogeneity was high (WMD = 50.99 m, 95% CI: 37.61, 64.36; I2=98.5%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3). The significant benefits in the 6MWT results remain true in different dialysis statuses (eFigure 4) and different modes of exercises (eFigure 5). The location of exercises did not have any impact on the 6MWT results (eFigure 6); however, the number of studies is too limited.

Abbreviations: 6MWT, 6-minute walk test; CI, confidence interval; WMD, weighted mean difference.

Although the impact of physical exercise on blood pressure was insignificant in the overall population (Figure 4), the subgroup analysis revealed that SBP reduced significantly with physical exercise in dialysis dependent patients by 9.27 (95% CI: −14.95, −3.58) mmHg (eFigure 7). No significant difference was observed among different exercise modes in the blood pressure (eFigure 8).

Effects of physical exercises on pulmonary outcomes

Twenty studies reported the effects of physical exercises on pulmonary outcomes, including 15 studies for VO2peak [16,30,38,50,55,57–59,63,65–70], 5 studies for FEV1 [2,16,34,42,46], and 6 studies for FVC [2,16,34,42,45,46], respectively. It should be noted that the studies that had reported FEV1 and FVC were all conducted in dialysis dependent populations. Physical exercises significantly improved all three indicators of pulmonary function (Figure 5). Subgroup analysis indicated the benefit of physical exercises on VO2peak was only significant in non-dialysis dependent patients (eFigure 9). Interestingly, a combination of aerobic exercise and resistance training is significantly better for VO2peak, whereas the benefits on FVC were only significant for aerobic exercises and resistance training independently (eFigure 10).

Abbreviations: CI, confidence interval; FEV1, forced expiratory volume in the first second; FVC, forced vital capacity; DD, dialysis dependent; VO2peak, peak oxygen uptake; WMD, weighted mean difference.

Effects of physical exercises on renal outcomes

The renal outcomes in dialysis dependent patients included Kt/V and URR, which were reported in 14 [10,19,25,32,37,40,45,65,71–76] and 4 [10,35,37,73] studies, respectively. The pooled Kt/V significantly increased (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.76, p = 0.001) in patients taking physical exercises and in patients taking aerobic exercises (SMD = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.08, p = 0.028) (eFigure 11). Similarly, the pooled URR significantly increased in patients taking physical exercise, although there was only data for aerobic exercises (eFigure 12). The renal outcomes in non-dialysis dependent patients included eGFR and serum Cr, which were reported in 16 [9,26,27,30,33,39,43,50,54,57,59,61,63,77–79]and 9 [27,33,39,54,57,61,66,79,80] studies, respectively. The pooled eGFR significantly increased in the overall population (WMD = 3.77 mL/min/1.73m2, 95% CI: 0.23, 7.32, p = 0.037) and in patients taking aerobic exercises or resistance training, independently (eFigure 13). However, the pooled change in serum Cr following exercise interventions was insignificant both in the overall population or each individual subgroup based on exercise modes (eFigure 14).

Effects of physical exercises on inflammation indices

Twenty-five studies reported the effects of physical exercises on inflammation indices, including 21 studies for CRP [9,19,21,25,27,29,30,33,39,45,55,57,63,71,74,76,81–85] and 7 studies for IL-6 [19,21,33,59,64,86,87], respectively. Generally, both CRP and IL-6 levels significantly decrease after physical exercises in comparison to the control groups (CRP: SMD=-0.29, 95% CI: −0.52, −0.07; IL-6: SMD=-0.38, 95% CI: −0.76, −0.00), although the heterogeneities are high (CRP: I2 = 66.4%, p < 0.001; IL-6: I2 = 69.2%, p = 0.003) (Figure 6). Subgroup analysis indicated the changes of CRP did not differ between dialysis dependent and non-dialysis dependent patients, while the changes of IL-6 were only significant in non-dialysis dependent patients (eFigure 15). The benefits of both CRP and IL-6 did not differ among exercise modes (eFigure 16).

Effects of physical exercises on laboratory tests

Altogether, 29 studies reported the effects of physical exercises on laboratory tests, including 18 studies for HGB [19,24,27,29,30,39,45,61,62,65,68,74,75,80,84,88–90] and 22 studies for ALB [19–21,24,25,27,29,32,33,45,54,62, 64,72,74,76,80,84,87,88,90,91], respectively. Both HGB and ALB increased significantly in the physical exercise interventional groups, although to a minor degree (Figure 7). The subgroup analysis according to the dialysis status indicated that the increase of HGB was slightly greater in dialysis dependent patients in comparison to non-dialysis dependent patients, whereas the increase of ALB was only significant in dialysis dependent patients (eFigure 17). The subgroup analysis according to the exercise mode showed that the mode of exercise did not change the beneficial effect of physical exercise on HGB and ALB (eFigure 18).

Effects of physical exercises on physical indices

A total of 30 studies reported the effects of physical exercises on physical indices, including 25 studies for BMI [10,19–21,30,32,39,43,47,50,54–57,59,63,64,66,76,77,80,83,87,90,92] and 13 studies for fat mass [19–21,29,30,33,43,55,61,71,77,83,84], respectively. The pooled BMI in the exercise interventional group did not differ significantly from that in the control group, while the fat mass significantly decreased in the exercise interventional group compared to that in the control group (SMD=-0.28, 95% CI: −0.54, −0.03, p = 0.03) (Figure 8). The subgroups analysis based on dialysis status (eFigure 19) and exercise mode (eFigure 20) indicated that either the dialysis status or exercise mode affected the impact of physical exercise on the two physical indices.

Risk of bias assessment

Critical appraisal indicated the overall quality of the included publications was fair based on the 7-item Cochrane criteria (eFigure 21). Blinding of participants was the domain with the highest proportion of high risk, followed by blinding of outcome assessment (eFigure 22), since most of the included studies did not conduct blinding or were not suitable for blinding. The overall strength of the evidence for all-mortality is moderate (eTable 3).

Publication bias

Visual inspections of the funnel plots indicated absence of asymmetry for the outcome of all-cause mortality (Egger’s test: p = 0.624) (eFigure 23), cardiovascular outcomes excluding DBP (Egger’s test: 6MWT, p = 0.462; SBP, p = 0.765; DBP, p = 0.025) (eFigure 24), pulmonary outcomes (Egger’s test: VO2peak, p = 0.190; FEV1, p = 0.355; FVC, p = 0.083) (eFigure 25), renal outcomes (Egger’s test: Kt/V, p = 0.318; eGFR, p = 0.447; serum Cr, p = 0.542) (eFigure 26), inflammation indices (Egger’s test: CRP, p = 0.472; IL-6, p = 0.884) (eFigure 27), laboratory tests (Egger’s test: HGB, p = 0.485; ALB, p = 0.252) (eFigure 28), and physical indices (Egger’s test: BMI, p = 0.234; fat mass, p = 0.908) (eFigure 29).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis focus on the impact of physical exercise on a comprehensive range of outcomes in CKD patients. Physical exercises can significantly reduce all-cause mortality, particularly in dialysis dependent patients. Cardiovascular function and pulmonary function were all improved after physical exercise interventions. Physical exercises also helped to increase the dialysis adequacy and preserve the residual renal function. In addition, physical exercises could reduce the CRP and IL-6 levels and increase the HGB and ALB levels. Although the BMI did not show a significant change, the fat mass decreased after physical exercise interventions in non-dialysis patients, suggesting an increase in lean body mass. The body composition shift is crucial in terms of metabolic benefit in this population. Furthermore, dialysis dependent patients gain more benefits from physical exercises in comparison to non-dialysis dependent patients in terms of all-cause mortality, blood pressure, and serum ALB. Intra-dialysis exercise helped to increase dialysis adequacy. The overall quality of the included publications was fair based on the critical appraisal.

CKD patients, particularly hemodialysis dependent patients, are highly sedentary, being at high risk of sarcopenia. Both short and long-term physical exercises for CKD have been demonstrated in a substantial number of trials to have a positive impact on patient and clinical-centered measures [8,93]. To our acknowledgement, there are similar systematic review and meta-analyses in literature[94–102], from which the evidence is still deficient due to the limited range of physical exercises and outcomes[94,97,100], investigations of biochemical axis instead of clinical indicators[95], restriction on specific population instead of general CKD patients[96,99,101,102], and non-English writing language[98]. Although the results in this study show that physical exercises surprisingly reduced the risk of all-cause mortality, which is the main studied outcome, by 46% in the whole population, the advantage must be interpreted with caution, since there is a lack of consensus definition in death in the included studies. Therefore, high-grade evidence from studies that non-selectively include a broad range of exercise types and have consistent definitions and measurement of potential outcomes relevant to physical exercises is still urgently needed for recommendation of physical exercises in the CKD management.

The pooled results of this work consistently demonstrated the benefits of various modes of physical exercises for CKD patients in multiple aspects including all-cause mortality, cardiopulmonary function, inflammation conditions, nutritional status, as well as residual renal function for pre-dialysis patients and dialysis adequacy for dialysis dependent patients. The finding that exercise significantly increases Kt/V and URR is clinically compelling. Among the included studies for the dialysis adequacy outcome, all studies except one were conducted in hemodialysis patients, and all the exercise interventions were performed during dialysis sessions. The increased muscle blood flow caused by exercise helps enhance urea clearance, thus increasing dialysis adequacy. Additionally, the results of subgroup analysis based on dialysis status revealed that dialysis patients benefited more from physical exercises than pre-dialysis patients in all-cause mortality, blood pressure, and serum ALB, providing more evidence for future guidelines on the implementation of physical exercise interventions in CKD patients.

Studies on the implementation of physical exercises require quantification and standardization of physical activities, which might be substantially driven by exercise prescription. Exercise prescription is a therapeutic recommendation, possibly prescribed by a doctor [103]. Besides the four adjustable and characteristic components, namely frequency, intensity, duration, and type [104], advice on daily physical exercises is preferable to be included in an exercise prescription. The wide promotion of exercise prescription also helps to improve the accuracy and precision of measuring the merits of physical exercises, which are both critical for studies in this field. Clinical trials that use exercise prescription to define the characteristics of exercise can precisely measure the benefits of physical activities, helping to clarify the best type, intensity, and mode of exercise for CKD patients and work out individualized protocols for different patients, as the so-called precision medicine requires.

Besides physical exercises, nutrition is another key element of lifestyle intervention to treat CKD. Accumulating evidence supports the promotion of a healthy dietary pattern in the CKD population [93]. A combination of nutrition and resistance exercises has been found to significantly improve the muscle strength in dialysis patients [105], yet there hasn’t been evidence on the impact of the combination of nutrition and physical exercises on disease oriented outcomes in CKD. Future studies that explore the effects of the combination of nutrition and exercise interventions, preferably using exercise prescription for standardization, will further help to enhance and individualize the CKD management, therefore improve the patient’s quality of life and clinical outcomes. Achieving this goal requires the joint participation of nephrologists, dietitians, physical therapists, nurses, and, last but not least, patients themselves.

This systematic review and meta-analysis are strengthened by a comprehensive search strategy and high-quality evidence obtained from RCTs. The study encompassed both dialysis-dependent and non-dialysis-dependent patients and included subgroup analyses to investigate the effects of dialysis status, exercise modalities, and exercise locations on various outcomes. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, certain outcomes related to the benefits of physical exercise, such as quality of life, muscle strength, and activity endurance, were not examined due to a limited number of available studies. Second, there was considerable heterogeneity in the definitions and units of some of the studied outcomes, constraining the precision of the pooled analyses. For instance, exercise intensity is a critical factor and likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity in some outcomes; however, the included studies demonstrated substantial variability in exercise modalities, complicating standardization efforts.

Conclusions

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis substantiate the perspective that physical exercise confers multifaceted benefits to patients with CKD, irrespective of the exercise modalities or settings. Notably, patients undergoing dialysis derive greater advantages from physical exercises in comparison to non-dialysis dependent patients in terms of all-cause mortality, blood pressure, and serum albumin. Furthermore, an augmentation of lean body mass, as indicated by stable BIM and reduced fat mass, is crucial in terms of metabolic benefit in this population.
