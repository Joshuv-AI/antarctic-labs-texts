Rapidly progressive giant renin secreting tumor: a rare case report
Authors: Zhan Wang, Liangrui Zhou, Qijun Li, Penghu Lian, Xinchun Yan, Diliyaer Dilixiati, Wenda Wang, Yushi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEleven years ago, a 25-year-old female patient developed hypertension. Eight years ago, her BP worsened and tests showed hypokalemia and elevated 24-h urinary potassium. Enhanced CT revealed a left renal upper pole nodule. Three months ago, enhanced MRI and CTU showed a huge left retroperitoneal cystic-solid mass. Endocrine tests revealed significantly elevated supine/upright renin activity and angiotensin II concentrations, slightly elevated aldosterone, and serum potassium value of 3.5 mmol/L. She underwent open radical left nephrectomy and postoperative pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of juxtaglomerular cell tumor. The endocrine tests and blood pressure turned completely normal 2 weeks after surgery.Keywords: Juxtaglomerular cell tumor, Reninoma, Hypertension, Hypokalemia

Introduction

Juxtaglomerular cells are critical for blood pressure regulation via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.1 Reduced renal perfusion triggers renin release, which converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I and subsequently to angiotensin II, inducing vasoconstriction and aldosterone secretion to maintain blood pressure.1

Reninoma, also known as juxtaglomerular cell tumor (JGCT), is a rare renin-secreting renal tumor, with indolent biological behavior and favorable prognosis, and is generally considered as benign.2, 3, 4 Since the first case reported by Robertson et al.5 in 1967, about 260 cases have been reported in the literature so far.2,6 Tumor cells can secrete renin and consequently cause clinical manifestations such as hypertension, hypokalemia, and headache.7 JGCT shows a female predilection and typically occurs in young adults.2 Most tumors are small, while malignant transformation is extremely rare.2 This study reports a rapidly progressive giant JGCT with atypical imaging and pathology, to improve clinical recognition and early diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female presented with hypertension for 11 years without obvious cause, with blood pressure 140–150/90–100 mmHg and no initial treatment. Eight years ago, her hypertension deteriorated to 180/120 mmHg with throbbing headache. Laboratory tests showed severe hypokalemia (serum potassium 2.1 mmol/L), elevated 24-h urinary aldosterone (22.60 μg/24h) and urinary potassium (122.80 mmol/24h), while plasma renin activity, angiotensin II, aldosterone, and other endocrine indicators were within normal limits. B-ultrasound detected a 2.8 cm hypoechoic nodule in the upper pole of the left kidney; enhanced CT showed a 3.7 × 3.1 cm non-enhanced exophytic lesion, misdiagnosed as a renal cyst (Fig. 1A and B). She was treated with spironolactone and potassium supplements, then switched to amlodipine besylate and telmisartan, with controlled blood pressure but intermittent potassium supplementation. No further follow-up was performed for 8 years.

Three months before admission, computed tomography urography (CTU) confirmed a 13.39 × 10.88 cm cystic-solid mass compressing the spleen, pancreas, left adrenal gland, splenic vein, and left renal vein (Fig. 1C–F). Further adrenal enhanced MRI revealed a huge cystic-solid retroperitoneal mass, suspected to be malignant left renal tumor (Fig. 2A–D). To exclude clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), 68Ga-NY104 PET/CT was performed, showing no tracer uptake and ruling out ccRCC (Fig. 2E–F). Somatostatin receptor imaging showed uneven peripheral uptake and central radioactive defect; 131I-MIBG imaging was normal. Endocrine tests revealed significantly elevated renin activity and angiotensin II, slightly increased aldosterone, and serum potassium 3.5 mmol/L (Table 1). Dynamic renal imaging showed left renal eGFR 17.40 ml/min·1.73m2, right renal eGFR 49.95 ml/min·1.73m2.

On November 24, 2025, open radical nephrectomy was successfully performed with intact tumor capsule. Gross examination showed a 13.5 cm diameter grayish-yellow tumor with central ischemic necrosis and cystic changes (Fig. 3A–B). Postoperative pathology revealed a 13 × 8.2 × 9.8 cm cystic-solid tumor in the left kidney, with solid areas grayish-white, soft, and partially gelatinous, without renal pelvis or sinus invasion (Fig. 3A–B). HE staining showed cord-like tumor cell arrangement with venous invasion (Fig. 3C). Pathological mitotic index was 4/50HP (Fig. 3D), and KI-67 was 3% (Fig. 3E). Immunohistochemistry confirmed the diagnosis of JGCT with CD34(+), Syn (weakly +), SMA (+), Vimentin (+), TFE3 (+), CAIX (−) and RCC (−) as depicted in Fig. 3F and 4A–F.

Postoperatively, serum renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone, and potassium rapidly normalized (Table 1). Blood pressure remained slightly elevated before discharge and fully recovered to 100–120/70–92 mmHg with serum potassium 4.57 mmol/L at follow-up on December 15, 2025.

Discussion

Juxtaglomerular cell tumor (JGCT, reninoma) is an exceptionally rare renin-secreting renal neoplasm predominantly affecting young females, typically small, indolent, and associated with classic hypertension-hypokalemia-hyperreninemia triad.2 Malignant JGCT with rapid enlargement, vascular invasion, elevated proliferative index, and cystic degeneration is sparsely documented, and standardized diagnostic criteria for atypical cases remain lacking.8, 9, 10 This case features a giant, rapidly progressive JGCT with misleading early laboratory and imaging findings with the aim to deepen the recognition of atypical JGCTs.

Young hypertensive patients with hypokalemia and non-enhancing renal masses should be presumed JGCT until proven otherwise because normal initial RAAS testing does not completely rule out JGCT. Based on the blood pressure and serum potassium, JGCTs could be divided into 3 types: typical (80% of cases; hypertension and hypokalemia), atypical (17% of cases; hypertension and normal potassium), and nonfunctioning (3% of cases; normal BP and potassium).11,12 Typical JGCTs will show significantly increased plasma renin activity, angiotensin II and aldosterone levels alongside decreased blood potassium levels.11 However, renin levels obtained from routine venous blood collection frequently result in false negatives.7 In our patient, severe hypertension and hypokalemia were present at initial presentation, yet plasma renin, angiotensin II, and aldosterone were all within normal limits at that early timepoint, leading to delayed diagnosis and 8 years of lost surveillance. In aspects of imaging tools, JGCT characteristically manifests as a poorly enhancing renal nodule on contrast CT/MRI, unlike the avid enhancement typical of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). For any young patient with secondary hypertension complicated by hypokalemia and a non-enhancing renal lesion, JGCT remains the top differential diagnosis. Serial RAAS testing after temporary withdrawal of interfering antihypertensives is mandatory to avoid false-negative biochemical screening. Small incidental renal lesions in patients with JGCT-compatible clinical symptoms require strict long-term surveillance. Small JGCTs are conventionally regarded as biologically inert and slow-growing, which may lead clinicians to adopt lax follow-up protocols after initial benign-appearing imaging. Our case illustrates the critical risk of inadequate monitoring: the initial 2.8 cm hypoechoic renal nodule was dismissed with only symptomatic medical management, without serial cross-sectional imaging. Over 8 years, the lesion underwent explosive growth to a 13.39 cm cystic-solid mass complicated by vascular compression, venous invasion, and early malignant features including elevated Ki-67 index and mitotic activity. Our case stressed the necessity of tight and regular imaging surveillance for small renal lesions identified in patients with unexplained hypertension and hypokalemia to capture rapid malignant progression at an operable stage.

Targeted multimodal imaging should be conducted to rule out the diagnosis of ccRCC and alternative pediatric renal tumors in ambiguous giant cystic-solid renal masses. Large, degenerated JGCTs develop overlapping imaging features with high-grade ccRCC and Wilms tumor, creating substantial pre-operative diagnostic uncertainty. Our diagnostic algorithm leveraged disease-specific nuclear medicine to resolve ambiguity: 68Ga-NY104 PET/CT targeting CAIX13 produced no radiotracer uptake, excluding ccRCC, while somatostatin receptor and 131I-MIBG scintigraphy ruled out neuroendocrine neoplasms. On conventional cross-sectional imaging, peripheral enhancement with central cystic necrosis and mass effect on retroperitoneal vessels mimics Wilms tumor, but the clinical context of adult-onset hypertension and hypokalemia differentiates JGCT from pediatric nephroblastoma. Multimodality imaging combining contrast-enhanced CT/MRI and CAIX-targeted PET/CT serves as a powerful non-invasive tool to narrow differential diagnoses for large atypical renal masses prior to surgical intervention.

The confirmed diagnosis of JGCTs relies on pathological examination. Histologically, most juxtaglomerular cell tumors are arranged in cords and sheets, with polygonal and spindle-shaped tumor cells as the main types.10 Under electron microscope, the presence of many dense and round, oval, or rhomboid secretory granules in the cytoplasm or within the Golgi apparatus is a unique morphological manifestation of JGCTs.4 Positive renin staining in tumor cells is a characteristic manifestation of JGCTs, but renin positivity can also be found in Wilms tumor, renal cell carcinoma, and renal oncocytoma.10 Positive CD34, vimentin, and SMA staining are supportive for the diagnosis of JGCTs.10,12 Malignant JGCTs is often large in diameter (>5.0 cm), and necrosis, vascular invasion, or pathological mitoses can be seen pathologically.10,14 The above characteristics often indicate a high risk of distant metastasis or local recurrence of JGCTs. In this case, HE staining showed polygonal tumor cells, and immunohistochemistry showed positive CD34, SMA, and vimentin, which was consistent with the diagnosis of JGCTs. However, the lack of renin staining is a drawback in our case due to the unavailability of renin antibody in our hospital. Additionally, ischemic necrosis, vein invaded, pathological mitoses (4/50HP) were observed, highly suggestive of its malignant essence. Furthermore, we also performed KI-67 staining to assess the tumor's proliferating ability. In previous report, most benign JGCT demonstrated a KI-67 value of less than 2%.10 In our case, the KI-67 reached 3%, indicating its potential malignancy. However, whether local recurrence or distant metastasis will occur remains to be further followed up.

In conclusion, we reported an extremely rare case of rapidly progressive giant JGCTs with atypical pathological manifestations; the patient's diagnosis process was tortuous, and various examinations were not completely consistent, which led to delayed diagnosis and treatment. It is hoped that this case can improve clinicians' understanding of JGCTs, thereby improving the early diagnostic ability of JGCTs and improving the prognosis of patients.
