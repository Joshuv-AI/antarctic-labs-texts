The coexistence and trajectories of posttraumatic growth and depreciation: a 36-month longitudinal qualitative narrative analysis
Authors: Mariusz Zięba, Katarzyna Wiecheć, Joanna Szablewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch into posttraumatic growth (PTG) and posttraumatic depreciation (PTD) has predominantly relied on cross-sectional quantitative designs, often overlooking the subjective nuances and temporal dynamics of adaptation. This study aimed to explore the long-term evolution of perceived posttraumatic outcomes and identify emergent domains of change that transcend traditional theoretical models.MethodsWe utilised a longitudinal qualitative design, following a purposive sample of 26 psychology graduates (23 women, 3 men; age M = 34.42, SD = 9.10) over a 36-month interval. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews at two time points: T1 (within 12 months of a seismic life event) and T2 (36 months later). Reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) was employed, integrating deductive theory-driven coding based on established models with the inductive discovery of emergent themes to map individual trajectories of change.ResultsPosttraumatic adaptation was characterised by a shift toward narrative complexity, with PTG and PTD coexistence increasing from 42.3% at T1 to 61.5% at T2. Core findings include the identification of emergent domains—Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation (growth) and Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity (depreciation)—which were present in over 70% of narratives. We identified four distinct longitudinal trajectories: Persistence, Transience, Latent Impact, and Emergence of Complexity. A dominant pattern was ‘Increased Strength at a Relational Cost,’ where personal empowerment coexisted with an enduring loss of interpersonal trust.ConclusionsPosttraumatic adaptation is a non-linear, ongoing process of narrative reconstruction that continues long after the initial crisis. The results underscore the limitations of cross-sectional snapshots and traditional inventories, highlighting the need for longitudinal monitoring to distinguish transient coping from permanent personality transformation. Clinically, the findings suggest that fostering authenticity and self-alignment may be vital for long-term recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04705-0.

Background

Seismic events, belief disruption, and posttraumatic outcomes

Human existence is inherently unpredictable, frequently confronting individuals with challenges that disrupt established patterns of functioning. While traditional diagnostic frameworks often rely on objective criteria, research into long-term adversity has increasingly adopted a broader psychological conceptualisation. This shift aims to prevent the dilution of the trauma construct’s meaning through its overextended application to various life stressors, focusing instead on the subjective disruption of an individual’s core belief systems rather than on specific event categories [1–3].

Central to this perspective is the metaphor of the ‘seismic event’—an experience that is sufficiently disruptive to shatter an individual’s assumptive world [4, 5]. According to the framework established by Janoff-Bulman [4], such crises evoke a profound sense of insecurity by challenging core, relatively stable beliefs regarding the perceived benevolence of the world, the meaningfulness of life, and the worthiness of the self. In this view, a traumatic event is defined not merely by its external characteristics, but by its capacity to exceed an individual’s adaptive resources and undermine these fundamental beliefs. Ultimately, it is not the event in isolation, but the individual’s cognitive struggle with this new reality and the subsequent process of reviewing core assumptions that is associated with the emergence of posttraumatic outcomes [6].

Within this cognitive struggle, two distinct yet often coexisting outcomes emerge: posttraumatic growth (PTG) and posttraumatic depreciation (PTD). PTG is conceptualised as a positive psychological change experienced as a result of the struggle with ‘seismic’ circumstances [5, 7]. According to their influential functional-descriptive model, this growth manifests across five domains: Personal Strength, New Possibilities, Relating to Others, Appreciation of Life, and Spiritual Change. Conversely, PTD represents the negative counterpart to these changes, involving a deterioration in functioning or a negative shift in self-perception (e.g., a diminished sense of personal strength or perceived loss of possibilities) [8, 9]. While this model is widely applied in longitudinal research, it has faced critiques regarding whether reported growth reflects actual personality shifts or transient, ‘illusory’ coping mechanisms [10, 11].

The posttraumatic paradox: independence and coexistence of outcomes

Historically, psychological inquiry into the aftermath of seismic life events has focused predominantly on two divergent paths: the manifestation of posttraumatic disorders and distress [12], or the paradoxical experience of PTG [6]. However, researchers have increasingly suggested that viewing posttraumatic change as a single dimension - ranging from ‘deterioration’ to ‘growth’ - is conceptually insufficient [8, 13]. In light of empirical findings, a multi-dimensional perspective has been supported, as individuals frequently report the simultaneous occurrence of both positive and negative cognitive changes [14–17].

PTG and PTD are now widely established as independent constructs [9, 18]. This independence is supported by international data showing that PTG and PTD are only minimally correlated [13] and demonstrate distinct associations with well-being [19, 20]. While PTD is consistently linked to distress [19] and posttraumatic stress symptoms [13], the relationship between PTG and these measures remains non-linear or equivocal [5, 21].

The coexistence of these outcomes often manifests as shifts across different life domains. In narrative analyses, researchers have observed that individuals may perceive enhanced personal agency and strength alongside a concurrent deterioration in interpersonal trust or social bonds [16]. Previous studies have identified that a significant proportion of survivors report the simultaneous presence of both growth and depreciation [13, 16]. This phenomenon is further explained by distinct cognitive mechanisms: deliberate, constructive rumination serves as a primary predictor of PTG, whereas intrusive, automatic thoughts are significantly associated with PTD and persistent distress [22–24]. Additionally, the quality of social interactions plays a critical role, with positive disclosure fostering growth whilst negative social reactions or unsupportive responses to one’s narrative are associated with increased depreciation [13].

The temporal gap: stability, dynamics, and measurement limits

Despite the proliferation of research on posttraumatic outcomes, several significant methodological and conceptual challenges remain unresolved [10, 25, 26]. A primary limitation identified by researchers is the overreliance on cross-sectional designs, which often reflect post hoc reconstructions of change rather than actual change. Consequently, most existing data provide only a static snapshot, potentially failing to capture the actual evolution of belief systems over time [10, 26]. This reliance on retrospective self-report measures, such as the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI), has been critiqued for its susceptibility to ‘positivity bias’ or the ‘growth illusion’ [11, 27]. Within this context, individuals may report perceived growth to align with cultural master narratives of redemption or as a mechanism to reduce immediate psychological tension [28, 29].

Furthermore, longitudinal findings regarding the stability of posttraumatic changes are notably inconsistent [5]. Whilst some studies indicate that PTG remains stable over several years [30], others have demonstrated either significant increases or declines over time [31–33]. Recent person-centred analyses have identified highly heterogeneous trajectories of adaptation, suggesting that individuals exposed to similar stressors follow unique paths of growth and depreciation depending on their psychosocial characteristics [18, 34]. A critical distinction must also be made between ‘benefit finding’ - a short-term coping strategy focused on positive reinterpretation – and genuine PTG, which involves enduring and stable revisions of fundamental assumptions about the self and the world [25, 35–37].

Standard quantitative measures often fail to capture the nuances of daily functioning or the ‘latent impact’ of an event that may only consolidate after significant temporal distance. As researchers have argued, while Tedeschi and Calhoun’s model [7] describes a narrative process of rebuilding one’s life, the subjective experience of change reported in questionnaires does not always mirror actual shifts in personality or behaviour [26, 34]. Qualitative longitudinal research is therefore essential to explore how these theoretical domains are lived and renegotiated over time, addressing the limitations of static measurement.

The present study

The current study utilised a longitudinal qualitative design to investigate the perceived long-term consequences of seismic life events (SLE) on individuals’ basic beliefs [4]. In response to the aforementioned methodological limitations and calls for longitudinal research, we aimed to identify and explore the nature of both positive and negative outcomes, conceptualised as posttraumatic growth and posttraumatic depreciation. A central focus of this inquiry was the examination of the domains in which these phenomena appeared and the specific patterns of their coexistence within individuals.

Furthermore, this research sought to examine the longitudinal dynamics and trajectories of these reported outcomes over a 36-month period, a timeframe designed to capture enduring cognitive reconstruction rather than transient coping strategies. By conducting a within-participant comparison of narratives across two waves, the study intended to identify common patterns of stability, emergence, and fluctuation in the perceived influence of the SLE on participants’ world-views. Finally, we aimed to identify valid outcomes—specifically emergent domains such as heightened self-awareness and increased assertiveness—that fall outside the traditional five-domain model of posttraumatic growth, thereby providing a more comprehensive and culturally nuanced understanding of posttraumatic adaptation.

Method

Research design and qualitative approach

This study utilised a longitudinal qualitative design to explore the long-term perceived consequences of a ‘seismic life event’ (SLE) on the fundamental belief systems of individuals. The design involved two primary waves of data collection, separated by a 36-month interval, to facilitate an in-depth examination of the evolution of participants’ experiences and narrative representations over time. The research was grounded in reflexive thematic analysis (RTA), following the methodological framework established by Braun and Clarke [38, 39]. This approach allowed for a multi-phase analytical strategy that integrated deductive theoretical coding with the inductive discovery of emergent themes, which was deemed essential for capturing the complexities of identity reconstruction following ‘seismic events’ [6].

Participants and recruitment process

The participants analysed in this study (N = 26) comprised a subset of a larger cohort from a four-year longitudinal project. The broader project involved three data collection waves, starting with 171 Master’s-level psychology students at T0 (85.4% female), recruited via an internal university research participation system. This number decreased to 78 participants at the second wave (T1) and ultimately resulted in 26 individuals who completed the final wave (T2) 36 months later. Sample attrition was associated with several factors, including voluntary withdrawal and organisational constraints regarding data anonymisation that prevented re-contacting a portion of the original sample.

The final sample consisted of 23 women and 3 men, with ages ranging from 22 to 55 years at T1 (M = 34.42, SD = 9.10). All participants were graduates by the time of the T2 interviews. While the baseline stage (T0) involved life-story interviews based on the protocol proposed by McAdams [40], for which students received course credits, the current analysis focuses exclusively on the SLE narratives collected at T1 and T2. T0 functioned as a pre-event baseline wave; the specific seismic events narrated by participants occurred after the T0 baseline but prior to the T1 interviews. The seismic events narrated by participants represented a broad spectrum of life-course disruptions. To protect participant anonymity, these events were grouped into five overarching categories (see Table 1).

Participation in the subsequent longitudinal waves was entirely voluntary and uncompensated. The study received approval from the university’s ethical commission, and informed consent was obtained prior to each interview wave.

Researchers description and reflexivity

The qualitative analysis of the interview transcripts was conducted primarily by the first author (M.Z.), a psychologist with over a decade of experience in qualitative methodology and narrative psychology. Interpretive credibility was supported through regular consultative discussions with the second author (K.W.), a psychologist and cognitive-behavioural psychotherapist specialising in trauma and basic beliefs. The researchers’ combined expertise formed the interpretive lens for this study; M.Z.‘s background in narrative psychology guided the focus on how stories evolved, whilst K.W.‘s clinical expertise provided a framework for identifying subtle shifts in cognitive representations.

The authors also considered their positioning as ‘insider researchers’, as the participants were psychology graduates (as described above) and the researchers were practising psychologists. This shared educational background and professional identity facilitated the rapid development of rapport and the established use of a common linguistic framework during the interviews. However, the researchers acknowledged the risk that this shared ‘specialised vocabulary’ could potentially lead to taking certain concepts or professional ‘jargon’ for granted. This potential for assumed understanding was actively addressed during the reflexive process to ensure that the depth of the participants’ personal meanings was not obscured by professional shorthand [39].

Beyond the shared educational background, the researchers attended to the potential influence of gender dynamics on the data collection process. The interviewers were all women, as were the vast majority of the participants. It was perceived that this shared gender identity facilitated communication and potentially enhanced the openness of the participants during the narration of sensitive life events. Regarding the three male participants, the researchers did not observe any discernible negative impact of the gender difference on the quality of the narrative disclosure or the depth of the interview process. Alongside gender, the reflexive process addressed the universal transition of interviewers between T1 and T2. While a change in interviewer may impact initial rapport, the presence of a ‘new listener’ often prompted participants to re-articulate their experiences with greater descriptive depth; because participants could not rely on shared prior knowledge with the researcher, they were encouraged to provide more comprehensive contextual explanations. Furthermore, the reflexive process specifically addressed the risk of demand characteristics. Given the participants’ background as psychology graduates, there was a possibility they might provide redemptive or complex narratives to align with perceived academic paradigms. To mitigate this, interviewers maintained a neutral, non-directive stance, explicitly validating all outcomes, including the absence of change. Prior to analysis, the researchers engaged in active reflexive engagement with their theoretical assumptions, interrogating how their existing knowledge of PTG and PTD models might influence the coding process. Rather than attempting to bracket prior expertise, the authors focused on managing subjectivity to ensure it served as a valuable interpretive resource while remaining open to emergent, inductive themes [16].

Data collection procedure and context

Data for the current analysis were collected through semi-structured interviews at two primary time points following a baseline assessment (T0). T1 interviews were conducted within 12 months of the occurrence of the seismic event. Because the T0 baseline interviews preceded these events, they provide a prospective reference point for the participants’ prior psychological functioning. T2 interviews were subsequently conducted an average of 36.6 months after T1 (SD = 7.2, range: 23–44 months). To verify longitudinal consistency, T2 interviewers were provided with a brief administrative label of the event identified at T1, though they remained blind to the specific narrative details and thematic classifications from that session. In all cases, the T2 interviewer was a different researcher from the one who conducted the T1 interview, a procedural decision made to mitigate demand characteristics and ensure participants did not feel compelled to perform a ‘growth story’ for a familiar researcher. Furthering high-quality data collection and methodological rigour, all interviews were conducted by trained psychologists with a minimum of two years of clinical experience. For the purpose of consistency, all members of the interviewing team were women. This approach differs from other recent longitudinal studies where familiarity with specific data collectors was maintained to foster rapport. In the current study, participants were already familiar with the semi-structured interview format and the recording process, having participated in a face-to-face interview in a university laboratory during the baseline stage (T0).

Interviews at T1 were conducted online using a custom-built, secure platform designed to ensure data safety. The interview procedure comprised two distinct phases. In the first phase, the ‘narrative phase’, participants were invited to provide a detailed account of the most difficult life event they had experienced in the preceding year. The initial instruction was designed to be open-ended, for example: ‘Please describe the most difficult event you have experienced in the last 12 months, including your thoughts and feelings at the time it occurred’.

The second phase involved a series of questions exploring whether the participant perceived any long-term changes resulting from the event. These questions were systematically structured to cover specific domains: self-perception (e.g., ‘In what way, if any, has this experience changed how you see yourself?’), interpersonal relationships (e.g., ‘Has the way you relate to other people changed as a result of this event?’), and world-views (e.g., ‘Has your perspective on the world or the future shifted since this experience?’). Further inquiries addressed life priorities, behavioural patterns, and emotional regulation. Throughout this phase, interviewers utilised neutral probes, such as ‘Could you tell me more about that?’ or ‘What does that change mean to you?’, to encourage deeper reflection without suggesting a specific valence. All questions were carefully phrased to remain neutral, allowing participants to report both positive and negative outcomes, or the absence of change.

The interview procedure at T2 was identical to the T1 protocol, with the critical requirement that participants revisited the same seismic event narrated during the first wave. To maintain absolute interpretive independence and avoid narrative contamination, the interviewers at T2 were blind to the content of the T1 interviews and were strictly instructed not to refer to any previous statements made by the participants.

Transcription, anonymisation, and word count

Audio recordings were transcribed at a strict ‘verbatim’ level, capturing all fillers, hesitations, and false starts to preserve the natural flow of speech and ‘paralinguistic detail’ [41]. A detailed system utilised specific codes for pause duration and non-verbal sounds. Following manual verification against original recordings, transcripts were fully anonymised (P01–P26), and all identifiers for people, places, and institutions were removed.

The length of the T1 interviews ranged from 1,731 to 12,596 words (M = 4,370.38, SD = 2,630.64), whilst the T2 interviews ranged from 1,196 to 10,656 words (M = 3,334.88, SD = 2,144.15). The total corpus comprised 113,630 words at T1 and 86,707 words at T2. A paired-samples t-test indicated that the T2 interviews were significantly shorter than the T1 interviews, t(25) = 2.65, p = .014, Cohen’s d = 0.52.

Analytical strategy

The analysis followed a systematic, three-phase procedure grounded in reflexive thematic analysis (RTA). Following the methodological framework established by Braun and Clarke [38, 39], the research utilised a recursive six-phase process: familiarisation with the data; generating initial codes; searching for themes; reviewing potential themes; defining and naming themes; and producing the final report. To ensure analytical clarity, we distinguished between four hierarchical levels of analysis. First, domains represent broad conceptual spheres of posttraumatic change, encompassing the five established theoretical domains of the PTG model as well as two inductively identified emergent domains; illustrative quotes for these are presented in Table 2. Second, subthemes refer to specific inductive patterns of experience identified within these broader domains, such as assertiveness or emotional calm. Third, profiles constitute individual-level classifications based on the cross-sectional balance of positive and negative outcomes at a single time point, such as PTG-only or balanced orientations. Finally, trajectories represent the longitudinal patterns identified by comparing T1 and T2 narratives to track the evolution of meaning over the 36-month study period, including patterns of persistence or transience.

Phase 1: deductive coding and construct definition

The initial phase involved deductive, theory-driven coding of all 52 transcripts guided by established models [6]. We identified instances of PTG and PTD across the five established domains while simultaneously utilising ‘PTG – other’ and ‘PTD – other’ subcategories to inductively capture valid outcomes falling outside the traditional framework. As the analysis progressed, these emergent subthemes coalesced into two distinct domains: ‘Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation’ for growth and ‘Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity’ for depreciation. The prevalence and frequency of these codes across both waves are summarised in Table 2.

Phase 2: analysis of coexistence and profiling

In the second phase, we conducted a qualitative exploration into the coexistence of PTG and PTD across the full dataset of 52 longitudinal transcripts. This interpretive work focused on the inter-domain level, analysing how outcomes were distributed across different spheres, and the intra-domain level, investigating dialectical outcomes where growth and depreciation were perceived within a single sphere of life. This phase allowed for the classification of participants into individual profiles based on their specific balance of reported changes.

Phase 3: longitudinal comparative analysis and trajectory mapping

The final phase involved a direct, within-participant comparison of the 52 transcripts (26 from T1 and 26 from T2). The analytic logic for identifying change relied on a systematic within-case comparison, where T1 and T2 narratives were analysed side-by-side to detect shifts in narrative complexity, emotional valence, and the subjective re-evaluation of the SLE’s impact. This side-by-side comparison, led by the first author, focused on narrative shifts in content and structure, tracking how the meaning of the event evolved over the 36-month period. Building on the coding from Phase 1, the authors identified longitudinal trajectory patterns: Persistence, Transience, Latent Impact, and the Emergence of Complexity Interpretations were iteratively refined through collaborative dialogue between authors to ensure findings remained grounded in the lived experiences of the participants.

Findings

The findings are organized to reflect the multi-phase analytical strategy. The first subsection details the results of the deductive coding, focusing on the prevalence of PTG and PTD at both time points. The subsequent subsection explores the nuanced themes that emerged from the inductive analysis. The final part of this section examines the longitudinal trajectories by comparing participants’ T1 and T2 narratives.

Categories of perceived posttraumatic outcomes

The analysis of the deductive coding is summarized in Table 2. In the sample of 26 participants, nearly all reported at least one posttraumatic growth (PTG) change (24 in T1; 23 in T2). In contrast, posttraumatic depreciation (PTD) was less common, identified in the narratives of approximately half the participants (12 in T1; 16 in T2). While the number of participants reporting PTG and the total instances of PTG codes remained stable across the two waves, both the number of participants reporting PTD and the total frequency of PTD codes increased over time.

A significant portion of the changes reported by participants was identified within the emergent domain of ‘Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation’, particularly for PTG. This category, capturing shifts in beliefs not fitting the established theoretical domains [42], was the most frequently coded for PTG at both time points. For PTD, whilst the ‘Relating to Others’ domain remained prominent, a notable increase was observed in the emergent domain of ‘Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity’ at T2.

The coding process revealed that the most frequently reported PTG and PTD domains were composed of distinct subcategories, highlighting the multifaceted nature of these broader constructs. To illustrate this, we now describe the most prominent of these sub-themes.

Posttraumatic Growth (PTG) domains

Relating to others

The most prominent themes in this domain occurred on two distinct levels. On a relational level, participants reported a heightened sense of closeness in specific relationships, often distinguishing between enhanced bonds with loved ones and a greater connection with other people, such as friends. For instance, one participant’s account of stronger friendships alongside a stable partnership illustrates the specific nature of these relational phenomena (P18,T2; see Table 2). On a more attitudinal level, a separate, frequently reported subcategory was a distinct social perspective, characterized by a greater sense of empathy and compassion for people beyond their immediate circle. As one participant explained:‘It definitely gave me more, (.) that kind of understanding for others. Because I always perceived myself as a person who demands a lot from herself, demands a lot from others, and, if someone said they were feeling unwell, I often thought to myself that they just didn’t feel like doing something. And I sort of learned for myself that even if someone doesn’t feel like doing something, there’s nothing wrong with that. And they can have various reasons for being that way. […] So, when it comes to my thinking about people, I have definitely become gentler, more understanding, more empathetic.’ (P08,T2).

‘It definitely gave me more, (.) that kind of understanding for others. Because I always perceived myself as a person who demands a lot from herself, demands a lot from others, and, if someone said they were feeling unwell, I often thought to myself that they just didn’t feel like doing something. And I sort of learned for myself that even if someone doesn’t feel like doing something, there’s nothing wrong with that. And they can have various reasons for being that way. […] So, when it comes to my thinking about people, I have definitely become gentler, more understanding, more empathetic.’ (P08,T2).

Others noted an increased tolerance for other people and a decreased tendency to judge them too quickly. An example of this is the following statement:‘I see the complexity more now, that people are very, very complicated. (.) But at the same time, it’s also hard for me to speak so unequivocally on various topics because I know that there are simply so many different truths. (.) I think I am just more, even more tolerant.’ (P21,T2).

This latter quote illustrates the occasional difficulty in assigning reported changes to a single PTG category. On one hand, it reflects a positive change in thinking about others, which can improve relationships. On the other hand, it is one of many statements from this participant indicating that the seismic event increased her reflexivity and tolerance not only for other people and their viewpoints but also for the complexity of the world itself.

Personal strength

Two main subcategories were distinguished in this area: an increased sense of control and agency (e.g., P18,T2), and an increased sense of self-worth. The latter is exemplified by this participant’s account:‘I definitely have, like, more trust in myself and, like, I’ve started to respect myself more, too. […] And, like, I think this situation also gave me something… maybe not a spectacular change in this regard, but something like, I perceive myself more positively now.’ (P11,T2).

New possibilities

The most prominent subcategory in this domain was an emphasis on living life consciously. For example:‘…thoughts and feelings connected with this came to the fore more intensely, that […] the pace of life is simply important. And the way, what meaning we give to some particular fragments or elements of it. That is, living life as consciously as possible […] whether it’s sadness, or it’s joy, whether we can consciously experience it.’ (P03,T2).

Another frequently reported theme was a re-evaluation of life priorities, which we categorized into sub-themes such as ‘health is most important’ and ‘family is most important.’ A few individuals also focused on new career plans they developed as a result of the seismic event (e.g., P10,T1).

Emergent domain: self-alignment and autonomous regulation

Within this category, we distinguished several prominent subcategories, including: self-awareness, assertiveness, emotional calm and distance, and self-compassion.

Self-awareness

A heightened sense of self-awareness as a consequence of the seismic event was a theme mentioned by half of the participants (13/26). They noted that their experience resulted in ‘deeper reflection on what is happening [and an encouragement] for deeper work on myself’ (P19,T1); learning to ‘listen to the voice that tells me how I should behave and what decision to make’ (P21,T2) and to ‘listen to [my] body’ (P26,T1); and ‘to be more authentic and trust myself’ (P06,T2). This subcategory is also illustrated by the narrative of P11,T1 in Table 3.

Assertiveness

A relatively large portion of participants (9/26) described a newfound sense of assertiveness, though they did not always use this specific term. This theme is exemplified by one account: ‘I think this situation taught me that you don’t always have to agree and be so compliant. That in other situations, I can also say no, and actually, what you’re thinking about now, you know, I don’t agree with it’ (P11,T1). Another participant framed it as discovering ‘healthy egoism or simply self-love’ (P08,T2). We considered whether it was appropriate to distinguish ‘assertiveness’ from ‘Personal Strength.’ While assertiveness can be seen as a form of personal strength, the phenomena reported by participants had a specific character, focusing on what one person termed ‘healthy egoism’ and affecting relationships by reducing compliance and submission to others’ opinions.

Greater emotional calm and distance

Nearly half of the participants (12/26) listed a greater sense of calm and emotional distance among the positive consequences of their SLE. For instance:‘When I look at it today from a distance, I think I handle situations much more coolly, like when something is happening, for example, somewhere in the family or concerning loved ones. I no longer have such a range of emotions, especially that kind of stress, fear, anxiety. I can somehow approach it with more distance.’ (P04,T2).

‘When I look at it today from a distance, I think I handle situations much more coolly, like when something is happening, for example, somewhere in the family or concerning loved ones. I no longer have such a range of emotions, especially that kind of stress, fear, anxiety. I can somehow approach it with more distance.’ (P04,T2).

Others noted a greater distance from ‘trivial things,’ enhanced composure, or enhanced emotion regulation skills.

Posttraumatic Depreciation (PTD) domains

Regarding PTD, we distinguished subcategories only for the ‘Relating to Others’ and ‘Others’ domains.

Relating to others

The most frequent theme (8/26) concerned reports of strained or damaged relationships with loved ones. This generally did not apply to all close relations, but specifically to those who did not offer support during the difficult event.

Some participants (5/26) also described a heightened sense of caution and distance towards people in general, saying, for example, that they ‘developed a thick skin towards others’ (P12,T2); had ‘less understanding or a bit less tolerance for people who feel sorry for themselves’ (P16,T2); or that when meeting new people, they now ‘give [themselves] even more time than before for it to become a close relationship’ (P24,T2).

A similar number (5/26) pointed to a diminished sense of trust in people and an endorsement of negative beliefs about others. As one participant described: ‘now, like, well, I know that it [evil] comes from people. That man is sometimes a wolf to man’ (P17,T1). Another said: ‘I now have this conviction that I have to be a bit more, maybe I want to be a bit more, careful with my trust towards other people’ (P22,T1).

Existential vulnerability and insecurity

Regarding the negative consequences of the SLE, we identified the emergent domain of ‘Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity’. Within this category, themes concerning a heightened sense of anxiety and uncertainty were distinguished, alongside a specific fear of mortality or illness. Participants described an intensified preoccupation with the ‘inevitability’ of aging and the fragility of life (e.g., P03, T2; P23, T2). This domain captures the profound disruption of the participant’s ‘assumptive world’, resulting in an enduring sense of existential precariousness.

In summary, the deductive analysis revealed a range of posttraumatic phenomena, with a notable prevalence of themes concerning interpersonal relationships and self-perception. The identification of the emergent domains—Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation and Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity—further underscores the diverse and individual nature of these experiences. However, this initial thematic overview does not reveal how these positive and negative outcomes are balanced within individual accounts. To address this, the following section provides an examination of the patterns of coexistence between PTG and PTD.

Profiles of coexistence and narrative ambivalence

As a next step in our analysis, we classified participants based on the balance of positive versus negative changes they attributed to the seismic life event. In both waves, profiles marked by exclusively positive change (PTG-only) or a predominance of positive change (PTG-dominant) clearly outnumbered those with PTD-dominant or PTD-only profiles.

Specifically, in T1, 14 (54%) participants were classified as PTG-only, 9 (35%) were PTG-dominant, 2 (8%) showed a balance of PTG and PTD, and 1 (4%) was PTD-only. In T2, the distribution shifted: 7 (27%) were PTG-only, 10 (38%) PTG-dominant, 5 (19%) balanced, 1 (4%) PTD-dominant, and 3 (12%) reported no change attributable to the SLE. Notably, all three ‘no-change’ cases in T2 had been classified as PTG-only in T1.

Overall, this pattern confirms that PTG is more common than PTD, while clearly demonstrating that the two can coexist within individuals. This coexistence also appeared to be more frequent at T2 (reported by 16 participants; 62%) than at T1 (11 participants; 42%). While a detailed analysis of these longitudinal shifts will be presented in a subsequent section, we first turn to a qualitative illustration of the most common patterns of co-occurrence. Our analysis revealed that participants most often reported positive and negative outcomes in different domains; however, some also described the coexistence of PTG and PTD within the same domain.

Coexistence across different domains

Increased strength at a relational cost

The most common pattern of coexistence across domains involved participants reporting a positive impact on their sense of control or self-worth (Personal Strength domain) while simultaneously experiencing a deterioration in at least some of their relationships with others (Relating to Others domain). A clear example of this is the account of a participant who experienced workplace mobbing:‘in the end, I, like, came out of it strengthened. […] I was also so calm, knowing that I have it in me to fight for myself, to not give in. And I didn’t know that side of myself. Because I had never, like, been in such a difficult situation before, specifically a professional one. […] and there’s another side to the coin: that I’m also a bit less trusting of the professional environment. Even in my current job where it’s very nice, really, and pleasant, when I started, I started with fear.’ (P17,T1).

The inverse pattern: depreciation in strength, growth in relationships

The coexistence of PTG in relationships and PTD in personal strength could also occur. For example, participant P16 noted that the purpose of her difficult experience was to ‘be brought down a peg’. A key consequence was the realization that her prior belief that ‘a person is limited by nothing’ and ‘can choose to do whatever they want’ was not confirmed by her experience. This illustrates a depreciation in her sense of personal agency, which coexisted with a significant growth in her relationships, as she noted that the same experience had greatly strengthened and brought her family closer together.

The bittersweet nature of assertiveness

Several participants noted that the seismic event led to their increased assertiveness, a theme we previously discussed within the Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation category. However, when viewed in the context of co-occurring PTG and PTD, it raises the question of whether this newfound assertiveness is a purely positive outcome. When asked about changes in her thinking about other people, one participant (P07) replied:I certainly became sure of the fact that if this was my goal, well, then I have to focus on it and I have to realize it. And at that moment, my well-being is the most important thing. Not the well-being of others.

I certainly became sure of the fact that if this was my goal, well, then I have to focus on it and I have to realize it. And at that moment, my well-being is the most important thing. Not the well-being of others.

On one hand, we see a form of personal strengthening, but on the other, a more negative shift in attitude towards other people and their needs. Another participant’s account has a similarly bittersweet quality: ‘what has definitely changed is that, basically, like, it’s cool that we’re like a family, cool, fine. But […] it’s not like if someone is my family, they have some kind of, I don’t know, greater rights.’ (P13,T1).

Confronting mortality: fear and growth

Several participants noted that the adverse event they described led to a heightened fear of the future or fear of death. At the same time, however, these same individuals perceived a positive impact of this experience in other areas, such as spiritual and existential development, as well as personal strength. One participant described this paradoxical connection as follows:‘Thoughts appear that are saturated with a kind of fear that wasn’t there before. On one hand, it’s an enrichment of life, and on the other, it’s also a kind of, well, preparing yourself for death, because it’s not a given that I will live to an old age; I could die even tomorrow, get killed in some accident or something. So, like, this way of thinking is just close to me, that (.) that death is an element of life and, […] I believe that these types of reflections, or even the feelings that come with them (.), that they are, well, truly conducive to a person’s development and to a certain kind of sensitivity.’ (P03,T2).

The patterns described above represent the most common, though not exhaustive, ways in which PTG and PTD coexisted across different domains. An equally prominent pattern, though one more difficult to quantify, was the coexistence of positive and negative outcomes within a single domain. In these cases, participants often expressed a distinct ambivalence about the consequences of their experience, as detailed below.

Coexistence within the same domain

We identified only one domain, Relating to Others, where the coexistence of positive and negative outcomes was frequent enough to constitute a clear pattern. Participants who noted this ambivalence most often described how, as a result of the adverse event, some relationships deepened while others weakened. The primary cause of this re-evaluation was the experience of support-or lack thereof-from specific individuals during their struggle with the seismic life event.

Strengthening some bonds, weakening others

A common pattern was the deterioration of ties with certain family members alongside a deepening of relationships with others. For example:‘It definitely had a further impact on my relationships with my family. In the sense that these relationships definitely cooled down a bit, so to speak. […] But I also think that it made me think, just generally, about my relationships. And in fact, I am trying to take better care of those relationships that I know were simply supportive for me during that most difficult time.’ (P13,T2).

In summary, these narratives illustrate that the domain of Relating to Others is a key arena for the complex interplay of posttraumatic growth and depreciation. The coexistence of PTG and PTD within this single domain is not uniform; rather, it manifests in diverse patterns of relational re-evaluation. As the examples show, this can involve a strengthening of supportive bonds alongside a cooling of unsupportive ones, a recalibration of family versus partner relationships, or even the formation of new connections that stand in contrast to older, strained ties. These findings highlight the nuanced and often dialectical nature of social adaptation following a seismic life event.

Dynamics and Trajectories of PTG and PTD

While the preceding analyses provided a cross-sectional snapshot of PTG and PTD, the longitudinal design of this study allows for an examination of their evolution. This section explores the dynamics and trajectories of these phenomena over the 3-year period between T1 and T2. Based on a within-participant comparison of the two interviews, our comparison of the two interviews allowed us to observe various longitudinal dynamics, such as the stability of some outcomes, the emergence or decline of others, and the transformation of their meaning, which are detailed below.

A quantitative comparison of the number of coded instances of positive (PTG) and negative (PTD) outcomes between T1 and T2 revealed substantial heterogeneity (see Supplementary Table S1 for participant-level data). For PTG, 8 (31%) participants produced more coded instances in T2 than in T1, while another 8 (31%) produced fewer; among the latter, 3 (12%) provided no PTG codes in T2 despite doing so in T1. For PTD, the pattern differed: 10 (38%) participants produced more coded instances in T2 than in T1, whereas 3 (12%) produced fewer. Only 6 (23%) participants showed comparable counts across waves (difference ≤ 1) for both PTG and PTD.

Because frequencies of coded instances alone do not capture the full picture, we next conducted a qualitative analysis to examine how the content and intensity of PTG and PTD shifted from T1 to T2 for each participant, and to identify common trajectory patterns over the three-year interval.

Shifts in narrative and the perceived significance of the SLE

We first examined the similarities and differences in the narrative content of the SLE as presented up to 12 months after the event (T1) and 3 years later (T2). In the vast majority of cases, we observed a high degree of similarity in the content of the story being told, which appears consistent with the literature on the memory of events with high emotional significance. There were, however, exceptions where the narrated stories differed in more or less significant details. A few individuals stated in T2 that they remembered the event poorly.

Re-evaluating the event’s significance

Several participants assessed in T2 that, with the passage of time, the significance of the event they narrated in T1 had turned out to be smaller than they had initially perceived, or even negligible. For example, participant P09, who in T1 spoke of the SLE (his mother’s cancer diagnosis) influencing a deepening of his relationships and spiritual development, indicated no such outcomes in T2, explaining it as follows:‘I think it might have had an impact back then. But from today’s perspective, no. I mean, I simply don’t have that event in my head anymore. […] I probably gave different answers before, but with the passage of time, the event just has less impact now. Than it had before.’ (P09,T2).

‘I think it might have had an impact back then. But from today’s perspective, no. I mean, I simply don’t have that event in my head anymore. […] I probably gave different answers before, but with the passage of time, the event just has less impact now. Than it had before.’ (P09,T2).

This participant noted the influence of subsequent events, saying:‘That event, like, it ended so favourably, so it’s just not as significant to me as it was then, it wasn’t so heavy. And in place of that event, like […] a lot of things have happened during this time. And I have other things in my head that have influenced what you are asking about.’ (P09,T2).

‘That event, like, it ended so favourably, so it’s just not as significant to me as it was then, it wasn’t so heavy. And in place of that event, like […] a lot of things have happened during this time. And I have other things in my head that have influenced what you are asking about.’ (P09,T2).

This was a common theme. For instance, participant P16, who spoke in T1 about the SLE (loss of a pregnancy) improving her relationships and changing her life priorities, stated in T2 that the event ‘probably doesn’t arouse any emotions in me at all today. But that’s because, so to speak, the story has a happy ending. I mean, after some time I got pregnant again and gave birth to a baby girl.’ A similar reappraisal was offered by P20, whose initial ‘complete tragedy’ of a relationship crisis transformed into the ‘happiest thing that could have happened’ because it led to a lasting marriage. She added, ‘it’s strange for me to talk about it now, because from this time perspective, I’ve completely forgotten about those events’ (P20,T2).

While several others also downplayed the impact of the SLE over time, there were isolated cases of the opposite, where an individual who perceived little impact in T1 saw a significant one in T2. In a few instances, the valence of the outcome also shifted. For example, P10 described a relationship deteriorating in T1 but stated in T2 that the same relationship had improved thanks to the SLE. Conversely, P07, who in T1 reported a PTD-like shift towards self-interest (‘my well-being is the most important thing. Not the well-being of others’), described a complete reversal in T2:‘I can’t imagine sacrificing family relationships for the sake of, I don’t know [achieving my own career goals]. I’m just not at that point in my life anymore. I was back then. But now, after these few years, not anymore.’ (P07,T2).

‘I can’t imagine sacrificing family relationships for the sake of, I don’t know [achieving my own career goals]. I’m just not at that point in my life anymore. I was back then. But now, after these few years, not anymore.’ (P07,T2).

Common trajectory patterns

‘Too soon for change’: the latent impact of the SLE

Especially in T1, some participants (19.2%, n = 5), when asked about the impact of the SLE, responded with noticeable hesitation and uncertainty. This might suggest that, at that point, their reflections on the event’s impact had not yet fully consolidated. One participant, asked in T1 about her self-perception, said:‘[…] now, like, I should probably say yes, that I feel super… but in fact it hasn’t really sunk in yet, because I’m still in this, you know, kind of shaky place […] Too soon. Like, maybe after the holidays I’ll say to myself: yes, I’m proud. […] But, as of today… I think it still needs a bit more time.’ (P08,T1).

‘[…] now, like, I should probably say yes, that I feel super… but in fact it hasn’t really sunk in yet, because I’m still in this, you know, kind of shaky place […] Too soon. Like, maybe after the holidays I’ll say to myself: yes, I’m proud. […] But, as of today… I think it still needs a bit more time.’ (P08,T1).

Another participant (P21) indicated almost no consequences of her SLE (a sudden family relocation to another continent) in T1, yet described her state in a peculiar way: ‘I feel this, this tension rising. Completely uncaused. Because I simply have, an ideal life situation right now. Really! […] Which makes it even stranger that I constantly feel this tension. And headaches’ (P21,T1). This may reflect a process of avoiding reflection and a predominance of intrusive rather than deliberate rumination at that early stage [43, 44]. Over three years later, the same person said of the SLE:‘I don’t know what I said back then, but […] if I had to make that decision again now, I would never agree to that move. […] Jesus, how hard this is for me now. […] So I think, it was awful. An awful event, an awful time for me […] I’m curious how I will see it in, like, five years. Because it’s already an interesting view from this three-year perspective. It gave me this opportunity, for such a reflection that, what I felt three years ago was the truth. […] it’s not like now three years have passed and oh Jesus, what was I thinking, what nonsense that was.’ (P21, T2).

‘I don’t know what I said back then, but […] if I had to make that decision again now, I would never agree to that move. […] Jesus, how hard this is for me now. […] So I think, it was awful. An awful event, an awful time for me […] I’m curious how I will see it in, like, five years. Because it’s already an interesting view from this three-year perspective. It gave me this opportunity, for such a reflection that, what I felt three years ago was the truth. […] it’s not like now three years have passed and oh Jesus, what was I thinking, what nonsense that was.’ (P21, T2).

In T2, P21 assessed that the SLE had profoundly influenced her thinking about herself and others, in both positive (PTG) and negative (PTD) ways. As Tedeschi & Calhoun [6] note, the process of PTG requires time for cognitive engagement and deliberate rumination. It is possible that the T1 interviews, sometimes conducted only a few months after the SLE, were too soon to reveal outcomes that were still developing.

The transience of change

Conversely, a number of participants (11.5%, n = 3) in T2 either explicitly declared the short-lived nature of the SLE’s impact or their reported PTG and PTD were clearly less intense and widespread than in T1. For instance, P04 stated in T2:‘For a short moment I slowed down a bit and it was actually quite significant and, like, it allowed me to really slow down in life and take care of other things. Unfortunately, later I quickly fell back into certain routines and I think that later, unfortunately, it was sort of forgotten.’ (P04,T2).

‘For a short moment I slowed down a bit and it was actually quite significant and, like, it allowed me to really slow down in life and take care of other things. Unfortunately, later I quickly fell back into certain routines and I think that later, unfortunately, it was sort of forgotten.’ (P04,T2).

Particularly difficult to interpret are the relatively numerous situations in our corpus where participants in T2 did not mention the impact of the SLE in domains where they had noted such an impact in T1. This could, of course, signify the transience of these outcomes. However, it could also be a consequence of our interview procedure, in which we avoided prompting for specific domains, and a participant may simply not have mentioned a particular topic during the conversation. We will return to this issue in the Discussion.

Persistence of PTG and PTD

The most common trajectory we observed was the relative persistence of PTG and PTD over time (42.3%, n = 11). Some participants, when describing the SLE’s impact in T1 and again 3 years later, used almost the exact same phrases. More often, the similarity concerned the domains of the outcomes. For example, participants who claimed in T1 that the SLE had deepened their relationships made a similar assessment in T2.

Qualitative comparison of the interviews revealed that even when the overall intensity of PTG seemed stable, the T2 account was often more reflective, or the outcomes concerned more general rather than specific beliefs. For instance, a report of a changed relationship with a specific person in T1 might evolve into a report of greater empathy for people in general in T2. This applied to negative outcomes as well. Deeper reflection could also mean a less straightforward and more nuanced picture. For example, P08, who in both interviews mentioned increased self-compassion, added a new reflection in T2- that this self-compassion could sometimes be a form of self-justification.

Emergence of greater PTD in T2

A substantial portion of participants (38.5%, n = 10) reported an increase in, or the emergence of previously unnoted, negative outcomes in T2. Generally, this did not coincide with a decrease in the significance of positive outcomes (PTG). Rather, the overall picture became more complex and varied. As P22 put it:‘It all unfolded so dynamically, it sort of showed me that (.) well, this world isn’t quite as black and white as I thought […]. this world became a bit less predictable for me […] it can surprise you and in this world, I also can’t take certain things for granted. […] but on the other hand, I discovered so much flavour in this world, so much vividness, so much colour and such beautiful emotions and beautiful stories and such that it’s precisely this unpredictability that makes life not boring.’ (P22,T2).

‘It all unfolded so dynamically, it sort of showed me that (.) well, this world isn’t quite as black and white as I thought […]. this world became a bit less predictable for me […] it can surprise you and in this world, I also can’t take certain things for granted. […] but on the other hand, I discovered so much flavour in this world, so much vividness, so much colour and such beautiful emotions and beautiful stories and such that it’s precisely this unpredictability that makes life not boring.’ (P22,T2).

For some, especially those whose SLE was related to illness or death, an additional consequence noted in T2 was an increased fear of death, but also a greater acceptance of mortality.

In summary, our longitudinal analysis did not reveal one single pattern of change, nor even a few clearly distinct clusters. Rather, the data show a great diversity of individual trajectories. We observed patterns of persistence, dissipation of initial outcomes, and the delayed emergence of both PTG and PTD. This suggests that the process of adaptation following a seismic life event is not a linear path toward resolution but an ongoing, dynamic, and highly individualized process.

It is important to note that these trajectories were not mutually exclusive; in several cases, participants’ narratives exhibited an intersection of patterns, such as an initial Latent Impact that subsequently evolved into the Emergence of Greater PTD at T2.

Discussion

The present study utilised a longitudinal qualitative framework to investigate the perceived long-term consequences of seismic life events over a 36-month period. Our findings, characterised by the rich narrative detail of the participants’ accounts, demonstrate that posttraumatic adaptation is not a linear progression toward recovery, but a complex and often paradoxical process of identity reconstruction. By following participants from the initial aftermath to a three-year follow-up, we were able to observe the emergence of outcomes that significantly transcend the traditional five-domain model of posttraumatic growth [6, 42].

Posttraumatic outcomes transcending the five-factor model

The findings reveal that in over 70% of narratives, participants reported posttraumatic changes that transcend the traditional five-domain framework [42]. The identification of two emergent domains - Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation for growth and Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity for depreciation - addresses a critical theoretical gap in current conceptualisations of the aftermath of trauma. This aligns with evidence that posttraumatic boundaries are broader than previously theorised, as survivors often define their recovery or struggle through dimensions such as individual growth or existential shifts that traditional inventories fail to examine [45].

Regarding PTG, narratives suggest that the primary legacy of a seismic event is a shift in narrative identity towards greater authenticity and internalised regulatory capacity. This is substantiated by the proposal that PTG is best understood as ‘growth in virtue’, where adversity provides unique conditions and opportunities for developing traits like fortitude, humility, and wisdom [46].

Conversely, the domain of Existential Vulnerability and Insecurity captures enduring negative shifts, involving heightened anxiety and a preoccupation with life’s fragility. These accounts reflect a profound disruption of the assumptive world [4], where depreciation represents an ontological shift towards existential precariousness rather than a mere absence of growth. The 36-month stability of these outcomes supports viewing posttraumatic change as an enduring personality transformation rather than a transient coping mechanism [26]. By uncovering these dimensions, the present study provides a comprehensive framework for navigating the long-term aftermath of crisis.

The coexistence of growth and depreciation

The findings provide robust empirical support for the simultaneous occurrence of positive and negative cognitive changes. This corroborates the assertion that PTG and PTD represent independent processes rather than opposite ends of a single continuum [9, 13]. Our results are strongly supported by recent mixed-methods evidence from Agtarap et al. [47], who utilised thematic analysis of free-response narratives to demonstrate that themes of growth and depreciation are not mutually exclusive and frequently co-occur within the same individual. Their results specifically highlight that PTD, such as perceiving oneself as weaker or less capable following a seismic event, often exists concurrently with reports of increased personal strength or the pursuit of new possibilities. This co-occurrence extends earlier observations [16] by indicating not only their presence across distinct domains but also the emergence of a more nuanced, dialectical representation of posttraumatic experience. Rather than reflecting merely ‘conflicting outcomes’, these results point to an integrative reorganisation of beliefs about the self, the world, and others. In line with the integration thesis, posttraumatic adaptation appears to involve the incorporation of existential vulnerability into a more differentiated and resilient self-concept. In this framework, growth in virtues—including emergent domains like self-awareness and assertiveness—does not eliminate perceived fragility but provides a reflexive framework for engaging with it [45, 46].

The observation that PTD was reported less frequently than PTG aligns with established empirical patterns in posttraumatic research. Quantitative evidence from Kroemeke et al. [20] indicates that a profile characterised by high growth and low depreciation is the most prevalent, accounting for 52.4% of their sample. Similarly, in qualitative research, reports of growth have been found to be far more prevalent; Zięba et al. [16] noted that predominantly positive changes (41.4%) outstripped predominantly negative reports (10%) by a wide margin. This pattern is further corroborated by the aforementioned thematic analysis of Agtarap et al. [47], in which growth themes were identified more than three times as frequently as those related to depreciation. This discrepancy across methodologies suggests that while PTD is an objective component of the posttraumatic experience, it may be less narratively salient than the socially and professionally reinforced framework of growth, or perhaps more readily integrated into a dialectical identity rather than being reported as a standalone deficit.

Our findings underscore that posttraumatic change is not a zero-sum game. Crucially, PTG may coexist not only with PTD but also with persistent PTSD symptoms in a ‘Janus-faced’ manner. In this framework, severe distress and functional gains reflect a complex, dialectical adaptation rather than a linear progression toward recovery [5, 21, 48]. This co-occurrence suggests that reports of growth do not signify the absence of pain or the resolution of trauma-related symptoms, but rather the ongoing cognitive struggle to integrate that pain into a revised life narrative.

Temporal dynamics: permanent change versus transient coping

The 36-month follow-up provided a critical temporal lens through which to evaluate the ‘growth illusion’ critique [10, 27]. The identification of the Transience of Change pattern suggests that initial perceptions of growth may sometimes represent transient benefit finding – a cognitive coping strategy aimed at immediate distress reduction [49] - rather than a stable revision of fundamental beliefs [26, 35]. However, the persistence of self-awareness and assertiveness in over half of the sample suggests that these specific outcomes are more likely to represent permanent cognitive transformations that withstand the passage of time.

Distinguishing PTG from the conceptually adjacent phenomenon of benefit finding is crucial to avoid reductionistic interpretations of posttraumatic change [50]. Rather than representing ‘toxic positivity’ or a redemptive narrative that invalidates pain, benefit findings serves as a functional cognitive-experiential process and a vital self-regulatory coping resource [51]. It facilitates a positive reframing that coexists with—rather than replaces—the ongoing struggle, reflecting an accurate assessment of incremental progress. Furthermore, our findings suggest that reports of growth are inherently subject to temporal salience. Our participants’ narratives suggested that these longitudinal shifts were primarily driven by ongoing deliberate rumination [24] and the gradual integration of the SLE into a broader life story [52], often facilitated by changing social contexts over the 36-month period. Recognising this fluidity prevents a static view of adaptation, suggesting that while some reports of growth may fluctuate, they remain essential components of the broader, dialectical process of navigating life after trauma.

Clinical and practical implications

The detailed thematic outcomes identified in this study have significant implications for clinical practice. Mental health professionals should be prepared for the simultaneous presence of positive and negative shifts, recognising that a client’s report of increased personal agency may coexist with heightened existential vulnerability. Therapy should move beyond a reductive focus on symptom reduction, aiming instead to help clients integrate these ambivalent changes into a cohesive narrative identity. Furthermore, the emergent domains of self-awareness and assertiveness suggest that fostering a client’s capacity for boundary-setting may be a vital component of supporting genuine, long-term posttraumatic transformation.

Strengths, limitations, and future research

The primary strength of this study lies in its longitudinal qualitative design, which allowed for the detection of non-linear adaptation patterns and trajectories – such as transience and latent impact - that are inherently inaccessible to cross-sectional research. By employing reflexive thematic analysis over a 36-month interval, we provided a ‘thick description’ of posttraumatic change, offering empirical weight to the critique of the ‘growth illusion’ while simultaneously identifying enduring intrapsychic transformations. Furthermore, the identification of emergent domains like self-awareness and assertiveness addresses a significant gap in the traditional five-domain model, providing a more culturally and contextually nuanced understanding of posttraumatic adaptation.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The sample consisted of psychology graduates, a group that may possess a more specialised vocabulary and a higher capacity for introspection. While this facilitated rich data collection, it also introduces the risk of demand characteristics, where participants might narrate their experiences to fit psychological archetypes. However, the presence of diverse trajectories—including those showing no change or persistent depreciation—suggests that participants felt free to deviate from socially desirable ‘growth’ narratives.

Additionally, significant attrition from the initial cohort and the reliance on retrospective self-reports may have introduced recall biases, although the longitudinal within-participant comparison partially mitigated these effects. The findings are also situated within a specific socio-cultural context, and the degree to which these emergent domains of growth apply to more diverse populations remains to be established.

Finally, the interpretative nature of reflexive thematic analysis must be acknowledged as a potential limitation. Coding complex domains such as personal strength or relating to others involves a degree of researcher subjectivity, as these categories are not fixed entities but are instead co-constructed through the interaction between the participant’s narrative and the researcher’s interpretive lens [53]. While we utilised reflexive practices to manage this subjectivity, the findings remains a ‘double hermeneutic’ in which the researchers are attempting to make sense of the participants’ own sense-making processes [54]. Consequently, while the qualitative depth of this study provides rich insights into the lived experience of posttraumatic change, these interpretations are inherently subjective and should be viewed as one of several possible ways to understand the narrated trajectories of the participants.

Future research should focus on the quantitative verification of the emergent domains identified in this study, particularly self-awareness and assertiveness, to determine their prevalence in larger, more representative samples. Furthermore, the application of person-centred quantitative methodologies, such as Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), latent class growth analysis (LCGA), or Cluster Analysis—as demonstrated in recent research identifying distinct PTG and PTD subgroups [18, 20, 55]—would be valuable for providing a broader empirical foundation for the complex co-occurrence patterns observed in our qualitative narratives.

Crucially, future longitudinal designs should account for individual and social factors that potentially modify the growth process, such as hope [56–58], self-compassion [59, 60], and compassion from others [60]. Following Gilbert’s conceptualization, compassion is a care-focused motivation rooted in evolved caring systems, defined as a sensitivity to suffering coupled with a commitment to its alleviation. Investigating whether fostering these resources—specifically through the activation of these caring systems to regulate the physiological impact of distress —facilitates the transition from intrusive distress to the deliberate, reflective processing necessary for lasting PTG remains a priority for both research and clinical practice.

It is also essential to explore how subsequent life events interact with these internal resources as moderators, as our data suggest that later experiences can significantly alter or even reverse the perceived impact of a prior seismic event. Finally, further qualitative inquiry into the ‘relational cost’ of growth could provide deeper insights into how individuals navigate the social friction that often accompanies increased authenticity and personal autonomy following a crisis.

Conclusions

The present 36-month longitudinal analysis challenges the linear and exclusively positive conceptualisations of posttraumatic adaptation often found in cross-sectional literature. Our findings demonstrate that the reconstruction of the ‘assumptive world’ following a seismic life event is a dynamic, ongoing process characterized by the persistent coexistence of Posttraumatic Growth (PTG) and Posttraumatic Depreciation (PTD). By tracking narrative trajectories over three years, we identified that adaptation frequently involves a trade-off, specifically the pattern of ‘Increased Strength at a Relational Cost,’ where gained autonomy and assertiveness may necessitate the loosening of social bonds.

The identification of emergent domains—specifically Self-Alignment and Autonomous Regulation for growth and Existential Vulnerability for depreciation—suggests that traditional measurement tools may overlook critical, culturally nuanced dimensions of recovery. Crucially, the longitudinal nature of this study offers a methodological resolution to the ‘growth illusion’ debate. By distinguishing between the ‘Transience’ trajectory (likely reflecting short-term coping or illusory growth) and the ‘Persistence’ trajectory (reflecting enduring schema change), we argue that genuine personality transformation is distinguishable from defensive denial, but only through the lens of time.

For clinical practice, these results suggest a paradigm shift away from purely symptom-reduction models or the pressure to find ‘silver linings.’ Interventions should instead focus on narrative integration, helping clients to tolerate the ambivalence of simultaneous growth and depreciation. Clinicians should be prepared to support clients in navigating the ‘relational costs’ of their growth, validating that increased self-awareness and boundary-setting (assertiveness), while psychologically protective, may disrupt existing social dynamics. Ultimately, posttraumatic adaptation should be understood not as a final state of recovery, but as a continuous process of navigating a more complex, and often more fragile, reality.
