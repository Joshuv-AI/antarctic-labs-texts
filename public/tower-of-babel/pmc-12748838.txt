Microvascular skeletal muscle oxygenation and exercise capacity in childhood cancer survivors compared to healthy controls: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nel Van Ermengem, Deveny Vanrusselt, Anne Uyttebroeck, Véronique Cornelissen, Sabine Verschueren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the study was to evaluate skeletal muscle oxygenation and exercise capacity in childhood cancer survivors (CCS), and assess their association, in comparison with healthy controls. Twenty-six CCS (12 male, 6 months-10 yrs after treatment, mean age 17.59 (IQR:5.21 yrs) and 26 matched control subjects were recruited. Subjects performed a maximal incremental cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) on a treadmill with measurement of VO2peak (peak oxygen uptake). Simultaneously, muscle oxygenation was measured in the gastrocnemius muscle via Near InfraRed Spectroscopy. Changes (µM) in deoxyhemoglobin (ΔHHb), total hemoglobin (ΔtHb), and tissue saturation index (ΔTSI) were calculated as the differences between maximal (ΔHHb/ΔtHb) or minimal (ΔTSI) exercise test- and rest values. CCS had a significantly lower VO2peak (-12.05 ml/kg/min, p < .001) compared to controls. CCS also demonstrated a significantly lower fractional O2 extraction (ΔHHb) during CPET (10.81µM (IQR:10.51) vs. 14.66µM (IQR:14.23), p = .036). In a univariate stepwise linear regression analysis, a significant overall model fit was found with ΔHHb and HRmax explaining 32.6% of the variance in VO2peak among CCS. These results indicate that both central and peripheral limiting factors might contribute to lower CRF in CCS, therefore both should be considered when designing exercise interventions aimed to enhance CRF in CCS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28861-2.Keywords: Childhood cancer survivor, Cardiorespiratory fitness, Near infrared spectroscopy, Muscle oxygenation, Muscle NIRS, ExerciseSubject terms: Cancer, Physiology, Oncology

Introduction

Childhood cancer survivors (CCS) are at risk of long-term adverse health effects, significantly impacting their quality of life1–3. Impaired cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is a prevalent long-term adverse effect among CCS3–5. Low CRF is strongly associated with elevated risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD), premature mortality, emotional distress and attainment of social roles6–10. The gold standard assessment method for CRF is a cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) with measurement of peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak)11,12. Ness et al. (2020) reported a four-fold increased hazard of death when exercise intolerance (VO2peak < 85% predicted) was present in long-term CCS with mixed diagnoses6. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the underlying mechanisms of reduced CRF and exploring strategies to enhance it, in order to improve long-term health outcomes in CCS.

Limiting factors of CRF can be defined as central and/or peripheral13,14. Central limiting factors refer to impairments in pulmonary- and cardiovascular function and have been relatively well-studied in CCS15–20. That is, cancer therapies have been shown to impair pulmonary function, potentially causing pulmonary fibrosis, increased dead space ventilation, and ventilation-perfusion mismatch, all of which have been linked to reduced VO2peak13,14,16,17,20. Additionally, cancer treatments can lead to cardiovascular complications, such as arterial stiffening and left ventricular dysfunction contributing to a lower VO2peak14,15,18,19. However, these central limitations only explain partly the reduced VO2peak in CCS14. Peripheral limitations may involve structural and functional impairments at the level of the skeletal muscle capillaries and mitochondria, induced by cancer therapies and leading to impairments such as fat infiltration-, atrophy- and reduced oxidative capacity of the skeletal muscle14,21,22. Additionally, peripheral limitations may arise as a consequence of physical inactivity and disuse. While we anticipate a combined contribution of primary microvascular alterations and secondary muscular deconditioning, the relationship between peripheral changes and reduced VO₂peak in CCS remains insufficiently understood14.

Muscle oxygenation can be assessed by Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS), a non-invasive method that enables real-time monitoring of tissue oxygen dynamics23. NIRS measures the absorption of near-infrared light by oxyhemoglobin and oxymyoglobin (O2Hb + Mb) and deoxyhemoglobin and deoxymyoglobin (HHb + Mb). Previous studies have found that changes in O2Hb during exercise are mostly derived from an increased cardiac output, reflecting central factors24. Changes in HHb on the other hand, are used as an estimate of skeletal muscle fractional O2 extraction, thus more reflecting peripheral factors24.

Despite its potential, NIRS has been infrequently utilized in CCS research. Lanfranconi et al. (2014) explored muscle O2 extraction in ten survivors of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)25 and reported impairments in muscle oxygen extraction compared to healthy controls. In adult cancer survivors the available results remain inconclusive. Ederer et al. (2016) used NIRS during a CPET in a group of adult cancer survivors with mixed diagnoses and found that the muscle HHb response, which was used to evaluate changes in fractional O2 extraction, was significantly lower in cancer survivors compared to controls at the ventilatory threshold26. In contrast, Grigoriadis et al. (2022) reported no significant differences in muscle tissue saturation or HHb changes during exercise between breast cancer survivors (median 27 months after treatment) and healthy controls27. The validity of NIRS has not yet been established in CCS. However, Tandirerung et al. (2024) demonstrated that NIRS is a reliable in vivo method for assessing skeletal muscle oxidative capacity in non-athletic young adults (mean age 26 years), providing a rationale for its exploratory use in clinical populations such as CCS28.

Given the limited knowledge about muscle oxygenation in CCS, and the potential impact of pediatric cancer treatments on skeletal muscle function14,29, this study aimed at comparing skeletal muscle oxygen extraction and its relation to exercise capacity in CCS to healthy controls. We expect to find impaired muscle oxygen extraction, contributing to lower VO2peak in CCS compared to healthy controls.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study included 26 CCS and age- and sex- matched healthy control subjects. All subjects were aged between 14 and 28 years. CCS were included if initial cancer treatment was completed between 6 months and 10 years prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria included any documented intellectual disability or depression, as well as an inability to complete behavioral data assessments. These in- and exclusion criteria were selected to ensure consistency with the objectives and design of a broader research initiative30. Healthy controls were excluded if chronic illness was present. The study (S65208) was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of University Hospitals Leuven (B3222021000452). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with parents acting as representatives for minors.

Outcomes

Cardiorespiratory fitness: CPET

Participants performed a maximal incremental CPET on a treadmill according to a modified Bruce protocol. Participants started with a standing rest electrocardiogram and measurement of blood pressure followed by a 2–3 min warm-up to allow participants to adapt to the face mask and the treadmill. In the main part of the test participants were required to walk at a constant pace of 5.6 km/h while the inclination of the treadmill increased with 2% every minute. This protocol was selected to get a maximal exercise response within 8–12 min31. Heart rate (HR) was monitored continuously by a 12-lead electrocardiogram and blood pressure was monitored every two minutes. Gas exchange was analyzed using a computer-based system (HP Cosmos; Acertys: Aartselaar). Breath-by-breath minute ventilation (Ve), oxygen uptake (VO2) and carbon dioxide exhalation (VCO2) were registered. Participants were instructed to perform a maximal test until symptom limitation and were not allowed to use the handrails during the test. The test was considered maximal if the respiratory exchange rate (RER), defined as the ratio of CO2 production and O2 consumption (VCO2/VO2), reached a value of ≥ 1.10 or if the maximal heartrate was > 95% of predicted heartrate32. VO2peak was the main outcome and is defined as the peak amount of oxygen (ml/kg/min) that was on average consumed during the test’s last 30 s output. Other outcomes included HR before the test (HRpre), maximal HR (HRmax), peak oxygen pulse (O2pulse) and first ventilatory threshold (VT1).

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)

Muscle oxygenation was assessed non-invasively using Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) with a Portamon device (Artinis Medical Systems, The Netherlands). Participants received no instructions regarding nutrition, hydration or exercise prior to the test. The device was positioned vertically on the medial head of the right gastrocnemius muscle. Skinfold thickness at the measurement site was recorded using a Baseline skinfold caliper (USA) to account for potential interference from subcutaneous adipose tissue. Once placed, the device was secured with a black bandage to prevent movement and block ambient light, ensuring accurate measurements. The gastrocnemius muscle was selected due to its greater involvement and mechanical strain during incremental exercise protocols compared to the vastus lateralis muscle. This increased activation is expected to provide more sensitive insights into muscle oxygenation dynamics and peripheral oxygen extraction. Additionally, the gastrocnemius typically has a thinner layer of subcutaneous adipose tissue, which enhances signal quality, a known challenge in NIRS measurements33. The NIRS system, operated via Oxysoft software, used three light-emitting diodes operating at wavelengths of 760 nm and 850 nm. Transmitter-receiver distances were 30, 35 and 40 mm, providing a measuring depth of approximately 1.5–2.0 cm. Tissue thickness at the site was within acceptable ranges for both CCS (0.3–1.5 cm) and controls (0.2–1.4 cm), ensuring no depth-related issues. Device parameters included a differential pathlength factor (DPF) set at four as default and a sampling frequency of 10 Hz. Data were down-sampled to 1 Hz and a moving Gaussian filter with a 3-second window was applied. Analyses were based on the average of the three optode signals.

NIRS provides insights into skeletal muscle oxygenation by measuring changes in oxyhemoglobin and oxymyoglobin (O2Hb + Mb) as well as deoxyhemoglobin and deoxymyoglobin concentrations (HHb + Mb)34. Since the Mb concentration remains largely constant during exercise, for the purpose of this report, we will refer to changes in raw signals as changes in O2Hb and HHb24,34. At a wavelength of 760 nm, HHb is the main absorber of near-infrared light, whereas at 850 nm O2Hb is the main absorber24. Since the measurement is performed over time, relative concentration changes of these chromophores can be calculated.

As previously stated, changes in O2Hb during exercise are mostly derived from an increased cardiac output, thus reflecting central factors24. HHb on the other hand, is used as an estimate of skeletal muscle fractional O2 extraction, thus reflecting peripheral factors24. The sum of O2Hb and HHb is defined as the total hemoglobin concentration (tHb) and reflects supply of oxygen to the peripheral muscle24,34. Tissue saturation (TSI) was calculated as the ratio of O2Hb to tHb (TSI = [O2Hb/tHb] × 100) and reflects the percentage of oxygen within the muscle tissue24. Oxygenation changes detected by NIRS mainly reflect changes in capillary (Hb-related) and intracellular (Mb-related) O2 levels34.

The PortaMon device demonstrated moderate-to-excellent relative reliability scores for TSI (ICC: 0.66), O2Hb (ICC: 0.75) and differential hemoglobin [O2Hb-HHb] (ICC: 0.77) during maximal exercise testing35–37.

Measurements were initiated with a 2-minute baseline recording at rest (standing), followed by continuous data collection throughout the CPET, including the warm-up phase, exercise phase, and a 3 min recovery period. Recovery was calculated as the difference between maximal attained TSI during 3 min recovery and TSI during rest. ΔHHb, ΔtHb and ΔTSI were evaluated from rest through the exercise test and recovery.

Medical information.

Medical information about cancer- and treatment characteristics was obtained through patients’ medical records.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS statistics (version 29.0.1.0) and figures were created using R (version 2023.06.2 + 561) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1). Normality was assessed via histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test and outliers were removed. Independent t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to analyze group differences between CCS and controls. Pearson (r)- and Spearman (ρ) correlations were used to calculate the strength of the relation between test variables. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (< 0.40), moderate (0.40 ≤ ρ/r ≤ 0.60), and strong (> 0.60). Effect sizes (cohen’ s d) were calculated to assess the practical significance of observed differences. Two-sided alpha (α) was set to 0.05 for statistical significance. A priori sample size calculation was performed using the effect size reported by Lanfranconi et al. (2014) (Cohen’s d = 1.19)25. Using this effect size, a two-tailed independent samples t-test with α = 0.05 and power = 0.80 indicated that a minimum of 13 participants per group would be required to detect a statistically significant difference. Our study included 26 participants per group, exceeding this threshold and ensuring adequate power to detect effects of similar magnitude. Univariate linear regression was used to estimate the relationship between VO2peak and independent variables (HRmax, ΔHHb), with sex and age as covariates. Since VO2peak and ΔHHb were non-normally distributed a logarithmic transformation and square root transformation were applied respectively to normalize the data. A forward stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted and the variables were selected based on p-values (< 0.05). The regression analysis was conducted in SPSS and assumptions for normality, linearity and homoscedasticity were verified prior to analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors (VIFs) with a limit of VIF < 5.

CPET

CPET outcomes of CCS and controls are presented in Table 3. Male and female CCS had a lower resting oxygen consumption and VO2peak compared to their healthy peers. No significant differences in HRpre and HRmax were found between CCS and controls (t50 = −1.845; p =.071, t49 = −1.034; p =.306). Test duration was significantly shorter in CCS compared to controls (9.08 min vs. 11.69 min, t50 = −4.951; p <.001). CCS reached VT1 earlier and had a lower VO2 at VT1. Furthermore, VO2 at VT1 was also lower when expressed as a percentage of the predicted VO2peak in CCS38,39. 50.0% of CCS had a clear O2pulse breakpoint during the CPET, compared to 19.2% of healthy controls. The VO2 value at O2pulse breakpoint (expressed as a percentage of VO2peak) was 68.6% vs. 75.1% in CCS compared to controls. All CCS and controls achieved RER ≥ 1.10 or HRmax > 95% predicted, indicating a maximal exercise test.

NIRS

A representative example of a NIRS measurement is shown in Fig. 1. In CCS, HHb and tHb increased steeply during warm-up and exercise and then stabilized. In controls, HHb and tHb increased gradually and further compared to CCS. TSI decreased more rapidly in CCS and similarly stabilized earlier compared to controls. NIRS outcomes of CCS and controls are provided in Table 3. CCS demonstrated a significantly lower ΔHHb compared to controls (10.81 µM vs. 14.66 µM, p =.036). ΔTSI and ΔtHb were lower in CCS than controls, although not significantly (18.22% vs. 22.85%, p =.173; 5.97 µM vs. 8.58 µM, p =.587). Differences in ΔHHb and ΔtHb between CCS and controls are presented in Fig. 2. To interpret subject’s recovery, the maximal TSI during 180 s after the test was calculated. CCS reached a higher overshoot in TSI during recovery compared to controls (5.31% vs. 3.11%), however the difference was not statistically significant (p =.074).

A post hoc power analysis was conducted for the observed group differences in ΔHHb. The calculated effect size was Cohen’s d = 0.81, indicating a large difference between CCS controls. With 26 participants per group, and using a two-tailed test with α = 0.05, the estimated statistical power was 0.82, confirming that the study was adequately powered to detect this effect.

Correlations of outcomes with VO2peak

A strong significant negative correlation between BMI and VO2peak (ml/kg/min) was found in CCS (ρ = − 0.73, p <.001), but not in healthy controls (r = −.32, p =.112). There was a moderate significant correlation between ΔHHb and VO2peak in CCS (ρ = 0.45, p =.029) and in controls (ρ = 0.41, p =.039), presented in Fig. 3. The correlation was stronger in female, compared to male CCS (ρ = 0.51, p =.064 vs. ρ = − 0.02, p =.960). To account for the non-normal distribution, ΔHHb are presented as Z-scores. No significant correlations were found between VO2peak and ΔtHb or ΔTSI in CCS and controls. No significant correlations were found between VO2peak and the age at diagnosis, time since diagnosis, treatment time and time between the end of treatment and inclusion. Stepwise linear regression

To investigate the relationship between VO2peak and other included variables in CCS, forward stepwise linear regression was applied. Through stepwise selection, following variables were included in the final model for CCS after adjustment for age and sex: HRmax (unstandardized β = 0.235, se = 0.273, t(22) = 0.861, p =.400) and ΔHHb (unstandardized β = 2.965, se = 1.676, t(22) = 1.769, p =.094). To address non-normal distribution, the dependent variable (VO2peak) was log-transformed, and ΔHHb was square-root transformed. A significant and good model fit was found F(22) = 3.657, p =.024 with an adjusted R square value of 0.326. This indicates that the included variables explained 32.6% of the variance in the log-transformed VO2peak. Through stepwise selection of the same variables in controls, no significant model was found. Results of the stepwise linear regression are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study assessed skeletal muscle oxygenation in CCS compared to matched healthy control subjects. Muscle oxygen extraction was significantly lower in CCS compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, the significant correlation between ΔHHb and VO2peak in CCS indicates that lower muscle O2 extraction is associated with lower CRF in this population. The regression model showed a significant overall fit, with HRmax and ΔHHb contributing to low VO₂peak.

Overall, VO2peak was 12.05 ml/kg/min (26.2%) lower in CCS compared to healthy controls, despite both groups delivering a similar maximal effort during the test. These findings were independent of the time since treatment, as no correlation was found between time since treatment and VO2peak. This suggests that CCS, whether 0.5 years or 10 years after treatment, have a lower exercise capacity compared to healthy controls. Similar findings on CRF in CCS have been reported previously3,40. Pegon et al. (2020) found a mean VO2peak that was 11.2 ml/kg/min lower in ALL survivors compared to healthy controls (mean age 12.2 years, mean 4.9 years since treatment)41. In line, Tsuda et al. (2023) found a mean VO2peak that was 4.5 ml/kg/min lower in 53 CCS with mixed diagnoses (mean age 14.7 years) that were more than one year after treatment42. Fyfe et al. (2024) reviewed the effect of anthracycline chemotherapy on VO2peak in CCS compared to healthy controls. The weighted mean difference of pooled VO2peak of 18 studies was − 7.08 ml/kg/min (95%CI: −7.75;−6.42), p <.01. This indicates a significant overall reduction in VO2peak in CCS compared to healthy controls43. Since lower CRF is associated with higher mortality6, higher cancer related fatigue30, higher risk of cardiovascular disease1 and worse social- and neurocognitive outcomes10,44, understanding the mechanisms underlying low CRF is crucial.

The differences in VO2peak were even more pronounced in female CCS, having on average a 30.8% lower VO2peak compared to healthy peers. Caru et al. (2018) found similar results, reporting that VO2peak was 22% lower in long-term CCS compared to controls and that female survivors were most affected45. Jarvela et al. (2010) reported similar findings in long term CCS. Additionally, they observed that 36% of female CCS engaged in low levels of physical activity (≤ 5 MET h/w), compared to 30% of male CCS46. This finding is consistent with other studies reporting lower physical activity levels in female CCS, possibly explaining their lower CRF compared to male CCS47,48. Other potential sex-specific reasons for lower VO2peak should also be considered. Raguindin et al. (2024) found that female CCS were at higher risk of developing persisting psychological distress compared to male CCS49. Furthermore, female CCS are at risk of treatment-related endocrine late effects, including ovarian insufficiency and reduced estrogen levels. These hormonal disruptions may negatively influence physiological systems involved in CRF, such as muscle strength, bone health, and cardiovascular function50,51. Though, the direct relationship between endocrine late effects and CRF in female CCS remains underexplored. These interpretations remain based on existing literature rather than direct testing in our cohort.

CCS demonstrated significantly lower fractional O2 extraction during CPET compared to healthy controls, as reflected in the difference in ΔHHb between groups. This indicates the presence of peripheral impairments in CCS, likely reflecting a defect in the integrated pathway of oxygen delivery-to-utilization within the muscle. This could be due to impaired microvascular function limiting O2 delivery to the mitochondria, intrinsic mitochondrial dysfunction limiting O2 utilization or a combination of both14. Supporting this, Kattan et al. (2025) systematically reviewed the impact of anthracycline chemotherapy on vascular endothelial function in childhood cancer patients and survivors, reporting a pooled standardized mean difference in flow mediated-dilation of − 0.71% (95% CI: − 0.99 to − 0.45, p <.001) across 12 studies. These findings indicate significantly poorer endothelial function, which likely contributes to microvascular limitations in this population52. However, the mechanisms underlying reduced fractional O₂ extraction remain speculative, as our study did not include direct assessments of vascular or mitochondrial function. The significant correlation between fractional O2 extraction and VO2peak indicates that peripheral oxygen extraction capacity may contribute to the reduced aerobic capacity in CCS. These findings align with those of Lanfranconi et al. (2014), who also used NIRS to assess muscle oxygenation in ALL survivors compared to controls25. The authors reported that 50% of the variance of VO2peak could be explained by ΔHHb as an independent variable. In this study, ΔHHb was not an independent predictor. However, the CCS in our study were older, had a longer mean time since treatment and the group consisted of different types of cancer diagnoses.

Further possible evidence of peripheral impairments was found in the results of VT1. CCS reached on average their VT1 sooner and had a lower VO2 at VT1, indicating that CCS have a more rapid lactate accumulation in the muscle and thus a diminished oxidative capacity53. Ederer et al. (2016) also found significantly lower ΔTSI, ΔtHb and earlier VT1 in adult cancer survivors with mixed diagnoses (median 40.5 months after treatment) compared to controls26. Grigoriadis et al. (2022) found no significant differences between breast cancer survivors (median 27 months after treatment) and healthy controls in ΔTSI and ΔtHb27.

Another interesting finding was the higher TSI overshoot during recovery in CCS compared to healthy controls. Although this finding was not significant, it may reflect altered recovery kinetics. One potential mechanism is impaired mitochondrial oxidative capacity, leading to reduced post-exercise oxygen consumption and consequently elevated tissue oxygenation. This observation aligns with previous findings of diminished oxidative metabolism14 and warrants further investigation.

In light of these findings, we further explored whether peripheral and/or central limiting factors were associated with the decrease of CRF in CCS.

The adjusted regression model including HRmax and ΔHHb explained 32.6% of the variance in VO₂peak and showed a significant overall fit, although neither predictor reached individual statistical significance. As stated previously, only Lanfranconi et al. (2014) investigated the link between ΔHHb and VO2peak in CCS25. To our knowledge, HRmax has not been identified as a predictor for lower VO2peak in CCS before. However, Bertrand et al. (2021) described altered HR responses and even chronotropic incompetence (or the inability of the heart to appropriately increase its rate during exercise) during CPET in 250 long-term childhood ALL survivors54. HRmax and ΔHHb were not included in the stepwise linear regression model for healthy controls, possibly pointing towards different underlying mechanisms to limitations of CRF in this group.

This evidence might indicate that CCS have both central and peripheral limitations to CRF, underscoring the need for comprehensive intervention strategies addressing both aspects.

Previous exercise interventions to improve VO₂peak in CCS have yielded mixed results55–58. This raises the question of whether the optimal exercise parameters were selected to attain the desired training effects. Training strategies for CCS may require tailored approaches that account for both central and peripheral CRF limitations.

In this study, CCS demonstrated a significantly lower fractional O2 extraction during CPET compared to healthy controls, indicating the presence of peripheral impairments in CCS. To determine the most effective exercise interventions for addressing skeletal muscle dysfunction, it is essential to identify underlying mechanisms for the peripheral impairments in CCS. Dillon et al. (2024) identified mitochondrial damage and dysfunction, a shift from oxidative to glycolytic muscle fibers, increased muscle fat infiltration and muscle atrophy as the most common contributors to skeletal muscle dysfunction in cancer survivors. These changes likely underlie the blunted oxygen extraction found in our study. They are driven not only by cancer treatments such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy but also by behavioral-related factors such as physical inactivity, sedentary behavior and comorbidities14,21,22,59. Grydeland et al. (2023) found lower physical activity and higher sedentary time in CCS (mean age: 12.2 years) compared to age- and sex matched reference values60. Tan et al. (2025) reviewed current evidence on body composition and metabolism of CCS. They found a higher prevalence of sarcopenic obesity and metabolic changes in CCS compared to healthy controls61. Differences between CCS and healthy controls might thus stem from a combination of treatment- and behavioral related factors.

To address mitochondrial damage and dysfunction, a recent review by Bishop et al. (2025)62 synthesized current evidence on the impact of exercise on mitochondrial characteristics. High volumes of aerobic exercise were found to be an important parameter to increase mitochondrial respiration, oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial content. Evidence on the optimal intensity of exercise is unclear, however mitochondrial protein synthesis increased more with higher than lower-intensity aerobic exercise and less through resistance exercise. More research is needed to optimize the prescription of exercise to improve mitochondrial characteristics and the effect on CCS.

Research on strategies to mitigate muscle fat infiltration and muscle atrophy are currently lacking in CCS. Krull et al. (2020) investigated the effects of a 24 week resistance training (compared to resistance training and protein supplementation) on lean body mass and muscle strength in 57 CCS (mean age 33.1 ± 7.0) and found significant increases in both groups for lean body mass and muscle strength63. Both groups trained 2x/week and increased resistance from 60% of 1RM to 80% of 1RM. Braam et al. (2018) compared an exercise intervention to usual care in cancer patients during and shortly after treatment (mean age 13.2 ± 3.1). Children performed an aerobic and resistance program (individualized weight bearing exercises) 2x/week for 12 weeks. A significant increase in muscle strength was found in the exercise group64.

In conclusion, to improve the possible peripheral limiting factors of CRF in CCS at this moment we can only conclude that more research should be done to identify optimal FITT characteristics (Frequency, Intensity, Type, Time) for this specific population.

There are a few limitations to the study. Firstly, a cross-sectional study provides a single-point-in-time view, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships or temporal trends. Secondly, there are a number of factors that we could not include but that can contribute to decreased CRF in CCS such as a difference in nutrition or physical activity between groups. Although we invited all eligible CCS and recruited healthy controls from different backgrounds, potential selection bias regarding anthropometric characteristics and fitness level should not be overlooked. Another limitation was the relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability of the results.

Further, a huge variability in the NIRS methodology exists which makes it sometimes difficult to compare results across studies. For example, in our study, NIRS was measured on the gastrocnemius muscle whereas in other studies it is often measured on the vastus lateralis muscle. Since the gastrocnemius muscle is strained more during an incremental protocol compared to the vastus lateralis muscle, we hypothesized that more significant changes in muscle oxygenation and oxygen extraction would occur. Besides the type of muscle, NIRS provides information only about the specific tissue beneath the sensor and does not accurately represent the entire muscle34.

Furthermore, ΔHHb is often calculated as the difference between the maximal attained HHb and HHb during venous occlusion. Since we did not incorporate occlusion measurements, these were replaced by the HHb value during rest. An advantage of using the HHb value in rest is that the occlusion measurement -which is often perceived as unpleasant - is not necessary26,27. However, measurement of HHb during venous occlusion gives a better understanding of whether the problem is in the supply or in the extraction of the oxygen. Without the occlusion measurement, the question remains whether the better HHb values in controls are due to better vascular function or a truly better extraction of oxygen65. To strengthen our conclusions, we performed a sensitivity analysis with an alternative calculation for ΔHHb, expressing it as the difference between ΔHHbmax and VT1 instead of rest. We found that ΔHHbmax-VT1 was also significantly lower in CCS compared to controls (1.19 (IQR:2.51) vs. 2.98 (IQR:3.55), p =.007), supporting our primary observation of impaired peripheral oxygen extraction in CCS. Correlations between ΔHHbmax-VT1 and VO2peak were weaker in CCS and controls (ρ = 0.343, p =.086 vs. ρ = 0.019, p =.927) compared to correlations between ΔHHbmax-rest and VO2peak. This should be explored in future research.

Conclusions

The VO2peak of CCS was 26.2% lower compared to matched control subjects and even 30.8% lower when only considering the female subjects. This indicates a significant and clinically relevant decrease in CRF in CCS. This study provided evidence of reduced fractional skeletal muscle O2 extraction in CCS and this was associated with the lower CRF. Furthermore, maximal HR and skeletal muscle oxygen extraction were identified as joint predictors of lower CRF in CCS, suggesting both central and peripheral limitations play a role in the reduced CRF. These results underscore the importance of addressing both peripheral and central limitations when designing exercise interventions, aimed at improving CRF in CCS. On top, future research should gain a deeper understanding of the different muscular changes underlying the altered oxygen extraction and how they impact CRF in CCS.
