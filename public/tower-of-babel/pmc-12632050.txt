Long non-coding RNA AFAP1-AS1 promotes alternative splicing of AXIN2 by facilitating SRSFs phase separation to induce drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Jiawei Ouyang, Yijie Zhang, Xiangchan Hou, Fang Xiong, Lei Shi, Yu Zhong, Liting Yang, Can Guo, Zhaoyang Zeng, Qijia Yan, Pan Chen, Wei Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer currently holds the highest global incidence and mortality rates among malignant tumors, with lung adenocarcinoma being the most prevalent and deadly subtype. Chemotherapy and targeted therapy remain the standard treatments for lung adenocarcinoma, but tumor resistance continues to be a significant clinical challenge. In this study, we identified that the long non-coding RNA actin filament associated protein 1 antisense RNA 1 (AFAP1-AS1), which is highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma, regulates RNA alternative splicing by recruiting splicing factors such as serine and arginine rich splicing factor 1 and 3 (SRSF1 and SRSF3), promoting their liquid-liquid phase separation. Among the affected splicing events, the exon 7 skipping of the key regulatory gene AXIN2, involved in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, is most notably regulated by AFAP1-AS1, resulting in the translation of a truncated AXIN2 isoform (AXIN2-S). This isoform facilitates the accumulation of β-catenin in the nucleus and the persistent activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, ultimately contributing to drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma. Our work uncovers a novel function of AFAP1-AS1 in regulating RNA alternative splicing through the modulation of splicing factors phase separation. Main

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNA longer than 200 nucleotides (nt). Growing evidence indicates that lncRNAs play essential roles in maintaining normal biological functions and in the development and progression of various diseases [1–3]. Actin filament associated protein 1 antisense RNA 1 (AFAP1-AS1), a lncRNA transcribed from the antisense strand of the AFAP1 gene, is highly expressed in several malignancies, including esophageal cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and liver cancer [4–9]. AFAP1-AS1 promotes proliferation, invasion, migration, and other malignant behaviors of cancer cells by functioning as a microRNA (miRNA) sponge or by interacting with various tumor-associated proteins, thereby influencing their expression, function, and localization. As such, AFAP1-AS1 is considered an oncogenic lncRNA with significant implications in cancer biology [10].

We previously observed that AFAP1-AS1 is markedly overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma and is strongly correlated with poor patient prognosis. Using RNA pulldown assay combined with mass spectrometry, we identified a set of proteins binding to AFAP1-AS1. Among these, we validated that AFAP1-AS1 interacts with smad nuclear interacting protein 1 (SNIP1). This interaction prevents the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of c-Myc, leading to its upregulation and the subsequent activation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related target gene transcription. This pathway enhances the invasion and migration abilities of lung adenocarcinoma cells, promotes tumor metastasis and offers a partial explanation for the link between AFAP1-AS1 expression and poor prognosis in patients [11].

Interestingly, among the proteins identified as potential AFAP1-AS1 binding partners through RNA pulldown coupled with mass spectrometry, we discovered a series of RNA splicing-related proteins. This finding suggests that AFAP1-AS1 may play a role in RNA alternative splicing in lung adenocarcinoma. In eukaryotes, especially in the human genome, most genes are “split genes,” where precursor mRNA (pre-mRNA) transcribed from the genome contains both exon and intron sequences. The pre-mRNA undergoes splicing and processing within the nucleus to form mature RNA, which is then exported to the cytoplasm to guide protein translation and carry out biological functions. Increasing evidence shows that during pre-mRNA splicing, various splicing patterns can generate different isoforms from the same pre-mRNA by selectively including or excluding exons or introns. These isoforms may encode proteins with distinct sequences and functions, expanding protein diversity and serving as a key regulatory mechanism in eukaryotic gene expression. Moreover, alternative splicing has been linked to the initiation and progression of malignant tumors [12, 13]. However, the specific role of AFAP1-AS1 in RNA alternative splicing remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we conducted experiments confirming that AFAP1-AS1 directly binds to RNA splicing factors, including serine and arginine rich splicing factor 1 and 3 (SRSF1 and SRSF3). This interaction promotes their liquid-liquid phase separation, thereby altering RNA alternative splicing in lung adenocarcinoma. We further discovered that AFAP1-AS1 regulates exon 7 skipping in AXIN2 pre-mRNA, a critical regulatory factor in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. This splicing event results in the expression of a AXIN2 short isoform (AXIN2-S), which inhibits the degradation of β-catenin. Consequently, β-catenin accumulates in the nucleus, activating the transcription of downstream target genes and promoting drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma.

Results

AFAP1-AS1 directly binds to SRSF1 and SRSF3

In our previous report, we employed RNA pulldown assay coupled with mass spectrometry analysis to identify proteins potentially interacting with AFAP1-AS1 in lung adenocarcinoma [11]. Our results identified multiple RNA splicing-related proteins that may bind to AFAP1-AS1 (Fig. S1A). Among these, we selected four top candidates—DEAD-box helicase 5 (DDX5), SRSF3, SRSF1, and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein M (HNRNPM)—for further analysis. Repeated RNA pulldown experiments followed by western blotting validated interactions between AFAP1-AS1 and SRSF1 as well as SRSF3, while interactions with DDX5 and HNRNPM were less pronounced (Fig. 1A, S1B). RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays confirmed AFAP1-AS1 binding to both SRSF1 and SRSF3 (Fig. 1B). Finally, fluorescence in situ hybridization-immunofluorescence assays demonstrated colocalization of AFAP1-AS1 with SRSF1 and SRSF3 in cells (Fig. 1C), and quantitative analysis of fluorescence colocalization was performed (Fig. S1C). These findings collectively indicate that AFAP1-AS1 interacts with RNA splicing factors, particularly SRSF1 and SRSF3.

To further define the specific binding regions of AFAP1-AS1 with SRSF1 and SRSF3, we utilized the catRAPID tool to predict potential interaction sites on AFAP1-AS1 (Fig. 1D). Based on these predictions, we initially generated three truncated mutants of AFAP1-AS1: Fragment A (400–2,832 nt), Fragment B (2,832–5,902 nt), and Fragment C (5,885–6,796 nt). RNA pull-down assays revealed that both SRSF1 and SRSF3 specifically interacted with Fragment A and Fragment C. Notably, two distinct binding peaks were predicted within Fragment A. To refine the binding sites, Fragment A was further divided into Fragment D (400–1,009 nt) and Fragment E (989–2,832 nt), and additional RNA pull-down assays were conducted. The results confirmed that both Fragment D and Fragment E could bind to SRSF1 and SRSF3, aligning with the in silico predictions. These findings indicate that the regions spanning 400–2,832 nt and 5,885–6,796 nt of AFAP1-AS1 are responsible for binding to these two splicing factors.

To identify the domains within SRSF1 and SRSF3 responsible for binding to AFAP1-AS1, we generated mutants lacking the RNA Recognition Motifs (RRMs) of SRSF1 (RRM1 and RRM2) and SRSF3 (RRM) (Fig. S1D). RNA pull-down and RIP assays showed that deletion of either RRM1 or RRM2 in SRSF1 did not abolish its interaction with AFAP1-AS1; however, simultaneous deletion of both domains markedly impaired binding. In contrast, deletion of the single RRM domain in SRSF3 completely disrupted its ability to bind AFAP1-AS1 (Fig. S1E–F). These results indicate that the RRM1 and RRM2 domains of SRSF1, as well as the RRM domain of SRSF3, are essential for their respective interactions with AFAP1-AS1.

AFAP1-AS1 regulates alternative splicing events in lung adenocarcinoma

SRSF1 and SRSF3 are key RNA splicing factors with broad roles in regulating RNA alternative splicing within cells [14–17]. We hypothesized that AFAP1-AS1 might influence RNA alternative splicing in lung adenocarcinoma cells through its interaction with SRSF1 and SRSF3.

To test this hypothesis, we performed AFAP1-AS1 overexpression and knockdown experiments in the lung adenocarcinoma cell line A549. Overexpression was achieved using an AFAP1-AS1 expression vector (with an empty vector as the negative control), while knockdown was carried out using specific antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) (with a non-targeting ASO as the control). High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was subsequently performed for each treatment group (Supplemental Table 1). Alternative splicing events were analyzed using the rMATS tool based on the RNA-seq data (Fig. 2A). The analysis identified 4,686 AFAP1-AS1–regulated alternative splicing events in the overexpression group and 4,549 events in the knockdown group. These events primarily involved five types of splicing patterns: skipped exon (SE), retained intron (RI), mutually exclusive exons (MXE), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), and alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS) (Fig. 2B).

We then integrated the two datasets to identify alternative splicing events that were significantly regulated in both the AFAP1-AS1 overexpression and knockdown groups. This analysis revealed 2,086 co-regulated alternative splicing events. These events were visualized in a heatmap, which demonstrated that AFAP1-AS1 plays a broad role in regulating alternative splicing in lung adenocarcinoma cells (Fig. 2C). As SE events were the predominant type among the identified categories, we filtered and selected the top-ranked alternative splicing events, focusing on splicing events in genes such as AXIN2, SP140 nuclear body protein like (SP140L), structural maintenance of chromosomes 5 (SMC5), and kinesin family member 13 A (KIF13A) (Supplemental Table 2) (Figs. 2D, S2A). Using primers specifically targeting the skipped exons of these events, we performed RT-PCR to amplify the corresponding mRNA fragments from cells overexpressing or knocking down AFAP1-AS1. Gel electrophoresis analysis confirmed that AFAP1-AS1 indeed promotes the alternative splicing of pre-mRNA for AXIN2, SP140L, SMC5, and KIF13A in lung adenocarcinoma cells, consistent with our splicing analysis results. Notably, the alternative splicing of exon 7 in AXIN2 pre-mRNA was most significantly regulated by AFAP1-AS1. Overexpression of AFAP1-AS1 increased the production of the short isoform AXIN2-S mRNA and decreased the level of the long isoform AXIN2-L mRNA, while knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 led to a decrease in AXIN2-S mRNA and an increase in AXIN2-L mRNA (Figs. 2E-F, S2B).

AFAP1-AS1 promotes the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3

During RNA splicing, multiple splicing-related proteins often form complexes to carry out their functions. To explore whether an interaction exists between the RNA splicing factors SRSF1 and SRSF3, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, confirming that SRSF1 and SRSF3 bind to each other in lung adenocarcinoma cells (Fig. S3A). Furthermore, AFAP1-AS1 overexpression enhanced, while its knockdown weakened, the binding affinity between SRSF1 and SRSF3, suggesting that AFAP1-AS1 may act as a scaffold to facilitate their interaction (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis also showed colocalization of SRSF1 and SRSF3 within cells, revealing an uneven nuclear distribution with spot-like condensates—consistent with liquid-liquid phase separation of intracellular proteins (Fig. S3B). Using the IUPred3 tool, we predicted intrinsically disordered protein regions in both SRSF1 and SRSF3, a characteristic feature of phase-separating proteins (Fig. S3C). Lung adenocarcinoma cells were transfected with expression vectors encoding EGFP-tagged SRSF1 and SRSF3, followed by treatment with the phase separation disruptor 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-hex). Fluorescence imaging revealed a marked reduction in SRSF1- and SRSF3-associated condensates after 1,6-hex treatment, indicating that these proteins are capable of undergoing phase separation in the nucleus (Fig. 3B).

To further investigate the influence of AFAP1-AS1 on the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3, we overexpressed or knocked down AFAP1-AS1 in A549 and PC9 cells and performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence assays to assess the intracellular localization and distribution of endogenous AFAP1-AS1, SRSF1 and SRSF3. Overexpression of AFAP1-AS1 resulted in an increase in the formation of punctate condensates formed by SRSF1 and SRSF3. In contrast, knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 led to a reduction in the formation of these condensates (Fig. 3C, S4A). Live-cell immunofluorescence assays further confirmed that AFAP1-AS1 mediates the phase separation of exogenous SRSF1 and SRSF3 (Fig. S4B). Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays demonstrated that AFAP1-AS1 overexpression accelerated fluorescence recovery rates for SRSF1 and SRSF3 condensates, whereas AFAP1-AS1 knockdown slowed their recovery (Fig. 3D, S4C-D). These findings indicate that AFAP1-AS1 drives the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3 through interactions.

AFAP1-AS1 regulates the alternative splicing of AXIN2 by promoting the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3

To confirm whether AFAP1-AS1 drives RNA alternative splicing events in lung adenocarcinoma cells by binding to and promoting the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3, we selected the exon 7 skipping event of AXIN2 pre-mRNA as a model. Firstly, we knocked down the expression of SRSF1 and SRSF3 in lung adenocarcinoma cells and observed that both knockdowns inhibited exon 7 skipping of AXIN2 pre-mRNA (Fig. 4A-B). When AFAP1-AS1 was overexpressed while simultaneously knocking down either SRSF1 or SRSF3, the exon 7 skipping effect induced by AFAP1-AS1 was suppressed, demonstrating the essential role of these splicing factors in the process (Fig. 4C).

Additionally, treatment with the phase separation inhibitor 1,6-hex in cells overexpressing AFAP1-AS1 reduced its promotion of exon 7 skipping in AXIN2 pre-mRNA, suggesting that AFAP1-AS1 regulates RNA alternative splicing by facilitating the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3 (Fig. 4D). Using the RBPmap database, we predicted the binding motifs of SRSF1 and SRSF3 and the potential binding sites for SRSF1 and SRSF3 on AXIN2 pre-mRNA (Fig. 4E-F). SRSF1 was predicted to bind AXIN2 pre-mRNA fragments 24,426–24,500 (intron 6), 24,609–24,776 (exon 7), and 24,897–24,905 (intron 7), while SRSF3 was predicted to bind fragments 24,492–24,501 (intron 6) and 25,043–25,080 (intron 7) (Fig. S5A). RIP assays confirmed that SRSF1 and SRSF3 bind to multiple sites within the intron 6-7 region of AXIN2 (Fig. 4G). This indicates that SRSF1 and SRSF3 interact with regions flanking exon 7 to facilitate its splicing. Additionally, we cloned fragments containing AXIN2 exons 6–8 along with the introns flanking exon 7 into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector to generate an exogenous AXIN2 minigene reporter (Fig. S5A). After transfecting this reporter into lung adenocarcinoma cells and overexpressing AFAP1-AS1, SRSF1, or SRSF3, we used RT-PCR followed by DNA gel electrophoresis to evaluate their direct regulatory effects on AXIN2 splicing. The results demonstrated that AFAP1-AS1, SRSF1, and SRSF3 each promoted splicing of the exogenous AXIN2 exon 7 (Fig. S5B–D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that AFAP1-AS1 promotes exon 7 skipping of AXIN2 pre-mRNA by driving the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3, leading to the aberrant production of the AXIN2-S transcript.

AFAP1-AS1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by promoting the expression of AXIN2-S

AXIN2, a critical component of the β-catenin destruction complex, facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin and plays a pivotal role in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, significantly contributing to the initiation and progression of various tumors [18–20]. To determine whether AFAP1-AS1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by modulating the alternative splicing of AXIN2, thereby promoting the malignant phenotype of lung adenocarcinoma, we constructed eukaryotic expression vectors encoding the two AXIN2 isoforms: AXIN2-L and AXIN2-S (Fig. S6A–C). Western blotting analysis revealed that the AXIN2-S isoform enhances β-catenin expression (Fig. 5A), suppresses its phosphorylation (Fig. 5B), and inhibits its ubiquitin-mediated degradation (Fig. 5C), resulting in increased nuclear accumulation of β-catenin in lung adenocarcinoma cells (Fig. 5D). In contrast, AXIN2-L had opposing effects, promoting β-catenin degradation (Fig. 5A–D).

Furthermore, overexpression of AFAP1-AS1 in lung adenocarcinoma cells was found to enhance β-catenin expression and inhibit its degradation. Conversely, knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 resulted in reduced total β-catenin levels, increased phosphorylation and ubiquitination-mediated degradation, and decreased nuclear accumulation (Fig. 5E–H). Notably, when AFAP1-AS1 was knocked down in lung adenocarcinoma cells while overexpressing the two AXIN2 isoforms, western blotting analysis revealed that overexpression of AXIN2-S partially rescued the reduction in β-catenin expression caused by AFAP1-AS1 knockdown. In contrast, overexpression of AXIN2-L led to the opposite result (Fig. 5I). These findings indicate that AFAP1-AS1 prevents β-catenin degradation by promoting the expression of the AXIN2-S isoform while suppressing the production of AXIN2-L. This process leads to increased nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that AFAP1-AS1 regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in lung adenocarcinoma cells [11]. To further explore whether AFAP1-AS1–mediated splicing regulation of AXIN2 promotes EMT through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we knocked down AFAP1-AS1 in lung adenocarcinoma cells while separately overexpressing the two AXIN2 isoforms. Western blotting analysis revealed that overexpression of AXIN2-S partially rescued the decreased expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin caused by AFAP1-AS1 knockdown, whereas overexpression of AXIN2-L showed no such effect (Fig. 5J).

AFAP1-AS1 promotes drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma by enhancing the expression of AXIN2-S

In our preliminary studies, we observed that lung adenocarcinoma patients with high AFAP1-AS1 expression had poorer prognoses. Previous research highlights the critical role of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in contributing to drug resistance in tumors [21–23], which is an important factor in treatment failure and adverse patient outcomes. Based on these findings, we further investigated whether AFAP1-AS1 promotes drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma cells through the upregulation of AXIN2-S expression.

Currently, chemotherapy and targeted therapy are the primary clinical treatment strategies for lung adenocarcinoma [24]. PC9 cells, which harbor EGFR mutations, are typically sensitive to the EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor osimertinib, whereas A549 cells, lacking EGFR mutations, do not exhibit this sensitivity [25]. To investigate the role of AFAP1-AS1 in drug resistance, we evaluated its impact on osimertinib resistance in PC9 cells and assessed its effects on resistance to the commonly used chemotherapeutic agents carboplatin and paclitaxel in both PC9 and A549 cells. Colony formation and cell viability assays demonstrated that overexpression of AFAP1-AS1 significantly enhances resistance to carboplatin and paclitaxel in A549 and PC9 cells, whereas knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 increases their sensitivity to these drugs (Fig. 6A, Fig. S7A). Similarly, AFAP1-AS1 overexpression or knockdown promoted or reduced osimertinib resistance in PC9 cells, respectively (Fig. 6B, Fig. S7C). To further assess the role of AXIN2 isoforms, we overexpressed AXIN2-L and AXIN2-S in A549 and PC9 cells. Overexpression of AXIN2-S enhanced cell resistance to carboplatin, paclitaxel (Fig. 6C, Fig. S7B), and osimertinib (Fig. 6D, Fig. S7D), while overexpression of AXIN2-L had the opposite effect, increasing drug sensitivity. In cells with AFAP1-AS1 knockdown, we observed increased sensitivity to carboplatin, paclitaxel, and osimertinib. However, overexpression of AXIN2-S in these AFAP1-AS1-knockdown cells reversed this sensitization, promoting drug resistance, whereas overexpression of AXIN2-L improved the drug sensitivity phenotype (Fig. 6E). Additionally, we examined the effect of AFAP1-AS1 expression on targeted therapy resistance using PC9 osimertinib-resistant cells (PC9-OR). Colony formation and cell viability assays demonstrated that AFAP1-AS1 overexpression increased resistance in PC9-OR cells, whereas AFAP1-AS1 knockdown partially restored their sensitivity to osimertinib (Fig. S7E–F). Overall, AFAP1-AS1 upregulates AXIN2-S expression, thereby enhancing chemoresistance and targeted drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma cells

AFAP1-AS1 promotes drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma: evidence from mouse model and clinical samples

To further evaluate the role of AFAP1-AS1 in drug resistance, we utilized in vivo models. PC9 cells were transfected with the ASO targeting AFAP1-AS1 to achieve knockdown. These AFAP1-AS1 knockdown or control PC9 cells were then injected subcutaneously into SCID mice to establish a tumor model. Two weeks post-injection, the mice were treated with either a combination of carboplatin and paclitaxel or osimertinib, administered every three days for two weeks (Fig. S8A). Notably, the drug treatments did not affect the body weight of the mice throughout the experimental period (Fig. S8B).

The results of subcutaneous tumor model experiments demonstrated that both the carboplatin-paclitaxel combination therapy and osimertinib therapy significantly reduced tumor volume and weight compared to the untreated group. In the untreated group, AFAP1-AS1 knockdown alone did not significantly impact tumor size or weight. However, in the groups receiving either the carboplatin-paclitaxel combination or osimertinib, knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 markedly reduced tumor volume and weight compared to the control group. These findings suggest that AFAP1-AS1 knockdown significantly enhances the efficacy of both chemotherapy and targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 7A-C).

In situ hybridization and immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated that the knockdown of AFAP1-AS1 significantly reduced its expression levels in the AFAP1-AS1 knockdown group compared to the control group, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in β-catenin expression (Fig. 7D, Fig. S8C). These results highlight the role of AFAP1-AS1 in promoting drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma cells in vivo. To further validate these findings clinically, tissue samples were collected from 38 patients treated with carboplatin-paclitaxel combination therapy and 63 patients treated with osimertinib. In situ hybridization analysis revealed that patients with low AFAP1-AS1 expression exhibited better treatment outcomes than those with high expression. Similarly, immunohistochemical analysis of β-catenin expression indicated that patients with low β-catenin levels responded more favorably to treatment than those with high β-catenin levels (Fig. 7E). Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between AFAP1-AS1 and β-catenin expression in these clinical tissue samples (Fig. 7F, Fig. S8D). These findings underscore the role of AFAP1-AS1 in contributing to treatment resistance in lung adenocarcinoma and suggest that AFAP1-AS1 represents a promising target for clinical diagnosis and therapeutic intervention in this malignancy.

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of both incidence and mortality among all malignancies worldwide [26]. Lung cancer is primarily classified into two subtypes: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among NSCLC, lung adenocarcinoma is the most common type, accounting for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases. It is also the most aggressive subtype, with the highest mortality rate. For patients with lung adenocarcinoma, a combination of paclitaxel and platinum-based drugs remains a standard treatment regimen. However, most patients eventually develop chemotherapy resistance, leading to poor prognosis overall [27]. Approximately 15–20% of lung adenocarcinoma patients harbor EGFR mutations [28] and osimertinib is currently the preferred targeted therapy for patients with these mutations [29]. Nonetheless, resistance to osimertinib remains a significant challenge and requires urgent attention [30–32]. Numerous studies have shown that abnormalities in gene expression regulation are closely linked to resistance in lung adenocarcinoma. In particular, an increasing body of research highlights the crucial roles of lncRNAs in the mechanisms underlying drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma [33–38]. Despite these advances, the exact mechanisms of resistance in lung cancer, especially in lung adenocarcinoma, are still not fully understood. Our previous research demonstrated that AFAP1-AS1 promotes invasion and metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma cells through interaction with the SNIP1 protein [11], which partially explains the poor prognosis observed in patients with high AFAP1-AS1 expression. However, beyond invasion and metastasis, tumor drug resistance is also likely a key contributor to the adverse outcomes in lung adenocarcinoma patients. In this study, using a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we showed that AFAP1-AS1 regulates exon 7 skipping in AXIN2 pre-mRNA splicing, thereby promoting the production of the AXIN2-S isoform, activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, and ultimately inducing drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma cells. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that AFAP1-AS1, a long noncoding RNA known to play critical regulatory roles in various malignancies [10], contributes to tumor drug resistance by modulating alternative splicing events.

LncRNAs regulate gene expression within cells through various mechanisms, playing diverse roles in the initiation and progression of malignant tumors. They often bind to other RNAs, such as acting as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) to sequester miRNAs, thereby modulating the expression of target mRNAs. LncRNAs can also directly interact with mRNAs to influence their stability or translation. Moreover, lncRNAs frequently interact with proteins, regulating intracellular signaling pathways. While numerous studies have shown that AFAP1-AS1 functions as a miRNA sponge to mediate biological effects, reports of its interactions with intracellular proteins to promote tumor progression remain limited [6, 39, 40]. In our previous studies, we found that AFAP1-AS1 exerts oncogenic effects in tumor cells through a ceRNA mechanism. However, its specific interactions with proteins remained unclear. Subsequent research revealed that AFAP1-AS1 binds to the SNIP1 protein, inhibiting the ubiquitination and degradation of c-Myc, inducing the expression of EMT-related molecules, and thereby promoting invasion and metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma. Unlike these earlier findings, this study is the first to demonstrate that AFAP1-AS1 regulates alternative splicing events in lung adenocarcinoma by interacting with splicing proteins, influencing tumor initiation and progression. Notably, AXIN2 is a key component of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We found that AFAP1-AS1 modulates Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation by regulating AXIN2 alternative splicing. Numerous studies have established that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a classical regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [41–44]. Our data further confirmed that AFAP1-AS1-mediated alternative splicing of AXIN2 closely correlates with the expression of EMT-promoting factors. During EMT, tumor cells transition from an epithelial to a mesenchymal phenotype, which not only enhances their migratory and invasive capabilities but also confers resistance to chemotherapy [45–47]. Therefore, this study focuses on AFAP1-AS1–regulated alternative splicing of AXIN2, a key Wnt/β-catenin pathway molecule, and its role in mediating drug resistance in lung adenocarcinoma cells.

Alternative splicing, a critical step in RNA maturation, allows for the generation of a diverse transcriptome from a limited number of genes, significantly increasing protein variety and quantity. This process enhances the diversity and complexity of genetic information while supporting its precise transmission, playing a vital role in various physiological processes. However, increasing evidence suggests that aberrant alternative splicing is often associated with the onset and progression of numerous diseases, including cancers. In tumor cells, dysregulated splicing factors frequently lead to the production of tumor-associated splice variants and disrupt signaling pathways [48, 49]. For instance, exon 6 skipping in Fas pre-mRNA generates the anti-apoptotic soluble isoform sFas, which inhibits apoptosis [50]. Similarly, exon 3 skipping in PDCD1 pre-mRNA produces the soluble isoform sPD-1, which blocks the interaction between PD-L1 and PD-1, impairing immune activation [51, 52]. In another example, the mutually exclusive splicing of PKM pre-mRNA yields the isoforms PKM1 and PKM2, whose ratio directly influences aerobic glycolysis in cells [53, 54]. Tumor cells also exploit RNA alternative splicing to develop drug resistance [55]. In prostate cancer, aberrant splicing of the androgen receptor (AR) gene generates the AR-V7 isoform, which is strongly associated with resistance to anti-androgen therapies such as abiraterone and enzalutamide [56]. Additionally, in vemurafenib-resistant melanoma cells, a BRAF splice variant lacking exons 4–8, and thus the RAS binding domain, has been linked to drug resistance [57]. Our research adds a novel finding to this growing field: AFAP1-AS1 facilitates exon 7 skipping in AXIN2 pre-mRNA, producing an altered ratio of AXIN2 isoforms. This aberrant splicing event affects the regulation and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, ultimately promoting lung adenocarcinoma progression. This mechanism represents a previously unreported role for AFAP1-AS1, broadening our understanding of its involvement in cancer biology.

AXIN2 is a pivotal regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Under basal conditions without Wnt activation, AXIN2 collaborates with other proteins, such as APC, to form the β-catenin destruction complex. This complex promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin, thereby preventing its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity. Conversely, upon Wnt activation, the β-catenin destruction complex becomes inactivated, enabling β-catenin to accumulate in the nucleus and drive the transcription of downstream target genes, including those associated with tumorigenesis [58, 59]. Traditionally, AXIN2 is regarded as a tumor suppressor gene due to its role in negatively regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, exhibiting tumor-suppressive functions in various cancer types. However, some studies have highlighted an oncogenic role for AXIN2 in breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and other malignancies [19, 60, 61]. These findings suggest that AXIN2 may have dual roles in cancer. We hypothesize that AXIN2’s influence on tumor initiation and progression may arise from the generation of functionally distinct isoforms via alternative splicing. In our study, we identified two AXIN2 splice variants in lung adenocarcinoma: AXIN2-L, which includes exon 7 and acts as an inhibitor of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and AXIN2-S, which skips exon 7 and activates the pathway. These findings indicate that the different splicing isoforms of AXIN2 have opposing roles in tumor biology, emphasizing the complexity of its function. In addition to the confirmed regulation of AXIN2 splicing, our RNA-Seq analysis and PCR validation revealed that AFAP1-AS1 also modulates alternative splicing of multiple other genes, including SP140L, SMC5, and KIF13A. SP140L typically functions as a transcriptional regulator [62]; SMC5 plays a key role in homologous recombination-mediated DNA damage repair [63]; KIF13A is involved in extracellular matrix synthesis and intracellular protein trafficking, contributing to tumor invasion, metastasis, and surface antigen presentation [64, 65]. The altered splicing patterns of these genes regulated by AFAP1-AS1 may generate aberrant protein isoforms that disrupt the homeostatic balance of various signaling pathways. The precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be elucidated. These findings suggest that AFAP1-AS1 establishes a broad and complex post-transcriptional regulatory network, modulating gene expression through a “splicing switch” mechanism, thereby influencing the initiation and progression of lung adenocarcinoma.

Of note, in the findings of this study, we observed that AFAP1-AS1, along with SRSF1 and SRSF3, exhibited a nuclear punctate distribution, resembling the characteristics of liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) of macromolecules within cells. LLPS is a process in which intracellular biomacromolecules spontaneously separate into dense and dilute phases. This phenomenon is driven primarily by multivalent weak interactions between RNA-protein or protein-protein molecules, which are considered critical for the formation of biomolecular condensates in the dense phase and serve as the driving force for phase separation [66–68]. These phase-separated intracellular compartments facilitate a range of biochemical reactions, including RNA processing events such as alternative splicing [69, 70]. Reports have shown that SRSF1 exhibits liquid-liquid phase separation properties, allowing it to form droplets through phase separation in extracellular environments [71]. SRSF3, a family member sharing similar protein domains with SRSF1, has been reported to form complexes with SRSF1 and other splicing factors within the nucleus, thereby influencing downstream gene splicing events [72–74]. Our findings further confirmed that AFAP1-AS1 actively promotes the phase separation of SRSF1 and SRSF3, leading to the skipping of exon 7 in AXIN2 pre-mRNA. This underscores the importance of AFAP1-AS1-mediated phase separation in regulating alternative splicing and its potential impact on tumor biology.

However, this study has certain limitations that warrant further investigation. In analyzing clinical samples from lung adenocarcinoma patients, we evaluated treatment response to chemotherapy or targeted therapy based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), using pre- and post-treatment imaging reports. The results indicated that high expression of AFAP1-AS1 was associated with poor treatment response. Nonetheless, due to the relatively small sample size and the fact that most patients were enrolled within the past five years—with few observed death events—our Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and multivariate Cox regression analysis did not show a statistically significant association between AFAP1-AS1 expression and overall survival. To better clarify the prognostic value of AFAP1-AS1, future studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods will be necessary. These efforts will help to further elucidate the impact of AFAP1-AS1 expression on survival outcomes in NSCLC patients receiving chemotherapy or targeted therapy.

In summary, we identified a novel mechanism by which AFAP1-AS1 promotes exon 7 skipping in AXIN2 pre-mRNA through its interaction with and the enhancement of SRSF1 and SRSF3 phase separation. This splicing event leads to the upregulation of the AXIN2-S isoform, which activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and enhances lung adenocarcinoma resistance to both chemotherapy and targeted therapies. This study expands our understanding of the functional role of AFAP1-AS1 in lung adenocarcinoma pathogenesis, highlighting its pivotal role in mediating drug resistance. Targeting AFAP1-AS1 could disrupt the phase separation of splicing-related proteins, interfere with the alternative splicing of AXIN2 pre-mRNA, and modulate the drug resistance phenotype in lung adenocarcinoma, ultimately improving clinical treatment efficacy. Furthermore, AFAP1-AS1 and its downstream molecules present promising opportunities as biomarkers for auxiliary diagnosis and prognosis evaluation in lung adenocarcinoma.

Methods

Clinical samples

A total of 38 lung adenocarcinoma tissue samples from patients treated with carboplatin-paclitaxel combination therapy and 63 tissue samples from patients treated with osimertinib were analyzed to assess the expression of AFAP1-AS1 and β-catenin using in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry. All clinical tissue samples were obtained from Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Central South University, and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

RNA extraction, PCR, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from lung adenocarcinoma cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, China). QRT-PCR reactions were performed with the 2×SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix Kit (Bimake, China), while conventional PCR reactions were conducted using the 2×T5 Super PCR Mix Kit (Tsingke, China). GAPDH served as an endogenous control for both mRNA and lncRNA analysis. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. P values were calculated using Student’s t-tests. All primers used in this study were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech, and their sequences are provided in Supplemental Table 3.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at appropriate concentrations, then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Images of the target bands were captured using a chemiluminescent imaging system (Servicebio, China). All experiments were conducted three times. The antibodies used are listed in Supplemental Table 4.

RNA pulldown assay

Linear DNA was generated from circular plasmids using specific restriction endonucleases and purified with a DNA purification kit (Omega, USA). The linear DNA was then transcribed into biotin-labeled RNA using the Biotin RNA Labeling Mix Kit (Roche, USA) and T7 RNA polymerase (Promega, USA) in vitro, followed by purification with the RNApure Tissue Kit (CWBIO, China). The biotinylated RNA was incubated with cell lysates at room temperature for 2 h. Streptavidin affinity magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, USA) were added to the RNA-protein mixture and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing the magnetic beads six times, the supernatant was collected for western blotting analysis. In this experiment, the Input group served as the positive control, the AFAP1-AS1 antisense group as the negative control, and GAPDH as the internal reference control. All experiments were conducted three times.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

Following the instructions provided with the Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA), antibodies were incubated with magnetic beads at room temperature for 1 h. The cell lysate was then added to the antibody-bound beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the magnetic beads were washed six times with RIP wash buffer, and the immunoprecipitated RNAs were purified and enriched. The target RNA was subsequently detected using qRT-PCR. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. P values were calculated using Student’s t-tests.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence

Cy3-labeled oligonucleotide probes targeting AFAP1-AS1 were purchased from RiboBio. Cell slides were placed at the bottom of wells in a 24-well plate, and an appropriate number of cells were cultured. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 20 min and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (Coolaber, China). Hybridization of the cells with the probes was performed overnight at 37 °C using the lncRNA FISH Probe Mix Kit (RiboBio, China). Following hybridization, the cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 30 min and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The cells were then incubated with a fluorescent dye-labeled secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with 1× DAPI solution (Invitrogen, USA). Imaging was performed using a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Leica, Germany) across three independent fields of view.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed using cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 1 mM EDTA; 1% Triton X-100; 150 mM NaCl) containing protease inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland) to obtain protein lysates. An appropriate amount of antibody was incubated with protein A/G magnetic beads (Bimake, USA) at room temperature for 2 h. After washing the beads twice, the antibody-coupled magnetic beads were added to the protein lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following six washes with wash buffer, the beads were eluted, and the supernatant was collected for western blotting analysis. In this experiment, the Input group served as the positive control, the IgG group as the negative control, and GAPDH as the internal reference control. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Live cell immunofluorescence

Cells transfected with the target fluorescent plasmid were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes (Nest, China). After the cells had adhered, the culture medium was replaced with phenol red-free medium. High-definition imaging was performed using the Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Leica, Germany) with a 63× oil immersion objective lens and a 488 nm laser. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. P values were calculated using Student’s t-tests.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

Cells transfected with the target fluorescent plasmid were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes (Nest, China). Once the cells had fully adhered, the culture medium was replaced with phenol red-free medium. Imaging was performed using the Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Leica, Germany). A designated area was subjected to photobleaching using a 488 nm laser at 100% power. During the fluorescence recovery phase, images were captured at 1-second intervals for a total of 50 frames. Fluorescence intensity in non-bleached areas was also recorded as a control for comparison. Quantitative Analysis of Fluorescence Recovery Rate: At time point t = 0, the difference in fluorescence intensity between the photobleached spot in the experimental group (blue curve) and the control group (red curve) was defined as the maximum photobleaching intensity. The half-recovery time (tₕr), defined as the time required for fluorescence intensity to recover to 50% of its maximum quenched value, was used to assess the fluorescence recovery rate across different treatment groups.

Colony formation assay

Lung adenocarcinoma cells were seeded at a density of 1500 cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured until visible colonies formed. The cells were then washed three times with 1× PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 20 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Biosharp, China) for 1 h. After staining, the cells were washed, air-dried, and photographed [75, 76]. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. P values were calculated using Student’s t-tests.

Cell viability assay

Lung adenocarcinoma cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 4000 cells per well. Carboplatin, paclitaxel, and osimertinib were added to the cell culture medium at varying concentrations and incubated for 48 h. After treatment, 0.5 mg/mL MTT (Beyotime, China) was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The supernatant was then discarded, and 200 µL of DMSO (Coolaber, China) was added to each well. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a multifunctional Enzyme Labeler (Molecular Devices, USA). The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated and plotted using GraphPad Prism.

RNA transcriptome sequencing and alternative splicing analysis

A549 cells were transfected with Empty Vector (EV), AFAP1-AS1 Overexpression Vector (OE), Antisense Oligonucleotide Negative Control (ASONC), or Antisense Oligonucleotide (ASO), with two biological replicates per group, yielding a total of eight experimental samples. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Transcriptome libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads in FASTQ format were processed with fastp to remove low-quality reads, yielding clean reads for downstream analysis. These clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM. Read counts for each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count. Alternative splicing events were analyzed using rMATS (http://rnaseq-mats.sourceforge.net/index.html). This tool calculates read coverage from BAM files and integrates gene annotation data to quantify alternative splicing events. Based on genomic exon annotations, splicing events in each sample were classified into five categories: skipped exon (SE), retained intron (RI), mutually exclusive exon (MXE), alternative 5’ splice site (A5SS), and alternative 3’ splice site (A3SS). For downstream analysis and screening, emphasis was placed on skipped exon (SE) events, the predominant type of alternative splicing identified. The exon inclusion level (IncLevel) and splice junction read counts were calculated by aligning junction reads to the inclusion and skipping isoforms. Differential alternative splicing events were identified through pairwise comparisons between treatment and control groups. Significant events detected by rMATS were visualized using the rmats2sashimiplot tool, which generates sashimi plots showing genomic coordinates, exon structures, and read densities, highlighting differences in exon inclusion or skipping between groups. Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis were performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were conducted using female SCID mice, approximately 4 weeks old, purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Central South University, and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier environment. To establish a subcutaneous tumor model, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5 per group), with each mouse receiving a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 10^6 PC9 cells. After two weeks, the mice were treated with intraperitoneal injections of drugs: carboplatin and paclitaxel (40 mg/kg of carboplatin and 10 mg/kg of paclitaxel) or osimertinib (5 mg/kg), administered every three days for a total of five treatments. Tumor volumes were measured every five days, and mouse weights were recorded every seven days. After 29 days, the mice were euthanized, and subcutaneous tumors were collected for weighing, imaging, and statistical analysis. Tumor tissues were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining, in situ hybridization, and immunohistochemical analysis. All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Central South University.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining, in situ hybridization, and immunohistochemistry

After dewaxing and rehydrating the paraffin-embedded tissue sections, the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, and the cytoplasm was stained with eosin. The sections were then dehydrated, dried, and mounted with neutral resin. Immunohistochemistry was performed using the Elivision™ Plus Polymer HRP (Mouse/Rabbit) IHC kit (Maxim, China) to assess the expression of β-catenin in the tissue samples. In situ hybridization was carried out according to the protocol provided with the In Situ Hybridization Kit (BOSTER, China) to detect AFAP1-AS1 expression. Scoring was based on both staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells. The proportion of positive cells was categorized as none (0), ≤ 25% (1), 25%−50% (2), 50%−75% (3), and > 75% (4), while intensity was graded as none (0), weak (1), moderate (2), and strong (3). The final score was derived by multiplying the intensity and proportion scores. For more details, please refer to Supplemental Table 5.

Online bioinformatics analysis

Using the RBPmap online prediction tool (http://rbpmap.technion.ac.il/), the target protein SRSF1 was selected from the database, or the recognition motif of SRSF3 was manually entered. The RNA sequence—specifically AXIN2 exon 7 along with its flanking intronic regions—was submitted for analysis. The output included predicted binding sites along with corresponding binding scores.

Using the catRAPID tool (http://service.tartaglialab.com/page/catrapid_group), the AFAP1-AS1 RNA sequence and the protein sequences of SRSF1 and SRSF3 (all in FASTA format) were uploaded to the catRAPID fragments module for analysis. This enabled the generation of RNA–protein interaction maps and prediction result matrices. All RNA and protein sequences were obtained from the NCBI Gene database.

The IUPred3 online tool (https://iupred3.elte.hu/) was used to predict intrinsic disorder in the protein sequences of SRSF1 and SRSF3. Amino acid sequences in FASTA format were input using default parameter settings. The output included disorder tendency scores for each residue, along with a visual representation of the predicted disorder profile. Scores range from 0 to 1, with values above 0.5 indicating disordered regions. All amino acid sequences were obtained from the NCBI Gene database.

The HIPLOT tool (https://hiplot.com.cn/home/index.html) was used to generate a heatmap based on the IncLevel differential expression matrix, which included 2,086 alternative splicing events from the AFAP1-AS1 overexpression and knockdown groups. The matrix was uploaded, and the analysis was performed using default parameter settings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-tests, while associations between categorical variables were evaluated with the Chi-square test. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine linear relationships between continuous variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
