Vitiligo like depigmentation in a patient with NHEJ1 deficiency related radiosensitive SCID: A case report
Authors: Alanoud Alhaider, Mansour Almutairi, Abdulaziz Alomari, Alwaleed Altuwaijri, Ghaida Almarshoud, Lamia Alakrash
Published: 2026

Introduction

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) represents a group of life-threatening primary immunodeficiencies characterized by defective T and B-lymphocyte function, typically presenting with severe and recurrent infections during early infancy.1 A subgroup of them, referred to as radiosensitive SCID, results from defects within the nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) pathway, which plays a crucial role in DNA double-strand break repair during V(D)J recombination. The NHEJ1 gene encodes for Cernunnos/XLF, an NHEJ1 scaffolding protein that is vital for DNA end ligation during this form of repair. NHEJ1 pathogenic variants lead to aberrant lymphocyte development and genomic instability, resulting in a T-B-NK+ immunophenotype, microcephaly, growth retardation, and increased sensitivity to ionizing radiation.2,3

Although the immunologic and developmental characteristics of NHEJ1-related SCID are well-documented, cutaneous or pigmentary changes have not been included in its clinical presentation. However, depigmentation syndromes with paired immune dysfunction, such as Chediak–Higashi, Griscelli, and Hermansky–Pudlak syndromes, result from mutations affecting lysosome-related organelle biogenesis or melanosome transport but not DNA repair.4,5 These conditions are characterized by silvery hair and hypopigmented skin resulting from abnormal melanosome distribution, frequently combined with qualitative or quantitative immunodeficiency.

We report a unique case of a 2 year-old girl with NHEJ1-related radiosensitive SCID who had congenital generalized depigmented skin, premature eczematous dermatitis and silvery-white scalp hair post hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Following matched-sibling HSCT, she exhibited progressive repigmentation of the hair and skin that paralleled immune reconstitution. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case associating pigmentary change with NHEJ1 deficiency, broadening the phenotypic range of DNA repair-related immunodeficiencies and raising the possibility of immune or DNA repair mechanisms in melanocyte homeostasis.

Case description

We report a case of a 2 year-old with genetically confirmed NHEJ1-related severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) presented with early onset of generalized depigmented skin. Her dermatologic course was notable for uniform depigmentation at birth, eczema during infancy, and the later development of patchy repigmentation and the emergence of silvery-white scalp hair following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

She was born at term after an uncomplicated pregnancy to consanguineous parents with no personal or family history of vitiligo. The parents reported that her skin appeared lighter than expected from birth and that she developed a generalized pruritic erythematous rash within the first month of life. Complete hair loss has also occurred 3 months of age with no history of abnormal hair pigmentation at that time.

At 5 months of age, she was referred to dermatology for persistent pruritic rash, hair loss, and generalized depigmented skin. Examination revealed well-demarcated depigmented patches involving approximately 90% of the body surface area, without associated palmoplantar keratoderma, nail dystrophy, or dental abnormalities. The scalp hair was sparse, fine, and black. The findings were consistent with early onset vitiligo and mild eczema. Tacrolimus 0.03% ointment once daily resulted in partial improvement of eczematous symptoms with no noted changes in pigmentation.

The patient’s medical history included oral thrush, pneumonia, recurrent otitis media, and failure to thrive. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole prophylaxis were required due to her medical history and laboratory results indicating lymphopenia (1.2 × 101/L) and hypogammaglobulinemia (IgG <1 g/L).

Whole-exome sequencing conducted in May 2023 confirmed the diagnosis of radiosensitive severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) due to a homozygous pathogenic variant in the NHEJ1 gene. At 9 months of age she received matched-sibling HSCT without conditioning chemotherapy, owing to the radiosensitivity risk linked to NHEJ1 deficiency. The post-transplant course was uncomplicated, with no clinical indications of acute or chronic graft versus host disease (GVHD) observed.

Pre-HSCT photographs were not available; the earliest images were obtained several months post-transplant, at the onset of repigmentation. By 2 to 3 months post-transplant, faint tan macules appeared on the trunk and extremities, gradually coalescing into areas of repigmentation (Fig 1, A and B). One year later, she showed diffuse residual depigmentation with sharply defined islands of normal pigmentation, most prominent on the face, hands, and feet (Figs 2 and 3, A and B). Scalp hair was fully regrown, thicker, and mixed black and silvery-white. Repigmentation continued through 2025, resulting in a mottled skin tone with progressive hair regrowth and no new inflammatory or suspicious lesions. At last follow-up, repigmentation remained incomplete, with persistent patchy depigmentation. Discussion

This case identifies pigmentary abnormalities as a novel feature of NHEJ1-related radiosensitive SCID, expanding its clinical spectrum beyond the currently recognized immunologic and developmental manifestations. Patients with this genetic defect usually present with recurrent infections, microcephaly, and sensitivity to radiation, but pigmentary disorders have not been previously described.6, 7, 8

Although SCID is a condition of immune deficiency, it can also include immune dysregulation. When some T or B cells develop abnormally, they may become autoreactive. Such cells could target melanocytes and lead to depigmentation.9, 10, 11 Similar findings have been noted in other DNA repair disorders like Artemis and LIG4 deficiencies, which share the same repair pathway as NHEJ1.9,10

Another possible explanation is that defective DNA repair makes melanocytes more fragile. Melanocytes are exposed to ultraviolet light and oxidative stress, and without proper DNA repair, they may be more prone to injury and death.12 Therefore, both immune dysregulation and intrinsic melanocyte damage could contribute to the congenital depigmentation in this setting.

After HSCT, the patient’s immune system was restored, and skin color gradually returned without other treatment. This observation has been described in other SCID patients after transplant, but not previously in those with NHEJ1 mutations.13

Although the clinical morphology and pattern of repigmentation are consistent with a vitiligo-like process, histopathologic confirmation was not obtained. Therefore, alternative. diagnoses must be considered. In particular, GVHD which in patients with SCID may arise from transplacental maternal T-cell engraftment even prior to transplantation.14 Maternal T-cell engraftment has been reported in up to 40% of patients with SCID and may lead to GVHD-like manifestations, most commonly involving the skin.15 However, a substantial proportion of patients with maternal T-cell engraftment remain clinically asymptomatic or exhibit only mild cutaneous findings.14,15 The absence of other clinical features of GVHD and the pattern of progressive perifollicular repigmentation following immune reconstitution favor a vitiligo-like mechanism over fixed or inflammatory dyschromia.

Overall, this case highlights that vitiligo, although rare, can develop in primary immunodeficiency syndromes. The observed repigmentation also emphasizes that immune correction through HSCT can lead not only to infection control but also to improvement of autoimmune or potential reversibility of pigmentary abnormalities after immune reconstitution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, congenital vitiligo-like depigmentation in NHEJ1-related SCID is an exceptional finding that adds to the phenotype of this rare disorder. In this case, the presence of generalized depigmentation from birth, the absence of progressive inflammatory or systemic features suggestive of GVHD, and the pattern of gradual repigmentation following immune reconstitution favor a vitiligo-like mechanism over GVHD or other causes of dyschromia. Recognizing this link may help clinicians identify autoimmune signs in immunodeficient patients and anticipate recovery after transplantation.
