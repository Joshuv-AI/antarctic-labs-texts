Comparison of human cerebral organoids infected with wild-type Zika versus attenuated DN-2 virus strains uncovers differences in host immune responses
Authors: Yun Ying Choo, Tuan Zea Tan, Summer Lixin Zhang, Hwee Cheng Tan, Eng Eong Ooi, John Jia En Chua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Zika virus (ZIKV), a neurotropic virus, poses significant global health challenges because of its ability to cause severe neurological complications and congenital brain abnormalities. Prenatal ZIKV infection impairs early brain development. However, details of the molecular mechanisms driving their virulence remain incompletely understood. Here, we performed comparatively analyses of infection outcomes caused by 2 different ZIKV strains – wild-type (WT) ZIKV versus an attenuated strain (DN-2) – in human cerebral organoids (hCOs) to dissect host responses that could contribute to pathogenicity. Although both viral strains productively infected hCOs with indiscernible gross pathological changes, differences in host responses and subtypes of cells infected discriminate WT ZIKV from DN-2 infections. Single cell RNA sequencing analyses uncovered differently expressed genes (DEGs) that were common to both virus strains, as well as DEGs that were specific to either WT ZIKV or DN-2. For common DEGs, WT ZIKV infections elicited stronger expression of key genes involved in host immune response pathways, such as IFIT2, DDX60, OAS1 and XAF1. Moreover, analyses of WT ZIKV-specific upregulated DEGs uncovered mobilisation of additional innate immune response pathways not found in DN-2. Additionally, while WT ZIKV infected a broad range of cell types in hCOs, DN-2 infections were predominantly limited to radial glial, which are neuroprogenitors essential for neurogenesis. These results highlight that differences in host immune responses and tropism distinguish neurotropic WT ZIKV and attenuated DN-2 infections, underscoring the mechanistic differences involved in pathogenicity. Background

Zika virus (ZIKV), a mosquito-borne flavivirus first isolated in 1947, was identified as an emerging global health threat following the outbreak in Brazil in 2015 [1, 2]. ZIKV infection in expectant women can lead to fetal developmental abnormalities, especially early brain development abnormalities and malformations [3], leading to conditions, such as microcephaly and congenital Zika syndromes (CZS) [4–8]. Pathologically, ZIKV infections have been associated with impaired neurogenesis, suppressed neural stem cell proliferation, and induced apoptotic depletion of neural progenitor cells (NPCs), subsequently disrupting embryonic brain development [9–11].

ZIKV infection triggers a slew of biological processes that contribute to its pathology in infected tissues [12–14]. In the brain, ZIKV disrupts neural developmental by directly targeting NPCs, leading to apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and inflammatory responses that contribute to neurological impairments in human brain [15–17]. The detection of viral RNA by the innate immune system activates signaling pathways that promote the expression of type I interferons and the release of proinflammatory cytokines, resulting in the upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) to enhance the antiviral response [18]. Subsequently, overactivation of innate immune responses in NPCs can inhibit gene expression important for neurogenesis in a cell cycle phase-dependent manner [17]. Additionally, neuroinflammation triggered by increased cytokine expression and other immune response genes also contributes to cytopathic effects during ZIKV infections [15, 19].

Nevertheless, naturally circulating ZIKV variants exhibit differences in pathogenicity in experimental models. For instance, the Asian strain (FSS13025) was shown to infect human NPCs less efficiently as compared to the African strain (MR766) [20]. Noteworthy, V473M substitution of the envelope protein significantly enhanced pathogenicity of the FSS13025 strain by enhancing virion assembly [21]. Similar approaches have uncovered how genetic changes in strains with differing infection outcomes differentially affect host cellular machineries, such as mitochondrial dynamics and endoplasmic reticulum remodeling [21–26]. However, many of these studies were conducted using cell lines of a particular cell type whereas comparative studies of ZIKV strains with differential infection outcome in more complex models, such as brain organoids, have not been fully explored. The latter is a powerful strategy to obtain insights into mechanistic differences that contribute to viral pathogenicity and disease severity in a tissue relevant setting.

Here, we compared differences in responses of human cerebral organoids (hCOs) infected with H/PF/2013, a neurovirulent WT ZIKV strain isolated in French Polynesia against the previously reported attenuated DN-2 [27]. The attenuated strain differs from H/PF/2013 at two positions in the M protein: L42F and M66V. Our analyses uncovered that WT ZIKV infection of hCOs triggered a robust set of innate immune responses characterized by increased expression of key immune response genes and strong activation of innate immune response pathways, as well as widespread infection across a diverse population of brain cells. In contrast, DN-2 infections were mostly restricted to radial glial (RG) and elicited a much milder host response. These findings identify differences in host immune response activation and cell tropism as key features underpinning ZIKV pathogenicity.

Methods

Cell lines and virus strains

C6/36 (ATCC CRL-1660, RRID: CVCL_Z230), Vero (ATCC CCL-81, RRID: CVCL_0059) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) unless otherwise stated. C6/36 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco), and Vero were cultured in DMEM media (Gibco), all of which were supplemented with 9% fetal calf serum (HyClone). All the cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma. The Zika virus (ZIKV) clinical strain, H/PF/2013, which was isolated from French Polynesia (KJ776791, from European Virus Archive), was passaged on C6/36. DN-2 strain obtained from a previous study [27] were cultured in Vero cells. After infection, the cells were maintained in 2% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in maintenance media. All experiments performed with ZIKV were based on viral titers obtained via plaque assays on BHK-21 or Vero cells.

Generation of human cerebral organoids (hCOs)

The research, as described in the manuscript, falls within current ethical standards and does not raise concerns regarding moral status or personhood at this stage of scientific development. The hESC line H1 (RRID: CVCL_9771) (WiCell, Madison, WI) was maintained on hESC qualified Matrigel coated cell culture plates in mTeSR™Plus (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 100–0276) and passaged with ACCUTASE™ (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 07920). hCOs were generated by using the STEMdiff™ Cerebral Organoid Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat# 08570), as mentioned previously [28]. hESCs were maintained in mTeSR™PLUS until 70% to 80% confluency and then dissociated with ACCUATSE™ to obtain single cells. EB (embryoid body) was generated by seeding 9,000 viable cells per well in a 96-well round bottom ultralow attachment microplate in EB formation media supplemented with ROCKi/Y-27,632 (Abcam, Cat#ab120129). After 5 days, the EBs were transferred to individual wells containing induction medium in 48-well suspension culture plates and grown for 2 days. On day 7, each EB was embedded in GFR Matrigel (Corning, growth factor reduced basement membrane matrix, Cat#354230) and transferred into a 6-well suspension culture plate containing expansion medium. On day 10, the expansion medium was removed from the hCOs and replaced with organoid maturation medium. The culture plates containing the hCOs were then placed onto an orbital shaker (Stuart, Cat# SSM1) in a 37 °C incubator with gentle agitation at 80 rpm to allow hCOs to develop in organoid maturation medium with medium change every 3 or 4 days. All the experiments in this study were performed with Day 60 hCOs.

Infection of hCOs with ZIKV

Day 60 hCOs cultured in 6-well suspension plates were inoculated with different ZIKV strains at an MOI of 1 for 1 h at 37 °C incubator, with gentle rocking every 10 min. The viral inoculum was completely aspirated, and the mixture was incubated with organoid maturation medium. Media were changed every 3 to 4 days. The supernatants collected before and after the media were stored for plaque assays. Seven 7 days post-infection, the supernatants were harvested for plaque assays, and hCOs were collected and fixed for immunohistochemistry (IHC) or for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).

Plaque assay

BHK-21 or Vero cells seeded in a 24-well plate, were inoculated with viral supernatant diluted in tenfold from 1:100 for 1 h in a 37 °C incubator, with gentle rocking every 15 min. The virus inoculum was aspirated and incubated with 0.8% CMC (carboxymethylcellulose) and 1% penicillin-Streptomycin in maintenance medium for an appropriate number of days, subsequently fixed with 20% formalin and washed with running water until the CMC was clear. Fixed cells were stained with 1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature, which was then washed away. The stained cells were dried overnight. The plaque-forming units per milliliter (pfu/mL) were calculated.

Cryosectioning and immunostaining

Whole hCOs were fixed with 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde) for overnight at 4 °C. The organoids were then washed with 1x PBS to remove any remaining PFA. Fixed hCOs were dehydrated with 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C for overnight and mounted with OCT (optimal cutting temperature) compound. Mounted hCOs were sectioned at 16 μm with a Leica cryostat and adhered to Superfrost microscope slides. The sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, washed with 1x PBS, blocked and permeabilized with blocking buffer containing 5% BSA (bovine serum albumin), 1% Triton X-100 and 1% DMSO in 1x PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking and permeabilization, hCO sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for overnight at 4 °C and washed with 1x PBS three times for 5 min. hCOs were then incubated with secondary antibodies, and the washing step was repeated. After the final wash, the sections were mounted with mounting media supplemented with DAPI.

The primary antibodies used for IF labeling were as follows: mouse anti-4G2 (ATCC, Cat# D1-4G2-4–15, RRID: AB_2940755), rabbit anti-SOX2 cy3-conjugated (1:200, Synaptic Systems, Cat# 347003C3) and rat anti-Ctip2 (1:20, Abcam, Cat# ab18465, RRID: AB_2064130). The secondary antibodies used were as follows: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A11001, RRID: AB_2534069), goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 647 (1:100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A21247, RRID: AB_2535813).

Tissue dissociation for single cell isolation for scRNAseq

Organoids were washed in 3 mL of prechilled Hank’s balanced salt solution (L0605–500, Biowest). Up to 15 mg of organoids were dissociated with 100 µL of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (25200056, Invitrogen). A wide-bore tip was used to gently break the organoids into smaller pieces, followed by incubation at 37 °C and 600 rpm for 5 min. At the end of the incubation, 1 ml of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (L0104–500, Biowest) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (S181H, Biowest) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (15140122, Gibco) was added, and the dissociated cell suspension was strained through a 40 μm filter. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g and 4 °C for 5 min, and the cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of prechilled resuspension buffer (1x phosphate-buffered saline (BUF-2041-1 × 1 L, 1st Base), 1% BSA (A7906, Sigma‒Aldrich) and 0.02% sodium azide (103694 M, VWR)). The cell suspension was strained through a 40 μm filter and centrifuged at 200 × g and 4 °C for 2 min to remove small debris. The cell pellets were then resuspended in 1 mL of prechilled resuspension buffer. The cells were stained with acridine orange/propidium iodide (F23001, Logos Biosystems) and counted using a LUNA-FX7 automated cell counter (Logos Biosystems).

Single-cell RNA sequencing library Preparation and sequencing

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were constructed via a GEXSCOPE™ single-cell RNA-seq library kit (4180011, Singleron Biotechnologies) according to the manufacturer´s instructions. Briefly, for each library, a cell suspension of a specified cell concentration was loaded onto a microfluidic chip to capture cells. The single-cell capture, lysis and mRNA capture steps were automated via the Singleron Matrix NEOTM system. The final single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with an S4 v1.5 flow cell with 150 bp paired ends.

Transcriptome data preprocessing

Fastq files were preprocessed via the CeleScope® tools (version 1.15.0; www.github.com/singleron-RD/CeleScope, Singleron Biotechnologies) to generate raw data via default parameters. Briefly, the cellular barcodes in Read 1 were used to demultiplex and identify reads of the same cell origin. Low-quality and adapter sequences were removed via Cutadapt (version 3.7) [29]. The mapping was performed via STARSOLO (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR/blob/master/docs/STARsolo.md) against a human genome build (GRCh38) with ENSEMBL Gene Annotation (Version 99) [30]. The reads were assigned to genes via the featureCount tool v2.0.6 (https://subread.sourceforge.net), and cell calling was performed by fitting a negative bimodal distribution and determining the threshold between empty wells and cell-associated wells [31]. The gene count matrix was then generated, providing the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for each gene and cell. On average, 83.1% of the reads (915 million total) were mapped to the human transcriptome, and 14,788 cells were detected per sample for analysis. Detailed quality control metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Single-cell data analysis

Downstream analysis was performed via the scRNA analysis pipeline CeleScoot 1.15.0 (https://github.com/singleron-RD/scrna). QC metrics, such as the number of genes detected per cell (nFeature_RNA) and the percentage of mitochondrial UMI (percent_mt), were extracted from the gene count matrix with the function calculate_qc_metrics. To bioinformatically remove nonviable cells, cells with a high percentage of mitochondrial counts (> 20%) were filtered out. To remove possible doublets, the cells with a high number of detected genes (> 5000) were filtered out. Cell debris characterized by a low number of detected genes (< 200) was also excluded from the data analysis. The single-cell gene expression matrix was subsequently imported into R v4.4.0 and processed via the R package Seurat v5.1.0 [32]. Briefly, the replicates were normalized and integrated via the Seurat canonical correlation analysis (CCA) algorithm, and clusters were identified via Seurat graph-based clustering on the UMAP generated from the first 30 CCAs. Cell type annotation was performed via the R package SingleR v2.6.0 on the basis of similarity to the annotated cell type in 2-month-old brain organoids [33]. GO analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were performed using Enrichr [34–36]. On the other hand, GO analysis of significant WT-specific and DN-2-specific upregulated DEGs was performed using Metascape v3.5 (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html) [37].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed on Prism (GraphPad, version 10.4.0) using Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA based on data distribution, unless otherwise stated.

Results

Wild-type ZIKV and DN-2 strains productively infect hCOs

Via serial passage of wild-type (WT) ZIKV, the French Polynesian H/PF/2013 clinical isolate, in cell cultures, we previously isolated and identified an attenuated strain of ZIKV, DN-2. The attenuated strain differs from H/PF/2013 at two positions in the M protein: L42F and M66V [27]. As compared to WT ZIKV, DN-2 showed diminished ability to disseminate on a cell monolayer to form small plaques in plaque assays but retained its ability to infect and activate dendritic cells. Importantly, mice infected with DN-2 exhibited 100% survival as compared to the uniform mortality in those infected with WT ZIKV. DN-2 could thus serve as a reference point to dissect the pathogenic host response in our brain organoid model, similar to what was previously done with a study using cell monolayer [38].

To examine the differences in infection outcomes between WT ZIKV and DN-2 in human cerebral organoids (hCOs), a model of the developing human brain, we inoculated hCOs with 1 MOI of either WT ZIKV or DN-2 virus at Day 60 (Fig. 1a) [28, 39]. Over a period of 7 days postinfection, we harvested culture supernatant and performed via plaque assays to monitor the progress of viral infection. By Day 2, we could already detect an increase in viral titer for both viral strains though the difference was not statistically significant between the two ZIKV strains (Fig. 1b). No discernible morphological changes or cytopathic effects could be observed in uninfected, WT ZIKV, DN-2 infected hCOs (Fig. 1c). Immunostaining with the 4G2 antibody, which detects the ZIKV envelope protein (ZIKV-E), revealed the colocalization of ZIKV-infected cells, with SOX2+ (neural progenitor marker) or CTIP2+ (early-born neuronal marker) cells (Fig. 1d) [10, 40, 41]. Taken together, these findings confirm earlier reports that WT ZIKV can infect hCOs [9, 10]. The results also indicated that DN-2 can productively infect hCOs.

Wild-type and DN-2 ZIKV share a common set of DEGs in infected hCOs

The comparability of viral titers between WT ZIKV and DN-2 allowed us to dissect the host response that distinguishes pathogenic from attenuated ZIKV infection without infection burden being a confounder. To elucidate this, we harvested RNA samples from uninfected, WT ZIKV- and DN-2-infected hCOs and subjected them to scRNAseq analyses. To identify gene expression signature associated with ZIKV infection, we aggregated the scRNAseq gene expression for each condition: uninfected, WT and DN-2. We applied filtering criteria for differentially expressed genes (DEGs), identifying those with a log2 fold change (FC) > + 0.3 or < −0.3, and p < 0.05 (Supp Fig. 1). The analysis between the WT ZIKV and DN-2 as compared to uninfected hCOs, resulted in the identification of a total of eight hundred and eighty-two (882) viral-induced DEGs (Fig. 2a; Supp Table S2). By comparing against uninfected organoids, we found 167 DEGs common to both WT ZIKV and DN-2. Among these common DEGs, one hundred and one (101) were consistently upregulated in both WT ZIKV and DN-2 infection, whereas sixty-six (66) were downregulated (Fig. 2b). Of the upregulated genes, OAS3, IFIT3, IFI6, IFIH1, IFIT1, ISG15, OAS1, MX1, and STAT1, which are key players in innate immune and inflammatory responses, have been previously identified in ZIKV-infected hCOs (Fig. 2c) [42–45]. These genes are frequently used as markers of ZIKV infection and to study key antiviral responses during ZIKV infection [18, 20, 46–48]. Upregulation of these markers confirm that DN-2, like WT ZIKV, can infect hCOs.

Strikingly, differences at the expression levels of these common DEGs could already be seen between WT ZIKV- and DN-2-infected hCOs. For instance, several of the marker genes, including IFI44, IFIT1, DDX58, OAS3 and XAF1, showed higher expression in WT ZIKV-infected hCOs, indicating a robust antiviral response induced in WT ZIKV-infected hCOs (Fig. 2d and e; Supp Table S3 and S4) [49]. Moreover, antiviral genes, such as TRIM22, DHX58, IFIT5, HERC6, and EIF2AK2, are more highly expressed in WT ZIKV infection, than in DN-2 (Fig. 2f) [50, 51].

Analyses of the commonly upregulated DEGs confirmed that many of them are enriched for gene ontology (GO) terms related to host defense responses and viral replication (Fig. 3a; Supp Table S5). In particular, upregulated DEGs of both strains show strong enrichment of genes involved in type I IFN signaling pathways, which are activated during ZIKV infection and are linked to its pathogenicity [52–54]. Remarkably, as observed for ZIKV marker genes, expression of both IFNα- and IFNβ- responsive genes were also significantly upregulated in WT ZIKV-infected hCOs as compared to DN-2-infected hCOs (Fig. 3b and c; Supp Table S6 and S7). Moreover, WT ZIKV infections trigger expression of IFNα response pathway across a broader range of cell types, including apical radial glial, outer radial glial, newborn cortical plate neuron, corticofugal projection neurons and cortical hem (Supp Fig. 2c). Together with the higher expression of antiviral genes as noted above, these results reinforced the notion that infections with WT ZIKV is associated with stronger activation of host immune response pathways as compared to DN-2 [27].

Comparison of strain-specific DEGs further supports stronger induction of host immune responses by wild-type ZIKV

In addition to common DEGs, we also uncovered DEGs that were expressed for one strain but not the other (Supp Table S2). Three hundred and ninety-one (391) WT ZIKV strain-specific DEGs were identified in addition to the previously mentioned common genes (Fig. 2b). Of this subset of DEGs, one hundred and twenty-eight (128) genes were upregulated and two hundred and sixty-three (263) downregulated (Fig. 4a). For DN-2, three hundred and twenty-four (324) strain-specific DEGs were identified, of which one hundred and eighty-eight (188) were upregulated and one hundred and thirty-six (136) downregulated (Fig. 4b).

GO enrichment analyses revealed further distinction in biological processes affected by WT ZIKV versus DN-2 infections. Noteworthy, upregulated WT ZIKV strain-specific DEGs strongly enriched for immune-related pathways, including antigen processing and presentation, activation of innate immune responses, and activation of leukocytes. This signified a heightened immune activation induced in response to WT ZIKV infection of the hCOs (Fig. 4c; Supp Table S8). Network visualization of the enriched clusters also showed tight coordination of host responses to WT ZIKV infection, such as MHC class I antigen presentation and immune response activation (Fig. 4d). Strikingly, enrichment of immune-related pathways was not observed in up-regulated DN-2 strain-specific DEGs. Instead, the DEGs were enrich for pathways involving in mitosis, cytoskeleton organization as well as forebrain development and neurogenesis (Fig. 4e and f; Supp Table S9).

Analyses of downregulated strain-specific DEGs for WT ZIKV confirmed earlier findings that ZIKV infections affect early brain formation, with enrichment of genes involved in neural tube formation and migration of neural progenitor cells, together with processes involved in cytoskeleton organisation (Fig. 4g; Supp Table S10) [14, 55]. Remarkably, biological processes such as axonogenesis, and synapse development and function are enriched in DN-2-specific downregulated DEGs (Fig. 4h; Supp Table S11) [56–58]. It would be of relevance to elucidate how DN-2 could affect neuronal development and function in future studies.

Wild-type and DN-2 strains of ZIKV show different infectivity across brain cell populations

ZIKV have been shown to preferentially target neuroprogenitor cells [9, 15, 20]. To determine if WT ZIKV and DN-2 might exhibit differences in their ability to infect sub-populations of cells in hCOs, we took advantage of the set of common DEGs shared between WT ZIKV- and DN-2-infected hCOs (collectively referred as ZIKV signature genes) to identify ZIKV-signature-enriched cells within each cell type (Fig. 2c) [47]. To assess the validity of our approach, we compared the analyses against using only known Zika marker genes [47] or by removing IFN-I response genes from the common DEGs, since type-1 IFN released by cells has been reported to signal to non-infected cells to activate inflammatory responses [59]. We observed significant correlation between all 3 strategies used (Supp Fig. 3). CellChat analyses did not detect the presence of significant inter-cellular IFN pathways, indicating that IFN-I signalling had minimal influence on the analyses (Supp Fig. 4) [60]. Finally, we also compared IFNα signature scores of Zika signature-enriched (infected) against non-Zika signature-enriched (uninfected) cells. This analyses indicated that Zika signature-enriched cells show higher IFNα responsive gene signature scores as compared to non-Zika signature-enriched cells (Supp Fig. 5).

We then used previously reported cellular markers for the different cellular subtypes present in hCOs. A total of 8 distinct cell types were mapped in our hCOs in our scRNAseq dataset (Supp Fig. 2, a and b) [27, 28, 33]. uMAP plots were then generated to visualize the distribution of Zika signature-enriched cells versus non-Zika signature-enriched cells (Fig. 5a and b). The percentages of Zika signature-enriched cells within each cell type are shown in for WT ZIKV (Fig. 5c) and DN-2 (Fig. 5d).

WT ZIKV show the broadest range of Zika signature-enriched cells, with approximately all cell clusters expressing Zika signature genes (Fig. 5a). In agreement with previous reports, radial glial (RG), which are the dominant neural progenitor cells (NPCs) present in hCOs were the most enriched in Zika signature, with 9.48% for apical radial glial (aRG) and followed by 8.64% for outer radial glial (oRG) [6, 7, 9, 16, 20]. Other Zika signature-enriched clusters include 1.22% for intermediate progenitors (IPs), 0.96% for cortical hem, 0.75% for corticofugal projection neurons (CFuPNs), 0.68% for newborn cortical plate neuron (NewbornCPN), 0.55% for unspecified projection neuron/NewbornCPN (UnspecifiedPN/NewbornCPN), and 0.41% for UnspecifiedPN (Fig. 5c).

In contrast, DN-2 exhibited a more restricted Zika signature enrichment profile, with oRGs (31.25%) being the predominantly Zika signature-enriched cell type (Fig. 5b). Other Zika signature-enriched clusters include aRGs (5.33%), IPs (3.41%), cortical hem (1.24%), CFuPNs (0.06%), NewbornCPN (0.26%) and UnspecifiedPN/NewbornCPN (0.03%) (Fig. 5d). Notably, the percentage of Zika signature-enriched cells in the oRG cluster was much higher in DN-2 versus WT ZIKV (31.25% in DN-2; 8.64% in WT ZIKV) even though the percentages of Zika signature-enriched aRG were more comparable (5.33% for DN-2; 9.48% for WT ZIKV). Thus, the ability of WT ZIKV to infect a broader range of cell types, together with its ability to elicit stronger host responses, likely underscores its ability cause greater damage in the brain [61–63].

Host immune responses are downregulated in DN-2-infected aRG

Infection of NPCs by ZIKV is thought to contribute to its effects on impaired brain development, including disruptions in neurogenesis, apoptosis induction, cortical thinning, and microcephaly [33, 64, 65]. Radial glial cells are neural progenitor cells (NPCs) that produce neurons and glia during brain development [66–68]. They are subdivided into apical radial glial (aRG) and outer radial glial (oRG), with aRGs residing in ventricular zone and maintaining the apical-basal polarity, while oRGs in the outer subventricular zone contribute to cortical expansion [67, 69–71]. Infection of both types of RG impair neurogenesis and cortical expansion, ultimately contributing to microcephaly [72–74].

Although a much larger proportion of Zika signature-enriched oRG were present in DN-2 infected hCOs as compared to WT ZIKV, GO enrichment analyses did not reveal major differences in DEGs expressed between oRGs in hCOs infected by either strain (Supp Table S12). Thus, differences in Zika signature enrichment profile of DN-2 versus WT ZIKV in oRG is unlikely to account for the differences in pathogenicity between the two strains.

Analyses of DEGs in Zika signature-enriched aRG of WT ZIKV- versus DN2-infected hCOs uncovered significant differences in the underlying biological pathways (Fig. 6a; Supp Tables 13, 14 and 15). Downregulated genes in Zika signature enriched aRG of DN-2-infected hCOs included interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as IFIT1, IFI27, and OAS1 (Fig. 6b). GO enrichment analyses further showed that these were enriched for biological processes involved in host immune responses, including interleukin-27 mediated signaling pathway, interferon-mediated signaling pathway, positive regulation of tumor necrosis factor production, and regulation of I-κB/NF- κB signaling (Fig. 6c; Supp Table 14). Upregulated DEGs in Zika signature enriched aRG of DN-2-infected hCOs enrich for biosynthetic pathways, including mRNA processing and translation, and included ribosomal protein genes such as RPL4, RPS3A, RPL7A and RPL24 (Fig. 6d and e; Supp Table 15). Concurrent suppression of host responses and hijacking of translational machinery has been reported for ZIKV [75, 76]. The upregulation of genes involved in these processes in DN2-infected hCOs is striking and suggests that DN-2 may impair the host’s antiviral defenses while altering biosynthetic pathways of relevance to host translational machinery, as also observed in flavivirus infections [77–79].

Discussion

While the majority of ZIKV infections results in mild clinical symptoms, severe outcomes leading to critical illness and lethality have been documented, particularly in unborn infants [80].

In this study, we compared the outcome of infections in hCOs caused by neurotropic WT ZIKV against the attenuated DN-2 strain to elucidate molecular determinants of ZIKV pathogenicity. Our findings demonstrate that WT ZIKV triggers a strong host antiviral immune response and is capable of infecting a broader group of cells whereas infections with DN-2 show reduced activation of host immune responses and more restricted tropism. These results provide valuable insights into the mechanisms associated with neurotropic ZIKV that contribute to its neuropathology in severe ZIKV infections.

Our data show that WT ZIKV triggers a stronger host immune response compared to DN-2, as evidenced by the upregulation of several key interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including IFIT1, OAS1, and STAT1, which are more highly expressed in WT ZIKV-infected hCOs [4, 49, 81, 82]. Moreover, GO analyses of WT ZIKV strain specific upregulated DEGs further highlight the activation of additional innate immune pathways, which are not observed in DN-2 strain-specific DEGs. Such strong upregulation of host immune responses in WT ZIKV infected cells indicate that the wildtype viral strain is more readily detectable by host immune system, which in turn triggers a strong inflammatory reaction [72]. The enrichment of neurogenic pathways in downregulated ZIKV-strain specific DEGs agree with earlier findings that the virus preferentially infects neuroprogenitor cells and affect early stages of brain development by limiting cell proliferation and ultimately causing microcephaly [13, 14]. The combination of robust immune reaction and disruption of cell development could contribute to the severe neuropathological outcomes observed in congenital Zika syndrome patients [3, 83].

In contrast to WT ZIKV, host immune responses triggered by DN-2 infection is considerably muted, with less expression of immune-related genes such as IFIT2 and MX1. This concurs with what was previously reported in a mouse model, where the inability of DN-2 infections to trigger pro-inflammatory responses and damage the blood-brain-barrier was thought to limit its spread into the central nervous system [27]. The absence of pattern recognition receptor signaling pathway pathways in upregulated DN-2-strain-specific DEGs, which were detected in upregulated WT ZIKV-strain-specific DEGs, could also offer an explanation for the comparatively weaker host defense response in DN-2 infections [84–86].

In addition to differences in immune response, WT ZIKV and DN-2 strains also exhibit differences in percentages of Zika signature enriched-cells in each cluster. WT ZIKV demonstrated a broader tropism, where Zika signature-enriched in both neural progenitors and differentiated neurons in hCOs. DN-2 infections were largely limited to neuroprogenitor cells, with neurons minimally infected. Despite an approximately 4-fold higher number of Zika signature-enriched oRGs, we could not identify significant differences in DEGs from infected oRG of either virus strains that could explain the higher infectivity of DN-2 for oRG.

Instead, upregulation of host immune responses in Zika signature-enriched aRG of WT ZIKV- versus DN2-infected hCOs, including stronger type I interferon response, may contribute to the severe depletion of the neural progenitor population, which are essential for generation of both neurons and glial cells [41, 74, 87]. Neuroinflammation in aRGs can disrupted cell cycle and division; the effect is particularly detrimental as aRGs are primary progenitors in early neurogenesis and their depletion can lead to severe cortical malformations [13, 69]. This disruption contributes to impaired neurogenesis and the hallmark of microcephaly associated with congenital Zika syndrome [6, 9, 10, 88]. We did not observe significant cytopathic effects in our study, which could arise from the relatively short incubation period used in here [47, 89].

The limitation of current study is the absence of microglia in the brain organoid system, which is essential in modulating neuroinflammation [90, 91]. Although our model does not incorporate microglia, our findings provide valuable insights to delineate the intrinsic responses of naïve neuronal cell types towards ZIKV infection. Nonetheless, a microglia-containing brain organoid models will be valuable as future work to comprehensively assess immune responses in extend to our current observations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identified differences in activation of host immune pathways as a key mechanism contributing pathogenicity in ZIKV infections. Further elucidation of these differences may provide insights into novel intervention strategies for patients suffering from severe ZIKV infections with brain complications.
