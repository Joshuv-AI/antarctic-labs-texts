Multimodal Mass Spectrometry Imaging in Atlas Building: A Review
Authors: Kyle A Vanderschoot, Kayle J Bender, Christopher M De Caro, Kelli A Steineman, Elizabeth K Neumann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn the era of precision medicine, scientists are creating atlases of the human body to map cells at the molecular level, providing insight into what fundamentally makes each cell different. In these atlas efforts, multimodal imaging techniques that include mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) have revolutionized the way biomolecules, such as lipids, peptides, proteins, and small metabolites, are visualized in the native spatial context of biological tissue. As such, MSI has become a fundamental arm of major cell atlasing efforts, as it can analyze the spatial distribution of hundreds of molecules in diverse sample types. These rich molecular data are then correlated with orthogonal assays, including histologic staining, proteomics, and transcriptomics, to analyze molecular classes that are not traditionally detected by MSI. Additional computational methods enable further examination of the correlations between biomolecular classes and creation of visualizations that serve as a powerful resource for researchers and clinicians trying to understand human health and disease. In this review, we examine modern multimodal imaging methods and how they contribute to precision medicine and the understanding of fundamental disease mechanisms.Keywords: Multimodal imaging, mass spectrometry, multiomics, tissue imaging, single-cell analysis

ATLAS EFFORTS AND ONGOING DEVELOPMENT

The first geographic atlases were created in the 16th century as explorers from around the world reported their travels and expanded our understanding of the globe. Over time, these atlases were refined with more detailed information, such as topography, as more sophisticated technology and data became available. Akin to geographic atlases, scientists have created and continually updated anatomical atlases at every structural level (e.g., organelle, cell, functional units, organs, organ systems) to identify and understand the physiological interactions that occur throughout the body at these various scales.1 Major histologic efforts have examined thousands of tissue sections across mammalian systems to further elucidate the functions and cellular compositions of the tissue’s functional units and how these units drive healthy organ function and its transition to a diseased state.2–4

Atlas efforts that covers the human body at various scales are expansive so, consortia between diverse scientific disciplines have come together to collaborate on these ventures. Some of the most recent consortium efforts are the Human Cell Atlas,5 Human BioMolecular Atlas Program (HuBMAP),6 Chan Zuckerberg Initiative,7 Human Tumor Atlas Network,8 Kidney Precision Medicine Project,9 and many others.10–15 These atlas efforts broadly have the goal of providing molecular insight into every cell, examining the biochemical species and their interactions across and beyond the entire central dogma of biology. Even more massive than the Human Genome Project,16 Human Cell Atlas and HuBMAP efforts aim to characterize all 37 trillion cells that compose the body, as opposed to the 20,000 genes in the human genome. These projects aim to analyze cellular networks to provide researchers with data on molecular differences across individuals, including factors such as demographics and comorbidities, to advance understanding with regard to human health and precision medicine.

Although characterizing trillions of cells is an ambitious task, these consortia serve as an opportunity to develop the next generation of biotechnological techniques that are capable of these feats.17,18 The techniques used in these efforts are well established but often do not individually provide sufficient detail to comprehensively demonstrate underlying biological mechanisms related to disease. To address this, multiple compatible modalities used in parallel enable the use of two or more analytical techniques on a single sample.19 This integration allows for comprehensive analysis of precious samples without sacrificing key biological data, such as functional cell types and biochemical mechanisms.20

Although major efforts have gone toward understanding the proteome, genome, and transcriptome, the metabolome and lipidome remains less understood given its vast chemical diversity. Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) is one of the few techniques capable of untargeted metabolomic characterization without disturbing the native spatial distribution of cells and their biochemical products. MSI has been applied to countless sample types and applications and will be the main focus in the multimodal approaches discussed here (Figure 1).21,22 MSI is at the core of expanding the molecular characterization capabilities of these atlases and can be orthogonally integrated with existing cellular profiling techniques that target the proteome and transcriptome to provide a complete picture of an individual’s phenotype at the spatial level. In this review, we highlight how MSI has impacted major cell atlasing efforts and its incorporation into multimodal imaging workflows focused on biomedical research.

OVERVIEW OF MSI

MSI has revolutionized the mapping and characterization of a wide range of diverse biomolecules across many unique disciplines and applications.23–34 With great success, MSI has been used to study small-molecule metabolites, lipids, glycans, peptides, and proteins, among other molecular classes, in their native biological contexts. There are a variety of MSI technologies that mainly vary by their sources of ionization. The most notable methods of ionization include secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS),35–37 nanospray desorption electrospray ionization (DESI),38–40 and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI).41–43 These ionization sources possess their own sets of strengths and limitations and are routinely used in MSI experiments to image different classes of molecules and samples, making them uniquely useful for atlasing efforts.

MALDI MSI is a laser-based MSI source that involves rastering an ultraviolet or infrared laser source across a tissue surface to desorb and ionize endogenous molecules within tissue.44 In addition to a laser, MALDI requires the application of a small organic matrix that is capable of absorbing applied laser energy to assist in the desorption and ionization process.45 There are several methods of applying matrix to a slide, including dried droplet, airbrush, nebulization, and sublimation. Generally, sublimation or automated sprayers are used for atlas building efforts owing to their enhanced reproducibility, which is necessary for large cohort studies that take months or even years for data acqusition.46 MALDI MSI has been used for both abiotic and biological studies, but it is particularly attractive for biological applications because it is less restricted by sample size while maintaining high sampling speeds and moderate spatial resolutions. Commercial systems offer spatial resolutions of less than 10 μm, and modified instruments can achieve resolutions of less than 1 μm (the average size of a mammalian cell is 10 μm3).47,48 Further, modern MALDI MSI instruments are equipped with an optical system that is capable of varying laser sampling rates, laser footprint sizes, and automated stage step sizes. Sampling rates can be adjusted to gather mass spectra from one spectrum per position to 10,000 spectra per position.34 For the HuBMAP, MSI sampling is reported as 1 mm2/2 h, allowing for large tissue sections to be sampled within a day.49

MALDI can also be used in conjunction with other spectroscopic techniques to provide additional characterization data orthogonal to mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) data already being acquired. Some of the most commonly used spectroscopic techniques that have been combined with MSI include nuclear magnetic resonance,50,51 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy,52–54 and Raman spectroscopy.55–57 More specialized techniques not as commonly applied in atlas building, such as microbeam x-ray fluorescence spectrometry58 and laser desorption/ionization from silicon nanospot arrays,59 have also been used with MALDI MSI.

DESI is another spatially oriented ionization platform that has been used in biological imaging. DESI utilizes electrostatically charged solvent droplets to extract, desorb, and ionize molecules of interest for mass spectrometry analysis.38,60 DESI has the distinct advantage of limited sample preparation and ambient ionization conditions to extract and analyze biomolecules of interest from virtually any type of biological tissue, even with drastic topography. Commercial DESI instruments have a spatial resolution as low as 30 μm, and further resolution is limited because of the size of the contact point applied by the sprayer.61 To address this, nanospray DESI achieves lower resolutions by forming a liquid bridge between two microfluidic silica capillaries. This liquid bridge then extracts and carries analytes to the electrospray ionization source. This liquid bridge extracts endogenous molecules from the sample surface, allowing optimized methods to achieve better than 10-μm spatial resolution in an imaging context.62

SIMS uses a focused primary ion beam to bombard a sample of interest (both inorganic63 and biological64), transferring energy from the ion beam to endogenous molecules through a collisional cascade, generating secondary ions through this process.65 The ion beam can be focused smaller than 100 nm, routinely achieving subcellular spatial resolutions.66,67 There are many different primary ion beams that can be used, with the most common being C60+, Arn+, (H2O)n+, Bi3+, SF5+, and Au3+.68 The energy associated with these ion beams generally results in in-source fragmentation, making it most beneficial for stable small-molecule metabolites, although there are examples in both proteomic analysis69 and lipidomic analysis.70 SIMS can be used in two ways: static SIMS and dynamic SIMS. Static SIMS refers to regimens that incorporate low primary ion doses (<1013 cm2) and beam currents (10 pA to 5 nA), and it is typically used for surface analysis.71 Dynamic SIMS results in rapid surface erosion because of the high primary ion doses and beam currents (1013 cm2 and >μA, respectively), making it more suitable for depth profiling and three-dimensional imaging.72 SIMS MSI has been used to analyze single cells, allowing for high-resolution characterization of the lipid membrane and small metabolites.37,73–77

The most important aspect of experimental design involves the tissue preparation strategy. Both fresh frozen and formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues are compatible with MSI. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue processing is commonly used for biobanked samples because it can be stored at ambient conditions for years. Although this method enhances tissue stability, the embedding and fixation process can crosslink or remove small molecules and lipids, hindering their detection during MSI experiments. Paraffin wax removal and antigen retrieval are often required and must be empirically optimized for each tissue type, introducing additional experimental variability. Fresh frozen tissue is generally preferred for MSI analysis, both with and without embedding. However, fresh frozen sections require more stringent storage and handling conditions to prevent degradation and loss of biochemical data, especially for MSI analysis.

In sum, each MSI technique possesses its own distinct advantages and disadvantages. Because of the scope of the consortium projects, scientists leverage each approach as well as pairwise combinations to maximize the chemical content that can be obtained. MSI has been used in many different aspects and is growing in popularity, especially as a spatial multiomics-compatible platform. Next, we will describe the additional modalities that can be used in conjunction with MSI to yield greater insights that have been or could be implemented in atlas construction efforts.

MSI WITH IMMUNOHISTOCHEMISTRY

As powerful as the molecular content afforded by MALDI MSI is, it is often without cellular context. Histologists have developed chemical staining techniques to visualize cells in general and antibody-based techniques such as immunocytochemistry and immunohistochemistry (IHC) to detect proteins of interest within specific cells and tissues, respectively.78 These chemical and immunochemical techniques have been coupled to MSI-based techniques to provide cellular identity and contextualize the metabolomics data acquired across the entire tissue section. Several traditional IHC methods, including direct and indirect antibody detect, have been employed in combination with MSI.79 Although these methods are complementary to each other, there is a need for high-throughput methods, including those that rely on fluorophore-labeling methods. These typically involve cyclic staining and imaging (tissue-based cyclic immunofluorescence [IF]),80,81 inactivating fluorescent reporter dyes,82 or removing the labeled antibody after imaging.83

Although each type of MSI is useful for atlas building in isolation, they can also be combined to expand the molecular coverage from a tissue. SIMS and MALDI MSI have been used together; notably, Kaya et al.84 demonstrated a multimodal approach that combines SIMS, MALDI MSI, and IHC. Time-of-flight SIMS was performed at an approximately 2-μm pixel resolution to image rodent cerebellum using a Bi3+ cluster dual polarity ion beam with a low-energy electron flood gun. This low-energy ion beam was used for ion cluster activation to avoid damaging the tissue while still detecting hard to ionize metabolites such as cholesterol, sterols, vitamin cofactors, and other neutral small metabolites. Next, 1,5-diaminonaphthalene was sequentially sublimed onto the tissue for lipid and peptide analysis at approximately 10-μm spatial resolution using MALDI MSI. Finally, the MALDI matrix was removed and IHC was performed to stain proteolipid protein and glial fibrillary acidic protein. Alternatively, other histologic stains, such as hematoxylin and eosin, can be incorporated to obtain information on protein content, nuclei location, and cell membrane boundaries. The group recommended performing SIMS prior to MALDI MSI to avoid losing bio-molecules when removing the MALDI matrix, although there are examples in which SIMS has been enhanced with the addition of a matrix. Regardless, this multimodal approach demonstrates the use of both SIMS and MALDI as well as high spatial resolution imaging; detection of neutral lipids, cholesterol, sterols, and vitamins; and integration with cell type through the use of IHC.

Similarly, nanospray DESI has been paired with IHC techniques to provide cellular identification with metabolomics data.85,86 Unsihuay et al.85 utilized nanospray DESI and IHC to depict oxidative and glycolytic metabolism and the associated fiber types found within murine gastrocnemius muscle. Because DESI does not require the assistance of a matrix and can analyze samples without much sample preparation, it is an optimal method for analyzing cells as close to their native active state as possible. The acquired metabolomics data are then contextualized with IHC to display the muscle fiber type. The study found that a chemical gradient of oxidative metabolites to glycolytic metabolites, going from deep to superficial, was correlated with the amount of type IIb myofiber present using IF. This relationship was further analyzed to identify the differences in phospholipids as well as acyl chain length differences because lipids are the primary source of energy in β-oxidation that supplies acetyl coenzyme A for oxidative phosphorylation.87

Many atlas efforts need to maximize the amount of data obtained from each sample, such that the complexity of the tissue is reflected in the depicted molecular landscape. This typically involves multiplexing so that correlations between cell types and the major biochemical processes they carry out can be observed. One of the most popular multiplexing techniques used in atlas consortia involves DNA barcoding strategies in which antibodies can be tagged with a unique DNA barcode sequence that allows for detection of the antibody by a complementary DNA oligonucleotide bound to a fluorophore. This allows for facile removal of fluorescence signal and cost-effective multiplexing of hundreds of unique targets, such as the approach: co-detection by indexing (CODEX).88,89 CODEX multiplexed IF (MxIF) has been applied to single sections of the human kidney to visualize 23 different antigens and is capable of indicating cell types in the collecting ducts, glomeruli, and proximal and distal tubules across healthy non-neoplastic and diabetic nephropathy patients.90 This analysis of relative expression and cell types is further combined with MALDI MSI to reveal cellular neighborhoods and differential metabolic states across disease.91 This multimodal approach can be repeated across serial sections to construct a multiscale atlas that describes the lipids and proteins that influence kidney health.92 Some of the largest differentiating lipids described in these atlasing efforts were 1-palmitoyl-2-azelaoyl PC, phosphatidylserine (26:0), and phosphatidylethanolamine (O-38:5). These lipids were most significantly altered when correlated with an individual’s body mass index, especially in cellular neighborhoods that contained disrupted calbindin expression or aquaporin 2 expression or both. Although the specific biological role these lipids play is still unknown, this demonstrates how multimodal approaches can uncover specific relationships between cell types and their functional states (Fig. 2). Although incredibly powerful, there are difficulties associated with combining CODEX MxIF with MALDI MSI regardless of its being added before or after. MALDI MSI is particularly destructive and can damage the tissue or reduce binding affinity of the added antibodies when it is performed before CODEX MxIF. Alternatively, CODEX MxIF can be performed prior to MALDI MSI, but the fixation and added solvents crosslink or remove molecules that are typically detected by MALDI MSI, similar to traditional IHC workflows.79

Although IF is the most prominent method integrated with MSI, spectral crowding limits the speed and incresases cost associated with highly multiplexed assays. Because the electromagnetic spectrum ranges from approximately 400 to 700 nm, standard fluorescence microscopes can fit four to seven fluorescent reporters within this range without having spectral crowding and bleed-through from overlapping excitation/emission peaks.95 Recent efforts have circumvented this fundamental limitation of fluorescence microscopy by leveraging unique mass reporters rather than fluorescent reporters and utilizing the high resolving power of the mass spectrometer. This simplifies the biological assay by using one instrument while also avoiding the throughput limitations associated with fluorescence microscopy. One example of using mass reporters in place of fluorophores involves using metal-labeled antibodies in a highly multiplexed manner, which is referred to as imaging mass cytometry. In this approach, metals are chelated and conjugated to antibodies and serve as indirect identification of the antibody they are associated with. This is a powerful technique that is naturally amenable to high multiplexing applications and has previously been shown to be compatible with MSI in cultured cells.96 The technique typically uses inductively coupled plasma as an ionization source coupled to a time-of-flight analyzer, as it is particularly beneficial when analyzing metals, but is also used with SIMS (multiplexed ion beam imaging by time of flight) and applied to tissue.97,98 Beyond SIMS, organic mass tag reporters have been adapted to MALDI and DESI MSI.99 Olejnik et al100,101 developed o-nitro-benzyl-based photocleavable mass tags (PC-MTs) that were then used with an NHS ester linker to couple a peptide-based PC-MT to an antibody or oligonucleotide for MALDI MSI. The tagged biomolecules are applied to the tissue and exposed to ultraviolet light to cleave the mass tag from the biomolecule without disturbing the spatial distribution. The PC-MT strategy has since been commercialized for MALDI IHC.102 MALDI IHC has been used as a guide for an all-in-one multiomics platform used to investigate and visualize a wide range of tissue types.103–105

MSI WITH SPATIAL TRANSCRIPTOMICS

Spatial transcriptomics is a rapidly evolving field involving the measurement and localization of gene expression that can be used to understand the mechanisms behind disease and disorder.106 Current commercial spatial transcriptomics approaches utilize DNA microarrays to capture and sequence RNA transcripts107,108 or in situ hybridization109–111 to detect and analyze gene expression. These technologies have been used to probe the transcriptome in its entirety across numerous tissue types and are more commonly combined with IHC approaches to visualize the proteome. However, this leaves the connection between the transcriptome and metabolome neglected and relatively unexplored.112 It is well known that biochemical signaling cascades utilize lipids and small molecules as key regulators in protein activity, which in turn affects gene expression and RNA abundance.113 Despite the importance of these connections, there are limited examples of multiomics or multimodal methods relating the transcriptome and metabolome.114 MSI is a natural fit for this application and is just beginning to expose some major factors that affect the relationship between the transcriptome and metabolome.

Kawashima et al.115 examined the distributions of various saturated and unsaturated phospholipids in patients diagnosed with ductal carcinoma (DC). The group’s hypothesis centered around the observation that phosphatidylinositol (PI) remodeling leads to compromise of the PI 3-kinase pathway, leading to abnormal cell division. The group used MSI to confirm that polyunsaturated PIs were more often found in invasive DC and inversely correlated with noninvasive DC samples by profiling the cells using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Conversely, monounsaturated PIs and some saturated PIs were correlated with normal and noninvasive DC samples, respectively. The group then orthogonally analyzed cells containing a known polyunsaturated PI that correlates with invasive DC—PI(18:0_20:3)—with RNA-seq transcriptomic and proteomic data. Of the 1,112 genes examined, the cells containing a high amount of PI(18:0_20:3) were also upregulating programmed death ligand 1 gene expression. This strongly supports the hypothesis that PI signaling is remodeled and skewed based on the degrees of unsaturation and ultimately aids in tumor immune evasion.

Although bulk RNA-seq is effective at garnering critical gene expression, spatial transcriptomics allows for context of the acquired data, much like IHC. An example leveraging the power and sensitivity associated with spatial transcriptomics comes from analyzing renal papillary samples from patients with calcium oxalate stones. Current pathophysiological understanding hypothesizes that these stones form as a result of oxidative stress and inflammation, creating osteogenic-like changes to the papilla.116 Canela et al.117 obtained human kidney papillary biopsies from patients who form kidney stones during nephrolithotomy and examined them using single-nucleus RNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and MxIF. The group was able to correlate the exact location of their samples using detailed measurements and the atlases constructed by HuBMAP and the Kidney Precision Medicine Project to provide anatomical context to the data they obtained. The group found that through their transcriptomics experiments, the most notable differences occurred between papillary and corticomedullary principal cells (PCs). In papillary PCs, small-molecule and ionic transporters, such as urea transporter 2 (Slc14a2) and aquaporin 2 (Aqp2), were upregulated, whereas corticomedullary cells expressed higher levels of epithelial sodium channels (ENaC, Scnn1g, Scnn1b) and PTH receptor (Pth2r). Two distinct kinds of sodium bicarbonate transporters, Slc4a4 and Slc4a7, were also found to be upregulated in papillary and corticomedullary PCs, respectively. This finding further underlines the crucial role the kidney plays in acid-base homeostasis. RNA-seq determined that regions of injury (caused by stone formation) with an accumulation of undifferentiated epithelial cells expressing high levels of Prom1, Igfbp7, and Spp1 tended to associate with papillary PCs. Mmp7 and Mmp9 were also highly localized to areas of disease, whereas Mmp7 was upregulated around papillary PCs near collecting ducts and Mmp9 was upregulated around regions of mineralization. Together, the group concluded that Mmp7 and Mmp9 are biomarkers and can be surveyed across multiple methods of analysis to predict disease progression and localization of potential areas of calcium oxalate mineralization. Future work will likely incorporate orthogonal spatial metabolomics measurements that can provide insight into how these cells are interacting with one another and within these cellular neighborhoods.

As has been shown, transcriptomic analysis provides rich biological insight but is much more powerful when combined with its native spatial context. Similar to IHC, spatial transcriptomics has been used in an all-in-one multiomics approach to profile cells but has limited examples of being used with MSI. This mainly revolves around the fact that strands of nucleic acids have large molecular masses, are not readily ionizable, and fragment easily under ionization conditions. To circumvent this limitation, unique mass tags are conjugated to complementary oligonucleotides that can then be ionized and detected during MSI. Le et al.118 describe a multiomics approach using in situ hybridization and IHC to detect lipids, small metabolites, proteins, and messenger RNA in Alzheimer’s disease samples. The group used time-of-flight SIMS MSI to examine the distribution of the biomolecules at a subcellular resolution. By using a mass tagging approach, the group was able to visualize metabolites, lipids, peptides, proteins, and messenger RNA. This multiomics approach allows for a much larger portion of biochemical space to be probed and a better capability of doing pathway analysis and analyzing expression levels directly from the same tissue. The integration of spatial transcriptomics with MALDI MSI holds potential to be impactful in understanding mechanisms of disease. The combination of transcriptomics and metabolomics has a much broader impact on understanding the context of gene expression and regulation than either of the techniques alone could produce.

MULTIMODAL MSI IN APPLIED PHARMACOLOGY AND MEDICAL IMAGING

MALDI MSI has identified biomarkers and metabolic signatures surrounding cancer diagnosis and prognosis.119,120 Previous strategies have used IHC to diagnosis disease severity but are incomplete, neglecting glycosylated products, phospholipids, and other key metabolites, which are detectable by MSI. MSI is capable of detecting drugs and their secondary metabolites de novo but can also be used to measure their penetration and bioavailability.121 The kidney is often responsible for filtering pharmaceuticals from the bloodstream and therefore serves as an interesting subject for pharmacologic and toxicologic examination. In a study done by Swales et al.,122 MSI was performed on a series of murine kidneys to examine the distribution of terfenadine, olanzapine, moxifloxacin, and erlotinib after cassette dose (simultaneous administration of all drugs) administration. The group dosed the mice and serum samples were collected at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h before euthanasia. The brain, kidneys, liver, and lungs were then analyzed with MALDI MSI, DESI MSI, or liquid extraction surface analysis-tandem mass spectrometry. Drug abundance decreased across 2 and 6 h time points, indicating that the kidney clears drugs within several hours. The intensities of erlotinib and terfenadine were widely spread across the cortex and medulla, whereas moxifloxacin and olanzapine were detected only within the medulla. These kinds of insights provide not only pharmacokinetic data but also insight into the mechanism of action and off-target biological effects.

IHC and other antibody-related assays are critical for diagnosing disease at the molecular level (Figure 3). Although these assays are excellent at identifying key biomarkers involved in specific diseases, they are often unable to describe drug/treatment interactions within an organ. Machálková et al.93 combined MALDI MSI and IF to describe drug penetration and diffusion of perifosine, an Akt pathway inhibitor, throughout an HT-29 colorectal cancer spheroid model. Proliferation (Ki-67), apoptosis (cleaved caspase 8), and metastasis (SNAIL/SLUG) were monitored using IF (Figure 3). The resulting registered images were segmented into layers to describe the distance from edge to center within the spheroid. Administering perifosine for 24 h yielded intense accumulation around and 160 μm within the spheroid as well as significant decreases in proliferation and metastasis and increases in apoptosis. This indicates not only successful demonstration of a drug diffusion model but also cell-cell biochemical communication.

MSI has also been used in conjunction with noninvasive techniques such as computed tomography (CT) utilizing the high ionization efficiencies of radiological dyes for radiometabolomics. Combining CT and MSI allows physicians and surgeons to reduce biopsy invasiveness and avoid unnecessary surgery. The combination of the two datasets then allows for accurate and precise determination to conclude whether the tumor is malignant, benign, or a mixture of the two. This approach was used by Klontzas et al.123 to analyze 33 different human patient samples using both CT and MALDI MSI, in which the authors determined that malignant tumors showed an upregulation in nicotinate and nicotinamide, which are associated with purine metabolism and N-methyltransferase activity within the PI3K/Akt/SP1/MMP2 pathway. In the context of renal cell carcinoma, this pathway is thought to increase the amount of acetyl coenzyme A available for lipid synthesis to promote cell proliferation, a hallmark of cancer.124 The serine-glycine-threonine synthesis pathway was also upregulated, feeding the pentose phosphate pathway, another primary metabolic pathway upregulated in renal cell carcinoma.125 The group proposed this multimodal approach to add molecular imformation to established biomarkers for quickly determining avenues for intervention.

DATA INTERPRETATION FROM MULTIMODAL IMAGING EXPERIMENTS

Multimodal imaging experiments excel at providing complementary data to enhance the amount of insight into a given biological sample. With each analytical technique bringing a unique dataset and data type, it is critical to understand data dependencies and the implications each visualization can have on a given experiment. Raw MSI data are typically gigabytes to terabytes of data that are then reduced to a smaller subset of biologically relevant data points. Raw data are preprocessed, which typically involves baseline subtraction, peak alignment, post-calibration, de-isotoping, and peak picking. Generally, peaks within a spectrum are fit to Gaussian and other functions or, more recently, to artificial neural networks to learn feature shapes given a predictable isotopic distribution.126–128 Once a list of m/z values is made, multivariate supervised or unsupervised statistical tests, such as principal component analysis (PCA), non-negative matrix factorization, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding, uniform manifold approximation and projection, or K-means clustering, can be used to analyze variance in the data across space.129 The interpretation of these tests can be used across sample types to analyze biological change in response to stimuli/disease and is often benefited by orthogonal information (e.g., cell type, disease state).

Broadly, supervised and unsupervised methods exist for data analysis. K-means clustering is an example of an unsupervised clustering algorithm that is helpful for segmenting histologic structures (e.g., tumor versus nontumor) and grouping data based on underlying features.130 K-means clustering analyzes the distribution of m/z values by assigning each pixel to a predefined number of classes using the squared Euclidean distance between the spectra to differentiate pixels that are alike from pixels that are different.131 The different clusters can then be assigned a correlation coefficient and grouped to assign a spatial boundary along pixels that belong to the same correlated group.132 Other types of clustering comprise hierarchical,133 density,134 and Jaccard-Louvain,135 among other methods,136,137 each of which represents a balance between local/global variance and amount of data. Linear and nonlinear regression models are examples of supervised analysis, in which two (or more) classifiers are used to define datasets (e.g., diseased and nondiseased) for parsing which parts of the data (often m/z value) enable separation between the two groups.138–140

Another common processing step is data reduction. PCA is performed to reduce the dataset dimensionality while preserving statistical variability.141 PCA generates components that arise from linear combination of the variables (e.g., m/z values) that contain the maximum amount of variance within the dataset.142 This process is then repeated to linearize another combination of the variables to maximize the variance orthogonally from the first component.143 This process is repeated until the number of variables included in the dataset is described. In the case that variables are uncorrelated, PCA will fail to synthesize the appropriate relationship.142 At the end of the algorithmic calculations, clusters are plotted on an axis to depict the degree of variability between the linearized components. This technique is beneficial to multimodal approaches because it examines the entire dataset to provide a concise and unbiased analysis of the collected data. After PCA, the data can then be visualized using a variety of approaches, including t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding and uniform manifold approximation and projection, which enable high-density data to be visualized on a single graph, with data points (e.g., pixels) being grouped based on their underlying similarity.128 Clustering methods, including those discussed earlier, can be performed on these plots.

Alternatively, image alignment and fusion are methods used in multimodal imaging that allow for better extrapolation of biological meaning from generated images.54,144,145 First, when dealing with images coming from different analytical instruments, accurate image alignment is crucial for interpretation of cellular and sub-cellular features (Figure 4).146,94 This is done through multiple means, including autofluorescence,147 hematoxylin and eosin staining,148 and computational alignment through the use of fiducial markers.149 Data fusion, by contrast, integrates multiple imaging data types to create a single image. This is a common goal when relating clinically used imaging techniques such as CT or MRI.150 More commonly, data fusion is aimed at integrating microscopy data with MSI data to enhance MSI spatial resolution.151,152 Data fusion can simplify interpretation tremendously and allow for more impactful visualization of key biological data.

Machine learning (ML), artificial intelligence (AI), and neural networks are becoming invaluable research tools.153–155 ML- and AI-interpreted MSI and multimodal imaging methods will stand at the forefront of personalized medicine. These tools require immense amounts of data to learn and train on, which these bioanalytical platforms can easily provide. In the atlas consortia described earlier, the data acquired across tens to hundreds of diverse individuals across sex, race, body mass index, and other physical categorizations all contribute to the variability and classifications ML and AI can take into consideration. In the case of HuBMAP, the HubMAP Integration, Visualization and Engagement collaboration manages collected data across the consortium, in addition to developing new tools to visualize and model data.156 HubMAP Integration, Visualization and Engagement has created different web-based applications that allow researchers the opportunity to upload and visualize data that can then be added to a data bank and later incorporated into an atlas according to tissue type and, if applicable, disease state. These databases, in addition to others, will serve as the foundation for ML and AI learning models to train and expand their abilities to one day assist in diagnosis.

CONCLUSIONS, CHALLENGES, AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS OF MULTIMODAL MSI

Multimodal MSI is an incredibly powerful and promising field; it stands to revolutionize how we understand biological systems. It enables the scientific community to think of a system from multiple vantage points, not only the potential of a cell but also downstream metabolomics. Although powerful, there are still significant challenges associated with spatial multiomics. First, the sheer size of the individual datasets (often in terabytes) requires specialized equipment. Second, there is discordance between different molecular datasets (i.e., genes can be upregulated but downstream metabolism remains unchanged or vice versa). Third, there are differences in spatial resolution (e.g., fluorescence microscopy has an order of magnitude higher spatial resolution than MALDI MSI). Fourth, there are issues with reproducibility between laboratories and most published scienctfic work is irreproducible.157,158 Fifth, expensive equipment is regularly required (and routinely outdated). Sixth, expertise is required in each modality. These challenges, although large, point to the expansiveness and need of the community to work together to address them. Until thoroughly addressed, spatially resolved multiomics measurements will be far from routinely performed despite their importance for translational medicine. As such, we encourage the use of open science and are consistently publishing and updating protocols on open-source venues such as protocols.io. Research focused on studying the reproducibility and applicability of these methods to each system is also desperately needed. Additionally, we believe that the future of spatial multiomics will consist of taking each of these disparate technologies and integrating them into a single system, facilitating their registration and integration. To date, this is already being accomplished by the spatial transcriptomics companies, 10x Genomics and NanoString, which are building proteomic capabilities into their spatial transcriptomics measurements, and spatial proteomics companies, such as Akoya, which are integrating spatial transcriptomics into their spatial proteomics measurements. Aside from AmberGen, which is integrating spatial proteomics with metabolomics, metabolomics measurements are left out of these platforms. This is likely a result of difficulties tagging small molecules and the limited technologies that are sensitive to them. As such, spatial experiments that integrate all three of these molecular classes are still somewhat distant and a major focus of future endeavors by our lab and others. Although these challenges appear daunting, they are akin to other major scientific advances, including ventures such as sequencing the entire human genome, which can now be performed for tens of dollars. We look ahead positively to the future of the emerging field of spatial multiomics and its use in atlasing efforts.
