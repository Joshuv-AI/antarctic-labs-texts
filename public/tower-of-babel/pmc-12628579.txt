Linalool as a potential agent against Toxoplasma gondii: an in vitro study
Authors: Kimia Goudarzi, Keivan Sahebi, Ehsan Zandi, Qasem Asgari, Fatemeh Askari, Aref Teimouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveToxoplasmosis, caused by Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii), is a common zoonotic disease worldwide. Novel therapeutic options are required due to the limited effectiveness and side effects of existing treatments. This study aims to evaluate the in vitro antiparasitic activity of linalool at various concentrations against tachyzoites of the T. gondii RH strain, in comparison with a positive control (saponin 0.2%) and a negative control (no treatment), using flow cytometric analysis.ResultsThe flow cytometry results revealed a dose-dependent anti-Toxoplasma activity for linalool, with 18.37%, 36.77%, 38.75%, 47.57%, and 50.63% mortality rates at the concentrations of 37.5, 75, 150, 300, and 600 µg/mL, respectively. The mortality rate at the concentration of 1200 µg/mL (74.44%) was slightly higher than that of the positive control group (67.87%). Our findings provide primary evidence for the extracellular potential of linalool against T. gondii tachyzoites. This study provides a rationale for further research to evaluate linalool’s intracellular and in vivo efficacy and safety.

Background

Toxoplasmosis, a widespread zoonotic opportunistic parasitic illness, is caused by the intracellular parasite Toxoplasma gondii [1]. The seroprevalence of human toxoplasmosis is highly dependent on geographic patterns and varies significantly across the world [2, 3], ranging from 16.4% in Asia to 61.4% in Africa [4]. The parasite can infect humans through the consumption of raw or undercooked meat, or contact with contaminated water or soil. In addition, vertical transmission from mother to fetus has also been reported [5]. Toxoplasmosis in healthy individuals is often asymptomatic; however, it can progress to life-threatening conditions in immunocompromised patients, such as patients with HIV [6], cancer, or organ transplant recipients [2]. In addition, congenital toxoplasmosis can lead to various complications, including abortion, premature birth, hydrocephalus, microcephaly, chorioretinitis, and anemia in newborns [7].

Due to the lack of an effective vaccine, controlling toxoplasmosis remains a significant challenge. Current treatments for toxoplasmosis include a combination of pyrimethamine and sulfonamides. Clindamycin, atovaquone, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and azithromycin are alternative options [8, 9]. Several studies have demonstrated that these drugs, while quite effective, are associated with multiple side effects, such as bone marrow suppression, liver and kidney damage, hypersensitivity reactions, and teratogenic effects [5, 10]. Moreover, the emergence of drug-resistant strains has complicated this situation [11]. Consequently, developing new agents with less toxicity and improved efficiency for toxoplasmosis treatment is of interest to researchers [12].

Recently, natural medicine has gained considerable attention for discovering and designing novel antimicrobial agents [13, 14]. Linalool is an aromatic monoterpene alcohol widely found in the essential oils (EOs) of several plants, such as Coriandrum sativum L., Lavandula angustifolia, Myrtus communis, and Pelargonium asperum [15, 16]. It is commonly used in cosmetics, perfumes, household products, pharmaceuticals, and the food industry. It has been shown that linalool has several pharmacological properties, including antimicrobial activity, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cholesterol, anti-cancer, and anxiolytic effects [17]. In particular, linalool was shown to be effective against various fungi, such as Candida albicans, Aspergillus flavus, Microsporum canis, and Microsporum gypseum [18–20]. Several studies have demonstrated anti-Toxoplasma activity for EOs rich in linalool [15, 21, 22]. Additionally, nanoparticles co-loaded with linalool and zinc oxide were shown to significantly reduce the viability of T. gondii tachyzoites [12]. Nonetheless, these formulations usually contain a combination of bioactive compounds, making it difficult to attribute a definitive anti-T. gondii effect specifically to linalool. Thus, the present study aims to uncover the utility of linalool as a novel antimicrobial agent against T. gondii RH strain tachyzoites.

Materials and methods

Materials

Linalool (CAS No.: 78-70-6, Sigma-Aldrich; Purity: ≥97%), dimethylsulphoxide (DMSO), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.

Animals

Five six-week-old female BALB/c mice weighing 20–25 g were purchased from the Laboratory Animals Breeding Center at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, located in Shiraz, Iran. Animals were kept under standard laboratory conditions with controlled temperatures of 22 ± 2 °C, a 12-h light–dark cycle, and ad libitum food and water supplies. The Animal Care Committee at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences has approved the study protocol (ethical code: IR.SUMS.AEC.1402.039). The research was conducted in accordance with all institutional and international guidelines concerning the care and use of animals. Additionally, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the United States National Institutes was properly followed.

Parasite preparation

Tachyzoites of the T. gondii RH strain were obtained by serial intraperitoneal (IP) passaging in BALB/c mice. The animals were physically restrained to fully restrict their movements for the experimental infection. Once immobilized, they received an IP inoculation in the lower left abdomen with about 200 µL of a suspension containing 5 × 106 tachyzoites/mL of T. gondii RH strain. After 96 h, the animals were euthanized via intramuscular injection of a combination comprising 300 mg/kg of 10% ketamine hydrochloride and 30 mg/kg of 2% xylazine hydrochloride. Death was verified by the absence of respiratory movements and the lack of a heartbeat, confirmed through auscultation with a stethoscope. Immediately afterwards, the peritoneal exudates were collected and centrifuged at 100 × g for 10 min at 24 ± 1 °C. The supernatant was then collected and centrifuged again at 2,000 × g for 10 min. The pellet, which contained tachyzoites, was washed three times with PBS (pH = 7.4) and counted using a hemocytometer slide. A concentration of 2 × 105 tachyzoites/mL of sterile PBS was chosen for the experiments.

In vitro evaluation of the anti-Toxoplasma effect of linalool

Linalool was serially diluted in 0.06% DMSO solution in PBS, with the six concentrations of 1200, 600, 300, 150, 75, and 37.5 µg/mL in Eppendorf tubes 1–6. The positive control (PC) and negative control (NC) groups (tubes 7 and 8) consisted of saponin 0.2% diluted in 0.06% DMSO and 0.06% DMSO solution in PBS, respectively. Using a hemocytometer slide, a 500 µl solution of parasites at a concentration of 2 × 105 tachyzoites/mL PBS was quantified and added to each Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, a 500 µl solution of different concentrations of linalool was added to each tube (except the NC tube) and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. Next, 50 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) was added to all tubes and incubated for another 30 min at 4 °C in the dark.

Finally, tubes were assessed with the flow cytometry method (BD FACSCalibur Flow-Cytometer, BD Biosciences, USA) to evaluate the antiparasitic effects of linalool against isolated tachyzoites of T. gondii RH strain. Intact tachyzoites (without PI or any antiparasitic agent) were used to set up the flow cytometer, and gating was based on the parasites’ specific size and distribution range. The device was configured to the PI color setting, and each tube was counted for approximately 10 thousand cells. The rate of parasite death was conclusively determined by assessing the dye permeability and fluorescence of PI.

Results

Using the flow cytometry method, T. gondii tachyzoites exposed to different treatments exhibited varying mortality rates (Fig. 1). The NC group showed a mortality rate of 11.25%, while the PC group demonstrated a mortality rate of 67.87%. The mortality rates of T. gondii tachyzoites at concentrations of 37.5, 75, 150, 300, 600, and 1200 µg/mL linalool were determined at 18.37%, 36.77%, 38.75%, 47.57%, 50.63%, and 74.44%, respectively. As depicted in Fig. 2, the anti-Toxoplasma effect of linalool was dose-dependent. Notably, linalool at 1200 µg/mL achieved a higher mortality rate (74.44%) than the positive control (67.87%).

Discussion

Natural medicine has been of interest to researchers for the development of novel therapeutic options against parasitic infections [23–26]. Linalool, widely found in many plants’ EOs, has promising antiparasitic activity [27–29]. The present study assessed the in vitro antiparasitic activity of linalool against tachyzoites of the T. gondii RH strain. We demonstrated a promising effect of linalool on T. gondii tachyzoites with an approximately 75% mortality rate at a concentration of 1200 µg/mL. This mortality rate exceeded the results obtained from the positive control group. Given the multiple side effects and limited efficacy associated with current treatments [10, 11], this study demonstrates that linalool has potential as a candidate for further research in the treatment of T. gondii.

Linalool has been widely used for its antiparasitic, fungicidal, insecticidal, bactericidal, and antiviral properties. In a study by Youssefi et al., linalool (up to 10 µg/mL) was not active against Leishmania infantum promastigotes cultured in an RPMI-1640 medium [30]. Similarly, in a study by Dutra et al. [28], while linalool was not active against promastigotes of L. infantum chagasi, it demonstrated an LD50 of 550 µg/mL against axenic amastigotes. Moreover, linalool reduced 60% and 70% of intracellular parasites in mouse peritoneal macrophages at concentrations of 250 and 350 µg/mL, respectively [28]. Additionally, linalool significantly reduced Trypanosoma cruzi trypomastigotes, with an inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 306 ng/mL. This effect was approximately 52 times more potent than benznidazole [29].

The in vitro and in vivo effects of linalool-zinc oxide nanoparticles (Lin‐ZNP) against toxoplasmosis and cystic echinococcosis were investigated [12, 27]. Lin-ZNP significantly decreased the viability of T. gondii tachyzoites and the size and number of tissue cysts in mice. Mechanistically, linalool led to a notable increase in the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and a reduction in the expression of T. gondii pathogenicity-related genes [12]. In another study, in a dose- and time-dependent manner, Lin-ZNP exhibited IC50 values at 60 min of 142.5 ± 2.13 and 258.9 ± 2.54 μm against Echinococcus granulosus protoscoleces in vitro and ex vivo, respectively [27]. In vivo experiments also confirmed a significant reduction in the number, diameter, and weight of the hydatid cysts. Notably, the lack of a treatment group receiving only linalool makes it difficult to conclude the actual anti-Toxoplasma and protoscolicidal activity of linalool and its potential synergism with Zn.

Although studies evaluating the activity of pure linalool against T. gondii are lacking, there are multiple studies on EOs with different contents of linalool. For instance, in a study by Yao et al. [15], the effect of Lavandula angustifolia EO was assessed against T. gondii. Plaque assay demonstrated significant inhibition of T. gondii growth compared to the untreated group. Moreover, the EO exhibited acceptable cytotoxicity with a cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of 4.48 mg/mL on human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cells. Tachyzoites exposed to the EO of L. angustifolia were deformed and shrunken in scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS), it has been shown that linalool (21.91%) and linalyl acetate (14.54%) are the two main compounds of L. angustifolia EO [31]. In another study by Shaapan et al. [22], Myrtus communis EO was evaluated for its effects on chronic toxoplasmosis in mice. Using GC/MS analysis, the primary chemical components of this EO were linalool, α-pinene, and 1,8-cineole. Treatment with M. communis EO led to a dose-dependent reduction in the number and size of T. gondii tissue cysts in experimental groups compared to the control group. Another EO extracted from Pelargonium X. asperum caused significant growth inhibition of T. gondii with a CC50 of 4.77 mg/mL [21]. Tachyzoites treated with this EO shrank with a reduction in their invasion potential.

The exact antimicrobial mechanisms of linalool are not fully understood. It has been shown that in L. infantum chagasi, linalool enhanced the activities of the protein kinases A and C (PKA and PKC) and decreased oxygen consumption [28]. Albalawi et al. demonstrated that Lin-ZNP could increase the gene expression of caspase-3 in E. granulosus protoscoleces; however, comparison groups treated with linalool or Zn alone were not included [27]. Moreover, the researchers found that Lin-ZNP caused a significant decrease in the interleukin-4 (IL-4) and IL-10, malondialdehyde (an oxidative stress marker), and nitric oxide (NO) levels, as well as upregulation of glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase gene expression in infected mice. Although studies on parasites are scarce, some studies on fungal species have revealed other potential mechanisms of action for linalool [19, 20]. In an in vitro study, Silva et al. observed that linalool compromised the cell membrane and caused intracellular material leakage in M. canis and M. gypseum [19]. Interestingly, clinical isolates of C. albicans resistant to fluconazole were sensitive to linalool at a dose of 256 µg/mL [20]. The study showed that linalool may compromise the integrity of the cell wall and plasma membrane in C. albicans. Moreover, molecular docking studies demonstrated that linalool might interact with three important enzymes in C. albicans, including 1,3-β-glucan synthase, lanosterol 14α-demethylase, and delta 14-sterol reductase, which are essential for cell wall integrity.

Chemotherapeutic agents targeting parasites must be highly selective. Sulfadiazine (as a current treatment of toxoplasmosis) has shown cytotoxic effect on some human cell lines, such as Huh-7 [32], or certain cancer lines (HepG2, and MCF7, IC₅₀ = 245.69 ± 4.1 and 215.68 ± 3.8 µM, respectively) [33]. By contrast, normal THLE2 hepatocytes were unaffected (IC₅₀ = 4159 ± 90.5 µM). It has been reported that the drug probably exerts its cytotoxic effect by inducing oxidative stress and altering membrane integrity [32, 33]. Linalool’s selective toxicity was investigated in mouse peritoneal macrophages, demonstrating no alteration in macrophage viability below 350 µg/mL and only a slight decrease of approximately 25% at 600 µg/mL. Macrophages tolerated concentrations up to 2.4 times higher than those that harmed internalized L. infantum [28]. Another study by Prashar [34], the cytotoxic effects of linalool were investigated on human skin endothelial cells and fibroblasts (HMEC-1, HNDF and 153BR). Linalool showed good cell viability (approximately 80%) in concentrations ≤ 0.44% (v/v), with 50% growth inhibitions of 0.060–0.069% (v/v) [34].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The in vitro design of this study uncovered promising results regarding the potential of linalool as a novel anti-Toxoplasma agent; however, in vitro studies using an appropriate intracellular model and in vivo experiments on acute and chronic toxoplasmosis are required. Furthermore, this study did not investigate the cytotoxicity of linalool against human cell lines. Future research should validate the selectivity and toxicity of this novel agent both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, molecular and mechanistic studies were not employed in the present work. Future studies should explore the molecular mechanisms of linalool against T. gondii to optimize its pharmacological properties.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrated that linalool has potent in vitro extracellular activity against T. gondii tachyzoites. Notably, the study revealed that at a concentration of 1200 µg/mL, linalool reached a mortality rate even higher than the positive control group (74.44% vs. 67.87%). These findings suggest that linalool may hold promise as a candidate for further development, either alone or in combination with other agents. Although the present study investigated extracellular effects of linalool against T. gondii tachyzoites, future studies should aim to assess its intracellular and in vivo efficacy and safety, as well as its molecular targets within both the parasite and the host.
