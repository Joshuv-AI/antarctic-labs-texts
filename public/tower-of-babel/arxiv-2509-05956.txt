Abstract
We formulate the Knapsack Contracts problem—a strategic version of the classic Stochastic Knapsack problem, which builds upon the inherent randomness shared by stochastic optimization and contract design . In this problem, the principal incentivizes agents to perform jobs with stochastic processing times, the realization of which depends on the agents’ efforts. Algorithmically, we show that Knapsack Contracts can be viewed as Stochastic Knapsack with costs and multi-choice , features that introduce significant new challenges. We identify a crucial and economically meaningful parameter—the Return on Investment (ROI) value. We show that the Inverse of ROI (or IOR for short) precisely characterizes the extent to which the approximation guarantees for Stochastic Knapsack extend to its strategic counterpart. For IOR of α \alpha , we develop an algorithm that finds an O ⁡ ( α ) O(\alpha) -approximation policy that does not rely on adaptivity. We establish matching Ω ⁡ ( α ) \Omega(\alpha) lower bounds, both on the adaptivity gap, and on what can be achieved without full distributional knowledge of the processing times. Taken together, our results show that IOR is fundamental to understanding the complexity and approximability of Knapsack Contracts, and bounding it is both necessary and sufficient for achieving non-trivial approximation guarantees. Our results highlight the computational challenges arising when stochasticity in optimization problems is controlled by strategic effort.

Introduction

The study of how incentives reshape classic algorithmic problems has been at the heart of algorithmic game theory research since its inception. In their seminal work,
Nisan and Ronen 2001
famously conjectured that adding incentives for
truthfulness
to the optimization problem of scheduling would make the problem extremely hard to approximate. Two decades later, intensive research has culminated in a proof of this conjecture
[
9
]
.
Many additional works explore the intersection of algorithm design with truthfulness constraints
[
27
,
25
,
10
,
6
,
8
,
22
,
23
]
.

Over recent years, the algorithmic game theory community has begun to explore incentive constraints beyond truthfulness. A recent focus is on an agent’s
earnestness
in exerting effort to complete a task, where the task is delegated to the agent by a principal. The main economic tool for incentivizing earnest effort is performance-based payment schemes, a.k.a.
contracts
.

Contracts are designed to create incentives that counteract
moral hazard
: When an agent exerts effort to carry out a job on behalf of a principal, this leads to a
stochastic
outcome that only partially reflects the agent’s effort. This stochasticity opens the possibility for the agent to claim they worked hard despite exerting a low level of effort, by blaming poor outcomes on “bad luck”. This phenomenon is known as moral hazard. A contract uses outcome-based payments and distributional knowledge to incentivize earnest effort, thus mitigating moral hazard.

The goal of this work is to initiate the study of the intersection of algorithm design with contractual incentives for earnest effort.

1.1
Problem Formulation

To achieve our goal, we seek an algorithmic optimization problem with a natural connection to contractual incentives—similar to the connection between scheduling and truthfulness
[
24
]
.
Our starting point is the observation that the field of contract design has a natural affinity to
stochastic
optimization, due to the nondeterminism inherent to both.
Building upon this connection, we formulate a model of Knapsack Contracts, in which jobs that need to fit into a given time frame are delegated to agents. In our model, the agents have costs for carrying out the jobs, and the completion times (a.k.a. job lengths or
sizes
) are a stochastic outcome of the level of effort. The latter makes our model a generalization of Stochastic Knapsack
[
11
]
. We show that the agents’ costs lead to incentive constraints that
make the problem significantly harder than classic Stochastic Knapsack.
Since Stochastic Knapsack has a central place in stochastic optimization
[
3
,
19
,
20
,
18
,
28
,
5
,
2
,
21
]
, we view its study through the prism of contract design as a potential gateway to deeper exploration of the intersection between the two areas.

Example 1
.

A PI (principal) wishes to run a set of experiments on the cloud before the deadline of a top AI conference. Each experimental task has a fixed value for the paper, which does not depend on how fast the cloud providers (agents) finish running the experiments. The PI does not benefit from experiments that finish running after the submission deadline. Despite this, she still needs to pay the providers for running them.
The PI must spend substantial funds from her research grant to incentivize the agents to use powerful but costly resources when processing the experiments, resulting in shorter running times.
This leads to an interesting tension between the PI’s benefit from an experiment, and how much they are willing to pay to incentivize an agent to run it quickly.
The problem of optimizing the experiments thus has a mixed-sign objective.

Generalizing
Example

1
, we consider a single principal and multiple agents with jobs that must be executed sequentially. Each agent
i
is associated with a fixed known value
v_{i}
to the principal for completing their job before the deadline expires. Moreover, each agent can choose one of
m
costly actions, and the chosen action
j\in[m]
determines a distribution
F_{i}^{j}
over the possible completion times of the job. The principal cannot directly observe the action taken by an agent, and can only inspect the completion times.
To incentivize agent
i
, the principal designs a contract
t_{i}
that maps the completion time
s_{i}
to a monetary transfer.
Each agent
i
wants to maximize their own utility: the transfer received minus the cost
c_{i}^{j}
exerted by them when taking action
j
. The principal wants to maximize the profit, which is the sum of values over all jobs completed before the deadline,
minus
the sum of transfers made to the agents (i.e., a mixed-sign objective). See
Section

2
for a formal problem description.

Relation to vanilla contract design.

Usually in contract settings,
the value created for the principal by the agent’s effort is stochastic.
Our model captures settings in which the principal’s value for the job delegated to the agent is fixed, but the time it takes the agent to complete the job is a random variable, and if the job doesn’t finish on time then the resulting value is zero.
This seemingly simple twist on the standard principal-agent model leads to new challenges.

Relation to
Stochastic Knapsack.

In the standard Stochastic Knapsack problem, there are
n
jobs to execute, each with a fixed value but stochastic size. A policy-maker executes jobs sequentially, gaining the value of any job that terminates before the deadline. This process ends once a job terminates after the deadline. The optimal policy for this problem can be highly complex, adaptive, and intractable.
Yet,
Dean et al. 2008
show that there exists a simple non-adaptive policy that approximates, up to a constant factor, the reward of the optimal adaptive policy.

In contrast, in our model, even if a job ends after the deadline, the principal must pay the agent according to the contract, for processing it.
As we show in Section
2.2
, our model corresponds to a version of Stochastic Knapsack
with job costs and multi-choice
,
and we show this makes a substantial difference relative to
[
11
]
. A first indication of the difference arises from the linear programming approach of
[
11
]
, which they use to establish their main result. In their setting, the integrality gap of the LP is bounded; however, in
Appendix

A
we demonstrate that the LP approach can grossly overshoot the true optimum in our setting.
In fact, as we shall see, in our model there is no constant-approximation non-adaptive policy;
any constant-approximation algorithm must adaptively choose the next job to execute. Intuitively, adaptivity is necessary to avoid the danger of incurring a very high cost, so high it overshadows any value the algorithm may obtain.

1.2
Our Results and Techniques

Return on Investment.

The example in
Appendix

A
is based on a “hard instance” of Stochastic Knapsack with job costs.
We show this instance (and in fact any other hard instance) has the following property: For some of the jobs, their cost almost “covers” their value. Thus, in order to incentivize such jobs, the principal has to pay the agent their cost, and therefore, they are left with a small return on investment (ROI). I.e., the ratio between the obtained profit and the transfer made is small.
Such jobs are intuitively problematic—if their cost must be reimbursed by the principal, but their value is not gained since they finish after the deadline, the loss can be substantial. We essentially show that these are the only problematic jobs.

In order to parameterize this, we inspect the Inverse of the ROI (or IOR) of a job (as the ROI becomes small, the IOR grows). We introduce an economically-meaningful parameter
\alpha
, which represents the worst IOR among all jobs—see
Definition

2.6
. Our main finding is that IOR is precisely the parameter that determines the loss in approximation due to moving from the classic Stochastic Knapsack problem, to a contract design problem subject to incentive constraints:

•

We show that the principal can efficiently and non-adaptively compute a set of contracts, which give an
O(\alpha)
-approximation to the profit of the (intractable and highly adaptive) optimal policy of the principal. See
Theorem

3.1
.

•

We show the existence of an instance such that any non-adaptive policy of the principal is bounded away by a factor of
\Omega(\alpha)
in terms of profit from the optimal adaptive policy. This establishes a tight
\Theta(\alpha)
adaptivity gap for the Knapsack Contracts problem. See
Theorem

4.1
.

•

We establish an information gap: Consider any policy (adaptive or not) that has limited information about the jobs’ size distributions in the form of finitely many moments, as well as access to the probability of knapsack overflow at every step. No such policy can guarantee an approximation factor better than
O(\alpha)
with respect to the profit of the optimal policy with full access to the size distributions. See
Theorem

5.2
.

We now describe our results in more detail as well as the techniques that enable them.

Positive results.

In
Section

3
, we provide an efficient algorithm that finds a non-adaptive set of contracts (see Section
2
for adaptivity notions), which give an
O(\alpha)
-approximation to the optimal profit the principal can obtain, where
\alpha
is the IOR parameter (
Theorem

3.1
). Our algorithm needs to choose in advance the order in which agents are to execute their jobs and which effort level to incentivize each agent. Each effort level induces a different expected transfer needed in order to incentivize it. As a first step, in
Lemma

2.1
, we reduce the Knapsack Contract problem to a variant of Stochastic Knapsack that we term the Multi-Choice Stochastic Knapsack Problem with Costs (
MSKC
). In this purely algorithmic problem, each job is assigned one of multiple combinations of cost and size distribution. When a policy attempts to execute a job, it has to pay the cost up front.

Our algorithm formulates an LP (see Eq. (
2
)) for the
MSKC
problem, where the weight of each job
i
and effort level
j
corresponds to the profit of executing job
i
at effort level
j
, and the size is the expected size of the job at effort level
j
. In
Dean et al. 2008
, an LP formulation of the problem gives an upper bound on the reward of the optimal adaptive policy, which is approximated by the reward of the best non-adaptive policy. In our case, the LP is still an upper bound on the optimal profit of an adaptive policy, but this upper bound can be a factor
\alpha
away (see
Appendix

A
for an example). We show that there exists a non-adaptive set of contracts that yields an
O(\alpha)
-approximation to the LP’s value.

The way in which we construct the set of contracts is the following. We first efficiently find a well-structured solution to the LP where each job is taken integrally at up to one effort level, except for at most a single job that can be taken as a convex combination of two effort levels. This is done by taking an arbitrary solution of the LP, and then shifting weights along the Pareto curves of jobs in order to iteratively move jobs to be taken integrally, while keeping the same solution value. Once we obtain the well-structured solution, we either use a standard bang-per-buck ordering of the jobs selected, or take a single job if its expected profit is larger than a predefined threshold. This threshold is set in a way that will (i) prevent a large loss for the bang-per-buck order in case the IOR parameter
\alpha
has a large value; and (ii) ensure a large profit when there’s a single job that gives such profit, but might have a low probability of being selected when jobs are executed in the bang-per-buck order.

Negative results.

In
Section

4
and
Section

5
we give further evidence for the role that IOR has in Knapsack Contracts. In
Section

4
we inspect the adaptivity gap, considering three types of policies:

1.

Fully adaptive policies
that can choose the next job to execute (if any) and at which effort level based on the entire history.

2.

Stopping-time adaptive policies
that chooses a fixed set of contracts (job/effort level pairs) up front at a fixed order, but stops once any future sequence of jobs has a negative expected profit. These types of policies avoid a large loss that the principal can incur due to the transfer component of the contract problem and are used in the algorithm of
Section

6
.

3.

Non-adaptive policies
that chooses a fixed set of contracts at a fixed order, and the process terminates either when the last job in the order has been executed, or when there’s a job that is finished after the deadline.

Dean et al. 2008
show that in the setting without costs, the reward of the optimal non-adaptive policy is within a factor 4 of the optimal adaptive policy. We show that when introducing costs, the ratio between these can be unbounded. Namely, for every IOR parameter
\alpha>1
, we show:

•

An instance where the ratio between the profit of stopping-time adaptive policies and non-adaptive policies is
\Omega(\alpha)
(
Section

4.1
).

•

An instance where the ratio between the profit of fully adaptive policies and stopping-time adaptive policies is
\Omega(\alpha^{1/3})
(
Section

4.2
).

Our instances are ones where each job has only a single effort level, demonstrating that the cost aspect by itself leads to a significant change relative to the setting without cost.

Information barrier.

In
Section

5
we inspect how much information is needed to get an
O(\alpha)
approximation in the purely algorithmic Stochastic Knapsack problem where there is an inherent cost for executing jobs (this corresponds to having a single effort level for each agent). In the purely algorithmic setting, our algorithm from
Section

3
only uses first moment information (the expectation) of jobs’ sizes, along with the probability each job’s size will exceed the deadline, and obtains an
O(\alpha)
approximation. We show that this information does not suffice if one wants to obtain a better approximation. Namely, for every finite
k
, there exists an instance where any (possibly adaptive) policy that can only access the
k
moments of the size distribution, along with the probability that each job will exceed the deadline, is bounded away by a factor of
\Omega(\alpha)
from the optimal policy (
Theorem

5.2
).

Bounded-size jobs.

In
Section

6
, we consider the case where the IOR parameter can be arbitrarily large, but each job’s probability to take more than
1-\delta
of the length of the time budget is small for
\delta\in(0,1)
. We give an efficient algorithm that finds a stopping-time adaptive set of contracts, which guarantees an
O(1/\delta)
approximation to the optimal profit of a fully-adaptive set of contracts.

In order to obtain this result, we first consider a variant of the problem where, when a job finishes after the deadline, the principal still obtains its reward, and then the process stops. For this variant, the algorithm from
Section

3
gives a constant approximation to the LP value. We then consider running this algorithm while imagining the deadline is
\delta
-fraction of the actual deadline. We show that in this case, having bounded-size jobs is equivalent to obtaining the reward of the first job that finishes after the deadline. Moreover, halving the deadline only causes a factor
\delta
loss in the objective. Note that (moderate) adaptivity is required for this variant as well, as non-adaptive policies lose a factor that grows with
\alpha
(see
Appendix

B
).

Ordered-Adaptive Contracts.

Motivated by real-world examples where jobs must be executed in a given order, in
Appendix

D
we study the model where the order is given, but for each job in the order, the principal can decide whether to execute it, and if so, at which effort level. In
Theorem

D.1
we give a resource augmentation-type result: given a time budget larger by a factor of
1+\epsilon
from the original time budget, one can compute an adaptive policy efficiently (in polynomial time in the number of jobs, effort levels, and
1/\epsilon
), which obtains a reward larger than the optimal adaptive policy for the original time budget. The policy, similar to
[
11
,
5
]
, defines a dynamic programming solution on carefully discretized rounded-down processing times. This result hints that our problem boils down to ordering the jobs; i.e., one may hope to find a nearly-optimal adaptive policy for our non-ordered model if one can efficiently find a near-optimal ordering of the jobs.

Related Work

The study of sequential contracts in
[
14
]
investigates the problem of incentivizing a single agent to perform multiple jobs in a sequential manner.
In contrast, our work considers a sequence of contracts in which the jobs are executed sequentially by different agents, with no interdependence between the jobs.
The works of
[
26
,
15
,
1
,
16
]
consider contract design subject to a limited monetary budget constraining the transfers from principal to agent.
In our work, we study a multi-agent setting under a time budget constraint on the agents’ actions, where time is limited and the principal must incentivize the agents to complete their jobs on time.

Classic contract theory.

Classic contract theory has a long-standing tradition in economics, focusing on incentive design under asymmetric information. A result by
Carroll 2015
shows that linear contracts are often robust across a range of environments, providing a foundation for the study of simpler contract forms. These classical models emphasize moral hazard, adverse selection, and trade-offs between risk and incentives.

Algorithmic contract theory.

In recent years, a series of studies has emerged that study contracts through the lens of the theory of computation, initiated by
[
12
,
4
]
. The authors of
[
12
]
raise concerns regarding the complexity and unintuitiveness of optimal contracts, while exploring the approximation ratios of simpler contract forms, such as linear contracts. The multi-agent setting was initially explored by
Babaioff et al. 2012
. A recent survey paper
[
13
]
provides an overview of developments in algorithmic contract theory.

2.2
Multi-Choice Stochastic Knapsack Problem With Costs

We define a generalization of Stochastic Knapsack, where items have multiple choices of assignment, and each is associated with a size distribution, and a cost. We show that our Knapsack Contracts problem reduces to this problem. This implies that our algorithmic results can be obtained in this setting, and the results will carry through to the contracts setting.

Definition 2.5

(Multi-Choice Stochastic Knapsack Problem With Costs)
.

An instance of the multi-choice stochastic knapsack problem with costs (or
MSKC
for short) consists of
n
items, where each item has
m
choices, each choice has a different cost and size distribution. That is
I=\{v_{i},\{p_{i}^{j},F_{i}^{j}\}_{j\in[m]}\}_{i\in[n]}.
An item
i
can be selected with a single choice
j
. In this case, the cost
p_{i}^{j}
must be incurred up front, and the item’s size is realized from distribution
F_{i}^{j}
. There is a budget of size
S
(
S=1
without loss of generality). A policy selects item-choice pairs sequentially, and terminates either when it decides not to select any item, or when the total size of selected items exceeds the budget. The profit of a policy is the total value of items selected that do not exceed the budget
minus
the total cost of the selected items, including the cost of an item that exceeds the budget, if one exists.

Adaptive, Stopping-time Adaptive and Non-adaptive policies for the
MSKC
problem are defined analogously to the way they are defined for the Knapsack Contracts problem.
The following lemma (Proof in
Appendix

) shows that in order to find an approximately optimal policy for the Knapsack Contracts problem, it suffices to find an approximately optimal policy for the
MSKC

Lemma 2.1

(Reduction to Multi-Choice Stochastic Knapsack with Costs)
.

Given an efficient algorithm that finds a policy that
c
-approximates the optimal policy
MSKC
problem, one can efficiently find a policy that
c
-approximates the optimal policy for the knapsack contracts problem.

Inverse of Return on Investment Parameter

.

We define

\mathrm{ROI}(i,j)\ =\ \frac{v_{i}\cdot\Pr_{s_{i}^{j}\sim F_{i}^{j}}[\,s_{i}^{j}\leq 1\,]\;-\;\tilde{t}_{i}^{j}}{\tilde{t}_{i}^{j}},

and

\alpha\;=\;\max_{i,j\in\mathsf{I}_{i}}\frac{1}{\mathrm{ROI}(i,j)}\,,

where
\mathsf{I}_{i}
is the set of implementable actions for job
i
. We sometimes refer to
\alpha
as the Inverse of ROI (or IOR) parameter.

Intuitively, a large value of
\alpha
signifies the presence of at least one job where for some effort level, the transfer required to incentivize it is much larger than the net profit from successfully executing the job before the deadline. We show a
\Theta(\alpha)
adaptivity gap and an
\Omega(\alpha)
information gap (
Section

5
), suggesting that
\alpha
captures precisely the “hardness” aspect for our problem.

Analogously, we define the IOR parameter for the
MSKC
problem as in
Definition

2.6
, but using
p_{i}^{j}
instead of
\tilde{t}_{i}^{j}
. Given a Knapsack Contracts instance with an
\alpha
IOR parameter, applying the reduction in
Lemma

2.1
yields an
MSKC
instance with the same
\alpha
as the analogous IOR parameter. This implies that any approximation as a function of
\alpha
a policy in the
MSKC
instance would obtain will imply the same approximation as a function of
\alpha
in the Stochastic Knapsack instance.

Algorithm 1

SKC (Stochastic Knapsack with Costs)

An overview of the algorithm.

We begin by solving
\Phi(1)
to obtain an optimal solution
x
with the properties established in
Lemma

3.2
: each item
i
has one choice
j
such that
x_{ij}=1
, except for possibly one item
i^{*}
with two choices
j,j^{\prime}
such that
x_{i^{*}j}+x_{i^{*}j^{\prime}}=1
. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of item-choice pairs selected by the LP, and let
r=|\mathcal{C}|
. If there exists an item with two positive choices, we remove these two choices and replace them with a single virtual choice; whenever the algorithm attempts to insert this virtual item, it randomly selects between the two original choices with probabilities proportional to their values in
x
. Since each item now has a single (possibly virtual) choice, we refer to each item-choice pair
(i,j)
by the item
i
. Note that from the properties of our solution
x
, it holds that
\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}}=\Phi(1)
and
\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\mu_{k}}\leq 1
. We then define a threshold
\mathcal{T}(\alpha)
according to which we decide whether to insert the item with the maximum profit
w_{\text{max}}
. If the threshold is not met, we insert the items in decreasing order of their density
\frac{w_{i}}{\mu_{i}}
, until the knapsack is full or no more items remain in the candidate set
\mathcal{C}
. Our threshold determines whether or not following the greedy ordering is worth the risk of overflow compared to the alternative of inserting the best item. The bigger
w_{\text{max}},\alpha
are, the more likely we are to insert the best item.

Note that this algorithm can be easily de-randomized by choosing the choice among the two choices that maximizes the expected gain.

Claim 3.8
.

\mathbb{E}[\textbf{SKC}]\geq\mathcal{T}=\frac{\Phi(1)}{4(1+\alpha)}

Proof.

First, if
w_{\max}>\mathcal{T}
, the algorithm attempts only item
(i_{\max},j_{\max})
, gaining an expected net reward of
w_{\max}
. Since
w_{\max}>\mathcal{T}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\textbf{SKC}]=w_{\max}>\mathcal{T}
, as required.

Now, consider the case
w_{\max}\leq\mathcal{T}
. Define
\textbf{SKC}_{k}
as the contribution of item
k
to the algorithm’s profit. It holds that

\E[\textbf{SKC}]=\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\E[\textbf{SKC}_{k}]}~.

We want to lower bound
\E[\textbf{SKC}_{k}]
. If item
k
is inserted and does not overflow, we obtain a reward of
v_{k}-p_{k}\geq w_{k}
. On the other hand, if the item overflows, we pay a cost of
p_{k}
. By the definition of the IOR parameter (
Definition

2.6
),
\alpha\geq\frac{p_{k}}{v_{k}\Pr[s_{k}\leq 1]-p_{k}}=p_{k}/w_{k}
, which implies that an overflowing job incurs a cost of
p_{k}\leq\alpha w_{k}\leq\alpha w_{\max}
.

We get that

\displaystyle\E[\textbf{SKC}_{k}]

\displaystyle=(v_{k}-p_{k})\cdot\Pr[k\mbox{ successfully inserted}]-p_{k}\cdot\Pr[k\mbox{ overflows}]

\displaystyle\geq(v_{k}-p_{k})\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mu_{j}}\right)-p_{k}\cdot\Pr[k\mbox{ overflows}]

\displaystyle\geq w_{k}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mu_{j}}\right)-\alpha\cdot w_{\max}\cdot\Pr[k\mbox{ overflows}],

(3)

where the first inequality follows from
Lemma

3.5
(with
t=1
), the second inequality follows from the definition of
w_{k}\leq v_{k}-p_{k}
and the argument above that
p_{k}\leq\alpha\,w_{\max}
.

In the above, this bound is also true for the virtual item
i^{*}
: the item’s expected truncated size is
\mu_{i^{*}}
, and its expected profit conditioned on the event that it was successfully inserted is at least
w_{i^{*}}
.

Summing over all
k\in[r]
, we have that

\displaystyle\E[\textbf{SKC}]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mu_{j}}\right)}-\alpha w_{\max}\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\Pr[k\mbox{ overflows}]}

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(1)-\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mu_{j}}-\alpha w_{\max}

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle=\Phi(1)-\sum_{1\leq j\leq k\leq r}{w_{k}\mu_{j}}-\alpha w_{\max}

\displaystyle=\Phi(1)-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq r}{w_{k}\mu_{j}}-\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}\mu_{k}}-\alpha w_{\max}

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(1)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,j=1}^{r}{w_{k}\mu_{j}}-\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}\mu_{k}}-\alpha w_{\max}

\displaystyle(**)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Phi(1)}{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}\mu_{k}}-\alpha w_{\max}

\displaystyle\sum_{k,j=1}^{r}{w_{k}\mu_{j}}\leq\Phi(1)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Phi(1)}{2}-w_{\max}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\mu_{k}}+\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Phi(1)}{2}-w_{\max}(1+\alpha)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\mu_{k}}\leq 1

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\Phi(1)}{4}\geq\frac{\Phi(1)}{4(1+\alpha)}=\mathcal{T}~.

\displaystyle w_{\max}\leq\mathcal{T}=\frac{\Phi(1)}{4(1+\alpha)}.

(4)

In
(*)
we use the fact that
\sum_{k\in[r]}\Pr[k\mbox{ overflows}]\leq 1
as at most one item overflows, and
\sum_{k=1}^{r}{w_{k}}=\Phi(1)
. In
(**)
we use the decreasing order of density: for every
j<k
it holds that
w_{k}\mu_{j}\leq w_{j}\mu_{k}
, and thus
w_{k}\mu_{j}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(w_{k}\mu_{j}+w_{j}\mu_{k}\right)
.

∎

4.1

Theorem 4.1

For every
\alpha

\frac{\mathrm{STOP\_ADAPT}(I)}{\mathrm{NON\_ADAPT}(I)}=\Omega(\alpha).

Proof.

For a constant

5
\Omega(\alpha)
Informational Gap

In this section we establish a lower bound that exposes a fundamental information gap in the problem of Stochastic Knapsack with Costs. Previous constant-factor approximation algorithms for Stochastic Knapsack (without costs) have used limited distributional information, specifically the expected (or truncated) sizes and the probability of fitting within the current capacity. In Proposition
5.1
we demonstrate that this information is insufficient. We show that any algorithm, even one that finds an adaptive policy, which relies solely on such limited data, cannot achieve a constant-factor approximation in the presence of costs. More generally, we prove in Theorem
5.2
that even providing the first
k
moments of each distribution for any constant
k
is insufficient. This showcases the inherent difficulty of the added costs, implying that future constant-approximation algorithms will need to utilize almost the full distributional structure in novel ways.

7
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work, we initiate the study of contract design where rewards are fixed, but the principal incentivizes the agents to invest effort in finishing their jobs on time. We identify an economically meaningful Inverse of the Return-on-Investment parameter,
\alpha
, which captures many aspects of the problem’s complexity.
Dean et al. 2008
show a constant adaptivity gap, while we show that in our case, the adaptivity gap is
\Theta(\alpha)
. As the optimal adaptive contract is intractable, a remaining open problem is whether one can efficiently compute an adaptive contract whose profit is a good approximation to the optimal adaptive contract.

In
Section

4.2
, we consider a policy with limited adaptivity: the order is non-adaptive, but the policy stops processing jobs once it expects a negative profit. We show that for such policies, their profit can be a factor
\Omega(\alpha^{1/3})
away from the optimal profit. As our algorithm in
Section

3
guarantees an
O(\alpha)
-approximation, this leaves a gap between the lower and upper bound for such policies. It is also interesting to consider other limited-adaptivity policies, e.g., a policy that predetermines the agents’ order, but not their effort levels.
Another direction is quantifying the profit loss from using a family of restricted contracts, such as contracts that do not pay agents who finish after the deadline.

Finally, many additional stochastic optimization problems, such as stochastic routing and others, have natural strategic variants were the uncertainty is controlled by an agent’s actions. E.g., in routing, service times are likely to result from a strategic choice of an economic entity. Marrying the classic algorithms developed for such problems with contractual incentives is an intriguing avenue for future research.

Appendix D
Ordered Adaptive Contracts

In many real-world scenarios, the principal lacks the flexibility to reorder agents at will. For example, this can be due to procedural requirements, like a fixed sequence of suppliers in a supply chain, or pre-determined project plan with sequential dependencies. In these settings, the principal’s adaptive power is limited to deciding, after observing the outcome of each task, whether or not to continue with the next agent, and if so – which effort level to incentivize. We define the Ordered Adaptive Contracts model:

Definition D.1

(Ordered Adaptive Contracts Model)
.

In the Ordered Adaptive Contracts model, the principal is given a fixed ordering
\sigma=(1,2,\ldots,n)
of the
n
agents. For each agent
i
in order, the principal offers a contract
t_{i}
, and the agent chooses an effort level
j
to maximize their utility. The agents execute their jobs sequentially in the given order. The principal observes the completion time of each job and may adaptively decide whether to proceed to the next agent or stop, but cannot change the order. The objective is to maximize the expected net profit (total value from completed jobs before the budget is exhausted minus total payments to agents), given the fixed order
\sigma
.

Since the order is fixed, we are interested in comparing our policy’s performance to the performance of the optimal policy operating on
the same order of agents
.
By using the same reduction to the multi-agent contract problem as shown in
Lemma

2.1
, we focus on solving the matching stochastic knapsack with costs problem. In a similar manner to the algorithm that finds an ordered adaptive policy presented in
[
11
]
and to the PTAS presented in
[
5
]
for the regular Stochastic Knapsack variant (single-choice, no-cost), we use a resource augmentation approach: the policy is allowed a knapsack of size
(1+\epsilon)
, while the optimal adaptive policy has a knapsack of size
1
. Our algorithm runs in time
O(\frac{n^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}})
, offering a tradeoff between the augmented size of the knapsack and the running time of the algorithm.

We use a dynamic programming algorithm with discretized sizes. We denote by
\mathcal{D}(i,k)
the optimal expected value that can be obtained from inserting items
i,...,n
, with
\delta k
capacity left in the knapsack, for some small
\delta>0
to be determined later. We are interested in computing
\mathcal{D}(1,\lfloor\frac{1}{\delta}\rfloor)
. If all of the real sizes are indeed multiples of
\delta k
, then one can obtain an optimal policy by computing:

\mathcal{D}(i,k)=\max\left\{\mathcal{D}(i+1,k),\max_{j\in[m]}\left\{v_{i}\cdot\Pr[s_{i}^{j}\leq\delta k]-p_{i}^{j}+\sum_{t=0}^{k}{\mathcal{D}(i+1,k-t)\cdot\Pr[s_{i}^{j}=t\delta]}\right\}\right\}.

With the base cases of
\mathcal{D}(n+1,k)=0
for all
k
, and
\mathcal{D}(i,k)=0
for
k<0
.

However, when the distributions are arbitrary, a slight deviation from the discretization can result in an arbitrarily bad solution. To overcome this, we must utilize the fact our policy is allowed a knapsack of size
1+\epsilon
. We do so by rounding down the true distribution at each step to the nearest multiple of
\delta
. By picking a small enough
\delta
, we ensure that the overall rounding error is not too large.

Theorem D.1
.

There exists an
O(\frac{n^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}})
time algorithm that finds a policy for the ordered adaptive knapsack with costs problem, where the policy has a knapsack of size
(1+\epsilon)
, and the optimal adaptive policy has a knapsack of size
1
. The policy achieves an expected profit of at least the profit of the optimal policy with a knapsack of size
1
.

Proof.

Let
OAC
be our algorithm and
\mathrm{ADAPT}
be the optimal adaptive policy. We set the discretization parameter
\delta=\epsilon/n
.

First, consider a modified problem where all item sizes
s_{i}^{j}
are rounded down to the nearest multiple of
\delta
:
\hat{s}_{i}^{j}=\delta\lfloor s_{i}^{j}/\delta\rfloor
. Let
\widehat{\mathrm{ADAPT}}
be the optimal expected profit for this problem with rounded-down sizes. Since for any policy, the probability of successfully fitting a set of items can only increase when item sizes are reduced, the optimal value for the rounded-down problem is an upper bound on the optimal value for the original problem. Thus,
\widehat{\mathrm{ADAPT}}\geq\mathrm{ADAPT}.

Our algorithm solves the dynamic program
\mathcal{D}
described above for the rounded-down sizes. This DP computes the exact optimal policy for the rounded-down problem. Denote by
\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}}
the policy derived from this DP.
When given a knapsack of capacity
1
, this policy achieves the optimal value for the rounded-down problem. Let
\widehat{\mathrm{profit}}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1)
be a random variable of the profit of the policy with the original capacity and rounded-down job sizes. We have that

\E[\widehat{\mathrm{profit}}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1)]=\widehat{\mathrm{ADAPT}}.
Combining these, we have
\E[\widehat{\mathrm{profit}}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1)]\geq\mathrm{ADAPT}.

Now, we analyze the performance of our algorithm, which executes the policy
\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}}
on the
original
item sizes
s_{i}^{j}
with an augmented capacity of
1+\epsilon
. Consider any execution path of
\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}}
where a sequence of items and choices
\mathcal{S}\subset[n]\times[m]
is attempted and successfully completed. According to the policy
\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}}
(which was computed for capacity 1 and sizes
\hat{s}
), the sum of the rounded-down sizes must be no more than 1:

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\hat{s}_{i}^{j}\leq 1.

The true total size of these items is
\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}s_{i}^{j}
. We know that for each item,
s_{i}^{j}=\hat{s}_{i}^{j}+\eta_{i}^{j}
, where
0\leq\eta_{i}^{j}<\delta
is the rounding error. The total size in the original problem is:

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}s_{i}^{j}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\hat{s}_{i}^{j}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\eta_{i}^{j}.

Since at most
n
items can be placed in the knapsack, the total accumulated rounding error is bounded:

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\eta_{i}^{j}\leq n\cdot\delta=n\cdot(\epsilon/n)=\epsilon.

Therefore, the true total size of the items is:

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}s_{i}^{j}\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\hat{s}_{i}^{j}+\epsilon\leq 1+\epsilon.

This shows that any sequence of items that fits within capacity 1 in the rounded-down instance is guaranteed to fit within the augmented capacity of
1+\epsilon
in the original instance. Since the policy
\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}}
makes decisions based on the sequence of outcomes (which are independent of the rounding), every successful path under capacity 1 with sizes
\hat{s}
is also a successful path under capacity
1+\epsilon
with sizes
s
. Moreover, it crucially follows that an item can cause an overflow under capacity
1+\epsilon
with sizes
s

only if
it causes an overflow under capacity 1 with sizes
\hat{s}
. Therefore, the expected profit can only be higher:

\E[\mathrm{profit}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1+\epsilon)]\geq\E[\widehat{\mathrm{profit}}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1)].

Chaining the inequalities, we get the final result:

\displaystyle\textbf{OAC}=\E[\mathrm{profit}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1+\epsilon)]\geq\E[\widehat{\mathrm{profit}}(\varphi_{\textbf{OAC}},\text{capacity}=1)]\geq\mathrm{ADAPT}.

The dynamic program
\mathcal{D}
has
O(n)\cdot O(n/\epsilon)
states, and computing each state takes up to
O(m\cdot n/\epsilon)
time. This results in a running time of
O(\frac{n^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}})
.

∎

\displaystyle=(x_{ij^{\prime}}^{*}-x_{ij^{\prime}})\mu_{ij^{\prime}}+(x_{ij^{\prime\prime}}^{*}-x_{ij^{\prime\prime}})\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}+(x_{ij}^{*}-x_{ij})\mu_{ij}

\displaystyle=x_{ij}\mu_{ij^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij}}{w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij^{\prime}}}+x_{ij}\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\frac{w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}}}{w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij^{\prime}}}-x_{ij}\mu_{ij}

\displaystyle=x_{ij}\left(\frac{\mu_{ij^{\prime}}(w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij})+\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}(w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}})-\mu_{ij}(w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij^{\prime}})}{w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij^{\prime}}}\right)~.

From double dominance we have that

\frac{\mu_{ij}-\mu_{ij^{\prime}}}{w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}}}\geq\frac{\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-\mu_{ij}}{w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij}}

Since
w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}}>0
and
w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij}>0
, we can cross-multiply:

(\mu_{ij}-\mu_{ij^{\prime}})(w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij})\geq(\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-\mu_{ij})(w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}})

Expanding both sides gives:

\mu_{ij}w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-\mu_{ij}w_{ij}-\mu_{ij^{\prime}}w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}+\mu_{ij^{\prime}}w_{ij}\geq\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}w_{ij}-\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}w_{ij^{\prime}}-\mu_{ij}w_{ij}+\mu_{ij}w_{ij^{\prime}}

We can cancel the
-\mu_{ij}w_{ij}
term from both sides. Rearranging the terms to match the numerator of our expression for
\Delta_{\mu}
yields:

\mu_{ij^{\prime}}(w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij})+\mu_{ij^{\prime\prime}}(w_{ij}-w_{ij^{\prime}})-\mu_{ij}(w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}-w_{ij^{\prime}})\leq 0~.

This confirms the numerator is non-positive. Since
w_{ij^{\prime\prime}}>w_{ij^{\prime}}
we conclude that
\Delta_{\mu}\leq 0
.

∎
