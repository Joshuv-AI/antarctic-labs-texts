Comparing the efficacy of ultrasound-guided versus anatomical localization for corticosteroid injection in the treatment of carpal tunnel syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shibo Fang, Hongbo Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS) remains a pervasive condition, often resulting in functional impairment and pain. This study examines whether ultrasound guidance enhances the efficacy and safety of corticosteroid injections for CTS compared to traditional injections based on anatomical landmarks.MethodsThis meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. We performed a systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library up to September 19, 2023. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ultrasound-guided(US) versus landmark-guided(LM) corticosteroid injections for CTS were included. Data were synthesized using a random-effects model.ResultsEight RCTs were included in the analysis. US was associated with a significant reduction in BCTQ symptom severity scores (Standardized Mean Difference [SMD] = -0.32, 95% CI -0.44 to -0.20; I²=0%) and functional status scores (SMD = -0.21, 95% CI -0.31 to -0.10; I²=0%). Nerve injury events were rare; while the direction of effect favored US, the evidence was too sparse to permit a reliable pooled estimate.ConclusionsUS corticosteroid injections provide modest, consistent improvements in symptoms and function over landmark techniques. Safety signals directionally favor US, but adverse-event data are sparse and not definitive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03926-y.Keywords: Carpal tunnel syndrome, Ultrasound-Guided, Corticosteroid injection, Anatomical localization, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS) is a commonly encountered neuropathic ailment affecting the upper limbs, primarily characterized by the entrapment of the median nerve as it traverses the carpal tunnel —a narrow anatomical passageway in the wrist. This entrapment induces ischemia of the nerve, impedes neural conduction, and, without timely intervention, can lead to irreversible nerve damage [1, 2]. The origins of CTS are diverse; while many instances are idiopathic with no discernible cause, the condition can also emerge secondary to factors such as traumatic injuries, vascular pathologies, systemic inflammation, excessive body weight, repetitive strain from occupational activities, the degenerative changes of aging or osteoarthritis, the physiological changes of pregnancy, thyroid dysfunction, or systemic autoimmune conditions [3, 4].

The therapeutic strategy for CTS, particularly in cases classified as mild to moderate, frequently includes the administration of corticosteroid injections directly into the carpal tunnel [5]. The purpose of these injections is to attenuate inflammatory processes and reduce swelling within the tunnel, thereby diminishing the compressive forces exerted upon the median nerve [6]. Historically, clinicians have relied on palpation of anatomical landmarks to guide these injections, a practice that has been standard for several decades. Yet, despite its widespread use, this traditional technique harbors inherent limitations—it does not consistently ensure the accurate placement of corticosteroids within the carpal tunnel, which can result in less-than-optimal therapeutic effects and, in some instances, exacerbate nerve trauma [7, 8]. The emergence of ultrasound technology in clinical settings has revolutionized the application of injectable treatments for CTS. Utilizing ultrasound guidance, clinicians can now obtain real-time, dynamic images of the carpal tunnel’s anatomy, allowing for unprecedented precision in needle placement [9]. This method substantially diminishes the risk of inadvertent injury to the median nerve, surrounding tendons, or blood vessels during the injection process. The superiority of ultrasound guidance in procedural accuracy when compared to anatomical landmark techniques is clear. However, there remains debate within the medical literature regarding the efficacy and safety of ultrasound-guided injections versus traditional methods [10]. Variability in reported outcomes has been noted, suggesting the necessity for a more thorough and methodical comparison of these two approaches.

The implementation of US injections has thus been progressively advocated by healthcare professionals due to its enhanced accuracy, which has been shown to positively influence the safety and efficacy profile of corticosteroid administration for CTS. While a notable meta-analysis by Yang et al. [9] synthesized evidence up to May 2020, the subsequent publication of several new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) necessitates an updated review. Such an update is crucial to increase the statistical power of the analysis, enhance the precision of the comparative effect estimates, and provide a more definitive assessment of the safety profile. Therefore, the objective of this study was to conduct a contemporary systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of US versus anatomical localization techniques for corticosteroid injections in CTS.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Throughout the conduct of our systematic review and the subsequent synthesis of our findings, we conformed stringently to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta- Analyses (PRISMA) directives [11]. Our meta-analytical approach was systematically designed within the PICO framework, the following dimensions: Patient (P), characterized as individuals afflicted with CTS; Intervention (I), specified as corticosteroid injections directed by US; Comparison (C), denoting the traditional corticosteroid injections deployed via anatomical landmark techniques; and Outcome (O), focusing on primary end points including the amelioration of symptomatic severity, enhancement of functional capacity in patients, and the electrophysiological alterations in the median nerve following the intervention.

An exhaustive search was executed across four electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, on September 19, 2023, without imposition of a temporal filter. The devised search schema utilized an array of key terminologies such as “carpal tunnel syndrome”, “ultrasound-guided”, “anatomical localization”, and “corticosteroid injection”. This lexicon was deliberately chosen to cover the expansive purview of the PICO schema, thereby assuring an inclusive extraction of pertinent research for the meta-analysis. No restrictions were placed on language. Additionally, the bibliographies of relevant articles were scrupulously examined by hand to unearth any supplementary records of potential interest.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Studies included in the systematic review needed to meet the following criteria: (1) Population: Adult participants with a clinical and/or electrophysiological diagnosis of CTS; (2) Interventions: Studies that employed US corticosteroid injections as the intervention; (3) Comparators: Studies must compare the US technique with the traditional anatomical landmark-guided injection technique; (4) Outcomes: Research articles must report on symptom severity, functional status, and/or electrophysiological changes in the median nerve following the intervention; (5) Language: Studies published in any language, with available translations or detailed English summaries. (6) Corticosteroid Regimen: Studies were included regardless of the specific type or dosage of corticosteroid used, provided that the same regimen was administered to both the US and anatomical landmark groups within each individual study.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Non-original Studies: Reviews, editorials, commentaries, letters, conference abstracts, and studies without primary data or full text available; (2) Non-relevant Population: Studies focusing on pediatric populations, animals, or in vitro models; (3) Incomplete Data: Studies with insufficient data on the methodology or outcomes that preclude an assessment of the quality and impact of the results.

Data extraction

During our systematic approach to literature review and data abstraction, each article was independently screened, and the pertinent data extracted by a pair of reviewers to mitigate bias and enhance reliability. Should any discordance arise in the evaluation process, the reviewers will engage in a dialogue to reconcile any differences and, if consensus cannot be reached, they will seek the judgment of third-party, independent reviewer. Data extraction encompassed the following elements: (1) Study Metadata: The title, authors’ names, publication date, and the study’s geographical location; (2) Study Characteristics: Participant demographics, comparability of age across patient groups, follow-up duration, and the interventional measures implemented, including injection techniques and pharmacological agents utilized; (3) Outcome Measures: This included scoring of symptom severity and functional status via the Boston carpal tunnel syndrome questionnaire (BCTQ), and the incidence of adverse events such as nerve injury. If key data were missing from the published manuscript, efforts were made to contact the original investigators via email to obtain the non-published data. This procedure aligns with the stringent standards requisite for a meta-analysis, ensuring a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence. Prespecified subgroup analyses (sex, age, baseline severity) were not feasible due to unreported stratified outcomes in primary studies.

Quality assessment

In the meta-analysis we conducted, the assessment of study quality was rigorously performed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool [12]. Each study was independently reviewed by a pair of evaluators who scrutinized key domains for bias risk, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, participant and staff blinding, completeness of outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and any additional biases. These domains were appraised and categorized as low, unclear, or high risk of bias. In instances of reviewer discordance, discussions ensued, and if consensus could not be reached, a third-party reviewer was engaged for arbitration. This systematic approach ensured the methodological soundness and credibility of the included studies in our meta- analysis.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous outcomes (BCTQ SSS and FSS) were synthesized as standardized mean differences (Hedges’g) under a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model; negative values favor US. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q (χ²) and quantified with I². To evaluate robustness, we conducted leave-one-out influence analyses. For rare adverse events (median-nerve injury), data were too sparse (including zero-event cells) to support a reliable pooled effect; therefore, we retained a single-study forest panel for transparency and summarized the direction of effect qualitatively. Potential small-study/publication bias was explored by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression; given the small number of included studies (k = 8), these assessments were interpreted cautiously as exploratory only. For multi-arm trials comparing more than one US group with a single LM control, ultrasound arms were combined into one group following the Cochrane Handbook; this is indicated in the table notes and figure legends. Where primary reports presented means with standard errors (SE), standard deviations (SD) were derived as SD = SE×√n. Prespecified subgroup analyses (sex, age, baseline severity) were not feasible because stratified data were not reported in the included studies. All tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Search results and study selection

At the outset of our systematic review and meta-analysis, a comprehensive initial search of various electronic databases yielded a pool of 1,026 potentially relevant articles. To streamline this collection, a deduplication algorithm was applied to remove multiple instances of the same study, ensuring singular representation of each research article. Subsequent to this deduplication process, a systematic screening of titles and abstracts was performed, adhering to explicitly established inclusion and exclusion criteria. These criteria were comprehensive, encompassing study design, participant demographics, clinical endpoints, and the robustness of the research methodology. From this process, 28 studies were earmarked for detailed full-text review. This review was executed independently by a team of investigators to prevent selection bias and to allow for a thorough and impartial evaluation of the literature. During this stage, 20 studies were excluded for reasons including: literature reviews (n = 8), studies with overlapping datasets (n = 1), reports with inadequate data (n = 8), and clinical investigations without comparator arms (n = 3). Consequently, a total of 8 studies satisfied our rigorous selection standards as set forth in the study protocol and were subsequently included in our meta- analysis [13–20] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The studies spanned from 2013 to 2019 and included a range of geographical locations: China, Iran, South Korea, and Turkey. The number of wrists assessed in the control and study groups varied across studies, with a total of 218 wrists evaluated in the control groups (landmark-guided) and 306 in the study groups (ultrasound-guided). The age of participants in the control groups had a mean ranging from 42.7 to 61.5 years, and the study groups had a mean age range from 46.0 to 59.4 years, indicating a middle-aged to older adul population. All studies utilized the BCTQ and neurophysiological measures as primary outcome indicators, with follow-up periods spanning 4 to 24 weeks to assess the efficacy of the interventions. The control groups’ interventions consisted of corticosteroid injections guided by anatomical landmarks, specifically distal or proximal to the wrist crease and on the ulnar side of the palmaris ongus tendon. In contrast, the study groups l received their interventions using US with various approaches, including in-plane, out-of-plane, and ulnar approaches to the proximal carpal tunnel entrance. Medications used across the studies involved compound betamethasone, methylprednisolone, lidocaine combined with triamcinolone, reflecting the commonality in pharmacological agents employed for corticosteroid injections in CTS treatment in Table 1. Results of quality assessment

The risk of bias for the eight included trials was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool, as visualized in the traffic-light plot (Fig. 2). The overall methodological quality was moderate. No study was rated at high risk of bias in any domain. The primary sources of potential bias were identified in the domains of measurement of the outcome (D4) and selection of the reported result (D5), where most studies were judged to have ‘some concerns’. This was often related to a lack of blinding of outcome assessors. Domains related to the randomization process (D1), deviations from interventions (D2), and missing outcome data (D3) were generally assessed as having a low risk of bias. Efficacy of US on BCTQ symptom severity (SSS)

All eight trials contributed data for the meta-analysis of BCTQ symptom severity scores. As shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3), the pooled analysis revealed that US led to a statistically significant reduction in SSS compared to the LM technique (Standardized Mean Difference [SMD] = −0.32, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.20). There was no evidence of heterogeneity between studies (I² = 0%).

Impact of US on BCTQ functional status (FSS)

Data on BCTQ functional status scores were also available from all eight trials. The pooled analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in functional status for the US group (SMD = −0.21, 95% CI −0.31 to −0.10). Consistent with the SSS outcome, no heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0%). The forest plot for this analysis is presented in Fig. 4.

Incidence of nerve injury adverse events

Data on nerve injury were sparsely reported. Only one trial (Lee 2014) provided sufficient data for inclusion in a quantitative analysis (Fig. 5). While the point estimate from this single study favored US (Odds Ratio < 1), the confidence interval was extremely wide and crossed the line of no effect. Therefore, due to the severe lack of data and high uncertainty, a reliable conclusion about the effect on nerve injury cannot be drawn from this meta-analysis.

Sensitivity and publication bias analyses

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for the SSS outcome confirmed the robustness of our findings. As shown in Fig. 6, the removal of any single study did not substantially alter the pooled effect estimate, which remained statistically significant in all iterations. Visual inspection of the funnel plot for SSS (Fig. 7) was inconclusive due to the small number of included studies. However, Egger’s linear regression test did not indicate significant funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting that publication bias is unlikely to be a major threat to the validity of our findings.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that US-guided corticosteroid injections yield statistically significant and consistent improvements for CTS compared with LM-guided techniques. Our pooled analysis of eight randomized controlled trials showed a modest but notable reduction in both symptom severity (SSS: SMD = − 0.32, 95% CI − 0.44 to − 0.20; I²=0%) and functional status (FSS: SMD = − 0.21, 95% CI − 0.31 to − 0.10; I²=0%). Regarding safety, median-nerve injury events were sparsely reported; the direction of effect favored US, but the evidence was too limited to support a reliable pooled estimate.

Our findings update and reinforce conclusions from prior meta-analyses (e.g., Yang et al. [9]). By incorporating several recent trials, with data through September 2023, the present study shows that the evidence supporting US has remained consistent in recent years. The significance of our study is twofold: first, it provides a contemporary synthesis of efficacy evidence; second, it clarifies safety—the risk of nerve injury appears low with either technique, and the direction of available data favors US, though definitive quantitative conclusions are precluded by sparse events.

A key consideration is the large, well-conducted trial by Roh et al. [15], which did not detect a statistically significant between-group difference. This underscores a strength of meta-analysis: individual studies—even large ones—may be underpowered to detect modest but clinically meaningful effects. By synthesizing the totality of evidence, our analysis achieves greater precision and reveals a consistent overall benefit. Several clinical factors may also explain the single-trial result—for example, highly experienced operators performing landmark-guided injections or differences in patient severity that narrow between-group contrasts. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that the pooled effect persisted when any single study, including Roh et al. [15], was excluded, indicating that our findings are robust and not driven by one trial. We further note the notable influence of Karaahmet et al. [13] and Roh et al. [15] as indicated by the leave-one-out analysis (Fig. 6). Excluding either study shifts the pooled SMD slightly (e.g., for SSS, SMD ranges from − 0.28 to − 0.35 upon exclusion), but does not alter the overall statistical significance or direction of the effect (95% CIs remain non-overlapping with zero), confirming the robustness of our conclusions. Potential reasons for their influence include their larger sample sizes relative to other included trials (Roh et al. [15]: n = 102; Karaahmet et al. [13]: n = 76) and specific methodological features, such as the multi-arm design in Karaahmet et al. [13] (which we combined per Cochrane guidelines to preserve valid contrasts). Despite this influence, the low heterogeneity across all studies (I²=0% for both primary outcomes) suggests consistent effects, and the meta-analysis provides a more powered synthesis than individual trials alone.

Despite clinical diversity across trials (e.g., steroid type/dose and in-plane vs. out-of-plane or ulnar vs. midline approaches; see Table 1), we observed no statistical heterogeneity for either primary outcome (I²=0%). This pattern suggests—without inferring causality—that accurate, real-time needle placement may contribute importantly to the observed benefits, potentially outweighing regimen differences in typical trial settings. For multi-arm trials with more than one US arm, we combined ultrasound arms following Cochrane guidance to preserve valid contrasts with landmark guidance. Thus, even though a small number of trials (e.g., Roh [15] and Karaahmet [13]) exert greater influence on precision, the absence of statistical heterogeneity indicates that their findings are aligned in direction with the broader evidence base.

This meta-analysis has limitations. First, the number of trials is limited (n = 8), restricting power for rare adverse events and rendering publication-bias assessments exploratory only. Second, most trials did not report stratified outcomes by demographic or clinical variables (e.g., age, sex, baseline CTS severity), precluding subgroup analyses to identify populations most likely to benefit. Finally, follow-up durations were predominantly short- to medium-term; the absence of long-term data limits insight into the durability of benefits and the long-term safety profile of US injections, which warrants further study.

Conclusions

US corticosteroid injection yields modest, consistent improvements in symptoms and function over landmark techniques. Safety signals directionally favor US, but the evidence for nerve-injury reduction is sparse and not yet definitive; larger trials with standardized adverse-event reporting are warranted.
