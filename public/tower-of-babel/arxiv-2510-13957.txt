Resonant Loop Interferometers for High-Frequency Gravitational Waves
Jan Heisig

Abstract
Gravitational waves at kilohertz and higher frequencies offer a unique probe of the early Universe at temperatures well beyond the reach of the cosmic microwave background, corresponding to energy scales ≳ 10 9 \gtrsim 10^{9}\, GeV. Existing detector concepts fall many orders of magnitude short of the big-bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) bound on the stochastic background in this regime. We propose a new interferometric architecture based on closed optical loops, in which the gravitational-wave–induced phase shift accumulates coherently over many traversals. This produces sharp, narrowband resonances whose predictable comb structure provides a distinct experimental signature. For square or triangular loops with parameters compatible with the Einstein Telescope infrastructure, and finesse values of order 500 500 , we project sensitivity that approaches and even surpasses the BBN bound up to tens of kilohertz after one year of integration. Such loop interferometers thus open a realistic and distinctive path toward exploring high-frequency stochastic gravitational-wave backgrounds.



I
Introduction

Gravitational waves (GWs) provide a unique observational window into the early Universe, reaching epochs far earlier than the cosmic microwave background (CMB). A stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) encodes information about high-energy processes in the primordial plasma, including phenomena far beyond the reach of laboratory experiments. In particular, features at frequencies
f\gtrsim\mathrm{kHz}
correspond to cosmic temperatures above
10^{9}\,\mathrm{GeV}
, and can thus probe physics at scales associated with grand unification and other extensions of the Standard Model.

Efforts to access this high-frequency regime are ongoing, ranging from resonant electromagnetic detectors to mechanical systems and novel interferometric concepts
Aggarwal:2020olq
;
Domcke:2022rgu
;
Aggarwal:2025noe
. These approaches face formidable challenges: thermal and quantum noise become increasingly severe at high frequency, and practical arm lengths are limited, leading to rapidly degrading sensitivity. As summarized in recent reviews
Aggarwal:2020olq
;
Domcke:2022rgu
;
Aggarwal:2025noe
, proposed instruments remain many orders of magnitude away from the sensitivity required to probe SGWB amplitudes at the level of the big-bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) bound.

We put forward a different strategy and propose to accumulate the GW-induced phase shift constructively in closed interferometric loops. The mechanism exploits the transverse–traceless (TT) nature of GWs: as the beam changes direction at each corner of the loop, its projection onto the oscillating strain alternates in phase. When the GW wavelength matches the loop geometry, these contributions stay synchronized and add coherently. Square or triangular geometries allow the effective baseline to grow with the number of traversals, without lowering the frequency cutoff. While the response is resonant—peaked at discrete frequencies determined by the loop geometry—this selectivity is advantageous: sharp spectral features would provide a smoking-gun signal, clearly distinguishable from smooth instrumental noise or astrophysical foregrounds. Such loops could, in principle, be embedded in terrestrial facilities, for example within the infrastructure foreseen for the Einstein Telescope (ET), or deployed in space. This opens a new avenue to explore the high-frequency SGWB and to approach sensitivities relevant for fundamental physics.

II
Concept and Principle

The central idea is to let a laser beam circulate through a
closed
interferometric loop such that the GW–induced phase shift adds
coherently
on every traversal. Figure
1
illustrates this basic principle for a square loop and normal incidence, chosen because its orthogonal sides align most directly with the quadrupolar pattern of the GW strain, making the effect most transparent. We also consider an equilateral triangular configuration, motivated by its natural compatibility with the triangular tunnel layout foreseen for the ET.

As the laser beam changes direction at each corner of the loop, its projection onto the GW strain alternates in phase. When the GW wavelength is commensurate with the loop perimeter, these alternating projections stay synchronized with the oscillation of the wave, so that each segment samples a systematic stretch or squeeze, schematically shown in Fig.
1
as the red counter-clockwise (CCW) and blue clockwise (CW) paths. In this way, the loop geometry converts the transverse nature of the GW into a constructive phase response at well-defined resonant frequencies.

The GW-induced phase shift along a path with tangent
\hat{e}^{i}
follows from the metric perturbation
h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})
in transverse–traceless gauge,

\Delta\phi\;=\;\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{\rm laser}}\,\frac{1}{2}\!\int\!h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})\,\hat{e}^{i}\hat{e}^{j}\,dx,

(1)

where the integral extends over the optical path and the GW is
evaluated along the null trajectory (retarded times). Explicit closed-form expressions for the accumulated phase along each segment, including retardation and polarization dependence, are provided in the Appendix.

In a closed loop, Eq. (
1
) becomes a sum over straight segments, each with a distinct projection onto the GW polarization tensors. For a GW incident along the
z
-axis, the four sides of a square loop contribute with alternating signs of
h_{xx}
and
h_{yy}
, leading to coherent addition whenever the GW completes an odd number of half-periods per traversal. Over
n
round trips, the total phase then grows linearly with
n
, corresponding to a power enhancement
\propto n^{2}
. Unlike in a standard arm-based Fabry–Perot interferometer, where the response rolls off once the GW wavelength becomes shorter than the
effective
travel distance of the laser, the closed-loop configuration realizes an effective baseline that grows with the number of round trips without worsening the high-frequency cut-off. The detector thus acts as a selective, high-
Q
resonant sensor at discrete frequencies determined by the loop size.

We parameterize the loop by a side (square) or segment (equilateral triangle) length
L
, so the perimeters are
4L
and
3L
, respectively. Resonances occur when the GW completes an odd number of half-periods per loop traversal. For the square (loop length
4L
), the principal resonances for a GW incident along the
z
-axis (
\theta=0
) are at

\lambda_{\rm GW}^{\rm res}=\frac{2L}{2\ell+1},\qquad\ell=0,1,2,\dots,

(2)

while for incidence along
\pm x
or
\pm y
(
\theta=\pi/2
with
\varphi=0
or
\pi/2
) they shift to

\lambda_{\rm GW}^{\rm res}=\frac{4L}{2\ell+1}.

(3)

For the equilateral triangle (loop length
3L
) with
\theta=0
one finds

\lambda_{\rm GW}^{\rm res}=\frac{3L}{2\ell+1}.

(4)

Oblique incidence (
\theta\neq 0
) produces predictable angular modulations of the resonance positions and amplitudes through the segmentwise projection factors and retardations. Crucially, these resonances reflect the quadrupolar TT structure: alternating projections at successive corners cause coherent addition only at discrete GW wavelengths, yielding a comb-like response. Local noise sources (shot noise, thermal fluctuations, laser jitter) lack this quadrupolar correlation and therefore do
not
reproduce the resonance comb, providing a smoking-gun signature of the SGWB.

Having established the resonance mechanism, we now outline a possible realization and corresponding readout scheme.
The square and equilateral triangle configurations are illustrated in Fig.
2
.
Other loop geometries are conceivable, but additional segments offer no practical gain in sensitivity. A continuous-wave laser is injected into a low-loss ring cavity whose total optical length is actively stabilized (e.g. via Pound–Drever–Hall locking) so that the cavity remains resonant to sub-wavelength precision.
After
n
round trips, the intracavity field is extracted and interfered with a phase-coherent local oscillator derived from the same master laser.
This balanced homodyne (or heterodyne) readout measures the phase difference between the circulating and reference fields, which—absent a GW—have equal optical length and therefore reject laser frequency noise in common mode.

(a) Square configuration with four mirrors, operated with CW and CCW beams for differential readout.
(b) Triangular configuration with three mirrors, typically operated with a single circulating beam.
In both cases, the input laser (left) is coupled into the resonator and the output field is interfered with a local oscillator at the detector (right).

In addition, the loop can be operated with two counter-propagating beams (CW and CCW) that experience the same cavity dynamics but opposite optical paths. Interfering the extracted fields from both directions forms a differential readout channel that further suppresses technical noise and isolates the GW-induced phase modulation in the beat envelope. The effectiveness of this differential mode depends on geometry and incidence angle: for the square loop, CW and CCW beams acquire opposite phase for normal incidence (
\theta=0
), making the differential signal particularly sensitive to GWs; for waves propagating in the plane (
\theta=\pi/2
), the relative phase shift is angular dependent (e.g.
\pi/2
for
\varphi=0
), still providing useful discrimination. In the triangular geometry, by contrast, symmetry leads to partial cancellation of the CW–CCW response over broad angular ranges, such that a single-direction phase readout (intracavity leakage interfered with the local oscillator) is more favorable. In our sensitivity estimates, we therefore adopt, for each geometry, the readout configuration that maximizes the sky-averaged response.

The concept is compatible with terrestrial infrastructures (e.g. embedding loops within long vacuum tunnels as foreseen for next-generation observatories) and could, in principle, also be implemented in space. Unequal optical paths and slow geometric drifts would mainly shift the resonance comb slightly; such effects can be tracked and compensated by the length-control system as long as they vary slowly compared to the servo bandwidth. These additional control challenges go beyond the present ground-based study but do not affect the basic resonant principle.

Finally, since GWs arrive from arbitrary directions with arbitrary polarization, it is convenient to characterize the detector by its root-mean-square (RMS) response,

\mathcal{R}_{\rm rms}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega\,\mathcal{R}^{2}(\theta,\varphi)},

(5)

with
\mathcal{R}(\theta,\varphi)=\Delta\phi/h_{0}
. This quantity smooths angular modulations while preserving the sharp resonance pattern and will serve as the basis for our sensitivity estimates in the next section.

III
Sensitivity and Feasibility

To quantify the reach of the loop interferometer we evaluate the sky-averaged response
\mathcal{R}_{\rm rms}(f)
and calibrate it against the ET high-frequency configuration.
In the long-wavelength regime (
\lambda_{\rm GW}\!\gg\!4nL
), the setup is equivalent to a Fabry-Pérot interferometer with effective length
L_{\rm eff}=2nL
, and the response scales linearly with
L_{\rm eff}
.
Matching the envelope of the response function to that of ET in this regime fixes the absolute normalization of the strain sensitivity, assuming interferometric precision comparable to the projected ET performance.
Beyond the ET cutoff frequency both detectors exhibit the universal
f^{5}
rise in
\Omega_{\rm GW}h^{2}
, making the calibration straightforward.

As a baseline we consider a triangular loop with side length
L=10\,\mathrm{km}
, compatible with the underground infrastructure foreseen for the Einstein Telescope
Kaiser:2020tlg
.
The loop is modeled as a high-finesse Fabry-Pérot–like ring cavity with finesse
\mathcal{F}=500
, corresponding to an average of
n\simeq 160
coherent round trips via
n\simeq\mathcal{F}/\pi
.
In practice, the circulating field samples a distribution of path lengths rather than a single fixed
n
, which smooths the fine structure between resonances but leaves their positions and depths essentially unchanged.
A finesse of this level is realistic given present mirror coatings and demonstrated resonator performance
LIGOScientific:2014pky
;
Aggarwal:2020umq
.
The resulting integrated sensitivity is shown in Fig.
3
(blue, main plot), together with the instantaneous strain response (blue, inset) that illustrates the underlying resonance pattern.
The triangular geometry reaches below the BBN bound at its first two resonances, probing stochastic backgrounds up to a few tens of kilohertz.
The first sharp dip of the blue curve in Fig.
3
, near
f\simeq 10\,\mathrm{kHz}
for the chosen parameters, corresponds to the fundamental geometric resonance where the GW wavelength matches the loop perimeter (
\lambda_{\rm GW}=3L
for the triangular case).

For illustration we also compute the sensitivity for a square loop with identical parameters (
L=10\,\mathrm{km}
,
\mathcal{F}=500
,
n\simeq 160
).
In this configuration the CW–CCW differential channel provides the strongest response, and its one-year integrated reach is shown in Fig.
3
(orange).
Both geometries can, in principle, attain sensitivities below the BBN bound at resonance, but the triangular setup is particularly natural for an ET-based implementation where the tunnel structure is already triangular.

The overall sensitivity scales with observation time
T
as
\propto T^{-1/2}
in the integrated case and with the number of effective loops
n
as
\propto n^{-1}
, reflecting the linear growth of the phase response with optical path length.
Reducing the side length
L
at fixed finesse does not improve the reach at fixed frequency: the gain from larger
L
is offset by the earlier onset of the geometric cutoff, so that the high-frequency tail
\Omega_{\rm GW}\!\propto\!f^{5}
remains independent of
L_{\rm eff}
.
This scaling behavior makes the ET-scale geometry a natural benchmark for high-frequency searches.
[Note that while optimized for high-frequency operation, the loop interferometer remains (in principle) sensitive also to GWs with wavelengths comparable to or larger than the loop size. In this long-wavelength regime the response reduces to that of a conventional Fabry–Pérot–type interferometer, though the CW–CCW differential signal vanishes as the relative phase shift tends to zero and the directional discrimination of the two-arm design is lost.]

The projected reach is significant: even the baseline triangular configuration probes
\Omega_{\rm GW}
below the BBN bound up to a few tens of kilohertz, directly testing cosmological models that generate high-frequency stochastic backgrounds at scales
\gtrsim 10^{9}
GeV.
The square geometry offers a complementary implementation with similar potential, while improvements in finesse or observation time would deepen the resonance dips shown in Fig.
3
.
Increasing the baseline would shift the resonance comb toward lower frequencies, eventually bridging the gap between ET’s maximum reach and the first resonance of the
10
km benchmark.
Together these features outline a realistic pathway toward exploring an uncharted region of the gravitational-wave spectrum, with a distinct resonance signature that stands out from instrumental or environmental noise and directly reflects the transverse–traceless nature of GWs, obviating the need for cross-correlations to identify a stochastic background.

The loop interferometer is operated as an optical resonator, with the total path length locked to an integer multiple of the laser wavelength (here
\sim 1064
nm) by active mirror stabilization. Optical stability at non-normal incidence (e.g. 45
∘
–60
∘
) is routinely achieved in high-finesse triangular ring cavities, such as the mode cleaners in GEO 600, which reach finesse values
\mathcal{O}(10^{3})
over
\sim 8
m paths.
The main technical challenges—low-loss coatings, alignment control, and efficient coupling—are well within the scope of next-generation interferometer technology and scale favorably with the planned ET infrastructure.

IV
Conclusions

We have presented a new interferometric concept for probing high-frequency GWs: closed optical loops in which the GW-induced phase shift accumulates coherently over many traversals.
The scheme exploits geometric resonances that produce sharp, narrowband sensitivity peaks.
This selectivity is a key feature: the predictable resonance pattern provides a distinctive, self-identifying signature of GWs that cannot be mimicked by instrumental noise or environmental disturbances, enabling detection of a stochastic background without relying on cross-correlation.

For a triangular
10\,\mathrm{km}
loop with a realistic finesse
\mathcal{F}\!=\!500
(effective
n\simeq 160
) and one year of observation, our projections show resonance dips that reach below the BBN bound up to a few
\times 10
kHz.
The square loop operated in a CW–CCW differential mode provides a complementary geometry with comparable potential.
Sensitivity scales as
h_{\min}\!\propto\!n^{-1}T^{-1/2}
, and the high-frequency envelope follows the universal
f^{5}
behavior in
\Omega_{\rm GW}
.
In cosmological terms, access to
f\gtrsim\mathrm{kHz}
corresponds to early-Universe temperatures
\gtrsim 10^{9}\,\mathrm{GeV}
(for horizon-scale generation), far beyond the CMB.

The main challenges are technical—mirror coatings, alignment control, and resonator stability—but they build directly on demonstrated interferometer technologies and are natural targets for next-generation facilities.
The proposed loop detectors thus offer a practical and distinctive avenue toward exploring high-frequency stochastic backgrounds, with resonance features that both enhance sensitivity and furnish an unmistakable experimental hallmark of the TT nature of GWs.
