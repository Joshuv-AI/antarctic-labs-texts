Neonatal PICC-related complications: Risk factors and evaluation of an optimized nursing care pathway in a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xuan Zhou, Qingqing Du, Ying Xia, Jingjing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are commonly used for long-term intravenous therapy in neonates, but catheter-related complications remain a concern that may affect treatment safety. Data on risk factors and nursing interventions in neonatal PICC care are limited. This retrospective cohort included 309 neonates who received PICCs between January 2023 and May 2025. We performed a cohort-wide risk factor analysis and a pre–post evaluation of an optimized nursing pathway implemented in April 2024, with groups defined by placement date. For outcome ascertainment, records were reviewed through 24 to 48 hours after catheter removal. Time to first PICC-related complication was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and multivariable Cox regression. Kaplan–Meier curves showed shorter complication-free catheter survival in extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks + 0 days), female neonates, those with ≥4 puncture attempts, and those with nonideal catheter tip positions (log-rank P < .05 for each comparison). In multivariable Cox regression, female sex (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 1.87, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.21–3.18; P = .015) and nonideal tip position (aHR = 3.01, 95% CI = 1.37–6.59; P = .006) remained independently associated with higher complication risk; single-attempt placement showed borderline lower risk than ≥4 attempts (aHR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.33–1.01; P = .053). The Standard group had higher complication risk than the Optimized group (hazard ratio = 3.41, 95% CI = 2.15–5.41; aHR = 3.50, 95% CI = 2.16–5.67; both P < .001), with the largest difference within 14 days after insertion; cumulative incidence of any complication was 37.0% versus 19.6% (risk ratio = 1.88, 95% CI = 1.29–2.74). Prematurity, female sex, nonideal catheter tip position, and higher puncture attempts were associated with an increased risk of PICC-related complications. The optimized nursing management pathway was associated with improved complication-free catheter survival and reduced complications in neonatal PICC care.

1. Introduction

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are an essential component of neonatal intensive care, providing stable vascular access for preterm and critically ill infants.[1] Despite their wide use, PICC placement is associated with complications such as infection, mechanical occlusion, and thrombosis,[1–3] which can result in early catheter removal, treatment interruption, and increased morbidity.[1,3] Although insertion and maintenance techniques have improved,[4] complication rates in clinical practice remain considerable,[1,2] and the contribution of modifiable factors requires further clarification.

Identifying factors that predispose to PICC-related complications is important for prevention in neonatal units. Studies have reported lower gestational age (GA) and birth weight, multiple insertion attempts, and catheter malposition as key risk factors.[5–8] Observational studies have also described the frequency and types of complications in specific neonatal populations. Wu et al[9] summarized complication patterns over 3 years in extremely and very low birth weight infants, and Elabbasy et al[10] identified catheter malposition and phlebitis as common findings, indicating the need for accurate insertion and consistent post-insertion monitoring.

In addition to risk factor analysis, structured nursing interventions have been investigated as approaches to reduce PICC-related complications. Hu et al[11] found that bundled care and pathway-based nursing practices reduced infection and mechanical complications. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Chen et al[12] reported that early risk recognition and targeted preventive care were associated with reductions in catheter-related thrombosis. However, real-world before-and-after evaluations of multidimensional nursing optimization remain limited, and few studies have examined both changes in complication risk and caregiver-reported outcomes such as caregiver satisfaction.

Based on this background, we analyzed catheter-related complications, time to the first PICC-related complication, and associated risk factors among 309 neonates who underwent PICC placement in our department between January 2023 and May 2025. In April 2024, an optimized nursing management pathway and staff training were introduced for neonatal PICC care, providing a real-world opportunity for a pre–post evaluation. This before–after comparison was non-randomized. For this pre–post evaluation, neonates were grouped according to the date of PICC placement (pre-implementation vs post-implementation), and outcomes were compared between groups; caregiver satisfaction was also assessed as an indicator of the overall care experience. By combining risk factor analysis with assessment of a structured, system-level intervention, this study provides evidence to guide quality improvement in neonatal PICC management.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and subjects of observation

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Neonatology Department of Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital. Data were obtained through a retrospective review and systematic extraction of electronic medical records, including medical, nursing, and treatment documentation. All data were de-identified prior to analysis. A total of 309 neonates who underwent PICC placement between January 2023 and May 2025 were included in the study cohort. Ethical approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hubei Maternal and Child Health Hospital (approval number: 2024-099-01). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations, including the Measures for the Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Humans. Because this was a retrospective study using routinely collected clinical data and involved no interventional procedures, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for cohort studies.

2.2. Data collection

Data for all eligible neonates were extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system and the standardized PICC insertion record using a predefined data collection form. We captured 4 domains: demographic characteristics, PICC characteristics, maintenance practices, and outcomes, with event onset times recorded where available. Complications were defined according to the Infusion Nurses Society guidelines (2021)[13] and verified using contemporaneous nursing documentation. Two trained reviewers independently extracted the data and cross-checked all entries; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. To ensure data quality, we conducted predefined range checks, applied internal consistency rules, and performed a random audit of 10% of records.

2.4. Grouping and outcome definitions

2.4.1. Study design and grouping

This study comprised 2 complementary analytic components: a cohort-wide risk factor analysis examining time to the first PICC-related complication (complication-free catheter survival) among all eligible neonates between January 2023 and May 2025; and a pre–post evaluation of the optimized nursing pathway implemented in April 2024. For the pre–post evaluation, neonates were categorized into the Standard group (January 2023–March 2024) or the Optimized group (April 2024–May 2025) based on the PICC placement date, and outcomes were compared between groups. 2.4.2. Standard and optimized care pathway

The standard care pathway reflected the existing neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) standard operating procedure routinely used during the pre-implementation period and comprised routine PICC insertion and maintenance practices, including aseptic technique, conventional securement, routine site/dressing assessment and patency checks, and standard management of catheter-related complications. The optimized nursing pathway was developed based on the Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice (INS, 2021)[13] and relevant evidence-based literature.[14–17] It was refined through NICU internal expert review, structured nurse training with competency assessment, pilot implementation, and iterative revisions before unit-wide rollout in April 2024. Compared with the Standard group, the Optimized group introduced structured improvements in 5 main areas: pre-insertion risk management: a comprehensive assessment was conducted before catheter insertion, incorporating GA, birth weight, vascular condition, coagulation profile, and infection status to guide individualized planning. Competency enhancement: standardized staff training and simulation-based workshops were provided, including ultrasound-guided insertion training and emergency scenario drills, to improve technical proficiency and reduce variability. Fixation and maintenance optimization: routine imaging verification of catheter tip position, use of enhanced fixation materials, and structured daily assessment forms supplemented routine visual inspection to support consistent maintenance quality. Complication response: multidisciplinary coordination and predefined response protocols were established for dislodgment, occlusion, infection, and thrombosis to enable early recognition and timely intervention. Family engagement: structured caregiver education was provided during hospitalization, covering catheter care, hygiene, and recognition of early complications to improve caregiver understanding and support continuity of care. No neonates were discharged home with a PICC in situ during the study period. Periodic audits and feedback were conducted after rollout to support implementation fidelity. 2.4.3. Clinical variables, follow-up, and outcomes

Clinical variables were collected and used in the risk factor and stratified analyses, including GA, number of puncture attempts, birth weight, catheter tip position, and sex.

GA at birth was recorded in completed weeks and days. GA categories were defined as extremely preterm (<28 weeks + 0 days), very preterm (28 weeks + 0 days to 31 weeks + 6 days), moderate-to-late preterm (32 weeks + 0 days to 36 weeks + 6 days), term (37 weeks + 0 days to 41 weeks + 6 days), and post-term (≥42 weeks + 0 days), based on the World Health Organization classification and the Defining “Term” Pregnancy Workgroup recommendations.[18–20]

The number of puncture attempts was categorized as a single attempt, 2 to 3 attempts, or 4 or more attempts. Catheter tip position was assessed on post-insertion radiographs. According to the Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice and published literature, the optimal distal tip location is in the superior vena cava or inferior vena cava and clearly outside the cardiac silhouette. Tip location was confirmed using imaging-based methods.[21–23] Tip position was operationalized using vertebral-level landmarks on radiographs and categorized as ideal (T5–T7), acceptable (T4 or T8), or nonideal (T1–T3, T9–T12, catheter folding, or dislodgment). Throughout the study period, neonatal PICCs used in our NICU were Medcomp (Medical Components, Inc.) thermosensitive polyurethane PICC sets with a 1.9 Fr catheter, a 20G introducer sheath, and a 50 cm length (VP1.9S50-C). Specifications were consistent between the Standard and Optimized periods.

The primary outcome was time to the first PICC-related complication, ascertained by the recorded onset/diagnosis time. Secondary outcomes included both clinical and care-quality outcomes. Clinical secondary outcomes included the overall incidence of any PICC-related complication and the distribution of complication types. For the pre–post evaluation of the nursing pathway, care-quality secondary outcomes included caregiver satisfaction scores and adherence scores. Medical and nursing records were reviewed from PICC insertion through 24 to 48 hours after catheter removal to capture complications documented shortly after removal. For time-to-event analyses, follow-up started at PICC insertion. Catheters without a recorded complication were right-censored at catheter removal, and review of records after removal was used for outcome ascertainment and did not extend follow-up time. When subsequent outcome data were unavailable due to transfer or discharge, catheters were right-censored at the last available assessment. Complication-free catheter survival time was calculated as the number of days from insertion to the first complication or censoring.

Caregiver satisfaction was assessed using a standardized 5-point Likert questionnaire (1 = very dissatisfied; 5 = very satisfied), completed by the neonate’s parents or primary caregivers, and adapted from parental satisfaction instruments previously used in neonatal intensive care settings.[24,25] Adherence was evaluated using a structured nursing checklist completed by trained nursing staff, focusing on dressing maintenance, catheter site hygiene, and scheduled inspection compliance.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk), and R software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were summarized as counts (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using the t test or Mann–Whitney U test, based on distribution. Time to the first PICC-related complication was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, with between-group differences assessed by the log-rank test. For the binary outcome of any PICC-related complication (yes/no), crude risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were additionally calculated to summarize between-group differences in cumulative risk. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs, adjusting for prespecified covariates (GA group, birth weight, sex, puncture attempts, and catheter tip position) to estimate independent associations and control for potential confounding. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals; when violations were detected, sensitivity analyses using time-varying Cox models were conducted as appropriate. Satisfaction and adherence scores were compared between groups using the t test or Mann–Whitney U test. All tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant. As this retrospective study included all eligible neonates during the predefined period, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The proportion of missing data was <1% for all variables; therefore, no imputation was performed.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of all neonates enrolled in the study are summarized in Table 1. A total of 309 neonates were included in this study. All eligible neonates meeting the predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria were included. The cohort comprised 198 males and 111 females, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.78:1. The mean birth weight was 1.92 ± 0.80 kg (median 1.65 kg; maximum 4.68 kg). The mean body length was 42.4 ± 5.63 cm (median 42.0 cm), and the mean head circumference was 29.7 ± 3.17 cm (median 29.5 cm). The median length of hospital stay was 33.0 days (mean 41.1 ± 31.7 days; maximum 128 days). The median PICC catheter dwell time was 14.0 days (mean 15.76 ± 8.54 days; maximum 60 days). The median postnatal age at PICC insertion was 4.0 days (mean 8.19 ± 13.39 days; maximum 92 days). Regarding Apgar scores, the mean score at 1 minute after birth was 7.53 ± 1.76, and the mean score at 5 minutes was 8.97 ± 0.95. Based on GA classification, there were 23 extremely preterm neonates (<28 weeks + 0 days, 7.4%), 118 very preterm neonates (28 weeks + 0 days to 31 weeks + 6 days, 38.2%), 107 moderate-to-late preterm neonates (32 weeks + 0 days to 36 weeks + 6 days, 34.6%), and 61 term neonates (37 weeks + 0 days to 41 weeks + 6 days, 19.7%). PICC placement was most commonly indicated for anticipated prolonged intravenous therapy, parenteral nutrition, infusion of hyperosmolar/irritant solutions, and difficult peripheral venous access. At admission, the most frequently recorded diagnoses were prematurity (248 cases), respiratory failure (247 cases), and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (231 cases), followed by low birth weight (203 cases) and extremely low birth weight (104 cases). Infectious conditions were documented in 109 cases, including neonatal pneumonia (36 cases) and sepsis (18 cases). Meconium aspiration syndrome (24 cases) and asphyxia (13 cases) were also recorded. Diagnoses could overlap, so percentages do not sum to 100%. According to the implementation timeline of the optimized nursing care pathway, patients were assigned to the Standard group (n = 146) and the Optimized group (n = 163). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the 2 groups (Table 2), with no statistically significant differences observed, indicating that the 2 groups were comparable at baseline.

3.2. Catheterization and complication profile

Among the 309 neonates, a total of 86 neonates (27.8%) developed PICC-related complications. The most common complication was limb swelling/redness, occurring in 40 neonates (12.9%), followed by catheter blockage/dysfunction in 9 neonates (2.9%), catheter dislodgment/malposition/fracture in 7 neonates (2.3%), mechanical phlebitis in 6 neonates (1.9%), thrombosis in 3 neonates (1.0%), and suspected catheter-related infection in 1 neonate (0.3%). Other rare or miscellaneous complications accounted for 20 neonates (6.5%). There were 223 neonates (72.2%) without complications (Fig. 3).

Distribution of PICC-related complication types. PICC = peripherally inserted central catheter.

3.3. Effect of catheter tip position on catheter survival rate

Survival analysis showed a significant difference in complication-free catheter survival across catheter tip position categories (log-rank test, χ2 = 7.00, degrees of freedom [df] = 2, P = .03). The Kaplan–Meier curves indicated that catheters with nonideal tip positions had the lowest complication-free survival probability (Fig. 4). In univariable Cox regression, compared with the ideal tip position group, the acceptable position group showed a higher hazard of complications, but the difference was not statistically significant (HR = 1.35, 95% CI = 0.81–2.27, P = .253). In contrast, the nonideal tip position group had a significantly higher hazard of complications (HR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.16–2.84, P = .009). In the multivariable Cox model adjusting for GA group, birth weight, neonatal sex, and puncture attempts, both the acceptable and nonideal tip position groups showed higher hazards than the ideal group (acceptable vs ideal: adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.01–3.98, P = .047; nonideal vs ideal: aHR = 3.01, 95% CI = 1.37–6.59, P = .006). The Schoenfeld residual test indicated a mild violation of the proportional hazards assumption for catheter tip position (P = .012), with deviation mainly during the early post-insertion period (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content).

Kaplan–Meier curve and risk table for PICC complication-free survival stratified by tip position. PICC = peripherally inserted central catheter.

3.4. Effect of neonatal gender on catheter survival

Survival analysis by sex showed a significant difference in time to PICC-related complications between male and female neonates (log-rank test, χ2 = 6.7, P = .01). As shown in Figure 5, complication-free survival probability was consistently higher in male neonates than in female neonates, with a more rapid decline over time in females. In univariable Cox regression, female sex was associated with a higher hazard of catheter-related complications compared with male sex (HR = 1.73, 95% CI = 1.14–2.63, P = .011). In the multivariable Cox model adjusting for GA group, birth weight, catheter tip position, and puncture attempts, the association remained (aHR = 1.87, 95% CI = 1.21–3.18, P = .015). The proportional hazards assumption for sex was satisfied (Schoenfeld residual test, P = .45; Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content).

Kaplan–Meier curve and risk table for PICC complication-free survival stratified by gender. PICC = peripherally inserted central catheter.

3.5. Effect of puncture attempts on catheter survival

Survival analysis by puncture attempt category showed a significant difference in time to PICC-related complications (log-rank test, χ2 = 9.00, df = 2, P = .010). As shown in Figure 6, neonates with ≥4 puncture attempts had the lowest complication-free survival probability over time. In univariable Cox regression, compared with the ≥4 puncture attempts group, the single-attempt group had a lower hazard of complications (HR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.28–0.79, P = .004), whereas the 2 to 3 attempts group did not differ significantly (HR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.41–1.33, P = .311). In the multivariable Cox model adjusting for GA group, birth weight, neonatal sex, and catheter tip position, the association for the single-attempt group remained borderline (aHR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.33–1.01, P = .053), while the 2 to 3 attempts group remained nonsignificant (aHR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.39–1.37, P = .331). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (Schoenfeld residual test, P = .24; Fig. S3, Supplemental Digital Content).

Kaplan–Meier curve and risk table for PICC complication-free survival stratified by puncture attempts. PICC = peripherally inserted central catheter.

3.6. Effect of GA on catheter survival

Survival analysis by GA group showed a significant difference in time to PICC-related complications (log-rank test, χ2 = 12.1, df = 3, P = .007). As shown in Figure 7, the extremely preterm group (<28 weeks + 0 days) had the lowest complication-free survival probability. In univariable Cox regression, compared with the extremely preterm group (<28 weeks + 0 days), the moderate-to-late preterm group (32 weeks + 0 days to 36 weeks + 6 days) showed a lower hazard of catheter-related complications, although the difference was not statistically significant (HR = 0.54, 95% CI = 0.23–1.25, P = .149). The term group (37 weeks + 0 days to 41 weeks + 6 days) had a significantly lower hazard (HR = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.14–0.94, P = .037), whereas the very preterm group (28 weeks + 0 days to 31 weeks + 6 days) showed no significant difference (HR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.44–2.29, P = .988). In the multivariable Cox model adjusting for birth weight, neonatal sex, catheter tip position, and puncture attempts, the association for the term group remained (term vs <28 weeks + 0 days: aHR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.18–0.97, P = .042), while the very preterm and moderate-to-late preterm groups remained nonsignificant (28 weeks + 0 days to 31 weeks + 6 days: aHR = 1.06, 95% CI = 0.48–2.34, P = .879; 32 weeks + 0 days to 36 weeks + 6 days: aHR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.26–1.38, P = .231). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (Schoenfeld residual test, P = .36; Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content).

Kaplan–Meier curve and risk table for PICC complication-free survival stratified by gestational age group. PICC = peripherally inserted central catheter.

3.7. Effect of different care pathways on catheter survival

Survival analysis by care pathway group showed a significant difference in time to PICC-related complications between the Standard and Optimized groups (log-rank test, χ2 = 30.5, df = 1, P < .001). As shown in Figure 8, the Optimized group had a higher complication-free survival probability than the Standard group. For any catheter-related complication (yes/no), complications occurred in 54/146 (37.0%) neonates in the Standard group and 32/163 (19.6%) in the Optimized group (Standard vs Optimized risk ratio = 1.88, 95% CI = 1.29–2.74). In univariable Cox regression, the Standard group had a higher hazard of catheter-related complications than the Optimized group (HR = 3.41, 95% CI = 2.15–5.41, P < .001). In the multivariable Cox model adjusting for GA group, birth weight, neonatal sex, puncture attempts, and catheter tip position, the association remained (aHR = 3.50, 95% CI = 2.16–5.67, P < .001). The Schoenfeld residual test suggested a mild violation of the proportional hazards assumption for group (Schoenfeld residual test, P = .049; Fig. S5, Supplemental Digital Content), and time-varying analyses indicated that the between-group difference was not constant over follow-up (Fig. 9).

Analysis of secondary outcomes showed that caregivers in the Optimized group reported higher caregiver satisfaction scores (4.48 ± 0.41) and adherence scores (4.57 ± 0.38) than those in the Standard group (satisfaction: 4.00 ± 0.54; adherence: 4.00 ± 0.49; both P < .001; Fig. 10). As shown in Figure 11, the Optimized group had lower proportions across most complication types, notably limb swelling/redness (6.7% vs 12.3%), catheter occlusion/dysfunction (3.1% vs 6.8%), and catheter dislodgment/displacement/fracture (1.8% vs 5.5%). Mechanical phlebitis (1.8% vs 3.4%), thrombosis (0.6% vs 2.7%), and suspected catheter-related infection (0.6% vs 2.1%) were also less frequent in the Optimized group. In sensitivity analyses excluding neonates with catheter dwell time <5 days, estimates were similar (HR = 3.27, 95% CI = 2.05–5.22, P < .001), with consistent results in the adjusted model.

4. Discussion

We analyzed 309 neonatal PICC insertions to characterize PICC-related complications, complication-free catheter survival, and associated risk factors. Few studies have systematically assessed the real-world impact of optimized nursing pathways on neonatal PICC safety in routine clinical practice. The overall complication rate was 27.8%, consistent with previous reports,[2,26] indicating that PICC-related complications remain a clinical concern despite advances in catheterization techniques. The proportion of suspected catheter-related infection was 0.3%, lower than rates reported in earlier studies,[27,28] which may reflect improved aseptic technique and routine surveillance practices. Mechanical complications, including occlusion, dislodgment, and local signs (e.g., limb swelling/redness), were still frequent, underscoring the need for further refinement of catheter materials and maintenance procedures.

In multivariable Cox regression, catheter tip malposition, GA, and female sex remained independently associated with PICC-related complications. Puncture attempts were also associated with complication hazard; however, after adjustment, the lower risk observed for single-attempt placement compared with ≥4 attempts was borderline (aHR = 0.58, P = .053), with a direction consistent with the unadjusted analysis. Multiple punctures may increase the likelihood of catheter-related mechanical and vascular complications, emphasizing the need for initial placement by experienced operators and the use of ultrasound guidance to reduce attempts. GA also affected catheter survival, with extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks + 0 days) showing the lowest complication-free survival probability, possibly related to vascular fragility and immature immune function.[29] Overall, the findings support the implementation of individualized management strategies for high-risk neonates, including appropriate device selection, accurate tip localization, and enhanced monitoring.

Female neonates had a higher hazard of catheter-related complications than males (HR = 1.73, P = .011), and this association remained after multivariable adjustment (aHR = 1.87, P = .015). In contrast to our results, some previous studies reported higher migration rates in male infants.[30] One possible explanation is differences in outcome definitions, as the present analysis focused on functional and infectious outcomes, whereas earlier studies emphasized mechanical displacement. Possible biological, anatomical, or care-related sex differences require further investigation in larger multicenter cohorts.

Nonideal catheter tip position was independently associated with an increased hazard of complications, consistent with previous findings.[12,26] In our adjusted analysis, nonideal tip position was associated with a substantially higher hazard of complications compared with ideal positioning (aHR = 3.01, P = .006), while the acceptable position category also showed increased hazard (aHR = 2.00, P = .047). Malposition may cause local hemodynamic disturbances, predisposing to occlusion, phlebitis, thrombosis, or drug extravasation. In addition, tip malposition can reflect procedural complexity, including difficult insertion conditions or suboptimal imaging guidance. Given that the proportional hazards assumption showed a mild early-period deviation for tip position, a combined approach using intraoperative ultrasound guidance and postoperative radiographic verification may be considered, particularly in high-risk insertions, to ensure accurate tip positioning and reduce early complications.

The Optimized group was associated with improved complication-free catheter survival and higher caregiver satisfaction in this cohort. Compared with the Optimized group, the Standard group had a significantly higher complication hazard (HR = 3.41, P < .001), particularly within the first 14 days after insertion. Adjusted analyses yielded consistent estimates (aHR = 3.50, P < .001). Caregiver satisfaction and adherence scores were also higher in the Optimized group. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the effect estimates. The improvement likely reflects systematic optimization across multiple stages of care. Pre-insertion risk assessment supported individualized planning for preterm infants, while standardized staff training and imaging guidance may have improved procedural accuracy. Enhanced fixation, structured daily assessment, and early response protocols may have reduced mechanical complications. Family education may have improved adherence and facilitated earlier recognition of adverse events during routine inpatient care. Taken together, the pathway elements established a closed-loop management system integrating evidence-based nursing practices into routine clinical workflows.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Most covariates met the assumption, although minor deviations were observed for catheter tip position and care pathway. To account for this, time-stratified Cox analyses were performed for the care pathway variable, showing that the protective effect of the Optimized group was strongest in the early post-insertion period and persisted over follow-up. Additional stratified analyses for catheter tip position were not conducted because of limited sample size, which could have led to underpowered subgroup estimates. The direction and magnitude of the association between tip malposition and higher complication hazard were consistent across the multivariable Cox model and Kaplan–Meier curves, supporting the robustness of this pattern. These findings emphasize the clinical relevance of accurate catheter tip positioning and suggest that a combined approach using intraoperative ultrasound guidance and postoperative imaging verification may be considered to reduce early complications. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these results and explore potential time-varying risk patterns.

This study was a single-center, before-and-after retrospective cohort, and neonates were not randomized. Group allocation was determined by the PICC insertion period, which may make the analysis susceptible to selection bias and temporal confounding. To address this, we adjusted for key covariates using multivariable models. However, residual confounding from unmeasured operator- and system-level factors cannot be fully excluded, including differences in technique and experience, staffing patterns, and concurrent practice changes over time. In addition, because data were derived from routine clinical records, the level of detail and consistency of some variables may have been limited. We also could not fully quantify implementation fidelity at the individual-catheter level, and variation in adherence to the optimized pathway may have contributed to heterogeneity in observed effects. Finally, as a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings to other NICUs with different patient populations and catheter practices may be limited.

Despite these limitations, this study identified key risk factors for PICC-related complications in neonates and evaluated the effectiveness of an evidence-based nursing pathway. The Optimized group was associated with a reduced hazard of complications and prolonged complication-free catheter survival, providing a structured framework to support data-driven improvements in neonatal intravenous therapy. This framework emphasizes standardization of key PICC care steps, strengthened staff training with audit and feedback, enhanced tip-position management, more consistent maintenance practices, and improved caregiver guidance to support adherence and early recognition of warning signs. In clinical practice, these components can be operationalized through checklists and standardized documentation, targeted competency training, routine monitoring of catheter performance and complication patterns, and feedback-driven quality improvement. Collectively, these measures offer a pragmatic approach to improving catheter safety and complication-free catheter survival. These findings may be most applicable to other tertiary NICUs with comparable patient populations and nursing standards.
