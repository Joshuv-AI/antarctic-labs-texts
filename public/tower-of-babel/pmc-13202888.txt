Genomic insights into Serratia bockelmanii strain 201: unlocking its potential for plant growth promotion and salt stress tolerance
Authors: Naima Sayahi, Nourelhouda Abdelkefi, Chantal Ebel, Tahar Mechichi, Stefaan Werbrouck, Moez Hanin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated Serratia bockelmanii sp. 201 (strain C2), a bacterium isolated from the rhizosphere of Opuntia ficus-indica. This strain demonstrates relevant plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits and enhances salt stress tolerance in crops. While its phenotypic characteristics have been documented, the genomic basis of these traits remains unexplored.ResultsWhole genome sequencing of the strain C2 revealed a 5.24 Mbp circular chromosome with 59.18% GC content, encoding 4,841 protein-coding genes. Pan-genome analysis demonstrated a 99.06% average nucleotide identity (ANI) with Serratia bockelmanii. Functional genomic annotation identified key plant growth-promoting (PGP) genes, including those involved in phosphate solubilization, siderophore biosynthesis, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production, and volatile organic compound (VOC) synthesis. Notably, the genome lacked high-risk antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes while possessing genes associated with stress tolerance. Furthermore, WGS illuminated the pathways and regulatory networks employed by strain C2 to synthesize various growth regulators, enhance nutrient availability, and consequently promote plant health and stress tolerance, with tomato seedling assays specifically confirming VOC-mediated salt stress alleviation by this strain.ConclusionsThis study provides the first genomic evidence for the multifunctional PGP traits and salt stress mitigation mechanisms of Serratia bockelmanii sp. 201. The absence of pathogenic markers supports its potential as a safe biofertilizer for saline agriculture. Moreover, in vitro tomato growth assays demonstrate the beneficial effects of VOCs emitted by Serratia bockelmanii sp. 201. Further studies are needed to profile these VOCs and to validate the performance of this PGPR strain under stress conditions in greenhouse and field trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05060-1.

Background

Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPRs) are beneficial microorganisms that enhance plant health and productivity. These bacteria improve nutrient availability like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium and improve plant health and stress tolerance [1–4]. PGPRs promote plant growth through several mechanisms. They produce phytohormones, such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), cytokinins, and gibberellins, which regulate root elongation, cell division, and shoot development [5]. In addition, PGPRs act as a protective shield against plant pathogens by producing antibiotics and siderophores, and by inducing systemic resistance [6].

These properties make PGPRs valuable tools for enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stresses, such as drought and salinity, as well as biotic stresses, such as fungal pathogens and pests [7, 8]. By harnessing their natural traits, PGPRs were proposed as biofertilizers [1] where PGPR-based formulations have been applied to seeds, soil, or directly to plant roots, offering a versatile approach to improving crop productivity and resilience [9]. These eco-friendly agricultural practices aim to reduce the farmers’ dependence on chemical fertilizers and pesticides, which are ecologically unsustainable and often harmful to human health [10]. However, the efficient global implementation of such sustainable farming practices is currently hindered primarily by a lack of strong understanding of the molecular mechanisms through which these PGPR enhance plant growth and stress tolerance. In recent years, whole genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as a powerful tool for unraveling the genetic basis of plant growth promotion traits in PGPR [11, 12]. Furthermore, WGS provides insights into the pathways and regulatory networks employed by PGPRs to modulate plant hormone levels, enhance nutrient availability, and to protect plants from stress and diseases [13].

Among the diverse genera of PGPR, the genus Serratia is renowned for its versatile metabolic capabilities and its potential to promote plant growth under stress conditions [14–16]. Species of the Serratia genus are known to produce IAA, siderophores, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and other secondary metabolites (i.e. prodigiosin) that enhance plant growth and stress tolerance [17, 18]. However, compared to other PGPR strains, the genetic determinants of Serratia species underlying plant growth promotion, plant health, and stress tolerance remain poorly explored. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and genome mining would significantly help dissect these traits and hence pave the way for its future use as a biofertilizer in sustainable crop production. The objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive genomic analysis of the PGPR strain Serratia sp. (strain C2), isolated from the rhizosphere of Opuntia ficus. This strain has been reported to exhibit multiple plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits and to enhance salt stress tolerance in barley and tomato. Notably, in tomato, the PGP effects of C2 were observed under combined salt stress and viral infection [19, 20]. The in-depth genomic analysis uncovered genetic blueprints for plant growth promotion and stress resilience. This research represents a crucial step toward understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the plant growth-promoting capabilities of Serratia sp. and its potential as a biofertilizer in future sustainable farming practices.

Methods

Bacterial strain

In this study, the strain C2 was isolated by Sayahi et al. 2022 [19]. Whole genome sequencing of strain C2 was supported by GetGenome (https://getgenome.net/) and the Sainsbury Laboratory, Norwich, UK. The WGS was performed by Microbes NG sequencing service, Birmingham, UK [21].

Genome assembly and annotation

Genomic sequencing of the bacterial culture was conducted by Microbes NG (Birmingham, UK), encompassing DNA extraction and sequencing procedures. Genome assembly adhered to protocols detailed on the Microbes NG website (https://microbesng.com/), utilizing Quast software version 5.0.2 for contig assembly from sequence reads. Subsequent genome annotation was performed using Prokka version 1.14.3 (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka), with protein-coding sequences and tRNAs predicted by Prodigal version 2.6, and rRNAs by ARAGORN version 1.2 [22, 23]. The circular genome map of strain C2 was generated using the Proksee database (https://proksee.ca/), and genomic features were analyzed with the RAST database(https://rast.nmpdr.org).

Whole-genome and pan-genome analysis

The rearrangement and the alignment of the genome of C2 with the reference genome was carried out using the mauve software (MAUVE v2.3.1) [24]. We used ‘align with progressive MAUVE’ as a tool for the alignment of C2 and Serratia bockelmanii reference SCPM-O-B-9795 (SDD005SM; GenBank accession no.GCA_029907835.1) downloaded from NCBI. For, the phylogenetic study, the whole genome of strain C2 was compared to fifteen complete genomes available in NCBI databases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). All accessions numbers can be retrieved in Supplementary Table 1. Roary tool from Galaxy (https://usegalaxy.eu/) was used to perform pan-genome analysis to identify and align strain specific and core genes [25]. The phylogenetic tree was inferred with the Neighbour-Joining method [26] using MEGA software [27]. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1000 replicates) is shown next to the branches (bootstrap > 50 is shown) [28]. The tree is drawn to scale, with branch lengths in the same units as those of the evolutionary distances used to infer the phylogenetic tree. The evolutionary distances (number of nucleotide substitutions per site) were computed using the Kimura-2-parameter method [29]. All ambiguous positions were automatically removed for each sequence pair. There was a total of 2,636 positions considered in the final dataset.

Analysis of average nucleotide identity

The estimation of the Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was performed between the whole genomes of the five most closely related strains to strain C2 using Fast ANI tool [30] from Galaxy Version 1.3. Bacterial strains that exhibited more than 95% ANI should belong to the same species [31]. The generated ANI matrix was visualized using cluster heatmap generated with Python v. 3.9.18 from Spyder v5.5.1[32].

Antimicrobial and virulence analysis

The CARD database (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database) was searched for the presence of AMR genes using a homology-based approach (BLASTX) against strain C2 genome sequence [33]. For the search, the BLAST hits were filtered to retain only those with at least 80% sequence identity and coverage of at least 80% of the subject protein's length.

BLAST output was filtered with a minimum of 80% identity and subject protein coverage. Similarly, the VFDB database was used against the assembled genomes with a minimum of 80% identity criteria using a homology-based approach (BLASTX) to identify the virulence genes [34].

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

Functional annotation of genes to KEGG categories and the reconstruction of KEGG pathways modules was performed using Blast KOALA [35]. The eggNOG mapper (v2.1.9) tool from Galaxy was used to assign genes to clusters of orthologous genes (COGs), followed by functional annotation to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories using eggNOG v5.0. Database [36, 37]. Gene sequences that were annotated and predicted were evaluated to assess the presence of specific pathways for plant growth-promoting traits.

Analysis of VOCs effects emitted after PGPR inoculation on plant growth under salt stress

Ten-day-old tomato seedlings were placed in Petri dishes (120 cm × 120 cm) containing Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium. A smaller Petri dish (30 mm) containing Luria Bertani medium, used for C2 culture (5 µL of C2 bacterial suspension (10⁸ CFU/mL)), was placed in the center of each dish. Four different treatments were tested: control (0 mM NaCl), and 150 mM NaCl, with or without bacterial inoculation.

The dishes were sealed with parafilm and incubated in a growth chamber at 23 °C under a 16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod. After 10 days, plant growth parameters, including stem length, root length, root biomass, and fresh weight, were assessed.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with values derived from at least six replicates. A one-way ANOVA was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20) to compare differences among treatments, followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison test to identify statistical significance at P < 0.05. The experiment was repeated three times to ensure consistent results. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0).

Results

Genome assembly and features

The whole genome of the strain C2 has been sequenced, and its general features have been outlined [21]. In this work a comprehensive study was performed, with the focus on all genomic features associated with PGP traits. To assess the genome assembly and annotation completeness of C2, we used BUSCO tools from usegalaxy.eu platform. Our results indicate excellent quality, with a very high completeness level of 99.8%. Out of a total of 440 expected BUSCO genes (n), 439 were identified as complete (C) and assigned as single copy (S) genes. Only 1 expected gene was entirely absent (Missing (M) 0.2%), which is negligible. In addition, neither fragmented (F), nor duplicated (D) genes were found.

The Prokka 14.3 annotation software (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka) showed that strain C2's genome is made up of a single circular chromosome that has approximately 5 Mbp with a GC content of 59,19% and a clear GC skew transition (Fig. 1a). Whole genome sequencing of strain C2 revealed a 92% genetic identity to Serratia bockelmanii (https://pubmlst.org/bigsdb). This high-accuracy identification of C2 supersedes the previous classification of C2 within the Siccibacter genus, which was based solely on partial 16S rRNA sequencing [19].

For subsequent comparative analyses, fifteen strains most closely related to strain C2 were selected based on pubMLST results. Serratia bockelmanii strain SCPM-O-B-9795 (GCF_029907835.1) served as the reference genome for an initial whole-genome sequence comparison. The alignment of the C2 genome with this reference using progressive MAUVE (Multiple Alignment of Conserved Genomic Sequence with Rearrangements) software allowed to assess rearrangements, inversions, duplications, and other complex genomic events. As shown in Fig. 1b, the alignment revealed extensive regions of high similarity, indicating a largely identical chromosomal architecture between the two strains. However, four major inversion events and three notable rearrangements between the C2 genome and the S. bockelmanii reference (SCPM-O-B-9795), were observed.

Phylogenetic tree and comparative analysis

The phylogenetic analysis of C2 and fifteen other Serratia strains was performed based on the available pan genomes (Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2a, a total of 11′974 pan-gene clusters were observed in C2’s genome. The genomic assemblies of the strain C2 identified 3′135 core (shared by all genomes), 5′156 cloud (shared by only one to three genomes), and 3′683 shell (moderately persisted genes that are shared by multiple genomes) genes. Obviously, cloud and shell genes together outbalance the core genes, reflecting genomic diversity, environmental adaptation, and horizontal gene transfer across the different Serratia strains.Fig. 2Phylogenetic analysis: (a) Pangenome pipeline analysis of the strain C2 (b) Average nucleotide identity (ANI) matrix showing genomic relationship performing multi-genome comparison among strain C2 and other strains from the NCBI Database

Phylogenetic tree analysis showed that strain C2 is closely related to S. bockelmanii sma 01, S. bockelmannii sma49, S. bockelmannii sma06, and S. bockelmannii SCPM-O-B-9795. Moreover, the average nucleotide identity (ANI) calculations among these five strains confirmed that S. bockelmanii sma06 and S. bockelmanii sma01 are the closest to strain C2, both exhibiting an ANI value of 99,06% (Fig. 2b), thus definitely placing strain C2 within the S. bockelmanii species.

Functional genomic annotation of the strain C2

Annotation of the strain C2 genome using RAST provided insights into its functional categories, known as subsystems. Approximately 70% of the annotated genes were assigned to known subsystems, while 30% remained unclassified (Fig. 3a). The genome was found to contain a total of 4,841 putative coding DNA sequences (CDSs). Categories with the highest gene counts included amino acid derivatives (394 genes), carbohydrates (358 genes), and protein metabolism (192 genes). Other significant subsystem categories were annotated as cofactors/vitamins (166 genes), fatty acid and lipid metabolism (98 genes), nucleotide metabolism (96 genes), stress response (90 genes), and membrane transporters (64 genes). Additionally, genes involved in sulfur, phosphorus, and aromatic compound metabolisms were detected. Patho-genome of strain C2

The CARD analysis of the strain C2 genome identified a few antibiotic multidrug resistance (AMR) genes, summarized in Table 2. The mexI gene encodes a component of the MexGHI-OpmD efflux complex involved in the efflux of antibiotics such as tetracyclins and fluoroquinolones. The oqxB gene encodes an RND efflux pump also contributing to antibiotic resistance. Genes SRT-1 and H-NS are linked to cephalosporin resistance, while tet (41) encodes a tetracycline efflux pump and AAC (6’)-Ic encodes an aminoglycoside acetyltransferase that inactivates aminoglycoside antibiotics [38, 39]. Furthermore, VFDB analysis identified only three virulence-related genes in C2: fliG, fliM, and fleH, all involved in flagellar motor function and adhesion (Table 3). Genomic basis of plant growth-promoting traits of the strain C2 Nutriment acquisition

For phosphorus utilization, the strain C2 possesses genes (gcd, pqq) involved in gluconic acid production for mineral phosphate solubilization, along with the phn operon for phosphonate degradation and regulatory genes (phoB/R) for phosphorus limitation response (Table 4 and Fig. 5). Additional phosphorus metabolism genes were also detected such as ppaC, ppa, ppx, phoA which facilitates the release of P from organic and other compounds in the environment. Another set of genes, called ugp, helps the strain use glycerol-3-phosphate, which is both a P source and a building block for important cell components. Putative nitrogen metabolism capabilities involve several gene clusters including those related to nitrate/nitrite reduction (narGHIJQ, nirD, nasD) to produce ammonium via a two-step process where nitrate serves as an electron acceptor under anaerobic conditions (Table 5, Fig. 5). This transformation is mediated by nitrate (narGHIJ) and nitrite (nirD, nasD) reductases [40]. Moreover, regulatory genes like narL, narX, and narQ allow the bacterium to sense and respond to nitrate/nitrite availability, modulating gene expression accordingly [40]. The ntaR gene, a regulator of the GntR family, is involved in metabolism regulation, possibly responding to root exudates and environmental nitrogen levels [40]. Auxin production

The strain C2 was previously reported to produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). The IAA production should occur primarily via the tryptophan Trp-dependent indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA) pathway, which is supported via Prokka, RAST, and eggNOG annotations, by the presence of two ipdC (encoding indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase) and aldH/T (catalyzing the final IAA conversion) genes (Table 6, Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3). A complete Trp biosynthesis operon (trpA-E) was also identified, ensuring precursor availability. Additionally, we noticed the presence of a gene, the product of which shares 43% identity with ABP1, a plant auxin-binding protein linked to IAA signalling [41]. GABA and polyamine metabolism

Genome annotation of the strain C2 with Prokka, RAST, and eggNOG annotation tools, revealed the presence of genes associated with γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) synthesis via polyamine catabolism, specifically through the PUU pathway (Fig. 5). Key enzymes identified included Prr_1 and Prr_2 (gamma-aminobutyraldehyde dehydrogenases) and PuuE (4-aminobutyrate aminotransferase). Furthermore, genes involved in polyamine biosynthesis (speA–F, speD, speE_1, speE_2, metK) and transport (potA–E, potF) were detected, indicating efficient polyamine metabolism and recycling within the strain.

Bacterial stress tolerance

The genome of strain C2 revealed numerous genes contributing to stress tolerance (Fig. 6). For instance, genes like mdoH, mdoG, and mdoC, involved in the synthesis of osmo-regulated periplasmic glucans, were identified, alongside betA and betB, which are crucial for the metabolism of osmoprotectants such as choline and betaine. Other genes regulating cellular osmolarity include aqpZ (Aquaporin Z), a water channel protein facilitating rapid water transport across the bacterial membrane, which helps maintain water balance under varying osmotic conditions [43]. Similarly, glpF (Glycerol uptake facilitator protein), a membrane protein, facilitates the uptake of glycerol, an important osmoprotectant that enables bacteria to balance internal osmolarity during hyperosmotic stress [44]. On the other hand, the strain C2 seems to be genetically well equipped to import and accumulate potassium (K+) ions, a well-known osmoticum [45]. For instance, the gene cluster KdpABCDE is crucial for K+ acquisition under low potassium conditions [46]. Additionally, K+ transporters such as Kup and TrkH, and antiporters like ChaA and NhaP2, were found, contributing to K+ homeostasis [47]. Furthermore, the strain C2 might use Na+ extrusion systems [48] to maintain low cytoplasmic sodium (Na+) levels while preserving osmotic balance during stress. This system is represented by a series of sodium transport genes, including nhaA, nhaR, nhaK, nhaB, nhaP, and yrbG which manage Na+ influx and efflux [49].

The genome also contains oxidative stress defense systems, including antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (sodA, sodB, and sodC), hydrogen peroxide catalases (katA and katG), hydroperoxide reductase (ahpC), thiol peroxidase (tpx), peroxiredoxins (bcp), peroxidase (efeB), nitric oxide dioxygenase (hmp). Additional genes involved in ROS detoxification systems were detected on C2’s genome. These systems include glutathione S-transferases (gstB), glutathione peroxidases (gpo and btuE), and the glutaredoxins (grx1234). The ability of C2 to manage osmotic and oxidative stress is further supported by a set of chaperones and proteases. GroL and GroS chaperonins, DnaK and DnaJ chaperones, and the ClpB heat shock protein assist in protein folding and prevent aggregation under thermal and oxidative stresses [50, 51].

Volatile organic compounds production

The genomic analysis of the strain C2 reveals key metabolic traits supporting its potential to emit VOCs. Notably, genes encoding acetolactate synthase (ilv) and acetolactate decarboxylase are present, indicating the capability to biosynthesize acetoin via the acetolactate pathway. Acetoin is a VOC known to enhance plant growth and improve tolerance to various stresses. The absence of genes encoding acetoin reductase suggests that C2 may not convert acetoin into 2,3-butanediol, as observed in Clostridium acetobutylicum and specific mutants of B. subtilis [52, 53]. However, the presence of genes encoding aldehyde and alcohol dehydrogenases suggests possible alternative pathways for the metabolism of acetoin, potentially leading to the generation of other active VOCs such as diacetyl. This metabolic flexibility may allow C2 to modulate its VOC emission profile in response to environmental stressors, including salinity.

The strain C2 VOCs emission enhances tomato growth under salt stress

To investigate the impact of strain C2's VOCs on plant stress response, we conducted an in vitro assay using tomato seedlings grown under standard or salt stress (150 mM NaCl) conditions. Ten-day-old seedlings were cultivated on sealed MS medium plates, with a 10⁸ CFU/ml suspension of C2 centrally applied (see Materials and Methods). As depicted in Fig. 7, the presence of strain C2 stimulated tomato growth on standard MS medium, resulting in a 31.4% increase in fresh weight. This beneficial effect was even more pronounced under salt stress. Seedlings inoculated with C2 exhibited a 24% increase in stem height, a 45% greater fresh weight, and 25% longer roots compared to non-inoculated control. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive genomic characterization of the strain C2, revealing its promising potential as a PGPR suitable for sustainable agricultural applications. The high-quality genome assembly of strain C2, supported by 99.8% BUSCO completeness and a well-annotated chromosomal architecture, confirms the robustness of the sequencing and annotation pipeline. The identification of C2 as Serratia bockelmanii, based on 92% WGS identity with related strains, demonstrates the limitations of relying solely on 16S rRNA for taxonomic classification [19] and underscores the importance of WGS for precise identification. Moreover, the phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses have led to high ANI value (99.06%) with other S. bockelmanii strains, supporting its taxonomic assignment within this species.

Building upon this taxonomic resolution, the observed genome structure characterized by several inversions and rearrangements relative to the reference genome suggests evolutionary divergence within the S. bockelmanii group. These structural variations may affect gene expression or regulation and could confer unique functional capabilities to strain C2. This assumption was further supported by a pan-genomic analysis including other Serratia strains which highlighted the genomic diversity within this genus. The strain C2 maintains a substantial number of core genes shared across strains, but harbors also unique genes that may contribute to its specific functional traits or ecological adaptations. Further exploration of C2's genome should help identify functional genes related to adaptation, competitiveness in the rhizosphere, and/or biotechnological applications.

The functional annotation of the strain C2 genome reveals a broad metabolic versatility, supporting its potential role in plant growth promotion and environmental adaptability. The dominance of genes related to amino acid derivatives, carbohydrate metabolism, and protein metabolism suggests robust biosynthetic and catabolic capacities important for nutrient cycling and interaction with plant hosts, similar to the metabolic framework observed in other PGPRs like Pseudomonas putida [54] and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens [55]. This functional richness is reinforced by the identification of genes involved in stress responses and membrane transport, further highlighting the strain’s capacity to survive and adapt under diverse environmental stresses, similar to the stress adaptation mechanisms documented in Azospirillum brasilens [56].

An additional layer of interest lies in the relatively high proportion of unclassified genes, which highlights the potential for novel functions yet to be explored. These uncharacterized elements could contribute to the unique capabilities of the strain C2.

Safety is another important consideration for PGPR application in farming practices. The presence of several AMR genes in strain C2 suggests some capacity for antibiotic resistance, particularly via efflux pumps such as MexGHI-OpmD and RND systems [57], which may contribute to environmental adaptability rather than clinical pathogenicity. However, the strain C2 lacks many key AMR genes typically found in multidrug-resistant clinical isolates of Serratia spp., including emrE, mexG, mexQ, mexP, golS, qnrB31, qnrB37, aadA16, fosA2 [57, 58], fosA3, opmE, blaOXA-9, blaTEM-1, and sul1 [59–61]. The identified flagellar genes are generally associated with bacterial motility and adhesion but are not linked to virulence, supporting the notion that strain C2 does not harbor significant pathogenic determinants. Overall, these genomic features suggest that the strain C2 is safe for agricultural use as a biofertilizer without posing significant health risks.

Beyond safety, strain C2 exhibits important traits associated with nutrient cycling and plant nutrition. In our previous study, strain C2 was reported to exhibit several PGP traits, such as siderophores and IAA production, as well as phosphate solubilisation [19]. The genomic study of strain C2 unveiled genetic blueprints controlling these PGP traits. The strain C2 seems to have indeed developed multiple systems to acquire and use phosphorus (Table 4 and Fig. 5), making it well-suited to survive under P-limited conditions. It can break down mineral phosphates by producing gluconic acid, thanks to genes like gcd and pqq. This acid helps P release that plants can use, which in turn improves plant growth [62]. The strain C2 can also break down phosphonates, an organic form of phosphate, using phn genes, allowing to use of this source that many other organisms cannot access. Moreover, the strain C2 seems to have special systems to take up P efficiently when it is in short supply. These systems are controlled by genes like phoB and phoR, which help the bacteria sense and adapt to low P-conditions, as previously reported for other beneficial bacteria, like Priestia filamentosa [63]. Additionally, C2 has other enzymes like ppaC, ppa, ppx, and phoA which degrade complex P compounds into simpler, bioavailable forms for cellular use. It is worth noting that along with all these genetic determinants for P solubilisation, the C2 strain genome encodes a phyC-like gene (a β-propeller phytase), a phosphatase that can hydrolyze the phytic acid to release Pi. This phytase activity produced by the strain C2 has resulted in enhanced growth of barley under P deficiency [64]. Other PGPR, like Pseudomonas and Bacillus, have shown similar abilities to improve plant growth and phosphorus uptake in crops like wheat, maize, and rice [65]. Therefore, C2’s ability to get P from different sources makes it also a great candidate for use as a P-biofertilizer, especially in P-deficient fields.

Furthermore, iron can also be utilized by strain C2 (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 2), via the production of siderophores, like many other PGPRs [66]. The strain C2 seems to be able to synthesize enterobactin and aerobactin, catecholate and hydroxamate-type siderophores, respectively (Table 9; Supplementary Fig. 2), both of which are essential for iron acquisition in iron-limited environments. The synthesis of enterobactin is facilitated by the presence of the ent gene cluster, while the fhu and feo gene clusters, enable the production of aerobactin. We previously tested the ability of the strain C2 to synthesize hydroxamates [19]. Moreover, PGPR are known to play a crucial role in improving nitrogen availability for plants by fixing atmospheric nitrogen, solubilizing nitrogen compounds, and facilitating their uptake [67]. The presence of genes involved in nitrogen fixation, dissimilatory nitrate reduction, iron-sulfur cluster assembly, and ammonia production in the genome of strain C2 (Table 5) strongly suggests its capacity to increase nitrogen availability in the soil. It is worth noting the absence of other essential nif genes (required for the nitrogenase activity), ruling out the possibility that this strain performs biological nitrogen fixation, common to symbiotic rhizobacteria. However, other gene clusters (i.e. atzF, urtA-E, nar nir, and nas genes) offer alternatives for nitrogen acquisition from other sources and through other processes. For instance, the strain C2 can perform dissimilatory nitrate reduction, a two-step process where nitrate serves as an electron acceptor under anaerobic conditions and is ultimately transformed into ammonia. This transformation is fulfilled by nitrate (narGHIJ) and nitrite (nirD, nasD) reductases [65]. On another hand, regulatory genes like narL, narX, and narQ allow the bacterium to sense and respond to nitrate/nitrite availability, modulating gene expression accordingly [40]. The ntaR gene, a regulator of the GntR family, is involved in metabolism regulation, possibly responding to root exudates and environmental nitrogen levels [40]. The presence of these genes in strain C2 suggests its potential as a nitrogen biofertilizer, offering a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers in agriculture. Similar traits have been observed in other PGPR, such as Paenibacillus polymyxa, known for its nitrogen fixation capabilities and production of antimicrobial substances that protect plants from pathogens [40].

Genomic analysis further identified critical genes involved in zinc solubilization, and previous phenotypic assays confirmed this trait [40]. By solubilizing insoluble zinc compounds, the strain C2 can significantly improve plant zinc acquisition, mitigating deficiency-related stunting and yield losses [68–70].

Furthermore, PGPRs play a fundamental role in plant development by synthesizing various phytohormones, particularly Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA). This phytohormone is central to root development, nutrient uptake, and plant stress tolerance, making IAA-producing PGPR valuable bio-stimulants in sustainable agriculture [71]. IAA biosynthesis in PGPR occurs through two main pathways: the tryptophan (Trp)-dependent and Trp-independent pathways [72]. The Trp-dependent pathway has been extensively studied and involves multiple routes, including the indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA), indole-3-acetamide (IAM), indole-3-acetonitrile (IAN), tryptamine (TAM), and indole-3-acetaldoxime (IAOx) pathways [73]. Among these, the IPyA and IAM pathways are the most prevalent in bacteria, as they facilitate efficient IAA production [74]. Genomic analysis revealed that the strain C2 seems to predominantly utilize the IPyA pathway for IAA biosynthesis (Fig. 5) as was previously reported for a Serratia marcescens strain [75]. This is reinforced by the presence of two copies of the ipdC gene (ipdC1 and ipdC2) (Table 6), which encode indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase, the key enzyme converting indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA) into indole-3-acetaldehyde (IAAld). The subsequent conversion of IAAld to IAA is facilitated by additional enzymatic steps (controlled by aldH/T genes), confirming the functional integrity of this pathway in strain C2. Strain C2's reliance on the IPyA pathway is further supported by the presence of a complete set of tryptophan biosynthesis genes (trpA, trpB, trpC, trpD, trpE) (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, although this PGPR strain possesses the iaaH gene encoding IAM hydrolase, it lacks the iaaM gene required for the IAM pathway. Experimental quantification of IAA production in strain C2 revealed that under optimal conditions with tryptophan supplementation, the strain produced 9.9 µg/mL of IAA, whereas in the absence of tryptophan, the production was 0.7 µg/mL [19], similar amounts to those observed in Pseudomonas fluorescens strain RE1 [76] further confirming its ability to produce IAA via the Trp-dependent IPyA pathway. Remarkably, beyond IAA production, the strain C2 genome possesses a gene encoding protein sharing a significant homology with ABP1, a member of the plant auxin-binding proteins (ABPs), which play a critical role in auxin perception and signaling, by modulating key developmental processes, including cell elongation, division, and differentiation, [77, 78]. More specifically, ABPs function by binding IAA and triggering rapid cellular responses, such as the activation of proton pumps and cytoskeletal rearrangements, which directly impact plant cell growth. Furthermore, ABPs interact with auxin signaling components like TIR1/AFB, which regulate gene expression involved in plant development and stress adaptation [79]. Therefore, the presence of a protein homologous to ABP1 in C2 suggests that this PGPR could optimize auxin signalling within the plant, thereby improving root architecture, and enhancing stress resilience.

PGPR colonize plant roots through various mechanisms, including chemotaxis and attachment. In the genome of strain C2, the fli genes responsible for producing the flagellar protein and motor system [80] were retrieved. It also has a cluster of che, mgl and rbs genes, the products of which are known to play a crucial role in bacterial chemotaxis and sugar transport pathways, ensuring the efficient uptake and utilization of nutrients [81, 82].

Parallel to its root colonization, strain C2's GABA metabolism provides another layer of stress adaptation. Previous studies have shown that the application of exogenous GABA can effectively reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, enhance membrane stability, and modulate phytohormone signaling, thereby improving tolerance to multiple stresses. Several key components involved in GABA metabolism were noted in the genome of C2, suggesting its ability to synthesize and utilize this metabolite (Fig. 5). However, the apparent absence of the gadA and gadB genes, which encode glutamate decarboxylases responsible for the direct conversion of glutamate to GABA, suggests that the primary GABA synthesis pathway in this strain might proceed through the catabolism of polyamines such as putrescine, spermidine, and spermine. The presence in the genome of C2 of several gene clusters involved in the synthesis of polyamines such as puuA_1, puuB_2, and puuC or speB, speA, speD, speC, speE, speF, and metK genes, corroborates this assumption. Bacterial-derived polyamines have been also shown to mitigate oxidative stress, enhance membrane stability, and improve plant resilience under abiotic and biotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and pathogen attack [83].

Phenotypic analyses revealed the capacities of the strain C2 to produce many hydrolytic activities such as amylase, lipase, protease, urease and cellulase [19]. The C2 genome analysis confirmed the presence of the genes responsible for these activities (Fig. 4). For instance, four genes bcsA, B, C and Z are involved in cellulase activities, and three chitinase encoding genes (chiA, chiA1 and chiB) were identified. When synthesized by PGPR, these enzymes contribute to biocontrol mechanisms by breaking down chitin, a major structural component of fungal cell walls, insects, and nematodes. Similar PGPRs belonging to Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Serratia, and Streptomyces genera produce chitinases that degrade fungal pathogens (Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Botrytis), triggering induced systemic resistance (ISR) in plants. Besides, the C2 genome hosts several genes encoding secondary metabolites known for their role in plant health and pathogen suppression (Table 8). Among those, the antimicrobial peptides microcin H47 and actinomycins [84–86] as well as the siderophore viobactin, which by sequestering iron, limit its availability to pathogenic microbes, restricting their growth while improving the survival of the producing strain [87, 88].

The strong PGP potential of strain C2 has been clearly demonstrated, along with its stress tolerance capacity enhancing its applicability across diverse environmental conditions. Various genes responsible for osmotic stress tolerance were found (Fig. 6) especially genes involved in the synthesis of osmo-regulated periplasmic glucans (OPGs) which are essential for cell integrity by retaining water and maintaining periplasmic pressure under hypoosmotic conditions [89]. Other genes participating in the biosynthesis of choline and betaine, which as osmoprotectants can help C2 ensure cellular stability and survival under stress conditions [90]. The water channel encoding gene aqpZ, the K+ uptake and (KdpABCDE) the Na+ (nhaA, nhaR, nhaK, nhaB, nhaP, and yrbG) transport systems may also contribute to preserving the cellular integrity under osmotic stress [91–97].

On the other hand, the C2 genome analysis revealed the presence of acetolactate synthase (ilv) and acetolactate decarboxylase, two key genes necessary for the acetoin biosynthesis via the acetolactate pathway. Acetoin is one of the most characterized VOCs and was reported to enhance plant growth and stress tolerance [98]. Acetoin might not be the only VOC produced by C2, as we identified aldehyde and alcohol dehydrogenase genes which could participate in acetoin metabolism, leading to the generation of other active VOCs such as diacetyl. As low-molecular-weight compounds that can diffuse through air and soil, these VOCs produced by various PGPR were shown to play a significant role in plant–microbe interactions, enhance plant growth, activate plant immune responses, and improve stress tolerance [99]. These VOCs are particularly important in saline environments, where they help plants cope with high salt concentrations by modulating stress-related signaling pathways and enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms [100]. It is worth noting that in our in vitro salt stress assays, we used a concentration of 150 mM NaCl, which has been widely applied in previous tomato salinity studies [101–103]. Moreover, our preliminary tests indicated that this concentration elicited reproducible physiological responses to VOCs emitted by strain C2, making it suitable for a sealed in vitro system. Our in vitro assays showed that VOCs produced by the strain C2 seem to enhance the tolerance of tomato seedlings to NaCl treatment, which was well illustrated by a significant increase in stem height, fresh weight, and root length. Such positive effect has also been observed with other PGPR producing VOCs such as the Pseudomonas chlororaphis O6 and the Bacillus GB03 strain [104].

Together, these functional genomic annotations and the in vitro growth assays highlight the multifaceted mechanisms by which strain C2 may promote plant growth and stress tolerance. While phenotypic improvements and genomic evidence suggest a potential involvement of strain C2's VOCs in salt stress alleviation, further chemical characterization would be required to confirm their identity and functional contribution. Future work will also include greenhouse and field trials incorporating multivariate analyses of multiple growth and yield traits at different developmental stages, to validate these genomic predictions under agronomically realistic conditions.

Conclusions

The genomic analysis of Serratia sp. revealed the presence of a wide range of genes involved in PGP traits, such as phosphate solubilization, dissimilatory nitrate reduction, as well as the production of siderophores, IAA and VOCs. The strain C2 demonstrates significant potential as a plant growth-promoting rhizobacterium (PGPR), capable of enhancing tomato tolerance to salt stress, which seems to involve VOC’s production. Moreover, the genomic insights presented here collectively support the strain’s potential as a bioinoculant with diverse functions in enhancing crop growth and resilience. Host-targeted metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses will further elucidate the functional contributions of PGP and stress-related pathways, as well as strain C2–emitted VOCs, in enhancing tomato salt stress tolerance. This current research constitutes a crucial milestone towards elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which this PGPR enhances plant tolerance to abiotic and biotic stresses, thereby supporting its potential as a safe, eco-friendly biofertilizer for sustainable agriculture.
