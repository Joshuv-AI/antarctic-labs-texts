Learning like a radiologist: a medical vision-language model for radiological image analysis via curriculum learning
Authors: Minhui Tan, Qingxia Wu, Boyang Zhang, Genqiang Ren, Jianlong Nie, Zhong Xue, Yiqiang Zhan, Sean Zhou, Xiaohuan Cao, Dinggang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical vision-language models (MVLMs) offer promise in clinical practice but face limitations in generalizability, data quality, and clinically meaningful evaluation. We propose RadiSim-CL, an MVLM trained via curriculum learning by simulating the three-phase pathway of a radiologist: foundational knowledge understanding, anatomical knowledge, and advanced diagnostic reasoning. To support this, we curate RadiSim, a 12-million image-text pair dataset aligned to these phases. We evaluate the model using a five-stage coarse-to-fine validation framework: (1) modality recognition, (2) anatomical recognition, (3) anatomical localization, (4) abnormality and disease diagnosis, and (5) disease differentiation and grading. This framework spans 24 zero-shot subtasks across MR, CT, and DR imaging. RadiSim-CL achieves comparable performance to state-of-the-art baselines in both foundational and anatomical tasks, and demonstrates superior capabilities in complex reasoning (e.g., an AUC of 0.953 for brain tumor diagnosis and an accuracy of 0.764 for meningioma grading). Ablation studies further confirm the curriculum’s effectiveness. RadiSim-CL thus offers a scalable, clinically aligned solution to enhance diagnostic precision.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Radiological imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and digital radiography (DR), are indispensable tools for diagnosis and treatment. However, the exponential growth in medical image volumes, which outpaces the healthcare workforce’s 0.7% annual growth1, presents unprecedented challenges to radiologists. This imbalance leads to increased workload, potential diagnostic delays, and an elevated risk of misdiagnosis. Consequently, there is an urgent need for advanced intelligent tools capable of enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency in clinical workflows.

Currently, the rapid advancement of medical vision-language models (MVLMs)1–23 presents a potential solution to the aforementioned challenges. However, existing models exhibit three limitations in clinical applications. First, some MVLMs2–12,20–24 focus on specific diseases or single imaging modalities (e.g., EchoCLIP8 for echocardiograms, and CT-GLIP20 for CT imaging). This inherent specificity fundamentally limits their generalizability, making them less effective for radiologists who routinely interpret a broad spectrum of diverse imaging types. Second, although some general-purpose models13–15,19,25 leverage large-scale publicly available datasets (such as those derived from PubMed Central (PMC)26) for vision-language alignment and supervised fine-tuning, their clinical applicability remains constrained. These datasets primarily suffer from suboptimal quality and noise, and often lack the structured and expert-verified annotations crucial for robust medical image understanding27. Third, current evaluation methods16,17 often focus on a limited set of tasks (e.g., question answering) and fail to reflect the hierarchical and progressive learning pathway characteristic of radiologists, beginning from foundational knowledge to highly specialized diagnostic judgments (Table 1). This deficiency restricts a comprehensive validation of model generalization capabilities across diverse modalities and diagnostic complexities.

To address these limitations, we propose RadiSim-CL, an MVLM trained via curriculum learning28 by simulating how radiologists gradually learn and acquire expertise. This learning paradigm is organized into three progressive knowledge-acquisition phases: foundational knowledge understanding, anatomical knowledge acquisition, and advanced diagnostic reasoning. Supporting this framework is RadiSim, a large-scale, multi-source radiology dataset comprising 12 million high-quality image-text pairs. These data are gathered and processed through a systematic pipeline to ensure both semantic consistency and clinical relevance. The dataset is further divided into three subsets aligned with distinct phases of training: general medical images from PMC, images with structured anatomical texts, and images with medical reports (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1). To effectively learn from these curated image-text pairs, RadiSim-CL adopts the sigmoid contrastive loss from SigLIP29. This sigmoid loss, combined with the curriculum-driven data organization of RadiSim, enables the overall model to robustly acquire knowledge (Fig. 1B).

For robust evaluation, we introduce a five-stage coarse-to-fine validation framework (Fig. 1C). This framework is explicitly aligned with the radiological training workflow, and is designed to assess the model’s acquisition of the three aforementioned knowledge types across different training phases. Specifically, the framework includes: (1) modality recognition, used to assess foundational understanding; (2) anatomical recognition and (3) anatomical localization, used to evaluate anatomical knowledge acquisition; (4) abnormality and disease diagnosis and (5) disease differentiation and grading, used to gauge advanced diagnostic reasoning. This comprehensive framework further comprises 24 zero-shot subtasks, ensuring a thorough assessment across MR, CT, and DR modalities.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that RadiSim-CL achieves competitive performance in foundational and anatomical tasks while surpassing baselines in complex reasoning tasks. Specifically, the model exhibits superior zero-shot capabilities in fine-grained tasks such as brain tumor subtyping, meningioma grading, and lung adenocarcinoma differentiation, outperforming existing state-of-the-art models. Furthermore, ablation studies confirm the critical role of our curriculum learning strategy, showing that aligning training phases with clinical logic enables the model to generalize effectively across diagnostic hierarchies without task-specific fine-tuning. Overall, RadiSim-CL establishes a scalable, clinically aligned paradigm for medical AI, offering the potential to enhance diagnostic precision and consistency in real-world workflows.

Results

Overall performance

Our evaluation employs a comprehensive five-stage coarse-to-fine validation framework designed to reflect the diagnostic workflow commonly used by radiologists. This framework progresses from initial image contextual understanding to detailed pathological assessment, using the diagnosis and grading of Lung Adenocarcinoma (LAC) from a CT scan as an illustrative example (Fig. 1C). The initial step involves accurately identifying the imaging modality (e.g., distinguishing a CT scan from MRI or DR). Following modality recognition, the model assesses major anatomical structures (e.g., identifying the heart and lungs within the CT scan). Subsequently, the model precisely localizes specific anatomical regions within the image (e.g., pinpointing the heart and lungs). Building upon accurate localization, the model transitions to detect and diagnose pathologies (e.g., classifying the CT with an LAC). Finally, at the highest level of reasoning, the model differentiates disease subtypes and assesses severity (e.g., classifying LAC into subtypes such as Atypical Adenomatous Hyperplasia (AAH), Minimally Invasive Adenocarcinoma (MIA), and Invasive Adenocarcinoma (IAC), and further grading invasive adenocarcinoma into well-differentiated (LC-IAC), moderately differentiated (IC-IAC), or poorly differentiated (HC-IAC) categories).

The following subsections detail RadiSim-CL’s performance across each of the above five stages, presenting the specific validation tasks and datasets for each of the 24 zero-shot subtasks. Our model outperforms existing VLMs across diverse modalities and tasks without task-specific fine-tuning.

Performance of modality recognition

Accurate recognition of imaging modalities is essential in hierarchical radiological reasoning, as interpretation strategies are influenced by the modality used. For instance, CT is often prioritized for detecting acute hemorrhage, while MRI is favored for detailed soft-tissue evaluation. To validate this foundational capability, we assessed the zero-shot classification performance of RadiSim-CL across MR, CT, and DR using a diverse collection of public and in-house datasets. Specifically, MR images were sourced from CHAOS30, AMOS31, TotalSegmentator-MR32, and RAOS33, CT images were obtained from CT-ORG34 and TotalSegmentator-CT35, and DR images were drawn from an internal clinical archive (as detailed in Supplementary Table S2).

To benchmark our architecture and training strategy, we compared RadiSim-CL with two baseline categories: CLIP-based medical models (CLIP36, MedCLIP25, PMC-CLIP13, PubMedCLIP19, and BiomedCLIP14) and SigLIP-based models, including both the general-domain SigLIP37 and the medical generalist MedSigLIP38.

As detailed in Supplementary Table S3, RadiSim-CL demonstrated high performance on the aggregated three-class classification task, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.996 (95% CI: 0.993–0.998). Performance remained consistent across individual modalities, with accuracies of 0.997 (95% CI: 0.995–0.999) for MR, 0.998 (95% CI: 0.996–0.999) for CT, and 0.996 (95% CI: 0.994–0.998) for DR. The narrow confidence intervals further indicate stable generalization across diverse test samples.

Among CLIP-based models, BiomedCLIP emerged as the strongest competitor, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.982 (95% CI: 0.977–0.986). However, it fell short of our model’s accuracy in MR (0.994, 95% CI: 0.991–0.996) and CT (0.988, 95% CI: 0.985–0.992). Other models, including CLIP, MedCLIP, PMC-CLIP, and PubMedCLIP, demonstrated markedly lower performance, particularly for CT recognition, where CLIP achieved an accuracy of only 0.331 (95% CI: 0.316–0.346) accuracy.

In the SigLIP-based comparison, our model consistently outperformed both the general-domain SigLIP (overall: 0.989, 95% CI: 0.986–0.992) and the medical generalist MedSigLIP (overall: 0.984, 95% CI: 0.979–0.988). While MedSigLIP showed a slight advantage in the DR sub-task (1.000 vs. 0.996), RadiSim-CL maintained a balanced superiority across the more information-dense MR and CT modalities.

Performance of anatomical recognition

As the subsequent step in hierarchical image understanding, we evaluated the model’s zero-shot capability to identify specific anatomical structures, validating fundamental image-text associations within relevant clinical contexts. Evaluations were conducted separately for MR and CT modalities (Fig. 2A–C and Supplementary Table S4).

For MR imaging, we conducted a ten-organ multi-label classification task on MR scans using the TotalSegmentator-MR dataset, which includes organs from multiple anatomical regions commonly encountered in clinical imaging. As shown in Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table S4, RadiSim-CL achieved the highest Macro AUC among all compared methods, with a value of 0.888 (95% CI: 0.885-0.891). This performance exceeded that of CLIP-based models, including BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.879, 95% CI: 0.876–0.883), PubMedCLIP (AUC: 0.589, 95% CI: 0.584–0.594), and standard CLIP (AUC: 0.464, 95% CI: 0.459–0.469). RadiSim-CL also outperformed SigLIP-based models, including MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.791, 95% CI: 0.786–0.796) and SigLIP (AUC: 0.603, 95% CI: 0.597–0.609).

For CT imaging, performance was evaluated on an eight-organ classification task across axial, coronal, and sagittal views using the 2D MediMeTA Abdominal CT dataset39 to test robustness against visual variability. As shown in Fig. 2B and reported in Supplementary Table S4, RadiSim-CL achieved accuracies of 0.765 (95% CI: 0.745–0.785) for the axial view, 0.711 (95% CI: 0.689–0.733) for the coronal view, and 0.683 (95% CI: 0.661–0.705) for the sagittal view. Among the baselines, MedSigLIP yielded the second-best results on the axial (0.650, 95% CI: 0.627–0.673) and sagittal (0.618, 95% CI: 0.594–0.641) views, while BiomedCLIP ranked second on the coronal view (0.644, 95% CI: 0.621–0.667). General-domain models and other medical baselines recorded lower accuracy scores. Specifically, while SigLIP showed numerically higher accuracy compared to CLIP, both models, along with the remaining baselines (e.g., MedCLIP, PMC-CLIP), maintained accuracies below 0.60 across all three imaging planes.

The confusion matrices in Fig. 2C further illustrate organ-level performance. On MR images, higher classification accuracy was observed for large organs such as the brain, heart, and liver, whereas lower accuracy was observed for smaller organs, particularly the pancreas. For CT imaging, the model maintained robust recognition of the heart and bladder across all views, with minimum accuracies of 86.3% and 74.9%, respectively. Notably, the model maintained consistent performance across all acquisition planes, despite the varying visual presentations of these structures. These findings underscore the model’s favorable understanding of anatomical structures and its ability to generalize in zero-shot scenarios, highlighting its potential for real-world clinical applications.

Performance of anatomical localization

Precise localization not only enhances diagnostic accuracy by guiding attention to relevant regions but also demonstrates the model’s understanding of spatial and morphological relationships within medical images. Hence, we further evaluated RadiSim-CL’s zero-shot capability in anatomical localization.

To evaluate this capability, we conducted zero-shot localization tasks across multiple organ systems utilizing both internal and external datasets. Performance was quantitatively measured using F1 scores, which evaluated the overlap between detection boxes derived from the model-generated attention maps and the anatomical regions annotated by experts. The evaluation datasets comprised our internal validation set and the publicly available benchmarks, including AMOS-CT, CT-ORG, and CHAOS-MR.

RadiSim-CL demonstrated superior localization performance compared to existing VLMs (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S5). In the internal validation dataset, it achieved the highest concordance with ground-truth boundaries for six out of nine organ categories. On external datasets, the model outperformed baseline methods (e.g., CLIP, PubMedCLIP, and BiomedCLIP) in over half of the evaluated organ categories. These results reflect generalization across various imaging modalities and anatomical structures without the need for task-specific fine-tuning.

A qualitative analysis of attention visualizations (Fig. 3) across organs, including the kidneys, pancreas, liver, and lungs, revealed three clinically relevant findings. First, RadiSim-CL exhibited precise attention localization, with activation concentrated in the central regions of target organs, closely corresponding to expert-annotated anatomical landmarks. Second, the spatial extent of the model’s attention closely aligned with the morphological contours of the organs, exhibiting delineations that resemble expert-annotated ground-truth regions. Finally, the model effectively distinguishes between paired organs (e.g., left and right kidneys) suggesting sensitivity to intensity-based contrast cues and understanding of bilateral anatomical symmetry and relationships. These results demonstrate that RadiSim-CL has learned anatomical knowledge with good interpretability, underscoring the benefits of our progressive, curriculum-based learning strategy.

Performance of abnormality and disease diagnosis

To evaluate the ability of RadiSim-CL to support pathology-related decision-making, we designed a series of zero-shot binary classification tasks for abnormality detection and disease diagnosis across MR, CT, and DR imaging modalities. The comparative results are visualized in Fig. 2E, with detailed performance metrics and confidence intervals provided in Supplementary Table S6.

For MR imaging, we tested brain tumor detection using the Br35H dataset40, and knee anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and meniscus injury identification using the MRNet dataset41. For brain tumor detection, RadiSim-CL achieved an AUC of 0.953 (95% CI: 0.945–0.961). Although MedSigLIP achieved a higher AUC of 0.980 (95% CI: 0.975–0.985), our model significantly outperformed BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.917, 95% CI: 0.907–0.927), SigLIP (AUC: 0.942, 95% CI: 0.934–0.950), and CLIP (AUC: 0.593, 95% CI: 0.571–0.613). For ACL tear detection, RadiSim-CL achieved an AUC of 0.725 (95% CI: 0.687–0.760), surpassing MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.548, 95% CI: 0.510–0.590) and BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.443, 95% CI: 0.405–0.483) by substantial margins. Similarly, for meniscus tears, our model (AUC: 0.643, 95% CI: 0.609–0.675) outperformed both MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.583, 95% CI: 0.551–0.614) and SigLIP (AUC: 0.524, 95% CI: 0.492–0.557).

For CT imaging, RadiSim-CL consistently outperformed all baselines across brain hemorrhage detection42,43, COVID-19 diagnosis44, and lymph node enlargement assessment42,45. For brain hemorrhage detection, it achieved exceptional performance (AUC: 0.902, 95% CI: 0.856–0.942), surpassing MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.848, 95% CI: 0.804–0.886). For COVID-19 diagnosis, our model achieved an AUC of 0.947 (95% CI: 0.926–0.963), outperforming BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.851, 95% CI: 0.820–0.878) and MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.659, 95% CI: 0.620–0.695). Notably, in the challenging lymph node assessment, RadiSim-CL achieved an AUC of 0.777 (95% CI: 0.747–0.806), while MedSigLIP (AUC: 0.509, 95% CI: 0.473–0.544) and SigLIP (AUC: 0.628, 95% CI: 0.593–0.661) showed lower performance.

For DR imaging, we evaluated pneumonia detection using both the PneumoniaMNIST46 and RSNA datasets47. MedSigLIP maintained a performance edge in this modality (e.g., PneumoniaMNIST AUC: 0.898, 95% CI: 0.874–0.922), reflecting its extensive X-ray pre-training. Nevertheless, RadiSim-CL demonstrated strong competitiveness against other benchmarks. It achieved an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.820–0.881) on PneumoniaMNIST, surpassing BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.808, 95% CI: 0.772–0.839) and SigLIP (AUC: 0.796, 95% CI: 0.761–0.832). On the RSNA dataset, our model (AUC: 0.761, 95% CI: 0.752–0.771) performed comparably to BiomedCLIP (AUC: 0.765, 95% CI: 0.756–0.775).

To further validate the discriminability of the learned features, we visualized feature embeddings using t-SNE for two tasks: brain tumor detection from MRI and pneumonia identification from DR images (PneumoniaMNIST). As shown in Fig. 2D, the plots exhibit a clear separation between normal and abnormal samples in both tasks, highlighting RadiSim-CL’s ability to distinguish between healthy and pathological cases. The model’s robust performance, achieved without task-specific fine-tuning, underscores its generalizable capacity to identify clinical abnormalities, representing a substantial advancement in medical image analysis capabilities.

Performance of disease differentiation and grading

Our assessment further extends to the highest level of radiological reasoning: differentiation of disease subtypes and severity levels, which is crucial for clinical management decisions. Validation was performed on internal MR and CT datasets as well as an independent publicly available external dataset48, with quantitative results detailed in Table S7 and qualitative results shown in Fig. 4B.

For the internal brain MR tasks, we evaluated tumor subtyping (glioma, meningioma, and solitary fibrous tumor) and meningioma grading (WHO grades 1–3). RadiSim-CL demonstrated superior fine-grained discrimination. In tumor subtyping, it achieved an accuracy of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.750–0.812), significantly surpassing the next-best performer, BiomedCLIP (0.613, 95% CI: 0.577–0.649). Notably, the medical generalist MedSigLIP struggled with this semantic differentiation, yielding an accuracy of 0.212. For meningioma grading, our model attained an accuracy of 0.764 (95% CI: 0.728–0.801). This performance exceeded MedCLIP (0.668, 95% CI: 0.627–0.709) and far outperformed BiomedCLIP (0.349, 95% CI: 0.308–0.390) and MedSigLIP (0.402, 95% CI: 0.359–0.444), indicating robust sensitivity to subtle structural changes associated with tumor aggressiveness.

For Lung CT imaging, we conducted a preliminary evaluation on LAC pathology prediction. For subtype classification, we performed a three-class differentiation among AAH, MIA, and IAC. RadiSim-CL achieved an accuracy of 0.806 (95% CI: 0.732–0.880), outperforming BiomedCLIP (0.648, 95% CI: 0.559–0.737) and MedSigLIP (0.546, 95% CI: 0.452–0.640). It should be noted that the confidence intervals for LAC subtyping are relatively wide due to the limited sample size of histopathologically confirmed cases (N = 108). Thus, these results should be interpreted as a preliminary validation of the model’s hierarchical reasoning capability. In the grading of invasive adenocarcinoma, we classified tumors into well-differentiated (LC-IAC), moderately differentiated (IC-IAC), and poorly differentiated (HC-IAC) categories. In this task, RadiSim-CL also demonstrated superior performance with an accuracy of 0.775 (95% CI: 0.748–0.802), compared with BiomedCLIP (0.544, 95% CI: 0.511–0.576).

To explicitly validate the model’s generalizability, we further tested it on an external MR dataset for brain tumor subtyping (glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumor). RadiSim-CL maintained its leading performance with an accuracy of 0.835 (95% CI: 0.822–0.848), outperforming the second-best model, BiomedCLIP (0.727, 95% CI: 0.711–0.743), with non-overlapping confidence intervals.

These results consistently illustrate RadiSim-CL’s exceptional capability in distinguishing subtle imaging features that are critical for disease subtyping and grading across different anatomical regions and modalities, supporting its potential to advance AI-assisted radiological assessment.

Impact of curriculum learning strategy

To evaluate the contribution of the curriculum training strategy while controlling for dataset scale, we conducted an ablation study comparing the proposed RadiSim-CL against a baseline model trained uniformly on the RadiSim (denoted as RadiSim-Uniform). We further analyzed intermediate checkpoints (RadiSim-CL1 and RadiSim-CL2) to trace the trajectory of capability acquisition (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S8).

Ablation study demonstrating the impact of the curriculum learning strategy.

Performance comparison between the proposed curriculum-based training (RadiSim-CL) and a uniform training baseline (RadiSim-Uniform), alongside intermediate checkpoints. A Accuracy in modality recognition. B Performance in anatomical recognition tasks. C AUC scores in abnormality and disease diagnosis. D Accuracy in fine-grained disease differentiation and grading.

Our quantitative comparisons between curriculum learning and uniform training revealed observable performance differences despite the use of identical training data. While the RadiSim-Uniform achieved comparable accuracy in foundational tasks such as modality recognition (overall ACC: 0.989, 95% CI: 0.986–0.992), it showed limitations in complex diagnostic reasoning tasks. For instance, in pneumonia diagnosis (PneumoniaMNIST), the RadiSim-Uniform performed poorly with an AUC of 0.315 (95% CI: 0.275–0.355), whereas the curriculum-trained RadiSim-CL achieved an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.820–0.881). Similarly, in knee ACL tear detection, the RadiSim-Uniform (AUC: 0.530, 95% CI: 0.493–0.570) underperformed compared with the final model (AUC: 0.725, 95% CI: 0.687–0.760). These results indicate that the curriculum strategy is associated with improved fine-grained feature learning, whereas uniform training demonstrates reduced performance on tasks requiring high-level pathological semantics.

Beyond the final performance gain, the phase-wise trajectory of these improvements supports the proposed “foundation-to-expert” learning strategy. Specifically, following training on foundational knowledge data, RadiSim-CL1 achieved high accuracy in modality recognition (overall: 0.986). However, its performance on anatomical and diagnostic tasks was limited, such as CT axial-view organ recognition (ACC: 0.527) and lung adenocarcinoma subtype classification (ACC: 0.417). After adding structure-related data, the model showed clear improvement in anatomical tasks. For example, in CT axial-view organ recognition, accuracy increased from 0.527 to 0.686. Initial improvements were also observed in diagnostic tasks, with the knee ACL tear detection AUC increasing from 0.534 to 0.565. The final training phase led to the largest improvement in disease classification. The AUC for brain tumor detection increased from 0.928 to 0.953. For more challenging grading tasks, such as meningioma grading, accuracy increased from 0.494 to 0.764, indicating a clear performance gain after diagnostic-level training.

Overall, these results show that the curriculum learning strategy improves performance progressively, with each training stage contributing to the final diagnostic capability of the model.

Discussion

In this study, we present RadiSim-CL, an MVLM developed by simulating the progressive learning pathway of radiologists through structured stagewise exposure to increasingly complex cases. Its design integrates three aligned components: a hierarchically structured dataset (RadiSim), a curriculum learning strategy, and a coarse-to-fine evaluation framework. This design enables the model to acquire clinical knowledge in a systematic manner, spanning foundational knowledge understanding, anatomical knowledge acquisition, and advanced diagnostic reasoning, and to demonstrate robust and interpretable performance across imaging modalities and diagnostic tasks in a zero-shot setting.

A key to RadiSim-CL’s performance lies in the co-design of the dataset and training strategy. Instead of relying on a single, mixed dataset and uniform training, RadiSim is structured into three progressive levels: general medical image-text pairs, anatomically labeled examples, and images with radiology reports. To effectively leverage this structure, we employed a curriculum learning strategy that progressively introduces these data subsets. The curriculum order is not merely an empirical choice but is designed to reflect the training hierarchy of clinical expertise. This ensures that the model first builds a solid foundation in anatomical semantics before progressing to high-level diagnostic reasoning. Our ablation study confirms that this structured exposure is critical. We observed that uniform training strategies often performed poorly on the complex diagnostic tasks (e.g., in pneumonia diagnosis), likely due to the difficulty of extracting high-level pathological semantics from noisy data without prior structural knowledge. In contrast, the curriculum-based approach was associated with improved performance on these tasks, underscoring the necessity of sequential learning for medical AI. To support efficient learning at this scale, we adopted the sigmoid loss from the SigLIP framework. This choice not only facilitates stable optimization and scalable training on massive datasets but also enables the model to effectively integrate complex clinical knowledge over time, providing the technical stability required for our curriculum-based approach.

To validate this progressive learning hypothesis, we established a five-stage hierarchical evaluation framework that mirrors the workflow of radiologists, ranging from modality recognition to disease differentiation. Performance across 24 subtasks supports the efficacy of this design. While baseline models often struggle as complexity increases, RadiSim-CL maintains robust performance throughout the diagnostic chain. Crucially, the significant performance margin observed in fine-grained tasks, such as LAC subtyping and meningioma grading, highlights a limitation of current generalist MVLMs: they often fail to capture subtle intra-class variations. RadiSim-CL, however, demonstrates a coherent diagnostic logic, distinguishing between clinically similar subtypes rather than merely recognizing broad disease categories. Nevertheless, although this five-stage framework provides step-by-step clinician-interpretable intermediate results, the model is intended solely as a decision-support or screening tool. It does not replace the comprehensive clinical synthesis required for independent diagnostic conclusions, particularly in high-stakes medical environments where human judgment remains essential.

A detailed examination of failure cases provides insight into the model’s decision boundaries and highlights several systematic sources of error. We examined misclassifications in the highly heterogeneous MRI-based multi-label organ classification task and clinical disease diagnosis tasks (Fig. 6). In the MRI-based multi-label organ classification task, errors stemmed from the small size of target organs in the ground truth (GT) and feature confusion caused by morphologically similar tissues (Fig. 6A, B). Specifically, in a case of polycystic kidney disease (Fig. 6A), massive lesions obscured the intrinsic anatomical structure of the kidneys. The model successfully identified the kidney despite its absence in the GT, which was later confirmed as a true positive via clinical re-evaluation, indicating an annotation error in the dataset rather than a model deficiency. For the MRI-based brain tumor diagnosis task, poor image contrast in the public Br35H dataset led to inaccurate predictions (Fig. 6C). Moreover, despite accurate tumor-negative annotations (GT: no brain tumor), diffuse lesion-mimicking signals (e.g., non-neoplastic parenchymal abnormalities) caused substantial visual confounding. The model failed to distinguish these diffuse signal changes from true tumor foci, resulting in false-positive misclassification of tumor-negative cases (Fig. 6D). Similarly, in RSNA pneumonia detection, clinical artifacts (e.g., support devices, and monitoring lines) introduced noise that misled the model (Fig. 6E, F). Lastly, in CT-based LAC classification, the model struggled to distinguish lesions from lesion-mimicking structures, such as pulmonary vessels, due to high morphological similarity.

Despite its strengths, RadiSim-CL still presents several limitations. First, the model is limited to static image-text pairs and lacks the incorporation of temporal or longitudinal information, which is vital for tracking disease progression. Second, while the model supports numerous foundational diagnostic tasks, it has yet to be extended to applications such as automatic report generation, multimodal EMR integration, or real-time decision support. Furthermore, although our current curriculum order has demonstrated superior performance, future research could further explore the impact of different knowledge sequencing strategies on the generalization ability of the final model. Lastly, further efforts are essential to enhance interpretability and address potential domain biases. Since our training data span diverse modalities and sources, there is a risk that the model may learn spurious correlations arising from distribution shifts rather than core diagnostic signals. Therefore, developing methods to detect and mitigate such “shortcut learning” (i.e., learning dataset-specific artifacts rather than generalizable diagnostic features), alongside quantifying predictive uncertainty, is critical for safe clinical adoption.

In conclusion, our model (RadiSim-CL) demonstrates strong versatility and robust performance across the radiological diagnostic spectrum. By closely simulating the hierarchical learning pathway of radiologists and leveraging a high-quality, multi-source dataset, RadiSim-CL offers a comprehensive approach to supporting clinical practice. While continued effort is needed to translate these technical capabilities into direct clinical benefits and address the identified limitations, RadiSim-CL represents a meaningful step towards developing an AI system that can augment radiological expertise across diverse diagnostic tasks.

Methods

Datasets for training RadiSim-CL

To address the limitations in representativeness and diversity found in existing datasets, we propose RadiSim, a comprehensive and high-quality radiology image-text dataset. Our initial collection comprised 61.18 million image-text pairs. These included 9.62 million pairs from private sources (i.e., collaborating hospitals) and 51.56 million pairs from seven publicly available repositories (i.e., PMC-OA13, CheXpert49, PubMed Central, Eurorad50, MIMIC-CXR51, OpenI52, and one anonymized licensed research dataset obtained under academic research authorization.) (see Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1). After a rigorous data cleaning and curation pipeline, we ultimately obtained 12 million high-quality radiology image-text pairs for training RadiSim-CL.

Data curation and categorization for progressive learning

Based on the textual characteristics and annotation quality of the data, we categorize them into three distinct groups. Each group is specifically designed to facilitate the progressive acquisition of knowledge by RadiSim-CL.

The first group focuses on foundational knowledge understanding, primarily consisting of general medical images from PubMed Open Access (PubMed OA raw data and PMC-OA). These broadly categorized images and texts are crucial for building the model’s initial visual-textual associations and recognizing basic imaging modalities. We extended previously available PMC-OA data (limited to articles published before September 2022) by crawling additional journal data up to September 2024. A total of 3,531,419 articles were collected.

Building upon this foundation, the second category targets anatomical knowledge acquisition by incorporating in-house private data, CheXpert, and the anonymized licensed research datasets. These sources contain varied labeled text encompassing imaging modalities, detailed anatomical structures, and initial abnormality descriptions. This structured information is essential for the model to identify and localize specific anatomical regions and structures.

Finally, the third group is dedicated to advanced diagnostic reasoning, drawing from public diagnostic datasets such as Eurorad, MIMIC-CXR, and OpenI. These datasets are characterized by their rich diagnostic information, often containing full medical reports with detailed findings, differential diagnoses, and disease grading informations. These well-aligned image-text pairs are instrumental in training the model to perform complex abnormality detection, disease diagnosis, differentiation, and grading.

Data processing pipeline

Our primary curation efforts concentrated on the first two data groups (PMC-derived data, and datasets with diverse labeled text) to significantly enhance semantic diversity and improve alignment quality.

For raw text and image data from PubMed Open Access, inspired by the PMC-CLIP construction approach, we developed a dedicated data cleaning and curation pipeline (Fig. 7A) to extract radiological images and pair them with relevant captions and inline captions. This PMC-derived image-caption data was further categorized based on structural composition into (1) composite captions with composite images, (2) non-composite captions with composite images, and (3) non-composite captions with non-composite images. These categories undergo a two-branch processing strategy. First, to enhance domain relevance, we applied radiology-specific keyword filtering to retain only those pairs strongly associated with medical imaging. Second, we used a large language model to decompose composite captions into fine-grained sub-captions. In parallel, composite images are processed through sub-image detection and cropping to isolate meaningful regions. Non-medical images were excluded using a ResNet101 classifier to ensure that only medically relevant content is preserved. The final step merged all qualified image-text pairs into the RadiSim training corpus, resulting in a diverse, domain-specific, and high-quality dataset tailored for advancing radiological vision-language modeling.

For datasets with diverse labeled text, we employed LLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL53, HuatuoGPT54) to generate detailed, semantically rich, and high-quality captions for each image, specifically focusing on anatomical details (Fig. 7B). This automated annotation process provided the structured information necessary for the model to identify and localize specific anatomical regions and structures.

Datasets for downstream evaluation

To rigorously assess model performance, we conducted comprehensive zero-shot evaluations across five critical diagnostic tasks that mirror the cognitive workflow of radiologists. For modality recognition, we utilized diverse datasets including CHAOS, AMOS, TotalSegmentator-MR, and RAOS for MR images; CT-ORG and TotalSegmentator-CT for CT images; and an in-house clinical collection for DR images. Anatomical recognition capabilities were evaluated using public MR scans from the TotalSegmentator-MR dataset and CT scans from the 2D MediMeTA Abdominal CT dataset. Spatial localization performance was tested on the internal CT dataset, AMOS-CT, CT-ORG, and CHAOS-MR datasets. For disease diagnosis, we employed multiple modality-specific collections: Br35H for brain tumors, MRNet for musculoskeletal assessment, specialized CT datasets for brain hemorrhage, COVID-19, and lymph node pathologies, and both PneumoniaMNIST and RSNA Pneumonia datasets for respiratory conditions. Disease differentiation and grading evaluations utilized proprietary clinical datasets with expert annotations. Detailed descriptions of all datasets are provided in the Supplementary Table S2. Crucially, we enforced a strict patient-level separation for all internal data, ensuring that the datasets utilized for downstream validation were completely independent of the pre-training corpus.

Model design

Unlike conventional contrastive learning approaches that use softmax normalization across the entire batch, we implemented the SigLIP pairwise sigmoid loss for training. This pairwise formulation is particularly advantageous in the medical imaging domain, where visually similar images may correspond to different clinical descriptions, and strict one-to-one matching is not always reliable. In such scenarios, softmax-based global normalization may penalize semantically plausible but unmatched pairs, leading to suboptimal representations. In contrast, SigLIP enables more flexible and tolerant modeling of image–text relationships, improving robustness in settings with subtle visual differences and overlapping semantic cues. A core innovation of RadiSim-CL lies in its curriculum learning strategy, which follows a three-stage curriculum from foundational knowledge understanding to anatomical understanding and advanced diagnostic reasoning. The model is fine-tuned in three sequential stages using cumulatively expanding subsets of the RadiSim dataset, each representing increasing levels of clinical complexity and knowledge specificity. Notably, for the initial foundational stage, to address the limited volume of strictly curated PubMed OA data, we augmented the training corpus with additional matched image-text pairs derived from the data cleaning pipeline, thereby ensuring sufficient data scale for robust representation learning. This staged approach not only facilitates learning from easy to difficult tasks, but also helps preserve previously acquired knowledge, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting. By progressively building upon prior representations, the model acquires a more robust and clinically coherent understanding of radiological data.

Implementation details

RadiSim-CL is implemented in Python (v3.10) using the PyTorch deep-learning framework. We employed a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture for the image encoder and initialized text encoder from BioMedBERT55. All images were uniformly resized to 224 × 224 pixels. Training was conducted using a batch size of 32k and a learning rate of 5 × 10−4, scheduled with warm-up over the first 2000 steps followed by cosine decay. Each stage was trained for 6–10 epochs using a fixed schedule, without reinitializing model parameters across stages. All experiments were conducted on eight NVIDIA L40 GPUs (48 GB each).

Adapting RadiSim-CL to various modality, anatomical and disease classification tasks

Despite being trained solely to encode radiological images and report text as semantically meaningful vector embeddings, RadiSim-CL demonstrated the adaptability to various downstream classification tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. We implemented a hierarchical zero-shot evaluation approach that mirrors the coarse-to-fine reasoning process radiologists employ in clinical practice, progressing from modality identification to anatomical recognition and finally to disease classification and subtyping.

For each classification task, we constructed a comprehensive set of text prompts representing the target classes. We crafted these prompts to capture the visual characteristics, anatomical features, and pathological findings typically described in clinical radiological interpretations. For modality recognition tasks, we used simple descriptive templates like “This is a [modality] image” or “This is a [modality] scan.” For anatomical recognition, we expanded the prompts to include organ-specific details such as “This is a scan of [anatomical region] [modality].” For disease classification and subtyping, we used more detailed prompts such as “[anatomical region] [modality] demonstrated [disease/subtype]” or “This is a scan of [anatomical region] [modality] showing [disease].” The classification process involved encoding these text prompts using RadiSim-CL’s text encoder and computing the cosine similarity between these text embeddings and the embeddings of radiological images. For each test image, the class with the highest similarity score was selected as the prediction.

Adapting RadiSim-CL to anatomical detection tasks

Beyond classification, RadiSim-CL demonstrated robust capability in localizing anatomical structures in radiological images through a zero-shot detection framework. This anatomical localization is pivotal in the radiological interpretation pipeline, serving as a critical intermediate step between modality recognition and disease classification in a coarse-to-fine clinical reasoning framework.

To implement this, we developed a zero-shot organ detection framework guided by textual prompts. Specifically, we extracted attention maps from all 12 heads in the final layer of the image encoder. We utilized a set of organ-specific text prompts to compute text–image similarity scores, which were transformed into multi-label probabilistic maps via a softmax operation. A thresholding operation was applied to the attention-derived responses in order to isolate candidate anatomical regions, followed by the extraction of corresponding bounding boxes. In our experiments, we applied this framework to localize 13 representative organs and one background class: “Brain”, “Stomach”, “Lungs”, “Heart”, “Breast”, “Liver”, “Spleen”, “Kidneys”, “Pancreas”, “Prostate”, “Bladder”, “Uterus”, “Knees”, and “Others”. We used RadiSim-CL’s text encoder to generate embeddings for these categories and projected them onto the image embedding space to obtain class-wise confidence scores. After normalizing these scores using a softmax function, we applied a threshold derived from the validation set to produce the final organ label maps and the bounding boxes.
