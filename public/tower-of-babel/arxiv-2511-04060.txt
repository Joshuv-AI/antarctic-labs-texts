A Generalized Back-Door Criterion for Linear Regression
Masato Shimokawa

Abstract
What assumptions about the data-generating process are required to permit a causal interpretation of partial regression coefficients? To answer this question, this paper generalizes Pearl’s single-door and back-door criteria and proposes a new criterion that enables the identification of causal effects not mediated by covariates. In addition, this paper elucidates the mechanism of post-treatment bias, showing that a repeated sequence of nodes can be a potential source of this bias. The results apply to linear data-generating processes represented by directed acyclic graphs with distribution-free error terms.

Introduction

Partial regression coefficients are sometimes regarded as causal effects, controlling for the other explanatory variables at fixed values.
Some researchers attempt to justify this interpretation by using the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem, presented by
9
and
16
, or the
regression anatomy
, described by
2
. However, it is important to note that neither theorem makes any reference to causal relationships.
Then, how can we ensure the validity of this interpretation?

To analyze causal structures, we need to make certain assumptions about them.
For example,
33
introduced
path analysis
and illustrated the structures using
path diagrams
.
This method has evolved through several studies, including
13
,
14
,
12
, and
5
, and is also known as linear
structural equation modeling
(SEM).
It is now widely used to analyze linear causal relationships.

As a generalization of this approach,
21
developed a framework for causal inference that can also be applied to non-linear causal structures.
Within this framework,
22
applied the
back-door criterion
—originally proposed by
20
as a method for covariate selection—to linear data-generating processes represented by
directed acyclic graphs
(DAGs), showing that it provides a sufficient condition under which a regression coefficient coincides with the total effect of the corresponding explanatory variable on the dependent variable.
The back-door criterion has since been generalized by
28
,
32
,
17
,
24
, and
25
.
In addition to the back-door criterion, other approaches, such as the
front-door criterion

(
21
)
,
conditional instrumental variables

(
6
)
, and
proxy variables
(
15
;
18
)
have been established for identifying total effects.

Meanwhile, some previous studies have explored the identification of partial causal relationships.

22
proposed the
single-door criterion
for identifying direct effects, and
3
and
27
examined the identifiability of path-specific effects.
6
,
31
,
8
,
7
, and
4
studied the identifiability of path coefficients algebraically by using a system of simultaneous equations based on the graph structure and second-order statistics.
10
and
11
proposed extensions of the front-door criterion that enable the identification of indirect effects. However, conditions that guarantee a partial regression coefficient coincides with the causal effect not mediated by covariates (i.e., the other explanatory variables) have been identified only in cases where either the direct or total effect is of interest.
Consequently, the logical validity of the causal interpretation of partial regression coefficients, as mentioned at the beginning, remains insufficiently established.
Given that regression analysis is widely used not only in academic research but also in various practical contexts, it is crucial to provide a theoretically sound basis for its interpretation.

In this paper, I propose the
selective-door criterion
, a generalization of both the single-door and back-door criteria.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

Providing a graphical criterion under which a partial regression coefficient represents the causal effect not mediated by the other explanatory variables.

•

Offering a rigorous proof of the relationships between partial regression coefficients and graphical criteria—including the single-door and back-door criteria—under the assumption that distribution-free error terms are mutually uncorrelated.

•

Clarifying the mechanisms of
post-treatment bias
based on graphical structures.

Unlike the direction of generalization taken in previous studies, the selective-door criterion focuses solely on preserving directed paths that remain unblocked by covariates.
As a result, it provides a clear framework for identifying partial causal relationships.
Furthermore, I prove the general relationship between partial regression coefficients and graphical criteria by relying on uncorrelatedness rather than on properties specific to linear Gaussian models. Although this approach may render the proof somewhat more verbose, it allows for a clearer demonstration that post-treatment bias can be characterized by a sequence of vertices formed by concatenating a directed path with a back-door path.
Since the properties of partial regression coefficients can be proven under specific distributional assumptions without loss of generality, the existing results of
30
and
22
are established by making use of properties of linear Gaussian models.
Perhaps for this reason, it does not seem to be widely recognized that the assumption of normality is in fact unnecessary.
The proof of the generalized theorem presented in this paper also reaffirms that normality does not need to be assumed.

Note, however, that the scope of this paper is limited to linear causal structures, as the main objective is to derive a broad sufficient condition that permits causal interpretations of partial regression coefficients.
Therefore, it remains an open question whether similar results hold for the probability distributions of causal models that allow for nonlinearity.

Section

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Model Setup

Let
\bm{V}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
be a finite set of random variables with nonzero variances.
Note that
\bm{V}
may include unobservable variables.
This paper considers the following model for the data-generating process:

X_{i}=\sum_{X_{j}\in\bm{PA}(X_{i})}a_{ij}X_{j}+u_{i},\quad X_{i}\in\bm{V},

(2.1)

where
\bm{PA}(X_{i})\subset\bm{V}\setminus\{X_{i}\}
denotes the set of immediate causes (parents) of
X_{i}
.
Here,
a_{ij}
is a nonzero constant referred to as the path coefficient from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
, and
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}
are error terms interpreted as aggregations of all causes not included in
\bm{V}
.
We do not assume any specific distribution for the error terms and assume that they are mutually uncorrelated.

Equation (
2.1
) can be rewritten as follows:

X_{i}=c_{i}+\sum_{X_{j}\in\bm{PA}(X_{i})}a_{ij}X_{j}+\tilde{u}_{i},\quad X_{i}\in\bm{V},

(2.2)

where
c_{i}=\,\mathrm{E}[u_{i}]
and
\tilde{u}_{i}=u_{i}-\,\mathrm{E}[u_{i}]
.
Moreover, the following vector form of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) is useful:

\bm{X}=\bm{A}\bm{X}+\bm{u}=\bm{c}+\bm{A}\bm{X}+\tilde{\bm{u}},

(2.3)

where
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})^{\top}
,
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})^{\top}
, and
\tilde{\bm{u}}=(\tilde{u}_{1},\dots,\tilde{u}_{N})^{\top}
.
Here,
\bm{A}
is an
N\times N
matrix, and
\bm{c}
is an
N\times 1
vector.
We make the following assumptions for (
2.3
).

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits(\bm{u})
is a positive definite diagonal matrix.

(ii)

\bm{A}
is a lower triangular matrix with all diagonal elements equal to zero.

Assumption
2.1
ensures that each causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
) is a DAG.
If we adhere to the view that the causal relationship between two variables at a given point in time is unidirectional, it is natural for the data-generating process to take the form of a DAG.
However, this assumption also implies that a causal ordering must be specified.
In practice, it is often necessary to consider multiple plausible causal orderings and compare the analytical results obtained under each scenario.

2.2
Regression on the Population

This section defines the population partial regression coefficient and describes its basic properties.
Throughout this subsection,
\bm{X}
denotes a general random vector unless stated otherwise.

Definition 2.2

(Population partial regression coefficient)
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and
\bm{X}
a random vector.
Suppose that the inverse matrix
\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}]^{-1}
and the vector
\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}Y]
exist.
Then,

\bm{\beta}_{Y\bm{X}}=\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{XX}^{\top}]^{-1}\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}Y]

is referred to as the
population partial regression coefficient
in the regression of
Y
on
\bm{X}
.

Definition 2.3

(Population linear regression equation)
.

Let
\bm{\beta}_{Y\bm{X}}
denote the population partial regression coefficient in the regression of
Y
on
\bm{X}
.
The equation

Y=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{Y\bm{X}}+\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}},

where
\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}}\coloneq Y-\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{Y\bm{X}}
,
is referred to as the
population linear regression equation
.

Since the population partial regression coefficient is a natural extension of the ordinary partial regression coefficient computed from a sample, it satisfies several analogous properties.

Proposition 2.4
.

For the residual
\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}}
, the following properties hold:

(i)

\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}}]=\bm{0}
.

(ii)

If
\bm{X}
includes a nonzero constant term, then
\,\mathrm{E}[\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}}]=0
and
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(\bm{X},\epsilon_{Y\bm{X}})=\bm{0}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
Y
be a random variable and
\bm{X}
a random vector, and suppose that the inverse matrix
\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}]^{-1}
and the vector
\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}Y]
exist.
Assume that the following relationship holds:

Y=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{b}+e,

where
\bm{b}
is a vector and
e
is a random variable satisfying
\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}e]=\bm{0}
.
Then, it follows that
\bm{b}=\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}]^{-1}\,\mathrm{E}[\bm{X}Y]
.

Assumption
2.1
also ensures the existence of the population partial regression coefficients for the random variables generated by equation (
2.3
).

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.
Then, for the random vector
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{+}=(1,\tilde{\bm{X}}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
\tilde{\bm{X}}
is any random subvector of
\bm{X}
generated by equation (
2.3
), the inverse matrix
\,\mathrm{E}[\tilde{\bm{X}}_{+}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{+}^{\top}]^{-1}
exists.

2.3
Causal Diagram and the Selective-Door Criterion

For a DAG
(\bm{V},\bm{E})
and distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
, the following notations are used throughout this paper:

•

\bm{AN}(X_{i})
: the set of ancestors of
X_{i}
;

•

\bm{DE}(X_{i})
: the set of descendants of
X_{i}
;

•

X_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}
: a directed edge from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
;

•

X_{j}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
: a directed path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
;

•

X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
: a back-door path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
.

Unless specified otherwise, a directed edge is regarded as a directed path of length one. In the following, we introduce several terms.

Definition 2.7

(Causal diagram)
.

Consider equation (
2.1
).
Let
\bm{V}
denote the set of nodes, and let
\bm{E}
denote the set of directed edges such that, for each
X_{i}\in\bm{V}
, the set
\bm{PA}(X_{i})
coincides with the parent set of
X_{i}
.
Then, the pair
(\bm{V},\bm{E})
is referred to as the
causal diagram
associated with equation (
2.1
).

According to the standard definition (see
23
), bidirected arrows are drawn between nodes whose error terms are dependent. However, in this paper, bidirected arrows are omitted when the corresponding error terms are uncorrelated. Next, we introduce the following concepts to visually identify the conditional relationships between variables.

Definition 2.8

(Blocking)
.

Let
\bm{S}
be a set such that
\bm{S}\cap\{X_{i},X_{j}\}=\emptyset
.
The set
\bm{S}
is said to
block
a path
P
between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
if it satisfies one of the following two conditions; otherwise,
\bm{S}
is said to
unblock
the path:

(i)

\bm{S}
contains a node on
P
that is not a collider in any v-structure along
P
; or

(ii)

For a v-structure along
P
, neither the collider nor any of its descendants belongs to
\bm{S}
.

20
focused on back-door paths, which can bias causal relationships, and established the back-door criterion.
This criterion for covariate selection provides a sufficient condition for identifying total causal effects.

Definition 2.9

(Back-door path)
.

A path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
that contains a directed edge into
X_{j}
is referred to as a
back-door path
from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
.

Definition 2.10

(Back-door criterion)
.

A set
\bm{S}
satisfying
\bm{S}\cap\{X_{i},X_{j}\}=\emptyset
is said to satisfy the
back-door criterion
relative to an ordered pair
(X_{j},X_{i})
if

(i)

no node in
\bm{S}
is a descendant of
X_{j}
, and

(ii)

\bm{S}
blocks every back-door path
X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
.

Although the back-door criterion serves as a powerful framework for covariate selection, it suffers from a limitation in capturing partial causal effects originating from specific paths. To address this issue, we relax the first condition of the back-door criterion and propose a generalized version.

Definition 2.11

(Selective-door criterion)
.

A set
\bm{S}
satisfying
\bm{S}\cap\{X_{i},X_{j}\}=\emptyset
is said to satisfy the
selective-door criterion
relative to an ordered pair
(X_{j},X_{i})
if

(i)

for any
X_{k}\in\bm{S}\cap\bm{DE}(X_{j})
such that there exists a back-door path
X_{k}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
that is unblocked by
(\bm{S}\cup\{X_{j}\})\setminus\{X_{k}\}
, every directed path
X_{j}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{k}
is blocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{k}\}
, and

(ii)

\bm{S}
blocks every back-door path
X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
.

Since the first condition (i) is weaker than that of the back-door criterion, the selective-door criterion allows some directed paths from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
to be blocked.
It should be noted, however, that blocking every non-directed path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
is not sufficient. This point can be verified in Example
4.1
in Section
4
.

2.4
Controlled Total Effects

In the literature on path analysis and structural equation modeling (SEM), several researchers have discussed quantities that represent linear causal relationships.

Definition 2.12

(Direct, indirect, and total effects)
.

Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
).

(i)

The direct effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
is the path coefficient
a_{ij}
, which corresponds to the directed edge
X_{j}\to X_{i}
.
If such a directed edge does not exist, the direct effect is defined to be
0
.

(ii)

The indirect effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
is the sum of the products of the path coefficients corresponding to the directed paths
X_{j}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
, excluding the direct edge
X_{j}\to X_{i}
.
If such a directed path does not exist, the indirect effect is defined to be
0
.

(iii)

The total effect
\tau_{ij}
of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
is the sum of the direct and indirect effects.

See
1
and
5
for details on the causal effects defined above.
In addition, this paper introduces a measure representing partial causal relationships.

Definition 2.13

(Controlled total effect)
.

Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
).
For a node set
\bm{S}\subset\bm{V}
satisfying
\bm{S}\cap\{X_{i},X_{j}\}=\emptyset
,
consider the subgraph
\bm{G}^{\prime}
obtained by removing all edges whose terminal nodes belong to
\bm{S}
.
Then, the
\bm{S}
-controlled total effect, denoted by
\tau_{ij|do(\bm{S})}
, is defined as the total effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
restricted to the subgraph
\bm{G}^{\prime}
.

Using the
do-operator

(
23
)
, this quantity can be “interpreted” as

\tau_{ij|do(\bm{S})}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\,\mathrm{E}[X_{i}|do(X_{j}=x_{j}),do(\bm{S}=\bm{s})].

Since independence among the error terms is not assumed, this equality cannot be derived based on
do-calculus

(
23
)
.
However, given that the notation involving the do-operator represents probabilistic concepts of the post-intervention distribution (i.e., the distribution obtained by replacing the right-hand side of the corresponding structural equation with a constant), this interpretation is appropriate.
See Example
4.2
in the next section for a concrete illustration of this measure.

The following proposition plays a key role in the proof of the main theorem in this paper.

Proposition 2.14

(Ancestral expansion)
.

Assume that (ii) in Assumption
2.1
holds.
Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
).
Then, for any subset
\bm{S}\subset(\bm{V}\setminus\{X_{i}\})
, the following holds:

X_{i}=\sum_{X_{j}\in\bm{S}}\tau_{ij|do(\bm{S}_{-j})}X_{j}+\sum_{X_{k}\in\bm{AN}(X_{i})\setminus\bm{S}}\tau_{ik|do(\bm{S})}u_{k}+u_{i},

(2.4)

where
\bm{S}_{-j}=\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
.

Note that, on the right-hand side of this equation, the coefficients
\tau_{ij|do(\bm{S}_{-j})}
corresponding to any random variables in
\bm{S}\setminus\bm{AN}(X_{i})
are equal to
0
. Since equation (
2.4
) is derived solely through successive substitution operations in the linear structural equations, it can be regarded as an alternative representation of the data-generating process.

3
Causation and Regression

3.1
Problem Formulation

This paper investigates the causal effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
under Assumption
2.1
.
Consider the following population linear regression equation:

X_{i}=\beta_{i0}+\beta_{ij}X_{j}+\sum_{X_{k}\in\bm{Z}}\beta_{ik}X_{k}+\epsilon_{i},

(3.1)

where
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus\{X_{i},X_{j}\}
denotes a set of covariates.
Although the coefficients and residual depend on all explanatory variables, only a subset of the indices is shown for simplicity.
The objective is to derive a sufficient condition on
\bm{Z}
under which
\beta_{ij}=\tau_{ij|do(\bm{Z})}
holds.
If the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
corresponds to the causal effect, then, under standard regularity conditions for random explanatory variables, the least squares estimator provides a consistent estimate of this effect.

3.2
Main Results

The selective-door criterion provides a sufficient condition of interest.

Theorem 3.1

(Selective-door adjustment)
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
). For any distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
and any subset
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus\{X_{i},X_{j}\}
satisfying the selective-door criterion relative to
(X_{j},X_{i})
, the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
3.1
) coincides with the
\bm{Z}
-controlled total effect
\tau_{ij|do(\bm{Z})}
.

In the case of linear regression, condition (i) of the selective-door criterion requires that if an explanatory variable

X_{k}\in\bm{Z}
is a descendant of
X_{j}
, then one of the following must hold:
(i) all directed paths from
X_{j}
to
X_{k}
are blocked by other explanatory variables, or
(ii) all back-door paths from
X_{k}
to
X_{i}
are blocked by explanatory variables that include
X_{j}
.

As corollaries, we obtain the following propositions.

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
). For any distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
, suppose that a subset
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus(\{X_{i},X_{j}\}
blocks every path between
X_{j}
and
X_{i}
.
Then, the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
3.1
) is equal to
0
.

Corollary 3.3

(Single-door adjustment)
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
). For any distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
, suppose that a subset
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus(\{X_{i},X_{j}\}\cup\bm{DE}(X_{i}))
blocks every path between
X_{j}
and
X_{i}
except for the directed edge
X_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}
.
Then, the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
3.1
) coincides with the direct effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
.

Corollary 3.4

(Back-door adjustment)
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
). For any distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
and any subset
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus(\{X_{i},X_{j}\}\cup\bm{DE}(X_{j}))
satisfying the back-door criterion relative to
(X_{j},X_{i})
, the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
3.1
) coincides with the total effect
\tau_{ij}
.

These corollaries were originally established in
30
and
22
based on conditional independence relations and the properties of conditional covariances in linear Gaussian models.
However, as also partially noted in
30
and
19
, when a constant term is included, the partial regression coefficients in linear structural equation models depend solely on the covariance matrix
(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{A})^{-1}\mathop{\rm Var}\nolimits(\bm{u})\{(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{A})^{-1}\}^{\top}
; therefore, their proof does not lose generality.
Here,
\bm{I}_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix.
A contribution of the present study is to show that linear causal effects not mediated by covariates can also be consistently estimated using ordinary least squares, even without assuming independence or normality of the error terms in the data-generating process.

In many empirical analyses, it is not uncommon for all explanatory variables to be regarded as causes of interest.
Thus, this paper also provides sufficient conditions under which all regression coefficients can be interpreted as causal effects.

Proposition 3.5
.

The following statements are equivalent.

(i)

For any
X_{j}\in\bm{S}
, the set
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
satisfies the selective-door criterion relative to
(X_{j},X_{i})
.

(ii)

For any
X_{j}\in\bm{S}
, the set
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
blocks every back-door path
X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
.

(iii)

For any
X_{j}\in\bm{S}
, the set
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
blocks every back-door path
X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
that contains no v-structure.

Condition
(iii)
is particularly easy to check because
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
blocks a back-door path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
that contains no v-structure if and only if
X_{i}\notin\bm{S}
and at least one node
X_{k}\in\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
lies on that back-door path.

3.3
Post-Treatment Bias

Biases that arise from conditioning on variables affected by a treatment (i.e., descendants in the graph) are referred to as
post-treatment bias
.
Examining the mechanism of this bias helps clarify why condition (i) of the selective-door criterion is required.

In addition to the set
\bm{Z}
in equation (
3.1
), the following sets are introduced:

\displaystyle\bm{S}

\displaystyle=\{X_{j}\}\cup\bm{Z},

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\,X_{k}\in\bm{S}\mid\exists\,P:X_{k}\dashleftarrow X_{i}\ \text{s.t.}\ P\ \text{is unblocked by}\ \bm{S}\setminus\{X_{k}\}\,\},

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\,X_{k}\in\bm{S}\mid\nexists\,P:X_{k}\dashleftarrow X_{i}\ \text{s.t.}\ P\ \text{is unblocked by}\ \bm{S}\setminus\{X_{k}\}\,\}.

The bias
\gamma_{ik}
corresponding to each coefficient
\beta_{ik}
in equation (
3.1
) is defined as

\gamma_{ik}\coloneq\beta_{ik}-\tau_{ik|do(\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{k}\})},\quad X_{k}\in\bm{S}.

Although bias is often defined as the difference from the total effect
\tau_{ij}
, this paper adopts the above definition because it is consistent with the interpretation of partial regression coefficients introduced at the beginning of Section 1, which is the main focus of the present study.

The following theorem characterizes post-treatment bias in the context of linear regression.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\bm{G}=(\bm{V},\bm{E})
be the causal diagram associated with equation (
2.1
). For any distinct nodes
X_{i},X_{j}\in\bm{V}
, suppose that a subset
\bm{Z}\subset\bm{V}\setminus\{X_{i},X_{j}\}
blocks every back-door path
X_{j}\dashleftarrow X_{i}
. Then, the bias of the coefficient
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
3.1
) is given by

\gamma_{ij}=-\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{S}_{1}}\gamma_{ip}\tau_{pj|do(\bm{S}_{2}\setminus\{X_{j}\})}.

As indicated by the back-door criterion, confounding bias can be eliminated by blocking all back-door paths.
The first condition (i) of the selective-door criterion implies that
\tau_{pj|do(\bm{S}_{2}\setminus\{X_{j}\})}=0
and can be understood as a condition to prevent post-treatment bias.
Furthermore, this perspective clarifies why merely blocking undirected paths is not sufficient to remove all sources of bias.
Since a path is usually defined as a sequence of distinct nodes, the sequence of nodes
X_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}\rightarrow X_{p}\leftarrow X_{i}
is, for instance, not considered a path. However, the estimated effect of
X_{j}
on
X_{i}
can still be distorted through this structure if
X_{p}
is conditioned on.
Another instance of such a structure is provided in Example
4.1
below.

\displaystyle=\sum_{X_{n}\in\bm{AN}(X_{k})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}}\tau_{kn|do(\bm{Z}^{*})}\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},e^{*}_{i})+\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{k},e^{*}_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},e^{*}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},e_{i})-\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(u_{n},\sum_{X_{m}\in\bm{Z}^{*}}\gamma_{im}X_{m}\right).

Hence, for nodes
X_{k}\in\bm{X}^{*}
and
X_{n}\in\bm{AN}(X_{k})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}
, it suffices to show the following:

(a)

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{k},e^{*}_{i})=0
,

(b)

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},e_{i})=0
if
\tau_{kn|do(\bm{Z}^{*})}\neq 0
,

(c)

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(u_{n},\sum_{X_{m}\in\bm{Z}^{*}}\gamma_{im}X_{m}\right)=0
if
\tau_{kn|do(\bm{Z}^{*})}\neq 0
.

(a). We focus on a property of
e^{*}_{i}
. We can partition the set
\bm{Z}^{*}
into two disjoint subsets,
\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
, such that they satisfy the following conditions:

•

For any
X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
, there exists a back-door path from
X_{p}
to
X_{i}
that is unblocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{p}\}
.

•

For any
X_{q}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
, there exists no back-door path from
X_{q}
to
X_{i}
that is unblocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{q}\}
.

For each
X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
, define

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{p}

\displaystyle=X_{p}-\sum_{X_{q}\in\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}}\tau_{pq|do((\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})\setminus\{X_{q}\})}X_{q}

\displaystyle=X_{p}-\sum_{X_{q}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}}\tau_{pq|do((\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})\setminus\{X_{q}\})}X_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X_{s}\in\bm{AN}(X_{p})\setminus(\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})}\tau_{ps|do(\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})}u_{s},\quad X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}.

The second equality follows from the first condition (i) in Definition
2.11
.
Suppose that the following population linear regression equation exists:

e_{i}=\tilde{\gamma}_{i0}+\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\tilde{X}_{p}+\tilde{e}_{i}.

We will show that
\tilde{e}_{i}=e^{*}_{i}
by mathematical induction.
Let the elements of

\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
be ordered by increasing indices as
X_{q^{1}},X_{q^{2}},...,X_{q^{M}}
.
For
X_{q^{1}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q^{1}},\tilde{e}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(\sum_{X_{s}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tau_{q^{1}s|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}\setminus\{X_{s}\})}X_{s}+\sum_{X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tau_{q^{1}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}u_{l}+u_{q^{1}},\tilde{e}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(\sum_{X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tau_{q^{1}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}u_{l}+u_{q^{1}},e_{i}-\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\tilde{X}_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(\sum_{X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tau_{q^{1}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}u_{l},e_{i}-\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\tilde{X}_{p}\right)

Note that for any
X_{s}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}\cap\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})
, the equality
X_{s}=\tilde{X}_{s}
holds due to the triangularity of the coefficient matrix
\bm{A}
and the minimality of
q^{1}
.
Furthermore, the following statements hold:

(1)

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{l},e_{i})=0
if
\tau_{q^{1}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}\neq 0,\quad X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
.

(2)

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{l},\tilde{X}_{p})=0
if
\tau_{q^{1}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}\neq 0,\quad X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
.

If
(1)
does not hold, then there exists one of the following paths:

•

X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
such that the paths
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
and
X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
are unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
\bm{S}
respectively.

•

X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
such that the subpaths
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
and
X_{l}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
are unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
\bm{S}
respectively.

However, the back-door paths
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{i}
and
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
would also be unblocked by
\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
; otherwise, there would exist a back-door path from some
X_{s}\in\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
to
X_{i}
that is unblocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{s}\}
, which contradicts
X_{q^{1}}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
.

If
(2)
does not hold, there exists a back-door path
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{p}
such that
X_{q^{1}}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{l}
is unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
X_{l}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{p}
is unblocked by
\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
.
In addition,
X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{1}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{l},\tilde{X}_{p})\neq 0
imply that
X_{l}\notin\bm{S}
, which contradicts Lemma
5.1
.

Therefore
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q^{1}},\tilde{e}_{i})=0
holds.
Next, for
q^{1}\leq q<q^{k}
, assume that
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q},\tilde{e}_{i})=0
holds, and we prove
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q^{k}},\tilde{e}_{i})=0
.
Since for any
X_{s}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}\cap\bm{AN}(X_{p^{k}})
, each random variable
X_{q}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}\cap\bm{AN}(X_{s})
is uncorrelated with
\tilde{e}_{i}
(due to
q<q^{k}
), it follows that

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{s},\tilde{e}_{i})=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(\tilde{X}_{s},\tilde{e}_{i})+\sum_{X_{q}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}}\tau_{sq|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}\setminus\{X_{q}\})}\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q},\tilde{e}_{i})=0.

Similarly to the case of
X_{q^{1}}
, we obtain

\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(X_{q^{k}},\tilde{e}_{i})=\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(\sum_{X_{l}\in\bm{AN}(X_{q^{k}})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tau_{q^{k}l|do(\bm{Z}^{*}_{1})}u_{l},e_{i}-\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\tilde{X}_{p}\right)=0.

Thus,
\tilde{e}_{i}
is uncorrelated with all nodes in
\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
, which implies that

\tilde{e}_{i}
is also uncorrelated with all nodes in
\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
.
Therefore, by Proposition
2.5
, it follows that

e^{*}_{i}=\tilde{e}_{i}=e_{i}-\tilde{\gamma}_{i0}-\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\tilde{X}_{p}.

This completes the proof of (a).

(b). For some
X_{n}\in\bm{AN}(X_{k})\setminus\bm{Z}^{*}
, suppose that both
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},e_{i})\neq 0
and
\tau_{kn|do(\bm{Z}^{*})}\neq 0
hold.
Then, there must exist one of the following paths:

•

X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
such that the paths
X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
and
X_{i}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
are unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}
and
\bm{S}
respectively.

•

X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
such that the subpaths
X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
and
X_{n}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{i}
are unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}
and
\bm{S}
respectively.

However, this contradicts the assumption that
X_{k}\in\bm{X}^{*}
, since such back-door paths would be unblocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{k}\}
.

(c). Note that from Proposition
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits

\displaystyle\left(u_{n},\sum_{X_{m}\in\bm{Z}^{*}}\gamma_{im}X_{m}\right)=\sum_{X_{p}\in\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}}\tilde{\gamma}_{ip}\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits\left(u_{n},\tilde{X}_{p}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{X_{s}\in\bm{AN}(X_{p})\setminus(\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})}\tau_{ps|do(\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2})}u_{s}.

For
X_{n}\in\bm{AN}(X_{k})\setminus\bm{S}
and
X_{k}\in\bm{X}^{*}
, if both
\mathop{\rm Cov}\nolimits(u_{n},\tilde{X}_{p})\neq 0
and
\tau_{kn|do(\bm{Z}^{*})}\neq 0
hold, then there exists a path
X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{p}
such that the subpaths
X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
and
X_{n}\rightarrow\rightarrow X_{p}
are unblocked by
\bm{Z}^{*}
and
\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
, respectively. Since the path
X_{k}\leftarrow\leftarrow X_{n}
is also unblocked by
\bm{Z}_{1}^{*}
, this contradicts Lemma
5.1
.

Therefore, conditions (a), (b), and (c) hold, and the conclusion follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

Since it is clear that
(i)

\Rightarrow

(ii)

\Rightarrow

(iii)
, it suffices to prove
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
.
Condition
(iii)
satisfies the first requirement of Definition
2.11
.
Consider the case where there exists a back-door path from
X_{j}
to
X_{i}
that contains one or more v-structures.
If this path is unblocked by
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
, then the collider or one of its descendants in the v-structure closest to

X_{i}
on this path must belong to
\bm{S}\setminus\{X_{j}\}
.
At least one of these nodes has an unblocked back-door path to
X_{i}
that does not contain any v-structures, which contradicts
(iii)
.
Therefore,
(iii)

\Rightarrow

(i)
holds.
∎

3.6
.

Here, we follow the notation used in the proof of Theorem
3.1
; hence, we have
\bm{S}_{1}=\bm{Z}^{*}_{1}
and
\bm{S}_{2}=\bm{X}^{*}\cup\bm{Z}^{*}_{2}
. The following equation follows from the proof.

\displaystyle e_{i}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{i0}+\sum_{X_{m}\in\bm{Z}^{*}}\gamma_{im}X_{m}+e^{*}_{i}

, the conclusion follows.
