Proclarix’ performance in ruling out patients with no or indolent prostate cancer: evaluation in a Danish population
Authors: Ralph Schiess, Alcibiade Athanasiou, Madlen M.E. Kasten, Torben F Hansen, Gitte E Kissow, Louise F Obro, Jonna S Madsen, Mads H Poulsen, Palle J Osther, Ahmed H. Zedan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPSA testing is widely used for the early detection of prostate cancer (PCa), but its low specificity leads to overdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions. Proclarix, a novel blood test combining serum levels of prostate specific antigen (PSA), percentage of free PSA (%fPSA), Cathepsin D (CTSD) and Thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) with age into a risk score, aims to improve risk stratification by predicting clinically significant PCa (csPCa). This study evaluated its diagnostic performance in a Danish population using retrospective serum samples collected consecutively from patients with suspected PCa.MethodsProclarix’ ability to reduce biopsies and detection of clinically insignificant PCa (ciPCa, defined as Grade Group < 2) was assessed in men with a PSA 2–10 ng/ml and a prostate volume of ≥ 35 ml (targeted population) compared with the percentage of free PSA (%fPSA) and the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer Risk Calculator (ERSPC-RC). The secondary analysis included the performance of Proclarix’ and Proclarix density compared with the %fPSA and PSA density (PSA-D) in a broader population with a PSA 2–20 ng/ml regardless of both prostate volume and DRE (extended population). Proclarix score is considered negative when it’s below the cutoff 10%.ResultsIn the targeted population (n = 373), a negative Proclarix test significantly reduced the probability of csPCa from 27% (pretest) to 5% (posttest, 95%CI: 0–10%), (p < 0.028) outperforming %fPSA (posttest 14%, 95%CI: 4–24%) and ERSPC-RC (posttest 20%, 95%CI: 4–36%). For the diagnosis of csPCa, Proclarix had a significantly (p < 0.01) greater specificity of 22% (95%CI: 17–27%) at 97% sensitivity (95%CI: 94–100%) and 95% NPV (95%CI: 90–100%) than did %fPSA and the ERSPC-RC, with 14% (95%CI: 10–18%) and 7% (95%CI: 4–11%) specificity, respectively. In the extended population (n = 656), Proclarix density had significantly (p < 0.01) greater specificity (39%, 95%CI: 35–44%) than did PSA-D (32%, 95%CI: 27–36%) at an equal sensitivity of 90%.ConclusionsProclarix reduces prostate biopsies and ciPCa detection while maintaining a low risk of missing csPCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16118-8.

Background

The early detection of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa), defined as International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) Grade Group (GG) ≥ 2, may decrease PCa mortality [1]. However, the common diagnostic approach for the early detection of PCa, which is primarily based on serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA), digital rectal examination (DRE), percent free PSA (%fPSA), PSA density (PSA-D), risk calculators, and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) scans or biparametric MRI scans, has been shown to have considerable limitations [2]. Consequently, the overdetection of clinically insignificant PCa (ciPCa) and high rates of unnecessary biopsies remain significant clinical challenges in PCa management, leading to health costs and potential complications such as infections or bleeding [3].

Several recent studies have highlighted the need for more targeted diagnostic approaches [4]. Advancements in imaging techniques, such as mpMRI, have enhanced the detection of csPCa but are still insufficient in isolation for population-wide screening. Hence, there is an increasing interest in noninvasive blood-based biomarkers that can complement existing methods, offering a more precise stratification of patients who require mpMRI and prostate biopsies.

Several blood-based biomarker tests have been developed for the detection of csPCa, including Proclarix, a Conformité Européenne (CE)-marked test designed to assess men with enlarged prostates (≥ 35 ml), unsuspicious DRE and serum total PSA (tPSA) between 2 and 10 ng/ml. Proclarix uses a combination of tPSA, fPSA, cathepsin D (CTSD), thrombospondin 1 (THBS1) and age [5] to determine the risk of csPCa. Previous studies have shown that Proclarix can predict csPCa [6, 7], correlates with the aggressiveness of PCa [8] and can be used as a “rule out” test, thus reducing the number of mpMRIs performed for PCa diagnosis [9–11]. In this study, our primary aim was to evaluate the clinical performance and safety of Proclarix in a Danish population, focusing on its ability to detect ciPCa or no PCa in men with suspected PCa in the Proclarix-targeted population in comparison to %fPSA and the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer risk calculator (ERSPC-RC, version 3/4, CE-Marked).

Given that there is limited evidence regarding the utility of Proclarix outside of its targeted population [12], our secondary endpoints included its clinical performance for csPCa in an extended population with a PSA of 2–20 ng/ml, regardless of prostate volume and DRE. Furthermore, we investigated a possible additional value for the use of Proclarix density.

Methods

Study design

In this retrospective study, the clinical performance of Proclarix was investigated in a prospective collected database and serum collection from patients with suspected PCa who were part of the Personalized Management of Prostate Cancer (PerPros) biobank at the Department of Urology, Vejle, Denmark (jr.nr: 18/11174). Patients’ clinical data and serum samples were collected as part of routine clinical practice.

Study population

In total, 808 patient samples were retrieved from the PerPros Biobank. All patients had been referred to the Department of Urology at Vejle Hospital or Esbjerg Hospital between the September 2015- March 2023 for further evaluation due to suspicion of PCa, which was mainly based on elevated PSA levels. The decision regarding further management is made in consultation with the patient, taking into account age, performance status, co-morbidities, and life expectancy. Since 2022, our center has fully adopted MRI following its inclusion in national and international guidelines [13, 14] for the triage of diagnostic evaluation for PCa. We excluded 10 patients due to missing prostate volume data, resulting in a total of 798 patients. Blood samples were collected prior to prostate biopsy. To obtain the serum, the blood samples were allowed to clot for 30–60 min after sampling, the blood was centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 min at room temperature, and the serum was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Biopsies of the prostate were conducted via transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided systematic biopsy (n = 692), MRI-guided biopsy (n = 28), or both (n = 78).

For the primary objective within the Proclarix targeted population, 373 of the 798 patients met the criteria of a PSA 2–10 ng/ml and a prostate volume ≥ 35 ml. For the secondary analyses in the extended population with a PSA of 2–20 ng/ml independent of prostate volume and DRE, 656 of the 798 patients met these inclusion criteria.

In this study, GG classification was determined by considering the results from initial biopsy, rebiopsy, and radical prostatectomy (RP). Rebiopsy upgrades superseded the GG from the initial biopsy, whereas downgrades did not. All the GGs resulting from RP took precedence over those from both biopsies. The volume of the prostate was determined using either ultrasound or MRI, depending on which imaging method was used for the biopsies.

Assessment of Proclarix

The frozen serum was stored locally at -80 °C until shipment on dry ice to Proteomedix (Zurich-Schlieren, Switzerland) for analysis. Proclarix measurements were performed blindly before any clinical information, including biopsy results, was available. Serum PSA and %fPSA were reanalyzed for all samples via the Roche Cobas immunoassay system (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). These values were used for statistical analysis. THBS1 and CTSD were measured via a CE-marked Proclarix kit (Proteomedix, Zurich-Schlieren, Switzerland) as described previously [15]. Proclarix risk calculation was performed according to the instructions for use. For the evaluation of clinical performance, a cutoff of 10% was used as recommended by the manufacturer.

Assessment of comparison parameters

Using the ERSPC-RC (version 3/4) [16], which includes age, PSA, prior prostate biopsy and, if available, volume and the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System score (PIRADS), we calculated the risk for detecting csPCa (defined by the ERSPC-RC as a tumor stage ≥ T2b and/or a Gleason score ≥ 7). For our study, we defined csPCa as a GG ≥ 2. The cutoff was determined according to ERSPC guidelines (detectable cancer risk > 20% or 12.5%-20% and significant cancer risk > 4%). The cutoff for %fPSA was set to 25% [17].

For the calculation of PSA density (PSA-D) and Proclarix density, prostate volume was measured by TRUS or mpMRI. Proclarix density was calculated according to the PSA-D by dividing the Proclarix risk score by the prostate volume. Cut-offs for PSA-D and Proclarix density were set to obtain a 90% sensitivity to allow a fair comparison.

Outcomes and outcome assessment

The primary study outcome was Proclarix’ performance, assessed by comparison with %fPSA and ERSPC-RC, in safely ruling out ciPCa or no PCa in its targeted population and thus safely detecting patients eligible to dismiss further investigation by MRI and prostate biopsies in the diagnostic PCa workup.

The secondary outcomes included Proclarix` performance in patients with a broader PSA range of 2–20 ng/ml, independent of prostate volume and DRE, and Proclarix density compared with PSA-D as a possible further diagnostic tool.

Statistical analysis

Differences in the results between sensitivity and specificity were assessed using the McNemar test and are presented with the 95% CI [18]. P values for differences in the negative predictive value (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV) were determined according to Moskowitz and Pepe [19], and p values for the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were determined as described in DeLong et al. [20], using the algorithm of Sun and Xu [21].

When comparing medians between two groups, a Mann–Whitney U test was used. A t-test was applied for comparisons of means between two groups when normality assumptions were met. For multiple group comparisons, t-tests with Bonferroni correction were performed to adjust for multiple testing.

Fagan’s nomograms [22] were performed using the nomogram function from Uncertain Interval R-package. The Fagan’s nomogram is a graphical tool for estimating how much the result on a diagnostic test changes the probability that a patient has a disease prior (pre-test) and after (post-test) performing the test.

Net benefit was determined according to Vickers et al. [23] defined as [True Positives - False Positives ÷ Pth (1- Pth)] ÷ N, where N is the total number of patients in the cohort and Pth is the threshold probability, the minimum probability of csPCa at which a patient would opt for biopsy.

For this analysis, we used biopsy thresholds of 5% and 10%. We assumed that a urologist would recommend a biopsy when the estimated risk of csPCa exceeds this range and would otherwise opt to monitor the patient.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Samples in this study were obtained from the PerPros biobank between September 2015 and March 2023. Our targeted population included 373 eligible patients with PSA serum levels ranging from 2 to 10 ng/ml and a prostate volume ≥ 35 ml, whereas the extended population included 656 patients with a PSA of 2–20 ng/ml. In the patient population undergoing only MR-guided biopsies (n = 28), 50% were from the control group, and one-third of patients diagnosed with PCa exhibited GG I. In cases where both MR-guided and TRUS-guided biopsies were conducted (n = 78), biopsy results were identical in 45% of the cases.

Targeted population

In the targeted population of patients with PSA levels of 2–10 ng/ml and prostate volumes ≥ 35 ml (n = 373), the Proclarix Risk Score was significantly correlated with GG (Fig. 1, p < 0.001). While the median Proclarix Risk Score of patients with no PCa or GG1 was not statistically different (p = 1.0), patients with GG2-5 had significantly higher median Proclarix Risk Scores than patients with no PCa or GG1 (p < 0.001).

Proclarix risk score correlation with GG in PCa patients detected via biopsy. The boxplot shows an increasing risk score for more significant cancers (p < 0.001)

Compared with %fPSA and ERSPC-RC, Proclarix demonstrated superior clinical performance in ruling out ciPCa or no PCa in the targeted population. The Fagan nomogram depicted in Fig. 2 illustrates the probability of a patient having csPCa given a test result (i.e., posttest probability) by entering the Likelihood Ratio for a positive or negative test outcome and the probability of the individual having csPCa before the test was run (pretest probability). The pretest probability of having csPCa was 27% in this population. A negative Proclarix Risk Score test result (≤ 10%) was associated with a 5% (95%CI: 0–10%) posttest probability of csPCa, which was significantly lower than the 14% probability observed with the %fPSA (p = 0.03) and the 20% (95%CI: 4–36%) probability with the ERSPC-RC (p = 0.03).

When a cutoff of 10% was used, Proclarix showed a sensitivity of 97%, specificity of 22%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 95% (95%CI: 90–100%), and positive predictive value (PPV) of 32% (95%CI: 27–37%). In comparison, %fPSA and ERSPC-RC had significantly lower specificities (14%, p < 0.01; 7%, p < 0.01). The absolute number of missed csPCa cases was not statistically different across the tests. Proclarix reduced the need for unnecessary biopsies by 22%, corresponding to 62 patients, while missing only 3 out of 101 csPCa cases (2 with GG2 and one with GG3), representing 3% of all clinically significant cases detected in this population. In comparison, the %fPSA saved 14% of the biopsies (n = 43 patients), while 6 cases of csPCa were missing. The ERSPC-RC saved 7% of the biopsies (n = 25 patients), while missing 5 cases of csPCa. Proclarix achieved the highest AUC, significantly higher than that of %fPSA (p = 0.045) and ERSPC-RC (p = 0.008) (Table 2).

Extended population

In the extended population PSA 2–20 ng/ml (n = 656, Supplemental Table 1), Proclarix maintained a high sensitivity of 96% (95%CI: 94–98%), outperforming %fPSA and ERSPC-RC in terms of specificity, with Proclarix showing 18% (95%CI: 14–22%) specificity (n = 81 patients) compared with 10% (95%CI: 7–13%) (p < 0.01, n = 51 patients) for %fPSA and 7% (p < 0.01, n = 37 patients) for the ERSPC-RC. Proclarix showed the highest net benefit However, the difference was less pronounced than in the intended-use population. Per 1,000 patients, about 30 additional biopsies would have been performed when using %fPSA or ERSPC-RC (Supplementary Table 2).

As presented in supplemental Table 3, when the sensitivity was matched at 90%, the Proclarix density exhibited significantly greater specificity (32% (95%CI: 27–38%), n = 97 patients and 39% (95%CI: 35–44%), n = 182) than did the PSA-D (19% (95%CI: 14–23%), p < 0.01, n = 61 patients and 32% (95%CI: 27–36%), p < 0.01, n = 153 patients, ) in both the targeted and extended populations.

Discussion

This study provides robust evidence supporting the clinical value of Proclarix as an effective diagnostic tool for ruling out ciPCa or no PCa in men with tPSA levels of 2–10 ng/ml and prostate volumes ≥ 35 ml. The key finding of this study was the low 5% posttest probability of csPCa following a negative Proclarix test, which was significantly superior to both the %fPSA and the ERSPC-RC. This highlights Proclarix’ ability to reduce unnecessary biopsies, a critical goal in PCa screening to avoid overdetection and overtreatment, while maintaining effective detection of csPCa.

The high sensitivity (97%) and NPV (95%) observed in the targeted population underscores the safety of using Proclarix as a rule-out test. In clinical practice, Proclarix` ability to maintain high sensitivity while improving specificity compared to existing tools, such as %fPSA and ERSPC-RC, facilitates more accurate identification of patients who may safely avoid biopsy​. Previous studies have demonstrated the limitations of the %fPSA, particularly its low specificity, which can lead to a higher rate of unnecessary biopsies [24]. Proclarix addresses this limitation, providing a more reliable alternative in the diagnostic process, demonstrating a greater net benefit relative to both %fPSA and ERPC-RC, leading to a reduction of irrelevant biopsies.

In the extended populations, Proclarix maintained its high sensitivity, suggesting its potential applicability beyond the current targeted population.

However, specificity was lower in population with the broader PSA range 2–20 ng/ml and no lower limit for prostate volume, leading to a greater number of unnecessary prostate biopsies. Therefore, we recommend utilizing Proclarix in men suspected of having PCa with tPSA levels between 2 and 10 ng/mL and consider extending this to a tPSA range of 2–20 ng/mL only if a repeat PSA test indicates a rise in PSA up to 20 ng/ml. Despite the lower specificity, Proclarix still outperformed %fPSA and ERSPC-RC in these extended analyses, reinforcing its clinical relevance.

Moreover, when considering prostate volume, Proclarix density exhibited even greater discriminative ability, offering higher specificity than traditional PSA-D in both the targeted population with prostate volume ≥ 35 ml and in the extended population regardless of prostate volume and DRE. This enhancement further solidifies Proclarix’ role as a valuable decision-making tool in PCa diagnostics. Utilizing Proclarix density could potentially save 32–39% of unnecessary prostate biopsies, depending on the study population. The Proclarix risk score is applicable in primary health care as an alternative biomarker for PSA in the screening setting, while Proclarix density could enhance risk classification for subsequent management. The establishment of an appropriate cutoff for Proclarix density in future studies will be crucial for maximizing its clinical utility.

These findings align with the literature, which emphasizes the importance of integrating novel biomarkers and diagnostic tools in PCa screening [25, 26]. In recent decades, numerous diagnostic biomarkers have emerged to improve the diagnosis of csPCa, particularly in men with PSA levels between 2 and 10. These include the Prostate Health Index, four kallikrein, and Stockholm 3. Despite the existence of promising and validated studies, most of these biomarkers are not extensively utilized in clinical practice [27]. International guidelines inconsistently recommend alternative biomarkers in place of or alongside PSA. The variability in biopsy contexts, varying MRI utilization, diverse data sets, and multiple biomarker thresholds complicate comprehensive evaluation for healthcare professionals [28]. The incorporation of Proclarix into clinical practice could significantly increase the precision of PCa diagnostics and management, contributing to a more personalized approach for treating patients at risk of PCa.

In this study, GG was determined from biopsy and, when available, from RP pathology. We acknowledge that biopsy-based and RP-based grading are not directly equivalent. However, biopsy GG is inherently limited by sampling variability and may underestimate tumour aggressiveness, whereas RP pathology provides a more comprehensive evaluation of the entire prostate specimen [29]. We therefore considered the inclusion of RP pathology, when available, to provide a more complete pathological reference for assessing the association between baseline Proclarix and tumour aggressiveness. This should be interpreted as an exploratory approach intended to reduce misclassification arising from biopsy under-sampling, rather than as a claim that RP and biopsy GG are interchangeable.

Finally, most patients were recruited prior to the complete implementation of MR in the diagnostic workup for PCa at our centre, consequently only 10% (38/373) of the men included in the targeted population and 11% (70/656) of the total population underwent mpMRI. Thus, we did not perform any subanalyses in these patients, as the small sample sizes did not allow for statistically robust results. We recognize that including men who underwent MRI-targeted biopsies (with or without systematic biopsies) in the current study may have resulted in a higher baseline cancer risk. This is due to enhanced lesion detection and sampling accuracy compared to standard TRUS-guided biopsy, which could potentially affect the disease prevalence and spectrum within the cohort. Nevertheless, the main study aim of this study was to correlate the test’s performance with the most accurate available reference standard for true disease status. Therefore, incorporating MRI-targeted biopsy results was deemed appropriate to better represent contemporary clinical practice, and these cases were retained in the analysis.

In summary, Proclarix demonstrated the ability to reduce the number of unnecessary biopsies safely by 22%, missing only 3 cases of csPCa out of 101, with 2 patients classified as GG2 and 1 patient classified as GG3. The growing body of evidence supporting the clinical utility of Proclarix highlights its potential as a transformative tool in ongoing efforts to improve PCa diagnostics.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the low use of MRI in the diagnostic workflow may limit the comprehensive assessment of csPCa, particularly for the post-test probability and NPV estimates. Second, the retrospective nature of the analysis may introduce inherent biases, such as selection bias, which could affect the generalizability of the findings. These factors underscore the need for prospective studies that incorporate mpMRI and standardized methodologies to validate the findings and enhance the clinical applicability of Proclarix in diverse patient populations.

Conclusions

With a 5% posttest probability of csPCa following a negative result, high sensitivity, and the ability to reduce unnecessary biopsies, Proclarix represents a valuable diagnostic tool in the early detection and management of PCa. These findings suggest that Proclarix could be safely integrated into clinical practice to reduce the burden of unnecessary biopsies while maintaining a high detection rate for csPCa. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm its clinical applicability and long-term benefits.
