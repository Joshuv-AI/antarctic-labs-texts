Neutral Deformation Modes of Minimal Surfaces
André M. Sonnet, Epifanio G. Virga

Abstract
Stretching, drilling, and bending are the independent deformation modes of a thin shell, each of which has an individual energy content. When the energy content of a mode vanishes, that mode is neutral . We characterize all neutral modes of deformation of minimal surfaces into minimal surfaces. A hierarchy is found among these: a stretching neutral mode (which is an isometry) is also drilling neutral, and a drilling neutral mode is also bending neutral. Thus, all isometries of a minimal surface are globally neutral and give rise to soft elasticity . More generally, all minimal surfaces can be classified relative to a reference one in terms of three energy contents, which can be given in closed form.



I
Introduction

Soft
thin shells are two-dimensional material bodies, likely to be more responsive than others to
drilling

In the variational theory that we proposed in

Our theory for soft thin shells is formally

Any new theory is likely (and also lucky) to have supporters and detractors (most go simply unnoticed). Especially on crowded grounds, as is that of shell theories, one needs direct experience to move forward. It is the purpose of this paper to put our theory to the test. Before attempting to solve specific boundary value problems with it, we found it expedient to see how the energy splitting characteristic of its construction behaves on a wealth of shell shapes and their deformations; of course, the wider the better. We chose the whole class of

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sect.
II

III

III
Modes of Deformation

In
Sonnet and Virga 2025a
we identified three distinct modes in the deformation of a thin shell: a metric one pertaining to a stretching of the material in the tangent plane, a drilling one pertaining to a rotation about the local surface normal, and a bending one pertaining
to a rotation about a vector tangent to the surface.

A starting point for arriving at elastic energy measures for the three
distinct modes is the polar decomposition (
25
).
In an isometry, the stretching tensors
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are simply
the projectors onto the tangent planes of
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}^{\ast}
,
and so
W_{\mathrm{s}}
given by

W_{\mathrm{s}}=\lvert\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\nu})\rvert^{2}=\lvert\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{P}(\bm{\nu}^{\ast})\rvert^{2}

(32)

is a natural choice for a pure measure of stretching.

The rotation
\mathbf{R}
in (
25
) can have contributions from both drilling and bending. It was proved in
Sonnet and Virga 2024
that any rotation can be uniquely decomposed into one about a given unit vector
\bm{e}
and one about a vector perpendicular to
\bm{e}
. Hence,
letting this
\bm{e}
be the normal
\bm{\nu}
, the rotation

\mathbf{R}
can be written in the form

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}^{b}(\alpha_{b})\mathbf{R}^{d}(\alpha_{d})

(33)

where
\mathbf{R}^{b}
is a rotation by an angle
\alpha_{b}
about a direction in the tangent plane and

\mathbf{R}^{d}
is a rotation by an angle
\alpha_{d}
about the surface normal. One might be tempted to use the two rotation angles
\alpha_{b}
and
\alpha_{d}
to construct measures of bending and drilling, but unfortunately
they are not frame invariant. A uniform rotation of the shell performed after the deformation
\bm{y}
would change the angles in an intricate way
Sonnet and Virga 2025a
.

A way forward was proposed in
Sonnet and Virga 2025a
. Instead of using the angles
\alpha_{b}
and
\alpha_{d}
directly, local averages over relative rotations are computed in small discs on the tangent plane
8
8

8

To be precise, the averages computed in
Sonnet and Virga 2025a

are of
\tan^{2}(\alpha_{b}/2)
and
\tan^{2}(\alpha_{d}/2)
.
. Using these averages, pure measures of drilling and bending can then be obtained in terms of the third-rank tensor

\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\mathbf{R},

(34)

the skew-symmetric tensor
\mathbf{W}(\bm{\nu})
associted with the normal

\bm{\nu}
via

\mathbf{W}(\bm{\nu})\bm{a}=\bm{\nu}\times\bm{a}

(35)

for all vectors

, and the curvature tensor
\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}
.
A pure measure of drilling is given by

W_{\mathrm{d}}:=\lvert\mathbf{W}(\bm{\nu})\circ\mathbf{H}\rvert^{2}

(36)

The elastic energy density associated with a deformation of a shell would then be

W_{\mathrm{b}}
can be computed explicitly in terms of systems of connectors for the moving frames
(\bm{e}_{u},\bm{e}_{v},\bm{\nu})
and
(\bm{e}_{u}^{\ast},\bm{e}_{v}^{\ast},\bm{\nu}^{\ast})
on the surfaces
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}^{\ast}
. The connectors are fields
(\bm{c},\bm{d}_{u},\bm{d}_{v})
and
(\bm{c}^{\ast},\bm{d}_{u}^{\ast},\bm{d}_{v}^{\ast})
, tangent to
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}^{\ast}
, that represent the derivatives of the frames’ basis vectors.
Explicitly, they obey the equations

\begin{cases}\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{e}_{u}=\bm{e}_{v}\otimes\bm{c}+\bm{\nu}\otimes\bm{d}_{u},\\
\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{e}_{v}=-\bm{e}_{u}\otimes\bm{c}+\bm{\nu}\otimes\bm{d}_{v},\\
\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}=-\bm{e}_{u}\otimes\bm{d}_{u}-\bm{e}_{v}\otimes\bm{d}_{v},\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}\nabla^{\ast}\!\!\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{e}_{u}^{\ast}=\bm{e}_{v}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{c}^{\ast}+\bm{\nu}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{d}_{u}^{\ast},\\
\nabla^{\ast}\!\!\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{e}_{v}^{\ast}=-\bm{e}_{u}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{c}^{\ast}+\bm{\nu}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{d}_{v}^{\ast},\\
\nabla^{\ast}\!\!\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}^{\ast}=-\bm{e}_{u}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{d}_{u}^{\ast}-\bm{e}_{v}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{d}_{v}^{\ast},\end{cases}

(39)

where
\nabla^{\ast}\!\!\!_{\mathrm{s}}
denotes the surface gradient on
\mathscr{S}^{\ast}
. We call
\bm{c}
and
\bm{c}^{\ast}
the
spin
connectors of the two frames and
(\bm{d}_{u},\bm{d}_{v})
,
(\bm{d}^{\ast}_{u},\bm{d}^{\ast}_{v})
their
curvature
connectors.
Because the curvature tensors are symmetric, the curvature connectors must obey the identities

\bm{d}_{u}\cdot\bm{e}_{v}=\bm{d}_{v}\cdot\bm{e}_{u}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{d}_{u}^{\ast}\cdot\bm{e}_{v}^{\ast}=\bm{d}_{v}^{\ast}\cdot\bm{e}_{u}^{\ast}.

(40)

In
Sonnet and Virga 2025a
, equations (
39
) were employed to compute the tensor
\mathbf{H}
as defined in (
34
) starting from the representation of
\mathbf{R}
in (

W_{\mathrm{d}}=4\lvert\mathbf{V}\bm{c}^{\ast}-\mathbf{R}\bm{c}\rvert^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\mathrm{b}}=4(\lvert\mathbf{V}(\nabla^{\ast}\!\!\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}^{\ast})\rvert^{2}-\lvert\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}\rvert^{2})^{2}.

(41)

As we are here dealing with deformations between minimal surfaces,
we can give the bending energy a simpler form. A simple direct computation or an application of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem can be used to express the trace of the square of the
curvature tensor in terms of the mean curvature
H
and the Gaussian curvature
K
as

\lvert\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu}\rvert^{2}=\operatorname{tr}((\nabla\!_{\mathrm{s}}\bm{\nu})^{2})=2(2H^{2}-K).

(42)

As for minimal surfaces
H=0
, we find by also using (
29
) that

W_{\mathrm{b}}=16\left(\frac{\lvert\bm{r}_{,u}^{\ast}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\bm{r}_{,u}\rvert^{2}}K^{\ast}-K\right)^{2}.

(43)

IV.2
Mode Neutrality

We say that a deformation is
neutral
with respect to one of the three deformation modes if the corresponding energy density is
zero under that deformation. We are particularly interested in deformations that
are
globally neutral
and are now in a position to derive criteria for global neutrality with respect to the three modes.

IV.2.1
Bending

The bending energy density (
53
) is
zero if and only if

\left(\frac{\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})}{1+\lvert h|^{2}}\right)^{2}=1.

(54)

As all three terms in the bracket on the left-hand side are real and strictly positive, this is equivalent to

\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert=\frac{1+\lvert h|^{2}}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}},

(55)

which is a non-linear complex differential equation for
h
. For reasons that will soon become apparent, we shall
simply call
neutral
any solution of (
55
).
We defer a discussion of the general solution of
(
55
) to Section
IV.3
, where (
55
) will also be given a geometric interpretation as an
area preserving
mapping. For now, we note that there are indeed solutions of (
55
), one of them being the identity
h(w)=w
, the case treated in
Sonnet and Virga 2024
.

IV.2.2
Drilling

The drilling energy density (
47
) is
zero if and only if

\alpha_{,u}=\left(\ln\frac{(1+\lvert h|^{2})\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}\right)_{,v}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{,v}=-\left(\ln\frac{(1+\lvert h|^{2})\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}\right)_{,u}.

(56)

By taking partial derivatives, it can be seen that this implies
that

\Delta\alpha=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta\ln\frac{(1+\lvert h|^{2})\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}=0.

(57)

The first of these equations is automatically satisfied: harmonic functions remain so under conformal mappings
(
Weinberger 1995
, §52)
, and so
as
\Delta^{\ast}\chi^{\ast}=0
also
\Delta\chi^{\ast}=0
and thus

\Delta\alpha=\Delta(\chi^{\ast}-\chi)=0
.
9
9

9

It is perhaps worth recalling that here
\Delta^{\ast}
denotes the Laplacian with respect to the starred variables
(u^{\ast},v^{\ast})
, whereas
\Delta
is the Laplacian in the
(u,v)
variables. To apply
\Delta
to a starred function, such as
\chi^{\ast}
or
\alpha^{\ast}
, the latter must be regarded as functions of
(u,v)
, upon use of (
15
).

Regarding the second equation, note that for an analytic
function
f(z)=\lvert f(z)\rvert\mathrm{e}^{i\arg f(z)}
we have that

\ln f(z)=\ln\lvert f(z)\rvert+i\arg f(z).

(58)

Thus wherever the logarithm of an analytic function is defined,
the logarithm of its modulus is the real part of an analytic function
and thus harmonic. Applied to
h^{\prime}
, this means that

\Delta\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert=0,

(59)

and thus a vanishing drilling energy density implies that

\Delta\ln\frac{1+\lvert h|^{2}}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}=0.

(60)

An elementary computation shows that

\Delta\ln(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})=\frac{4}{(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})^{2}}\quad\text{and so}\quad\Delta^{\ast}\ln(1+\lvert h|^{2})=\frac{4}{(1+\lvert h|^{2})^{2}}.

(61)

As
\Delta=\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}\Delta^{\ast}
, see for example
(
Kythe 2019
, p. 548)
, we conclude that

\Delta\ln\frac{1+\lvert h|^{2}}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}=4\left(\frac{\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}}{(1+\lvert h|^{2})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})^{2}}\right),

(62)

which is zero if and only if
h
satisfies (
55
),
i.e.
, if
h
is neutral. Thus a drilling-neutral deformation
is automatically also bending-neutral.

IV.2.3
Stretching

The stretching energy density (
46
) is
zero if and only if

\mathrm{e}^{\Phi^{\ast}-\Phi}\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert\frac{1+\lvert h|^{2}}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}=1.

(63)

Dividing by the exponential and taking the logarithm shows
that this implies

\ln\frac{(1+\lvert h|^{2})\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert}{1+\lvert w\rvert^{2}}=\Phi-\Phi^{\ast}.

(64)

With this, and setting

\varphi:=\Phi^{\ast}-\Phi,

(65)

Equations (
56
) simply become

\alpha_{,u}=-\varphi_{,v}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{,v}=\varphi_{,u}.

(66)

That these equations are satisfied is a straightforward consequence
of the Cauchy-Rieman equations for
\ln F=\Phi+i\chi
,

\ln F^{\ast}=\Phi^{\ast}+i\chi^{\ast}
, and
h
. Thus a stretching neutral deformation is automatically also drilling neutral (and hence bending neutral).

IV.2.4
Consequences of Mode Neutrality

We have established a hierarchy of neutral modes, in particular we have that

W_{\mathrm{s}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad W_{\mathrm{d}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad W_{\mathrm{b}}=0.

(67)

Furthermore, as
W_{\mathrm{b}}=0
if and only if Equation (
55
) holds, whenever any mode is neutral, so is
h
. We note that
if there exist indeed isometric deformations of minimal surfaces
of the type (
12
), then
(
67
) shows that all modes are neutral and the
energy (
38
) vanishes. Thus such isometries provide an
example of
soft elasticity
.

In order to indentify some concrete neutral deformations, we now proceed to establish the restrictions placed by mode neutrality on
the minimal surfaces represented via
F
and
F^{\ast}
. Naturally, the restrictions will be more severe when there are more neutral modes.

We start by considering
W_{\mathrm{s}}=W_{\mathrm{d}}=W_{\mathrm{b}}=0
. By using (
55
) in (
64
), we obtain that
with
\varphi
as in (
65
) we have

\varphi=-2\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert,\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}}.

(68)

Furthermore, using (
55
) in (
56
)
shows that

\alpha_{,u}=(2\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert)_{,v}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{,v}=-(2\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert)_{,u}.

(69)

As
\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert
and
\arg h^{\prime}
are conjugate harmonic functions, see (
58
), we conclude that

\alpha=\alpha_{0}-2\arg h^{\prime}

(70)

with some constant
\alpha_{0}
. As
F^{\ast}=\mathrm{e}^{\varphi+i\alpha}F
,
see (
44
),
we find that

F^{\ast}=\frac{1}{\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{i(\alpha_{0}-2\arg h^{\prime})}F=\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha_{0}}\frac{F}{h^{\prime 2}}.

(71)

When
h
is the identity and so
h^{\prime}=1
, this becomes

F^{\ast}=\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha_{0}}F
, which is the
Bonnet transformation
of
F
, the same responsible for changing a catenoid into a helicoid (see, for example, pp. 102 and 149 of
Dierkes et al. 2010
).

Let us now assume that
W_{\mathrm{s}}\neq 0
but that still
W_{\mathrm{d}}=W_{\mathrm{b}}=0
.
In that case, as
h
is neutral, (
70
) still holds, but we need a replacement for (
68
). We
can use (
55
) in (
46
) to find
that

W_{\mathrm{s}}=2\left(\mathrm{e}^{\Phi^{\ast}-\Phi}\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}-1\right)^{2}=2(\lambda-1)^{2},

(72)

where we have set

\lambda:=\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\varphi}.

(73)

Thus in this case the relation between
F
and
F^{\ast}

becomes

F^{\ast}=\frac{\lambda}{\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{i(\alpha_{0}-2\arg h^{\prime})}F=\lambda\mathrm{e}^{i\alpha_{0}}\frac{F}{h^{\prime 2}}.

(74)

Finally, let us consider a situation in which
W_{\mathrm{s}}\neq 0\neq W_{\mathrm{d}}
while still
W_{\mathrm{b}}=0
. We can again use
\lambda
as in (
73
),
but as (
56
) no longer holds, we instead use
(
55
) in the drilling energy density (
47
) which thus becomes

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{d}}

\displaystyle=-K(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})^{2}\Biggl\{\left[\alpha_{,u}-\left(2\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert\right)_{,v}\right]^{2}+\left[\alpha_{,v}+\left(2\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert\right)_{,u}\right]^{2}\Biggl\}

\displaystyle=-K(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})^{2}\Biggl\{\left[\alpha_{,u}+\left(2\arg h^{\prime}\right)_{,u}\right]^{2}+\left[\alpha_{,v}+\left(2\arg h^{\prime}\right)_{,v}\right]^{2}\Biggl\}

\displaystyle=-K(1+\lvert w\rvert^{2})^{2}(\nabla\beta)^{2},

(75)

where we have once again used that
\ln\lvert h^{\prime}\rvert
and
\arg h^{\prime}
are conjugate harmonic functions and defined a harmonic function
\beta

via

\beta:=\alpha+2\arg h^{\prime}.

(76)

As now
\alpha=\beta-2\arg h^{\prime}
, we see that the function
\beta
replaces the constant
\alpha_{0}
in (
70
) and
the relation between
F
and
F^{\ast}

now is

F^{\ast}=\lambda\mathrm{e}^{i\beta}\frac{F}{h^{\prime 2}}.

(77)

To summarise, for a deformation to be
bending neutral
,
we need
F^{\ast}
to be related to
F
via (
77
)
with an
h
that satisfies (
55
), a harmonic function
\beta
, and a stretching
\lambda
. If furthermore
\beta
is constant, then (
74
) holds, and the deformation
is also
drilling neutral
. In this case, if
\lambda=1
,
then (
71
) is satisfied, and the deformation is also an
isometry
.

IV.3
Möbius Transformation

A special deformation of the type (
12
) is
the
Goursat transformation

Goursat 1888
. It is obtained by using for
h
a Möbius transformation
10
10

10

An effective visual representation of (
78
) can be found in
Arnold and Rogness 2008
.

h(w)=\frac{aw+b}{cw+d}\quad\text{with}\quad ad-bc\neq 0,\quad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C},

(78)

and by defining

F^{\ast}\left(h(w)\right):=\frac{F(w)}{(h^{\prime}(w))^{2}},

(79)

where, by (
78
),

h^{\prime}=\frac{ad-bc}{(cw+d)^{2}}.

(80)

It was already mentioned by Goursat that for the special
Möbius transformations of the type

h(w)=\frac{aw-\bar{c}}{cw+\bar{a}},

(81)

which is obtained from (
78
) by setting

and

,

(
Goursat 1888
, p. 142)
(see also § 774 of

).

The parameters featuring in (
78
) or (
81
) are notoriously redundant; if all are multiplied by the same complex number,
h
remains unaltered. Often the following normalization conditions are adopted for (
78
) and (
81
),

ab-cd=1\quad\text{or}\quad|a|^{2}+|c|^{2}=1.

(82)

It is a straightforward computation to show that
h
in the form (
81
) satisfies
(
55
). Equation (
79
) shows that
then (
71
) is satisfied with
\alpha_{0}=0
and
so all the modes are neutral and the elastic energy (
38
)
vanishes, as it should for a rigid rotation.

It can be shown that conformal mappings of the type
(
81
) are indeed the only solutions of
(
55
). To see this, we first need to provide a geometric interpretation of (
13
).

Letting
\mathbb{S}^{2}
be the unit sphere in three-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathscr{E}
, we denote by
\pi
the stereographic projection of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
from its North pole
N
onto the equatorial plane, which we identify with the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. As shown, for example, in
(
Needham 2021
, p. 73)
, the arc-length element
\mathrm{d}s
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is related to the corresponding arc-length element
|\mathrm{d}w|
on
\mathbb{C}
through the equation

\mathrm{d}s=\frac{2}{1+|w|^{2}}|\mathrm{d}w|.

(83)

Rewriting (
55
) in the equivalent form

\frac{2}{1+|w^{\ast}|^{2}}|\mathrm{d}w^{\ast}|=\frac{2}{1+|w|^{2}}|\mathrm{d}w|,

(84)

where use has also been made of (
13
), we readily conclude that any solution
h
of (
55
) is indeed a locally
area preserving
mapping of
\Omega
onto
\Omega^{\ast}
.

Thus, a neutral
h
as a mapping from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{C}

corresponds via the stereographic projection
\pi
to
an area preserving mapping
\hat{h}
of the Riemann sphere

\hat{\mathbb{C}}

onto itself.
11
11

11

The Riemann sphere
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is nothing but the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
made compact by the addition of an extra point
\infty
, the point at
infinity
. If, by the stereographic projection
\pi
, we identify
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{N\}
,
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is then identified with the whole of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
(see, for example,
(
A.Jones and Singerman 1987
, Sect. 1.1)
).
As both
h
and
\pi
are
conformal, so is
\hat{h}
. But a classical theorem says that all conformal mappings
\hat{h}
of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
into itself are represented by (
78
) (see, for example
(
Hilbert and Cohn-Vossen 1990
, p. 269)
) and it is a simple matter to show (see Appendix
B
) that a Möbius transformation as in (
78
) is area preserving if and only if it can be set in the form (
81
), and so it amounts to a rigid rotation of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Said differently, any conformal, area preserving mapping of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
onto itself
is an isometry generated by a uniform rotation in
\mathscr{E}
.
12
12

12

A somewhat weaker form of this result can also be given a direct proof, which is presented in
Sonnet and Virga 2025b
among other metric implications for the kinematics of surfaces in three-dimensional space, much in the same spirit as
Seguin and Fried 2022
.

In light of this, all modes of soft elasticity (
71
)
are either simply a rigid rotation of the minimal surface
when
\alpha_{0}=0
or, for any other constant value of
\alpha_{0}
,
a rigid rotation combined with a Bonnet transformation.
13
13

13

This is just the same characterization for all isometries of minimal surfaces in three-dimensional space proved in
(
Lawson 1971
, p. 159)
.
Thus, an isometry of a minimal surface is globally neutral, but it may fail to be a uniform rotation.

A further consequence of the fact that (

) is that, for bending neutral deformations,

can be replaced by the identity

, with no effective loss of generality, as done in

; the only difference is that the

of
\mathscr{S}^{\ast}
would be the same as that of
\mathscr{S}
, instead of being rotated in space.
14
14

14

By spherical image of a surface, we mean the image of the Gauss map
\bm{\nu}:\mathscr{S}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
associated with the surface.
Under a bending neutral deformation with

as in (

necessarily the other rotated in space.

V
Conclusions

Stretching, drilling, and bending are the independent deformation modes occurring in a soft thin shell. For each, we proposed in
Sonnet and Virga 2025a
a specific energy content, the combination of which provides the strain energy density of a second-grade variational theory.

Here, we considered all deformations of minimal surfaces into minimal surfaces and studied the deformation modes that selectively switch off one energy content; we called them
neutral
.

A precise hierarchy has emerged. A stretching neutral mode, which is an
isometry
, is also drilling neutral, and a drilling neutral mode is also bending neutral. Bending neutral modes, on the other hand, can be neither drilling nor stretching neutral. The hardest, that is, most energetic deformations of a minimal surface into another harbour no neutral mode, whereas isometries are the softest of all, as they take place at no energy cost.

We hope that having characterized the energy modes of minimal surfaces may foster a better understanding of the energetics of soft thin shells. We found a means to classify all minimal surfaces relative to a reference one in terms of three different energies stored in them. It would be interesting to see whether this classification prompts any new pattern to emerge in the wide, fascinating realm of these surfaces. Our method actually goes beyond this realm: some of the conclusions reached here are indeed valid in a more general setting, as will be discussed in
Sonnet and Virga 2025b
.

Area preserving Möbius transformations

We are looking for Möbius transformations
