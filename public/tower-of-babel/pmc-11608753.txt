Development and performance evaluation of a clinical prediction model for sepsis risk in burn patients
Authors: Weiqing Luo, Lei Xiong, Jianshuo Wang, Chen Li, Shaoheng Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a common and severe complication in burn patients and remains one of the leading causes of mortality. This retrospective study aimed to develop a predictive model for the risk of in-hospital sepsis among burn patients treated at Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital between January 2022 and January 2024, with the goal of improving clinical outcomes through early prevention based on risk stratification. A total of 302 eligible patients were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio for model development and validation, respectively. Predictive factors were initially selected using LASSO regression, followed by logistic regression analysis to establish the prediction model and construct a nomogram. The final model incorporated 4 independent predictors: burn area (odds ratio [OR] = 1.043, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.026–1.062/1%), hemoglobin (OR = 0.968, 95% CI: 0.954–0.980/1 g/L), diabetes (OR = 10.91, 95% CI: 2.563–56.62), and potassium (OR = 3.091, 95% CI: 1.635–6.064/1 mmol/L). The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve were 0.875 and 0.861 for the training and validation cohorts, with Youden indexes of 0.634 and 0.600, respectively. The calibration curve and decision curve analysis demonstrated good predictive accuracy and clinical utility of the model. These findings suggest that our developed model exhibits robust predictive performance for the risk of in-hospital sepsis in burn patients, and early prevention strategies based on risk stratification may potentially improve clinical outcomes.

1. Introduction

Sepsis is a severe infectious disease caused by an abnormal inflammatory response to infection, significantly impacting patient safety.[1] Due to varying economic and medical conditions across different countries and regions, the incidence of sepsis in burn patients ranges from 8% to 42.5%, making it a common and serious complication that is one of the leading causes of death.[2,3] Despite advances in the economy and healthcare leading to a recent decline in the incidence and mortality of sepsis, it remains a major cause of global health loss.[4]

Studies have shown that early prevention and treatment of sepsis in burn patients can effectively reduce mortality and improve prognosis.[5] The advancement of machine learning has enhanced the prediction efficiency of sepsis risk in burn patients.[6] However, previous studies often included subjective factors such as treatment measures in their predictive indicators or lacked nomograms, significantly reducing the clinical applicability of the prediction models. Currently, the development of an efficient and stable prediction model for sepsis risk in burn patients remains a focus of medical research.

This study aims to establish an efficient and stable prediction model by collecting clinical indicators and demographic data at patient admission, excluding subjective factors such as treatment measures. This model is expected to assist clinicians in the early identification of high-risk individuals and the implementation of interventions, thereby improving the clinical outcomes of the patients, while providing foundational information and insights for future research.

2. Methods

2.1. Data collection

The clinical data of burn patients treated in the Burn Department of the Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital of Jinan University from January 2022 to January 2024 was collected. Based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, 302 patients were ultimately included. Patients were divided into 2 groups based on the occurrence of sepsis: the sepsis group (N = 80) and the non-sepsis group. The patients were randomly split into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3 to construct and validate the model, respectively, as illustrated in Figure 1. The selection of variables was based on clinical relevance and data availability according to clinical guidelines and expert opinions from the Burn Department. The variables included laboratory indicators, demographic characteristics, and burn characteristics at admission, while excluding subjective factors such as treatment interventions. The diagnosis of sepsis followed the Sepsis 3.0,[1] and burn characteristics followed the International Society for Burn Injuries Practice Guidelines for Burn Care, Part 2.[7]

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) met the burn standard; (2) admitted within 24 hours after burn injury; (3) did not have any psychiatric disorders. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients who were discharged midway or died during hospitalization without experiencing sepsis; (2) patients with severe infections present at admission; (3) patients with malignant tumors; (4) patients with missing clinical data; (5) patients with congenital or immune system diseases.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R version 4.2.2. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as median (interquartile range), depending on the distribution. Categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. For continuous variables, differences between 2 groups were evaluated using an unpaired t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. For categorical variables, differences between groups were assessed using the χ² test or Fisher exact test. Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded from the dataset, whereas those with <20% missing data were handled using a “simple imputation” method.

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation, implemented via the “glmnet” package in R, was employed to select the optimal combination of influencing factors, using lambda 1se as the criterion. Subsequently, significant risk factors were determined through multiple logistic regression using the “glm” package. A nomogram to predict the risk of sepsis was constructed using the “rms” package in R. The model’s performance was validated using 500 bootstrap resamples. Furthermore, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calibration curves were generated using the “fbroc” and “rms” packages, respectively, to assess the model’s discriminative ability and calibration. The clinical utility of the model was evaluated through a decision curve analysis (DCA) curve, performed using the “rmda” package. Finally, the model’s robustness was assessed by comparing the ROC and DCA curves of the model and individual risk factors and by evaluating the distribution trend of patients’ Nomoscore using box plots (P < .05 was considered statistically significant).

2.4. Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital of Jinan University (Approval Number: 2024-287-01) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the noninterventional and retrospective nature of the study.

The incidence of sepsis during hospitalization was 26.49%. Patients were randomly split into a training set (N = 211) and a validation set (N = 91) at a 7:3 ratio. 3.3. Logistic regression analysis

Using the predictors identified by LASSO regression as independent variables, a multivariate logistic regression model was employed for further analysis (Table 2). The results demonstrated that 4 factors significantly affected the incidence of sepsis in burn patients: burn area (odds ratio [OR] = 1.043, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.026–1.062, P < .001), potassium (OR = 3.091, 95%CI: 1.635–6.064, P = .001), diabetes (OR = 10.91, 95%CI: 2.563–56.62, P = .002), and hemoglobin (OR = 0.968, 95%CI: 0.954–0.980, P < .001).

3.5. Evaluation and validation of nomogram prediction model

The area under the curve (AUC) of the nomogram in the training group and validation group are 0.875 (95%CI: 0.819–0.930) and 0.861 (95%CI: 0.774–0.947), respectively, showing no significant difference between the groups (Fig. 4). The Youden index of the ROC curves for the training and validation sets are 0.634 and 0.600, respectively, with kappa values of 0.612 and 0.588, and F1 values of 0.714 for both groups. These results indicate that the model has good discriminatory ability.

ROC curve of the nomogram. ROC curve = receiver operating characteristic curve.

To assess the consistency between predicted and actual probabilities in these 2 groups, a calibration plot and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test were employed. The results were χ2 = 5.129 (P = .744) in the training group (Fig. 5A) and χ2 = 9.622 (P = .293) in the validation group (Fig. 5B), which demonstrate good predictive accuracy of the nomogram. DCA was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the model in the training group (Fig. 6A) and validation group (Fig. 6B), showing that the net benefits of these 2 groups were significantly higher than the 2 extreme cases where no patients had endoscopic activity, and all patients had endoscopic activity. Thus, the nomogram is a valuable tool for guiding therapeutic decisions.

3.6. Rationalization of nomogram prediction model

By comparing the AUC and DCA of the nomogram with each individual factor in the validation set, it is evident that the nomogram outperforms each individual factor (Fig. 7A and B). Utilizing the optimal cutoff point of the ROC curve from the validation set, burn patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on their nomogram scores (Fig. 7C), with an optimal cutoff point corresponding to a nomogram score of 162.257 (Youden index = 0.637). Finally, we calculated each patient’s nomogram score and displayed their distribution using a box plot (Fig. 7D), revealing a statistically significant difference in nomogram scores between sepsis and non-sepsis patients in the validation group (P < .001).

4. Discussion

Sepsis remains a significant clinical challenge due to its complex mechanisms and high mortality rate.[8] An epidemiological study indicated that the incidence of sepsis in adult burn patients is 26%, with a mortality rate of 34.4% among those who develop sepsis.[9] Sepsis is one of the main causes of death in burn patients, early detection and intervention are crucial in reducing the rates of secondary organ failure and mortality in patients.[3,10] This study developed a nomogram model through retrospective analysis to predict the risk of septicemia occurring during hospitalization in burn patients. The independent predictors included in the model were burn area, serum potassium, diabetes, and hemoglobin. The model’s AUC, DAC, and calibration curves all demonstrated good predictive performance, indicating that the established prediction model has strong clinical utility. The early and objective nature of these indices enhances their clinical utility.

Burns disrupt the skin’s physical barrier, opening the body to microbial invasion, exacerbated by the hypermetabolic response following burns, leading to severe infections.[11,12] As the area of burns increases, the risk of patients developing infections and mortality rates also increase.[13] This study similarly concluded that the burn area is an independent risk factor for patients developing sepsis. Early interventions for patients with extensive burns, such as enhanced wound care, use of antibiotics, skin grafting, and human growth hormone application, can effectively reduce wound infection rates.[3]

Serum potassium was identified as a major risk factor for mortality in sepsis patients.[14] Sutton et al[15] found that higher potassium levels were positively correlated with increased hospital stay and overall mortality risk in sepsis patients, a conclusion echoed by our study. Potassium is a vital electrolyte whose disturbance increases the risk of multi-organ dysfunction and death.[16] Studies have shown that early pharmacological blockade of potassium channels can significantly improve inflammatory parameters and cardiovascular function in septic rat models, thereby reducing mortality.[17] This suggests that elevated serum potassium may increase infection risk by exacerbating cardiovascular damage and inflammation. However, recent MICC research indicates a U-shaped relationship between serum potassium levels and 28-day mortality risk in sepsis patients, where both hypokalemia and hyperkalemia increase mortality risk.[18] Thus, maintaining normal potassium levels in sepsis patients is prudent, though the optimal range requires further investigation.

Diabetes, with its associated immune dysfunction, endothelial disorder, and increased inflammatory response, impairs wound healing and raises infection risk following skin injury.[19] The study demonstrated that elevated posttraumatic blood glucose levels were positively associated with adverse outcomes in patients, regardless of diabetes status.[20] However, diabetic patients were more prone to stress-induced hyperglycemia following physical trauma compared to nondiabetic individuals. Therefore, monitoring and maintaining normal blood glucose levels in burn patients is essential.

Anemia is a common complication in sepsis, primarily attributed to inflammation-related mechanisms (“anemia of inflammation”).[21,22] Low hemoglobin levels, as a critical oxygen carrier, lead to tissue hypoxia and ischemic damage, exacerbating disease progression.[23] Studies highlight a strong association between hemoglobin concentration and sepsis outcomes, with low hemoglobin as a predictor of poor prognosis or increased mortality.[24] Our findings concur with these studies. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and may involve tissue hypoxia, ischemic damage, and impaired antibiotic absorption.[23,25] Research indicates that patients with hemoglobin < 7.2 g/dL benefit from hemoglobin supplementation.[26]

Of course, our study had certain limitations. It is a single-center, small-sample retrospective study lacking external validation. The limited laboratory indices did not incorporate additional biomarkers such as interleukin-6 and procalcitonin. Finally, we did not stratify variables with known nonlinear relationships, such as serum potassium and hemoglobin levels. Future research should include large-scale, multi-center studies incorporating more comprehensive indices to refine the predictive model, validated through cohort studies.

5. Conclusion

The predictive model for sepsis risk developed in this study for burn patients is expected to assist clinicians in the early identification of high-risk individuals and the implementation of interventions, thereby improving the clinical outcomes of the patients. Moreover, our findings identify burn area, serum potassium, diabetes, and hemoglobin as independent predictors of sepsis risk, providing a reference for future research in this field.
