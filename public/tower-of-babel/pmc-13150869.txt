Experiences of families participating in a 10-week family-focused e-Health healthy lifestyle programme for school-aged children with overweight or obesity: a qualitative study
Authors: Diana Zhu, Aimee L Dordevic, Zoe E Davidson, Simone Gibson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore families’ experiences participating in a 10-week web-based lifestyle programme for school-aged children with overweight or obesity.DesignA qualitative study using inductive analysis of semi-structured interview data.SettingVictoria, Australia.ParticipantsFamilies (children aged 7–13 years with overweight or obesity—body mass index ≥85th percentile—and accompanying parent) recruited for a randomised controlled trial that evaluated the effectiveness of the web-based programme and who received the programme (n=102 children/85 families) were invited to participate in a semi-structured interview at 3 months post-programme.InterventionFamilies received a 10-week family-focused electronic health (e-Health; web-based) lifestyle programme with health coaching sessions—an evidence-based programme adapted from its in-person, group-based counterpart.ResultsA total of 28 families, including 34 children (eight siblings) and mostly mothers, shared their experiences. 10 themes were identified on family members’ experiences and aligned with the socioecological model: intrapersonal—knowledge development on healthy living; experiences and stigma related to overweight, obesity or weight; engaging with structural features of the web-based programme, interpersonal—family dynamic; connections with others (non-healthcare professionals) outside of home; relationship with healthcare professionals, environmental/institutional—impact of COVID-19 lockdowns; health-promoting environments; promotion of and access to overweight or obesity management programmes; web-based programme as part of a larger or established system. Each theme highlighted factors that influenced programme uptake and engagement.ConclusionsValuable insights were gained on ways to better adapt e-Health (web-based) lifestyle programmes for children with overweight or obesity. Families perceived advantages in a web-based lifestyle programme and highly regarded humanised features and elements comparable to conventional in-person programmes. Further research is needed to explore the perspectives of families from diverse populations, fathers and families who decline participation in the follow-up period. Web-based lifestyle programmes that incorporate contemporary e-Health technologies, including responsive AI, also warrant further investigation to maximise programme benefits.Trial registration numberACTRN12621001762842.Keywords: Obesity, Overweight, Family, Digital Technology

Introduction

The prevalence of children living with obesity, including severe obesity, continues at unprecedentedly high rates worldwide.1,4 Concurrently, limited evidence-based weight management services are available or accessible to families.1,5 With families, including children, extensively using the internet and various digital platforms in their daily lives, delivering healthcare services through digital technologies (ie, electronic health (e-Health) intervention), may be a favourable way to transform conventional services for managing childhood obesity.6 Existing evidence also suggests the need for digital skills to be cultivated from childhood to minimise the worsening of economic, social and cultural inequalities; therefore, online education programmes, including e-Health interventions, may serve as additional formal opportunities to strengthen digital competency.7 8 There is a growing body of evidence supporting the use of e-Health interventions, including for treating childhood overweight or obesity.9,12 Research has found that some such interventions were effective in improving children’s weight-based and psychosocial health outcomes and supporting lifestyle modifications conducive to children’s health.9 13 Most studies, however, have focused on short-term programme efficacy (immediately post-programme completion, with or without follow-up at 6 months or less), parent- or child-focused as opposed to family-centred interventions or e-Health interventions administered as adjunct components to conventional in-person services.10 12

Due to the vast, progressive and dynamic nature of technological developments, better understanding of participants’ experiences of e-Health interventions for managing childhood obesity is needed to appreciate the mechanisms underlying effective e-Health programmes.10 Existing studies have focused on parents’ perceptions, including their expectations of an e-Health lifestyle programme or experiences with parent-focused e-Health interventions.14 15 To maximise the benefits of e-Health interventions for managing childhood obesity, and in the context of socioecological changes following the COVID-19 pandemic, more research is needed to explore the lived experiences of families (the participating children and parents) engaging with family-focused e-Health healthy lifestyle programmes.

This study presents the qualitative follow-up data from a randomised controlled trial (RCT) that evaluated the effectiveness of one such e-Health intervention, the online Better Health Programme (BHPO). BHPO is a web-based (ie, key contents, including programme activities delivered through a website) healthy lifestyle programme with health coaching sessions delivered over the phone—adapted from the evidence-based, in-person group-based counterpart (BHPO).16 The RCT demonstrated that the intervention was effective in influencing clinically meaningful improvements in children’s body mass index (BMI) z-scores, psychosocial and lifestyle behaviour change outcomes in the short-term. This study aims to explore families’ experiences participating in BHPO.17 BHPO will be referred to hereon as a web-based programme.

Methods

Design

This study used a qualitative descriptive design with an interpretivist lens, employing inductive analysis of interview data collected from families included in the RCT, which evaluated the effectiveness of the web-based programme (ACTRN12621001762842). The RCT’s study protocol, including data collection and analysis of the qualitative component of the study, has been detailed elsewhere and is described in brief below (online supplemental material 1).16 The study design of the RCT, including recruitment for this qualitative study, is shown in figure 1. All research activities, including the delivery of the web-based programme, occurred following the end of lockdowns in Victoria due to the COVID-19 pandemic. This qualitative design was chosen to allow for the opportunity to develop a rich understanding of families’ lived experiences with an e-Health intervention, an underexplored phenomenon in childhood obesity treatment.18

Participants and recruitment

Children aged 7–13 years with overweight or obesity (BMI≥85th percentile; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention definition), who were otherwise generally healthy, living in Victoria, Australia and accompanied by a parent (ie, parent-child/siblings unit referred to as family from hereon) were recruited for the RCT from April 2022 to July 2023.19 The overall study recruited a total of 148 children (125 families), who were randomly assigned to receive the web-based programme immediately (intervention group) or following completion of a 10 weeks waitlist control period (control group). A total of 102 children (intervention—58, control—44) from 85 families (intervention—47, control—38) completed the RCT (10 weeks). Families received the web-based programme during Australian primary school terms or midterm schedules, from Term 2 2022 to Term 4 2023. Convenience sampling was used to invite families to participate in a semi-structured interview with a study investigator at 3 months post-programme completion regardless of whether they were in the intervention or waitlisted control group. RCT participation included incentives, in which children received a gift voucher, valued up to $A100 in the follow-up period post-programme (from 3 to 12 months). Families were reminded of incentives during recruitment for interviews. Once written consent from parents and assent from children were received, families were scheduled for interviews from October 2022 to April 2024 based on programme completion dates and families’ availability. Although this study focused on gaining insights from child/siblings and their parent recruited to the RCT and received the web-based programme, other members within the same family were also invited to partake in interviews if interested.

Intervention

The 10-week web-based programme is an evidence-based family-focused healthy lifestyle programme that is entirely technology-based, with content delivered through a password secured website. Through the website, families accessed weekly online modules targeting the family unit, including materials on healthy eating, physical activity and elements of health-supporting behavioural change strategies (eg, collaborative skills-building, self-efficacy, goal-setting, attitude/motivations, behaviour modelling). Families also received weekly 30-minute phone coaching sessions with a health professional coach (eg, dietitians/nutritionists, exercise physiologists), usually at the same time per week and based on families’ availability, preferences and schedules. Additionally, families received access to a private Facebook group with other families receiving the web-based programme. Families also received mailouts of physical resources, including sports equipment, practical tools and weekly summaries of the online content, from the administrating organisation Better Health Company (https://betterhealthcompany.org/), throughout the web-based programme. The programme included a reward system, whereby children may receive a gift voucher, valued up to $A70 (programme sessions that ran prior to Australian primary-school term 1, 2023) or $A50 (programme sessions that ran from Australian primary-school term 1, 2023 and onwards), for completing all programme activities.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with interested families or parents via Zoom videoconferencing or telephone, to explore their experiences of participating in the web-based programme. To support children’s engagement in dialogues, study investigators planned to interview the parent and children together first, followed by the parent alone. Parents and children, however, were given the flexibility to speak with study investigators together or alone during interview. An initial interview guide (online supplemental material 2) was developed by study investigators (SG, DZ) based on narrative interview methods, including questions to encourage families to reflect on their journey prior to, during and after receiving the web-based programme. Interviews lasted up to 1 hour. Study investigators (SG, DZ) met regularly to discuss interview questions and findings to ensure that questions comprehensively explored and captured the lived experiences of families, deliberately exploring both positive and challenging experiences. With families’ agreement, all interviews were audio recorded and auto-transcribed verbatim using Zoom videoconferencing. The interviewer also took manual notes during interviews to support reflections of conversations. A study investigator (DZ) verified the transcripts with the audio recording and revised the auto-transcript where needed prior to analysis. Study investigators regularly reflected on the information power of interviews, namely changes to the strength of dialogue.20

Data analysis

Transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.21 The process involved familiarisation with the data (reading interview transcripts), manual line-by-line coding of the data and development and refinement of categories to identify overarching themes. Three transcripts were selected at random and independently coded by two authors (DZ, SG). Codes were discussed and refined, after which the first author coded the remaining papers, with regular discussions with another author (SG) to confirm the coding process and discuss new codes as they arose. The first author then developed the codes into categories. Categories were critically reviewed with at least one other author (SG), including regular discussions to synthesise categories and identify overarching themes. The authors recognised that themes appeared to align with the constructs of the socioecological model, suggesting an interplay of various socioecological factors influences the development and progression of childhood overweight and obesity.22 A consensus was reached by the authors to present the narrative of the themes in alignment with the constructs of this framework to describe families’ experiences: intrapersonal factors, interpersonal factors and environmental/institutional factors.

Study investigators practised individual and team reflexivity during study design, data collection or analysis to acknowledge and discuss the influence of their biases.23 Investigators regularly engaged in team discussions, during which they identified, acknowledged and applied their diverse expertise in qualitative methodology, paediatric nutrition, childhood obesity and e-Health to develop the study design, collect data and make sense of the data regarding families’ experiences with e-Health interventions. Authors reflected on their professions (as dietitians or nutritionists, researchers and educators) and experience in paediatric nutrition, weight management, education and qualitative research. One author had extensive experience in qualitative research (SG) and was therefore the primary point of contact for all other study investigators regarding study methodology queries and debriefing sessions. The lead author had developing expertise in qualitative research and, therefore, had training and support in interviewing participants and analysing data. Authors’ professional backgrounds supported their ability to maximise strategies to sensitively engage in dialogue with families or interpret data within the context of childhood obesity. Authors also reflected on their biases and the potential influence of their role as researchers during the research process on families’ reflections or willingness to share their reflections during the interview.

Patient and public involvement

Families or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis or reporting of this research.

Results

A total of 28 families, including 34 children (eight siblings), were interviewed. Participating children (44% girls) were on average aged 10.8 (SD 1.7) years, and mostly interviewed with their mothers (n=26 mothers and n=2 fathers). 32% of families were single-parent households. Four interviews were conducted with the parent only (two children declined to participate altogether and were aware of their parent’s participation; two children provided assent but did not engage in conversation during the interview). One interview was conducted with a parent who provided secondary support for the participating child during the web-based programme (ie, supported the child to complete a few modules when the primary parent was unavailable). All but one family consented to the recording of the interview; therefore, audio transcripts from 27 interviews were available for analysis. Most interviews lasted for 22–56 min, with one interview being 12 min. Children were present and engaged in the interviews for approximately half of the time.

Children and their parents’ narrative reflections on their experiences participating in the web-based programme were summarised in 10 identified themes and then grouped to the components of the socioecological model (figure 2). Overall, their recollections were related to factors that influenced programme uptake or engagement. Experiences of the web-based programme were either exclusive to parents or children (referenced as parents or children, respectively) or shared by both (referenced as families). To protect participant confidentiality, children were reported as being either primary- or secondary-school aged (< 12 years or ≥12 years old, respectively).

Intrapersonal factors

Intrapersonal factors related to the participating family member’s (ie, child or parent) concept of self in the context of obesity and their understanding or expectations of lifestyle programmes for children. The main themes at this tier included: (1) knowledge development on healthy living, (2) experiences and stigma related to overweight, obesity or weight and (3) engaging with structural features of the web-based programme.

Theme 1: Knowledge development on healthy living

Families conveyed how their existing and developing knowledge on healthy living influenced their experiences with the web-based programme. Most parents had some baseline knowledge of healthy living, including an understanding of health-supporting behaviours (ie, healthy eating and physical activity) and the importance of adopting a healthy lifestyle. They gained this knowledge through the internet, books or from their lived experiences managing overweight, obesity or other health conditions, such as diabetes. Some parents reported they were more knowledgeable on these topics and that the web-based programme did not meet their expectations regarding the level of information. They expressed wanting more specific or deeper information compared with content they received, such as the psychology behind eating or food choices. They also noted that this may have discouraged their own participation in all or parts of the web-based programme.

Whereas some parents reported knowing about healthy living, at least for themselves as adults, they acknowledged their uncertainty and unsuccessful attempts to educate their children on basic health-supporting behaviours. They perceived the web-based programme as an opportunity to deliver the information on healthy living to their children in a manner that was tailored and accessible and conveyed by a third-party source. Some parents presumed that the information was new for their children and were optimistic about the potential benefits of the learning experience for them.

I find it difficult being a single parent to provide the information to them, like I might know the right information, but I just don’t know how. They just don’t receive it well when it’s coming from me, because to them it seems like it’s just another person telling them off. (Parent # 23, mother of secondary-school aged son)

On the other hand, some parents developed practical knowledge on healthy living throughout the programme, which allowed them to identify areas of improvement for themselves and their family. For instance, some parents felt like they knew the concepts of healthy living but lacked the insight and skills to apply this information in daily life. They recounted their own struggles with regularly practising healthy lifestyle behaviours and anticipated the programme could teach them ways to establish these behaviours with their children. Additionally, some parents shared that the web-based programme supplemented their factual knowledge with practical ideas they could implement with their children, including setting small, achievable goals, which enabled them to apply their knowledge and actively tailor lifestyle changes in ways that were acceptable for their children.

As an adult, we probably understand [healthy food choices] ourselves. I think the programme probably just highlighted a little bit more, and highlighted that the choices that we make, and I think we go through that ourselves when we look at it, so probably just highlighted that for us and made us a bit more aware. (Parent # 5, father of primary-school aged son)

It was really interesting to go from that feeling of, ‘Oh I know this’ to, I was learning things like the food label reading, I kind of have a gist of how to do it, but it’s good to find out exactly how to do it, and what to look for, so it’s been good. (Parent # 3, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

Some children also shared that they were familiar with content provided in the programme, having learnt the information from school or the internet. Being knowledgeable about programme content in turn sometimes influenced their disinterest towards the programme or discouraged their engagement in activities.

[Modules] were quite easy and bland, and listening to all of the things whatever about, just felt painful to listen to, or for just 20 minutes. (Child # 2, male, secondary-school aged)

Conversely, some children appreciated the re-exposure to information and noted that the programme was more comprehensive than previous sources and were able to enhance their knowledge. Some parents also perceived the programme as an opportunity for themselves and their children to reinforce past learnings. Some families shared that both during or after the programme, children were able to recall learnings on healthy living by referring to the programme’s physical resources (summaries of online education content) or their own handwritten notes supporting them to apply their knowledge.

Generally, families expressed that learning new information on healthy living was enjoyable and supported their engagement. Some parents also expressed that the programme inspired them to further explore health-related topics beyond the web-based programme following programme completion.

Theme 2: Experiences and stigma related to overweight, obesity or weight

Families’ experiences with overweight or obesity or changes to children’s weight impacted their motivation to commence the web-based programme. Some parents recounted that their children’s sudden weight gain encouraged them to actively find support to improve their family’s lifestyle habits. A parent shared that her child’s decreased self-confidence after being teased by his peers for his weight motivated her to commence the web-based programme. Another parent was concerned with her daughter’s keenness to lose weight and negative perception of her body image. On the other hand, some parents relayed that for their children, decreased weight-related self-confidence discouraged their children’s initial willingness to participate. They reported that their children saw the programme as a criticism of them or their weight.

At first [he] was a little bit reluctant because he thought the programme was about him being, as he put it, fat, and he just thought it was something, because he felt he was fat and that it was just all about that and focused on that. (Parent # 7, mother of primary-school aged son)

Some parents had lived experiences with overweight or obesity which influenced their perception of programmes for addressing childhood obesity, including the web-based programme. For instance, they recalled trying various ways to lose weight, including fad diets. They were reminded of their negative experiences with obesity treatment, and some held dismissive assumptions about the web-based programme at programme commencement. With lived experiences of overweight or obesity themselves, some parents conveyed the complexity of obesity and recounted most people’s lack of understanding of the onset of overweight or obesity. They voiced their expectation for the programme to address this gap or change the current narrative of overweight or obesity, which some parents felt were needs that the programme did not meet.

Some parents shared their hesitation to discuss weight with their children and negatively perceived weight-related information provided in the programme. They were resistant to exposing their children to weight-related content for reasons including the fear of promoting weight or body image insecurities or decreased self-confidence in their children. They stated that the weight-specific information was irrelevant or inconducive to teaching their children about healthy living.

I think I’ve been talking to you about discussing, how we approach discussing weight with young children, especially young girls. There was, I can’t remember which module it was now, it was maybe week 3, kind of [early] in the programme, and there was this section at the beginning which wasn't related to any of the challenges or any of the other information in the module. (Parent # 9, mother of primary-school aged daughter)

Some parents mentioned that during the programme, their children became more likely to show their concerns for their weight, for instance, noticing their children started regularly weighing themselves or voiced anxiety with their weight. Other parents felt that the programme supported their children to feel comfortable to communicate their concerns about their weight or body image or engage in weight-related discussions.

Theme 3: Engaging with structural features of the web-based programme

Families frequently recalled features of the programme they liked and influenced their engagement, optimism or curiosity towards the programme. Parents were attracted to the programme for its focus on healthy living, the weekly delivery of new material or being free of charge. Some families expressed that they liked that the programme was adaptable to their busy schedules, daily stressors, children’s interests and cultures. They were able to engage with the programme at their own time and pace and decide whether or how to use programme resources, including supplementary material (eg, physical information sheets, Facebook group). Families also valued features such as the child-friendly elements—visuals (ie, videos, images, animations) featuring families, simple language and interactive content on the website—tangible resources and being delivered online. They particularly enjoyed regularly setting and achieving goals or executing challenges in an individualised manner, as children were eager to receive the rewards or incentives at programme completion.

It was good, had different options and different methods of delivery of content, was good, like verbal, written and moving image, made it engaging, and clicking on different options. (Parent # 6, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

There were programme features that some families reported to discourage their engagement or create ambivalence towards making and sustaining healthy lifestyle behaviour changes. Some children found the robotic sounding voices in the videos impersonal and off-putting. They instead recommended audio that imitates voices of characters from games or shows they enjoyed. Some families’ appreciation for humanised features was also conveyed in their reflections on their contact time with their health coach during the programme. They preferred coaching sessions to be conducted as video conferencing rather than phone calls. Whereas overall families found the programme content generalisable to them, some families found some activities were not tailored to the children’s age or personality.

Families were mostly accepting of the timeline of the programme; however, some parents thought that more time was needed to make lifestyle behaviour changes and establish healthy lifestyle habits. Most children also shared that they struggled to remain motivated to continue health-supporting behaviours post-programme completion. Being that their children were unable to maintain healthy lifestyle behaviours, particularly in the long-term, some parents considered the programme unhelpful. Some parents in turn recommended offering a top-up web-based programme to families.

It’s been up and down, sometimes it’s been hard with commitment to continue doing that, so sometimes it does feel like I don’t, but I do feel like I’ve walked away more informed from them, so I think I should be better, but sometimes I just don’t feel like I have the willpower. (Child # 26, male, secondary-school aged)

Interpersonal factors

Interpersonal factors related to family members’ past, existing or newly formed relationships within and external to own families during the web-based programme that influenced their experiences with the web-based programme. The main themes at this tier included: (1) family dynamic, (2) connections with others (non-healthcare professional) outside of home and (3) relationship with healthcare professionals.

Theme 4: Family dynamic

Participating in the web-based programme was perceived to strengthen family relationships, where families recounted connecting in mutually beneficial ways. Families generally appreciated opportunities to realise common interests and collaborate with each other to improve their health. They enjoyed these chances to bond through programme activities, previously absent before joining the web-based programme. Some families shared that the programme helped them realise the benefits of connecting with their own family to improve health collectively. They expressed that it was this connection that supported them with behaviour change.

Yesterday we all went to the park after dinner to do, just throwing the ball and things like that. So yeah, yeah, I think that was one area that made me feel we could definitely improve on as a family was to get out and do things more or join up with friends to do active things. (Parent # 21, mother of primary-school aged sons)

When we first started, we didn't really have time to do all this stuff, but now, after the programme, we've kind of like made time. We’re like, well we can spend time with our family still, but also still do all these other sports and still eat at the dinner table together, and it’s kind of helped us see that we can make time for things. (Child # 15a, female, secondary-school aged)

Most parents agreed that the programme helped them recognise the importance of involving all family members to support their children in making lifestyle behaviour changes. Some parents conveyed the importance of both parents and grandparents being involved in implementing the lifestyle changes. This included prioritising their children’s health-related goals and consistency with routines, to maximally support their children’s engagement with the programme and optimise mutual benefits. Some parents recognised that they were key motivators for their children to adopt and sustain healthy lifestyle behaviours. They recounted their children’s dissatisfaction to complete programme activities (ie, make healthy lifestyle behaviour changes) on their own, without the participation of other household members. Some parents also shared that their children’s progress with making healthy lifestyle changes motivated them to adopt and sustain healthy lifestyle behaviours themselves. Some parents reflected on their own shortcomings with modelling healthy lifestyle behaviours.

She was doing that the whole, you know, eating more healthy, with the wholegrain bread and the flour, and thinking about after dinner, not just having that lovely piece of chocolate cake, but also wholegrain and fruit. At one point she did become frustrated, and so did I because she said, ‘I'm eating the watermelon while you guys are all eating…’ whatever it was that was on the cards, like apple pie, and so yeah, that was a bit tricky, because I thought [she’s] right here, we need to put, we're not just making her eat it, but we need to make a conscious effort too. (Parent # 3, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

Families relayed that the participation of siblings (directly or indirectly) in programme commitments motivated engagement of participating children in the web-based programme. Parents valued the teamwork and competition between siblings to achieve programme-related goals. Most children found it memorable to apply learnings with their siblings, which enhanced their learning or maintenance of changed behaviours. Some families also highlighted that the programme empowered older siblings to model and educate their younger siblings on healthy lifestyle behaviours.

Parents reported that the web-based programme helped them enhance their parenting skills with respect to establishing healthy lifestyle habits with their children. Some parents conveyed that the programme supported them to act as a leader for their children. This included learning to take charge when setting boundaries, suggesting ideas or creating opportunities or regimes during non-school hours to apply programme learnings. For instance, some parents restricted their children’s access to digital devices or reduced unhealthy foods in their children’s lunch boxes, to encourage their children to achieve daily targets for physical activity or dietary intake, respectively. They reported the programme helped them honour their children’s autonomy and create opportunities for children to take initiative with their health. Parents recounted that the programme enhanced their communication skills with their children. They felt supported by the programme to discuss topics they may have initially avoided or were hesitant to have with their children (eg, discussions on weight or body image). Some parents also shared that their children were more willing and open to discuss their health-related concerns with them during and after the programme compared with programme commencement.

I actually found that quite good parenting support through it as well, like about knowing what to prioritise…TV screen time was like a big challenge in our household and even being able to say like, ’this was one recommendation from the [programme],’ like having that back up there when I was talking to them, really got them to listen a bit. (Parent # 23, mother of secondary-school aged son)

On the other hand, some parents described their children experiencing a decreased sense of independence during the programme, particularly with older school-aged children, when completing the programme under their supervision. They conveyed their uncertainty and discomfort navigating this shift in their usual connection with their children during the programme.

He found the, probably the consistency difficult, and then also the reliance on me is difficult. So, what I mean by that is because of our relationship, he has a lot of autonomy, so I'm not there going, ‘Study hard, do this, you've got to do this.’ So I think what’s difficult around it is that alignment with the both of us. (Parent # 26, mother of secondary-school aged son)

Post-programme completion, some children recounted that a decrease in their parents’ support or their parents’ re-engagement in unhealthful behaviours hindered their maintenance of healthy lifestyle behaviours. Some parents acknowledged that during busier times of the year, such as during school terms, they were less likely to involve their children in lifestyle activities, such as shopping for groceries, which they admitted were missed opportunities to help them maintain programme learnings.

Child: I stopped after I got the prizes.

Parent: Why didn’t you continue on? What would motivate you to continue on?

Child: Dad and mum reminding me about all the stuff.

Parent: He did enjoy coming to the shops a bit more to check those labels and things and yeah, he’s right, I think we both probably let it slip a little bit ourselves in terms of, we've probably taken it for granted as a simple little things like the yoghurt. We've made those changes both of us, but probably not engaging him as much as what we could do as well.

(Child # 5, male, primary-school aged; Parent # 5, father)

Theme 5: Connections with others (non-healthcare professional) outside of home

The key feature of the web-based programme intended to foster connections with other families was the private Facebook group. Parents noted that their children were either unaware or chose not to use this programme feature. Children broadly agreed with their parents regarding minimal opportunities to connect with other participating families during the programme. Parents stated that they joined the Facebook group and understood its purpose; however, they did not engage with it or used it passively as a resource for ideas. They conveyed their wariness to post content online, discomfort with sharing information involving their family and potential privacy breaches of digital platforms. Some parents also wanted to minimise their own screen time or use of social media platforms or felt shy to post. Some parents would have liked for the Facebook group to involve ongoing discussions between families throughout the programme. They described that their interactions with other participating parents were limited to the occasional responses to their posts (eg, liking posts or brief comments of approval). Some parents were sceptical towards the platform’s ability to prompt connection building with others. They further shared that the overall inactivity in the Facebook group discouraged their participation.

I just thought more activities by other families, I was hoping more questions or, ‘I’m having this…’ not a problem, but ‘How could I get around this situation at home?’ I mean, I don't know how it was meant to be, but I was hoping for a little bit more of, ‘My daughter or my son isn't very motivated this week, how can I get them motivated?’ or I don't know what I was expecting, but it was on the cusp of greatness for me. (Parent # 3, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

Nevertheless, some parents recounted their positive experiences indirectly connecting with other families through their posts during the programme. They enjoyed learning about ways that other families completed programme activities and were inspired by other children’s achievements. This in turn reinforced their motivations to continue engaging with the programme.

I would do it because everyone was showing what was in their boredom busters box. I also thought the food one, like the tortilla chip, so we had to do some more healthy alternatives, I found that kind of inspirational, and I was hearing, well the people that were taking charge, a lot of families, ‘Oh my daughter or my son actually cooked this,’ which was quite inspirational for us, because [she’s] taken up a little bit more cooking now and a little bit more independence providing meals for us as a family, which is wonderful. (Parent # 3, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

Some parents conveyed that they felt that they already established strong relationships with others outside their family (eg, friends, colleagues or community members), finding the programme’s Facebook group unnecessary. They however agreed with its inclusion as an optional component of the programme.

Some children actively involved their friends with the programme by sharing their learnings or undertaking new health-supporting activities with them. On the other hand, parents who wanted more connections with others outside their family were keen for the programme to provide opportunities to build these connections to support healthy living. Some parents also recommended the provision of information on the number of families concurrently undertaking the programme with them during their term to encourage potential interactions between participating families.

Some families alluded to the limitations of a web-based vs an in-person or group-based version of a healthy lifestyle programme in the context of connecting with other families. They showed interest in a programme comprising in-person group-based activities for families to interact with other families. Some parents envisioned benefits of offering a follow-up in-person programme involving group-based exercises to support them in maintaining healthy lifestyle behaviours.

Some parents dismissed the need to connect with other families during the programme. They perceived their children’s participation in the programme as a personal journey or exclusively concerning their own family.

It was our personal journey, not to share with the outside world. Self-awareness and health and wellbeing and um living a wholesome life. (Parent # 6, mother of secondary-school aged daughter)

I haven't contacted any other families. No, like I think it’s a very personal thing. People have different lifestyle, different routines, different cultures, different styles of eating, and yeah, it’s bit odd [to contact them]. (Parent # 12, father of primary-school aged son)

Theme 6: Relationship with healthcare professionals

Families discussed their experiences with healthcare professionals both externally and within the programme (eg, health coaches). Some parents reflected on their previous experience with healthcare professionals (eg, paediatrician, general practitioner, dietitian) to improve their families’ lifestyle habits, which influenced their expectations of the programme. They recounted the limitations of these experiences with healthcare professionals, including the provision of general advice untailored to children. Some parents therefore anticipated for the programme to satisfy this gap.

Families largely conveyed positive experiences with their coaches during the programme. The regular and personalised support received from their coaches was essential to their engagement with the programme. Parents relayed that the coaches served as the external voice they needed to motivate their children with undertaking healthy lifestyle behaviours. Families appreciated their coach’s responsiveness, flexibility and consistency.

It’s so easy to want to do it, but to get it buried by other things that you need to do, so the fact that I knew that someone was going to call me, you know, the motivation to make sure that we just you know, ticked that box. (Parent # 4, mother of primary-school aged son)

When we didn't feel like doing something, we would tell our coach, and she would try motivate us by like, ‘If you can do this by like the end of this week, you can get an extra point’ or ‘You would be rewarded something’. (Child # 19a, female, primary-school aged)

Families also valued the rapport they built with their coaches, particularly the coach they connected with at the beginning or were scheduled with for much of the programme. Some parents found that being able to have the phone-based coaching sessions conducted from their preferred location worked well for their family, and that their coach had the ability to foster active participation of their normally taciturn children. Some families however suggested that being able to see their coach (eg, receiving their weekly calls via a video conferencing platform) would have strengthened participating children’s attentiveness and interactions with them.

Although families acknowledged the coaches as being of high calibre overall, they preferred to have the same coach throughout the programme. Families who experienced a change to their coach, related to the rescheduling of their coaching session and unavailability of their original coach, recounted the possible differences in their coach’s coaching approach and children’s consequent confusions with achieving goals.

Child # 15b: I found [the coaching sessions] okay, they weren’t the best.

Child # 15a: Especially since we had 3 of them. Probably just don't swap and change coaches, because every time we swap, they kind of talk about the same thing that we talked about the week before because each coach hadn’t known what we had already talked about, so we had to kind of talk about same thing each week.

(Child # 15a, female, secondary-school aged; Child # 15b, female, primary-school aged)

A parent shared that whereas she appreciated the coach’s dedicated time to follow-up on her child’s progress with weekly goals, she felt the coach’s role was too passive to influence her child to make lifestyle behaviour changes. She wanted the coach to more actively facilitate her child to make these changes.

Environmental or institutional factors

Environmental or institutional factors related to characteristics of systems, including organisations, informal networks or settings that families may be exposed to or embedded in, may influence their experience with the web-based programme. The main themes at this tier are (1) impact of COVID-19 lockdowns, (2) health-promoting environments, (3) promotion of and access to overweight or obesity management programmes and (4) web-based programme as part of a larger or established system.

Theme 7: Impact of COVID-19 lockdowns

Some parents recalled that the COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns initiated or exacerbated their families’ engagement in unhealthful lifestyle behaviours and contributed to signs of sudden health-related changes in their children. They described a drastic, sudden increase to their children’s weight and change in fitness levels, which prompted their sense of urgency to reassess their families’ lifestyle habits and seek support. Some parents also relayed that following the COVID-19 pandemic and prior to programme enrolment, they more keenly reflected on their health or health-related behaviours or were open to making lifestyle changes. They recounted feeling hopeful and excited about the web-based programme, particularly at programme commencement.

The fact that he’s weighing a bit more, not trying to body shame him and that sort of stuff obviously, but the fact that he was weighing more than his sister who’s obviously older than him, was a bit of a concern for us, just because we as adults know the associated health issues that come with a bit of that. And yeah, it happened pretty quickly for him. He was always really lean little boy. And then yeah, sort of shot up, and we’ve noticed in his footy team, his fitness certainly diminished quite substantially now, it’s certainly in comparison to his peers. (Parent # 5, father of primary-school aged son)

Some families implied that the COVID-19 pandemic increased children’s feelings of boredom. Some children noted being motivated by measures to attenuate boredom, perceiving the programme as a new activity which encouraged their willing to participate.

Families approved of the programme’s online delivery. Parents suggested that the COVID-19 pandemic created a new norm for families, where the younger generations in particular were well-versed in navigating technology or digital features. As parents felt the COVID-19 pandemic was detrimental to their children’s health and development, they appreciated the opportunities the programme provided for their children to learn to reflect, think creatively, and that changes or outcomes related to their lifestyle were gradual.

Everything has to be a quick fix, which is TV or games, like there’s no, and it’s always, ‘I’m bored, I’m bored, I’m bored.’ So I found that ideas that the [programme] gave us really got them to think outside the box and not automatically be that ‘I’m bored’, that’s the first thing that comes out of their mouth when they haven't even thought about different things that they can do. (Parent # 23, mother of secondary-school aged son)

Theme 8: Health-promoting environments

Families recounted that their living spaces or physical environments outside of home impacted participating children’s engagement with the web-based programme. They noted that their participation in suggested physical activities depended on their access to facilities, such as parks, gyms or pools. Some parents shared that when their children were exposed to environments inconducive to their health, like school canteens or nearby supermarkets charging a higher price for nutrients, compared with energy-dense foods, they were less likely to meet the programme’s weekly behaviour targets. Parents appreciated the programme’s content on ways to navigate such environments, which empowered their family to practise healthy lifestyle behaviours regardless of location or physical environments.

It’s all fine to say that there’s the red and green food groups at the canteen, but every kid’s always gonna pick the Red food group because it’s the cheaper option for mum and dad as well? And that’s the downfall of the canteen, it’s not offering enough healthier choices. (Parent # 20, mother of primary-school aged daughter and son)

She didn't really want to do [skipping], because well, we don’t, we have a really small house, so there wasn't really the space to do them. (Parent # 22, mother of primary-school aged daughter)

Theme 9: Promotion of and access to overweight or obesity management programmes

Parents described the lack of available or accessible paediatric community-based lifestyle programmes in Victoria. Parents generally recounted discovering the web-based programme through random browsing over the web or school newsletters. They also appreciated the ease of enrolment, including contact with the programme administering organisation or research team, attributable to the programme being offered as part of a research project. Some parents conveyed that they were frustrated with the lack of guidance or communication on such programmes. They shared that the information they received from paediatricians was on services that did not meet their needs or that their children were ineligible for (eg, dietetics service, weight management programmes in specialised healthcare system settings).

If you had a lot of problems, and it was already really full on, you could get clinical, medical kind of help for them (Parent # 4, mother of primary-school aged son)

Families were keen to promote the web-based programme post-programme completion. Some parents shared their enthusiasm to discuss programme benefits and had referred other relatives and friends to participate. They felt that it was important to advocate for more (web-based) lifestyle programmes in Victoria and involve support from the government or councils to upscale interventions. Some families proposed the need to promote paediatric lifestyle programmes through child-friendly social media campaigns, likened to initiatives to promote other areas of health (eg, oral health or skin cancer awareness).

[My son] had brought up something about school and the Anti-Cancer Council with sunscreen, and he was like it would have been good if the [programme] had talked about the importance of sunscreen too, like obviously not in a wrong context, but even just having a little sample of sunscreen and hat that promoted your [programme]. (Parent # 23, mother of secondary-school aged son)

Theme 10: Web-based programme as part of a larger or established system

Some families recommended incorporating the web-based programme into school systems, to maximise the benefits of the programme. Some parents perceived programme content on healthy eating as appropriate to enhance the school’s curriculum on healthy living. They also thought that this would provide families with additional equipment or space they may need to meet programme targets (eg, related to increasing physical activity levels).

This sort of stuff, I think I was telling [my son], it should be taught at schools. This is life skills, you know, to understand these basic stuff, we would have such a healthier society. I believe, I know it’s easier said than done, it’s probably not gonna happen, but you know, I just wish that you know, these are important stuff that kids should be learning about. (Parent # 27, mother of primary-school aged son and daughter)

Schools try and encourage them, children to be active and doing sport, and talk about that aspect of health in PE and things, but I think this is much more comprehensive and more detailed, where you know, they learn to read packaging. So I think that’s a valuable tool, so that just raising their awareness that oh, there might be actually heaps of sugar in that I didn’t realise or you know, so that could be a good thing that schools could teach. (Parent # 21, mother of primary-school aged sons)

Some children suggested receiving the programme during school hours would have enhanced their ability to concentrate and retain information presented in the modules. On the other hand, some families appreciated receiving the programme outside of school hours to distinguish programme activities from schoolwork.

Discussion

This study found that participating children and parents mostly had agreeable experiences completing a web-based healthy lifestyle programme. Comparable to other studies on web-based programmes for improving children’s health, families in this study favoured programme features consistent with established evidence-based qualities of conventional in-person paediatric weight management programmes: family-focused, application of behavioural and self-management principles, and having relatable, adaptable or practical content.1424,31 The web-based nature of the programme was well-received by families in this study. This is consistent with other studies on paediatric web-based programmes for families, where families, including younger participating school-aged children, were generally comfortable accessing key education content through a website and using e-Health technologies.24 29 30 Following the COVID-19 pandemic, families appear to be increasingly competent using various e-Health technologies and more willing to engage with e-Health programmes to support children’s health.614 32,34 Families in this study thought that the web-based rather than in-person delivery of the programme promoted accessibility and programme engagement, and allowed for extended access. Some families saw greater potential in web-based as opposed to in-person programmes to serve as follow-up interventions and enable ongoing support, which is important for maintaining healthy lifestyle behaviours in the long-term.24 30 Likewise, the maintenance of healthy lifestyle behaviours is essential for the management of childhood obesity.12 30 35 Web-based programmes may potentially upscale paediatric lifestyle programmes and better support the continuation of care needed for the management of childhood overweight or obesity.

On the other hand, family accounts helped identify programme characteristics influencing families’ engagement, including limitations of the programme, which can be generalised to all delivery modes of paediatric weight management services or lifestyle programmes. Various socioecological factors influence families’ experiences with childhood overweight or obesity and their engagement with healthcare services for its management.12 36 37 Consistent with other studies, our findings suggest that the development of future e-Health lifestyle programmes should account for these socioecological factors and maximise elements supporting family engagement. This includes the flexibility to choose when, where and whether to access programme resources, access to practical information, and content tailored to children specific to their age group (using visuals, simple language, interactive activities or games).1224 26 30 36,38

Family accounts also highlighted families’ complex and pervasive experiences with weight stigma, including parents’ discomfort or ambivalence towards weight-related information or discussions. Consistent with clinical guidelines for childhood obesity and existing literature, education content of interventions for childhood obesity, including e-Health, should be mindful of weight stigma by carefully considering language and activities.39 40 Findings merit further research to identify strategies to minimise weight stigma while empowering parents to support healthy behaviour changes in interventions for childhood obesity.

The minimisation or omission of in-person interactions in web-based programmes risks compromising human experience, which is valued by families seeking healthcare services, including e-Health programmes, to improve children’s health.24 29 31 Other studies have found that families expect to have reduced feelings of connectedness with others during web-based programmes, which they perceived as inferior to conventional counterparts.24 29 However, as reported by other studies, families in this study noted that the web-based programme supported them to develop new or reinforce existing relationships with people, including strengthening connectedness with their own family members, healthcare professionals or other families.14 24 29

Families in this study reported that the preservation of human experience should be prioritised to maximise families’ positive experiences and engagement with web-based lifestyle programmes. Valuable insights were gained on strategies recommended by families to improve human experience. Incorporating features that enable interactions with others, such as video conferencing instead of phone calls, human voice-overs as opposed to automated or machinelike audio narrations in pre-programmed content or gamified elements (eg, interactive activities, particularly activities encouraging the participation of other family members), can improve the human experience with web-based lifestyle programme for childhood obesity.2429,31 Human experience is individualised; whereas the online modules in the web-based programme of this study consisted of narrations with human voice-overs, some children perceived it as machinelike. Prioritising attributes that are familiar and personable to children (eg, voice-overs that imitate voices of characters well known to children or content featuring children or families as protagonists) can optimise human experience in web-based programmes.24 E-Health technologies are evolving rapidly, even since the writing of this manuscript; children’s digital experiences are progressively changing and involving diverse technologies. Artificial intelligence (AI) is among the digital technologies that are increasingly contributing to children’s daily experiences.7 41 42 Responsive AI technology is rapidly developing to manifest compassionate healthcare and increasingly demonstrating health-supporting impact in paediatric healthcare.2443,45 Responsive AI features may also improve the personalisation of paediatric web-based programmes, by empowering families to take active roles in managing children’s health, such as deciding whether they require any or further contact with healthcare professionals to meet their unique and dynamic needs.43 45 Although the responsiveness of the web-based programme experienced by families in this study was related to weekly human-to-human phone coaching sessions with a healthcare professional, responsive AI technologies may exemplify contemporary digital technologies children are increasingly exposed to and have the potential to satisfy some of families’ needs during the programme (eg, informational needs) or in follow-up interventions.

Study results may be used to inform the development and tailoring of paediatric e-Health (web-based) lifestyle programmes to improve the management of childhood overweight or obesity. Key considerations are outlined below.

E-Health (web-based) interventions should prioritise the following features to encourage engagement by families: family-focused, contents that employ behavioural and self-management principles, and relatable, practical and adaptable material, content tailored to children (optimise use of visuals, simple language, interactive activities, adapted to different age groups), mindful of weight stigma by carefully considering language and activities.

This is one of few qualitative studies that explore the experiences of both the participating children and their parents with an evidence-based, web-based healthy lifestyle programme for families with children living with overweight or obesity. The study captured families’ lived experiences and encouraged dialogue, including with children. Parent involvement is important to the management of childhood overweight and obesity; existing evidence suggests that the effectiveness of paediatric lifestyle behaviour change interventions is enhanced when programmes prioritise the parent-child relationship.24 46 47 Exploring the perceptions of both children and their parents is vital to better understand the role of parents and establish optimal strategies to involve parents in the management of childhood overweight and obesity through e-Health.46 47 Similar to studies that examined parental involvement in other lifestyle programmes for managing childhood overweight or obesity, it was often highlighted during interviews in this study that parents were agents of behaviour change in children.46 47 This study also provides insight into the agreement of perspectives and views between children and their parents. Nevertheless, there are several limitations to consider. This included that children mostly shared their experiences alongside their parents during the interviews. Parents may support or hinder children’s responses and therefore influence the richness of data collected from children. Some of the identified themes may be more reflective of the experiences of participating parents alone with the web-based programme, related to some children’s diffidence to engage with conversation, overall inability to recall their experience or non-involvement with some features of the web-based programme. Being that most families who enrolled in the main study and received the web-based programme included mothers and identified as Australian, findings may be unrepresentative of the perspectives of fathers or families identifying with other ethnicities. This study employed convenience sampling, where families who chose to participate potentially had more positive experiences compared with families who declined participation or disengaged prior to commencing the web-based programme. Childhood obesity also disproportionately impacts populations; however, the programme may not have reached families at highest risk for childhood obesity or most impacted by the service gap of conventional weight management programmes.17 48 Further research is acutely needed to explore the perspectives of these families to optimise engagement strategies, equitably deliver e-Health programmes for childhood overweight and obesity and minimise the worsening of economic, social and cultural inequalities.

Conclusion

Web-based lifestyle programmes have the potential to preserve established evidence-based elements of conventional paediatric lifestyle programmes and be enjoyed by families, while presenting new opportunities to enhance programme accessibility and engagement. This study highlighted key considerations to better adapt e-Health (web-based) lifestyle programmes for children with overweight or obesity. Further research is needed to explore web-based programmes incorporating contemporary e-Health features, specifically that aim to enhance human experience using responsive AI, to better meet families’ needs managing childhood overweight or obesity.
