Andrei-Alexandru Manea, Jindřich Libovický

Abstract
. This survey examines multilingual vision-language models that process text and images across languages. We review 31 models and 21 benchmarks, spanning encoder-only and generative architectures, and identify a key tension between language neutrality (consistent cross-lingual representations) and cultural awareness (adaptation to cultural contexts). Current training methods favor neutrality through contrastive learning, while cultural awareness depends on diverse data. Two-thirds of evaluation benchmarks use translation-based approaches prioritizing semantic consistency, though recent work incorporates culturally grounded content. We find discrepancies in cross-lingual capabilities and gaps between training objectives and evaluation goals.



1.
Introduction

Vision-language models are machine learning systems designed for the joint processing of natural language and visual data, enabling tasks such as visual question answering or image retrieval based on text. Building upon recent advances in language modeling and computer vision, this class of models has recently made unprecedented progress. However, most of the model capabilities are showcased and evaluated in English. There is relatively less work focusing on other, often less-resourced languages, where the performance significantly lags behind English and other well-supported languages. Still, there are researchers who put significant effort into making vision-language models accessible around the globe, both by developing models with multilingual capabilities and by developing benchmarks that enable better evaluation of how vision-language models perform across languages. In this article, we provide a comprehensive survey of both multilingual vision-language models and multilingual benchmarks, identify and categorize the main challenges in this area, and discuss how models and benchmarks address these challenges.
We focus exclusively on models that process static images alongside language, excluding those designed for videos or for generating images from text.

Vision-language models closely follow development in language modeling. Historically, many advancements in language modeling have been first developed for English (e.g., the original masked-language modeling BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
in late 2018), with their multilingual adaptations and vision-language adaptations (e.g., LXMERT
(
Tan and Bansal 2019
)
in summer 2019) occurring later. Multilingual extensions of vision-language models remain even further behind (M3P
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
, the first XLM-RoBERTa-based vision-language encoder preprinted in the summer of 2020). However, multilingual modeling is critical to the global accessibility of systems based on language models. Joint modeling of language and vision in a multilingual context poses unique challenges that go beyond text-only multilingual modeling.

An important motivation for the early research in vision-language modeling was the belief that real-world referential grounding, which is essential for understanding meaning, could not be achieved with text data alone
(
Chrupała et al. 2015
;
Kádár et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Tan and Bansal 2020
)
. The goal was to use vision to improve language modeling. The main argument was that human language understanding is inseparable from interaction with the physical world, which provides grounding for utterances. Images in vision-language models were seen as the element providing the necessary grounding through visual representations.
Some researchers take this debate further, arguing that true grounding is unattainable without direct experience with the world and that without it, learning meaning is fundamentally impossible
(
Bender and Koller 2020
)
. Nowadays, there is also substantial counter-evidence against this version of the argument
(
Andreas 2022
;
Søgaard 2023
;
Gubelmann 2024
)
, both theoretical and empirical.

Regardless of the theoretical motivations, most practical approaches to vision-language modeling have relied on pre-trained language representations developed without visual data. Similarly, visual representations are often trained using explicit or implicit language-based signals.
However, even image representations are implicitly grounded in language, as they are typically trained using datasets that include language labels or captions (e.g., labels in ImageNet
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
come from the lexical database WordNet
(
Miller 1995
)
). Recent advancements in multimodal large language models (LLMs) have shifted this perspective, with many systems transforming visual representations into the language representation space, relying on language-only pre-training
(
Eichenberg et al. 2022
)
.

Vision-language models are used in many applications. It includes tasks requiring reasoning over both textual and visual modalities, such as visual question answering (VQA)
(
Antol et al. 2015
)
or image captioning. These models have also been instrumental in advancing technologies for accessibility, such as image description systems for visually impaired users
(
Merchant et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024a
)
.

Most vision-language research focuses on the English language, and both multilingual models and multilingual evaluation benchmarks are derivatives of original English models and datasets.
This poses challenges, such as biases against social groups or nations, and maintaining performance across diverse languages and cultures.
Addressing these challenges is critical for scaling such systems to real-world applications, particularly in global contexts where linguistic and cultural variation must be accounted for
(
Sheng et al. 2021
;
Steed et al. 2022
;
Karamolegkou et al. 2024
)
.

language neutrality
and

2.
Conflicting Goals in Multilingual Vision-Language Models

Developing multilingual vision-language models presents a tension between two competing goals that are rarely mentioned explicitly:
language neutrality
and
cultural awareness
. Language neutrality means that we want to represent the same concepts in the same way across languages, ensuring that intersubjective concepts remain consistent regardless of linguistic or cultural context (e.g., a picture of a dog is always a picture of a dog regardless of the language). Conversely, cultural awareness emphasizes the need to adapt representations and outputs to reflect the cultural and contextual nuances of different languages, in extreme cases requiring distinct interpretations for the same input (in Western culture, dogs are associated with loyalty, whereas in Chinese or Arabic, calling someone a dog might be insulting). This section explores the nature of these conflicting goals and illustrates the challenges they present.

Language neutrality ensures that models provide consistent outputs for inputs that are objective or universal in nature, regardless of the language used. Examples of such use cases are scientific and factual information (the Pythagorean theorem should be the same in every language, as its truth is invariant to cultural or linguistic differences) or recognizable landmarks (an image of the Eiffel Tower should be identified as Eiffel Tower regardless of the language). In an extreme case, language neutrality would imply removing anything language-specific from the model
(
Libovický et al. 2020
)
and, in turn, drastically limit the possibility of reaching cultural awareness. Therefore, it is, in practice, replaced by a less strict requirement of cross-lingual alignment
(
Hämmerl et al. 2024
)
that conceptually relies on representation similarity. It can either be a strong alignment, cross-lingually matching concepts to be the closest one in the other language, or a weak alignment, which only requires the possibility to fine-tune models for downstream performance consistently across languages, even if the model was fine-tuned in one language.

One of the most frequent ways of evaluating multilingual and cross-lingual capabilities is zero-shot cross-lingual transfer. In this setup, we fine-tune a multilingual model using training data in one language, but test the model using other languages that the underlying model was pre-trained for but were not part of the task-specific fine-tuning. The most common evaluation scheme is fine-tuning in a high-resource language, typically English, and testing the model on low-resource languages.

Therefore, in multilingual NLP, some sort of language neutrality is the implicit assumption behind many standard evaluation tasks. For instance, cross-lingual natural language inference (XNLI)
(
Conneau et al. 2018
)
, one of the most frequently used cross-lingual evaluation benchmarks, was created by translating the test set of the English MultiNLU dataset into 14 languages of diverse language families. Thanks to the fact that one of the most famous benchmarks for natural language inference, SNLI
(
Bowman et al. 2015
)
was created by reusing the sentences from the Flickr30k dataset
(
Young et al. 2014
)
, there is also the vision-language version of this task
(
Xie et al. 2019
)
, including its cross-lingual version that is a part of the IGLUE benchmark
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
.
The big advantage of such an approach is that images remain the same, and the accompanying text is a literal translation of each other. Therefore, the results are directly comparable across languages. However, this might come at the expense of the text not being representative of the languages.

The assumption of language neutrality also underlies the problem of multimodal machine translation
(
Specia et al. 2016
;
Elliott et al. 2017
;
Barrault et al. 2018
)
, which was posed as a literal translation of image captions with the focus on potential ambiguity between languages. That includes both structural language phenomena (e.g., when translating from English into languages with grammatical gender, the image might provide the necessary information) or lexical ambiguities (e.g., the English word “rock” can be translated into German and Czech as “Felsen” and “skála” meaning a rock formation or as “Stein” and “kámen” meaning a stone). Here, we still assume that it is possible and desirable to have an exact semantic equivalent of a sentence given a fixed image.

The neutrality assumption is crucial in applications requiring factual consistency across languages. Also, some degree of language neutrality or cross-lingual alignment is required for cross-lingual transfer. However, achieving this goal becomes challenging when concepts or entities have no exact counterpart across languages or are interpreted differently due to cultural factors.

In contrast to language neutrality,
cultural awareness
emphasizes the need to adapt representations and outputs to account for linguistic, cultural, and contextual nuances. It recognizes that language is socially constructed and rooted in the experiences, traditions, and values of its speakers. Being culture-aware often requires different outputs for the same input across cultures.

Languages often encode culturally specific information that cannot be directly translated without a loss of meaning or context. In an American context, a family meal might evoke a scene of a Thanksgiving dinner with turkey and pie, while for a user in Japan, it might involve miso soup. Similarly, for an image of a typical home, a German speaker might imagine a European-style house with a gable roof made of clay tiles and windows with potted geraniums and wooden shutters, which would differ from a user in Sub-Saharan Africa visualizing a structure adapted to local climates and materials. Concepts that are seemingly the same are associated with physically and socially different objects.

At the same time, developing culturally aware systems comes with risks. One major concern is the potential for harmful stereotyping. While adapting outputs to cultural norms, systems might inadvertently overgeneralize or reinforce biases, portraying cultures in reductive ways. For instance, representing certain regions exclusively through traditional imagery might fail to reflect modern diversity and dynamics.

From a technical perspective, evaluating cultural awareness is much more difficult than language neutrality. In tasks such as XNLVI, we have exact semantic equivalents between languages and identical test sets with directly comparable statistics. Having culture-aware but still identical tests is not possible. Benchmarks explicitly focusing on culture-awareness, such as MARVL
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
for reasoning and XM3600
(
Thapliyal et al. 2022
)
for image captioning and retrieval, have images from different regions of the world with local textual counterparts. Even though the authors of the benchmarks aim at comparable test sets, the numbers are not directly comparable across languages. This issue gets more pronounced with generative tasks, where frequently used overlap-based metrics, such as BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, chrF
(
Popović 2015
)
, or ROUGE
(
Lin 2004
)
scores are inherently in different scales for different languages.

Paradoxically, while language neutrality and cultural awareness often appear to be in conflict, they ultimately stem from the same ethical principles: the commitment to equality, fairness, and non-discrimination. Language neutrality ensures that users, regardless of their linguistic background, can access consistent and objective information, aligning with the principle of fairness by treating all languages equally. On the other hand, cultural awareness seeks to provide equitable and meaningful experiences by respecting the contexts of different cultures, avoiding the one-size-fits-all paradigm that has historically marginalized non-Western perspectives.
In the rest of the paper, we use these two perspectives to assess how multilingual vision-language models are trained and evaluated.

3.
Technical Prerequisities

Multilingual vision-language models combine techniques from natural language processing and computer vision. To understand how these models work, we first need to cover the key building blocks they use. This section reviews three main components: language modeling for processing text, multilingual modeling for handling multiple languages, and image representation for extracting visual features.

3.1.
Language Modeling

Language modeling provides the foundation for processing and generating human language through probabilistic prediction of word sequences. Modern language modeling employs two primary training paradigms: masked language modeling for encoder architectures and autoregressive language modeling for decoder-based generative models. In both cases, the core training signal derives from predicting missing or subsequent words, serving as a proxy for language understanding. Encoder-decoder models, which are also occasionally used, following the machine translation tradition, typically employ denoising objectives on input text
(
Raffel et al. 2020
)
.

While language modeling has its origin in n-gram models
(
Brown et al. 1992
)
and also many neural architectures have been used for language modeling
(
Bengio et al. 2003
;
Mikolov et al. 2011
)
, the field gained significant momentum with the introduction of the Transformer architecture in 2017 for neural machine translation
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
. Transformers are neural networks alternating self-attention layers that capture text structure
(
Mareček and Rosa 2019
;
Voita et al. 2019
)
, and feed-forward layers, interpreted as key-value memories storing linguistic and factual information
(
Geva et al. 2021
)
. The first notable pre-trained Transformer language model was BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, which established the paradigm of pre-training on large corpora followed by fine-tuning for downstream tasks such as classification and sequence labeling.

Early generative models, such as the first iteration GPT
(
Radford et al. 2018
)
, initially showed limited utility compared to pre-trained encoders. However, this landscape transformed dramatically through model scaling, as demonstrated by GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
and GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, the discovery of scaling laws
(
Hoffmann et al. 2022
)
, and advances in instruction fine-tuning
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
that go beyond pre-training with next word prediction.

3.2.
Multilingual Modeling

Soon after monolingual BERT, multilingual variants appeared. Notable early models include mBERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
and XLM-RoBERTa
(
Conneau et al. 2020
)
, which remained state-of-the-art for several years. These massively multilingual encoders feature expanded vocabularies but employ similar training procedures to their monolingual counterparts, with upsampling strategies for low-resource languages. While initially covering approximately 100 languages, subsequent models like Glot500
(
Imani et al. 2023
)
extend coverage to 500 languages.
These models enable cross-lingual transfer, where fine-tuning on one language yields performance gains in other supported languages.
Dufter and Schütze 2020
hypothesize that limited parameter constraints force models to derive cross-lingual representations.

While decoder models attempted similar principles through large vocabularies and language balancing—exemplified by XGLM
(
Lin et al. 2022
)
and BLOOM
(
Scao et al. 2022
)
—they achieved less success compared to contemporary large language models
(
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023b
;
Bai et al. 2025
)
that, despite predominantly English training data and fewer parameters, demonstrate strong performance across select languages. Training often incorporates parallel data, though performance degrades with decreasing digital resource availability—in extreme cases, models may struggle to distinguish grammatical from ungrammatical sentences
(
Shen et al. 2024
)
.

3.3.
Image Representation

Image representation in vision-language models relies on several architectures that extract visual features. Vision Transformers (ViT)
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
was invented as a competitive alternative to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), with ViT models trained on large datasets like ImageNet-21k
(
Ridnik et al. 2021
)
at 224×224 resolution. ViT decomposes input images into fixed-size patches treated as sequences of tokens, similar to words in natural language processing, and processes them through transformer encoders. Recent scaling efforts have produced large-scale models such as ViT-22B
(
Dehghani et al. 2023a
)
.

Convolutional architectures remain widely used, particularly ResNet variants
(
He et al. 2016
)
were frequently used in vision-language models. EfficientNet
(
Tan and Le 2019
)
improves upon that by simultaneously optimizing depth, width, and resolution. These models are typically pre-trained on ImageNet
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
, a large-scale dataset containing millions of labeled images across thousands of classes.

Some vision-language models do not use features from the entire image, but only representations of objects detected in the image.
For object detection tasks, Faster R-CNN
(
Ren et al. 2017
)
integrates CNN backbones with Region Proposal Networks to generate object proposals and classification heads. It processes entire images through the backbone network to produce feature maps, which are then used for proposal generation and object classification.

Recently, self-supervised approaches have gained prominence, with models like BEiT
(
Bao et al. 2022a
)
applying masked image modeling to vision transformers, and BEiT v2
(
Peng et al. 2022
)
extending this approach with vector-quantized visual tokenizers to enhance semantic representations. These methods are analogous to self-supervised learning in language models like BERT.

4.
Vision-Language Models

Vision-language models combine pre-trained language representations with visual encoders to enable joint processing of textual and visual information. Most vision-language models follow a general architecture pattern: they take a language model, combine it with a pre-trained image representation, and often keep components frozen while training specific fusion layers or cross-modal attention mechanisms. Historically, progress in vision-language models typically follows innovations that first appear in language modeling and computer vision.
For instance, BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
was introduced in late 2018, with multimodal adaptations like LXMERT
(
Tan and Bansal 2019
)
following in mid-2019. Similarly, the scaling successes of models like GPT-3
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
in 2020 were followed by large-scale vision-language models like CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
in 2021.

The field has developed several distinct architectural approaches for fusing visual and textual information. Following the categorization by
Fields and Kennington 2023
for English-only models, we identify multiple architecture types:

One-Tower Encoders

These architectures combine embeddings from both images and text into a single input stream processed by a shared encoder, treating them as a single sequence, so the model shares parameters for text and image contextualization. This approach is used in models like UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, UC2
(
Zhou et al. 2021
)
, and M3P
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
.

Two-Tower Encoders

These models employ separate encoders for images and text, with their intermediate outputs passed to a cross-modal encoder. Models like LXMERT
(
Tan and Bansal 2019
)
, UNITER variations
(
Kwon et al. 2023
)
, and BridgeTower
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
use this design. Sequences of text embeddings and image region representations are processed by independent Transformer encoders with a distinct set of parameters. This is more parameter-efficient; however, it limits the interaction of image and text. The cross-modal encoder applies attention mechanisms where one modality generates queries while the other provides keys and values, facilitating nuanced interactions between visual and textual representations.

Dual Encoders

These architectures process each modality separately, with fusion occurring only at the final stage through similarity measures or joint embeddings. CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
, ALIGN
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
, and MURAL
(
Jain et al. 2021a
)
are examples of this approach. They are particularly effective for retrieval tasks because they allow pre-computing the representation. However, they are less suited for complex reasoning tasks because they do not allow interaction between the text and visual input earlier than in the last layer.

Encoder-Decoders

These models go beyond representation learning to include generative capabilities. Models like BLIP
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, SimVLM
(
Wang et al. 2022b
)
, and MAPL
(
Mañas et al. 2023
)
incorporate decoders for tasks requiring text generation, such as image captioning or open-domain visual question answering.

Beyond these established categories, we identify additional architecture types:

Combo Encoders

These models represent a variant of two-tower architectures where fusion is done not only within the cross-modal encoder but also between the unimodal encoder representations using an additional loss as in dual encoders. ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, X2-VLM
(
Zeng et al. 2023a
)
, and related models
(
Zeng et al. 2023b
)
use multiple fusion points with different loss functions to improve multimodal alignment.

Mixture-of-Modality-Experts

These architectures add modality-specific modules within transformer architectures. VLMo
(
Bao et al. 2022b
)
and BEiT
(
Wang et al. 2022a
)
replace standard feed-forward layers with three specialized layers for vision, text, and cross-modal representations, with task-dependent switching mechanisms. This approach addresses parameter inefficiencies of one-tower encoders while keeping the possibility of cross-modal interaction.

Decoder Models

These models extend generative large language models by integrating vision components. Models like MAGMA
(
Eichenberg et al. 2022
)
, mBLIP
(
Geigle et al. 2024
)
, and others
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Mañas et al. 2023
)
map visual features into the language model space, enabling multimodal input processing within generative frameworks. Because all parameters are shared for both text and vision modality, they can be considered generative analogs to one-tower encoders.

In addition to categorizing models based on how they combine modalities, vision-language models can also be classified according to how they represent and process visual information. There are three main approaches:

•

Multimodal Single Streams.
These models concatenate visual and textual features into a unified input sequence that is processed by a single transformer architecture. Visual features are typically encoded and treated as special tokens that can be intermixed with text tokens. MAGMA
(
Eichenberg et al. 2022
)
exemplifies this approach by creating joint representations directly from the unified multimodal input stream.

•

Vision-to-LLM Mapping.
This category includes models that map visual representations into the language model’s embedding space through two distinct strategies.

–

Continuous representations.
Visual features are mapped into a continuous numerical space that aligns with language model embeddings, allowing seamless integration without discretization. Examples include MAPL
(
Mañas et al. 2023
)
and mBLIP
(
Geigle et al. 2024
)
.

–

Discrete tokens.
Visual features are quantized into discrete units that mimic the structure of text tokens, enabling direct processing by language models designed for discrete inputs. This approach is adopted by models such as SEED
(
Ge et al. 2024
)
, LaVIT
(
Zhan et al. 2024
)
, and AnyGPT
(
Jin et al. 2024
)
.

•

Hybrid Fusion.
Some models combine multiple representation strategies within different components of their architecture. For instance, one module might process concatenated multimodal streams while another handles discrete visual tokens, with outputs integrated for final predictions
(
Diao et al. 2023
)
.

Among the most influential models, CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
introduced a dual-encoder architecture trained with contrastive learning on 400 million image-text pairs from the web. The model uses separate encoders for images (typically Vision Transformers or ResNets) and text (GPT-like transformers), learning to maximize cosine similarity between matching image-text pairs while minimizing similarity between non-matching pairs. UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
employs a one-tower approach with four pre-training tasks: masked language modeling, masked region modeling, image-text matching, and word-region alignment using optimal transport. ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
combines the benefits of contrastive learning and cross-modal fusion by first aligning image and text representations through contrastive loss before fusing them via cross-modal attention, additionally introducing momentum distillation to handle noisy web data.

4.2.
Data and Pre-training

Vision-language models typically employ several pre-training objectives adapted from both computer vision and natural language processing. The most important training tasks include:

Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

This approach directly adapts BERT’s training objective to multimodal settings. The model receives sentences or multimodal inputs with randomly masked text tokens and must predict the masked tokens using both textual context and visual information. Even if the training starts with a pre-trained text encoder, MLM is used for continuous training. Most encoder-models (UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, and VisualBERT
(
Li et al. 2019
)
) use MLM.

Masked Region Modeling (MRM)

This serves as the visual counterpart to MLM. The model receives images with specific regions hidden by binary masks and must reconstruct the masked regions. Reconstruction targets can range from raw pixels to higher-level visual features such as object categories or mean color values. UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
and VL-BERT
(
Su et al. 2020
)
incorporate MRM variants in their training.

Image-Text Matching (ITM)

This task requires the model to determine whether a given image and caption correspond to each other. This binary classification task helps the model learn global correspondences between visual and textual content. UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, and BLIP
(
Li et al. 2022
)
utilize ITM as part of their multi-task training.

Contrastive Learning (CL)

This approach learns to align positive image-text pairs while separating negative pairs within a batch. Given a batch of image-text pairs, the model maximizes similarity between semantically related pairs while minimizing similarity between unrelated pairs. This approach, popularized by CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
, has proven highly effective for learning aligned multimodal representations. Other models adopting contrastive learning include ALIGN
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
and ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
.

Additional specialized objectives include optimal transport-based word-region alignment
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, momentum distillation for handling noisy data
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, and various generative objectives for models with decoder components
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022b
)
. The choice of pre-training objectives significantly influences model capabilities and downstream performance across different task categories.

4.3.
Frequent Evaluation Tasks

Vision-language models are evaluated across three main categories of downstream tasks that assess different aspects of multimodal understanding. In this overview, we focus on tasks that are also relevant in the multilingual context.

Multimodal Reasoning

These tasks test the model’s ability to grasp relationships between images and text at logical and semantic levels, combining visual and textual information. The tasks are typically formulated as classification, which allows straightforward evaluation using the accuracy and F-score.

•

Visual Question Answering
(
Hudson and Manning 2019
;
Antol et al. 2015
)
— using an image and a question, the model provides a one-word answer as a multilabel classification task.

•

Natural Language Vision Inference
(
Xie et al. 2019
)
— considering a context image and 2 captions, the model outputs the relationship between the 2 sentences as either logical entailment, contradiction, or neutrality.

•

Natural vision-language Reasoning
(
Suhr et al. 2019
)
— using two images and one caption that describes one visual aspect, the model answers either True or False.

•

Visual Commonsense Reasoning
(
Zellers et al. 2019
)
— considering not only an image but also a list of regions in the image, the model has to answer multiple-choice questions.

•

Image Captioning
(
Karpathy and Fei-Fei 2017
;
Chen et al. 2015
)
— can only be tested with encoder-decoder and decoder-only models, computes the image representation and translates it into descriptive text. Unlike previous tasks, image captioning is an open-ended generation task, which makes automatic evaluation more difficult.

Cross-modal Similarity.

Here, the focus is on understanding the correspondence and similarity relationships between images and texts.
It is typically evaluated using Image-Text Retrieval
(
Young et al. 2014
;
Chen et al. 2015
)
. In this setup, we compute the image and text representations and search for the most similar cross-modal pairs.

Computer Vision Tasks.

Vision-language models are often evaluated on tasks that are traditionally considered to belong to computer vision. These tasks emphasize visual details, often requiring precise localization or attribute recognition.

•

Image Classification, Object Detection, Semantic Segmentation — pure computer vision tasks that evaluate visual understanding capabilities

•

Visual Grounding
(
Kazemzadeh et al. 2014
)
— using an image and query searching for a visual concept, the model has to predict its bounding box

•

Referring Expression Comprehension
(
Kazemzadeh et al. 2014
)
— retrieval tasks applied on proposed regions and images; the performance is measured on both the recognized region and the intersection over union of the bounding box

•

Open Vocabulary Attribute Detection
(
Bravo et al. 2023
)
— object detection that requires identifying additional attributes such as color, texture, and other fine-grained optical properties

Additionally, specialized benchmarks evaluate downstream tasks across different domains that go beyond the semantic understanding of scene images and focus on the visual understanding of documents. Examples of such tasks are SEED-Bench for VQA
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
, which covers multiple domain dimensions, MathVista
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
, which specializes in mathematical reasoning, and ChartQA
(
Masry et al. 2022
)
, which focuses on charts and diagrams.

Our survey covers 29 multilingual vision-language models spanning from 2020 to 2025, revealing several evolutionary patterns in the field. Architecturally, we observe a clear progression from encoder-based models (13 models) to decoder-only architectures (13 models), with fewer encoder-decoder (2 models). This shift mirrors broader trends in NLP, where autoregressive generation has demonstrated better scalability and flexibility.

Parameter scales have increased dramatically over this period. Early encoder models typically contained 100–400 million parameters, while recent decoder-only models span from 1 billion to 2 trillion parameters in the case of mixture-of-expert LLaMA 4.

Language coverage patterns reveal interesting disparities between implicit and explicit multilingual support. While many models implicitly support 100+ languages through their underlying language encoders (particularly those based on XLM-RoBERTa or mBERT), explicit multilingual training data often covers far fewer languages. For instance, mUNITER and xUNITER
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
rely purely on the multilingual capabilities of pre-trained representations without using any multilingual multimodal data, whereas models like MURAL
(
Jain et al. 2021a
)
and mSigLIP
(
Zhai et al. 2023
)
incorporate large-scale multilingual collections spanning over 100 languages.

Training data strategies include many approaches. Some models use minimal multilingual adaptation, e.g., through synthetic code-switching
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
or small-scale parallel datasets
(
Zeng et al. 2023b
)
(CCLM with 19M parallel sentences), while others leverage massive multilingual collections
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
(ALIGN with 1.8B image-text pairs, mSigLIP
(
Zhai et al. 2023
)
with 30B pairs). Commercial open-weight models lack transparency in this regard, as technical reports focus primarily on architectural innovations while providing limited details about training data composition and language distribution.

Evaluation practices typically assess performance in fewer languages than those supported during training. Most benchmarks evaluate 4–46 languages, significantly less than the 100+ languages many models theoretically support. This evaluation gap shows the relative scarcity of multilingual vision-language benchmarks and the field’s continued bias toward high-resource languages. We discuss the evaluation in more detail in Section
6
.

Typically, the development of a multilingual vision-language model starts with a multilingual language-only model and then adds multimodal capabilities via continued pre-training with pre-trained vision representations. Some models take the opposite approach: they start with an English-only vision-language model and extend to multiple languages by replacing the text encoder. Models like mCLIP
(
Carlsson et al. 2022
)
, PEFT-CLIP
(
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
, and CliCoTea
(
Karoui et al. 2023
)
demonstrate that existing English vision-language models can be successfully adapted through knowledge distillation or parameter-efficient fine-tuning with competitive performance.

As we discussed in Section
2
, there are two conflicting requirements for multilingual vision-language models: language neutrality and cultural awareness. Language neutrality can be easily incorporated in the training objectives to enforce consistent representations across languages, for instance, models employing contrastive learning on translated captions, which we often do in encoder models (e.g., NLLB-CLIP
(
Visheratin 2023
)
, CliCoTea
(
Karoui et al. 2023
)
). However, explicit cultural awareness objectives remain absent from current training procedures, as it is not clear how this could be achieved. Cultural awareness is primarily achieved through diverse training data that includes authentic images from different cultural contexts and authentic descriptions in the local languages (e.g., ALIGN
(
Jia et al. 2021
)
, mBLIP
(
Geigle et al. 2024
)
, Centurio
(
Geigle et al. 2025
)
). For commercial models, this information is often undisclosed (e.g., LLaMA 4, Gemma 3
(
Team et al. 2025
)
).

5.2.
Detailed Model Overview

M3P

M3P
(
Ni et al. 2021
)
is one of the earliest attempts at creating a unified multilingual VL encoder through multitask pre-training. The model uses a one-tower architecture that processes three distinct types of input: multilingual text, code-switched text with images, and monolingual texts with images. Visual features are extracted using Faster R-CNN
(
Ren et al. 2017
)
to generate region proposals, while text is tokenized using standard embedding approaches. A key innovation in M3P is Multimodal Code-switched Training (MCT), which randomly replaces tokens in sentences with their translations in different languages to encourage fine-grained alignment between images and non-English languages. This code-switching strategy operates exclusively on the textual modality and aims to map objects across different modalities and texts in various languages into a common semantic space.

UC2

Zhou et al. 2021
build a multilingual VL encoder by using complete multilingual sentences. Their approach involved translating the Conceptual Caption dataset
(
Sharma et al. 2018
)
into six languages and using additional pre-training objectives. The first objective, Masked Region-to-Token Modeling, requires the model to classify masked image regions using pseudo-labels generated by external object detectors. The second innovation, Early Adaptation, introduces a linear projection layer that maps visual region representations closer to the token embedding space by minimizing cosine similarity between visual features and corresponding text token embeddings from the same object detector labels. As M3P, it uses XLM-R to initialize the language encoder.

mUNITER and xUNITER

Concurrent with the release of the MARVL dataset,
Liu et al. 2021
developed two multilingual extensions of UNITER
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
. These models maintain the original UNITER architecture while replacing the text encoder: mUNITER substitutes BERT with multilingual BERT
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, while xUNITER employs XLM-R
(
Conneau et al. 2020
)
. Unlike the previous models, it does not use multilingual training data and only relies on the multilingual capabilities of XLM-R in a zero-shot setup.

ALIGN

Jia et al. 2021
developed ALIGN as a dual-encoder model trained using contrastive learning objectives similar to CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
. The model was trained with one large 1.8 billion image-text pair spanning more than 100 languages. It uses EfficientNet-based visual representations.

MURAL

Jain et al. 2021b
as an extension of ALIGN with 6 billion parallel text pairs across different languages. These parallel text pairs were generated using machine translation, and the model employs the same dual-encoder architecture and pre-trained weights as ALIGN. The additional cross-lingual contrastive objective aims to improve alignment between languages in the shared embedding space.

CCLM

Zeng et al. 2023b
use the X
2
-VLM
(
Zeng et al. 2023a
)
architecture with XLM-R
(
Conneau et al. 2020
)
for text processing, and a dedicated cross-modal encoder. The model accepts either image-text or bilingual text pairs as input, with unimodal representations feeding into contrastive loss functions. The cross-modal encoder implements two-tower logic with cross-attention mechanisms between parallel processing streams. While the authors leverage X
2
-VLM’s pre-training on 1B image-text pairs, their multilingual training only uses 19M parallel sentences.

mCLIP

Carlsson et al. 2022
addressed the computational expense of multilingual vision-language pre-training by adapting existing English CLIP models rather than training from scratch. Their approach involves replacing CLIP’s English text encoder with multilingual alternatives such as multilingual BERT, XLM-RoBERTa, or Swedish BERT. The adaptation process employs knowledge distillation, where the multilingual model (student) learns to replicate the embeddings of the original English encoder (teacher) by minimizing the mean squared error between their sentence representations on parallel texts.

PEFT-CLIP

Zhang et al. 2023b
identified performance disparities across languages in mCLIP, attributing these inconsistencies to uneven language distribution during pre-training. To address this limitation, they employed machine translation to get more parallel sentences. Their training objective combines the original cross-lingual loss with an additional term that minimizes mean squared error between translated and natural sentence representations. They use parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques, including Adapters, Compacter, and LoRA
(
He et al. 2023
)
, to reduce computational requirements. Despite these innovations, improvements on retrieval datasets such as XTD
(
Aggarwal and Kale 2020
)
and Multi30k
(
Barrault et al. 2018
)
remained modest, typically ranging from 1–2 percentage points compared to mCLIP.

NLLB-CLIP

Visheratin 2023
used the NLLB translation model
(
Team et al. 2022
)
to extend vision-language capabilities to the 200 languages covered by FLORES-200
(
Team et al. 2022
)
. The approach involved translating two existing retrieval datasets—XTD
(
Aggarwal and Kale 2020
)
and Flickr30k
(
Young et al. 2014
)
—into multiple languages. They replace the text encoder with LASER NLLB, and the entire model undergoes fine-tuning on the expanded multilingual datasets. Evaluation results demonstrate substantial improvements for low-resource languages.

UxCLM

The training strategy proposed by
Li et al. 2023a
uses weakly supervised multilingual vision-language pre-training for an encoder model. It uses a different dataset structure, consisting of 4M text-image pairs, 19M parallel translation pairs, and 800M unlabeled sentences. The training methodology unifies existing techniques through contrastive learning applied to both multilingual and multimodal data, while masked language modeling operates across multilingual sentences, parallel sentence pairs, and multimodal inputs. Evaluation on the IGLUE benchmark
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
shows substantial improvements over previous multilingual vision-language encoders.

XVL-MAML

Hu and Keller 2023
introduced a meta-learning framework for cross-lingual vision-language transfer, adapting the X-MAML algorithm
(
Nooralahzadeh et al. 2020
)
to multimodal settings. Using the models xUNITER and UC2 and the contrastive loss, the training procedure splits each language’s dataset into support and query subsets, computing gradients on the support set to obtain updated parameters (
\theta^{\prime}
), then evaluating on the query set with the updated parameters. This process repeats across multiple tasks, with initial parameters (
\theta
) updated based on aggregated gradients from all tasks.

CliCoTea

Karoui et al. 2023
proposes a cross-lingual transfer framework that adapts existing English vision-language models. By translating English captions from the IGLUE collection
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
using the Google API, it generates approximately 200,000 parallel samples per target language. Using ALBEF
(
Li et al. 2021
)
as the base architecture, the English BERT is replaced with a multilingual BERT encoder. Token-level alignments between English and target languages are computed using Awesome Align
(
Dou and Neubig 2021
)
. The multilingual model (student) learns to minimize the mean squared error with respect to the English model’s (teacher) contextualized token embeddings.

mBridgeTower

Inspired by adaptation methods from CliCoTea
(
Karoui et al. 2023
)
and mCLIP
(
Carlsson et al. 2022
)
,
Manea and Libovický 2025
developed a combined learning objective for cross-lingual transfer in vision-language models. The approach extends BridgeTower
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
to multilingual applications using various parallel datasets, with ablation analysis revealing that translated task-specific data provides the most significant improvements. Primary evaluation focuses on reasoning datasets including MARVL and M5-VGR, where results approach but do not exceed state-of-the-art performance levels achieved by other vision-language encoders.

mSigLIP

Zhai et al. 2023
replaced CLIP’s softmax-based contrastive learning with element-wise sigmoid operations. Thus, improving efficiency when the batch size remains below 16,000 samples. mSigLIP training utilizes 30 billion image-text pairs from WebLI across 109 languages. To manage the computational demands of large multilingual vocabularies, the authors introduced “bottlenecked token embedding”, decomposing the traditional
N\times D
embedding matrix (
N
is multilingual vocabulary size,
D
model dimension) into an
N\times K
lookup table and a
K\times D
projection matrix, where
K\ll D
, significantly reducing memory requirements.

SigLIP 2

Tschannen et al. 2025
introduced an enhanced version of SigLIP featuring both the original dual-encoder architecture and an additional autoregressive decoder for generative tasks. The vision encoder incorporates several innovations: preservation of image aspect ratios
(
Dehghani et al. 2023b
)
, support for variable sequence lengths following FlexiViT
(
Beyer et al. 2023
)
, and local-to-global consistency loss inspired by SILC
(
Naeem et al. 2024
)
. The consistency loss employs a self-distillation framework where the student model learns from partial views of training images to match the teacher’s full-view representations
(
Maninis et al. 2024
)
. This approach improves not only traditional vision-language tasks but also computer vision tasks.

Pali-X

Pali-X
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
is based on the 32-billion parameter UL2 encoder-decoder model
(
Tay et al. 2023
)
with a T5-based architecture
(
Raffel et al. 2020
)
. The vision part is the scaled-up Vision Transformer with 22B parameters
(
Dehghani et al. 2023a
)
, providing patch embeddings that are directly used as an input one-tower configuration. Pre-training uses more objectives than previous models, incorporating traditional vision-language tasks such as image captioning, optical character recognition, object detection, and span corruption similar to text-only training. The model goes beyond static images and also allows video processing.

LLaVA

LLaVA
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
was one of the first models that added vision capabilities into an instruction-tuned LLM. It combines Vicuna
(
Chiang et al. 2023
)
, a decoder-only model derived from the first LLaMA version
(
Touvron et al. 2023a
)
and CLIP-ViT visual encoder. The architecture processes visual patches and text tokens within a unified stream. While instruction tuning data is primarily in English, the underlying LLaMA weights incorporate multilingual knowledge from training on the ShareGPT4V dataset
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, which includes Chinese and Japanese content. The subsequent LLaVA 1.5 release
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
refined the visual encoder and expanded both pre-training and evaluation datasets, but without covering more languages.

mBLIP

mBLIP
(
Geigle et al. 2024
)
re-aligns an image encoder previously tuned to an English LLM (BLIP-2) to new multilingual LLMs (mT0 and BLOOMZ), using only a few million multilingual training examples derived from machine-translating English data into 95 languages. The architecture consists of three components: a Vision Transformer, a Query-Transformer (Q-Former), and a multilingual language model. During training, only the Q-Former, projections, and query tokens are updated while using LoRA
(
He et al. 2023
)
for parameter-efficient adaptation of the decoder. Evaluation on IGLUE (classification) and XM3600 (retrieval) benchmarks shows competitive performance with state-of-the-art models while significantly outperforming English-only Vision-LLMs like LLaVA 1.5. However, models released later outperform mBLIP in text generation tasks.

Pangea

The Pangea model
(
Yue et al. 2025a
)
was developed using a massive multilingual dataset collection ranging from traditional vision-language tasks to multilingual document chart question answering. The training corpus contains 40% English content, with the remaining 60% distributed across multiple languages. Two decoder-only variants are available, both utilizing CLIP-ViT features: one based on Vicuna 1.5
(
Chiang et al. 2023
)
and another on Qwen 2
(
Yang et al. 2024b
)
. Evaluation results demonstrate better performance compared to LLaVA across several multilingual benchmarks, including MARVL and CVQA.

PALO

PALO
(
Rasheed et al. 2025
)
presents vision-language models in two versions: a 13B parameter version derived from LLaVA and a lightweight 1.7B parameter variant based on MobileVLM
(
Chu et al. 2023
)
. The larger version processes visual patch embeddings from CLIP-ViT, while MobilePALO employs depth-wise separable convolutions for more efficient visual token downsampling. Pre-training involves only the visual projection modules on the CC-594k captioning dataset, followed by instruction tuning on manually verified machine-translated conversations from the LLaVA-Bench
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
dataset. The authors report balanced performance across ten languages, though specific evaluation tasks and detailed metrics were not provided in their initial publication.

Maya

Alam et al. 2024
developed Maya as a multimodal extension of Aya-23
(
Aryabumi et al. 2024
)
. The image patch embeddings are provided by the SigLIP vision encoder
(
Zhai et al. 2023
)
. The training process involves filtering the LLaVA pre-training dataset for toxic content, translating it into eight languages, followed by instruction tuning. The train set was publicly released. Despite the multilingual training approach, the authors do not provide a comprehensive evaluation of multilingual reasoning tasks and only evaluate the model on English multimodal sets.

Centurio

Geigle et al. 2025
conducted several experiments on multilingual capabilities in vision-language models, using Maya and Qwen 2.5 as text decoders. Their investigation reveals that balanced instruction tuning data distribution (50% English, 50% other languages) consistently improves performance across all language tiers defined by
Joshi et al. 2020
. The study addresses language fidelity issues, where models generate responses in languages other than the prompt input, and demonstrates its significant impact on multilingual performance. The performance across multilingual benchmarks is competitive even with the GPT-4
(
Song et al. 2025
)
.

Qwen 2-VL

Wang et al. 2024
introduced the vision-language variant of Qwen 2
(
Yang et al. 2024b
)
with two innovations. Naive Dynamic Resolution enables the processing of images at various resolutions without distortion, while Multimodal Rotary Position Embedding (M-RoPE) facilitates positional encoding fusion across text, images, and videos through three-dimensional encoding (temporal, height, and width). The model family spans from 2 billion to 72 billion parameters, with textual training involving approximately 1.4 trillion multimodal tokens. The largest version demonstrates superior performance compared to GPT-4o across diverse benchmarks, including multilingual optical character recognition in eight languages and various English-centric multimodal tasks. As in the case of other commercial open-weight models, the report overly focuses on architecture and little is known about the training data.

Qwen 2.5-VL

Bai et al. 2025
enhanced the Qwen 2-VL architecture with a redesigned vision encoder that applies self-attention only to four transformer layers while employing windowed attention with 112
\times
112-pixel windows (corresponding to 8
\times
8 patches) for the remaining layers. Additional optimizations include replacing layer normalization with RMSNorm
(
Zhang and Sennrich 2019
)
and standard activation functions with SwiGLU
(
Dauphin et al. 2017
)
in feed-forward layers. Visual features use the same M-RoPE positional encoding before projection into the text embedding space via a group projector that spatially clusters four adjacent patches, reducing token count while maintaining spatial coherence. Although pre-training data covers ten languages, detailed language-specific performance metrics were not reported.

Phi-4-VL

Phi-4-Mini
(
Microsoft et al. 2025
)
is one of the smaller multimodal LLMs, with only 3.8B parameters. Besides text and static images, it can also work with speech. The architecture uses grouped query attention with 24 query heads and 8 key-value heads, reducing KV cache consumption by approximately two-thirds. Long context support is enabled through LongRoPE
(
Ding et al. 2024
)
, which extends context windows to 2048k tokens through non-uniform interpolation and extrapolation of positional encoding signals. While the model supports multilingual speech input across eight languages, the authors explicitly state that multilingual image-text capabilities are not supported, although empirically, it does.

LLaMA 3.2

Grattafiori et al. 2024
released multiple LLaMA variants, with version 3.2 being the only multimodal iteration (version 3.3 being available for text-only applications). The architecture combines a MetaCLIP-ViT image encoder
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, a vision projector, and an LLaMA decoder for multimodal stream processing. The training is done in five stages: language model pre-training, multimodal training, vision adapter training, model fine-tuning, and speech adapter training. Multimodal training uses 6 billion image-text pairs across multiple objectives, with representations in seven non-English languages. Despite multilingual training data, the authors restrict official support to English for image-text applications while supporting 34 languages for speech processing, similar to Phi-4-VL.

LLaMA 4

Current information about LLaMA 4 comes primarily from blog announcements rather than comprehensive technical reports. The architecture follows LLaMA 3.2; however, it is a mixture-of-experts architecture
(
Jacobs et al. 1991
;
Fedus et al. 2021
)
that routes multimodal streams through different feed-forward-network (FFN) sublayers: both shared FFN experts and 15 specialized FFN experts, combining outputs via residual connections. The Behemoth variant contains 288 billion active parameters within a 2 trillion parameter total architecture, subsequently distilled into smaller variants (Maverick and Scout), with Scout optimized for efficiency while supporting 10 million token context lengths.

Pali-Gemma

Beyer et al. 2024
introduced one of the first multilingual vision-language models based on the Gemma models. It was trained on the WebLI dataset containing samples across more than 100 languages. Unfortunately, this primary training resource remains unavailable to the research community. The model incorporates pre-training objectives, including multi-object detection through Pix2Seq
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
and instance segmentation. Pix2Seq reformulates object localization as a sequence generation task using autoregressive transformer decoders rather than traditional regression objectives. Performance evaluation on reasoning tasks such as MARVL demonstrates competitive accuracy comparable to Centurio.

Gemma 3

Gemma 3
(
Team et al. 2025
)
represents the latest iteration in the Gemma family, incorporating Grouped Query Attention modules. Similar to Qwen 2.5-VL, the architecture combines local sliding window attention with global self-attention modules in a 5:1 ratio, supporting context windows up to 128k tokens during pre-training. The SigLIP vision encoder processes images at a fixed 896×896 resolution, potentially causing distortion for non-square inputs. To address this limitation, the authors introduce Pan and Scan, an inference-time adaptive windowing algorithm that segments images into non-overlapping patches, resizes them to the required resolution, and processes them through the encoder. Multilingual evaluation focuses primarily on text-only datasets.

The evaluation of multilingual vision-language models requires carefully designed benchmarks that can assess performance across diverse languages and cultural contexts. Unlike monolingual evaluation, multilingual benchmarks must navigate the fundamental tension between language neutrality and cultural awareness discussed in Section
2
. This challenge is reflected in the variety of approaches taken by existing datasets, ranging from direct translation of English content to culturally grounded, independently developed evaluations.

We collect and analyze 22 recent multilingual vision-language benchmarks, summarized in Table
2
. These datasets span various tasks, including visual reasoning, image-text retrieval, visual question answering, and image captioning.

For each dataset, we report the number of languages covered by the dataset.
We categorize languages using the resourcefulness tiers from
Joshi et al. 2020
, ranging from tier 5 (high-resourced) to tier 0 (left-behind languages), and visualize the distribution in the table.
To assess the linguistic diversity of each benchmark, we use two metrics introduced by
Ploeger et al. 2024
: Mean Pairwise Syntactic Distance (MPSD), which computes the average syntactic distance between all possible language pairs in a dataset based on typological features from the URIEL database, and GramBank Feature Value Inclusion (GBI), which measures the proportion of all possible grammatical feature values from the GramBank database that are represented by the languages in the dataset
We collect the number of native speakers (L1) for each language and estimate the percentage of the world’s population that is covered by the dataset.

The number of texts in the table is the total number of samples from all languages. Since images are not language-dependent, we list only the unique ones. To show also if the texts are translations of each other and if images are the same, for each language, we inserted the symbols
=
and
\neq
in the number cells. Whereas there are many datasets for evaluating language neutrality, where the images are the same and sentences are translations of each other, we have not found a dataset with the same texts but with language or region-dependent images.
Finally, we summarize the focus of the dataset using a single keyword.

6.1.
General Trends and Observations

Language Coverage

The benchmarks vary in both the number of tested languages and typological diversity. While some datasets like MVL-SIB
(
Schmidt et al. 2025
)
support as many as 199 languages, most benchmarks focus on 4-30 languages. Most datasets cover less than one third of the world’s population, with 199-language MVL-SIB covering 70% of the population; however, with only 70 images that are not representative of the whole world.

Most benchmarks focus on rather high-resource languages, with tier 4–5 languages dominating the distributions shown in Table
2
. Notable exceptions include ArtElingo-28 and MVL-SIB, which incorporate substantial numbers of low-resource languages (tiers 1-2).

Linguistic Diversity

High-diversity datasets such as ArtElingo-28 (MPSD: 72.0) and MARVL (MPSD: 69.1) prioritize typological diversity over simple language count, while larger collections like XM3600 have a higer number of languages, however, much less diverse (36 languages, MPSD: 62.9). The highest morphological diversity is achieved by WIT (GBI: 96.4) and MVL-SIB (GBI: 95.4), both being based on data extracted from Wikipedia.

Task Coverage and Scale

Visual reasoning tasks dominate, with datasets like MARVL, M5-VGR, and xGQA testing semantic understanding via logical inference. Retrieval benchmarks such as xFlickrCo and XTD focus on cross-modal similarity assessment, while generative tasks are represented by image captioning datasets like XM3600. Scale varies dramatically, from synthetic datasets like SMPQA (50 images) to bigger collections like xGQA (85k images).

Evaluation Paradigms

The benchmarks can be categorized into three primary evaluation paradigms based on their approach to multilinguality. Translation-based datasets such as XVNLI, xGQA, and Multi30k maintain identical visual content while translating textual components, enabling direct cross-lingual performance comparison and supporting language neutrality evaluation. Culture-aware datasets like MARVL, ArtElingo-28, and WorldCuisines use region-specific images and culturally-grounded content, explicitly testing cultural awareness capabilities. Other datasets like XM3600 (images from around the world with independent captions in different languages) and CVQA (local images with native language questions and English translation) attempt to balance semantic consistency with cultural authenticity.

Cultural vs. Semantic Focus

Roughly two-thirds of the benchmarks primarily evaluate semantic consistency and language neutrality, following translation-based methodologies that enable controlled cross-lingual evaluation. The rest, at least to some extent, evaluate cultural awareness through diverse imagery, subjective evaluation criteria, or region-specific content.
We observe an increased interest in culture-aware evaluation starting in 2024, so this distribution might change in the future.

Aggregated Benchmarks

Several recent publications have introduced benchmark collections that aggregate previously released datasets, creating comprehensive evaluation suites. Notable examples include IGLUE
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
and M5
(
Schneider and Sitaram 2024
)
, both of which incorporate the MARVL reasoning dataset
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
. While these collections aim at providing standardized evaluation frameworks, they might create confusion regarding dataset novelty and evaluation scope. In this survey, we only list the original contributions of each collection rather than benchmarks taken over from previous work.

Methodological Limitations

Current benchmarks have several systemic limitations.
First, most datasets rely heavily on English as an intermediate language, either through direct translation or English-centric image collections.
Second, visual content often originates from mostly Western sources (e.g., Flickr), potentially introducing cultural bias. Moreover, many of the images are more than ten years old, and thus might not reflect that, for instance, fashion and many technological artifacts might look different.
Third, evaluation metrics typically assume semantic equivalence across languages, which may not capture culturally dependent interpretation differences.
Finally, the scarcity of large-scale, culturally authentic datasets limits the comprehensive evaluation of cultural awareness capabilities in vision-language models.

6.2.
Detailed Benchmark Overview

MARVL

The Multicultural and Multilingual Visual Language (MARVL) dataset, introduced by
Liu et al. 2021
, is an evaluation benchmark designed for visual-language reasoning. Unlike other datasets that were translated from original English datasets, MARVL was independently developed for five languages, focusing on cultural awareness: Turkish, Swahili, Chinese, Indonesian, and Tamil. The dataset consists of image-text pairs, where, given two ordered images and a textual hypothesis, models must determine whether the hypothesis is true or false.

XVNLI

Part of the IGLUE collection
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
, the Cross-lingual Visual Natural Language Inference (XVNLI) dataset extends the task of natural language inference (NLI) into the multimodal and multilingual world. The dataset builds upon the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) corpus
(
Bowman et al. 2015
)
, its multimodal version
(
Xie et al. 2019
)
, incorporating images from Flickr30k
(
Young et al. 2014
)
and its multilingual version (XNLI)
(
Agić and Schluter 2018
)
, covering 3 more languages by translating the original English sentences. The task involves classifying relationships between an image and two textual hypotheses into entailment, neutral, or contradiction categories.

xGQA

Derived from the English-only GQA dataset
(
Hudson and Manning 2019
)
, xGQA introduces a multilingual visual question answering benchmark. The test set has been manually translated into eight languages. It contains visually grounded questions that models must answer using a predefined set of approximately 2000 labels. Unlike most other IGLUE subsets, xGQA includes an extensive training set, totaling 2.4M sentences and 85k unique images.

xFlickrCo

The xFlickrCo dataset, also a part of the IGLUE benchmark
(
Bugliarello et al. 2022
)
, is an evaluation set designed for cross-lingual image-text retrieval. It consists of 2k images, equally sourced from the Flickr30k dataset
(
Young et al. 2014
)
and the COCO
Karpathy split

(
Lin et al. 2014
;
Karpathy and Fei-Fei 2017
)
.

WIT

The Wikipedia-based Image-Text (WIT) dataset
(
Srinivasan et al. 2021
)
contains images extracted from Wikipedia articles along with their captions extracted from the articles’ abstracts, curated for vision-language retrieval tasks. The IGLUE version of WIT contains only a subset of the original dataset, with 11 out of the 108 original languages. Many captions in Wikipedia are originally in the local languages, and many of them are translations. It is, therefore, not entirely clear to what extent this benchmark measures cultural awareness.

Multi30k

Multi30k
(
Barrault et al. 2018
)
is a multilingual image-text retrieval dataset originally designed to replicate Flickr30k with multilingual annotations. Initially, it was developed for English and German
(
Elliott et al. 2016
)
, later extended to English-French
(
Elliott et al. 2017
)
and English-Czech
(
Barrault et al. 2018
)
. The dataset focuses on disambiguation and semantic equivalence across languages.

XM3600

Thapliyal et al. 2022
built XM3600 for image captioning, using 3600 images, each with different captions from across the world from different cultures. Although the number of languages is not huge, it has very high morphological diversity in terms of GBI.

MaXM

MaXM
(
Changpinyo et al. 2023
)
transforms XM3600 captions into question-answer pairs across seven languages through a systematic pipeline involving English translation, automated question generation, and human validation. This methodology ensures semantic consistency across languages while maintaining the cultural diversity of the underlying XM3600 imagery.

XTD

Aggarwal and Kale 2020
extended the MSCOCO2014 test set
(
Rajendran et al. 2016
)
, which has 1k image-text pairs, employing human annotators to translate it into 9 languages. Additionally, they included Japanese captions from the STAIR dataset
(
Yoshikawa et al. 2017
)
, which refer to the same images. The entire set was validated in the retrieval context by comparing multilingual cross-modal embedding methods.

CoMMuTE

CoMMuTE
(
Futeral et al. 2023
)
specifically targets lexical ambiguity in multimodal machine translation from English into Czech, German, and French. Starting with 155 ambiguous sentences, each accompanied by image pairs and corresponding ground-truth translations, the dataset evaluates the ability to resolve textual ambiguity using the visual context.

ArtElingo-28

ArtElingo-28
(
Mohamed et al. 2024
)
prioritizes cultural awareness through subjective evaluation of artistic content. The dataset presents paintings with captions across 28 languages, accompanied by nine emotion labels. With languages predominantly from Africa and Southeast Asia (plus English), ArtElingo-28 achieves high typological diversity (MPSD 72.0) and substantial morphological coverage (GBI 83.6). The focus on subjective emotional responses directly challenges language neutrality assumptions, making it a primary benchmark for cultural awareness evaluation.

M5

M5
(
Schneider and Sitaram 2024
)
collects existing datasets and introduces M5-VGR for vision-grounded reasoning and M5-VLOD for vision-language outlier detection. Both tasks cover 13 languages, including very low-resource languages like Berber. M5-VGR follows MARVL’s reasoning scheme, while M5-VLOD requires identifying contextually inconsistent images from sets of five candidates. The inclusion of diverse language families and task variety supports the evaluation of both language neutrality and cultural awareness. Figure
1
and Figure
5
show one example from each subset.

Multilingual Memes

Dimitrov et al. 2024
created a dataset for a 2024 SemEval shared task on classifying persuasion techniques in memes. The dataset covers a few European languages and is only available upon request.

WorldCuisines

WorldCuisines
(
Winata et al. 2025
)
is a cultural evaluation with benchmark food-related visual question answering across 24 languages. The dataset achieves high typological diversity (MPSD 65.5). This represents one of the clearest examples of cultural awareness evaluation, where correct answers depend on culture-specific knowledge rather than language-neutral facts.

xMMMU and xChatBench

Both datasets from the Pangea collection
(
Yue et al. 2025b
)
extend English benchmarks using machine translation. xMMMU translates MMMU
(
Yue et al. 2024
)
validation data across seven languages for multimodal reasoning evaluation, while xChatBench provides conversational data for eight languages. Like other translation-based benchmarks, it prioritizes language neutrality while maintaining task complexity across languages.

MTVQA

MTVQA
(
Tang et al. 2024
)
addresses text-rich image understanding across 10 languages. With 28.6k text samples and 8.7k images, the dataset tests in-image text understanding across scripts and writing systems. The focus on text recognition and comprehension supports primarily language-neutral evaluation.

CVQA

CVQA
(
Romero et al. 2024
)
explicitly targets cultural diversity through multiple-choice visual question answering across 31 language-country pairs. Achieving high typological diversity (MPSD 67.7) and morphological coverage (GBI 88.2), CVQA provides bilingual annotations (English plus target language) for each item. Having an English translation also allows for partially evaluating language neutrality. One sample from the original publication is in Figure
7
.

MVL-SIB

MVL-SIB
(
Schmidt et al. 2025
)
achieves the broadest linguistic coverage with 200 languages, though using only 70 unique images. The cross-modal topical matching task requires selecting appropriate captions for image sets across massive linguistic diversity (MPSD 70.8, GBI 95.4). Despite limited visual content, the exceptional linguistic coverage makes MVL-SIB valuable for assessing language neutrality. One sample from the original publication is in Figure
6
.

Drawing-based datasets

Our table contains three additional multilingual sets which are rather focused on drawings, charts, and plots, than on scene images.
SMPQA
is a synthetic dataset with only 50 images, generated from a pattern of rules by
Geigle et al. 2025
, which can be easily translated.
M3Exam

(
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
and
EXAMS-V

(
Das et al. 2024
)
consist of collected exam questions, both text-only and multimodal, from different parts of the globe. We reported only the multimodal samples in Table
2
.

7.
Conclusions

This survey examines multilingual vision-language models and their evaluation benchmarks. It analyzes 31 models and 21 benchmarks released between 2020 and 2025 and discusses patterns in development timelines, training objectives, and evaluation practices.

Multilingual vision-language models lag behind their monolingual counterparts and language-only multilingual models. The field has moved from encoder-only architectures (13 models) optimized for classification to decoder-only models (13 models) capable of generative tasks, with parameter counts increasing from 100-400 million to over 2 trillion parameters.
However, the biggest and best-performing models from commercial entities suffer from transparency issues. Their documentation emphasizes architectural innovations while providing limited details about training data composition and language distribution. This undermines reproducibility and assessment of actual multilingual capabilities.

We identify a fundamental tension between
language neutrality
and
cultural awareness
in multilingual vision-language modeling. Language neutrality seeks consistent representations across languages for objective concepts, enabling cross-lingual transfer. Cultural awareness emphasizes adaptation to linguistic and cultural contexts, recognizing that meaning varies across communities.

Current training methodologies often explicitly optimize for language neutrality through contrastive learning on translated captions, cross-lingual alignment objectives, and parallel data, especially with encoder models. However, there are no explicit cultural awareness objectives we could optimize for, as the cultural diversity always needs to come from diverse training data.

Approximately two-thirds of multilingual evaluation benchmarks prioritize language neutrality when relying on translation-based methodologies (XVNLI, xGQA, Multi30k), with identical visual content and texts that are translations of each other. However, we observe increased interest in cultural awareness from 2024 onward, with benchmarks like ArtElingo-28, WorldCuisines, and CVQA incorporating region-specific imagery and culturally-grounded content.

Current evaluation benchmarks often rely on classification tasks, which do not adequately assess the generative capabilities of contemporary models. While classification enables straightforward, rigorous evaluation, it fails to capture the challenges of open-ended generation, cultural appropriateness, and linguistic authenticity across languages. Current language generation metrics often fail to capture generation quality in an appropriate way.

The tension between language neutrality and cultural awareness remains unresolved. While models demonstrate cross-lingual transfer capabilities, the emerging interest in cultural representation in evaluation benchmarks highlights gaps between evaluation goals and training methodologies.
