Insights into SUMOylation in gastric cancer: molecular mechanisms and emerging therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Peyman Tabnak, Mohammad Ebrahimnezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gastric cancer remains a significant global health challenge due to its aggressive behavior, high mortality rates, and limited treatment success. Growing research underscores the critical role of SUMOylation, a reversible post-translational modification, in regulating key cellular processes such as DNA repair, gene expression, genomic stability, and signaling pathways. Aberrant SUMOylation is closely linked to gastric cancer development, metastasis, and resistance to therapies, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target. This review consolidates current knowledge of the SUMOylation system, which involves activating (E1), conjugating (E2), and ligating (E3) enzymes, alongside SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs) that reverse the modification. We explore how SUMOylation influences cancer-promoting and tumor-suppressing pathways by stabilizing or degrading critical proteins, modulating immune responses, driving epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and contributing to chemoresistance. Furthermore, we discuss promising therapeutic approaches targeting SUMOylation, including small-molecule inhibitors, natural bioactive compounds, and synthetic lethal strategies that exploit SUMO pathway weaknesses. Preclinical studies suggest these approaches could enhance chemotherapy and immunotherapy effectiveness, offering new precision treatment options. Deepening our understanding of SUMOylation in gastric cancer is vital for developing innovative, mechanism-driven therapies to improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

In 2022, gastric cancer affected nearly one million people worldwide, causing over 769,000 deaths and ranking as the fifth most common cancer and leading cause of cancer mortality globally (Bray et al. 2024). This malignancy arises from the transformation of gastric epithelial cells, driven by Helicobacter pylori infection, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle factors, with key biochemical markers, including tumor suppressors like p53 and oncogenes, further exacerbated by epigenetic changes such as CpG island methylation and mutations (Kolgi et al. 2024).

Among post-translational modifications (PTMs) fueling cancer hallmarks like uncontrolled proliferation, apoptosis evasion, and metastasis, SUMOylation emerges distinctly through its reversible attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to target lysines, finely tuning nuclear protein dynamics, DNA repair, and stress responses that are profoundly dysregulated in gastric cancer (Dutta and Jain 2023; Pan and Chen 2022). In contrast to ubiquitination (which mainly directs degradation) or phosphorylation (which modulates enzymatic activity), SUMOylation uniquely stabilizes oncoproteins, drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and imparts therapy resistance, establishing it as an underexplored yet prime intervention target (Celen and Sahin 2020; Liu et al. 2022). As a non-canonical PTM, SUMOylation critically governs gastrointestinal tumor development, invasion, and resistance by orchestrating genomic stability, DNA damage responses, and cell cycle progression pathways increasingly targeted therapeutically (Liu et al. 2023).

Notably, polymorphisms in SUMOylation-related genes elevate gastric cancer (GC) risk; for instance, the CA genotype confers a 1.71-fold increased susceptibility, while CC offers protection (Luo et al. 2017). Building on this, a recent review illuminated SUMOylation's orchestration of intestinal homeostasis, immune responses, and host-microbiota interactions, with profound implications for inflammatory bowel diseases and colorectal cancer mechanisms modulating epithelial integrity, signaling, and gut functions that position it as a therapeutic linchpin (Ma et al. 2024). Yet, despite these gut-wide insights and parallels in epithelial barriers, immune modulation, and oncogenic cascades between stomach and intestines, SUMOylation's specific contributions to gastric carcinogenesis remain underexplored. This gap underscores the urgency to probe its mechanistic roles for novel diagnostics and therapies. Given SUMOylation's centrality in cancer progression and the dearth of dedicated GC-focused reviews, we herein dissect its pathogenesis contributions to illuminate targeted opportunities.

Overview of SUMOylation

SUMOylation represents a kind of post-translational, reversible protein modification due to the attachment of SUMO-modified proteins to target proteins; modifications control multiple aspects of cell physiology, such as transcription, DNA damage repair, signaling transduction, and regulating protein stability. A cascade of successive enzyme-mediated steps represents the activation, conjugation, ligation, and deSUMOylation of SUMOylation. Maturation of SUMO proteins

The maturation of SUMO proteins involves a precise, multi-stage sequence that prepares them for their regulatory functions in cellular processes. These proteins are initially produced as immature precursors bearing extended C-terminal sequences, which must be cleaved by dedicated SUMO-specific proteases from the SENP family. This proteolytic trimming exposes a conserved di-glycine (GG) motif at the protein's C-terminus, a structural feature indispensable for enabling downstream interactions with target substrates (Vertegaal 2022; Shkolnik et al. 2024; Nayak and Amrute‐Nayak 2020).

Activation of SUMO by E1 enzymes

The activation of SUMO by E1 enzymes represents a vital first step in the SUMOylation pathway, a post-translational modification critical for regulating diverse cellular processes. This activation is mediated by a heterodimeric E1 enzyme complex consisting of SAE1 (SUMO-activating enzyme subunit 1) and SAE2 (SUMO-activating enzyme subunit 2) (Wang et al. 2021a). The complex catalyzes an ATP-dependent reaction that begins with the adenylation of the C-terminal glycine residue of SUMO, resulting in the formation of a SUMO-AMP intermediate (Rytz et al. 2023). Next, a thioester bond is established between the cysteine residue in the active site of SAE2 and the C-terminal glycine of SUMO, creating a SUMO-E1 complex. This activation prepares SUMO for its transfer to the E2 conjugating enzyme, enabling the continuation of the SUMOylation process (Akimoto et al. 2022). The accurate and efficient activation of SUMO by E1 enzymes is essential for ensuring the fidelity of the SUMOylation process and its critical role in preserving cellular homeostasis.

Conjugation by E2 enzyme

The conjugation of SUMO by the E2 enzyme is a crucial stage in the SUMOylation pathway. After activation by the E1 enzyme, the SUMO protein is transferred to the active site of the E2 conjugating enzyme through a transesterification reaction. During this process, SUMO forms a thioester bond with the catalytic cysteine residue of Ubc9 (Varejão et al. 2020). Unlike ubiquitination, SUMOylation predominantly depends on the direct recognition of specific consensus motifs (ψKxE) on substrate proteins by Ubc9 (Yau et al. 2020). Ubc9 transfers SUMO onto the lysine residues within these motifs of substrate proteins. Most of the time, this is sufficient to achieve conjugation. For certain substrates, however, E3 ligases are necessary for increasing efficiency or specificity (Shi et al. 2022). The E2 enzyme’s role in SUMOylation is crucial, as it mediates substrate interaction and provides the precision needed for this tightly regulated modification process (Zhao et al. 2021).

Ligation to target proteins

The attachment of SUMO to target proteins is a tightly regulated process primarily executed by the E2-conjugating enzyme Ubc9, with significant assistance from specific E3 ligases, including the PIAS family, RanBP2, and the Polycomb group protein Pc2 (Wu and Huang 2023). Ubc9 specifically recognizes a consensus sequence in target proteins, typically ψKxE, where ψ represents a hydrophobic residue, K is the lysine to be modified, x is any amino acid, and E stands for glutamic acid (Wei et al. 2024). Upon binding, Ubc9 catalyzes the transfer of the activated SUMO from its own cysteine to the lysine of the substrate protein, forming an isopeptide bond. E3 ligases, including PIAS proteins, enhance this ligation by stabilizing the association of Ubc9 and substrate. In some cases, non-consensus lysine residues can also be modified, which usually requires the assistance of these or other E3 ligases for efficient ligation. This ligation step ensures the covalent attachment of SUMO, thereby altering the functional or structural properties of the substrate protein, influencing its activity, stability, or interaction with other cellular factors (Liu et al. 2021a).

Reversibility: DeSUMOylation

DeSUMOylation, the reversal of SUMOylation, is facilitated by a specialized group of enzymes named SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs) (Calderon-Rivera et al. 2024). These enzymes cleave the isopeptide bond between the C-terminal glycine of SUMO and the ε-amino group of the lysine residue on the target protein, thereby freeing SUMO for recycling and returning the substrate protein to its unmodified state (Chen et al. 2020a). SENPs have a dual function in regulating SUMOylation: they process precursor SUMO proteins into their mature forms prior to conjugation and also remove SUMO from modified substrates. Different SENP isoforms, such as SENP1, SENP2, SENP3, and SENP6, exhibit substrate specificity and subcellular localization, enabling precise regulation of SUMOylation in various cellular compartments and processes. For instance, SENP1 and SENP2 are implicated in nuclear processes, while SENP3 and SENP6 are involved in maintaining protein homeostasis by deconjugating SUMO chains. The reversibility of SUMOylation allows cells to dynamically regulate protein function, localization, and interactions in response to environmental cues, ensuring a balance between SUMOylation and deSUMOylation for proper cellular function (Jiao et al. 2024; Tokarz and Woźniak 2021).

SUMOylation regulators in gastric cancer

Small ubiquitin-related modifiers

SUMO1

SUMO-1 plays a significant role in GC cell proliferation and apoptosis by influencing various cellular processes. In gastric cancer tissues, SUMO-1 expression is significantly elevated compared to adjacent normal tissues, indicating its potential role in tumor progression. SUMOylation, mediated by SUMO-1, affects critical pathways such as the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which promotes cell proliferation and survival. Silencing the SUMO-1 gene using shRNA has been shown to reduce the proliferation rate of GC cells, induce G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest, and promote apoptosis. This gene silencing also leads to the downregulation of key proteins, including Bcl-2 and c-myc, which are associated with anti-apoptotic and proliferative functions, as well as mutant p53, a factor contributing to tumor aggressiveness (Jin et al. 2017). In GC, SUMO1 impacts proteins such as SP3, where SUMOylation suppresses its transcriptional activity, thereby influencing downstream gene expression. Specifically, SUMO1-dependent SUMOylation at key residues modulates tumor progression by either repressing tumor-suppressor pathways or enhancing oncogenic signaling. The effects of SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation on GC cells include promoting proliferation, survival, and metastatic potential. It also affects the cell cycle by altering the expression of regulatory proteins, leading to increased cell growth and reduced apoptosis. Furthermore, by targeting pathways like TGF-β1/SMAD2/3, SUMO1 contributes to the EMT and enhances the stemness of disseminated tumor cells, facilitating metastasis (Li et al. 2021). In gastric cancer, MORC2 impacts by modulating the SUMOylation of the transcription factor C/EBPα through SUMO1, which is crucial in controlling the balance between cell differentiation and proliferation. MORC2 interacts with the TE-III domain of C/EBPα and promotes its SUMOylation at lysine residue K161 via SUMO1 conjugation. This post-translational modification destabilizes C/EBPα by enhancing its proteasomal degradation and reducing its nuclear localization, effectively downregulating its tumor-suppressive and differentiation-promoting activities. The resulting loss of C/EBPα-mediated differentiation favors cell proliferation, contributing to gastric tumorigenesis. Clinically, MORC2 overexpression correlates with poor differentiation, advanced tumor stages, and reduced patient survival. By promoting SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation, MORC2 establishes itself as a critical oncogene in gastric cancer, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target (Liu et al. 2019). Researchers have observed a significant increase in the SUMOylation of Rev-Erbα by SUMO1 in gastric cancer tissues and cell lines. This modification promotes the ubiquitination of Rev-Erbα, leading to its proteasome-dependent degradation. SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation, observed for the first time in gastric cancer, was more pronounced compared to SUMO2, highlighting its specific role. The loss of Rev-Erbα stability due to SUMOylation reduces its ability to repress the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, VEGF, and IL-10. This results in an amplified inflammatory response, which is associated with increased glycolysis (evidenced by elevated lactate levels), fueling cancer cell proliferation and tumor progression. Thus, SUMOylation by SUMO1 emerges as a pivotal mechanism contributing to the dysregulation of inflammation and metabolism in gastric cancer, presenting a potential therapeutic target (Ke et al. 2022). A study investigated the role of TRPV1, a non-selective cation channel with high calcium permeability and tumor-suppressive properties in gastrointestinal cancers, in GC progression. The findings underscored the crucial role of TRPV1 SUMOylation, facilitated by SUMO1, in inhibiting GC progression by enhancing TRPV1’s membrane localization and activating the TRPV1-Ca2⁺-AMPK signaling pathway. SUMO1 conjugation at lysine 823 of TRPV1 reduces its interaction with the adaptor protein AP2M1, thereby limiting TRPV1 endocytosis and increasing its membrane presence. This enhanced localization promotes Ca2⁺ influx and AMPK pathway activation, significantly suppressing GC cell proliferation and migration. Conversely, mutation of the SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation site (K823R) disrupts these tumor-suppressive effects, leading to increased tumor growth and aggressiveness both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, an engineered TRPV1 variant with a bidirectional SUMO motif (EψKψE) enhances SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation, further strengthening its inhibitory impact on GC progression. From a therapeutic perspective, the TAT-AP2M1-176–185 peptide interferes with the TRPV1-AP2M1 interaction, boosting TRPV1 membrane localization and AMPK activation, ultimately reducing GC growth and metastasis in xenograft models (Yang et al. 2024). Likewise, Specificity protein 1 (SP1) is a transcription factor that regulates gene expression, critical for cell growth, differentiation, and survival. In GC, SP1 expression is notably elevated, especially in drug-resistant cases, and correlates with poor patient outcomes. Low SUMOylation levels of SP1, mediated by SUMO1, lead to increased SP1 expression and activity. This upregulation promotes the transcription of SNHG17, a tumor-promoting long non-coding RNA (lncRNA). SNHG17 acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), binding to miR-23b-3p and preventing it from inhibiting Notch2, a gene that drives tumor progression and chemoresistance. Functional experiments demonstrated that SP1 depletion reduces SNHG17 levels, enhances miR-23b-3p activity, and inhibits Notch2, restoring cisplatin sensitivity in resistant GC cells. In vivo, SP1 knockdown decreased tumor growth and proliferation markers, confirming its role in GC progression and chemoresistance. These findings suggest that targeting the SP1/SNHG17/miR-23b-3p/Notch2 axis could provide novel therapeutic strategies for overcoming chemoresistance in GC (Huang et al. 2022). Similarly, SUMO1 facilitates the SUMOylation of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNP K), a process essential for stabilizing hnRNP K and enhancing its interaction with β-catenin in GC. However, SDCBP2-AS1 disrupts this mechanism by binding to the KH3 domain of hnRNP K, thereby obstructing its primary SUMOylation site (K422). By inhibiting SUMO1-mediated SUMOylation, SDCBP2-AS1 promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of hnRNP K (Fig. 2). As a result, the destabilized hnRNP K has a diminished capacity to interact with β-catenin, leading to increased ubiquitination and degradation of β-catenin itself. The decline in β-catenin levels prevents its nuclear translocation and the activation of oncogenic transcriptional targets, including MYC, CCND1, and MMP7, ultimately suppressing gastric tumorigenesis and metastasis (Han et al. 2022). In gastric cancer cells (BGC-823), treatment with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) enhances the SUMOylation of RARα, a modification that increases its stability, prolongs its half-life, and maintains its nuclear localization. This SUMO-1-mediated stabilization facilitates the heterodimerization of RARα with retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα), enhancing the transcriptional activity of the complex. SUMO-2/3

SUMO-2/3 is pivotal in gastric cancer progression by regulating the stability and subcellular localization of key oncogenic proteins, including NSUN2. Through a specific SUMO-interaction motif (SIM) in the 236–240 amino acid region, SUMO-2/3 directly interacts with NSUN2, promoting its SUMOylation. This post-translational modification stabilizes NSUN2 and promotes its nuclear translocation, where it exerts its biological effects. SUMOylation also prevents NSUN2 from accumulating in the cytoplasm, ensuring its proper localization for catalyzing m5C RNA methylation (Hu et al. 2021). Similarly, SUMO-2/3 plays a crucial role in gastric cancer by regulating the subcellular localization and stability of Death Domain-Associated Protein (DAXX), a multifunctional protein involved in tumorigenesis. Through SUMOylation, SUMO-2/3 promotes the nuclear translocation of DAXX, altering its biological function. Elevated nuclear DAXX (nDAXX) expression is linked to poorer prognosis, driving cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In contrast, cytoplasmic DAXX (cDAXX), which remains stable in the absence of SUMO-2/3 interaction, exhibits tumor-suppressive properties, including reduced proliferation and increased apoptosis (Fig. 2) (Chen et al. 2020b). SUMOylation of hnRNPM by SUMO2 plays a pivotal role in its splicing regulatory activity and response to stress in gastric cancer cells. Under heat-shock conditions, hnRNPM's SUMOylation is enhanced, facilitating its relocalization from spliceosome components to the nuclear matrix, which corresponds to a splicing-inactive state. IQGAP1 is critical for this process, as its absence results in aberrant hnRNPM SUMOylation and localization patterns, mimicking the effects of heat-shock even under normal conditions. HnRNPM’s proper SUMOylation is essential for its alternative splicing activity, as inhibition of SUMOylation with the SUMO inhibitor 2-D08 disrupts hnRNPM's subnuclear relocalization and its ability to regulate AS events. These findings establish SUMOylation as a key regulatory mechanism for hnRNPM's function in splicing and stress response in gastric cancer cells, with IQGAP1 serving as a central mediator of this process (Fig. 2) (Birladeanu et al. 2021).

SUMO-specific proteases

SENP2

SENP2, a member of the SUMO protease family, plays a crucial role in regulating SUMOylation. Its silencing disrupts SUMOylation pathways, leading to alterations in cellular processes essential for GC progression. Downregulation of SENP2 decreases the expression of key oncogenic markers such as β-catenin, Cyclin D1, and Bcl-2, while simultaneously inducing G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest and promoting apoptosis in GC SGC-7901 cells. SENP3

SENP3 enhances Sp1 protein levels by de-conjugating SUMO2/3 chains, thus preventing Sp1 from being targeted for degradation by RNF4 via the ubiquitin–proteasome system in GC. This regulation appears to be particularly significant in cancer cells, where both SENP3 and Sp1 levels are increased, suggesting a mechanism that could contribute to the overaccumulation of Sp1 in cancerous tissues. In addition, oxidative stress, such as exposure to hydrogen peroxide, can upregulate SENP3 and subsequently increase Sp1 levels in gastric cancer cells, further emphasizing the role of SENP3 in regulating Sp1 stability under stress and in tumors (Wang et al. 2016a). SENP3 is shown to enhance the transcriptional activity of the EMT-inducing factor FOXC2 by de-SUMOylating it at lysine 214, enabling FOXC2 to upregulate mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin. SENP3 expression correlates with enhanced cell migration, invasion, and metastasis in both in vitro gastric cancer models and in vivo mouse models. Elevated SENP3 levels are associated with increased ROS, linking oxidative stress to EMT progression. Additionally, human gastric cancer specimens reveal a strong association between SENP3 expression and lymph node metastasis. These findings establish SENP3 as a key mediator of ROS-induced EMT, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to curb metastasis in gastric cancer (Fig. 3) (Ren et al. 2014).

SENP5

SENP5 has been identified as a key oncogene in gastric cancer, with its elevated expression closely associated with tumorigenesis and metastasis. Its transcription is regulated through the formation of a DNA-lncRNA triplex between the SENP5 promoter and the GAU1 lncRNA, a process guided by peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA). This triplex structure facilitates the recruitment of the histone methyltransferase SET1A to the SENP5 promoter, enhancing H3K4 trimethylation and promoting SENP5 transcriptional activation. Silencing SENP5 significantly reduces cell proliferation, colony formation, and metastatic potential in vitro, while also suppressing tumor growth and systemic metastasis in vivo. Furthermore, high SENP5 expression correlates with poor prognosis in gastric cancer patients, underscoring its pivotal role in tumor progression (Fig. 3) (Zhang et al. 2024).

SUMO E ligase proteins

SUMO E1s

SAE1 plays a crucial role in GC by regulating the SUMOylation process, which is vital for various cellular functions, including the DNA damage response. In GC, SAE1 enhances the SUMOylation of XRCC4, a key component of the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) DNA repair pathway, ensuring its proper nuclear localization and functionality in repairing DNA double-strand breaks. Elevated SAE1 expression supports tumor survival by maintaining genomic stability and enabling efficient DNA damage repair in cancer cells. Targeting SAE1, particularly through miR-129-3p-mediated inhibition, disrupts SUMOylation, impairs DNA repair, increases DNA damage, and ultimately suppresses GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis. These findings underscore SAE1’s oncogenic role in gastric cancer and its potential as a therapeutic target (Zhang et al. 2019). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a crucial role in upregulating SAE1, a key component of the SUMOylation pathway, during Helicobacter pylori infection, thereby driving gastric tumorigenesis and progression. H. pylori, particularly through its virulence factor CagA, induces chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in gastric tissues, leading to excessive ROS production. These ROS contribute to increased expression of SAE1 and other SUMOylation-related proteins (SUMO1 and SUMO2/3), enhancing the modification of key cellular substrates involved in oncogenic signaling. Elevated SAE1 levels facilitate cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by promoting EMT, a critical process in cancer metastasis. Conversely, silencing SAE1 disrupts these oncogenic processes and downregulates factors such as IGF-1, suppressing tumor progression. This ROS-mediated upregulation of SAE1 fosters a tumor-promoting microenvironment, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in gastric cancer associated with H. pylori infection (Fig. 3) (Shi et al. 2024).

SUMO E2

In gastric cancer, UBC9 expression has been identified as a significant biomarker influencing patient outcomes, particularly in those receiving first-line FOLFOX chemotherapy. Patients with low UBC9 expression had a median survival of 12.4 months, compared to 8.8 months in those with high UBC9 levels (P = 0.01). As an E2 enzyme in the SUMOylation pathway, UBC9 plays a crucial role in the DNA damage response, including the regulation of BRCA1 function and the recruitment of 53BP1 to DNA damage sites. Dysregulation of UBC9 can compromise DNA repair efficiency, leading to poorer survival outcomes. While UBC9 did not show a significant impact on second-line chemotherapy response, its involvement in DNA repair underscores its potential as a predictive biomarker and a target for optimizing treatment strategies in gastric cancer (Fig. 3) (Wei et al. 2014).

SUMO E3 ligases

PIAS family

PIAS1 is significantly downregulated in gastric cancer, and its overexpression inhibits EMT, a key driver of metastasis. By suppressing the IL-6-induced activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, PIAS1 reduces the expression of EMT markers such as Snail, Twist1, and vimentin, while increasing E-cadherin levels, restoring epithelial characteristics. Functionally, PIAS1 overexpression decreases cell migration, invasion, and viability in an inflammatory microenvironment. Additionally, PIAS1 downregulates MMP-9, further limiting metastatic potential. These findings establish PIAS1 as a crucial inhibitor of EMT and a promising therapeutic target for reducing metastasis and improving outcomes in gastric cancer (Wang et al. 2018). In gastric cancer, PIASxα acts as a critical SUMO E3 ligase that facilitates the SUMOylation of p38α at lysine 152 (K152), a modification essential for p38α’s nuclear localization and interaction with MK2. This SUMOylation promotes the generation of ROS via MK2 activation, creating a positive feedback loop where ROS stabilize PIASxα, further enhancing p38α SUMOylation and ROS production. Clinically, PIASxα expression is significantly elevated in gastric cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, and higher PIASxα levels are associated with poor survival outcomes. Targeting PIASxα-mediated SUMOylation could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy to disrupt this feedback loop, suppress ROS-driven tumor survival, and impede cancer metastasis (Wang et al. 2021b). Likewise, in gastric cancer, PIAS2β was identified as a critical mitotic protein through its interaction with the spindle and centrosomal machinery. Silencing PIAS2β using dsRNAi in HGC-27 cells, a model of undifferentiated gastric carcinoma, led to significant cell growth inhibition and cell death, highlighting its essential role in mitotic processes. Mechanistically, PIAS2β is involved in SUMOylation of key mitotic proteins, including PSMC5 (a proteasome subunit) and TUBB3 (a tubulin protein), which are necessary for proper spindle assembly and proteasome regulation during cell division. The loss of PIAS2β disrupted these processes, causing mitotic catastrophe characterized by centrosome disorganization, untimely proteasome activation, and failure in chromosome-microtubule attachment (Rodrigues et al. 2024). In addition, PIAS3 expression is markedly reduced in gastric cancer tissues and cell lines compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues and normal gastric mucosa cells. Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed this reduction, revealing that only 16.36% of gastric cancer tissues exhibited positive cytoplasmic PIAS3 staining (++/+++), whereas 58.18% of non-tumor tissues displayed similar staining patterns. This decline in PIAS3 expression was associated with heightened STAT3 activity, a crucial driver of tumor progression and metastasis. Survival analysis identified PIAS3 expression and lymph node metastasis as independent prognostic indicators for overall survival, with higher PIAS3 levels correlating with better patient outcomes. These findings indicate that PIAS3 downregulation plays a significant role in gastric cancer advancement and poor prognosis, highlighting its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in gastric cancer (Li et al. 2015). In more detail, PIAS3 expression is markedly reduced at both the protein and mRNA levels in gastric carcinoma tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. This loss of PIAS3 is strongly correlated with larger tumor size and poorer differentiation, although it does not appear to be linked to patient age, gender, or lymphatic metastasis. PIAS3 is primarily localized in the nucleus and cytoplasm of gastric mucosal cells, where it functions as a key inhibitor of STAT3, a transcription factor known to drive cancer progression. Its diminished expression in gastric cancer likely results in unchecked STAT3 activity, fostering tumor growth and malignancy. Beyond its role in STAT3 inhibition, PIAS3 is also involved in the regulation of transcription factors and protein degradation pathways, including ubiquitination, further emphasizing its tumor-suppressive function in gastric cancer (Liu et al. 2011). miR-18a, a key oncogenic microRNA, is significantly upregulated in gastric adenocarcinoma (GAC) tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues. miR-18a directly targets and suppresses PIAS3, a negative regulator of STAT3, resulting in enhanced STAT3 activity. This activation promotes the expression of anti-apoptotic (Bcl-xL, Survivin) and proliferative (c-Myc) genes, contributing to tumor growth and survival. The findings establish a mechanistic link between miR-18a overexpression and STAT3-mediated oncogenic signaling in GAC. Therapeutically, targeting miR-18a or restoring PIAS3 function offers potential strategies to counteract GAC progression. These insights underscore the pivotal role of miR-18a in gastric adenocarcinogenesis through modulation of the STAT3 pathway (Wu et al. 2013). PIAS4 plays a pivotal role in GC by mediating the SUMOylation of lysine-specific demethylase 5B (KDM5B) under hypoxic conditions. This post-translational modification stabilizes KDM5B by preventing ubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation, thereby enabling GC cells to adapt to hypoxia. PIAS4 directly interacts with KDM5B in the nucleus, and its SUMO E3 ligase activity is essential for the hypoxia-induced SUMOylation of KDM5B. Loss of PIAS4 results in reduced KDM5B protein stability, increased ubiquitination, impaired cell viability, reduced colony formation, and elevated apoptosis in hypoxic GC cells. Furthermore, PIAS4 knockdown diminishes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) protein levels and its downstream target genes, such as VEGFA and GLUT1, underscoring its importance in hypoxia adaptation. By regulating KDM5B and indirectly promoting HIF-1α signaling, PIAS4 supports tumor growth and survival in hypoxic environments, making it a potential therapeutic target for disrupting hypoxia adaptation in GC (Fig. 3) (Zhou et al. 2021).

CBX4

Chromobox4 (CBX4), a SUMO E3 ligase, is significantly overexpressed in GC tissues at both mRNA and protein levels compared to normal tissues, correlating with higher tumor grade, advanced cancer stages, and shorter overall survival (OS). Its elevated expression is linked to poor prognosis, making it a potential prognostic biomarker. Functional studies reveal that silencing CBX4 inhibits cell proliferation, induces G1 cell cycle arrest, and promotes apoptosis in GC cells. Pathway enrichment analyses associate CBX4 with critical cancer-related processes, including stem cell pluripotency, Hippo signaling, Notch signaling, and N-glycan biosynthesis. CBX4 interacts with BMI-1, another oncogene, and both are co-expressed in GC tissues, potentially contributing to tumor progression. Additionally, CBX4 expression negatively correlates with the infiltration of immune cells such as B cells and macrophages, suggesting its role in immune modulation. With a 9% genetic alteration rate in GC samples, primarily involving enhanced mRNA expression, CBX4 emerges as a promising target for therapeutic intervention and personalized cancer management (Pan et al. 2021). There is a significant association between the CBX4 rs77447679 polymorphism and the risk of GC in a Chinese population. Individuals carrying the CA genotype exhibit a 1.71-fold increased risk of GC compared to those with the CC genotype, which appears protective (P = 0.018; OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.10–2.66). The C to A substitution likely increases CBX4 expression, contributing to GC development. As a SUMO E3 ligase and a component of the polycomb repressive complex 1, CBX4 regulates gene expression and tumorigenesis, implicating this polymorphism as a potential genetic marker for GC susceptibility. Further research is required to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms (Luo et al. 2017). In GC, CBX4 plays a pivotal role in driving tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy. Its expression is significantly elevated in gastric cancer tissues and is strongly associated with poor patient prognosis. CBX4 enhances cancer cell proliferation, suppresses apoptosis, and prevents low-dose cisplatin (CDDP)-induced cellular senescence by inhibiting key senescence-associated pathways. At the molecular level, CBX4 promotes the stability and nuclear localization of YAP1, a critical effector of the Hippo signaling pathway, by mediating its SUMOylation at lysine residues K97 and K280. This modification competitively blocks YAP1 phosphorylation at serine 127 (S127), preventing its proteasomal degradation and cytoplasmic sequestration, thereby sustaining its oncogenic activity in the nucleus. CBX4 expression is controlled by the transcription factor CEBPB, which is downregulated under low-dose CDDP treatment, leading to decreased CBX4 levels and enhanced cellular senescence. Furthermore, inhibiting CBX4 sensitizes gastric cancer cells to chemotherapy, whereas its overexpression contributes to drug resistance. Targeting CBX4 with inhibitors such as UNC3866, in combination with low-dose CDDP, effectively induces cellular senescence, reverses chemoresistance, and suppresses tumor growth in gastric cancer models, underscoring its potential as a promising therapeutic target (Fig. 3) (Gu et al. 2024).

TRIM27

Tuftelin (TUFT1) has a critical role in gastric cancer progression by driving tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through activation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Its expression is significantly elevated in gastric cancer and is linked to poor clinical outcomes, including advanced tumor stages, increased lymph node metastasis, and reduced overall survival. The oncogenic activity of TUFT1 is dependent on its SUMOylation at lysine 79 (K79), a modification catalyzed by the E3 SUMO ligase TRIM27. This SUMOylated form of TUFT1 acts as an upstream regulator of AKT activation and directly stimulates mTOR signaling by interacting with Rab GTPase-activating protein 1 (RABGAP1), facilitating the perinuclear accumulation of the mTORC1 complex. The loss of SUMOylation significantly diminishes TUFT1’s ability to promote malignancy and AKT/mTOR signaling. These findings establish TUFT1 as a key driver of gastric cancer progression and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target or biomarker for disease management (Fig. 3) (Wang et al. 2023).

TRIM28

As a SUMOylation E3 ligase, TRIM28 is vital in promoting GC progression by stabilizing PD-L1, a key immune checkpoint protein, thereby enabling immune evasion. TRIM28 interacts directly with PD-L1 and enhances its SUMOylation, which prevents its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation via the proteasome pathway. This stabilization prolongs PD-L1's half-life and sustains its expression on the cell surface, leading to reduced activation and infiltration of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, TRIM28 activates the TBK1-IRF1 and TBK1-mTOR pathways through K63-linked polyubiquitination of TBK1, further upregulating PD-L1 transcription. This dual regulatory effect at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels contributes to tumor growth, immune suppression, and poor prognosis in GC patients, positioning TRIM28 as a critical driver of GC progression and a potential therapeutic target (Fig. 3) (Ma et al. 2023).

Other regulators of SUMOylation

K5

Plasminogen kringle 5 (K5) exerts its tumor-suppressive effects in gastric cancer through a dual mechanism that targets both angiogenesis and apoptosis resistance. It inhibits tumor angiogenesis by promoting the SUMOylation and ubiquitination of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), which enhances its degradation via the von Hippel-Lindau protein (VHL). This degradation reduces the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a key driver of tumor-associated blood vessel formation. Concurrently, K5 suppresses apoptosis resistance by downregulating glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), a crucial anti-apoptotic factor, through inhibition of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK) phosphorylation. This suppression triggers caspase-7 activation, leading to programmed cell death in gastric cancer cells. Targeting SUMOylation in gastric cancer

Targeting SUMOylation is promising in cancer therapy due to its central role in regulating cell cycle progression, genome stability, and gene expression, processes often dysregulated in cancer. Inhibiting SUMOylation disrupts cancer cell proliferation, particularly by inducing mitotic defects and chromosomal instability, while simultaneously enhancing antitumor immune responses by activating interferon signaling. This dual mode of action, directly targeting cancer cell survival and reactivating immune responses, makes SUMOylation inhibitors highly effective. Furthermore, novel small molecule inhibitors like TAK-981 and ML-792 show selective activity against the SUMO pathway with minimal effects on related pathways, reducing potential off-target toxicity. Their clinical potential is bolstered by emerging evidence of synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors and cell cycle-targeting drugs, positioning SUMO inhibition as a versatile and powerful strategy in cancer therapy (Kukkula et al. 2021; Du et al. 2021; Gu et al. 2023).

Natural products

Natural products have emerged as a significant source of SUMOylation inhibitors, targeting various components of the SUMOylation pathway to impede cancer progression. Compounds like ginkgolic acid, davidiin, and tannic acid inhibit SUMO-E1 by blocking the formation of the E1-SUMO complex, demonstrating selective inhibition of SUMOylation over ubiquitinylation. Other natural products such as spectomycin B1 target SUMO-E2 (Ubc9), reducing cancer cell proliferation and hormone receptor activity in hormone-dependent cancers. SENPs, the deSUMOylating enzymes, are inhibited by natural products like momordin Ic and streptonigrin, which promote apoptosis and reduce tumor growth by destabilizing oncogenic proteins like HIF-1α. Additionally, redox-active natural products like β-lapachone generate ROS to disrupt SUMOylation through enzyme crosslinking. These compounds provide valuable scaffolds for drug development, although challenges like off-target effects and in vivo efficacy remain significant [Reviewed by (Brackett and Blagg 2021)].

Ginkgoic acid

Ginkgolic acid (GA), a natural compound derived from the Ginkgo biloba plant, exhibits significant anti-cancer properties (Liang and Yang 2020). It impedes GC cell proliferation, migration, and EMT by inhibiting the SUMOylation of the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R). GA blocks the binding of SUMO1 to IGF-1R, preventing its nuclear accumulation, a process critical for activating transcriptional programs that promote cancer progression. Specifically, nuclear IGF-1R directly binds to the promoter of SNAI2, a key EMT regulator, enhancing its transcription. By inhibiting IGF-1R SUMOylation, GA reduces nuclear IGF-1R levels, thereby downregulating SNAI2 expression and suppressing EMT-related markers like Vimentin and MMP-2 while upregulating E-cadherin. This mechanism disrupts GC cell invasion and tumor progression, both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting GA’s potential as a therapeutic agent targeting SUMOylation-dependent pathways in GC (Liu et al. 2021b).

Tanshinone IIA

Tanshinone IIA, a natural compound derived from the medicinal herb Salvia miltiorrhiza, counteracts cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer by regulating SLC7A11 expression through multiple mechanisms. In cisplatin-resistant gastric cancer cells, SLC7A11 protein expression is upregulated, driving resistance. Tanshinone IIA reduces SLC7A11 protein levels without affecting its mRNA expression, indicating regulation at the post-transcriptional level. This suppression occurs via the inhibition of PIAS4-mediated SUMOylation, a modification that stabilizes SLC7A11 protein and enhances its role in resistance. By decreasing SUMOylation, Tanshinone IIA destabilizes SLC7A11, restoring cisplatin sensitivity. This effect results in increased oxidative stress, enhanced apoptosis through elevated caspase-3 activity, and reduced cancer cell survival, demonstrating its potential as a therapeutic agent for overcoming cisplatin resistance (Qin et al. 2024).

Melatonin

Melatonin (MLT), a multifunctional hormone primarily produced by the pineal gland, is well known for its role in regulating circadian rhythms and exhibiting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor effects. In gastric cancer, MLT suppresses tumor growth and angiogenesis by targeting the nuclear receptor RZR/RORγ, which is abnormally overexpressed in tumor tissues. By downregulating RZR/RORγ, MLT disrupts the tumor’s adaptation to hypoxia by reducing the expression of SENP1, a key regulator of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) stability and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion. The suppression of SENP1 activity prevents HIF-1α stabilization and its transcriptional activation, thereby impairing tumor-associated angiogenesis and growth in both in vitro and in vivo models. These findings suggest that MLT may serve as a promising therapeutic agent in gastric cancer by targeting the RZR/RORγ-SENP1-HIF-1α-VEGF signaling axis (Wang et al. 2016b).

Synthetic lethal lead

AL-GDa62 is a synthetic lethal compound identified as a promising therapeutic lead for targeting E-cadherin-deficient gastric cancer. It was developed through the optimization of a quinoline-based compound, demonstrating selective cytotoxicity in CDH1-deficient cells compared to wild-type cells. AL-GDa62 exerts its effect by inhibiting key proteins such as TCOF1, ARPC5, and UBC9, as identified through thermal proteome profiling. Specifically, it inhibits the SUMOylation process, a post-translational modification critical for protein stability, localization, and activity. Conclusion, limitations, and future perspectives

SUMOylation plays a crucial role in gastric cancer pathogenesis by regulating key cellular processes such as DNA repair, transcriptional regulation, protein stability, and signal transduction. This post-translational modification serves as a molecular switch that modulates the activity of various oncogenes and tumor suppressors, significantly impacting tumor progression. In GC, SUMOylation is involved in maintaining genomic stability, evading apoptosis, promoting EMT, and fostering chemoresistance, making it a key factor in tumor development and progression. SUMO regulators, including SUMO proteins, SUMO E1/E2/E3 ligases, and SUMO-specific proteases (SENPs), play a significant role in GC by modulating the SUMOylation status of key proteins. SUMOylation influences both oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways: oncogenes such as SP1, hnRNP K, and NSUN2 are stabilized through SUMOylation, promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis, whereas tumor suppressors such as C/EBPα are degraded upon SUMOylation, further driving carcinogenesis. The classification of SUMO-regulated proteins in GC highlights that while SUMOylation can enhance oncogenic signaling by preventing proteasomal degradation of oncogenic proteins, it can also repress tumor suppressors by facilitating their degradation, thereby accelerating tumor progression. Furthermore, SUMOylation is tightly linked to stress responses in GC cells. Heat shock and oxidative stress, particularly induced by Helicobacter pylori infection, create a positive feedback loop in which SUMOylation enhances stress-induced survival mechanisms. This SUMOylation-driven feedback loop stabilizes oncogenic proteins, promotes inflammatory signaling, and aids in DNA repair, allowing cancer cells to withstand oxidative damage and evade cell death. Additionally, SUMOylation enhances DNA repair mechanisms in GC cells, protecting them from genotoxic stress and preventing the accumulation of DNA damage, which otherwise could trigger apoptosis. This protective role further strengthens the survival and proliferation of GC cells, contributing to therapy resistance. Despite the growing evidence of SUMOylation’s impact on GC, significant gaps remain in our understanding of how SUMO regulators precisely contribute to tumor biology. A major limitation is the lack of comprehensive studies evaluating the therapeutic potential of targeting SUMOylation in GC. While a few preclinical studies have explored SUMO inhibitors, most have focused on natural compounds such as ginkgolic acid and tanshinone IIA. There is a need for more extensive research, including the discovery of new natural compounds, development of synthetic SUMO-targeting drugs, and exploration of nanoparticle-based delivery systems for targeted SUMO inhibition in GC. Additionally, the function of SUMO regulators, particularly SENPs, remains largely unexplored. Given their role in reversing SUMOylation and regulating protein stability, further investigation into SENPs could reveal novel insights into GC progression and potential therapeutic targets. Future studies should aim to uncover the precise mechanisms by which SUMOylation interacts with oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways in GC, paving the way for novel targeted therapies. In conclusion, SUMOylation is a key regulatory mechanism in GC that promotes tumor survival, progression, and therapy resistance. While targeting SUMOylation presents a promising therapeutic strategy, further research is essential to fully understand its molecular landscape and develop effective interventions. Expanding studies on SUMO-targeting drugs and unraveling the role of SUMO regulators in GC will be critical for advancing precision medicine in gastric cancer treatment.
