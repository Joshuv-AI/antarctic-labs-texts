Bounded cohomological induction for transverse measured groupoids
Tobias Hartnick, Filippo Sarti

Abstract
. We establish an induction isomorphism in the context of measurable bounded cohomology of discrete measured groupoid, which generalizes the Eckmann-Shapiro isomorphism in bounded cohomology of lattices due to Burger and Monod. In our wider setting, the role of lattices is taken by the class of transverse measured groupoids ( 𝒢 , ν ) (\mathcal{G},\nu) associated with a cross-section Y Y in a pmp dynamical system ( X , μ ) (X,\mu) of a lcsc group G G such that the associated hitting time process of Y Y is locally integrable. Typical examples are given by pattern groupoids of strong approximate lattices. Under the assumptions that G G is unimodular we show that the measurable bounded cohomology of ( 𝒢 , ν ) (\mathcal{G},\nu) is isomorphic to the continuous bounded cohomology of G G with coefficients in L ∞ ​ ( X , μ ) \mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu) . As a consequence, if G G is amenable, then ( 𝒢 , ν ) (\mathcal{G},\nu) is boundedly acyclic, and in general the restriction map H cb ∙ ​ ( G , ℝ ) → H mb ∙ ​ ( ( 𝒢 , ν ) , ℝ ¯ ) \textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}}) is injective. Moreover, it follows from known results in continuous bounded cohomology that if G G is a semisimple higher rank Lie group of Hermitian (respectively complex classical) type, then the second (respectively third) measurable bounded cohomology of ( 𝒢 , ν ) (\mathcal{G},\nu) is generated by the restriction of the bounded Kähler class (respectively bounded Borel class). These are the first explicit computations of non-trivial bounded cohomology groups of measured groupoids which are not isomorphic to an action groupoid.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Bounded cohomological induction and rigidity

The interplay between locally compact groups and their lattices plays a central role in modern rigidity theory. Many properties of locally compact groups are inherited by their lattices, and many properties of discrete groups are inherited by their lattice envelopes, sometimes under additional cocompactness or integrability assumptions. For example, in geometric group theory the quasi-isometry type of a locally compact group is inherited by cocompact lattices, and many analytic properties of topological groups like amenability and Property (T) hold for a lattice in a locally compact group if and only if they hold for the ambient group. On the level of group cohomology (with trivial real coefficients, say), one can show that the continuous cohomology of a locally compact group injects into the cohomology of any of its
uniform
lattices; this is a consequence of the classical Eckmann–Shapiro induction isomorphism in cohomology and does in general not hold for
non-uniform lattices. Similar, but stronger results hold also in bounded cohomology; since these results can be seen as the starting point of the present investigation, we will describe them in more detail.

Firstly, if
\Gamma
is a lattice in a locally compact second countable (lcsc) group
G
, then by work of Burger and Monod
[
BM99
]
there is an isometric induction isomorphism (a la Eckmann-Shapiro)

(1)

\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R})\cong\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(G/\Gamma)),

where the left-hand side denotes bounded cohomology of
\Gamma
with trivial real coefficients and the right-hand side denotes continuous bounded cohomology of
G
with coefficients in the Banach
G
-module
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(G/\Gamma)
. This implies, secondly, that the natural restriction map
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R})
is injective. Finally, stronger results can be established for specific classes of groups. For example, it was showed by Monod
[
Mon10
]
that for semisimple Lie groups the restriction map happens to be an isomorphism within a certain range. In order to prove the induction isomorphism one makes use of the identification of continuous bounded cohomology with
measurable
bounded cohomology, and indeed (
1
) is better understood as a statement about
measurable
bounded cohomology of locally compact groups.

Bounded cohomological induction is a central tool in establishing rigidity results for lattices in Lie groups; for example, it can be used to prove Mostow rigidity (via a dual version of the classical Gromov-Thurston argument
[
BBI13
]
). Modern applications of bounded cohomological induction to rigidity theory started from the Burger–Monod proof of triviality of higher rank lattice actions on the circle
[
BM99
]
and include applications to Kähler rigidity
[
BI04
]
and Hermitian higher Teichmüller theory
[
BIW14
]
.

The present article is concerned with a generalization of Burger-Monod-Eckmann-Shapiro induction in the context of measurable bounded cohomology of discrete measured groupoid. In this wider context, the homogeneous dynamical system
G\curvearrowright G/\Gamma
will be replaced by a general non-homogeneous pmp system, and the role of lattices will be taken by a class of discrete measured groupoids known as
transverse measured groupoid
associated with certain cross sections
Y\subset X
.

1.2.
Measurable bounded cohomology of discrete measured groupoids

In
[
SSa
,
SSb
]
, Alessio Savini and the second named author introduced a version of measurable bounded cohomology which applies to measured groupoids. We briefly recall the definition; see Section
2
below for general background on measured groupoids. Given a discrete measured groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
with target map
t
we define a sequence of Borel spaces
\mathcal{G}^{[n]}
by

\mathcal{G}^{[n]}\coloneqq\{(g_{0},\dots,g_{n-1})\in(\mathcal{G}^{(1)})^{n}\mid t(g_{0})=\dots=t(g_{n-1})\}.

This projects to
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
with countable fibers via the map
(g_{0},\dots,g_{n-1})\mapsto t(g_{0})
, and hence admits a unique
\sigma
-finite Borel measure which projects to
\nu
and has fiber counting measures; this allows us to define a family of Banach spaces
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n]})
.

We say that a function class
[f]\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n+1]})
is
\mathcal{G}
-invariant
if for almost all
(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathcal{G}^{[n+1]}
and all
g\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
with
t(g_{0})=t(g)
we have

f(g^{-1}g_{0},\dots,g^{-1}g_{n})=f(g_{0},\dots,g_{n}).

This defines a subspace
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n]})^{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n]})
, and we have well-defined differentials

\displaystyle d^{n}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n+1]})^{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[n+2]})^{\mathcal{G}},

\displaystyle d^{n}f(g_{0},\dots,g_{n+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}(-1)^{j}f(g_{0},\dots,\widehat{g_{j}},\dots,g_{n}).

Following
[
SSa
]
we then define the
measurable bounded cohomology
of
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
with coefficients in the trivial line bundle
\underline{\mathbb{R}}
over
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
as

\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})\coloneqq\textup{H}^{\bullet}(0\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[1]})^{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[2]})^{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}^{[3]})^{\mathcal{G}}\to\dots).

1.3.
Transverse measured groupoids

Our first task is to identify a suitable replacement for lattices in the world of discrete measured groupoids; we take inspiration from the theory of approximate lattices
[
BH16
]
, and more precisely the dynamical approach via transverse systems as considered in
[
BHK25
,
BH25
,
ARBC25
]
.

Consider a probability-measure preserving (pmp) action
G\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
of a unimodular lcsc group on a standard probability space and fix a Haar measure
m_{G}
for
G
. We then obtain a Borel action groupoid
G\ltimes X
which admits an invariant measure
\mu
for the Haar system defined by
m_{G}
.

We say that a Borel subset
Y\subset X
is a
cross section
if
GY=X
and for every
x\in GY
the
hitting time set

Y_{x}\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid gx\in Y\}
is non-empty and locally finite. In this case, the restriction Borel groupoid
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq(G\ltimes X)|_{Y}
has countable
t
-fibers, and the invariant probability measure
\mu
on
X
induces an invariant
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\nu
for
\mathcal{G}
, variously known as the
transverse measure
or
Palm measure
of
(X,\mu,Y)
(see
[
ARBC25
]
). We are going to assume that
\nu
is
finite
, which amounts to local integrability of the point process
x\mapsto Y_{x}
; we then refer to
(X,\mu,Y)
as an
integrable transverse
G
-system
and to the discrete measured groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
as the associated
transverse measured groupoid
.

It turns out that transverse measured groupoids generalize lattices. Indeed, if
\Gamma
is a lattice in a lcsc group
G
, then
G
acts on
X\coloneqq G/\Gamma
fixing a unique probability measure
\mu
on
X
, and it we set
Y\coloneqq\{\Gamma\}
, then
(X,\mu,Y)
is an integrable transverse
G
-system. The associated transverse measured groupoid has a single object
\{e\Gamma\}
with automorphism group
\Gamma
and can hence be identified with
\Gamma
; the transverse measure is the point measure at
\Gamma
of total mass given by the covolume of
\Gamma
. By definition, the measurable bounded cohomology of
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
is then just the bounded cohomology of
\Gamma
.

1.4.
Bounded cohomological induction for transverse measured groupoids

From now on, let
G
denote a unimodular lcsc group with Haar measure
m_{G}
. The main result of this article is the following version of Burger–Monod–Eckmann–Shapiro induction:

Theorem 1
.

If
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
denotes the transverse measured groupoid of an integrable transverse
G
-system
(X,\mu,Y)
, then there is an isometric isomorphism

\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})\cong\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)).

For the remainder of this subsection,
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
denotes the transverse measured groupoid of an integrable transverse
G
-system
(X,\mu,Y)
.
We spell out a few basic consequences of Theorem
1
; the most basic consequence is as follows:

Corollary 2
.

If
G
is amenable, then
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
is boundedly acyclic, i.e.
\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})
vanishes for all
k>0
.

To describe the consequences of Theorem
1
beyond the amenable case, we introduce the following notation. Let
p
be an admissible probability measure on
G
; then every class in
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{n}(G;\mathbb{R})
can be represented by
a
G
-invariant continuous bounded
n
-cocycle
c:G^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is harmonic with respect to
p^{\otimes n}
. It turns out that

\mathrm{res}(c):\mathcal{G}^{[n+1]}\to\mathbb{R},\quad((g_{0},y_{0}),\dots,(g_{n},y_{n}))\mapsto c(g_{0},\dots,g_{n}).

defines a
\mathcal{G}
-invariant bounded measurable
n
-cocycle for
\mathcal{G}
and that there is a well-defined restriction map

\mathrm{res}^{\bullet}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}}),\quad[c]\mapsto[\mathrm{res}(c)].

If we denote by
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0}\subset\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)
the subspace of functions of integral
0
, then we obtain:

Corollary 3
.

The restriction map
\mathrm{res}^{\bullet}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})
is injective with cokernel isomorphic to
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0})
and does not increase seminorms.

If
G
is a connected simple Lie group, then we can apply the results of Monod from
[
Mon10
]
. Indeed, the module
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0}
is a semi-separable coefficient
G
-module, and if
\mu
happens to be ergodic, then it does not admit any invariant vectors. By the aforementioned work of Monod, this implies:

Corollary 4

(Monod)
.

If
G
is a connected simple Lie group and
\mu
is
G
-ergodic, then for all
0\leq k<2\cdot\mathrm{rk}_{\mathbb{R}}(G)
the restriction map
\mathrm{res}^{k}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})
is an isometric isomorphism.

In the setting of Corollary
4
, it is conjectured that the continuous bounded cohomology
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})
is isomorphic to the corresponding continuous cohomology; currently this is only known in low degrees. More precisely, by results of Burger and Monod
[
BM99
]

the space
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{2}(G;\mathbb{R})
is non-trivial if and only if
G
is Hermitian, in which case it is one-dimensional, generated by the bounded Kähler class
\kappa_{b}^{G}

[
BM99
]
. Similarly, it was recently established in a series of papers
[
DlCM23
,
DlCMH25
,
DlCMH23
]
that if
G
is a complex simple Lie group of classical type, then
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{3}(G;\mathbb{R})
is one-dimensional and generated by the bounded Borel class
\beta_{b}^{G}
. If
G
is Hermitian (respectively complex classical), we thus denote by
\kappa_{b}^{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\mathrm{res}(\kappa_{b}^{G})
(respectively
\beta_{b}^{\mathcal{G}}\coloneqq\mathrm{res}(\beta_{b}^{G}
)); then Corollary
4
implies:

Corollary 5

(Burger-Monod, De la Cruz Mengual)
.

Let
(X,\mu,Y)
be an ergodic transverse
G
-system where
G
is a connected simple Lie group or real rank
\geq 2
.

(i)

If
G
is Hermitian, then
\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{2}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{R}\cdot\kappa_{b}^{\mathcal{G}}
; otherwise
\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{2}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})=0
.

(ii)

If
G
is complex classical, then
\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{3}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{R}\cdot\beta_{b}^{\mathcal{G}}
.

These are the first computation of a non-trivial bounded cohomology groups of a discrete measured groupoid which is not isomorphic to an action groupoid. Let us mention in passing that
in view of recent results of Monod
[
Mon24
]
(in combination with
[
Mon10
]
) we also have the following vanishing result:

Corollary 6

(Monod)
.

Let
(X,\mu,Y)
be an ergodic transverse
G
-system where
G=\mathbf{G}(k)
and
\mathbf{G}
is a simple algebraic group over a non-Archimedean local field
k
. Then
\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})=0
for all
0<k<2\cdot\mathrm{rk}(G)
.

1.5.
Application to measured subsets

Our motivation in studying measurable bounded cohomology of transverse groupoids comes from the study of approximate lattices as introduced in
[
BH16
]
; more generally, transverse measured groupoid arise naturally in the study of discrete subsets of locally compact groups. Let us briefly describe this connection.

Every lcsc group
G
acts continuously on the space
\mathrm{Cl}(G)
of closed subsets of
G
via
g\cdot P\coloneqq Pg^{-1}
, where the latter is equipped with the Chabauty–Fell topology. Given a locally finite subset
P_{o}\subset G
we denote by
X^{+}(P_{o})
the orbit closure of
P_{o}
in
\mathrm{Cl}(G)
and define its
hull
by
X(P_{o})\coloneqq X^{+}(P_{o})\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. With the restricted action, this is a lcsc
G
-space which admits a canonical cross section given by

Y(P_{o})\coloneqq\{Q\in X(P_{o})\mid e\in Q\}.

The associated transverse groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}
is called the
pattern groupoid
of
P_{o}
and contains combinatorial information about
P_{o}
. More precisely, we define the collection of
marked patches
of
P_{o}
as

\mathcal{P}(P_{o})\coloneqq\{(B\cap P_{o}g^{-1},B)\mid B\subset G\text{ bounded},g\in G\}.

If
(\alpha,B)\in\mathcal{P}(P_{o})
, then
\alpha
is called a
B
-patch
of
P_{o}
, and we say that
P_{o}
has
finite local complexity
(FLC) if it has finitely many
B
-patches for every bounded
B\subset G
; equivalently, the symmetrization
S(P_{o})\coloneqq P_{o}P_{o}^{-1}
does not accumulate at the identity. If
P_{o}
has FLC, then the associated pattern groupoid is given by

\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}^{(0)}=\{Q\subset S(P_{o})\mid e\in Q,\mathcal{P}(Q)\subset\mathcal{P}(P_{o})\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}^{(1)}=\{(q,Q)\mid q\in Q\in\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}^{(0)}\}.

If
P_{o}
and
P_{1}
are FLC sets in possibly different lcsc groups
G
and
H
, then they are called
pattern-isomorphic
if the associated pattern groupoids are isomorphic. This is the case if the local groups
S(P_{o})
and
S(P_{1})
(with partial group operations given by restriction from the ambient lcsc group) are isomorphic and implies that there there is bijection between
\mathcal{P}(P_{0})
and
\mathcal{P}(P_{1})
which is compatible with inclusions, i.e.
P_{o}
and
P_{1}
are combinatorially equivalent in the sense that they have “matching patches”. The
pattern isomorphism problem
for FLC sets asks for a classification of FLC sets in lcsc groups up to pattern-isomorphism or, more realistically, for isomorphism invariants of pattern groupoids a.k.a.
pattern invariants
.

We say that a FLC set
P_{o}\subset G
is
measured
if the hull system
G\curvearrowright X(P_{o})
admits an ergodic invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}_{\mathrm{erg}}(X(P_{o}))^{G}
. Examples of measured FLC sets include strong approximate lattices in the sense of
[
BH16
]
, and in particular regular model sets in the sense of
[
BHP17
]
. One can show that in the case of a measured FLC set the transverse system
(X(P_{o}),\mu,Y(P_{o}))
is automatically integrable, hence gives rise to a finite invariant measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}
. We define the
bounded cohomology
of
P_{o}
with respect to
\mu
as

\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}((P_{o},\mu);\mathbb{R})\coloneqq\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{\bullet}((\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}}).

If we assume that
P_{o}
has
finite Voronoi volume
in the sense that all Voronoi cells of
P_{o}
in
G
have uniformly bounded Haar volume (e.g. if
P_{o}
is cocompact), then one can show that every finite invariant measure on
\mathcal{G}_{P_{o}}
is proportional to a transverse measure of an invariant probability measure on
\mu
. This then implies that for every
k\geq 0
the set

\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{k}(P_{o};\mathbb{R})\coloneqq\{[\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{k}((P_{o},\mu);\mathbb{R})]\mid\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}_{\mathrm{erg}}(X(P_{o}))^{G}\},

where
[\cdot]
denotes equivalence classes up to isometric isomorphism, is a pattern invariant of
P_{o}
. Theorem
1
now allows us to compute this pattern invariant in certain cases:

Corollary 7
.

If
P_{o}\subset G
is a measured FLC set of finite Voronoi volume, then

\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{k}(P_{o};\mathbb{R})=\{\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu))\mid\mu\in\mathrm{Prob}_{\mathrm{erg}}(X(P_{o}))^{G}\}.

Corollary 8
.

If
G
is a higher rank simple Lie group and
P_{o}\subset G
is a strong approximate lattice of finite Voronoi volume, then

\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{2}(P_{o};\mathbb{R})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{[\mathbb{R}]\},&\text{if $G$ is of Hermitian type};\\
\{[0]\},&\text{else.}\end{array}\right.

This implies that a strong approximate lattice in a higher rank simple Lie group of Hermitian type cannot be pattern isomorphic to an approximate lattice in a higher rank Lie group which is not of Hermitian type (assuming both are of finite Voronoi volume). While in principle this indicates the usefulness of bounded cohomology in classifying approximate lattices up to pattern isomorphism, we should point out that (as observed by Björklund and the first named author) in the context of higher rank Lie groups much stronger pattern invariants arise via Furman’s theory of measure equivalence rigidity
[
Fur99
]
.

1.6.
An application to discretization

Let
G
be a unimodular lcsc group and denote by
G^{\delta}
the underlying discrete group. If
G
admits a lattice
\Gamma
, then the corresponding restriction map factors as

\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(G^{\delta};\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(\Gamma;\mathbb{R}).

Since the restriction map is injective, we can thus infer from the existence of a lattice in
G
the injectivity of the
discretization map

\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(G^{\delta};\mathbb{R})
. A similar argument can now also be run in the groupoid context. If we define the
return time set
of a transverse
G
-system
(X,\mu,Y)
as
\Lambda(Y)\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid gY\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}
, then this leads to the following result:

Corollary 9
.

If there exists an integrable transverse
G
-system with discrete return time set, then the discretization map
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{\bullet}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{b}}^{\bullet}(G^{\delta};\mathbb{R})
is injective.

It is well-known that
every
lcsc group admits an integrable transverse system, but it seems to be an open problem whether we can always arrange the return time set to be discrete. This is related to the question whether every lcsc group admits a measured subset
P_{o}
such that
P_{o}P_{o}^{-1}
is locally finite.

1.7.
On the proof of Theorem
1

The first ingredient in our proof of Theorem
1
is the simple observation (due to Björklund and the first named author) that transverse groupoids are
measure equivalent
to the ambient lcsc group in a suitable sense. This can be seen as a measurable version of the classical fact that
G\ltimes X
and
\mathcal{G}
are Morita-equivalent if the defining cross section
Y
happens to be cocompact.

Our second ingredient is a result of Monod and Shalom
[
MS06
]
which states that bounded cohomology of discrete groups is well-behaved under measure equivalence, or rather its straight forward generalization to discrete measured groupoids. Combining both ingredients we deduce that Theorem
1
holds if
G
happens to be discrete and
\mu
is ergodic.

Going beyond the discrete case, we expect that measurable bounded cohomology of (non-discrete) measured Borel groupoids is also well-behaved under suitably-defined measure equivalence, which would imply Theorem
1
(at least for ergodic actions). However, we are currently lacking the technology concerning Borel groupoids to establish such a general result.

In view of this shortfall, our proof in the non-discrete case works by imitating (rather than generalizing) the proof in the discrete case and making a number of (rather non-trivial) technical adjustments by hand. This is where our third ingredient comes in, a construction of a cohomological transfer spaces, which is inspired by a construction of Ph. Blanc
[
Bla79
]
in the lattice case, or rather a variant of this construction as presented by Monod in
[
Mon01
]
. As a by-product, we also see that no ergodicity hypothesis is required in Theorem
1
.

2.
Background on measurable bounded cohomology of groupoids

2.1.
Groupoids and their actions

§ 2.1
.

A
groupoid
is a small category
\mathcal{G}
whose morphisms are all invertible. We denote the set of objects and the set of morphisms respectively by
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
and, given a composable pair
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
, we write
gh\coloneqq g\circ h
. The
target
and the
source
maps are indicated respectively by
t:\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and by
s:\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Given a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and
x,y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we define subsets of
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
by

\mathcal{G}_{x}\coloneqq s^{-1}(x),\quad\mathcal{G}^{y}\coloneqq t^{-1}(y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}_{x}^{y}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}_{x}\cap\mathcal{G}^{y}.

We often consider
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
as a subset of
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
via the embedding
x\mapsto 1_{x}
. Under this identification we then have
s(g)=g^{-1}g
and
t(g)=gg^{-1}
.
The
underlying equivalence relation

R_{\mathcal{G}}
of
\mathcal{G}
is the equivalence relation on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
given by
xR_{\mathcal{G}}y
iff
\mathcal{G}_{x}^{y}\neq\emptyset
. We say that a subset
U\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is
invariant
if it is invariant under
R_{\mathcal{G}}
, i.e.

\forall\,g\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}:\;s(g)\in U\iff t(g)\in U.

A groupoid is called
principal
if
|\mathcal{G}_{x}^{y}|\leq 1
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
; in this case we have
|\mathcal{G}_{x}^{y}|=1
if and only if
xR_{\mathcal{G}}y
, and hence
\mathcal{G}
is uniquely determined by
R_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Notation 2.2
.

Given sets
A,B,C
and maps
f:A\to C
and
g:B\to C
we denote by

A\;{}_{f}\negthickspace\times_{g}B\coloneqq\{(a,b)\in A\times B\mid f(a)=g(b)\}

the associated
fiber product
; if
f
and
g
are clear from context we also write
A\times_{C}B
. In particular, if
\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid,
A
is a set and
t_{A}:A\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a map, then we write

\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A\coloneqq\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\;{}_{s}\negthickspace\times_{t_{A}}A.

We denote by
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}^{(1)}{}_{s}\negthickspace\times_{t}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
the space of composable arrows and by
\mathcal{G}^{(n)}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}^{(1)}{}_{s}\negthickspace\times_{t}\dots\ {}_{s}\negthickspace\times_{t}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
the space of paths of length
n
in
\mathcal{G}
.

§ 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid. A
left
\mathcal{G}
-space
is a set
A
together with an
anchor map

t_{A}:A\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and an
action map

\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A\to A
,
(g,a)\mapsto g\cdot a
such that the following properties hold:

(i)

t_{A}(g\cdot a)=t(g)
for all
(g,a)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A
;

(ii)

1_{y}\cdot a=a
for all
y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
a\in t_{A}^{-1}(y)
;

(iii)

(gh)\cdot a=g\cdot(h\cdot a)
, whenever
(gh,a)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A
and
(g,h\cdot a)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A
.

We also say that
\mathcal{G}
acts on
A
and write
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright A
. Note that (ii) and (iii) imply in particular that
g^{-1}\cdot(g\cdot a)=a
for all
(g,a)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A
.

Example 2.4
.

The following are examples of groupoid actions:

(i)

Every group
G
can be considered as a groupoid
\underline{G}
with a single object
*
with automorphism group
G
; then a
\underline{G}
-action is just a
G
-action in the usual sense.

(ii)

Every groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acts on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
with anchor map the identity and action given by
gx=t(g)
if
s(g)=x
, and on

\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
with anchor map
t
and action given by
g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\coloneqq g_{1}g_{2}
.

(iii)

If a groupoid acts on a set
A
with anchor map
t_{A}:A\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, then it also acts on the fiber product
A^{[n]}\coloneqq A{}_{t_{A}}\negthickspace\times\ldots\negthickspace\times_{t_{A}}A
: The anchor map is given by

t_{A}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})=t_{A}(a_{0})=\dots=t_{A}(a_{n-1}),

and if
t_{A}(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})=s(g)
, then

g\cdot(a_{0},\dots,a_{n-1})=(g\cdot a_{0},\dots,g\cdot a_{n-1})
. We refer to this as the
diagonal action
of
\mathcal{G}
on
A^{[n]}
.

(iv)

Combining (ii) and (iii), every groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acts diagonally on

\mathcal{G}^{[n]}=\{(g_{0},\dots,g_{n-1})\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\mid t(g_{0})=\dots=t(g_{n-1})\}

by left-multiplication with anchor map
t(g_{0},\dots,g_{n-1})\coloneqq t(g_{0})=\dots=t(g_{n-1})
.

Construction 2.5

(Action groupoid)
.

If
A
is a left
\mathcal{G}
-space, then the associated
left action groupoid

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{G}\ltimes A
is given by
\mathcal{L}^{(0)}=A
,
\mathcal{L}^{(1)}=\mathcal{G}^{(1)}{}_{A}\times_{t_{A}}A
with structure maps
s(g,a)=a
,
t(g,a)=g\cdot a
and

(g,a)(h,b)=(gh,b)
if
a=h\cdot b
.

Similarly, the
right action groupoid

\mathcal{R}=A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is given by
\mathcal{R}^{(0)}=A
,
\mathcal{R}^{(1)}=A\;{}_{t_{A}}\negthickspace\times_{t}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
with structure maps
s(a,g)=g^{-1}\cdot a
,
t(a,g)=a
and
(a,g)(b,h)=(a,gh)
if
g^{-1}\cdot a=b
.

In particular, given a group action
G\curvearrowright A
we obtain groupoids
G\ltimes A
and
A\rtimes G
.

§ 2.6
.

Using action groupoids we can efficiently define morphisms between
\mathcal{G}
-sets: If a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acts on sets
X
and
Y
, then a map
\pi:X\to Y
is called
\mathcal{G}
-equivariant
if there is a functor
\pi_{*}:\mathcal{G}\ltimes X\to\mathcal{G}\ltimes Y
satisfying

\pi^{(0)}_{*}(x)=\pi(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi^{(1)}_{*}(\gamma,x)=(\gamma,\pi(x)).

We can also use action groupoids to define invariant subsets. By definition, a subset
U
of a
\mathcal{G}
-set
A
is
\mathcal{G}
-invariant
if it is
(\mathcal{G}\ltimes U)
-invariant in the sense of §
2.1
. More explicitly, this means that
U=\mathcal{G}U
, where the latter is defined as

\mathcal{G}U=\{g\cdot u\mid(g,u)\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}A\}.

Construction 2.7

(Restriction)
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid and
Y\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a subset, then the
restriction

\mathcal{G}|_{Y}
is the groupoid with sets of objects and morphisms given by

\mathcal{G}|_{Y}^{(0)}\coloneqq Y\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}|_{Y}^{(1)}\coloneqq\{g\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\mid\{s(g),t(g)\}\subset Y\}

and structure maps given by the restrictions of the structure maps of
\mathcal{G}
.

§ 2.8
.

If
A
is a left
\mathcal{G}
-space, then we denote by
R_{A}
the underlying equivalence relation of
R_{\mathcal{G}\ltimes A}
. The associated partition of
X
is then denoted by
\mathcal{G}\backslash A
, and we denote by
\pi:A\to\mathcal{G}\backslash A
,
a\mapsto[a]
the canonical projection. In the case of a group action, this recovers the usual quotient of
A
by
\mathcal{G}
. We say that the
\mathcal{G}
-action is
free
, if
\mathcal{G}\ltimes A
(or, equivalently,
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
) is a principal groupoid. In this case,
a subset
{\mathcal{F}}\subset A
is called a
strict fundamental domain
for the
\mathcal{G}
-action if it intersects each class in
R_{\mathcal{G}\ltimes A}
in precisely one element; then
\pi
restricts to a bijection
{\mathcal{F}}\to\mathcal{G}\backslash A
.

2.2.
Borel groupoids and Haar systems

§ 2.9
.

A measurable space is called a
standard Borel space
if it is isomorphic to a Borel subset of a Polish space. If
(A,\mathcal{B})
is a standard Borel space and
\tau
is a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
\mathcal{B}
, then
(A,\mathcal{B},\tau)
is called a
Lebesgue space
; if moreover
\tau(A)=1
then it is called a
standard probability space
. For sake of brevity, we will usually omit the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
from the notation.

Given a Lebesgue space
(A,\tau)
we denote by
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(A)
the space of bounded real-valued Borel functions on
A
and by
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(A,\tau)
the space of equivalence classes of such functions, where two functions are considered equivalent if they agree
\tau
-almost everywhere. If the measure
\tau
is clear from context we also write
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(A)\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(A,\tau)
.

§ 2.10
.

Let
\pi:A\to B
be a Borel map between standard Borel spaces. A
Borel system
of measures for
\pi
is a collection
\lambda=\{\lambda^{b}\}_{b\in B}
of
\sigma
-finite measures with
\lambda^{b}(A\setminus\pi^{-1}(b))=0
for every
b\in B
and such that the map

B\to\mathbb{R}\,,\;\;\;b\mapsto\lambda^{b}(f)

is Borel for every Borel function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
.

§ 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid and let
\mathcal{B}
be a
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
; we equip
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\subset\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\subset\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\times\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
with the respective trace
\sigma
-algebras. We then say that
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{B})
(or just
\mathcal{G}
for short) is a
Borel groupoid
if
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
are standard Borel spaces and multiplication
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
and inversion
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
are Borel; by §
2.1
this implies that also the source and target map are Borel, and hence
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
is standard Borel.

Example 2.12
.

(i) If
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel groupoid and
A
is a standard Borel space, then
A
together with an action of
\mathcal{G}
is called a
Borel
\mathcal{G}
-space
if the anchor map and the action map are Borel; this implies that
\mathcal{G}\ltimes A
and
A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
are again Borel groupoids. In particular, if
G
is a lcsc group and
A
is a Borel
G
-space, then
G\ltimes A
and
A\rtimes G
are Borel groupoids.

(ii) If
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel groupoid and
Y\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is Borel, then the restriction
\mathcal{G}|_{Y}
is a Borel groupoid when equipped with the trace
\sigma
-algebra.

Definition 2.13
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel groupoid, then a Borel system
\lambda=\{\lambda^{y}\}_{y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
for
t:\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
of measures is called a
Haar system
provided

\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(gh)d\lambda^{s(g)}(h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(h)d\lambda^{t(g)}(h)\quad\text{for every }g\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}.

Remark 2.14
.

The existence of a Haar system is a restrictive condition for a Borel groupoid; it forces the groupoid to be the underlying Borel groupoid of a lcsc groupoid with open source and target maps. Moreover, Haar systems are not unique in any reasonable sense.

Example 2.15
.

The following examples provide Haar systems in all cases which are of interest to us here:

(i) If
G
is a lcsc group, then a Haar system for
\underline{G}
is the same a left-Haar measure for
G
.

(ii) If
\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid whose target map has countable fibers, then a Haar system for
\mathcal{G}
is given by counting measures on fibers; this is called the
counting Haar system
.

(iii) Let
\mathcal{G}
be a groupoid with Haar system
\lambda
and let
A
be a Borel
\mathcal{G}
-space with anchor map
t_{A}:A\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
. Then a Haar system for
\mathcal{R}\coloneqq A\rtimes\mathcal{G}
is given by
\lambda_{A}=(\delta_{a}\otimes\lambda^{t_{A}(a)})_{a\in A}
. Indeed, if
(a,g)\in\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
, then
t_{A}(a)=t(g)
and
t_{A}(g^{-1}\cdot a)=s(g)
, hence

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}^{(1)}}f((a,g)(b,h))\,d\lambda_{A}^{s(a,g)}(b,h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(a,gh)d\lambda^{t_{A}(g^{-1}\cdot a)}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(a,gh)\,d\lambda^{s(g)}(h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(a,h)d\lambda^{t(g)}(h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(a,h)d\lambda^{t_{A}(a)}(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}^{(1)}}f(b,h)\,d\lambda_{A}^{t(a,g)}(b,h).

(iv) In the situation of (iii) we can also define a Haar system
\lambda^{\prime}_{A}
for
\mathcal{G}\ltimes A
as the push forward of
\lambda_{A}
via the Borel isomorphism

(A\rtimes\mathcal{G})^{(1)}\to(\mathcal{G}\ltimes A)^{(1)},\quad(a,g)\mapsto(g^{-1},g^{-1}\cdot a).

2.3.
Measured groupoids and their Lebesgue spaces

§ 2.16
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
be a Borel groupoid with Haar system.

(i) Given a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
we define a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure
\nu\circ\lambda
on
\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
by

\nu\circ\lambda(f)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(g)d\lambda^{y}(g)d\nu(y).

We say that
\nu
is
invariant
(respectively,
quasi-invariant
) under
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
if
\nu\circ\lambda
(respectively its measure class) is invariant under the inversion map
g\mapsto g^{-1}
. If
\lambda
is clear from context, we simply say that
\nu
is invariant (or quasi-invariant) under
\mathcal{G}
.

(ii) More generally, let
A
be a Borel
\mathcal{G}
-space and let
\tau
be a
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
A
. We then say that
\tau
is
invariant
(respectively,
quasi-invariant
) under
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
if it is invariant (respectively quasi-invariant) under
(A\rtimes\mathcal{G},\lambda_{A})
.

More explicitly, this means that the measure
\tau\circ\lambda_{A}
on
(A\rtimes\mathcal{G})^{(1)}
as given by

(\tau\circ\lambda_{A})(f)=\int_{A}\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}f(a,g)\,d\lambda^{t_{A}(a)}(g)d\tau(a)

is invariant (respectively quasi-invariant) under the map
(a,g)\mapsto(g^{-1}\cdot a,g^{-1})
.

Definition 2.17
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
be a Borel groupoid with Haar system.

(i)

If
\nu
is a
\mathcal{G}
-quasi-invariant
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
, then
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
is called a
measured groupoid
.

(ii)

If
A
is a Borel
\mathcal{G}
-space and
\tau
is a
\mathcal{G}
-quasi-invariant
\sigma
-finite Borel measure on
A
, then
(A,\tau)
is called a
Lebesgue
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
-space
.

(iii)

If
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
is a measured groupoid then a
Lebesgue
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
-space

(A,\tau)
is a Lebesgue
(\mathcal{G},\lambda)
-space such that
(t_{A})_{*}\tau=\nu
.

Remark 2.18
.

If
(A,\tau)
is a Lebesgue
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
-space, then there exists a Borel system
\{\tau^{y}\}_{y\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
for
t_{A}:A\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that

(2)

\tau(f)=\int_{\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}\int_{A}f(a)\,d\tau^{y}(a)\,d\nu(y).

Definition 2.19
.

A measured groupoid
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
is called
ergodic
if every invariant Borel subset
U\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
satisfies either
\nu(U)=0
or
\nu(\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\setminus U)=0
.

§ 2.20
.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a Borel groupoid with countable
t
-fibers and
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
is a measured groupoid such that
\lambda
is the counting Haar system, then we refer to
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
as a
discrete measured groupoid
. Moreover, we will usually drop
\lambda
from notation and just refer to
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
as a discrete measured groupoid.

Example 2.21
.

(i) If
G
is a lcsc group, then
(\underline{G},\delta_{*},\lambda^{*})
with
\delta_{*}
the Dirac mass on the single object
*
and
\lambda^{*}
a left-Haar measure of
G
, is an ergodic measured groupoid. If
G
is discrete and countable, then
(\underline{G},\delta_{*})
is a discrete measured groupoid.

(ii) Consider a non-singular (i.e. measure class preserving Borel) action
G\curvearrowright(A,\tau)
of a lcsc group
G
with left-Haar measure
m_{G}
on a Lebesgue space. If we define
\lambda_{A}^{a}\coloneqq\delta_{a}\otimes m_{G}
for every
a\in A
, then

(A\rtimes G,\tau,\lambda_{A})
is a measured groupoid, which is ergodic if and only if the action is ergodic in the usual sense. In particular, if
G
is a countable discrete group, then
(A\rtimes G,\tau)
is a discrete measured groupoid.

(iii) If
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
is a measured groupoid and
Y\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
is a subset, then there is in general no way to equip the restriction
\mathcal{G}|_{Y}
with the structure of a measured groupoid unless
\nu(Y)>0
, in which case
\mathcal{G}|_{Y}
inherits a structure of measured groupoid by restricting the Haar system and the measure on units.

We are going to discuss more interesting examples of discrete measured groupoids in Subsection
3.2
below.

Definition 2.22
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
and
(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
be measured groupoids.

(i) A
strict homomorphism
between
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
and
(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
is a measurable map
f:\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1)}
such that
f_{*}(\nu\circ\lambda)

is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu^{\prime}\circ\lambda^{\prime}
, and for every composable pair
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
we have

(f(g),f(h))\in\mathcal{H}^{(2)}
and
f(gh)=f(g)f(h)
.

(ii) A strict homomorphism
f:\mathcal{G}^{(1)}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1)}
is called an
isomorphism
if it is bijective and the inverse map
f^{-1}
is also a strict homomorphism. In this case we say that
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
and
(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
are
strictly isomorphic
.

(iii) We say that
(\mathcal{G},\nu,\lambda)
and
(\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
are
weakly isomorphic
if there exist Borel subsets
A\subset\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
and
B\subset\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
of positive measure such that the restriction groupoids
\mathcal{G}|_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}|_{B}
are strictly isomorphic.

The following criterion for weak isomorphism of ergodic, principal, discrete measured groupoids due to Furman
[
Fur99
, Proposition 2.3]
will be used in the proof of our main theorem for discrete groups:

Proposition 2.23
.

Let
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
and
(\mathcal{H},\nu^{\prime})
be ergodic, principal, discrete measured groupoids. Then
(\mathcal{G},\nu)
and
(\mathcal{H},\nu^{\prime})
are weakly isomorphic
provided that there exist Borel maps
p:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
and
q:\mathcal{H}^{(0)}\to\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
such that

(i)

p_{*}\nu\prec\nu^{\prime}
and
q_{*}\nu^{\prime}\prec\nu
;

(ii)

(p(t(g)),p(s(g)))\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
(q(t(h)),q(s(h)))\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)}
,
h\in\mathcal{H}^{(1)}
;

(iii)

(q(p(x)),x)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
(p(q(y)),y)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
,
y\in\mathcal{H}^{(0)}
.

2.4.
Measurable bundles of Banach spaces

§ 2.24
.

If a group
G
acts on a set
A
, then it also acts on the set of real-valued functions
{\mathcal{F}}(A,\mathbb{R})
on
A
by
g\cdot f(a)\coloneqq f(g^{-1}\cdot a)
, and the fixpoints are precisely the
G
-invariant functions. On the other hand, if a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
acts on a set
A
, then there is a no induced action on functions, since there is no reasonable way to define an anchor map. Nevertheless, to define cohomology, we need to introduce a notion of “invariant functions”.
Following
[
SSb
]
we now discuss a suitable framework to discuss invariants in the context of measurable bounded cohomology. We refer also to
[
FD88
]
for a deeper discussion about these topics.

§ 2.25
.

Let
(\Omega,\tau)
be a Lebesgue space and let
{\mathcal{E}}=(E_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
be a family of Banach spaces indexed by
\Omega
. We refer to a map

\sigma:\Omega\to\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}E_{\omega}\quad\text{such that}\quad\sigma(\omega)\in E_{\omega}\text{ for all }\omega\in\Omega

as a
section of
\mathcal{E}
. A collection
{\mathcal{M}}
of sections of
\mathcal{E}
is called a
measurable structure
if the following hold:

(i)

\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}
whenever
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}
;

(ii)

\varphi\cdot\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}
whenever
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is
\tau
-measurable;

(iii)

if
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}
then
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\omega\mapsto\lVert\sigma(\omega)\rVert_{E_{\omega}}
is
\tau
-measurable;

(iv)

if
(\sigma_{n})
is a net in
{\mathcal{M}}
and
\sigma_{n}(\omega)\rightarrow\sigma(\omega)
for
\tau
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
, then
\sigma\in{\mathcal{M}}
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a measurable structure, then we refer to
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M})
as a
measurable bundle (of Banach spaces over
(\Omega,\tau)
)
and to elements of
\mathcal{M}
as
measurable sections
of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{M})
. We will often drop
\mathcal{M}
from notation and simply talk about measurable section of the measurable bundle
\mathcal{E}
.

Given
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}
we set

\lVert\sigma\rVert:\Omega\rightarrow[0,\infty),\ \ \ \lVert\sigma\rVert(x)\coloneqq\lVert\sigma(x)\rVert_{E_{x}}.

and define the space of

§ 3.1

Consider a probability measure preserving (pmp) Borel action of

we also define the

\Lambda(Y)\coloneqq\bigcup_{y\in Y}Y_{y}=\{g\in G\mid gy\cap Y\neq\emptyset\}.

We recall from the introduction that a Borel subset

is called a

if
GY=X
and for every

the

is locally finite in the sense that for every compact subset

as a

-system

is countable, since

\sigma

Example 3.2

(Integrable transverse systems)
.

Let
Y\subset X
be a Borel subset such that
GY=X
and such that the point process

is locally integrable, i.e.

(7)

\int_{X}|Y_{x}\cap L|\,d\mu(x)<\infty\quad\text{for every compact subset }L\subset G.

This implies that for every compact
L\subset G
the intersection
Y_{x}\cap L
is finite for almost all
x
; since
G
is
\sigma
-compact this implies that the subset

X_{0}=\{x\in X\mid Y_{x}\text{ is locally finite}\}\subset X

is conull, and one can check that it is Borel. Since
Y_{gx}=Y_{x}g^{-1}
for all

and
x\in X
it is also
G

Y_{0}\coloneqq X_{0}\cap Y
, then
Y_{x}=(Y_{0})_{x}
for all

. This shows that

(X_{0},\mu|_{X_{0}},Y_{0})
is a transverse

-system whose associated point process is locally integrable; we refer to such a system as an
integrable transverse system
.

From now on,
(X,\mu,Y)
denotes a transverse

Lemma 3.3
.

The action map
a:G\times Y\to X
,
a(g,y)\coloneqq gy
has countable fibers.

Proof.

If
x\in X
and
(g,y)\in a^{-1}(x)
, i.e.

, then

g\in Y_{x}^{-1}\text{ and }y=g^{-1}x\implies(g,y)\in Y_{x}^{-1}\times Y_{x}x.

The lemma then follows since
Y_{x}
is countable.
∎

Construction 3.4

(Cocycles over transverse systems)

By Lemma
3.3

We now fix such a Borel section once and for all and define a Borel function

By definition we then have

\beta(x)\in Y_{x}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(y)=e\quad\text{for all }x\in G\times Y\text{ and }y\in Y.

4.
Measurable bounded cohomology and measure equivalence

We now digress to prove a version of our main theorem for (ergodic) transverse groupoids over countable
discrete
groups
G
. This version has a clean conceptual proof in terms of measure equivalence, which is not available in the non-discrete case due to current technical limitations in the theory of non-discrete Borel groupoids. Nevertheless, the proof in the discrete case should be a seen as a blueprint for the general case. The key observation is that measure equivalent discrete measured groupoids have closely related bounded cohomology, as first established by Monod and Shalom
[
MS06
]
in the group case and recently reproved by the second-named author and Savini
[
SSa
]
in the groupoidal language.

4.1.
Couplings and measure equivalences

§ 4.1
.

A

§ 4.3

If

Proposition 4.4
.

Measure equivalence is an equivalence relation on the class of discrete measured groupoids.

Proof.

Compared to the group case, the main subtlety arises from the fact that
\nu
need not be invariant, and hence
\nu\circ\lambda
is in general not equal but only equivalent to its image
(\nu\circ\lambda)^{-1}
under the inversion map. This can be resolved by replacing
\nu\circ\lambda
by its symmetrization
(\nu\circ\lambda)+(\nu\circ\lambda)^{-1}
and then the proof is as in the group case:

A
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{G})
-coupling is given by
(\Omega,m)\coloneqq(\mathcal{G}^{(1)},(\nu\circ\lambda)+(\nu\circ\lambda)^{-1})
with the
(\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G})
-action given by

(g_{1},g_{2})\cdot h\coloneqq g_{1}hg_{2}^{-1}\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{G}^{(1)},g_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{t(h)},g_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{s(h)}

This shows reflexivity, and symmetry is immediate since any
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
-coupling
(\Omega,m)
can be seen as
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})
-coupling by simply swapping the actions.

As for transitivity, assume that we are given a
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
-coupling
(\Omega_{1},m_{1})
with fundamental domains

and

and a
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
-coupling

with fundamental domains

and

. We now define

; then
\mathcal{H}

, and there are commuting actions of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{K}
on the factors. We thus obtain a

Indeed, a strict

and since

{m}(\widetilde{X})=m_{1}(X)m_{2}(\mathcal{H}\backslash\Omega_{2})=m_{1}(X)m_{2}(Z_{2})<+\infty.

Similarly, a

6.4.
An explicit implementation via harmonic cocycles

We keep the notation of the previous section; our goal is to provide an explicit implementation of the induction isomorphism
t^{n}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{n}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X))\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{n}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})
on the cocycle level. For this we are going to need some standard results about Poisson boundaries; a convenient reference is
[
Fur02
]
.

§ 6.11
.

A probability measure
p
on
G
is called
admissible
if some convolution power
p^{\ast n}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
m_{G}
and the support of
p
generates
G
as a semigroup. For the remainder of this section we fix an admissible probability measure
p
and denote by
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(G)=\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{p}(G)
the space of bounded measurable functions
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
which are
p
-harmonic
in the sense that

f(g)=\int_{G}f(gg^{\prime})\,dp(g^{\prime})\quad\text{for all }g\in G.

Any such function is automatically continuous by admissibility of
p
, and there is an amenable Lebesgue
G
-space
(B,\tau)=(B_{p},\tau_{p})
, called the
Furstenberg-Poisson boundary
of
(G,p)
, such that the
Poisson transform

\mathcal{P}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B,\tau)\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(G),\quad\mathcal{P}f(g)=\int_{B}f(g\xi)\,d\tau(\xi)

is an isometric isomorphism. The construction is compatible with products, i.e. for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain an isometric isomorphisms
\mathcal{P}^{n}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B^{n},\tau^{\otimes n})\to\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(G^{n})
by coordinate-wise action and integration. This induces a chain map

\mathcal{P}^{\bullet+1}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B^{\bullet+1}\times X,\tau^{\otimes\bullet+1}\otimes\mu)^{G}\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(G^{\bullet+1}\times X,m_{G}^{\otimes n+1}\otimes\mu)^{G}

which on the level of cohomology induces the isomorphism
(G\to B)^{\bullet}
from §
6.8
. In particular, every bounded cohomology class with coefficient in
L^{\infty}(X)
can be represented by a Borel function which is harmonic (and hence continuous) in the
G
-variables.

Throughout this section,
(X,\mu,Y)
denote an integrable transverse system over a unimodular lcsc group
G

7.1.
Restriction and induction

§ 7.1
.

We denote by
i_{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)
the inclusion of constants and set

\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0}\coloneqq\left\{[f]\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)\mid\int f\,d\mu=0\right\}

We then have a short exact sequence of coefficient
G
-modules given by

(37)

0\to\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{i_{c}}\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)\xrightarrow{p_{\mu}}\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0}\to 0,

where

p_{\mu}(f)\coloneqq f-\int f\,d\mu\cdot 1_{X}.

The short exact sequence (
37
) is actually split-exact, since we can define an explicit left-splitting by

s_{\mu}:\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)\to\mathbb{R},\quad f\mapsto\int_{X}f\,d\mu.

This implies that for every
k\geq 0
the map
(i_{c})^{k}_{*}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu))
is injective and its left-inverse
(s_{\mu})^{k}_{*}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu))\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})
is surjective; moreover, we have
a short exact sequence

0\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{(i_{c})^{k}_{*}}\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu))\xrightarrow{(p_{\mu})^{k}_{*}}\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0})\to 0.

Note that, by definition, the maps
i_{c}
and
s_{\mu}
are norm-non-increasing, and hence
(i_{c})^{k}_{*}
and
(s_{\mu})^{k}_{*}
are seminorm-non-increasing for every
k\geq 0
.

Definition 7.2
.

The
restriction
and
induction map
are defined as the linear maps

\mathrm{res}^{k}_{G,\mathcal{G}}:\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{ind}^{k}_{\mathcal{G},G}:\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})

given by composing
(i_{c})^{k}_{*}
and
(s_{\mu})^{k}_{*}
respectively with the isomorphism
t^{k}
from Construction
6.9
.

From §
7.1
we deduce:

Proposition 7.3
.

For every
k\geq 0
the map
\mathrm{res}^{k}_{G,\mathcal{G}}
is injective, its left-inverse
\mathrm{ind}^{k}_{\mathcal{G},G}
is surjective, and both of these maps are seminorm-non-increasing.
Moreover, there is a short exact sequence

0\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{res}^{k}_{G,\mathcal{G}}}\textup{H}_{\textup{mb}}^{k}((\mathcal{G},\nu);\underline{\mathbb{R}})\to\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0})\to 0,

hence
\mathrm{res}^{k}_{G,\mathcal{G}}
is an isometric isomorphism if and only if
\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{k}(G;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(X,\mu)_{0})=0
.

From Proposition
6.13
we deduce:

Corollary 7.4

(Implementation of restriction)
.

Let

be a bounded harmonic
G
-invariant cocycle representing a class
\alpha\in\textup{H}_{\textup{cb}}^{n}(G;\mathbb{R})

is represented by the cocycle
