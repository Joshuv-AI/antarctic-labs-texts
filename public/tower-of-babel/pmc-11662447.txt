De-etiolation is Almost Color Blind: The Study of Photosynthesis Awakening under Blue and Red Light
Authors: Agnieszka K Banaś, Katarzyna Leja, Piotr Zgłobicki, Paweł Jedynak, Ewa Kowalska, Wojciech Strzałka, Joanna Grzyb, Beata Myśliwa-Kurdziel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe synthesis and assembly of functioning photosynthetic complexes in chloroplasts developing from etioplasts during the de-etiolation of angiosperm seedlings are imperative for the plant’s autotrophic lifestyle. This study compared the de-etiolation process under monochromatic red or blue light of equal photon flux density during a 24-h illumination period of etiolated Arabidopsis seedlings. The aim was to elucidate the impact of these light wavelengths on the etioplast-to-chloroplast transformation and the initiation of light-dependent photosynthetic reactions. Both treatments led to the formation of functional young chloroplasts; however, the etioplast-to-chloroplast transition and the assembly of photosynthetic complexes occurred unevenly, with individual steps tuned by red or blue light. Ultrastructural analysis suggested faster prolamellar body’s disassembly under blue light, while low-temperature fluorescence studies indicated a slower transformation of protochlorophyllide to chlorophyllide, and chlorophyll a, under these conditions. The red light further promoted the synthesis of chlorophyll b and LHCII antenna proteins. However, the efficiency of antennae in dissipating excess absorbed energy was higher for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light; the maximum quantum yield of the photosystem II reached 0.81 after 24-h de-etiolation, equivalent to mature plants. Blue light seemed to enhance the development of well-functioning photosystems (I and II) and antennae. These findings are important for gaining a deeper understanding of photoreceptor regulation of de-etiolation and for utilizing selected light regimes to improve crop yield.Keywords: Carotenoids, Chlorophyll, CLSM, FLIM, Fluorescence, Gene expression

Introduction

Plant life is strictly light-dependent and light-regulated from seed germination till senescence. Photosynthesis, which enables plant autotrophic lifestyle, utilizes the red and blue ranges of the sunlight to produce organic compounds from CO2 and H2O. At the same time, light triggers plastid signals that, among others, regulate transcription of numerous genes localized both in chloroplast and nuclear genomes (Gollan et al. 2015; Yuan et al. 2017). Light-activated plastid signals act in concert with photoreceptor-mediated pathways to modulate plant responses. In this way, biochemical and physiological processes are optimized to enable the plant to survive and adapt to certain environmental conditions.

Photoreceptors are activated by light within specific wavelength ranges corresponding to the absorption properties of their chromophores. Among plant photoreceptors, UVR8 is specific for UV-B light, and there is a group of proteins that perceive blue light, including cryptochromes (CRYs), phototropins and ZTL/FKF/LKP (ZEITLUPE, FLAVIN-BINDING, KELCH REPEAT, F-BOX 1 and LOV KELCH PROTEIN 2). Additionally, phytochromes (PHYs) well-known for their role as red/far-red photoreceptors (Paik and Huq 2019) also absorb blue light. However, it has been demonstrated that only a limited number of plant responses triggered by blue-light illumination are controlled by phytochromes (Reed et al. 1994, Casal and Mazzella 1998, Neff and Chory 1998).

Light plays an especially crucial role in angiosperm development. Angiosperm seedlings germinating in the absence of light undergo a developmental program known as skotomorphogenesis (Josse and Halliday 2008, Armarego-Marriott et al. 2020). They exhibit distinctive morphology, characterized by a long hypocotyl, an apical hook, as well as small and yellowish cotyledons (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Jedynak et al. 2022). At the cellular level, proplastids develop into etioplasts that house inner membranes with a characteristic shape, and comprising a paracrystalline lipid structure, known as a prolamellar body (PLB), which is surrounded by prothylakoids (Solymosi and Schoefs 2008, Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). In addition, in the absence of light, the biosynthesis of chlorophyll (Chl) pauses at the protochlorophyllide (Pchlide) synthesis stage, leading to the controlled accumulation of this compound in the etioplast inner membranes (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Fujii et al. 2019). A light-dependent Pchlide:NADPH oxidoreductase (LPOR; EC 1.3.1.33), the key regulatory photoenzyme in Chl biosynthesis (Gabruk and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2015, Heyes et al. 2021, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021), primarily accumulates in PLBs as ternary Pchlide–LPOR–NADPH complexes (Dehesh and Ryberg 1985, Lindsten et al. 1988, Blomqvist et al. 2008). Concurrently, the biosynthesis of certain protein subunits of photosynthetic complexes is also inhibited (Plöscher et al. 2011, Pipitone et al. 2021). This inhibition is caused among others by binding of PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) into promoters of photosynthesis-associated nuclear genes (Leivar and Monte 2014). In addition to their nuclear functions, PIFs inhibit expression of plastid-encoded photosynthetic genes as well as the assembly of plastid-encoded RNA polymerase (PEP). The detailed mechanism of this anterograde signaling between nuclear localized PIFs and PEP-dependent expression of plastid genes is unknown (Yoo et al. 2019). Only recently has it been shown, that GUN1, a well known player in chloroplast retrograde signaling, is responsible for blocking of premature chloroplast development and inhibition of expression of photosynthesis-associated genes in etiolated seedlings (Hernández-Verdeja et al. 2022).

As far as LPOR is concerned, angiosperms exhibit variations in the number of genes encoding these enzymes (Gabruk and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2020). In Arabidopsis thaliana, used typically as a model plant, three LPOR genes and three LPOR protein isoforms have been found (Armstrong et al. 1995, Su et al. 2001): LPOR-A, which predominantly accumulates in etiolated seedlings and is degraded along with de-etiolation, LPOR-B, present throughout the whole plant life, and LPOR-C that accumulates in the presence of light (Runge et al. 1996, Frick et al. 2003, Paddock et al. 2010).

When exposed to light, etiolated angiosperm seedlings undergo de-etiolation, i.e. a transition between skoto- and photomorphogenesis (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Armarego-Marriott et al. 2020, Hernández-Verdeja et al. 2020, Pipitone et al. 2021). The transition is orchestrated by numerous regulators, including COP/DET/FUS (CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC/DE-ETIOLATED/FUSCA) and SPA (SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA) proteins, which alter the activity of approximately 30% of Arabidopsis genes (Ma et al. 2001). During this transition, two opposite processes, retrograde signals from plastids mediated by GUN1 and light-activated photoreceptor-specific signaling pathways interact (Hernández-Verdeja et al. 2022). As de-etiolation progresses, GUN1 is degraded and light-triggered processes begin to play an increasingly important role. Light immediately triggers the conversion of Pchlide to chlorophyllide (Chlide), which is catalyzed by LPOR (Gabruk and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2015, Heyes et al. 2021, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). Photoreceptors, mainly PHYs and CRYs, are activated by light in parallel with Pchlide photoconversion. PHYs and CRYs interact with PIFs leading to their degradation (Pedmale et al. 2016, Xu et al. 2016). Complementing action of these two classes of photoreceptors during de-etiolation process is confirmed by Chl accumulation and proper development of even quintuple phy mutants (lacking PHYA–E) grown under blue light (Strasser et al. 2010). PIF degradation seems to be an important step in the shift from skoto- to photomorphogenesis. Assembly of PEP and activation of photosynthesis-associated plastid- and nuclear-encoded genes are between the connected events (Shin et al. 2009, Yoo et al. 2019). As the light illumination is continued, the plastid signals start to play a role in the de-etiolation process. According to the model proposed by Diaz and coworkers (Díaz et al. 2018), switch from nuclear-encoded polymerase (NEP) into PEP-dependent transcription during de-etiolation is regulated by the ferredoxin–thioredoxin reductase system activated as the photosynthesis is established.

At the etioplast level, structural changes in its inner membranes during de-etiolation occur in parallel with the photoreduction of Pchlide to Chlide, and subsequent reactions of Chl biosynthesis, such as Chlide esterification and the synthesis of Chl b (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). These structural changes commence with PLB dispersion, observed within the first minutes of de-etiolation, followed by the formation of the lamellae system. Eventually, this transformation leads to the conversion of PLB and prothylakoids into the thylakoid membrane system, characteristic of chloroplasts (Kowalewska et al. 2016). Numerous studies have been performed using fluorescence spectroscopy at 77K on characteristics of Pchlide accumulated in etiolated tissues and on the investigation of spectral changes induced by the photoreduction of Pchlide to Chlide. In addition to that, there were studies on spectral changes occurring during the assembly of Chl–protein complexes, accompanying the transition from etioplast to chloroplast (see for review, Schoefs and Franck 2003, Belyaeva and Litvin 2011, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). In particular, several Pchlide spectral forms have been described, reflecting whether Pchlide molecules are bound to the LPOR enzyme and whether they are aggregated (Böddi et al. 1989, 1992).

De-etiolation has been studied for a long time (see for review, Mohr and Schäfer 1983, Sundqvist and Dahlin 1997, Heyes et al. 2021). However, in almost all studies on early de-etiolation stages, white light with mostly undefined wavelength characteristics or monochromatic light, either blue or red, was used to induce the process. Considering the current knowledge about different photoreceptors and signaling pathways triggered by blue or red light, our present work aims to fill the gap in the understanding of de-etiolation. We conducted a comparative characterization of de-etiolation of A. thaliana seedlings under blue and red light while maintaining equal photon flux density to verify the importance of these light wavelengths for the etioplast-to-chloroplast transformation and the initiation of light-dependent photosynthetic reactions. This research is an essential starting point for future studies involving photoreceptor mutants.

Results and Discussion

Plastid imaging suggests different rate of de-etiolation under blue and red light

Confocal laser scanning microscopy–fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy overview of de-etiolation.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) techniques were used to image cellular structures in developing seedlings by leveraging the fluorescent properties of accumulated Chl and its precursors, Pchlide and Chlide. For these experiments, seedlings de-etiolated under blue or red light at the same photon flux density (60 μmol photons/m2/s) were collected and fixed at various time points during the light treatment: 15 or 30 min, and 3, 6 or 24 h. The FLIM technique records not only fluorescence emissions at specific wavelength, but also allows for the determination of fluorescence decay time/fluorescence life time (τ) at every pixel in the image. This capability allows for discrimination between fluorophores with the same emission spectrum but different decay. That may simply result in enhanced image contrast, as is the case here. FLIM is also a useful tool for studying the environment around the fluorophore, as well as photochemical processes in which the fluorophore is involved. In our study, τ serves as an indicator of Chl organization within the membranes. Free Chl/Chlide and its complexes with proteins of lower oligomerisation states exhibit longer τ values, while the formation of well-organized antennae and antennae–photosystem complexes results in a shortening of τ.

The etiolated seedlings, collected as the starting material for CLSM–FLIM de-etiolation studies, exhibited fluorescent structures with spectral characteristics typical of Pchlide. Representative images are shown in Fig. 1. The overall shape of PLBs, as deduced from the spatial distribution of Pchlide fluorescence, is consistent with the contours of PLBs previously demonstrated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM; Grzyb et al. 2013). In etiolated samples, clear, compact regions of Pchlide fluorescence, predominantly circular in shape, resemble the cores of PLBs, observed in multiple TEM studies (Staehelin 2003, Kowalewska et al. 2016, 2019, Liang et al. 2022). With an increase in de-etiolation time, the fluorescent area of PLBs clearly expanded, and young chloroplasts were observed after 24 h of de-etiolation, regardless of whether the seedlings were exposed to blue or red light. The indication of chloroplast presence is a fluorescence area that is larger than that of PLBs. For chloroplast fluorescence contours are clearly distinguishable, without lighter fluorescence zone that was characteristic of the initial stage of de-etiolation. Moreover, grain-like shapes of grana stacks can be distinguished within chloroplast fluorescent area.

Based on CLSM images, it can be proposed that de-etiolation initially occurred more rapidly under blue than under red light. Compact fluorescent spots were mostly observed in etiolated seedlings, while after 30 min of blue/red light illumination, structures with an intensively fluorescent core and a clearly detectable light zone appeared. These structures might be interpreted as results of the formation of lamellar-like structures (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Kowalewska et al. 2016, Pipitone et al. 2021, Liang et al. 2022). Similar observations of PLBs as fluorescent spots, which enlarged and spread upon 2 h of irradiation with white light have been recently reported by Liang et al. (2022). In this experiment, PLBs of Arabidopsis cotyledon etioplasts were imaged with electron tomography. Light-induced PLB enlargement was also observed in AFM and TEM studies (Grzyb et al. 2013).

The unraveling and expansion of PLBs corresponded well with changes in fluorescence lifetime (τ), as measured with FLIM (Supplementary Fig. S2). However, changes in τ need to be interpreted with caution because fixing conditions, specifically the presence of glutaraldehyde and β-mercaptoethanol, can affect the fluorescence properties of the pigments (Pchlide, Chlide and/or Chl). Despite these limitations, promising trends can still be observed. The transient increase in the average τ was observed at the very beginning of the greening process, and τ started to decrease after approximately 30 min of de-etiolation. The final τ of about 1.5 ns was reached with the faster kinetics for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light (Supplementary Fig. S2). Again, the timing of the maximum τ suggests that de-etiolation occurs faster under blue light.

We attempted to verify these observations and eliminate any potential unknown effects of fixation on the recorded τ. For this purpose, we conducted an experiment referred as ‘greening on a microscope slide’ (Supplementary Fig S3). For this, a live, etiolated seedling was mounted between microscopic glass slides and then exposed to either blue or red light, with images captured every 5 min. During illumination, performed between collections of images, the sample stayed at the same place at the microscope stage to ensure the observation of the same PLB during the whole experiment. However, a precise recording of the same tissue spot in living cotyledons over extended time in our system was not feasible, mainly due to micromovements of the tissue. Therefore, without reliable correlation, drawing clear conclusions from the obtained results is challenging. These results are presented as the supplementary data only, to illustrate the earlier described trend of changes in τ and PLB architecture (Supplementary Fig S4). It is worth noting, however, that during the first 60 min of this experiment, the average τ increased. This corresponds well with the observation of τ of seedlings fixed at 0–30 min of greening.

High-resolution SIM2 imaging of de-etiolation stages

As we observed variations in the overall distribution of PLB characteristics, depending on the spot on the seedling images described above, we conducted a systematic analysis using high-resolution structured illumination microscopy 2 (SIM2) on fixed samples corresponding to those imaged with CLSM–FLIM. The spatial resolution of SIM2 technique is as low as 60 nm, enabling a significantly more precise estimation of PLB size compared to regular CLSM. Exemplary images and their analysis are shown in Supplementary (II.2; Image 1).

The analysis of size distribution of fluorescent spots in SIM2 images indicated the domination of the spots with the area below 0.1 μm2 (Fig. 2A). It cannot be excluded that these spots are a noise. However, they may also originate from the smallest PLBs or PLB fluorescing parts in etiolated samples, or from isolated thylakoids and the smallest grana, provided that they already appeared during the de-etiolation. Therefore, it was hard to set an arbitrary background cutoff level without losing information about the smallest fluorescing structures. To omit this problem, we compared not histograms but cumulative counts (Fig. 2B). Based on Fig. 1 (Pchlide/Chlide/Chl channel, etiolated samples) and Fig. 2B, one may claim that PLBs in etiolated cotyledons are compact and occupy the average area of less than 0.7 μm2. Assuming PLBs have circular shape, their diameter should be approximately of 1 µm, which is slightly smaller than that estimated using transmission electron microscopy and electron tomography (Pipitone et al. 2021, Liang et al. 2022). The observed dissimilarities may result from differences in methodologies, as CLSM and SIM2 recognize only fluorescent structures. After only 15 min of illumination with either blue or red light, the PLBs expanded to 1.5–2 μm2 (Fig. 2A). At this time point, the biggest differences were observed between the effects of blue and red light, namely, the observed enlargement of the size of fluorescent spots, representing PLB fluorescent area was much greater in seedlings de-etiolated under red light compared to those under blue light (Fig. 2B). The average area occupied by fluorescent spots per cell also changed with the duration of greening (Fig. 2C). This parameter was the smallest in etiolated cells, increased after 15 min of illumination and dropped slightly after 30 min (Fig. 2C). Further greening resulted in a decrease in the size of fluorescent spots, probably related to reorganization of PLBs leading to the formation of separated thylakoids (Fig. 2A), which had been already shown (Solymosi and Aronsson 2013, Kowalewska et al. 2016, Liang et al. 2022). It is known to occur when Chlide, formed from Pchlide accumulated during etiolation, is already present in the cell and is concurrently esterified toward Chl synthesis (Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). Further degradation of PLBs and the formation of mature chloroplasts require de novo synthesis of Chl (Pipitone et al. 2021), resulting in a pronounced increase in fluorescence area after 3 h, and then after 6 h of de-etiolation.

The early chloroplasts were already present after 6 h of greening, and they were slightly smaller than chloroplasts in cotyledons greened for 24 h (compare Fig. 3A-C and E-G). With maturation of chloroplasts, the fluorescent area per chloroplast increased (Fig. 3G and H), what most likely corresponded to the number of thylakoid membranes. For greening under blue light this change was small (the most frequent occurrence of “fluorescent area per chloroplast” was about 30% for both 6 h and 24 h, although the Gaussian fit of distribution was broader for 24 h samples). For greening under red light, there was an increase of fluorescent area per chloroplast from about 25% at 6 h to even 40% at 24 h (Fig. 3G and H). It needs to be remembered that the increase of fluorescent area (but not fluorescence intensity) per chloroplast might be a result of both increase of amount of thylakoids and grana unstacking, as unstacked grana occupy higher area. SIM2 resolution is about 60 nm, thus the one pixel might represent either a single layer of the thylakoid lamellar membrane or a short granum. As SIM2 processing consists a deconvolution step, it loses the information of a relative pixel intensity and does not distinguish between a single layer of the thylakoid lamellar membrane and a small granum. Similar interpretation of chloroplast structure obtained by SIM2 imaging was already mentioned by Iwai and coworkers (Iwai et al. 2018).

Last steps of Chl biosynthesis as monitored by fluorescence emission at 77 K

The analysis of the 77 K Chl fluorescence emission spectra was conducted to investigate the correlation between the development of etioplasts into mature chloroplasts, as visualized by the research described earlier, and the process of Chl biosynthesis and photosystem formation.

Fluorescence emission spectra measured at 77 K for etiolated seedlings before the de-etiolation process (Supplementary Fig. S5) were typical for this stage of plant development and consisted of two bands (Böddi et al. 1992, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021, Jedynak et al. 2022). The first band, with a maximum at 631 nm, originates from the pool of Pchlide, which did not undergo immediate photoreduction. The second band, with a maximum at 654 nm, represents Pchlide bound to LPOR within ternary Pchlide–LPOR–NADPH complexes, referred to as photoactive Pchlide (Virgin 1981, Schoefs 2001). This band was no longer visible in the spectra measured after 5 s of illumination with either blue or red light (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating that the whole of this Pchlide pool was converted to Chlide. This confirms that the blue and red light intensities used in our de-etiolation experiments (i.e. 60 μmol photons/m2/s photon flux density) were sufficient to trigger rapid photoreduction of all the photoactive Pchlide.

The emission spectrum of the produced Chlide had a maximum at 690 nm, characteristic of Chlide–LPOR–NADP+ complexes (Schoefs 2001, Schoefs and Franck 2008). The left shoulder of the main Chlide band indicates a persistent release of Chlide from the complexes with LPOR, resulting in the fluorescence maximum at 675 nm (Schoefs 2001). In the case of seedlings de-etiolated for 15 min under red light, the blue shift of the Chlide fluorescence band was nearly complete, with the maximum stabilizing at 677 nm (Fig. 4A). The presence of only a slight right-side asymmetry of this band indicated that the shift was still in progress. Nevertheless, the maximum position remained unchanged during the subsequent 15 min of greening, indicating that this maximum was characteristic for the Chlide released from LPOR complexes. In contrast, in the spectra recorded for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light, a split peak in this wavelength region was observed (Fig. 4B). The differential spectrum clearly revealed that this complex band included two peaks with maxima at 677 nm and 690 nm, visible as positive and negative peaks, respectively (Fig. 4C). These peaks originated from Chlide released from the complexes with LPOR and from those bound in Chlide–LPOR–NADP+ complexes (Schoefs 2001). Interestingly, the latter band was still present in the spectra measured after 30 min of illumination under blue light (Fig. 4C). However, in this case, it was a slightly blue-shifted, suggesting partial disaggregation of Chlide–LPOR complexes (Schoefs 2001). It seems that during the first 15–30 min of de-etiolation, we observe two ongoing pathways of Pchlide photoreduction: one faster, involving a rapid release of Chlide, and the other, with the exchange of NADP+ to NADPH, followed by a release of Chlide and requiring a longer time (Lebedev and Timko 1998). The first, faster photocycle seems highly promoted in seedlings de-etiolated under red light, while both are activated in those greening under blue light. So far, there is no data directly comparing the activity of isolated LPOR under blue and red light. Nevertheless, Kósa & Böddi (Kósa and Böddi 2012) observed the dominance of 675 nm Chlide form when they used red laser lines (632.8 nm or 654 nm); the occurrence of 688 nm Chlide form was found for high flux densities (360 µmol/m2/s, i.e. six times higher than we used).

In addition to Chlide fluorescence bands, a Pchlide fluorescence band with a maximum at 631 nm was observed in both red and blue light-exposed seedlings and de-etiolated for either 15 or 30 min (Fig. 4 A, B). The differential spectra indicated a higher relative amount of Pchlide in seedlings de-etiolated under red light (Fig. 4C). This effect was also noticeable after just 5 s of light exposure, although the differences in the respective spectra were very subtle (Supplementary Fig. S5, compare blue and red lines). The intensity of the 631–632 nm band decreased with de-etiolation time, it was completely invisible after 6 h of greening.

The release of Chlide from the complexes with LPOR is the starting point for the assembly of photosynthetic complexes, provided that their protein components are already present in the cell. Free Chlide can be esterified directly into Chl a or became a substrate for Chl b synthesis (Schoefs 2001, Solymosi and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2021). The synthesis and assembly of the functional photosynthetic complexes occurs within a dozen hours (Pipitone et al. 2021, Liang et al. 2022) and can be monitored with fluorescence spectroscopy at 77 K (Kowalewska et al. 2016, Leonowicz et al. 2018).

The fluorescence emission spectra of mature chloroplasts are complex and, in general, encompass two bands originating from complexes of PSII with its antennae, located around 683 nm, and from PSI with its antennae, located around 730 nm respectively (Andreeva et al. 2003, Yamamoto et al. 2013). The spectra, measured at 77 K, for seedlings harvested after 3, 6 and 24 h of greening under red or blue light are shown in Fig. 4A and B. After 3 h of de-etiolation under both light regimes, the main fluorescence band had its maximum at 679 nm (Fig. 4A, B), although the corresponding differential spectrum revealed some differences (Fig. 4C). In particular, the presence of a negative band around 730 nm in the differential spectrum suggests that the assembly of PSI complexes and/or the PSII-to-PSI energy transfer wase more efficient under blue light. Nevertheless, the effects of greening under both light regimes were almost indistinguishable at this de-etiolation point.

The position of the fluorescence maximum (679 nm) detected after 3 h of de-etiolation was slightly red-shifted compared to that of the released Chlide/Chl (677 nm), indicating that the process of Chl incorporation into the photosynthetic complexes had already begun. This band continued shifting, and ultimately, after 24 h of greening, under both red and blue light, it was observed in the range of 682–683 nm, corresponding to the characteristic position of mature PSII (Andreeva et al. 2003, Yamamoto et al. 2013). At the same time, in the PSI fluorescence range, there was an increase in the intensity of the characteristic band and its slight red shift to 730–732 nm. Once again the maximum position observed for samples that were de-etiolated for 24 h under both light regimes agree with characteristics of the mature PSI (Andreeva et al. 2003, Yamamoto et al. 2013).

Analysis of the differential spectra (Fig. 4C) reveals more profound differences between de-etiolation under blue and red light. Specifically, a higher fluorescence intensity of PSI complexes was observed after 6 h of greening under blue light compared to red light. Additionally, there are some variations in the PSII fluorescence band recorded for 24-h de-etiolated samples, likely reflecting different relative intensities of fluorescence components originating from PSII antenna complexes. These differences suggest that the photosynthetic activities of samples de-etiolated in blue and red light may vary.

Blue light enhances the assembly of a fully functioning photosynthetic apparatus

The assembly of the photosynthetic machinery accompanies the light-induced transformation of etioplasts to chloroplasts. Monitoring of the emerging photosynthetic activity during de-etiolation was achieved through the analysis of in vivo Chl fluorescence, a non-invasive method for assessing the photosynthetic activity of PSII (Roháček et al. 2008, Murchie and Lawson 2013).

Maximal PSII quantum yield

In our experiment, Arabidopsis seedlings reached measurable Chl fluorescence levels in vivo after a minimum of 3 h of de-etiolation under both blue and red light conditions. The maximal PSII quantum yield (QYmax), also well known as Fv/Fm parameter, increased throughout the de-etiolation period. (Table 1). QYmax values were higher for de-etiolation under blue-light, and after 24 h, it exceeded 0.8, a typical value for healthy, mature chloroplasts (Björkman and Demmig 1987). In contrast, QYmax level was significantly lower in the case of de-etiolation under red light and did not reach the optimal value within the investigated greening period.

The earlier studies on de-etiolation of Arabidopsis seedlings under white light (40 μmol photons/m2/s) showed an increase in QYmax from 0.5 at 4 h to above 0.8 after 14 h of de-etiolation (Pipitone et al. 2021), which is similar to what we observed in seedlings illuminated with blue light (Table 1).

Induction of photosynthetic parameters under actinic light followed by dark relaxation

A comprehensive understanding of the operation of the photosynthetic apparatus can be achieved by examining fluorescence induction under actinic light and subsequent reversion to a dark-adapted state. This experiment was conducted using white actinic light of different photon flux densities: weak (i.e. 170 µmol/m2/s), which was already twice the intensity of the light in the growth chamber, (i.e. 60 µmol/m2/s), and strong (i.e. 2,100 µmol/m2/s), comparable to full sunlight illumination (e.g. Varella et al. 2011). This approach revealed the effect of de-etiolation under monochromatic light (blue or red) on photosynthetic performance under both weak and strong light conditions.

The results obtained with the use of weak actinic light are shown in Fig. 5. First, we focused at QY parameter (known also as ΦPSII), which represents an instantaneous PSII quantum yield during light adaptation followed by dark relaxation (Genty et al. 1989) and serves as an indicator of the effective PSII quantum yield. The QY reaches values between 0 and the QYmax (Pfündel 1998, Demmig-Adams et al. 2012); values of 0.6–0.7 were reported for mature Arabidopsis plants (Prinzenberg et al. 2020). In our experiments, an increase in the QY parameter was indeed observed when the weak actinic light was turned on, regardless of the treatments applied during seedling de-etiolation. What distinguishes the seedling cohorts was the kinetics of changes and the maximum QY level reached in the light-adapted state. The highest observed values of this parameter (about 0.55), similar for seedlings de-etiolated under red and blue light, were observed after 24 h of de-etiolation. The QY levels measured in seedlings that were de-etiolated for 3 and 6 h were similar for the same light regime, reaching about 0.25 for red light and 0.4 for blue light. Furthermore, the kinetics of the observed changes was similar—the maximal increase of the QY parameter occurred within the first 30 s after turning on the actinic light, and then the plateau was reached. This suggests that the application of weak actinic white light improves electron transfer downstream of PSII in seedlings de-etiolated under both monochromatic lights.

The 6-day-old etiolated seedlings were de-etiolated under 60 μmol photons/m2s of blue or red light for 3, 6 or 24 h. Actinic light intensity of 170 μmol photons/m2/s (weak light) was turned on for 70 s (indicated as the yellowish rectangle on the figures). Subsequently, a 100 s relaxation period followed to return to the dark adapted state. Average values with respective standard errors are shown for three biological replicates; each replicate consisted of about 40 seedlings.

Another parameter derived from the analysis of the induction-relaxation experiment is NPQ, which stands for non-photochemical quenching (Horton and Ruban 1992), providing information about thermal dissipation of excess energy absorbed by photosynthetic antennae (Croce and Van Amerongen 2014). NPQ kinetics showed similar shapes regardless of the color of light used during seedling de-etiolation (Fig. 5). Interestingly, the increase in NPQ values measured under weak actinic light was only transient, lasting for the first 30 s, followed by a decrease of this parameter. The maximal NPQ achieved varied among the curves. NPQ levels were higher for 3 and 24 h of de-etiolation under blue light compared to red light, specifically 0.4 versus 0.6 at 3 h, and 1 versus 1.2 at 24 h, respectively. Interestingly, after 6 h of de-etiolation, the maximum NPQ values were similar (around 0.7) for both light regimes. The occurrence of an NPQ maximum in light means that the mechanism of thermal dissipation of excess excitation energy is quickly induced and then reduced, which indicates an increase in the energy utilized to power electron transport. Obviously, after turning off the actinic light, a further decrease in NPQ was observed.

The qP parameter, representing the coefficient of photochemical quenching and providing information about the utilization of absorbed energy to power electron transport (Horton et al. 1996) also showed rapid growth during the first 30 s of illumination under weak actinic light (Fig. 5). After that time, differences among the samples were observed. In the case of seedlings de-etiolated for 3 h and 6 h under red light, the maximal qP stayed below 0.6, which was lower than the level reached by seedlings exposed to blue light. Furthermore, a slight increase in this parameter was noticed for seedlings de-etiolated for 24 h under both red and blue light, with both curves showing high similarity. Additionally, seedlings de-etiolated under red light exhibited relatively slow qP dark-relaxation. The qP parameter estimates the fraction of opened PSII reaction centers with its acceptor site (QA) oxidized and ready to participate in electron transport. In this context, the participation of opened PSII reaches lower levels in seedlings that were de-etiolated under red light for a short period (3 and 6 h) compared to those exposed to blue light. Additionally, the qP in these seedlings remained below 1 during dark relaxation, indicating a decreased capacity for reoxidation of the plastoquinone (PQ) pool in darkness. Conversely, after 24 h of de-etiolation, the fraction of open PSII was similar for seedlings from both light regimes.

Biphasic character of QY, NPQ and qP induction curves obtained for weak actinic light was well synchronized in time (Fig. 5). These parameters rapidly increased during the first 30 s of illumination, reflecting the rapid initiation of the electron transport chain and dissipation of excess energy in these light conditions. In turn, the observed decrease in NPQ in subsequent seconds of illumination reveals further adjustment of the efficiency of the electron transport chain to light conditions, likely associated with PSI activity, the formation of ATP and NADPH, and the reactions of the Calvin–Benson cycle. Altogether, it appears that all investigated seedlings were ready to perform photosynthesis under weak actinic light conditions. The Rfd parameter, which represents the relative fluorescence decrease ratio during actinic light illumination, reveals differences among the investigated seedlings both in terms of de-etiolation time and the color of light used to induce the de-etiolation process (Fig. 5). During the initial 3 h of de-etiolation, higher Rfd values were obtained for seedlings from blue light, while those from red light reached these values after 6 h. However, after 24 h, similarity was observed between seedlings from red and blue light. Among the discussed parameters, Rfd correlates most strongly with net photosynthesis in mature plants, serving as both an empirical estimator of plant vitality and a stress indicator (Lichtenthaler et al. 2005).

In the case of measurements of the induction of photosynthetic activity under strong actinic light (i.e. 2,100 µmol/m2/s), very low values of the QY parameter (below 0.1) were observed (Fig. 6), regardless of the light color applied during seedling de-etiolation. Almost negligible values of the effective PSII quantum yield indicate a low efficiency of electron transport in developing thylakoids under strong light. The QY values observed after turning off the actinic light were much lower than the respective QYmax, especially for seedlings de-etiolated under red light for 3 or 6 h, indicating a slow dark relaxation of the photosynthetic complexes, primarily due to the decreased capacity of the PQ pool reoxidation in darkness. This observation suggests that strong actinic light-induced processes that are at least partially irreversible, likely due to the still not fully assembled photosynthetic machinery. From this perspective, the adaptation of the photosynthetic machinery to strong light appeared slightly improved in seedlings de-etiolated under blue light.

The 6-day-old etiolated seedlings were de-etiolated under 60 μmol photons/m2/s of blue or red light for 3, 6 or 24 h. Actinic light intensity of 2,100 μmol photons/m2/s (strong light) was turned on for 70 s (indicated as the yellowish rectangle on the figures). Subsequently, a 100-s relaxation period followed to return to the dark adapted state. The NPQ values induced by strong actinic light were higher in seedlings that underwent longer de-etiolation periods under blue light, and after 24 h of de-etiolation, they reached the levels found for mature A. thaliana Col-0 plants (Rungrat et al. 2016). This again reveals that the chloroplasts formed under blue light were equipped with a more perfectly assembled photosynthetic machinery. The NPQ values measured for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light for either 3 or 24 h surpassed respective NPQ values obtained for seedlings exposed to red light. Similar NPQ induction curves were obtained for seedlings de-etiolated under red light for 24 or 6 h, albeit higher than those observed for seedlings de-etiolated for 3 h. Intriguingly, after 6 h of de-etiolation, the NPQ induction curves for both light regimes (blue and red light) showed striking similarity. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that NPQ relaxation in darkness remained incomplete in seedlings de-etiolated under blue light for 3 or 6 h. This cannot be excluded, that the observed effect results from activation of slow NPQ components, such as the zeaxanthin-dependent qZ component (Dall’Osto et al. 2005), the antenna-quenching component (qH) (Malnoë et al. 2018) as well as a photoinhibitory quenching (qI), which is associated with closed reaction centers of PSII caused by photoinhibition (Krause 1988, Ware et al. 2015, Rungrat et al. 2016).

Similar to the experiments with the use of weak actinic light, all parameters measured under strong actinic light increased during the first 30 s of illumination and then reached a plateau. When strong actinic light was applied, the qP values were relatively low (below 0.2), compared to at least 0.5 at weak actinic light (Figs. 6 vs 5) for seedlings from both blue and red light. This means a small pool of opened PSII engaged in electron transport. During dark relaxation, the qP parameter almost immediately increased to its maximal value, close to those measured in the experiment with weak actinic light only in seedlings de-etiolated under blue light (Figs. 6 vs 5). Conversely, the qP stayed below 1 in seedlings de-etiolated under red light, with slight improvement of this parameter for longer de-etiolation period. When considering the same de-etiolation period, the Rfd parameter was higher for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light, indicating again a better efficiency of photosynthesis under strong light in these plants (Lichtenthaler et al. 2005). Interestingly, no differences were found between seedlings de-etiolated under red light for 6 or 24 h, while in the case of seedlings exposed to blue light, a further increase of Rfd was observed between 6 and 24 h of de-etiolation.

Seedling response to rapid increase of photon flux density

To gain insight into the state of the photosynthetic apparatus in de-etiolated seedlings and to learn more about their fitness, we examined the so-called ‘light curves’ allowing to check the response of photosynthetic parameters to increasing photon flux density (White and Critchley 1999). Photochemical and non-photochemical quenching parameters, qP and NPQ, respectively, calculated based on the light curves obtained for seedlings de-etiolated for 6 or 24 h are shown in Fig. 7. Almost no differences in qP and NPQ parameters were observed between seedlings de-etiolated for 24 h under both red and blue light, and those de-etiolated under blue light for 6 h, except the initial points. This suggests that after 24 h of de-etiolation under red light and already after 6 h of de-etiolation under blue light, the seedling chloroplasts developed mechanisms that allow them to quickly adapt to increasing light intensity. In turn, the range of qP and NPQ changes observed in seedlings de-etiolated under red light for 6 h was the smallest, with NPQ not exceeding 1.5 and qP remaining above 0.4.

Fluorescence response of seedlings to rapid increase of photon flux density. The parameters: NPQ and qP (mean ± SE; n = 4) were calculated from light curves measured for white actinic light (30–2,100 µmol photons/m2/s) for seedlings de-etiolated under blue or red light (60 µmol photons/m2/s) for 6 or 24 h, as indicated in the legend. Each replicate (n) consisted of about 40 seedlings.

These data reveal that 6 h of de-etiolation under blue light is sufficient to assemble antennae capable of dissipating excess absorbed energy to a level similar to that of seedlings de-etiolated for 24 h. Furthermore, seedlings de-etiolated for 24 h under red light exhibited the same quenching parameters as seedlings de-etiolated under blue light for 6 h. Similar relationships were observed for photochemical quenching, representing the photosynthetic activity of PSII. Interestingly, seedlings de-etiolated for 6 h under red light showed lower NPQ values and higher qP values, implying a shift in the utilization of absorbed energy toward photochemical processes in PSII.

Regulation of the expression of genes involved in building of photosynthetic complexes

The studies described earlier, performed using fluorescence methods, revealed differences in de-etiolation kinetics under red and blue light, especially for seedlings de-etiolated for 3 and 6 h. Many indications suggest that the assembly of the photosynthetic apparatus occurs more rapidly in blue light than in red light. The efficient assembly of the photosynthetic apparatus requires co-ordinated expression of multiple genes encoding chloroplast proteins absent in etioplasts but essential for building photosynthetic complexes (Armarego-Marriott et al. 2019, Pipitone et al. 2021), as well as Chl synthesis, which is halted during seedling cultivation in darkness (Solymosi and Schoefs 2010, Jedynak et al. 2022). These processes are essential during seedling de-etiolation and holds special importance because the production of excessive proteins, which is energy-consuming, would become a limiting factor for dark-germinated and dark-grown seedlings. Since photosynthesis is still inactive, plant survival depends on the materials accumulated in seeds. However, the activation of photosynthesis and harnessing light energy as soon as light becomes available, give the plant an advantage in a competitive environment.

The synthesis of Chls and proteins that constitute both photosystems, including their antenna complexes, must be tightly correlated. The specific regulatory roles of particular light wavelengths in these processes are hardly understood. However, akin to other developmental processes, one may expect that blue and red light may regulate these processes differently. It is also important to note that both blue and red light are effectively absorbed by Pchlide and Chl (e.g. Fiedor et al. 2003, Mysliwa-Kurdziel et al. 2004). Therefore, in addition to their regulatory role, these wavelengths may also trigger the LPOR-catalyzed reaction, as well as power photosynthesis. In this study, we focused our attention on seedlings de-etiolated for 3 and 6 h under blue or red light to examine the impact of these light wavelengths on the expression of selected genes compared to etiolated seedlings. First, we investigated genes associated with Chl biosynthesis (Fig. 8), specifically those encoding different forms of LPOR (i.e. LPOR-A, LPOR-B and LPOR-C genes) and Chl oxygenase (i.e. CAO gene), which is necessary for the production of Chl b via the oxygenation of Chlide a (Espineda et al. 1999).

Each bar corresponds to an average of six (A–D) or four (E–H) biological replicates. Each replicate consisted of about 120 seedlings. Error bars show standard error. Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences between samples (*P = 0.01–0.05; **P = 0.001–0.01, ***P < 0.001, calculated with Kruskal–Wallis test).

It is widely known that the mRNA levels of LPOR-A and LPOR-B genes are high in etiolated seedlings to ensure sufficient protein content necessary for the efficient conversion of Pchlide into Chlide, thus preventing photobleaching (Lebedev et al. 1995, Sperling et al. 1997). After just 3 h of de-etiolation with blue light, we found a significant decrease in the levels of LPOR-A mRNA (Fig. 8A), which is consistent with previous literature data using white light (Armstrong et al. 1995). This reduction remains evident after 6 h of de-etiolation (Fig. 8E). A similar tendency was observed under red light, although these differences were not statistically significant. The high levels of LPOR-A and LPOR-B in etiolated seedlings are maintained by EIN3 and PIF1 transcription factors, which organize into a homotetrameric form with high DNA affinities in darkness (Wu et al. 2020). Different processes are involved in the switch of LPOR-A and LPOR-B expression during de-etiolation. Light induces the expression of miP1a and miP1b microproteins, which bind to PIFs and EIN3 blocking their oligomerization (Wu et al. 2020). Additionally, red light activates PHYB, which binds to EIN3 and leads to its rapid degradation (Shi et al. 2016). As a result, EIN3 and PIF1-dependent transcription of LPOR-A is inhibited by light. However, the complete set of signaling pathway components responsible for the control of LPOR-A mRNA levels during de-etiolation under blue and red light and the details of their functioning are not yet fully understood. Blue light-specific pathways, at least under the conditions used, appear to be more effective than red light-specific ones in reducing LPOR-A mRNA levels (Fig. 8). Less is known about the mechanism of LPOR-B regulation by light (Yuan et al. 2017). In our study, a significant increase of LPOR-B expression was observed during prolonged de-etiolation under red light (Fig. 8B, F). Interestingly, over shorter period, a slight but statistically insignificant, transient decrease in the LPOR-B transcript level was observed in seedlings de-etiolated under blue light (compare Fig. 8B and F). A similar transient decrease of LPOR-B mRNA during de-etiolation under white light has been previously observed (Armstrong et al. 1995, Su et al. 2001, Masuda et al. 2003). Our results suggest that this effect may be either specific to the blue light regulatory pathway or that activation of this pathway is somehow delayed compared to that activated by red light. The latter possibility is inferred from the observation that the LPOR-B mRNA level in seedlings de-etiolated under red light for 3 h remained similar to that in etiolated seedlings, and was even slightly higher after 6 h of de-etiolation. However, to confirm this hypothesis, more detailed studies are needed, covering more time points of de-etiolation.

The levels of LPOR-C mRNA increased over time under blue light conditions (Fig. 8C, G). Similar tendency was observed under red light; however, it was not statistically significant. The light-induced increase in LPOR-C mRNA level has already been shown to result from the dissociation of JMJ17 from the LPOR-C promoter and the binding of the HY5 transcription factor (Toledo-Ortiz et al. 2014, Islam et al. 2021). The mechanism involves CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1)-SUPPRESSOR OF PHYTOCHROME A-105 (COP1-SPA) E3 via HY5 (Osterlund and Deng 1998), blue light-activated CRY1 via SPA and COP1 signaling (Lian et al. 2011, Liu et al. 2011) and blue light-induced SPA2 degradation in a PHYA-dependent manner (Chen et al. 2015).

It is worth noting that blue light clearly influenced the amounts of LPOR-A and LPOR-C transcripts during de-etiolation. The effect of red light was not statistically significant, but the same trend was observed as for blue light, i.e. a decrease in the level of LPOR-A mRNA and an increase in the level of LPOR-C mRNA. It is possible that the signaling pathways dependent on blue and red light activate the same effectors influencing the expression of both genes, but they act with different strength, with a clear dominance of those dependent on blue light.

Both blue and red light significantly stimulated the expression of the CAO gene during de-etiolation (Fig. 8D, H). The effect was statistically significant after 3 h but not after 6 h of de-etiolation period. While the trend suggests that red light stimulates this increase more strongly at both time points, we were unable to demonstrate statistical significance. The CAO enzyme plays a crucial regulatory role in the biosynthesis of Chl b (Espineda et al. 1999), which is essential for the formation and stability of light-harvesting complexes (LHC) and the stacking of grana (Tanaka et al. 2001, Harper et al. 2004). By modulating Chl a/b ratio, plants can adjust the abundance of LHC and the supramolecular structure of photosynthetic complexes, thereby adapting to fluctuating light conditions in the environment (Tanaka and Tanaka 2011, Voitsekhovskaja and Tyutereva 2015). During de-etiolation, Chl b and LHC proteins need to be synthesized in parallel. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of genes encoding selected Chl a/b-binding proteins, specifically Lhca4 (encoding the LHCA4 subunit of LHCI) and Lhcb2.2 (encoding one of the subunits of LHCII trimers; Fig. 9A, B, E, F). We observed the induction of these genes by both red and blue light, with a slightly stronger effect in each case under red light (Fig. 8D, F). However, this effect appeared statistically significant only for red light. Blue light had statistically significant impact only on the mRNA level of Lhcb2.2 after 3 h of de-etiolation. The similar effect of red light, more clearly visible after the shorter illumination period, suggests that the expression of CAO, LHCA4 and LHCB2.2 during de-etiolation may be controlled by the same pathway. Although there are no statistically significant differences in CAO, Lhcb2.2 and Lhca4 mRNA levels between seedlings de-etiolated under blue and red light, a trend toward slightly higher levels of all transcripts under red light illumination was observed at both time points investigated.

Further, we analyzed the expression of chloroplast-encoded PSBD gene, coding subunit D of photosystem II (Fig. 9C, G). This gene is regulated by five PEP-recognized promoters, including the blue-light-responsive promoter (BLRP), which is recognized by SIG5 transcription factor (Tsunoyama et al. 2004). During de-etiolation, blue light perceived by CRY1, CRY2 and PHYA, induces the expression of SIG5, leading to initiation of PSBD expression (Thum et al. 2001, Tsunoyama et al. 2004, Hwang et al. 2022). Based on this literature, we had expected that blue light would be more effective in upregulating PSBD mRNA in our model. We found only a slight increase of the level of this transcript under blue light (Fig. 9C, G). Even though, the values were not significantly different, they suggest the presence of a general trend that may become more pronounced as de-etiolation progresses. The de-etiolation conditions used in the study did not affect the mRNA levels of the psaA gene (Fig. 9D, H). The amount of this transcript was comparable in etiolated seedlings and those irradiated with blue or red light at both examined time points. In line with that, PSI fluorescence band also became clearly visible after 6 h of de-etiolation (Fig. 4B).

Our results suggest that the observed differences in the development of chloroplasts under blue and red light are accompanied by only subtle differences in the expression of genes encoding components of the photosynthetic apparatus. Therefore, changes in the mRNA profile do not directly translate into the observed phenotypes, suggesting the importance of other factors. We also aimed to verify our conclusions by checking the levels of proteins encoded by the studied genes using Western blot technique. However, at all-time points studied, the protein levels remained below the sensitivity of the method (Supplementary II.2 and Fig. S6).

Chlorophyll and carotenoid synthesis varies during greening under blue and red light

Results presented so far suggest that the level of photosynthetic pigments may vary under different light regimes, especially at 3–6 h de-etiolation time. To verify this hypothesis, we compared the pigment composition for seedlings de-etiolated under blue or red light at these two aforementioned time points. First, the most expected finding is the increase of total Chl amount between 3rd and 6th hour of de-etiolation (Fig. 10A). During this time, total Chl content increased by three times for de-etiolation under blue light and about twice for that under red light. With lower Chl amount, found for seedlings de-etiolated for 3 h under red light, we may postulate stimulation of Chlide esterification leading to Chl synthesis by blue light. In line with that assumption is the similar content of Chlide for both light regimes (Fig. 10B). The content of Chlide increased between 3rd and 6th hour of greening, very similarly in blue and red light, suggesting that the light color is not a regulating factor here. Faster Chlide to Chl conversion under blue light is evident when analyzing Chlide to Chl ratio (Fig. 10C). After 3 h of de-etiolation, the Chl pool contained Chl a and Chl b, in the ratio of 0.8–1.2 in seedlings exposed to red and blue light, respectively (Fig. 10D). These values are quite low in comparison to a typical for mature plant. Lower Chl a/b ratio was found for greening under red light, indicating that red light stimulates the reaction catalyzed by CAO. This is in line with the above-mentioned increase of CAO mRNA in seedlings from the same light conditions (Fig. 8H). Even though the differences in CAO mRNA levels were small and did not reach statistical significance, it is still worth noting the consistent trend between the mRNA levels and the relative amount of Chl b.

The cumulative content of carotenoids also increased between 3rd and 6th hour of greening. However, the changes were very small compared to the clear induction of Chl synthesis. Slightly higher carotenoid content was noticed in case of de-etiolation performed under blue light (Fig. 11A) that was similar to observations of the Chl content (Fig. 10A), although the effect was much smaller. The amount of carotenoids, especially in comparison to Chl content, was high (Fig. 11B), what is reminiscent of etiolated state (Jedynak et al. 2022). As expected, while de-etiolation is proceeding, the carotenoids/Chl ratio decreases (Fig. 11B). This process is promoted by blue light.

We also verified, whereas the sub-pathways of carotenoids synthesis, namely the α,β branch leading to lutein synthesis and the β,β branch leading to violaxanthin via β-caroten (Cazzonelli et al. 2009), are regulated by light. Indeed, we found that relative Lu to Vx content is higher for greening under red light (Fig. 11C). Bearing in mind that lutein and then violaxanthin are the most abundant carotenoids in etiolated A. thaliana, and usually lutein exceeds violaxanthin or their content are similar (Park et al. 2002, Myśliwa Kurdziel et al. 2012, Bykowski et al. 2020, Jedynak et al. 2022), it becomes evident that blue light promotes β,β branch of carotenoid synthesis during de-etiolation. Interestingly, light regime also influences conversion of β car to Vx. This process was much more efficient for seedlings de-etiolated under blue light (Fig. 11D).

It is worth to note that the calculated standard deviation for most measurements was much higher for the 3 h de-etiolation time point than for the 6 h one. We may hypothesize that, in the case of pigment synthesis, the 3 h time period might be close to a critical point when the induction or stimulation of the involved synthesis pathways occurs. Therefore, at this point, the variations between seedlings might be greater than when the process has already been running for some time and has reached a steady-state.

Summary of findings from all performed experiments

Even though the process of de-etiolation of angiosperm seedlings has been studied for over half a century (early studies are well summarized by Virgin 1981, Sundqvist and Dahlin 1997, Solymosi and Aronsson 2013), many aspects of this process, especially regarding its regulation, remain unexplained. The process is inherently complex and multidimensional, regulated at various levels encompassing morphological, structural, ultrastructural and molecular changes (Armarego-Marriott et al. 2020). Previous research has primarily focused on seedlings of diverse plant species, including wheat, barley, rice, peas, beans and tobacco, with relatively less attention given to A. thaliana. Notably, these species harbor varying numbers of genes encoding light-dependent LPOR (Gabruk and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2020), a pivotal photoenzyme initiating de-etiolation and maintaining Chl biosynthesis. Moreover, comparing results across studies is challenging due to variations in the light composition used for de-etiolation, often employing white light with incomparable spectra.

In the present study, we compared the de-etiolation process in Arabidopsis seedlings exposed to red or blue light of equal photon flux densities. Our investigation primarily focused on dynamics of photosynthetic apparatus development and the structural transformation from etioplasts to chloroplasts. While detailed discussions of specific findings are provided in the chapters above, this section provides a concise summary in the proposed scheme of blue and red light de-etiolation pathways (Fig. 12).

Simplified scheme of the processes, important during the first 24 h of de-etiolation and their stimulation by light color. All the processes are observed in seedlings de-etiolated under blue or red light. The observed stimulation by a particular light regime is indicated by blue/red colored light bulb. Note, that indication of stimulation does mean that the process is still present under second light regime, only occurring with slower rate. LPOR—light-dependent Pchlide: NADPH oxidoreductase, all isoforms; PLB: prolamellar bodies; Pchlide: protochlorophyllide a; Chlide a: chlorophyllide a; Chlide b: chlorophyllide b; PSI: photosystem I; PSII: photosystem II; LHCII: light harvesting complex II; Lut: luteine; Vx: violaxanthin; β-car: β-carotene.

Both red and blue light used in our experiment caused a complete photoreduction of photoactive Pchlide, i.e. Pchlide that is bound within Pchlide:LPOR:NADPH complexes accumulated in etiolated seedlings (Supplementary Fig. S5). What is more, all the data presented earlier allow us to conclude that there is no separate component of the greening process dependent solely on either red or blue light. It is widely accepted, that photo-dependent pathways in plants are triggered by a set of photoreceptors, but there is no general ‘white light (equally blue and red) triggered’ photoreceptor. Therefore, we may conclude that the PHY- or CRY-dependent pathways are used to modulate the de novo biosynthesis of photosynthetic components rather than initiating the de-etiolation process at the very beginning. In line with this, there are observations of triple photoreceptor mutant (phyAphyBcry1) grown either in white, blue or red light (Neff and Chory 1998). These plants, showing strongly hampered growth and severe phenotypic defects, still turn green, albeit with Chl levels much lower than those in WT plants. A particularly low level of Chl (around nine times lower than in WT) was found in phyAphyBcry1 seedlings grown under blue light, while in red light grown seedlings it reached almost 30% of the WT level. This still leaves room for activity of other phytochrome isoforms (i.e. PHYC-PHYE). However, the observation of the de-etiolation process in the quintuple phytochrome mutant contradicts this possibility (Hu et al. 2013).

What then remains to act is a Pchlide, bound within Pchlide:LPOR:NADPH complex (so-called photo-active Pchlide). In a sense, the molecules of photoactive Pchlide serve as photoreceptors in the de-etiolation process. However, given that the Pchlide photoconversion reaction is irreversible, it should be noted that the definition of a photoreceptor is not fully met, as with each subsequent turnover of LPOR, a different Pchlide molecule acts as a photoreceptor. Pchlide absorbs both red and blue light, although the extinction coefficient is generally higher in a blue region of light spectrum (e.g. Mysliwa-Kurdziel et al. 2004). Taking into account the light sources used in our experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1), Pchlide extinction in blue is four times higher than in red. Even for that great difference in probability of photon absorption between blue and red light, all of photoactive Pchlide was converted to Chlide in a single 5 s flash experiment regardless of the light color used (Supplementary Fig. S5). This means that the amount of available photons was sufficient to saturate LPOR activity. Nonetheless, the energy of a blue photon is about 1.44 times higher than that of a red one, and the absorption of the blue photon excites Pchlide to a higher energy state compared to the absorption of the red photon, with the possibility of dissipation of this excess energy as thermal radiation which may slightly tunes a protein/PLB microenvironment. One must add to this the energy that was absorbed by the Pchlide molecules not bound to LPOR, and not consumed for Pchlide to Chlide conversion, which is also, at least partially, dissipated in the form of heat. Hence, the energy acquired by the sample at the beginning of de-etiolation process is significantly higher when exposed to blue light. This may influence the speed of the process of disaggregation of LPOR oligomers as well as the release of Chlide from the Chlide-LPOR complexes, which are key steps in PLB transformation into thylakoids (Schoefs 2001, Nguyen et al. 2021). Such alternations could lead to measurable changes that we observed in microscopic images (Fig. 2). Additionally, PLB transformation depends on various factors, including the CURT1 protein. The involvement of this protein is not being considered here, as it is already produced in darkness in etiolated seedlings and curt1 knockout mutant has already abnormal PLB morphology (Armbruster et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2022).

The color of the light used during de-etiolation has a significant impact on the fate of Chlide. Blue light promotes its esterification, leading to the synthesis of Chl a, while red light slightly favors the Chlide a to Chlide b conversion, resulting in a lower Chl a/b ratio (Fig. 10). Red light stimulates Chl b synthesis and, along with the relatively high carotenoid content in etiolated cotyledons, it could promote the assembly of LHCII (confirmed by the higher expression of genes encoding LHC proteins and CAO, respectively, Fig. 8). However, PSII seems in a worse, although functional, state since it did not reach QYmax level characteristic for healthy plants (Table 1). The relatively low expression of PSBD may be in line with the PSII lower performance (Fig. 9). The scenario might be valid until new biosynthetic processes are started. Additionally, at this stage, the role of LPOR-A isoform decreases in favor of LPOR-B, and especially LPOR-C. Although the enzymatic mechanisms of all three LPOR isoforms have been shown to be similar, some differences in LPOR-C efficiency have been noted (Gabruk et al. 2015, Gabruk and Mysliwa-Kurdziel 2020).

In terms of carotenoids, their synthesis pathway is also differently regulated under blue and red light conditions. Blue light stimulates the synthesis of β,β carotenoids, including violaxanthin—the substrate of the xanthophyll cycle, which is involved in the fast NPQ components (Demmig et al. 1987, Demmig-Adams 1990, Demmig-Adams et al. 2014). This may explain why we observed efficient NPQ earlier during de-etiolation under blue than under red light (Fig. 7; Niyogi et al. 1998). Carotenoids are already present in etiolated seedlings, and in the initial stages of de-etiolation, there is a large excess of carotenoid pool in relation to the Chl pool. Despite this, during the first stages of de-etiolation, de novo carotenoid synthesis begins, resulting in an additional increase in the total carotenoid content by about 12% between 3 h and 6 h of illumination, regardless of the light color used (Fig. 11). Blue light had a slightly stronger stimulating effect. It may result from the activation of a photoreceptor, possibly through a cryptochrome-dependent pathway. Another possibility is that carotenoid synthesis is stimulated by the accumulation of Chl, ensuring the maintenance of the optimal carotenoid to Chl ratio. Since Chl synthesis is more efficient under blue light, the carotenoid synthesis it induces would be also more efficient. In general, it is challenging to clearly distinguish between these possibilities; a lot of research shows interdependence in pigment production and their impact on to the expression of proteins forming the photosynthetic apparatus (Herrin et al. 1992). The most probable scenario involves a complex regulation dependent on multicomponent feedback loops, with pigments affecting protein levels and vice versa. Since photosynthetic complexes bind both Chls and carotenoids, the levels of their component proteins are likely to influence synthesis pathways of both type of pigments.

The still open question is, when de novo biosynthesis must begin, or—in other words, how much thylakoids can be built by reusing of lipids, proteins and pigments already present in the cell (Wietrzynski and Engel 2021). The basal level of greening, found for phy and cry mutants suggests, that there is a light triggered, photoreceptor independent signal, leading to the gradual, although slow, synthesis of Chls and other chloroplast components (Strasser et al. 2010). This leads to the conclusion, that the basic signal inducing the further biosynthetic processes might be Chlide/Chl acting as components of feedback loops inducing expression of proteins forming photosynthetic machinery. In turn the excess of these proteins, in the absence of available Chls to bind, stimulates Chl biosynthesis.

De novo biosynthesis of components needed during chloroplast assembly might be still supplied by spare materials, present in a seed endosperm. Effective and efficient acceleration of biosynthesis should, however, be enabled by the newly acquired, during greening, ability to photosynthesize. In our experiments, the seedlings start to photosynthesize after 3 h of greening (Figs. 5–7); the efficiency of this process increases as the illumination time increases. Our results suggest also, that some kind of checkpoint(s) occurs between 3rd and 6th hour of greening. It may simply be insufficient performance of newly built-up photosynthetic machinery, resulting in too low ΔpH and proton motive force, as the consequence of the lack of well-formed thylakoid which results in a relatively low level of harvested energy. The difference in formation of thylakoids is evident based on microscopy images at 3 h and, to a lower extent, at 6 h of de-etiolation (Fig. 1). It corresponds to results of Pipitone et al. (Pipitone et al. 2021), who observed a start of drastic increase in accumulation of photosynthetic proteins after 4 h of de-etiolation, what corresponded to a significant growth of chloroplast volume. Our observation of chloroplast morphology led to the same conclusions, with young chloroplasts becoming clearly distinguishable after 6 h of greening and then increasing in volume (Fig. 3). Pipitone et al. (Pipitone et al. 2021) suggested, based on a Western-blot approach, that PSII and its antennae are produced first, while the accumulation of PSI (PsaC and PSaD proteins) is delayed and occurs until approximately 12 h of greening. However, they also noted, by mass spectrometry analysis, the early presence (even in etiolated seedlings) of several proteins building PSI (PsaA, PSaB). Unfortunately, we were not able to detect any reliable immunoblot signal in seedlings de-etiolated for either 3 or 6 h (Supplementary Fig S6). On the other hand our 77 K fluorescence data indicate the appearance of PSI-related 730 nm fluorescence emission maximum after only 3 h of greening (Fig. 4). The literature does not provide a clear answer to the question regarding the relationship between the fluorescence intensity of PSI and PSII at 77 K, and the actual content of these complexes in photosynthetic membranes. Based on the available 77 K fluorescence spectra for mature Arabidopsis chloroplasts, which are characterized by PSI:PSII ratio of approximately 1:1 (Fan et al. 2007), it can be assumed that the fluorescence intensity of PSI is about twice as high as that of PSII at 77 K. This suggests that in our study, the optimal PSI:PSII fluorescence ratio is reached after 24 h of greening, while PSI fluorescence is underrepresented before that time. The increase in PSI fluorescence reflects both the synthesis of PSI complexes and the possible transfer of energy absorbed by PSII to PSI, both of which occur during de-etiolation. This should be reflected in observed photosynthetic parameters, especially in NPQ. Under blue light, the optimal PSI:PSII fluorescence ratio seems to be achieved earlier than under red light, which is reflected in NPQ value (Figs. 5–6). When the relative level of PSI fluorescence is closest to optimal PSI:PSII ratio, there is more effective reoxidation of PSII. In this case, PSI simply functions as an electron sink, allowing for faster PSII performance, as indicated by the analysis of photosynthetic activity (Figs. 5–6).

The differences in photosynthetic performance, i.e. under-optimal PSII efficiency, less efficient NPQ and qP, and lower Rfd values, found for red-greened seedlings even after 24 h of illumination, suggest that some component, needed for fine-tuning of photosynthetic machinery to its optimal performance, is blue-light induced, most probably via the cryptochrome-dependent pathway. However, it is difficult to postulate which specific protein is the missing one. Known mutants of regulatory proteins, such as the kinases STN7 and STN8, and the phosphatases that reverse their effect, TAP38 and PBCP (Khuong et al. 2019), are characterized by almost the same values of Fv/Fm as WT plants. The decrease of Fv/Fm value is not observed in mutants of peripherial components of PSI, such as PsaG and PsaK (Varotto et al. 2002). However, downregulation of PsaD, the core protein of PSI necessary for ferredoxin binding and reoxidation of PSI results in a decrease in Fv/Fm to 0.77 (from 0.83) and qP to 0.7 (from 0.95 typical for WT plants; Ihnatowicz et al. 2004). Knockout of the gene encoding the PGR5 protein, involved in PSI reactivation via cyclic electron transfer, also results in a moderate decrease in Fv/Fm (to about 0.7) (Tikkanen et al. 2017). The similarity in photosynthetic parameters between mutant plants described in the literature cited above and those described in our experiments does not directly imply that our greening plants are suffering from PsaD or PGR5 deficiency; rather it simply suggests less functional PSI pool, due to PSI/PSII imbalance or PSI overreduction. The latter implies accumulation of NADPH and lack of NADP+.

Summarizing, the regulation of de-etiolation under both blue and red light appears to be complex, with certain processes taking precedence over others in each case. What complicates the picture even more is the lack of linearity in de-etiolation; some processes are activated, while others lack their importance. Therefore, drawing conclusions about de-etiolation regulation by light must be done with caution, concerning not only the intensity of light but also its spectrum. The light intensity used here is the most similar to the growth conditions in the temperate zone, where Arabidopsis naturally grows; therefore, our results are representative to those conditions. However, the impact of different intensities of monochromatic light on the de-etiolation process, representing varied environmental conditions, is interesting, and our results might serve as a starting point for further investigations.

Materials and Methods

Plant material and culture conditions

Seeds of A. thaliana wild-type (WT) Columbia (N60000) were obtained from Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (Nottingham, UK). The seeds were surface sterilized [5 min in water with a drop of Tween 20, followed by 5 min in 70% ethanol, 5 min in 50% commercial bleach (ACE) and washed 3 times, 5 min each, with sterile, deionized water] and sown in vitro on plastic Petri dish plates with Gamborg (B5, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) medium pH 5.7 with 1% sucrose (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) and 0.7% agar (BTL, Poland). The plates were wrapped with two layers of the aluminum foil and put into a toolbox to ensure dark conditions. After 2 days of stratification at 4°C the toolbox with plates was transferred into the room with (22 ± 2)°C for 6 days. At 10 a.m. on the 6th day the plates were unwrapped from foil and illuminated with blue (458 nm) or red (658 nm) light of 60 µmol photons/m2/s flux density with custom-made illuminators with light-emitting diodes (the blue and red light spectra are given in the Supplementary Fig. S1). The exact duration of illumination was specified in the description of individual methods (see further). The control (etiolated) seedlings, necessary for certain experiments remained in darkness throughout the whole illumination period.

Tissue imaging during de-etiolation

Fixation of the samples

Seedlings, at a given point of de-etiolation process, were collected and directly placed in a fixing mixture, composed of chloroplast isoosmotic buffer with addition of 1.6% glutaraldehyde and 1% β-mercaptoethanol in darkness in the room temperature for 25 min (Rowan and Bendich 2011). Fixed seedlings were stored in a fixing solution at 4°C. To visualize auxiliary nuclear and chloroplast DNA, 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added 15 min prior to imaging to a final concentration of 1 μg/ml.

CLSM and FLIM imaging

For CLSM and FLIM, fixed seedlings were mounted between microscopic glass slides. Imaging was performed using the Stellaris confocal system (Leica, Germany) with a tunable pulsed white laser. An HC PL Apo CS2 100/1.4 oil objective was used. Emission wavelengths were set with a monochromator. Image collection and FLIM processing were carried out using LasX software (Leica, Germany). Pchlide/Chlide/Chl emission was excited with a white pulse laser, set at 470 nm. Emission was collected at 650–750 nm range. For auxiliary DAPI emission (DNA staining), 405 nm laser diode was used, and emission was collected at 420–480 nm range.

High-resolution imaging

SIM and its upgraded version, SIM2, is a version of superresolution microscopy, relying on acquiring multiple images (in our case, 13) of the same sample. The images are collected with the use of special lattices, generating 13 different interference patterns. The high-resolution image is then reconstructed from these 13 images. Reconstruction algorithms include calibration with fluorescent beads of known size to avoid artifacts. As the final image is already deconvolved, it loses information about fluorescence intensity of particular pixels (Y. Ma et al. 2021). For this method, fixed seedlings were mounted between microscopic glass slides and imaged using the Elyra7 confocal system (Zeiss, Germany), equipped with an HC PL Apo 100/1.4 oil objective. Fluorescence of Pchlide/Chlide/Chl was excited with a 488 nm laser diode, and emission was collected with a long pass 590 nm filter. For the additional DAPI channel, 405 nm excitation diode and 420–480 nm emission band pass filter was applied. SIM2 processing, increasing the resolution to 60 nm, was performed using Zen Black (Zeiss, Germany) dedicated software. No additional processing was applied except contrast adjustment for easier reading.

For the parametrization of PLBs observed at early steps of greening, and chloroplasts that appeared after 6 h of greening, images were analyzed using Fiji software, with a self-written half-automated routine (Supplementary II.1). At least nine images, collected from three independent seedlings for each experimental variant were analyzed, to provide a total count of 70–90 chloroplasts (or the corresponding number of PLBs). The obtained parameters were pooled and further subjected to frequency count analysis using OriginPro software.

Fluorescence emission spectra at 77 K

Fluorescence emission spectra were measured using a steady-state spectrofluorimeter (Perkin Elmer LS-55B, UK) equipped with a liquid nitrogen device for measurements at 77 K. The experimental setup was as follows: excitation wavelength—440 nm; emission range—600 to 780 nm; excitation and emission slits—10 and 5 nm respectively; scan speed—150 nm/min. The internal long pass 515 cutoff filter was used in the emission channel. Each spectrum represents an average of 3–5 measurement repetitions. A background curve was recorded for frozen glycogen in water. After background subtraction, the spectra were corrected for the wavelength-dependent sensitivity of the photomultiplier and normalized to their area under the curve. Wavelength reproducibility was ± 0.5 nm.

Sample preparation for low-temperature spectroscopy

Approximately 20 cotyledons and 200 μl of 25 mM HEPES-NaOH buffer (pH 7.5) supplemented with EDTA (1 mM), MgCl2 (1 mM) and sorbitol (0.4 m) were ground with a pestle in a mortar. Utensils and the buffer were prechilled on ice. The obtained homogenate was placed in a glass capillary (2.5 mm diameter, 7 cm length), immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then used to measure fluorescence spectra. Samples were prepared under dim green light. The preparation of the homogenate was repeated four times for fresh portions of cotyledons, and the appropriate spectra were averaged. All chemicals were purchased from Merck (former Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO, USA).

Chlorophyll in vivo fluorescence

Sample preparation

Fluorescence measurements were performed on seedlings grown on Petri dishes, with approximately 30–40 seedlings per dish, at specific points during the de-etiolation process. Each dish was pre-incubated in darkness for 10 min immediately before measurement. Image and setting optimization was performed before darkening. The dishes remained covered all the time to prevent the plants from drying out. It has been previously verified that the lid does not interfere with fluorescence measurements.

In vivo Chl fluorescence images of whole A. thaliana seedlings were captured using a pulse-modulated Open Fluor-Cam FC 800-O/1010 fluorimeter and FluorCam7 software (PSI, Drasov, Czech Republic). An additional lens (M-55 Canon) was placed in the front of the camera, allowing for a 2–2.5 times magnification of the images of the examined seedlings. Standard protocols, namely ‘Quenching’ and ‘Light Curve’, were applied. The first protocol was used to observe fluorescence induction under actinic light followed by relaxation in darkness. The second protocol was employed to observe fluorescence under increasing light intensity. White actinic light (ACT2) was used in all experiments, with the shutter set to ‘0ʹ and the sensitivity typically between 30% and 40%. The intensity of the saturating pulses was set at 3,700 μmol photons/m2/s. Fluorescence induction was observed under two intensities of actinic light: 170 or 2,100 μmol photons/m2/s. The actinic light was turned on for 100 s, and then switched off for the subsequent 90 s during the experiment. In the case of light curve measurements, the intensity of the white actinic light was changed within the range of 30 to 2,100 μmol photons/m2/s, with the intensity increasing each time after the saturating pulse. The following parameters were considered for the analysis (Maxwell and Johnson 2000, Murchie and Lawson 2013): QYmax—the maximum PSII quantum yield in dark-adapted state; QY—the effective PSII quantum yield induced in light; NPQ—the non-photochemical quenching; qP—the photochemical quenching, which estimate of the fraction of open PSII reaction centers (PSIIopened/(PSIIopened+ PSIIclosed); Rfd—fluorescence decline ratio, an empiric parameter used to assess plant vitality (Roháček et al. 2008). Each experiment was repeated five times, and the average values of the aforementioned parameters, along with their respective standard errors, were calculated. A new portion of seedlings (i.e. another Petri dish) was used for each fluorescence measurement, and each dish with seedlings was used for measurements only once.

Measurements of gene expression

For real-time PCR experiments, 6-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings grown in vitro, either etiolated or de-etiolated for 3 or 6 h under 60 μmol/photons/m2/s of either red light (658 nm) or blue light (458 nm) were used. The samples were collected immediately, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Total RNA was extracted with the Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA Kit with On-Column DNase I Digestion Set (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, 1,000 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed with the RevertAid M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) using oligo(dT)18 primers. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green JumpStart Taq ReadyMix (Sigma Aldrich) and a thermocycler (QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System; Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific), with all reactions run in triplicates. GeNorm v3.4 (Vandesompele et al. 2002) was used to calculate the normalization factor for the PDF and SAND reference genes (Czechowski et al. 2005). To compare results from different qPCR runs, the relative expression of each gene in a sample was calculated with respect to the mean Ct value for all samples in the same PCR analysis. Each analysis included the entire set of samples, i.e. etiolated and illuminated with blue and red light. Samples de-etiolated for 3 and 6 h were analyzed separately. The relative expression of each gene in etiolated sample was normalized to unity. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are provided in the Supplementary (Table S1). PCR conditions were as follows: 10 min at 94°C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 94°C, 15 s at the temperature given in Supplementary (Table S1), and 15 s at 72°C. The specificity of the obtained products was verified on a dissociation curve at the end of each run (measured at 72–94°C and temperature increase of 0.3°C/s).

Pigment analysis by HPLC

The 6-day-old etiolated Arabidopsis seedlings grown in vitro were de-etiolated under 60 μmol photons/m2/s of either red light (658 nm) or blue light (458 nm) for 3 or 6 h. Subsequently, they were collected immediately after the end of the illumination period, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Pigment extraction and analysis were performed using a previously described method (Latowski et al. 2005, Myśliwa Kurdziel et al. 2012). Briefly, weighted seedlings (100–700 mg of fresh weight) were homogenized in acetonitrile:methanol (72:8, v/v, to a final volume of 4 ml with the addition of CaCO3 for buffering. The extract was then centrifuged (13 000 × g, 3 min), and 100 µl of filtrated (0.22 µm) supernatant was loaded on an octadecasilane column (Tracer Excel 120 ODSA 5 μm 25 × 0.4 cm, Teknochroma) connected to HPLC system (PU-2089 Plus, JASCO), coupled with a UV-VIS detector (MD-2015 Plus, JASCO). UV-VIS absorption spectra were measured between 350 and 750 nm (±1.5 nm). The elution protocol included acetonitrile:methanol:H2O (72:8:1; v/v/v) over 24 min (at a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min), followed by a quick switch to an isocratic methanol:ethyl actetate; (1:1; v/v) between 25 and 29 min (at a flow rate of 1.7 ml/min), and finally, acetonitrile:methanol:H2O up to 34 min. HPLC grade solvents were purchased from VWR (Gdansk, Poland). Pigments were identified based on the elution time and the absorption spectrum. The total pigment amount was quantified based on the area under the respective chromatogram peak and normalized to the sample fresh weight. The experiment was performed in quadruplicate, each being the independent biological replicate.
