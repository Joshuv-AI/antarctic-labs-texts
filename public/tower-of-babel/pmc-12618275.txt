Eating disorders behind cosmetic requests: the gatekeeping role of plastic surgeons
Authors: Emrah Işıktekin, Salih Kılıç
Published: 2025

To the Editor-in-Chief,

It is a common occurrence for plastic surgeons to evaluate patients in their early adulthood who exhibit a marked underweight condition yet express a desire for cosmetic procedures such as rhinoplasty, jawline contouring, or liposuction. While superficially appearing to be a straightforward cosmetic request, such requests often reveal a more intricate clinical foundation. A desire to change the appearance of a body part may signal an eating disorder. For some, the plastic surgery visit is one of the few medical encounters where this problem can be recognized.

Within surgical populations, the prevalence of eating disorders has historically been underestimated. Distorted body image, nutritional compromise, and low body mass index are not uncommon among candidates for aesthetic surgery, and each threatens perioperative safety and outcomes. Even when technically impeccable procedures are performed, they can result in dissatisfaction or complications when the primary driver is psychiatric rather than anatomical.

A growing body of research has documented the convergence of disordered eating behaviours with demand for aesthetic procedures [1]. Compared to the general population, cosmetic surgery candidates more often exhibit restricted eating, purging, and distorted body images. However, there is a paucity of universally accepted, practical screening tools for initial deployment. Brief instruments such as the SCOFF questionnaire, the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q; or its short form, EDE-Q7), and the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI) offer feasible options for routine clinical use.

In our clinic, the EDE-Q7 has been integrated into the preliminary assessment of patients who present with cosmetic requests in the context of markedly low BMI or disproportionate preoccupation with appearance. While the original questionnaire was found to be concise and straightforward to administer, further revisions to its wording were deemed necessary to enhance specificity for aesthetic surgery. The adapted items employ a plastic-surgery-oriented register, explicitly addressing dietary restriction undertaken to “prepare” for surgery, rigid preoperative weight rules, and the influence of social-media comparison on motivation and self-image (Table 1). This approach maintains the validated structure of the EDE-Q7 while capturing clinically salient nuances pertinent to surgical consultations. In practice, it facilitates naturalistic risk identification within routine anamnesis, thereby improving diagnostic sensitivity and patient safety. Recent psychometric research lends support to the reliability and structural validity of the scale in women diagnosed with anorexia nervosa [2], thereby emphasizing its pertinence at the surgical interface. Notwithstanding, the paucity of systematic research within the domain of aesthetic surgery remains a notable gap [3]. Despite the meticulous attention given to perioperative risks such as bleeding, infection, and scarring, psychiatric risks are often overlooked. However, it is crucial to recognize that untreated eating disorders have some of the highest mortality rates among mental illnesses.

Consequently, the scope of the aesthetic surgeon’s practice encompasses more than mere technical execution. Surgeons, in their capacity as gatekeepers, are charged with the responsibility of recognizing psychological distress, determining when to defer or decline surgery, and orchestrating timely referrals for mental health evaluations. In many cases, one or two targeted inquiries (“Are you often dissatisfied with your weight or shape?”; “Do you avoid eating for fear of gaining weight?”) may reveal underlying distress.

The pervasive influence of social media further exacerbates this responsibility. Patients are increasingly presenting with requests that align with filtered images and algorithmic beauty trends—cultural scripts that have the potential to obscure body-image pathology. Maintaining critical awareness of how such external pressures shape expectations and clinical decisions is integral to ethical practice.

In conclusion, patients seeking aesthetic surgery constitute a pivotal yet under-recognized group in whom early identification of eating disorders is both feasible and essential. A substantial body of research has repeatedly demonstrated a high prevalence among surgical candidates [1], a finding that has been further substantiated by psychometric research [2]. However, this crucial aspect remains frequently overlooked within the specialty [3]. Incorporating structured tools such as the EDE-Q7—and, where appropriate, the adapted version contextualized for plastic-surgery practice (Table 1)—provides a pragmatic framework to strengthen preoperative evaluation, enhance safety, and optimize outcomes. It is acknowledged that this perspective is informed by a selection of observations and extant literature rather than large-scale prospective data. However, the emerging evidence suggests the need for systematic, multidisciplinary pathways in aesthetic surgery. By adopting this gatekeeping role, plastic surgeons not only uphold the technical standards of the discipline but also the broader ethical duty to safeguard the psychological wellbeing of vulnerable patients in their care.
