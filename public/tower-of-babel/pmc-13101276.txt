Systemic failures in pediatric dental care in Serbia demand immediate attention
Authors: Rasa Mladenovic
Published: 2026

Despite clear international guidance, including recommendations from the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry and the World Health Organization advocating the first dental visit within the first year of life, Serbia has not yet established national guidelines or structured referral pathways for early preventive examinations. In contrast, many European health systems have successfully integrated early dental screening into routine pediatric care. In Serbia, however, the first dental visit remains largely symptom-driven and occurs only when pain or advanced disease develops, reflecting a systemic failure in prevention rather than patient-related factors.1,2

The problem is further exacerbated by the insufficient adoption of contemporary, evidence-based caries management strategies. Minimally invasive concepts, particularly selective caries removal, are strongly supported by international evidence and have been specifically highlighted as highly relevant for underserved populations in the Balkan region.3 However, despite the availability of such regionally contextualized evidence, their implementation in daily clinical practice remains limited. This persistent gap between evidence and practice reflects not only a lack of structured knowledge translation but also systemic inertia in adopting modern, patient-centered approaches.

The situation is further complicated by the absence of mandatory and rigorously enforced continuing professional development, resulting in some practitioners continuing to apply outdated and unnecessarily invasive treatment philosophies. At the same time, the primary healthcare system remains poorly integrated, with limited collaboration between pediatricians and dental professionals, lack of national preventive programs, minimal use of caries risk assessment tools, and insufficient parental education.

Equally concerning is the historically restrictive regulatory framework regarding the behavioral management of children during dental treatment. Until recently, inhalation sedation with nitrous oxide—a well-established, safe, and minimally invasive technique—was practically unavailable in routine practice. This limitation has hindered the delivery of child-centered care and contributed to increased reliance on general anesthesia for procedures that could otherwise be managed minimally invasively.1

The current situation is not merely a clinical concern but a clear indicator of systemic failure. The continued neglect of both international guidelines and region-specific evidence represents a missed opportunity to implement cost-effective, minimally invasive, and patient-centered care. Without urgent implementation of evidence-based national guidelines, promotion of minimally invasive dentistry, enforcement of continuing professional development, and meaningful strengthening of primary healthcare infrastructure, Serbia risks perpetuating preventable harm among its youngest population and widening the gap between evidence and practice.
