Prevalence and factors associated with short birth interval among women of reproductive age in Assosa district, Benishangul Gumuz Regional State, Ethiopia
Authors: Yihenew Mitiku Alemu, Molalign Gualu Gobena, Maru Zewdu Kassie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA short birth interval is defined as a period of less than 33 months between two consecutive live births. It has a significant impact on population size and the health of mothers and children. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with short birth intervals among women of reproductive age in Assosa district, Benishangul Gumuz Regional State.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was employed, with data collected from 1520 women aged 15–49 years using structured questionnaires and interview. The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and accelerated failure time models to identify factors associated with short birth intervals.ResultsThe study found that the prevalence of short birth interval was 55.1%. The results indicated that women’s educational level, the number of children a woman has, age at first marriage, husband’s education level and contraceptive use were significantly associated with short birth intervals.ConclusionsPromoting family planning, enhancing maternal and paternal education, conducting awareness campaigns in communities where early marriage is prevalent, and working with religious and community leaders to challenge cultural norms are crucial strategies for reducing the prevalence of short birth intervals and their associated consequences.Keywords: Survival data analysis, Short birth interval, Acceleration factor

Introduction

A short birth interval is defined as a period of less than 33 months between two consecutive live births [1]. It has a significant impact on population size and the health of mothers and children [2]. Evidence suggests an association between short birth intervals and higher infant mortality [1, 3]. Shorter birth intervals have also been linked to an increased risk of premature birth, low birth weight, maternal morbidity and mortality [3, 4].

Short birth intervals continue to be a problem in both developed and developing countries. For example, in Uganda and Zimbabwe, short birth intervals are associated with adverse health effects on babies, children, and mothers [5]. In the United States, pregnancies occurring less than 18 months are associated with delayed prenatal care, preterm birth and neonatal morbidity, and poor birth outcomes such as low birth weight [6]. In Ethiopia, women with birth intervals of less than 2 years face an increased risk of maternal health problems and death at a young age [7].

The hypothesis of maternal dissatisfaction suggests that if the birth interval is shortened, the mother may not fully recover from her previous pregnancy. As a result, the fetus may not develop optimally, and the child born after a short interval may face a higher risk of death [8]. In the context of chronic food insecurity, the female body prioritizes its own well-being over fetal health when allocating energy and nutrients [9].

In many low-income countries, pregnancy and childbirth complications are the primary causes of death for women of childbearing age. It is estimated that 99% of maternal mortality occurs in developing countries with complications from pregnancy and childbirth complications being the primary contributors [10]. Studying childbirth timing and intervals is important for ensuring the health and survival of both children and mothers. Unintended pregnancies and short delivery intervals are factors associated with maternal and infant mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia [11]. Published studies have shown that short birth intervals are associated with an increased risk of maternal mortality, anemia, uterine rupture, malnutrition, hypertension, and maternal mortality [12].

A birth interval of three to five years is considered optimal, as it better protects the health of mothers and their children compared to intervals of 2 years or less [13]. This interval allows mothers more time to recover before becoming pregnant again, which increases the likelihood of full-term pregnancies and healthy deliveries. Additionally, there is less competition for breastfeeding, food, and nutrition among children [14]. It has also been suggested that breastfeeding has a positive impact on infants’ physical and intellectual development. For babies and children under 5 years of age, birth intervals of at least three years are associated with the lowest risk of death [15]. Studies have shown that optimal birth intervals can improve children’s health outcomes. If all women had optimal birth intervals, child survival could increase annually, while infant mortality could decrease [16].

A gap of at least 36 months between births reduces the risk of sudden infant mortality [17]. The median birth interval in Ethiopia is 34.5 months [18]. Infants born less than 2 years after a previous birth have a higher under-5 mortality rate. The mortality rate for children under 5 is significantly higher for those born less than 2 years after the previous birth (114 deaths per 1,000 births) and for those born 3 years after the previous birth (44 deaths per 1,000 live births). In general, 22% of children are born within two years of their siblings [19].

In recent years, policymakers and planners have paid much attention to the time period of birth and its determinants. Several studies have investigated and identified various risk factors that contribute to the long duration of delivery. Positive correlations between the period of breastfeeding and the length of the gap among deliveries have been documented in the experience of many countries [20]. The sex distribution of the index’s children can be considered a determinant of the time interval of birth [21]. The death of an earlier child is associated with a short period of birth. The place of residence also has an important effect on the length of the gap between deliveries [22]. The mother’s age at first childbirth, education level, lack of optimal breastfeeding methods, duration of previous birth, employment status, sex composition of child survival and mass media have also been found to be determinants of the length of the birth interval [23].

In Ethiopia, there is limited research on short birth intervals that uses survival models, particularly in the study area. Recent studies on birth intervals in Ethiopia primarily focus on Binary logistic regression analyses, but survival models, which could provide deeper insights into the timing of births, remain underutilized. While there is considerable research on birth intervals in Ethiopia, studies focusing specifically on Assosa District or the Benishangul Gumuz region are limited. Understanding birth intervals and their determinants is crucial for the region to develop and strengthen child spacing programs, ultimately improving maternal and child health. Therefore, this study was conducted to identify the socioeconomic, demographic, and biological determinants influencing short birth intervals in the Assosa district.

Methods

Study design and area

A cross-sectional study was conducted with women of reproductive age in Assosa district, Benishangul Gumuz region, Western Ethiopia from 5 September to 25 December 2023.

Variables in the study

The dependent variable in this study was the short birth interval, defined as the period from one birth to the next pregnancy being less than 24 months, or an interval between births of less than 33 months. We included women with a record of at least 2 and at most 5 births in the past 5 years to minimize recall bias, as the birth interval was measured based on the respondent’s memory.

The independent variables related to the socioeconomic, demographic, and reproductive health behaviors of women included maternal education, maternal age at first birth, age at first marriage, age at last delivery, marital status, wealth index, husband’s educational status, mother’s employment status, husband’s occupational status, place of delivery for the preceding child, duration of breastfeeding (measured in years), antenatal care (ANC) visits during the previous pregnancy, place of residence, husband’s beliefs about delivery intervals, use of contraception, and religion. These variables were used to determine the length of the delivery gap between mothers.

Socioeconomic status measurement

In this study wealth categories were defined using a quantitative method based on household economic status, not based on participants’ self-assessments. A wealth index was generated through principal component analysis, incorporating indicators like asset ownership, housing materials, water sources, and sanitation facilities. Based on the resulting index scores, participants were then grouped into four categories: rich, middle, poor, and very poor. This method aligns with standard practices used in surveys such as the Demographic and Health Surveys.

Study population

The study population included women of childbearing age who had at least two consecutive births, with the most recent birth occurring within five years prior to the data collection period.

Sampling method

A multi-stage cluster sampling method was employed to select the necessary participants. Assosa District consists of 78 kebeles (74 rural and 4 urban). In the first stage, the kebeles were grouped into 9 distinct clusters. In the second stage, three clusters were randomly selected from the total clusters within the district. In the third stage, households within each selected cluster were randomly chosen to participate in the study. The probability-proportional-to-size allocation technique was used to determine the study units included in each kebele. Within each selected household, only one woman of reproductive age (15–49 years) was selected for the interview. If there were multiple eligible women, one was chosen randomly. The study units were subsequently interviewed for women of childbearing age who had at least two and at most five successive deliveries within the past 5 years prior to data collection.

Sample size determination

To determine the sample size, a pilot survey of 150 women of reproductive age was conducted in a kebele outside the main study sites in Assosa District, Ethiopia. The pilot group shared similar socio demographic and cultural characteristics with the main study population, ensuring representativeness and providing reliable data to the sample size calculations, especially regarding the expected prevalence of short birth intervals. The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, with 47% as the proportion of women with short birth intervals from a pilot survey. A 95% confidence level, a 3.55% margin of error, and a design effect of 2 were applied. A margin of error of 3.55% was chosen to improve the accuracy of short birth interval prevalence estimates, considering the importance of the issue for regional maternal and child health initiatives. This reduced margin was considered suitable and attainable with the resources available. The final sample size was 1,520.

Data collection procedures

The data were collected using a structured questionnaire and interviews. The interview method was employed to gather detailed information about the history of birth intervals from the 1,520 study participants. Data were collected by trained data collectors who were recruited from the local area under the supervision of the researchers. They were familiar with the local language and culture, which helped facilitate effective communication and data collection. The data collectors were trained to follow the structured interview protocol and ensure that the data were accurately recorded. The response rate in this study was 100%. These full participation rates was achieved due to that all women agreed to participate, and no individuals were excluded from the study. Participants have a high level of interest in the study topic, especially since it is directly relevant to their health, and careful planning of the data collection process.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study units were women of childbearing age who had at least 2 but no more than 5 consecutive births within the 5 years prior to data collection. Women with a history of 6 or more consecutive births, those unable to communicate due to severe illness, and mothers with a history of twins or abortions were excluded from the study.

Method of data analysis

Accelerated failure time model

Parametric models are well suited for the analysis of survival data, but the probability distribution of survival time available in these models is relatively small. In these situations, the accelerated failure time (AFT) model is an alternative to the PH model for analyzing time-to-event data. The AFT model measured the direct effect of explanatory variables on survival time, not risk. This feature makes it easier to interpret the results because the parameters measure the effect of the corresponding covariates on the mean survival time.

The AFT model shows that the survival function of an individual with covariate X at time t is the same as the survival function of an individual with a baseline survival function at time , where = ( …, ) is a vector of regression coefficients. In other words, the accelerated failure-time model is defined by this relationship.

In this study, Weibull AFT, log-logistic AFT, and log-normal AFT models were considered. The rationale for choosing Weibull AFT, Log-logistic AFT, and Log-normal AFT models over other models is their flexibility, interpretability, ability to handle skewed data, and robustness in modeling survival times. These models are also well-suited for comparing how various factors influence the timing of reproductive events like birth intervals. Parametric AFT models based on weibull, log-logistic and log-normal distributions for survival times are most commonly used in practice [24].

Results

Descriptive results

A total of 1,520 women from Assosa district were included in the study, with a response rate of 100%. Among the women who responded to the questionnaire, 837 (55.1%) had a short birth interval, and 683 (44.9%) had a long birth interval. The majority of respondents, 475 (31.3%), were between 45 and 49 years of age.

Regarding the employment status of women, approximately 93.3% of the respondents were unemployed. Of the total, 54.4% of women had more than 3 children. Around 38.8% of the women were illiterate, 30.5% had elementary education (grades 1–6), 14.9% had completed grades 7–8, and 6% had a diploma or higher. Approximately 17% of households were classified as poor, 64.1% as middle income, 9.5% as very poor, and 9.4% as rich. At the time of the survey, 37.3% of the women were Orthodox, 62.6% were Muslim, and 0.1% was Protestant. The majority (94.9%) of the respondents were rural residents. About 11.1% of the women gave birth to their first child before the age of 15, 58.6% had their first child between the ages of 16 and 20, and 24.9% had their first child between the ages of 21 and 25. Approximately 30.5% of the women were married before the age of 15, 43.7% were married between the ages of 15 and 20, and the remaining 25.8% were married after the age of 20.

Of the total number of respondents, 37.5% had a birth interval of 1 year, 17.6% had a birth interval of 2 years, and 9.7% had a birth interval of 3 years. The overall median birth interval for women was 2 years (95% CI = 1.858, 2.142). More than half (52.8%) of the women gave birth in a clinic. Among the total, 74.5% of the husbands had positive beliefs about birth spacing. Overall, 50.6% of the women used contraceptive methods (Table 1).

The acceleration factors for women attending primary level (1–6), primary level (7–8), grades 9–12, and diploma or above were 1.01, 1.17, 1.12, and 1.51, with 95% CIs of (1.00, 1.15), (1.03, 1.34), (1.11, 1.33) and (1.11, 1.71), respectively. This indicates that, compared to illiterate women, those with a primary level education (1–6), a primary level education (7–8), a grade 9–12 and a diploma or higher tends to have longer birth intervals.

The acceleration factor for women with 3 or more children was 0.88, with 95%CI (0.26, 0.90). This implies that women who had 3 or more children had a shorter survival time between births compared to women with 2–3 children, who were used as the reference group. The acceleration factors for age in the first marriage category of 15–20 years and over 20 years were 1.14 and 1.16, with 95% CIs of (1.01, 1.27) and (1.02, 1.31), respectively, when less than 15 years was used as the reference category. These results suggest that women who married at ages 15–20 years and over 20 years had longer birth interval survival times compared to women who married at age 15 or younger.

The acceleration factors for husbands attending primary education (1–6), grade 9–12 and diploma or higher were 0.85, 1.53, and 2.13, with 95% CI; (-0.32, 0.91), (1.16, 1.68), and (2.06, 2.26), respectively, using an illiterate husband as a reference. This indicates that women with illiterate husbands tend to have longer birth intervals compared to women whose husbands have only primary education (1–6). Additionally, women whose husbands have an education level of grades 9–12, diploma or higher experience longer birth intervals than those with illiterate husbands. The acceleration factor for women not using contraceptives was 0.26, with a 95% CI of (0.08, 0.88), using contraceptive use as the reference category. This implies that women who do not use contraception have shorter survival times between birth intervals compared to women who use contraception.

Discussion

The prevalence of short birth intervals in this study was 55.1%. This result is slightly lower than the findings from southern Ethiopia (57.6%) [25], but higher than a study conducted in Dabat district, Northwest Ethiopia (39.1%) [26]. In our study, the woman’s education level, number of children, age at first marriage, husband’s education level, and contraceptive use were identified as determinants of short birth intervals (SBI).

The findings of this study indicate that women’s education level significantly influences the survival time of SBI of mothers at the 5% significance level, contributing to a longer duration of SBI. A study in India showed that education was strongly associated with longer birth intervals [23]. A similar study conducted at Arba Minch, southern Ethiopia, showed that mothers with no formal education were more likely to have a shorter delivery interval [27]. This finding is also consistent with a study performed in the Illubabor Zone, southwestern Ethiopia [28]. This could be due to the fact that women with higher education levels may have better awareness of contraception, family planning, and health risks associated with closely spaced pregnancies. They may be more likely to delay pregnancies or use contraception, thereby extending the birth interval. Another finding of our study was that women with 2–3 children had a lower likelihood of having SBI than women with three or more children. A study by [11] in Ethiopia also showed that the number of surviving children is an important predictor of unwanted pregnancies. This might be women with two or three children tend to have longer birth intervals, as they may be more likely to plan or delay subsequent pregnancies. Our study focused on women who had given birth 2 to 5 times in the past five years to help minimize recall bias. However, this selection strategy may have introduced bias: women who give birth more frequently (with shorter intervals between births) were more likely to be included, while those with longer intervals and typically fewer children may have been underrepresented. As a result, this could have led to an overestimation of short birth intervals among women with higher numbers of children, potentially contributing to the observed inverse relationship between the number of children and the length of birth intervals.

The outcomes of this research showed that age at first marriage was a significant determinant factor for SBI. The result indicated that women aged 15 to 20 years and above 20 years at first marriage had longer survival at the time of birth than women aged 15 years or younger at first marriage. This study was similar to that of studies conducted in Jordan [29] and south west Ethiopia [30], It was reported that marriage before the age of 18 increased the likelihood of having a short birth interval. This might be women who marry at younger ages are more likely to experience short birth intervals due to a combination of biological, social, psychological, and cultural factors. From a biological perspective, younger women generally have higher fertility, shorter periods of postpartum amenorrhea, and a faster return to ovulation, all of which can raise the chances of conceiving shortly after a previous birth. Psychologically, they may have limited knowledge or decision-making power about family planning and may be more susceptible to society, family, or partner pressures regarding childbearing, contributing to shorter birth intervals.

The study showed that the husband’s educational level was a significant predictor of SBI. The results showed that women with illiterate husbands had longer birth intervals than those with husbands who had primary-level (1–6) husbands. This might be that men without formal education may be less likely to hold formal jobs and more involved in subsistence or informal labor, which could shape family planning choices such as preferring fewer children or delaying childbirth due to financial instability. Moreover, differences in gender roles and health literacy may influence reproductive decisions in distinct ways depending on educational background. It is also conceivable that illiteracy, in some settings, aligns with stronger adherence to traditional norms, including prolonged postpartum abstinence, but women whose husbands had education levels of grade 9–12, and diploma or higher had longer birth intervals than those with illiterate husbands. The results of this study are consistent with findings from research conducted in Arba Minch district, western Ethiopia [31], and Dabat district, Northwest Ethiopia [26]. It was observed that women whose husbands had received formal education were less likely to experience a short birth interval compared to women whose husbands had no formal education. A study by [32] also revealed that the education of husbands was a major determinant of birth interval in Pakistan. This might be a more educated husband may be more knowledgeable about the health risks associated with closely spaced pregnancies and may be more likely to encourage family planning and use of contraceptives.

Study Participants who had never used contraceptives before conceiving their last child had a greater risk of having SBIs than their peers. This finding is similar to that of a study performed in Haiti and Sudan, which showed that the use of contraception was significantly associated with an optimum birth interval [33]. Other studies from southern Ethiopia also reported that nonuse of contraceptive methods was a significant factor related to SBIs [25]. It is obvious that women who use contraceptives effectively are less likely to become pregnant before they are physically, mentally, or financially prepared for the next child. This reduces the chance of having closely spaced births, which is a significant risk factor for SBIs.

Although this study identified several factors significantly linked to short birth intervals, it is essential to understand that these factors likely do not act independently. For instance, maternal education may influence birth spacing indirectly by affecting awareness and use of family planning, which are themselves shaped by cultural norms and gender roles. Likewise, economic status can impact access to healthcare and contraceptive options. Gaining insight into how these elements interact within the unique cultural and socioeconomic setting of the Assosa District is vital.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The study used Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) model which allows for a precise estimation of the time to the event data, accounting for factors that accelerate or delay the event. This is particularly useful in understanding birth intervals in a way that is not just binary (short vs. long intervals) but can give more insights into how different factors influence the timing of births. The AFT model can be applied when hazards (the risk of a short birth interval occurring) are non-proportional, offering a more nuanced approach than models that assume proportional hazards, such as Cox regression model. The ability to assess how different factors accelerate or delay short birth intervals can help design more effective health interventions, such as promoting family planning or maternal health services in ways that specifically address the timing aspects of birth spacing. Additional strengths of our study were it used community-based design, and high response rate. These elements collectively strengthen the validity and generalizability of our findings.

The study has several limitations. First, since the study uses a cross-sectional design, it provides data at a single point in time, making it difficult to determine causal relationships between factors and short birth intervals. Second, women may have difficulty accurately recalling the exact timing of their births, which could affect the accuracy of data on birth intervals, and we included only women with 2 to 5 births in the past 5 years is that it may introduce selection bias by excluding women who have had more than five births. Third, factors such as biological, social, and cultural practices may play a significant role in determining birth intervals, but these factors were not fully captured in the study due to budget limitations and the difficulty of measuring them. Additionally, the presence of confounding factors, like socio-economic status, access to healthcare services, cultural practices influencing fertility behaviors, and nutritional status of women. These factors may interact with both the exposure and outcome variables, potentially biasing the observed associations, which were not adequately controlled for, might distort the results or obscure the true determinants of short birth intervals. Future studies should consider using longitudinal approaches, including women who have had more than five births and considering social and cultural factors.

Conclusions

In this study, AFT models were used to determine the determinants of short birth intervals among women of reproductive age in the Assosa district, Benishangul Gumuz Regional State. The study used different AFT models with different baseline distributions. Using the AIC, the log-normal AFT model was found to be the best among the other AFT models. The results of the log-normal AFT model indicated that women’s educational level, number of children, age at first marriage, husband’s educational level, and contraceptive use were significant predictors of short birth intervals among women in the Assosa district. Promoting family planning, enhancing maternal and paternal education, conducting awareness campaigns in communities where early marriage is prevalent, and working with religious and community leaders to challenge cultural norms are crucial strategies for reducing the prevalence of short birth intervals and their associated consequences.
