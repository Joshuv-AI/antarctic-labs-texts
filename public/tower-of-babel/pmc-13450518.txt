Optimizing nitrogen and potassium fertilization enhances yield–protein balance in soft wheat
Authors: Jiawen Zhao, Zihong Li, Xiaofei Ding, Conghao Gao, Dong Jiang, Zhenglai Huang, Shangyu Ma, Yonghui Fan, Zhen Zhang, Zhuangzhuang Sun, Wenjing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrogen and potassium fertilization are key factors influencing the yield and quality of soft wheat. However, most existing studies have focused on single responses such as yield or grain protein content, and a systematic understanding of nitrogen–potassium interactions in regulating nitrogen metabolism and transport–partitioning processes, as well as their roles in the coordinated “high yield–low protein” mechanism, remains insufficient. This study investigated two soft wheat cultivars, Wanximai 0638 and Yangmai 20, under four nitrogen application levels (0, 150, 180, and 210 kg·ha⁻1), three N basal-to-topdressing ratios (8:2, 7:3, and 6:4), and two K fertilization treatments (single basal application versus split application at a 5:5 basal-to-topdressing ratio). The topdressing fertilizers were applied at the jointing stage. By analyzing indicators, including grain yield (GY), grain protein content (GPC), flour solvent retention capacity (SRC), nitrogen accumulation and translocation, nitrogen use efficiency (NUE), physiological nitrogen use efficiency (NPE), chlorophyll content (SPAD), and key nitrogen metabolism enzyme activities, the comprehensive effects of nitrogen application and potassium split application on soft wheat yield and quality were elucidated. The results revealed that the nitrogen rate, topdressing nitrogen ratio, and potassium split application method significantly influenced GY, GPC, SRC, nitrogen accumulation and translocation, NUE, NPE, SPAD, and nitrogen metabolism enzyme activities. With increasing nitrogen rate and topdressing proportion, activities of nitrogen metabolism enzymes were enhanced. Compared with 180 kg·ha⁻1 nitrogen and 7:3 or 8:2 base-to-topdressing ratios, applying 210 kg·ha⁻1 nitrogen with a 6:4 ratio increased pre-anthesis nitrogen translocation contribution to grains and post-anthesis nitrogen accumulation, leading to GY increase of 18.2%–33.5%. However, NUE declined by 5.3%–8.2%, and GPC exceeded the standard for soft wheat. Optimizing potassium fertilization revealed that under a nitrogen rate of 180 kg·ha⁻1, combining a 7:3 nitrogen base-to-topdressing ratio with potassium split application significantly improved NUE compared to one-time basal potassium application. This was achieved by increasing the number of effective spikes and grains per spike, leading to a 13.3% increase in GY. Additionally, potassium application negatively regulated wheat nitrogen assimilation and utilization, thereby reducing GPC (total effect = − 0.042), exhibiting a usual “protein dilution effect” that contributes to the quality formation of soft wheat. In conclusion, under the production conditions of rice–wheat rotation in the Jianghuai region of China, a recommended nitrogen application rate of approximately 180 kg·ha⁻1, combined with a 7:3 base-to-topdressing ratio and potassium split application (5:5), can effectively maintain high GY while controlling GPC in soft wheat.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Nitrogen application, Split potassium fertilization, Soft wheat, Quality, Yield

Introduction

Soft wheat is a specialized type of wheat primarily used for the production of soft baked products such as biscuits and cakes [1]. Compared with bread wheat, soft wheat is characterized by a lower protein content and a weaker gluten-forming capacity, which limits its ability to maintain gas cell stability during dough mixing and fermentation [2]. Although these properties are unfavorable for the production of leavened, strong-gluten bread, they are particularly advantageous for the processing of biscuits, cakes, and other products that require a fine, tender, or crumbly texture. With the continuous improvement of living standards, the demand for soft wheat in China has increased steadily, making its cultivation techniques and production management important research topics in agricultural science.

Nitrogen is one of the primary nutrients required for wheat growth [3] and is typically absorbed by roots from the soil and translocated to aboveground organs and grains [4, 5]. Studies have demonstrated that nitrogen fertilization directly affects nitrogen metabolism, uptake, and utilization in crops, thereby influencing yield and quality [6]. Nitrogen application can also delay crop senescence, promote aboveground biomass accumulation and grain development, and consequently increase GY [7]. Moreover, split nitrogen application can enhance wheat yield and grain protein content (GPC) by enhancing nitrogen translocation, metabolic enzyme activities, and nitrogen accumulation in grains [8]. It also ensures a continuous nutrient supply during the later stages of development, supporting effective spike formation and grain filling [9–11]. Therefore, optimizing nitrogen application can help achieve high grain yields while meeting desired GPC targets [12]. Potassium, another essential macronutrient, plays a role in nitrogen uptake and translocation. It also contributes to wheat grain development by regulating plant water balance and enzyme activity [13, 14]. Research has indicated that potassium fertilization enhances photosynthetic efficiency and nitrogen metabolism, thereby promoting yield improvement [15]. Compared with one-time basal potassium application, split potassium application has been proven to reduce potassium leaching while improving nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) and GY [16, 17].

Currently, soft wheat production still faces numerous challenges with respect to fertilizer management. Conventional fertilization practices are generally nitrogen-centered, with relatively limited attention given to potassium application, and excessive nitrogen input is common in soft wheat production systems [18, 19]. This not only leads to inefficient nitrogen use but also intensifies the conflict between maintaining a low grain protein content and achieving higher yield, thereby becoming a key factor constraining the promotion of high-yield and high-quality cultivation technologies for soft wheat. In contrast, appropriate potassium application can increase thousand-kernel weight and thus contribute to yield improvement [20]. Additionally, the synergistic effects of nitrogen and potassium fertilization in promoting crop growth have been well documented in rice [21], rapeseed [22], and potato [23]. The combined application of nitrogen and potassium can regulate photosynthetic capacity [24], promote aboveground biomass development, and enhance nitrogen uptake and utilization efficiency in wheat [25]. Consequently, optimizing the use of nitrogen and potassium fertilizers in soft wheat cultivation to ensure a balance between yield and quality remains a critical area of research.

Although previous studies have demonstrated that nitrogen and potassium fertilization play important regulatory roles in wheat yield and quality formation, current research on their interactive effects still has several limitations. (1) Most studies focus on single indicators such as yield or grain protein content, lacking a systematic investigation of the whole-process nitrogen pathway—including nitrogen uptake, assimilation, translocation, and remobilization—to elucidate the coordinated “high yield–low protein” regulatory mechanism. (2) For soft wheat, studies addressing the relationship between its “low protein requirement” and fertilization management remain limited, and precise fertilization strategies that can be directly applied in production are still insufficient. Based on these gaps, this research comprehensively examines different nitrogen application rates, topdressing ratios, and potassium fertilization strategies. By analyzing wheat GY, GPC, flour solvent retention capacity (SRC), nitrogen accumulation and translocation, NUE, NPE, and key nitrogen metabolism enzyme activities, this study aimed to elucidate the combined effects of nitrogen application and split potassium fertilization on soft wheat. The findings will contribute to optimizing fertilization strategies to improve yield and quality, providing a theoretical basis for precise nutrient management of soft wheat in rice–wheat rotation regions of China.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and methods

The experiment was conducted from 2022 to 2024 at the Central Anhui Experimental Station of Anhui Agricultural University, located in Lujiang County, Hefei, Anhui Province, China (31°48’N, 117°23’E). Two soft wheat cultivars, Wanximai 0638 and Yangmai 20, were used as experimental materials. The seeds were provided by the Lu’an Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Anhui Province, and the Lixiahe Agricultural Research Institute of Jiangsu Province, respectively. Subsequent sampling and material collection were also carried out at the same experimental station. The experiment was conducted using a split-plot design, with nitrogen application levels (N) as the main-plot factor, potassium fertilizer application method (K) and nitrogen basal-to-topdressing ratios (F) as the sub-plot factors, each plot had an area of 18 m2 (3 × 6 m), each treatment was replicated three times. Four nitrogen application levels were established: N0 (0 kg·ha⁻1), N1 (150 kg·ha⁻1), N2 (180 kg·ha⁻1), and N3 (210 kg·ha⁻1). Three nitrogen basal-to-topdressing ratios were tested: F1 (8:2), F2 (7:3), and F3 (6:4) using urea (46.0% N) as the nitrogen source. Potassium fertilizer was applied at 150 kg·ha⁻1 in two treatments: A single basal application (K1) and a split application with a 5:5 basal-to-topdressing ratio (K2), using granular potassium chloride (60.0% K2O) as the potassium source. Phosphorus (P) fertilizer was applied as a single superphosphate (15.0% P2O5) at a rate of 75 kg·ha⁻1 in a single basal application, topdressing fertilizers were applied at the jointing stage. All other agronomic practices followed local high-yield wheat cultivation management. The initial soil nutrient content in the 0–20 cm soil layer before wheat sowing is displayed in Table 1. Additionally, climatic conditions differed between the two growing seasons, with relatively higher precipitation occurring during the late wheat growth stage in the first season, whereas greater temperature fluctuations and precipitation were observed during the winter period in the second season. Detailed meteorological conditions of the experimental site are presented in Fig. 1.

Determination items and methods

At anthesis, wheat plants with synchronized anthesis and uniform growth status were marked, with approximately 300 plants per plot. This method facilitated subsequent data measurement and sampling.

Yield and its components

At maturity, a 1 m2 area with uniform growth was selected from each treatment plot to determine the number of spikes. Additionally, 30 individual stems were collected and analyzed indoors to determine the number of grains per spike (GN). Subsequently, the wheat was harvested and threshed, and the thousand-grain weight (TWG) and grain yield were measured at a grain moisture content of 14.0%.

Protein components and content

The protein component content was determined using a sequential extraction method. Albumin, globulin, gliadin, and glutenin were extracted sequentially using distilled water, 10.0% sodium chloride solution, 75.0% ethanol solution, and 0.2% sodium hydroxide solution, respectively. For each extract, 1 mL of the sample solution was mixed with 5 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue. The mixture was allowed to stand for 2 min and then subjected to spectrophotometric measurement at 595 nm. The protein component concentrations were determined based on a standard curve, and their contents were calculated accordingly. The total nitrogen content in the grain was measured using an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Hanon, K9860, China), while the protein content was calculated by multiplying the nitrogen content by 5.70.

Solvent retention capacity

Wheat samples from different treatments were milled using a laboratory mill (Brabender, Quadrumat Junior, Germany). Solvent retention capacity (SRC) was determined according to AACC 56-11.02 using 50.0% sucrose, 5.0% lactic acid, and 5.0% sodium carbonate solutions prepared accurately. A 50 mL empty centrifuge tube with a cap was weighed and recorded as W1. Subsequently, 5.000 ± 0.050 g of flour with a known moisture content was weighed and recorded as W2 and transferred into the centrifuge tube. Then, 25 mL of the corresponding solvent was added, and the mixture was shaken for 20 min to ensure thorough mixing. After incubation, the flour–solvent suspension was immediately centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was decanted, and the centrifuge tube was inverted on absorbent paper for 10 min to drain excess liquid. The tube was then weighed, and the total mass was recorded as W3. Finally, SRC values were calculated using the following equation:

SRC (%) = [(W3 − W1) / W2 − 1] × [86 / (100 − flour moisture)] × 100.

Nitrogen accumulation, translocation, and utilization in plants

At the anthesis and maturity stages, 20 individual stems were sampled and separated into spikes and other plant parts. The samples were inactivated at 105 ℃ for 0.50 h, then oven-dried at 70 ℃ to a constant weight. The dried samples were ground and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. The nitrogen content was determined using the Kjeldahl method following digestion with a H₂SO₄-H₂O₂ mixture, as described by Chen et al. [26]. Further analysis with an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer was conducted (Hanon, K9860, China). The relevant calculations were performed according to the formulas proposed by Moll et al. [27] and Delogu et al. [28]:

NTA = FN – VN;

NTA = FN – VN;

CTG = NTA / YN × 100%;

CTG = NTA / YN × 100%;

NAA = TN – FN;

NAA = TN – FN;

CPA = NAA / YN × 100%;

CPA = NAA / YN × 100%;

NPE = (GY150−210 − GY0) / (TN150−210 − TN0).

NPE = (GY150−210 − GY0) / (TN150−210 − TN0).

NUE = GY / TN.

NUE = GY / TN.

The nitrogen translocation amount before anthesis (NTA), the contribution rate of pre-anthesis nitrogen translocation to grain (CTG), the nitrogen accumulation amount after anthesis (NAA), and the contribution rate of post-anthesis nitrogen accumulation (CPA) were calculated. Additionally, physiological efficiency of nitrogen fertilizer (NPE) and nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) were determined, while NUE reflects the plant’s capacity to convert absorbed nitrogen into GY. The calculations were based on the following parameters: Nitrogen accumulation at anthesis (FN), nitrogen accumulation in vegetative organs at maturity (VN), nitrogen accumulation in wheat grains at maturity (YN), and total aboveground nitrogen accumulation at maturity (TN). Grain yield under nitrogen fertilization (GY150−210) and without nitrogen fertilization (GY0) were recorded, along with TN under nitrogen application (TN150−210) and without nitrogen application (TN0). Grain yield (GY) was also measured.

Chlorophyll content

The SPAD value of functional leaves was measured at the jointing, anthesis, and grain-filling stages using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502, Konica Minolta, Japan).

Enzyme activities related to nitrogen metabolism

At the jointing, anthesis, and 10 days after anthesis, the main functional leaves from 10 individual stems were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at − 80 ℃ for further analysis. The activities of nitrate reductase (NR, EC 1.6.6.1), glutamine synthetase (GS, EC 6.3.1.2), and glutamate synthase (GOGAT, EC 1.4.7.1) in the functional leaves. The activities of NR, GS, and GOGAT were determined according to the methods described by Cui et al. [29], Nagy et al. [30], and Suzuki et al. [31], respectively.

Data analysis

Data processing, statistical analysis, and figure preparation were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016, SPSS 27, and Origin 2021. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 27 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). For yield and its components measured across two growing seasons, each combination of N, F, and K, models including N, F, K, year (Y), and their interactions were constructed for variance analysis. Tukey’s test was adopted as a post hoc test at the significance level of P < 0.05. For nitrogen accumulation and translocation-related parameters, which were measured in only one growing season, the effects of year and its associated interactions were excluded from the statistical analysis. In addition, we also conducted linear regression analysis using SPSS 27. Mantel tests were carried out using the “linkET” package in R version 4.3.1 to comprehensively evaluate the correlation patterns among traits. Stepwise regression models were constructed in RStudio to identify the optimal model equations for variables influencing grain yield (GY) and grain protein content (GPC), respectively (P < 0.05). Key variables jointly affecting both GY and GPC were then extracted from the two optimal models. Finally, a partial least squares structural equation model (PLS-SEM) was established using the “plspm” package in RStudio to quantify the integrated effects of agronomic management practices on GY and GPC via key intermediary variables.

Results and analysis

Grain yield and its components

ANOVA indicated that nitrogen rate, topdressing nitrogen ratio, and potassium split application all exhibited significant or highly significant effects Spike number, GN, and TWG, thereby influencing GY in soft wheat. Spike number, GN, TWG, and GY increased with higher nitrogen application rates. Under the same nitrogen application rate, compared with F1, increasing the proportion of topdressed nitrogen increased spike number and GN by 7.4%–15.9% and 5.9%–10.4%, respectively, leading to a 15.9%–31.9% increase in GY. Under the same nitrogen application rate and topdressing proportion, split potassium application resulted in a 4.7% increase in GN and a 6.0% increase in spike number compared to a single basal application, significantly enhancing GY by 10.9%. Among the treatments, increasing nitrogen rate in combination with a higher topdressing nitrogen ratio and potassium split application exhibited an overall upward trend in GY (Table 2).

Grain protein composition and content

GPC increased with higher nitrogen application rates (Fig. 2). Compared with the N0 treatment, average protein content under N1, N2, and N3 treatments increased by 25.3%, 32.3%, and 44.9%, respectively. Under the same nitrogen rate, compared with F1, the average increases under F2 and F3 treatments were 3.5% and 7.7%, respectively. Moreover, under identical nitrogen rate and topdressing ratio, split potassium application increased GPC—on average, the K2 treatment increased protein content by 3.6% compared with K1. Across treatments, the combination of increased nitrogen rate, higher topdressing ratio, and split potassium application led to a general upward trend in GPC. However, the gliadin-to-glutenin ratio exhibited a decreasing trend, indicating that gliadin was more responsive to nitrogen and potassium regulation than glutenin. Wheat grains with protein content below 11.5% are considered optimal for soft-textured baked goods [32]. Over the two-year experiment (2022–2024), WXM0638, being more sensitive to nitrogen than YM20, exhibited GPC that frequently exceeded 11.5% under N3 treatment.

Trade-off between GY and GPC in soft wheat

Both GY and GPC showed significant positive correlations with nitrogen application rate (Table 3). Within the nitrogen range of 0–210 kg·ha⁻¹, the F1 (8:2) topdressing ratio required an increase in nitrogen application rate to 218 kg·ha⁻¹ to meet the quality requirements for soft wheat, yet the maximum GY achieved was only 6681 kg·ha⁻¹. The F2 (7:3) treatment exhibited the highest yield slope (39.993), indicating that nitrogen contributed most effectively to yield formation under this topdressing ratio. Under F2, the required nitrogen rate to meet soft wheat standards decreased to 203 kg·ha⁻¹, and GY increased to 7136 kg·ha⁻¹. Although the F3 (6:4) treatment showed a slightly lower yield slope, it had the greatest GPC slope (0.0306), suggesting that increasing the proportion of late topdressing nitrogen tends to cause excessive grain protein content in soft wheat. For potassium treatments, K2 produced a higher yield slope than K1, but both remained lower than those observed under the nitrogen topdressing treatments. GPC slopes under potassium treatments were also relatively small (0.0167–0.0178), with slightly lower R² values, indicating that potassium application timing had a comparatively weaker effect on GY and GPC in soft wheat.

To further assess the combined effects of nitrogen application and split potassium application on the responses of GY and GPC, treatments were compared under conditions meeting the quality requirements for soft wheat. Using N2K1F1 as the control, the N2K1F2 combination increased GY by 14.3%, and N2K2F2 increased GY by 29.5%. However, GY for N1K2F2 was 20.5% lower than that of N2K2F2. The average GPC of the N3K2F2 treatment was 11.8%, and N2K2F2 reduced it by 7.2%. Therefore, considering both yield and quality, the combination of 180 kg·ha⁻¹ nitrogen application, a 7:3 topdressing ratio, and potassium application at the jointing stage (N2K2F2) is more conducive to achieving high yield with appropriate protein content, thereby optimizing soft wheat quality.

SRC of flour

As illustrated in Fig. 3, SRC increased with higher nitrogen application rates and greater proportions of topdressed nitrogen. Furthermore, the split potassium application enhanced SRC. Across the 2022–2024 experiments, the variation ranges for water SRC, sodium carbonate SRC, sucrose SRC, and lactic acid SRC were 53.42–71.16, 70.74–87.68, 88.75–102.23, and 74.14–100.67, respectively. Notably, sucrose SRC and lactic acid SRC were significantly higher than water SRC and sodium carbonate SRC, indicating that the flour maintained moderate gluten network strength while exhibiting good extensibility, which is favorable for the processing of soft-end-use products such as biscuits and cakes.

Nitrogen accumulation and translocation

ANOVA indicated that nitrogen rate, topdressing nitrogen ratio, and potassium split application all exhibited significant or highly significant effects on nitrogen accumulation and translocation in soft wheat. NTA, CTG, and NAA increased with higher nitrogen application rates and topdressing ratios, while the CPA exhibited an opposite trend (Table 4). Under the same nitrogen application rate and topdressing proportion, compared with K1, the K2 treatment resulted in average increases of 12.4% in NTA, 1.8% in CTG, and 4.9% in NAA, while CPA decreased by an average of 5.8%. Among the treatments, increasing the nitrogen rate in combination with a higher topdressing nitrogen ratio and potassium split application promoted nitrogen accumulation and translocation in soft wheat.

NUE

Increasing nitrogen application rates led to a decrease in NPE and NUE (Fig. 4). Compared with N1, nitrogen fertilization treatments reduced NPE and NUE by 6.9%–14.8% and 6.2%–14.6%, respectively. However, as the proportion of topdressed nitrogen increased, NPE and NUE increased by 5.5%–9.2% and 2.0%–3.2%, respectively, compared with F1. Under the same nitrogen application rate and topdressing proportion, split potassium application increased NPE and NUE by an average of 1.9% and 3.9%, respectively, compared to basal potassium application.

SPAD values

The SPAD values of functional leaves in wheat followed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with plant growth, peaking around the anthesis stage (Fig. 5). Overall, increasing nitrogen application while increasing the topdressed nitrogen proportion and applying split potassium fertilization resulted in an upward trend in SPAD values, which could delay leaf senescence.

Key enzyme activities of nitrogen metabolism

The activities of key nitrogen metabolism enzymes (NR, GS, and GOGAT) in the primary functional leaves of wheat exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with plant growth, reaching peak values around anthesis (Fig. 6). The activities of NR, GS, and GOGAT increased with higher nitrogen application rates and topdressing ratios. Under the same nitrogen application rate and topdressing proportion, split potassium application resulted in higher enzyme activities than a single basal application. Compared with K1, the K2 treatment increased NR, GS, and GOGAT activities at anthesis by 7.6%, 7.3%, and 5.4%, respectively, and at the grain-filling stage by 9.9%, 9.5%, and 6.4%, respectively.

Subsequently, the common influencing factors for GY and GPC were analyzed using PLS-SEM (Fig. 8). The analysis revealed that nitrogen rate (N), topdressing nitrogen ratio (F), potassium fertilization (K), and year (Y) influenced the GY and GPC of soft wheat through multiple coupled pathways. The model displayed a good fit (GoF = 0.90) with strong explanatory power for GY (R² = 0.95) and GPC (R² = 0.84). Nitrogen application significantly enhanced GN (path coefficient = 0.652, P < 0.001), Nitrogen Metabolic Pathway Enzymes (NMPEs, path coefficient = 0.478, P < 0.001), and Agronomic-Physiological Indicators (API, path coefficient = 0.675, P < 0.001), thereby indirectly increasing GY (total effect = 0.625) and GPC (total effect = 0.571). Optimization of the topdressing nitrogen ratio improved nitrogen assimilation and utilization, while potassium application negatively regulated wheat nitrogen assimilation and utilization, thereby reducing GPC (total effect = − 0.042), exhibiting a usual “protein dilution effect” that contributes to the quality formation of soft wheat. Significant differences were observed among years for all indicators.

Discussion

Soft wheat requires appropriate fertilization strategies to maintain low GPC while maximizing GY [33]. However, nitrogen (N) fertilization generally increases GY and GPC simultaneously [34]. In this study, increasing the nitrogen application rate and topdressing ratio indirectly enhanced GY (total effect = 0.943) by promoting GN and API pathways, while also promoting GPC formation (total effect = 0.807) through the NMPEs. Among these pathways, API was the primary mediator of GY formation (total effect on GY = 0.841), indicating that yield enhancement resulting from increased nitrogen application and higher topdressing ratios was mainly attributable to improved nitrogen accumulation and photosynthetic capacity [35, 36]. In contrast, NMPEs was the dominant mediator of GPC formation (total effect on GPC = 0.938), suggesting that increases in GPC under higher nitrogen input and topdressing ratios were largely dependent on the regulation of key nitrogen metabolism enzymes [37, 38]. Consistent with this interpretation, Zhang et al. [8] reported that the activities of key nitrogen metabolism enzymes are closely associated with nitrogen assimilation and translocation in crops. Rational nitrogen application enhances enzyme activity, thereby promoting efficient nitrogen uptake and utilization and ultimately increasing both GY and GPC. In addition, SPAD values of functional leaves increased significantly with higher nitrogen rates and topdressing ratios, further confirming that sufficient nitrogen supply enhances nitrogen accumulation and photosynthetic capacity. These results were consistent with those reported by Xiao et al. [39] and Beyyavas et al. [40]. However, with increasing nitrogen application rates, aboveground nitrogen accumulation at maturity increased significantly, whereas the corresponding increase in GY was relatively limited, resulting in a decline in NUE. Meanwhile, the additional nitrogen input was not proportionally converted into GY, leading to a reduction in NPE. These findings suggest that under higher nitrogen application levels, nitrogen partitioning in soft wheat may preferentially favor vegetative organ accumulation and grain protein synthesis, which may be associated with the higher nitrogen sensitivity of soft wheat [41].

This study further revealed a significant positive correlation between NTA and NAA, whereas CPA was negatively correlated with NPE. These relationships indicate that a higher pre-anthesis nitrogen translocation capacity is beneficial for maintaining grain nitrogen supply during the grain-filling period and improving nitrogen reutilization efficiency. In contrast, greater post-anthesis nitrogen uptake may promote the allocation of more nitrogen toward protein synthesis, thereby reducing NPE to a certain extent [42, 43]. In addition, the topdressing nitrogen ratio exerted a positive regulatory effect on both NUE and NPE, suggesting that an appropriate increase in topdressing nitrogen can alleviate the decline in nitrogen use efficiency under high nitrogen conditions by enhancing photosynthetic performance and nitrogen assimilation during grain filling, as well as promoting nitrogen translocation to grains. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Tian et al. [44] and Ali et al. [15]. However, the PLS-SEM results showed that increasing the proportion of topdressed nitrogen positively contributed to GPC formation (total effect = 0.236). When the nitrogen rate of 210 kg·ha− 1, a higher topdressing ratio resulted in excessively high GPC, thereby compromising the processing quality of soft wheat. Similarly, under 180 kg·ha–1 nitrogen application, increasing the topdressing ratio to 6:4 also caused GPC to exceed soft wheat quality standards. These findings indicate that, in soft wheat production, a trade-off exists between improving nitrogen efficiency and maintaining low-protein quality characteristics, and enhanced nitrogen efficiency does not necessarily lead to quality optimization. Therefore, rational adjustment of nitrogen application rates and topdressing nitrogen ratios not only helps optimize nitrogen assimilation and translocation but also promotes balanced grain development [45, 46].

Split potassium application also played an important role in regulating yield formation and quality traits in soft wheat. In the present study, compared with single basal potassium application, split application significantly increased NR and GOGAT activities in flag leaves, promoted nitrogen accumulation and translocation, and ultimately resulted in a slight increase in GPC under the measured conditions. Interestingly, in the PLS-SEM model, potassium application exhibited negative path coefficients with NMPEs and GPC. This discrepancy suggests that potassium fertilizer may exert both direct promotive effects on grain protein formation and indirect regulatory effects mediated through population growth and dry matter accumulation. Previous studies have demonstrated that split potassium application can enhance leaf photosynthetic capacity and assimilate transport efficiency, thereby promoting grain filling and dry matter accumulation [47–49]. Meanwhile, increased carbohydrate accumulation may reduce the nitrogen concentration per unit of dry matter, resulting in a “protein dilution effect” [50]. Therefore, although split potassium application promoted the activities of key nitrogen metabolism enzymes and pre-anthesis nitrogen translocation to a certain extent, its stimulatory effect on grain dry matter accumulation may have been stronger, ultimately leading to a negative effect on GPC in the overall model. This suggests that the regulatory effect of potassium fertilizer on GPC is not unidirectional, but rather results from the combined effects of enhanced nitrogen assimilation and increased carbon assimilation [51–53]. In addition, the present study found that split potassium application significantly increased NR and GOGAT activities at anthesis and enhanced the mobilization and redistribution of pre-anthesis nitrogen, thereby contributing to yield formation. However, during the middle and late grain-filling stages, potassium application accelerated photosynthetic carbon assimilation and leaf senescence, leading to a decline in nitrogen assimilation activity during the later growth stages [54], which consequently inhibited the continued accumulation of grain protein. This phenomenon may also be associated with antagonistic interactions between potassium and nitrogen metabolism [55, 56]. Therefore, in soft wheat, rational combined application of nitrogen and potassium fertilizers may achieve a balance between high yield and desirable protein content through coordinated regulation of nitrogen accumulation and dry matter formation. However, this study did not involve different potassium fertilizer application rates; therefore, the interpretation of the synergistic effects between potassium and nitrogen remains limited. Future research will further incorporate a range of potassium fertilizer gradients to conduct systematic experiments, in order to improve and refine the understanding of nitrogen–potassium synergistic regulatory mechanisms. In addition, this study is limited by a single experimental site and a relatively short experimental duration. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings needs to be further validated and refined through multi-site, long-term field experiments in future research. Furthermore, comprehensive evaluation incorporating fertilizer input costs and economic benefits can be performed to enhance the practical application value of the research results in agricultural production.

Overall, appropriately reducing nitrogen application rates and optimizing the topdressing nitrogen ratio, in combination with split potassium application, can improve nitrogen use efficiency and coordinate the formation of GY and GPC. This further indicates that the coordinated regulation of nitrogen and potassium is an important approach for achieving the “high-yield–low-protein” quality target in soft wheat, which is consistent with the findings of Chen et al. [57] and Yu et al. [58].

Conclusion

In the rice-wheat rotation system of the Jianghuai region in China, a comprehensive analysis of the interactions among nitrogen application rate, the ratio of topdressing nitrogen, and the potassium split application revealed that these three factors jointly exert significant effects on yield formation, quality regulation, and NUE in soft wheat. A nitrogen rate of approximately 180 kg·ha⁻1, coupled with a topdressing ratio of 7:3 and potassium splitting at the jointing stage (basal: topdressing = 5:5), significantly improved the activity of key nitrogen metabolism enzymes. This strategy enhanced biomass accumulation and translocation, optimized GPC to meet the processing quality requirements for soft wheat, and ensured grain yields exceeding 6,500 kg·ha⁻1. Therefore, under similar production conditions, it is recommended that farmers adopt a fertilization strategy consisting of a nitrogen application rate of approximately 180 kg·ha⁻1, a base-to-topdressing N ratio of 7:3, and a potassium split application with a basal-to-topdressing ratio of 5:5. This management practice can effectively maintain relatively high grain yield while controlling grain protein content in soft wheat.
