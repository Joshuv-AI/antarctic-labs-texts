High prevalence of coxsackievirus B antibodies in patients with cardiac sarcoidosis: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kimberly Lovell Donnelly, Jack Wilmoth, William Osborne, Jared Heavens, Steven Haworth, Sahil Pandya, Tarun Dalia, Mark Hamblin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is a potentially life-threatening manifestation of sarcoidosis that is frequently underdiagnosed. Identifying accessible biomarkers that could identify cardiac involvement would improve early detection. Coxsackievirus B (CVB) is proposed as a potential infectious trigger in sarcoidosis, but its relationship to CS remains poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational cohort study of patients with CS and available CVB antibody testing at the University of Kansas Health System between 2011 and 2025. Seropositivity was compared with published external comparator cohort populations, including participants from the MAVERIC registry and previously reported population-based studies. Patients ≥18 years who met Heart Rhythm Society or Japanese Circulation Society criteria for probable or definite CS were included. Demographic data, imaging findings, clinical characteristics and CVB antibody titres were extracted from the electronic medical record.ResultsA total of 56 patients with confirmed CS were included. Overall, 51 of 56 patients (91%) demonstrated CVB antibody positivity, with 80% exhibiting moderate or high titres. In an external comparator cohort population CVB positivity was 14.5%. Advanced imaging findings were consistent with cardiac involvement, including delayed gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MRI in 78% of patients and myocardial 18-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography in 87%. Conventional screening tests demonstrated limited sensitivity with abnormalities on ECG, echocardiography and ambulatory monitoring observed in a minority of patients.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a high prevalence of CVB antibody positivity among patients with CS. These findings suggest that CVB serology may represent a low-cost screening biomarker to help identify patients who may benefit from further evaluation with advanced cardiac imaging.Keywords: Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Positron-Emission Tomography, Myocarditis

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Cardiac sarcoidosis is frequently underdiagnosed because conventional screening tools have limited sensitivity, while advanced imaging modalities are costly. Prior research suggested infectious triggers such as coxsackievirus B (CVB) may contribute to sarcoidosis, but the relationship between CVB positivity and cardiac sarcoidosis had not previously been evaluated.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a systemic inflammatory disease characterised by granulomatous inflammation in one or more organs in the body.1–3 Granulomas are clusters of inflammatory cells, such as monocytes, macrophages, fibroblasts, epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells and CD4+T lymphocytes, that form in response to an antigen such as a virus, bacteria, fungus, or other foreign material.4 The aetiology of sarcoidosis is unknown, but research suggests that the exaggerated granulomatous reaction may be due to an unknown antigen exposure (such as bacteria, virus or chemical) in individuals who are genetically susceptible.5–7 In a 1999 American Thoracic Society (ATS) statement on sarcoidosis, coxsackievirus B (CVB) is listed as an agent suggested to be involved in the aetiology of sarcoidosis;1 however, no references were provided to support this finding.

In 90% of cases, sarcoidosis affects the lungs and mediastinal/hilar lymph nodes, but extrapulmonary manifestations are estimated to occur in up to 50% of patients.8 Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) can be an uncommon but clinically important manifestation.9 Depending on the population studied, the leading cause of death in patients with sarcoidosis is either pulmonary or cardiac complications.1 10 Clinical diagnosis of CS is reported in 2%–5% of cases, but a recent Swiss study showed that more routine use of advanced cardiac imaging between 2010 and 2021 correlated with an increase in clinical diagnosis from 5% to 15%.11 This enhanced detection was associated with a decrease in mortality from CS. It also suggests that the previously reported prevalence of CS is likely underrepresenting the number of actual cases.9 12 Especially when autopsy studies have clearly demonstrated a much higher prevalence of cardiac involvement. It is estimated that 25% of patients with sarcoidosis in North America have cardiac involvement and an even higher likelihood of CS is noted in the Japanese population, with estimates reported at 50% of patients or higher.8 9 13 14 Additionally, only 40%–50% of patients with CS are diagnosed during their lifetime. In some of those cases, ‘clinically silent’ CS was attributed to a sudden cardiac death event.15 16 In patients with sarcoidosis who have no symptoms and unremarkable routine testing, such as ECG, echocardiogram or Holter monitor, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging has shown that 13%–55% of patients with extracardiac sarcoidosis have cardiac involvement.9 17 Increased use of advanced cardiac imaging, including CMR and 18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), is contributing to increased detection and a rising prevalence of CS as noted in the previously mentioned Swiss Study.11 18 19

Because the diagnosis of CS is complex, cardiomyopathy or sudden arrhythmia related death may represent the initial manifestation of the disease, with incidence rising as CS becomes more clinically apparent.14 20 Given the severity of disease at presentation, there is a critical need for earlier diagnosis of CS and more effective diagnostic tools to predict cardiac involvement, enabling timely initiation of treatment to prevent life-threatening outcomes. Simple, accessible methods are urgently needed to identify and predict the development of CS.

Although advanced and more sensitive imaging techniques have recently improved the diagnosis of CS, their high cost and limited availability restrict widespread use. As outlined, traditional diagnostic tests also have well-recognised limitations in sensitivity and specificity. To address these gaps, we propose the use of a simple blood test to support diagnosis and potentially identify patients that might develop CS. Through clinical observation, we noted an apparent higher prevalence of CS patients exhibiting CVB positivity. We hypothesise that prior exposure to CVB increases the risk of CS, and that patients with CVB positivity should undergo more thorough evaluation for both symptomatic and subclinical CS at the time of sarcoidosis diagnosis, with closer longitudinal follow-up for potential disease development.

Methods

Participant data were obtained from the University of Kansas Health System electronic medical record (EMR). The original dataset was collected between 2011 and 2025 per standard of care. Data were abstracted from the University of Kansas Health System’s EMR using standardised data collection forms in REDCap by trained research personnel. The following information was extracted to evaluate the prevalence of CVB positivity in patients with CS: demographics (sex, race and ethnicity, age at sarcoidosis diagnosis and age at CS diagnosis), disease presentation, CVB positivity and test results. CVB positivity was measured using a commercial assay that targets IgG, IgM and IgA (total) but does not differentiate.

Participant data were queried from an internal electronic health record search tool for patients seen at The University of Kansas Health System from 2011 to 2025, ≥18 years of age, with the diagnosis of sarcoidosis, searched by International Classification of Diseases 10th revision code (ICD-10) and with CVB antibody testing available. Each patient was then reviewed for inclusion and determined eligible for inclusion if they met the Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) guidelines of probable or definite CS9 or the Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) 2016 guidelines for CS.12 21 Because no contemporaneous cohort of patients with sarcoidosis without cardiac involvement underwent routine CVB testing at our institution, seropositivity was compared with published external comparator cohorts, including participants from the MAVERIC registry and prior population-based studies.

Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarised as means±SD unless otherwise specified. Differences in categorical distributions were assessed using Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests with Monte Carlo simulation for r×c contingency tables. Continuous or ordinal outcomes were compared across groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests, with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. P values from multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure.

Results

In the initial cohort, 101 patients were identified. Patients were then adjudicated for the diagnosis of CS and included in the analysis if they met the HRS guidelines of probable or definite CS9 or the JCS 2016 guidelines for CS.12 21 Patients with inadequate or inconclusive diagnostic imaging were excluded (n=37), as were patients that erroneously resulted without a diagnosis of sarcoidosis (n=8), leaving a total of 56 patients for analysis.

Descriptive characteristics of the study cohort are summarised in table 1. The cohort included 31 males (55%) and 25 females (45%). Regarding ethnicity, 55 participants (98%) were non-Hispanic or Latino. By race, 43 participants (77%) were White, 11 Black (20%), 1 American Indian or Alaska Native (2%), and 1 (2%) identified as other. The mean age at sarcoidosis diagnosis was 52.2±12.5 years, and the mean age at CS diagnosis was 56.8±10.3 years. Among the 56 patients, 49 (88%) underwent extracardiac biopsy, of which 44 (90%) demonstrated granulomatous inflammation. Endomyocardial biopsy was performed in only 2 patients (4%), and neither yielded a positive result. In total, 46 patients (82%) met the HRS criteria for probable CS, and 51 patients (91%) met the JCS criteria with all patients satisfying at least one of the two guideline definitions.

Baseline organ involvement was also assessed, with most patients exhibiting pulmonary (51 patients, (91%)) and lymph node (40 patients, (71%)) involvement. Notably, three patients (5%) had isolated cardiac involvement without evidence of extracardiac disease. Two patients had CMR with delayed gadolinium enhancement (DGE) in a non-vascular distribution typical of CS with exclusion of other causes. The third patient had focal FDG uptake on serial cardiac PET imaging with the exclusion of other causes leading to diagnosis of probable CS.

CVB positivity is summarised in table 2. Positivity was defined as a titre greater than 1:8 or 1:10, depending on the assay used. Any antibody titre greater than 1:8 or 1:10 indicates the presence of detectable CVB antibodies. In clinical practice, however, a single titre of ≥1:80, or a demonstrated fourfold rise in paired titres is typically required to support the diagnosis of an active or recent infection. Titres below this diagnostic threshold are still considered positive but represent low-level seropositivity rather than definitive evidence of acute infection. Positivity was then classified into three categories: low, moderate and high. Low positivity was defined as a titre greater than 1:8 or 1:10 (depending on the assay) but less than 1:80. Moderate positivity was defined as a titre of 1:80 to 1:160 and high positivity as a titre greater than 1:160. Overall, 51 of 56 patients (91%) with CS demonstrated CVB positivity. Notably, 80% of these patients exhibited moderate (1:80 or 1:160) or high (>1:160) titres, suggestive of recent or active infection.

The serotype-specific distribution of CVB positivity is shown in table 3. Serotype B4 exhibited the highest prevalence, with 48 patients (86%) testing positive, whereas serotype B6 demonstrated the lowest, with only 1 patient (2%) testing positive. The differences in prevalence across CVB serotypes were significantly different (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test with Monte Carlo simulation, p<0.001). Among positive samples, the distribution of antibody titre level also differed significantly across CVB serotypes (Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test with Monte Carlo simulation, p<0.001). Of note, patients often tested positive for more than one serotype. Individual patient-level CVB antibody results are provided in online supplemental information.

Of the total cohort, 36 patients (64%) underwent CMR at the time of diagnosis (table 4). The mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 49%±15%, and the mean right ventricular ejection fraction was 46%±13%. Among those with CMR, 28 patients (78%) in the cohort demonstrated DGE. Among the patients who underwent CMR, 15 demonstrated high CVB antibody positivity, 16 had moderate positivity, 4 had low positivity, and 1 patient tested negative (table 5). DGE was observed in 14 of 15 patients (93%) with high antibody positivity and in 10 of 16 patients (63%) with moderate positivity. All 4 patients (100%) with low antibody positivity demonstrated DGE. The single patient who tested negative for CVB antibodies was also negative for DGE.

Among the study cohort, 52 patients (93%) underwent cardiac FDG-PET at the time of diagnosis (table 4), of whom 45 (87%) demonstrated myocardial FDG uptake. Maximum Standardized Uptake Value (SUVmax) values were available for 43 of these 45 patients (96%), with a mean SUVmax of 7.00±3.45. Among the 43 patients with available data, 26 (61%) had more severe disease with an SUVmax≥6 (mean=9.17 ± 2.66), whereas 17 (40%) had an SUVmax<6 (mean=3.70 ± 1.01). Of the 43 patients with available SUVmax data, 24 demonstrated high CVB antibody positivity, with a mean SUVmax of 6.47±3.02 (table 6). 10 patients had moderate CVB positivity, with a mean SUVmax of 7.30±3.86, and four patients had low CVB positivity, with a mean SUVmax of 5.21±1.34. In contrast, the five patients who were negative for CVB antibodies had a higher mean SUVmax of 10.43±4.25. However, the differences in SUVmax by CVB level of positivity were not significantly different (p=0.208).

In the three patients with isolated CS, cases were reviewed by a multidisciplinary committee. They met four of the five major criteria for CS meeting the JCS guidelines including positive FDG-PET imaging, after exclusion of other causes, diagnosis was made by consensus decision from the multidisciplinary committee.

Among conventional screening modalities (table 7), ECG evaluation showed that 20 patients (42%) had a QRS duration ≥120 ms, with an equal distribution of left bundle branch block and right bundle branch block. Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) were observed in nine patients (19%), while fewer than 5% demonstrated significant atrioventricular (AV) block and one patient (2%) was in atrial fibrillation. On echocardiogram, the mean LVEF of all patients (n=41) was 48.1%±15.2%, with 16 patients (39%) showing a reduced LVEF below 50% and 7 patients (17%) showing an LVEF <35%. Among patients who underwent ambulatory monitoring, five patients (25%) had a PVCs burden exceeding 3%, nine patients (45%) demonstrated supraventricular tachyarrhythmias and six patients (30%) exhibited ventricular arrhythmias.

Discussion

The diagnosis of CS is challenging and frequently involves extensive testing with low sensitivity for detection of the disease. Based on low-quality evidence, the ATS guidelines recommend obtaining only a baseline ECG in sarcoidosis patients without cardiac symptoms but note that only about 7% demonstrate abnormalities on ECG.2 The two studies cited in support of this recommendation reported widely divergent findings: one showed a sensitivity of 9% and specificity of 97%, while the other demonstrated a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 73%. Other studies have shown that ECG alone has limited diagnostic utility for CS, with a sensitivity of 21% and specificity of 81%.22 Silverman et al found that 25% of patients had normal premortem ECGs but were found to have extensive myocardial infiltration on autopsy.23 Holter monitoring is more effective than ECG in detecting arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities, with findings more common in patients with cardiac involvement than in those without.17 However, as a stand-alone diagnostic tool, its performance is limited, with a sensitivity of 59% and specificity of 58%, and is typically only abnormal in about 5% of patients.22 Echocardiographic abnormalities are observed in approximately 11% of patients, but sensitivity is only estimated at around 27%.22

Advanced cardiac imaging is recommended only when there is sufficient clinical suspicion based on symptoms or abnormalities on prior tests.2 17 Both CMR and FDG-PET demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity with estimated sensitivity reported at 95% and 84%, respectively, with similar specificity at around 82%–85%.24

In 2019, the MAVERIC registry, which included our institution, pursued FDG-PET imaging in patients with symptomatic, frequent premature ventricular contractions with PVC burden greater than 5000 PVCs in 24 hours to assess for the prevalence of myocarditis as an aetiology.25 With 107 patients enrolled, among the 55 (51%) FDG-PET-positive patients, 13 patients, 24%, were found to have a probable or definite diagnosis of CS, and 42 patients, 76%, showed idiopathic myocarditis. In the cohort, 14.5% (8 of 55) of the patients with myocarditis tested positive for coxsackie virus type B antibodies (CVB) and 11.5% (6/52) of the cohort without myocarditis tested positive for CVB. This is similar to prior reports on the prevalence of CVB positivity in the general population that range from 4% to 27%.25–28 Interestingly, the eight patients in the MAVERIC registry with CVB antibodies and myocarditis all had CS

We acknowledge that an important limitation is the absence of a contemporaneous sarcoidosis cohort without cardiac involvement. Consequently, this study cannot determine whether CVB seropositivity is associated specifically with CS or with sarcoidosis more generally. CVB serology was not used to guide immunosuppressive management. Treatment decisions were based on contemporary guideline-directed management of CS.

Sarcoidosis has been associated with an infectious trigger for the disease. Proteomic studies have shown the presence of mycobacterial proteins in biopsy samples from >40% of patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis and propionibacterium in 30% of patients with cutaneous sarcoidosis. Multiple other infectious organisms have been associated with sarcoidosis.28 Based on the findings from the MAVERIC registry and prior work showing a high prevalence of CVB in patients with sarcoidosis, along with the known method of viral entry into cardiac myocytes, we hypothesised that there would be a strong association with the presence of CVB antibodies and CS.

In our present study, we found that 91% of patients with CS were positive for antibodies against CVB with 80% showing moderate or high levels of antibody titres. Due to the retrospective nature of the investigation, we have the contemporaneous MAVERIC registry as a comparator group showing a prevalence of 11.5% in a healthy cohort and 14.5% in patients with myocarditis (although a notably lower prevalence when patients with CS are excluded).

Similar to the established medical literature, the prevalence of abnormalities on traditional screening studies (ECG, echocardiogram and Holter) for CS was low. On cardiac FDG-PET imaging, 25 of 45 patients (61%) had an SUVmax >6 with a mean of 9.17. On CMR imaging 28 of 36 patients (78%) were noted to have DGE. Both of these findings are notable as both have been associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure and sudden cardiac death over the next 5 plus years.29 30

This retrospective study suggests that CVB antibody positivity is high in patients with CS. The cost of testing each serotype is low, making it a potentially cost-effective screening biomarker with potential for high sensitivity for CS.

These results will need to be confirmed in prospective studies, but if validated, screening for CS could potentially be done at a low cost with more targeted application of CMR or FDG-PET, and it would suggest a pathologic mechanism for the development of CS that may open the door for novel treatment approaches and/or preventive vaccination in high risk patients.

Conclusions

This is the first study assessing CVB positivity as a potential screening biomarker in CS. We demonstrated that in patients with established CS, there is a strong association with antibodies to coxsackie virus type B. CVB positivity in this population was also associated with the presence of DGE on CMRI which is also associated with high-risk features for ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure and sudden cardiac death. These results suggest that a simple blood test may help identify when advanced cardiac imaging should be employed to aid in the diagnosis of CS. If results are validated prospectively, these findings suggest that CVB serology may become a useful adjunctive biomarker for identifying patients who warrant further evaluation with advanced cardiac imaging and this would be the first correlation of CS to a specific antigen exposure. It could open the door for changes in treatment or preventative action in high-risk patients.
