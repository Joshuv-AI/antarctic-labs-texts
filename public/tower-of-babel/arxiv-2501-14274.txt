Exotic proper actions on homogeneous spaces via convex cocompact representations
Maciej Bocheński, Yosuke Morita

Abstract
We construct a series of homogeneous spaces G / H G/H of reductive type which admit proper actions of discrete subgroups of G G isomorphic to cocompact lattices of O ⁡ ( n , 1 ) \OO(n,1) ( n = 2 , 3 , 4 n=2,3,4 ) but do not admit proper actions of non-compact semisimple subgroups of G G . The existence of such homogeneous spaces was previously not known even for n = 2 n=2 . Our construction of proper actions of discrete subgroups is based on Guéritaud–Kassel’s work on convex cocompact subgroups of O ⁡ ( n , 1 ) \OO(n,1) and Danciger–Guéritaud–Kassel’s work on right-angled Coxeter groups. On the other hand, the non-existence of proper actions of non-compact semisimple subgroups is proved by the theory of nilpotent orbits and elementary combinatorics.



1
Introduction

1.1
The problem and the main result

Let
G
be a linear reductive Lie group and
H\subset G
a reductive subgroup.
If a discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset G
acts properly on the homogeneous space
G/H
,
then the double coset space
\Gamma\backslash G/H
is called a
Clifford-Klein form
.
We also say that
\Gamma
is a
discontinuous group
for
G/H
.
We are interested in the non-Riemannian case, i.e. the case where
H
is non-compact.
In this situation, not every discrete subgroup of
G
acts properly on
G/H
.
Indeed, lattices of
G
never act properly on
G/H
by, for instance, Moore’s ergodicity theorem.

Fortunately, there is a convenient way of obtaining non-trivial examples of Clifford–Klein forms:
if a reductive non-compact subgroup
L
of
G
acts properly on
G/H
, then any closed subgroup of
L

(and in particular, any discrete subgroup of
L
) also acts properly on
G/H
.
Proper actions of discrete subgroups obtained in this way,
as well as the associated Clifford–Klein forms, are called
standard
.
For some
G/H
, this construction even yields
cocompact
proper actions:

Example 1.1

(Kobayashi
[
Kob89
, Prop. 4.9]
; see also Kulkarni
[
Kul81
, Thm. 6.1]
)
.

Let
G=\OO(2n,2)
,
H=\UU(n,1)
, and
L=\OO(2n,1)
.
Then, we have
G=LH
, and furthermore,
H\cap L=\UU(n)
is compact.
Thus,
L
acts properly and transitively on
L/(H\cap L)\cong G/H
.
Taking
\Gamma
to be a cocompact lattice of
L=\OO(2n,1)
,
we obtain a compact Clifford–Klein form of
G/H=\OO(2n,2)/\UU(n,1)
.

Of course, not all proper actions are standard.
In fact, it is now known that some compact Clifford–Klein forms are non-standard in a very strong sense:

Example 1.2

(Lee–Marquis
[
LM19
, Thm. A]
+ Guéritaud–Guichard–Kassel–Wienhard
[
GGKW17
, Cor. 1.9]
for
n=2,3,4
; Monclair–Schlenker–Tholozan
[
MST23+
, Cor. 1.9]
for general
n
)
.

For any
n\geqslant 2
, there exists a compact Clifford–Klein form of
\OO(2n,2)/\UU(n,1)

whose discontinuous group
\Gamma
is not commensurable to any lattice of any linear reductive Lie group.

In general, a proper action which is not even a deformation of a standard proper action is called
exotic

(see Salein
[
Sal00
]
and Lakeland–Leininger
[
LL17
]

for other examples of exotic compact Clifford–Klein forms).

One of the main themes in the theory of Clifford–Klein forms is the following general question,
with varying meanings of the word ‘large’:

To what extent the existence of a ‘large’ discontinuous group
\Gamma
for
G/H
implies
the existence of a ‘large’ reductive subgroup
L
acting properly on
G/H
?

One instance of this question, where ‘large’ means ‘cocompact’, is Toshiyuki Kobayashi’s conjecture which asserts that

a homogeneous space of reductive type which admits a compact Clifford–Klein form
also admits a standard compact Clifford–Klein form

(
[
Kob97
, §6, (vi)]
,
[
Kob01
, Conj. 4.3]
).
It is recognized as one of the biggest open problems in this area, and its solution seems to be still out of reach.
Note that the conjecture does
not
say that every compact Clifford–Klein form is standard.
In particular, Example
1.2
is not a counterexample to this conjecture.

Another important topic in this area to study the above question for a
fixed
class of discrete groups
\Gamma
.
In this paper, we study this problem in the case where

\Gamma
is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
for
n=2,3
, and
4
.
A simplified version of our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.3

(cf. Theorem
1.12
)
.

There is a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type with the following two properties:

•

For
n=2,3
, and
4
, there exists a discrete subgroup of
G

which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.

•

There does not exist a non-compact semisimple subgroup of
G
which acts properly on
G/H
.

In other words, an analogue of Kobayashi’s conjecture is
false
in this setting:
the above
G/H
admits a proper action of a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)\ (n=2,3,4)
,
whereas it does not admit a
standard
one.

1.2
Comparison to previous results

When
\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
L\cong\mathbb{R}
, the problem was completely settled by Kobayashi in his Ph.D. thesis.
In this case, the existence of proper actions is equivalent to the existence of standard ones:

Fact 1.4

(Kobayashi
[
Kob89
, §4]
)
.

For a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type, the following three conditions are equivalent:

•

(P-inf)
—

G/H
admits a proper action of an infinite discrete subgroup of
G
.

•

(P-Z)
—

G/H
admits a proper action of a discrete subgroup of
G
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.

•

(P-R)
—

G/H
admits a proper action of a reductive subgroup of
G
isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
.

Furthermore, these conditions are also equivalent to
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}G>\rank_{\mathbb{R}}H
,
where
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}
stands for the real rank of reductive Lie groups.

Fact
1.4
is called the
Calabi–Markus phenomenon

because this kind of phenomenon was first found in their work on Lorentzian geometry
[
CM62
]
.

Remark 1.5
.

The above terminologies (P-inf), (P-Z) and (P-R) are not conventional at all.
We just introduced them in order to facilitate the comparison of various results in this area.
The same applies to the other conditions which we shall introduce below.

The ‘second smallest case’, namely, the case where
L
is locally isomorphic to
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})

(or equivalently, to
\OO(2,1)
), has been studied actively in the last three decades.
In this case, the natural classes of
\Gamma
to be considered are surface groups and free groups,
as they are respectively the classes of cocompact and non-cocompact lattices of
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, up to commensurability.
Thus, let us introduce the following three conditions on a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type:

•

(P-sl
2
R) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a closed subgroup of
G
locally isomorphic to
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
.

•

(P-surf) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a discrete subgroup of
G
isomorphic to a surface group of genus
\geqslant 2
.

•

(P-free) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a discrete subgroup of
G
isomorphic to a non-abelian free group.

We also consider:

•

(P-nva) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a non-virtually abelian discrete subgroup of
G
.

An easy observation is that the following implications hold:

(\text{P-sl}_{2}\text{R})\ \Rightarrow\ (\text{P-surf})\ \Rightarrow\ (\text{P-free})\ \Rightarrow\ (\text{P-nva})\ \Rightarrow\ (\text{P-inf}).

Also, it is easy to see that the condition (P-sl
2
R) is equivalent to the following:

•

(P-ss) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a non-compact semisimple subgroup of
G
.

Let us now review two important results on this problem, due to Benoist and Okuda, respectively.
Firstly, Benoist proved the following result in the mid-90s:

Fact 1.6

(Benoist
[
Ben96
]
)
.

Let
G/H
be a homogeneous space of reductive type.

(1)

The conditions
(P-free)
and
(P-nva)
are equivalent. Moreover, they are also equivalent to

\mathfrak{a}^{-w_{0}}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
.

(2)

The condition
(P-inf)
does not imply
(P-free)
.
For example,
G/H=\SL(3,\mathbb{R})/\SO(2,1)
is
(P-inf)
but not
(P-free)
.

We do not explain the meaning of the last condition in Fact
1.6
(1), as it is not used in this paper.
The important point is that it is completely root-theoretic and easily checkable for a given
G/H
.
Unlike Kobayashi’s result mentioned above, proper actions constructed by Benoist are usually non-standard:
they come from a version of the ping-pong lemma.

Much later, Okuda proved the following result:

Fact 1.7

(Okuda
[
Oku16
]
)
.

The condition
(P-free)
does not imply
(P-sl
2
R)
. More precisely, if
G=\SL(5,\mathbb{R})
and

H=\exp(\mathfrak{h}),\qquad\mathfrak{h}=\left\{\diag(t_{1},\dots,t_{5})\ \middle|\ \sum_{i=1}^{5}t_{i}=0,\ 3t_{1}+2t_{2}+t_{3}=0\right\},

then
G/H
is
(P-free)
but not
(P-sl
2
R)
.

In Subsection
5.1
,
we review other previously known examples of homogeneous spaces which are (P-free) but not (P-sl
2
R).

Remark 1.8
.

Okuda proved earlier in
[
Oku13
]
that, if
H
is a
symmetric subgroup
of
G

(i.e. is locally a fixed-point subgroup of some involution on
G
),
then (P-sl
2
R) is equivalent to
\mathfrak{a}^{-w_{0}}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
.
Thus, the five conditions (P-ss), (P-sl
2
R), (P-surf), (P-free), and (P-nva) are all equivalent in this case.

In summary, the following implications and non-implications were previously known:

(P-sl
2
R)
(P-surf)
(P-free)
(P-inf)
(P-ss)
(P-nva)
(P-Z)
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{(P-R)}.}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\diagup}
[
Oku16
]
[
Ben96
]
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\diagup}
[
Ben96
]
[
Kob89
]
[
Kob89
]

Our Theorem
1.3
for
n=2
now shows that (P-surf) does not imply (P-sl
2

We still do not know if (P-free) is equivalent to (P-surf) (see Conjecture
5.1
).

1.3
A detailed version of the main result

Let us now explain our main results in more detail.
For brevity, we introduce yet another notation:

•

(P-cocH
n
) —
G/H
admits a proper action of a discrete subgroup of
G
isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
.

Remark 1.9
.

As we mentioned already, since
\OO(2,1)
and
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
are locally isomorphic,
the condition (P-cocH
2
) is equivalent to (P-surf).

For
n=2,3
, and
4
, we prove the following sufficient condition for (P-cocH
n
):

Theorem 1.10

(see §
2.1
for notation)
.

Let
G
be a linear reductive Lie group and
H
a reductive subgroup of
G
.
Suppose that
L
is a reductive subgroup of
G
satisfying the following two properties:

•

L
is locally isomorphic to
\OO(n,1)\times\OO(N,1)
, where
(n,N)=(2,3),(3,6)
, or
(4,8)
.

•

\mathfrak{a}_{L}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
.

Then, there exists a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
L

which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.
In particular,
G/H
is
(P-cocH
n
)
in this situation.

In fact, there are continuously many such
\Gamma
up to conjugation (see Remark
3.6
).

Remark 1.11
.

If
\mathfrak{a}_{L}\cap W\mathfrak{a}_{H}=0
, the above theorem is an immediate consequence of
the properness criterion by Kobayashi and Benoist (Fact
2.1
).
The theorem says that a weaker condition
\mathfrak{a}_{L}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
suffices.

Theorem
1.10
enables us to construct an infinite series of homogeneous spaces of reductive type
which are not (P-ss) but (P-cocH
n
) for
n=2,3
, and
4
:

Theorem 1.12
.

For
m\geqslant 2
, let
G=\OO(m+1,m)
or
\OO(2m+1,\mathbb{C})
, and let
H
be a reductive subgroup of
G
such that

\mathfrak{a}_{H}=\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid 2t_{1}+t_{2}+t_{3}+\dots+t_{m-1}=0\}\subset\mathfrak{a},

where
\mathfrak{a}
is identified with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in the standard way.

(1)

Let
(n,N)=(2,3),(3,6)
, or
(4,8)
.
If
m\geqslant N
, then
G/H
is
(P-cocH
n
)
,
i.e. there exists a discrete subgroup of
G
which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.

(2)

If
m\geqslant 4
, then
G/H
is not
(P-ss)
,
i.e. there does not exist a non-compact semisimple subgroup of
G
which acts properly on
G/H
.

Remark 1.13
.

In the above theorem,
H
can have a rather large semisimple part.
For instance, one can take
H
to be isomorphic to
\SL(m-2,\mathbb{R})\times\OO(1,2)\times\mathbb{R}
in the real case
(and similarly,
\SL(m-2,\mathbb{C})\times\OO(3,\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{C}^{\times}
in the complex case).

Let us make a few comments on the proofs of Theorems
1.10
and
1.12
.

The proof of Theorem
1.10
uses the theory of
convex cocompact representations
.
More precisely, utilizing Guéritaud–Kassel’s work
[
GK17
]
,
we reduce our problem to finding a cocompact lattice
\Gamma
of
\OO(n,1)
and
a small deformation of its standard convex cocompact representation

\Gamma\hookrightarrow\OO(n,1)\hookrightarrow\OO(N,1)

which has a nice property (namely, being
strictly dominated
by the original representation).
Such a cocompact lattice and its deformation are known to exist for
(n,N)=(2,3),(3,6)
, and
(4,8)

by Danciger–Guéritaud–Kassel’s work on right-angled Coxeter groups
[
DGK20
]
,
hence we obtain Theorem
1.10
for these values of
(n,N)
.
Theorem
1.12
(1) is an easy consequence of Theorem
1.10
.

Remark 1.14
.

Theorem
1.10
can be seen as a generalization of Danciger–Guéritaud–Kassel
[
DGK20
, Prop. 1.8]

from
G/H=(\OO(N,1)\times\OO(N,1))/{\Delta\OO(N,1)}
to a more general homogeneous space.
Note, however, that our setting is more complicated:
one cannot directly obtain proper actions on
G/H
from metric contractions on the hyperbolic space.

On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
1.12
(2) is based on
the theory of
nilpotent orbits
in semisimple Lie algebras.
It reduces our tasks to show some combinatorial assertion (namely, (
\ast_{m}
) in Section 4).
That assertion is proved by a careful case-by-case analysis, which nevertheless is completely elementary.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Linear reductive Lie groups

Let
G
be a linear reductive Lie group and denote by
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra. Choose a Cartan involution
\theta
of
G
.
We obtain a Cartan decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p},\qquad\mathfrak{k}=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid\theta(X)=X\},\qquad\mathfrak{p}=\{X\in\mathfrak{g}\mid-\theta(X)=X\},

and
\mathfrak{k}
is the Lie algebra of the maximal compact subgroup

K=\{k\in G\mid\theta(k)=k\}
of
G
.
Choose a maximal abelian subspace
\mathfrak{a}
of
\mathfrak{p}

and denote by
\Sigma=\Sigma(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{a})
the restricted root system of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Choose a subset
\Delta\subset\Sigma
of simple restricted roots and denote by

\Sigma^{+}\subset\Sigma
and
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}\subset\mathfrak{a}

the set of positive restricted roots and the closed positive Weyl chamber with respect to
\Delta
, respectively.

We allow
G
to have multiple connected components,
but we still assume that the root-theoretically defined restricted Weyl group, denoted as
W
,
coincides with the group-theoretically defined one:

W=N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a}).

This always holds when
G
is of inner type (and in particular, when
G
is connected).
Another example of such
G
is
\OO(p,q)
with
p\neq q
.

Fix a
G
-invariant and
\theta
-invariant symmetric bilinear form such that
(X,Y)\mapsto-B(\theta X,Y)

is positive definite (if
G
is semisimple, one may take
B
to be the Killing form).
Let
\|{-}\|\colon\mathfrak{a}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be the norm associated with
B|_{\mathfrak{a}\times\mathfrak{a}}
.
We regard
\mathfrak{a}
and its subset
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
as metric spaces by the distance function induced from this norm.

A closed subgroup
H
of
G
is called a
reductive subgroup
of
G

if it is reductive as an abstract Lie group, and furthermore,
\theta(H)=H
for some Cartan involution
\theta
on
G
.
The homogeneous space
G/H
is said to be
of reductive type
in this setting.
It is known that any connected subgroup of
G
which is semisimple as an abstract Lie group is
closed and a reductive subgroup in
G
.
If
G
is equipped with a specified Cartan involution
\theta
and a specified maximal abelian subspace
\mathfrak{a}
of
\mathfrak{p}
,
every reductive subgroup
H
of
G
is implicitly assumed to satisfy the following two conditions:

•

\theta(H)=H
.

•

\mathfrak{a}_{H}=\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{h}
is a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}\cap\mathfrak{h}
.

Note that one can always achieve these conditions by replacing
H
with its conjugate in
G
.

We say that an element of
\mathfrak{g}
is
nilpotent
(resp.
hyperbolic
) if it is conjugate to some element in

\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

(resp.
\mathfrak{a}
). If
G
is semisimple, then
X\in\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent (resp. hyperbolic) precisely when

\ad(X)\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent (resp. diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
) as a linear endomorphism.
A conjugacy class of nilpotent (resp. hyperbolic) elements in
\mathfrak{g}
is called
a
nilpotent
(resp.
hyperbolic
)
orbit
.
The name comes from an obvious fact that conjugacy classes of elements in
\mathfrak{g}
are nothing but
\Ad(G)
-orbits.

Let
\mu\colon G\to\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
be the
Cartan projection
on
G
,
namely,
\mu(g)
is the unique element of
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
whose exponential
\exp(\mu(g))\in G
is contained in
KgK
.
We have the following criterion for the properness of actions:

Fact 2.1

(Kobayashi
[
Kob89
]
,
[
Kob96
, Th. 1.1]
, Benoist
[
Ben96
, Th. 5.2]
)
.

Let
H
and
L
be two closed subgroups of
G
. Then,
L
acts properly on
G/H
if and only if
the set

\mu(L)\cap\overline{N}(\mu(H),R)

is bounded in
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
for any
R\geqslant 0
,
where
\overline{N}(-,R)
signifies the closed
R
-neighbourhood.

For readability purpose, we shall write
\mu_{j}(\gamma)
instead of
\mu(j(\gamma))
whenever
j\colon\Gamma\to G
is a group homomorphism and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

2.2
Convex cocompact representations into
\OO(n,1)

Throughout this subsection, let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group, and let

\lvert-\rvert\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{N}

be the word length function associated with a fixed finite and symmetric generating set of
\Gamma
.
We write

\mathcal{H}^{n}=\OO(n,1)/(\OO(n)\times\OO(1))

for the
n
-dimensional real hyperbolic space. We always assume that
n\geqslant 2
and that
\Gamma
is infinite.

Definition 2.2
.

A representation
j\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
is called
convex cocompact

if it has the following two properties:

(i)

The kernel of
j
is finite.

(ii)

There exists a nonempty
j(\Gamma)
-invariant closed convex subset of
\mathcal{H}^{n}

on which
j(\Gamma)
acts cocompactly.

We write
\Hom^{\textup{cc}}(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))
for the subspace of
\Hom(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))

consisting of convex cocompact representations.

Let us recall two basic properties of convex cocompact representations:

Fact 2.3

(see e.g. Bourdon
[
Bou95
, §1.8]
)
.

A representation
j\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
is convex cocompact if and only if
there exist
\varepsilon,M>0
such that,
for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we have

\|\mu_{j}(\gamma)\|\geqslant\varepsilon\lvert\gamma\rvert-M.

Fact 2.4

(see e.g. Bowditch
[
Bow98
, Prop. 4.1]
)
.

The subspace
\Hom^{\textup{cc}}(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))
is open in
\Hom(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
N\geqslant n\geqslant 2
, and let
\Gamma
be a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
.
It follows immediately from Fact
2.4
that any small deformation of the composite

\Gamma\hookrightarrow\OO(n,1)\hookrightarrow\OO(N,1),

where the latter embedding is the standard block-diagonal one,
is a convex cocompact representation of
\Gamma
into
\OO(N,1)
.

Now, let us briefly summarize (some part of) the work by Guéritaud–Kassel
[
GK17
]
,
which we shall use in Section
3
.
Note that, for
n=2
, the results below are proved earlier in Kassel’s Ph.D. thesis
[
Kas09
, Chap. 5]
:

Fact 2.6

(Guéritaud–Kassel
[
GK17
, Lem. 4.7 (1)]
)
.

Let
j\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
be a convex cocompact representation and

\rho\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
a representation.
Then, there exists a
(j,\rho)
-equivariant Lipschitz map from
\mathcal{H}^{n}
to itself.

For

and
\rho
as above, define

to be the
best Lipschitz constant
, i.e. the infimum of
c\geqslant 0

(j,\rho)
-equivariant

-Lipschitz map
from
\mathcal{H}^{n}
to itself.
We say that

.

Remark 2.7
.

In general, the infimum may not be attained. See
[

, §10.3]
.

Fact 2.8

(Guéritaud–Kassel
[
GK17
, Prop. 1.5]
)
.

The following map is continuous:

C_{\textup{Lip}}\colon\Hom^{\textup{cc}}(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))\times\Hom(\Gamma,\OO(n,1))\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0},\qquad(j,\rho)\mapsto C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho).

In the above situation, we define

C_{\textup{Car}}(j,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
to be the infimum of
c\geqslant 0
satisfying

\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\left(\|\mu_{\rho}(\gamma)\|-c\|\mu_{j}(\gamma)\|\right)<\infty.

It follows easily from the definition that
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)\geqslant C_{\textup{Car}}(j,\rho)
. We thus have:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
j\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
be a convex cocompact representation and

\rho\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
a representation.
If
c>C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)
, there exists
M>0
such that, for any
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we have

c\|\mu_{j}(\gamma)\|+M\geqslant\|\mu_{\rho}(\gamma)\|,

where
\mu_{j}=\mu\circ j
and
\mu_{\rho}=\mu\circ\rho
.

Proof.

This follows from the inequality
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)\geqslant C_{\textup{Car}}(j,\rho)

and the definition of
C_{\textup{Car}}
.
∎

Furthermore, the following holds:

Fact 2.10

(Guéritaud–Kassel
[
GK17
, §7 and Cor. 1.12]
)
.

Let
j\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
be a convex cocompact representation and

\rho\colon\Gamma\to\OO(n,1)
a representation.
Then, we have the following:

(1)

C_{\textup{Car}}(j,\rho)<1
if and only if
\rho
is strictly dominated by
j

(i.e.
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)<1)
.

(2)

If
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)\geqslant 1
, then
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,\rho)=C_{\textup{Car}}(j,\rho)
.

From this, one immediately obtains the following:

Lemma 2.11
.

For any convex cocompact representation

,
we have
C_{\textup{Lip}}(j,j)=1
.

Proof.

This follows from
C_{\textup{Car}}(j,j)=1
and Fact
2.10
(2).
∎

2.3

\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
-actions

In this subsection,
G
is assumed to be a linear
semisimple
Lie group for simplicity.
The standard textbook for the results in this subsection is Collingwood–McGovern
[
CM93
]
.

We first observe that, since
G
is linear,
any Lie algebra homomorphism from
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
to
\mathfrak{g}
lifts uniquely to a Lie group homomorphism from
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
to
G
.
To put differently, the classification of connected subgroups in
G
locally isomorphic to
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to
the classification of Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
It is customary to describe the latter in the following language:

Definition 2.15
.

A triple
(h,e,f)
of elements in
\mathfrak{g}
is called an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple
if

[h,e]=2e,\qquad[h,f]=-2f,\qquad[e,f]=h.

The elements
h,e,f\in\mathfrak{g}
are called
the
neutral
,
nilpositive
, and
nilnegative element
of the triple, respectively.

In other words,
(h,e,f)
is called an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple if the following is a Lie algebra homomorphism:

\varphi\colon\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\to\mathfrak{g},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&-a\end{pmatrix}\mapsto ah+be+cf.

Our convention is that the trivial triple
(h,e,f)=(0,0,0)
is an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple.

We now recall two fundamental results on
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples:

Fact 2.16

(Jacobson–Morozov; see e.g.
[
CM93
, Thms. 3.3.1 and 9.2.1]
)
.

Every nilpotent element in
\mathfrak{g}
is a nilpositive element of some
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Fact 2.17

(Kostant; see e.g.
[
CM93
, Thms. 3.4.10 and 9.2.3]
)
.

Let
(h,e,f)
and
(h^{\prime},e,f^{\prime})
be two
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples in
\mathfrak{g}
with the same nilpositive element
e
.
Then, there exists
g\in\Stab_{G}(e)
such that
h^{\prime}=\Ad(g)h
and
f^{\prime}=\Ad(g)f
,
where
\Stab_{G}(e)
is the stabilizer of
e
in
G
.

These two facts imply that the assignment
(h,e,f)\mapsto e
induces a bijection
from the set of conjugacy classes of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples in
\mathfrak{g}
to the set of nilpotent orbits in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let
(h,e,f)
be an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple in
\mathfrak{g}
.
The hyperbolic orbit
\Ad(G)h
depends only on the nilpotent orbit
\Ad(G)e
,
hence we call it the

associated with
\Ad(G)e
.
It follows that each distinguished hyperbolic orbit in
\mathfrak{g}
intersects
\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
at exactly one point,
which we call the

.

5
Some open questions

In this section, we discuss some open questions raised by this work.

5.1
On proper actions of surface groups

Concerning connections between the conditions
(P-sl
2
R), (P-surf), (P-free), (P-nva), and (P-inf), the last remaining problem is the following:

Conjecture 5.1
.

The condition
(P-free)
does not imply
(P-surf)
,
namely, there exists a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type
which admits a proper action of a non-abelian free discrete subgroup of
G

but does not admit a proper action of a discrete surface subgroup of
G
of genus
\geqslant 2
.

As far as the authors know, there are no available tools to prove the failure of (P-surf)
other than showing
\mathfrak{a}^{-w_{0}}\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
(i.e. showing the failure of (P-free)).
We need a new necessary condition for a homogeneous space to be (P-surf) to solve this conjecture.

Before our work, there were four known examples of homogeneous spaces of reductive type
which are (P-free) but not (P-sl
2
R):

(i)

Okuda’s first example which we restated in Fact
1.7
.

(ii)

An example given in the first author’s paper
[
Boc17
, Ex. 1]
, where

(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h})=(\mathfrak{o}(4,4),\,\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathbb{R})
.

(iii)

Two examples given in the first author’s joint work
[
BdGJT22+
, Thm. 2]

with de Graaf, Jastrzȩbski, and Tralle, where

(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{h})=(\mathfrak{e}_{7(7)},\,\mathfrak{f}_{4(4)}\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R}))
and
(\mathfrak{e}_{8(8)},\,\mathfrak{f}_{4(4)}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2(2)})
.

One can easily see that the example (ii) satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.10
for
n=2

(and therefore, is (P-surf)).
On the other hand,
\SL(5,\mathbb{R})
does not admit a reductive subgroup locally isomorphic to
\OO(2,1)\times\OO(3,1)
,
hence our method does not apply to the example (i).
We have not checked yet if our method works for the two exceptional examples (iii).
We thus ask:

Problem 5.2
.

Determine if Okuda’s homogeneous space
(i)
and the two exceptional homogeneous spaces
(iii)

are
(P-surf)
or not.

See Remark
5.18
for our failed attempt to show that Okuda’s homogeneous space is (P-surf).

We also make the following conjecture:

Conjecture 5.3
.

For any linear real simple Lie group
G
with sufficiently high real rank,
there exists a reductive subgroup
H
of
G
for which
G/H
is
(P-surf)
but not
(P-ss)
.

Remark 5.4
.

The conjecture does not hold when
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}G\leqslant 3
. See Remark
5.10
below.

We are inclined to believe that Theorem
1.10
is enough to show this conjecture
(even for any
G
of real rank
\geqslant 5
, perhaps), although it may well be wrong.
The case where
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}G=4
should be difficult: as we explained above, our method does not work for
G=\SL(5,\mathbb{R})
.

5.2
On proper actions of other discrete subgroups

Theorem
1.12
showed that not only (P-surf) but even (P-cocH
4
) is not enough to ensure (P-ss).
We conjecture the following stronger result:

Conjecture 5.5
.

For any
n\geqslant 2
,
there is a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type with the following two properties:

•

G/H
is
(P-cocH
n
)
,
i.e. there exists a discrete subgroup of
G
which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.

•

G/H
is not
(P-ss)
,
i.e. there does not exist a non-compact semisimple subgroup of
G
which acts properly on
G/H
.

By the discussion in Section
3
, this conjecture would follow once we could prove the following:

Conjecture 5.6
.

For any
n\geqslant 2
, there exists a cocompact lattice
\Gamma
of
\OO(n,1)
and
N\geqslant n

such that any neighbourhood of the standard embedding

\Gamma\hookrightarrow\OO(n,1)\hookrightarrow\OO(N,1)

in
\Hom(\Gamma,\OO(N,1))
contains a representation strictly dominated by it.

It is known that, if
n\geqslant 5
,
there does not exist a
cocompact
right-angled reflection group in
\OO(n,1)

(Potyagailo–Vinberg
[
PV05
, §2]
),
which is why Conjecture
5.6
is open in these dimensions.
It is, however, still possible to ask the following question:

Question 5.7

(cf. Kapovich
[
Kap05
]
, Desgroseilliers–Haglund
[
DH13
]
)
.

Which discrete group admits an embedding into a convex cocompact right-angled reflection group in
\OO(n,1)
,
with
n
arbitrarily large?

For instance, if we could find a cocompact lattice of
\OO(k,1)

which embeds into a convex cocompact right-angled reflection group in
\OO(n,1)
(for
n
sufficiently large),
then we would be able to conclude from Theorem
3.8
that (P-cocH
k
) does not imply (P-ss).

Remark 5.8
.

Many discrete groups are known to admit virtual embeddings into right-angled Coxeter groups.
For instance, Bergeron–Haglund–Wise
[
BHW11
, Thm. 1.8]
proved that
every arithmetic lattice of
\OO(n,1)
of simplest type virtually embeds into some right-angled Coxeter group.
However, it seems difficult to control the signature of Tits–Vinberg representations
of the target right-angled Coxeter group.

5.3
On proper actions of semisimple Lie groups

As we mentioned in Remark
1.8
, Okuda proved that
the condition
\mathfrak{a}^{-w_{0}}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
implies (P-sl
2
R) when
H
is a symmetric subgroup of
G

(and thus, the conditions (P-ss), (P-sl
2
R), (P-surf), (P-free), and (P-nva) are all equivalent in this case).
In particular, exotic phenomena like Theorem
1.3
or Fact
1.7

never happens for such
G/H
.
The first author, Jastrzȩbski, Okuda, and Tralle proved in
[
BJOT15
, Prop. 4.1]
that
the same holds whenever
\rank_{\mathbb{R}}H=1
.
It would be worthwhile to find other classes of
G/H
having this property:

Problem 5.9
.

Find many more classes of homogeneous spaces of reductive type for which

\mathfrak{a}^{-w_{0}}\not\subset W\mathfrak{a}_{H}
implies
(P-sl
2
R)
.

Remark 5.10
.

In fact, we recently found two other classes of
G/H
with the above property,
which we would like to discuss in another paper:

•

\rank_{\mathbb{R}}G\leqslant 3
.

•

H
is locally a fixed-point subgroup of some inner automorphism of
G
.

Given that the condition (P-sl
2
R) is now relatively well-understood,
it is natural to search for homogeneous spaces that
do admit a proper action of a closed subgroup locally isomorphic to
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})

but do not admit proper actions of ‘larger’ reductive subgroups.
In general, given two real semisimple Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{l}
,
it seems very difficult to classify all conjugacy classes of subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{l}
.
However, when
\mathfrak{l}=\mathfrak{o}(3,1)
or
\mathfrak{o}(2,2)
(i.e.
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
or
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
),
we expect that the classification is, in principle, manageable:
classify all subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})
,
and then, check if each subalgebra in the list is ‘compatible’ with the Satake diagram of
\mathfrak{g}

(in a similar sense as Okuda
[
Oku13
]
).
We therefore ask the following:

Problem 5.11
.

Develop an efficient method to verify if a given homogeneous space of reductive type
admits a proper action of reductive subgroup locally isomorphic to
\OO(3,1)
(or equivalently,
\SL(2,\mathbb{C})
),
and classify all reductive symmetric spaces having this property.

Kazuki Kannaka told us that he accomplished the classification when
G/H
is a classical symmetric space (private communication).

Question 5.12
.

Does an analogue of Okuda’s theorem
[
Oku13
]
hold for
\OO(3,1)
?
More precisely, is there a reductive symmetric space
G/H
with the following two properties?

•

G/H
is
(P-cocH
3
)
, i.e. there exists a discrete subgroup of
G
which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(3,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.

•

There does not exist a reductive subgroup which is locally isomorphic to
\OO(3,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
.

It may be also interesting to study analogues of Problem
5.11
and Question
5.12
for other semisimple Lie groups (e.g.
\OO(2,2)
or
\OO(4,1)
). Okuda
[
Oku14
]
gave a necessary condition for a reductive symmetric space to admit proper actions of
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
or
\SU(2,1)
.

5.4
Other questions

The discrete subgroup
\Gamma
obtained by Theorem
1.10
is contained in
L
.
In particular, it is not Zariski dense in
G
(unless
L=G
).
It leads us to consider the following question:

Question 5.13
.

In the setting of Theorem
1.12
(1), is there a Zariski dense discrete subgroup of
G

which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(n,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
?

We have the following partial result, which unfortunately does not generalize to
(n,N)=(3,6),(4,8)
:

Proposition 5.14
.

In the setting of Theorem
1.12
(1) with
(n,N)=(2,3)
, there exists a Zariski-dense discrete surface subgroup of
G
which acts properly on
G/H
.

Proof.

Take a discrete subgroup
\Gamma
of
G
which is isomorphic to a cocompact lattice of
\OO(2,1)
and acts properly on
G/H
. We denote by
j\colon\Gamma\hookrightarrow G
the embedding.
Passing to finite-index subgroups if necessary, we may assume that
\Gamma
is a surface group of genus
\geqslant 2(\dim G)^{2}
. Then, there is a Zariski-dense representation of
\Gamma
into
G
which is arbitrarily close to
j
by Kim–Pansu
[
KP15
, Thm. 1]
. On the other hand, by Remark
3.5
and Kassel–Tholozan
[
KT24+
, Cor. 6.3 (2)]
, any small deformation of
j
is a sharp embedding into
G
with respect to
H
. In particular, it acts properly on
G/H
.
∎

Recall that the main motivation of this study is the relation between
the existence of a ‘large’ discontinuous group
\Gamma
for
G/H
and
the existence of a ‘large’ reductive subgroup
L
acting properly on
G/H
.
One standard way to measure the ‘largeness’ of
\Gamma

is its
virtual cohomological dimension
, denoted as
\vcd(\Gamma)
.
Recall the following well-known result:

Fact 5.15

(Kobayashi
[
Kob89
, Cor. 5.5]
)
.

Let
G/H
be a homogeneous space of reductive type,
and let
\Gamma
be a virtually torsion-free discrete subgroup of
G
acting properly on
G/H
.
Then, we have

\vcd(\Gamma)\leqslant\dim(G/K)-\dim(H/(K\cap H)),

with equality if and only if
\Gamma
acts cocompactly on
G/H
.

We ask the following variant of Kobayashi’s standard quotient conjecture:

Question 5.16
.

Assuming that a homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type is not
(P-ss)
,
can we say anything about the virtual cohomological dimension of a discontiuous group for
G/H
?
Is it even possible for such
G/H
to admit a cocompact discontinuous group,
besides trivial cases (e.g.
G/H
is compact or
H
contains the semisimple part of
G
)?

Of course, if a non-compact homogeneous space
G/H
of reductive type is not (P-ss)
but admits a cocompact discontinuous group,
then it is (an extremely strong instance of) a counterexample to Kobayashi’s conjecture.

In this paper,
we used convex cocompact representations of discrete groups into
\OO(n,1)
to construct exotic proper actions.
It is natural to study if analogous classes of representations produce further examples:

Problem 5.17
.

Construct new examples of exotic proper actions on homogeneous spaces of reductive type via
one of the following classes of representations:

(i)

Convex cocompact representations into other simple Lie groups of real rank
1
.

(ii)

Tits–Vinberg representations of general right-angled Coxeter groups.
In particular, the one-parameter family considered in Danciger–Guériaud–Kassel
[
DGK20

Remark 5.18
.

We record our failed attempt to construct
a proper surface group action on Okuda’s homogeneous space (Fact
1.7
)
using Hitchin representations into
\SL(3,\mathbb{R})
.
Let
\Gamma
be a surface group of genus
\geqslant 2
, and consider a discrete embedding

\Gamma\xrightarrow{(j,\rho)}\SL(2,\mathbb{R})\times\SL(3,\mathbb{R})\hookrightarrow\SL(5,\mathbb{R}),

where
j
is a discrete embedding and
\rho
is a Hitchin representation.
Our idea was to use Tholozan’s inequality
[
Tho17
, Th. 3.9]
and
the fact that Hitchin representations avoid the walls of the Weyl chamber
(Kapovich–Leeb–Porti
[
KLP14+
, Th. 1.8]
, Guéritaud–Guichard–Kassel–Wienhard
[
GGKW17
, Th. 1.3]
).
Unfortunately, they are not enough to ensure the existence of a proper surface group action on Okuda’s homogeneous space.

Remark 5.19
.

We here point out a minor mistake in
[
Tho17
, Th. 3.9]
.
The original statement of the theorem is not correct as written, whilst the following slight modification is true:

Theorem
.

Assume that
\Phi
is not identically zero.
For each
t>0
, let
\rho_{t}\colon\Gamma\to\PSL(3,\mathbb{R})
be the Hitchin representation associated with
(J,t\Phi)
,
and let
j\colon\Gamma\to\PSL(2,\mathbb{R})
be the Fuchsian representation uniformizing
(S,J)
.
Then, there exist
A,B>0
such that, for a sufficiently large
t>0
,

At^{1/3}L_{j}\leqslant L_{\rho_{t}}\leqslant Bt^{1/3}L_{j}.

The issue in the original proof of the upper-bound estimate is that the PDE in the last
paragraph of p.1399 is wrong: the correct PDE is

\Delta\log(\sigma_{t})=-1+\sigma_{t}^{2}-2t^{3}\sigma_{t}^{-4}|\Phi|^{2}

(see e.g. Wang
[
Wan91
, Prop. 3.3]
or Lofrin
[
Lof01
, Prop. 3.6.1]
).
Fortunately, since the leading term of the PDE is unchanged, one can prove the above modified estimate as follows.
Arguing as in the first paragraph of p.1400, one obtains

\sigma_{t}(x_{m})^{6}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{m})^{-2})\leqslant 2t^{2}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{2}.

Assume that
t\geqslant 2\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{-1}
(recall that
\Phi
is not identically zero).
It follows from the estimate
\sigma_{t}\geqslant t^{1/3}|\Phi|^{1/3}
in the third paragraph of p.1400 that

\sigma_{t}(x_{m})^{6}(1-\sigma_{t}(x_{m})^{-2})\geqslant(1-2^{-2/3})\sigma_{t}(x_{m})^{6}.

Thus, we have

\sigma_{t}\leqslant 2^{1/6}(1-2^{-2/3})^{1/6}\|\Phi\|_{\infty}^{1/3}

on
S
, and therefore,

L^{B}_{\rho_{t}}\leqslant 2^{1/6}(1-2^{-2/3})^{1/6}\|\Phi\|^{1/3}_{\infty}L^{P}.

The original proof of the lower-bound estimate implicitly assumes that
\Phi
is not identically zero, too
(see the fourth paragraph of p.1400).
