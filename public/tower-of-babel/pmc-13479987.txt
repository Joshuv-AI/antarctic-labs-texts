Electronic health record adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in hospitals: an integrative framework based on a systematic review
Authors: Judith Engels, Annet de Lange, Rogier van de Wetering, Jol Stoffers, Joris Van Ruysseveldt, Maitta Spronken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorldwide, many hospitals have adopted an Electronic Health Record (EHR) and are currently in the post-implementation phase. To exploit EHRs to their full potential, healthcare professionals must continuously engage in adaptive behaviors to cope with (changes and updates in) EHR systems, and explore existing and novel EHR functionalities. This systematic review aimed to (a) identify factors related to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in hospitals, and (b) develop a multidisciplinary integrative framework.MethodsA PRISMA-compliant systematic review was conducted. We searched the databases PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and PsycInfo for the period from 2019 to 2023. After validation of the eligibility criteria by two independent reviewers, the first reviewer performed title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening. Data extraction was carried out by the first reviewer and risk of bias assessment by two independent reviewers. Subsequently, the first reviewer summarized the collected data using a narrative synthesis approach in collaboration with the other reviewers.ResultsOut of the 3,585 studies emerging from databases and citation searching, 14 studies were included in this study. Regarding EHR adaptation, psychological, work design, technological, and organizational factors were identified. Regarding EHR innovation, predominantly psychological and work design factors were found. Healthcare professionals’ knowledge and proficiency, task prioritization, recognition of EHR benefits, and their mindset towards change were relevant psychological/individual factors. Important work design factors were time and information availability, workflow impact, task dynamism, technostress, role clarity, and autonomy. Well-designed and functioning EHR modifications or novel EHR tools were significant technological factors. Identified organizational factors were available resources, an organizational climate of innovation and change, and comprehensive change and human resource management practices accompanying EHR changes.ConclusionsMost of the eligible studies dealt with EHR adaptation, and a few with EHR innovation. Based on the review findings, an integrative framework was developed that entails key factors for EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in the post-implementation phase of EHRs. Thereby, this review provides first directions for healthcare organizations to exploit the EHR’s full potential.RegistrationThe study was registered in Prospero on March 16, 2024 (CRD42024520547; last update: June 30, 2025).Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14486-2.

Background

Electronic health records (EHRs) have been implemented globally in healthcare [1]. The initial adoption phase primarily involved the digitization and substitution of paper-based records, focusing on basic administrative and clinical functionalities such as electronic documentation, order entry, and results viewing [2–4]. The post-implementation phase is aimed at optimization and innovative use, and is characterized by the integration of advanced functionalities, such as advanced clinical decision support, interoperability across systems, and data analytics [3]. Generally, the implementation of EHRs has been associated with improved information coordination and exchange, optimized medical decision-making, increased efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and improved quality of care [4, 5].

Many hospitals are currently in the post-implementation phase and seek to exploit EHRs to their full potential [6, 7]. While adequate technological functioning is a prerequisite for long-term implementation success, fully reaping the benefits of EHRs depends on healthcare professionals’ adaptive and innovative user behavior to a large extent [8]. To date, a multidisciplinary overview of factors associated with EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals is missing. This is needed to obtain a better understanding of critical barriers and facilitators for post-implementation success, and provide directions for promoting adaptive and innovative EHR user behavior, thus contributing to reaching the full potential of EHRs.

Most prior reviews focused on barriers and facilitators related to adoption and (early) implementation of EHRs. Barriers identified in these studies included limited technical experience, resistance to change or health information technology, lack of time, workflow disruptions, system complexity, and costs [9–13]. Facilitators included individual computer self-efficacy and perceived EHR benefits, healthcare professionals’ autonomy, system accessibility and usability, communication about change, leadership, training programs, and participation of end-users before EHR implementation [9–13]. As such, multidisciplinary factors related to the individual, work design, technological, and organizational level play a role in the early implementation phase of EHRs.

Against the background of the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act [14], the “meaningful use” regulation for EHRs [15], and national laws on EHRs [16], EHR systems are currently widely implemented [6, 7, 17]. However, little is known about the post-implementation phase and how to successfully sustain and improve EHR usage. This phase is generally characterized by frequent changes and updates in existing EHR functionalities, as well as the introduction of new functionalities [18], and regularly, their customization and modification by individual users are possible [19]. One review specifically focused on success factors for the adoption of advanced EHR features in primary care [20]. Among others, this review showed that a lack of physician readiness and concerns about the EHR system’s functionality were barriers, and adequate technical support and training were facilitators for the maturation of healthcare professionals’ EHR use [20].

Importantly, healthcare professionals’ ability to perform the dual task of both exploitation and exploration of EHR functionalities, which we call ambidextrous EHR use, is highly relevant in the post-implementation context [8, 21]. Regarding exploitation, healthcare professionals must not only adopt (new) EHR functionalities, but also continuously engage in adaptive behaviors. User adaptation is conceptualized as a continuum from assimilation and use of technology as intended to active customization of functionalities [18, 22]. This includes both changing their behavior to align (better) with prescribed work processes within the EHR, and/or proactively making changes within the EHR to improve work processes [23, 24]. It may be noted that this may also prevent EHR workarounds that are frequently used by healthcare professionals to manage discrepancies between workflows prescribed in the EHR on the one hand and preferred workflows in practice on the other hand, which often endanger patient safety, and quality and efficiency of care [25–27]. With regard to exploration, healthcare workers’ innovative use of EHRs is crucial, in terms of experimenting with existing or novel EHR functionalities to improve work processes, or achieve outcomes beyond the original intent of the functionalities [8].

Identifying predictors of EHR adaptation and innovation is essential, as different factors may be relevant in the post-implementation phase compared to the initial adoption and implementation phase [28]. From a socio-technical systems view, the post-implementation success of technological healthcare innovations depends on managing the complex interplay between healthcare professionals, tasks, technologies, and organizations [29]. Thus, a multidisciplinary approach is required, considering insights from psychology, information sciences, and management, to gain a comprehensive understanding of factors associated with EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals. While we acknowledge that external factors (e.g. regulatory and financial factors) may play a role in EHR adaptation and innovation, this review focused on psychological/individual, work design, technological, and organizational (internal) factors [20]. Against this background, this systematic review aimed to (a) identify critical barriers and success factors related to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in hospitals, and (b) develop a multidisciplinary integrative framework of EHR adaptation and innovation.

Methods

This systematic review was reported using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [30]. We chose this systematic approach to identify reliable evidence and synthesize the findings to develop an integrative framework [31]. The protocol of this systematic review was registered on March 16, 2024 within Prospero, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (registration number: CRD42024520547). It was last updated on June 30, 2025.

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

The search strategy was developed according to the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [32] in consultation with an information specialist of Maastricht University library. To cover medical informatics, organizational, and psychological literature, we conducted searches in the scientific databases PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and PsycInfo from January 2019 to December 2023. In 2021, hospitals’ overall use of EHRs in OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) countries exceeded 75% and thus, a large proportion of hospitals is currently past the initial adoption phase of EHRs [33]. EHRs have changed substantially over the past decade, among others in terms of system functions [34], and an estimation of adoption levels based on Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics’ Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM) indicated that most hospitals would be at advanced EHR use stages (5–7) by 2020 [35]. Therefore, and as the research in this area is evolving at a rapid pace, the period of five years has been selected to ensure the inclusion of the most recent and comprehensive information available in this domain and to integrate diverse perspectives, methodologies, and advancements in the post-implementation phase of EHRs (in earlier years, many hospitals were likely still in earlier stages of implementation).

Our search strategy is based on the population, concept, and context (PCC) framework [36]. Applied to our research question, the following main concepts were defined: physicians and nurses (population), factors related to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals (concept), and hospitals (context). In line with the multidisciplinary perspective adopted in this systematic review, psychological, work design, technological, and organizational factors related to EHR adaptation and EHR innovation among healthcare professionals were included. First, a search string was developed for the PubMed database, integrating Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms ([37]; see Table 1 and Supplementary File 1). Next, this search strategy was adapted for the other selected databases using proximity operators for the concept of EHR adaptation and innovation in Scopus [38], and additionally considering controlled vocabulary for Embase (Emtree subject headings) [39] and PsycInfo (subject terms) [40] (see Supplementary File 1). Study selection and data collection

As a preparatory step, 3,043 articles were imported into EndNote [42] and deduplicated following Bramer et al.’s six-step process [43], resulting in 2,617 references that were subsequently imported into Covidence (a web-based tool for systematic review management) [44]. Covidence identified an additional 70 duplicates, leaving 2,547 articles for screening, which was conducted in two phases: initial (title/abstract) screening and full-text screening.

During initial screening, two independent reviewers (JE, MS) screened 30 titles and abstracts using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Table 2). After initially low inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s Kappa [45]), partly due to a low number of hits, criteria were refined until satisfactory agreement was reached (κ = 0.74). Subsequently, the first reviewer (JE) screened the remaining articles, consulting the second reviewer (MS) when in doubt; unresolved cases were forwarded to full-text screening. In total, 46 additional duplicates were identified, and 118 articles were included after initial screening. In the full-text phase, two reviewers (JE, MS) screened 10 articles to further refine eligibility criteria, followed by five additional full-texts yielding perfect agreement (κ = 1.0). The remaining articles were screened by the first reviewer (JE) with consultation from the second reviewer (MS), and a third reviewer (AdL) in cases of disagreement. The prioritization and sequential exclusion approach [46] was applied to decrease the subjectivity in reporting reasons for article exclusion. For this purpose, the eligibility criteria were ordered as displayed in Table 2. In case an article had more than one criterion for exclusion, only the first applicable reason for exclusion following the sequence was mentioned. Additionally, we conducted forward or backward citation search using ‘Citationchaser’ [47] for all 13 studies included after full-text screening.

Data collection was conducted using a self-developed data extraction template integrated in Covidence, which was successfully pilot-tested on three articles by two independent reviewers (JE, MS). The following data were collected from the included articles by the first reviewer (JE): Author(s), year of publication, publishing outlet, country of research, study characteristics (study aim, study design, data collection, population characteristics, setting), EHR vendor, and the theory behind the study.

Study quality assessment

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [48] was used for the quality assessment of the included qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods studies. This tool allows the appraisal of the study’s methodological quality, starting with screening questions for all study types that are followed by five core quality criteria for each study design [49]. For a better presentation of the quality of the included studies, it is advised to give detailed information for the ratings of each criterion instead of using a scoring system [49]. A corresponding quality appraisal template was created in Excel. Two reviewers (JE, MS) independently appraised the quality of the study data that was relevant for our research aim (see Supplementary File 2). Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was assessed as satisfactory with high consensus (κ = 0.93).

Data synthesis

As qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method studies are included in this systematic review, we used the common data-based convergent synthesis design [50], in which a single synthesis method is applied for the combined analysis of qualitative and quantitative data. Within this approach, quantitative data was transformed into qualitative data (codes/themes) utilizing a narrative synthesis approach. The thematic synthesis of the solely qualitative data was conducted according to the three-step process by Thomas and Harden [51]: 1) inductive coding, 2) developing “descriptive themes”, and 3) generating of “analytical themes”. The computer software ATLAS.ti for Windows (version 25.0.1.32924) [52] was used for the thematic synthesis. Based on the inductive codes, we derived descriptive themes that captured the meaning of the inductive codes. The generation of analytical themes was done by going “beyond the content of original studies” [51]. Within this systematic review, we assigned the developed descriptive themes to psychological, work design, technological, and organizational factors (related to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals) for answering our research question, and developing a multidisciplinary integrative framework. Data synthesis was carried out by the first reviewer in collaboration with the other involved reviewers.

Seven studies were conducted in academic hospitals [18, 54, 55, 57–60], four studies in regular hospitals [56, 61, 63, 64], two studies in large hospital systems [53, 62], and one study in a teaching hospital [28]. With regard to the EHR vendor, seven hospitals used an EPIC EHR [18, 55, 56, 58, 59, 62, 63], and one hospital used a Cerner EHR [61]; six studies did not mention an EHR vendor [28, 53, 54, 57, 60, 64]. EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals

Our review aimed at identifying multidisciplinary factors related to EHR adaptation and innovation by healthcare professionals. Regarding these two concepts, only two studies resulting from our search explicitly investigated these two concepts [18, 28]. Beyond this, the other identified studies did not mention these terms, but aligned with our definitions of EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals. For EHR adaptation, most eligible studies were set in a context where either an explicit prescribed work process within the EHR existed (e.g. [53, 54, 56]) or an implicit one [55, 58]. In these studies, modifications/new functionalities were integrated into the EHR to facilitate (better) alignment with prescribed administrative and clinical work processes [53–60, 63, 64]. In addition, two studies dealt with healthcare professionals proactively making changes within the EHR to improve work processes in terms of optimizing clinical workflows [62] or ensuring optimal clinical task processing [61]. Depending on the study, adaptive behavior was measured on the individual, team, or hospital level. With regard to EHR innovation among healthcare professionals, one additional study investigated a user-driven EHR optimization program which entails physicians building, experimenting with and deploying new EHR functionalities and tools [62].

Psychological/individual factors

Barriers

First, several studies suggest that healthcare professionals’ lack of knowledge about newly introduced EHR functionalities or workflow changes hindered EHR adaptation [53, 59, 61]. Another barrier lies in insufficient task execution in terms of not prioritizing the clinical (EHR) task or forgetting parts of clinical task processing, (e.g. forgetting to discuss patient’s code status within the admission process) [59]. Furthermore, a number of studies dealt with healthcare professionals’ attitudes (towards EHR optimization/change). One study found healthcare professionals’ resistance towards new, standardized ways of working within a user-driven EHR optimization program to be an individual barrier to EHR innovation [62]. In addition, healthcare professionals’ fear of harming patients when aligning with EHR modifications [53] and their discomfort with (part of) their clinical task, for instance goal-of-care-conversations, hindered EHR adaptation [59].

Facilitators

One study pointed towards EHR user proficiency being a relevant individual facilitator for EHR innovation, namely the individual habit to use the EHR [28]. Further studies indicated that the recognition of benefits of EHR (modifications) (compared to previous care processes) supported EHR adaptation [18, 53]. In addition, various studies identified a positive mindset towards change as a facilitator for EHR adaptation. It was beneficial if healthcare professionals were dedicated and open towards new ways of providing care, and if they maintained a positive mindset towards (technological) change [18, 53]. One study suggests that healthcare professionals’ personal innovativeness, in terms of willingness to try out any new technology, supported EHR innovation [28].

Work design factors

Barriers

Two studies showed that general time constraints (of patient admission), for instance due to staff shortage or a busy service hindered EHR adaptation [53, 59]. In addition, disruptions to current workflows due to EHR modifications (e.g. upgrades or major updates) were slowing healthcare professionals down and acted as a further barrier for EHR adaptation [18]. Another barrier was a lack of information caused by patients not being able or refusing to provide relevant information for (EHR) care processes or their families being unreachable [59]. Moreover, one study found that lacking clarity about responsibilities within new workflows, in terms of determining when a nurse and when a doctor is responsible, hindered EHR adaptation [53]. If physicians were too strictly controlled and were lacking autonomy within a user-driven EHR optimization program (EPIC physician builder program), this was a barrier to EHR innovation [62].

Facilitators

One study suggests the following three work design facilitators for EHR innovation: EHR task time criticality, which is the urgency when accessing information through the EHR, and EHR task mobility, which is the extent to which a task was being performed in different locations using the EHR [28]. Additionally, perceived technostress caused by technological job demands including required changes in work habits, ongoing skill development, and time-related constraints facilitated EHR innovation [28].

Technological factors

Barriers

One study pointed towards poor functioning of an EHR functionality as a barrier to EHR adaptation: Missing functionalities, for instance an EHR inbox, was a hindering factor [61]: Although nurse practitioners could autonomously order and interpret diagnostic tests, the absence of an EHR inbox functionality for nursing staff resulted in test results being attributed to supervising physicians rather than the ordering nurses which led to time-consuming workarounds for nursing staff. Consequently, these workarounds could lead to patient safety risks in terms of failing to respond to results in a timely manner.

Facilitators

Many included studies dealt with EHR modifications, the implementations of novel EHR tools, and facilitating characteristics of new EHR functionalities. If EHR modifications aimed at optimizing workflows were carried out, these adjustments were facilitators for EHR adaptation. For instance, the integration of automated templates (Epic SmartPhrases), EHR tabs and note types supported documentation of goals-of-care-conversations [55]. Similarly, implementing an EHR tab and a standardized field led to improved uptake and documentation of the “Awakening and breathing coordination, delirium monitoring and management, and early exercise/mobility bundle” [53]. Introducing a code status best practice alert (BPA) increased code status documentation [59]. Moreover, changes in a plasma order set in terms of placing an important indication in a prominent position led to decreased inappropriate plasma use [54]. The creation of an EHR dashboard for test result management in combination with introducing in-built functionalities (e.g. inboxes and reminders) led to a decrease in unacknowledged test results and inadequate documentation, and an increase in compliance rate for acknowledged test results [63]. Modifications to an EHR sepsis tool aimed at optimizing usability increased tool utilization and overall sepsis bundle compliance [54]. Furthermore, EHR workflows were optimized to enhance communication and administrative task processing (e.g. registration, billing, scheduling) and to improve first case on time start (i.e. key performance indicator of operating room’s efficiency) [56].

Additionally beneficial for EHR adaptation was the integration of novel tools into the EHR. Such tools were e.g. a computerized clinical decision support (CCDS) tool for guide-compliant phototherapy ordering [58], a clinical aligned pain assessment tool [57] or a pediatric early warning score tool [64].

Regarding EHR modifications/new functionalities, two studies investigated characteristics that facilitated EHR adaptation. The adaptability of a new EHR functionality to fit within current care processes or to better meet patient needs was a facilitator for EHR adaptation [53]. The compatibility of new functionalities with individually preferred provision of care also facilitated EHR adaptation [53]. Moreover, the simplicity and the trialability of new EHR functionalities (i.e. an experimentation possibility on a limited or trial basis) [53] supported EHR adaptation. An optimal design of new EHR functionalities displaying important information in a prominent position [54] was additionally beneficial for EHR adaptation. Furthermore, the ease of transporting the EHR device (i.e. EHR portability) facilitated EHR innovation [28].

Organizational factors

Barriers

With regard to general resources, one study found that staff shortage and staff turnover were barriers to EHR adaptation [53]. A further organizational barrier was related to dealing with general cultural change [53]. In the context of EHR optimizations, three studies suggest that poor EHR-related change management hindered EHR adaptation and innovation. Too frequent electronic communication about EHR changes causing e-mail fatigue was a barrier to EHR adaptation [18]. Additionally, if there were competing priorities or initiatives beside the implementation of a new EHR functionality or a lack of coordination with other specialties involved in new EHR processes, these were barriers to EHR adaptation [53]. With respect to change processes, bureaucratic processes in the form of a strict formal approval procedure within a user-driven EHR optimization program (Epic physician builder program) were hindering EHR innovation [62]. Concerning EHR-related human resource management, two studies identified that a lack of training for new EHR functionalities was a barrier to EHR adaptation [53, 59].

Facilitators

One study pointed to facilitating conditions for EHR adaptation, namely the general availability of resources as well as a generally existing change/innovation climate within hospital departments, encouraging new ways of providing care [53]. Plenty of studies dealt with the promotion of change accompanying the introduction of EHR modifications or the implementation of novel EHR tools. For instance, a comprehensive EHR-related change management (e.g. according to Lewin’s change management model) was applied [60] or Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles using a robust feedback mechanism were established [64] for the introduction of EHR modifications or novel EHR tools. In addition, a stepwise implementation strategy in terms of implementing new EHR content one at a time [53], the development or modification of hospital policies and guidelines [54, 63], or the establishment of a key performance indicator (KPI) for the compliance rate within a new EHR functionality [63] were considered. Moreover, feedback on user performance by monitoring healthcare professionals’ use of new EHR content and providing targeted feedback on user performance [53, 60, 63, 64] supported the introduction of EHR modifications and novel tools. Communication (e.g. via meetings, emails, word-of-mouth) prior, during or post implementation of new EHR functionalities or novel EHR tools was an additional change management practice [18, 53, 56, 58, 63]; for instance, EHR modifications or novel tools were integrated as discussion points in routine meetings and daily assessments [53, 64]. Furthermore, establishing a task force, which is an interdisciplinary team for optimizing EHR workflows, was another practice that accompanied EHR modifications [55, 56, 60]. Additionally, super users or physician or nurse champions were deployed [53, 56, 64], and a user driven EHR optimization program (Epic physician builder program) aimed at optimizing configuration and use of the EHR [62] was carried out. Moreover, the establishment of co-worker support for the implementation of novel EHR tools [64] and a good co-worker relationship in general [53] were beneficial factors. Lastly, the provision of implementation tools (e.g. personal testimony via video, posters, pocket cards, huddle boards, tool scoring charts, verbal or e-mail reminders or reminders posted on computer screens) [53, 60, 64] supported the introduction of EHR modifications and novel EHR tools.

Various studies suggest that further facilitating factors for EHR adaptation lied in EHR-related human resource management practices that accompany EHR modifications or the implementation of novel EHR tools. For instance, new EHR workflows were integrated in orientation for new staff [53]. (Change) leadership in terms of strong leaders or leaders who were made responsible for EHR workflow changes were useful companions [53, 63]. Furthermore, healthcare professionals were involved in the development of new EHR functionalities or novel tools from the start of the change process [18, 53, 56, 64]. Other EHR-related human resource management practices involved creating opportunities to learn from colleagues, for instance through sharing of EHR tools or observation [18], or in-person and electronic tailored education and (re-) training regarding EHR modifications or novel EHR tools (provided by super trainers who were training experts for the floor) [18, 53–56, 59, 60, 63, 64]; dissemination formats for education and (re-) training were e.g. meetings/events, e-mails, online-modules, refresher videos, newsletter, and tip sheets. Finally, it is noted that one study found that healthcare professionals receiving training and implementation tools regarding EHR modifications have adapted slightly less well than healthcare professionals who only received an electronic notification on the EHR modifications, possibly due to confounding factors [53].

Discussion

Although many hospitals worldwide are currently in the post-implementation phase of EHR-systems, a multidisciplinary overview of factors associated with EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in the post-implementation phase was still lacking [6]. Hospitals must create conditions where adaptive and consistent EHR use are balanced and mutually reinforcing in clinical practice. Ambidextrous hospitals thus understand how individual, work design, technological, and organizational factors interact to support both stability and innovation with respect to EHR use. Therefore, integrating ambidexterity into the study of EHR adaptation and innovation helps identify pathways for hospitals to achieve the full potential of EHR investments while remaining responsive to changing care demands [8, 65]. Following this theoretical lens, our systematic review aimed to: (a) identify critical barriers and success factors related to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals in hospitals, and (b) develop a multidisciplinary integrative framework of EHR adaptation and innovation.

Across the 14 included studies, several themes emerged for psychological, work design, technological and organizational barriers and facilitators to EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals. Compared with previous reviews on early stages of EHR implementation [9–13], our review findings suggest that various factors are relevant across different implementation phases. Regarding psychological/individual factors, healthcare professionals’ attitudes and mindset [18, 28, 53, 59, 62], their knowledge about EHR (modifications) [53, 59, 61], and recognition of EHR (modifications) benefits [18, 53] were identified in reviews on adoption and (early) implementation as well. The work design factors time availability [53, 59] and autonomy [62], and the technological factor EHR usability [61], also appear to play a role throughout initial and post-implementation phases. With regard to organizational factors, organizational culture [53], EHR change communication (e.g. [18, 56, 58, 63]), leadership [53, 63], EHR training (e.g. [18, 53, 60]), and participation of end-users [56, 64] have been identified as relevant factors across implementation phases. This review showed that organizational factors could act either as a barrier or a facilitator. For instance, strong change management and human resource management practices facilitated EHR adaptation and innovation, while deficiencies impeded these post-implementation outcomes, which is comparable with review findings on the initial adoption phase of EHRs where disadvantageous organizational conditions contributed to resistance, inefficiencies, and user burden impeding EHR adoption [4, 66].

As mentioned in the introduction, predictors of EHR adoption in the early implementation phase may partly differ from predictors of adaptation and innovation in the post-implementation phase. Factors additionally relevant for EHR adaptation seem to center around continuous EHR development and improvement by modifying the EHR system (e.g. [53, 54, 55]) and integrating novel EHR tools [57, 58, 64], accompanied by various advanced EHR-related change efforts (e.g. [56, 60, 64]) and expanded EHR-related human resource management practices (e.g. (re)training; [56]). Regarding EHR innovation, one study indicated that additional individual characteristics (e.g. personal innovativeness, EHR proficiency) and work design factors (e.g. EHR task dynamism and technostress) may particularly play a role in later implementation stages [28].

Based on our findings, an integrative framework on EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals was developed (Fig. 2). The integrative framework suggests that factors within all four multidisciplinary categories are related to EHR adaptation, with technological and organizational factors having received most attention in the literature. This might be due to the fact that these factors are most obvious when technologies at the workplace are concerned, and due to their close interaction, in the sense that the success of technologies requires appropriate organizational practices [67]. Some psychological and work design factors may partly be reflected in broader organizational factors. For instance, healthcare professionals’ readiness for change or technological job demands and autonomy might be targeted within organizational interventions. Importantly, this emphasizes the interdependencies between the four multidisciplinary categories. Interestingly, regarding EHR innovation it appeared that relevant factors were predominantly psychological/individual or related to work design. This either suggests that EHR innovation has not been investigated much, or that EHR adaptation has a variety of predictors and seems to apply to all healthcare professionals, whereas EHR innovation is more likely to apply to a select group of enthusiasts having facilitating job characteristics. In some way, EHR innovation seems to be a step beyond EHR adaptation, which is exploring new paths beyond exploiting the existing within healthcare organizations’ ambidexterity [8].

If the aforementioned multidisciplinary factors are considered in hospital practice, EHR workarounds used by healthcare professionals that potentially endanger quality and efficiency of care [25] may be prevented by facilitating healthcare professionals’ alignment with prescribed work processes in the EHR system. Additionally, dealing with these factors in hospital practice, could facilitate the exploration of EHRs, and jointly with EHR exploitation, contribute to hospitals’ ambidexterity in the post-implementation phase [65].

Limitations and future research

Within our systematic review, EHR adaptation was conceptualized as healthcare professionals (better) aligning with prescribed EHR work processes, and/or proactively making changes within the EHR. In addition to the compliance-oriented form of adaptation, the proactive form is important as employees are increasingly expected to deal with complex and unforeseen issues (such as EHR changes and updates) [24]. While recent scientific literature suggests that certain EHR-systems allow substantial customization and modification [19], it may be noted that the extent to which healthcare professionals can engage in EHR adaptation and innovation is subject to constraints of the vendor design, which can substantially limit customizability [68]. When EHR-systems allow less customization, changing one’s behavior to align (better) with prescribed work processes within the EHR may be more applicable than proactively making changes within the EHR to improve work processes. Most of the included studies dealt with healthcare professionals’ alignment with an explicit prescribed EHR work process which either lied in improved administrative task processing (e.g. [56, 59, 64]) or clinical task processing (e.g. [54, 58, 63]). As EHR adaptation is a rather new field of research, we included studies in which EHR adaptation was assessed on the individual [61, 62], team, or hospital level (e.g. [55, 58, 60]). Future studies may disentangle different types and assessments of EHR adaptation. EHR innovation among healthcare professionals has received limited attention in the literature. It is therefore not possible to answer conclusively whether the predominantly identified psychological/individual and work design factors are most crucial for innovative user behavior, or whether technological and organizational factors also play a relevant role.

With regard to the different stages of EHR implementation, partly the same factors were relevant, but partly there were also differences between implementation phases. It remains open whether the factors identified for the post-implementation phase are actually exclusive to this phase, or whether these factors are also relevant to the initial adoption phase but were simply not investigated. Furthermore, some factors were well-substantiated, while others were only investigated in a singular study. In the majority of studies, EHR modifications or the implementation of novel EHR tools were investigated in the first place, accompanied by corresponding change and human resource management practices. In these cases, a conclusion can only be drawn about the combination of technological and organizational factors in relation to EHR adaptation. Only one study carried out separate analyses for EHR modifications combined with an electronic notification on the one hand (basic intervention) and EHR modifications combined with training, clinical champions, and staff participation (enhanced intervention); remarkably, the first strategy led to better EHR adaptation by healthcare professionals, possibly because of the uncontrolled initiation of additional implementation strategies within the basic intervention group (e.g. trainings, clinical champions, super-trainers, reminders) and departmental encouragement of innovation and new ways of care [53].

In general, the findings of our systematic review were primarily derived from qualitative data of the included studies, mainly focusing on EHR adaptation by healthcare professionals. As this review focused on scientific studies on EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals, grey literature was not included. Quantitative evidence for predictors of both EHR adaptation and innovation is predominantly lacking. Furthermore, it remains open if EHR adaptation and EHR innovation can fully be separated or if there is an interplay of both in terms of healthcare professionals engaging in both exploitative and explorative use behaviors [69]. Regarding the setting of the included articles, studies were predominantly conducted in the United States. Except one study, there is no evidence for Europe, although many hospitals are past the initial EHR implementation phase there [7, 70]. Although the time frame of this study (2019–2023) was carefully selected [35], we acknowledge that some earlier studies on the post-implementation phase could be disregarded.

Based on the aforementioned limitations and gaps in the literature, several avenues for future research are derived. First, future research on EHR adaptation should aim to disentangle predictors based on different contextual settings – specifically, contexts with explicitly prescribed EHR workflows versus those with more implicit processes – as well as distinguish between the two forms of EHR adaptation. Second, to advance our understanding of the factors underlying EHR innovation, exploratory research methods, such as semi-structured interviews, are recommended. Third, to determine whether the identified factors remain consistent or change over time, studies employing pre- and post-EHR implementation designs in earlier versus later (post-)implementation stages should be conducted, with particular attention to predictors across different (post-)implementation phases. Fourth, given the general lack of quantitative evidence supporting predictors of both EHR adaptation and innovation, future studies should seek to provide such evidence by employing longitudinal, multilevel, and quasi-experimental quantitative designs to identify predictors and causal mechanisms, and explore potential intercorrelations and interactions among predictors, in order to account for their interdependencies. EHR system log data (including for instance function usage, mouse-click patterns) can provide objective insights into EHR adaptation and innovation among healthcare professionals. This information may be combined with other available information within hospitals, such as user characteristics (e.g. specialty), technological changes (e.g. EHR system update), interventions (e.g. training), and employee surveys (e.g. user satisfaction). In addition, openEHR platforms (combining a stable reference model and flexible archetypes [71]), such as the Better.care platform [72], could be investigated as potential drivers for EHR adaptation and innovation. Fifth, grey literature may be included in future (scoping) reviews to also cover practice-based evidence (e.g. from operation reports). Sixth, due to the limited understanding of healthcare professionals’ ambidexterity in the post-implementation phase of EHRs, future research should examine the relationship between EHR adaptation and innovation. Seventh, as evidence from European contexts is scarce – and considering potential differences from the United States due to differing incentive programs [14, 15] – future studies should be conducted within European healthcare systems.

Conclusions

This systematic review identified a range of multidisciplinary factors associated with EHR adaptation and, to a lesser extent, factors related to EHR innovation among healthcare professionals. Further research is needed to examine predictors across the various phases of EHR implementation, the different forms of EHR adaptation, and, more fundamentally, EHR innovation. Additionally, greater attention to healthcare professionals’ ambidexterity is essential to develop a comprehensive understanding of the key factors necessary for fully realizing the potential of EHR systems. Based on the findings of this review, an integrative framework was developed that provides directions for healthcare organizations in the post-implementation phase of EHRs. If organizational strategies are aligned with this, EHR adaptation (exploitation) and EHR innovation (exploration) by healthcare professionals are promoted which assists healthcare organizations to realize the EHR’s potential.
