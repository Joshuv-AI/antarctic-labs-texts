Pulmonary venous Doppler as a marker of restrictive atrial physiology in fetal critical aortic stenosis after fetal valvuloplasty
Authors: Nageswara Rao Koneti, Shweta Nathani Bakhru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFetal balloon aortic valvuloplasty (fBAV) is performed in carefully selected fetuses with critical aortic stenosis to prevent progression to hypoplastic left heart syndrome. We report a fetus with critical aortic stenosis who underwent successful fBAV at 26 weeks’ gestation with restoration of antegrade aortic flow. Serial fetal echocardiography subsequently demonstrated progressive restriction and eventual functional closure of the foramen ovale despite technically successful valvuloplasty. Pulmonary venous Doppler revealed a prominent atrial reversal wave with prolonged reversal duration, while mitral inflow showed marked E-wave predominance, indicating elevated left atrial pressure and severe left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. After birth, the neonate developed pulmonary hypertension physiology with persistently elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure despite postnatal balloon aortic valvotomy. This case highlights that pulmonary venous Doppler provides a valuable noninvasive marker of restrictive atrial physiology and may identify fetuses at risk of adverse neonatal hemodynamics despite successful relief of aortic valve obstruction.

INTRODUCTION

Critical aortic stenosis diagnosed during fetal life represents a progressive disease spectrum characterized by left ventricular pressure overload, myocardial dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, and the development of endocardial fibroelastosis. Progressive deterioration of left ventricular function may lead to loss of antegrade transaortic flow and evolution toward hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS).[123] Recognition of this pathophysiological progression has led to the development of fetal balloon aortic valvuloplasty (fBAV), which aims to relieve valvar obstruction, restore antegrade aortic flow, and preserve the potential for postnatal biventricular circulation in carefully selected fetuses.[34]

While relief of valvar obstruction is the primary goal of fBAV, the overall physiological outcome is also influenced by atrial decompression and pulmonary venous hemodynamics. In normal fetal circulation, oxygen-rich blood entering the right atrium is preferentially directed through the foramen ovale into the left atrium. In fetuses with critical aortic stenosis, however, left ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction, mitral regurgitation, and persistent left ventricular outflow obstruction may elevate left atrial pressure. In this setting, the foramen ovale becomes an important decompressive pathway, and atrial shunting may become bidirectional or predominantly left to right.

If the interatrial communication becomes restrictive or closes prematurely, effective left atrial decompression is lost, resulting in pulmonary venous hypertension and abnormal pulmonary venous Doppler patterns. These changes may reflect elevated left atrial pressure and evolving restrictive atrial physiology, which can adversely affect neonatal pulmonary vascular adaptation.[15]

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 24-year-old primigravida with a diamniotic, dichorionic twin pregnancy was referred at 26-week gestation for fetal cardiac evaluation. Fetal echocardiography demonstrated critical aortic stenosis in one fetus with left ventricular systolic dysfunction, evolving endocardial fibroelastosis, mild mitral regurgitation, and left atrial dilatation [Figure 1a-c]. Color Doppler demonstrated a narrow turbulent jet across the aortic valve [Figure 1b]. Pulsed-wave Doppler confirmed increased velocity across the stenotic aortic valve [Figure 1d].

Given the risk of progressive left ventricular dysfunction and evolution toward HLHS, the fetus underwent fBAV at 26-week gestation. The procedure was performed after obtaining a favorable fetal position with the anterior thorax accessible to the operator. Following maternal and fetal anesthesia, an 18-gauge Chiba needle was advanced under continuous ultrasound guidance through the maternal abdomen, uterine wall, fetal chest wall, and into the fetal left ventricle. The needle was positioned coaxially toward the left ventricular outflow tract and close to the aortic valve. A preprepared balloon–guidewire assembly was then introduced through the needle, with the guidewire extending beyond the balloon tip to facilitate crossing of the stenotic aortic valve. After successful crossing of the stenotic valve, balloon inflation was performed, resulting in restoration of antegrade flow across the aortic valve [Figure 2a-c]. The procedure resulted in successful dilation of the aortic valve with restoration of antegrade flow across the valve and mild aortic regurgitation on immediate postprocedural assessment [Figure 2d].

Serial fetal echocardiographic follow-up demonstrated persistent left atrial enlargement and left ventricular dysfunction. Right ventricular size and systolic function remained normal throughout follow-up, without significant tricuspid regurgitation. Ductal dimensions and Doppler flow patterns remained stable, and no significant ductal flow reversal was observed. A bidirectional interatrial shunt was present with predominant left-to-right flow. The foramen ovale became progressively restrictive and was functionally closed between 29- and 30-week gestation. Mitral inflow Doppler demonstrated a prominent early diastolic E wave (≈80–90 cm/s) with a smaller A wave (≈20 cm/s), indicating dominant early ventricular filling and impaired ventricular compliance [Figure 3a].

Pulmonary venous Doppler interrogation demonstrated systolic and diastolic forward flow velocities of approximately 38–40 cm/s with a prominent atrial reversal wave measuring approximately 18–19 cm/s [Figure 3b]. The duration of the atrial reversal wave was estimated to be approximately 80–85 ms at a sweep speed of 75 mm/s, exceeding the abnormal threshold (>77 ms) associated with elevated left atrial pressure and restrictive atrial physiology.[1]

Delivery occurred at 38-week gestation by elective cesarean section. Postnatal echocardiography demonstrated moderate residual aortic stenosis and persistent left ventricular dysfunction and absence of a patent interatrial communication [Figure 3c].

On day 3 of life, the infant underwent cardiac catheterization and balloon aortic valvotomy for residual aortic stenosis. Baseline left ventricular pressure measured 95/30 mmHg with a peak gradient of 30 mmHg across the aortic valve. Balloon dilation was performed using a 5 mm × 20 mm Tyshak mini balloon catheter, resulting in a modest reduction of the transvalvular gradient to 22 mmHg. However, left ventricular end-diastolic pressure remained elevated (~20 mmHg), consistent with persistent diastolic dysfunction [Figure 3d].

DISCUSSION

This case highlights the importance of atrial and pulmonary venous physiology in determining perinatal outcomes in fetuses with critical aortic stenosis undergoing fBAV. Although the primary goal of fBAV is relief of left ventricular outflow obstruction, restoration of antegrade aortic flow does not necessarily normalize fetal hemodynamics.

In the present case, despite technically successful fetal valvuloplasty, progressive restriction and eventual closure of the interatrial communication resulted in persistent left atrial hypertension and pulmonary venous congestion. Pulmonary venous Doppler interrogation provides a valuable noninvasive clue into fetal left atrial pressure and ventricular compliance. In our patient, the abnormal pulmonary venous Doppler findings likely reflected the interaction of two related physiological processes. First, persistent left ventricular diastolic dysfunction resulted in impaired ventricular compliance and elevation of left atrial pressure, supported by the E-dominant mitral inflow pattern prenatally and the elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure documented after birth. Second, progressive restriction and eventual closure of the foramen ovale removed an important decompressive pathway for the left atrium. Thus, although restrictive left ventricular physiology was probably the primary determinant of the prominent pulmonary venous atrial reversal wave, evolving interatrial restriction likely amplified pulmonary venous hypertension and contributed to the severity of the Doppler abnormalities.

Following recognition of restrictive interatrial physiology, the option of a repeat fetal intervention to decompress the left atrium was discussed by the multidisciplinary team. However, given the twin gestation and concern regarding procedure-related complications, particularly premature delivery, conservative surveillance was continued.

Previous studies have demonstrated that abnormalities such as prolonged atrial reversal duration and increased reversal velocity are associated with restrictive atrial septum and adverse neonatal outcomes in fetuses with left heart obstructive lesions.[12] In our patient, pulmonary venous Doppler demonstrated a prominent atrial reversal wave with a duration of approximately 80–85 ms together with increased reversal velocity [Figure 3b]. These findings, combined with an E-wave dominant mitral inflow pattern [Figure 3a], strongly suggest elevated left atrial pressure and impaired ventricular compliance.

Following birth, balloon aortic valvotomy resulted in a modest reduction of the transvalvular gradient and improvement in systemic arterial pressure. However, the persistently elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure confirmed the presence of significant diastolic dysfunction, consistent with the prenatal Doppler findings.

Chronic elevation of left atrial pressure during fetal life may also influence pulmonary vascular development. Pulmonary venous hypertension can lead to pulmonary vascular and lymphatic remodeling, including pulmonary lymphangiectasis, which has been described in fetuses with HLHS and restrictive atrial septum.[5] These changes may impair pulmonary vascular adaptation after birth and contribute to severe pulmonary hypertension in the neonatal period.

CONCLUSION

Restrictive interatrial physiology may significantly influence perinatal hemodynamics in fetuses with critical aortic stenosis even after technically successful fBAV. Pulmonary venous Doppler interrogation, particularly the presence of prominent atrial reversal flow and prolonged reversal duration, provides important noninvasive insight into elevated left atrial pressure and ventricular diastolic dysfunction. Recognition of these physiological markers may help identify fetuses at risk of adverse neonatal adaptation despite relief of valvar obstruction.
