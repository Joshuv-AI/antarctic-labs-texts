Causal Relationship Between Galectins With Neuroblastoma: A Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Chunxi Long, Ping Liang, Xiaoqin Liu, Yali Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGalectins are a family of proteins that are involved in various aspects of cancer, including tumor growth, cell migration, invasion, and metastasis. Galectins are subdivided into three families: prototype (such as Gal-1), chimera type (such as Gal-3), and tandem repeat type (such as Gal-9). Despite the evidence linking galectins to the risk of neuroblastoma (NB), the causal association between galectins and NB remains unclear. The primary objective of this study is to clarify the causal effect of galectins (including Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9) on NB risk through a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study. The MR study was performed using summary statistics from extensive genome-wide association studies. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to the exposure variables were used as instrumental variables. The primary outcome was NB risk, and the exposure variables included Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9. We chose inverse-variance weighted (IVW), constrained maximum likelihood and model averaging (cML-MA), MR-robust adjusted profile score (MR-RAPS), simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode as the statistical methods to assess the causal effect; and the IVW method was the primary statistical method. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were used as the evaluation indices for causality. According to the IVW with fixed-effect results, genetically predicted Gal-9 was associated with an increased risk of NB (OR = 1.639, 95% CI 1.171–2.293, P = 0.004). Similar risk estimates were obtained using cML-MA, MR-RAPS, simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode. Additionally, the IVW with fixed-effect revealed there was no statistically significant association between Gal-1 and NB (P = 0.605), or between Gal-3 and NB (P = 0.258). No evidence of reverse causality was observed in the MR Steiger test. The results of the leave-one-out sensitivity test confirmed the stability and reliability of the causal effect estimates. This study identified a possible causal association between Gal-9 levels and the risk of NB through a two-sample MR analysis, which might provide preliminary yet insightful findings into the role of Gal-9 in the development of NB. Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is a pediatric malignancy derived from primitive neural crest cells outside the skull and is considered one of the most common malignant solid tumors [1, 2]. NB accounts for about 10% of childhood cancers [3], and causes 12% of cancer mortality in children [4]. Currently, tumor heterogeneity, resistance mechanisms, and toxicity have rendered the outcomes of NB therapy suboptimal [5]. Therefore, it is imperative to identify novel biomarkers associated with NB in order to enhance our understanding of disease pathogenesis, improve risk stratification, and develop targeted interventions.

Galectins, a family of proteins that exhibit affinity for β-galactosides, have been increasingly associated with overall survival (OS) in various cancer types, based on emerging research evidence [6, 7]. Galectins, especially Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9, play a crucial role in tumor immune escape [8], which may serve as markers of tumor progression and prognosis [9]. Studies have found that Gal-1 participates in the immune escape mechanism of NB [10], and facilitates tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis [11]. Furthermore, Gal-1 expression was found to be upregulated in clinical and preclinical models of aggressive NB [12]. Elevated serum levels of Gal-1 correlate with poorer clinical features in patients with NB [13]. Similarly, Gal-3 was found to promote angiogenesis in NB [14]; however, another study found that Gal-3 expression may be associated with a better prognosis in NB [15]. There is currently no evidence that Gal-9 is related to NB risk. However, high serum Gal-9 levels have been found to be associated with the progression of cervical cancer [16] and chronic lymphocytic leukemia [17], as well as being elevated in patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-enriched subtype breast cancer [18]. Additionally, research has found that Gal-9 is highly expressed in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [19] and involved in the progression of multiple myeloma [20]. However, most of these studies are observational in nature, which makes it difficult to establish causality and control for potential confounding variables. There is a lack of longitudinal evidence demonstrating a causal relationship between galectins (Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9) and NB risk.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological method that employs single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to establish a causal relationship between exposure and outcome [21]. According to research findings, MR can avoid the impact of potential confounding factors and provide more robust evidence for establishing causal relationships, making it widely utilized in various clinical research studies [22, 23]. In this study, we aimed to conduct a two-sample MR study to investigate the causal inference between galectins and NB risk.

Methods

Data Design and Data Sources

We designed a two-sample MR study to explore the potential causal relationship between galectins (including Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9) and NB risk. The assumptions for conducting an MR study are as follows: (1) The chosen IVs should demonstrate a significant association with Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9 (2). The selected IVs should not be associated with any confounding factors between galectins (Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9) and NB (3). The impact of the selected IVs on NB should occur solely through a direct pathway involving Gal-1, Gal-3, or Gal-9, without influence from alternative pathways. Figure 1 shows the overview of the study design.

Overview of the current Mendelian randomization study

The genetic data on galectins and NB were derived from the Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary data on the IEU Open GWAS Project website, which serves as a publicly accessible database containing comprehensive genetic association information available for querying or downloading (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The NB data were obtained from Capasso et al. (published in 2013) [24] and consist of 4881 samples of European ancestry, of which 1627 were cases and 3254 were controls. Gal-1 and Gal-9 data were obtained from Sun et al. and include 3301 individuals of European ancestry [25]. The GWAS summary statistics for Gal-3 were obtained from Folkersen et al. [26] and encompass 21,758 individuals of European ancestry (Supplementary Table 1). Our study involved a secondary analysis of publicly available data; ethical approval or informed consent was not required.

Instrumental Variables Selection

Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the flowchart for the IVs of each galectin. Firstly, all SNPs included in this MR study satisfied P < 1 × 10–5 to ensure a significant correlation between SNPs and galectins. Then, SNPs demonstrating linkage disequilibrium (LD) (clump window size: 20,000 kb, r2 = 0.01) and those that were palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies were excluded. Finally, we calculated the F-statistic value for each SNP, and SNPs with F-statistics < 10 were deemed weak IVs; these weak IVs needed to be excluded.

Statistical Analysis

We chose inverse-variance weighted (IVW), constrained maximum likelihood and model averaging (cML-MA), MR-robust adjusted profile score (MR-RAPS), simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode as the statistical methods to assess the causal effect between Gal-1, Gal-3, Gal-9, and NB risk. Odds ratios (ORs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify the causal effect. IVW was used as the primary statistical method in this MR study. The selection of the IVW with a fixed-effects model or random-effects model was based on the presence of heterogeneity. In cases where significant heterogeneity was observed, an IVW with a random-effects model was utilized. The cML-MA method is an MR technique grounded in model averaging methodology that obviates the need for the Instrument Strength Independent of Direct Effect (InSIDE) assumption when managing pleiotropic effects, whether they are linked or independent [27]. The MR-RAPS estimation method is a statistical approach designed to correct for pleiotropy by employing robust adjusted profile scores [28]. The weighted median method exhibits greater robustness to invalid IVs and remains capable of producing reliable estimates even when over half of the assigned weights correspond to valid IVs [29]. The MR-Egger regression method was used to assess potential horizontal pleiotropy of SNPs, with a significance level of P < 0.05 indicating its existence. Cochran’s Q test was employed to identify heterogeneity among SNPs, and a significance level of P < 0.05 suggests the presence of heterogeneity. In addition, we employed the MR Steiger directionality test to further evaluate the potential causal association between the assumed exposure and outcome. Leave-one-out analysis was performed to identify any single SNP that may influence the estimated causal effects. P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant level. All these MR analyses were performed using the R software with the “TwoSampleMR” package.

Causal Effects of Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9 on NB Risk

Similar risk estimates were obtained using cML-MA, MR-RAPS, simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode (Table 2). Additionally, the IVW with fixed-effect revealed that there was no statistically significant association between Gal-1 and NB (P = 0.605), as well as between Gal-3 and NB (P = 0.258). Discussion

In this study, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis to investigate the association between galectins and NB risk for the first time. The results indicated that genetically predicted Gal-9 levels may be significantly associated with an increased risk of NB. However, no evidence was found to support a causal relationship between genetically predicted Gal-1, Gal-3, and NB risk.

As a member of the galactoside-binding lectin family, Gal-9 belongs to the subgroup of tandem repeat-type galectins and is widely expressed in various immune cells and human tissues [30]. In recent years, the prognostic value of Gal-9 has been assessed in various cancer types; however, its prognostic significance exhibits heterogeneity across different cancer types. It has been reported that higher expression levels of Gal-9 in squamous cell carcinoma were associated with better prognosis [31]. A review concluded that Gal-9 can inhibit cell proliferation of colorectal cancer cell lines and gastric cancer cell lines by inducing apoptosis via miRNA alteration [6]. In hepatitis B virus-associated hepatocellular carcinoma, Jiao et al. found that positive Gal-9 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis [32]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there have been few reports on the effect of Gal-9 on NB risk in previous studies. Our MR analysis based on the primary IVW method revealed a causal relationship between genetically predicted Gal-9 levels and an increased susceptibility to NB. Other sensitivity analysis methods (including cML-MA, MR-RAPS, Simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode) also indicated that genetically predicted Gal-9 levels were a risk factor for NB development, even though some methods did not yield statistically significant results. Studies have shown that Gal-9 can guide tumor cells to evade immune surveillance and promote tumor growth by inducing angiogenesis [33, 34]. The study conducted by Yuan et al. revealed a positive correlation between Gal-9 expression and immune checkpoint molecules, as well as M2 tumor-associated macrophage markers [35]. This suggests that Gal-9 may play a role in tumor immunosuppression or immune evasion through the involvement of M2 tumor-associated macrophages. Increased expression and secretion of Gal-9 stimulate the production of chemokines, which support tumor growth and contribute to a poor prognosis [36]. In addition, the two genes related to the SNP of Gal-9 are SPSB1 and SMU1 in our MR study. The SMU1 gene, located on human chromosome 9p21.1, encodes a protein containing a WD40 domain, which places it within the WD40-repeat protein family. SMU1 is involved in a variety of cellular processes through its receptor/regulatory module, including signal transduction, pre-mRNA processing, and regulation of cytoskeletal assembly. A previous study has demonstrated that SMU1 overexpression enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion of gastric cancer cells, suggesting that SMU1 plays a role in the malignant progression of gastric cancer [37]. Furthermore, some evidence links elevated SPSB1 levels to breast cancer progression [38, 39]. Although no existing research reports have specifically investigated the relationship between SPSB1 and SMU1 in the context of NB, our findings suggest that the expression of these genes may play a role in the progression of NB. In future studies, it is imperative to elucidate the precise mechanism underlying the association between Gal-9 and NB risk. Although our findings suggest that genetically predicted Gal-9 levels may be associated with an increased risk of NB, the magnitude and validity of this association could be influenced by various factors, including patients’ underlying health conditions and treatment-related variables. Moreover, given the limited number of SNPs related to Gal-9 that were analyzed in our study, the results should be interpreted with caution. Consequently, the observed associations between genetically predicted Gal-9 levels and NB risk warrant further validation through large-scale and more comprehensive investigations.

Previous observational studies indicated associations between Gal-1/Gal-3 levels and the risk of NB [10–12]. However, in the current MR study, our results provide no genetic evidence for an association between genetically predicted Gal-1, Gal-3, and the risk of NB. This finding suggests that the observed association from population-based studies might be biased by underlying confounders, and indicates that Gal-1 and Gal-3 may not be risk factors for NB. It is worth noting that the number of SNPs related to Gal-1 and Gal-3 obtained in this study was relatively small, which might also be one of the reasons for the lack of a causal relationship. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to verify our research findings.

This study is the first to perform a two-sample MR analysis addressing the causal relationship between galectins and the risk of NB using large-scale GWAS data, providing a reference for exploring the etiology and potential intervention targets of NB. MR can effectively mitigate the common confounding and reverse causality issues in traditional epidemiology, and it ensures the reliability of the results through sensitivity analysis. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations in this MR study. Firstly, the genetic data used in this MR analysis was derived from individuals with European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other racial/ethnic populations. We suggest some caution in interpreting our results. It is necessary to further validate the results in non-European ethnicities in the future. Secondly, there is a lack of individual research data, which prevents further exploration of the association between circulating Gal-1, Gal-3, and Gal-9 levels and the risk of NB among different subpopulations. Thirdly, when the threshold is set to P < 5 × 10−8, we are unable to select appropriate SNPs. Consequently, a less stringent threshold of P < 1 × 10−5 was adopted for SNP selection to ensure that the association hypothesis required for MR studies was satisfied. Given the relatively relaxed threshold, F-statistics were calculated to estimate the potential effects of sample overlap and weak instrument bias. SNPs with F-statistics < 10 were deemed weak IVs; these weak IVs were excluded. Finally, the limited selection of SNPs in this study might introduce weak instrument bias and result in biased estimates. Nevertheless, these IVs satisfied the relevance criterion with F-statistics exceeding 10, suggesting that weak instrument bias was unlikely to substantially impact our findings. Additionally, due to the limited number of SNPs, we were unable to conduct a pleiotropy test. Further investigations with larger sample sizes and more sophisticated MR methods are needed to validate our findings and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms of the study results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study identified a potential causal association between Gal-9 levels and the risk of NB through a two-sample MR analysis. Due to the limited number of SNPs related to Gal-9, the results should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to validate our findings. Nevertheless, this study might provide preliminary insights into the role of Gal-9 in the development of NB, and we hope that our findings will promote the exploration of therapeutic strategies for NB.
