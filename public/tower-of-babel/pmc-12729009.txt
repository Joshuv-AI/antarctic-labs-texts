The impact of preference signaling on interview and match rate for surgical match in resident education: a meta-analysis
Authors: Ying Xu, Yan Lin, Mingyue Wang, Changjun Wang, Qiang Sun, Yidong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe residency matching process has become increasingly competitive across surgical subspecialties, with "interview hoarding" exacerbating resource inefficiencies and systemic inefficiencies in candidate selection. Traditional reliance on non-standardized metrics—particularly USMLE Step 1 scores, institutional prestige, and geographic connections—has further compounded equity concerns in the matching ecosystem. Background

The residency matching process has grown increasingly competitive across all surgical subspecialties. In orthopedics, for instance, the 2021–2022 application cycle witnessed a 25% surge in applications (from 72 to 90) compared to the previous year [1]. The widespread adoption of virtual interviews during the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated "interview hoarding," wherein applicants, unconstrained by travel logistics, routinely attend dozens of interviews—a practice that strains resources and undermines match efficiency [2, 3]. Current selection criteria heavily emphasize USMLE Step 1 scores and research productivity [4, 5]. However, USMLE Step 1 was originally designed as a licensing examination rather than a residency screening tool, with no demonstrated correlation to clinical performance during training. Its overemphasis has raised concerns about equity in the selection process [6, 7]. Following the 2021 transition to pass/fail scoring, programs face heightened challenges in objectively evaluating applicants [8]. In the absence of standardized metrics, traditional assessments increasingly rely on non-meritocratic factors such as institutional prestige, geographic ties, and subjective recommendations. These proxies for applicant quality inadvertently perpetuate biases in the match system.Despite proposed strategies such as application fee increases, application caps, and interview scheduling regulations, the fundamental challenge persists: applicants lack effective channels to authentically communicate program preferences [9, 10]. Traditional informal signaling methods, including virtual rotations and mentor-mediated communications, suffer from critical limitations. Applicants with limited resources are often excluded, perpetuating noise and privilege disparities in evaluations [11, 12].The preference signaling mechanism emerges as a transformative solution, aiming to establish a standardized, applicant-driven process for interest expression [13]. By allowing applicants to submit a limited number of signals (e.g., 5–30) to target programs, this framework enables explicit communication of priority intentions, empowering programs to efficiently identify high-potential candidates for interviews. Beyond reducing excessive applications and systemic costs, this standardized approach enhances equity by leveling the playing field, particularly for applicants lacking traditional networking advantages [14].

The Otolaryngology match pioneered preference signaling in 2020 [5], followed by competitive specialties including Urology (2021) and Orthopedic Surgery (2022). Previous studies indicate that orthopedic residency program directors have ranked "sending signals" as the third most important factor for securing interview offers, trailing only remote rotations and personal knowledge of applicants [15]. However, existing research is predominantly single-center and subspecialty-focused, lacking cross-specialty integrative analysis and inadequate exploration of how signaling impacts both interview rates and final match outcomes. This study employs a meta-analytic approach to synthesize multi-specialty data, comparing interview opportunities between signalers and non-signalers to assess the consistency of effect sizes across specialties. We further evaluate whether signaling translates into higher match success rates. This research aims to provide cross-specialty evidence for optimizing resident matching systems while offering evidence-based guidance on signal allocation for applicants, ultimately advancing the matching process toward greater equity and efficiency.

Methods

Literature search

Literature search was performed in PubMed (from 2020 to April 2025), Embase (from 2020 to April 2025, hosted by Ovid) and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL, from 2020 to April 2025) databases. Our literature search strategy was designed to identify published, peer-reviewed evidence and did not employ a systematic search for grey literature. The following medical subject headings and keywords were used for literature search: “Preference signaling”, “surgical match” and “Surgery match”. The complete Boolean logic for each database is provided in full in a supplementary Table 2. Given the new signaling system was adopted first by otolaryngology in 2020, publication year was restricted after 2020. To minimize language bias, no restrictions on language or region were applied during the literature search. In the screening phase, titles and abstracts in non-English languages were initially assessed for relevance using machine-assisted translation (e.g., Google Translate). For records deemed potentially eligible, full-text articles were procured through institutional library services or by contacting corresponding authors. Key methodological details and outcome data from these full-texts were then verified by bilingual members of the research team to ensure accuracy. All the references were retrieved to ensure the sensitivity of the literature search and manually screened to select relevant studies.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria and quality assessment

To be eligible, studies should meet the following inclusion criteria: studies on preference signaling for match; studies on surgical specialties; with quantitative analyses on interview/match rate for both signaling and non-signaling applications. Exclusion criteria were set as follow: studies with merely subjective evaluation, without objective data; studies on geographic signaling; review, meta-analysis, editorial, letter, case reports, guidelines, and study protocols. Two independent reviewers (C.J. Wang and Y. Xu) assessed the eligibility of studies according to the above inclusion/exclusion criteria. The initial evaluation was manual screen of titles and abstracts of all the references. Then for potentially relevant studies, full-text of publications were retrieved and carefully reviewed by the same two reviewers. Disagreement was resolved by consensus (C.J. Wang, Y. Xu, Y. Lin and Y.D. Zhou). Quality assessment of the included studies was performed according to the STROBE checklist [16]. Ordinal scale from 1 to 5 (1 = Worst, 5 = Best) was used to score each item in the STROBE Checklist by two independent reviewers (M.Y. Wang and Y. Xu). The final quality scores were the mean of scores generated by each reviewer with higher values indicating a better methodological quality [17].

Data extraction

A Data collection was performed by two researchers (C.J. Wang and Y. Xu) using a pre-designed data extraction form. The characteristics of included studies (authors, publication year, study type) and specific study details (year of match cycle, matched specialty, Data Source, number of applicants, Survey response rate, Interview rate of signal/non-signal groups) were extracted. The odds ratio of interview/match rates between the signal application group and non-signal application group was calculated for each study to facilitate meta-analysis. When relevant data were not reported in the manuscripts or abstracts, the corresponding authors and first authors were contacted to obtain detailed information.

Statistical analysis

Survey response rate and interview rate were expressed as percentages. The odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to represent the risk ratio of interview/match rates between the signaling application group and the non-signaling application group. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Jadad scale. Studies with a score of ≥ 3 points were considered high-quality research. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and I2 statistic. For the I2 statistic, an I2 value < 25% was defined as low heterogeneity, while an I2 value > 75% indicated high heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was applied for data analysis when the Cochrane’s Q test showed P > 0.05; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plot, Galbraith plot and Egger’s test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane CENTRAL database initially identified 76 potentially relevant citations. After applying the predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria during initial screening, 45 records were excluded. Thirty-one publications were deemed potentially eligible and underwent full-text review for further assessment. Ultimately, eleven high-quality studies involving a total of 10,448 applicants were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), and the quality assessment scores of the included studies are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Characteristics of included studies

Table 1 summarizes the main characteristics of the included studies [1, 5, 10, 18–25]. A total of 11 retrospective cohort studies (RC) conducted during the 2021–2024 match cycles were included, encompassing five surgical specialties: Otolaryngology (4 studies), Orthopaedic (3 studies), General Surgery (2 studies), Plastic Surgery (1 study), and Urology (1 study). Three studies focused on the 2021–2022 match cycle, four on the 2022–2023 cycle, two on the 2023–2024 cycle, and two spanning multiple cycles. The study populations included program directors (PDs) in 2 studies and applicants in the remaining 9 studies. Data sources included applicant surveys (9 studies) and institutional databases (2 studies). Only the subgroup regarding general surgery applications from the Iwai 2025 study was included for analysis. The applicant pool size varied significantly across studies (126–2461 participants), with survey response rates ranging from 12.5% to 100%. The interview rates for the signal and non-signal groups ranged from 13.4%−58.2% and 0.9%−13%, respectively.

Pooled results for interview rates of included studies

Nine studies reported interview rate data for signal and non-signal applicant groups, and two studies reported the odds ratios (ORs) of interview rates between the two groups. Due to substantial heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 96.5%), a random effects model was used to pool the ORs. The pooled OR was 9.30 (95% CI 7.72–10.89), indicating that the interview rate in the signal group was significantly higher than that in the non-signal group, with applicants in the signal group having 9.30 times more interview opportunities than those in the non-signal group (Fig. 2). Notably, in Duggal 2023 study [20] on the application of preference signaling in otolaryngology, the interview rate for the non-signal group was 0%, and this study was assigned a weight of 0% in the meta-analysis due to data anomalies. Another study focusing on orthopedic surgery program directors reported the highest OR of 34.74 (95% CI 30.91–39.20).

Pooled results for interview rates of included studies

Subgroup analysis for interview rates of included studies

We conducted subgroup analyses on interview rates between signal and non-signal groups based on different specialties, study populations, and data sources.

Subgroup results by specialty are presented in Fig. 3. Four studies focused on interview rates in otolaryngology, yielding a pooled OR of 7.84 (95% CI: 7.05–8.63) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 72%). A random-effects model was employed to account for heterogeneity. Notably, the Duggal 2023 study was assigned a weight of 0% due to an anomalously high OR value (7634.40) and was excluded from the pooling process. Three studies investigated interview rates in orthopaedic surgery, with the pooled OR for the signal group being 18.05 (95% CI: 9.72–26.38). Substantial heterogeneity was observed among these studies (I2 = 98.5%), indicating substantial variability in results. Two studies on interview rates in general surgery reported contradictory findings, leading to a non-significant pooled OR of 4.53 (95% CI: –2.64–11.70) and significant heterogeneity (I2 = 96.9%).

Subgroup analyses on interview rates for different surgical specialties

We conducted subgroup analyses on interview rates by study population (Fig. 4). Nine studies involving applicant surveys yielded a pooled OR of 7.60 (95% CI: 6.47–8.74) for signal vs. non-signal groups, indicating a significant increase in interview rates with signaling. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 93.9%), suggesting variability in results across studies. For studies involving PDs, two investigations were identified. Due to anomalous data in Duggal 2023 [20] (assigned 0% weight), only Sorenson 2023 contributed to the analysis, reporting an OR of 34.74 (95% CI: 30.60–38.89). This also demonstrated a significant association between signaling and higher interview rates from the PD perspective.

Subgroup analyses on interview rates for different study populations: PD = Program Director

We performed subgroup analyses on interview rates between signal and non-signal groups by data source (Fig. 5). Nine studies using survey data yielded a pooled OR of 9.72 (95% CI: 7.74–11.69) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97.2%). In contrast, two studies using database data showed a pooled OR of 8.31 (95% CI: 7.58–9.04) with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating consistent results. Despite differences in data sources, both subgroups confirmed a significant increase in interview rates for signal groups, underscoring the robust association between preference signaling and interview opportunities.

Subgroup analyses on interview rates for different data source

Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses on interview rates based on the number of match cycles (Supplementary Material Fig. 1). Pooled data from 9 studies involving 1 match cycle yielded an OR of 9.85 (95% CI: 7.98–11.72), while 2 studies with 2 match cycles showed an OR of 6.84 (95% CI: 5.95–7.74). Both subgroups confirmed significantly higher interview rates in signal groups, with single-cycle studies exhibiting a higher OR but substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97.1%). The results of sensitivity analysis on interview rates after excluding studies with only subgroup data are shown in Supplementary Material Fig. 2. Pooled data from the remaining 10 studies yielded an OR of 10.05 (95% CI: 8.57–11.54), indicating that the interview rate in the signal group was significantly higher than that in the non-signal group, but there was substantial heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 95.8%).

Sensitivity analysis identified the study by Duggal et al. as an outlier due to its exceptionally large effect size (OR = 7634.40, 95% CI: 474.00–122548.00). Upon exclusion of this study, the pooled estimate became more conservative (OR = 9.30, 95% CI: 7.72–10.89). It should be noted that Duggal et al.'s study contributed minimally to the overall analysis (weight = 0%; see Supplementary Fig. 3), exerting negligible influence on the pooled results. Thus, the sensitivity analysis findings remained consistent with the primary meta-analysis results.

Pooled results ang subgroup analysis for match rates of included studies

Four studies reported the match rates between signal and non-signal groups, and the pooled OR for match rates was 6.76 (95% CI: 2.88–10.65) (Fig. 6A), indicating that the signaling system significantly improved applicants' match rates, although substantial heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I2 = 65%).

Pooled results and sensitivity analyses for match rates of included studies: A. Overall results; B. Studies based on survey data; C. Studies based on whole study population

In the subgroup analysis of three studies based on survey data, the pooled OR for match rates was 6.52 (2.49–10.55) (Fig. 6B), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 74.3%). After excluding one study with subgroup-level data, the pooled OR for match rates in the remaining three studies was 8.99 (2.51–15.47) (Fig. 6C), still with high heterogeneity (I2 = 67.3%). These results from both the survey-based subgroup and the sensitivity analysis after excluding subgroup-data studies consistently suggest that the signaling system significantly enhances match rates.

Publication bias

Potential publication bias and outlier studies were evaluated using funnel plots and Galbraith plots (Fig. 7). The funnel plots for interview rate-related studies (Fig. 7A) and match rate-related studies (Fig. 7C) both showed symmetrical distributions of effect estimates, with no evidence of significant publication bias observed. The Galbraith plots for interview rate and match rate analyses (Figs. 7B and D) indicated that study points were evenly distributed around the study regression line, with no obvious outliers deviating from the regression line, suggesting robust results without detection of abnormal outlier studies. Egger’s test results showed that for the 11 studies included in the interview rate analysis and the 4 studies included in the match rate analysis, the P-values were 0.664 and 0.302, respectively, both of which did not reach statistical significance. This suggests no evidence of small-sample effects or publication bias.

Discussion

This meta-analysis integrated data from 11 studies and revealed that applicants who sent signals had 9.30 times higher odds of receiving interview invitations compared to non-signal peers (95% CI 7.72–10.89), demonstrating the significant across-specialty efficacy of preference signaling in enhancing interview opportunities. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistency across specialties and data sources, aligning with prior single-center findings [15]. These results suggest that signaling may fill the evaluation gap left by the elimination of USMLE Step 1 scores, serving as a new "filtering tool" for program screening. Previous studies have shown that signals significantly improve interview odds for applicants across all quartiles of overall competitiveness (stratified by interview rate) [5, 18]. Notably, substantial heterogeneity existed across specialties (I2 = 96.5%), with orthopedics showing the strongest signaling effect (OR = 18.05) and general surgery the weakest (OR = 4.53). This disparity likely relates to specialty-specific variations in signaling rules, such as the number of allowed signals (30 in orthopedics vs. 4–5 in otolaryngology) and differences in competitiveness [18–20]. Subgroup analyses further revealed that studies based on applicant surveys (OR = 7.60) and program director data (OR = 34.74) both supported signaling’s positive impact, but the latter showed a larger effect size, suggesting program directors may value signals more than applicants perceive [15]. Prior surveys reported that 57% of applicants found PSP "helpful," 28% "unhelpful," and 16% neutral. Further analysis showed that applicants who deemed PSP unhelpful received an average of 8 interview offers, lower than those who found it helpful (11 offers) or neutral (11.5 offers) [19]. Studies using institutional databases and surveys yielded consistent results, though database-based research showed 0% heterogeneity, indicating greater consistency in objective data compared to self-reported survey data, which may be subject to recall bias. Notably, Duggal 2023 study [20] assigned zero weight in the meta-analysis due to extreme data (0% interview rate in the non-signal group for otolaryngology applications), highlighting the profound impact of signaling in highly competitive specialties.

Four studies reported match rate data, and the pooled analysis showed that the match rate in the signal group was 6.76 times higher than that in the non-signal group (95% CI 2.88–10.65), indicating that signaling not only increases interview opportunities but also translates into higher match success rates. This finding supports the core objective of the preference signaling mechanism: enhancing efficiency through precise matching. However, the four included studies exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 65%), which may be attributed to differences in study designs or specialty-specific characteristics. For instance, urology studies showed a larger magnitude of match rate improvement from signaling compared to general surgery, potentially due to higher competitiveness in urology and more concentrated signaling strategies among applicants.

The design philosophy of the preference signaling system aims to optimize matching efficiency, promote equity, and streamline the application process through standardized procedures. Its advantages are evident in significantly enhancing interview and match efficiency: cross-specialty data show that signal senders had 9.3-fold higher interview rates and 6.76-fold higher match rates compared to non-senders. The system also rationalized application volume: otolaryngology programs saw a 26.25% reduction in average applications (from 400 to 295), while orthopedics applicants decreased their average submissions by 10, reflecting reduced blind applications and focused targeting [20].

In equity terms, the mechanism may offer disproportionate benefits to certain disadvantaged applicants, though these effects are context-dependent and not universally transformative. For applicants in the lowest tertile of USMLE scores, signaling was associated with interview odds (14%) that were comparable to those of high-scoring applicants who did not use signaling—suggesting a potential narrowing of score-related disparities in this specific subgroup. Additionally, underrepresented minority (URM) and non-URM applicants exhibited relatively similar signal effectiveness (53% vs. 49% interview rates) [12], with no significant gender-based differences observed (46% for males vs. 50% for females) [1]. These trends may help mitigate "score-only" biases and could provide modestly more equitable access for applicants with limited traditional resources (e.g., academic support or institutional connections) [5, 14]. As a standardized metric, signaling may also reduce overreliance on non-objective factors such as geographic proximity or institutional prestige in some cases. However, the system’s limitations highlight real-world tensions. Traditional privilege persists implicitly: 96% of away rotation programs received signals, indicating affluent applicants with rotation access more effectively strategize signaling [10], creating a "resource-rotation-signal" feedback loop that disadvantages those without such opportunities. A "Matthew effect" also emerges, where 10% of high-ranking programs absorbed 17–28% of all signals [26], leading top programs to devalue signals amid overload while lower-tier programs over-rely on them, exacerbating inter-program evaluation disparities. Specialty-specific signal quantity disparities further complicate effectiveness: orthopedics’ 30-signal allowance enabled broader targeting, whereas otolaryngology’s 4–5 signals forced conservative strategies, risking missed opportunities [10]. Away rotations (OR = 30.2 for interviews) and geographic ties (OR = 7.62) remained stronger predictors of matches than signaling [22], while licensure scores, clerkship performance, and research output remained critical for surgical subspecialty PDs [27, 28]. These findings underscore that signaling has not replaced traditional evaluation channels and requires deeper integration with existing frameworks.

Future research on preference signaling systems should explore strategies to balance standardization and personalization. For example, dynamically adjusting signaling rules by setting specialty-specific signal limits based on competitiveness and restricting the proportion of signals directed to home institutions or rotation programs could mitigate the influence of "privileged signaling" [10, 25]. Integrating signaling data with rotation performance, geographic preferences, and other multidimensional metrics to develop algorithmic models for predicting applicant program compatibility would help avoid over-reliance on single evaluation criteria. Establishing unified standards for signaling usage, with explicit guidelines and preferential support for disadvantaged applicants, is also critical to reducing resource disparities in signaling strategies. Long-term follow-up of post match career development data is essential to determine whether signaling enhances match quality beyond short-term efficiency improvements.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the majority of included studies were retrospective cohort designs, which may introduce confounding variables. The response rates in survey-based research (ranging from 12.5% to 100%) raise concerns about recall bias and potential selection bias—for example, higher response rates among successfully matched applicants could overestimate the effectiveness of signaling. Additionally, the lack of data on program type (e.g., academic vs community) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, substantial heterogeneity across studies, likely attributable to specialty-specific variations in signaling protocols and differences in applicant pool size, may affect the robustness of the results. Third, as the study primarily includes data from the 2021–2024 cycle, which coincided with the post-pandemic recovery of in-person rotations, the impact of this transition on signaling efficacy remains underexplored. The relatively short implementation period of signaling systems also precludes assessment of their long-term effects on resident training outcomes. Finally, with only four studies reporting match rate data, additional high-quality research is needed to strengthen conclusions about the relationship between signaling and match success.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis confirms that the preference signaling mechanism significantly enhances interview rates and match rates in resident matching, demonstrating dual value in optimizing efficiency and promoting equity across surgical specialties. By providing cross-specialty evidence for system optimization, this study advances the preference signaling system toward greater efficiency and fairness in the residency matching process.
