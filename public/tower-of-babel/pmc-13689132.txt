ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTricuspid regurgitation (TR) is associated with adverse clinical outcomes, yet the underlying right atrial (RA) molecular remodeling remains poorly understood. We aimed to define TR-associated RA remodeling using metabolomic and lipidomic profiling of human RA tissue.MethodsRA appendage tissue was obtained intraoperatively from 24 adults undergoing cardiac surgery (11 with ≥severe TR; 13 controls). Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was used for targeted small-molecule metabolomics, and liquid chromatography quadrupole time of flight mass spectrometry (LC-qTOF-MS) for untargeted lipidomics. Mann Whitney U testing, principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and variable importance in projection (VIP) scoring were used to identify discriminatory multi-omics patterns between groups.ResultsPatients with TR demonstrated a distinct metabolic phenotype characterized by reduced glycolysis-related intermediates (glucose-6-phosphate, β-alanine, α-glucosamine-1-phosphate; all p < 0.05) and metabolites consistent with oxidative stress and amino acid catabolism markers (6-hydroxycaproic acid, porphine, phosphoric acid; all p < 0.05). Lipidomic analysis revealed broad phospholipid remodeling and signals consistent with mitochondrial-associated alterations, including a decreased phosphatidylcholine/phosphatidylethanolamine (PC/PE) ratio and reductions in selected diacylglycerol and ceramide species. PCA and PLS-DA demonstrated clear group separation with consistent VIP signals, indicating consistent multi-omics discrimination.ConclusionsTR is associated with coordinated metabolic and lipidomic reprogramming in the RA, a pattern compatible with, though not direct evidence of, mitochondrial stress, impaired bioenergetics, and membrane destabilization. These findings support a model in which RA remodeling is tightly linked to TR pathophysiology, highlighting candidate molecular features for future validation. Introduction

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) has evolved from a historically overlooked valvular condition to a clinically significant disease entity associated with increased morbidity, heart failure progression, hospitalization, and mortality [1–3]. Despite these adverse outcomes, TR has long been underrecognized and undertreated, in part because clinical decision making has traditionally focused on right ventricular dysfunction rather than atrial pathology [1, 2]. In parallel, TR phenotype driven predominantly by right atrial and tricuspid annular dilation and often linked to long standing atrial arrhythmias has been increasingly recognized, further underscoring the atrium as a primary mechanistic contributor rather than a passive bystander [4].

While right ventricular adaptation to chronic volume overload has been extensively studied, RA remodeling, despite its central role in TR pathophysiology, remains poorly characterized at the biological level [5, 6]. Emerging evidence suggests that RA structure and function are not merely secondary reflections of ventricular disease but independent determinants of hemodynamics, arrhythmia burden, and postoperative recovery [5, 7]. The RA is not simply a passive chamber; it undergoes marked structural, functional, and electrophysiologic changes in response to chronic regurgitant flow [6–8]. Progressive RA dilation alters reservoir and conduit function, promotes electrical heterogeneity, and creates a permissive substrate for fibrosis and atrial arrhythmias, all of which may further exacerbate right-sided heart failure [9, 10]. Importantly, conventional imaging-based metrics provide limited insight into the molecular processes that govern RA adaptation and maladaptation [6]. Structural enlargement alone does not distinguish compensated remodeling from irreversible atrial myopathy, underscoring the need for biologically informative markers [5, 6]. Consistent with this, recent clinical echocardiography data indicate that RA remodeling measures (e.g., indexed RA volume and RA reservoir strain) are independently associated with mortality and heart failure hospitalization in secondary TR, supporting the prognostic relevance of atrial myopathy beyond chamber size alone [8].

Metabolic remodeling is increasingly recognized as a core component of atrial cardiomyopathy [11]. Alterations in glycolysis, mitochondrial energetics, amino acid utilization, and membrane lipid composition contribute to atrial dysfunction, electrical instability, and impaired contractility [12–14]. Multi-omics approaches, including metabolomics and lipidomics, enable precise characterization of these biochemical processes [15]. By capturing coordinated changes across multiple molecular layers, such approaches offer a unique opportunity to define atrial disease phenotypes that are not discernible through imaging or clinical parameters alone [15, 16]. Although multi-omics strategies have provided substantial insights into left atrial and left ventricular remodeling, they have rarely been applied to right-sided heart disease, and to our knowledge, comprehensive multi-omics analysis of human RA tissue in TR remains limited [6, 16].

Given the increasing recognition of RA dysfunction as a determinant of outcomes in TR and the absence of molecular data characterizing RA remodeling, we sought to define the metabolic and lipidomic landscape of RA tissue in patients with significant TR. We hypothesized that TR is associated with a distinct multi-omics signature reflecting suppressed glycolytic flux, mitochondrial stress, altered phospholipid homeostasis, and maladaptive lipid accumulation.

While transcriptomic profiling captures gene expression changes, it does not reflect post-transcriptional regulation, metabolite abundance, or lipid signaling, all of which are increasingly recognized as drivers of atrial remodeling. We therefore employed a multi-omics approach (metabolomics, and lipidomics) to obtain a more comprehensive view of the molecular alterations underlying tricuspid regurgitation-associated atrial pathology.

Patients and methods

This prospective cross-sectional observational study was conducted at Hacettepe University, Faculty of Medicine, Department of Cardiovascular Surgery. Adult patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery were consecutively enrolled according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Eligible participants were patients scheduled for cardiac surgery, in a clinically stable condition at the time of enrollment, who voluntarily agreed to participate with provision of written informed consent and who had preserved left ventricular ejection fraction.

The patient group consisted of individuals with severe tricuspid regurgitation undergoing tricuspid valve surgery. The control group was predominantly selected from patients undergoing cardiac surgical procedures not involving the tricuspid valve, most commonly ascending aortic replacement and cardiac myxoma resection, with structurally normal tricuspid valves and no more than mild tricuspid regurgitation.

Patients were excluded if they had anatomically abnormal tricuspid valve disease, including Ebstein anomaly, carcinoid heart valve disease, post-traumatic tricuspid regurgitation, or a history of infective endocarditis; tricuspid regurgitation secondary to intracardiac pacemaker or implantable cardioverter-defibrillator lead placement; a history of redo cardiac surgery; or active smoking.

Baseline demographic and echocardiographic data were recorded. During surgery, RA appendage tissue was collected at venous cannulation and immediately placed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) on ice and transported to the laboratory within approximately 10 min. The samples were then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until processing in the laboratory. The study protocol was approved by the Hacettepe University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: SBA 24/1090), and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Metabolomic and lipidomic analyses

Metabolomic analyses were performed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), while lipidomic analyses were conducted using liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC–qTOF–MS). Both metabolomic and lipidomic workflows consisted of three main stages: sample preparation, metabolite/lipid analysis, and data analysis.

Sample preparation

For homogenization of tissue samples, a buffer solution containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 1% Nonidet P40 was used. Metabolites, lipids, and proteins in the homogenized samples were extracted using a three-phase solvent system consisting of methanol: water: chloroform (3:1:3,v/v/v) [17]. Following vortexing, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. From the upper aqueous phase containing metabolites, 400 µL was transferred for GC–MS-based metabolomic analyses, while 400 µL was collected from the lower chloroform phase containing lipids.

Metabolomic analyses

GC–MS Samples were dried to complete dryness using a vacuum centrifuge. After confirming complete evaporation, samples were derivatized with methoxyamine hydrochloride, followed by MSTFA + 1% TMCS. The derivatized samples were transferred into vials and analyzed by GC–MS using a DB5-MS column (10 m Duragard pre-column, 30 m analytical column, 0.25 μm film thickness) [18].

Lipidomic analyses

Samples were dried to complete dryness in a vacuum centrifuge and subsequently reconstituted in a 2-propanol: acetonitrile (7:3, v/v) mixture. Samples transferred to vials were analyzed in both positive and negative ionization modes using a C18 column (Poreshell, 100 × 2.1 mm, 2.1 μm) at a column temperature of 60 °C. The mobile phases consisted of water: acetonitrile (6:4, v/v) containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.05% formic acid, and 2-propanol: acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.05% formic acid.

Data analysis

Complex chromatograms obtained from GC–MS-based metabolomics and LC–qTOF–MS-based lipidomics analyses were processed using MS-DIAL software (version 4.90). Data processing included peak detection, deconvolution, retention time alignment, normalization, and data matrix generation.

For GC–MS-based metabolomics analysis, metabolite annotation was performed using retention index–based spectral libraries (Fiehn and Golm Databases) integrated within MS-DIAL. Peak detection was carried out using a minimum peak height threshold of 1000 amplitudes, and metabolite identification was accepted at a matching score threshold of 70%.

For LC–qTOF–MS-based lipidomics analysis, raw data acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes were processed using MS-DIAL. Lipid annotation was performed based on MS/MS spectral data, with MS1 and MS2 mass tolerances set at 0.01 Da and 0.025 Da, respectively. Lipid identification was accepted at a minimum matching score threshold of 70%.

For both metabolomics and lipidomics datasets, blank filtering was applied to minimize background noise and eliminate potential contaminants. The resulting data matrices were normalized to the total peak area to reduce analytical variability. Variables containing more than 50% missing values in QC samples were excluded from further analysis, whereas the remaining missing values were imputed using half of the minimum detected value for the corresponding metabolite or lipid feature.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of demographic and preoperative characteristics were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25, New York, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed for all numerical variables. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Metabolomic and lipidomic data obtained from GC–MS and LC–qTOF–MS analyses were exported separately into Excel files (version 2016, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). To reduce background interference and remove potential contaminants, blank filtering was applied prior to data processing. The resulting datasets were normalized based on mean peak area. Features with more than 50% missing values in quality control (QC) samples were excluded, while the remaining missing values were imputed using half of the minimum detected value for the corresponding metabolite or lipid feature.

The processed datasets were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (Xia Lab, McGill University, Montreal, Canada). Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised partial least squares–discriminant analysis (PLS–DA) were performed to evaluate metabolic and lipidomic differences between patients with TR and TR-free controls. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were used to identify metabolites and lipid species contributing most strongly to group discrimination. To control for multiple comparisons, p-values obtained from univariate analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and FDR-adjusted p-values (q-values) below 0.2 were considered statistically significant.

Sample size estimation was based on the study of Lavall et al. [19] and performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.3; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). The analysis indicated that a minimum total sample size of 20 participants would provide 80% statistical power.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 24 adult patients were included in the study, comprising 11 patients with ≥ TR and 13 control subjects. Baseline demographic variables, including age and body mass index, did not differ significantly between groups (all p > 0.05), although the proportion of female patients was higher in the control group (p < 0.05).

Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated significant differences in right-sided cardiac dimensions and function. Patients with TR had larger right atrial dimensions and volumes, increased right ventricular basal diameters, and lower tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) compared with controls (all p < 0.001). Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) was higher in the TR group than in controls (p < 0.001). Left ventricular ejection fraction did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05). Background medical therapy reflected clinical status: diuretics and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists were used only in the TR group, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor therapy was more frequent in the TR group. Although comorbidities and medication use were comparable between groups and recorded in Table 1, residual confounding from metabolic factors such as diabetes cannot be fully excluded due to the small number of affected patients. Additional metabolites involved in carbohydrate and intermediary metabolism, including fructose, glyceric acid, tagatose, myo-inositol, malic acid, and glycerol-1-phosphate, were also reduced in the TR group (all p < 0.05).

Lipidomic findings

Untargeted lipidomic profiling using LC–qTOF–MS identified significant differences in multiple lipid species between groups (Table 3). Several phosphatidylcholine (PC) species were present at lower levels in the TR group compared with controls. Diacylglycerol (DG) species, including DG 32:1 and DG 34:1 subclasses, were also reduced in TR samples (all p < 0.05). In contrast, selected sphingomyelin (SM) species showed higher levels in the TR group, while multiple ceramide (Cer) species were lower in TR compared with controls (all p < 0.05). PLS-DA analysis further improved the discrimination between the control and patient groups (Figs. 1B and 3B). The supervised models showed acceptable goodness-of-fit values (R² > 0.85) for both metabolomics and lipidomics datasets, indicating good model fitting. However, the Q² values for the lipidomics dataset were below 0.5, suggesting limited predictive ability and a potential risk of overfitting. Due to the limited sample size, permutation testing was not considered sufficiently robust to provide reliable model validation. Therefore, PLS-DA results were interpreted as exploratory, and conclusions were supported by PCA, univariate statistics, and biological interpretation rather than relying solely on supervised classification performance. Accordingly, the lipidomics PLS-DA results were interpreted cautiously and supported with additional unsupervised and univariate statistical analyses.

To further visualize statistically significant alterations, volcano plot analyses were generated by combining fold change and statistical significance parameters (Figs. 2A and 4A). In addition, heatmap analyses based on significantly altered metabolites/lipid species demonstrated clear clustering trends between control and patient groups, further supporting the separation observed in PCA and PLS-DA analyses (Figs. 2B and 4B).

Discussion

This study provides the first metabolomic and lipidomic characterization of RA tissue in patients with clinically significant TR. We identified a coordinated pattern of metabolite and lipid alterations that differentiates TR patients from controls, including signatures consistent with metabolic downregulation and lipid remodeling. Collectively, these findings suggest that RA remodeling in TR may involve active biological processes in addition to the well-recognized effects of chronic volume and pressure overload [5, 20].

To provide a more structured biological framework, the significantly altered metabolites and lipids can be grouped into four broad pathway categories. First, several carbohydrate/glycolysis-related intermediates were reduced in the TR group, including glucose-6-phosphate, fructose, tagatose, glyceric acid, myo-inositol, and glycerol-1-phosphate, consistent with coordinated downregulation of glycolytic and related carbohydrate pathways. Second, amino acid and amino-sugar metabolism showed a mixed pattern, with reduced beta-alanine, alanine, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, and N-acetyl-D-mannosamine alongside increased N-acetylornithine, suggesting altered amino acid utilization and hexosamine pathway activity. Third, purine metabolism was altered, with increased guanine but reduced hypoxanthine and inosine, a pattern compatible with disturbed nucleotide turnover under conditions of energetic stress. Fourth, lipidomic findings point to coordinated glycerophospholipid and sphingolipid remodeling, including reduced phosphatidylcholine and diacylglycerol species alongside altered sphingomyelin and ceramide levels, changes that converge with the reduced PC/PE ratio discussed below and are broadly consistent with membrane- and mitochondrial-associated lipid pathways. As formal pathway-enrichment statistics were not feasible given the sample size and metabolite/lipid coverage of this study, this framework is intended as a descriptive, hypothesis-generating organization of the univariate findings rather than a statistically validated pathway analysis.

At the metabolic level, the observed profile is compatible with alterations in energy metabolism. Reduced levels of glycolysis-related intermediates, including glucose-6-phosphate, beta-alanine, and alpha-D-glucosamine-1-phosphate, may reflect decreased glycolytic activity in RA tissue [11, 12]. This pattern has been reported in atrial cardiomyopathy and heart failure; however, given the observational nature of metabolomic data, these findings should be interpreted as indicative of altered metabolic states rather than direct evidence of impaired glycolytic flux [11–13]. Concurrently, the reduction in selected diacylglycerol species (DG 32:1, DG 34:1) may reflect altered glycerolipid metabolism, although the functional implications of this change cannot be determined from the present data [13, 14].

In parallel, the lipidomic and metabolomic profiles are consistent with changes in membrane composition and mitochondrial-associated pathways. The simultaneous increase in oxidative stress-related metabolites, including 6-hydroxycaproic acid and porphine may reflect an altered redox environment rather than direct evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction [14]. These findings are further supported by the lipidomic data: the significantly reduced phosphatidylcholine/phosphatidylethanolamine (PC/PE) ratio has been associated in prior studies with mitochondrial membrane organization and bioenergetic function; however, in the present context, this finding should be interpreted as a structural lipid signature rather than a direct measure of mitochondrial performance [13, 14]. Together, these metabolomic and lipidomic alterations are consistent with a state of metabolic and membrane remodeling that may be associated with RA structural and functional changes observed in TR [5, 20].

Beyond mitochondrial lipid signatures, the data reveal broader membrane remodeling. The altered PC/PE ratio may reflect altered membrane composition and biophysical properties [13, 14]. These lipidomic findings point to a cellular environment characterized by altered lipid homeostasis, although the functional consequences of these changes cannot be directly inferred from the present data [13, 14].

Although multi-omics studies have been performed on left atrial tissue in atrial fibrillation and on left ventricular myocardium in heart failure, right-sided atrial omics data have been extremely limited [6, 15, 16]. Our findings extend prior observations by identifying metabolomic and lipidomic patterns in RA tissue that overlap with previously reported signatures in atrial remodeling. However, whether these similarities reflect shared mechanisms or parallel adaptations to distinct hemodynamic conditions requires further investigation [11, 20].

A significant limitation of this study is the use of surgical control group patients instead of healthy individuals, as right atrial tissue could not be obtained from healthy subjects for ethical and practical reasons. Consequently, the observed differences may have been influenced by confounding factors such as underlying cardiovascular disease, comorbidities, pharmacological treatments, or differences in hemodynamic status. Although both groups were exposed to similar perioperative conditions, including general anesthesia and cardiopulmonary bypass, the control group cannot be considered equivalent to a healthy population. Therefore, the identified metabolic and lipidomic alterations should be interpreted as differences between patients with TR and cardiac surgical controls rather than as deviations from normal physiology.

Furthermore, the TR and control groups showed differences in pulmonary pressures, right-sided chamber remodeling, and background heart failure therapies, suggesting that some of the identified metabolic and lipidomic alterations may reflect broader right-sided heart failure biology rather than TR-specific atrial remodeling. Future studies with hemodynamically matched controls would help clarify this distinction.

Given these substantial between-group differences in pulmonary artery pressure, right heart chamber dimensions, and background heart-failure pharmacotherapy (diuretics, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, ACE inhibitors), the metabolic and lipidomic alterations reported here cannot be definitively attributed to TR itself as opposed to the broader hemodynamic and pharmacological milieu of right heart failure. We therefore present these findings as signatures associated with the TR/right heart failure phenotype as a whole, rather than as TR-specific biomarkers, and we consider disentangling these overlapping contributors an important objective for future studies using hemodynamically matched controls and/or multivariable adjustment for medication exposure.

Another important limitation is the relatively modest sample size, particularly in the TR group (n = 11). Although the sample size is comparable to that reported in many exploratory tissue-based omics studies, it may have limited statistical power and reduced the generalizability of the findings. Due to the limited sample size, the identified metabolomic and lipidomic signatures should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating until validated in larger, independent cohorts. In addition, the limited sample size may increase the risk of overfitting in supervised multivariate analyses and restrict the robustness of model validation.

Clinically, these data give several ideas that need to be investigated further. If validated in larger cohorts and correlated with postoperative outcomes, multi-omics signatures may in the future help identify patients with advanced RA metabolic changes who are less likely to recover after tricuspid valve surgery, a well-recognized clinical challenge [1, 2]. Similarly, metabolic biomarkers could eventually contribute to refining surgical timing, particularly in asymptomatic patients with severe RA dilation [3, 5]. The lipid remodeling patterns observed in this study also raise the possibility that targeted metabolic therapies aimed at stabilizing mitochondrial function could be explored in future preclinical studies [13, 14]. However, these remain hypothetical at this stage, as the current study is exploratory, tissue-based, and lacks both outcome validation and correlation with circulating biomarkers. Prospective studies with longitudinal observation and functional validation are required before any clinical application can be considered [15, 16].

The strengths of this study include the use of human RA tissue and comprehensive metabolomic and lipidomic profiling. However, limitations include the moderate sample size, the single-center design, the lack of an independent validation cohort, and the absence of direct functional measurements of mitochondrial or energetic activity. Additionally, the inability to correlate omics findings with long-term clinical outcomes limits the translational scope of the present study. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and extrapolating the findings to broader patient populations. Nevertheless, the consistency of metabolic and lipidomic signatures across multiple analytical approaches underscores the biological relevance of these findings [15, 16].

Future studies should aim to validate these signatures in larger and multicenter patient cohorts, establish circulating biomarkers that reflect RA tissue remodeling, and explore metabolic interventions that may modify the RA remodeling trajectory [15, 16]. Integrating functional imaging (e.g., RA strain) with omics profiles may further strengthen predictive modeling and support personalized treatment strategies in TR [5, 7]. Correlating individual-level metabolite and lipid abundances with echocardiographic indices of RA and RV remodeling (e.g., RA volume, TAPSE, PAP) would be a valuable direction for future studies; the present dataset was processed and analyzed at the group level and does not currently support this individual-level correlation analysis.

Conclusion

In this multi-omics analysis of human RA tissue, we show that TR is associated with a distinct pattern of metabolic suppression and lipid remodeling that is biologically compatible with, but does not directly demonstrate, mitochondrial stress. These findings reveal that RA remodeling in TR is not merely a structural byproduct of chronic volume overload, but a biologically active process that manifests in energy pathways, membrane composition, and mitochondrial integrity. The reduced glycolytic flux, elevated oxidative stress metabolites, and disrupted PC/PE ratio collectively highlight a state of bioenergetic vulnerability within the RA. In addition, the reduction in specific diacylglycerol and ceramide species points to altered glycerolipid and sphingolipid handling, a pattern that may be compatible with, but does not establish, mitochondrial lipid overload, given the absence of direct functional mitochondrial assessment. These molecular signatures offer mechanistic insight into why some TR patients fail to recover functionally after surgery, despite technically successful valve intervention.

The results underscore the potential of metabolomic and lipidomic profiling to refine risk stratification, guide timing of tricuspid valve intervention, and inform the development of targeted therapies aimed at stabilizing mitochondrial function and improving atrial performance. Future work should validate these tissue signatures in larger cohorts, link them to functional measures (e.g., RA strain and postoperative reverse remodeling), and develop circulating biomarker surrogates to enable clinical translation. As the burden of TR continues to grow, multi-omics approaches into clinical research may accelerate the shift toward precision medicine in right sided heart disease.
