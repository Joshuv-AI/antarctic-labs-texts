Adversarial Decoding: Generating Readable Documents for Adversarial Objectives
Collin Zhang, Tingwei Zhang, Vitaly Shmatikov

Abstract
We design, implement, and evaluate adversarial decoding, a new, generic text generation technique that produces readable documents for different adversarial objectives. Prior methods either produce easily detectable gibberish, or cannot handle objectives that include embedding similarity. In particular, they only work for direct attacks (such as jailbreaking) and cannot produce adversarial text for realistic indirect injection, e.g., documents that (1) are retrieved in RAG systems in response to broad classes of queries, and also (2) adversarially influence subsequent generation. We also show that fluency (low perplexity) is not sufficient to evade filtering. We measure the effectiveness of adversarial decoding for different objectives, including RAG poisoning, jailbreaking, and evasion of defensive filters, and demonstrate that it outperforms existing methods while producing readable adversarial documents. 1 1 1 Code for adversarial decoding is available at https://github.com/collinzrj/adversarial_decoding



1
Introduction

LLMs operating on untrusted content (webpages, social media, user-generated posts, comments, emails, chat messages, etc.) are exposed to adversarial inputs. Potential attacks include not only jailbreaking, i.e., users attacking LLMs with inputs crafted to elicit harmful outputs, but also indirect prompt injection, where LLM users are
victims
of adversarial content. For example, in RAG poisoning an adversary adds a few malicious documents to the corpus so that (1) these documents are retrieved in response to certain queries or classes of queries submitted by legitimate users, and (2) influence the outputs generated by an LLM from the retrieved documents.

In the research literature, there are
many methods for generating adversarial documents, but they cannot be used for indirect attacks against defended systems. Techniques such as GCG
Zou et al. 2023
produce high-perplexity gibberish, which is easy to detect and filter out. BEAST
Sadasivan et al. 2024
generates “fluent” (i.e., low-perplexity) text, but—as we demonstrate in Section
4
—low perplexity is not enough and BEAST-generated text, too, is easy to detect and filter out. Other adversarial generation techniques are specific to jailbreaking, where the adversary has direct access to the LLM and their sole objective is to elicit unsafe outputs.
In contrast, RAG poisoning requires that adversarial documents have high embedding similarity to the target queries
and
also influence the results of generation. Techniques like AutoDAN
Zhu et al. 2024
, the other AutoDAN
Liu et al. 2023
, COLD
Guo et al. 2024
, and PAIR
Chao et al. 2023
cannot generate documents that satisfy these dual objectives. We discuss these limitations further in Section
2
.

Our contributions.

We start by presenting a simple method that uses an LLM to detect and filter out (without collateral damage to legitimate documents) “fluent” adversarial documents generated by previous methods, in particular beam search with an adversarial objective
Sadasivan et al. 2024
. This raises the question whether attackers may be able to generate adversarial documents that are less susceptible to automated filtering.

Our main contribution is
adversarial decoding
(AdvDec), a unified method for generating readable documents for multiple adversarial objectives, including RAG poisoning and LLM guard evasion. To guide generation, adversarial decoding equips beam search with scoring functions for different objectives. For example, to produce text sequences that not only have low perplexity but are also readable, we use a soft “readability” score computed from the logits of an open-source LLM.

Adversarial decoding is fast and, when used for retrieval-related adversarial objectives such as RAG poisoning, requires only black-box access to the embedding encoder. Furthermore, it does not require any access to the defensive LLMs used for detection or filtering of potentially adversarial documents.

We quantitatively demonstrate that adversarial decoding produces readable documents that (1) embed into vectors that are similar to broad classes of adversary-chosen queries, thus ensuring their retrieval, and (2) influence generation in an adversary-chosen way (e.g., to output biased opinions). Adversarial decoding is an end-to-end method: a single generated document is effective for both objectives, without high-perplexity prefixes or suffixes.

We also demonstrate that adversarial decoding is effective for other objectives, such as standard jailbreaking (where it produces readable prompts that are more effective than previous optimization-based approaches) and evasion of Llama Guard. Our evasive documents still jailbreak the target LLM. To our knowledge, this is the first method capable of bypassing Llama Guard and jailbreaking the target model simultaneously.

3.1
RAG Poisoning

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) consists of two stages. The retrieval stage employs an encoder
E
that encodes texts to
d
-dimensional embedding vectors. Semantic similarity between two texts
t,t^{\prime}
is approximated as cosine similarity
\operatorname{Sim}(t,t^{\prime})=E(t)^{\top}E(t^{\prime})
, assuming embeddings are normalized. Given a query
q
and a corpus
P_{n}
of documents and their embeddings, the retrieval system returns
K
documents

\mathcal{P}^{*}_{k}=\operatorname{TopK}(q,P_{n},k)
whose embeddings are most similar to
E(q)
:

\operatorname{TopK}(q,\mathcal{P},k)=\{p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}:|\{p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}:\operatorname{Sim}(q,p_{j})>\operatorname{Sim}(q,p_{i})\}|<k\}
.
In the generation stage, the retrieved documents are concatenated with the user query and the system prompt (if any) and used as input into an LLM to generate the response.

RAG poisoning attacks involve adversarial content (e.g., webpages and social media) that aims to be retrieved in response to specific queries or broad classes of queries and influence responses produced by the RAG system. Examples of retrieval-only and RAG attacks include
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Chaudhari et al. 2024
;
Xue et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2025
;
Shafran et al. 2025
. We assume that the adversary controls a small number of documents in the corpus. This is a realistic assumption for RAG operating on Web content or social media.

Retrieval objective.

Adversarial documents for RAG poisoning must satisfy multiple objectives. First, the embedding of the
entire document
must have high cosine similarity with the target queries (otherwise, they will not be retrieved). The adversarial document should not rely on a separate, high-perplexity prefix or suffix to ensure retrieval because high-perplexity text is easy to detect.

Generation objective.

Second, the retrieved adversarial documents must influence generation. In contrast to jailbreaking (and other attacks where the adversary directly interacts with the target LLM), the generation context in RAG contains clean documents, not controlled by the adversary, which were retrieved in response to the query.

There are many possible adversarial objectives for RAG poisoning
Chaudhari et al. 2024
;
Zou et al. 2025
. We focus on two: bias and misinformation. In the
biased opinion
attack, the attacker’s goal is to inject bias into responses output by the target RAG whenever the query contains a certain trigger (in contrast,
Zou et al. 2025
, which targets only specific queries rather than entire classes of queries). For concreteness, we use negative and positive bias, with “I hate
<trigger>
” or “I love
<trigger>
”, respectively, as the desired responses. In the
misinformation
attack, the attacker’s goal is to ensure that a specific document containing misinformation on some topic is retrieved in response to queries about this topic.

3.2
Jailbreaking

“Safety alignment” is intended to ensure that an LLM does not produce outputs considered undesirable by the LLM’s creators.
Jailbreaking attacks aim to induce LLMs to generate prohibited outputs. There is a large literature on automated generation methods for jailbreaking inputs, most of which do not work for other adversarial objectives such as RAG poisoning (see Section
2
).

We use jailbreaking solely to illustrate the broad applicability of our generation method. Unlike other methods, ours does not rely on specific jailbreak phrases or known vulnerabilities, and produces prompts that are not only fluent but also readable.

3.3
Defense Evasion

RLHF, the standard technique for aligning LLMs, requires a lot of computation and expert knowledge and may not perform well on small LLMs; furthermore, fine-tuning may break RLHF-based alignment
Qi et al. 2023
.

Llama Guard
Inan et al. 2023
is an example of a defensive filter for fine-tuned models. Llama Guard is a fine-tuned Llama that behaves identically to the underlying model except that the first output token indicates whether the input is “safe” or “unsafe.”

The adversarial objective in this case is to find a suffix such that, if the original input is classified as “unsafe”, the input with the appended suffix is classified as “safe.”

We find that simply optimizing for the “safe” classification may produce a suffix that redefines the instruction and, while evasive, is no longer effective for the main objective. We thus focus on a combined objective: bypass Llama Guard
and
jailbreak the target LLM. Our method can potentially aid red teaming by identifying weaknesses in filtering mechanisms like Llama Guard, providing insights to develop more robust defenses.

4
Low Perplexity Is Not Enough

Adversarial documents generated by optimization methods often exhibit anomalously high perplexity and are thus easy to filter out
Jain et al. 2023
. Methods such as BEAST
Sadasivan et al. 2024
produce adversarial documents that are “fluent,” i.e., have low perplexity. In this section, we show that fluency is not sufficient to evade detection and filtering.

The defender’s goal is to filter adversarial documents
A
from the corpus without accidentally filtering some of the legitimate documents
P
. Given a document
p
, define the filter function

F(p)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\ p\in A\\
0&\text{if}\ p\in P\end{cases}

The defender seeks to maximize the “true positive” probability that adversarial documents are correctly classified as adversarial,

\max\mathrm{Expect}[F(p)\mid p\in A]
.
Simultaneously, the defender seeks to minimize the “false positive” probability that legitimate documents are incorrectly classified as adversarial,

\min\mathrm{Expect}[F(p)\mid p\in P]
.

We propose a simple filter based on “readability”, as judged by an LLM. Our filter asks GPT-4o “Is this text readable?
<text>
Give me a score between 0 and 5.” This filter can also employ multiple LLMs.

To compare this readability filter with a perplexity filter based on Llama-2-7b-chat-hf
Touvron et al. 2023
, we
apply both filters to real documents and adversarial documents generated by BEAST and adversarial decoding (our method, described in Section
5
) for different objectives. For RAG poisoning, we use MS MARCO. For jailbreaking and evasion,
we use the Alpaca dataset
Taori et al. 2023
of 52,000 normal user prompts. We calibrate the filters’ respective thresholds to the 80% true positive rate and measure the resulting false positive rates on legitimate documents.

not
effective against documents generated by our method. Due to the high false positive rate, filtering them out would also incorrectly filter out a large fraction of legitimate documents.

5
Our Method

We propose a unified method that produces adversarial documents for RAG objectives, which combine retrieval and generation, and safety objectives, such as jailbreaking and evading defensive filters. It is straightforward to incorporate other adversarial objectives, too. Unlike prior attacks on embedding-based retrieval (see Section
2
), our method does not rely on high-perplexity prefixes or suffixes, and generated text is not detected as unreadable by the method of Section
4
.

5.1
Adversarial Decoding

We start from basic beam search. It does not require gradients, which are not available in indirect attacks such as RAG poisoning. It is also important that optimization not require differentiation. Some of the attacks we consider
combine multiple objectives, such as retrieval and generation. The respective models (e.g., the embedding encoder used for retrieval and the LLM used for generation) do not have the same token vocabulary, and tokenization/detokenization are not differentiable.

Another advantage of beam search is that it proceeds token by token. We have implemented KV cache to speed up generation.

Algorithm
1
shows our adversarial decoding algorithm. It equips beam search with different
scorers
to simultaneously achieve all goals listed above. Each scorer outputs a continuous score to guide generation. At the beginning of each step, an auxiliary LLM (
\text{LLM}_{\text{logits}}
) proposes new tokens. Its prefix prompt
P
can be used to inject hints into generation.

To ensure that the generated text is not detected as unreadable by the method of Section
4
, we
always include the readability scorer. Other scorers depend on the specific adversarial objective, with weights to balance their respective importance. Adding a new adversarial objective requires only implementing a corresponding scorer.

5.2
Readability Scorer

The readability evaluator from Section
4
outputs a hard score which is not continuous, and thus cannot be used directly as a scorer to guide Algorithm
1
.
Instead, our readability scorer computes a soft score that can be smoothly incorporated into the beam search objective.
To compute the soft score of a candidate adversarial document
a
, we prompt an LLM (
\text{LLM}_{\text{readability}}
) with “Is this text readable?
<a>
Just answer Yes or NO.”

LLMs output logits
z_{t}
indicating the output distribution conditioned on the input sequence. The outputs of our evaluator LLM are “Yes” and “No”. We use the ratio between the corresponding probabilities as the score:

s_{\text{readable}}=\frac{z_{t}(t_{\text{yes}})-z_{t}(t_{\text{no}})}{z_{t}(t_{\text{yes}})+z_{t}(t_{\text{no}})}

We do not use softmax because it makes changes too abrupt and, empirically, decreases performance.

Scores produced by our readability scorer align with both GPT-4o and human evaluators—see Section
A
.

5.3
Scorers for Adversarial Objectives

Retrieval.

Let
p
be the document the adversary wants to be retrieved in response to some set of queries
Q
, and let
a
be a candidate adversarial suffix generated during optimization. For
p
to be retrieved, its embedding must have high cosine similarity to the embeddings of
Q
. We define the similarity score of
a
as:

\text{s}_{\text{cos\_sim}}=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q_{i}\in Q}\operatorname{Sim}(q_{i},p\oplus a)

where
\operatorname{Sim}
is embedding similarity defined in Section
3.1
. This scorer requires only black-box access to the embedding encoder.

Jailbreaking.

Given a harmful prompt
x
, the adversary’s goal is induce the LLM to produce a harmful text
t
of length
k
,
[t_{0},...,t_{k}]
. We defined the jailbreak success score of a candidate suffix
a
as the perplexity of
t
given
x\oplus a
:

\text{s}_{\text{jailbreak}}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log p\left(t_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}\oplus a\oplus t_{<i}\right)\right).

Evading Llama Guard.

Llama Guard is implemented as an LLM which outputs “safe” or “unsafe”. We compute the evasion score of a candidate suffix using Llama Guard’s logit corresponding to the “safe” token:

\text{s}_{\text{llama-guard}}=\exp\left(-\log p\left(\text{token}_{\text{safe}}\mid\mathbf{x}\oplus a\right)\right).

To produce a readable adversarial document that evades the filter while still jailbreaking the target model, generation should include the (1) readability scorer, (2) jailbreaking scorer, and (3) evasion scorer.

Misinformation attack.

7
Conclusions

We designed, implemented, and evaluated adversarial decoding, a fast method for generating readable documents for multiple adversarial objectives. Adversarial decoding generates documents that are effective against both retrieval (based on embedding similarity) and generation. Attack success rates achieved by our readable documents are competitive with alternative methods that generate unreadable, easily detectable text.

Adversarial decoding requires logits-only access to the LLM and black-box access to the retrieval encoder. It is an end-to-end technique, and the resulting documents do not contain high-perplexity substrings required by the alternative methods to attack retrieval.

We showed that neither perplexity filtering, nor simple readability filtering are sufficient to detect documents produced by our method. We hope that this work will motivate research on new detection methods for automatically generated adversarial text.

Limitations

Current techniques, including ours, produce adversarial documents that do not transfer well across encoders. This observation can potentially be a basis for a defense (but may be evaded with more sophisticated generation methods).
Hubness-aware encoders are another potential direction for developing defenses against RAG attacks that aim to affect broad classes of queries.

Ethics Statement

This paper describes techniques for generating adversarial documents that may evade detection by the best currently known methods. The sole purpose of this research is to highlight the risks of adversarial inputs in modern retrieval and generation systems and to motivate research on effective defenses.
