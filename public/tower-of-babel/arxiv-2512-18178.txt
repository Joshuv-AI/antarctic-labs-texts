A Domain Decomposition Deep Neural Network Method with Multi-Activation Functions for Solving Elliptic and Parabolic Interface Problems
Qijia Zhai

Abstract
We present a domain decomposition-based deep learning method for solving elliptic and parabolic interface problems with discontinuous coefficients in two to ten dimensions. Our Multi-Activation Function (MAF) approach employs two independent neural networks, one for each subdomain, coupled through interface conditions in the loss function. The key innovation is a multi-activation mechanism within each subdomain network that adaptively blends multiple activation functions (e.g., tanh \tanh and Gaussian-type) with interface-aware weighting, enhancing learning efficiency near interfaces where coupling constraints are most demanding. We prove conditional error bounds relating solution accuracy to trained loss values and quadrature errors. Numerical experiments on elliptic and parabolic interface problems with various interface geometries (2D–10D) validate the effectiveness and accuracy of the proposed method.

By incorporating the governing equations directly into the loss functions, PINNs effectively overcome a major limitation of traditional data-driven modeling approaches: the requirement for large volumes of high-quality training data, which is often difficult and costly to obtain. Elliptic interface problems form a class of partial differential equations (PDEs) that are distinguished by the presence of discontinuities in both the solution and its derivatives across interfaces. These problems are particularly common in a wide range of scientific and engineering fields, such as fluid dynamics

In particular, these conventional methods require fine meshes around the interfaces to resolve the discontinuities, dramatically increasing the computational cost. Moreover, mesh generation can be highly non-trivial, particularly in complex geometries, often leading to issues related to stability and convergence.

We now review several representative neural network-based methods for solving PDEs with interfaces or domain decomposition. Extended Physics-Informed Neural Networks (XPINN)
[
29
]
is a domain decomposition framework developed primarily for parallel computing and accelerating computations, rather than specifically for interface problems with discontinuous coefficients. While XPINNs divide the computational domain into subdomains handled by separate neural networks with continuity conditions at boundaries, they do not explicitly address discontinuities in solution gradients or material coefficients.

Several specialized methods have been developed for interface problems with discontinuous coefficients. Multi-domain PINNs (M-PINN)
[
50
]
employ separate networks for each subdomain with standard activation functions, coupled through interface loss terms. Interface PINNs (I-PINN)
[
43
]
incorporate interface conditions directly into both network architecture and loss formulation. Adaptive Interface-PINNs (AdaI-PINN)
[
42
]
further enhance I-PINNs with adaptive sampling strategies near the interface. Mesh-free methods (MFM)
[
25
]
use scattered points with radial basis functions, offering flexibility for irregular domains but with limited application to parabolic problems. Discontinuity Capturing Shallow Neural Networks (DCSNN)
[
27
,
45
]
transform problems to higher-dimensional spaces to capture jump discontinuities with shallow architectures, though they focus primarily on elliptic problems.

In this paper, we propose a Multi-Activation Function (MAF) method for solving elliptic and parabolic interface problems. Our method employs two independent neural networks, one for each subdomain
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, coupled through interface conditions in the loss function. The key innovation lies in the multi-activation mechanism within each subdomain network: instead of using a single fixed activation function, we adaptively combine multiple activation functions (e.g.,
\tanh
and Gaussian-type) with interface-aware weighting. This design allows each network to efficiently capture sharp variations required to satisfy interface constraints near
\Gamma
, while maintaining smooth approximations in the bulk. Our approach offers: (i) unified treatment of elliptic and parabolic systems through space-time formulation; (ii) geometric flexibility for arbitrary interface configurations (static, moving, rotating, deforming) without remeshing; (iii) high-dimensional capability up to 10D; and (iv) conditional error estimates linking network parameters to solution accuracy.

Unlike XPINN, which was designed for general domain decomposition, our method explicitly handles discontinuous coefficients and flux jumps. Compared to M-PINN, I-PINN, and AdaI-PINN, which address interface problems through standard activation functions, architectural modifications, or adaptive sampling respectively, MAF introduces learnable, spatially-adaptive activation function blending within each subdomain network. Unlike DCSNN, which transforms to higher-dimensional spaces, our method works directly in the original space with interface-aware activation weighting. Importantly, while existing methods (MFM, DCSNN, M-PINN, I-PINN, AdaI-PINN) have not been demonstrated for parabolic interface problems with moving interfaces, our MAF method handles such problems effectively.

Our theoretical contributions include conditional error bounds adapted from the generalization error framework of Mishra and Molinaro
[
37
]
for interface problems with jump conditions. These bounds relate solution accuracy to trained loss values, quadrature errors, and sampling density, though practical performance depends on network architecture, optimization algorithms, and hyperparameters best validated through experiments.

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

. Throughout this paper, we denote the unit normal vector
\bm{n}
on
\Gamma
pointing from
\Omega_{1}
to
\Omega_{2}
. Although we assume that
\Gamma
is Lipschitz, our method can accommodate more general interfaces in practice.

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u\bigr)

\displaystyle\llbracket u\rrbracket

\displaystyle=g_{D},

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,

, and
\bm{n}
denotes the unit normal vector on
\Gamma
pointing outward from
\Omega_{1}

\beta(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}\beta_{1},&\text{if }\bm{x}\in\Omega_{1},\\

\left.\llbracket u(\bm{x})\rrbracket\right|_{\Gamma}=\left.u_{1}(\bm{x})\right|_{\Gamma}-\left.u_{2}(\bm{x})\right|_{\Gamma},

u(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}u_{1}(\bm{x}),&\text{if }\bm{x}\in\Omega_{1},\\

Problems of this type arise in a variety of applications, including composite materials, fluid dynamics with interfaces, and heat transfer in layered media. The jump conditions on
\Gamma
reflect discontinuities in the solution and/or its flux due to contrasting material properties. Our goal is to accurately capture these jumps without resorting to body-fitted meshes or complicated mesh refinement near
\Gamma
.

T>0
be a fixed final time, and let
\Omega_{1}(t)

\Omega_{2}(t)

\Gamma(t)

\displaystyle u_{t}-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u\bigr)

\displaystyle=f,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{1}(t)\cup\Omega_{2}(t),

(2)

\displaystyle\llbracket u\rrbracket

\displaystyle=g_{1},

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=g_{2},

\bm{n}

\Gamma

\Omega_{1}

\beta(\bm{x})\geq\beta_{0}>0

\beta(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}\beta_{1},&\text{if }\bm{x}\in\Omega_{1},\\
\beta_{2},&\text{if }\bm{x}\in\Omega_{2},\end{cases}

\left.\llbracket u(\bm{x},t)\rrbracket\right|_{\Gamma}=\left.u_{1}(\bm{x},t)\right|_{\Gamma}-\left.u_{2}(\bm{x},t)\right|_{\Gamma},

These parabolic problems model time-dependent processes, such as heat diffusion or mass transfer, where different regions of the domain possess distinct material properties. The additional jump conditions on
\Gamma
are analogous to those in the elliptic case, reflecting the discontinuities in the solution and flux across the interface. The initial condition

In the subsequent sections, we present a novel deep learning framework that employs multiple activation functions to address both the elliptic interface problem (
1
) and the parabolic interface problem (
2
). Our proposed methodology circumvents the traditional challenges associated with interface problems by introducing a specialized neural network architecture that naturally accommodates jump conditions. This approach eliminates the need for body-fitted meshes or complex geometric adaptations, offering a more flexible and computationally efficient solution strategy. The framework’s ability to handle both stationary and time-dependent interfaces, coupled with its capacity to capture sharp discontinuities in the solution and its derivatives, makes it particularly well-suited for a wide range of practical applications.

3
Multi-Activation Function Method

In this section, we describe our multi-activation function approach for solving interface problems using deep neural networks (DNNs). Our method adopts a domain decomposition strategy where two independent neural networks are employed, one for each subdomain
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. These networks share the same architecture but have independently trained parameters, and they are coupled through interface conditions in the loss function. Each network incorporates a multi-activation function mechanism to enhance learning efficiency, particularly for satisfying the interface constraints.

Throughout this section, we use the following notation conventions:
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
denotes the neural network function for subdomain
\Omega_{i}
as a mapping, while
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x})
denotes its evaluation at a point
\bm{x}\in\overline{\Omega}_{i}
. When discussing function properties (e.g., approximation, hypothesis space), we use
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
; when discussing pointwise values (e.g., loss function evaluations), we use
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x})
.

3.1
Deep Neural Network Architecture

We employ two neural networks
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}
to approximate the solution in
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively. Each network has
(L-1)
hidden layers with general activation functions. We treat the
d
-dimensional input as the 0-th layer and the one-dimensional output as the
L
-th layer. Let
m_{l}
be the number of neurons in the
l
-th layer. In particular, we set
m_{0}=d
and
m_{L}=1
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote
[k]:=\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
.

Parameterization.

For each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
(
i=1,2
), we collect all the parameters of the corresponding network into a vector

\bm{\theta}_{i}=\bigl(\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}_{i}^{[1]}),\,\operatorname{vec}(\bm{b}_{i}^{[1]}),\,\cdots,\,\operatorname{vec}(\bm{W}_{i}^{[L]}),\,\operatorname{vec}(\bm{b}_{i}^{[L]})\bigr),

where for each layer
l\in[L]
, we have
\bm{W}_{i}^{[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}\times m_{l-1}}
and
\bm{b}_{i}^{[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}
. The size
M
of each network is the total number of parameters:

M\;=\;\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\bigl(m_{l}+1\bigr)\,m_{l+1}.

(3)

We call these parameters
W_{i,jk}^{[l]}
(weights) and
b_{i,j}^{[l]}
(biases). Note that
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
are independently trainable, allowing each network to specialize in approximating the solution within its respective subdomain. The hypothesis space for each subdomain is
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\{u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}:\bm{\theta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
.

Activation Functions.

For each subdomain network
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
(
i=1,2
), we introduce activation functions of the form

\sigma_{j}^{[l]}:\mathbb{R}\;\to\;\mathbb{R},\quad\text{for }l\in[L-1],\;j\in[m_{l}].

We interpret the 0-th layer output as the identity map on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[0]}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}\quad\text{for all }\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then for
l\in[L-1]
, the layer output
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[l]}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}
is defined componentwise by

\displaystyle\bigl(u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[l]}(\bm{x})\bigr)_{j}\;=\;\sigma_{j}^{[l]}\Bigl(\bigl(\bm{W}_{i}^{[l]}\,u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[l-1]}(\bm{x})+\bm{b}_{i}^{[l]}\bigr)_{j}\Bigr),\quad j\in[m_{l}].

(4)

For the final layer
l=L
, we set

u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[L]}(\bm{x})\;=\;\bm{W}_{i}^{[L]}\,\bigl(u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[L-1]}(\bm{x})\bigr)\;+\;b_{i}^{[L]},

(5)

yielding a scalar output
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}):=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{[L]}(\bm{x})
for subdomain
\Omega_{i}
. Although both networks share the same architecture, their parameters
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
are trained independently, allowing each network to specialize in its respective subdomain. In many applications, the activation functions
\sigma_{j}^{[l]}
are chosen to be the same (e.g.,
\tanh
or Sigmoid) for simplicity. Note that for second-order PDEs, smooth activation functions such as
\tanh
are preferred over non-smooth ones like ReLU, since the PDE residual requires computing second-order derivatives of the network output.

3.2
Discrete Loss Function for the Elliptic Interface Problem

We now introduce the loss function used in our MAF formulation. Our goal is to approximate the interface problem (
1
) by enforcing the PDE and jump conditions in a collocation sense. Specifically, we sample points in the sub-domains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, on the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and on the interface
\Gamma
, then define a discrete loss that couples the two subdomain networks
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}
.

Interior Losses.

Let
\{\bm{x}_{k}^{\Omega_{i}}\}_{k=1}^{M_{i}}\subset\Omega_{i}
,
i=1,2
, be sets of collocation points in each sub-domain. For each subdomain, we approximate the PDE residual term using the corresponding network:

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\;:=\;\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}\Bigl|\,-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta_{i}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\bigr)(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Omega_{i}})\;-\;f(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Omega_{i}})\Bigr|^{2},

(6)

where
\beta_{i}
is the constant diffusion coefficient in
\Omega_{i}
. The derivatives here can be computed by automatic differentiation.

Boundary Loss.

To enforce the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
, we sample
M_{\partial\Omega_{i}}
points
\{\bm{x}_{k}^{\partial\Omega_{i}}\}_{k=1}^{M_{\partial\Omega_{i}}}\subset\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma
for each subdomain and define

\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\;:=\;\frac{1}{M_{\partial\Omega_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\partial\Omega_{i}}}\Bigl|\,u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\partial\Omega_{i}}\bigr)\;-\;g_{D}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\partial\Omega_{i}}\bigr)\Bigr|^{2}.

(7)

Interface Loss.

The interface loss is crucial as it couples the two independent networks by enforcing the jump conditions. We sample
M_{\Gamma}
points

\{\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\}_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\subset\Gamma

and define:

\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\;:=\;\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\Bigl(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{M_{\Gamma}}\,\bigl|u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)-g_{1}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)\bigr|^{2}

\;+\;\frac{\gamma_{2}}{M_{\Gamma}}\bigl|\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma}\bigr)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr).

Here,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
are weights to balance the importance of each jump condition. Note that
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}
are evaluated at the same interface points but represent the solution limits from each subdomain.

Total Loss.

Combining the above terms, the total loss for our elliptic interface problem couples both networks:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\;:=\;\mathcal{L}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{2}(\bm{\theta}_{2})\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1})\;+\;\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{2}).

(8)

We seek to find

\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}).

If
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
denotes the minimizer, then the corresponding neural network solutions are
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*}}
for
\Omega_{1}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}}
for
\Omega_{2}
.

Training Dynamics.

In practice, gradient-based methods are used to update both parameter sets
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
jointly according to

\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\;=\;-\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\bigr),\quad i=1,2,\\[6.0pt]
\bm{\theta}_{i}(0)\;=\;\bm{\theta}_{i,0},\end{cases}

(9)

during training. Variants of (stochastic) gradient descent (SGD), ADAM or second-order methods can be employed, and automatic differentiation helps compute the gradients efficiently. Note that the interface loss
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
introduces coupling between the two networks, ensuring consistency of the solution and flux across the interface.

3.3
Extension to Parabolic Interface Problems

For parabolic interface problems, we extend our approach by treating time as an additional dimension in the neural network architecture, effectively working in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The domain
\Omega
is divided into two subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
separated by an interface
\Gamma(t)
. This formulation allows us to handle various interface configurations:
This formulation allows us to handle static interfaces where
\Gamma(t)=\Gamma
remains fixed, moving interfaces where
\Gamma(t)
translates according to a prescribed velocity field, and rotating interfaces where
\Gamma(t)
undergoes rotational motion about a fixed axis.
Each subdomain network now accepts input
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The loss function for the parabolic case in each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
(
i=1,2
) includes an additional temporal derivative term:

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{p}(\bm{\theta}_{i}):=\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}\Bigl|\partial_{t}u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta_{i}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Omega_{i}},t_{k})\bigr)-f(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Omega_{i}},t_{k})\Bigr|^{2},

(10)

with the interface conditions coupling the two networks:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}^{p}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\Bigl(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{M_{\Gamma}}\bigl|u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)-g_{1}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)\bigr|^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{M_{\Gamma}}\bigl|\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}\bigl(\bm{x}_{k}^{\Gamma},t\bigr)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr),

where
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
,
M_{\Gamma}
are the numbers of sampling points for the interior domains and interface condition.

The initial condition loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{0}}\bigl|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k},0)-g_{0}(\bm{x}_{k})\bigr|^{2},\quad\text{where }\bm{x}_{k}\in\Omega_{i}(0),

(12)

where
M_{0}
is the number of sampling points for the initial condition.

For the Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega(t)
, we define:

\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\frac{1}{M_{\partial\Omega}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\partial\Omega}}\bigl|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k}^{\partial\Omega},t_{k})-g_{D}(\bm{x}_{k}^{\partial\Omega},t_{k})\bigr|^{2},\quad\text{where }\bm{x}_{k}\in\Omega_{i}(t_{k}).

(13)

The total loss function combines all these components:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\mathcal{L}_{1}^{p}(\bm{\theta}_{1})+\mathcal{L}_{2}^{p}(\bm{\theta}_{2})+\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}^{p}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\mathcal{L}_{0}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\mathcal{L}_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}).

(14)

3.4
Multi-Activation for Enhanced Learning Near Interfaces

Although our domain decomposition approach employs separate networks for each subdomain—where each network only needs to approximate a smooth solution within its domain—the
interface loss term

\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
imposes strong constraints that require both networks to rapidly adapt their behavior near the interface
\Gamma
. To facilitate efficient learning under these interface constraints, we propose incorporating
multi-activation functions
within each subdomain network.

While the true solution is smooth (e.g.,
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
) within each subdomain, the interface conditions create a challenging optimization landscape where networks must satisfy jump conditions exactly at the interface while maintaining smooth approximations in the bulk. Standard single-activation networks using a single smooth activation (e.g.,
\sigma^{[l]}=\tanh
) may require excessive parameters to achieve this dual objective efficiently.

We propose a multi-activation mechanism that allows each layer
l
to blend multiple candidate activations. Concretely, for each subdomain network, we define

\sigma^{[l]}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\omega_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\tanh\bigl(W_{1}^{[l]}\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{[l]}\bigr)\;+\;\omega_{2}(\mathbf{x})\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(W_{2}^{[l]}\,\mathbf{x})^{2}}{\gamma}\Bigr),

where
\gamma
is a positive constant,
W_{1}^{[l]}
and
W_{2}^{[l]}
are learnable weights, and
\omega_{1}(\mathbf{x})
,
\omega_{2}(\mathbf{x})
are interface-aware weighting functions satisfying

\omega_{1}(\mathbf{x})\to 0\text{ as }\mathbf{x}\to\Gamma,\quad\omega_{1}(\mathbf{x})\to 1\text{ as }\mathbf{x}\to\partial\Omega,

and

\omega_{2}(\mathbf{x})\to 1\text{ as }\mathbf{x}\to\Gamma,\quad\omega_{2}(\mathbf{x})\to 0\text{ as }\mathbf{x}\to\partial\Omega.

These weighting functions can be derived from a level set or distance function indicating proximity to
\Gamma
. This design allocates specialized activation behavior near the interface where satisfying the jump conditions is most critical.

The multi-activation design enhances each subdomain network’s ability to efficiently satisfy the interface constraints while maintaining accurate approximations in the bulk. By combining different activation functions and weighting them adaptively based on location, each network can dedicate appropriate representational capacity to regions where the interface loss imposes the strongest gradients during training.

The combination of
\tanh
and Gaussian-type activation functions provides several advantages for learning near interfaces:

•

The
\tanh
function is infinitely differentiable (
C^{\infty}
), ensuring that second-order derivatives required for evaluating the PDE residual are well-defined throughout the domain.

•

The Gaussian-type activation
\exp\!\bigl(-(W_{2}^{[l]}\,\mathbf{x})^{2}/\gamma\bigr)
introduces
localized sensitivity
near the interface, enabling efficient satisfaction of jump conditions without disrupting the solution approximation far from
\Gamma
.

•

The parameter
\gamma
provides an additional degree of freedom to control the spatial extent of interface-focused learning.

•

The bounded outputs of both activations contribute to numerical stability during training, particularly when the interface conditions involve flux matching with high-contrast coefficients.

•

The Gaussian-like decay characteristics complement the global smoothness of
\tanh
, creating a more expressive function space that can efficiently represent solutions satisfying both bulk PDE residuals and interface constraints.

In summary, while each subdomain network approximates a smooth solution, the multi-activation mechanism
improves training efficiency
by providing specialized representational capacity near the interface where the coupling constraints are enforced. This reduces the total number of parameters and training iterations needed to achieve a given accuracy compared to standard single-activation architectures.

In the subsequent section, we present a comprehensive error analysis of our multi-activation function method. We establish theoretical convergence rates and estimate a priori error in appropriate function spaces. The analysis considers both the neural network architecture’s approximation capabilities and the impact of the discrete loss function minimization. We derive bounds for the solution error regarding the network width, depth, and the number of training points, accounting for the presence of interface discontinuities and the effectiveness of our multi-activation strategy.

4
Error Estimation for Multi-Activation Function Method for Elliptic Interface Problem and Parabolic Interface Problem

In this section, we present an error analysis framework for the proposed multi-activation function method. Our analysis is based on the generalization error estimation framework for physics-informed neural networks developed by Mishra and Molinaro
[
37
]
, which we adapt to interface problems with jump conditions. We also draw upon related error analysis techniques from
[
14
,
51
]
.

Throughout this section, we use the following notation:

•

\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
denotes the function space for the solution in subdomain
\Omega_{i}
. For elliptic problems with classical solutions, we take
\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})=C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
, the space of twice continuously differentiable functions on
\overline{\Omega}_{i}
. More generally,
\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})=H^{s}(\Omega_{i})
for appropriate Sobolev regularity
s\geq 2
.

•

\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
denotes the corresponding space-time function space for parabolic problems.

•

\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
denotes the norm in the space
\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
.

Remark 4.1
(Scope and Limitations of the Error Analysis)

The error bounds presented in this section provide
upper bounds
on the generalization error, relating the solution error to the trained loss values and quadrature errors. These bounds are conditional on several assumptions:

•

The neural network has sufficient approximation capacity to represent the solution with small residuals;

•

The optimization algorithm successfully finds parameters that minimize the loss function;

•

The sampling points are distributed appropriately to approximate the relevant integrals.

The actual error in practice depends on factors not captured by these bounds, including the choice of network architecture, optimization algorithm, initialization, and hyperparameters. Therefore, while these theoretical results provide useful guidance, the numerical experiments in Section
5
serve as the primary validation of our method’s effectiveness.

4.1
The Least-Squares DNN Approach for Elliptic Interface Problems

It is natural to rewrite the elliptic interface problem (
1
) as a minimization problem in order to apply the DNN approach. There are different ways to do this, following similar approaches in the literature. In this work, we adopt the LS formulation which minimizes residuals by defining an LS functional that incorporates all equations introduced in (
1
) together. This approach has been widely used in physics-informed neural networks for various PDEs, including elliptic interface problems.

In particular, for (
1
), we define a least-squares formulation as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Omega_{i}}^{2}+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\partial\Omega_{i}/\Gamma}^{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\|\mathcal{I}_{1}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Gamma}^{2}+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\|\mathcal{I}_{2}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Gamma}^{2},

where
\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
,
\omega_{\mathcal{B}}
,
\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}
, and
\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}
are prescribed weight coefficients for each corresponding term. The operators are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u})-f,\quad\text{in }\Omega_{i},\quad i=1,2,

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}-g_{D},\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}/\Gamma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\llbracket\tilde{u}\rrbracket-g_{1},\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u}\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket-g_{2},\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

Then, the LS problem is to find
u\in\mathbb{V}
such that

\mathcal{R}(u)=\min_{\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{V}}\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u}),

(17)

where
\mathbb{V}
is a suitable space for the LS solution.

As shown in Section
3
, we employ two independent neural networks to approximate the exact solution
u
in (
1
):

u_{i}\approx u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega_{i},\quad i=1,2,

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
d
is the spatial dimension. Each network
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
has the same architecture but with independently trainable parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}
.

Based on the LS formulation and the DNN approximation, we seek to find the parameter pairs
(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
such that:

\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathcal{R}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and the total loss functional,
\mathcal{R}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
, according to the LS functional, is defined as:

\mathcal{R}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\mathcal{R}(u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x}),u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x})).

(18)

Since the loss function contains integrals that cannot be computed exactly, we approximate them by the Monte Carlo integration based on a set of sampling points. More precisely, we introduce the following mean square error (MSE) formulations to approximate corresponding integrals:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{\theta}_{i})|^{2},\quad i=1,2,

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{\theta}_{i})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}|\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}|\mathcal{I}_{2}(\bm{x}_{k};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})|^{2},

where
M_{i}
,
M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
, and
M_{\Gamma}
are the number of sampling points used for the interior part in each subdomain, the boundary part (
M_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+M_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}=M_{\partial\Omega}
), and the two interface conditions, respectively. If one of them equals 0, then the corresponding loss functional or the MSE is dropped. By slightly abusing the notation, we use
\bm{x}_{k}

(k=1,\cdots)
to represent sampling points and drop the dependence of
\bm{x}
on the discrete level.

Thus, the total discrete loss is defined as:

\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and our DNN method for (1) solves the following minimization problem:

\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})=\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\right].

To solve the elliptic interface problem using the proposed method, we formulate the following total LS formulation for
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})\cup H^{1}(\Omega_{2})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left|-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u}_{i})-f\right|^{2}\,d\bm{x}\right.

(20)

\displaystyle\left.+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}/\Gamma}\left|\tilde{u}_{i}-g_{D}\right|^{2}\,ds\right)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\int_{\Gamma}\left|\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{2}-g_{1}\right|^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\int_{\Gamma}\left|\beta_{1}\nabla\tilde{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}-\beta_{2}\nabla\tilde{u}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}-g_{2}\right|^{2}\,ds,

where
\tilde{u}_{i}=\tilde{u}|_{\Omega_{i}}
denotes the restriction of
\tilde{u}
to subdomain
\Omega_{i}
.
The DNN approximation of this problem defines
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x})
as an approximation of
u
in subdomain
\Omega_{i}
, leading to the discretized loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}\left|-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x}_{k})\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k}))-f(\bm{x}_{k})\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\left|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k})-g_{D}(\bm{x}_{k})\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\left|u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x}_{k})-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x}_{k})-g_{1}(\bm{x}_{k})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\left|\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x}_{k})\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x}_{k})\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}(\bm{x}_{k})\right|^{2}.

The optimization problem is then solved using the Adam optimizer, with gradients computed through automatic differentiation. The optimal parameter sets
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
give the approximate solutions
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*}}(\bm{x})
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}}(\bm{x})
for the elliptic interface problem in
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively.

4.2
Error Estimation for the Elliptic Interface Problem

4.2.1
Abstract Framework for Error Analysis

Following the abstract framework developed by Mishra and Molinaro
[
37
]
for PINNs error analysis, we establish two major assumptions necessary for the error analysis of our two-network domain decomposition method applied to the elliptic interface problem (
1
). The key insight from
[
37
]
is to formulate stability-type conditions that relate the solution error to the PDE residuals. In our setting, we have two independent networks
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}
approximating the solution in
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
respectively, coupled through interface conditions.

Assumption 4.1

We assume that the elliptic interface problem (
1
) satisfies a
conditional stability estimate
: for the exact solution
u_{i}
in subdomain
\Omega_{i}
and any sufficiently regular approximation
v_{i}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
(in our case,
v_{i}=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
from network
i
),
i=1,2
, there exist exponents
0<\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}},\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}},\beta_{\Gamma}\leq 1
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-v_{i}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{pde}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\left(\|-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u_{i})-(-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla v_{i}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}\right.\right.

(22)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|u_{i}-v_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right]+\left(\|\llbracket u\rrbracket-\llbracket v\rrbracket\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket-\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla v\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right\},

where the constant
C_{pde}
depends on
\|u_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
,
\|v_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
, the coefficient
\beta(\bm{x})
, the domain geometry, and the regularity of the underlying elliptic interface problem.

This assumption encapsulates a stability property of the PDE: small residuals in the PDE, boundary conditions, and interface conditions imply small errors in the solution. The exponents
\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}},\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}},\beta_{\Gamma}
depend on the specific problem and may be less than 1 for ill-posed or weakly stable problems. For typical well-posed elliptic interface problems with smooth data, such stability estimates hold with
\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}=\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\beta_{\Gamma}=1
; see, e.g.,
[
21
,
8
]
for classical regularity theory of elliptic equations with discontinuous coefficients. Note that the constant
C_{pde}
depends on the coefficient contrast ratio
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}
; for high-contrast problems (e.g.,
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}\gg 1
or
\ll 1
), this constant may become large, which should be considered when interpreting the error bounds.

Assumption 4.2

Consider an integral
I(g):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}g(\bm{x})\,d\bm{x}
and a quadrature rule using
N
quadrature points
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}

(1\leq i\leq N)
, i.e.,
I_{N}(g):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}g(\bm{x}_{i})
,
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
1\leq i\leq N
. We assume the quadrature error is

|I(g)-I_{N}(g)|\leq C_{quad}N^{-\alpha},\quad\alpha>0,

(23)

where the constant
C_{quad}
depends on the dimension of the domain and the property of the integrand
g(\bm{x})
.

Based on the above two assumptions, we have the following framework of the error estimation for the proposed two-network domain decomposition method applied to the elliptic interface problem. The key feature is that the error bound explicitly accounts for the separate approximations
u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}
and
u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}
in each subdomain and their coupling through the interface loss terms.

Theorem 4.1

Let
u_{i}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
,
i=1,2
, be the classical solution of the elliptic interface problem in subdomain
\Omega_{i}
. Let
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
be the numerical approximation obtained by the neural network with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}
for subdomain
\Omega_{i}
. Under Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
, we have the following generalization error estimation:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\text{pde}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right]\right.

(24)

\displaystyle\left.+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{{\mathcal{I}_{2}}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\beta_{\Gamma}}\right\},

where
C_{\text{pde}}
is a positive constant depending on
\|u_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
,
\|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
,
\beta(\bm{x})
, and the domains
\Omega_{i}

(i=1,2)
. Note that the interior and boundary loss terms
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
depend only on the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}
of network
i
, while the interface loss terms
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}
depend on both parameter sets
(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
, reflecting the coupling between the two networks.

Proof 4.1

Let
e^{u}_{i}:=u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
be the error in subdomain
\Omega_{i}
, where
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
is the approximation from network
i
with optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}
. By a direct calculation, we have, for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla e^{u}_{i}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{i},

(25)

\displaystyle e^{u}_{1}-e^{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\nabla e^{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla e^{u}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

\displaystyle e^{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma,

where
\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}:=\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta_{i}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\bigr)+f
is the PDE residual for network
i
,
\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}:=u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{*}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}^{*}-g_{1}
is the solution jump residual (coupling networks 1 and 2),
\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}:=\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{*}\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}^{*}\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}
is the flux jump residual (also coupling both networks), and
\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}:=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}-g_{D}
is the boundary residual for network
i
.

Using Assumption
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{pde}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right]\right.

(26)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right\}.

To further estimate the terms on the right-hand side, we use Assumption
4.2
. For the interior residual of network
i
, we have

\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}.

(27)

Similarly, for the boundary residual (depending on
\bm{\theta}_{i}
) and the interface residuals (depending on both
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
):

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}},

(28)

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\beta_{\Gamma}},

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{2}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\beta_{\Gamma}}.

Substituting these estimates into the previous inequality completes the proof. The key observation is that the interface loss terms couple both networks through the parameters
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
, while the interior and boundary terms depend only on the respective subdomain network.

4.2.2
Application to the Elliptic Interface Problem

Following the abstract error analysis framework established in the previous section, we now apply our theoretical results to the specific elliptic interface problem (
1
). Let
e_{i}^{u}:=u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}}^{i,*}
,
i=1,2
, be the errors in each subdomain.

The following theorem provides an error bound under the stability assumption. We note that similar results have been established in
[
37
]
for standard PINNs; here we adapt the framework to interface problems.

Theorem 4.2

Let
u_{i}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
,
i=1,2
, be the classical solution in each subdomain of the elliptic interface problem (
1
). Let
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
be the numerical approximations obtained by the DNN method. Under Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
, with the stability exponents
\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}=\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}=\beta_{\Gamma}=1
(well-posed case), the following error bound holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|e_{i}^{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}\leq C_{pde}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*})+\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})+\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})\right.

(29)

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{2}C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{2}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}}\right],

where
C_{pde}
is the stability constant from Assumption
4.1
, depending on
\|u_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
,
\|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}
, the coefficient
\beta(\bm{x})
, and the domain geometry.

Proof 4.2

The proof follows directly from applying Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
. By Assumption
4.1
, the solution error is bounded by the residuals:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|e_{i}^{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{pde}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|\mathcal{R}_{i}^{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|\mathcal{R}_{i}^{g_{D}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}\setminus\Gamma)}\right.

(30)

\displaystyle\left.+\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right\},

where
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{f}
,
\mathcal{R}_{i}^{g_{D}}
,
\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}
, and
\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}
are the PDE, boundary, and interface residuals of the neural network approximation.

By Assumption
4.2
, the
L^{2}
norms of the residuals can be related to the discrete loss functions plus quadrature errors. For example,

\|\mathcal{R}_{i}^{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*})+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}.

Applying similar bounds to all residual terms and combining yields the stated result.

Remark 4.2
(Interpretation and Limitations)

The error bound in Theorem
4.2
should be interpreted carefully. The theorem guarantees that
if
a small-loss parameter pair
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
is found,
then
the generalization error is small, provided the stability constant
C_{pde}
is not too large. Importantly,
it does not guarantee that the optimization process will find such a parameter pair
. For interface problems with high-contrast coefficients (e.g.,
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}\gg 1
or
\ll 1
), the constant
C_{pde}
may be large, potentially degrading the error bound. The theorem assumes that
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
is a (local) minimizer of the joint loss function
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
; in practice, optimization algorithms may not find the global minimum, introducing an additional optimization error not captured by this bound. Note that since the two networks are coupled through the interface loss terms, the optimization must jointly minimize over both parameter sets. The bound also assumes both neural networks can achieve small residuals in their respective subdomains, requiring sufficient network width, depth, and appropriate activation functions—conditions that the multi-activation strategy in Section
3.4
is designed to facilitate.

Remark 4.3
(Role of Multi-Activation Functions)

The error bound in Theorem
4.2
requires activation functions that are sufficiently smooth to allow evaluation of second-order derivatives in the PDE residual. The
\tanh
and Gaussian-type activations used in our multi-activation strategy (Section
3.4
) satisfy this requirement, being
C^{\infty}
functions. In contrast, non-smooth activations such as ReLU are not suitable for second-order PDEs since their second derivatives are not well-defined (ReLU has a discontinuous first derivative at zero). The multi-activation strategy contributes to achieving smaller loss values
\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
in practice by providing specialized activation behavior near the interface, where satisfying the jump conditions is most challenging. This practical benefit is demonstrated in the numerical experiments of Section
5
.

4.3
The Least-Squares DNN Approach for Parabolic Interface Problems

We extend the least-squares (LS) formulation approach to the time-dependent parabolic interface problem (
2
). This extension introduces additional complexity due to the temporal dimension and the potentially moving interface
\Gamma(t)
.

Following the approach presented for elliptic problems, we rewrite the parabolic interface problem as a minimization problem suitable for the DNN approach. We define an LS functional that incorporates all equations from (
2
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Omega_{i}(t)}^{2}+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\partial\Omega_{i}(t)/\Gamma(t)}^{2}+\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\|\mathcal{T}_{i}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Omega_{i}(0)}^{2}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\|\mathcal{I}_{1}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Gamma(t)}^{2}+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\|\mathcal{I}_{2}(\tilde{u})\|_{0,\Gamma(t)}^{2},

where
\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}
,
\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
,
\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}
,
\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}
, and
\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
are prescribed weight coefficients for each corresponding term. The operators are defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}_{t}-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u})-f,\quad\text{in }\Omega_{i}(t),\quad i=1,2,

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}-g_{D},\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}(t)/\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\llbracket\tilde{u}\rrbracket-g_{1},\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u}\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket-g_{2},\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(\bm{x},0)-g_{0},\quad\text{on }\Omega_{i}(0).

The LS problem is to find
u\in\mathbb{V}
such that

\mathcal{R}(u)=\min_{\tilde{u}\in\mathbb{V}}\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u}),

(33)

where
\mathbb{V}
is a suitable space to which the LS solution belongs, considering both spatial and temporal regularity requirements.

To approximate the exact solution
u
in (
2
), we employ two independent neural networks:

u_{i}\approx u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x},t),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega_{i}(t),\quad i=1,2,

where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in[0,T]
, and
d
is the spatial dimension. Each network
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
has the same architecture but with independently trainable parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}
.

Based on the LS formulation and the DNN approximation, our parabolic DNN method is defined as finding
(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
such that:

\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathcal{R}(\bm{x},t;\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and the total loss functional is defined as:

\mathcal{R}(\bm{x},t;\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\mathcal{R}(u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x},t),u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x},t)).

(34)

Since the loss function contains space-time integrals that cannot be computed exactly, we approximate them using Monte Carlo integration based on sampling points. We introduce the following mean square error (MSE) formulations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}|\mathcal{L}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k};\bm{\theta}_{i})|^{2},\quad i=1,2,

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}|\mathcal{B}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k};\bm{\theta}_{i})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}|\mathcal{I}_{1}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}|\mathcal{I}_{2}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k};\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}|\mathcal{T}_{i}(\bm{x}_{k},0;\bm{\theta}_{i})|^{2},

where
M_{i}
,
M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
,
M_{\Gamma}
, and
M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}
are the number of sampling points used for the interior part in each subdomain, the boundary part (
M_{\mathcal{B}_{1}}+M_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}=M_{\partial\Omega}
), the interface conditions, and the initial condition(
M_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}+M_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}=M_{0}
), respectively.

Thus, the total discrete loss is defined as:

\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}):=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}),

and our DNN method for (
2
) solves the following minimization problem:

\min_{(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}}\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}).

To provide a more explicit representation, we express the total LS formulation for
\tilde{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1}(t))\cup H^{1}(\Omega_{2}(t))
in the space-time domain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\tilde{u}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega_{i}(t)}\left|\tilde{u}_{it}-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla\tilde{u}_{i})-f\right|^{2}\,d\bm{x}dt\right.

(36)

\displaystyle\left.+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}(t)/\Gamma(t)}\left|\tilde{u}_{i}-g_{D}\right|^{2}\,dsdt\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}(0)}\left|\tilde{u}_{i}(\bm{x},0)-g_{0}\right|^{2}\,d\bm{x}\right)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}\left|\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{2}-g_{1}\right|^{2}\,dsdt

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}\left|\beta_{1}\nabla\tilde{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}-\beta_{2}\nabla\tilde{u}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}-g_{2}\right|^{2}\,dsdt,

where
\tilde{u}_{i}=\tilde{u}|_{\Omega_{i}(t)}
denotes the restriction of
\tilde{u}
to subdomain
\Omega_{i}(t)
at time
t
.

The DNN approximation of this problem leads to the discretized loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})+\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\right)

(37)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\omega_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\frac{1}{M_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{i}}\left|\frac{\partial u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}}{\partial t}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})-\nabla\cdot(\beta_{i}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k}))-f(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\right|^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\omega_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\left|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})-g_{D}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\right|^{2}+\omega_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\frac{1}{M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\left|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{x}_{k},0)-g_{0}(\bm{x}_{k})\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\left|u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})-g_{1}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\omega_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{1}{M_{\Gamma}}\sum_{k=1}^{M_{\Gamma}}\left|\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}(\bm{x}_{k},t_{k})\right|^{2}.

4.4
Error Estimation for the Parabolic Interface Problem

4.4.1
Abstract Framework for Error Analysis

Following the abstract framework presented in Section
4.1
, we establish two major assumptions necessary for the error analysis of the DNN method applied to the parabolic interface problem (
2
).

Assumption 4.3

For the exact solution
u_{i}
in subdomain
\Omega_{i}(t)
and any sufficiently regular approximation
v_{i}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
(in our case,
v_{i}=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
from network
i
),
i=1,2
, the differential operators in the parabolic interface model problem satisfy, for
0<\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}},\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}},\beta_{\Gamma},\beta_{\mathcal{T}}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-v_{i}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{pde}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\left(\|u_{i,t}-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u_{i})-(v_{i,t}-\nabla\cdot(\beta(\bm{x})\nabla v_{i}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}\right.\right.

(38)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|u_{i}-v_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right]+\left(\|\llbracket u\rrbracket-\llbracket v\rrbracket\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla u\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket-\llbracket\beta(\bm{x})\nabla v\cdot\bm{n}\rrbracket\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\|u_{i0}-v_{i0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\right\},

where the constant
C_{pde}
depends on
\|u_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}
as well as the regularity property of the underlying parabolic interface model problem.

Assumption 4.4

Consider an integral
I(g):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}g(\bm{x},t)\,d\bm{x}dt
and a quadrature rule using
N
quadrature points
(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})\in\mathcal{D}

(1\leq i\leq N)
, i.e.,
I_{N}(g):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}g(\bm{x}_{i},t_{i})
,
w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
1\leq i\leq N
. We assume the quadrature error is

|I(g)-I_{N}(g)|\leq C_{quad}N^{-\alpha},\quad\alpha>0,

(39)

where the constant
C_{quad}
depends on the dimension of the domain and the property of the integrand
g(\bm{x},t)
.

Based on the above two assumptions, we have the following error estimation framework for the proposed two-network domain decomposition method applied to the parabolic interface problem.

Theorem 4.3

Let
u_{i}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
,
i=1,2
, be the classical solution of the parabolic interface problem in subdomain
\Omega_{i}(t)
. Let
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\in\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
be the numerical approximation obtained by the neural network with parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}
for subdomain
\Omega_{i}(t)
. Under Assumptions
4.3
and
4.4
, we have the following generalization error estimation:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\text{pde}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\right]\right.

(40)

\displaystyle\left.+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{{\mathcal{I}_{2}}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\beta_{\Gamma}}\right\},

where
C_{\text{pde}}
is a positive constant depending on
\|u_{i}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}
,
\|u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}
,
\beta(\bm{x})
, and the domains
\Omega_{i}(t)

(i=1,2)
. Note that the interior, boundary, and initial loss terms depend only on the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}
of network
i
, while the interface loss terms depend on both parameter sets
(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
.

Proof 4.3

Let
e^{u}_{i}:=u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
be the error in subdomain
\Omega_{i}(t)
, where
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
is the approximation from network
i
with optimal parameters
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}
. By a direct calculation, we have, for
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle e^{u}_{i,t}-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta_{i}\nabla e^{u}_{i}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{i}(t),

(41)

\displaystyle e^{u}_{1}-e^{u}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\nabla e^{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla e^{u}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\quad\text{on }\Gamma(t),

\displaystyle e^{u}_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{0}}_{i}\quad\text{on }\Omega_{i}(0),

\displaystyle e^{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t),

where
\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}:=\partial_{t}u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\beta_{i}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\bigr)-f
is the PDE residual for network
i
,
\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}:=u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{*}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}^{*}-g_{1}
is the solution jump residual (coupling both networks),
\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}:=\beta_{1}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{1}}^{*}\cdot\bm{n}-\beta_{2}\nabla u_{\bm{\theta}_{2}}^{*}\cdot\bm{n}-g_{2}
is the flux jump residual (also coupling both networks),
\mathcal{R}^{g_{0}}_{i}:=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}(\cdot,0)-g_{0}
is the initial condition residual for network
i
, and
\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}:=u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}-g_{D}
is the boundary residual for network
i
.

Using Assumption
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])}

\displaystyle\leq C_{pde}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}\right]\right.

(42)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{0}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\right\}.

To further estimate the terms on the right-hand side, we use Assumption
4.4
. For the interior residual of network
i
, we have

\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{f}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}M_{i}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{L}_{i}}}.

(43)

Similarly, for the boundary and initial condition residuals (depending on
\bm{\theta}_{i}
) and the interface residuals (depending on both
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
):

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{D}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega_{i}(t)\setminus\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{B}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}},

(44)

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{1}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{1}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}\beta_{\Gamma}},

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{2}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)^{\beta_{\Gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*}))^{\beta_{\Gamma}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{I}_{2}}M_{\Gamma}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}\beta_{\Gamma}},

\displaystyle\left(\|\mathcal{R}^{g_{0}}_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i}(0))}\right)^{\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{*}))^{\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}+C_{\text{quad}}^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{T}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\beta_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}.

Substituting these estimates into the previous inequality completes the proof. As in the elliptic case, the interface loss terms couple both networks through the parameters
(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{2}^{*})
.

4.4.2
Application to the parabolic interface problem

Let
e^{u}_{i}:=u_{i}-u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
,
i=1,2
, be the errors of the parabolic interface problem (
2
). Following the abstract analysis presented in subsection
4.3
and adapting the framework from
[
37
]
, we state the following conditional error bound.

Theorem 4.4

Let
u_{i}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
,
i=1,2
, be the classical solution of the presented parabolic interface problem; correspondingly, let
u_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}^{*}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i}(t)\times[0,T])
be the numerical approximations obtained by the DNN method. Under Assumptions
4.3
and
4.4
, we have the following conditional error bound:

\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}|e_{i}^{u}(\bm{x}_{i},t)|^{2}d\bm{x}dt

(45)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}T(1+C_{2}Te^{C_{2}T})\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(\bm{\Theta}^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{2}C_{quad}^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}M_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}^{-\alpha_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}}\right]

(46)

The error bounds presented in this section provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the proposed method. The bounds are conditional on stability assumptions (Assumptions
4.1
and
4.3

The bounds suggest that achieving small training loss values is necessary (though not sufficient) for obtaining accurate solutions, justifying loss monitoring during training as a practical convergence indicator. The sampling density affects the error through quadrature error terms, which decrease as the number of collocation points increases.

The bounds do not explicitly account for the approximation capacity of specific neural network architectures, the convergence behavior of optimization algorithms, hyperparameter choices (learning rate, batch size), or the effect of different activation function choices. Given these limitations, the numerical experiments in the following section serve as the primary validation of the method’s effectiveness across various interface configurations and dimensions.

5.2
Parabolic Interface Problem

We present numerical studies of parabolic interface problems with both fixed and moving interfaces. While existing methods (MFM, DCSNN, M-PINN, I-PINN, AdaI-PINN) have shown success for elliptic interface problems, they have not been demonstrated for parabolic equations with moving interfaces. Therefore, we compare our MAF method exclusively with XPINN for these challenging scenarios.

We consider model problem (
2
) with domain
\Omega=[0,3.5]\times[0,3.5]
,
t\in[0,1]
and four distinct interface configurations. In all cases, the exact solution is
u=0.01e^{t}e^{x}e^{y}
in
\Omega_{1}(t)
and
u=e^{t}\sin(x)\sin(y)
in
\Omega_{2}(t)
, all reletive boundary conditions, interface conditions, initial conditions, source terms can be calculated according to the exact solutions. We treat time as an additional dimension, solving the problem in a
(2+1)
-dimensional space-time continuum
[0,3.5]\times[0,3.5]\times[0,1]
. The time interval is partitioned into 10 sub-intervals (
\Delta t=0.1
). Our sampling strategy uses random collocation points (200, 400, 1000) throughout the space-time domain, with structured sampling (20, 40, 100 points) at temporal interfaces for boundary and interface conditions.

Conclusion

We have developed a domain decomposition-based deep neural network framework with multi-activation functions (MAF) for solving elliptic and parabolic interface problems with discontinuous coefficients. Our method employs two independent neural networks, one for each subdomain, coupled through interface conditions in the loss function. Within each network, a multi-activation mechanism with interface-aware weighting enhances training efficiency near interfaces where coupling constraints are most demanding. This approach naturally accommodates solution and flux jumps across material interfaces without body-fitted meshes or explicit interface tracking. We presented conditional error bounds adapted from the generalization error framework of Mishra and Molinaro
[
37
]
, relating solution accuracy to trained loss values and quadrature errors. Extensive numerical experiments validate the method’s effectiveness: for elliptic problems in 2D–10D with various interface geometries (straight lines, ellipses, sunflower shapes, flowers, and hyperspheres), MAF achieves competitive or superior accuracy compared to XPINN, MFM, DCSNN, M-PINN, I-PINN, and AdaI-PINN; for parabolic problems with static and moving interfaces—where existing methods (MFM, DCSNN, M-PINN, I-PINN, AdaI-PINN) have not been demonstrated—MAF outperforms XPINN by 1–2 orders of magnitude across fixed, translating, deforming, and star-shaped interface configurations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The theoretical error bounds are conditional on stability assumptions that may not hold uniformly for high-contrast coefficient problems. Practical performance depends significantly on hyperparameter choices, network architecture, and optimization strategies requiring problem-specific tuning. Future work could explore advanced training strategies (adaptive sampling, curriculum learning) for higher-dimensional problems, extend the framework to nonlinear PDEs and coupled multiphysics problems, investigate multi-activation architectures from a theoretical perspective, and incorporate probabilistic approaches for uncertainty quantification.
