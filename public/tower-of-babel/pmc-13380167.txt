Time-Updated Hematuria and Kidney Disease Progression in IgAN
Authors: Chen Tang, Feng-Lei Si, Pei Chen, Wan-Yin Hou, Hong-Yu Yang, Ji-Cheng Lv, Su-Fang Shi, Xu-Jie Zhou, Li-Jun Liu, Hong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHematuria is a hallmark feature of IgA nephropathy (IgAN); however, its long-term impact on kidney outcomes remains incompletely characterized because of time-dependent confounding and lack of a standardized cutoff. This study evaluated the longitudinal association between hematuria and kidney disease progression.MethodsWe included 1771 participants with biopsy-proven IgAN. Hematuria was categorized into the following 4 groups: < 10, 10 to < 20, 20 to < 100, and ≥ 100 red blood cells (RBC)/μl. The primary outcome was a composite kidney outcome (40% estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] decline or kidney failure). Conventional Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess baseline hematuria, whereas marginal structural models (MSMs) were employed to evaluate time-updated hematuria, accounting for time-dependent confounders and fluctuations in clinical parameters.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 48 months, the primary outcome occurred in 487 (27.5%) participants. In the fully adjusted baseline Cox model, baseline hematuria was not significantly associated with the primary outcome. In contrast, MSMs revealed that time-updated hematuria ≥ 100 RBC/μl was significantly associated with an increased risk of progression (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.53; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.14–2.06). The adverse impact was particularly pronounced in subgroups with a baseline eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and those with baseline proteinuria ≥ 0.5 g/d. Even moderate time-updated hematuria (20 to < 100 RBC/μl) was correlated with a substantially elevated risk (HR: 1.89; 95% CI: 1.33–2.72) in the subgroup with eGFR <60 ml/min per 1.73 m2.ConclusionOur findings suggest that persistent hematuria ≥ 100 RBC/μl may serve as a valuable risk stratification standard, particularly given the significantly magnified risk in patients with pre-existing renal impairment or uncontrolled proteinuria.Keywords: hematuria, IgA nephropathy, kidney disease progression, marginal structural models

As a widespread form of primary glomerulonephritis around the world, IgA nephropathy remains a significant contributor to the development of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).1 Converging evidence from the UK National Registry of Rare Kidney Diseases and China indicates that despite current treatment strategies, patients with IgAN continue to experience poor renal outcomes, often progressing to ESKD over a few decades.2,3 As a result, early identification and prompt intervention targeting modifiable prognostic factors, such as proteinuria and blood pressure, are critical to halt disease progression in IgAN.4

Hematuria, whether macroscopic or asymptomatic microscopic, is widely acknowledged as a typical clinical manifestation of IgAN, occurring in 70% to 100% of cases, especially among children and young adults during the early stages of the disease.5 Previous studies have demonstrated that both the severity and persistence of hematuria are independent predictors of poor renal outcomes in IgAN,6, 7, 8, 9, 10 with remission status highlighting therapeutic benefits and better renal survival.11, 12, 13 Despite its prognostic significance, hematuria remains underused in clinical practice, as evidenced by exclusion from contemporary multicenter trials and inadequate incorporation in guidelines, including the recent Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes 2025 update.14 This is partly because of the absence of standardization and daily variability in hematuria.15 More importantly, throughout the longitudinal follow-up, time-dependent confounders could lead to biased estimates, since hematuria tends to increase as kidney function deteriorates, and, in turn, elevated levels of hematuria may influence future kidney function.16

To fill these essential knowledge gaps, causal inference analysis methods were used to conduct an in-depth analysis of the association between hematuria and kidney outcomes in IgAN,17 based on longitudinal hematuria measurements from an observational cohort.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at Peking University First Hospital, including adult patients with biopsy-proven IgAN enrolled between January 2003 and June 2024. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) secondary forms of IgAN (e.g., because of systemic lupus erythematosus or IgA vasculitis); (ii) advanced renal insufficiency at baseline, defined as an eGFR < 20 ml/min per 1.73 m2; (iii) missing baseline histopathological data; or (iv) insufficient longitudinal follow-up data (e.g., only a baseline visit or missing key follow-up measurements required for time-varying analysis).

Following these criteria, a total of 1771 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1). The date of the first visit to our center was defined as the baseline. From this baseline, patients were observed until the first occurrence of a primary outcome event, death, or their last recorded follow-up visit.

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital.

Exposure

The primary exposure was hematuria, analyzed both at baseline and as a time-varying variable. Data on hematuria status were collected at baseline and subsequently at 6-month intervals throughout the follow-up period. Measurements were performed using successive generations of the Sysmex UF series automated analyzers (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), transitioning from earlier models (e.g., UF-1000i) to the current third-generation UF-5000. All models used fluorescence flow cytometry to ensure objective RBC quantification,18 with standardized calibration and quality control protocols maintained throughout the study period to ensure data consistency across equipment upgrades.

Across all analyses, hematuria was stratified into the following 4 hierarchical categories: (i) Minimal or absent: < 10 RBC/μl; (ii) Mild: 10 to < 20 RBC/μl; (iii) Moderate: 20 to < 100 RBC/μl; and (iv) Severe: ≥ 100 RBC/μl.15 For clinical reference, an automated count of 20 RBC/μl is approximately equivalent to 5 RBC per high power field.19

Outcome

The primary outcome was a composite kidney disease progression event of IgAN, defined as a ≥ 40% decline in eGFR from baseline or the onset of ESKD (eGFR < 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2, dialysis initiation, or kidney transplantation). Participants were censored at their last known follow-up in the event of death or if they were lost to follow-up for more than 1 year. Death occurring before the primary outcome was handled as a competing risk.

Assessment of Covariates

We collected data on a comprehensive set of covariates. Baseline covariates included age, sex, body mass index, albumin, proteinuria, eGFR, mean arterial pressure (MAP), diabetes history, and MEST-C scores (Oxford classification of IgAN). The following variables were treated as time-dependent and were updated at 6-month intervals: albumin, proteinuria, eGFR, MAP, and use of renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, other antihypertensive medications (α-blocker, β-blocker, calcium channel blocker, diuretic), hydroxychloroquine, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, corticosteroids, and other immunosuppressants. All treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating physicians and were guided by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines and individual patient characteristics. If multiple measurements were available within a 6-month interval, the one closest to the target time point was used.

The eGFR was estimated using serum creatinine based on the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula.20 All laboratory tests were conducted following standard protocols in the Clinical Laboratory Department at Peking University First Hospital.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized across the different hematuria categories. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and compared using 1-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test.

We calculated the incidence rates of the primary outcome by dividing the number of events by the total person-years of follow-up, expressed as events per 1000 person-years. Given the competing risk of death, cumulative incidence functions were used to estimate the probability of the composite kidney outcome, and Gray’s test was used to compare differences across groups.21

Main Analyses

To assess the association between hematuria and kidney disease progression, we first used standard Cox proportional hazards models with baseline hematuria and adjustment for baseline covariates, including age, sex, body mass index, albumin, proteinuria, eGFR, MAP, diabetes, and MEST-C scores.

To further account for time-varying confounding, we used MSMs to estimate the causal effect of time-updated hematuria on the primary outcome. MSMs are designed for longitudinal observational studies in which exposures, covariates, and treatments change over time, particularly when time-varying covariates may both predict subsequent exposure and be influenced by previous exposure or treatment.17,22 Figure 1 depicts the hypothesized causal relationships among the time-updated hematuria, time-dependent confounders and the kidney outcome.

Time-dependent confounders included albumin, proteinuria, eGFR, systolic BP, diastolic BP, the usage of renin angiotensin system inhibitors, other antihypertensive medications (α-blocker, β-blocker, calcium channel blocker, diuretic), hydroxychloroquine, SGLT2 inhibitors, corticosteroids, and other immunosuppressants. BP, blood pressure; eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate; SGLT2, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2.

The implementation of MSMs followed our previously described protocols23 and is detailed in the Supplementary Methods. Briefly, we constructed stabilized inverse probability of exposure weights and inverse probability of censoring weights for each patient at every 6-month follow-up interval to create a pseudo-population where the exposure is independent of measured confounders. Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to prevent potential instability. Results were presented as HRs and 95% CIs.

Subgroup Analyses

To evaluate potential effect modifiers, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age (< 40 vs. ≥ 40 years), baseline proteinuria (< 0.5 vs. ≥ 0.5 g/day), and baseline kidney function (eGFR < 60 vs. ≥ 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2), including tests for interaction.

Handling of Missing Data

Missing baseline data were imputed using chained equations incorporating all baseline covariates; for consecutive missing values in longitudinal follow-up variables, the last observation carried forward method was applied.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Further information on the statistical analysis is available in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 1771 participants, categorized into 4 groups according to baseline hematuria levels, are summarized in Table 1. Among the total cohort, the median age was 36 (IQR: 29–45) years, and 49.9% were male. The median initial eGFR was 74.2 (IQR: 49.8–99.0 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and the median proteinuria level was 1.28 (IQR: 0.66–2.34 g/d). Across the 4 hematuria categories, participants with higher baseline hematuria levels tended to be younger and exhibited better-preserved renal function (higher eGFR). Conversely, these patients also presented with higher baseline proteinuria levels and a higher prevalence of crescents (C1). Regarding therapy, patients with more severe hematuria were more frequently treated with corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants compared with those in the lower hematuria groups.

Adverse Kidney Event according to Baseline Hematuria Categories

During a total of 8932 person-years of follow-up (median 48 months [IQR: 30–78]), the primary composite kidney outcome was observed in 487 participants (27.5%). Compared with participants with baseline hematuria < 10 RBC/μl, the incidence rates of the composite kidney outcome were comparable across higher hematuria categories (Table 2). Concomitantly, 2 participants died before experiencing the primary kidney outcome and were censored at the time of death. The estimated cumulative incidence of composite kidney outcome was similar across baseline hematuria categories, with no statistically significant differences observed (P = 0.8) (Supplementary Table S2).

Hematuria and the Risk of Kidney Disease Progression

First, we assessed the association between baseline hematuria levels and the risk of the primary outcome using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for baseline covariates. In the fully adjusted model, no graded association was observed between baseline hematuria and the outcome. Compared with the reference group (hematuria < 10 RBC/μl), the HRs (95% CI) for the hematuria categories of 10 to < 20, 20 to < 100 RBC/μl and ≥ 100 RBC/μl were 1.14 (0.73–1.79), 1.09 (0.74–1.59), and 1.11 (0.76–1.61), respectively.

The longitudinal distribution of hematuria categories over the first 5 years of follow-up is shown in Figure 2. We then evaluated the association between time-updated hematuria and the composite kidney outcome using MSMs, accounting for time-dependent confounders and hematuria fluctuations. Notably, a significantly increased risk was observed at time-updated hematuria levels of ≥ 100 RBC/μl (HR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.14–2.06). Conversely, the category 20 to < 100 RBC/μl showed no significant risk increase versus the reference group (HR: 1.19; 95% CI: 0.93–1.53) (Table 3).

Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses using MSMs revealed that the associations between time-updated hematuria and the primary outcome were significantly modified by baseline eGFR and proteinuria levels (both P for interaction < 0.001; Figure 3). Specifically, the risk associated with elevated time-updated hematuria was particularly pronounced in participants with a baseline eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or proteinuria ≥ 0.5 g/d. Among participants with proteinuria ≥ 0.5 g/day, time-updated hematuria ≥ 100 RBC/μl was associated with a significantly increased risk (HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.22–2.27), whereas no such association was observed in those with baseline proteinuria < 0.5 g/d. Similarly, in the subgroup with eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2, even moderate time-updated hematuria (20 to < 100 RBC/μl) was correlated with a substantially elevated risk (HR: 1.89; 95% CI: 1.33–2.72). In contrast, no significant interactions were observed for sex (P for interaction = 0.881) or age (P for interaction = 0.567).

Discussion

The prognostic value of hematuria in IgAN has remained controversial for decades, primarily because of the absence of standardized measurement, inconsistent clinical thresholds, and inherent fluctuations. This study aimed to evaluate the association of baseline and time-updated hematuria with kidney disease progression in a large-scale cohort of patients with IgAN. Our findings indicate that initial hematuria levels did not correlate with long-term kidney outcomes. However, by applying MSMs to construct a pseudo-population and effectively adjust for time-dependent confounding, we demonstrated that persistent high-grade hematuria (specifically ≥ 100 RBC/μl) is a potent driver of disease progression. This detrimental effect was particularly pronounced in patients with impaired renal function or uncontrolled proteinuria. The results suggest that persistent hematuria acts as a critical biomarker of ongoing disease activity and a complementary target in clinical management.

Earlier investigations largely concentrated on assessing microscopic hematuria at the time of biopsy; however, findings have been conflicting. Notably, only a few reports have demonstrated a clear association with adverse renal events.24, 25, 26 This inconsistency suggests that single-point measurements may fail to adequately capture the longitudinal fluctuations of hematuria throughout the disease course. Therefore, more recent studies have shifted focus to time-averaged hematuria or hematuria remission as indicators for the risk of progression to ESKD. Our center has previously validated this concept by using objective metrics derived from an automated urine particle analyzer to assess hematuria severity. We found that an early remission of persistent hematuria (within 6 months postbiopsy) was significantly associated with a reduced incidence of kidney disease progression.7 This study extends our previous work by expanding the cohort size while maintaining the use of automated analysis to minimize operator bias. Furthermore, by employing MSMs to rigorously account for dynamic covariates—such as immunosuppressive therapy, proteinuria, and blood pressure during follow-up—we are able to better quantify the burden of persistent hematuria and its impact on prognosis.

One key insight from this cohort is that hematuria severity showed effect modification by baseline kidney function and proteinuria, with a stronger association with kidney disease progression in patients with reduced kidney function and higher proteinuria. The occurrence of hematuria in IgAN signifies active glomerular inflammation driven by the aberrant production of galactose-deficient IgA1, which triggers the formation of pathogenic immune complexes.27 Once erythrocytes cross the damaged filtration barrier, the release of hemoglobin and iron induces intratubular oxidative stress, triggering cytotoxicity and apoptosis in both tubular cells and podocytes.28 Patients with pre-existing kidney dysfunction and reduced nephron reserve may therefore be more vulnerable to these downstream injurious effects.

On the other hand, the stronger association observed in patients with higher proteinuria is also consistent with existing evidence and our previous findings.6,7 Proteinuria is currently a major biomarker for both identifying high-risk patients and tracking therapeutic response in IgAN.29 It can promote tubular chemokine expression, complement activation, and inflammatory cell recruitment, thereby amplifying interstitial fibrosis and kidney injury.30 Of note, complement activation is also central to hematuria pathogenesis; in animal models, hematuria develops only after complement engagement.31,32 In this setting, hematuria may coexist with, or further exacerbate, an already active inflammatory milieu, suggesting that these 2 biomarkers may act synergistically to drive disease progression. At the same time, partially independent drivers may also exist. In an experimental model of IgAN, treatment with an IgA1 protease reduced IgA1 deposition and significantly decreased hematuria without a substantial effect on proteinuria.33 Therefore, our subgroup findings should be interpreted as evidence of effect modification rather than proof of a single causal pathway.

These mechanistic insights are increasingly supported by recent therapeutic advances targeting the same pathogenic pathways. For example, the APPLAUSE-IgAN trial showed that iptacopan, a factor B inhibitor, reduced complement-mediated injury and was associated with a higher proportion of patients achieving negative or trace hematuria compared with placebo.34,35 In addition, agents targeting A Proliferation-Inducing Ligand and B-cell Activating Factor, such as sibeprenlimab and atacicept, have shown promising effects on both proteinuria and hematuria by suppressing pathogenic Gd-IgA1 production.36,37 Together, these findings highlight hematuria as a dynamic marker of disease activity and treatment response in the modern management of IgAN.

Our study’s strengths include its large-scale longitudinal design and frequent follow-up, which provided detailed progression data. The application of MSM allowed us to capture the long-term impact of hematuria while adjusting for time-varying factors like kidney function and treatments that are often missed in traditional models. Furthermore, automated urinalysis offers high precision in quantifying RBC counts, enabling us to distinguish subtle differences at low levels and to identify the exact threshold at which hematuria becomes clinically significant. Combined with standardized, automated urinalysis, these findings establish a more dependable clinical reference range for hematuria in IgAN. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as with any observational study, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of residual confounding by unobserved factors. Second, although we discussed the pivotal role of emerging therapies—including inhibitors of A Proliferation-Inducing Ligand and B-cell Activating Factor, and the complement system—their specific impact could not be analyzed in this cohort because of limited clinical exposure during the study period. Hence, the extent to which these novel agents might modify the risk of hematuria compared with conventional management remains to be elucidated. Third, equipment changes or upgrades over the long study period may have introduced some measurement variability in hematuria assessment. Although automated analyzers generally provide acceptable analytical consistency,38,39 minor differences across analyzer generations cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, we used broad clinically relevant hematuria categories rather than exact absolute cell counts to mitigate potential bias arising from instrument-related variability. Fourth, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups warrants caution, as the influence of hematuria may differ in other populations compared with the predominantly Asian population studied here. Finally, although the associations identified via MSM offer a framework for causal interpretation, the observational nature of this study precludes definitive conclusions regarding causality. Further validation through randomized controlled trials is necessary to confirm these relationships.

In conclusion, our longitudinal analysis using MSMs demonstrates that persistent high-grade hematuria (≥ 100 RBC/μl) is a critical and independent predictor of kidney disease progression in IgAN, particularly in patients with impaired kidney function or uncontrolled proteinuria. These results highlight the clinical value of continuous monitoring and indicate that achieving hematuria remission is essential in these high-risk populations.
