Abstract

The maximum of δ m n ​ ( Δ ) \delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta) over Δ ∈ ( 𝐇 n ⊔ ∂ 𝐇 n ) 1 + n \Delta\in(\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{1+n} is denoted μ m n ∈ [ 0 , + ∞ ] \mu^{n}_{m}\in[0,+\infty] . (maximal inradius)

1
Geometry of the algebraic hyperbolic space

In this section, after recalling some geometry of the Lorentz-Minkowski space time, we define the compactified real algebraic hyperbolic space of dimension
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
denoted
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
by constructing three models, and use each of them to discuss some of its properties.

The Minkowski hyperboloid model is suited to compute metric quantities (angles, distances and volumes) in
\mathbf{H}^{n}
using the algebra of the scalar product.
The Cayley-Klein projective model is adapted for drawing and reasoning with incidence and orthogonality relations between points, lines and quadrics.
We will use the Euclidean ball model to work around a distinguished point (the incenter of a simplex) as it provides a conformal Euclidean model of its tangent space.

1.1
Geometry of the Lorentz-Minkowski space-time

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The Minkowski space of dimension
1+n
is the oriented
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with the symmetric bilinear form of signature
(1,n)
defined for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
by
\langle x,y\rangle=x_{0}v_{0}-\sum_{i>0}x_{i}v_{i}
.
The
Gram matrix
of a sequence
(w_{0},\dots,w_{l})\in(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})^{l}
is defined by
G(u)=(\langle w_{i}\mid w_{j}\rangle)_{i,j}
. This is a real symmetric matrix of signature
(s_{+},s_{0},s_{-})
where
s_{+}\leq 1
and
s_{-}\leq n
.
An
orthonormal frame
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
whose Gram matrix is
\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,\dots,-1)
.

For
r\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathbf{X}_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\colon\langle x,x\rangle=r\}
be the pseudo-sphere of radius
r
and denote by
\mathbf{Y}_{r}=\{x\in\mathbf{X}_{r}\colon x_{0}\geq 0\}
its intersection with the upper-half space.
The isotropic cone
\mathbf{X}_{0}
is asymptotic to the
2
-sheeted hyperboloid
\mathbf{X}_{+1}
and to the
1
-sheeted hyperboloid
\mathbf{X}_{-1}
.
In the projective space
\mathbf{P}{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}}
, the non-degenerate quadric
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbf{S}^{n-1}
has inside
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}}
homeomorphic to the open ball
\mathbf{B}^{n}
and outside
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{-1}}
homeomorphic to
\mathbf{S}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
.

\mathbb{R}^{1+2}

\mathbf{Y}_{r}\subset\mathbf{X}_{r}
for
r=\{-1,0,1\}
.

The orthogonality relation projectivizes to polarity with respect to the quadric
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
.

The group
\GO(1,n)
acts transitively on
\mathbf{X}_{+1}
, and the connected component of the identity
\GO^{+}(1,n)
acts transitively on
\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
.
The stabiliser of the point
o=(+1,0,\dots,0)
is the maximal compact connected subgroup
\GO(n)
.
More generally,
\GO(1,n)
acts transitively on bases of
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with a given Gram matrix: the unique linear matrix sending one basis to the other must preserve the quadratic form.
It follows that
\GO(1,n)
acts transitively on sequences of
l\in\mathbb{N}
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with a given Gram matrix.

For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\setminus\{0\}
, its orthogonal hyperplane denoted
u^{\perp}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\colon\langle u\mid x\rangle=0\}
is naturally co-oriented as the boundary of the closed half-space
u^{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\colon\langle u\mid x\rangle\geq 0\}
.
Since
\langle\mid\rangle
is non-degenerate, the orthogonality
u\mapsto u^{\perp}
projectifies to an isomomorphism between the projective space
\mathbf{P}{(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}
and its dual
\mathbf{P}{(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}^{*}
, which is double covered by the isomorphism induced by
u\mapsto u^{+}
between the Grassmanians of oriented lines
\mathbf{P}^{+}{(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}
and of co-oriented hyperplanes
\mathbf{P}^{+}{(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}^{*}
.
This isomorphism called
polarity
with respect to
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
or
\mathbf{P}^{+}{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
can be constructed by straight lines and incidence relations between point, lines and the quadrics.

In
(\mathbb{R}^{1+n},\langle\mid\rangle)
, a basis
(v_{i})
has a unique dual basis
(v_{j}^{\star})
defined by
(\langle v_{i}^{\star}\mid v_{j}\rangle)_{ij}=\operatorname{Id}_{1+n}
.
The decomposition of
p\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
as
p=\sum_{0}^{n}\lambda_{k}v_{k}
has coordinates
\lambda_{k}=\langle p\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle
also given by:

\lambda_{k}=\frac{\det G(v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1},p,v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n})}{\det G(v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1},v_{k},v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n})}

Indeed,
\lambda_{k}
is the unique linear form on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
which vanishes on
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}\setminus\{v_{k}\}
and takes the value
1
at
v_{k}
, and the same goes for the scalar product with
v_{k}^{\star}
and that ratio of determinants.

1.2
The Minkowski hyperboloid model

The
Minkowski hyperboloid model

of
\mathbf{H}^{n}
is given by endowing
\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
with the Riemannian metric obtained by restricting the form
-\langle\mid\rangle
to its tangent spaces.
For example, the tangent space at
o=\mathbf{Y}_{+1}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\colon x_{0}=1\}
is the horizontal hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Euclidean metric
-\langle\mid\rangle
.

For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the geodesic subspaces of dimension
k
in
\mathbf{H}^{n}=\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
are its (non-empty) intersections with the linear spaces of dimension
1+k
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
(to which the restriction of the form
\langle\mid\rangle
has signature
(k,1)
).
Hence the notions of linear rank, span and independence in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
restrict to the corresponding notions of geodesic rank, span and independence in
\mathbf{H}^{n}
.

For
v_{0},\dots,v_{m}\in\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
, their geodesic convex hull is obtained by intersecting
\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
with the positive cone over their linear convex hull
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot\Conv(v_{0},\dots,v_{m})
, or equivalently (by convexity of
\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
) with their convex hull with the origin
\Conv(0,v_{0},\dots,v_{m})
.
This is a hyperbolic polytope whose geodesic boundary consists of portions of the hyperboloid.

The
Lorentzian sphere model
for
\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
namely the sphere of asymptotic directions of the hyperbolic space can be represented by any section of the projectivization map
\mathbf{Y}_{0}\to\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{Y}_{0}}
, such as
\mathbf{Y}_{0}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\colon x_{0}=+1\}
.
(The compactification will appear in the next
Section

1.3
.)

We define
(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{\star}
as the set of hyperspaces (geodesic subspaces of codimension
1
), and
(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{+}
as the set of closed half-spaces.
For
u\in\mathbf{X}_{<0}
of negative norm, we denote the geodesic hyperspace
H_{u}=u^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
and the closed half-space
H_{u}^{+}=u^{+}\cap\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
.
In restriction to
u\in\mathbf{X}_{-1}
, the maps
u\mapsto H_{u}
and
u\mapsto H_{u}^{+}
are two-to-one and one-to-one parametrizations of
(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{\star}
and
(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{+}
.

We now use this Minkowski hyperboloid model to express the distances and angles between points and hyperspaces of
\mathbf{H}^{n}
using the algebra of the scalar product.

For
x,y\in\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
, their distance is obtained by integrating the Riemannian metric along the segment of hyperbola
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot\Conv(x,y)\cap\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
yielding
\cosh(d(x,y))=\langle x\mid y\rangle
.

For distinct
x,y\in\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
, the difference
u=x-y
has negative norm and the hyperspace
H_{u}=u^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
consists of the set of points equidistant to
x
and
y
.
For
x\in\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
and
u\in\mathbf{X}_{-1}
the distance from
x
to
H_{u}
is given (in
[
10
, §3.2]
) by
\sinh d(x,H_{u})=\lvert\langle x,u\rangle\rvert
and the
\operatorname{sign}(\langle u\mid x\rangle)\in\{-1,0,+1\}
tells respectively whether
x
lies in
H_{u}^{+}\setminus H_{u}
or
H_{u}
or
\mathbf{H}^{n}\setminus H_{u}^{+}
.
For non-proportional
u,v\in\mathbf{X}_{-1}
, the relative positions of the hyperspaces
H_{u}
and
H_{v}
in
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
is given (in
[
10
, §3.2]
) by the
\operatorname{sign}(1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2})\in\{+1,0,-1\}
according to:

\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert<1
:

H_{u}
and
H_{v}
intersect in
\mathbf{H}^{n}
at a dihedral angle given by

\cos\angle(u^{\perp},v^{\perp})=\langle u,v\rangle
;

\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert=1
:

H_{u}
and
H_{v}
are disjoint but have exactly one common asymptotic direction in
\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
;

\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert>1
:

H_{u}
and
H_{v}
are separated in
\mathbf{H}^{n}
by a positive distance, equal to the length of the unique geodesic arc orthogonal to both
H_{u}
and
H_{v}
, given by
\cosh d(H_{u},H_{v})=\lvert\langle u\mid v\rangle\rvert
.

In each case, the

determines the relative position of the half-spaces
H_{u}^{+}
and
H_{u}^{-}
.
The difference
w=(u-\sign(\langle u\mid v\rangle)v)
has negative norm and

is a geodesic hyperspace of points equidistant to

and

H_{u-v}
is equidistant to
H_{u}
and
H_{v}
(so it shares their common asymptotic direction)

\lvert\langle u,v\rangle\rvert<1

is orthogonal to the orthogeodesic of

and
H_{v}
at its midpoint

1.3
The Cayley-Klein projective model

The
Cayley-Klein projective model
for
\mathbf{H}^{n}
is the subset of lines inside the cone
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{>0}
.
The boundary
\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
is the projectivised isotropic cone
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{0}}
.
Their union
\mathbf{P}{(\mathbf{X}_{\geq 0})}
defines the topology on the compactified hyperbolic space
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
.
The space of geodesics hyperspaces
(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{\star}
corresponds by polarity with respect to the quadric to the points outside of the quadric
\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{-1}}
.
Hence every point of
\mathbf{P}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})=\mathbf{P}{(\mathbf{X}_{+1})}\sqcup\mathbf{P}{(\mathbf{X}_{-1})}\sqcup\mathbf{P}{(\mathbf{X}_{0})}
has a geometric interpretation and this defines the topology (and geometry) on the extended hyperbolic plane
(\mathbf{H}^{n})\sqcup(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})\sqcup(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{\star}
.

The projectivization
\mathbf{X}_{>0}\to\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}}
is a homeomorphism in restriction to
\mathbf{Y}_{+1}
, so imposing it to be an isometry leads to defining the distance distance between
[x],[y]\in\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}}
as the hyperbolic angle:

\cosh d([x],[y])=\langle x,y\rangle/\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle}
.
We may also express this distance in purely projective terms using the cross-ratio of an aligned quadruple of distinct points
(x^{\prime},x,y,y^{\prime})
given in any affine chart containing them by
[x^{\prime},x,y,y^{\prime}]=\frac{(x^{\prime}-y)(x-y^{\prime})}{(x^{\prime}-x)(y-y^{\prime})}
.
For distinct points
x,y\in\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}}
, the line
(xy)
intersects the quadric
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{0}
in two other points
\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}
such that
(x^{\prime},x,y,y^{\prime})
lie on this order on the line
(xy)
and we have
\exp 2d(x,y)=\lvert[x^{\prime},x,y,y^{\prime}]\rvert
.

In this model, the isometry group
\Isom(\mathbf{H}^{n})
is identified with
\mathbf{P}{\GO(1,n)}
.

The Cayley-Klein projective model of the extended
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup(\mathbf{H}^{n})^{\star}
is especially adapted to draw and reason with incidence and orthogonality relations (between points, lines and the quadric), through straight lines constructions (see Figures

and
4
).
One may also compare distances geometrically (but their analytic and algebraic manipulation are less amenable).

Indeed, for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the geodesic subspaces of dimension
k
in
\mathbf{H}^{n}=\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}}
are the intersections with the projective linear spaces of dimension
k
in
\mathbf{P}\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.
Hence the notions of projective linear rank, span and independence in
\mathbf{P}(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})
restrict to the corresponding notions of geodesic rank, span and independence in
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
.

For
x\in\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{X}_{+1})
and
u\in\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{X}_{-1})
, the orthogonal projection of the point
x\in\mathbf{H}^{n}
on the hyperspace
H_{u}\subset\mathbf{H}^{n}
is obtained as the intersection of the line
(xu)
with the hyperspace
u^{\perp}
.
For
u\in\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathbf{X}_{-1})
, one may visualize the boundary
\partial H_{u}=u^{\perp}\cap\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{X}_{0})
as the set of tangency points between the quadric
\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{X}_{0})
and the lines (or hyperplanes) in the pencil based at
u
.
The half-spaces
H_{u}^{+}\subset\mathbf{H}^{n}
hyperplanes correspond by duality to the signed-points outside the quadric
u\in\mathbf{P}^{+}(\mathbf{X}_{-1})=\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{X}_{-1})\times\{-1,+1\}
(in bijection with
\mathbf{X}_{-1}
).

1.4
The Euclidean ball model

The
Euclidean ball model
is obtained by endowing the affine chart of
\mathbf{P}{(\mathbb{R}^{1+n})}
given by the horizontal affine hyperplane at unit height
o+\mathbb{R}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}\colon x_{0}=+1\}
with the structure of a Euclidean plane with origin
o
and scalar product
(x,y)\mapsto-\langle x-o\mid y-o\rangle
.
This yields a homeomorphism between the quadric pair
(\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{0}},\mathbf{P}{\mathbf{X}_{+1}})
and
(\mathbf{S}^{n-1},\mathbf{B}^{n})
, which is equivariant under the actions of
\GO(n)
viewed as the stabiliser of

in

Hyperbolic simplices

2.1
Definition of simplices and related geometric constructions

Hyperbolic simplices and their skeleta

A
simplex

\Delta
in
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space is an element of the compact
(\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}
, that is a sequence of
n+1
points
v_{0},\dots,v_{n}
in
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
called its
vertices
.
The simplex is
ideal
when all its vertices lie in the boundary, namely when it belongs to the compact
(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}
.

For
m\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, a
m
-face
of
\Delta
is the convex hull of
1+m
of its vertices.
Its
m
-skeleton

\Delta^{(m)}
is the union of its
m
-faces.
The Hausdorff distance from
\Delta^{(n)}
to the
m
-skeleton
\Delta^{(m)}
is

\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)=\max\{d(p,\Delta^{(m)})\colon p\in\Delta^{(n)}\}.

The function
\delta^{n}_{m}\colon(\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}\to[0,\infty]
is continuous so it admits a maximum
\mu^{n}_{m}=\max_{\Delta}\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)
and this maximum must be achieved on a closed subset.
We are interested in characterizing, for fixed
n>m>0
, the simplices which maximize
\mu^{n}_{m}
and showing that this maximum is uniformly bounded.

Since
\delta^{n}_{m}
is increasing under inclusions of
n
-skeleta, and every simplex has its
n
-skeleton contained in that of an ideal simplex, the maximal loci of
\delta^{n}_{m}
are contained in the subset of ideal simplices.
(However, we do not yet restrict the discussion to ideal simplices.)

A simplex is called
total
when its vertices are independent, that is when its
n
-skeleton
\Delta^{(n)}
has non-empty interior (or equivalently positive
n
-volume).
The maximal locus of
\delta^{n}_{m}
must be contained in the set of total simplices, so from now on we assume that
\Delta
is total.

Minkowski model and homogeneous coordinates

For
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, the
k
-th hyperface
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}=\Conv(\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}\setminus\{v_{k}\})
is co-oriented as the boundary of
\Delta^{(n)}
.
In the projective model
\mathbf{P}\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, it spans a co-oriented hyperplane
\Span(\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)})
that is dual (by polarity with respect to the quadric
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}_{0}
) to a signed point
v_{k}^{\star}\in\mathbf{P}^{+}\mathbf{X}_{-1}
.
In particular
\Delta^{(n)}
is the intersection of the half spaces
H_{v_{k}^{\star}}^{+}
.

In the Minkowski model
(\mathbb{R}^{1+n},\langle\cdot\mid\cdot\rangle)
, there is a unique lift of the dual vertices
v_{k}^{\star}\in\mathbf{X}_{-1}
, hence a unique lift of the vertices
v_{k}\in\mathbf{Y}_{\geq 0}
satisfying
\langle v_{k}\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle=1
, so that the bases
(v_{i})
and
(v_{j}^{\star})
are dual.
This defines the
Minkowski model
of a simplex
\Delta
and its dual
\Delta^{\star}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
.

The pair of dual bases
\Delta,\Delta^{\star}
yields a coordinate system on
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
where
p=\sum_{0}^{n}\lambda_{k}v_{k}
has coordinates
\lambda_{k}=\langle p\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle
also given by the expression:

\lambda_{k}=\frac{\det G(v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1},p,v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n})}{\det G(v_{0},\dots,v_{k-1},v_{k},v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n})}.

This yields our homogeneous coordinate system for
p\in\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{Y}_{\geq 0}
.

Orthogonal projections

For
p\in\mathbf{H}^{n}
, its orthogonal projection on the hyperspace
H_{v_{k}^{\star}}
can be constructed in the projective model as the intersection
p_{k}=[pv_{k}^{\star})\cap\Span(\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)})
, so if
p=\sum\lambda_{i}v_{i}
then
p_{k}=\sum_{i\neq k}\lambda_{i}v_{i}
.
The distance is given by
\sinh d(p,p_{k})=\rvert\langle p,v_{k}^{\star}\rangle\lvert
.

Beware that for some simplices, there are points
p\in\Delta^{(n)}
whose orthogonal projection on a hyperspace
\Span(\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)})
lies outside the hyperface
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
, and one may construct such simplices that are total and ideal as soon as
n\geq 3
(see Figure
6
).
However, for any
p\in\Delta^{(n)}
, if a hyperspace
\Span(\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)})
is closest than any other, namely
\langle p\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle=\min_{i}\{\langle p\mid v_{i}^{\star}\rangle\}
, then its orthogonal projection
p_{k}
belongs to the hyperface
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
.

For example, the orthogonal projection
h_{k}
of
v_{k}
on
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
has homogeneous coordinates
h_{k}=\lambda_{k}v_{k}+\lambda_{k}^{\star}v_{k}^{\star}
satisfying
0=\langle v_{k}^{\star}\mid h_{k}\rangle=\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k}^{\star}
thus
h_{k}=v_{k}+v_{k}^{\star}
.

Gram matrices and dual gram matrices

The
dual Gram matrix
of
\Delta
is
G(\Delta^{\star})=(\langle v_{i}^{\star}\mid v_{j}^{\star}\rangle)_{0\leq i,j\leq n}
.
Since any two hyperfaces
\partial_{i}\Delta^{(n)},\partial_{j}\Delta^{(n)}
share some common vertices in
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
, we have
\lvert\langle v_{i}^{\star},v_{j}^{\star}\rangle\rvert\leq 1
, so the dual Gram matrix
G^{\star}(\Delta)
consists of the cosines
\cos(\theta_{ij}^{\star})
of their dihedral angles denoted
\theta_{ij}^{\star}=\angle(\partial_{i}\Delta^{(n)},\partial_{j}\Delta^{(n)})
.
The
Gram matrix
of
\Delta
is
G(\Delta)=(\langle v_{i}\mid v_{j}\rangle)_{0\leq i,j\leq n}
.
Recall that the vertices
v_{i}\in\mathbf{Y}_{>0}
have their norms defined by the duality condition
\langle v_{i}\mid v_{i}^{\star}\rangle=1
.

Lemma 2.1

(inverse Gram matrices)

Proof.

Let us express the basis
\Delta
in terms of the dual basis
\Delta^{\star}

defined by

satisfy
\langle v_{i}\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle=\sum_{j}c_{ij}\langle v_{j}^{\star}\mid v_{k}^{\star}\rangle

Regular simplices

A total simplex
\Delta
of
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
is
regular
when its stabilizer in
\Isom(\mathbf{H}^{n})
acts transitively on its flags, so that it is isomorphic to the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
of its vertices
\{v_{0},\dots,v_{n}\}
.

A regular simplex
\Delta
must have dual Gram matrix
G(\Delta^{\star})
with diagonal
-1
and constant off-diagonal
c\in[-1,1]
.
We will deduce from
Corollary

2.3
that it exists
\iff c\in(1/n,1/(n-1)]
.
In that case, it is unique up to the
\GO(1,n)
-action, since it is transitive on sequences in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
with a given Gram-matrix.
Let us compute its Gram matrix
G(\Delta)
.

Denoting
\operatorname{J}_{1+n}
the matrix with entries
1
, we may write
G(\Delta^{\star})=c\operatorname{J}_{1+n}-(1+c)\operatorname{Id}_{1+n}
.
The operator
\operatorname{J}_{1+n}
(having eigenvalues
(1+n)
with multiplicity
1
and
0
with multiplicity
n
), commutes with
\operatorname{Id}_{1+n}
(having eigenvalues
1
with multiplicity
1+n
).
Thus
G(\Delta^{\star})
has eigenvalues

with multiplicity
1
and
c0-(1+c)1
with multiplicity
n
, so
\det(-G(\Delta^{\star}))=(1-cn)(1+c)^{n}
.
In particular
G(\Delta)^{\star}
is invertible
\iff c\notin\{-1,1/n\}
which we now assume, and note that
\det(-G(\Delta^{\star}))<0\iff c>1/n
.

Since
\operatorname{J}_{1+n}
generates a commutative algebra of dimension
2

G(\Delta)=y\operatorname{J}_{1+n}-x\operatorname{Id}_{1+n}
such that
G(\Delta)G(\Delta^{\star})=\operatorname{Id}_{1+n}
to find

and

.
For later purposes we note that
\sum_{ij}G(\Delta)_{ij}=(n+1)/(cn-1)

In particular,
\Delta
is ideal
\iff G(\Delta)
has diagonal
0

\iff y=x\iff c=1/(n-1)

g(\Delta)
has off-diagonal

\sum_{ij}(\Delta)_{ij}=n^{2}-1

\leq\tanh^{-1}(1/n)
, with equality if and only if it is ideal and regular.

Remark 2.7

(two coordinate systems)
.

The Minkowski model of
\Delta
in
\mathbf{Y}_{\geq 0}
defines a homogeneous coordinate system for th projective space
\mathbf{P}{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}}
, that was used in the proof of
Proposition

2.2
.
The Euclidean representative of
\Delta
in
\mathbf{B}^{n}
defines a barycentric coordinate system for the Euclidean chart
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that will be used in the proof of
Theorem

2.6
.
These coordinate systems are related by the heights, but we will not explicit this relation.

Proof.

Since every total simplex of
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
is contained in an ideal one, it suffices to consider total simplices
\Delta
of
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
that are ideal.
We may work with the incentred Euclidean model: by Proposition
2.3
their regularity are equivalent, and their spheres centred at their common incenter
o
coincide with their radii related by the increasing function
r\mapsto\tanh{r}
.
Hence we must show that a Euclidean simplex
\Delta=(v_{0},\dots,v_{n})
inscribed in the unit sphere
\mathbf{S}^{n-1}
whose incenter
o
lies at the origin has inradius at most
1/n
with equality if and only if it is regular.

In the barycentric coordinate system of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
\Delta=(v_{k})\subset\mathbf{S}_{o}^{n-1}
, the origin is a convex combination
o=\sum\lambda_{k}v_{k}
of the vertices with weights
\lambda_{k}>0
satisfying
\sum_{0}^{n}\lambda_{k}=1
.
The Euclidean distance from
o
to its orthogonal projection
v_{k}^{\prime}
on
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
is at most the distance from
o
to
\tfrac{-\lambda_{k}}{1-\lambda_{k}}v_{k}=\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda_{k}}\sum_{j\neq k}\lambda_{j}v_{j}\in\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
, that is
\tfrac{\lambda_{k}}{1-\lambda_{k}}
, with equality if and only if
v_{k}^{\prime}=\tfrac{-\lambda_{k}}{1-\lambda_{k}}v_{k}
, implying that
(v_{k}o)
intersects
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
orthogonally at
v_{k}^{\prime}
.
Hence the Euclidean inradius satisfies
\tanh(\delta^{n}_{n-1})\leq\min\{\lambda_{k}/(1-\lambda_{k})\colon 0\leq k\leq n\}\leq\left(\tfrac{1}{n+1}\right)/\left(1-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\right)=\tfrac{1}{n}
with equality to
\tfrac{1}{n}
if and only if all
k
we have
\lambda_{k}=\tfrac{1}{n+1}
and
(v_{k}o)
intersects
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
orthogonally at
v_{k}^{\prime}=\tfrac{-1}{n}v_{k}
.
The case of equality implies that
\Delta
admits
o
as an orthocenter, and as
o
coincides with the incenter and circumcenter, we deduce from
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
that
\Delta
must be regular.
∎

Now fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n>m>0
, and recall from
Section

2.1
that the continuous bounded function
\delta^{n}_{m}\colon(\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
achieves its maximum
\mu^{n}_{m}
on a closed subset of total ideal simplices.
We wish to characterize this maximizing locus.

We know from
Theorem

2.6
that for
n\geq 2
, the total ideal simplices
\Delta\subset(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}
with maximal inradius
\delta^{n}_{n-1}(\Delta)=\mu^{n}_{n-1}
are regular and
\tanh(\mu^{n}_{n-1})=1/n
.

Theorem 2.8

(maximal Hausdorff distance to
m
-skeleton)
.

For integers
n>m>0
, a simplex
\Delta
in
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
satisfies
\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)=\mu^{n}_{m}
if and only if
\Delta
is total, ideal and regular.
Moreover,
\mu^{n}_{1}
is given
by
\left(\tanh\mu^{n}_{1}\right)^{2}=\tfrac{n-1}{2n}
so
\mu^{n}_{1}
grows as
n\to\infty
to
\mu^{\infty}_{1}=\tanh^{-1}(1/\sqrt{2})=\log(1+\sqrt{2})
.

Proof.

Fix a (total ideal) simplex
\Delta
of
\mathbf{H}^{n}\sqcup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
.

For
0\leq n^{\prime\prime}\leq n^{\prime}\leq n
define
\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime}}(\Delta)
to be the maximum on its
n^{\prime}
-faces of their Hausdorff distance to their
n^{\prime\prime}
-skeleton:

\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime}}(\Delta)=\max\{\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime}}(F)\colon F\subset\Delta,\,\operatorname{Card}(F)=1+n^{\prime\prime}\}
.
Recall that for every point in a
n^{\prime}
-face
p\in F^{(n^{\prime})}
, its orthogonal projection on the closest linear span of a hyperface, say
\Span\partial_{0}F^{(n^{\prime})}
, actually belongs to the hyperface
\partial_{0}F^{(n^{\prime})}
.
Hence for
n^{\prime\prime\prime}\leq n^{\prime\prime}\leq n^{\prime}
, the orthogonal projection from any point
p^{\prime}\in F^{(n^{\prime})}
to a closest point
p^{\prime\prime\prime}\in F^{(n^{\prime\prime\prime})}
factors through an orthogonal projection to a closet point
p^{\prime\prime}\in F^{(n^{\prime\prime})}
, so by the Pythagorean theorem for hyperbolic triangles we have
\cosh d(p^{\prime},F^{(n^{\prime\prime\prime})})=\cosh d(p^{\prime},F^{(n^{\prime\prime})})\cosh d(p^{\prime\prime},F^{(n^{\prime\prime\prime})})
.
Taking the maximum over
p^{\prime}\in F^{(n^{\prime})}
yields
\cosh\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\Delta)\leq\cosh\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime}}(\Delta)\cosh\delta^{n^{\prime\prime}}_{n^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\Delta)
.
Consequently
\cosh\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)\leq\prod_{m+1}^{n}\cosh\delta^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime}-1}(\Delta)
which is
\leq\prod_{m+1}^{n}\cosh\mu^{n^{\prime}}_{n^{\prime}-1}
with equality only if
\Delta
is regular.

For such a regular simplex
\Delta
, the supremum defining
\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)=\sup\{d(p,\Delta^{(m)})\colon p\in\Delta^{(n)}\}
is achieved (only) at its incenter
o
, and equals its distance to any
m
-face.
It could be calculated from the product formula
\cosh(\mu^{n}_{m})=\prod_{m<n^{\prime}\leq n}\cosh\tanh^{-1}(1/n^{\prime})
using
\cosh\tanh^{-1}(x)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
but let us provide a direct computation of
\mu^{n}_{1}
using the incentred Euclidean model.

Consider a total regular Euclidean simplex
\Delta=(v_{0},\dots,v_{n})
inscribed in the unit sphere
\mathbf{S}_{o}^{n-1}
and let
\nu^{n}_{1}
be the distance from the origin to any edge
(v_{i}v_{j})
.
We have
\mu^{n}_{1}=\tanh^{-1}(\nu^{n}_{1})
.
Since the points
(v_{k},o,v_{k}^{\prime},v_{k}^{\star})
are aligned in this order, the unit tangent vectors
e_{i}^{\prime},e_{j}^{\prime}
at
o
point in the opposite direction to the vertices
v_{i},v_{j}
, thus denoting their common angle
\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{ij}^{\prime}
we have
\nu^{n}_{1}=\cos(\theta^{\prime}/2)
hence
(\nu^{n}_{1})^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos\theta^{\prime})
.
To compute
\cos(\theta^{\prime})=\langle e_{i}^{\prime}\mid e_{j}^{\prime}\rangle_{o}
we square the norm of
0=(\sum_{0}^{n}e_{k}^{\prime})
to find
0=\sum_{i,j}\langle e_{i}^{\prime}\mid e_{j}^{\prime}\rangle_{o}=(n+1)+2\binom{n+1}{2}\cos\theta^{\prime}
hence
\cos\theta^{\prime}=-1/n
.
Finally
(\tanh\mu^{n}_{1})^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-1/n)

thus
\mu^{n}_{1}
grows as
n\to\infty
to
\mu^{\infty}_{1}=\tanh^{-1}(1/\sqrt{2})=\log(1+\sqrt{2})
.
∎

Corollary 2.9

(Hausdorff distance convex hull to
1
-skeleton)
.

For any subset
X\subset\mathbf{H}^{n}\cup\partial\mathbf{H}^{n}
, every point of
\Conv(X)
is at distance at most
\log(1+\sqrt{2})
from

.

2.4
Locating and counting points in
\Delta
realising
\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)

Recall from
Corollary

2.3
the equivariant correspondence between total ideal simplices in
(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}
and total Euclidean simplices inscribed in
(\mathbf{S}^{n-1})^{n+1}
whose incenter lies at the origin.

The
[
4
, Theorem 3.2 (ii)]
shows that a total simplex
\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equicentered (its incenter
o
coincides with its circumcenter) if and only if it is equiradial (its hyperfaces all have the same circumradius).
In that case, the incenter
o
projects orthogonally on each face
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
to its circumcenter
v_{k}^{\prime}
.
However
v_{k}^{\prime}
may be distinct from the incenter of
\partial_{k}\Delta^{(n)}
, in which case the distance from
o
to the
(n-2)
skeleton will not be the maximal distance
\delta^{n}_{n-2}
.
Indeed, we propose the proof of following Proposition as an exercise for the reader.

Proposition 2.10

(when does
d(o,\Delta^{(m)})=\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)
?)
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
0<m<n-1
.
Consider a total ideal simplex
\Delta\in(\partial\mathbf{H}^{n})^{n+1}
.

If its incenter
o
realizes the maximal distance to the
m
-skeleton, namely
d(o,\Delta^{(m)})=\delta^{n}_{m}(\Delta)
, then
\Delta
is regular; thus if
\Delta
is not regular then there are at least
2
maximizers of
\delta^{n}_{m}
.

Problem 2.11

(maximizers of
\delta^{n}_{m}
)
.

Each
(n+1)
-set of
m
-faces
\{F_{0}^{(m)},\dots,F_{n}^{(m)}\}\in\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\binom{\Delta}{1+m}}{1+n}\right)

defines a decreasing intersection of convex sets in
\Delta^{(n)}
by
C_{t}(F)=\{p\in\Delta^{(n)}\colon d(p,F_{i}^{(m)}\geq t\}
, hence a point maximizing the distance to these faces, that is a local maximizer of the Hausdorff distance from
\Delta^{(n)}
to
\Delta^{(m)}
, which for some
F
will be a global maximer of
\delta^{n}_{m}
.

What can be the number of distinct local global minimizers and where can they be located?

Note that the isometry group of the simplex
\operatorname{Stab}(\Delta^{(n)})\subset\Isom(\mathbf{H}^{n})
acts on that set of
(1+n)
-sets of
m
-faces.
However the description and enumeration of distinct (local or global) maximizers is more subtle than the question of classifying (all or certain) of the orbits under this action, since it may happen that two
(1+n)
-sets of
m
-faces yield the same (local or global) maximizer without belonging to the same orbit.

Example 2.12

(disphenoids)
.

The tetrahedra in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that are equicentred or equiradial are called
disphenoids
, and have various equivalent characterisations (see
[
8
]
), such as having opposite edges of the same length and acute triangle faces.
This shows that acute triangles parametrize disphenoids by folding along their midlines (joining the mid-edges) as in Figure
9
, and this parametrization is equivariant under the actions of the groups of Euclidean similitudes.
