Measurement-aligned Flow for Inverse Problem
Shaorong Zhang, Rob Brekelmans, Yunshu Wu, Greg Ver Steeg

Abstract
Diffusion models provide a powerful way to incorporate complex prior information for solving inverse problems. However, existing methods struggle to correctly incorporate guidance from conflicting signals in the prior and measurement, especially in the challenging setting of non-Gaussian or unknown noise. To bridge these gaps, we propose Measurement-Aligned Sampling (MAS), a novel framework for linear inverse problem solving that can more flexibly balance prior and measurement information. MAS unifies and extends existing approaches like DDNM and DAPS, and offers a new optimization perspective. MAS can generalize to handle known Gaussian noise, unknown or non-Gaussian noise types. Extensive experiments show that MAS consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods across a range of tasks.

Introduction

Inverse problems are prevalent in image restoration (IR) tasks, including super-resolution, inpainting, deblurring, colorization, denoising, and JPEG restoration
(
chung2022diffusion
;
kawar2022denoising
;
saharia2022image
;
wang2022zero
;
lugmayr2022repaint
;
mardani2023variational
;
song2023pseudoinverse
;
kawar2022jpeg
)
. Solving an inverse problem involves recovering an unknown original image
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
based on information from a prior distribution,
\pi(x_{0})
, and noisy measurements
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
generated through a forward model:

y=\mathcal{H}(x_{0})+\epsilon.

(1)

Here
{\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
represents measurement noise,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is drawn from data distribution
\pi_{0}(x_{0})
,
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the measurement function, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denotes the degraded measurement or observed image. A useful motivating example is a high-resolution image
x_{0}
, with a noisy degraded image
y
and known corruption process.

Pretrained diffusion or flow models provide a prior distribution
\pi_{0}(x_{0})
that significantly facilitates the solution of inverse problems. DPS
(
chung2022diffusion
)
and
\Pi
GDM
(
song2023pseudoinverse
)
estimate conditional scores directly from the measurement model by leveraging score decomposition to guide each diffusion sampling iteration. In contrast, FPS
(
dou2024diffusion
)
, DAPS
(
zhang2024improving
)
, and MPGD
(
he2023manifold
)
align denoiser outputs directly with measurements, thereby avoiding backpropagation. However, these methods are not explicitly designed to handle high levels of Gaussian noise, as addressed by approaches like DDNM
(
wang2022zero
)
and DDRM
(
kawar2022denoising
)
.

Moreover, the above approaches lack the ability to effectively regulate the ‘strength’ and ‘type’ of the alignment between measurements and model predictions. They also commonly assume a known forward operator
\mathcal{H}
and Gaussian noise with known variance, i.e.,
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m})
. In practical settings, noise frequently deviates from Gaussian assumptions—exhibiting characteristics like salt-and-pepper, periodic, or Poisson distributions—or is completely unknown. Additionally, the forward measurement operator may be uncertain or inaccurately specified. Effectively addressing inverse problems under these more general and realistic conditions remains an open and challenging research area.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
Measurement-Aligned Sampling
(MAS), a novel framework for solving linear inverse problems. MAS provides additional optimization perspectives and generalizes approaches such as DDNM and DAPS for linear inverse problems. Furthermore, our proposed “overshooting” technique achieves superior restoration quality compared to DDNM across various inverse problem scenarios.

•

We develop new techniques to handle Gaussian noise and unknown noise. Specifically, we extend strategies akin to DDNM
+
to address noisy inverse problems with known Gaussian noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}\mathbb{I}_{m})
. Moreover, our novel parameterization scheme allows us to effectively handle noisy inverse problems with unknown or non-Gaussian noise structures and even non-differentiable measurements, such as JPEG restoration, without requiring explicit knowledge of the forward operator or noise level. The comparison of method applicability across different inverse problems is shown in

1
.

•

Our experimental results demonstrate state-of-the-art performance across a diverse set of tasks, see examples in
Fig.

and experiments in

.

Background

Notation
. Our time notation follows diffusion literature where
t=T
corresponds to the source distribution and
t=0
corresponds to the target distribution, consistent with the use of pretrained diffusion models in our experiments. In contrast, flow-based models typically adopt the reverse, with
t=0
as the source distribution.

2.1
Conditional Diffusion and flow models

Given training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{0}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from target distribution
\pi_{0}(x_{0})
,
x_{0}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the goal of generative modeling is to draw new samples from
\pi_{0}
. In the context of conditional generation, suppose that we have data samples from a joint disribution
(x_{0}^{i},y)\sim\pi(x_{0},y)
, where
x_{0}
,
y
are dependent, and
y
could be class labels or text information, for example.

For conditional generative modeling, we seek to draw new
samples from
\pi(x_{0}\mid y)
for a given condition
y
. Conditioned flows
(
zheng2023guided
)
build a
marginal probability
path
p_{t\mid y}

using a mixture of interpolating densities:

p_{t\mid y}(x_{t}\mid y)=\int p_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})\pi(x_{0}\mid y)dx_{T}
, where
p_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{0})
is a probability path interpolating between noise and a single data point
x_{T}
. In general, the conditional kernel
p_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})
is given by a Gaussian distribution:
p_{t}(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};\alpha_{t}x_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the Gaussian kernel,
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}
are differentiable functions. Then we can sample from the conditional distribution
p_{0\mid y}(x_{0}\mid y)
by simulating a stochastic process
p_{t\mid y}(x_{t}\mid y)
from time
t=T
to
t=0
, which is given by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

dx=\left[(\dot{\alpha}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\xi_{t})\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x,y)+\xi_{t}x_{t}\right]dt+\epsilon_{t}dW_{t},

(2)

where
\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t},y]
and
\xi_{t}=\frac{\dot{\sigma}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}-\frac{\epsilon_{t}^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}
. Then
x_{0}
could be sampled by solving the SDE described in
Eq.

2
. Although different sampling methods can be chosen, generally, the iteration follows the form:

x_{t-\Delta t}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x,y)+b_{t}x_{t},c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I}).

(3)

2.2
Training-free conditional inference

Given pretrained unconditional diffusion models
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\approx\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t}]
, training-free approaches for conditional inference allows us to approximate the ideal denoiser
\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)

(
pokle2023training
)
:

\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t},y]\approx\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}}\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p(y\mid x_{t}).

(4)

For inverse problems or conditioning problems where
p(y\mid x_{0})
is known but
p(y\mid x_{t})
is intractable
(
chung2022diffusion
;
wu2024practical
)
, denoising predictors
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(t,x_{t})
provide a convenient single-sample approximation for
p(y|x_{t})
,
p(y\mid x_{t})\approx p(y\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(t,x_{t}))
.
For example,
\Pi
GDM
(
song2023pseudoinverse
)
assumes
p_{t}(x_{0}\mid x_{t})\approx\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(t,x_{t}),r_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, then

p_{t}(y\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=\int p(y|x_{0})p(x_{0}|\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})dx_{0}=\mathcal{N}(y;H\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m}+r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}).

(5)

Then the gradient guidance can be approximated by differentiating through the denoising predictor network using the chain rule:

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p(y\mid x_{t})\approx\left[(y-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})^{T}(r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I})H\frac{\partial\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(t,x_{t})}{\partial x_{t}}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(6)

Substituting
Eq.

6
into
Eq.

4
, we obtain an estimate of the ideal denoiser
\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)
. While this method works well in practice to produce the desired guidance effect (
Section

5
), it still relies on a crude approximation of the expectation in
Eq.

5
and requires backpropagation through the denoising network. In this work, we present an alternative approach to estimate
\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)
based on unconditional denoising predictors
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}(t,x_{t})
and the measurement
y
.

3
Methodology

3.1
Measurement-Aligned Sampling for linear inverse problem

Measurement-Aligned Sampling aims to estimate the desired conditional denoiser
\tilde{x}_{0}(t,x_{t},y)=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t},y]
at time
t
by utilizing two measurements: an approximated unconditional denoiser
\hat{x}^{\theta}_{0}(t,x_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t}]
and the measurement
y
. MAS performs posterior sampling
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)
based on the following probabilistic model:

Prior:

\displaystyle\phantom{\tilde{x}_{0}}\quad p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},\rho_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}),

(7a)

Likelihood:

\displaystyle~~\phantom{\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}}\quad p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})\coloneqq\int p(y|x_{0})p(x_{0}|\tilde{x}_{0})dx_{0},

(7b)

where
\rho_{t}
is a hyperparameter defining the prior variance.

Typically, computing the exact likelihood in
Eq.

7b
is infeasible since
p(x_{0}\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
is unknown.
\Pi
GDM
(
song2023pseudoinverse
)
assume
p(x_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},r_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, where
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\approx\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t}]
. In our work, we revise this assumption to
p(x_{0}\mid\tilde{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{x}_{0},r_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
. Consequently, for linear inverse problem where
p(y\mid x_{0})\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(Hx_{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, the likelihood
p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
has similar Gaussian form as in
Eq.

5
:

p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(y;H\tilde{x}_{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m}+r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}).

(8)

Given the prior
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})
in
Eq.

7a
and the likelihood in
Eq.

7b
, the posterior distribution
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)
can be analytically derived via Bayesian linear regression, as detailed in
Proposition

3.1
. See
Appendix

A
for the full derivation and proofs.

Proposition 3.1

(Bayesian Linear Regression)
.

If
p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
and
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})
are both Gaussian or can be approximated as such,
p({y}\mid\tilde{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(H\tilde{x}_{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}{\mathbb{I}}+r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}})
,
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},\rho_{t}^{2}{\mathbb{I}_{d}})
, then
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{post}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}})
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}(r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m})^{-1}H\right)^{-1},

(9)

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{post}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}\left[\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{2}}\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}(r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m})^{-1}y\right].

(10)

This result provides a concrete way to sample
\tilde{x}_{0}
from the posterior
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})
without backpropagation through the denoising network, as is needed in
\Pi
GDM
(
song2023pseudoinverse
)
.
We discuss implementation of this scheme before generalizing to non-Gaussian noise models in Sec.
4
.

3.2
Implementation

Posterior sampling.

Given the posterior distribution
p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)
explicitly derived via Bayesian linear regression in
Proposition

3.1
, we can efficiently sample
\tilde{x}_{0}\sim p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)
. Defining
\eta_{1}\coloneqq{r_{t}^{2}}/{\rho_{t}^{2}}
,
\eta_{2}\coloneqq\sigma_{y}^{2}/\rho_{t}^{2}
, posterior sampling
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{ps}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{post}},\Sigma_{\text{post}})
can be implemented by a single draw of
\epsilon_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{ps}}

\displaystyle=Y^{-1}[\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y+\rho_{t}\epsilon_{p}],\quad\epsilon_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})

(11)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad W

\displaystyle\coloneqq\eta_{1}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}\mathbb{I}_{m},\quad Y\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}H,

then
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{ps}}\sim p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y)
.

Posterior mean.

In the implementation, posterior sampling in
Eq.

11
introduces additional noise
\rho_{t}Y^{-1}\epsilon_{p}
, which leads to noisy restoration in the experiment, See
Appendix

G
for more details. For simplicity and improved reconstruction quality, we could directly replace
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{ps}}
in
Eq.

11
by the posterior mean
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]
:

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=Y^{-1}[\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y]

(12)

Efficient linear solves via the SVD of
H
.

Computing the inverse
W^{-1}
and
Y^{-1}
in
Eq.

11
and
Eq.

12
naively can be computationally expensive. Thus, in practice, we would like to avoid directly computing the inverse. Let
H=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}
with singular values

s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
.
Then

W^{-1}=U\,\mathrm{diag}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\eta_{1}s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}}\Bigr)U^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad Y^{-1}=V\,\mathrm{diag}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\,1+s_{i}^{2}/(\eta_{1}s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2})\,}\Bigr)V^{\mathsf{T}},

(13)

see
Appendix

A
for the proof. Hence both
W^{-1}v
and
Y^{-1}u
reduce to inexpensive diagonal scalings in the SVD basis, avoiding the calculation of any explicit matrix inversion or square-root.

3.3
Exploring alignment trade-offs via an optimization perspective

We can interpret the posterior mean
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
from an optimization perspective to explicitly see how our approach incorporates alignment from the prior and the measurement. Specifically,
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
minimizes the following objective function (see
Appendix

A
for the proof):

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=\arg\min\{\|\tilde{x}_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\|^{2}+\|y-H\tilde{x}_{0}\|_{W^{-1}}^{2}\}

(14)

where
W=\eta_{1}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}\mathbb{I}_{m}
(with
\eta_{1}\geq 0,\eta_{2}\geq 0
) emerges as a metric that reflects the relative importance of measurement fidelity and prior regularization. As
\eta_{1}=0,\eta_{2}>0
,
Eq.

14
reduced to:

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=\arg\min\{\|\tilde{x}_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta_{2}}\|y-H\tilde{x}_{0}\|^{2}\}

(15)

This reduced form represents a standard Tikhonov regularization (ridge regression) problem. The choice of
\eta_{2}
here controls the trade-off between fidelity to the measurements
y
and
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}
. While as
\eta_{1}>0,\eta_{2}=0
, the optimization takes on the following generalized least squares (GLS) form:

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=\arg\min\{\|\tilde{x}_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}\|y-H\tilde{x}_{0}\|_{(HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}}^{2}\}

(16)

Here, the weighting term
(HH^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}
indicates a Mahalanobis-type distance metric, emphasizing directions corresponding to smaller singular values of
H
.
The choice of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
significantly affects the reconstruction quality
, as will be demonstrated experimentally.

Remark 1

(
Connection with DAPS

(
zhang2024improving
)
)
.

As
\eta_{1}=0
,
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{ps}}
is reduced to posterior sampling in DAPS.

Remark 2

(
Connection with DDNM

(
wang2022zero
)
)
.

As
\eta_{2}=0
and
\eta_{1}\rightarrow 0
,
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}\rightarrow\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{DDNM}}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\dagger}(y-H\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})
. Thus, in this limiting case, MAS recovers DDNM.

\eta_{1}
and

\eta_{1}
controls the trade-off between
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}

. (b) Adjusting
\eta_{2}

\eta_{1}
.

(
Overshooting trick
)
.

Theoretically,
\eta_{1}\geq 0
since
\eta_{1}\coloneqq r_{t}^{2}/\rho_{t}^{2}
, however, the posterior mean in
Eq.

12
allows negative
\eta_{1}
. As shown in
Fig.

2
, negative
\eta_{1}
leads to overshooting, which demonstrates better performance than DDNM in the experiments.

4
Generalizing to noisy inverse problems

4.1
Identifying the introduced noise

To illustrate our ideas, we first consider a simple forward model:
y=x_{0}+\epsilon_{y}
. Calculating the posterior mean
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
(
Eq.

12
) leads to:

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+\frac{y-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+\frac{x_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\frac{\epsilon_{y}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}},

(17)

where
\epsilon_{y}/(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1)
is the additional noise introduced to
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
, and will be further introduced into
x_{t-\Delta t}
. To address this issue, we propose two approaches for addressing known Gaussian noise (
Section

4.2
) and unknown noise
Section

4.3
.

4.2
Addressing Gaussian noise with known variance

To handle Gaussian noise with known variance, we modify
Eq.

17
and
Eq.

3
as:

\tilde{x}_{0}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\lambda_{t}}\frac{y-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1},\quad x_{t-\Delta t}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}+b_{t}x_{t},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma_{t}}\mathbb{I}).

(18)

Here
\lambda_{t}
and
r_{t}
are two parameters that can control the total noise introduced to
x_{t-\Delta t}
. In our work, we adopt similar two principles as DDNM
+

(
wang2022zero
)
for handling Gaussian noise: (i) the total noise introduced in
x_{t-\Delta t}
should be
\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
to conform the correct distribution of
x_{t-\Delta t}
in
Eq.

3
; (ii)
\lambda_{t}
should be as close to
1
as possible to maximize the preservation of
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
. As
\epsilon_{y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, principle (i) and principle (ii) are equivalent to:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{t}\lambda_{t}\sigma_{y}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}\right)^{2}+\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle=c_{t}^{2},\quad\lambda_{t}=\begin{cases}1,&c_{t}\geq\dfrac{a_{t}\sigma_{y}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{c_{t}(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1)}{a_{t}\sigma_{y}},&c_{t}<\dfrac{a_{t}\sigma_{y}}{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1}\end{cases}.

(19)

The more accurate version for general form of
H
could be found in
Appendix

B
. Note that the revision didn’t introduce additional parameters.

4.3
Addressing unknown noise and non-differentiable measurements

Addressing unknown noise or non-Gaussian noise.
When the measurement noise
\sigma_{y}
is non-Gaussian or unknown, it becomes difficult to ensure that the total noise in
x_{t-\Delta t}
follows the desired distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, To address this, we continue to sample
x_{t-\Delta t}
using
Eq.

3
. Next, the noise introduced to
x_{t-\Delta t}
is the sum of two components:

\epsilon_{\text{ng}}=(a_{t}\lambda_{t}\epsilon_{y})/(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1),\quad\epsilon_{\text{g}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I}).

(20)

Here
\epsilon_{\text{ng}}
is related to the noise introduced by unknown noise
\epsilon_{y}
, while
\epsilon_{\text{g}}
is the noise added by the diffusion process. To minimize the effect of unknown noise
\epsilon_{\text{ng}}
, it is desirable for
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+1
to be sufficiently large. However, smaller values of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
result in better consistency with the measurement
y
, as illustrated in
Fig.

2
. To balance this trade-off, we propose using a small
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
during the early stages of sampling to fully exploit measurement information. As sampling progresses,
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
should be gradually increased to suppress the impact of
\epsilon_{\text{ng}}
. The underlying intuition is that, in the early sampling stage,
x_{t}
is still highly noisy and
a_{t}\approx 0
, so the influence of
\epsilon_{\text{ng}}
is negligible even when
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
is small. As shown in
Fig.

3
,
\tilde{x}^{\text{pm}}
is initially more aligned with the degraded observation, but progressively shifts toward
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}
as sampling evolves.

For a general degradation operator
H
, we recommend setting
\eta_{2}=ka_{t}/c_{t}
, where
k
is a constant determined by the characteristics of the introduced noise. The rationale behind this design choice is further detailed in
Appendix

B
.

Addressing non-differentiable measurements.
For solving inverse problem with non-differentiable measurements such as JPEG restoration and quantization, the degraded images can be viewed as "noisy images" with unknown noise, modeled by
y=x+\epsilon_{y}
. In these scenarios, our proposed strategy naturally extends by treating the unknown degradations as implicit noise.

5
Experiments

5.1
Experimental setup

Dataset
. We evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed approach on FFHQ
256\times 256

(
karras2019style
)
and ImageNet
256\times 256

(
deng2009imagenet
)
. Following DAPS
(
zhang2024improving

Related work

Diffusion models have also been successfully applied to linear inverse problems, including, compressed-sensing MRI (CS-MRI), and computed tomography (CT)
(
kadkhodaie2021stochastic
;
song2020score
;
chung2022improving
;
kawar2022denoising
;
song2021solving
)
. They have also been extended to non-linear inverse problems such as Fourier phase retrieval, nonlinear deblurring, HDR, and JPEG restoration
(
chung2022diffusion
;
song2023pseudoinverse
;
chung2023parallel
;
mardani2023variational
)
. RED-diff
(
mardani2023variational
)
leverages stochastic optimization to approximate the true posterior. Besides, Chung et al.
(
chung2023decomposed
)
combine the diffusion sampling and Krylov subspace methods, which negates the need to compute the manifold-constrained gradient. InverseBench
(
zheng2025inversebench
)
presents a benchmark for critical scientific applications, which present structural challenges that differ significantly from natural image restoration tasks.

In recent years, diffusion bridge models have been successfully applied to various inverse problems
(
liu2023i2sb
;
zhou2023denoising
;
albergo2023stochastic2
)
. These models are more task-specific and directly build probabilistic paths from corrupted to clean images, showing strong performance. Exploring alignment techniques within diffusion bridges is a promising direction for further improvement.

7
Conclusion

MAS improves upon existing methods by explicitly aligning the sampling process with measurement data, offering a broader optimization perspective that generalizes approaches like DDNM and DAPS. Beyond the noise-free case, MAS can be extended to: (1) known Gaussian noise, (2) unknown or non-Gaussian noise through adaptive parameterization, and (3) non-differentiable degradations (e.g., JPEG) by decoupling the forward operator from sampling. Extensive experiments show that MAS consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods across a wide range of inverse problems.

Limitations
. (i) While MAS can handle non-differentiable measurements like JPEG restoration, it does not support general non-linear inverse problems. (ii) Tweedie’s formula
boys2023tweedie
could be used to improve the MAS prior by better estimating the covariance of
p(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
. Also, enhancing the likelihood model
p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
may further improve performance.

Proof.

Write

\Sigma_{y}\;\coloneqq\;r_{t}^{2}\,HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{m},\qquad\Sigma_{0}\;\coloneqq\;\rho_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{d},\qquad\mu_{0}\;\coloneqq\;\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}.

(21)

Because both the likelihood

p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(H\tilde{x}_{0},\Sigma_{y}\bigr)

and the prior

p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu_{0},\Sigma_{0}\bigr)

are multivariate Gaussians, their product is proportional to another Gaussian.
Using Bayes’ theorem and taking logarithms,

\log p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid y,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\!\Bigl[(y-H\tilde{x}_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}^{-1}(y-H\tilde{x}_{0})+(\tilde{x}_{0}-\mu_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}(\tilde{x}_{0}-\mu_{0})\Bigr]+C.

(22)

Expand the quadratic terms and collect everything that depends on
\tilde{x}_{0}
:

\displaystyle\log p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid y,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+H^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}^{-1}H\bigr)\tilde{x}_{0}+\tilde{x}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu_{0}+H^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}^{-1}y\bigr)+C^{\prime}.

(23)

The exponent is a (shifted) quadratic form in
\tilde{x}_{0}
; completing the square yields

\log p(\tilde{x}_{0}\mid y,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta})=-\tfrac{1}{2}\!\bigl(\tilde{x}_{0}-\mu_{\mathrm{post}}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{-1}\bigl(\tilde{x}_{0}-\mu_{\mathrm{post}}\bigr)+C^{\prime\prime},

(24)

where

\boxed{\;\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=\bigl(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+H^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}^{-1}H\bigr)^{-1}\;}\quad\text{and}\quad\boxed{\;\mu_{\mathrm{post}}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}\,\bigl(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\mu_{0}+H^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{y}^{-1}y\bigr)\;}.

(25)

Substituting
\Sigma_{0}^{-1}=\rho_{t}^{-2}\mathbb{I}_{d}
and

\Sigma_{y}^{-1}=(r_{t}^{2}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{m})^{-1}

gives precisely the expressions in (
9
).
Hence the posterior distribution is the multivariate Gaussian stated in the proposition.
∎

V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and

\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(s_{1},\dots,s_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
where
r=\operatorname{rank}(H)
and
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}>0
.
For any scalars
\eta_{1}\geq 0
and
\eta_{2}>0
define

W\coloneqq\eta_{1}HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}\mathbb{I}_{m},\qquad Y\coloneqq\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}H.

Then

W^{-1}=U\,\operatorname{diag}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\eta_{1}s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}}\Bigr)_{i=1}^{m}U^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad Y^{-1}=V\,\operatorname{diag}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\,1+s_{i}^{2}/(\eta_{1}s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2})\,}\Bigr)_{i=1}^{d}V^{\mathsf{T}}.

(26)

(When

s_{i}\!=\!0

Proof.

(i) Inverting
W
.

Using the SVD,

W=\eta_{1}U\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf{T}}U^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}U\mathbb{I}_{m}U^{\mathsf{T}}=U\!\bigl(\eta_{1}\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}\mathbb{I}_{m}\bigr)U^{\mathsf{T}}.

U

and simplify with

Proposition A.2
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
,
\eta_{1}\geq 0
and
\eta_{2}>0
.
Define

W\;=\;\eta_{1}\,HH^{\mathsf{T}}+\eta_{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{m},\qquad Y\;=\;\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}H.

For any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
consider the
strictly convex quadratic

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{x}_{0})\;=\;\bigl\|\tilde{x}_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\;+\;\bigl\|y-H\tilde{x}_{0}\bigr\|_{W^{-1}}^{2},\qquad\|v\|_{W^{-1}}^{2}=v^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}v.

Its unique minimiser is

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\mathrm{pm}}\;=\;Y^{-1}\!\bigl[\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y\bigr].

(27)

Proof.

Expand
\mathcal{L}
and take its gradient:

\nabla_{\tilde{x}_{0}}\mathcal{L}\;=\;2\bigl(\tilde{x}_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr)\;-\;2H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\!\bigl(y-H\tilde{x}_{0}\bigr).

Setting
\nabla_{\tilde{x}_{0}}\mathcal{L}=0
gives the

\bigl(\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}H\bigr)\tilde{x}_{0}\;=\;\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y,\qquad\text{that is, }Y\,\tilde{x}_{0}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y.

Because
\eta_{2}>0
implies
W\succ 0
, we have
W^{-1}\succ 0
and hence

Y=\mathbb{I}_{d}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}H\succ 0
; thus
Y
is invertible and
(
27
) follows.

Finally, the Hessian of
\mathcal{L}
is
2Y\succ 0
, so

\mathcal{L}
is strictly convex and the stationary point (
27
)
is indeed its unique global minimiser.

A.4
Proof of
Remark

2
.

Proof.

We start with the posterior expectation

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}H^{\dagger}H\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{2}}\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}H^{\dagger}y\right).

(28)

We want to examine the limit as

\frac{r_{t}}{\rho_{t}}\rightarrow 0,

(29)

which implies that
r_{t}^{2}/\rho_{t}^{2}\rightarrow 0
. To make this explicit, set

\epsilon=\frac{r_{t}^{2}}{\rho_{t}^{2}},

(30)

so that
\epsilon\to 0
. Then we can write

\frac{1}{\rho_{t}^{2}}=\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}\,\epsilon,

(31)

and the expression becomes

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=\left(\frac{\epsilon}{r_{t}^{2}}\,\mathbb{I}+\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}H^{\dagger}H\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{r_{t}^{2}}\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}H^{\dagger}y\right).

(32)

Factor out
1/r_{t}^{2}
from both terms:

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=\left(\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}\Bigl(\epsilon\,\mathbb{I}+H^{\dagger}H\Bigr)\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{r_{t}^{2}}\Bigl(\epsilon\,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\dagger}y\Bigr)\right).

(33)

The
1/r_{t}^{2}
factors cancel (since the inverse of
aM
is
M^{-1}/a
), yielding

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=\left(\epsilon\,\mathbb{I}+H^{\dagger}H\right)^{-1}\left(\epsilon\,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\dagger}y\right).

(34)

To analyze the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, decompose the space into two orthogonal components:

•

The range (or row space) of
H
, on which
H^{\dagger}H
acts as the identity.

•

Its nullspace, on which
H^{\dagger}H
is zero.

Let

P=H^{\dagger}H,

(35)

which is the orthogonal projection onto the row space of
H
. Then any vector
v
can be decomposed as

v=Pv+(I-P)v.

(36)

Notice that
H^{\dagger}y
lies in the row space (i.e.
P\,H^{\dagger}y=H^{\dagger}y
) and that
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}
can be decomposed as

\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}=P\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+(I-P)\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}.

(37)

Since the eigenvalues of
P
are 0 and 1, the matrix
\epsilon\,I+P
has eigenvalues
\epsilon
(on the nullspace of
P
) and
1+\epsilon
(on the row space). Hence, its inverse acts as:

•

Multiplication by
1/\epsilon
on the nullspace,

•

Multiplication by
1/(1+\epsilon)
on the row space.

Thus, we have

\left(\epsilon\,I+P\right)^{-1}\left(H^{\dagger}y+\epsilon\,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\bigl(H^{\dagger}y+\epsilon\,P\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr)+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\bigl(\epsilon\,(I-P)\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr).

(38)

Simplify this to obtain

\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\,H^{\dagger}y+\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\,P\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+(I-P)\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}.

(39)

Now, taking the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
:

•

\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\to 1
,

•

\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\to 0
.

Therefore, the limit becomes

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=H^{\dagger}y+(I-P)\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}.

(40)

Recalling that
P=H^{\dagger}H
, we rewrite this as

H^{\dagger}y+\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}-H^{\dagger}H\,\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\dagger}\bigl(y-H\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr).

(41)

Thus, in the limit where
\frac{r_{t}}{\rho_{t}}\to 0
, we indeed have

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{x}_{0}\mid\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta},y]=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\dagger}\bigl(y-H\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}\bigr).

(42)

This shows that, as the relative measurement noise
r_{t}
becomes much smaller compared to the prior uncertainty
\rho_{t}
, the posterior expectation is the projection of
\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}
onto the subspace
\{x:Hx=y\}
.
∎

Appendix B
Additional method details

B.1
Addressing Gaussian noise

Consider noisy image restoration problems in the form of
y=Hx+\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\epsilon_{y}}
, where
\epsilon_{y}
is the added noise. Then the measurement
y
can be decomposed to the sum of clean measurement
y^{\text{clean}}\coloneqq Hx
and measurement noise
\epsilon_{y}
. Calculating posterior mean
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
leads to:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}

\displaystyle=Y^{-1}[\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y]

(43)

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+Y^{-1}H^{T}W^{-1}y

(44)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y^{-1}H^{T}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}
is the extra noise introduced into
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
and will be further introduced into
x_{t-\Delta t}
.

To address Gaussian noise with known variance, we modify
Eq.

12
and
Eq.

3
as:

\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Sigma_{t}}[(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y]

(45)

x_{t-\Delta t}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pe}}(t,x,y)+b_{t}x_{t},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Phi}_{t}\mathbb{I})

(46)

Then
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
is:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Sigma_{t}}[(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y]

(47)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Sigma_{t}}(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+Y^{-1}H^{T}W^{-1}y^{\text{clean}}}_{\coloneqq\;\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{clean}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Sigma_{t}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}

(49)

Then the iteration of the sampling process is:

\displaystyle x_{t-\Delta t}

\displaystyle=a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}(t,x,y)+b_{t}x_{t}+\epsilon_{\text{new}},\quad\epsilon_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Phi_{t})

(50)

\displaystyle=a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{clean}}+b_{t}x_{t}+\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a_{t}\sigma_{y}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}}_{\coloneqq\epsilon_{\text{intro}}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}}

(51)

Suppose
\Sigma_{t}=V\mathrm{diag}\{\lambda_{t1},\cdots,\lambda_{td}\}V^{T}

\Phi_{t}=Vdiag\{\gamma_{t1},\cdots\gamma_{td}\}V^{T}
. Then the introduced noise
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a_{t}\sigma_{y}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}
is still a Gaussian distribution:
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,VD_{t}V^{T})
, with
D_{t}=diag\{d_{t1},\cdots,d_{td}\}
:

d_{ti}\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{a_{t}^{2}\,\sigma_{y}^{2}\,s_{i}^{2}\lambda_{ti}^{2}}{\bigl[(\eta_{1}+1)\,s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}\bigr]^{2}},&s_{i}\neq 0,\\[8.0pt]
0,&s_{i}=0,\end{cases}

(52)

The choice of and
\Phi_{t}
need to ensure the total noise injected to
x_{t-\Delta t}
conforms the iteration in
Eq.

3
.

\epsilon_{\text{new}}+\epsilon_{\text{intro}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})

(53)

To construct
\epsilon_{\text{new}}
, we define a new diagonal matrix
\Gamma_{t}(=diag\{\gamma_{t1},\cdots\gamma_{td}\})
:

\gamma_{ti}\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle c_{t}^{2}-\frac{a_{t}^{2}\,\sigma_{y}^{2}\,s_{i}^{2}\lambda_{ti}^{2}}{\bigl[(\eta_{1}+1)\,s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}\bigr]^{2}},&s_{i}\neq 0,\\[8.0pt]
c_{t}^{2},&s_{i}=0,\end{cases}

(54)

Now we can yield
\epsilon_{\text{new}}
by sampling from
\mathcal{N}(0,V\Gamma_{t}V^{T})
to ensure that
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
. We need to make sure
\lambda_{ti}
guaratees the noise level of the introduced noise do not exceed the pre-defined noise level
c_{t}
, we also hope
\lambda_{t_{i}}
as close as
1
as possible. Therefore,

\lambda_{ti}\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1,&c_{t}\geq\frac{a_{t}\,\sigma_{y}\,s_{i}}{(\eta_{1}+1)\,s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}},\\
\frac{c_{t}((\eta+1)s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2})}{a_{t}\sigma_{y}s_{i}},&c_{t}<\frac{a_{t}\,\sigma_{y}\,s_{i}}{(\eta_{1}+1)\,s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}},\\[8.0pt]
1,&s_{i}=0.\end{cases}

(55)

B.2
Addressing unknown noise and non-differentiable measurements

As the measurement noise
\epsilon_{y}
is non-Gaussian or unknown, it’s difficult to ensure the total noise introduced in
x_{t-\Delta t}
is
\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
. In this case, we calculate
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
using
Eq.

12
and update
x_{t-\Delta t}
using
Eq.

3
. Then
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
is:

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}

\displaystyle=\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+[(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}y]

(56)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+(Y^{-1}-\mathbb{I}_{d})\hat{x}_{0}^{\theta}+Y^{-1}H^{T}W^{-1}y^{\text{clean}}}_{\coloneqq\;\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{clean}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}

(58)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y^{-1}H^{T}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}
is the extra noise introduced into
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}
and will be further introduced into
x_{t-\Delta t}
:

\displaystyle x_{t-\Delta t}

\displaystyle=a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{pm}}+b_{t}x_{t}+\epsilon_{\text{new}},

(59)

\displaystyle=a_{t}\tilde{x}_{0}^{\text{clean}}+b_{t}x_{t}+\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a_{t}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}}}_{\coloneqq\;\epsilon_{\text{intro}}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}},

where
\epsilon_{\text{new}}
the noise added by diffusion process, which should be specifically designed to ensure
x_{t-\Delta t}
is sampled from correct distribution as in
Eq.

3
, i.e., the total noise
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
. However, as
\epsilon_{y}
is unknown noise, we have no information about the introduced noise
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}
. To solve this problem, we made the following principles: (i) despite that fact that we cannot guarantee
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
, we still hope
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}+\epsilon_{\text{new}}
is as close to
\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
as possible; (ii) small
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are helpful to maximize the alignment to measurement
y
. Notably,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\text{intro}}

\displaystyle=a_{t}Y^{-1}H^{\mathsf{T}}W^{-1}\epsilon_{y}

(60)

\displaystyle=a_{t}V\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{s_{i}}{(\eta_{1}+1)s_{i}^{2}+\eta_{2}}\right)U^{T}\epsilon_{y}

(61)

\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are two variables that control the noise level of
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}
. In the implementation, we still sample
\epsilon_{\text{new}}
from Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,c_{t}^{2}\mathbb{I})
. Then the problem becomes how to select
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
to meet the above 2 principles. For common image restoration tasks like SR, Deblurring, inpainting, Colorization, The maximum eigenvalue value
s_{\max}=\max\{s_{i}\}<=1
. Therefore, adjusting
\eta_{2}
is more likely to reduce the strength of
\epsilon_{\text{intro}}
.

Fix a tiny baseline
\eta_{1}\in[-0.4,0)\cup(0,0.1)
, for example, we can choose
\eta_{2}(t)=ka_{t}/c_{t}
, where
k
is a constant wihch depends on the measurement noise
\epsilon_{y}
. This meets Principle (i) by bounding the extra noise, and Principle (ii) by keeping both
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}
small enough to preserve measurement alignment.

Appendix C
Limitations

There are some limitations that deserve further study.

•

Theoretical extension.
While MAS can, in principle, be generalized to nonlinear inverse problems, explicitly formulating the likelihood term
p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
becomes challenging. Developing effective sampling techniques under this setting is a promising direction for future research.

•

Improved prior modeling.
Boys et al.
[
2
]
leverage Tweedie’s formula to compute the covariance of the Gaussian projection of
p(x_{0}\mid x_{t})
, which could be incorporated to enhance the MAS prior in
Eq.

7a
. Moreover, improved modeling of the likelihood
p(y\mid\tilde{x}_{0})
may further boost the performance of MAS.

Appendix D
Impact Statement

Our method can improve image restoration under challenging noise and degradation conditions, which may benefit applications in medical imaging, scientific visualization, cultural heritage preservation, and general photography. However, it is important to note that as with many generative and restoration models, our method could be misused for malicious image manipulation.
