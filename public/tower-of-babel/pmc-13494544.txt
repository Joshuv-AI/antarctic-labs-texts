Uncertainty-aware graph structure optimization with ensemble learning for enhanced cancer gene identification
Authors: Zihan Hu, Xiangzheng Fu, Ruyi Zheng, Yangyuan Chen, Tao Wang, Linlin Zhuo, Yiting Ke, Zhen Li, Quan Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe heterogeneity and noise of biological networks reduce prediction accuracy. We propose NexusGene, a computational framework that integrates uncertainty-aware graph structure learning (UnGSL) with clustering-enhanced ensemble learning to improve cancer gene identification in complex biological networks. UnGSL refines the graph structure by quantifying uncertainty in node features and adjusting edge weights to mitigate the influence of noisy or unreliable data. Additionally, a clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategy reduces prediction bias and false negatives by optimizing base learners based on gene distribution patterns and combining predictions from multiple models. NexusGene was benchmarked against six current methods across seven pan-cancer and 31 cancer-specific networks, consistently achieving the best overall performance, with particularly strong gains in heterogeneous settings. These results establish NexusGene as a robust and interpretable framework for cancer gene discovery across diverse cancer contexts.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of death globally, driven by complex genetic and epigenetic alterations that disrupt normal cellular regulation.1,2 Mutations, gene fusions, and abnormal expression of cancer-associated genes can lead to hallmark oncogenic behaviors, including uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and metastasis.3,4 These alterations often activate proto-oncogenes or inactivate tumor suppressor genes, ultimately driving malignant transformation. With nearly 19 million new cases and 10 million cancer-related deaths annually,5 the need to identify genes involved in tumorigenesis is critical. Such genes offer insights into the molecular underpinnings of cancer and represent potential targets for precision diagnostics and personalized therapies.

The advent of high-throughput genomic technologies has revolutionized cancer gene discovery. Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing are now routinely used to comprehensively detect genetic variants in tumor samples.6,7 Leveraging these platforms, researchers have identified numerous cancer-associated genes and established curated databases such as COSMIC6 and TCGA,7 which collectively integrate mutation data from over 542,000 tumor samples, covering 83 major cancer genes and 49 gene fusion pairs.8 These resources provide a standardized foundation for large-scale cancer genomics research. Despite these advances, conventional approaches remain limited by their reliance on known mutational hotspots and their inability to resolve the complexity of gene regulatory and interaction networks. Challenges such as high false-positive rates and incomplete functional annotation hinder the accurate identification of relevant cancer genes.

In recent years, machine learning has been widely applied to the prediction and identification of cancer driver genes. For instance, DriverML,9 developed by Han et al., quantifies the functional impact of mutations using supervised learning to classify driver genes. Similarly, PCDG-Pred10 integrates diverse genomic features to distinguish driver genes from passenger genes with high efficiency. These models typically rely on features such as mutation frequency, evolutionary conservation, and gene co-expression, and have achieved moderate success in identifying cancer-associated genes.11 However, their performance remains limited by factors including insufficient sample annotation, model overfitting, and high false-positive rates.11 More fundamentally, most existing models lack the capacity to represent biological network topology and often ignore the multimodal regulatory relationships that govern gene function. For example, miR-21 promotes metastasis by suppressing the tumor suppressor PTEN,12 while the lncRNA HOTAIR represses oncogenic loci such as CDKN2A/B by altering chromatin architecture.13 These examples underscore the complexity of gene regulation, which involves dynamic, context-specific interactions among microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcription factors (TFs), and protein-coding genes. Therefore, there is an urgent need to move beyond unidimensional analysis and develop models capable of integrating multi-omics interaction networks to enable accurate and biologically informed cancer driver gene identification.

Graph representation learning (GRL) models, including graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and graph attention networks (GATs), have emerged as powerful tools for cancer gene identification due to their capacity to model complex biological network topologies. By integrating multi-omics data—such as gene regulatory and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks—these methods have substantially improved prediction accuracy by capturing functional associations between genes. For instance, EMOGI employs GCNs to integrate multi-omics features with PPI networks for efficient pan-cancer driver gene screening.14 CGMega introduces a graph attention mechanism and transformer architecture to identify cancer gene modules informed by 3D genome data, achieving a 12% improvement in AUROC over conventional approaches.15 TREE combines transformers with a meta-path sampling strategy to jointly leverage multi-omics data and network structure, resulting in notable gains in accuracy, generalizability, and interpretability.16 Other models, such as MTGCN (multi-task node and edge prediction),17 HGDC (heterogeneous graph diffusion-based representation learning),18 and SCIS-CDG (contrastive learning-based graph comparison),19 further demonstrate technical advantages across varied cancer genomics tasks. Despite these advances, major challenges remain. First, the quality of node features in heterogeneous networks is often poor; for example, over 30% of edges in PPI networks may represent noise. Second, gene networks are frequently treated as undirected and static, disregarding the directionality and dynamic nature of biological regulation. Third, most existing models rely on point estimates and lack mechanisms to quantify prediction uncertainty, such as confidence in gene-gene associations, which may hinder clinical applicability.

To overcome key limitations in graph-based cancer gene identification, we developed NexusGene, a framework that combines uncertainty-aware graph structure learning (UnGSL) with a multi-level ensemble integration strategy. UnGSL incorporates a Bayesian graph attention network (B-GAT) to estimate uncertainty in both node- and edge-level features. This enables dynamic refinement of network topology by down-weighting noisy or unreliable interactions, improving the quality of graph-based representation learning. To further enhance model robustness, we designed an ensemble learning module that aggregates predictions from multiple base models using an adaptive weighting scheme. The ensemble is optimized through clustering-based fine-tuning, which calibrates model parameters according to gene distribution characteristics and mitigates both prediction bias and false negatives.20 Across multiple benchmark gene interaction networks, NexusGene consistently outperforms six existing graph-based models, demonstrating improved accuracy and stability in cancer gene identification. These results highlight the utility of incorporating uncertainty modeling and ensemble strategies for extracting robust biological insights from heterogeneous omics networks.

Results

Model overview

Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the NexusGene framework. The model begins with the collection of four types of genomic data, DNA methylation, gene mutations, copy number variations, and gene expression, from 16 cancer types, covering genes, TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs. These features are integrated with both homogeneous gene-gene networks and heterogeneous networks involving genes, TFs, miRNAs, and miRNAs/lncRNAs. NexusGene applies UnGSL to dynamically refine network topology and predict cancer-associated genes by jointly optimizing Margin loss and graph structure learning (GSL) loss. To improve generalization, a clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategy is employed, wherein the training data are partitioned into four clusters and merged iteratively to generate multiple model variants, whose predictions are merged to produce the final output.Figure 1Architecture of the NexusGene model(A) Multi-omics data collection.(B) Homogeneous/heterogeneous network construction.(C) Cancer gene prediction using UnGSL.(D) Ensemble learning with clustering-enhanced parameter fine-tuning. In module (C), during the pre-training phase, the omics feature-derived weight matrix and the omics feature matrix are jointly fed into the GNN model, which is trained using both the margin loss and the GSL loss. Node confidence is not computed at this stage. During the fine-tuning phase, the UNGSL component shares the same GNN encoder from pre-training, generates node confidence, updates the weight matrix, and is trained with the GSL loss. Unlike pre-training, this phase excludes the margin loss. A new weight matrix is also produced during this step. In module (D), each UNGSL component functions as a base learner, and the weight matrices generated by all base learners are integrated to construct the final weight matrix, referred to as the refinement map. The refinement map is then combined with the original gene network to train the cancer gene prediction model. Under the semi-supervised learning setting, the original gene network includes all gene nodes from both the training and test sets, although only the training labels are used to compute the loss. In contrast, under the supervised learning setting, the network includes only the training set nodes. It is crucial to emphasize that the refinement map is entirely derived from the training set nodes.

Performance comparison

To evaluate the effectiveness of NexusGene in cancer gene identification, we conducted comprehensive benchmarking across seven pan-cancer biological networks and 31 cancer-specific networks, comparing it with six state-of-the-art methods: LAP, DW, LINE, EMOGI, GAT, and TREE. As shown in Figures 2A and 2B, NexusGene achieved the highest AUROC and AUPRC scores across all pan-cancer networks, consistently outperforming the suboptimal TREE model. This highlights its superior ability to distinguish cancer genes from non-cancer genes. Boxplot analysis (Figures 2C and 2D) further demonstrates NexusGene’s robustness: its distributions exhibit higher medians and narrower interquartile ranges, indicating both stronger and more stable performance across networks. In contrast, models such as DW show wider variability, with lower minimum and less consistent maximum performance. Additionally, t-SNE visualizations of multi-omics features (Figures 2E–2H) reveal clear separation between predicted cancer (red) and non-cancer (green) genes in low-dimensional space, with minimal overlap. This suggests that NexusGene effectively captures feature distinctions, enabling confident classification. Furthermore, under identical supervised learning settings, we evaluated the NexusGene model, the classical GAT, and the state-of-the-art TREE model. As shown in Tables S3–S5, NexusGene consistently outperformed all comparison models across the three network scenarios, demonstrating superior generalization and stability, particularly compared to the transformer-based TREE method. In pan-cancer networks (see Table S3), NexusGene achieved an average AUPR of 69.40%, surpassing TREE by approximately 1.7 percentage points, indicating its enhanced capability to capture global structural and multi-omics interaction patterns across cancers. In cancer-specific homogeneous networks (see Table S4), NexusGene achieved an average improvement of about 5.3 percentage points, reflecting stronger noise suppression and structural discrimination capabilities. In heterogeneous networks (see Table S5), it maintained its leading performance, with an average AUPR gain of roughly 3.5 percentage points, further validating its robustness and scalability under complex heterogeneous regulatory relationships.

The overall performance gains can be primarily attributed to the UnGSL and the clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategies integrated into NexusGene. UnGSL adaptively adjusts edge weights according to node confidence, effectively mitigating low-quality node effects and reinforcing reliable regulatory connections, thus enabling more accurate topological representations in complex biological networks. Meanwhile, the ensemble learning module reduces prediction bias and enhances overall robustness through the fusion of multiple sub-models. This dual mechanism allows NexusGene to maintain stable and high-level performance across diverse cancer types and network densities.

We conducted experiments using different values of K to determine the appropriate number of clusters. Specifically, we set K ∈6,21 and performed evaluations on the STRING-db, IREF v15, CPDB, MULTINET, PCNET, MTG, and LTG datasets. The results summarized in Table S6 indicate that NexusGene maintains stable performance across different cluster settings, with K = 4 achieving the highest AUPR values (75.18%–81.84%) on most datasets, including IREF v15, CPDB, MULTINET, PCNET, and TMG. The choice of K = 4 not only yields the best predictive performance but also reflects a balance between biological interpretability and computational efficiency. The clustering results may correspond to major functional modules within cancer networks, such as proliferation, apoptosis, metabolism, and immune regulation. Although larger K values increase model complexity, considering overall accuracy, interpretability, and efficiency, K = 4 represents the most appropriate choice. Future work may explore adaptive clustering strategies to further enhance the model’s generalization ability.

Ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the contribution of core components (Figures 4D and 4E). And “w/o GSL” indicates that the model was trained without the GSL pre-training stage, “w/o UNGSL” denotes that the uncertainty-aware component was removed, and “w/o ensemble learning” refers to the model trained using a single subgraph without the ensemble learning strategy. The complete model achieved the best performance across all seven pan-cancer networks, showing a notable improvement in average AUPR. Removing graph structure learning (w/o GSL) led to a decrease of approximately 2%–6% in AUPR, demonstrating that pre-trained graph structure optimization is essential for capturing global network dependencies. Excluding the uncertainty-aware module (w/o UNGSL) caused a more pronounced decline, with some datasets such as PCNET and TMG exhibiting drops exceeding 10%, highlighting the importance of node-entropy-based uncertainty modeling in reducing noise propagation and enhancing high-confidence connections. Eliminating the ensemble learning module and training on a single subgraph also resulted in performance degradation, suggesting that multi-subgraph integration effectively mitigates structural bias and strengthens generalization. Overall, GSL, UNGSL, and the ensemble learning module function cooperatively in NexusGene, forming a complementary mechanism that enhances robustness and predictive accuracy in complex heterogeneous biological networks.

As shown in Figure 5A, NexusGene consistently outperforms baseline models across all four modalities, with particularly strong performance in GE and CNA-based predictions. These results underscore the model’s capacity to effectively integrate heterogeneous omics data for cancer gene identification. Figure 5B extends this analysis across seven pan-cancer networks, further validating the robustness of NexusGene under varying data conditions. Despite the complexity introduced by randomized data splits, NexusGene maintains high performance across all modalities, suggesting resilience to data noise and heterogeneity. Figures 5C and 5D reveal network- and cancer-type-specific dependencies on different omics features. For instance, CNA data contributes substantially to predictions in STAD, while GE features are more predictive in UCEC. This context-specific heterogeneity highlights the nuanced regulatory landscapes of different cancers. Overall, these findings demonstrate the effectiveness of NexusGene in leveraging multi-omics data and emphasize the need for cancer-type-specific interpretation of feature importance. Advancing cancer gene discovery

Additionally, we selected a subset of genes with unknown labels from the pan-cancer CPDB dataset and included them in the test set. NexusGene was then used to predict their labels, to assess the model’s capability to identify potential cancer-associated genes. The top-scoring genes included CCNA2, GTSE1, RACGAP1, MAPK6, SERPINE1, KSR1, ASAP2, NCK1, BCL2L14, SMAD5, SMAD9, ARID3A, SNW1, and L3MBTL1. We subsequently performed functional enrichment analysis on these genes, as illustrated in Figure 6. The analysis revealed significant enrichment in GO terms such as GO:0015631 (tubulin binding) and GO:0032233 (positive regulation of actin filament bundle assembly), suggesting their involvement in cytoskeletal regulation, cell division, and migration. Genes with microtubule-binding activity (for example, CCNA2, GTSE1, and RACGAP1) are essential for maintaining microtubule stability and chromosome segregation, and their aberrant activation can lead to cell-cycle dysregulation and genomic instability. Meanwhile, genes associated with actin filament assembly (such as ASAP2, NCK1, SERPINE1, and KSR1) contribute to stress fiber formation and cytoskeletal remodeling, promoting cell migration and invasion, which are characteristic hallmarks of tumor progression.Figure 6Enrichment analysis results of the predicted high-scoring genesTop: gene ontology biological process (GOBP) enrichment analysis. Overall, this gene set encompasses multiple core biological processes involved in cancer progression, including uncontrolled cell proliferation, enhanced cellular motility, aberrant signal transduction, and transcriptional dysregulation. Several genes in this set, such as CCNA2, MAPK6, and SERPINE1, have already been recognized as cancer driver genes, whereas the remaining genes exhibit strong functional and pathway-level associations with cancer phenotypes, suggesting potential oncogenic or tumor-promoting roles. Importantly, these genes were not originally annotated as cancer-related in the CPDB dataset; however, NexusGene assigned them high oncogenicity scores. The enrichment analysis, highlighting GO:0015631 (tubulin binding) and GO:0032233 (positive regulation of actin filament bundle assembly), indicates that these genes participate extensively in cytoskeleton remodeling, microtubule binding, and actin filament assembly, processes closely associated with cell proliferation, migration, and tumor metastasis. These findings demonstrate that NexusGene transcends the limitations of traditional database annotations by uncovering genes with latent oncogenic mechanisms that remain uncharacterized at the network structural and functional levels. This underscores NexusGene’s predictive power and biological sensitivity in capturing cancer-driving signals and identifying candidate oncogenes.

Discussion

In this study, we developed NexusGene, a computational framework for the accurate identification of cancer driver genes in complex biological networks. Through extensive benchmarking, we demonstrated that NexusGene consistently outperforms six state-of-the-art graph-based methods, including LAP, DW, LINE, EMOGI, GAT, and TREE, across diverse network settings, affirming both its predictive efficacy and architectural robustness. NexusGene achieved superior classification performance across both pan-cancer and cancer-type-specific networks. On seven pan-cancer datasets, it consistently attained AUC values exceeding 90.0% and achieved the highest AUPRC across all baselines, significantly outperforming the second-best model TREE. Boxplot analyses confirmed the model’s stability, while t-SNE visualizations illustrated its capacity to distinguish cancer from non-cancer genes via learned feature representations. In cancer-specific evaluations spanning 31 networks, NexusGene maintained its advantage, particularly in heterogeneous graphs. This high and stable performance is attributable to two core modules: the UnGSL module, which refines edge connectivity via node-level entropy and adaptive thresholding, and the clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategy, which fine-tunes model parameters based on topological substructure.

At the omics integration level, NexusGene demonstrated a strong capacity to incorporate diverse genomic modalities, including SNV, METH, GE, and CNA, with particularly strong predictive performance observed for GE and CNA. Performance across multiple pan-cancer datasets confirmed the robustness of its multi-omics integration. Importantly, the relative contribution of each omics feature varied across both network types and cancer contexts. For example, STRING-db networks were more dependent on GE, while Multinet emphasized METH; at the cancer-specific level, CNA contributed significantly in STAD, whereas GE was more predictive in UCEC. These results emphasize the necessity of context-aware modeling and highlight NexusGene’s flexibility in adapting to heterogeneous data landscapes.

To assess the biological relevance of the predicted genes, functional enrichment analyses were conducted. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in hallmark cancer-related processes, such as cell-cycle regulation, resistance to apoptosis, and kinase activity. KEGG pathway analysis further implicated these genes in canonical oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K-Akt/MAPK, and p53, as well as viral infection-related pathways, suggesting potential relevance to tumor microenvironmental dynamics and virus-driven carcinogenesis. Together, these findings delineate a multidimensional oncogenic landscape characterized by proliferation, survival, and immune-microenvironmental adaptation, and support a dual-driver paradigm involving both oncogene activation and tumor suppressor inactivation.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. The model’s performance depends on the quality and completeness of input networks, which may vary across data sources. Future work could extend NexusGene to incorporate additional omics layers such as proteomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics, as well as clinical annotations, to enhance predictive accuracy and translational potential. Moreover, while enrichment analyses offer indirect support for the biological plausibility of predictions, experimental validation of identified cancer drivers will be essential to confirm their mechanistic roles and clinical relevance. In summary, NexusGene offers a robust and interpretable framework for integrative cancer gene prediction, with demonstrated advantages in performance, data adaptability, and biological insight. Its flexible architecture and strong generalizability make it a promising tool for advancing our understanding of cancer genetics and guiding future therapeutic exploration.

Method details

Graph structure learning (GSL)

GSL refers to a family of methods that aim to automatically construct or optimize graph topologies to better reflect the true relationships among entities.26 Unlike conventional graph neural networks (GNNs), which operate on fixed graph structures, GSL learns the graph itself and is particularly suited for scenarios with missing, incomplete, or noisy connectivity data. GSL has demonstrated broad utility across domains, including social networks, recommender systems, and bioinformatics.27,28 In biological applications, GSL can infer or refine interaction networks among biomolecules, thereby improving representation quality and predictive performance. It also enhances model robustness to graph noise, facilitates the integration of multimodal omics data (e.g., sequence, structure, and function), and supports tasks such as driver gene identification and protein interaction prediction.29

Formally, let a cancer gene network be defined as G=(V,E,A,X), where V is the set of biomolecular nodes {v1,v2,⋯,vn}, including genes, TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs. E denotes the set of edges representing interactions or regulatory relationships. The initial adjacency matrix A∈Rn×n is defined such that Aij = 1 if there is an edge between nodes vi and vj, and Aij = 0 otherwise. The node feature matrix X=[x1,⋯,xn]∈Rn×d contains multi-omics features such as SNVs, CNAs, METH, and GE. We define the diagonal degree matrix D such that Dii=∑jAij+1, and apply row normalization with self-loops to yield the normalized adjacency matrix: Aˆ=D−1(A+I), where I is the identity matrix. In the GNN message-passing paradigm, node features are propagated through Aˆ, and layer-wise updates are expressed as:(Equation 1)H(l+1)=σ(AˆH(l)W(l)),where σ is a non-linear activation function and W(l) is the learnable weight matrix at layer l.

GSL firstly construct an adjacency matrix S∈Rn×n based on X, where Sij denotes the edge weight (cosine similarity) from node uj to ui:(Equation 2)Sij=Sji=(1−α)ϕ(Xi,Xj)+αAij,where ϕ(·) denotes cosine similarity. If Sij>0, a edge exists with corresponding weight; if Sij = 0, no edge is inferred. The structure S is typically passed to a GNN encoder to generate low-dimensional node embeddings Z = GNN(S,X). The training objective combines a graph structure loss LGSL, which measures the discrepancy between predicted labels and ground truth Y, and a regularization term Lmargin to enforce desired structural properties on S:(Equation 3)L=LGSL(Z,Y)+λLmargin(S,Y),

Despite its utility, standard GSL frameworks face two critical limitations.30,31 First, they rely solely on embedding similarity without considering the quality of the node information. Nodes with high uncertainty, such as those derived from noisy gene expression data, may propagate unreliable information through symmetric edges, degrading the representations of downstream targets such as driver genes. Second, the imposed symmetry constraint (Sij = Sji) hinders the modeling of non-reciprocal biological interactions. For instance, regulatory influence may flow unidirectionally from high-confidence TFs to low-confidence lncRNAs, a relationship not captured by symmetric graphs.

Uncertainty-aware graph structure learning (UnGSL)

UnGSL addresses key limitations of traditional GSL methods by incorporating uncertainty estimation and structure optimization, thereby enhancing information quality assessment and structural flexibility. This makes UnGSL a robust framework for tasks such as biological network modeling and multi-omics data integration. In biological networks, the information quality of biomolecular nodes, such as genes, TFs, and RNAs, can vary significantly. However, most existing GSL methods treat all nodes equally, overlooking differences in node confidence. This uniform treatment can amplify the influence of low-quality nodes, resulting in noisy message propagation and degraded model performance. Following previous work,32 we propose an improved version of UnGSL, which integrates node entropy-based uncertainty estimation, learnable neighbor partitioning, and edge reweighting into the GSL process. This approach enables dynamic filtering of neighbor contributions and enhances the model’s ability to learn reliable structures.

UnGSL quantifies the information quality of each node using Shannon entropy, computed from its predicted class probability distribution. For a node vi with classification probabilities, entropy is calculated as:(Equation 5)H(vi)=−∑c=01pi,clog(pi,c+ϵ),where pi,c denotes the classification probability produced by the GNN(A, X) model trained during the pretraining stage. And ϵ denotes an infinitesimal value, set to 1e−8 by default to avoid a zero value. High entropy indicates low confidence and high uncertainty (i.e., flat probability distribution). Low entropy reflects high confidence and high information quality (i.e., peaked distribution).

The confidence value Ci for node i is constrained within the range [0, 1]. Subsequently, a pairwise confidence matrix Mconf of size N × N is generated to refine matrix S:(Equation 7)Mconf[i,j]=Ci+Cj2,

The weights of the remaining edges are set to 0, yielding the final weight matrix Sfinal. The matrices Sfinal and X are then fed into the pre-trained GNN for fine-tuning. During fine-tuning, a new optimized weight matrix Sout is generated.

By incorporating node-level uncertainty modeling and edge reweighting, UnGSL enables selective, unidirectional information flow, allowing low-quality nodes to benefit from high-confidence neighbors while preventing reverse noise propagation. This design significantly improves robustness and accuracy in cancer gene identification tasks, while retaining the scalability and efficiency of embedded GSL methods.

Clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategy

To exploit functional and structural similarity within gene networks, we introduce a clustering-enhanced ensemble learning strategy.20 In the first stage, nodes within training set are clustered according to their multi-omics features into K (4 default) mutually exclusive clusters. Each cluster is sequentially excluded to construct its corresponding subgraph, for which a GSL model is pre-trained. Subsequently, a UNGSL model is fine-tuned on these subgraphs. Each subgraph’s UNGSL model shares the same GNN encoder as its corresponding GSL model and focuses on capturing local topological features, including node entropy–based uncertainty and confidence. During this stage, each UNGSL model generates an optimized weight matrix Sout. The four optimized weight matrices are integrated by taking the element-wise maximum across matrices, producing a refined graph that preserves high-confidence connections while complementing potential regulatory relationships uncovered by local subgraph models.

In the second stage, two independent classification models are trained in parallel: one on the original gene network and the other on the refined graph from the first stage. Both models share the same GNN architecture but are optimized on different graph structures to capture global and local structural information. Under the semi-supervised setting, the original gene network includes all nodes from both training and test sets, but the loss is computed only with the labels of training nodes. In contrast, under the supervised setting, the original network contains only training nodes. It is important to emphasize that the refined graph is derived solely from the training nodes.

During inference, average pooling is applied to the predicted logits of the two models on the test-set nodes, and the final carcinogenicity score is obtained using the sigmoid function. This two-stage ensemble framework integrates global and local information from the gene network, enhancing performance in terms of network heterogeneity, noise robustness, and generalization.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Datasets, task definition, and evaluation metrics

NexusGene was evaluated as a node-level binary classification task, in which known cancer genes were treated as positive samples and known non-cancer genes as negative samples. The analyses used pan-cancer networks, cancer-specific networks, LTG cancer-specific networks, multi-omics features, and gene annotations. Multi-omics data for genes, TFs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs were obtained from the TCGA database and processed following established pipelines14,16 to construct gene feature matrices. Biological network information included seven pan-cancer networks (five homogeneous and two heterogeneous) and 31 cancer-specific interaction networks compiled from public resources.16 Gene annotations used for supervised learning were curated from the NCG database,21 COSMIC Cancer Gene Census (CGC),33 and and PubMed-derived records obtained via DigSee,34 while negative gene sets were adopted from EMOGI.14 All datasets were standardized to ensure consistency across different network types and cancer contexts; details of the datasets are provided in Note S1, Tables S1, and S2. Because cancer gene identification is class-imbalanced, model performance was evaluated using AUROC and AUPRC, with AUPRC used as the primary metric.

Model comparison and performance quantification

NexusGene was compared with LAP,22 random walk-based DW,23 and four neural network-based approaches: LINE,24 GAT,25 EMOGI14 and TREE.16 All methods used the same predefined training, validation, and test splits; the training set was used for model optimization, the validation set for hyperparameter tuning, model selection, and early stopping, and the test set only for final evaluation. AUROC and AUPRC were calculated on the test set. To reduce the effects of random initialization and stochastic training, each experiment was independently repeated five times using different random seeds, and unless otherwise stated, results are reported as the mean of five independent runs. Boxplots were used to summarize cross-network performance distributions, with the center line indicating the median, boxes indicating the interquartile range, whiskers indicating the minimum and maximum values, and crosses indicating the mean. t-SNE visualizations were used only to qualitatively assess representation separability and were not used for statistical testing.

Robustness analysis, functional interpretation, and statistical methods

Model robustness and module contributions were assessed using few-shot learning, parameter sensitivity analysis, and ablation studies. The hyperparameters α, p, and λ were selected based on validation performance and cross-dataset stability, and ablation studies evaluated the contributions of GSL, UNGSL, and ensemble learning. Multi-omics contribution analysis included SNVs, DNA methylation, gene expression, and CNAs, and SHAP 0.41.0 was used where applicable to estimate feature importance. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on 14 high-confidence candidate genes predicted by NexusGene using Metascape for GO Biological Process, GO Molecular Function, and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. Multiple testing was controlled using FDR, and FDR <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All computational analyses were performed using Python 3, with key dependencies including h5py 3.1.0, numpy 1.19.5, scikit-learn 0.24.2, SHAP 0.41.0, and torch_geometric. Except for functional enrichment analysis, no Student’s t test, ANOVA, or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to the main model comparisons.
