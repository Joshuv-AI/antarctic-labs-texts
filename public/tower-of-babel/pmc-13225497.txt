Impact of ERAS pathway-based perioperative management on postoperative recovery and psychological status in thyroid cancer surgery patients: A retrospective study
Authors: Hui Xu, Miao Pan, Lin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols have demonstrated benefits in various surgical specialties, but their impact on psychological outcomes in thyroid cancer patients remains underexplored. This study comprehensively evaluated the effects of ERAS implementation on both postoperative recovery and psychological well-being in thyroid cancer surgery patients. This retrospective cohort study included 278 thyroid cancer patients undergoing thyroidectomy after 1:1 propensity score matching. Patients treated from January 2024 to December 2024 were managed according to the ERAS protocol (ERAS group, n = 139), while those treated from January 2023 to December 2023 received conventional perioperative care (control group, n = 139). The ERAS protocol comprised multimodal perioperative interventions including patient education, optimized anesthesia, early mobilization, and psychological support. Primary outcome was length of hospital stay. Secondary outcomes included early recovery metrics, pain scores (Visual Analog Scale), opioid consumption, hospitalization costs, psychological status (Self-rating Anxiety Scale, Self-rating Depression Scale), quality of life (36-Item Short Form Survey), and complications. The ERAS group demonstrated significantly shorter hospital stay and superior early recovery outcomes including earlier oral intake, mobilization, and drain removal (all P < .001). Pain scores were consistently lower at all postoperative timepoints, with reduced opioid consumption (P < .001). Total hospitalization costs were significantly reduced in the ERAS group (P < .001). ERAS patients showed significantly lower anxiety and depression scores throughout recovery (P < .001) and higher quality of life scores at discharge and 1 month postoperatively (P < .05). Total complication rates were reduced (23.74% vs 35.97%, P = .026), with significant decreases in transient hypocalcemia, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and hypothermia. ERAS implementation in thyroid cancer surgery is associated with improved postoperative recovery, reduced complications, decreased costs, and enhanced psychological well-being, supporting its adoption as a comprehensive perioperative care strategy addressing both physical and psychological dimensions of recovery.

1. Introduction

Thyroid cancer has emerged as one of the most rapidly increasing malignancies worldwide, with significant implications for public health systems globally. According to the GLOBOCAN database, approximately 586,000 new cases of thyroid cancer were reported globally in 2020, making it the 10th most common cancer worldwide.[1] In 2022, the global age-standardized incidence rate reached 10.1 per 100,000 in women and 3.1 per 100,000 in men, with projections suggesting a 34% increase in new cases by 2050.[2] This epidemiological landscape underscores the growing burden of thyroid cancer management and the critical need for optimized perioperative care strategies.

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) represents a multimodal, evidence-based perioperative care pathway designed to reduce surgical stress, accelerate functional recovery, and minimize postoperative complications.[3] Since its introduction in the early 2000s, ERAS protocols have demonstrated significant benefits across various surgical specialties, including colorectal, orthopedic, and gynecologic surgery.[4–6] The fundamental principles of ERAS encompass preoperative patient education and optimization, intraoperative standardization of care, and early postoperative mobilization, and accelerated return to normal diet and activities.[7] A systematic review and meta-analysis by Chorath et al demonstrated growing evidence supporting the implementation of ERAS protocols in thyroidectomy, showing significant reductions in hospital length of stay and costs without increasing complication or readmission rates.[8] Similarly, a prospective study at Shanghai Ruijin Hospital demonstrated that ERAS protocols significantly reduced pain scores, length of stay, and hospitalization costs compared to conventional management, while maintaining comparable complication rates.[9]

Beyond physical recovery outcomes, emerging evidence suggests that perioperative management may significantly impact patients’ psychological well-being. Thyroid surgery, particularly for cancer, has been associated with increased risk of anxiety and depression.[10] A large population-based study by Choi et al revealed that patients undergoing total thyroidectomy faced a 27% increased risk of depression before surgery, with postoperative risk escalating by 81% and remaining significantly elevated for over a year.[11] Furthermore, A study demonstrated that approximately 10% of thyroid surgery patients experienced psychological morbidity during the perioperative period, with somatic symptoms positively correlated with psychological status.[12] The psychological burden associated with cancer diagnosis, surgical stress, and concerns about postoperative outcomes can substantially impair patients’ quality of life and recovery trajectory.[13] However, limited research has specifically examined whether ERAS protocols can mitigate these psychological effects in thyroid cancer patients.

Most existing studies have focused primarily on traditional surgical metrics such as length of stay, complication rates, and pain scores, with limited attention to the psychological dimensions of recovery.[8,9] Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the impact of an ERAS protocol on both postoperative recovery and psychological status in thyroid cancer patients undergoing thyroidectomy. We hypothesized that ERAS pathway implementation would not only accelerate physical recovery but also improve patients’ psychological well-being and overall quality of life compared to conventional perioperative management.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Thyroid Surgery, The First People’s Hospital of Jiangxia District, Wuhan, Hubei University of Medicine, from January 2023 to December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients who underwent thyroidectomy for thyroid carcinoma between January 2023 and December 2024 were consecutively enrolled. The inclusion criteria were: age 18 to 75 years; pathologically confirmed thyroid carcinoma (papillary or follicular carcinoma); first-time thyroidectomy (unilateral or bilateral thyroidectomy with or without lymph node dissection); American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-III;[14] and complete medical records and follow-up data. The exclusion criteria were: concurrent other malignancies; severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction; psychiatric disorders requiring active treatment or cognitive impairment; preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy; emergency or revision surgery.

Patients treated from January 2024 to December 2024 were managed according to the ERAS protocol (ERAS group), while those treated from January 2023 to December 2023 received conventional perioperative care (Control group). Between January 2023 and December 2024, 360 patients underwent thyroidectomy for thyroid cancer. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 339 patients were eligible, with 178 in the ERAS group and 161 in the control group. After 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), 139 patients in each group were included in the final analysis.

2.2. ERAS protocol

The ERAS protocol was developed based on the ERAS Society guidelines for head and neck cancer surgery with adaptations for thyroidectomy,[15] in conjunction with the systematic review evidence for thyroid surgery[8] and the Chinese Expert Consensus on ERAS in Thyroid Surgery.[16] The protocol comprised the following key elements:

2.2.1. Preoperative phase

First, patient education and psychological support. Multimodal health education through videos, printed materials, and one-on-one counseling covered ERAS concepts, surgical procedures, and recovery expectations. The teach-back method verified comprehension while nursing staff addressed concerns to alleviate anxiety, integrated as fundamental ERAS components recognizing psychological stress reduction as intrinsic to the pathway. Second, medical optimization. Comprehensive assessment evaluated cardiovascular risk, thyroid function, respiratory status, and nutritional status using Nutritional Risk Screening 2002. Patients received targeted interventions including pulmonary therapy, thyroid hormone normalization, and smoking/alcohol cessation counseling for minimum 2 weeks. Third, preoperative preparation. Optimized fasting permitted solids until 6 hours and clear liquids until 2 hours before surgery. Carbohydrate loading (800 mL at 10 hours, 400 mL at 2 hours preoperatively) reduced insulin resistance and anxiety. Skin preparation involved cleansing without routine hair removal. Patients performed respiratory training including diaphragmatic breathing 3 times daily for 10 to 20 minutes.

2.2.2. Intraoperative phase

First, anesthetic management. General anesthesia was induced using short-acting agents (propofol, remifentanil, rocuronium) to facilitate rapid emergence. Ultrasound-guided bilateral superficial cervical plexus block or local anesthetic infiltration was routinely performed, which effectively reduced opioid consumption and the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). The depth of anesthesia was monitored with the Bispectral Index and maintained between 40 and 60. Second, fluid management and hemodynamic optimization. A goal-directed fluid therapy strategy was adopted, primarily utilizing balanced crystalloid solutions to maintain euvolemia and avoid fluid overload. This approach aimed to ensure tissue perfusion, promote wound healing, and reduce cardiopulmonary complications. Third, hypothermia prevention and organ preservation. Active warming measures, including forced-air warming blankets, were used to maintain core body temperature ≥ 36°C. Meticulous surgical techniques were emphasized to preserve the recurrent and superior laryngeal nerves, with intraoperative neuromonitoring employed when necessary. Parathyroid glands were preserved in situ or autotransplanted when necessary, utilizing carbon nanoparticle imaging for enhanced identification. Lymphatic structures were carefully managed to prevent chylous fistula formation.

2.2.3. Postoperative phase

First, early recovery. Patients received water after awakening, advancing to cool liquids at 6 hours then regular diet targeting 25 to 30 kcal/kg/day. Early mobilization began with in-bed exercises, progressing to ambulation for minimum 2 hours on postoperative day 1. Second, complication management. Multimodal PONV prophylaxis combined antiemetics with non-pharmacological approaches. Prophylactic calcium and vitamin D supplementation prevented symptomatic hypocalcemia. Vigilant 12 to 48 hour monitoring detected hemorrhage, respiratory difficulty, nerve injury, and hypocalcemia. Nursing staff provided continuous emotional support through proactive communication throughout recovery. Third, rehabilitation. Individualized neck functional exercises began on postoperative day 1, including neck rotation and shoulder movements performed 5 to 10 repetitions 3 times daily. Thyroid hormone replacement initiated based on surgical extent. Drains were removed when criteria met. Structured follow-up provided guidance with thyroid function testing at 4 to 6 weeks and ultrasound at 3 to 6 months.

The control group received conventional perioperative care including preoperative fasting from midnight, restriction of oral intake for 6 hours after surgery, encouraged ambulation starting from 10 to 12 hours postoperatively, and routine postoperative monitoring.

2.3. Outcome measures

2.3.1. Primary outcome

The primary outcome was length of hospital stay, defined as days from surgery to discharge meeting standard discharge criteria.

2.3.2. Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes encompassed multiple domains of recovery and safety:

Early recovery metrics: time to first postoperative oral intake (hours from surgery completion to first liquid or food consumption), time to first postoperative ambulation (hours from surgery completion to first out-of-bed walking), time to drain removal (days from surgery to removal of surgical drains), total drainage volume (cumulative output in milliliters from all drains until removal), and 30-day readmission rate (proportion of patients requiring hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge for any cause related to the surgery).

Psychological status was assessed by Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS) preoperatively, on postoperative day 3, and at discharge. The SAS (Cronbach α = 0.82) is a 20-item scale used to measure the frequency of anxiety symptoms, with each item scored on a 4-point Likert scale.[18,19] The total raw score (ranging from 20–80) is converted to a standard index score by multiplying by 1.25, with a standard score of 50 as the cutoff point for clinically significant anxiety; higher scores indicate more severe anxiety. The SDS (Cronbach α = 0.78) similarly contains 20 items assessing depressive symptoms, and its total raw score is converted to a standard index score using the same method, with a standard score of 53 as the cutoff for clinically significant depression;[20,21] higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms.

2.4. Data collection

Data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system by 2 independent researchers who were blinded to the study objectives. Extracted data included demographic information, clinical characteristics, surgical details, laboratory results, postoperative outcomes, and follow-up information. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior investigator.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp.) and R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent t-tests; non-normal data as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests.

Repeated measures data (pain scores, psychological assessments, quality of life) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects, fixed effects for time, group, and time × group interaction, and unstructured covariance matrix. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

To minimize selection bias and balance baseline characteristics between groups, PSM was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.02. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression including covariates: age, sex, body mass index, ASA classification, smoking history, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, surgical procedure type (lobectomy/total thyroidectomy), and lymph node dissection extent. The C-statistic for the propensity score model was 0.73 (95% confidence interval: 0.68–0.78), indicating good discriminative ability. Matching quality was assessed by standardized mean differences (< 0.10 indicating adequate balance). Missing data < 10% were handled by multiple imputation; otherwise complete case analysis was performed. Two-tailed P < .05 was considered significant. Given the multiple secondary endpoints examined, results should be interpreted with appropriate consideration of the exploratory nature of some analyses.

3.3.1. Early recovery metrics

Patients in the ERAS group demonstrated significantly superior outcomes across all early recovery endpoints (Table 2). Time to first ambulation (P < .001), with the ERAS group achieving mobilization within 6 hours compared to 10 hours in the control group. 3.3.3. Psychological status and quality of life

Baseline anxiety and depression scores were comparable between groups (Figs. 1B and 1C). Mixed-effects model analysis of anxiety scores revealed significant main effects of time (F = 76.60, P < .001), group (F = 56.15, P < .001), and time × group interaction (F = 15.22, P < .001). Post hoc comparisons demonstrated that SAS scores decreased significantly over time in both groups, with the ERAS group showing greater improvement at all postoperative time points (all P < .001). Similarly, for SDS scores, significant effects were observed for time (F = 22.62, P < .001), group (F = 73.30, P < .001), and time × group interaction (F = 5.76, P = .003), indicating superior psychological recovery in the ERAS group. (Fig. 1C)

SF-36 scores were comparable between groups preoperatively. 3.4. Postoperative complications

The overall complication rate was significantly lower in the ERAS group compared to the control group (Table 4). Among surgical complications, the incidence of transient hypocalcemia was significantly reduced in the ERAS group (P = .020), while permanent hypoparathyroidism rates showed no significant difference between groups (P > .05). Transient recurrent laryngeal nerve injury occurred less frequently in the ERAS group, though this difference did not reach statistical significance, and no permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve injuries occurred in the ERAS group compared to 2 cases in the control group. Other surgical complications, including hemorrhage requiring intervention, surgical site infection, chylous fistula, and seroma formation, all occurred less frequently in the ERAS group without reaching statistical significance (P > .05).

4. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study evaluated the impact of an ERAS protocol on postoperative recovery and psychological outcomes in thyroid cancer patients undergoing thyroidectomy. Our findings demonstrated that ERAS implementation was associated with significant improvements in both physical recovery metrics and psychological well-being compared to conventional perioperative care.

The primary outcome of reduced hospital stay in our ERAS group aligns with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses. A recent multi-institutional study by Machado et al reported significant reductions in total length of stay following ERAS implementation for thyroid and parathyroid surgery, Though their median stay reduction was more modest (9.2 vs 7.5 hours for same-day discharge patients),[24] Our study focused on inpatient thyroidectomy with more complex cases, explaining the longer absolute stay times but similar relative improvements. Similarly, Zheng and Zhang prospective study in Shanghai demonstrated a mean hospital stay reduction of approximately 1.5 days with ERAS protocols.[9] In our study, the reduction in hospital stay observed in the ERAS group may reflect differences in baseline practices across institutions as well as the comprehensiveness of our ERAS protocol, which incorporated extensive preoperative patient education, optimized anesthesia management, and structured early mobilization strategies. However, it is important to note that baseline conventional care practices varied across institutions, and direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

The significant reduction in postoperative pain scores observed in our ERAS group is consistent with emerging evidence on multimodal analgesia in thyroid surgery.[25] Yip et al[26] 2020 landmark study demonstrated that implementation of enhanced recovery protocols specific to cervical endocrine surgery resulted in substantial reductions in opioid consumption, with some patients requiring no opioids during their postoperative hospitalization. Our finding of reduced morphine equivalents supports this trend toward opioid-sparing strategies. The incorporation of regional anesthesia techniques, such as superficial cervical plexus blocks, likely contributed to improved pain control in our cohort, though we acknowledge that the relative contribution of individual ERAS components cannot be determined from our study design.

Perhaps most notably, our study demonstrates that ERAS implementation conferred significant psychological benefits, as evidenced by substantially lower anxiety and depression scores throughout the postoperative period. This focus on psychological well-being is particularly warranted given the established burden of psychological distress in thyroid cancer survivors. Longitudinal studies indicate that anxiety and depression can persist for years after treatment,[27,28] a finding underscored by a 2025 systematic review emphasizing the high prevalence of psychological distress, fear of recurrence, and impaired quality of life in this population despite a favorable prognosis.[29] The Chinese Expert Consensus on ERAS in Thyroid Surgery explicitly recognizes this challenge, advocating for integrated psychological support as a fundamental component of perioperative care.[16] Our results empirically affirm the value of this recommendation.

The mechanisms underlying the psychological benefits observed in our ERAS cohort likely involve multiple interrelated factors. Preoperative education and psychological support, which were integral components of our ERAS protocol, may have reduced preoperative anxiety and established realistic postoperative expectations. Evidence from other surgical specialties supports the psychological benefits of ERAS protocols. Ji et al[30] retrospective cohort study in patients undergoing percutaneous kyphoplasty demonstrated that ERAS protocols significantly reduced both depression and anxiety scores at postoperative follow-up periods, with the intervention group achieving faster psychological recovery and maintaining psychological stability compared to the control group. In total hip arthroplasty, a recent study by Xing et al[31] showed that ERAS protocols exerted beneficial influences on patient satisfaction while significantly mitigating pain catastrophizing and reducing surgery-related anxiety and depression, with these improvements persisting through 6 months postoperatively. Furthermore, a randomized controlled study by Zhang et al[32] in andrological surgery patients demonstrated that psychological care combined with ERAS management significantly improved SAS and SDS scores at discharge compared with baseline and control groups, while also shortening postoperative hospital stay and reducing pain scores. In our study, reduced postoperative pain through multimodal analgesia, earlier mobilization facilitating return to normal activities, and expedited recovery minimizing hospitalization duration may have collectively contributed to improved mood and reduced distress. The structured emotional support provided by nursing staff throughout the ERAS pathway, as an integral component of our protocol, may have further enhanced psychological resilience during the recovery period. Consistent with these psychological improvements, our study also demonstrated superior quality of life outcomes in the ERAS group, with significantly higher SF-36 scores observed at discharge and maintained at 1 month postoperatively, suggesting sustained benefits beyond the immediate perioperative period.

Regarding complications, our study demonstrated a significant reduction in overall complication rates, particularly for transient hypocalcemia and PONV. Based on the systematic review and meta-analysis, the incidence of post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia can reach up to 38% for transient cases and 3% for permanent cases under specific high-risk conditions, and a comprehensive approach integrating risk factor assessment and biochemical monitoring can effectively mitigate the burden of this complication.[33] Our observed rate of transient hypocalcemia (12.95% in the ERAS group) falls within the lower end of this reported range. The reduced incidence may be attributable to prophylactic calcium and vitamin D supplementation protocols and meticulous parathyroid preservation techniques, though the retrospective nature of our study precludes definitive causal inference. A 2025 comprehensive review by Liu et al identified multiple risk factors for post-thyroidectomy hypocalcemia, including surgical extent, inadvertent parathyroidectomy, and patient-specific factors such as vitamin D status.[34] Our ERAS protocol’s emphasis on careful surgical technique and standardized supplementation likely contributed to the favorable outcomes observed. The reduction in PONV represents an important quality of care improvement. PONV not only causes significant patient discomfort but can also increase the risk of cervical hematoma formation and prolong hospital stay.[35] Our multimodal PONV prophylaxis strategy, incorporating both pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches, appears effective and aligns with contemporary best practices in perioperative care. The cost reductions observed in our study demonstrate the economic value of ERAS implementation. Shorter hospital stays, reduced complication rates, and decreased opioid requirements all contributed to these savings. These findings support the value proposition of ERAS protocols in an era of increasing healthcare cost consciousness,[36] though institution-specific cost structures may influence the magnitude of savings achieved.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our results. The retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability and introduces potential for selection bias despite PSM. Temporal changes in surgical technique, anesthesia practices, or institutional protocols between the control period (2023) and ERAS period (2024) may have influenced outcomes independent of the ERAS protocol itself. The study population comprised predominantly low-risk, early-stage thyroid cancer patients with relatively low comorbidity burden, which may limit applicability to more complex patient populations. In addition, we could not assess the relative contributions of individual ERAS components to the observed outcomes. Future prospective studies with factorial designs would be valuable to identify the most impactful elements of ERAS protocols for thyroid surgery. While our psychological assessments demonstrated statistically significant improvements, the clinical significance of the observed differences and their long-term persistence beyond hospital discharge require further investigation.The relatively short follow-up period (30 days for readmissions, 1 month for quality of life) precluded assessment of longer-term outcomes such as cancer recurrence, permanent complications, or sustained psychological benefits.

Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable evidence supporting the implementation of ERAS protocols in thyroid cancer surgery, with the novel addition of demonstrating significant psychological benefits. The integration of psychological support and patient education within perioperative care pathways represents an important holistic approach to cancer care. Future multicenter, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings, assess long-term outcomes, and identify optimal ERAS protocol configurations for different patient populations and surgical indications.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ERAS pathway implementation for thyroid cancer surgery is associated with improved postoperative recovery, reduced complications, decreased healthcare costs, and enhanced psychological well-being. While these results are encouraging, they should be interpreted within the context of our study’s limitations. The observed benefits suggest that comprehensive ERAS protocols addressing both physical and psychological aspects of recovery may enhance the quality of care for thyroid cancer patients. Notably, tailored psychological interventions for thyroid cancer surgery patients are vital for addressing their unique challenges, including depression and anxiety; customizing these strategies to individual risk factors may further enhance their effectiveness. However, careful patient selection, individualized risk assessment, and ongoing quality monitoring remain essential components of optimal thyroid cancer management. Further research is needed to establish evidence-based guidelines for ERAS implementation across diverse clinical settings and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the psychological benefits observed.
