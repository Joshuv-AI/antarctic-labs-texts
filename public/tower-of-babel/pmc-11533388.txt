Concordance between humans and GPT-4 in appraising the methodological quality of case reports and case series using the Murad tool
Authors: Zin Tarakji, Adel Kanaan, Samer Saadi, Mohammed Firwana, Adel Kabbara Allababidi, Mohamed F. Abusalih, Rami Basmaci, Tamim I. Rajjo, Zhen Wang, M. Hassan Murad, Bashar Hasan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAssessing the methodological quality of case reports and case series is challenging due to human judgment variability and time constraints. We evaluated the agreement in judgments between human reviewers and GPT-4 when applying a standard methodological quality assessment tool designed for case reports and series.MethodsWe searched Scopus for systematic reviews published in 2023–2024 that cited the appraisal tool by Murad et al. A GPT-4 based agent was developed to assess the methodological quality using the 8 signaling questions of the tool. Observed agreement and agreement coefficient were estimated comparing published judgments of human reviewers to GPT-4 assessment.ResultsWe included 797 case reports and series. The observed agreement ranged between 41.91% and 80.93% across the eight questions (agreement coefficient ranged from 25.39 to 79.72%). The lowest agreement was noted in the first signaling question about selection of cases. The agreement was similar in articles published in journals with impact factor < 5 vs. ≥ 5, and when excluding systematic reviews that did not use 3 causality questions. Repeating the analysis using the same prompts demonstrated high agreement between the two GPT-4 attempts except for the first question about selection of cases.ConclusionsThe study demonstrates a moderate agreement between GPT-4 and human reviewers in assessing the methodological quality of case series and reports using the Murad tool. The current performance of GPT-4 seems promising but unlikely to be sufficient for the rigor of a systematic review and pairing the model with a human reviewer is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-024-02372-6.

Introduction

Case reports and case series remain as an essential study design in the medical literature, offering valuable insights into clinical anomalies, novel treatments, rare side effects, and lead to generating broader research hypotheses and development of subsequent controlled studies [1]. On occasions, case series are the only available evidence to guide clinical practice and they are synthesized in systematic reviews. For example, the Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines on aortic dissection [2] had to base some if its recommendations on a systematic review of small surgical case series [3]. However, the assessment of the methodological quality of these reports is challenging due to the lack of a control group and the inability to map noncomparative inferences to the classical epidemiological bias domains, such as confounding. The Murad tool was developed in 2018 to assess the methodological quality of case reports and case series and has been used in over a thousand systematic reviews providing face validity [4]. This tool evaluates methodological quality across four domains: selection, ascertainment, causality, and reporting, with specific signaling questions for each. It is widely adopted in evidence-based medicine for its structured approach. This tool is essential for making informed decisions in the absence of higher-level evidence, particularly in rare or unique clinical scenarios.

Nevertheless, assessing study quality is a time-consuming step in systematic reviews that can be associated with subjectivity and inconsistency [5]. Artificial intelligence (AI) has been proposed as a solution to enhance the process of systematic reviews [6, 7]. The advent of advanced models such as large language models (LLM) and the recent Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) may enhance the traditional process of methodological quality assessment. Such models can process large amounts of text, evaluate data and text, and may provide utility in standardizing and streamlining the appraisal of case reports and case series. Although LLMs have been tested for the appraisal of nonrandomized studies [8], to our knowledge, there has been no evaluation of LLM in using the Murad tool. The aim of this study was to compare human and AI (GPT-4) assessments of the methodological quality of case reports and case series. This work serves as a case study in exploring the potential for using AI as a research assistant to make systematic reviews more efficient and less time-consuming.

Methods

This study is reported in accordance with the guidelines of methodological research [9] and the LLM-SR framework for integrating large language models into systematic reviews [8].

Search strategy and study identification

We searched Scopus to identify all systematic reviews (SRs) that cited the publication of the Murad instrument for assessing the methodological quality of case reports/series [4]. We limited our search to systematic reviews in journal articles that had a publication year of 2023 or 2024, and were written in English. All original case report/series included in the identified SRs were included as long as the same tool was used for their appraisal. We excluded systematic reviews that did not use the Murad Tool, assessed only overall quality without reporting specific judgments about the domains of the tool, or did not conduct the assessment by two or more reviewers.

Data entry into chat-GPT

We conducted several pilot tests to determine the most effective method of obtaining methodological quality assessments using Chat-GPT (GPT-4). We built a GPT agent designed specifically to assess methodological quality of case reports and case series based on the 8 signaling questions in the tool. The agent can be accessed here: (https://chat.openai.com/g/g-c2mQM3Mjx-case-study-assessor). Using the ChatGPT interface, we directly uploaded each study to the agent which was instructed to provide its assessment in tabular format. We used the following prompt: “Please assess the methodological quality of the attached case report / series using the Murad tool’s 8 questions. Respond with ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, or ‘not applicable’ and provide reasoning for each response. Present the full assessment in tabular format with columns for each question, your response, and the reasoning.” The GPT agent was provided with a table outlining the 8 leading question of the tool and prompted to assess methodological quality. The agent was not specifically trained on using the tool.

Statistical analysis

One reviewer extracted methodological quality judgments from each SR and a second reviewer verified the extraction. We measured the raw percent agreement and the Gwet agreement coefficient between the judgments of the reviewers in each SR and GPT-4. We chose the agreement coefficient by Gwet over other measures such as kappa or pi statistics because it is less susceptible to the phenomenon of the kappa paradox that occurs when the agreement categories have extreme distribution and high agreement by chance [10–12].

Since 3 of the 8 signaling questions in the tool deal with causality and the tool publication suggests not using these questions if the question of the study does not address causality, we conducted sensitivity analyses in which we excluded systematic reviews that did not use these 3 causality items. We also evaluated the impact of the journal impact factor on the agreement measures, by stratifying the analysis by journals with impact factor < 5 versus ≥ 5. Considering the possibility that GPT-4 answers may change over time, we repeated the main analysis a second time and compared the results with the original analysis.

Analysis was conducted using the R package (irrCAC) [13] in R software package (R Core Team (2022). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL https://www.R-project.org).

Results

The initial search yielded 205 SRs, from which we eventually included 37 SRs that met the eligibility criteria and included 797 studies (Supplemental Fig. 1 and Supplemental Box 1 and Box 2). The first GPT-4 appraisal was performed between the 15th and 22nd of December 2023 and the repeat appraisal performed between February 1st and 7th of 2024. A sample response table for one of the included studies is provided in (supplemental Table 1).

The observed agreement ranged between 41.91% and 80.93% across the eight signaling questions. The chance adjusted agreement coefficient ranged from 25.39 to 79.72%. The results are summarized in Table 1. The lowest agreement was noted in the first signalling question about selection of cases.

Excluding the systematic reviews that did not use the 3 causality questions demonstrated similar results (supplemental Table 2). These 3 causality questions are most relevant in cases of adverse events and may be deemed inapplicable for studies that do not involve adverse events. There were also no important differences in agreement based on using a sample of studies published in journals with impact factor < 5 vs. ≥ 5 (supplemental Table 3). Repeating the main analysis using the same prompts demonstrated very high agreement between the two GPT-4 attempts, except for the first question about the selection of cases in the first and second rounds of appraisal conducted in December and February, respectively (supplemental Table 4). The agreement between human reviewers and the second round of GPT-4 was similar to the main analysis except for the first signalling question about case selection, which improved from 41.91 to 73.8% (supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

The current case study suggests at least a moderate agreement between the published judgments of SR reviewers and GPT-4 text analysis when assessing methodological quality of case reports and case series applying a widely used appraisal tool with face validity. Two of the signaling questions of the tool had the lowest agreement. The first one was about case selection, which is not surprising. It is often challenging to determine if a published case series includes the whole experience of the investigators, which would be a more representative sample [14]. This information is often not explicitly reported in published manuscripts and hard to determine. The second question related to causality and is about whether alternative causes that may explain the observation have been ruled out. Determining if a case series has done sufficient workup to exclude the differential diagnosis requires clinical judgment with substantial disagreement among clinicians themselves [15, 16]. The third signaling question asking whether the outcome adequately ascertained had the highest agreement. This question is typically easy to answer considering that case series are based on the presence of a clinical outcome that defines the cases.

We hypothesized that journals with higher impact factor, which theoretically may have better reporting [17, 18], would have better agreement between humans and AI. However, this was not the case. We also expected that signaling questions aiming to explore causality, such as in a case series that report drug side effects will be difficult to answer by GPT-4; however, agreement measures were similar when such cases were excluded. Expecting that AI output can vary over time, we repeated the same evaluation with the same prompt after approximately 5 weeks. The agreement between the two rounds was high except for the first question about case selection. The agreement between GPT-4 and humans was similar between the two GPT-4 rounds, except for that same first question, which substantially improved in the second round. LLM’s are probabilistic models that introduce randomness during their generation process, leading to different output even with identical prompts, model version and training data [19]. AI particularly struggles with inconsistent reporting styles across different publications, which can lead to variable interpretations of the same underlying data and this was reported in a previous risk of bias evaluation of randomized trial reports by AI [20].

The patterns of GPT-4 answers and rationale varied across the different domains. For the question regarding whether the patients represent the whole experience of the investigator, single case reports were often labeled as “unclear”, which is a reasonable response. In many other instances with a larger number of patients, the answer to this question included terms such as “comprehensive” and “thorough capture.” The question about ascertainment of the exposure was frequently answered as “adequate” when details about patient’s history were provided. The question about outcome ascertainment was usually answered with specific details, such as “outcomes such as intraoperative and postoperative complications are meticulously documented and analyzed, providing a clear understanding of the technique’s effectiveness and safety.” For the causality question about whether alternative causes had been ruled out, GPT-4 answers often included a specific differential diagnosis, for example” differential diagnoses such as pyogenic granuloma, candidiasis, and impetigo were considered and ruled out through pathological evaluation and mycologic/bacteriologic examinations.” On other occasions, the answers were generic such as” the report includes a detailed differential diagnosis and investigation to rule out other potential causes of the cardiac event.” GPT-4 answers to the question about dose-response effect were well justified with sufficient specificity, such as “although the amount of Kratom used is mentioned, the report does not establish a clear dose-response relationship between the quantity of Kratom and the severity of the stroke.” The question about adequacy of follow up was also answered with good justification. For example, GPT-4 concluded that the length of follow up was sufficient when the outcome was post-surgical complications and the patient was followed up until discharge, and on another occasion concluded that follow up of 2 weeks was sufficient for the outcome of acute liver injury. Studying the rationale provided by GPT-4 will be an interesting future study that can shed light on opportunities for further developments.

Overall, the performance of GPT-4 seems promising but unlikely to be sufficient for the rigor of a systematic review. Until further advancement in large language models and improved accuracy, we recommend pairing the model with a human reviewer to perform judgments in duplicate. In addition, GPT-4 can process vast amounts of information rapidly, providing a quick preliminary assessment that can guide systematic reviewers in other ways, such as highlighting areas of potential concern that warrant closer scrutiny, thus acting as a sieve that prioritizes human analytical efforts. Moreover, GPT-4 can serve educational purposes helping less experienced researchers identify possible risk of bias and understand the criteria used in such assessments.

This analysis included 797 case series, a fairly large number that represents a strength of this work. Limitations relate to the retrospective nature of published SR authors’ judgments and the fact that human appraisal is certainly not a gold standard for methodological quality with limited inter-rater reliability even between humans [21]. The findings are based on a specific methodological quality assessment tool and may not be generalizable to other tools or types of studies. The tool’s binary nature (Yes/No responses) may pose challenges for AI interpretation, potentially limiting the nuanced assessment that tools for more complex study designs might have.

Conclusion

GPT-4 had moderate agreement with human systematic reviewers when evaluating the methodological quality of case reports and case series using a standardized tool. AI and LLM’s may improve the efficiency of systematic reviews. At the present time, duplicating the appraisal of methodological quality by a human reviewer remains needed.
