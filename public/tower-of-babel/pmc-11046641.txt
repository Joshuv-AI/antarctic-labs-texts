A case of ANCA-associated vasculitis presenting as a chronic cough with night sweats in an adult male
Authors: Jacob Corum Williams, Ryan Malcolm Hum, Silke Brix, Pauline Ho
Published: 2023

Introduction

Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) describes a group of conditions characterised by small vessel inflammation in the presence of ANCA antibody production.1 AAV generally presents with systemic involvement including constitutional, ear nose and throat (ENT), respiratory, renal, abdominal, cutaneous or neurological involvement, with specific presentations depending on the specific subtype.1, 2 It is a rare disorder, with significant implications in cases of delayed or missed diagnosis, including organ-threatening and life-threatening disease.3 The variable and non-specific presentation of AAV makes delayed diagnosis not uncommon.3

Materials and methods

A white man in his sixties presented to the emergency department (ED) with a 1-month history of exertional dyspnoea, dry cough, intermittent night sweats and fatigue. He was otherwise fit and well, a keen cyclist, and took no regular medications. He was an ex-smoker. He had an elevated CRP of 116 and a normal chest X-ray. He was treated as a chest infection with antibiotics and a referral to the respiratory clinic was sent to rule out a lung malignancy. As an outpatient, he had a CT thorax showing chronic airway disease associated with inflammatory changes and nodules (Fig 1). Results and discussion

Repeat blood tests showed persistently raised C-reative protein and normal renal function. Urinalysis showed significant haematuria which prompted investigations for vasculitis. His ANA and ANCA-MPO antibodies were positive. He was seen on an urgent basis in the vasculitis clinic. Urinalysis showed haemoproteinuria with a new impairment of renal function developing in just two weeks. Renal biopsy demonstrated focal segmental necrotising glomerulonephritis and acute vessel vasculitis. As this patient had a confirmed diagnosis of AAV, he was started on a remission induction regime with IV methylprednisolone and IV cyclophosphamide with a good clinical response and preserved renal function.

Conclusion

AAV is a challenging diagnosis due to its non-specific and varied presentation and low incidence rate. It is unlikely that a diagnosis of AAV was considered by the ED, GP or respiratory clinic. This delay in diagnosis could have had catastrophic consequences.3 A low threshold for urinalysis and ANCA antibody testing should be implemented where clinically appropriate, to preserve renal function and reduce morbidity and mortality in patients with potential AAV.
