Fast Sampling for Flows and Diffusions with Lazy and Point Mass Stochastic Interpolants
Gabriel Damsholt, Jes Frellsen, Susanne Ditlevsen

Abstract
Stochastic interpolants unify flows and diffusions, popular generative modeling frameworks. A primary hyperparameter in these methods is the interpolation schedule that determines how to bridge a standard Gaussian base measure to an arbitrary target measure. We prove how to convert a sample path of a stochastic differential equation (SDE) with arbitrary diffusion coefficient under any schedule into the unique sample path under another arbitrary schedule and diffusion coefficient. We then extend the stochastic interpolant framework to admit a larger class of point mass schedules in which the Gaussian base measure collapses to a point mass measure. Under the assumption of Gaussian data, we identify lazy schedule families that make the drift identically zero and show that with deterministic sampling one gets a variance-preserving schedule commonly used in diffusion models, whereas with statistically optimal SDE sampling one gets our point mass schedule. Finally, to demonstrate the usefulness of our theoretical results on realistic highly non-Gaussian data, we apply our lazy schedule conversion to a state-of-the-art pretrained flow model and show that this allows for generating images in fewer steps without retraining the model.

Background

A basic goal of generative AI is to sample from a non-trivial target distribution, potentially in a very high-dimensional space, accessible only through a finite dataset of samples. Specifically, let
X^{(1)},X^{(2)},\ldots,X^{(N)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution with density
\rho_{X}
. We then wish to generate new samples
X\sim\rho_{X}
.

Various techniques for solving this basic generative problem exist. Many modern methods are based on
dynamical systems
. The idea is to learn the dynamics of a dynamical system with an analytically samplable initial law, typically taken to be standard Gaussian, and with final law
\rho_{X}
. Samples from
\rho_{X}
can then be generated by sampling from a standard Gaussian and integrating the dynamics from the initial to the final time.

We will use the flexible framework of
stochastic interpolants

(
2
)
which unifies flow and diffusion based generative models. We briefly introduce this framework and then state our contributions.

1.1
Spatially linear stochastic interpolant

The stochastic interpolant framework allows one to couple arbitrary distributions, and even arbitrarily many of them
(
1
)
, possibly in spatially non-linear ways and even using matrix coefficients
(
21
)
. We will exclusively work with the simplest and most commonly encountered setting, namely
the “spatially linear one-sided stochastic interpolant”
(
2
, eq. 4.15)
which we denote

stochastic interpolant
or
just

Definition 1.1

(stochastic interpolant)
.

Given independent random variables
Z,X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id),X\sim\rho_{X}
and non-negative scalar functions
\alpha,\beta:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right)
satisfying the below criteria, the
stochastic interpolant
or simply

I_{t}=\alpha_{t}Z+\beta_{t}X,\quad t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right],

with
\alpha,\beta\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
satisfying the boundary conditions
\alpha_{0}=\beta_{1}=1,\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}=0
and the monotonicity conditions
\dot{\alpha}_{t}<0
and
\dot{\beta}_{t}>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
.

Note that

One of the primary objects of interest is the following.

Definition 1.2

(schedule)

The function pair

in

or simply

The schedule is a hyperparameter that determines how

To proceed we define the following functions.

Definition 1.3
.

For an interpolant as in
Definition

1.1
with density
\rho:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\infty}}\right)

(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\eta_{Z}(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Z\mid I_{t}=x\right]\mathclose{},

“noise predictor”

\displaystyle\eta_{X}(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\mid I_{t}=x\right]\mathclose{},

“data predictor”

\displaystyle s(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\nabla\log\rho(t,x),

“score”

\displaystyle b^{\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\dot{\alpha}_{t}\eta_{Z}(t,x)+\dot{\beta}_{t}\eta_{X}(t,x)+\varepsilon_{t}s(t,x).

“drift”

The central idea behind the stochastic interpolant framework is to construct a family of

Theorem 1.4

(SDE)

Let

be any non-negative scalar function. Then the solutions to the SDE

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}}=b^{\varepsilon}(t,X^{\varepsilon}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(1)

With the above result we can pick any non-negative diffusion scale

Assumption 1.5
.

The data density satisfies

4
Intra-interpolant conversion formulas

In this section we fix the schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
and show how to convert between the functions in
Definition

1.3
. The proofs can be found in
Appendix

A
.

The following diffusion scale plays an important role in our work. We define it now and motivate it later.

Definition 4.1
.

Given an interpolation schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
satisfying the criteria in
Definition

1.1
we denote by
\varepsilon^{*}_{t}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right]
the scalar function

\varepsilon_{t}^{*}=\alpha_{t}^{2}\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}}{\beta_{t}}-\alpha_{t}\dot{\alpha}_{t},

where at
t=0
the equation is interpreted as a right-limit in the extended reals.

We start by showing that for a fixed interpolation schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
, any one of the functions from
Definition

1.3
uniquely defines all remaining functions through affine transformations that simplify using the above definition for
\varepsilon^{*}
. This result generalizes the well-known result relating the score to the noise predictor
(
2
, Theorem 8)
and
14
for the ODE case
\varepsilon\equiv 0
.

Proposition 4.2

(intra-interpolant conversion formulas)
.

For a fixed interpolation schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
and non-negative
\varepsilon\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
, each of the functions
\eta_{Z},\eta_{X},s,b^{\varepsilon}
uniquely defines all of the others on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, each function can be expressed in terms of the score as

\displaystyle\eta_{Z}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{t}s(t,x),

\displaystyle\eta_{X}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\lparen x+\alpha_{t}^{2}s(t,x)\right\rparen\mathclose{}/\beta_{t},

\displaystyle b^{\varepsilon}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\left\lparen\varepsilon_{t}^{*}+\varepsilon_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{}s(t,x)+(\dot{\beta}_{t}/\beta_{t})x.

The next theorem characterizes
\varepsilon^{*}
as the statistically optimal diffusion scale in a precise sense. This is essentially equation 13 in
8
but our proof is simpler.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume we are given statistically learnt estimates
\hat{\eta}_{Z},\hat{\eta}_{X},\hat{s}
or
\hat{b}^{\varepsilon}
of the true functions from
Definition

1.3
with remaining estimates given by
Proposition

4.2
. Let

solve the SDE (
1

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\hat{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t}}=\hat{b}^{\varepsilon}(t,\hat{X}^{\varepsilon}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},

solved forward in time
t
with
\hat{X}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
independent of
W
. Then the minimizer of the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the path measures of

and

\argmin_{\varepsilon\geq 0,\varepsilon\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right])}\kldiv\mathopen{}\left\lparen X^{\varepsilon},\hat{X}^{\varepsilon}\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\varepsilon^{*},

and the minimum is

\kldiv\mathopen{}\left\lparen X^{\varepsilon^{*}},\hat{X}^{\varepsilon^{*}}\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\int_{0}^{1}\varepsilon^{*}_{t}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left\lVert s(t,I_{t})-\hat{s}(t,I_{t})\right\rVert\mathclose{}^{2}\right]\mathclose{}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Unfortunately, the statistically optimal diffusion scale

is ill-behaved numerically in the sense that for any schedule

satisfying

Inter-interpolant conversion formulas

Definition 5.1

(linear interpolant)

Let
\overline{\alpha}_{t}\coloneqq 1-t,\overline{\beta}_{t}\coloneqq t

\overline{I}_{t}\coloneqq\overline{\alpha}_{t}Z+\overline{\beta}_{t}X

\overline{\eta}_{Z},\overline{\eta}_{X},\overline{s},\overline{b}^{\overline{\varepsilon}}

Proposition 5.2

For the linear interpolant
\overline{I}_{t}
from

\overline{\varepsilon}^{*}_{t}=(1-t)/t

\int_{s}^{t}2\overline{\varepsilon}^{*}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=2\mathopen{}\left\lparen\log(t)-t-\log(s)+s\right\rparen\mathclose{}

0<s\leq t\leq 1
and the integral is infinite for

Definition 5.3
.

For

we define the functions

c_{t}\coloneqq\alpha_{t}+\beta_{t},\quad u_{t}\coloneqq\beta_{t}/c_{t}.

The interpretation is that

The following proposition can be seen as a supplement to

Proposition 5.4

(inter-conversion formulas)
.

For a schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
and non-negative
\varepsilon\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
, the functions
\eta_{Z},\eta_{X},s,b^{\varepsilon}
are uniquely defined from any of the functions
\overline{\eta}_{Z},\overline{\eta}_{X},\overline{s},\overline{b}^{\overline{\varepsilon}}
associated with the linear interpolant. In particular,

\displaystyle\eta_{Z}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\overline{\eta}_{Z}\mathopen{}\left\lparen u_{t},x/c_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{},\quad\eta_{X}(t,x)=\overline{\eta}_{X}\mathopen{}\left\lparen u_{t},x/c_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{},

\displaystyle=(1/c_{t})\overline{s}\mathopen{}\left\lparen u_{t},x/c_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{},

6
Point mass schedule

We use the preceding results to extend
Definition

1.1
to transport to the target distribution from a point mass instead of a (non-degenerate) Gaussian. We delay the motivation to the next section.

Definition 6.1

(point mass schedule and interpolant)
.

A function pair
\alpha,\beta\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
satisfying

1.

\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}=0,\beta_{1}=1
,

2.

\alpha_{t}>0,\dot{\beta}_{t}>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
,

3.

\beta_{t}=o(\alpha_{t})\text{ as }t\to 0_{+}
(equivalently
u_{0}=0
),

4.

\alpha_{t}^{2}=O(\beta_{t})\text{ as }t\to 0_{+}
,

5.

\dot{u}_{0}\coloneqq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0_{+}}\dot{u}_{t}<\infty
,

6.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\beta_{t}/\alpha_{t})>0
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
(equivalently
\dot{u}_{t}>0
),

we call a
point mass schedule
. The associated interpolant we call a
point mass interpolant
.

Remark 6.2
.

The condition
\alpha_{0}=\beta_{0}=0
is sufficient and necessary for the density of the interpolant to collapse to a point mass at
t=0
. The condition
u_{0}=0
is sufficient and necessary for the initial drift to be analytically samplable; if otherwise
u_{0}>0
we would have to sample the non-trivial law of
\overline{I}_{u_{0}}\neq\overline{I}_{0}
to sample the initial drift, which is impossible in practice. The condition
\alpha_{t}^{2}=O(\beta_{t})\text{ as }t\to 0_{+}
is technical and can be relaxed; see the remarks at the end of the proof of
Theorem

6.3
. The assumption
\dot{u}_{0}<\infty
gives
u\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
, which is not strictly necessary but we impose it for simplicity. The last assumption is equivalent to
\dot{u}_{t}>0
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
so that
t\mapsto u_{t}
is a proper time change.

Remarkably, the initial drift of the point mass interpolant can be defined pointwise from the initial condition of the linear interpolant, even in the ODE case, as we show below.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)
be a point mass schedule as in
Definition

6.1
. Then given any non-negative bounded diffusion scale
\varepsilon\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
, the SDE (
1
) is well-defined in the classical sense for
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right]
with initial position
X_{0}^{\varepsilon}=0
, and the initial drift is well-defined in distribution. It holds that
\law\mathopen{}\left\lparen X_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\law\mathopen{}\left\lparen I_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{}
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
and
Theorem

5.5
still applies: in particular, the point mass SDE solution and initial drift relates to the solution and initial condition of the linear interpolant as
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=c_{t}\overline{X}^{\overline{\varepsilon}}_{u_{t}}
for
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
and
b^{\varepsilon}(0,X_{0}^{\varepsilon})=\mathopen{}\left\lparen\dot{c}_{0}-\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}(\varepsilon_{t}/\alpha_{t})\right\rparen\mathclose{}\overline{X}_{0}^{\overline{\varepsilon}}
.

The initial drift in

\varepsilon\equiv 0

\varepsilon_{t}\in O(\alpha_{t})

7
Lazy schedules under Gaussian data assumption

In this section, we make the oversimplifying assumption that
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
. This allows us to identify families of schedules for which the SDE drift is identically zero, the motivation being that such schedules minimize the energy required to transport from base to target measure. We call these schedules
lazy
. In the experimental section, we demonstrate empirically that the results we obtain under the Gaussian data assumption are useful even in realistic, high-dimensional and highly non-Gaussian settings.

7.1
Relation to flows and diffusions

To simplify the presentation we focus on two natural choices for the diffusion scale:
\varepsilon\equiv 0
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}
. The former corresponds to ODE sampling and reduces to standard flow matching under the linear interpolant (see
Definition

5.1
). The latter reduces to sampling a time-reversed Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as in diffusion modeling (
Proposition

B.5
) and we will henceforth refer to this as “statistically optimal SDE sampling”. Numerical integration is simpler for ODE sampling, but statistically optimal SDE sampling is maximally robust to approximation errors in the learned drift estimate as per
Theorem

4.3
. Throughout we use the notation
b\coloneqq b^{\varepsilon\equiv 0}
for the ODE velocity and
b^{*}\coloneqq b^{\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}}
for the statistically optimal SDE drift.

7.2
Identifying lazy schedules

Requiring the velocity and drift to be identically zero under the Gaussian data assumption removes one degree of freedom in the schedule.

Proposition 7.1

(lazy schedule families)
.

Assume that
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
. Then for all
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
it holds that

1.

b(t,x)=0\quad\iff\quad\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}=1.

In words, the ODE velocity is identically zero if and only if the schedule is variance preserving.

2.

b^{*}(t,x)=0\quad\iff\quad\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}=\beta_{t}.

This implies
\alpha_{0}=0,\beta_{t}=o(\alpha_{t}),\alpha_{t}^{2}=O(\beta_{t})\text{ as }t\to 0_{+}
. We have that
\dot{u}_{0}<\infty\iff\dot{\beta}_{t}=O(\sqrt{\beta_{t}})
in which case the schedule is a point mass schedule per
Definition

6.1
. It also holds for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
that
\varepsilon^{*}_{t}=\dot{\beta}_{t}/2
so that

\int_{s}^{t}2\varepsilon^{*}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\beta_{t}-\beta_{s}\quad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq 1.

Proposition

7.1
curiously shows that requiring the ODE velocity to be identically zero under the Gaussian data assumption amounts to choosing a variance preserving schedule, which is typically used in diffusion models but not in flow models. On the other hand, for statistically optimal SDE sampling it instead leads to point mass schedule, which is underexplored in the literature.

Remark 7.2
.

From
Proposition

7.1
together with the inevitable boundary conditions
\beta_{0}=0,\beta_{1}=1
we see that
\int_{0}^{1}2\varepsilon^{*}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=1
, i.e. the quadratic variation of the martingale part of solutions to our SDE from
t=0
to
t=1
are fixed at 1. This is remarkable since for
\alpha_{0}=1
the same integral is fixed at
\infty
by
Proposition

B.1
. This result holds even when
X
is not normally distributed. However, one easily shows that normalizing this quadratic variation by the total variance gives
\int_{0}^{t}(2\varepsilon^{*}_{u})/\beta_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=[\log(\beta_{u})]_{0}^{t}=\infty
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right]
, resembling the result from
Proposition

B.1
.

A natural next question is how to eliminate the last degree of freedom. We give two natural examples below and then discuss why the second example is useful in practice.

Example 7.3

(
\beta_{t}=t
)
.

Setting
\beta_{t}=t
gives, in the ODE case,
\alpha_{t}=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
. Interestingly, this is
not
the usual linear schedule used in flow matching models. Also
\dot{\alpha}_{1}=-\infty
. Considering instead the SDE case we get
\alpha_{t}=\sqrt{t(1-t)}
and
2\varepsilon^{*}_{t}=1
. When
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
, the solution to the SDE is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]}
with
W_{0}=0
. Also
\dot{\alpha}_{0}=\infty
and
\dot{\alpha}_{1}=-\infty
. The endpoint explosion of
\dot{\alpha}
in these schedules is worrisome.

Example 7.4

(
u_{t}=t
)
.

Define
d_{t}:=(1-t)^{2}+t^{2}
. Then for the ODE case requiring
u_{t}=t
gives the schedule
\alpha_{t}=(1-t)/\sqrt{d_{t}},\beta_{t}=t/\sqrt{d_{t}}
. Then
\dot{\alpha}_{0}=0
,
\dot{\alpha}_{1}=-1
and
\dot{\beta}_{0}=1,\dot{\beta}_{1}=0
. Considering instead the SDE case gives the schedule
\alpha_{t}=t(1-t)/d_{t},\beta_{t}=t^{2}/d_{t}
which is a point mass schedule per
Proposition

7.1
since
\dot{\beta}_{t}=O(\sqrt{\beta_{t}})
as
t\to 0_{+}
. We now have
\dot{\alpha}_{0}=1
,
\dot{\alpha}_{1}=-1
and
\dot{\beta}_{0}=\dot{\beta}_{1}=\varepsilon^{*}_{0}=\varepsilon^{*}_{1}=0
, and also
\dot{c}_{0}=1=\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\varepsilon_{t}^{*}/\alpha_{t}
so that the initial drift is zero per
Theorem

6.3
. The fact that all functions are bounded everywhere in both schedules is attractive from a numerical perspective.

We can further characterize and motivate the constraint
u_{t}=t
in
Example

7.4
as follows. Consider the log-signal-to-noise ratio
\lambda_{t}\coloneqq\log(\beta_{t}^{2}/\alpha_{t}^{2})
. Thus,
\lambda_{0}=-\infty
and
\lambda_{1}=\infty
.
3
3

3

Even if the schedule is a point mass schedule we still have the limit
\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\beta_{t}/\alpha_{t}=0
.
It might be natural to require
\lambda_{t}
to be linear in
logit-time
. Specifically, define
\tau(t)\coloneqq 2\log(t/(1-t))
, with inverse
t(\tau)=(1+\exp(-\tau/2))^{-1}
, i.e. the logistic function with growth rate
1/2
. Then requiring
\lambda_{t}=\tau(t)
is equivalent to requiring
u_{t}=t
.

7.3
Lazy schedule transformation of a pretrained flow matching model

Based on what we have shown so far, we can convert any flow or diffusion model (more generally any stochastic interpolant) with an arbitrary schedule to its corresponding lazy schedule variant (under the Gaussian assumption). The motivation is that numerically solving the ODE or SDE is easier when the dynamics are gentle. That the dynamics in practice become gentler under the lazy schedule even when we seriously violate the Gaussian data assumption is strongly suggested by the empirical results in the next section. To make the schedule conversion particularly simple, we focus on the
u_{t}=t
case considered in
Example

7.4
. We focus on converting from a flow matching model velocity
v^{\text{flow}}
, which is identical to the linear ODE velocity
\overline{b}
of the linear interpolant
\overline{I}_{t}
from
Definition

5.1
(see also
Section

B.1
).

Proposition 7.5

(linear velocity to lazy ODE velocity)
.

Define the schedule

\alpha_{t}\coloneqq(1-t)/\sqrt{d_{t}},\quad\beta_{t}\coloneqq t/\sqrt{d_{t}},\quad d_{t}\coloneqq(1-t)^{2}+t^{2},

as in
Example

7.4
and assume we are given access to the ODE velocity
\overline{b}=v^{\text{flow}}
of the linear interpolant from
Definition

5.1
. Then the velocity
satisfies

b(t,x)=((1-2t)/(d_{t}))x+(1/\sqrt{d_{t}})\overline{b}(t,\sqrt{d_{t}}x),

for all
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, the initial velocity satisfies

b(0,z)=z+\overline{b}(0,z)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proposition 7.6

(linear velocity to lazy SDE drift)
.

Define the schedule

\alpha_{t}\coloneqq t(1-t)/d_{t},\quad\beta_{t}\coloneqq t^{2}/d_{t},\quad d_{t}\coloneqq(1-t)^{2}+t^{2},

as in
Example

7.4
and assume we are given access to the ODE velocity
\overline{b}=v^{\text{flow}}
of the linear interpolant from
Definition

5.1
. Then the statistically optimal SDE drift satisfies

b^{*}(t,x)=(2/d_{t})((1-2t)x+t\overline{b}(t,(d_{t}/t)x)),

for all
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the initial drift is identically zero, i.e.
b^{*}(0,0)=0
.

We can restate
Propositions

7.5
and
7.6
as particularly simple algorithms for how to solve the lazy ODE and SDE using the linear ODE velocity. Pseudocode for these algorithms is given in
Appendices

D
and
D
.

We stress that we can prove results similar to
Propositions

7.5
and
7.6
but from a pretrained diffusion model instead of flow model, or more generally from a pretrained stochastic interpolant with any schedule and any training objective. This is omitted for brevity.

Remark 7.7
.

Note that
Propositions

7.5
and
7.6
generally do not hold exactly when we only have access to an approximate linear flow velocity
\hat{v}^{\text{flow}}\approx v^{\text{flow}}
learned from data, as is always the case in practice.

9
Discussion

We stress that since the PRX model outputs a learned approximation
\hat{v}^{\text{flow}}\approx v^{\text{flow}}
the flow-to-lazy conversion is inexact as noted in
Remark

7.7
. The fact that the lazy schedule performs statistically better than the linear flow schedule that the PRX model was trained with is therefore very convincing and leads us to hypothesize that training a model from scratch using e.g. the lazy ODE schedule from
Example

7.4
would lead to significantly bigger improvements in sampling quality.

We also stress that the slow convergence of the SDE does
not
allow us to conclude that the statistically optimal SDE generates poorer images than the ODE. The more interesting metric, which is harder to measure, is
distributional fit to the test dataset
and the tradeoff between that and ease of numerical integration for the ODE versus the SDE. We leave this to future work but note that the sample images in
Appendix

F
indicate that using a moderate amount of solver steps (say
n_{i}\geq 64
) for the statistically optimal SDE generates aesthetically pleasing and detailed images, especially with the lazy schedule.

On the theoretical side it would be valuable to extend the lazy schedule family beyond the Gaussian data assumption. We hypothesize that for the statistically optimal SDE one will end up with a point mass schedule under reasonable assumptions on the data density
\rho_{X}
.

to the ODE velocity (
\varepsilon\equiv 0
) , which when applied to the linear interpolant evaluated in
(t,(d_{t}/t)x)
reads

\overline{b}^{*}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{d_{t}}{t}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}=2\overline{b}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{d_{t}}{t}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}-\frac{\dot{\overline{\beta}}_{t}}{\overline{\beta}_{t}}\frac{d_{t}}{t}x=2\overline{b}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{d_{t}}{t}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}-\frac{d_{t}}{t^{2}}x.

Inserting this into the expression for
b^{*}(t,x)
gives

b^{*}(t,x)=\frac{1-2t^{2}-d_{t}}{td_{t}}x+\frac{2t}{d_{t}}\overline{b}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{d_{t}}{t}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\frac{2}{d_{t}}\mathopen{}\left\lparen(1-2t)x+t\overline{b}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{d_{t}}{t}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}\right\rparen\mathclose{},

which is still only valid for
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right]
. This proves the first part of the proposition. To analyze the case
t=0
we substitute
(t/d_{t})x
for
x
to get

b^{*}\mathopen{}\left\lparen t,\frac{t}{d_{t}}x\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\frac{2t}{d_{t}^{2}}(1-2t)x+\frac{2t}{d_{t}}\overline{b}(t,x)\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\to 0_{+},

since
d_{0}=1
and
\overline{b}(0,x)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}-x<\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As also
(t/d_{t})x\to 0
as
t\to 0_{+}
we see that the initial drift satisfies
b^{*}(0,0)=0
, as claimed. This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Relationship to flows and diffusions

In this section we rigorously describe how the stochastic interpolant from
Definition

1.1
relates to the objects studied in flow and diffusion models, respectively. The relationship to diffusion models is analyzed in section 5.1 in
(
2
)
but to the best of our knowledge the particular connection to the statistically optimal diffusion scale
\varepsilon^{*}
(see
Definition

4.1
and
Theorem

4.3
) as presented in
Proposition

B.5
has not been pointed out before.

First we prove some fundamental properties of the optimal diffusion scale
\varepsilon^{*}
.

Proposition B.1
.

For any schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
satisfying the criteria in
Definition

1.1
,
\varepsilon^{*}
from
Definition

4.1
satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0_{+}}\varepsilon^{*}_{t}=\infty\quad\text{if }\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}}{\beta_{t}}\text{ exists},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\infty}}\right)\quad\forall t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right),

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{*}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\infty\quad\forall t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right].

Proof.

We have

\varepsilon_{0}^{*}\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\varepsilon^{*}_{t}=\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\mathopen{}\left\lparen\alpha_{t}^{2}\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}}{\beta_{t}}-\alpha_{t}\dot{\alpha}_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{}=\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}}{\beta_{t}}-\dot{\alpha}_{0}=\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log(\beta_{t})-\dot{\alpha}_{0}=\infty,

if
\lim_{t\to 0_{+}}(\dot{\beta}_{t}/\beta_{t})
exists since
\alpha_{0}=1,\mathopen{}\left\lvert\dot{\alpha}_{0}\right\rvert\mathclose{}<\infty,\beta_{0}=0
as well as
\beta_{t}>0
for
t>0
. Strict positivity of
\varepsilon^{*}
for
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
follows from the monotonicity assumptions
\dot{\alpha}_{t}<0
and
\dot{\beta}_{t}>0
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
.
\varepsilon^{*}_{1}=0
follows from
\alpha_{1}=0,\beta_{1}=1,\lvert\dot{\alpha}_{1}\rvert<\infty,\lvert\dot{\beta}_{1}\rvert<\infty
.

To see that
\varepsilon^{*}
is not integrable, we use
\dot{\alpha}_{t}<0
for
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)
to bound
\varepsilon_{t}^{*}\geq\alpha_{t}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log(\beta_{t})
and write

\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon_{u}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}\int_{s}^{t}\varepsilon_{u}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\geq\alpha_{t}^{2}\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}\int_{s}^{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}\log(\beta_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\alpha_{t}^{2}(\log(\beta_{t})-\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}\log(\beta_{s}))=\infty\quad\forall t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right],

since
\beta_{0}=0
and
\beta\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right])
. This concludes the proof.
∎

B.1
Flow models

In a flow model (or more precisely a
flow matching model
) one considers the time-dependent random variable
(1-t)Z+tX
for
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
and
X\sim\rho_{X}
independent with
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
. One then utilizes that the ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Y^{\text{flow}}_{t}=v^{\text{flow}}(t,Y^{\text{flow}}_{t})

with velocity field

v^{\text{flow}}(t,x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\mid(1-t)Z+tX\right]\mathclose{}-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Z\mid(1-t)Z+tX\right]\mathclose{}

for
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
generates
\rho_{X}
, i.e.
Y^{\text{flow}}_{1}\sim\rho_{X}
whenever
Y^{\text{flow}}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
. One sees that this corresponds exactly to the linear interpolant from
Definition

5.1
, i.e.
\overline{I}_{t}=(1-t)Z+tX
and

\overline{b}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[\dot{\overline{I}}_{t}\mid\overline{I}_{t}=x\right]\mathclose{}=v^{\text{flow}}(t,x)

for all
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where we used the notation
\overline{b}\coloneqq\overline{b}^{\overline{\varepsilon}\equiv 0}
for the ODE velocity of the linear interpolant from
Definition

5.1
.

B.2
Diffusion models

In a variance-preserving diffusion model (or more precisely a
score-based generative model
) one corrupts data
\tilde{Y}_{0}=X\sim\rho_{X}
into noise
\tilde{Y}_{\infty}=Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
through an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process run from time
0
to
\infty
(or some finite but large
T
in practice which introduces a bias),

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{s}=-f(s)\tilde{Y}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+g(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{s},

with smooth and non-negative
f,g
satisfying

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\infty,

so that the stationary distribution is indeed standard Gaussian, i.e.
\tilde{Y}_{\infty}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\id)
.

One then uses Anderson’s time reversal
(
4
)
to construct the unique reverse-time process run from
\infty
(or
T
) to
0
that turns noise into data with the same joint distribution as the forward process. This reverse-time process depends on the score function of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with initial distribution
\rho_{X}
.

To compare diffusion models to flow models and stochastic interpolants one must compactify time via some
C^{1}
monotonically increasing bijection
\phi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right)\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)
satisfying
\phi(0)=0
and
\lim_{s\to\infty}\phi(s)=1
, with inverse
\psi\coloneqq\phi^{-1}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right)
. One can then define
Y_{\tau}\coloneqq\tilde{Y}_{\psi(\tau)}
for
\tau\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right)
, and by a limiting argument extend to
\tau\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
. Using similar standard stochastic calculus techniques as in the proof of
Theorem

5.5
one gets that

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{\tau}=-f(\psi(\tau))\psi^{\prime}(\tau)\,Y_{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+g(\psi(\tau))\sqrt{\psi^{\prime}(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{\tau},

with

W_{\tau}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\psi(\tau)}\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}(s)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{W}_{s}.

In particular, if we take
\phi(s)=1-\exp(-s)
so that
\psi(\tau)=-\log(1-\tau)
and
\psi^{\prime}(\tau)=1/(1-\tau)
, we get the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{\tau}=-\frac{f(-\log(1-\tau))}{1-\tau}\,Y_{\tau}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\frac{g(-\log(1-\tau))}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}.

As the following
Proposition

B.5
shows, when one chooses the statistically optimal diffusion scale
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}
in the stochastic interpolant framework, the corresponding reverse-time SDE becomes an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in the time range
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
. Before stating this result we need to introduce the
backward drift
as in
(
2
)
as well as the core result analogous to
Theorem

1.4
along with an intra-interpolant conversion result similar to
Proposition

4.2
.

Definition B.2

(backward drift)
.

For an interpolant as in
Definition

1.1
and
\varepsilon:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right],\varepsilon\in C^{1}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right])
we define the backward drift

\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{\varepsilon}}(t,x)\coloneqq\dot{\alpha}_{t}\eta_{Z}(t,x)+\dot{\beta}_{t}\eta_{X}(t,x)-\varepsilon_{t}s(t,x).

We refer the reader to
2
for a proof of the following result.

Theorem B.3
.

Define
\tau\coloneqq 1-t
and
\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}}\coloneqq-W_{\tau}=-W_{1-t}
. Then the solutions to the family of reverse-time SDEs

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}}=-\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{\varepsilon}}(\tau,\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}},

solved forward in reverse-time
\tau
with
\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{0}}\sim\rho_{X}
independent of
\overset{\leftarrow}{W}
, satisfy
\law(\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}})=\law(I_{1-\tau})
for all
\tau\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
.

The following proposition can be proved analogously to
Proposition

4.2
.

Proposition B.4
.

For a fixed interpolation schedule
(\alpha,\beta)
and
\varepsilon:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,\infty}}\right)
, the backward drift from
Definition

B.2
can be expressed in terms of the score through the equation

\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{\varepsilon}}(t,x)=\mathopen{}\left\lparen\varepsilon_{t}^{*}-\varepsilon_{t}\right\rparen\mathclose{}s(t,x)+\frac{\dot{\beta}_{t}}{\beta_{t}}x,

for all
(t,x)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,1}}\right)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We now state and prove our own result.

Proposition B.5
.

Let
\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{*}}\coloneqq\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{\varepsilon^{*}}}
be the statistically optimal (in forward-time) reverse-time drift for any (non point mass) stochastic interpolant as per
Definition

1.1
. Then the associated reverse-time SDE from
Theorem

B.3
is a time-scaled Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process of the form

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}=-\frac{\dot{\beta}_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}},

solved forward in reverse time
\tau
with initial condition
\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{0}}\sim\rho_{X}
.
If additionally the interpolant is variance-preserving, i.e.
\alpha_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}=1
for all
t\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
, then the reverse-time SDE reduces to

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}=-\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}}.

Proof.

The SDE from
Theorem

B.3
reads

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}}=-\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{\varepsilon}}(\tau,\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{\varepsilon}_{\tau}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}}.

Using
Proposition

B.4
with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}
gives

\overset{\leftarrow}{b^{*}}(\tau,x)=\frac{\dot{\beta}_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}}x,

so that the SDE from
Theorem

B.3
reduces to

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}=-\frac{\dot{\beta}_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{\tau}^{*}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}},

as claimed. Assuming now that
\alpha_{\tau}^{2}+\beta_{\tau}^{2}=1
, i.e. assuming that the schedule is variance-preserving, gives

\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}=(1-\beta_{\tau}^{2})\frac{\dot{\beta}_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}}-\alpha_{\tau}\left(\frac{-\dot{\beta}_{\tau}\beta_{\tau}}{\alpha_{\tau}}\right)=\frac{\dot{\beta}_{\tau}}{\beta_{\tau}},

so that the SDE from
Theorem

B.3
reduces to

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}=-\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}\overset{\leftarrow}{X^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2\varepsilon^{*}_{\tau}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overset{\leftarrow}{W_{\tau}},

as claimed.
∎

Appendix E
ODE and SDE numerical integration schemes

Below we give numerical schemes for solving the SDE from
Theorem

1.4
with drift
b^{\varepsilon}
and additive diffusion scale
\varepsilon
,

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}}=b^{\varepsilon}(t,X^{\varepsilon}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\varepsilon_{t}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

With
\varepsilon\equiv 0
the SDE degenerates to an ODE and e.g. the Euler-Maruyama scheme becomes the explicit Euler scheme. Note that the experiments either use
\varepsilon\equiv 0
or
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}
, and with the schedules we consider the quadratic variation from time
s
to
t
(
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
) under the latter diffusion scale,
\int_{s}^{t}2\varepsilon_{u}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
, can be calculated analytically (see
Proposition

7.1
and
Proposition

5.2
) and is therefore used in the below schemes and in our experiments.

For a total of
N>1
solver steps we use a fixed step size of
\Delta t=\frac{1}{N}
. We denote the solution after
n\in\mathopen{}\left\{0,1,\ldots,N\right\}\mathclose{}
steps by
Y_{n}
at time
t_{n}=n\Delta t
. For
n<N
the independent Wiener process increments from time
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
are denoted
\Delta W_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t\id)
and we define
V_{n}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\left\lparen\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}2\varepsilon_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right\rparen\mathclose{}\Delta W_{n}/\Delta t
. With the “corrected” solution at time
n
denoted by
\tilde{Y}_{n}
and initially defined as
\tilde{Y}_{0}\coloneqq Y_{0}
the update step from
Y_{n}
to
Y_{n+1}
is then defined as below.

Euler-Maruyama

Y_{n+1}=Y_{n}+\Delta tb^{\varepsilon}(t_{n},Y_{n})+V_{n}.

Predictor-Corrector

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Y}_{n}+\Delta tb^{\varepsilon}(t_{n},Y_{n})+V_{n},

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Y}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathopen{}\left\lparen b^{\varepsilon}(t_{n},Y_{n})+b^{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\right\rparen\mathclose{}+V_{n}.

Heun

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Y}_{n}+\Delta tb^{\varepsilon}(t_{n},\tilde{Y}_{n})+V_{n},

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Y}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathopen{}\left\lparen b^{\varepsilon}(t_{n},Y_{n})+b^{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\right\rparen\mathclose{}+V_{n}.

We use a variant of the predictor-corrector and Heun scheme in which the final, returned solution is
Y_{N}
so that the corrected final solution
\tilde{Y}_{N}
is never computed. This prevents us from ever having to evaluate the drift
b^{\varepsilon}
at
t=1
.
8
8

8

Evaluating the drift at
t=1
is in fact not a problem for us. The PRX flow model we use uses linear ODE velocity parameterization and no division by zero (which can potentially occur since
\alpha_{1}=0
) is introduced in our conversion formulas from this linear velocity to the ODE velocities and SDE drifts we consider.

Note that the “correct” step for the predictor-corrector scheme is equivalent to the Heun scheme. They both use an Euler-Maruyama “predict” step and only differ in whether the drift in this step is evaluated in
Y_{n}
or
\tilde{Y}_{n}
. This detail means that the Heun scheme uses two drift evaluations per step while the Euler-Maruyama and predictor-corrector schemes only use one. Since evaluating this drift function in practice amount to doing an expensive forward pass in a neural network the Heun scheme is therefore roughly twice as slow as it requires
2N-1
function evaluations instead of
N
. To compare the three schemes fairly, we used half the amount of steps for the Heun scheme compared to the predictor-corrector and Euler-Maruyama schemes.

For the PRX model and settings used in our experiments we find that the predictor-corrector scheme generally converges faster as a function of drift evaluations than the Euler-Maruyama and Heun scheme, especially for SDE generation. This is indicated by
Figure

and

6
which consider sample images generated with the standard linear flow model schedule using the ODE generation (
\varepsilon\equiv 0
) and statistically optimal SDE generation (
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{*}
), respectively, for the same prompt “Two husky’s hanging out of the car windows”. One sees that the predictor-corrector scheme converges to the reference image at
4096
steps (at which the Heun scheme has used
2048
steps) slightly faster than the Euler-Maruyama and Heun scheme. All schemes more or less converge to the same reference image, as expected.
