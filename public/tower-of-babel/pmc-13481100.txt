Effective Control of Synchronous Male Breast and Prostate Cancer with Androgen Deprivation Therapy: A Case Report
Authors: Megumi Furukawa, Naomi Gondo, Reiko Mitsueda, Akiko Arimura, Yoshitaka Fujiki, Junko Kawano, Shuichi Kanemitsu, Megumi Teraoka, Yasuyo Ohi, Yoshiaki Rai, Yoshiaki Sagara, Yasuaki Sagara, Shinji Ohno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONMale breast cancer (BC) is a rare disease, accounting for less than 1% of all BCs, and its synchronous occurrence with prostate cancer (PC) is exceedingly rare. Male BC is generally managed according to treatment strategies established for female BC; however, the role of androgen receptor (AR) signaling and androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) in male BC remains unclear. In particular, the optimal treatment strategy for patients with synchronous male BC and PC has not been established, and it is unknown whether ADT for PC exerts therapeutic effects on AR-positive male BC.CASE PRESENTATIONA 74-year-old Japanese man was diagnosed with synchronous estrogen receptor (ER)-positive, AR-positive, luminal A-like male BC (cStage IIA) and metastatic PC (Stage IV). Given that the prognosis was primarily determined by metastatic PC, ADT consisting of a gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist and an AR antagonist was initiated prior to breast surgery. Notably, marked tumor regression was observed in the breast lesion during ADT. Subsequent mastectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy revealed ypT1c pN0 disease.CONCLUSIONSThis case represents a rare clinical scenario in which standard ADT successfully controlled both metastatic PC and operable ER- and AR-positive male BC without upfront breast surgery. This case suggests that ADT may represent a potential initial therapeutic option for synchronous malignancies, particularly in patients in whom surgery is not feasible or can be deferred.

INTRODUCTION

Male BC is a rare disease, accounting for less than 1% of all BCs.1,2) The synchronous occurrence of male BC and PC is extremely uncommon, and no standardized treatment strategies have been established due to the limited number of reported cases. Herein, we report a case of synchronous cStage IIA male BC and Stage IV PC in which ADT effectively controlled both malignancies. In previously reported cases of synchronous male BC and PC, operable BC has typically been managed surgically before the initiation of ADT for PC. In contrast, our case demonstrated effective control of operable BC with ADT without upfront breast surgery.

This represents one of the few reported cases in which ADT successfully controlled both metastatic PC and operable ER-positive and AR-positive male BC in a synchronous setting. This case highlights the potential role of ADT as an initial treatment strategy in synchronous male BC and PC.

For male BC, genetic counseling regarding BRCA testing was offered, but the patient declined genetic testing. At the time of diagnosis, laboratory data showed no remarkable abnormalities, the patient’s general condition was good, and there was no significant pain from bone metastases. Although the BC was technically resectable and no evidence of regional lymph node involvement was observed, the urgency for surgery was considered low. Because the prognosis was primarily determined by PC, treatment for PC was prioritized, and ADT was initiated. In this case, ADT consisted of contemporary total androgen blockade with a GnRH antagonist plus an AR antagonist.

At 6 months after initiation of therapy, laboratory tests showed CEA 1.9 ng/mL and CA15-3 10.9 U/mL, both within normal limits, while PSA had decreased to 3.19 ng/mL. CT scan demonstrated shrinkage of the prostate and regression of the enlarged pelvic lymph nodes. Notably, marked shrinkage of the breast tumor was also confirmed (Fig. 3). As the patient remained in good general condition and both tumors were controlled, breast surgery was reconsidered in consultation with the urology department, and approximately 6 months after starting ADT, a mastectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy was performed. The breast tumor measured 37 mm radiologically before ADT and 12 mm pathologically in the surgical specimen, accompanied by fibrosis and histiocytic infiltration. The chronological clinical course, including ADT administration, serial tumor marker measurements, radiologic response, and surgical intervention, is summarized in Fig. 4. The pathological therapeutic effect was grade 1b. No metastasis was identified in the sentinel lymph nodes. The tumor was classified as ypT1c pN0, consistent with a non-pathological complete response. Hematoxylin and eosin staining of the surgical specimen revealed residual invasive carcinoma and DCIS (Fig. 5). The presence of residual invasive carcinoma and DCIS strongly supported the diagnosis of primary breast carcinoma rather than metastatic PC.

For postoperative adjuvant therapy in ER-positive male BC, tamoxifen is generally recommended. However, in this case, because the tumor had already been controlled by ADT and prognosis was determined by PC, no additional treatment for BC was considered necessary. The patient has remained under follow-up while continuing ADT for PC. Unfortunately, detailed information on the course of PC treatment is limited, as management was performed at another institution.

DISCUSSION

Synchronous occurrence of male BC and PC is extremely rare, and no standard treatment strategies have been established. Because of the paucity of cases, clinical trials specifically targeting male BC have not been conducted, and treatment is generally based on guidelines for female BC.2) When treating synchronous malignancies, priority is usually given to the cancer with the greater prognostic impact. Some reports have described male BC arising during ADT for PC, but no tumor regression was observed. For instance, in the case reported by Tamaki et al.,5) BC developed during ADT for PC, and breast surgery was subsequently performed.

To the best of our knowledge, our case represents one of the few reported cases in which contemporary ADT controlled both metastatic PC and synchronous operable ER- and AR-positive male BC without upfront breast surgery.

In our case, after consultation with urologists at another institution, metastatic PC was prioritized, and ADT was initiated before breast surgery. Additional endocrine therapy with tamoxifen was not introduced because suppression of gonadal function by the GnRH antagonist was expected to reduce circulating estrogen levels. Furthermore, the BC was considered operable, and surgery rather than neoadjuvant endocrine therapy would ordinarily have been the standard treatment.

Interestingly, regression of the BC was observed during the course of ADT. ADT is the standard treatment for metastatic PC, and previous reports have also shown its activity in male BC, particularly in ER- and AR-positive tumors, making it a rational therapeutic approach in synchronous malignancies.

Previous studies, including the classical report by Lopez et al., demonstrated that total androgen blockade with a GnRH agonist and antiandrogen could induce tumor regression in metastatic male BC, with a response rate of 64%, including 2 complete responses, highlighting the hormone dependency of this disease.6) However, those studies exclusively addressed inoperable or metastatic cases. Unlike metastatic male BC, where experimental endocrine strategies such as ADT may be considered due to limited options, operable cases are generally managed according to guidelines for female BC, with upfront surgery followed by adjuvant therapy.

A previous case report has described regression of male BC in the setting of synchronous PC and metastatic male BC treated with an LHRH agonist alone.7) However, in that report, the BC was inoperable and treatment was limited to systemic therapy.

In contrast, our case demonstrates that contemporary standard ADT—comprising a GnRH antagonist and an AR antagonist—simultaneously controlled both metastatic PC and synchronous, operable ER- and AR-positive male BC. This finding suggests that modern ADT may represent a rational initial treatment strategy, potentially as an alternative to upfront surgery, in the management of synchronous PC and male BC.

Possible mechanisms by which ADT may have contributed to BC control in this case include:

1)Suppression of peripheral estrogen production through gonadotropin inhibition with a GnRH antagonist, leading to reduced ER signaling.

2)Direct blockade of tumor growth signals through inhibition of AR-driven pathways.

3)Modulation of ER–AR crosstalk, whereby endocrine therapy may disrupt the interplay between androgen and estrogen signaling pathways.

With respect to the first mechanism, suppression of gonadotropin secretion by GnRH antagonists reduces gonadal androgen production. Because circulating estrogens in men are largely derived from peripheral aromatization of androgens, this may lead to a reduction in estrogen levels and subsequent attenuation of ER-mediated proliferative signaling. Therefore, reduction of estrogen levels induced by GnRH antagonists may have contributed to tumor regression in the present case.8) Given the strongly ER-positive (>90%) luminal A-like phenotype of the tumor, attenuation of ER-mediated signaling through estrogen depletion may have played a substantial role in the observed therapeutic response.

Regarding the second mechanism, AR blockade may also have contributed to tumor control, although its relative contribution compared with estrogen depletion remains uncertain. Clinical evidence supporting AR antagonists in BC remains limited but is evolving. Early-phase clinical trials in AR-positive, ER-negative BC, particularly within subsets of TNBC, have demonstrated proof of concept for AR-targeted therapy, with clinical benefit observed in a proportion of patients.9)

More recent studies suggest that the effects of AR antagonists may depend on tumor biology, particularly the balance between AR and ER signaling. Experimental studies have shown that AR blockade can suppress proliferation of AR-positive BC cells and may influence both androgen- and estrogen-related signaling through ER–AR crosstalk. However, the precise role of AR signaling in BC remains incompletely understood.10–12)

Interestingly, in contrast to studies evaluating AR antagonists, recent clinical evidence has also demonstrated clinical activity of AR agonists in selected subtypes of BC. A phase II study of the selective AR modulator enobosarm demonstrated clinical benefit in patients with AR-positive, ER-positive, and HER2-negative advanced BC, with treatment response correlating with AR expression levels.13) These findings suggest that AR signaling may influence treatment response in a subset of BCs. However, the apparently conflicting clinical activity observed with both AR antagonists and AR agonists in BC highlights the complexity of AR biology, and its precise role remains incompletely understood.

Regarding the third mechanism, accumulating evidence suggests that AR can modulate ER transcriptional activity in ER- and AR-positive BC in a context-dependent manner. Mechanistically, AR has been shown to competitively bind to estrogen response elements, displacing ER from chromatin and thereby suppressing ER-driven transcription, while also regulating ERα signaling through multiple pathways.14) However, the present case was treated with an AR antagonist, and it remains unclear whether these mechanisms are directly applicable to male BC. The unique endocrine milieu of male patients may result in biological responses that differ from those observed in female BC.

Additional immunohistochemical analysis of the surgical specimen demonstrated persistent ER expression (>90%), decreased PgR expression (30%), a HER2 score of 0, a low Ki-67 labeling index (<5%), and reduced but persistent AR positivity (70%). Compared with the pretreatment core needle biopsy, AR expression decreased from nearly 100% to 70% after ADT, while ER expression remained strongly positive. Although intratumoral heterogeneity cannot be excluded, these findings raise the possibility that ADT altered hormone receptor expression in the tumor. Whether these immunophenotypic changes reflect a biological effect of androgen deprivation or the selective survival of specific tumor cell populations remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Importantly, all of the above clinical and preclinical data are based on female BC, and their applicability to male BC remains uncertain. Given the markedly different endocrine environment between men and women, male BC likely has a distinct biological background. However, owing to the rarity of male BC, no clinical studies have directly addressed this issue.

Our case has several limitations. Detailed information regarding PC treatment was limited, as management was performed at another institution, and this is a single case report, precluding generalization.

In the present case, ADT was initiated before surgery for synchronous operable male BC and metastatic PC, resulting in regression of both malignancies. This case suggests that, in the unique clinical setting in which prognosis is primarily determined by Stage IV PC, ADT may exert meaningful antitumor activity against operable ER- and AR-positive male BC. Therefore, an initial surgery-sparing approach with ADT may represent a reasonable therapeutic option in selected patients with synchronous malignancies, particularly when general anesthesia carries substantial risk or when surgery can be safely deferred.

CONCLUSIONS

This case represents an extremely rare instance of synchronous operable male BC and metastatic PC. Upfront ADT resulted in regression of both malignancies. These findings suggest that ADT may represent a reasonable initial treatment option in selected patients with synchronous malignancies, particularly when general anesthesia carries substantial risk or when surgery can be safely deferred.
