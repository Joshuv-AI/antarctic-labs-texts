Heterogeneous trajectories of appendicular skeletal muscle mass change and cognitive impairment in community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Hongyue Liu, Mingliang Huang, Maoxin Liu, Qiuping Ma, Lili Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the heterogeneous trajectories of Appendicular Skeletal Muscle mass (ASM) change and their association with cognitive impairment in Chinese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults, thereby providing evidence for the early identification of high-risk populations and the development of individualized interventions. This study used longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2020. Demographic information and body composition data of participants were collected. ASM was calculated using the height-adjusted ASM index (ASM/height\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2}$$\end{document}, kg/m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2}$$\end{document}). Cognitive function was assessed across four domains: orientation, calculation, memory, and drawing. Group-based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) was applied to identify ASM change patterns, while logistic regression was used to analyze the association between different trajectories and cognitive impairment. Subgroup analyses were also conducted to explore potential interactions. A total of 7,973 middle-aged and older adults 45 years were included, and three ASM change trajectories were identified: low rapid decline (38.8%), moderate stable (41.1%), and high slow decline (20.1%). Compared with the high slow decline group, the moderate stable group (OR=1.44, 95%CI=1.19–1.75) and low rapid decline group (OR=2.11, 95%CI=1.67–2.71) had significantly higher risks of cognitive impairment. Subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction between hypertension and ASM trajectories (P<0.05), with a stronger association between rapid ASM decline and cognitive impairment in individuals with hypertension. ASM exhibits significant heterogeneous trajectories. The low-level rapid decline pattern is an important risk factor for cognitive impairment, and its adverse effects are more prominent in the hypertensive population.

Introduction

With the accelerating global population aging, cognitive impairment has become a critical public health concern threatening middle-aged and older adults’ health and societal development. Closely associated with dementia, it imposes heavy individual and societal burdens1. Over 50% of individuals with cognitive impairment progress to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or other dementias within 4–6 years2. In 2019, approximately 55 million people worldwide lived with dementia, projected to rise to 139 million by 20503. China accounts for 25.5% of the global population of AD and related dementias (ADRD) (13.14 million). By 2019, China’s standardized ADRD prevalence (788.3/100,000) and mortality (23.3/100,000) slightly exceeded global averages, with rising trends in cognitive impairment and dementia among older adults4. As the world’s most populous aging nation, China faces heightened challenges in cognitive impairment management. Thus, research on the early detection and intervention of cognitive decline is vital for health outcomes in middle-aged and older adults.

Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass (ASM), a key indicator reflecting individual muscle reserve and physical function, is commonly used in the diagnosis and stratification of sarcopenia5. In recent years, growing research has focused on the potential association between sarcopenia and age-related cognitive decline. A study by Hu et al.6 found that older adults with sarcopenia had significantly lower cognitive scores than those without, suggesting sarcopenia as an independent risk factor for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). Results from systematic reviews and meta-analyses also indicate that both sarcopenia and probable sarcopenia are significantly associated with cognitive impairment7. Additionally, studies on Chinese community-dwelling elderly populations have identified a significant positive correlation between sarcopenia and MCI, independent of age, gender, and other lifestyle factors8. Cross-cultural evidence has been provided by studies from multiple countries9, though this relationship was not confirmed in South Africa where socioeconomic disparities are more pronounced10. These discrepancies suggest that environmental, nutritional, and socioeconomic factors may play regulatory roles in the relationship between muscle health and cognitive function. Meanwhile, the reported prevalence of MCI in sarcopenic patients varies considerably across studies (7.5%–69.3%)11, reflecting significant heterogeneity in muscle mass changes among individuals. While numerous cross-sectional studies have revealed correlations between sarcopenia and cognitive decline, analyses based on single time points or simple linear trends fail to capture the underlying patterns of ASM changes over time. In fact, individuals exhibit marked heterogeneity in ASM trajectories, and such differences may exert varying impacts on cognitive aging. Therefore, characterizing potential ASM change trajectories using longitudinal follow-up data and exploring their long-term effects on cognitive function are crucial for deepening our understanding of the dynamic relationship between sarcopenia and cognitive decline.

Based on this, this study utilized longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) spanning 2011 to 2020. The data of the CHARLS is available for free on the Peking University Open Research Data Platform (https://charls.charlsdata.com/). Employing Group-based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM), it constructed heterogeneous Appendicular Skeletal Muscle mass (ASM) change trajectories among Chinese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years and above. It further explored the relationship between these dynamic change trajectories and cognitive impairment, aiming to directly identify high-risk subgroups and provide a basis for personalized therapeutic interventions for high-risk populations.

Methods

Study population

Data were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older12. A nationwide baseline survey was conducted in 2011, with follow-up surveys administered to participants every two years. This study used the 2011 wave (Wave 1, N=17,708) as the baseline, and follow-up data were collected in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), and 2020 (Wave 5). Cognitive function in 2020 was defined as the follow-up outcome. In line with the study objectives, the following inclusion criteria for participants were established: (1) Aged \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 45 years; (2) Completed the 2011 CHARLS baseline survey; (3) Completed all follow-up surveys in 2013, 2015, and 2020. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Missing data on cognitive function scores during the 2020 follow-up; (2) Self-reported cognitive impairment at baseline; (3) Missing key variables for calculating ASM (Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass). Based on the above inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 7,973 participants were finally included in the analysis. The sample selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Exposure variable: assessment of ASM

Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass (ASM) was calculated using a validated formula specifically developed for the Chinese population13. Previous studies have confirmed that this formula exhibits a high level of consistency with the assessment results obtained via Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA), making it an effective alternative evaluation method. The adjusted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document} of the equation model was 0.90, with a standard error of estimate (SEE) of 1.63 kg14. The calculation formula for ASM is as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} ASM = 0.193 \times Weight\ (kg) + 0.107 \times Height\ (cm) - 4.157 \times Gender - 0.037 \times Age\ (yr) - 2.631 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Wherein, a gender code of 1 was assigned to males and 2 to females. Body weight was measured using an Omron TMHN-286 scale, while height was measured with a SecaTM213 stadiometer. Muscle mass has an inherent association with body composition. Individuals with a larger body size may have greater muscle mass, whereas obese patients tend to have lower muscle mass15. To eliminate variations in body size, the ASM index normalized by the square of height(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {ASM/height}^2$$\end{document}, kg/m²) was used for analysis. This index was calculated by dividing ASM by the square of height (in meters).

All CHARLS protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Peking University. The IRB approval number for major household surveys, including anthropometric measurements, is IRB00001052-11015; the IRB approval number for biomarker collection is IRB00001052-11014.

Cognitive assessment

This study adopted the cognitive function assessment method from the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS)16. Participants’ cognitive status was comprehensively evaluated through face-to-face tests across four dimensions: orientation, calculation, memory, and drawing. The Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS) was used to assess orientation and calculation. Orientation items included the current year, month, day, weekday, and season. The total score for the orientation dimension was 5 points, with 1 point awarded for each correct answer. For the calculation test, participants were required to subtract 7 from 100 consecutively for five times, with 1 point given for each successful subtraction. Ten words were read aloud to each participant randomly, and immediate word recall was assessed by counting the number of words the participant could recall instantly. Delayed word recall was evaluated after the participant completed the depression scale, calculation, and drawing tests. The total memory score, defined as the sum of immediate and delayed word recall scores, was 20 points, with 1 point for each correctly recalled word. Interviewers presented a picture of two overlapping stars to check whether participants could draw them correctly, so as to test their drawing ability. Participants received 1 point if they drew correctly. The total cognitive score was the sum of scores from orientation (5 points), calculation (5 points), memory (20 points), and drawing (1 point), with a maximum possible total score of 31 points17. Cognitive impairment was generally defined as a total cognitive score less than 11 points18–20.

Covariates

Covariates were identified using the structured CHARLS questionnaire. Based on previous studies, this research included potential confounding covariates at baseline, which were categorized into the following groups: Sociodemographic factors: gender, place of residence, marital status, and educational level; Lifestyle and behavioral habits: smoking, alcohol consumption, daily sleep duration, and physical activity level; Health status and chronic disease history: self-reported health status (good/fair/poor), hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and chronic pain; Psychological and functional status: Activities of Daily Living (ADL) score, Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) score, depressive status, life satisfaction, hearing level, and visual level; Physiological and biochemical indicators: height, weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, grip strength, waist circumference, blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Physical activity was assessed based on self-reported frequency and intensity of weekly activities. Participants reported engagement in vigorous physical activity (VPA), moderate physical activity (MPA), and light physical activity (LPA). Active physical activity was defined as participation in moderate or vigorous physical activity for at least 10 minutes continuously on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 4 days per week. A binary variable was constructed (Active = 1 for moderate-to-vigorous physical activity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 4 days/week; Active = 0 otherwise). Body Mass Index (BMI) was defined as weight (in kg) divided by the square of height (in m). Depression was assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), with a total score of 30.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software (Version 4.5.1). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequency (percentage). For intergroup comparisons: Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for continuous variables; The chi-square test (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document} test) was applied for categorical variables. The longitudinal change pattern of Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass (ASM) was analyzed using Group-based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM), with the “traj” package in Stata used for model construction. Age was set as the time scale, and 1 to 5 potential trajectory classes were fitted. The fitting effects of different polynomial functions (linear, quadratic, cubic) were compared to select the optimal model structure. Model selection was based on a comprehensive evaluation of three criteria21: Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC); Average posterior probability (AvePP > 0.70); Proportion of members in each trajectory class (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}5%). After identifying the trajectory groups, binary Logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between ASM trajectory groups (independent variable) and cognitive impairment (dependent variable). Two regression models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted model; Model 2: Adjusted model, with adjustments for sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, anthropometric indicators, blood biochemical indices, and health-related factors. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 7,973 participants were included in this study, with a mean age of 57.2 years, among whom males accounted for 48.40%. A total of 1,588 participants developed MCI in 2020, with an incidence rate of 19.92%. Compared with the non-cognitive impairment group, participants in the cognitive impairment group were older on average, had a higher proportion of females, were mostly rural residents, and had lower educational levels. In terms of health status, the cognitive impairment group had a higher proportion of poor self-rated health, higher rates of depression and physical pain, higher ADL and IADL scores, and significantly poorer hearing and vision. Regarding lifestyle factors, the proportions of smoking and drinking were lower in the cognitive impairment group. In addition, the proportion of individuals classified as physically active was significantly lower in the cognitive impairment group compared with the non-cognitive impairment group. Physical indicators showed that grip strength was significantly lower in the cognitive impairment group, and ASM was significantly lower in the cognitive impairment group than in the non-cognitive impairment group at all waves. In terms of blood biochemical indicators, the levels of total cholesterol and HDL in the cognitive impairment group were higher than those in the non-cognitive impairment group. Overall, individuals in the cognitive impairment group were more likely to be older, female, rural residents, with lower educational levels, poor health status, declined daily living functions, and lower skeletal muscle mass and grip strength. Fit of the trajectory of changes in ASM

A 5-class trajectory model was constructed in an incremental manner and compared to determine the best-fitting model for the heterogeneity of dynamic changes in ASM during the study period (Table 2). The results showed that as the number of models gradually increased, the BIC value was the smallest in the 3-class model (2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document}BIC). All AvePP values were greater than 0.9, and the proportion of subgroups within each class was more than 5%. Thus, the 3-class model was identified as the optimal model in the study. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests

Subgroup analyses revealed that across subgroups with different characteristics, both the Moderate stable group (Class 2) and Low-rapid decline group (Class 1) showed an increased risk of cognitive impairment compared with the High-gradual decline group (Class 3). A significant interaction was identified between ASM trajectories and hypertension (P for interaction = 0.042). Among individuals with hypertension, the association between poorer ASM trajectories and cognitive impairment risk was significantly stronger (Class 1: OR = 2.11, 95%CI = 1.30–3.42); in contrast, while the association remained significant in individuals without hypertension, the effect size was smaller (Class 1: OR = 1.45, 95%CI = 1.21–1.89). No interactions with ASM trajectories were observed in subgroup analyses of residence, marital status, diabetes, heart disease, alcohol consumption, smoking, chronic pain, depression and physical. Discussion

In this study, we explored the change trajectories of appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) based on a nationally representative sample of 7,973 middle-aged and older adults in China. The results showed that there were three distinct ASM trajectories among Chinese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults, namely Class 1 (Low-rapid decline group), Class 2 (Moderate stable group), and Class 3 (High-gradual decline group). The baseline levels of the three ASM trajectories differed significantly, and the risk of cognitive impairment varied across different ASM trajectories. Furthermore, among individuals with hypertension, the Low-rapid decline group was an important risk factor for cognitive impairment, which suggests that early intervention may significantly reduce the risk of cognitive impairment in individuals with hypertension who have low ASM and experience rapid ASM decline.

Our study demonstrated that ASM decreases with increasing age, which is consistent with previous findings. Cawthon et al.22 used group-based trajectory modeling to characterize age-related changes in bone mineral density, grip strength, walking speed, and appendicular lean mass among older men. They found that the change trajectories of these variables could be categorized into three groups (“high,” “moderate,” and “low”), and there was almost no crossing or convergence of trajectories between groups as age increased. Welch et al.23 observed in hospitalized older adults that changes in indicators such as muscle quantity, muscle quality, and function might not be significant at the population mean level, but multiple heterogeneous trajectories existed at the individual level. Additionally, Liu et al.24 applied a dual group-based trajectory model to simultaneously characterize the longitudinal changes of ASM index (ASMI) and insulin sensitivity (eGDR) in community-dwelling older adults, identifying five distinct ASMI trajectory types. Among these, low-level or declining trajectories were significantly associated with the risk of heart failure, further confirming that the trajectories of muscle mass changes over time are diverse and clinically meaningful. Due to differences in trajectory fitting methods, study populations, and other factors, the trajectory patterns obtained from various studies may not be consistent. However, all studies indicate that muscle mass decline in middle-aged and older adults is not a single linear process, but rather exhibits multiple heterogeneous trajectories, with significant differences in decline rate, reversibility, and health outcomes among groups with different trajectories.

Emerging genomic evidence suggests that skeletal muscle mass is a moderately to highly heritable phenotype, with genetic variation contributing substantially to inter-individual differences in baseline muscle mass and age-related decline. Previous studies have demonstrated that lean mass and related muscle phenotypes exhibit significant heritability, indicating that intrinsic biological factors partially determine individual muscle reserve25. Moreover, genetic determinants are implicated not only in baseline muscle quantity but also in the trajectory of muscle loss across the lifespan26,27. Variants in genes regulating muscle protein synthesis and degradation, mitochondrial dynamics, inflammatory signaling pathways, and neuromuscular junction integrity may accelerate or attenuate age-associated muscle decline28.

Populations with lower baseline ASM or ASM decline had a higher incidence of cognitive impairment. The results of this study showed that compared with the High-gradual decline group, participants in the Moderate stable group and Low-rapid decline group had a 1.46-fold and 2.20-fold increased risk of cognitive impairment, respectively. This finding supports that ASM decline is a risk factor for cognitive decline, which is consistent with previous studies29,30. A 10-year longitudinal study in the UK also confirmed that older adults with sarcopenia exhibited significant declines in memory and verbal fluency during the 10-year follow-up, and this association persisted even after adjusting for multiple confounding factors31. Results from a US NHANES study found that low muscle mass was not only associated with cognitive decline but also with increased mortality32. Our results suggest that maintaining a relatively high ASM level with gradual decline may have a protective effect on cognition, while a trajectory of rapid ASM decline reflects potential poor health status and may be accompanied by irreversible pathological changes.

ASM decline may be associated with underweight, which has been confirmed as a risk factor for cognitive decline33. A sedentary lifestyle, characterized by low physical activity level, plays a pivotal role in this process. Sedentary lifestyles and nutritional deficiencies can exacerbate ASM decline: middle-aged and older adults tend to reduce physical activity, which leads to a significant decrease in muscle strength. Muscle atrophy makes it more difficult for middle-aged and older adults to exercise, further reinforcing a sedentary lifestyle34,35. This creates a vicious cycle that not only accelerates muscle loss but also directly increases the risk of cognitive impairment by reducing cerebral blood flow and neuroplasticity36. Additionally, particularly in the middle-aged and older adults., insufficient dietary protein intake makes it hard to maintain muscle mass and strength37. Moreover, reduced outdoor activities result in insufficient ultraviolet radiation and decreased vitamin D production, thereby accelerating ASM decline38,39. ASM decline may be a precursor to dementia31. A meta-analysis showed that the odds of developing mild cognitive impairment in the sarcopenia group were 1.58 times higher than those in the non-sarcopenia group, and the odds of developing Alzheimer’s disease in sarcopenic patients were 2.97 times higher than those in non-sarcopenic individuals40. This may be because sarcopenic patients have increased muscle catabolism, releasing large amounts of pro-inflammatory factors (such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}), which trigger systemic chronic low-grade inflammation. The inflammation further spreads to the central nervous system, activating neuroinflammatory responses, damaging nerve cells, disrupting synaptic connections, leading to cognitive decline and subsequent dementia41.

In subgroup analyses of this study, the risk of cognitive impairment in the Low-rapid decline group and Moderate stable group was significantly higher than that in the High-gradual decline group across most strata. This suggests that ASM decline is a universal risk factor for cognitive impairment, even after controlling for multiple sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical factors including physical activity. Notably, we identified a significant interaction between hypertension and ASM trajectories, which is consistent with the findings of Ishikawa et al42. For individuals with hypertension, the strength of the association between rapid ASM decline and cognitive impairment was significantly enhanced; in this population, moderate-to-low ASM levels or rapidly decreasing ASM trajectories are important risk factors for cognitive impairment. Existing evidence indicates that hypertension can lead to insufficient perfusion of the brain and skeletal muscle through pathways such as vascular remodeling, inflammation, and oxidative stress, thereby accelerating the development of sarcopenia. In turn, decreased muscle mass further reduces the secretion of neurotrophic factors and impairs the regulation of cerebral blood flow. Eventually, this forms a “muscle–vascular–brain” interaction pathway, exacerbating the deterioration of cognitive function43. This is consistent with the results of another longitudinal study based on the CHARLS database44.

Strengths and limitations

From a clinical and public health perspective, the identification of ASM trajectories facilitates the early detection of populations at high risk of cognitive impairment at the community level. Based on our study, to address the global rise in the prevalence of cognitive decline and dementia, we recommend greater attention to ASM in individuals aged 45 years and above. In particular, for older adults with comorbid hypertension, enhanced ASM monitoring and comprehensive interventions are warranted. The Global Consensus on Optimal Exercise Recommendations for Enhancing Healthy Longevity in Older Adults (ICFSR)36 suggests that for adults aged 60 years and above with sarcopenia or mild cognitive decline, multicomponent exercise programs centered on resistance training should be prioritized, combined with nutritional support if necessary, to simultaneously improve muscle function and delay cognitive decline.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large nationally representative sample, 9-year follow-up data, and trajectory modeling methods, which collectively reveal the relationship between dynamic ASM changes and the risk of cognitive impairment. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, ASM was estimated using a formula rather than gold-standard methods such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which may introduce some errors. Second, the criteria for the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in the fourth wave differed from those in the first three waves; to ensure consistency, we excluded data from the fourth wave. Third, loss to follow-up and selection bias existed during the follow-up period. Fourth, this study is observational, making it difficult to draw causal inferences. More comprehensive data would be required to extend the conclusions to the entire Asian region. Finally, as the CHARLS database lacks genetic data, we could not directly examine the influence of specific variants or quantify the heritable component of the observed ASM-cognition link. Future studies integrating genomic data are needed to disentangle the genetic and environmental contributions to this relationship.
