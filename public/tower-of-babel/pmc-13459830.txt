Limb salvage using acute shortening and gradual lengthening after failed free flap in a hemodialysis patient with Gustilo IIIB open tibial fracture: a case report

Published: 2026

Introduction

Gustilo IIIB open tibial fractures are among the most severe limb injuries, characterized by extensive soft tissue loss, high infection rates, and complex reconstructive challenges [1, 2]. The standard treatment strategy involves aggressive debridement, stable fixation, and microsurgical free flap coverage [2, 3].

However, outcomes are significantly worse in patients with chronic kidney disease undergoing hemodialysis. These patients exhibit impaired immune function, increased susceptibility to infection, and altered bone metabolism, all of which adversely affect fracture healing and soft tissue reconstruction [4, 5]. In addition, vascular fragility and compromised microcirculation further reduce the success rate of microsurgical procedures.

The situation becomes even more challenging in cases with limited vascular supply. In single-vessel limbs, such as those perfused only by the posterior tibial artery, the risk of flap failure is markedly increased. When initial microsurgical reconstruction fails, repeated attempts are associated with a high risk of complications and poor outcomes [3].

Acute shortening and gradual lengthening (ASGL), based on the principles of distraction osteogenesis described by Ilizarov, represents a vascular-independent reconstructive strategy [6, 7]. Acute shortening facilitates immediate soft tissue closure, while gradual lengthening restores limb length and promotes biological regeneration [8, 9].

We report a case in which ASGL was successfully used to achieve limb salvage after failed free flap reconstruction in a hemodialysis patient with a single-vessel limb.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old man undergoing maintenance hemodialysis for chronic kidney disease sustained a severe lower limb injury in a traffic accident caused by drowsy driving. He was initially transported to a referring hospital by helicopter emergency medical services.

On arrival at the referring hospital, he was diagnosed with an open tibia and fibula shaft fracture associated with a large soft tissue defect measuring approximately 20 × 10 cm extending from the anterior to lateral aspect of the lower leg (Fig. 1A, B). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a single-vessel limb, with only the posterior tibial artery remaining patent (Fig. 1C).

On the day of injury, irrigation and debridement were performed at the referring hospital, followed by temporary external fixation (Fig. 1D). The soft tissue defect was managed using negative pressure wound therapy, and the clinical appearance after temporary stabilization is shown in Fig. 1E. Repeated debridement was subsequently performed at the referring hospital during the early post-injury period (Fig. 2A). On day 7 post-injury, a free latissimus dorsi flap was performed at the referring hospital (Fig. 2B).

B Free latissimus dorsi flap performed at the referring hospital (day 7). C Flap necrosis due to infection and poor vascularity on day 10, followed by transfer to our institution; anteroposterior radiograph obtained after acute shortening of 75 mm at our institution. D Additional debridement and soft tissue coverage using local muscles, including the gastrocnemius and anterior tibial muscles, to reduce dead space at our institution. E Split-thickness skin grafting performed after infection control was achieved (day 17)

However, on day 10 post-injury, flap necrosis due to infection and poor vascularity occurred, and the patient was urgently transferred to our institution.

Upon arrival at our institution, repeat irrigation and debridement were performed. The fracture site appeared to be markedly contaminated and associated with compromised local vascularity. In addition, the condition of the bone suggested that partial bone resection might be necessary. Under these circumstances, further microsurgical reconstruction was considered less favorable, and a vascular-independent salvage strategy was selected.

Acute shortening of 75 mm was performed to enable soft tissue coverage using local muscles. Dead space was reduced, and the shortened segment was subsequently covered with local muscle tissue, including the gastrocnemius and anterior tibial muscles (Fig. 2C, D). Stabilization was achieved using a circular external fixator, and a limb salvage strategy based on acute shortening and gradual lengthening (ASGL) was adopted.

After stable soft tissue coverage had been achieved, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) was applied using continuous suction at 125 mmHg with polyurethane foam dressings. On day 17 post-injury, split-thickness skin grafting was performed after infection control had been achieved at our institution (Fig. 2E). On day 22, proximal tibial osteotomy was carried out. After a latency period of one week, distraction osteogenesis was initiated.

Because regenerate formation was weak, distraction was performed at a reduced rate of 0.25 mm twice daily. Radiographs obtained approximately 120 days after initiation of distraction demonstrated about 6 cm of lengthening (Fig. 3A). Due to insufficient regenerate strength, deformity occasionally occurred during lengthening and was corrected using a hexapod external fixator (Fig. 3B). Further lengthening was achieved up to approximately 8 cm (Fig. 3C).

Gradual lengthening using ASGL and long-term outcome. A Radiograph obtained approximately 120 days after initiation of distraction, showing approximately 6 cm of lengthening. B Deformity occurring during distraction due to insufficient regenerate strength, corrected using a hexapod external fixator. C Radiograph after approximately 8 cm of lengthening. D Anteroposterior radiograph at 8-year follow-up demonstrating solid bone union. E Lateral radiograph at 8-year follow-up. F Clinical photograph showing stable standing posture at final follow-up. G Clinical photograph demonstrating independent gait at final follow-up

Bone regeneration progressed satisfactorily, and the external fixator was removed 17 months after injury. Bone union was achieved without additional procedures such as an accordion maneuver or open release of fibrous tissue at the docking site. At final follow-up 8 years postoperatively, radiographs demonstrated solid bone union (Fig. 3D, E). Clinically, the patient was free of knee and ankle pain and had returned to agricultural work, with a stable standing posture and independent gait (Fig. 3F, G). Functional outcomes were favorable, with a JOA knee score of 90 and a JSSF ankle-hindfoot scale score of 87 at final follow-up. In addition, patient-reported outcomes assessed using the SF-36 demonstrated generally favorable physical and social functioning, consistent with the patient’s return to agricultural work.

Discussion

This case highlights the importance of strategic decision-making in limb salvage for Gustilo IIIB open tibial fractures in high-risk patients.

Gustilo IIIB fractures are associated with extensive soft tissue damage and a high risk of infection, requiring combined orthopaedic and microsurgical approaches for optimal outcomes [1–3]. The “fix and flap” strategy has been widely accepted as the standard treatment for such injuries [2]. However, outcomes may be less favorable in patients with significant comorbidities.

Patients undergoing hemodialysis represent a particularly challenging population, as they may exhibit increased susceptibility to infection, impaired bone healing, and vascular fragility [4, 5]. These factors can compromise both fracture healing and soft tissue reconstruction. In the present case, these systemic factors were further compounded by local vascular compromise, as only the posterior tibial artery remained patent. Such single-vessel limbs may be associated with an increased risk of microsurgical failure, particularly in the presence of infection [3].

Following necrosis of the initial free flap, we considered that repeat microsurgical reconstruction might have a limited likelihood of success and could further compromise the remaining vascular supply. In addition, at the time of transfer, the fracture site appeared markedly contaminated and associated with compromised local vascularity, and the condition of the bone suggested that partial bone resection might be necessary. Under these circumstances, a vascular-independent salvage strategy was considered appropriate.

We selected acute shortening and gradual lengthening (ASGL) using the Ilizarov method. Acute shortening was performed to facilitate soft tissue coverage using local muscles; this approach allowed reduction of dead space, and the shortened segment was subsequently covered with local muscle tissue, including the gastrocnemius and anterior tibial muscles. This step contributed to infection control and soft tissue stabilization. By converting a large soft tissue defect into a condition amenable to local muscle coverage through acute shortening, further microsurgical reconstruction was avoided.

We agree that soft tissue reconstruction should follow a stepwise approach, beginning with the simplest and most reliable options, particularly in patients with significant comorbidities. Pedicled flaps such as gastrocnemius or proximally based soleus flaps are important options in selected cases. In the present case, however, the defect was extensive, and the condition of the surrounding tissues after flap failure was considered suboptimal for reliable pedicled flap coverage. Notably, local muscle tissue was ultimately utilized following acute shortening, which is consistent with the principle of using simpler and reliable tissue coverage when feasible.

Gradual lengthening based on distraction osteogenesis subsequently restored limb length while maintaining biological activity. The Ilizarov method has been reported to enhance local blood flow and support both bone and soft tissue regeneration [6, 10]. Importantly, this technique does not rely on vascular anastomosis, which may be advantageous in cases with compromised vascularity or failed prior reconstruction.

Compared with bone transport, ASGL allows earlier soft tissue management by enabling immediate closure through acute shortening. Previous studies have demonstrated the usefulness of acute shortening and re-lengthening techniques in managing combined bone and soft tissue defects [8, 9]. This approach may be particularly useful in selected cases with extensive soft tissue loss and infection.

In complex Gustilo IIIB injuries, alternative strategies such as secondary amputation, cross-leg flap reconstruction, and bone transport after soft tissue reconstruction should also be considered. Secondary amputation may provide a shorter and more predictable recovery in selected cases, particularly in patients with severe comorbidities. Cross-leg flap is a reliable option for soft tissue coverage; however, it requires prolonged immobilization, which may be associated with an increased risk of complications, particularly in elderly or medically compromised patients such as those undergoing hemodialysis. In addition, the physical and psychological burden associated with prolonged bed rest should be considered. Bone transport after soft tissue reconstruction is another established strategy, although it may require a longer treatment course. In the present case, ASGL allowed immediate dead space reduction and local soft tissue coverage while avoiding further microsurgical procedures.

An orthoplastic approach, involving early collaboration between orthopaedic and plastic surgeons, is an important aspect of the management of severe open fractures. Early multidisciplinary decision-making may help optimize treatment selection and potentially reduce complications such as flap failure.

In addition, circular external fixation provides stable fixation while minimizing further soft tissue injury and allows simultaneous deformity correction [11, 12]. Several practical considerations were important in this case, including adjustment of the distraction rate in the setting of impaired bone metabolism, careful monitoring of regenerate formation, and meticulous pin site care to prevent infection. These aspects may be particularly relevant in high-risk patients such as those undergoing hemodialysis. In this case, no major pin tract complication requiring surgical revision occurred.

Bone union was achieved without additional procedures such as an accordion maneuver or open release of fibrous tissue at the docking site. Nevertheless, mild procurvatum deformity and incomplete radiographic remodeling remained at long-term follow-up. In this high-risk limb salvage case, functional recovery was prioritized.

Long-term outcomes are critical in evaluating limb salvage strategies. In the present case, the patient remained pain-free and returned to agricultural work at 8-year follow-up, without recurrence of infection. These favorable clinical outcomes were supported by objective functional scores, with a JOA knee score of 90 and a JSSF ankle-hindfoot scale score of 87 at final follow-up. In addition, patient-reported outcomes assessed using the SF-36 demonstrated generally favorable physical and social functioning, consistent with the patient’s return to agricultural work. Such outcomes support the clinical usefulness of ASGL in selected cases.

Based on the present case and existing literature, ASGL may be considered in patients with failed or infeasible microsurgical reconstruction, compromised vascular supply such as a single-vessel limb, extensive soft tissue defects requiring effective dead space management, and high-risk host conditions such as infection or hemodialysis. The key reproducible elements include acute shortening to facilitate immediate soft tissue coverage, reduction of dead space using local muscle tissue, and subsequent gradual lengthening through distraction osteogenesis.

Overall, this case suggests that ASGL may be considered as a practical option in complex limb salvage when microsurgical reconstruction is unlikely to succeed, particularly in high-risk patients with compromised vascularity.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single case, which limits generalizability. Second, no direct comparison with alternative strategies, such as amputation, cross-leg flap reconstruction, or repeat microsurgical reconstruction, was performed. Third, the indication for limb salvage was based on clinical judgment, and selection bias cannot be excluded. Fourth, although favorable long-term outcomes were observed, functional assessment remained limited, and patient-reported outcomes were not analyzed in greater detail. Fifth, a formal cost analysis and broader resource considerations were not performed. Finally, the applicability of ASGL to other high-risk populations, including patients with different vascular conditions or comorbidities, remains to be clarified.

Conclusions

In patients with Gustilo IIIB open tibial fractures complicated by hemodialysis and limited vascular supply, ASGL using the Ilizarov method may represent a valuable vascular-independent salvage strategy. This approach enables infection control, soft tissue management, and bone reconstruction, resulting in durable long-term functional recovery even in high-risk conditions.
