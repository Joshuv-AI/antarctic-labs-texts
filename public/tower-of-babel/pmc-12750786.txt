Content and structural needs assessment for an artificial intelligence education mobile app in healthcare: a mixed methods study
Authors: Seyyedeh Fatemeh Mousavi Baigi, Reyhane Norouzi Aval, Masoumeh Sarbaz, Seyyed Mohammad Tabatabaei, Khalil Kimiafar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify and prioritize the core content and structural requirements for developing a high-quality mobile app designed to teach AI concepts and skills in a healthcare context.MethodsA mixed-methods design was employed. First, two systematic reviews were conducted: [1] a review of scholarly articles to extract educational frameworks for AI in healthcare, and [2] a review of 47 AI education apps from three app stores (Google Play, App Store, Café Bazaar), assessed using the Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS). As no healthcare-specific AI education apps were found during the search, general-purpose AI learning apps were included, which constitutes a limitation in terms of domain specificity. Based on these insights, a preliminary content framework was developed and validated by 12 experts in medical informatics and health information management. Subsequently, a structural needs assessment was carried out with 97 healthcare students using custom-designed questionnaires. Open-ended responses were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis method.ResultsThe systematic review of 37 articles revealed 10 key domains essential for AI education in healthcare, including foundational knowledge, data science, practical clinical applications, ethics, and communication. The app review showed a mean MARS quality score of 2.92 out of 5, highlighting significant deficiencies in content coherence, interactivity, and privacy implementation. Expert validation confirmed all proposed domains, and thematic analysis of expert feedback led to the inclusion of an additional domain: Practical Tools and Platforms. Healthcare students strongly favored features such as interactive learning, offline functionality, and personalized learning paths (mean scores > 4.76/5), with no significant differences across gender or field of study.ConclusionThis study presents a validated, evidence-based framework for developing a healthcare-focused AI education app. The finalized structure includes 11 content domains and 20 prioritized structural features aimed at promoting practical, ethical, and engaging learning experiences. The findings underscore the urgent need for structured, user-centered digital tools to prepare healthcare students and professionals for the responsible integration of AI into clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08328-8.

Introduction

In recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning and deep learning algorithms, has experienced significant growth, leading to widespread applications across educational and professional domains [1, 2]. As AI continues to transform healthcare and other sectors, the demand for comprehensive, specialized training to prepare the future workforce for the effective integration of AI into clinical practice has intensified. AI education must extend beyond theoretical knowledge to include the development of practical competencies and the ability to utilize AI-based tools effectively in healthcare environments [3]. While AI education in healthcare has been widely studied from a global perspective, integrating local perspectives is crucial for designing effective interventions. For example, recent research by Younesian et al. (2025) on Iranian physicians’ views regarding AI’s role in healthcare highlights regional concerns about trust and AI’s potential to augment or replace human expertise. These insights demonstrate the need for AI education tools that are not only globally relevant but also culturally and professionally tailored to meet the specific needs of healthcare professionals [4].

Despite its growing importance, recent studies have highlighted insufficient levels of AI knowledge, attitudes, and practical skills among healthcare professionals and students, although many hold a positive outlook on the future of AI in healthcare [5]. A systematic review found that more than half of healthcare students reported low levels of AI knowledge and skills and limited hands-on experience, underscoring the urgent need for practical, application-focused AI training in health education [5]. Another study reported low AI literacy among healthcare professionals and students, with significant improvements following targeted educational interventions [4]. These findings highlight the critical need to incorporate effective, accessible, and practice-oriented AI education into healthcare curricula.

To address these gaps, educational mobile applications (apps) have emerged as effective tools that offer interactive, personalized, and flexible access to AI training, enhancing the reach and impact of educational programs [6, 7]. These apps employ multimedia formats, including text, images, videos, and podcasts, to create engaging learning experiences and provide learners with convenient access to high-quality educational resources anytime and anywhere [8, 9].

Despite the proliferation of AI educational apps, their quality and content consistency remain variable, necessitating systematic evaluation using standardized tools. The Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS) provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating app quality across dimensions such as engagement, functionality, aesthetics, content quality, and subjective user experience [10].

Developing effective AI education tools requires a clear understanding of the content and structural needs of the target users. To inform the design of our mobile app for AI education in healthcare, we first conducted a systematic review of the literature to extract and synthesize existing frameworks and key domains for AI education, identifying essential competencies, instructional strategies, and ethical considerations pertinent to AI literacy in healthcare. We then performed a systematic review of existing AI educational mobile apps across three major app stores, systematically evaluating them using the MARS framework to assess current gaps and best practices in app-based AI education.

Based on insights from the literature and app evaluations, we developed a preliminary content and structural framework for an AI education app in healthcare. To validate this framework, we designed a structured questionnaire to gather expert feedback on content priorities and to assess structural usability with healthcare students. Content validity was confirmed through expert review, while structural usability and acceptability were assessed by healthcare students to ensure the design aligns with user needs and practical app in learning environments.

Given the accelerating integration of AI in healthcare, this study aims to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical competency by identifying and prioritizing content and structural elements critical for developing an effective AI education mobile app for healthcare students and professionals. By aligning educational content with evidence-based frameworks and user-centered needs assessments, this study contributes to the development of a high-quality, accessible, and practical AI educational intervention, supporting the advancement of AI competencies within healthcare education and clinical practice.

Methods

Systematic review

The initial objective of this study was to identify and review existing studies and mobile apps specifically designed for AI education within the healthcare domain. However, our systematic searches revealed no studies or mobile apps exclusively focused on AI education in healthcare. Consequently, we expanded our scope to include general AI educational mobile apps regardless of domain while concurrently reviewing studies proposing content frameworks and curricular guidelines for AI education within healthcare settings.

The systematic review was conducted on January 31, 2025. Two researchers with expertise in health information technology and prior training in mobile app evaluation using the MARS systematically searched for AI educational mobile apps across three official app stores: Google Play, Cafe Bazaar, and the Apple App Store. The search was conducted using the keywords: “Artificial Intelligence”, “AI”, “Machine Learning”, and “Deep Learning” in both English and Persian.

In parallel, a systematic review was conducted across four major databases: Scopus, PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science to identify studies that proposed or described content frameworks, curricular models, or competency guidelines for AI education in healthcare. Three concept blocks of keywords were searched across the title, abstract, and keyword fields of articles, combining the following terms: (“artificial intelligence education” OR “AI education” OR “artificial intelligence literacy” OR “AI literacy”) AND (“educational framework” OR “curriculum” OR “content framework” OR “curriculum development” OR “training framework” OR “educational programs”) AND (“healthcare” OR “health care” OR “health” OR “medical education” OR “health professions education” OR “clinical education”). Equivalent search structures were adapted for each database using platform-specific syntax, such as MeSH terms in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase, when applicable. The full, database-specific search strategies are available in Supplementary Materials 1.

Eligibility criteria

For the app store review, mobile apps were included if their primary objective was to teach concepts and applications related to AI. Mobile apps that focused on topics unrelated to AI or machine learning were excluded. Additionally, apps in languages other than Persian and English, those with significant technical issues, or those requiring unreasonably high download costs were excluded. However, mobile apps offering free initial download and use with partial paid content remained eligible for evaluation in this study.

For the systematic review, studies were included if they proposed or described content frameworks, curricular guidelines, or structured educational models specifically for AI education within the healthcare domain. Studies addressing general AI education frameworks outside healthcare were excluded. Additionally, letters to the editor and studies that did not present a structured content framework or educational program were excluded.

Selection of studies and mobile apps

The titles and download pages of the identified mobile apps were independently screened by two reviewers to determine eligibility. Eligible mobile apps were entered into a centralized database, and duplicates were removed. The remaining mobile apps were then downloaded and reviewed in detail. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, a third reviewer (supervisor) was consulted for final decisions.

For the systematic review, independent screening of titles and abstracts was performed according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Articles not meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded. The full texts of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and assessed for eligibility independently by two researchers. Discussions were conducted to resolve disagreements, and when a consensus was not reached, the third author made the final decision.

Data extraction and quality assessment of mobile apps

Two health information technology specialists independently evaluated the identified mobile apps using the MARS, whose Persian version has demonstrated high reliability (coefficient = 0.94) [11]. Prior to the assessment, the reviewers watched the official MARS training video and received the necessary instructions to ensure consistency in evaluations [12]. Each app was assessed using MARS’s 23 items across five sections (Engagement, Functionality, Aesthetics, Information Quality, Subjective Quality), and six perceived impact items evaluating the app’s influence on knowledge, attitudes, intention to change, and behavioral change likelihood. Each item was scored on a 5-point scale (1 = inadequate, 5 = excellent), with the overall quality score calculated as the mean across the five primary sections.

For structured data extraction, a standardized checklist was applied to the mobile apps, capturing: app name, version, year (last version), platform (Café Bazaar, Google Play, App Store), paid content (free or in-app purchases), downloads, developer, affiliation (academic/commercial), theoretical background/strategies, core educational topics, technical and privacy aspects, privacy statements, app functionality description, user ratings, and selected comments.

Data analysis included calculating the mean scores assigned by both reviewers for each app. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations) were used to summarize app quality overall and by MARS sections, facilitating a clear comparison of app quality and content alignment across evaluated mobile apps.

Data extraction and quality assessment of included studies

To ensure methodological rigor, all included studies in the systematic review were appraised using a structured quality assessment protocol based on four predefined criteria: [1] clarity of the research objective [2], appropriateness of the study design [3], consistency between data analysis and study design, and [4] sufficiency and relevance of supporting evidence for the conclusions. Only studies that satisfactorily met al.l four criteria were included in the final review.

Two independent reviewers conducted the assessments. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when necessary. This approach ensured the inclusion of studies with sound methodological foundations, thereby enhancing the validity and reliability of the overall synthesis.

A structured checklist was also used to extract key data from the included studies, including: study reference, country of origin, year of publication, study method, target population, and core educational topics related to AI education in healthcare.

Validity and reliability

The face and content validity of the questionnaires were evaluated by five experts (two health information management specialists, two medical informatics specialists, and one biostatistics specialist), and necessary modifications were applied.

Subsequently, content validity (CVI and CVR) was calculated using the Delphi method with the participation of six experts (two PhD-level AI specialists, two medical informatics specialists, and two health information management specialists), yielding a CVI value of 0.98.

The reliability of the instruments was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, resulting in a value of 0.89 for the content validation questionnaire and 0.76 for the structural needs assessment questionnaire.

The questionnaires were collected in-person, with the researcher present to clarify any ambiguities during completion. The content and structural needs assessment questionnaires are available in Supplementary Materials 2 and 3, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The obtained quantitative data were aggregated and summarized using descriptive statistics to provide a clear basis for inference and interpretation. The chi-square test was applied to examine the independence between qualitative variables. All quantitative analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 16.

For qualitative analysis, open-ended questions were analyzed using thematic content analysis to interpret the meaning of responses provided by professors and students. The analysis followed the Braun and Clarke method of thematic analysis [13, 14]. Responses were initially coded using pre-established codes identified in prior studies, and in instances where responses did not align with existing codes, new codes were generated by the research team.

To ensure reliability of the coding, a peer review approach was employed [15, 16]. Two peer reviewers with expertise in medical informatics were invited to review and confirm the generated codes. In cases where disagreements occurred between the two reviewers, a third reviewer was engaged to resolve discrepancies and finalize the codes. The intercoder agreement rate for both questions exceeded 80%.

Following the completion of the coding process, similar codes were grouped into overarching themes for structured interpretation [13].

Results

Systematic review

A total of 247 records were retrieved through database searches. After removing 67 duplicates, 180 unique records were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 140 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Forty full-text articles were sought for retrieval, all of which were successfully obtained and assessed for eligibility. Three studies were excluded due to a lack of specific focus on AI education within the healthcare domain, resulting in the inclusion of 37 studies, representing 47 individual reports.

In parallel, 47 mobile apps were identified from three official app stores (Google Play, Café Bazaar, and Apple App Store). All identified apps were retrieved, assessed in full, and included in the evaluation, as they met the predefined eligibility criteria. Descriptive characteristics of the included mobile apps

A total of 47 mobile apps designed for AI education were included for evaluation in this study. All identified apps met the eligibility criteria and were retained for detailed assessment. Among these, 23 apps were available on the App Store/iOS, 12 apps on Google Play, and 12 apps on Café Bazaar. All apps were developed and published by commercial developers or companies, with none supported directly by universities or governmental institutions. In terms of accessibility, 33 apps were offered free of charge, while 14 apps required in-app purchases or paid versions for full access to content. The number of downloads varied widely, ranging from over 10 to more than 100,000 downloads, indicating differences in user reach and popularity across platforms. All the evaluated apps were designed to deliver theoretical and practical education in AI; however, none were specifically developed to teach AI within the healthcare domain. Regarding privacy and data protection, fewer than 15% of the apps included clear privacy statements or options for creating secure user accounts. Among the reviewed apps, Artificial Intelligence by Softecks, Learn AI & ML with Python by Coding & Programming, and Artificial intelligence__AL by Woow apps had the highest recorded downloads, each exceeding 100,000 installations, indicating a higher level of user engagement and interest.

App quality assessment results

The mean quality scores of the evaluated AI learning mobile apps ranged from 1.80 to 4.10, with an overall mean of 2.92 (SD: 0.58) on a 1 (inadequate) to 5 (excellent) scale. The highest domain-specific score was observed in functionality (mean: 3.12, SD: 0.63), followed by information quality (mean: 3.05, SD: 0.59). Lower scores were found in aesthetics (mean: 2.94, SD: 0.61) and subjective quality (mean: 2.76, SD: 0.65), indicating these as areas where the evaluated apps may require improvement to enhance user engagement and satisfaction.

Among the evaluated apps, Learn AI with Python and Mila, AI Learning achieved the highest overall quality scores (mean: 4.10, SD: 0.37), demonstrating strong performance across engagement, functionality, aesthetics, and content quality. In contrast, AI & ChatGPT Training (FA) and Learn AI & ML with Python (Google Play) received the lowest overall scores (mean: 1.80, SD: 0.50), reflecting limitations in design, content depth, and user-perceived value.

The perceived impact domain demonstrated a moderate average score (mean: 2.79, SD: 0.57), reflecting the apps’ influence on users’ knowledge acquisition, attitudes toward AI, and behavioral intentions regarding AI-related practices. Despite relatively acceptable functionality and information scores, the lower subjective quality ratings highlight that many apps may not fully meet user expectations in terms of motivation and sustained engagement.

Artificial Intelligence by Softecks, Learn AI & ML with Python by Coding & Programming, and Artificial intelligence__AL by Woow Apps had the highest recorded download numbers (over 100,000 installs), aligning with higher user interest and potential utility despite variability in MARS ratings across these apps.

Overview of included studies

A total of 37 studies were identified through a systematic review of major databases [17–53]. The methodologies employed across these studies included 16 systematic and narrative reviews [17, 19, 21, 26, 28, 29, 32, 39, 43, 45, 48, 51–53] and three Delphi studies [25, 30, 47]. Other methodologies encompassed workshop development and evaluation, cross-sectional surveys, the design and assessment of educational interventions, perspective analyses, and framework-based mixed-methods approaches [18, 20, 22–24, 27, 31, 33–38, 40, 41, 44, 46, 49, 50]. Among these, seven studies were conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including the Philippines, Turkey, Colombia, Pakistan, India, UAE/India, and Oman [46–51, 53], while 28 studies were conducted in high-income countries (HICs) such as Canada, the United States, Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, South Korea, Hong Kong, Japan, Finland, and Singapore [17–45]. Development of an adaptive core content framework

Based on the systematic review of 37 included studies [17–53] and the comprehensive evaluation of AI educational mobile apps, a unified adaptive framework of core content topics for AI education in healthcare was developed. This framework integrates content derived from the highest-quality studies and systematically mapped mobile app functionalities, ensuring alignment with real-world competencies needed for medical learners and healthcare professionals. The framework encompasses ten major domains, including foundational AI concepts, data science literacy, practical healthcare applications, ethical and legal considerations, communication and humanistic skills, applied technical skills, curriculum integration strategies, advanced AI topics, critical evaluation of AI tools, and research enhancement using AI. Each domain is further divided into targeted subcategories informed by both systematic review and mobile app content analysis to ensure a pragmatic, learner-centered structure for implementation in medical curricula. A detailed mapping of these domains, corresponding subcategories, associated mobile apps, and aligned references is presented in Table S6.

Qualitative thematic analysis of expert recommendations

The open-ended qualitative responses of 10 health informatics and health information management specialists were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis to refine the initial AI in Health Education framework.

Pre-established codes were derived from the systematic review of 47 AI education apps and 37 content framework papers. Experts were invited to suggest additional modules or refinements to improve the educational app framework for teaching AI in health.

Ten primary themes emerged from the analysis, representing the most frequently emphasized and practically relevant content areas recommended by experts. As illustrated in Fig. 2, Health Data Management & Infrastructure was the most prominent theme, followed by Practical Tools and Platform Training, and Transparency and Explainability. Inferential statistics

Chi-square tests were conducted to examine the association between gender, field of study, attitudes toward learning with the app, and prioritization of app structural features. The results indicated no statistically significant differences across gender and field of study regarding students’ attitudes and their prioritization of structural features (p = 1.00). This consistency suggests universal high interest and demand for mobile-based AI education across gender and disciplinary backgrounds among students.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study sought to explore and define the essential content and structural features of an educational mobile app for AI literacy in healthcare. By synthesizing findings from a systematic review, evaluating existing educational mobile apps using the MARS tool, and integrating insights from both experts and end-users, we developed and validated a comprehensive framework intended to guide the future development of a learner-centered AI education mobile app.

Our findings indicate significant variability in the quality and focus of existing AI-related educational mobile apps, with an average MARS score of 2.92 out of 5. Functionality was rated highest, while engagement and aesthetics lagged behind, and privacy features were poorly implemented or entirely absent. These findings align with recent studies that similarly point to the fragmented development and lack of standardization in mHealth education mobile apps, particularly those targeting complex domains like AI [7, 11, 54]. These shortcomings emphasize the need for structured, evidence-based design in mobile app development to ensure educational effectiveness, especially in fields requiring conceptual and practical understanding such as AI.

The content framework derived from the systematic review encompassed ten core domains essential for AI education in healthcare: [1] Foundations and Basic Concepts of AI & Machine Learning [2], Data Science & Data Literacy [3], Practical Applications of AI in Health [4], Ethics, Law, and Governance in AI [5], Communication and Humanistic Skills in AI [6], Practical and Applied Skills [7], Enhancing the Quality of Medical Education with AI [8], Advanced Topics [9], Evaluation and Critical Analysis of AI Tools, and [10] Enhancing Research Quality with AI. These domains echo the competencies proposed in international AI literacy frameworks [17–19]. Notably, the emphasis on both theoretical and humanistic components — such as algorithmic transparency and patient-centered communication — reflects growing recognition of the dual technical-ethical nature of AI integration in healthcare. While the ethical considerations around AI in healthcare are crucial, it is equally important to address issues of fairness, bias, and equity. AI systems, by their nature, can perpetuate or even exacerbate biases if not properly designed and trained, particularly when applied to sensitive healthcare environments [55]. Our framework acknowledges these concerns by emphasizing the need for fairness and equity in AI education, ensuring that future healthcare professionals are equipped to understand and address potential biases in AI tools. By integrating these ethical principles into the design of our educational app, we aim to foster a critical awareness of AI’s potential biases, ensuring that students not only learn how to utilize AI but also understand how to mitigate its risks in clinical practice.

The qualitative findings, drawn from expert commentary via open-ended questions, led to the addition of a new thematic domain: “Practical Tools and Platforms.” This domain underscores the need to expose learners to real-world applications (e.g., FHIR, EHR systems, and basic platform literacy), bridging the gap between abstract AI concepts and clinical reality. This result resonates with recent commentaries advocating for hands-on exposure to clinical informatics tools as part of AI literacy [21, 22].

In parallel, the structural needs assessment conducted among healthcare students (n = 97) revealed a preference for accessible, personalized, and interactive learning experiences. Features such as offline functionality, multimedia content, and modular structure were highly rated — supporting the idea that digital learning platforms must cater to flexibility and learner autonomy [6, 9, 56, 57]. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating broader pedagogical frameworks, such as constructivism and self-regulated learning, in the design of AI education apps. According to studies by Patrick et al. [58] and Parsons et al. [59], learner-centered approaches that promote active engagement, autonomy, and personalized learning paths are critical for fostering deeper understanding and sustained motivation. These theories advocate for creating learning environments that allow students to take control of their learning process, set personal goals, and adjust their learning strategies, which align with the features highly rated by our participants, such as offline functionality, modular structures, and multimedia content. By embedding such theoretical principles, the app could enhance learners’ ability to manage their learning, making it more adaptive and effective in a rapidly evolving technological landscape. Similarly, addressing concerns related to trust and competence in AI use, as highlighted by Firuzpour et al. [60] in their study on physician confidence in Iran, can enhance the app’s ability to effectively engage healthcare professionals. By integrating the identified gaps in physician trust and AI competence, our app framework aims to build confidence through practical, targeted educational experiences. No statistically significant differences were observed between students based on gender or academic background, suggesting that the proposed structure holds cross-demographic applicability. However, the uniformly high ratings observed (mean >4.7 across most features) may partially reflect a ceiling effect or social desirability bias. While responses likely represent genuine interest in accessible AI education, it is important to note that participants were informed they would receive free access to the finalized app, which may have influenced the positivity of responses. Future studies should explore alternative data triangulation methods, such as behavioral usage metrics or follow-up engagement data, to mitigate this potential bias and validate user preferences more robustly.

An important implication of our findings is the integrated design of AI education that addresses both competence and confidence. While many learners express interest in AI, previous studies have shown a lack of confidence due to limited exposure and unclear curricular structures (20, 50). Our validated framework helps mitigate this issue by aligning content with learners’ expectations and pedagogical standards, and is positioned to support both formal curricula and self-directed learning pathways.

While our framework addresses ethical and legal dimensions through global standards such as GDPR and ISO/IEC 42,001, its practical application in low-resource settings presents unique challenges. Kaushik et al. [61] identified major implementation barriers in LMICs, including limited health data literacy, inconsistent data systems, and regulatory ambiguity—factors that complicate the translation of international frameworks into actionable local practice. Similarly, Yu and Yu [55] introduced the concept of the “AI Deployment Paradox,” which suggests that poorly contextualized AI initiatives may exacerbate rather than reduce health disparities. Their thematic analysis emphasizes three key issues: data poverty and contextual misalignment; trade-offs between cost-effectiveness and health equity; and the risk of technological colonization. These insights underscore the importance of embedding locally sensitive governance models and participatory ethics into AI education, ensuring that learners are not only aware of international standards, but also capable of critically examining their relevance and adaptability in diverse healthcare environments. Also, this highlights the need for ongoing, longitudinal studies to better understand how AI education tools perform in real-world settings. Long-term evaluations will be essential to assess the sustained impact on learning outcomes, motivation, and academic achievement. While our current framework lays the foundation for an effective AI education tool, future research will need to focus on evaluating the app’s effectiveness over time, particularly in diverse healthcare settings.

This study presents several notable strengths, including the use of multiple data sources (systematic review, mobile app analysis, expert and user input), the mobile app of the validated MARS tool for evaluating existing AI education apps, and a strong user-centered design approach. Although developing a mobile app prototype was beyond the scope of the current research, the validated content and structural framework offers a solid foundation for future development and implementation. In addition, it is important to highlight that, given the fast-evolving nature of AI technologies and the educational needs of healthcare students, adopting an agile approach to the development of educational mobile apps is crucial. This approach allows for rapid iteration and adaptation to meet the immediate needs of learners, ensuring that students are not deprived of essential educational tools due to delays in app design and implementation. This responsiveness to user feedback and evolving technological trends is key to ensuring that the app remains relevant and effective as AI technologies continue to advance.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. While this study utilized open-ended questions and thematic analysis for qualitative insights, we acknowledge that more in-depth qualitative methods, such as student interviews or focus groups, are essential to gain a richer understanding of learners’ experiences. Future phases of this research will incorporate these qualitative approaches to provide a more comprehensive perspective on user engagement and the effectiveness of the app in addressing learners’ needs. Both the student and expert participants were recruited from a single institution, which may introduce institutional bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Although we included individuals from a variety of academic levels and disciplines to improve representativeness, further multi-institutional validation is needed to strengthen external validity.

Additionally, the app review included both Persian and English-language applications to capture a broader range of available resources. While this bilingual inclusion expanded the scope of analysis, it may have introduced regional or cultural biases, potentially limiting the broader applicability of the results.

The study’s focus on healthcare students and professionals may also restrict generalizability to learners in non-health domains, such as engineering, business, or the social sciences.

Finally, due to methodological heterogeneity among the included studies in the systematic review, a standardized quality appraisal tool (e.g., AMSTAR 2 or MMAT) was not applied. Instead, we used a predefined set of four criteria to ensure consistent and rigorous assessment across diverse study designs.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study developed and validated a multidimensional framework for the design of an AI literacy mobile app tailored to healthcare education. Integrating evidence from a systematic literature review, app quality analysis using MARS, and stakeholder input, the resulting framework addresses both content and structural dimensions across eleven core domains, from foundational AI concepts to applied and ethical considerations.

The findings reveal major gaps in current AI educational apps—particularly in interactivity, content coherence, and privacy—underscoring the need for structured, user-centered design. The inclusion of a “Practical Tools and Platforms” domain, based on expert recommendations, enhances the framework’s applicability to real-world training. Student feedback further supports the demand for interactive, flexible mobile learning environments.

This framework offers a strong basis for developing AI education tools in healthcare and can inform future curricular design and interdisciplinary training. Further research should test its scalability and relevance beyond the health sector.
