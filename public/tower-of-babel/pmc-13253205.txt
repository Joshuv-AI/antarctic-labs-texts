Polyelectrolyte nanoparticles enable intracellular delivery of STING protein fragments for ovarian cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Justin A Kaskow, Altaf Bacchus, Victoria F Gomerdinger, Adam G Berger, Ze-Hua Li, Thomas J Gerton, Mellany A Smith, Andrea Li, Julia Treese, Aidan Kindopp, Daniela M Dinulescu, David A Barbie, Paula T Hammond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic activation of stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling shows promise as a method of inflaming the tumor microenvironment to generate an anti-cancer immune response. Frequent loss of STING expression in many human cancers renders small-molecule agonists ineffective in cancer cells. We previously demonstrated that delivering a fragment of the STING protein, dubbed STINGΔTM, to the cytosol can bypass this challenge by directly activating downstream signaling molecules in the cytosol; however, this strategy requires an effective cytosolic protein delivery vehicle to be therapeutically active. In this work, we develop a poly(β-amino ester) (PBAE) degradable polycation nanoparticle formulation to target ovarian cancer and deliver bioactive STINGΔTM protein to the cytosol. Screening experiments reveal that increasing PBAE hydrophobicity enables efficient protein encapsulation and delivery to the cytosol. We then develop a pH-shift-mediated method to assemble smaller, negatively charged nanoparticles composed of STINGΔTM protein, PBAE, and a polyanion. The polyanion can tune the cell type specificity of STING signaling activation in vitro, with poly(L-glutamate) and poly(L-aspartate) preferentially activating signaling in ovarian cancer cells. In a syngeneic mouse model of ovarian cancer, nanoparticles accumulate preferentially in tumors throughout the abdomen, slow tumor growth, and extend survival. Overall, the development of a tumor-targeted PBAE nanoparticle enabled the treatment of ovarian cancer through the direct intracellular delivery of STING protein, yielding an immunotherapy that shows promise for the treatment of cancers with dysregulated STING.Keywords: Nanoparticle, Polyelectrolyte, Cancer, STING, Immunotherapy, Protein

Introduction

The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a therapeutic target of great interest for cancer immunotherapy. This innate immune pathway detects the presence of DNA in the cytosol and results in the production of immunostimulatory type I interferons (IFNs) and chemokines such as CXCL10 [1,2], giving STING a central role in the generation of spontaneous immune responses against cancer [3,4]. Small-molecule STING agonists, which bind to and induce the multimerization of STING protein to enable downstream signaling, have demonstrated strong efficacy in preclinical models with an ability to generate robust anti-tumor immune responses and eliminate tumors, both alone and as combination therapies [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Despite potential clinical utility, human clinical trials of STING agonists in cancer have thus far shown only modest efficacy [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Challenges limiting STING agonists include rapid clearance of these small molecules [12], systemic toxicity [12,16], and frequent inactivation of STING signaling in many human cancers by epigenetic silencing [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. STING inactivation in cancer has been hypothesized to be a mechanism of resistance to STING agonist immunotherapy [23]. While it is still possible to activate STING in host immune and stromal cells, it has been demonstrated that activation of STING in cancer cells in addition to host cells can improve anti-cancer immune responses [[23], [24], [25]] by inducing secretion of additional anti-tumor cytokines [[24], [25], [26]] and enhancing cancer cell immunogenicity [23,25,[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Overall, this phenomenon demonstrates a need to develop better strategies to activate STING signaling. The ideal therapeutic would activate STING in cancer and immune cells in tumors even when signaling is dysregulated, while avoiding systemic STING activation to limit toxicity.

Ovarian cancer represents an area of great clinical need, where STING activation seems promising but dysregulated STING signaling is common and limits traditional STING agonists. High grade ovarian cancer is discovered at an advanced stage for the majority of patients [31]. At this point, the 5-year survival rate is just 25% and recurrence rates are approximately 70% with standard-of-care chemotherapy [32]. Despite the clinical success of immunotherapies like checkpoint blockade in many other cancers, ovarian cancer has not responded to these treatments in clinical trials [33,34]. The lack of response is suspected to be caused by poor T cell infiltration and immunosuppressive myeloid cells in ovarian cancer [35,36]. STING has been shown to recruit and activate T cells as well as repolarize immunosuppressive myeloid cells [37], making STING activation a promising option to repolarize the ovarian cancer microenvironment from immunosuppressive to inflamed and enable the generation of an anti-cancer immune response. However, STING silencing is very common in ovarian cancer, with over 70% of patients with stage III disease found to have lost STING expression [19]. STING silencing renders traditional small molecule STING agonists ineffective in ovarian cancer cells, again motivating the development of strategies capable of overcoming dysregulated STING signaling.

We previously reported on a strategy to overcome STING silencing in cancer. The recombinant cytosolic domain of the STING protein lacking its transmembrane domain (STINGΔTM, Fig. 1A) is capable of activating downstream signaling even in cells that do not express STING [38]. When multimerized and delivered to the cytosol, STINGΔTM mimics the multimeric active state of the STING protein, allowing for the direct activation of downstream kinase TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) [38,39]. The ability to act further downstream in the signaling cascade bypasses the common roadblock posed by silencing of STING in cancer cells (Fig. 1B). Earlier work used the ligand 2′,3′-cGAMP (cGAMP) to convert STINGΔTM from an inactive dimeric state [38,40,41] into an active tetramer [38]. The cGAMP-STINGΔTM tetramer complex is a multifunctional therapeutic that can both act as a carrier to deliver cGAMP to cells to activate functional endogenous STING or act as an exogenous STING mimic to directly activate TBK1 and IRF3 (prior work is summarized in greater detail in Table S1). Treatment with free cGAMP-STINGΔTM complex has been examined previously by our group and has demonstrated promising efficacy in a variety of murine cancer models [38,[42], [43], [44]]. However, there is no known route for STINGΔTM to efficiently enter the cytosol to engage its targets TBK1 and IRF3 without a carrier, suggesting that the major mechanism of therapeutic efficacy observed in prior carrier-free in vivo studies is improved cGAMP delivery. Having a STING agonist dependent therapeutic effect is not ideal when targeting STING-silenced cancer cells that do not respond to cGAMP. Since TBK1 and IRF3 are present in the cytosol, these molecules are most effectively activated when STINGΔTM can efficiently reach the cytosol. In fact, cGAMP-STINGΔTM only showed activity on STING-silenced cells in vitro when delivered to the cytosol with a carrier, and showed no activity when dosed carrier-free [39]. These results suggest that carrier-based delivery to the cytosol will have an advantage over carrier-free strategies when it is beneficial to maximize the direct activation of TBK1 and IRF3, as is the case for STING silenced cancer.

We recently discovered that STINGΔTM can strongly activate TBK1 and IRF3 even without the addition of a ligand like cGAMP [39], presenting the opportunity to develop a simplified therapeutic incorporating the protein STINGΔTM alone. This activity is possible because polymer and lipid carriers used to deliver STINGΔTM to the cytosol can promote the conversion of inactive dimeric STINGΔTM protein into an active multimer, through a nonspecific mechanism that appears to be distinct from typical ligand induced polymerization [39]. STINGΔTM multimers were observed to form punctate structures that colocalized with TBK1 in cells, leading to TBK1 and IRF3 phosphorylation, and the transcription of IRF3 controlled genes including Type I IFN [39]. Delivery of STINGΔTM alone to the cytosol showed preliminary therapeutic promise, inducing a desirable Type I IFN response in a much wider fraction of cancer cell lines than a traditional small molecule STING agonist [39]. Prior experiments delivering STINGΔTM without a ligand were all carried out with transfection reagents in vitro, necessitating the development of a cytosolic delivery vehicle that is biocompatible and effective in vivo to advance this strategy as an immunotherapy. Beyond cytosolic delivery, ovarian cancer introduces a challenging delivery problem because it is discovered at an advanced stage for a majority of patients, where metastases have spread throughout the abdomen and beyond [31]. Metastasis makes the intratumoral administration route used for many STING agonists unfeasible and necessitates systemic delivery, where ovarian cancer targeting would be desirable to maximize therapeutic efficacy.

A number of polymer- [45,46], lipid- [[47], [48], [49]], and protein-based [50,51] carriers have been developed to encapsulate therapeutic proteins into nanoparticles and deliver them across the cell membrane in vivo. Proteins vary widely in size, shape, charge, and stability, making it difficult to develop a one size fits all solution for protein delivery; thus, it is important to consider the specific features of a protein cargo of interest. STINGΔTM is a 29.8 kDa protein (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1A) found mainly as a homodimer [38]. It is predicted to have a net negative charge at physiological pH, but an isoelectric point of 5.1 means STINGΔTM will be inconveniently net neutral under the acidic conditions commonly used for polymer and lipid nanoparticle assembly (Fig. 1D,E, Fig. S1B). Despite its near neutral net charge, STINGΔTM is predicted to have large patches of both positive and negative charge on its surface at both pH 5.2 and 7.2 (Fig. 1F and G). Finally, a unique challenge of STINGΔTM delivery is that it is inactive in its native dimeric state [38,40,41], so the delivery vehicle must also induce STINGΔTM multimerization to convert it into an active from [39]. For this purpose, we selected poly(β-amino esters) (PBAEs) as a modular and biodegradable polycation to develop a carrier for STINGΔTM [52]. Initially developed for electrostatic complexation and delivery of nucleic acids [52], PBAEs have been successfully adapted for protein delivery by incorporating monomers or end-caps with protein affinity such as carboxylate [45], guanidine [53], and boronic acid [46]. Moreover, our prior work demonstrated that a commonly used PBAE exhibited a preliminary ability to condense STINGΔTM into an active multimer and weakly activate downstream signaling [39]. Thus, we demonstrated that PBAEs are promising for cytosolic delivery of STINGΔTM but that further engineering is needed to facilitate effective delivery in vivo. To address the need for systemic delivery to ovarian cancer metastases, our group has previously used electrostatic assembly as a tool to coat nanoparticles with polyelectrolytes and screen for surface chemistries that enable ovarian cancer targeting [54]. The top hits from this screen were the polypeptides poly(L-aspartate) (PLD) and poly(L-glutamate) (PLE), which demonstrate effective ovarian cancer targeting in vitro and in murine models [54]. Recent work suggests that this targeting is driven by high avidity binding interactions between PLE or PLD and amino acid transporters that are overexpressed on the surface of ovarian cancer cells, notably the glutamate transporter SLC1A5 [55]. We previously applied these ovarian cancer targeting polyelectrolytes to improve the efficacy of liposomal and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles delivering siRNA, cytokines, or small molecule inhibitors [[56], [57], [58], [59], [60]] and now we apply ovarian cancer targeting polyelectrolytes to PBAE nanoparticles for protein delivery.

In this work, we develop a targeted PBAE nanoparticle to encapsulate and deliver STINGΔTM protein to the cytosol as an ovarian cancer immunotherapy. We screen a library of PBAEs to find that increased hydrophobicity enables effective encapsulation and functional delivery of STINGΔTM in an active state. A pH-shift based assembly procedure was developed to form negatively-charged, colloidally-stable nanoparticles containing STINGΔTM, the PBAE, and a polyanion. Tuning the polyanion component of the nanoparticle allowed for cell type specificity in Type I IFN response activation between ovarian cancer and immune cells in vitro, and for tumor specificity to be improved in a mouse model of metastatic ovarian cancer in vivo. Finally, as a therapeutic proof of concept, we demonstrate that STINGΔTM delivery with this nanoparticle carrier is able to activate a Type I IFN response in STING silenced ovarian cancer cell lines, as well as slow tumor growth and improve survival in a mouse model of ovarian cancer.

Protein production

Expression vectors

The plasmids used for the expression of mouse STINGΔTM, as well as the inactive S365A mutant, in E. coli were generated in previous work [38,61]. These plasmids contain the cytoplasmic domain of the mouse STING protein (amino acids 138 to 378) with a hexa-histidine-tag at the N-terminus connected by a TEV cleavage site, cloned into the pSH200 expression vector. Amino acid sequences of these proteins are included as Table S2. Mouse STINGΔTM has previously been demonstrated to be cross-reactive in human cells [39].

Protein expression and purification

Protein was expressed and purified based on a method described previously [38,39,61]. His-tagged STINGΔTM protein was expressed in BL21 DE3 (New England Biolabs). Transformed E. coli were picked from glycerol stocks into 50 mL of LB Broth and grown at 37 °C with 220 rpm orbital shaking overnight. Overnight culture was transferred into 950 mL of LB Broth and cultured at 37 °C until OD600 reached 0.4, and then induced with 1 mM IPTG at 18 °C overnight. Culture was centrifuged to pellet cells, which were washed with 1x PBS. Cells were lysed at room temperature for 20 min in 20 mL protein binding buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 0.5 M sodium chloride, and 10 mM imidazole) with 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 mg/mL lysozyme, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 tablet cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail, then sonicated at 18 W (with 2-s on and 5-s off intervals) for a total of 5 min in ice-water. Cell lysate was then centrifuged at 21,000g, 4 °C for 30 min and the supernatant was incubated with 1 mL of HisPur Cobalt Resin and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-114 for 1 h at 4 °C. Cobalt Resin was then washed with two 30 min, incubations in 10 mL of protein binding buffer and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-114, transferred to a Poly-Prep chromatography column and washed with 15 mL of protein binding buffer. The protein was eluted from the resin with 3 mL of protein elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 0.5 M sodium chloride, and 150 mM imidazole) and then exchanged into protein storage buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM sodium chloride, 1 mM DTT, and 10% (v/v) glycerol) using a 10 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter. Protein was stored at −80 °C until use.

Protein characterization

Protein concentration was quantified by DC (detergent compatible) protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories) following manufacturer instructions. Protein identity and purity were verified by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) under reducing conditions (10 mM DTT) using Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast protein gels (Bio-Rad) against Precision Plus Protein Dual Color Standards (Bio-Rad) following manufacturer instructions. Protein isoelectric point (pI) was measured using Novex isoelectric focusing (IEF) protein gels, pH 3-10 (Thermo Scientific) against a 9 protein, pI 4.45–9.6 standard (Bio-Rad 1610310). LPS levels in protein were quantified with HEK-Blue mTLR4 reporter cell (InvivoGen) following manufacturer instructions. When needed, Pierce High Capacity Endotoxin Removal Spin Columns (Thermo Scientific) were used to further remove LPS from protein following manufacturer instructions, with the modification that the protein incubation step was lengthened to be overnight at 4 °C. All STINGΔTM was purified until LPS levels were <0.5 EU/mg.

Protein labeling

STINGΔTM protein was exchanged into 100 mM sodium bicarbonate solution (pH 8.2 to 8.5) using a 10 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter to reach a final concentration of 4 mg/mL. This solution was added to a tube containing 8 mol equiv of either sulfo-Cy3 NHS ester, sulfo-Cy5 NHS ester or sulfo-Cy7 NHS ester (Lumiprobe) and mixed via pipet until homogeneous. The mixture was covered in foil and allowed to react at 4 °C overnight. Unreacted fluorophore dye was removed by exchange into protein storage buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM sodium chloride, 1 mM DTT, and 10% (v/v) glycerol) by using a 10 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter. Protein was sterilized with a 0.2 μm cellulose acetate filter and then stored at −80 °C until use. The removal of unreacted fluorophore was confirmed with thin layer chromatography (TLC) using silica gel 60 F254 (Sigma) as the stationary phase and methanol as the mobile phase. Unreacted dyes migrated near the solvent front with retention factors (Rf) of 0.8 to 0.9, while the purified labeled protein did not migrate (Rf = 0) and did not contain any apparent unreacted dye at Rf = 0.8 to 0.9. Average fluorescent tag incorporation was quantified as 1.4 (sulfo-Cy3-STINGΔTM), 1.2 (sulfo-Cy5-STINGΔTM), or 1.0 (sulfo-Cy7-STINGΔTM) dye molecules per STINGΔTM molecule using a procedure described by the vendor. Briefly, absorbances were measured using a NanoDrop OneC ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) to determine dye and protein concentrations. Sulfo-Cy3 concentration was measured using absorbance at 548 nm (εCy3 = 162,000 M−1 cm−1), sulfo-Cy5 concentration was measured using absorbance at 646 nm (εCy5 = 271,000 M−1 cm−1), and sulfo-Cy7 concentration was measured using absorbance at 750 nm (εCy7 = 240,600 M−1 cm−1). STINGΔTM concentration was measured using absorbance at 280 nm (εSTINGΔTM = 20,400 M−1 cm−1, calculated with SnapGene v7.0.1), correcting for dye absorbance at 280 nm using a dye specific correction factor (CF280,Cy3 = 0.06, CF280,Cy5 = 0.04, CF280,Cy7 = 0.04).

Predicting protein net and surface charge

STINGΔTM charge was predicted from its sequencing using the python package peptides.py version 0.3.4 [62] using the ‘Lehninger’ pKa scale [63]. The electrostatic surface potential of the crystallizable domain of mouse STING (PDB entry 4KC0) [64] was solved and visualized at pH 5.2 and pH 7.2 using PDB2PQR (version 3.6.1) and APBS (Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver, version 3.4.1) software [65]. The PROPKA model [66] was used to estimate protein pKa values and the PARSE forcefield [67] was used in PDB2PQR. For visualizations, electrostatic potential is mapped on the solvent accessible surface of the protein in units of [kT/e].

Nanoparticle generation and characterization

Poly(β-amino ester) synthesis and characterization

Poly(β-amino ester) (PBAE) synthesis was performed in previous work, using the same previously characterized batches of polymer [68]. Briefly, amines (piperazine and 3-bis(4-piperidinyl)propane)) and diacrylates (1,6-Hexanediol diacrylate, 1,4-butanedioldiacrylate, triethylene glycol diacrylate, and 1,9-bis(acryloyloxy)nonane) were reacted at a 1.02 molar ratio of amine to acrylate to generate an amine terminated polymer. Reactions were performed in anhydrous THF under nitrogen at 50 °C for 48 h. Polymers were purified thrice by reprecipitation in ice-cold hexanes (Poly2) or a 50:50 mix of hexanes and diethyl ether (Poly0, Poly2O, and Poly3). Polymers were thoroughly dried prior to use. Polymer identity was verified with 1H NMR (Bruker AVANCE Neo spectrometer at 500 MHz), molecular weight was measured with MALDI-TOF (Bruker MicroFlex), and clog P of the single polymer repeat unit was calculated with ChemDraw version 22.0. Solid PBAEs were stored at room temperature in a vacuum desiccator in nitrogen-flushed vials. PBAE stocks were dissolved in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) by 30-60 min of vortex mixing, and stored at −20 °C until use.

PBAE nanoparticle assembly

Stock solutions of STINGΔTM in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), Poly3 in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), and polyanion in 200 mM HEPES (pH 7.2) were prepared to achieve the desired polyanion:Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio with the Poly3 stock solution fixed at 2 mg/mL (near the upper limit for solubility). The Poly3 solution was added into the STINGΔTM solution at a 1:1 vol ratio with thorough pipette mixing. The polyanion solution in 200 mM HEPES (pH 7.2) was then added into the STINGΔTM solution at a 1:1 vol ratio with thorough pipette mixing, where a clear increase in solution turbidity was observed upon mixing indicating nanoparticle formation. Particles were purified and concentrated as needed using 100 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter (2000g). Particles were prepared freshly and used within 4 h of assembly.

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential measurement

The Z-average diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of the nanoparticles were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and the zeta potential by laser doppler anemometry, using a Malvern Zetasizer Pro Red (Malvern Panalytical). The measurements were performed at 25 °C with a red laser (wavelength = 633 nm) and a detection angle of 173°. Each data point reported is the mean of technical replicates for an independently assembled nanoparticle formulation.

Transmission electron microscopy

For negative-stain electron microscopy (negative stain TEM), 10 μL of the nanoparticle sample in buffer solution was applied to a 200-mesh copper grid coated with a continuous carbon film. After 90 s, excess solution was removed by gently touching the grid edge with a Kimwipe. Next, phosphotungstic acid (1% aqueous solution, negative stain) was applied to the grid. After 30 s, the excess stain was similarly removed, and the grid was air-dried at room temperature. The prepared grid was mounted on a JEOL single-tilt holder and inserted into the TEM column. For cryo-transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM), 3 μL of the nanoparticle sample (exchanged into mQ water using 100 kDa MWCO Amicon centrifugal filter at 2000g) in mQ water was applied to copper grids coated with a continuous carbon film. The grids were pre-treated with oxygen plasma using a Solarus 950 Gatan Advanced Plasma System. Excess sample on the grid was gently blotted using the Gatan Cryo Plunge III, followed by rapid plunging into liquid ethane to vitrify the sample. The grid was then mounted on a Gatan 626 single tilt cryo-holder, which was subsequently inserted into the TEM column. Both the specimen and the holder tip were maintained at cryogenic temperatures with liquid nitrogen to ensure preservation throughout the transfer and imaging process. For both negative stain TEM and Cryo-TEM, imaging was performed on a JEOL 2100 FEG microscope using a minimum dose method to reduce electron beam damage to the sample. The microscope was operated at 200 kV, with magnifications between 10,000x and 60,000x to evaluate particle size and distribution. All images were captured using a Gatan 2k x 2k UltraScan CCD camera.

Poly3 and STINGΔTM complex formation DLS experiment

DLS measurements were made of STINGΔTM and Poly3 solutions individually, upon initial mixing, and after the pH shift to examine when particle formation occurred. STINGΔTM protein was diluted to 1 mg/mL with 1x PBS (pH 7.4) or 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) to a final volume of 40 μL, which were analyzed using DLS. Poly3 solution at 2 mg/mL in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) was analyzed using DLS. The 1 mg/mL STINGΔTM solution (40 μL) and 2 mg/mL Poly3 solution (40 μL) were mixed well by pipette, then analyzed using DLS immediately. 80 μL of 200 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) was added to this solution and mixed well by pipette, then analyzed by DLS immediately. The average light scattering intensity corrected for the attenuator was measured by the DLS (derived mean count rate). Scattering intensity is a metric known to increase with both increased particle hydrodynamic size and particle concentration. Average scattering intensity was first corrected by subtracting the average scattering intensity of a matched buffer, then divided by a dilution factor to correct for the dilution that occurs as components are mixed together and HEPES buffer is added to shift the pH.

Analysis of STINGΔTM multimerization during complex formation using fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)

Sulfo-Cy3-labeled STINGΔTM and Sulfo-Cy5-labeled STINGΔTM were diluted to make a solution containing 0.5 mg/mL sulfo-Cy3-labeled STINGΔTM and 0.5 mg/mL sulfo-Cy5-labeled STINGΔTM (1 mg/mL total STINGΔTM) (FRET solution) with 1x PBS (pH 7.4) or 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2). Matched solutions of sulfo-Cy3-labeled STINGΔTM and unlabeled STINGΔTM (donor-only solution) or sulfo-Cy5-labeled STINGΔTM and unlabeled STINGΔTM (acceptor-only solution) were prepared as controls. A 2 mg/mL Poly3 solution in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) was added to the 1 mg/mL STINGΔTM solution in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) at a 1:1 vol ratio, then mixed well by pipette. A 200 mM HEPES solution (pH 7.2) was added to the mixture of STINGΔTM and Poly3 in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2) at a 1:1 vol ratio, then mixed well by pipette. Samples of STINGΔTM in 1x PBS, STINGΔTM in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), and STINGΔTM and Poly3 in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), were diluted to 0.25 mg/mL total STINGΔTM with a matched buffer before analysis, to match the final 0.25 mg/mL total STINGΔTM concentration of the STINGΔTM and Poly3 mixture that was diluted with HEPES.

Fluorescence values were measured in a 384-well black plate using an Infinite MPlex Plate Reader (Tecan). Corrected FRET efficiency (FRETN) was calculated as described previously [69,70]. Briefly, samples containing both donor and acceptor as well as matched acceptor-only and donor-only controls were evaluated for fluorescence intensity in the donor (550 nm excitation/590 nm emission), acceptor (630 nm excitation/670 nm emission), and FRET (550 nm excitation/670 nm emission) channels. Donor- and acceptor-only controls were used to correct FRET channel fluorescence for donor or acceptor emission in the channel, so that only FRET signal was measured. Corrected FRET channel fluorescence was further corrected for the concentration of the donor and acceptor to generate FRETN. Reported FRETN values were normalized so the average FRETN of STINGΔTM in 1x PBS has a value of 1.

Native PAGE gel shift encapsulation assay

PBAE nanoparticles were prepared as described above, both containing STINGΔTM and without STINGΔTM to control for artifacts caused by nonspecific staining of polymers. A concentration-matched free STINGΔTM control was prepared in a matched buffer: STINGΔTM was diluted to 0.5 mg/mL in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), then mixed with 1:1 (v/v) ratio 200 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.2). All samples were diluted at 1:1 (v/v) ratio in Native Sample Buffer, and 12 μL (1.5 μg STINGΔTM per lane) was loaded into a 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein gel. Native PAGE was run in 1× tris/glycine buffer at 100 V for 1 h using a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell (Bio-Rad) and a PowerPac™ HC High-Current Power Supply (Bio-Rad). Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining solution for 5 min, destained in a 10:40:50 (v/v/v) acetic acid:methanol:water for 30 min, and then further detained in DI water overnight. Gels were imaged with transillumination and automatic exposure time on an ImageQuant LAS 4000 (General Electric).

In vitro experiments

Cell lines

HEK293T pGL4.45 cells are a reporter derivative of HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216) generated previously in our lab [38]. Briefly, HEK293T were stably transfected with the pGL4.45[luc2P/ISRE/Hygro] vector (Promega) to allow for luciferase induction in response to IRF activation. RAW-Lucia ISG (rawl-isg), THP1-Dual (thpd-nfis), and HEK-Blue mTLR4 (hkb-mtlr4) reporter cells were purchased from InvivoGen. SKOV3 (HTB-77) and CAOV3 (HTB-75) cells were purchased from ATCC. Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assays

5.6 × 104 cell/mL cells were added to a 96-well plate in 180 μL of the media used to culture each cell line (described above), then allowed to incubate for 24 h. Cells were treated with 20 μL of PBAE nanoparticle prepared as described above to reach the final concentration specified in each figure caption, then allowed to incubate for 24 h. Plates were centrifuged at 500g to settle suspended cells, then the supernatant was sampled and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

DuoSet ELISA kits for human CXCL10 (R&D Systems DY266), mouse CXCL10 (R&D Systems DY466), human IFN-β (R&D Systems DY814), and mouse IFN-β (R&D Systems DY8234) were used with 1-Step TMB ELISA Substrate Solution (Thermo Scientific) following manufacturer instructions. Cytokine concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic curve-fit of a standard curve, with the bottom parameter constrained at the mean absorbance of the blank (GraphPad PRISM version 10.2.3). All calibration curves showed a good fit with coefficient of determination R2 > 0.95. The minimum detectable dose (MDD) was calculated by adding two standard deviations to the mean absorbance of the blank and converting this to a concentration using the standard curve. The MDD was 8.0 pg/mL for human CXCL10, 21 pg/mL for mouse CXCL10, 7.8 pg/mL for human IFN-β, and 4.6 for mouse IFN-β. Where no analyte was detected, the concentration was set at MDD for plotting and statistics, as the MDD is an upper bound for the highest possible concentration of analyte that could be present in these samples.

Animal studies

Animal Care and use

All animal experiments were approved by the MIT Committee on Animal Care (CAC, protocol number 2404000660) and were conducted under the oversight of the Division of Comparative Medicine (DCM). Female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and housed at Koch Institute for Integrative Cancer Research at MIT animal facility in cages of no more than five animals with controlled temperature (25 °C), 12 h light–dark cycles and free access to food and water.

Tumor model

Three million luciferized BPPNM cells were suspended in 200 μL 1x PBS and the injected IP in 8-9 week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Tumor burden was monitored using 2D IVIS bioluminescence imaging. Mice were injected IP with 200 μL of 15 mg/kg D-luciferin, sodium salt (GoldBio, LUCNA), and luminescence was measured 15 min later using the IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (Perkin Elmer). Missed IP injections where minimal bioluminescent signal was observed occurred for 10 of 309 injections (3%), in line with the previously reported 3-10% failure rate of IP injection [72,73]. Missed injections were not repeated and excluded from the study. Images were analyzed with Living Image Software version 4.7.2. Luminescence is reported as total flux in [p/s].

One day prior to treatment, mice were divided into groups with comparable distributions of tumor burden measured by IVIS bioluminescence. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 μg of STINGΔTM-loaded nanoparticle suspended in 200 μL 5% dextrose twice weekly starting 10 days after tumor inoculation (doses on day 10, 14, 17, 20, 24, and 27). 5% dextrose was used as a vehicle control. For survival studies, mice were monitored and euthanized when body condition score dropped below 2, weight loss exceeded 20%, or poor responsiveness was observed.

Nanoparticle biodistribution

The BPPNM model was initiated as described above, with mice moved onto AIN-93M Maintenance Purified Diet (TestDiet 1810541) at least one week prior to biodistribution measurements. One day prior to treatment, mice were divided into groups with comparable distributions of tumor burden measured by IVIS bioluminescence. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 60 μg of sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM-loaded nanoparticle suspended in 300 μL 5% dextrose 14 days after tumor inoculation. 5% dextrose was used as a vehicle control. Live mice were longitudinally imaged by IVIS at 5 min, 1 h, 3 h, 8 h, and 24 h to track nanoparticle fluorescence in the abdomen, measuring the fluorescence of sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM using a 745 excitation and 800 emission filter. Mice were euthanized at 24 h after dosing. Immediately following, necropsies were performed to harvest upper genital tract (UGT), omentum, liver, kidneys, spleen, intestines, heart and lungs from each mouse. Organs were immersed in RPMI 1640 media in 24-well plates and placed on ice until imaging. Immediately prior to imaging, organs were submerged in 1 mg/mL D-luciferin solution in 1x PBS for 2 min and imaged by IVIS. Organs were imaged using bioluminescence to track BPPNM tumors. Nanoparticles were imaged using fluorescence of sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM using a 745 excitation and 800 emission filter. Living Image Software was used to measure the bioluminescent total flux in [p/s], fluorescent total radiant efficiency in [p/s]/[μW/cm2], fluorescence average radiant efficiency in [p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2], and area in [cm2] of each organ. To correct for background fluorescence, average fluorescence of the organ in the vehicle 5% dextrose treated mice was subtracted from each measurement. The fraction of recovered fluorescence was computed as the total radiant efficiency of the organ divided by sum of the total radiant efficiency for all organs recovered from an individual mouse.

A randomly selected mouse from each group was imaged by cryo-fluorescence tomography (CFT). Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation at 24 h after dosing and immediately frozen by slow immersion in a hexanes and dry ice bath for 5 min. Frozen mice were stored at −20 °C for at least 24 h to dry and then embedded in with optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Mice were sectioned and imaged using an Xerra Cryofluorecence Tomography (CFT) imaging system (EMIT Imaging) using 55 μM sections. For each section, a white light image was captured. Fluorescence images were also captured with 470 nm excitation and 511 nm emission to measure GFP expressed by BPPNM and 730 nm excitation and 794 nm emission to measure sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM loaded in nanoparticles, using optimized exposure times. Images were processed with Fiji version 2.16.0.

Data analysis and statistics

All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad PRISM version 10.2.3. Statistical significance was computed by ordinary one-way or two-way one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey correction for multiple comparisons, as specified in figure captions. Results were considered significant at: ∗∗∗∗ = P < 0.001, ∗∗∗ = P < 0.001, ∗∗ = P < 0.01, ∗ = P < 0.05, and ns = not significant. Data is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), or geometric mean ± geometric SD, as specified in the figure caption.

Results

A hydrophobic PBAE delivers STINGΔTM to cytosol

A library of PBAEs, previously synthesized and tested in our lab for siRNA delivery [68], were screened for their ability to deliver STINGΔTM protein to the cytosol (Fig. 2A). PBAEs were designed to have variable hydrophobicity and protonatable amine pKa values. Number average molecular weights (Mn) ranged from 2.0 to 3.8 kDa (Table S3) [68], within the range of molecular weights used in other studies evaluating PBAE for nucleic acid delivery [[74], [75], [76]]. PBAEs were screened with a functional delivery assay (Fig. 2B–C) using a HEK293T-derived IRF reporter cell line previously developed in our lab [38]. With this cell line, successful delivery of STINGΔTM in an active state to the cytosol triggers the downstream transcription factor IRF3 and results in the expression of a luciferase reporter. Functional STINGΔTM delivery increased with PBAE hydrophobicity within the set of PBAEs tested (Fig. 2B). The most hydrophobic polymer, Poly3, was able to deliver STINGΔTM and activate IRF signaling nearly as effectively as the commercial transfection reagent TransIT-X2 over a range of polymer to STINGΔTM (w/w) ratios from 1 to 4. Higher doses of Poly2 and Poly3 caused drops in cell viability (Fig. 2C), indicating that toxicity of these polycations must be weighed against increased transfection efficiency at higher weight to weight (w/w) ratios. A Poly3 to STINGΔTM w/w ratio of 2 was selected for all formulations moving forward to balance delivery efficacy and toxicity.

The formation of particulate complexes was then examined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure particle hydrodynamic size and scattered light intensity as a rough metric for particle concentration. Dilution of STINGΔTM protein in the 25 mM, pH 5.2 sodium acetate buffer used to solubilize PBAEs resulted in increased light scattering compared to dilution in 1x PBS (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2), likely indicating a small degree of protein aggregation is induced by the reduced pH and ionic strength of the sodium acetate buffer. For nucleic acid cargos, polymer complex assembly is driven by electrostatic forces and typically occurs immediately after the cargo and PBAE are mixed in an acidic buffer [52,68]. We instead observed that light scattering did not greatly increase upon mixing STINGΔTM and Poly3 compared to STINGΔTM alone in sodium acetate buffer (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2). This observation suggests that STINGΔTM assembly with Poly3 to form particles was limited under these conditions. Diluting this mixture into a high concentration HEPES buffer at pH 7.2 to shift closer to physiological pH resulted in an order of magnitude increase in scattering intensity and the formation of a well-defined particle population with a cleaner correlogram (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2). These results suggest that a shift to neutral pH is required to enable complexation of STINGΔTM by Poly3. Compared to the other three PBAEs examined, Poly3 leads to the highest scattered light intensity and smallest particle size (Fig. S3). This result suggests that the strong functional delivery ability of Poly3 is correlated to its ability to form high concentrations of smaller particles that are better suited for cellular uptake compared to other PBAEs.

Given the requirement for STINGΔTM to be converted into a multimeric state for activity [39], we used FRET to probe which step during particulate complex formation drives STINGΔTM multimerization (Fig. 2E). Normalized FRET efficiency did not increase greatly when STINGΔTM was first mixed with Poly3 at pH 5.2, but then increased substantially when pH was shifted upward by dilution in HEPES buffer at pH 7.2 (Fig. 2E), coinciding with particle formation. Increased FRET suggests that STINGΔTM molecules enter closer proximity with each other, providing evidence that complexation by Poly3 to form particles can also simultaneously promote STINGΔTM multimerization.

Combined, these results led us to hypothesize that hydrophobicity is a major driver of particle assembly. As Poly3 is deprotonated above its pKa of 6.2 [68] during the pH shift, a loss of charge and degree of hydration increases hydrophobic interactions with STINGΔTM protein to enable assembly into particles (Fig. 2F). We used this process of shifting the pH by dilution of STINGΔTM and Poly3 in sodium acetate into a concentrated HEPES buffer to generate all particles going forward. These particles had an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 600 nm by DLS and were weakly positively charged based on electrophoretic motility measurements (Fig. 2G–H). Assembly of these particles appeared inconsistent between batches, with DLS sizes ranging over an order of magnitude from 200 to 2000 nm. Negative stained room temperature transmission electron microscopy (TEM) suggested that the large particles observed by DLS are aggregates of many smaller 20-50 nm particles (Fig. S4A). While effective in vitro, the large size, aggregation, and inconsistency in particle assembly demonstrated a need to improve this formulation.

Incorporation of a polyanion in PBAE nanoparticles

We hypothesized that incorporating a polyanionic component in the PBAE formulation would improve stability by giving particles a strong negative surface charge to increase electrostatic repulsion between particles. Polyanion in a high concentration HEPES buffer was mixed with the STINGΔTM and Poly3 solution in sodium acetate (Fig. 3A) to allow polyanion to be present when particle formation was initiated by pH shift. By varying the mass ratio of model polyanion poly(L-aspartate) (PLD) to Poly3, we found that incorporating a high enough mass of polyanion allowed for the consistent formation of sub-200 nm, sub-0.2 polydispersity, and strongly negatively charged nanoparticles (Fig. 3B–C). Increasing the PLD:Poly3 mass ratio above 1:1 did not result in major changes in size, polydispersity, or charge, so this mass ratio was selected for future experiments. The 2:2:1 PLD:Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio nanoparticles appeared to be homogenous spherical particles in negative stained TEM (Fig. S4B) and Cryo-TEM images (Fig. 3D, Fig. S5). These nanoparticles also showed nearly complete encapsulation of STINGΔTM with a gel shift assay, where no free, unencapsulated STINGΔTM protein was observed when unpurified nanoparticles were run in a Native PAGE gel (Fig. 3E). We then examined stability of the 2:2:1 PLD:Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio nanoparticles. Nanoparticles remained colloidally stable for 24 h up to physiological ionic strength, with minimal change in Z-average diameter or polydispersity in 150 mM sodium chloride or 1x PBS (Fig. S6). Examining longer term storage at 4 °C over weeks, nanoparticles showed excellent colloidal stability (Fig. S7A and B), but bioactivity measured in HEK293T IRF3 reporter cells was completely lost within days (Fig. S7C). For this reason, we used freshly assembled PBAE nanoparticles for all experiments.

To better understand how formulation parameters impact nanoparticle properties and STINGΔTM delivery ability, we performed univariate sweeps of key formulation parameters centered at the 2:2:1 PLD:Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio nanoparticles formed in pH 7.2 HEPES buffer (Fig. S8). Increasing the Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio at a constant PLD:Poly3 ratio resulted in stronger STING activation in HEK293T reporter cells, but also increased particle size. Similarly, decreasing the pH of HEPES buffer using to shift pH during assembly resulted in stronger STING activation but larger particle size, although the effect of pH change was smaller compared to varying particle composition. The tradeoff between minimizing particle size and maximizing STING activation in cells makes it difficult to predict the formulation that will translate to the highest efficacy in vivo. We chose to move forward with the 2:2:1 PLD:Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio nanoparticle formed in pH 7.2 HEPES buffer to balance strong activity with a smaller particle size.

Polyanions alter cellular specificity of STING activation

Having developed a strategy to form PBAE nanoparticles containing PLD, we then examined if other polyanions could be incorporated into the PBAE nanoparticles using the same strategy. Our lab has previously screened polyanionic surface coatings on nanoparticles to discover polyanions that can improve targeting of ovarian cancer [54]. These polyanions have successfully been applied to improve nanoparticle formulations treating mouse models of ovarian cancer, including poly-lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) formulations for the delivery of small molecule inhibitors [59] and liposomal formulations for the delivery of cytokines [56,77] or nucleic acids [57]. We sought to examine if the same polyanions would improve ovarian cancer targeting of STINGΔTM when incorporated in a PBAE nanoparticle, as prior work has demonstrated that nanoparticles with different core compositions can behave differently even when they share the same polyanion surface chemistry [78]. We tested a small library of polyanions (Fig. 4A), including previous ovarian cancer targeting hits poly(L-glutamate) (PLE) and poly(L-aspartate) (PLD) [54,55], polyanions expected to be more biologically inert including PLD-block-poly(ethylene glycol) (PLD-PEG) and poly(acrylate) (PAA), as well as a polyanion that has previously shown very low ovarian cancer association, dextran sulfate (DS) [54]. Polyanions were incorporated into the nanoparticle formulation with a constant Poly3:STINGΔTM mass ratio of 2 and a polyanion:Poly3 mass ratio ranging from 0.6 to 1, selected to achieve a negative surface charge (Table S4). All nanoparticles incorporating polyanions had an average size near or below 200 nm (Fig. 4B), and an average surface charge below −30 mV (Fig. 4C), with the exception of PLD-PEG which remained more neutral at approximately −20 mV. All formulations showed no detectable free STINGΔTM after particle formation using a native PAGE gel shift assay (Fig. S9), indicating encapsulation efficiencies of STINGΔTM approaching 100%.

Nanoparticles were tested on a panel of cell lines including murine and human ovarian cancer (BPPNM and SKOV3), murine and human myeloid cells (RAW264.7 and THP1), and HEK293T. Functional activation of downstream signaling was measured as IRF3 transcription factor activity in reporter cell lines (RAW264.7, THP1, and HEK293T) or levels of the downstream chemokine CXCL10, commonly used to measure STING activation [29,79,80] (BPPNM and SKOV3) (Fig. 4D, Fig. S10). STINGΔTM doses were independently optimized for each individual cell line in the 5-20 μg/mL range, to maximize STING activation so that differences between nanoparticles become more apparent. Formulations containing no polyanion, labeled “Poly3” in figures, were broadly able to activate strong STING signaling in all cell lines. PLE and PLD containing nanoparticles recapitulated previously observed ovarian cancer specificity, retaining strong activity in ovarian cancer lines while showing low activity in immune cell lines. DS containing nanoparticles recapitulated poor ovarian cancer activity, showing nearly no functional signaling activation in ovarian cancer cell lines. On the other hand, PAA containing nanoparticles showed reduced activity in ovarian cancer cell lines, while maintaining activity in both immune cell lines. We then examined if the polyanionic component of the nanoparticle impacted stability by testing if nanoparticles retained a functional ability to activate downstream STING signaling in the HEK293T IRF3 reporter after incubation in ascites fluid. All nanoparticles retained similar or increased activity after 24 h incubation in ascites, but had reduced activity by 48 h, except for the PLD and PLD-PEG nanoparticles which showed the best longer-term stability in a relevant biological fluid (Fig. S11). Altogether, these results demonstrate that the polyanion component of a PBAE nanoparticle can be used to tune the cellular specificity in STING activation. We decided to move forward with the PLD nanoparticle formulation due to its strong STING activation and specificity for ovarian cancer cell lines, consistent formation of small nanoparticles, and stability in ascites among the set of polyanions tested.

PBAE nanoparticles accumulate in metastases throughout the abdomen

We then examined the tumor targeting ability of the PBAE nanoparticles by performing biodistribution studies (Fig. 5A). We selected the BPPNM syngeneic mouse model of ovarian cancer [71], which recapitulates the abdominal metastases commonly observed in ovarian cancer patients [81,82]. We examined DS and PLD formulations, since these formulations demonstrated minimal ovarian cancer transfection and ovarian cancer specific transfection in vitro, respectively. Poly3 nanoparticles without added polyanion were used as a control. Nanoparticles were injected intraperitoneally (IP), as this route of administration has been observed to improve targeting of abdominal tumors when compared to intravenous administration [54,83], and is used clinically for ovarian cancer treatment [83,84].

C57BL/6J mice were injected with 3 × 106 Luc+ BPPNM cells IP. At day 14, mice were treated with 60 μg sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM delivered by Poly3 nanoparticle, DS nanoparticle, or PLD nanoparticle or a 10% dextrose vehicle control administered IP. (B) Necropsies were performed 24 h after administration and IVIS was used to measure fluorescence of sulfo-Cy7-labled STINGΔTM in each organ reported as a fraction of total recovered fluorescence signal (N = 4-5 mice). (C) Representative IVIS images showing luminescence of Luc+ BPPNM (top) and fluorescence of sulfo-Cy7-labled STINGΔTM (bottom) for each formulation. Mice were flash frozen 24 h after administration and CFT was used to image fluorescence of (D) GFP from BPPNM and autofluorescence of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and (E) sulfo-Cy7-labled STINGΔTM in whole mouse for PLD nanoparticle treated condition. Two-way ANOVA on fraction of recovered fluorescence, with Tukey correction for multiple comparisons, comparing only different treatments within each organ. Data represented as mean ± standard deviation. All significant comparisons are shown. ∗∗∗∗ = P < 0.001, ∗ = P < 0.05.

First, we looked at nanoparticle pharmacokinetics to understand the clearance rate of the different nanoparticles from the abdomen, by tracking the sulfo-Cy7-labeled STINGΔTM fluorescence with an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). All nanoparticles had similar kinetic profiles, with sulfo-Cy7-labled STINGΔTM fluorescence in the abdomen peaking at 8 h post administration and starting to decrease by 24 h (Fig. S12). Although differences were minor between nanoparticle formulations up to 8 h, the PLD nanoparticle was cleared most rapidly and yielded approximately half the fluorescence signal at 24 h compared to Poly3 and DS nanoparticles (Fig. S12). Necropsies were performed 24 h after IP administration to track tumor and nanoparticle accumulation at the organ level using ex vivo IVIS. Solid tumors, observed by bioluminescence, occurred primarily in the omentum with metastases present throughout the abdominal cavity, commonly associating with upper genital tract (UGT), liver, and intestines (Fig. 5B, Figure S13, Figure S14). To examine nanoparticle specificity for the tumor, we examined the fraction of STINGΔTM fluorescence that accumulated in each organ by IVIS. All nanoparticles had high on-target accumulation in the tumored omentum with the intestines being the primary site of off-target accumulation (Fig. 5B and C). Incorporating either polyanion in the nanoparticle formulation increased on-target accumulation in the omentum while decreasing off-target accumulation in the intestines in comparison to the Poly3 control nanoparticle (Fig. 5B). PLD and DS nanoparticles did not show any statistically significant differences in the fraction of STINGΔTM fluorescence that accumulated in any of the organs analyzed (Fig. 5C). Moreover, IVIS images show that STINGΔTM fluorescence colocalized well with tumor bioluminescence in all organs analyzed (Fig. 5B), demonstrating that nanoparticles preferentially accumulate at sites of metastases even in organs that typically have lower nanoparticle accumulation. We validated these findings using cryo-fluorescence tomography (CFT) to examine tumor and nanoparticle biodistribution at higher resolution throughout an entire mouse in three dimensions. Tumors were tracked by GFP fluorescence, although autofluorescence from the gastrointestinal track (GI) was also observed in this channel (Fig. S15, Video S1). STINGΔTM fluorescence for all nanoparticles accumulated throughout the abdomen and colocalized well with tumors (Fig. 5D–E, Fig. S16, Video 1). Overall, these results demonstrate that IP administered PBAE nanoparticles can deliver STINGΔTM with high tumor specificity to metastatic lesions throughout the abdomen. Incorporating polyanions in the nanoparticle appeared to improve on-target accumulation in tumors, however, the polyanion component of the formulation did not have a major impact on STINGΔTM biodistribution.

STINGΔTM delivery with PBAE nanoparticle has therapeutic efficacy in ovarian cancer models

As a proof of concept, the PBAE nanoparticle containing PLD was tested for the therapeutically effective STINGΔTM delivery in cell line and mouse models of ovarian cancer. STING expression was examined in a panel of human and murine ovarian cancer cell lines by Western blot (Fig. 6A). We selected a subset of cells from this panel including SKOV3 and KURAMOCHI with minimal STING expression, BPPNM and A2780 with low STING expression, and KPCA.C with high STING expression to test for functional STING activation after treatment with the PBAE nanoparticle (Fig. 6B). Cells were treated with PBAE nanoparticle loaded with STINGΔTM or the inactive control mutant STINGΔTM S365A, which lacks a key phosphorylation site and is incapable of activating the transcription factor IRF3 [85]. As an additional control to determine if functional STING activation by a small molecule STING agonist was possible, cells were treated with the clinically relevant STING agonist ADU-S100 [5,12] at a 50 μM dose, higher than the 2-10 μM range of reported EC50 values [[86], [87], [88]]. All cell lines treated with STINGΔTM showed significantly elevated levels of CXCL10 measured by ELISA compared to cells treated with buffer or the STINGΔTM S365A control. Improved activity of STINGΔTM compared to the STINGΔTM S365A control confirms that activity is related to the on-target activation of the IRF3 transcription factor by STINGΔTM. STINGΔTM delivery was also effective even in SKOV3 and A2780 cell lines, which did not respond to ADU-S100. Similar trends were observed for IFN-β expression levels in all human cell lines; however, IFN-β levels could not be measured for mouse cell lines because they were below the limit of detection (Fig. S17). Overall, these results confirm that using a PBAE nanoparticle for cytosolic STINGΔTM delivery is capable of activating desired innate signaling in a variety of ovarian cancer cell lines, including cells with defective STING signaling that do not respond to a traditional STING agonist.

We then examined efficacy of the PBAE nanoparticle containing PLD nanoparticle in vivo, using a syngeneic BPPNM mouse model of ovarian cancer (Fig. 6C) [71]. This model was selected as it had the lowest endogenous STING expression among the mouse models tested (Fig. 6A). Mice were treated with nanoparticles loaded with STINGΔTM, nanoparticles loaded with the inactive STINGΔTM S365A mutant control, or a 5% dextrose vehicle control. A 100 μg STINGΔTM dose was selected as the minimum dose with a notable therapeutic effect (Fig. S18). Nanoparticle treatment was well tolerated, with no difference in mouse mass change compared to the dextrose vehicle over the first four doses and up to day 20 (Figure S19B,C and Figure S20A). Signs of undesirable systemic immune activation were not observed, with no CXCL10 or IFN-β detected in the serum 6 h after nanoparticle treatment (Fig. S21). Ascites is the buildup of fluid in the abdomen, a common symptom of ovarian cancer that is recapitulated in the BPPNM model [71]. We did not quantitatively measure ascites buildup, but observed qualitative signs of ascites formation such as abdominal distension and rapid weight gain in Fig. S20A were common in dextrose treated mice but occurred infrequently in nanoparticle treated mice. Treatment appeared to slow tumor growth, with tumor burden measured by IVIS bioluminescent signal being significantly lower for STINGΔTM nanoparticle treated mice compared to vehicle treated mice at multiple time points after day 20 (Fig. 6D, Figure S19D,E, Figure S20B, Figure S22). Ultimately, STINGΔTM nanoparticle treatment resulted in a significant median survival increase of 1 week compared to the vehicle, a 22% extension in survival (Fig. 6E). STINGΔTM-loaded nanoparticle consistently improved survival compared to nanoparticles loaded with STINGΔTM S365A control providing evidence that on-target STINGΔTM interactions with IRF3 and the resulting cytokine response are responsible for therapeutic benefit, although these effects did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.055 by Log-Rank test, Fig. 6E). Overall, this proof of concept experiment provides evidence that the STINGΔTM-loaded nanoparticle formulation can have a therapeutic effect in an ovarian cancer model.

Discussion

We demonstrated that increasing PBAE hydrophobicity enables encapsulation and delivery of functional protein into the cytosol. This result aligns with prior literature on adapting PBAEs from the delivery of nucleic acids to proteins. Electrostatic interactions alone often are not sufficient for efficient protein delivery, so the PBAE are often modified to strengthen other interactions that improve protein affinity [45,46,53]. In our case, increasing hydrophobicity by increasing the hydrocarbon chain length of the PBAE backbone improved protein affinity enough to enable encapsulation and cytosolic delivery. Leveraging hydrophobic interactions for an effective encapsulation of proteins has previously been reported for other cargos [89]. Poly3 did not form complexes with STINGΔTM observable by DLS at pH 5.2, but did at pH 7.2, indicating that a further increase in hydrophobicity upon deprotonation of Poly3 amines above its pKa at 6.2 is required for sufficiently strong PBAE-STINGΔTM interactions to enable complexation. The pH increases from 5.2 to 7.2 also shifts the net charge of STINGΔTM from neutral to weakly negative, likely strengthening electrostatic interactions between STINGΔTM and Poly3 and further promoting complexation at pH 7.2. We did not examine the effect of hydrophobic PBAE interactions on STINGΔTM's folded structure, but it is possible that increased polymer hydrophobicity could result in denaturation. STINGΔTM has rather uniquely been shown to retain its functional ability to activate downstream signaling molecules TBK1 and IRF3 after denaturation [39], presumably because key TBK1 and IRF3 interaction motifs are found in a disordered segment of the protein. While denaturation is not a barrier for STINGΔTM delivery, it would be interesting to examine if a hydrophobic PBAE is capable of delivering other proteins such as enzymes with activity more dependent on their folded structure.

We were able to successfully incorporate polyanions in a PBAE formulation to generate nanoparticles with a negative zeta potential and uniform particle size. Unlike our group's prior work adsorbing polyelectrolytes on the surface of an existing nanoparticle, we instead incorporated the polyanion at the point of nanoparticle formation itself so that polyanions are presumably incorporated throughout the nanoparticle instead of just at the surface. PBAE nanoparticles appeared homogeneous when observed under CryoTEM, without any obvious internal structure. Despite this different nanoparticle arrangement, we were able to recapitulate trends observed in prior work screening polyanions for ovarian cancer specificity in vitro [54], with PLE and PLD enabling and DS inhibiting functional STING signaling activation in ovarian cancer cell lines. These results add to the growing body of evidence that PLE and PLD have broad utility in improving ovarian cancer targeting in a wide variety of nanoparticle systems [54,[56], [57], [58], [59]]. It is also interesting to note that these trends from the prior ovarian cancer cell screen measuring only nanoparticle association with cells [54] hold even in the functional delivery screen performed in this work, where PBAE nanoparticles must associate with the cell, get internalized, escape the endosome, and release STINGΔTM in its bioactive form into the cytosol. In contrast to the strong impact of polyanion on functional STINGΔTM delivery in vitro, the polyanion component of the nanoparticle formulation had only a minor impact on in vivo biodistribution at the organ level. In all nanoparticles examined, biodistribution was quite favorable, with a large fraction of nanoparticles accumulating in tumor-rich organs such as the omentum and good accumulation in metastases throughout the abdomen. The incorporation of a polyanion in the Poly3 nanoparticle made a significant change in organ accumulation trends, but differences between the PLD and DS nanoparticle were not statistically significant. The discrepancy between the polyanion component of the nanoparticle causing a strong difference in functional activity in vitro but minimal change in organ level biodistribution in vivo warrants further investigation in future work. Detailed examination of nanoparticle association, trafficking, and endosomal escape could yield mechanistic insight into how different polyanions alter the specificity of functional activity and how functional activity is correlated with nanoparticle association. It would also be valuable to measure the cellular biodistribution of PBAE nanoparticles in vivo in key organs like the tumor, to determine if in vitro functional activity trends hold at the cellular level in vivo.

Lastly, we demonstrated the capacity of STINGΔTM to activate desirable innate immune response in STING-silenced ovarian cancer cell lines and ultimately show a therapeutic benefit in a murine model of ovarian cancer. These results make this work a successful proof of concept for applying the cytosolic delivery of STINGΔTM as an immunotherapy. However, the overall therapeutic benefit was modest, only slowing tumor growth instead of eliminating the tumor, and improving median survival by a week. Efficacy of this strategy would need to be improved and demonstrated in additional models to warrant further translation towards the clinic as an ovarian cancer immunotherapy. The 100 μg dose used in therapeutic studies approaches the upper limit for nanoparticle concentration we were capable of generating using the methods described in this work, so enhancing potency is the logical next step to improve efficacy. Exploration of a broader set of PBAE structures and further optimization of nanoparticle composition are both promising options to improve potency. Combination with chemotherapy to induce cancer cell death or immune checkpoint blockade to leverage the inflamed tumor microenvironment have mechanistic potential for synergistic improvements in efficacy. Given the known importance of STING activation in immune and stromal cells in the tumor [5,8,90], it would also be reasonable to examine nanoparticles with polyanion components capable of activating STING signaling in other cell types such as PAA, provided they maintain strong accumulation in the tumor to limit systemic toxicity.

Beyond improving therapeutic efficacy, further translation of PBAE nanoparticles to deliver STINGΔTM would require more detailed characterization of the immunological effects of this therapy in the tumor microenvironment to validate its mechanism of action. Although we did not see signs of severe toxicity like weight loss or systemic increases in inflammatory cytokines, comprehensive safety studies would be required, particularly if potency was improved. In addition to systemic immune activation, intestinal inflammation would be crucial to examine given that intestines are the primary off-target site of nanoparticle accumulation. One limitation of the nanoparticle formulation developed in this work was poor stability in solution at 4 °C, leaving long-term storage as a challenge that remains to be solved to enable further translation of this formulation. We anticipate that lyophilization could enable longer term storage as this approach has been effective for other PBAE nanoparticles [91,92], however, future work will be required to find lyophilization conditions that effectively stabilize both the size and activity of this formulation. Finally, benchmarking against existing small molecule STING agonists or other similar options like mRNA encoded STING mimics [93] would be important to demonstrate the therapeutic utility of PBAE nanoparticles for STINGΔTM delivery.

Conclusion

We developed a nanoparticle carrier for the cytosolic delivery of STINGΔTM protein to enable its use as an ovarian cancer immunotherapy, ultimately showing tumor specific accumulation and a modest survival extension in a mouse model of ovarian cancer. While our previous work showed that cytosolic delivery of STINGΔTM can activate desired innate immune signaling in vitro, we have now demonstrated the application of cytosolic STINGΔTM delivery in vivo. To develop an effective nanoparticle formulation, we discovered that increasing PBAE hydrophobicity can enable STINGΔTM encapsulation and functional delivery to the cytosol. We also developed a pH-shift-based nanoparticle assembly method that allowed for the simultaneous incorporation of polyanions into PBAE nanoparticles to yield negatively charged, stable nanoparticles. The polyanions PLD and PLE improved the ovarian cancer targeting of STING signaling activation at a cellular level. This work motivates further exploration of cytosolic STING protein fragment delivery as an immunotherapy that shows promise even when STING signaling is dysregulated.
