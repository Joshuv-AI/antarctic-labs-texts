Strengthening Midwifery Services for Improved Maternal and Newborn Health Outcomes in Somalia: A Narrative Review of Epidemiological Patterns, Workforce Gaps, and Strategic Interventions
Authors: Nahwa Hassan Mohamud, Mohamed Ibrahim Muhudin, Fardowso Dahir Warsame, Salad Halane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite ongoing efforts to strengthen reproductive and maternal healthcare services, maternal and newborn mortality remains a major public health challenge in Somalia. Limited access to skilled birth attendants, shortages of qualified midwives, weak healthcare infrastructure, and persistent socioeconomic and security challenges continue to adversely affect maternal and newborn health outcomes in the country. This narrative review aimed to examine the epidemiological patterns of maternal and newborn health in Somalia, assess workforce gaps in midwifery services, and identify strategic priorities for strengthening these services. A literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and reports from the World Health Organization, United Nations Population Fund, United Nations Children’s Fund, and Federal Government of Somalia. A total of 55 records were identified, of which 18 were excluded as duplicates or irrelevant titles/abstracts, leaving 37 records screened and assessed for full-text eligibility; following full-text assessment, 22 publications were excluded (not focused on maternal/newborn health or midwifery in Somalia, insufficient data or unclear methodology, outside the review scope, or full text unavailable), and 15 studies and reports were included in the final review. The findings revealed that Somalia continues to experience among the highest maternal and neonatal mortality rates globally, with only 32% of births attended by skilled health personnel; this synthesis indicates that persistently low skilled birth attendance is closely linked to an inequitably distributed and under-resourced midwifery workforce, rather than any single isolated cause. Major workforce challenges include shortages and unequal distribution of midwives, inadequate training capacities, and poor working conditions. By explicitly linking epidemiological indicators to workforce production, distribution, and retention data, this review offers a more targeted evidence base than previous descriptive accounts, with direct implications for national midwifery workforce policy and investment planning. Strengthening midwifery education, workforce retention, professional regulation, and access to skilled maternal healthcare services is essential to improve maternal and newborn health outcomes in Somalia.Keywords: Somalia, midwifery, maternal health, newborn health, maternal mortality, neonatal mortality, skilled birth attendance

Introduction

Maternal and newborn health remains a major global public health priority and is a key indicator of health system effectiveness. Despite considerable progress in reducing maternal mortality over the past two decades, preventable deaths during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period continue to pose significant challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 260,000 women died from pregnancy-related causes worldwide in 2023, with nearly 92% of these deaths occurring in low- and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs). Sub-Saharan Africa continues to bear the greatest burden of maternal mortality globally, accounting for the majority of maternal deaths and highlighting persistent inequities in access to quality maternal healthcare services.1,2

Somalia has one of the highest maternal and newborn mortality rates in the world. Recent estimates indicate that the maternal mortality ratio ranges between 563 and 621 deaths per 100,000 live births, substantially higher than the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030.3,4 Newborn survival also remains a major concern, with neonatal mortality estimated at approximately 34–36 deaths per 1000 live births and stillbirth rates among the highest globally.5 These indicators reflect substantial challenges in ensuring access to quality reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health services across the country.

The high burden of maternal and newborn mortality in Somalia is closely associated with decades of armed conflict, political instability, population displacement, recurrent droughts, poverty, and fragile healthcare systems. These factors have contributed to inadequate healthcare infrastructure, shortages of skilled healthcare workers, limited availability of essential medicines and supplies, and substantial geographical disparities in access to healthcare services.6 Rural and nomadic populations are particularly affected, often facing long distances to health facilities and limited access to emergency obstetric and newborn care services. Consequently, many women continue to deliver at home without the assistance of skilled birth attendants, increasing the risk of maternal and neonatal complications and mortality rates. Access to skilled maternal healthcare services is limited in Somalia. Evidence indicates that only approximately 32% of births are attended by skilled health personnel, while the utilization of antenatal, delivery, and postnatal healthcare services remains suboptimal.7 The limited coverage of skilled birth attendance is particularly concerning because the majority of maternal deaths are preventable through timely access to evidence-based interventions delivered during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. Improving access to skilled maternal healthcare providers is therefore considered one of the most effective strategies for reducing maternal and newborn mortality in low-resource settings.8

Midwives play a central role in improving maternal and newborn health outcomes by providing comprehensive care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. Their responsibilities include antenatal care, skilled attendance during delivery, newborn care, family planning services, health promotion, early identification of pregnancy-related complications, and timely referral for emergency obstetric care when necessary. The WHO and the International Confederation of Midwives recognize midwives as essential healthcare professionals capable of delivering the majority of evidence-based interventions needed to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity.9

Despite their critical contribution to maternal and newborn health, Somalia faces a significant shortage and unequal distribution of qualified midwives in rural areas. The country continues to experience challenges related to inadequate training capacity, limited professional development opportunities, workforce attrition, insecurity, and insufficient deployment of skilled personnel to rural and underserved communities.6 These workforce constraints limit the availability of skilled birth attendance and reduce the capacity of the health system to provide quality maternal and newborn healthcare services. Furthermore, many midwives work in resource-constrained settings characterized by shortages of equipment, supplies, transportation, and supportive supervision, which can adversely affect the quality of services and patient outcomes. Recognizing these challenges, the Federal Government of Somalia, together with international partners such as the WHO, UNICEF, and UNFPA, has implemented several initiatives aimed at strengthening reproductive, maternal, newborn, child, and adolescent health services. These initiatives include expanding midwifery education programs, strengthening community-based healthcare services, improving referral systems, and increasing access to emergency obstetric and newborn care. Nevertheless, substantial gaps remain in workforce capacity, service coverage, healthcare quality, and health system resilience, hindering progress toward national and global maternal and newborn health targets.6

Safe pregnancy and childbirth are basic human rights that must be upheld. In Somalia, the mortality rate for mothers and newborns due to preventable issues is alarmingly high, necessitating urgent action. The country is experiencing a critical shortage of skilled birth attendants, with only 1.5 midwives per 10,000 people, far below the WHO’s recommended 14 per 10,000. To address this shortfall and ensure the safety of mothers and infants, an additional 20,000 midwives are urgently required.7

Given the pivotal role of midwives in reducing preventable maternal and newborn deaths, a comprehensive understanding of the epidemiological burden, workforce challenges, and strategic opportunities for strengthening midwifery services is essential. Therefore, this narrative review aims to examine the epidemiological trends of maternal and newborn health in Somalia, assess the current status of the midwifery workforce, identify existing gaps and challenges in service delivery, and explore strategic priorities for strengthening midwifery services to improve maternal and newborn health outcomes in the country.

While the overall burden of maternal and newborn ill health in Somalia has recently been summarized in a broader narrative review of health-system challenges and public health priorities by Mohamoud et al, the present review is deliberately narrower and more specific in scope: it focuses on the midwifery workforce as the pivotal determinant of skilled birth attendance, systematically linking epidemiological indicators to workforce production, distribution, and retention data in order to generate targeted, actionable recommendations for midwifery policy and investment, rather than a general health-system overview This distinction addresses an important gap in the existing literature, since strategies to improve maternal and newborn health cannot be separated from the specific professional cadre responsible for delivering the majority of essential interventions Comparable workforce and access challenges have also been documented in other fragile and conflict-affected settings in sub-Saharan Africa, such as South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where insecurity, weak regulatory systems, and workforce maldistribution similarly constrain progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals; situating Somalia’s experience within this broader regional context underscores both the transferability of the strategic priorities identified in this review and the urgency of sustained investment in the midwifery workforce.

Methods: Narrative Approaches

A narrative review was conducted to synthesize the evidence on maternal and newborn health outcomes, midwifery workforce gaps, and strategic priorities for strengthening midwifery services in Somalia. A literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect, as well as reports from the World Health Organization (WHO), United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), and the Federal Government of Somalia Ministry of Health. The search covered publications published between 2000 and 2026 using keywords including “midwifery”, “midwives”, “maternal health”, “newborn health”, “maternal mortality”, “neonatal mortality”, “skilled birth attendance”, “health workforce”, and “Somalia”. A total of 55 records were identified: 50 through database searching (PubMed, n=15; Scopus, n=12; Web of Science, n=10; Google Scholar, n=13) and 5 through additional sources (WHO, UNFPA, UNICEF, and Ministry of Health reports). After removing 8 duplicate records and 10 records with irrelevant titles or abstracts (n=18 excluded), 37 records were screened and subsequently assessed as full-text articles/reports for eligibility. Following full-text assessment, 22 records were excluded for the following reasons: not focused on maternal/newborn health or midwifery in Somalia (n=9), insufficient data or unclear methodology (n=6), outside the review scope (n=4), and full text unavailable (n=3). The remaining 15 sources were included in the final review. The findings were narratively synthesized to describe the epidemiological patterns, workforce challenges, and strategic interventions for strengthening midwifery services in Somalia. Epidemiological Patterns

Somalia continues to experience one of the highest burdens of maternal and newborn mortality globally, despite ongoing efforts to strengthen reproductive and maternal health services. Recent estimates indicate that the maternal mortality ratio (MMR) remains approximately 621 deaths per 100,000 live births, placing Somalia among the countries with the highest maternal mortality burden worldwide.2 Although significant progress has been made compared with previous decades, maternal mortality remains substantially higher than the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target of fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030.10 Newborn health outcomes are equally concerning. Neonatal mortality is estimated at approximately 36 deaths per 1000 live births, while stillbirth rates remain high at approximately 28 per 1000 births.4 The leading causes of neonatal mortality include birth asphyxia, prematurity, neonatal sepsis, and complications related to inadequate access to skilled obstetric and newborn care. Neonatal mortality accounts for nearly one-third of all under-five deaths in Somalia, highlighting the need for improved maternal and newborn healthcare services throughout the continuum of care.7

Access to essential maternal healthcare services is limited. Findings from the Somalia Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) 2020 revealed that only 31% of women received adequate antenatal care, 24% completed at least four antenatal care visits, and only 21% delivered in health facilities. Furthermore, only 32% of births were attended by skilled health personnel.4,7 These low service utilization rates contribute substantially to preventable maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Several structural factors continue to drive poor maternal and newborn health outcomes in Somalia. Decades of armed conflict, recurrent droughts, population displacement, poverty, weak health infrastructure, and shortages of skilled healthcare workers have significantly limited access to quality reproductive and maternal healthcare services, particularly in rural and nomadic communities.11 Consequently a result, substantial inequalities persist in maternal healthcare utilization across geographic regions and population groups. These epidemiological indicators are not independent of one another: the persistently low proportion of skilled birth attendance is closely linked to the geographic maldistribution of the midwifery workforce described below, while broader socioeconomic factors, including poverty, low female educational attainment, and restricted household decision-making power over care-seeking, further compound the effect of workforce shortages on maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Workforce Gaps in Midwifery Services in Somalia

A well-trained and adequately distributed midwifery workforce is essential for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality rates. However, Somalia faces a critical shortage of qualified midwives in the country. Evidence suggests that the country requires approximately 20,000 additional midwives to meet existing maternal and newborn healthcare needs and achieve the recommended workforce standards.12 The shortage of midwives is compounded by their inequitable distribution across the country’s regions. Most qualified midwives are concentrated in urban centers, while rural, remote, and conflict-affected areas continue to experience severe workforce shortages. Many trained midwives are reluctant to accept deployment to underserved areas due to insecurity, poor living conditions, limited career advancement opportunities, and inadequate financial incentives.12 Consequently, women in rural communities often rely on traditional birth attendants or deliver without assistance from skilled professional midwifery. Midwifery education and training capacity have improved in recent years through support from the Federal Government of Somalia, UNFPA, and other development partners. Since 2009, approximately 2000 midwives have graduated from UNFPA-supported midwifery schools, and a nationally standardized midwifery curriculum has been implemented.13 Nevertheless, workforce production remains insufficient to address current needs. Challenges, including limited clinical training opportunities, inadequate professional regulation, workforce attrition, and weak continuing professional development systems, continue to affect the quality and retention of midwives. Additionally, many practicing midwives work under difficult conditions characterized by shortages of medical equipment, inadequate supervision, limited transportation for referrals, and insufficient staffing. These challenges negatively affect service quality and contribute to burnout and workforce turnover.14 Although the approximately 2000 midwives trained since 2009 represent meaningful progress, this output covers only about one-tenth of the estimated 20,000 additional midwives required, indicating that, at current graduation and deployment rates, existing training programmes are narrowing the workforce gap only marginally and would need to expand substantially in scale, geographic targeting, and retention effectiveness to meet national workforce targets within a realistic timeframe.

Strategic Priorities for Strengthening Midwifery Services in Somalia

Expanding Midwifery Education and Training Capacity

Expanding accredited midwifery schools, increasing student enrollment, and strengthening competency-based training are essential to address the shortage of skilled midwives and to improve the quality of maternal and newborn care.

Strengthening Recruitment, Deployment, and Retention of Midwives

Effective recruitment and retention strategies, including financial incentives and career development opportunities, are needed to ensure the equitable distribution of midwives, particularly in rural and underserved areas.

Enhancing Professional Regulation and Continuing Professional Development

Strengthening licensing, accreditation, and continuing professional development programs can improve professional standards, clinical competence, and quality of care.

Improving Working Conditions and Supportive Supervision

Providing adequate equipment, essential supplies, supportive supervision, and safe working environments can enhance workforce performance, motivation, and retention of healthcare workers.

Strengthening Maternal and Newborn Health Service Delivery

Expanding access to skilled birth attendance, antenatal care, postnatal care, family planning, and emergency obstetric and newborn care services is critical for reducing maternal and neonatal mortalities.

Promoting Community Engagement and Demand for Skilled Birth Attendance

Community awareness programs and engagement of community and religious leaders can increase the utilization of maternal health services and encourage facility-based deliveries.

Leveraging Partnerships and Sustainable Financing

Strong collaboration among the government, development partners, donors, and professional associations is necessary to strengthen midwifery education, workforce capacity, healthcare infrastructure, and the long-term sustainability of maternal health programs.

Discussion

This review highlights the persistent challenges facing maternal and newborn health in Somalia, despite ongoing efforts to strengthen reproductive and maternal healthcare services. Somalia continues to report one of the highest maternal mortality ratios globally, with an estimated 621 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, while neonatal mortality remains approximately 36 deaths per 1000 live births.2 These findings indicate that significant gaps remain in access to quality maternal and newborn healthcare services, particularly in rural and underserved communities. A major finding of this review is the limited availability of skilled birth attendants. According to the Somalia Health and Demographic Survey 2020, only 32% of births were attended by skilled health personnel and only 21% occurred in health facilities.4 Similar findings have been reported across fragile and conflict-affected settings in sub-Saharan Africa, where shortages of skilled healthcare workers and weak health systems contribute to poor maternal and neonatal outcomes.12 Increasing access to skilled midwifery care is therefore essential for reducing preventable maternal and newborn deaths.

The review also identified significant shortages and maldistribution of midwives as major barriers to improving maternal health outcomes in the country. Although Somalia has expanded midwifery education programs in recent years, the number of trained midwives remains insufficient to meet population needs.12 Furthermore, most midwives are concentrated in urban areas, leaving rural and nomadic populations with limited access to skilled maternal healthcare services. Similar workforce challenges have been documented in other low-income countries, where inadequate recruitment, deployment, and retention strategies contribute to persistent healthcare inequities.9 Another important finding is the need to strengthen midwifery education and professional development. Evidence from the State of the World’s Midwifery report demonstrates that investments in quality midwifery education, regulation, and workforce support can substantially improve maternal and newborn health outcomes.9 Strengthening competency-based training, continuing professional development, and professional regulation may enhance the quality of care provided by midwives and improve workforce retention.

These findings further emphasize the importance of creating an enabling environment for midwives. Shortages of essential medicines, medical equipment, referral systems, and supportive supervision continue to affect service delivery in many health facilities across Somalia.15 Addressing these health system constraints is critical because the effectiveness of midwives depends not only on their skills but also on the availability of functional healthcare infrastructure and supportive working conditions. Evidence from WHO and UNFPA suggests that well-trained and adequately supported midwives could prevent more than 80% of maternal deaths, stillbirths, and neonatal deaths when integrated within strong health systems.9 Therefore, strengthening midwifery services represents one of the most cost-effective strategies for improving maternal and newborn health outcomes in Somalia.

Taken together, the sources reviewed here broadly agree in identifying workforce shortage and maldistribution, weak service infrastructure, and low demand-side utilization as the principal drivers of poor maternal and newborn outcomes in Somalia; however, they diverge in their reported estimates of maternal mortality (563–621 deaths per 100,000 live births) and neonatal mortality (34–36 deaths per 1000 live births), reflecting differences in survey methodology, geographic coverage, and the timing of data collection across a fragile and only partially accessible territory. A persistent question raised by this synthesis is why maternal and neonatal mortality remain so high despite more than a decade of midwifery education expansion and donor-funded programming; the evidence suggests this reflects a scale mismatch, in which the pace of workforce production and deployment has not kept pace with population growth, attrition, and the geographic redistribution needed to reach rural and nomadic communities, a pattern also reported in other fragile settings such as South Sudan and Afghanistan, where donor-funded training programmes have similarly outpaced health systems’ capacity to retain and deploy graduates. This distinguishes the contribution of the present review from the broader health-system overview provided by Mohamoud et al, by isolating workforce production-versus-retention dynamics as a specific, addressable bottleneck rather than treating workforce shortage as one of many undifferentiated system weaknesses.3

Limitation

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, it does not follow the rigorous methodology of a systematic review and may, therefore, be subject to selection bias. Second, peer-reviewed literature specifically addressing midwifery services in Somalia is limited, necessitating reliance on government reports, policy documents, and publications from international organizations. Third, variations in data sources and reporting methods may have affected the comparability of some findings. Despite these limitations, this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence and offers important insights into the epidemiological burden, workforce challenges, and opportunities for strengthening midwifery services in Somalia.

Conclusion

Maternal and newborn mortality remains a major public health challenge in Somalia, driven by limited access to skilled birth attendance, shortages of qualified midwives, and weaknesses in the healthcare system. By linking these epidemiological patterns directly to workforce production, distribution, and retention data, this review’s principal contribution is to identify the midwifery workforce pipeline, rather than health-system weakness in general, as a specific and measurable priority for policy action. Despite progress in midwifery education and maternal health programs, significant workforce and service delivery gaps remain. Priority areas for future research include prospective evaluation of the retention and deployment outcomes of existing training programmes, and disaggregated regional data to track progress toward equitable workforce distribution. Strengthening midwifery services through expanded education and training, improved workforce retention, supportive working conditions, and increased access to quality maternal healthcare is essential to achieve this goal. Sustained investment in the midwifery workforce and health system strengthening can substantially improve maternal and newborn health outcomes and support progress toward Sustainable Development Goals in Somalia. These lessons are also relevant to other fragile and conflict-affected settings facing comparable workforce constraints, where similarly targeted, workforce-focused investment may accelerate progress toward global maternal and newborn health targets.
