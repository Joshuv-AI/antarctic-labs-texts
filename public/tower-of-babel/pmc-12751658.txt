Real-world use of non-pregnancy-specific automated insulin delivery systems during gestation and delivery: a case series
Authors: Federica Carrieri, Maria Pompea Antonia Baldassarre, Sara Coluzzi, Giorgia Centorame, Agostino Consoli, Gloria Formoso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining optimal glycemic control in pregnant women with type 1 diabetes (T1D) remains a clinical challenge despite advancements in technology. Although not all automated insulin delivery (AID) systems are approved for gestational use, their use has increased in clinical practice. Limited real-world data exists on their effectiveness and safety throughout pregnancy as well as delivery.MethodsRetrospective case series including ten women with T1D who used AID systems (MiniMed 780G or Control-IQ) from preconception or early pregnancy through delivery at a single Italian Diabetes and Pregnancy Clinic between January 2020 and March 2025. Glucose metrics were analyzed from data-sharing portals across five time points (preconception, each trimester and delivery). Anthropometric data, safety and pregnancy outcomes were also evaluated.ResultsMean preconception HbA1c was 7.2 ± 1.0% and improved to 6.2 ± 0.3% by the third trimester. Time in range rose progressively, averaging 70.7 ± 11.2% in late pregnancy, with 5/10 individuals achieving the > 70% target. Coefficient of variation and time above range decreased with no severe hypoglycemia. Most women maintained AID mode during labor; mean TIRp at delivery was 70.1 ± 14.6%. Neonatal outcomes were generally favorable, with no stillbirths or neonatal deaths.ConclusionsAID systems can efficiently and safely support glycemic control during pregnancy and delivery in women with T1D when guided by expert teams and specific protocols. Although not currently approved for use during pregnancy, these systems may represent a viable alternative to pregnancy-specific algorithms.Keywords: Advanced hybrid closed-loop, Automated insulin delivery, Delivery, Pregnancy, Real world use, Type 1 diabetes

Introduction

Achieving optimal glycemic control during pregnancy is crucial for women with type 1 diabetes (T1D) to minimize the risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes [1, 2]. Despite improvements in the management of T1D during pregnancy, achieving desired glycemic targets and controlling maternal-fetal outcomes remains a challenge [3].

The Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) in Pregnant Women with T1D trial (CONCEPTT) demonstrated that merely using CGM during pregnancy, compared to standard care, improved both glycemic control and pregnancy outcomes [4]. This finding was so groundbreaking that CGM use is now recommended for all individuals with T1D [5, 6], even during pregnancy [7]. Sensor-augmented pump (SAP) therapy has been widely used during gestation, although no SAP system has ever received approval for use during pregnancy. Automated insulin delivery (AID) systems represent a significant step forward, as they integrate CGM data with insulin pump therapy and employ sophisticated algorithms to adapt insulin delivery based on real-time glucose levels and predicted trends [8]. This technology has the potential to improve glucose control, reduce the risk of hypoglycemia, and alleviate the burden of diabetes self-management during pregnancy [9, 10].

Currently, CamAPS FX (CamDiab Ltd., Cambridge, UK) is the only commercially available algorithm designed to personalize specific glycemic targets (e.g., below 100 mg/dL) in alignment with the recommended targets for pregnancy [11]. Despite its recent approval by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [12], its adoption remains somehow limited. This is primarily due to its exclusive compatibility (in some regions) with Android smartphones and to the fact that not all healthcare systems worldwide authorize the prescription of compatible devices. Furthermore, in some settings, transitioning from other AID systems during pregnancy may not always be feasible or comfortable for women. As a result, many continue to use their current AID system throughout gestation, even though most of them are still not approved for pregnancy use. In fact, the high adoption of the different AID systems during pregnancy in clinical practice, recently led to the publication of an expert consensus statement aiming to provide practical guidance [13].

A recent randomized controlled trial, the CRISTAL study, demonstrated the efficacy of the Minimed 780G AID system (Medtronic Inc.,Northridge, CA, USA) in improving overnight time in range, reducing time below range, and enhancing treatment satisfaction in women with T1D during pregnancy, compared to standard insulin therapy [14]. Although the study failed to demonstrate a superiority in achieving overall time in target range [14], AID therapy with MiniMed 780G seemed to be cost-saving compared to standard care for pregnant women with T1D, primarily due to a reduction in hospitalizations for severe hypoglycemia and poorly controlled diabetes [15]. Promising results were also reported from the CIRCUIT trial in which the Tandem T: Slim system operating in Control-IQ (Tandem Diabetes Care Inc.,San Diego, CA, USA) mode demonstrated a significant improvement in Time In Pregnancy Range (TIRp) compared with standard care [16].

While awaiting approval of these systems for use during pregnancy, real-world data on their application in routine clinical practice, although mostly derived from case series reports and a few observational studies, can nevertheless provide valuable insights and inform clinical decisions[17–19].

This retrospective case series reports real-life data from ten pregnant women with T1D who used AID systems from early pregnancy until delivery. Additionally, the challenges and potential benefits associated with AID technology in these women were explored.

Methods

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational study designed to investigate, in a real-world clinical setting, the use of AID systems during pregnancy and delivery in women with T1D.

Women who received care at the Diabetes, Pregnancy, and Technology outpatient clinic (Endocrinology and Metabolism Unit, Pescara, Italy) from January 2020 to March 2025 and who agreed to use an AID system not approved for use during gestation (providing written informed consent) were included in the study.

Women using continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) systems not integrated with CGM, SAP therapy, multiple daily injections (MDI) or AID systems for which full clinical data access was unavailable were excluded.

Women were followed according to standard clinical practice, through outpatient visits every 2–4 weeks, depending on clinical needs, independently of the subsequent decision to include them in the retrospective analysis.

Data were collected for five follow-up windows: the preconception period (T0), defined as the last three months before conception; the first trimester (T1), between weeks 4 and 13 of gestation; the second trimester (T2), between weeks 14 and 27 of gestation; the third trimester (T3), between weeks 28 and 40 of gestation and delivery.

At baseline (T0) information on maternal age, disease duration, preconception folic acid use, smoking habits, presence of comorbidities, microvascular or macrovascular diabetes complications, and the type of technology used before conception was collected. Microvascular complications were defined as neuropathy, retinopathy, macular edema, chronic kidney disease, or microalbuminuria, while macrovascular complications were defined as a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack, myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, coronary artery disease or peripheral arterial disease.

Clinical data were acquired through careful consultation of the electronic medical record currently used in almost all diabetes outpatient clinics in Italy (Metaclinic/METEDA, San Benedetto del Tronto, AP, Italy). CGM and integrated insulin pump data were analyzed from data-sharing portals: Glooko (Glooko, Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA), Dexcom Clarity (Dexcom, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and CareLink (Medtronic, Inc., Northridge, CA, USA).

Data gathered from these platforms included clinical targets for continuous glucose monitoring as per the 2019 Consensus [20]: time in pregnancy range (TIRp) from 63 to 140 mg/dL (3.5–7.8 mmol/L), time below pregnancy range (TBRp) < 63 mg/dL (3.5 mmol/L), time above pregnancy range (TARp) >140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L), time in range (TIR) from 70 to 180 mg/dL (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time below range (TBR) < 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), and time above range (TAR) >180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L).

Additionally, for each time window, the following parameters were collected: average sensor glucose (ASG), glucose coefficient of variation (CV), time using AID mode, sensor use, average total daily insulin dose (TDD), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), active insulin time, and time using Sleep and Exercise mode. Active insulin time and time spent in Sleep or Exercise modes were collected for the MiniMed 780G and T: slim X2 with Control-IQ systems, respectively, as these are system-specific settings.

CGM metrics were calculated considering the last 30 days before each visit at T0, T1, T2, or T3. At T0, CGM-based targets for the general population (TIR, TAR, TBR) were considered, whereas at T1, T2, T3, and delivery, pregnancy-specific CGM targets (TIRp, TARp, TBRp) were applied. TIRp during delivery was calculated using the shortest applicable window available on the platforms: the 24 h corresponding to the day of delivery for Control IQ (Glooko) and 72 h for the 780G (Carelink).

Anthropometric data, safety outcomes, pregnancy and neonatal outcomes were also recorded. Maternal outcomes included diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), severe hypoglycemia, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia), premature rupture of membranes (PROM) and worsening or new onset of microvascular and macrovascular complications. Neonatal outcomes included macrosomia, newborns large for gestational age (LGA), small for gestational age (SGA), neonatal hypoglycemia, jaundice, need for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, stillbirth, and neonatal death. The need for antenatal induction of fetal lung maturity was also documented. The birth weight, length and head circumference percentiles were calculated using the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards calculator[21].

During pregnancy, women were provided with clinical recommendations and strategies based on the Expert Guidance published in 2023[13] and the clinical experience of the Center. During labor, delivery, and the immediate postpartum, women were advised to use AID mode and were provided with a personalized protocol tailored by the supervising diabetologist. Both the woman and her partner were educated on how to modify the system settings in accordance with the personalized plan. Throughout labor and delivery, continuous support was provided by experienced obstetric team on duty in the delivery suite. The case series adheres to the tenets of Good Clinical Practice and to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the local ethics committee (number 183/2025).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population. Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. No inferential statistical analyses were performed.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Women characteristics and comorbidities are detailed in Table 2. This case series included ten women with T1D (mean age 34.2 ± 3.7 years). Six pregnancies were planned, and none reported smoking habits or any other serious medical conditions at T0. The mean diabetes duration was 17.2 ± 7 years, and four out of ten women were nulliparous. Two women used the T: slim X2 insulin pump with Control-IQ technology throughout pregnancy and delivery, while the remaining eight used the MiniMed 780G system in SmartGuard mode. All women were already on insulin pump therapy and CGM prior to conception. One (Case #1) initially used a non-integrated CSII system and transitioned to the MiniMed 780G in AID mode at 11 weeks of gestation due to suboptimal glycemic control. All individuals were treated with rapid-acting lispro insulin.

The average preconception HbA1c was 7.2 ± 1.0% (55.2 ± 10.9 mmol/mol), indicating suboptimal glycemic control in most individuals [7]. One woman (Case #4) exhibited notably higher levels (9.4%, 79 mmol/mol) (Fig. 1). When excluding this outlier, the mean preconception HbA1c improves to 6.9 ± 0.6% (51 ± 6.6 mmol/mol). Moreover, HbA1c levels showed a significant improvement as pregnancy progressed, reaching an average of 6.2 ± 0.3% (44.3 ± 3.3 mmol/mol) (Fig. 1). Notably, Case #4 exhibited a marked reduction in the third trimester, with an HbA1c of 6.2% (44 mmol/mol), corresponding to a delta of 3.2% (35 mmol/mol).

All women in our case series population adhered to the recommendations outlined in the Materials and Methods section during pregnancy, labor, and delivery (Table 1).

Women using the MiniMed 780G appropriately lowered their glycemic target as advised. Among Control-IQ users, Case #9 maintained Sleep Mode for 100% of the time during the first trimester, 96% during the second trimester, and 68% during the third trimester achieving excellent glycemic control (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). In contrast, Case #7, due to a strong fear of hypoglycemia, began using Sleep Mode nearly only in the third trimester, concurrently decreasing use of Exercise Mode ( Sleep Mode use increased from 31% in the first trimester to 97% in the third trimester).

Mean gestational weight gain (GWG) was 11.9 ± 4.1 kg (Table 4). Five women achieved GWG within the range recommended by current guidelines [27]: three had inadequate GWG, while two exceeded the recommended limits.

During pregnancy, sensor usage consistently remained above 90% on average, as did time spent in AID mode. A decline in AID usage was observed in the third trimester, as Case #4 and Case #8 discontinued SmartGuard due to suboptimal glycemic control (at gestational week 30 for Case #4 and 29 for Case #8). The MiniMed 780G’s flexibility to switch between SmartGuard system turned off mode and AID modes based on patient needs proved valuable in clinical management, particularly for Case #4, a young woman with limited disease awareness and acceptance, presenting challenges in both prenatal and postnatal diabetes management. Indeed, she experienced a marked HbA1c, TIRp, TARp and CV improvement (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4) after deactivating SmartGuard mode during third trimester.

Using AID technology during pregnancy, our cohort demonstrated progressive improvements in TIRp (Fig. 2), TARp (Fig. 3), TAR, CV (Fig. 4), and ASG. Specifically, the mean TIRp in the third trimester reached 70.7 ± 11.2%, with 5 out of 10 women meeting the recommended target of > 70%. The mean TARp was 27.4 ± 11.2%, TAR mg/dL was 3.3 ± 3.8%, CV was 29.8 ± 5.1%, and ASG was 121.9 ± 11 mg/dL (6.8 ± 0.6 mmol/L).

TBR remained essentially stable, as did active insulin time, which was set to 2 h for all women using MiniMed 780G during pregnancy. As expected, due to increasing insulin requirements and pregnancy-related insulin resistance, TDD nearly doubled from preconception to the end of gestation. The percentage of insulin delivered through boluses progressively increased from preconception to the third trimester (from 54.1 ± 7.5% to 65.0 ± 12.5%), likely reflecting the gradual implementation of strategies to meet the stringent glycemic targets of pregnancy. A similar trend was observed for both systems.

Maternal and neonatal outcomes

All maternal and neonatal outcomes are summarized in Table 4. Eight women underwent scheduled delivery (four via induction of vaginal labor and four via planned cesarean section).

Pregnancy resolution was planned according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidelines [28] as well as to patient’s clinical conditions. All women initiated antenatal fetal surveillance between 28 and 32 weeks of gestation. Fetometry and amniotic fluid assessment were repeated every 1–4 weeks depending on the obstetric findings, glycemic control, and the presence of comorbidities. Delivery was scheduled between 36 and 38.6 weeks of gestation for most women. Seven out of ten delivered after 37 weeks. Case #1 underwent induction at 34 weeks due to premature rupture of membranes; Case #4 was induced at 36 weeks for poor metabolic control and high obstetric risk but ultimately required an emergency cesarean section after failed induction; and Case #6 delivered by emergency cesarean at 34 weeks following new-onset, uncontrolled gestational hypertension. Cases #1, #4, and #6 received a single course of corticosteroids (12-mg doses of betamethasone administered intramuscularly 24 h apart) to promote fetal lung maturation, as they were considered at risk for preterm delivery [29]. The AID mode was maintained throughout and after steroid administration, with tailored adjustments initiated following the first dose and continued for up to 48 h after the second dose according to a center-specific protocol. For women using the MiniMed 780G system, the carbohydrate-to-Insulin ratios (CI) were tightened by 50%, and hyperglycemia was corrected using assisted carbohydrate estimation. For those on the Control-IQ system, a secondary basal profile was created, increasing basal infusion rates by 50%, reducing the CI by 50%, and decreasing the correction factor (CF) by 30%.

All women kept on wearing their insulin pumps during labor and delivery, and eight out of ten maintained the AID mode throughout the process. The average TIRp at delivery was 70.1 ± 14.6%. Similar tight glycemic control was observed with AID versus SmartGuard system turned off (mean TIRp 71.1 ± 14.9% vs. 72%, mean ASG 126.3 ± 18.8 mg/dl vs. 115 mg/dl, mean SD 40.9 ± 12.7 mg/dl vs. 39 mg/dl, mean CV 32.1 ± 7.2% vs. 34%, mean insulin delivered through boluses 54.9 ± 17.2% vs. 51.5%).

No preeclampsia, eclampsia, maternal DKA or severe hypoglycemic episodes requiring third party assistance occurred, nor was there any worsening or new onset of microvascular or macrovascular complications.

The average neonatal parameters were: birthweight 3346.3 ± 485.1 g (83 ± 22° centile), length 50.6 ± 2.5 cm (85 ± 19.2° centile) and head circumference (HC) 34.6 ± 1.2 cm (84.1 ± 16.9° centile). The mean Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min were 7.9 ± 0.7 and 8.7 ± 0.5, respectively. Five women delivered a large for gestational age (LGA) newborn, but only Case #4 had a neonate with fetal macrosomia (> 4 kg), specifically 4020 g at 37 weeks of gestation, reflecting poor glycemic control maintained from the periconceptional period through the second trimester. No newborn was classified as small for gestational age (SGA).

Four neonates experienced transient hypoglycemia, managed with oral feeding. Two developed neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy. Three infants were admitted to the NICU: two due to prematurity (born at 34 weeks of gestation) and one due to respiratory distress at birth.

Most neonatal complications (including macrosomia, jaundice, hypoglycemia, and respiratory distress) were observed in Case #4, a woman with known difficulties in managing her T1D and suboptimal glycemic control both during preconception and throughout pregnancy.

Discussion

This case series offers real-world insights into the use of non-pregnancy-specific AID systems throughout pregnancy and delivery in women with T1D. Our findings indicate that, when managed by experienced clinicians using tailored protocols, AID technology can effectively support glycemic control and contribute to moderately favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes even though some algorithms are not yet formally approved for use in pregnant populations.

In our cohort, preconception use of AID systems (in 9 out of 10 women) facilitated near-target glycemic control during peri-conceptional period [HbA1c 7.2 ± 1.0% (55.2 ± 10.9 mmol/mol), TIR 68.4 ± 16.3%]. Three pregnancies started with an HbA1c > 7.5% (Fig. 1, Case #1, #4 and #7), all involving unplanned pregnancies. Notably, Case #1 was referred to our center already pregnant, using flash glucose monitoring (FGM) and CSII; while, Case #4 had a long-standing history of poor disease acceptance, often manifesting as disengagement and reduced adherence to diabetes self-management.

Notwithstanding differences in baseline glycemic control, a consistent trend of progressive improvement was observed across all women throughout gestation. TIRp increased while TARp, CV, ASG decreased, mirroring findings from the CRISTAL as well as CIRCUIT trials [14] as well as real-world evidence from Quiros et al. [18], and other case series [17, 30]. Moreover, HbA1c and TIRp improvements were most marked during the second and third trimesters, reaching an average TIRp of 70.7 ± 11.2% in the third trimester (range 54–92%).

These improvements are clinically relevant, as metabolic control during third trimester remains a key risk factor for adverse neonatal outcomes [2] and even modest increases in TIRp and reductions in TARp (5%) during the second and third trimesters have been associated with lower risks of LGA, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and NICU admissions [31]. Third trimester TIRp, achieved in our population (70.7 ± 11.2%), is consistent with data from the CRISTAL study (TIR 66.5 ± 10.0%) and the large real-world cohort analyzed by Quiros et al. (TIR 70.4 ± 13.0%), highlighting the reproducibility of these results in a different clinical setting and population [14, 18]. However, it is worth noting that only half of the women in our cohort achieved a TIRp >70% in the third trimester, a rate similar to that reported by Quiros et al. (44.8%) [18]. These findings highlight that the adoption of AID systems during pregnancy represents a major advancement in optimizing glycemic control and enhancing maternal self-management, despite most systems not yet al.lowing the setting of pregnancy-specific glycemic targets. Nevertheless, further research and the development of pregnancy-tailored technologies are essential to increase the proportion of women achieving the recommended glycemic targets.

Glycemic variability also improved progressively, with CV decreasing from 34.1 ± 4.3% in the first trimester to 29.8 ± 5.1% at delivery, remaining below the recommended 36%[20]. Time below range was consistently < 3%[20] across trimesters, with no reported severe hypoglycemia, further supporting the role of AID systems in minimizing glucose excursions and hypoglycemic risk during pregnancy.

In addition, while in the Spanish cohort AID users gained more weight compared to standard care [18], only 2 of 10 women in our population exceeded the GWG limits [27], likely reflecting the impact of structured dietary counselling routinely provided in our care model.

Among maternal outcomes, no safety concerns were identified. None of the women experienced a worsening or new onset of microvascular or macrovascular complications. Notably, Case #4 showed a marked HbA1c reduction (−3.2%, 35 mmol/mol, from before conception to the third trimester) without any evidence of new or progressive complications. This observation is consistent with recent real-world evidence indicating that pregnant women with T1D are not at increased risk of developing sight-threatening retinopathy compared with matched nonpregnant controls [32].

Despite the use of different AID systems (MiniMed 780G and T: slim X2 with Control-IQ), adherence to sensor use and AID mode remained high, indicating good acceptance. Unlike other real-world studies reporting over 95% time in AID mode [18], two women in our cohort (both using MiniMed 780G) required temporary discontinuation of SmartGuard mode during third trimester due to suboptimal glycemic control. The ability of the MiniMed system to toggle between AID and manual modes proved clinically useful and, in selected cases, may be beneficial during postpartum. For instance, when the algorithm performs too aggressively, turning off the SmartGuard mode, with a basal rate reduced by 50–70% of the TDD after delivery, may be considered. Conversely, the ability to adjust basal infusion rates within the Control-IQ algorithm to better meet the stringent glycemic targets of pregnancy may have facilitated the continued use of AID mode throughout gestation.

To date, no real-world studies have specifically focused on labor and delivery. In a prespecified, secondary observational analysis of the CRISTAL trial, intrapartum AID use was associated with higher TIRp without increases in TBRp [33]. Our data confirm that AID systems can maintain glycemic stability during childbirth, with a mean TIRp of 70.1 ± 14.6% (range, 42–91%), and that this result was comparable between women who kept the AID mode active during labor and those who delivered with the SmartGuard system turned off (AID on: TIRp 71.1 ± 14.9%, ASG 126.3 ± 18.8 mg/dL, SD 40.9 ± 12.7 mg/dL; SmartGuard off: mean TIRp 72%, mean ASG 115 mg/dL, mean SD 39 mg/dL). This suggests the feasibility and safety of continued AID use during labor, especially when combined with a structured peripartum protocol and multidisciplinary coordination. It remains to be defined which factors may guide the decision to continue using the AID system or switch to manual mode during labor and delivery.

Despite overall good outcomes, variability in self-management and patient engagement clearly influenced clinical results. Most neonatal complications were observed in Cases #1, #4, and #6; namely, Case #1 and #4 had poorer glycemic control in early pregnancy, Case #6 had an urgent c-section at 34 gestational weeks due to sudden onset of uncontrol hypertension. Notably, Case #4 reflects how insufficient engagement with diabetes care may limit the benefits of advanced technologies, underscoring the continued need for patient education and psychological support.

Furthermore, Case #7 illustrates how behavioral factors can significantly influence glycemic management. Due to a pronounced fear of hypoglycemia, Exercise Mode was activated for roughly 30% of the first trimester, causing the algorithm to operate with higher glucose targets than those recommended during pregnancy (Table 1). Indeed, this pregnancy was further complicated by the delivery of an LGA infant with neonatal hypoglycemia. This underscores the importance of early and comprehensive patient education to promote and ensure appropriate device use and achieve optimal glycemic control both during preconception planning and throughout pregnancy.

Although no studies to date have been explicitly designed to evaluate the impact of AID systems on maternal-fetal outcomes, existing evidence indicates that the risk of LGA infants remains high (ranging from 50% to 69% in most studies [14, 18, 34]) which aligns with our findings (50%). This rate remains unacceptably elevated, particularly considering that the ultimate goal extends beyond glycemic control to include the overall well-being of both mother and child. Promising preliminary data have emerged only from the AiDAPT trial, where the incidence of LGA was reported at 39% among women who used a pregnancy-specific AID system [11]. This algorithm demonstrated a 5% improvement in TIRp by the 12th week of gestation compared to standard care [11]. This finding is especially relevant, as early optimization of glycemic control has been associated with improved neonatal outcomes, including a reduced risk of LGA [35]. In our cohort, the recommended glycemic target was, on average, achieved later in pregnancy, which may partly explain the observed frequency of LGA, consistent with current literature [14, 18, 34].

Nonetheless, this case series has some limitations, including a limited sample size, the absence of a control group and limited generalizability. The cohort was highly selected: women were all Caucasian, largely free of microvascular complications, well controlled at baseline and already using insulin pump and CGM therapy before pregnancy. As such, larger and more robust studies will be required to confirm these findings in the context of real-world evidence.

Conclusion

This case series highlights the potential AID systems as a practical and effective tool for achieving glycemic targets in pregnant women with T1D, both during pregnancy and labor, when guided by expert clinical teams and specific protocols. Although not specifically approved for use during gestation, devices such as the MiniMed 780G and Control-IQ have shown improvements in glycemic outcomes, suggesting they may represent a reasonable alternative to pregnancy-specific algorithms in selected cases. This is particularly relevant given that whether it is appropriate to switch from a system that has achieved adequate glycemic control, despite not being specifically designed for pregnancy, remains an open question in real-world clinical practice. Ultimately, further advancements and larger-scale studies are needed to determine whether these technologies can translate into meaningful improvements in maternal and neonatal outcomes.
