Smoothness Adaptivity in Constant-Depth Neural Networks: Optimal Rates via Smooth Activations
Yuhao Liu, Zilin Wang, Lei Wu, Shaobo Zhang

Abstract
Smooth activation functions are ubiquitous in modern deep learning, yet their theoretical advantages over non-smooth counterparts remain poorly understood. In this work, we study both approximation and statistical properties of neural networks with smooth activations for learning functions in the Sobolev space W s , ∞ ​ ( [ 0 , 1 ] d ) W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d}) with s > 0 s>0 . We prove that constant-depth networks equipped with smooth activations achieve smoothness adaptivity : increasing width alone suffices to attain the minimax-optimal approximation and estimation error rates (up to logarithmic factors). In contrast, for non-smooth activations such as ReLU, smoothness adaptivity is fundamentally limited by depth: the attainable approximation order is bounded by depth, and higher-order smoothness requires proportional depth growth. These results identify activation smoothness as a fundamental mechanism, complementary to depth, for achieving optimal rates over Sobolev function classes. Technically, our analysis is based on a multi-scale approximation framework that yields explicit neural network approximators with controlled parameter norms and model size. This complexity control ensures statistical learnability under empirical risk minimization (ERM) and avoids the impractical ℓ 0 \ell^{0} -sparsity constraints commonly required in prior analyses.

Introduction

Neural networks constitute a central model class in modern machine learning, with applications spanning computer vision, natural language processing, scientific computing, and generative modeling
(
35
;
70
;
53
;
52
)
. A central theoretical question concerns how architectural design gives rise to their observed performance. Critically, activation functions introduce the nonlinearities that enable the composition of representations across layers. Clarifying their structural role and their interaction with other architectural dimensions such as width and depth is therefore essential for a principled understanding of neural network expressivity and generalization.

In the early development of neural networks, sigmoid-type activations were introduced as differentiable surrogates of hard threshold units
(
43
;
55
)
.
However, their saturation in the tails leads to near-zero derivatives, resulting in vanishing gradients and hindering the training of deep networks
(
27
;
4
)
.
To mitigate this issue, the non-smooth Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was introduced and quickly became dominant due to its simplicity and its avoidance of gradient saturation
(
46
;
20
)
. Since then, ReLU has played a crucial role not only in influential architectures such as AlexNet
(
35
)
and ResNet
(
24
)
, but also in much of the theoretical analysis of deep learning
(
56
;
18
)
.

Recently, activation design has witnessed a renewed embrace of smooth activations after years of ReLU dominance—though not as a return to classical sigmoid-type functions. Contemporary choices such as Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU)
(
25
)
and Sigmoid Linear Unit (SiLU)
(
54
;
19
)
, together with their gated variants like SwiGLU
(
57
)
, are designed to preserve smoothness while mitigating gradient saturation.
This emphasis on smoothness is particularly evident in scientific computing, where higher-order derivatives are often required, for example in neural PDE solvers
(
53
;
71
;
41
)
. Moreover, smooth activations have also become standard components of modern large-scale models. They are adopted in language models such as GPT
(
52
)
, LLaMA
(
21
)
, and DeepSeek
(
38
)
, and are widely used in vision transformers and diffusion models
(
16
;
26
)
.

This raises a natural question:

What are the theoretical advantages of smooth activations over their non-smooth counterparts?

Early studies of neural networks with smooth activations were rooted in classical approximation theory
(
13
;
45
;
51
)
. These works established approximation error bounds for shallow networks, but typically without explicit control of parameter norms or model complexity. Thus, the approximation properties of smooth activations were not analyzed under complexity constraints that are essential for learning guarantees.
In contrast, much of modern deep learning theory has focused on networks with non-smooth activations, particularly ReLU
(
77
;
56
)
. Consequently, the extent to which activation smoothness itself contributes to approximation efficiency under explicit complexity control has not been systematically characterized.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, we take a step toward addressing this gap. We study neural networks with smooth activation functions for learning functions in the Sobolev space
W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
for arbitrary
s>0
. Our analysis is constructive: we explicitly build network approximators with carefully controlled complexity and derive corresponding finite-sample estimation rates. Together, these results provide a unified characterization of approximation and statistical performance under smooth activations. The main contributions are summarized below.

•

Smoothness adaptivity at constant depth.

We prove that
constant-depth
(depth
6
or
7
, depending on the metric)
neural networks equipped with smooth activation functions
achieve the optimal approximation rate

O(N^{-s/d})

for arbitrary smoothness
s>0
, where
N
denotes the total number of parameters.
Building upon this constructive approximation, we further establish that learning with ERM attains the minimax-optimal estimation rate

O(n^{-2s/(2s+d)})

up to logarithmic factors, where
n
is the sample size. Thus, both approximation and statistical optimality are achieved at constant depth.
Unlike prior works
(
3
;
56
;
66
;
48
)
, this adaptivity is “automatic”—it requires neither increasing the network depth nor imposing intractable
\ell^{0}
-sparsity constraints.

•

Depth bottleneck for non-smooth activations.

We establish an approximation lower bound for constant-depth ReLU networks. It shows that non-smooth activations
cannot attain the minimax-optimal rate for arbitrary smoothness at fixed depth; rather, their achievable rate is intrinsically limited by depth.
This yields a provable separation between smooth and non-smooth activations. Complementary numerical experiments demonstrate that shallow networks with smooth activations exhibit faster generalization convergence when learning smooth targets, empirically supporting the theoretical separation.

In summary, our results offer a smoothness-adaptivity perspective on the approximation and generalization advantages of smooth activations over their non-smooth counterparts. This perspective provides a principled explanation for the widespread empirical adoption of smooth activations in modern architectures.

Technically, our analysis builds on two main ingredients:
(i) a novel multiscale approximation scheme for piecewise constant functions that eliminates the need for sparsity constraints (Appendix
B.4
); and
(ii) a weighted superposition principle that lifts localized approximation guarantees to global
L^{\infty}
error bounds (Appendix
B.8
).

Rethinking the role of depth.

Our findings motivate a reconsideration of the role of depth in existing deep learning theory.
A substantial body of work establishes generalization guarantees for deep ReLU networks
(
77
;
37
;
56
;
34
;
66
)
, reinforcing the view that increasing depth is essential for achieving smoothness adaptivity
(
67
;
69
)
.
By contrast, we show that when smooth activations are employed, constant depth suffices to attain optimal rates over Sobolev classes.
Taken together, these results indicate that depth is not the only mechanism underlying smoothness adaptivity; activation regularity itself provides an alternative and theoretically sufficient route.

Preliminaries

Notations.

We write
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{0,1,2,\dots\}
for the set of non-negative integers, and

\mathbb{N}^{d}\coloneqq\{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}):\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\,i=1,\dots,d\}

for its
d
-fold Cartesian product. For a multi-index
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
we define
|{\bm{\alpha}}|\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}
.
For a positive integer
K
, we write
[K]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,K\}
and
[K]^{d}
for its
d
-fold Cartesian product.
For non-negative functions
f
and
g
, we write
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
(equivalently,
f(x)=O(g(x))
)
to mean that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
f(x)\leqslant C\,g(x)
for all
x
under consideration.
We write
f(x)=\widetilde{O}(g(x))
to suppress polylogarithmic factors, i.e.,

f(x)=O\bigl(g(x)\polylog(x)\bigr)
.
Moreover, we write
f(x)\eqsim g(x)
if both
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
and
g(x)\lesssim f(x)
hold.
For a region
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the indicator function is denoted by
\mathbbm{1}_{D}(\cdot)
,
i.e.,
\mathbbm{1}_{D}(\bm{x})=1
if
\bm{x}\in D
and
0
otherwise.

The target function class.

Throughout the paper, we fix the domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
and consider the Sobolev space
W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)
with
s>0
, which serves as the target function class in our analysis. Concretely,
W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)
is defined as follows:

•

(Integer-order Sobolev spaces) For
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the space
W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)
consists of functions
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that the weak derivatives
D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
|{\bm{\alpha}}|\leqslant s
. The norm is given by

\|u\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)}=\max_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|\leqslant s}\|D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},\quad u\in W^{s,\infty}(\Omega).

•

(Fractional-order Sobolev spaces) Let
s=m+\zeta
, where
m=\lfloor s\rfloor
and
\zeta\in(0,1)
.
For
u\in W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)
, define the Hölder seminorm

[u]_{C^{m,\zeta}(\Omega)}=\max_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|=m}\sup_{\bm{x}\neq\bm{y}\in\Omega}\frac{|D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}u(\bm{x})-D^{\bm{\alpha}}u(\bm{y})|}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}^{\zeta}}.

We define

W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)=\{u\in W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)\,:\,[u]_{C^{m,\zeta}(\Omega)}<\infty\}

equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\Omega)}+[u]_{C^{m,\zeta}(\Omega)}.

The neural network model.

We consider the model of fully-connected networks
with activation function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
applied componentwise.
For depth
L\geqslant 1
, width
M\geqslant 1
, and parameter bound
B>0
, let

\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}},M,B)=\Big\{\bm{x}\mapsto\bm{W}_{L}\,\phi\!\big(\bm{W}_{L-1}\phi(\cdots\phi(\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{1})\cdots)+\bm{b}_{L-1}\big)+\bm{b}_{L}:\ (\bm{W}_{\ell},\bm{b}_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{L}\in\Theta\Big\},

where the parameter set
\Theta=\Theta(L,d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}},M,B)
is given by

\Theta:=\Big\{(\bm{W}_{\ell},\bm{b}_{\ell})_{\ell=1}^{L}:\;\bm{W}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\;\bm{W}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}\ (2\leqslant\ell\leqslant L-1),\;\bm{W}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times M},

\qquad\qquad\bm{b}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\ (1\leqslant\ell\leqslant L-1),\;\bm{b}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}},\;\|\bm{W}_{\ell}\|_{\infty,\infty}\leqslant B,\;\|\bm{b}_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leqslant B\ (1\leqslant\ell\leqslant L)\Big\},

where
\|\bm{W}\|_{\infty,\infty}:=\max_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
and

\|\bm{b}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}|b_{i}|
.

The activation assumptions.

Our primary objective is to understand how structural properties
of the activation function influence the approximation power
of neural networks. Specifically, we impose the following assumptions on
\phi
.

Assumption 3.1

(Smoothness)
.

\phi
is infinitely differentiable and is not a polynomial.

Assumption 3.2

(Lipschitz continuity)
.

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

This ensures that the activation grows at most linearly at infinity. It excludes activation functions with super-linear growth, such as SwiGLU
(
57
)
, whose tail behavior is quadratic.
We emphasize that this assumption is imposed for technical simplicity; our analysis can be extended to activations with polynomial growth tails with minor modifications.

Assumption 3.3
.

The activation function
\phi
satisfies one of the following:

•

(Heaviside-like) There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\lvert\phi(t)-H(t)\rvert\leqslant C_{1}\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\lvert t\rvert}\right\},\quad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

where
H(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\infty)}(t)
is the Heaviside function.

•

(ReLU-like) There exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\lvert\phi(t)-\mathrm{ReLU}(t)\rvert\leqslant C_{2},\quad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R},

(2)

where
\mathrm{ReLU}(t)=\max\{t,0\}
is the ReLU function.

Examples.

Most activation functions used in practice satisfy the above assumptions. We list several representative examples together with their explicit forms:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}},\qquad\tanh(t)=\frac{e^{t}-e^{-t}}{e^{t}+e^{-t}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{SiLU}(t)

\displaystyle=t\,{\mathrm{sigmoid}}(t),\quad\mathrm{GELU}(t)=t\,\Phi(t),

where
\Phi(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
is the cumulative distribution function of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
In particular,
\mathrm{GELU}
and
\mathrm{SiLU}
are ReLU-like, whereas
\phi_{\tanh}(\cdot)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\tanh(\cdot))
and the sigmoid function are Heaviside-like.
See Appendix
A.2
for detailed verification.

4
Approximation Theory: Smoothness as an Alternative to Depth

The following theorem establishes an
L^{2}
-approximation of functions in
W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
using constant-depth neural networks.

Theorem 4.1

(
L^{2}
approximation)
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
. For any
s>0
and any
f^{\star}\in W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
with

\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
, and for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant-depth neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M_{\epsilon},B_{\epsilon})

with

L=6,\qquad M_{\epsilon}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{2s}},\quad B_{\epsilon}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{\max\left\{\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+d,\frac{d}{s}+2,\frac{d+4}{2s}+5,\lceil s\rceil\right\}},

(3)

such that

\left\|g-f^{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant\epsilon.

A proof sketch of this theorem is provided in Section
7.1
, and the detailed proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Specifically, to achieve approximation accuracy
\epsilon
, the required network width satisfies

M_{\epsilon}\lesssim\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{2s}}
.
Since the depth is constant, the total number of parameters scales as

N=O(M_{\epsilon}^{2})=O(\epsilon^{-\frac{d}{s}})
.
Equivalently, in terms of the parameter budget
N
, this yields the approximation rate

\|g-f^{\star}\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{d})}\lesssim N^{-s/d},

which matches the optimal stable approximation rate for
W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})

(
14
)
. In comparison,
12
obtain a comparable approximation order only under explicit
\ell^{0}
-sparsity constraints on the number of nonzero parameters, while the total parameter count grows at a strictly higher polynomial order in
\epsilon^{-1}
. Furthermore, our construction guarantees that the parameter norms are polynomially controlled, i.e.,
B_{\epsilon}=O(\mathrm{poly}(\epsilon^{-1}))
, a vital property that ensures statistical learnability from finite samples.

Remark 4.2

(Constant depth)
.

Theorem
4.1
shows that smooth activation functions enable constant-depth networks to adapt to arbitrarily high smoothness orders of the target function. Notably, the required depth remains fixed (here
L=6
), independent of both the target accuracy
\epsilon
and the smoothness level
s
.
By contrast, existing approximation results for non-smooth activations typically require the network depth to grow either with the target accuracy (e.g.,
77
) or with the smoothness level (e.g.,
49
;
40
).

Remark 4.3

(Width–norm trade-off)
.

Theorem
4.1
provides a sufficient joint scaling of network width and parameter norm to achieve a target approximation accuracy. In particular, it guarantees the existence of
(M_{\epsilon},B_{\epsilon})
with polynomial dependence on
\epsilon^{-1}
.
It, however, does not fully characterize the trade-off between width and parameter norm. That is, we do not determine the best achievable approximation accuracy under prescribed width
W
and parameter-norm bound
B
. Such a characterization would provide a more refined understanding of the approximation process. For our purposes, polynomial control of both width and norm suffices, as it enables the subsequent generalization analysis.

As a refinement of Theorem
4.1
, we also establish an
L^{\infty}
approximation result.

Theorem 4.4

(
L^{\infty}
approximation)
.

Suppose
\phi
and
f^{\star}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
. Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M_{\epsilon},B_{\epsilon}),

with

L=7,\quad M_{\epsilon}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{d}{2s}},\quad B_{\epsilon}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{\max\left\{\frac{d^{2}}{2s}+d,\frac{d}{s}+2,\frac{d+4}{2s}+1,\lceil s\rceil,\frac{6}{s}+4\right\}},

(4)

such that

\|g-f^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant\epsilon.

Compared with the
L^{2}
approximation guarantee in Theorem
4.1
, the
L^{\infty}
result only requires a modest increase in depth from
6
to
7
. The proof for
L^{\infty}
approximation follows a strategy similar to that of Theorem
4.1
, with the addition of a single layer designed to implement a weighted superposition principle. This mechanism effectively upgrades localized approximation guarantees to a global
L^{\infty}
bound. For a complete derivation, we refer the reader to Appendix
B.8
.

5
Learning Theory: Achieving Optimal Risk without Sparsity

We now leverage the above constructive approximation results to derive generalization bounds for learning target functions in
W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
.

Let
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. samples generated according to

y_{i}=f^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})+\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(5)

where
\bm{x}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\rho
with
\rho
being a distribution supported on
[0,1]^{d}

and the noises
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
are independent of
\bm{x}_{i}
. We consider ERM over the network class:

\widehat{f}_{n}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M_{n},B_{n})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(y_{i}-(\mathbb{T}_{F}f)(\bm{x}_{i})\Big)^{2}.

(6)

Here
\mathbb{T}_{F}
denotes the truncation operator, defined for
F>0
by

(\mathbb{T}_{F}f)(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}f(\bm{x}),&\text{if}\;|f(\bm{x})|\leqslant F,\\
\operatorname{sign}(f(\bm{x}))F,&\text{if}\;|f(\bm{x})|>F.\end{cases}

This truncation ensures uniform boundedness of the hypothesis class and is standard in generalization analysis of ERM estimators.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
and assume the noise variance
\sigma^{2}\gtrsim 1
. Fix any
s>0
and let
f^{\star}\in W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})\cap C([0,1]^{d})
satisfy
\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant 1
. Then, for any positive integer
n
, choosing

L=7,\quad M_{n}\eqsim n^{\frac{d}{4s+2d}},\quad B_{n}\eqsim n^{\max\left\{\frac{d}{2},1,\frac{2s+d+4}{2(2s+d)},\frac{s\lceil s\rceil}{2s+d},\frac{4s+6}{2s+d}\right\}},\quad F=2,

(7)

we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathbb{T}_{F}\widehat{f}_{n}-f^{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\right]\lesssim n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d}}\log n,

(8)

where the expectation is taken over the sampling of the training data.

We provide a proof sketch of this theorem in Section
7.3
, with the complete derivation deferred to Appendix
C
. The fundamental insight of the proof lies in the fact that the constructive approximation in Theorem
4.1
allows for precise control over the complexity of the hypothesis class.

It is well known that the minimax optimal rate
for learning functions in the Sobolev class

W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})

is
O(n^{-\frac{2s}{2s+d}})

(
64
;
68
)
. Therefore,
Theorem
5.1
shows that,
when equipped with smooth activation functions,
constant-depth neural networks achieve this optimal rate
(up to logarithmic factors). In contrast, for non-smooth activations, achieving the same rate typically requires the network depth to grow with the sample size
(
56
;
66
;
48
)
. Compared to these prior works, we successfully remove the requirement of
\ell^{0}
-sparsity constraint, thereby rendering the ERM practically implementable.

In Theorem
5.1
, empirical risk minimization is performed over neural networks whose parameters satisfy an
\ell^{\infty}
-norm constraint. A slight modification of the proof shows that the same optimal risk bound holds under the more commonly used
\ell^{2}

\ell^{2}

Remark 5.2
.

In Theorem
5.1
, no structural assumption is imposed on the input distribution
\rho
.
This distribution-free guarantee stems from the
L^{\infty}
approximation result (Theorem
4.4
).
The underlying reason is that
L^{\infty}
control provides uniform pointwise error bounds, which immediately control the
\rho
-weighted
L^{2}
risk for any probability measure
\rho
.
By contrast,
L^{2}
approximation with respect to the Lebesgue measure only ensures average control under a reference measure and does not preclude the error from concentrating on regions where
\rho
assigns substantial mass.
Consequently, employing the
L^{2}
approximation result (Theorem

\rho

6
The Depth Bottleneck for Non-Smooth Activations

We first establish a quantitative limitation of constant-depth ReLU networks, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
D.1
.

Proposition 6.1

(Approximation lower bound for constant-depth ReLU networks)
.

Fix a depth
L\geqslant 2
and a smoothness parameter
s>0
. Then there exists a constant

C_{s,L}>0
, depending only on
s
and
L
, such that for every
M\geqslant 2
,

\sup_{\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1}\;\,\inf_{g\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ReLU},L}(1,1,M,\infty)}\|g-f^{\star}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\;\geqslant\;C_{s,L}\,(M\log M)^{-2\min\{L-1,s\}}.

For fixed depth
L
, the total number of parameters satisfies

N\asymp M^{2}L\asymp M^{2}.

Therefore, up to logarithmic factors, the approximation rate is lower bounded by

N^{-\min\{L-1,s\}}
, which saturates at order
N^{-(L-1)}
once
s>L-1
.
This shows that constant-depth ReLU networks cannot achieve smoothness adaptivity for arbitrarily large
s
by increasing width alone. This stands in sharp contrast to Theorem
4.1
, where constant-depth networks with smooth activations achieve approximation rates of order
N^{-s}
for arbitrary
s>0
. Hence, smooth activations enable full smoothness adaptivity even at fixed depth, whereas ReLU networks exhibit an intrinsic smoothness ceiling determined by depth. For simplicity, we consider only the one-dimensional case. We expect analogous bounds to hold for general
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, although a full treatment is left for future work.

Intuitively, this saturation phenomenon stems from the piecewise linear structure of ReLU networks.
For fixed depth
L
, a ReLU network represents a piecewise linear function whose number of linear regions grows at most polynomially in the width
M
, with the polynomial degree determined by
L
(independent of
s
).
Consequently, the effective approximation order is fundamentally limited, preventing the network from fully exploiting higher-order smoothness.
If the depth
L
is allowed to grow with the total number of parameters (or equivalently, with the target accuracy) or with the smoothness level
s
, then smoothness adaptivity can be recovered
(
77
;
37
;
40
)
.
For example,
40
establish that the approximation error of ReLU networks satisfies

O(M^{-2s/d}),

provided that the depth satisfies
L\gtrsim s^{2}
.

Numerical evidence for generalization superiority.

Having established a sharp approximation separation at constant depth, we now investigate its implications for finite-sample learning.
Deriving a sharp
generalization
lower bound for constant-depth neural networks with non-smooth activation functions—analogous to Proposition
6.1
—is technically challenging.
The main difficulty lies in the fact that classical information-theoretic tools for lower bounds, such as Fano’s inequality
(
10
;
68
)
, are formulated in a minimax framework over
all
estimators and therefore do not directly capture model-specific structural constraints (e.g., fixed depth and non-smooth activations).
Obtaining model-specific lower bounds of this type remains an interesting open direction.

Nevertheless, we provide empirical evidence that supports the generalization separation.
We generate a smooth target function using random Fourier features and learn it using two-layer neural networks equipped with various activation functions. Training is performed using full-batch Adam optimizer to minimize the empirical risk. For each activation, the learning rate and
\ell^{2}
-regularization hyperparameter are tuned over the same grid, and we report the best achieved performance. Further implementation details are deferred to Appendix
D.2
.

While optimization effects cannot be completely ruled out, these empirical results support that smooth activations enable constant-depth networks to better exploit target smoothness, thereby improving sample efficiency when learning smooth functions.

8
Conclusions

We have developed a unified, constructive analysis of both approximation and generalization for neural networks equipped with smooth activation functions over the Sobolev space
W^{s,\infty}([0,1]^{d})
.
We constructed explicit neural network approximators whose parameter norms are carefully controlled.
These networks attain the minimax-optimal approximation rate for arbitrary smoothness
s>0
, thereby demonstrating
smoothness adaptivity
at fixed depth.
The norm-controlled construction enables a sharp statistical analysis, showing that empirical risk minimization over this model class achieves the minimax-optimal estimation rate (up to logarithmic factors).
Moreover, we established approximation lower bounds for ReLU networks, showing that their smoothness adaptivity is fundamentally limited by depth.
Taken together, these results reveal that depth is not the sole mechanism for achieving smoothness adaptivity; activation smoothness provides an alternative route.

Looking ahead, several important directions remain open.
First, while our results establish statistical optimality in the noisy regression setting, the learning behavior in the noiseless regime remains unclear
(
8
)
.
Second, in scientific computing applications such as PDE solvers, performance is often evaluated under stronger norms, such as Sobolev norms.
The approximation and estimation rates of neural networks with smooth activations under these stronger norms, as well as their potential optimality in this regime, remain largely unexplored.
Clarifying these questions would further illuminate the role of activation smoothness in high-accuracy numerical and scientific learning tasks.

A.2
Verification of Smooth Activation Assumptions

In this section, we formally verify that widely adopted smooth activation functions—including sigmoid,
\frac{1}{2}(\tanh+1)
, SiLU, and GELU—satisfy Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
. The condition of being infinitely differentiable and non-polynomial, as stipulated in Assumption
3.1
, is trivially satisfied for these aforementioned activations. Regarding Assumption
3.2
, observe that for all considered functions, the first derivative
\phi^{\prime}
satisfies the uniform bound
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\prime}(t)|<2
. Consequently, by the mean value theorem, these activations possess the Lipschitz continuity property required by Assumption
3.2
with
\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}=2
. The remainder of this section focuses on verifying the approximation properties outlined in Assumption
3.3
.

Heaviside-like case.

We verify that the sigmoid and
\frac{1}{2}(\tanh+1)
activation functions satisfy the Heaviside-like condition.

•

Sigmoid function:
Consider the sigmoid activation
\sigma(t)=(1+e^{-t})^{-1}
. We observe

–

For
t>0
,

|\sigma(t)-H(t)|=\left|\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}-1\right|=\frac{e^{-t}}{1+e^{-t}}<e^{-t}.

–

For
t<0
,

|\sigma(t)-H(t)|=\left|\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}-0\right|=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}=\frac{e^{t}}{1+e^{t}}<e^{-|t|}.

Note that
e^{-|t|}\leqslant\min\{\frac{1}{e|t|},1\}
, the assumption holds with
C_{1}=1
.

•

Tanh-based function:
Consider the activation function
\phi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}(\tanh(\cdot)+1)
. We analyze its approximation to
H(\cdot)
as follows:

–

For
t>0
,

|\phi(t)-H(t)|=\left|\frac{1}{2}(\tanh(t)+1)-1\right|=\frac{1}{2}(1-\tanh(t))=\frac{e^{-t}}{e^{t}+e^{-t}}<e^{-2t}.

–

For
t<0
,

|\phi(t)-H(t)|=\left|\frac{1}{2}(\tanh(t)+1)-0\right|=\frac{1}{2}(\tanh(t)+1)=\frac{e^{t}}{e^{t}+e^{-t}}<e^{2t}=e^{-2|t|}.

Note that
e^{-2|t|}\leqslant\min\{\frac{1}{2e|t|},1\}
, the assumption holds with
C_{1}=1
.

ReLU-like case.

We verify that the GELU and SiLU activation functions satisfy the ReLU-like condition.

•

SiLU:
Consider the SiLU activation
\phi(t)=t\sigma(t)
, where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function. The deviation is as follows:

–

For
t\geqslant 0
,

|\phi(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|=|t\sigma(t)-t|=t(1-\sigma(t))=\frac{te^{-t}}{1+e^{-t}}=\frac{t}{1+e^{t}}.

–

For
t<0
,

|\phi(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|=|t\sigma(t)-0|=|t|\sigma(t)=\frac{|t|}{1+e^{-t}}=\frac{|t|}{1+e^{|t|}}.

Note that
f(u)=\frac{u}{1+e^{u}}\leqslant 1
for
u\geqslant 0
, the assumption holds with
C_{2}=1
.

•

GELU:
Consider the GELU activation
\phi(t)=t\Phi(t)
, where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution defined as

\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx.

The error satisfies

–

For
t\geqslant 0
,

|\phi(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|=|t\Phi(t)-t|=t(1-\Phi(t)).

–

For
t<0
,

|\phi(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|=|t\Phi(t)-0|=|t|\Phi(t).

By symmetry, we have
\Phi(-|t|)=1-\Phi(|t|)
; thus,
|G(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|=|t|(1-\Phi(|t|))
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We estimate the tail integral as follows:

1-\Phi(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{x}{t}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\frac{1}{t\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[-e^{-x^{2}/2}\right]_{t}^{\infty}=\frac{e^{-t^{2}/2}}{t\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Multiplying by
t
, we obtain
|\phi(t)-\text{ReLU}(t)|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
, the assumption holds with
C_{2}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}
.

\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[\widetilde{K}]^{d}

\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K,\bm{v}}\coloneqq\left(\prod_{l=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\left[2(i_{l}-1)K+2j_{l}+v_{l}-1\right]}{2K^{2}},\frac{\left[2(i_{l}-1)K+2j_{l}+v_{l}+1\right]}{2K^{2}}\right)\right)\bigcap[0,1]^{d},

where
\bm{i},\bm{j}\in\{0,1,\cdots,K\}^{d}
. For
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{6K^{2}}\right)
, we define the associated interior region as:

\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta)\coloneqq\left(\prod_{l=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\left[2(i_{l}-1)K+2j_{l}+v_{l}-1\right]}{2K^{2}}+\delta,\frac{\left[2(i_{l}-1)K+2j_{l}+v_{l}+1\right]}{2K^{2}}-\delta\right)\right)\bigcap[0,1]^{d},

and the associated band region is:

\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta):=\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K,\bm{v}}\setminus\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta).

Finally, we define the overall shift interior and band regions as:

\Omega_{\mathrm{int}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta):=\bigcup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[\widetilde{K}]^{d}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta),\quad\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta):=\bigcup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[\widetilde{K}]^{d}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(\delta).

B.2
Bramble-Hilbert Lemma

In this subsection, we approximate the target function
f^{\star}
by a piecewise polynomial.
A closely related conclusion can be found in
6
; for the self-contained presentation, we restate the argument and provide a proof.

Lemma B.1

(Bramble-Hilbert lemma)
.

For
s>0
, let
f^{\star}\in W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists a piecewise polynomial
p
of order
\lceil s\rceil-1
on the partition
\{\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}
such that

\|f^{\star}-p\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leqslant c_{1}(s,d)\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)}K^{-2s}.

Specifically, the piecewise polynomial
p
can be written as

p(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}p_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K}}(\bm{x}),

where each polynomial
p_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|<\lceil s\rceil}a_{{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{i},\bm{j}}\bm{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}
has order
\lceil s\rceil-1
and the coefficients satisfy

|a_{{\bm{\alpha}},\bm{i},\bm{j}}|\leqslant c_{2}(s,d)\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\Omega)},\quad\forall\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d},|{\bm{\alpha}}|<\lceil s\rceil.

Here,
c_{1}(s,d)
and
c_{2}(s,d)
are two constants that depend only on the smoothness
s
and dimension
d
.

Proof.

We construct a polynomial on each small cube
\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}
to approximate
f^{\star}
locally. For ease of notation, we neglect the subscript
\bm{i},\bm{j}
when there is no confusion. Let
\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K}
be a cube with side length
h
(
h=K^{-2}
for partition
\{\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}
) and
f^{\star}\in W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
. We are going to construct a polynomial
p
of order
\lceil s\rceil-1
on
\widetilde{\Omega}
such that

\|f^{\star}-p\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leqslant c_{1}(s,d)h^{s}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})},

and the coefficients of
p
satisfy

|a_{{\bm{\alpha}}}|\leqslant c_{2}(s,d)\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}.

We then give the construction. Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a cut-off function satisfying the following conditions:

•

\psi
is supported on
\widetilde{\Omega}
;

•

\psi
is non-negative, i.e.,
\psi(\bm{y})\geqslant 0
for all
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

•

\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=1
.

Then we define the averaged Taylor polynomial of order
\lceil s\rceil-1
of
f^{\star}
as

\left(Q^{\lceil s\rceil-1}f^{\star}\right)(\cdot)=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}T^{\lceil s\rceil-1}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star}(\cdot)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

where
T^{\lceil s\rceil-1}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star}
is the Taylor polynomial of order
\lceil s\rceil-1
of
f^{\star}
at
\bm{y}
:

\left(T_{\bm{y}}^{\lceil s\rceil-1}f^{\star}\right)(\cdot)=\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<\lceil s\rceil}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})(\cdot-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}.

We claim that
p=Q^{\lceil s\rceil-1}f^{\star}
satisfies the desired approximation property and coefficient bound. In the following, we will treat the cases of integer
s
and non-integer
s
separately.

Case 1:
s=m
is an integer.

In this case, we have
\lceil s\rceil=m
. We note that the target function
f^{\star}
admits the following Taylor expansion with integral remainder:

f^{\star}(\bm{x})=\left(T^{m-1}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star}\right)(\bm{x})+\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The above Taylor formula is classical for
C^{\infty}
functions. Since here
f^{\star}\in W^{m,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})
does not necessarily guarantee the pointwise existence of
D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}
, it should be understood in the weak sense.
By integrating the above equation against the cut-off function
\psi(\cdot)
over
\widetilde{\Omega}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f^{\star}(\bm{x})-(Q^{m-1}f^{\star})(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(f^{\star}(\bm{x})-(T^{m-1}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star})(\bm{x})\right)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}\sup_{\bm{y}}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}|\psi(\bm{y})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{1}(m,d)h^{m}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}.

To bound the coefficients, we expand the polynomial
Q^{m-1}f^{\star}
and rewrite it as

\displaystyle(Q^{m-1}f^{\star})(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<m}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}\left(\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\right)(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<m}\sum_{{\bm{\gamma}}+{\bm{\beta}}={{\bm{\alpha}}}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}\binom{{{\bm{\alpha}}}}{{\bm{\gamma}}}\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}}(-1)^{|{\bm{\beta}}|}\left(\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\bm{y}^{\bm{\beta}}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{\bm{\gamma}}|<m}\bm{x}^{\bm{\gamma}}\underbrace{\left(\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<m,{{\bm{\alpha}}}\geqslant{\bm{\gamma}}}\frac{(-1)^{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}}|}}{({{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}})!{\bm{\gamma}}!}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\bm{y}^{{{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}}}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\right)}_{\eqqcolon a_{\bm{\gamma}}}.

The coefficients
a_{\bm{\gamma}}
can be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle|a_{\bm{\gamma}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<m,{{\bm{\alpha}}}\geqslant{\bm{\gamma}}}\frac{1}{({{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}})!{\bm{\gamma}}!}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}|\bm{y}^{{{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}}}||D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})||\psi(\bm{y})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|<m,{{\bm{\alpha}}}\geqslant{\bm{\gamma}}}\frac{1}{({{\bm{\alpha}}}-{\bm{\gamma}})!{\bm{\gamma}}!}\|D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}|\psi(\bm{y})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{2}{(m,d)}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}.

Case 2:
s=m+\zeta
is a non-integer with
m=\lfloor s\rfloor
and
\zeta\in(0,1)
.

In this case, we have
\lceil s\rceil=m+1
. Again, we expand
f^{\star}
to order
m-1
:

f^{\star}(\bm{x})=(T^{m-1}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star})(\bm{x})+\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

By integrating against
\psi
, we have

\displaystyle f^{\star}(\bm{x})-(Q^{m}f^{\star})(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(f^{\star}(\bm{x})-(T^{m}_{\bm{y}}f^{\star})(\bm{x})\right)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle-\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}\left(D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x}))-D^{{\bm{\alpha}}}f^{\star}(\bm{y})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)\psi(\bm{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|}\left(\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}|\bm{x}+t(\bm{y}-\bm{x})-\bm{y}|^{\zeta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)|\psi(\bm{y})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{|{{\bm{\alpha}}}|=m}\frac{1}{{{\bm{\alpha}}}!}\left(\sup_{\bm{y}}|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{m+\zeta}\right)\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}|\psi(\bm{y})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}(1-t)^{\zeta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle c_{1}{(s,d)}h^{s}\|f^{\star}\|_{W^{s,\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}.

The bound for the coefficients can be obtained in the same way as in Case 1. In this way, we complete the proof of the lemma.
∎
Next, to approximate piecewise polynomials, we construct approximations for monomials, piecewise constant functions, and their products in the subsequent subsections.

B.3
Approximation of Monomials

In this subsection, we use a shallow neural network to approximate monomials by implementing a central difference scheme. Related constructions appear in
51
. We restate the argument here to clarify that the method admits explicit norm control and to keep the presentation self-contained.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{m})
, define

B_{m}(\bm{q};k)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)\left[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})\right]^{k},

where
S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})
is defined by

S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}q_{i}.

Then the following holds:

•

If
k<m
, we have
B_{m}(\bm{q},k)=0
.

•

If
k=m
, we have
B_{m}(\bm{q},m)=m!\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}
.

Proof.

By the multinomial theorem, we expand
[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})]^{k}
as follows:

[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})]^{k}=\sum_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|=k}\binom{k}{{\bm{\alpha}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)\bm{q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}},

Substituting this into the definition of
B_{m}
, we obtain

B_{m}(\bm{q};k)=\sum_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|=k}\left(\frac{1}{2^{m}}\binom{k}{{\bm{\alpha}}}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}^{1+\alpha_{i}}\right)\right)\bm{q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}.

The inner sum over
\bm{\nu}
vanishes unless each exponent
1+\alpha_{i}
is even, i.e. unless
\alpha_{i}
is odd for all
i=1,\dots,m
. Hence,

B_{m}(\bm{q};k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|=k\\
\alpha_{i}\text{ all odd }\end{subarray}}\binom{k}{{\bm{\alpha}}}\bm{q}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}.

If
k<m
, such a multi-index
{\bm{\alpha}}
cannot exist: requiring all
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 1
forces
|{\bm{\alpha}}|\geqslant m>k
. Thus
B_{m}(\bm{q};k)=0
in this case. If
k=m
, the only admissible multi-index is
{\bm{\alpha}}=(1,1,\dots,1)
. Therefore,

B_{m}(\bm{q};m)=m!\,q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m},

which completes the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
B.2
, we can employ the central difference scheme to approximate
q_{1}q_{2}\cdots q_{m}
.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
and suppose
\phi\in C^{m+1}(\mathbb{R})
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

satisfy
\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})\neq 0
.
For any vector
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and step size
0<h<1
, define the function
T_{m}^{(x_{0})}
as

T_{m}^{(x_{0})}(\bm{q},h)=\frac{1}{2^{m}h^{m}\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)\phi\left(x_{0}+h\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}q_{i}\right).

Then,

\left|T_{m}^{(x_{0})}(\bm{q},h)-\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right|\leqslant\frac{hA^{m+1}}{(m+1)!|\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})|}\sup_{|t-x_{0}|\leqslant A}\left|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)\right|,

where
A\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|q_{i}|
.

Proof.

By Taylor’s theorem with the integral remainder, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\phi(x_{0}+t)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{\phi^{(k)}(x_{0})}{k!}t^{k}+\frac{t^{m}}{(m-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{m-1}\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+st)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Setting
t=hS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})
, where
S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}q_{i}
, and inserting this expansion into the definition of
T_{m}^{(x_{0})}
gives

T_{m}^{(x_{0})}(\bm{q},h)=\frac{1}{h^{m}\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{h^{k}\phi^{(k)}(x_{0})}{k!}B_{m}(\bm{q};k)+R,

(10)

where

B_{{m}}(\bm{q};k)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})]^{k},

and the remainder term
R
is

R=\frac{1}{2^{m}(m-1)!\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})]^{m}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{m-1}\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+shS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q}))\mathrm{d}s.

By Lemma
B.2
, one has
B_{m}(\bm{q};k)=0
for all
k<m
. Hence all lower-order contributions vanish, and only the remainder term in (
10
) remains. We decompose the argument of
\phi^{(m)}
in
R
as

\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+shS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q}))=\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})+\left[\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+shS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q}))-\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})\right].

Using Lemma
B.2
again and the identities

B_{m}(\bm{q};m)=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)[S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})]^{m}=m!\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i},\quad\quad\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{m-1}\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{m},

we obtain the following error estimate

\displaystyle\left|T_{m}^{(x_{0})}(\bm{q},h)-\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{m}(m-1)!|\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})|}

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\Bigg[|S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})|^{m}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{m-1}

\displaystyle\times\left|\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+shS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q}))-\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})\right|\mathrm{d}s\Bigg].

By the mean value theorem,

\left|\phi^{(m)}(x_{0}+shS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q}))-\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})\right|\leqslant sh|S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})|\sup_{|t-x_{0}|\leqslant sh|S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})|}\left|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)\right|\leqslant shA\sup_{|t-x_{0}|\leqslant A}\left|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)\right|,

where the last inequality comes from
0<h<1,0<s<1
and
|S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})|\leqslant A\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}|q_{i}|
. Using the bounds
|S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})|^{m}\leqslant A^{m}
and

\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{m-1}s\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{m(m+1)},

we finally obtain

\left|T_{m}^{(x_{0})}(\bm{q},h)-\prod_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right|\leqslant\frac{hA^{m+1}}{(m+1)!\,|\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})|}\sup_{|t-x_{0}|\leqslant A}\left|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)\right|.

∎

With Lemma
B.2
, we can construct two-layer neural networks to approximate all monomials of the form
x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}
.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and let
{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
satisfy

\|{\bm{\alpha}}\|_{0}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}=m\geqslant 1
.
Assume
\phi\in C^{m+1}(\mathbb{R})
is non-polynomial. Then for any
Q>0
and
sufficiently small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,2}(d,1,2^{m},B_{\epsilon}),

with
B_{\epsilon}
satisfying

B_{\epsilon}=\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{m}\frac{(mQ)^{m(m+1)}\left[\sup_{|t-t_{0}|\leqslant mQ}|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)|\right]^{m}}{[2(m+1)!]^{m}|\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})|^{m+1}}.

(11)

for some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\sup_{\bm{x}\in[-Q,Q]^{d}}\bigl|g(\bm{x})-x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\bigr|<\epsilon.

Proof.

Since
\phi\in C^{m+1}(\mathbb{R})
and is not a polynomial, there exists
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})\neq 0
. Partition the index set
{1,\cdots,m}
into disjoint subsets
I_{1},\cdots,I_{d}
defined by

I_{l}=\left\{i\;:\;1+\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\alpha_{j}\leqslant i\leqslant\sum_{l=0}^{l}\alpha_{j}\right\},\quad 1\leqslant l\leqslant d,

where
\alpha_{0}=0
. Then
|I_{l}|=\alpha_{l}
for every
l
. Define the neural network

g(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2^{m}h^{m}\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})}\sum_{\bm{\nu}\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}\right)\phi\left(t_{0}+h\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j\in I_{l}}\nu_{j}\right)x_{l}\right),

where
h>0
is a parameter to be chosen. Setting

q_{j}=x_{l},\quad j\in I_{l},\quad\mathrm{for}\;l=1,\cdots,d,

then expression inside
\phi
becomes
t_{0}+hS_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})
with
S_{\bm{\nu}}(\bm{q})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}q_{i}
. Thus, by Lemma
B.3
, we have

|g(x)-x_{1}^{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}|\leqslant\frac{hA^{m+1}}{(m+1)!|\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})|}\sup_{|t-t_{0}|\leqslant A}\left|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)\right|,

where
A=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|q_{i}|=\sum_{l=1}^{d}\alpha_{l}|x_{l}|
. Since
|x_{l}|\leqslant Q
, we have
A\leqslant mQ
. Choose

h=\frac{\epsilon(m+1)!|\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})|}{(mQ)^{m+1}[\sup_{|t-t_{0}|\leqslant mQ}|\phi^{(m+1)}(t)|]}.

With this choice,

\sup_{\bm{x}\in[-Q,Q]^{d}}|g(\bm{x})-x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}|\leqslant\epsilon.

There are
2^{m}
neurons in the hidden layer of
g
and all weights and biases in
g
are bounded in magnitude by

\max\left\{t_{0},\;\frac{1}{2^{m}h^{m}|\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})|},\;h\left|\sum_{j\in I_{l}}\nu_{j}\right|\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{m}h^{m}|\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})|},

for sufficiently small
\epsilon
. This equals
B_{\epsilon}
as stated in (
11
). We complete the proof.
∎

Remark B.5
.

In Lemma
B.4
, the condition “sufficiently small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
” implies the existence of a threshold
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that the statement holds for all
0<\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
. Crucially,
\epsilon_{0}
is independent of the parameters
K
and
\delta
, but is allowed to depend on problem specifications, such as activation
\phi
, dimension
d
, smoothness
s
, among others. We adopt this convention throughout the subsequent analysis.

Remark B.6
.

If the input amplitude
Q
in Lemma
B.4
is independent of
\epsilon,\delta,K
, then norm
B_{\epsilon}
defined in (
11
) satisfies
B_{\epsilon}\lesssim(1/\epsilon)^{m}
.

The construction in Lemma
B.4
relies on the existence of a point
x_{0}
at which
\phi^{(m)}(x_{0})\neq 0
. The following lemma establishes that for any smooth nonpolynomial function
\phi
, there exists such a point for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma B.7

(
9
;
15
)
.

Let
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
If
\phi
is not a polynomial, then there exists a point
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})\neq 0,\quad m\in\mathbb{N}.

In the subsequent analysis, if
\phi
satisfies the smoothness assumption in Assumption
3.3
, we assume that the point
t_{0}
is such that
\phi^{(m)}(t_{0})\neq 0,m\in\mathbb{N}
.

As a corollary of Lemma
B.4
, we obtain an approximation of the identity function, which serves as a fundamental tool in the subsequent analysis.

Corollary B.8
.

Fix
Q>0
. Let
\phi
be an activation function satisfying Assumption
3.1
with
\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})\neq 0
. For any
L\geqslant 2
, and sufficiently small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(1,1,2,B_{\epsilon}),

with
B_{\epsilon}
satisfying

B_{\epsilon}\leqslant\frac{L-1}{\epsilon}\frac{Q^{2}\sup_{|t-t_{0}|\leqslant Q}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)|}{4|\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})|^{2}},

such that

\sup_{x\in[-Q,Q]}|g(x)-x|<\epsilon.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.4
, there exist neural networks
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L-1}
such that for each
i
,

\sup_{x\in[-Q-1,Q+1]}|\nu_{i}(x)-x|\leqslant\frac{\epsilon}{L-1}.

Each network
\nu_{i}
has depth 2, width 2, and parameter norm bounded by

\|\theta(\nu_{i})\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{L-1}{\epsilon}\frac{Q^{2}\sup_{|t-t_{0}|\leqslant Q}|\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)|}{4|\phi^{\prime}(t_{0})|^{2}}.

(12)

We define the composite network
g\coloneqq\nu_{L-1}\circ\nu_{L-2}\circ\cdots\circ\nu_{1}
. Then for
x\in[-Q,Q]
, the approximation error is bounded by:

|g(x)-x|\leqslant\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}|\nu_{l}\circ\cdots\circ\nu_{1}(x)-\nu_{l-1}\circ\cdots\circ\nu_{1}(x)|+|\nu_{1}(x)-x|\leqslant(L-1)\frac{\epsilon}{L-1}\leqslant\epsilon.

It follows that the depth of
g
is
L
, its width is
2
. Since the parameter norm bound (
12
) holds for each composition
\nu_{i}
, it also applies to
g
.
This completes the proof.
∎

B.4
Approximation of Piecewise Constants

In this subsection, we construct shallow neural network approximations for the following piecewise constant functions, consisting of
K^{2d}
distinct pieces:

C(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}c_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}}(\bm{x})

(13)

We begin by reformulating
C(\bm{x})
using the following lemma.

Lemma B.9
.

Let
C(\bm{x})
be defined by (
13
). Then

C(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left(\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}c_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{1},\bm{j}}^{K}}\left(\bm{x}-\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\right).

(14)

Here,
\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}
denotes the lower-left vertex associated with the cell
\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}
. Specifically, for
\bm{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})
,

\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}\coloneqq\left(\frac{i_{1}-1}{K},\cdots,\frac{i_{d}-1}{K}\right).

Proof.

For any
\bm{i}\in[K]^{d},\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{1},\bm{j}}^{K}}\left(\bm{x}-\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{1},\bm{j}}^{K}}\left(\bm{x}-\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\right).

Substituting this representation of
\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}^{K}}(\bm{x})
into (
13
), summing first over
\bm{i}
and subsequently over
\bm{j}
, yields the desired expression for the piecewise constant function
C
in (
14
).
∎

With the reformulation of
C
provided by Lemma
B.9
, the problem of approximating a piecewise constant function with
K^{2d}
pieces is reduced to approximating
K^{d}
distinct piecewise constant functions, each of which consists of
K^{d}
regions of the form
\{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}\}_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}
. More explicitly, for each
\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}
, we consider the function

C_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}c_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}\left(\bm{x}\right),\quad\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}.

(15)

It is important to note that all these
K^{d}
piecewise constant functions share exactly the same partition
\{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}\}_{\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}}
.
Besides, we also need to extract the position of
\bm{x}
relative to the lower-left corner
\bm{a}_{\bm{i}}^{K}
of the region
\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}
containing it, and subsequently determine which refined grid cell
{\Omega_{\bm{1},\bm{j}}^{K}}
this relative coordinate falls into.

We first construct approximations of
indicator functions in one dimension.

Lemma B.10
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumption
3.3
.
For any
a<b
and
\delta\in\bigl(0,\tfrac{b-a}{3}\bigr)
, and any
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exists a neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,2}\left(1,1,2,\frac{4C_{1}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta}\right),

where
C_{1}
is the constant specified in Assumption
3.3
, such that

•

(Approximation)
|g(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)|<\epsilon,\quad x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b]
.

•

(Boundedness)
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2(C_{1}+1)
.

Proof.

Define

g(x)=\phi\left(\beta\left(x-\left(a+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right)-\phi\left(\beta\left(x-\left(b-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right),

(16)

where
\beta>0
will be chosen later.
For every
x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{[a+\frac{\delta}{2},b-\frac{\delta}{2})}(x)

\displaystyle=H\left(\beta\left(x-\left(a+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right)-H\left(\beta\left(x-\left(b-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right),

Hence, still for
x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b]

\left|g(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)\right|\leqslant\left|(\phi-H)\left(\beta\left(x-\left(a+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right)\right|+\left|(\phi-H)\left(\beta\left(x-\left(b-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right)\right)\right|.

By the Heaviside-like assumption on
\phi
, each term is bounded by
2C_{1}/(\beta\delta)
. Choosing
\beta=\frac{4C_{1}}{\delta\epsilon}
therefore gives

\left|g(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)\right|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b].

Moreover, since
\|H\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
and
\phi
satisfies the Heaviside-like condition, we have
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant C_{1}+1
. Consequently

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2(C_{1}+1).

Finally, from the explicit construction (
16
), each parameter involved in
g
is bounded in magnitude by

\max\left\{\frac{4C_{1}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta},1\right\}=\frac{4C_{1}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta},

for sufficiently small
\epsilon
. We complete the proof.
∎

Lemma B.11
.

With the same assumptions as in Lemma
B.10
, except that
\phi
is a ReLU-like activation function, there exists a neural network

g\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,2}\left(1,1,4,\frac{4C_{2}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta}\right),

where
C_{2}
is the constant specified in Assumption
3.3
, such that

•

(Approximation)
|g(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)|<\epsilon,\quad x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b]
.

•

(Boundedness)
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta\delta}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-a))-\frac{1}{\beta\delta}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-(a+\delta)))

(17)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta\delta}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-(b-\delta)))+\frac{1}{\beta\delta}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-b)),

where
\beta>0
is a parameter to be chosen. One checks directly that

\psi(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x),\quad x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b]

(18)

and that
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
.

Now construct
g
by replacing each ReLU activation in (
17
) with
\phi
. The ReLU-like condition implies the uniform approximation bound

|\psi(x)-g(x)|\leqslant\frac{4C_{2}}{\beta\delta},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Choosing
\beta=\frac{4C_{2}}{\epsilon\delta}
and combining (
18
) gives

|g(x)-\mathbbm{1}_{[a,b)}(x)|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad x\notin[a,a+\delta]\cup[b-\delta,b].

Furthermore, since
\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1
, it follows that for sufficiently small
\epsilon
,

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 1+\epsilon<2.

Finally, using the explicit construction of
g
, every parameter in the network is bounded in magnitude by

\max\left\{\frac{1}{\beta\delta},\beta(|a|+|b|+1)\right\}=\max\left\{\frac{\epsilon}{4C_{2}},\frac{4C_{2}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta}\right\}=\frac{4C_{2}(|a|+|b|+1)}{\epsilon\delta}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We next construct approximations of indicator functions in general dimensions
d\geqslant 1
. The following two lemmas characterize neural network approximations of all indicator functions associated with the coarse grid.

Lemma B.12
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
. Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
with
K
sufficiently large.
Then for any sufficiently small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and any

\bm{i}\in[K]^{d}
, the
\bm{i}
-th output

•

(Approximation)
\left|[g(\bm{x})]_{\bm{i}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}}(\bm{x})\right|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad\bm{x}\notin\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\mathrm{band}}(\delta).

•

(Boundedness) For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

|[g(\bm{x})]_{\bm{i}}|\leqslant\begin{cases}2^{d+1}(1+C_{1})^{d},&\textrm{Heaviside-like}\;\phi,\\
2^{d+1},&\textrm{ReLU-like}\;\phi,\end{cases}

(19)

where
C_{1}
is the constant appearing in Assumption
3.3
.

Proof.

We prove the result for the Heaviside-like
\phi
, and the proof for the ReLU-like
\phi
follows in the same manner. For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by invoking Lemma
B.10
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon_{1}>0
and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{3K})
, there exist
dK
sub-networks

h_{l,i}\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,2}\left(d,1,2,B_{\epsilon_{1}}\right),

with
B_{\epsilon_{1}}=\frac{12C_{1}}{\delta\epsilon_{1}}
satisfying, for all
l\in[d]
,
i\in[K]

\left|h_{l,i}(\bm{x})-\mathbbm{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{K},\frac{i}{K})}(x_{l})\right|\leqslant\epsilon_{1},\quad x_{l}\notin\left[\frac{(i-1)}{K},\frac{(i-1)}{K}+\delta\right]\cup\left[\frac{i}{K}-\delta,\frac{i}{K}\right],

and
\|h_{l,i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant 2(1+C_{1})
. Furthermore, by lemma
B.4
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
, there exists a neural network

\psi\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,2}(d,1,2^{d},B_{\epsilon_{2}}),

with
B_{\epsilon_{2}}\lesssim(1/\epsilon_{2})^{d}

such that for

Next, for
\bm{i}=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{d})
, we define the
\bm{i}
-th output of
g
as

For sufficiently small
\epsilon_{1}

\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\bm{i},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta)

|h_{l,i_{l}}(\bm{x})-1|\leqslant\epsilon_{1}

\left|\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l,i_{l}}(\bm{x})-1\right|\leqslant\max\left\{(1+\epsilon_{1})^{d}-1,1-(1-\epsilon_{1})^{d}\right\}<2d\epsilon_{1},\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\bm{i},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta).

Conversely, for
\bm{x}\notin\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{K}

l
such that
|h_{l,i_{l}}(\bm{x})|\leqslant\epsilon_{2}

\left|\prod_{l=1}^{d}h_{l,i_{l}}(\bm{x})\right|\leqslant 2^{d}(1+C_{1})^{d}\epsilon_{1}\quad\bm{x}\notin\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{M,K}.

By choosing
\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\epsilon}{2^{d+1}(1+C_{1})^{d}}
and
\epsilon_{2}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, we obtain

\left|[g(\bm{x})]_{\bm{i}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{\bm{i}}^{M,K}}\left(\bm{x}\right)\right|<2^{d}(1+C_{1})^{d}\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}<\epsilon,\quad\bm{x}\notin\Omega_{\bm{i},\mathrm{band}}^{K}(\delta).

Moreover, since
\|h_{l,i_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant 2(1+C_{1})
, we obtain

\left|[g(\bm{x})]_{\mathrm{i}}\right|\leqslant 2^{d}(1+C_{1})^{d}+\epsilon_{2}<2^{d+1}(1+C_{1})^{d},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

Finally, regarding the network architecture, the neural network
g
consists of
2dK
neurons in the first hidden layer and
2^{d}K^{d}
neurons in the second hidden layer. Therefore, for sufficiently large
K
, the width of
g
is bounded as
\max\{2dK,2^{d}K^{d}\}\leqslant 2^{d+1}K^{d}
. Given that
\|\theta(h_{l,i})\|_{\infty}=B_{\epsilon_{1}},\|\theta(\psi)\|_{\infty}=B_{\epsilon_{2}}
, and the connecting weights between the networks (output layer for
h_{l,i}
and the input layer for
\psi
)

O(1)
, we conclude that
\|\theta(g)\|_{\infty}

B_{\epsilon,\delta}\lesssim\max\left\{B_{\epsilon_{1}},B_{\epsilon_{2}},1\right\}\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{3}{\epsilon\delta},\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{d}\right\},

This completes the proof.
∎

Using the neural network approximations for all indicator functions over the coarse grid in Lemma

Lemma B.13
.

Let

\displaystyle\left|[g(\bm{x})]_{l}-\left(x_{l}-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{i-1}{K}\mathbbm{1}_{[\frac{i-1}{K},\frac{i}{K})}(x_{l})\right)\right|

\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\epsilon_{2}=\frac{\epsilon}{2K}

21

Finally, each network
\psi_{l}
and each subnetwork

employs two hidden neurons. Consequently,
g
comprises a total of
2d(K+1)
hidden neurons, and its width is therefore bounded by
6dK
for all sufficiently large

\|\theta(g)\|_{\infty}
is bounded by

B_{\epsilon,\delta,K}\leqslant\max\left\{B_{\epsilon_{1}},B_{\epsilon_{2},\delta}\right\}\lesssim\frac{K}{\epsilon\delta}.

This completes the proof.

Lemma B.16
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
. Let
C(\bm{x})
be given in (
13

sufficiently large.
Then for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0

(23)

such that the following holds:

•

(Approximation)
|g(\bm{x})-C(\bm{x})|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad\bm{x}\in\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega^{K}_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}(\delta)
.

•

(Boundedness) For
\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
,

c_{\max}4^{d+3},&\textrm{ReLU-like}\;\phi,\end{cases}

where
C_{1}
is the constant appearing in Assumption
3.3
.

Proof.

We prove the result for the Heaviside-like
\phi
, and the proof for the ReLU-like
\phi
follows in the same manner. Let
\epsilon_{1}
be sufficiently small. We begin by considering the neural network constructed in Lemma
B.13
with sufficiently small
\epsilon_{1}
. From the construction of
\psi_{1}
, we have
\|\theta(\psi_{1})\|_{\infty}\lesssim\max\{c_{\max}K^{d}/(\epsilon_{1}\delta),c_{\max}^{d}K^{d^{2}}/\epsilon_{1}^{d}\}
. By the approximation guaranty for
\psi_{1}
, we have

\left|[\psi_{1}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}-C_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\right|\leqslant\epsilon_{1},\quad\bm{x}\in\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta).

Next, by Corollary
B.8
, we obtain
K^{d}
subnetworks
\{\psi_{2,\bm{j}}\}_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}
, each with depth 3 and
\|\theta(\psi_{2,\bm{j}})\|_{\infty}\lesssim 1/\epsilon_{1}
the norm of parameters in the input layer for each

is bounded by
O(1)
. These subnetworks satisfy

\left|\psi_{2,\bm{j}}(x)-x\right|\leqslant\epsilon_{1},\quad\mathrm{for}\;|x|\leqslant c_{\max}2^{d+2}(1+C_{1})^{d},\quad\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}.

Define the network
\psi_{3}
by

so that
\psi_{3}
has depth
4
and width

. The norm of parameters connecting
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2,\bm{j}}
is bounded by
\|\theta(\psi_{1})\|_{\infty}
, since the since the weights in the input layer of
\psi_{2,\bm{j}}
are
O(1)
. Therefore, we have the bound for
\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty}

\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty}\lesssim\max\left\{\|\theta(\psi_{1})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{2,\bm{j}})\|_{\infty}\right\}\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{(c_{\max}+1)K^{d}}{\epsilon_{1}\delta},\frac{c_{\max}^{d}K^{d^{2}}}{\epsilon_{1}^{d}}\right\}.

For the constructed
\psi_{3}
, we have

\left|[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}-C_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\right|\leqslant 2\epsilon_{1},\quad\bm{x}\in\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta)

and

\left|[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right|\leqslant|[\psi_{1}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}|+\epsilon_{1}\leqslant c_{\max}2^{d+3}(1+C_{1})^{d},\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}.

Let
\psi_{4}
be the network constructed in Lemma
B.15
with

. The depth and width of
\psi_{4}
are
4
and

\psi_{4}

.
For the constructed
\psi_{4}

\left|[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}-\widetilde{I}_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\right|\leqslant\frac{\epsilon_{1}}{K^{d}},\quad\bm{x}\in\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta),

where

is the shorthand notation for

\sum_{\bm{l}\in[K]^{d}}|[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{l}}|\leqslant 2^{d+2}(1+C_{1})^{d}\quad\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Again by Lemma
B.4

\epsilon_{2}

K^{d}

is bounded by
O(1)

\displaystyle\left|g(\bm{x})-C(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\left([\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}},[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right)-\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}C_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\widetilde{I}_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left|\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\left([\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}},[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right)-[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left|[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}-C_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\widetilde{I}_{\bm{j}}(\bm{x})\right|

where we choose

in the last inequality. Moreover, we have the uniform bound for the output of
g
as

\displaystyle|g(\bm{x})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\left([\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}},[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right)-[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}+[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left|\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\left([\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}},[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right)-[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right|+\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left|[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\epsilon_{2}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}\left(\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\psi_{3}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}\right)[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\epsilon}{2}+c_{\max}2^{d+3}(1+C_{1})^{d}\sum_{\bm{j}\in[K]^{d}}[\psi_{4}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\epsilon}{2}+c_{\max}2^{2d+5}(1+C_{1})^{2d}\leqslant c_{\max}4^{d+3}(1+C_{1})^{2d},\quad\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}.

g

5
, and the width can be bounded by
2^{d+2}K^{d}+2^{d+2}K^{d}=2^{d+3}K^{d}
. The norm of parameters connecting
\psi_{3},\psi_{4}
to
\{\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\}_{\bm{j}}
is bounded by
\max\left\{\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{4})\|_{\infty}\right\}
, since the weights in the input layer of
\{\psi_{5,\bm{j}}\}_{\bm{j}}
are
O(1)
. Therefore,
\|\theta(g)\|_{\infty}
is bounded by

\displaystyle B_{\epsilon,\delta,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{4})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{5,\bm{j}})\|_{\infty}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{(c_{\max}+1)K^{d}}{\epsilon_{1}\delta},\frac{c_{\max}^{d}K^{d^{2}}}{\epsilon_{1}^{d}},\frac{K^{d}}{\epsilon_{1}\delta^{2}},\frac{K^{d^{2}}}{\epsilon_{1}^{d}},\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim(c_{\max}+1)^{2d}\max\left\{\frac{K^{d^{2}}}{\epsilon^{d}},\frac{K^{2d}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{K^{d}}{\epsilon\delta^{2}}\right\},

as stated in (
23
). This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark B.17
.

In fact, one can approximate piecewise constant functions with
K^{2d}
pieces over refined grids by directly approximating all indicator functions on each of the
K^{2d}
refined grid cells. However, although the number of non-zero parameters in the newly constructed network is
O(K^{2d})
, the width of the network is also
O(K^{2d})
, leading to a total number of parameters that grows as
O(K^{4d})
. This discrepancy between the count of non-zero parameters and the total parameter space imposes an impractical
\ell^{0}
-sparsity constraint on the newly constructed network.

\bm{x}\in\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{int}}^{K}(\delta)

\displaystyle\left|g(\bm{x})-C(\bm{x})\bm{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\psi_{4}(\psi_{2}(\bm{x}),\psi_{3}(\bm{x}))-\psi_{2}(\bm{x})\psi_{3}(\bm{x})\right|+|\psi_{2}(\bm{x})\psi_{3}(\bm{x})-C(\bm{x})\bm{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon_{1}+|\psi_{2}(\bm{x})||\psi_{3}(\bm{x})-C(\bm{x})|+|C(\bm{x})||\psi_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon_{1}+2\epsilon_{1}+5c_{\max}\epsilon_{1}\leqslant\epsilon,

where we choose
\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\epsilon}{3+5c_{\max}}
in the last inequality. Moreover, we can bound
g(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}\in[0,1)^{d}
as follows:

\displaystyle|g(\bm{x})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\psi_{4}(\psi_{2}(\bm{x}),\psi_{3}(\bm{x}))-\psi_{2}(\bm{x})\psi_{3}(\bm{x})\right|+|\psi_{2}(\bm{x})\psi_{3}(\bm{x})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon_{1}+2\times c_{\max}4^{d+3}(1+C_{1})^{2d}\leqslant c_{\max}4^{d+4}(1+C_{1})^{2d}.

Finally, the depth of
g
is
6
, and its width can be bounded by
2^{d+3}K^{d}+\max\{2^{m},4\}\leqslant 2^{d+4}K^{d}
. The norm of parameters connecting
\psi_{2},\psi_{3},\psi_{4}
is bounded by
\max\left\{\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{4})\|_{\infty}\right\}
, since the weights in the input layer of
\psi_{4}
are
O(1)
. Therefore,
\|\theta(g)\|_{\infty}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle B_{\epsilon,\delta,K}

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left\{\|\theta(\psi_{2})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{3})\|_{\infty},\|\theta(\psi_{4})\|_{\infty}\right\}

\displaystyle\lesssim(c_{\max}+1)^{3d+m}\max\left\{\frac{K^{d^{2}}}{\epsilon^{d}},\frac{K^{2d}}{\epsilon^{2}},\frac{K^{d}}{\epsilon\delta^{2}},\frac{1}{\epsilon^{m}}\right\},

as stated in (
24
). This concludes the proof.
∎

B.7
Approximation of Weight Functions

In the remaining part of this section, we strengthen the approximation result of Theorem
4.1
by improving the error guarantee from the
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
to
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
. The constructed networks given in Theorem
4.1
fail to achieve an uniform
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
approximation on the region
\cup_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\Omega_{\bm{i},\bm{j},\mathrm{band}}^{K}(\delta)
. The obstruction stems from the fact that indicator functions associated with this region cannot be uniformly approximated, as shown in Lemma
B.12
. To circumvent this issue, we introduce a weight function that assigns negligible mass to the associated region, thereby suppressing the approximation error.

We begin by formally defining the basis functions employed in the construction of the weight functions, which are tailored to the activation function classes as specified in Assumption
3.3
.

Definition B.20

(Basis function)
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumption
3.3
. For any
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and any
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{12K^{2}}\right)
, and any
\beta>0
, we define the basis function
B_{\phi}^{\beta,\delta,K}
as follows:

•

If
\phi
is Heaviside-like, then:

B_{\phi}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)=\phi\left(\beta(x-3\delta)\right)-\phi\left(\beta(x+3\delta-K^{-2})\right).

•

If
\phi
is ReLU-like, then:

\displaystyle B_{\phi}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)=\frac{1}{2\delta\beta}\Big[\phi\left(\beta(x-2\delta)\right)

\displaystyle-\phi\left(\beta(x-4\delta)\right)

\displaystyle-\phi\left(\beta(x-K^{-2}+4\delta)\right)+\phi\left(\beta(x-K^{-2}+2\delta)\right)\Big].

Building upon the basis functions established in Definition
B.20
, we proceed to define the univariate weight functions as follows.

Definition B.21

(Univariate weight function)
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumption
3.3
. For any
K\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and any
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{12K^{2}}\right)
and any
\beta>0
, we define the primary weight function
w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,1}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
piecewise via translation:

w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,1}(x)\coloneqq B_{\phi}^{\beta,\delta,K}\left(x-\frac{k}{K^{2}}\right),\quad x\in\left[\frac{k}{K^{2}},\frac{k+1}{K^{2}}\right),

(28)

where
k=0,1,\cdots,K^{2}-1
.

The complementary weight function
w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,2}
is defined as:

w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,2}(x)\coloneqq 1-w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,1}(x),\quad x\in[0,1).

Finally, we impose periodic boundary conditions such that
w^{\beta,\delta,K}_{\phi,i}(1)\coloneqq w_{\phi,i}^{\beta,\delta,K}(0)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

The univariate weight functions exhibit the following properties.

Proposition B.22
.

Let
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}
and
w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}
be the 1D weight functions defined in Definition
B.21
. For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and any
\beta\geqslant\frac{2}{\delta\epsilon}\max\left\{C_{1},C_{2}\right\}
, the following properties hold:

•

(Partition of unity)
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)+w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)=1,\quad x\in[0,1].

•

(Locally quasi-vanishing behavior) The weight functions are effectively supported away from band regions:

|w_{\phi,i}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad x\in\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,i}(2\delta),\quad i=1,2,

where the band regions
\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,i}(2\delta)
are defined as:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,1}(2\delta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{k=0}^{K^{2}}\left[-2\delta+\frac{k}{K^{2}},2\delta+\frac{k}{K^{2}}\right]\bigcap[0,1],

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,2}(2\delta)\coloneqq\bigcup_{k=0}^{K^{2}-1}\left[\frac{2k+1}{2K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{2k+1}{2K^{2}}+2\delta\right].

•

(Boundedness)
\max\left\{\|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])},\|w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\right\}\leqslant 2C_{1}+3
.

Proof.

By Definition
B.21
, the weight functions
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}
and
w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}
are periodic with period
T=K^{-2}
. It therefore suffices to verify the stated properties on the interval
[0,K^{-2})
. Moreover, the partition-of-unity property follows directly from Definition
B.21
. We then prove the remaining two properties. We first consider Heaviside-like activations and then deal with the ReLU-like case.

•

(Heaviside-like)
Let
\chi
be the indicator function defined by

\chi(x)\coloneqq H(\beta(x-3\delta))-H(\beta(x+3\delta-K^{-2}))

We have the following properties.

–

For
x\in[0,2\delta]\cup[\frac{1}{K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{K^{2}}]
,
\chi(x)=0
.

–

For
x\in[\frac{1}{2K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{2K^{2}}+2\delta]
,
\chi(x)=1
.

On the interval
I\coloneqq[0,2\delta]\cup[\frac{1}{2K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{2K^{2}}+2\delta]\cup[\frac{1}{K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{K^{2}}]
, the deviation between
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)
and
\chi(x)
is bounded by:

\displaystyle|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)-\chi(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|(\phi-H)(\beta(x-3\delta))\right|+|(\phi-H)(\beta(x+3\delta-K^{-2}))|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}\frac{C_{1}}{\beta}\left({|x-3\delta|}^{-1}+{|x+3\delta-K^{-2}|}^{-1}\right)\overset{(b)}{\leqslant}\frac{2C_{1}}{\beta\delta}\overset{(c)}{\leqslant}\epsilon,

where
(a)
follows from the Heaviside-like assumption in (
1
),
(b)
follows from the fact that
x\in I
ensures the distance from
x
to
3\delta
and
K^{-2}-3\delta
is at least
\delta
, and
(c)
follows from
\beta\geqslant\frac{2C_{1}}{\delta\epsilon}
.

The locally quasi-vanishing properties for weight functions follow directly:

\displaystyle|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)|=|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)-\chi(x)|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad x\in[0,2\delta]\cup\left[\frac{1}{K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{K^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle|w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)|=|1-w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)|=|\chi(x)-w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad x\in\left[\frac{1}{2K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{2K^{2}}+2\delta\right].

Moreover, by the Heaviside-like assumption in (
1
) and
\|\chi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1
, we obtain:

\|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 2C_{1}+1,\quad\|w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1+\|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 2C_{1}+2.

•

(ReLU-like)
We define the nominal trapezoidal profile
g
via:

\displaystyle\chi(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\delta\beta}\Big[\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-2\delta))

\displaystyle-\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-4\delta))

\displaystyle-\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-K^{-2}+4\delta))+\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-K^{-2}+2\delta))\Big].

Similar to the Heaviside case, the function
g
satisfies

–

For
x\in[0,2\delta]\cup[\frac{1}{K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{K^{2}}]
,
\chi(x)=0
.

–

For
x\in[\frac{1}{2K^{2}}-2\delta,\frac{1}{2K^{2}}+2\delta]
,
\chi(x)=1
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the deviation between
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)
and
\chi(x)
is bounded as:

\displaystyle\left|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)-\chi(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\delta\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}|\phi(\beta(x-2j\delta))-\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-2j\delta))|

(29)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\delta\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{2}|\phi(\beta(x-K^{-2}+2j\delta))-\mathrm{ReLU}(\beta(x-K^{-2}+2j\delta))|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta\beta}\overset{(b)}{\leqslant}\epsilon,

where
(a)
follows from the ReLU-like assumption in (
2
) and
(b)
follows from
\beta\geqslant\frac{2C_{2}}{\delta\beta}
. The locally quasi-vanishing properties follow the same logic as the Heaviside-like case.

Regarding boundedness, since
\|\chi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1
, and combining the error bound in (
29
), we establish the boundedness for the weight functions:

\|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1+\epsilon<2,\quad\|w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1+\|w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1])}<3.

∎

We now generalize the weight functions to arbitrary dimensions using a tensor product approach.

Definition B.23

(Multivariate weight functions)
.

Let the parameters
\beta,\delta,K
and the activation function
\phi
satisfy the same conditions given in Definition
B.21
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and a multi-index
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})\in[2]^{d}
, we define the
d
-variate weight function
w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as:

w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(\bm{x}):=\prod_{l=1}^{d}w_{\phi,v_{l}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{l}).

The multivariate weight functions satisfy the following properties.

Proposition B.24
.

Let
w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}
denote the
d
-variate weight functions defined in Definition
B.23
. For any sufficiently small
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and any
\beta\geqslant\frac{2(2C_{1}+3)^{d-1}}{\delta\epsilon}\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
, the following properties hold:

•

(Partition of unity)
\sum_{\bm{v}\in[2]^{d}}w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(\bm{x})=1,\quad\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}
.

•

(Locally quasi-vanishing behavior) The weight function is effectively supported away from the band region
\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(2\delta)
:

|w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(\bm{x})|\leqslant\epsilon,\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(2\delta).

•

(Boundedness)
\left\|w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant(2C_{1}+3)^{d}
.

Proof.

We now establish the three properties:

•

(Partition of unity)
Exploiting the tensor product structure, the summation over the multi-index
\bm{v}\in[2]^{d}
factorizes into a product of univariate sums:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{v}\in[2]^{d}}w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v_{1}\in[2]}w_{\phi,v_{1}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{1})\left(\sum_{v_{2}\in[2]}w_{\phi,v_{2}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{2})\left(\cdots\left(\sum_{v_{d}\in[2]}w_{\phi,v_{d}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{d})\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{v_{l}\in[2]}w_{\phi,v_{l}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{l})\right)=1,\qquad\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d},

where we use the partition of unity property for univariate weight functions
w_{\phi,1}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)+w_{\phi,2}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x)=1
from Proposition
B.22
.

•

(Locally quasi-vanishing behavior)
Let
\epsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
and assume
\beta\geqslant\frac{2}{\delta\epsilon_{1}}\max\left\{C_{1},C_{2}\right\}
. Consider an arbitrary point
\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(2\delta)
. By the definition of the multivariate band region, there exists at least one coordinate index
l\in[d]
such that
x_{l}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,v_{l}}(2\delta)
. Thus, we obtain:

\left|w_{\phi,\bm{v}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(\bm{x})\right|=|w_{\phi,v_{l}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{l})|\prod_{k\neq l}|w_{\phi,v_{k}}^{\beta,\delta,K}(x_{k})|\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}(2C_{1}+3)^{d-1}\epsilon_{1}\overset{(b)}{\leqslant}\epsilon,\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{band}}^{K,\bm{v}}(2\delta),

where
(a)
follows from the locally quasi-vanishing behavior of the univariate weight function
w_{\phi,v_{l}}^{\beta,\delta,K}
and the boundedness of the remaining
d-1
univariate weight functions as given in Proposition
B.22
, and
(b)
is obtained by setting
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon/(2C_{1}+3)^{d-1}
and choosing
\beta\geqslant\frac{2(2C_{1}+3)^{d-1}}{\delta\epsilon}\max\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
.

•

(Boundedness)
Leveraging the univariate bounds established in Proposition
B.22
, we deduce the uniform bound for multivariate weight functions:

Lemma
B.25
establishes the approximation of the univariate weight functions outside the coarse band region. To achieve a uniform approximation over the entire interval
[0,1]
, we multiply the network output by a spatial quasi-indicator function supported on the complement of the coarse bands. This strategy exploits the fact that within the coarse band regions, the target weight functions are quasi-vanishing, while the neural network output remains uniformly bounded. Consequently, this multiplication effectively suppresses the error in the band regions.

In the following, We provide the definition for the quasi-indicator functions.

Definition B.26

.

Let
\phi

•

\displaystyle I_{\phi,1}^{\widetilde{\beta},{\delta},K}(x):

4
, width
O(K)
, and its parameter norm bounded by

This concludes the proof.

Lemma

characterizes the trade-off between approximation accuracy and the complexity of the model class, as measured by the covering number. We now provide an upper bound for the covering number of the model class defined in Section

Lemma C.3

(Covering number bound)
.

Let
\phi
satisfy Assumption
3.2
. The covering number of
\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,W,B)
with input
\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d}

can be bounded by

\log\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M,B),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty})\leqslant 2(L+d)M^{2}\log\left(\frac{4^{L+1}d(\max\{\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}},1\}M)^{L}B^{L+1}}{\tau}\right),

(52)

for
B\geqslant\max\left\{1,\frac{|\phi(0)|}{\max\{\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}},1\}(d+1)}\right\}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume
\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\geqslant 1
.
Now suppose that a pair of different two networks
g,\widehat{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M,B)
given by

g(\bm{x})=(\bm{W}_{L}\phi(\cdot)+\bm{b}_{L})\circ\cdots\circ(\bm{W}_{1}\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{1}),\quad\widehat{g}(\bm{x})=(\widehat{\bm{W}}_{L}\phi(\cdot)+\widehat{\bm{b}}_{L})\circ\cdots\circ(\widehat{\bm{W}}_{1}\bm{x}+\widehat{\bm{b}}_{1})

with

\|\bm{W}_{l}-\widehat{\bm{W}}_{l}\|_{\infty,\infty}\leqslant\varrho,\quad\|\bm{b}_{l}-\widehat{\bm{b}}_{l}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\varrho,\quad 1\leqslant l\leqslant L.

For the network
g,\widehat{g}
, we recurrently define
\{\bm{z}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L},\{\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}
as

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{z}}_{0}\coloneqq\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\quad\bm{z}_{1}\coloneqq\bm{W}_{1}\bm{z}_{0}+\bm{b}_{1},\quad\bm{z}_{l}\coloneqq\bm{W}_{l}\phi(\bm{z}_{l-1})+\bm{b}_{l},\quad\text{for }2\leqslant l\leqslant L,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{z}}_{0}\coloneqq\bm{x}\in[0,1]^{d},\quad\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1}\coloneqq\widehat{\bm{W}}_{1}\widehat{\bm{z}}_{0}+\widehat{\bm{b}}_{1},\quad\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l}\coloneqq\widehat{\bm{W}}_{l}\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l-1})+\widehat{\bm{b}}_{l},\quad\text{for }2\leqslant l\leqslant L,

The network outputs are then
g(\bm{x})=\bm{z}_{L},\widehat{g}(\bm{x})=\widehat{\bm{z}}_{L}
. Firstly, we prove by induction that

\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})\|_{\infty}\leqslant d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}B)^{l}M^{l-1},\quad 1\leqslant l\leqslant L-1.

For the base case
l=1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\|\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1}\|_{\infty}+|\phi(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left(\|\widehat{\bm{W}}_{1}\|_{1,\infty}\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}+\|\widehat{\bm{b}}_{1}\|_{\infty}\right)+|\phi(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}(d+1)B+|\phi(0)|\leqslant 2\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}(d+1)B\leqslant 4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}dB,

For inductive steps, assume that for
k\leqslant l
, the inequality
\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{k})\|_{\infty}\leqslant d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}B)^{k}W^{k-1}
holds. For
k=l+1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l+1})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left(\|\widehat{\bm{W}}_{l+1}\|_{1,\infty}\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})\|_{\infty}+\|\widehat{\bm{b}}_{l+1}\|_{\infty}\right)+|\phi(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left(d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}B)^{l}M^{l-1}\times(MB)+B\right)+|\phi(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left(d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}M)^{l}B^{l+1}+B\right)\leqslant d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}B)^{l+1}M^{l}.

Thus, by induction, the bound holds for all
1\leqslant l\leqslant L-1
.

We now bound the error between two networks. In particular, we will prove by induction that

\|\bm{z}_{l}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 2d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}MB)^{l-1}\varrho.

For the base case
l=1
, we have:

\displaystyle\|\bm{z}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{1}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|(\bm{W}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{W}}_{1})\bm{x}+(\bm{b}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{b}}_{1})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\bm{W}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{W}}_{1}\right\|_{1,\infty}\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}+\|\bm{b}_{1}-\widehat{\bm{b}}_{1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant(d+1)\varrho<2dM\varrho.

For inductive steps, assume that for
k\leqslant l
, the inequality
\|\bm{z}_{l}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l}\|_{\infty}\leqslant(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}})^{l}M^{l}B^{l-1}\varrho
holds. For
k=l+1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{z}_{l+1}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l+1}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{W}_{l+1}\phi(\bm{z}_{l})+\bm{b}_{l+1}-(\widehat{\bm{W}}_{l+1}\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})+\widehat{\bm{b}}_{l+1})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\bm{W}_{l+1}\right\|_{1,\infty}\left\|\phi(\bm{z}_{l})-\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\bm{W}_{l+1}-\widehat{\bm{W}}_{l+1}\right\|_{1,\infty}\left\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\bm{b}_{l+1}-\widehat{\bm{b}}_{l+1}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant(MB)\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\left\|\bm{z}_{l}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l}\right\|_{\infty}+(M\varrho)\left\|\phi(\widehat{\bm{z}}_{l})\right\|_{\infty}+\varrho,

\displaystyle\leqslant(MB)\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\times 2d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}MB)^{l-1}\varrho+(M\varrho)\times d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}B)^{l}M^{l-1}+\varrho

\displaystyle\leqslant 2d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}MB)^{l}\varrho.

Thus, by induction, the approximation bound holds for all
1\leqslant l\leqslant L
. Then by choosing
\varrho=\frac{\tau}{2d(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}MB)^{L}}
, we have

\|g-\widehat{g}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}\leqslant\|\bm{z}_{L}-\widehat{\bm{z}}_{L}\|_{\infty}\leqslant(4\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}})^{L}M^{L}B^{L-1}\varrho\leqslant\tau.

The total number parameters for
g,\widehat{g}
is given by

\displaystyle P=(Md+M)+\sum_{l=2}^{L-1}(M^{2}+M)+(1\cdot M+1)=(L-2)M^{2}+(L+d)M+1\leqslant 2(L+d)M^{2}.

Therefore, the covering number is bounded by

\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M,B),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty})\leqslant\left(\frac{2B}{\varrho}\right)^{P}\leqslant\left(\frac{4^{L+1}d(\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}M)^{L}B^{L+1}}{\tau}\right)^{2(L+d)M^{2}},

which implies that

\log\mathcal{N}(\tau,\mathcal{H}^{\phi,L}(d,1,M,B),\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty})\leqslant 2(L+d)M^{2}\log\left(\frac{4^{L+1}d(\|\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}M)^{L}B^{L+1}}{\tau}\right),

as stated in (
52
). This concludes the proof.
∎

In this section, we establish the lower bounds on the approximation error for finite-depth neural networks employing non-smooth activation functions. Additionally, we detail the experimental setup used to compare the generalization error of smooth versus non-smooth activation functions.

D.1
Proof of Proposition
6.1
(ReLU Lower Bound)

We derive an
L^{2}([0,1])
lower bound for approximating functions in the Sobolev ball

\{f:\|f\|_{W^{s,\infty}([0,1])}\leqslant 1\}
by constant-depth ReLU networks. The proof consists of two parts:
(i) a direct piecewise-linear lower bound based on the quadratic test function

f^{\star}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}
, and (ii) a general lower bound for ReLU networks.
While a similar argument for the first part appears in
77
, we present a self-contained and clarified proof for completeness.

Step 1: A local
L^{2}
lower bound for linear approximation of
x^{2}
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a closed interval of length
l
, and let
h
be any linear function. Then

\int_{I}(x^{2}-h(x))^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{180}\,l^{5}.

(61)

Proof.

Let
I=[u,v]
with
l=v-u
and midpoint
m=(u+v)/2
. Define
e(x)=h(x)-x^{2}
. With the shift

t=x-m
, since
h
is linear we may write
e(t+m)=-t^{2}+at+b
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{I}(e(x))^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}(-t^{2}+at+b)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{l/2}\Big(t^{4}+(a^{2}-2b)t^{2}+b^{2}\Big)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{l^{5}}{80}+\frac{l^{3}}{12}a^{2}-\frac{l^{3}}{6}b+lb^{2}.

Completing the square in
b
gives

\int_{I}(e(x))^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{l^{5}}{80}-\frac{l^{5}}{144}+\frac{l^{3}}{12}a^{2}+l\Big(b-\frac{l^{2}}{12}\Big)^{2}\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{180}l^{5},

which proves (
61
).
∎

Step 2: A lower bound for piecewise-linear approximators.

We next derive a quantitative lower bound for approximating the quadratic target using Step 1.

Lemma D.2

(Lower bound for approximating
x^{2}
by ReLU networks)
.

Let
f^{\star}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}
on
[0,1]
. For any depth
L\geqslant 2
and width

,

\inf_{g\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{ReLU},L}(1,1,M,\infty)}\|g-f^{\star}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{12\sqrt{5}}\,(M+1)^{-2(L-1)}.

Proof.

Any ReLU network
g
is a continuous piecewise linear function on
[0,1]
. Therefore there exists
a partition
\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
of
[0,1]
into intervals such that
g
is linear on each
I_{j}
.
In one dimension, each hidden layer of width
M
can introduce at most
M
new breakpoints within
each interval produced by the previous layers, so the total number of linear pieces satisfies

K\;\leqslant\;(M+1)^{L-1}.

(62)

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{K}|I_{j}|=1
and
x\mapsto x^{5}
is convex, Jensen’s inequality yields

\sum_{j=1}^{K}|I_{j}|^{5}\;\geqslant\;K\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|I_{j}|\Big)^{5}\;=\;\frac{1}{K^{4}}.

(63)

On each interval
I_{j}
,
g
is linear, so applying Lemma
D.1
and scaling by
(1/2)^{2}

gives

\int_{I_{j}}\big(f^{\star}(x)-g(x)\big)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{4}\int_{I_{j}}\big(x^{2}-\widetilde{h}_{j}(x)\big)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{720}|I_{j}|^{5},

for some linear
\widetilde{h}_{j}
(namely
\widetilde{h}_{j}=2g|_{I_{j}}
). Summing over
j
and using
(
62
)–(
63
), we obtain

\|g-f^{\star}\|_{L^{2}([0,1])}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{I_{j}}\big(g(x)-f^{\star}(x)\big)^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{720}\sum_{j=1}^{K}|I_{j}|^{5}\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{720}\,K^{-4}\;\geqslant\;\frac{1}{720}\,(M+1)^{-4(L-1)}.

Taking square roots proves the claim.
∎

Step 3: A lower bound for ReLU networks.

The following is a specialization of the lower bound in
40
and
63

Lemma D.3

(

63

Fix

Proof of Proposition

By Lemma

D.2
Setup for Generalization Experiments

In this section, we describe the experimental setup of the generalization separation in detail.

Data generation and target function.

We consider a target
f^{\star}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

f^{\star}(\bm{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\cos(\bm{w}_{k}^{\top}\bm{x}+b_{k}).

Throughout, we fix the input dimension
d=5
and the number of random Fourier features
K=50
. The parameters are sampled independently as

•

\bm{w}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
,

•

b_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)
,

•

a_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

For a given sample size
n
, the training dataset
\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
is generated by sampling
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{d})
and setting

y_{i}=f^{\star}(\bm{x}_{i})+\xi_{i},\qquad\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

with
\sigma=0.1
. We evaluate generalization performance across sample sizes

n\in\{1024,1448,2048,2896,4096,5792\}.

Model architecture.

We use a fully connected network with a single hidden layer to compare different activation functions. The hidden width is fixed to
M=6000
for all experiments. We compare the non-smooth ReLU activation with two smooth activations, namely GELU and
\tanh
.

Training and hyperparameter tuning.

We minimize the empirical mean-squared error (MSE)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_{i}-f(\bm{x}_{i})\bigr)^{2},

where
f
denotes the neural network predictor. Optimization is carried out using full-batch Adam for
50{,}000
epochs with a cosine learning-rate decay schedule. For each sample size
n
, we perform a grid search over

•

learning rates
\eta\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}
,

•

L^{2}
regularization coefficients
\lambda\in\{10^{-5},5\times 10^{-5},10^{-4},5\times 10^{-4},10^{-3},5\times 10^{-3},10^{-2},5\times 10^{-2},10^{-1}\}
.

The hyperparameter pair
(\eta,\lambda)
is selected by the smallest generalization error on a noiseless test set. We repeat the entire procedure over
5
independent runs and report the average of the resulting best generalization errors. This protocol mitigates sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and yields a robust estimate of the empirical convergence behavior.
