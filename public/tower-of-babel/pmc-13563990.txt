Prediction models for curve progression in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: a systematic review with exploratory meta-analysis of discrimination
Authors: Jiayuan Huang, Baodong Wang, Dengfeng Xu, Haotian Chen, Yunyun Xu, Xinjie Chen, Qiang Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPredicting curve progression is central to adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) care, but the robustness and clinical readiness of existing prediction models remain uncertain.ObjectiveTo review AIS progression models across clinically distinct prediction tasks and treatment contexts and, where operational eligibility criteria were met, summarise discrimination using metric-stratified exploratory meta-analyses.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science and Embase were searched to 26 April 2026. Full-text studies developing, validating, updating or evaluating prognostic models for AIS progression were eligible. Data extraction was informed by CHARMS, reporting by TRIPOD, and study-level risk of bias was assessed using PROBAST. Binary-outcome AUCs and survival/time-to-event C-statistics were synthesised separately using random-effects models. A combined cross-metric estimate was examined only as a secondary sensitivity analysis.ResultsForty-eight studies reporting 63 models were included. Models ranged from regression, risk scores and nomograms to machine learning, deep learning, radiomics and multimodal approaches. Thirty-one models reported an AUC or C-statistic, but only 17 provided usable variance information. Six publication-level estimates contributed to the quantitative synthesis. Three binary-outcome AUCs yielded a pooled estimate of 0.794 (95% CI 0.726–0.849; I²=93.9%), whereas three survival/time-to-event C-statistics yielded 0.883 (95% CI 0.850–0.909; I²=0.0%). The two estimates were not directly comparable because the underlying tasks, treatment contexts and outcome structures differed. The secondary combined estimate was 0.839 (95% CI 0.788–0.879; I²=91.8%) and had no single clinical interpretation. Calibration, clinical utility, external validation and implementation reporting were limited. Forty-four of 48 studies (91.7%) were rated at high overall risk of bias.ConclusionsAIS prediction evidence is best interpreted through six clinically distinct prediction tasks. Transportability is constrained by treatment context, outcome definition, skeletal maturity and validation design. The pooled estimates were derived from small selected subsets and should be interpreted as exploratory findings secondary to the task-specific narrative synthesis. Future studies should standardise prediction targets, report calibration and clinical utility, and undertake prospective external validation within the intended care pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10314-0.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a common spinal deformity in young people. In clinical practice, one of the main questions is whether the curve will progress [1, 2]. Some patients deteriorate quickly during growth and may need bracing or surgery. Others remain stable and can be managed with observation alone [1, 3]. Progression is related to skeletal maturity, growth potential, baseline Cobb angle, curve pattern, sex and treatment adherence. These factors rarely act in isolation, which makes risk assessment difficult when based on a single clinical measure [3–6]. In spine clinics, prediction is used at several distinct decision points: choosing an observation interval for patients who are being monitored, identifying when a curve is approaching a bracing-treatment threshold, estimating brace-failure risk during active brace treatment, and monitoring progression toward surgical thresholds. Earlier and more reliable prediction could therefore support pre-treatment risk conversations, observation or bracing triage, brace-response monitoring and timely referral, while reducing unnecessary imaging or intervention [7, 8].

A wide range of models has been proposed to predict AIS progression. These include regression models, risk scores, nomograms, machine learning, deep learning, radiomics and multimodal approaches [6, 9].

Although several models report encouraging discrimination, their clinical usefulness remains uncertain. Studies use different outcome definitions, populations, treatment settings, predictors and validation strategies [7, 8, 10]. These differences matter because the clinical decision being predicted is not always the same. Models developed in untreated or pre-brace cohorts mainly estimate spontaneous or baseline progression risk and may support Risser/Sanders/Lonstein-Carlson-type risk conversations, observation-interval planning, bracing-threshold triage or surgical-threshold monitoring. In contrast, brace-treatment models estimate progression under the modifying effects of brace prescription, brace design, in-brace correction, adherence and individual treatment response, and are more relevant to brace-response or brace-failure monitoring [11, 12]. Therefore, model performance observed in one treatment context may not be transportable to another without targeted validation. External validation is often limited, and calibration, transparency and clinical utility are not consistently reported [13–15]. As a result, models are difficult to compare, and their readiness for clinical use is unclear.

The expansion of AI, machine learning and deep learning has broadened the field further. These methods can use imaging, three-dimensional morphology, surface topography, wearable-sensor data and multimodal inputs, and may capture patterns that conventional models miss [6–10, 16]. Even so, many such models are based on small datasets and remain vulnerable to overfitting. Their interpretability, reporting quality and external reproducibility are also often limited [10, 13, 14, 17]. Whether they provide more reliable or generalisable prediction than traditional models therefore remains unresolved.

This review mapped and evaluated prediction models for AIS curve progression across clinically distinct decision contexts. We summarised model characteristics, predictors, outcomes, validation methods, performance, risk of bias and applicability and, where operational eligibility criteria were met, conducted exploratory meta-analyses of AUC or C-statistic estimates [7, 10, 18]. The review focused on the clinical task supported by each model and on the availability of calibration, clinically actionable thresholds, implementation details and prospective workflow evaluation.

Methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed prediction models for curve progression in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) [7]. The review followed PRISMA 2020 [19]. Data extraction was informed by CHARMS [18], reporting by TRIPOD and TRIPOD + AI [14], and risk of bias and applicability were assessed using the classic PROBAST tool [20], as prespecified in the registered protocol. PROBAST + AI [17] was not applied as a separate formal tool because this would have introduced a post hoc dual-assessment framework across a mixed evidence base. AI-specific concerns, including data provenance, data splitting, possible leakage, preprocessing, model selection, tuning, calibration, independent validation and implementation transparency, were considered where reported and were mapped to the relevant classic PROBAST domains or summarised narratively.

The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261403259). The review question, eligibility criteria, information sources, screening, data extraction, PROBAST assessment and narrative and quantitative synthesis were prespecified. After full-text extraction and before quantitative pooling, the synthesis was refined into six treatment-oriented prediction-task categories because the included studies differed substantially in clinical decision, treatment context, outcome and prediction horizon. Model family and validation strategy were retained as secondary methodological descriptors. The principal protocol-specified components and the major analytic refinements or post hoc exploratory analyses relevant to the final synthesis are summarised in Supplementary Table 4.

Eligibility criteria

We included full-text original studies of AIS patients if they developed, validated, updated or evaluated a prognostic prediction model addressing one or more prespecified AIS progression-related prediction tasks. Because these tasks differed in target population, index date, treatment context, outcome and prediction horizon, eligibility was framed using a PICO-T structure rather than a single undifferentiated “curve progression” endpoint. Eligible prediction tasks included untreated or pre-brace natural-history progression, progression to a bracing-treatment threshold, progression to severe curvature or surgical threshold, brace-treatment failure or progression during bracing, continuous future Cobb-angle or three-dimensional morphology prediction, and short-term or intervention-specific curve-change prediction. The PICO-T framework for each eligible task is shown in Table 1. Studies also had to report at least one performance measure, such as AUC, C-statistic, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, calibration, Brier score or prediction error.

PICO-T framework for eligible AIS progression-related prediction tasks

No restriction was placed on modelling framework. Eligible models included statistical and regression models, risk scores, nomograms, machine-learning or deep-learning models, imaging or radiomics models and multimodal models. We excluded studies of non-AIS populations, studies without prognostic prediction, studies that did not develop or evaluate a model, diagnostic-only studies, conference abstracts, protocols, reviews, editorials, preprints without peer-reviewed full-text publication, publications with insufficient data, and reports without extractable model-level information.

Information sources and search strategy

PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection and Embase were searched from database inception to 26 April 2026, as prespecified in the registered protocol. No language restriction was applied. The databases were selected to provide complementary biomedical, clinical, radiological and multidisciplinary coverage.

The search combined AIS terms with terms for prediction models, prognosis, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, radiomics and progression-related outcomes, including curve progression, bracing or surgical thresholds, brace failure and final Cobb angle. A validated prognosis filter was not used because the review sought to capture heterogeneous statistical, imaging and AI-based prediction studies.

Scopus, IEEE Xplore, the Cochrane Library and dedicated preprint servers were not searched. Backward citation searching was conducted by screening the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews, whereas forward citation tracking was not performed. These choices may have reduced retrieval of engineering-oriented, computer-vision or other emerging technical studies. In total, 943 records were identified: 205 from PubMed, 671 from Web of Science and 67 from Embase. The complete database-specific search strategies, dates, search sets and record counts are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection and data extraction

Records were imported into EndNote and deduplicated. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts and assessed full-text reports using prespecified criteria. Pre-consensus include-versus-exclude decisions were used to calculate percent agreement and unweighted Cohen’s kappa with asymptotic 95% confidence intervals. Disagreements were resolved by discussion; no third-reviewer adjudication was required.

Two reviewers independently extracted study- and model-level data using a standardised form. Study-level items included setting, design, sample size, population, prediction task, index time point, treatment context, outcome definition, prediction horizon and follow-up. Model-level items included predictors, modelling method, validation strategy and performance, including discrimination, calibration, prediction error and clinical-utility measures. For AI/ML/DL and image-based models, additional information on data-splitting level, patient-level independence, leakage safeguards, preprocessing, segmentation, augmentation, feature engineering, tuning, model selection, interpretability and implementation transparency was reviewed where reported. Detailed extraction items are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Potential overlap across publications was assessed using trial or registry names, institution, author group, recruitment period, eligibility criteria, sample size, treatment context, outcome definition and data platform. Overlap was classified as confirmed, suspected, likely independent or unclear. Development and evaluation samples were considered separately, and absence of an explicit overlap statement was not taken as evidence of independence.

Outcomes and model counting

The principal synthesis was organised by PICO-T prediction-task category rather than by a single undifferentiated curve-progression endpoint. Performance was interpreted within each model’s population, treatment context, outcome and prediction horizon. Quantitative synthesis was restricted to AUC or C-statistic estimates with usable variance information.

A distinct model was defined by its prediction target, modelling method and predictor set. Models with different targets, outcomes, curve types, input regions or implementations were counted separately, whereas repeated validation estimates or follow-up time points from the same model were not counted as separate models. All eligible models were retained in the narrative synthesis.

Within each publication, one representative estimate was retained for quantitative synthesis. The model identified by the original authors as final or clinically intended was prioritised; otherwise, selection was based on clinical relevance, reporting completeness, validation independence and variance availability rather than the magnitude of discrimination. Potential dependence across publications was examined separately through source-cohort-restricted sensitivity analyses.

Risk of bias and applicability

Risk of bias and applicability were assessed using classic PROBAST [20]. Risk-of-bias domains were participants, predictors, outcome and analysis; applicability domains were participants, predictors and outcome. Two reviewers independently completed study-level assessments and resolved disagreements through discussion. Pre-consensus reviewer-level PROBAST ratings were not retained in a recoverable format; therefore, valid domain-level or overall Cohen’s kappa statistics could not be calculated retrospectively.

Formal PROBAST assessment was conducted at the study or report level. In multi-model publications, a domain was rated high when at least one reported model or analysis raised a clearly high-risk concern; when no high-risk concern was identified but information was insufficient for a low-risk judgement, the domain was rated unclear. For AI/ML/DL and image-based studies, structured consideration was additionally given to data provenance, patient-level splitting, possible leakage, preprocessing, feature and model selection, hyperparameter tuning, missing-data handling, calibration, internal validation and independent evaluation. Code, trained weights and implementation materials were treated as reproducibility and translation characteristics rather than automatic indicators of bias.

Because the included reports did not consistently provide model-specific signalling-question information, study-level ratings were not propagated to the 63 individual models, and no formal model-level PROBAST distribution was calculated. Model-specific concerns were summarised narratively and described as reporting uncertainty when information was insufficient. All formal PROBAST counts and percentages therefore use the 48 included studies as the denominator.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The primary synthesis was task-specific and narrative. Interpretation prioritised the intended clinical decision and treatment context, followed by outcome definition and prediction horizon; metric type, model family and validation strategy were treated as secondary methodological characteristics. Natural-history progression, progression to a bracing threshold, brace-treatment failure, severe-curve or surgery-related progression, continuous morphology prediction and short-term intervention-specific change were not considered estimates of a common clinical target.

Task-specific quantitative synthesis was considered when at least three publication-level estimates met the operational criteria for the same clinical decision, treatment context, outcome family, prediction horizon and performance metric. Because most task categories did not meet this threshold, they were synthesised narratively. Within the quantitative component, binary-outcome AUCs and survival/time-to-event C-statistics were synthesised separately as the primary metric-specific analyses. The two metrics were not converted into one another and were not compared inferentially. A combined six-estimate cross-metric summary was retained only as a secondary sensitivity analysis and was not interpreted as a common clinical performance parameter.

Quantitative eligibility was assessed using a structured framework covering AIS population, index time point, treatment context, outcome family and threshold, prediction horizon, metric type, variance availability, validation source and estimate independence. Models were not included in the relevant metric-specific synthesis when one or more core eligibility requirements were not met. Participants had to have AIS, and prediction had to precede the target outcome. Binary-outcome models had to predict longitudinal small-to-moderate progression, defined in the eligible studies as a Cobb-angle increase of approximately 5° to 10° and/or progression to a final Cobb angle of at least 25°. Survival models had to predict time to severe curvature or surgery, defined as Cobb angle at least 45° or spinal fusion. Models of brace failure during active treatment, postoperative outcomes, continuous Cobb angle, three-dimensional morphology or short-term intervention response were not included in these metric-specific syntheses.

Eligible estimates had to report a binary AUC or survival/time-to-event C-statistic with a directly reported or reconstructable standard error, variance or 95% confidence interval. One representative estimate was retained per publication. When multiple estimates were available, priority was given to true external or cross-platform validation, independent test-cohort validation, internal split-sample or cross-validation, optimism-corrected resampling and, finally, apparent performance. Selection was not based on the magnitude of discrimination.

Potential cross-publication dependence was assessed through source-cohort auditing. Source-cohort-restricted sensitivity analyses retained no more than one estimate from each linked Wang and Dolan publication cluster, and all four possible representative combinations were examined without reference to effect magnitude. These analyses were performed only for the combined cross-metric dataset because restriction left fewer than three estimates in each metric-specific group.

Selective availability of variance information was examined descriptively among the 31 models reporting an AUC or C-statistic. Models with versus without usable variance information and quantitatively included versus nonpooled models were compared on source-study sample size, model family, validation level and reported discrimination. Formal PROBAST ratings were not included because risk of bias was assessed at study or report level. Continuous variables were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges and categorical variables using counts and percentages; no inferential tests were performed.

Discrimination estimates were transformed to the logit scale and back-transformed for presentation. Standard errors were reconstructed from 95% confidence intervals when necessary. Random-effects models used the DerSimonian-Laird estimator with normal-approximation 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using τ², I² and Cochran’s Q. Secondary analyses examined external or cross-platform validation, estimates from source studies rated at low overall risk of bias, and leave-one-out influence. No meta-regression or inferential subgroup comparison was performed. Model type was not analysed quantitatively because it was completely confounded with metric type. Calibration and clinical-utility results were synthesised narratively.

Statistical software

Analyses were performed in R. Random-effects models were fitted using the metafor package with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator and normal-approximation inference. Cohen’s kappa coefficients and asymptotic 95% confidence intervals for title-and-abstract screening and full-text assessment were calculated from the reviewers’ pre-consensus 2 × 2 classification tables. Metric-stratified primary analyses, secondary cross-metric and validation-strategy analyses, risk-of-bias sensitivity analyses and leave-one-out analyses were also conducted in R.

Results

Study selection

The search yielded 943 records. After deduplication, 757 records underwent title-and-abstract screening and 56 reports underwent full-text assessment. Pre-consensus agreement was 91.8% at title-and-abstract screening (κ = 0.682, 95% CI 0.610–0.754) and 89.3% at full-text assessment (κ = 0.573, 95% CI 0.280–0.865). All disagreements were resolved by discussion. Forty-eight studies reporting 63 prediction models were included (Fig. 1).

Overlap between the selected evaluation samples in the linked Wang and Dolan publication clusters could not be determined; their estimates were retained in the main exploratory analyses and examined in source-cohort-restricted sensitivity analyses.

Variance availability was selective. Models with usable variance arose from larger studies than models without usable variance, were more frequently traditional models and more often used external, cross-platform or independent validation (Table 2). The six quantitatively included models similarly arose from larger studies and more often used independent validation than the 25 nonpooled models. Their median discrimination was modestly higher, although nonpooled estimates ranged from 0.700 to 0.930. Availability bias therefore cannot be excluded, but exclusion from pooling did not simply identify models with poorer reported performance.

Characteristics of included studies and clinical task-specific synthesis

The included studies were published between 2004 and 2026. They were conducted mainly in Canada, Hong Kong/China, the United States, Japan and mainland China, with additional studies from Europe, Korea and multinational cohorts. Sample size ranged from 6 to 2,317 participants, with a median of approximately 168. Several publications represented linked analyses from the same clinical trial, institutional registry or imaging-research programme. The counts of 48 publications and 63 model specifications therefore describe the volume of published studies and distinct reported models rather than 48 fully independent patient cohorts or 63 statistically independent evidence sources. Related publications were grouped by source-cohort cluster in the narrative synthesis and were not interpreted as independent external replication.

Study designs included retrospective and prospective cohorts, validation studies, multicentre cohorts and methodological modelling studies. Treatment settings included untreated natural-history or observation cohorts, routine monitoring cohorts, bracing cohorts, mixed-treatment cohorts and intervention-specific cohorts. These contexts were clinically important because untreated models mainly estimated spontaneous or baseline progression, whereas bracing models estimated progression under the modifying effects of brace indication, brace type, prescribed wear time, adherence, in-brace correction and treatment response. In mixed-treatment cohorts, observed progression may reflect both baseline risk and treatment decisions made during follow-up.

The 63 models used a broad range of methods. Traditional approaches included logistic regression, Cox regression, linear mixed models, discriminant analysis, risk scores and nomograms. AI/ML/DL approaches included random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, support vector or ensemble methods, convolutional neural networks, transformer-based models and capsule networks.

Common predictors included age, sex, baseline Cobb angle, curve pattern, main curve location, skeletal maturity, Risser grade, Sanders stage, bone age, growth measures, brace-wear time and in-brace correction. Other models incorporated radiomics, three-dimensional spinal morphology, surface topography, ultrasound indices, wearable-sensor data, biomarkers, bone mineral density, electromyography or patient-reported outcomes.

Outcome definitions also varied and were treated as task-defining features. Models addressed small Cobb-angle progression thresholds, bracing thresholds such as Cobb ≥ 25° or ≥ 30°, severe-curve or surgical endpoints such as Cobb > 40°, ≥ 45° or ≥ 50°, brace-treatment failure, future or final Cobb angle, three-dimensional deformity change, stable versus progressive status and short-term curve change. These endpoints were mapped to the PICO-T categories before interpretation because they support different clinical decisions and should not be treated as interchangeable. Validation strategies ranged from apparent performance and internal resampling to independent testing, true external validation and cross-platform validation, although the latter two were uncommon. Calibration was rarely reported. Table 3 therefore provides only a concise clinically interpretable summary, while full extraction details are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Natural progression in untreated or observation cohorts was the largest category, comprising 20 models from 16 studies. These models were intended primarily for baseline risk stratification, surveillance planning and pre-treatment counselling. However, progression thresholds ranged from approximately 5° to 10°, follow-up horizons varied, and some cohorts experienced treatment changes during follow-up. Reported AUCs varied across studies, but formal task-specific pooling was not considered clinically defensible because of differences in outcome threshold, prediction horizon, treatment exposure and validation design.

Nine models from seven studies predicted progression to a bracing-treatment threshold, whereas 11 models from nine studies predicted severe curvature, surgical thresholds or spinal fusion. The former category was most relevant to early treatment triage and timing of brace initiation, while the latter addressed high-risk referral and surgical-threshold monitoring. Reported AUCs were approximately 0.74–0.92 for bracing-threshold models and often approximately 0.88–0.93 for severe-curve or surgery-related models when available. These values were not pooled or compared directly because the studies used different Cobb-angle thresholds, maturity criteria, treatment backgrounds, prediction horizons and validation strategies.

Seven models from seven studies predicted brace-treatment failure. These models were interpreted separately from untreated progression models because their predictions were conditioned by brace prescription, brace design, prescribed wear time, adherence, in-brace correction and treatment response. Thirteen models from 10 studies predicted continuous Cobb angle or three-dimensional morphology, and three models from three studies addressed short-term or intervention-specific outcomes. These models commonly reported MAE, RMSE, R², correlation or platform-specific accuracy rather than AUC and were therefore retained in the task-specific narrative synthesis rather than combined with binary or time-to-event discrimination estimates.

Calibration, clinical utility, and model transparency

Calibration and clinical utility were poorly reported. Only 8 of 63 models included any calibration assessment, four presented calibration plots and two reported Brier scores. Only two models evaluated decision curves or net benefit. Thresholds and risk strata were reported by 39 models but were heterogeneous and usually specific to the original study. Consequently, reliable absolute-risk estimation and the clinical value of model-guided decisions could not be established.

Implementation transparency was also limited. Some statistical models provided equations, coefficients, nomograms or decision rules, but complete predictor coding, intercepts, transformations and score-to-risk mappings were often unavailable. Many AI/ML/DL studies lacked sufficient information on patient-level splitting, preprocessing, hyperparameters, calibration, code, trained weights or inference pipelines. These omissions limited reproduction, independent validation and clinical implementation.

Risk of bias and applicability

At study level, the participants domain was rated low risk in 42 of 48 studies (87.5%), predictors in 31 (64.6%), outcome in 47 (97.9%) and analysis in 5 (10.4%). Overall risk of bias was low in 4 studies (8.3%) and high in 44 (91.7%). No risk-of-bias domain or overall judgement was rated unclear. Overall applicability concern was low in 17 studies (35.4%) and high in 31 (64.6%); domain-specific results are shown in Fig. 2.

High risk in the analysis domain was mainly associated with small samples or few events, unclear missing-data handling, weak feature selection, inadequate control of overfitting, sparse calibration, limited independent validation and incomplete performance reporting. For AI/ML/DL models, additional concerns included patient-level data separation, leakage across repeated observations, preprocessing relative to data partitioning, tuning and model-selection procedures.

Formal PROBAST results are reported only at study or report level. Study-level ratings were not propagated to the 63 individual models because model-specific signalling-question information was often insufficient. Model-specific concerns were retained narratively but do not constitute formal PROBAST classifications. Pre-consensus reviewer-level PROBAST ratings were unavailable, so chance-corrected agreement could not be calculated.

Model performance and narrative synthesis

Performance reporting was heterogeneous. Thirty-one models reported an AUC or C-statistic, with additional studies reporting accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, predictive values or prediction error. Direct comparison was limited by differences in clinical task, treatment context, outcome definition, prediction horizon and validation design. Discrimination did not establish reliable absolute-risk estimation because calibration was seldom assessed. Apparent and internally validated estimates were considered more vulnerable to optimism than independent, external or cross-platform estimates.

Traditional models were generally more interpretable but were often not externally validated. AI/ML/DL models used imaging, three-dimensional morphology, surface topography, sensor or multimodal data, but commonly had small samples, complex pipelines, limited transparency and insufficient independent validation. Models unsuitable for meta-analysis remained in the task-specific narrative synthesis. No model had sufficient evidence to support routine clinical implementation.

Combining all six estimates irrespective of metric type yielded a secondary pooled estimate of 0.839 (95% CI 0.788–0.879; I²=91.8%; τ²=0.133). This estimate has no single clinical estimand and was not used as the principal quantitative conclusion. Discussion

The principal contribution of this review is a six-category clinical taxonomy that organises AIS prediction models according to the decision they are intended to support: untreated or observation-based natural progression, progression to a bracing-treatment threshold, progression to severe curvature or surgery, brace-treatment failure, continuous future Cobb-angle or three-dimensional morphology prediction, and short-term or intervention-specific curve-change prediction. These tasks occur at different stages of the AIS care pathway and should not be interpreted as interchangeable manifestations of a single “curve progression” outcome.

Recent AIS prediction studies illustrate these distinctions. Chu et al. [6] and Fang et al. [8] developed models using imaging and clinical information in settings related to early assessment or curve-progression prediction. Ohyama et al. [9] evaluated progression using longitudinal imaging information, whereas Smith et al. [11] and Dolan et al. [12] addressed prognosis and individualised bracing benefit in BrAIST-related populations. Parent et al. [38] focused on predicting future untreated curve angle across growth, and Khodaei et al. [47] investigated prognostic parameters for curve progression. These studies are clinically relevant but address different populations, treatment contexts, outcomes and decision points. They should therefore be interpreted as complementary task-specific contributions rather than independent replications of one common prediction problem.

Transportability across these tasks cannot be inferred from discrimination alone. Treatment context determines whether a model estimates untreated natural-history risk, risk before brace initiation, progression during active brace treatment or progression under mixed conservative management. Outcome definition determines whether the model is intended to support surveillance, bracing-threshold triage, brace-failure monitoring or surgical-risk referral. Skeletal maturity influences the underlying growth-related risk and the time available for progression, while validation design determines whether reported performance reflects internal reproducibility or transportability to another institution, imaging platform, population or care pathway.

These dimensions interact. A model developed in skeletally immature untreated patients should not automatically be applied to actively braced patients, and a model predicting a 5° increase should not be treated as a surrogate for progression to 45°, 50° or surgery. Similarly, a high internally validated AUC does not establish performance in populations with different Risser or Sanders distributions, brace-prescription thresholds, follow-up schedules or imaging protocols. Clinical applicability therefore requires validation within the specific prediction task, maturity stage and treatment environment in which the model will be used.

The quantitative synthesis was secondary to this task-specific clinical interpretation. Three binary-outcome AUCs yielded a pooled estimate of 0.794, whereas three survival/time-to-event C-statistics yielded a pooled estimate of 0.883. Both analyses included only three publication-level estimates. The two results should not be compared directly because metric type was completely confounded with model family in the quantitatively analysable subset: all binary AUCs originated from AI/ML/DL models, whereas all survival/time-to-event C-statistics originated from traditional models. The groups also differed in prediction task, outcome severity, treatment context and validation design. The numerical difference therefore cannot be attributed independently to metric type, modelling approach or predictive superiority.

The combined six-estimate cross-metric analysis yielded a discrimination estimate of 0.839. However, this value does not represent a common clinical estimand because it combines binary AUCs and survival/time-to-event C-statistics from clinically distinct prediction tasks. It should not be interpreted as the average performance of AIS prediction models, a field-level benchmark or evidence that the included metrics are clinically interchangeable. Its role is limited to a secondary sensitivity analysis showing the numerical result obtained when all six quantitatively analysable estimates are combined.

Selective availability of variance information may also have influenced the quantitative results. Models with usable variance information arose from larger source studies, were more frequently traditional models and more often used external, cross-platform or independent validation than models without usable variance. The six quantitatively included models also arose from larger studies and more often used independent validation than the nonpooled models. Their median reported discrimination was modestly higher. These findings suggest that the quantitative subset may be enriched for methodologically more mature and more completely reported models.

This pattern should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The nonpooled group contained a wide range of reported performance, including several estimates higher than those in the pooled subset, and quantitative eligibility was not determined by the magnitude of the AUC or C-statistic. Availability bias is therefore more likely to have arisen because mature validation studies were more likely to report confidence intervals, standard errors or other variance information required for meta-analysis. Consequently, the metric-specific pooled estimates may not represent all AIS prediction models, particularly newer or less completely reported AI/ML/DL models.

Dependence across publications was another important consideration. Several reports arose from the same clinical trial, institutional registry or imaging-research programme. The two Chalmers publications used the same brace-treatment development and test samples. The Wang publications arose from a linked and expanded Hong Kong imaging programme, and the Dolan publications shared BrAIST-derived development data. Publication and model counts may therefore overstate the number of independent patient evidence streams when related reports are interpreted separately.

The patient-level relationship between the specific evaluation samples in the Wang and Dolan publication clusters could not be established from the published reports. The original six publication-level estimates were therefore retained, but the analyses were interpreted as potentially affected by residual dependence. Source-cohort-restricted sensitivity analyses yielded combined estimates ranging from 0.825 to 0.846, close to the original combined estimate of 0.839. This supports numerical stability within the available dataset but does not establish patient-level independence or remove the conceptual heterogeneity of the underlying prediction tasks.

The methodological quality of the evidence was limited. PROBAST rated 44 of 48 studies as having an overall high risk of bias, mainly because of concerns in the analysis domain. Common problems included small sample size, few outcome events, unclear handling of missing data, weak variable selection, inadequate control of overfitting, sparse calibration, limited external validation and incomplete performance reporting. These problems can produce overly optimistic estimates, particularly in small datasets and complex AI/ML/DL models. This pattern is consistent with broader machine-learning prediction-model research, in which high risk of bias has been associated with small samples, inadequate handling of missing data and insufficient control of overfitting [63]. The limitations observed in AIS prediction models therefore reflect a wider challenge in developing robust, reproducible and clinically trustworthy prediction tools.

Formal PROBAST assessment in this review was conducted at the study or report level. The included reports did not consistently provide sufficient model-specific signalling-question information to support defensible formal ratings for all 63 models, particularly in publications reporting multiple model specifications. Study-level judgements were therefore not propagated to individual models, and no formal model-level low-, high- or unclear-risk distribution was calculated. Model-specific concerns were summarised narratively when sufficient information was available, but these observations should not be interpreted as formal PROBAST classifications.

Classic PROBAST was retained as the formal risk-of-bias tool because it was prespecified in the registered protocol and could be applied consistently across the mixed evidence base. A separate formal PROBAST + AI assessment was not introduced during revision. Nevertheless, AI-specific concerns highlighted by PROBAST + AI were considered through structured extraction and interpretation of data provenance, data-unit definition, patient-level independence, train-tune-test separation, preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, model selection, leakage safeguards, missing-data handling, calibration, independent validation and implementation transparency.

This structured appraisal did not identify a credible subgroup of AI/ML/DL models that warranted a more favourable clinical interpretation than that derived from classic PROBAST. Instead, it reinforced the same overall concerns, including limited independent validation, incomplete reporting of leakage safeguards and model-selection procedures, sparse calibration and poor implementation reproducibility. Although a formal parallel PROBAST + AI assessment might have produced more granular domain-level classifications, the available evidence did not suggest that it would materially alter the principal review-level conclusion regarding limited clinical readiness.

The credibility of reported performance also depended strongly on validation strategy. Apparent performance is the least reliable because the same data are used for model development and evaluation. Split-sample validation and hold-out testing provide some separation but may still share the same institution, clinicians, imaging protocol, patient spectrum and treatment practice. Cross-validation, bootstrapping and jackknife resampling are useful for assessing internal reproducibility and correcting optimism, but they remain internal procedures and cannot establish transportability to another clinical setting.

Independent testing provides stronger evidence when the test set is kept fully separate from model development, but it should not automatically be equated with true external validation when it comes from the same institution, registry or imaging workflow. True external validation requires evaluation in a distinct cohort, preferably from another institution, region, time period or clinical care setting. For imaging and AI/ML/DL models, cross-platform validation is especially important because performance may be affected by radiographic acquisition, image quality, landmark annotation, segmentation, scanner or imaging system and preprocessing procedures. High AUC values derived only from apparent or internal validation should therefore be interpreted cautiously, consistent with broader evidence that inadequate validation and insufficient control of overfitting can produce results that do not transport to new settings [63].

Most studies focused on discrimination, whereas calibration, clinical utility and implementation transparency were seldom addressed. Discrimination reflects the ability to rank patients by relative risk, whereas calibration describes agreement between predicted probabilities and observed progression. These concepts are not interchangeable. A model may correctly rank two patients while providing inaccurate absolute-risk estimates for both. A predicted 30% risk is clinically meaningful only when approximately 30 of 100 comparable patients experience the outcome.

This distinction has practical consequences in AIS care. Systematic overestimation may lead to unnecessary short-interval radiographs, earlier or more intensive bracing, increased anxiety and potentially avoidable referral. Underestimation may delay closer surveillance, brace initiation or surgical-risk assessment in patients who are genuinely at high risk. Only 8 of the 63 models reported any calibration assessment, and only two assessed decision curves or net benefit. Without calibration, it remains uncertain whether predicted probabilities correspond to true progression risk at clinically relevant thresholds. Without clinical-utility analysis, it is also unclear whether model-guided care would improve decisions compared with usual clinical assessment.

Future validation studies should therefore report calibration curves, calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope and Brier score and, where appropriate, recalibration in external populations. Calibration should also be examined within clinically meaningful subgroups defined by skeletal maturity, baseline Cobb angle, curve pattern, treatment context and prediction horizon. Decision-curve analysis or net benefit should be used to evaluate whether model-guided management improves decisions at thresholds relevant to observation, bracing or surgical referral.

Implementation transparency was similarly inconsistent. Some statistical models provided equations, coefficients, nomograms or decision rules, whereas others lacked complete predictor coding, intercepts, transformations or score-to-risk mappings. Many AI/ML/DL models did not provide code, trained weights, complete parameter settings or reproducible inference pipelines. These omissions limit reproduction, independent validation and clinical implementation.

The value of interpretable and externally testable inputs is illustrated by Parent et al. and Khodaei et al. [38, 47]. Parent et al. used prior untreated radiographs to predict future curve angle across growth, whereas Khodaei et al. identified and validated prognostic parameters for curve progression. These studies are not automatically ready for clinical adoption, but they illustrate an important translational principle: models are easier to reproduce, validate and implement when inputs are clinically meaningful, clearly defined and measurable outside the original development setting. In contrast, models that depend on poorly described preprocessing, unavailable trained weights, proprietary inference procedures or unclear input formats remain difficult to test independently, even when reported discrimination is promising.

AI/ML/DL models nevertheless remain promising because they can combine imaging, three-dimensional morphology, surface topography, smartphone photographs, sensor data and multimodal features and may identify nonlinear patterns that simpler models miss. This direction is consistent with recent discussions of AI-based risk prediction, digital twins and multimodal artificial intelligence in spine care, which emphasise both the potential of integrated data-driven modelling and continuing barriers involving validation, usability, workflow integration and impact evaluation [64, 65].

However, the current evidence base remains immature. Many studies used small samples, complex architectures and incomplete reporting of tuning procedures. Apparent performance, cross-validation or single-centre hold-out testing may overstate generalisability when development and testing share the same imaging workflow, annotation style, population spectrum or treatment practice. Image-level, curve-level or visit-level splitting may also produce optimistic performance when repeated observations from the same patient are allocated to different data partitions. Similarly, preprocessing, landmark extraction, augmentation, feature selection or hyperparameter tuning performed before data splitting or with access to validation data can introduce leakage.

Comparable implementation challenges have been described across clinical AI, where promising algorithmic performance frequently fails to translate into clinical impact without attention to workflow integration, generalisability, usability, safety, governance and prospective evaluation [66]. These issues are especially relevant in AIS because performance may vary across institutions, radiographic positioning and acquisition protocols, surface-topography platforms, ultrasound or MRI protocols, brace types, brace-wear monitoring, surgical-referral thresholds and skeletal-maturity distributions.

Treatment context is also particularly important for AI/ML/DL models because these systems may learn patterns associated not only with spinal morphology but also with local treatment pathways, brace-prescription thresholds, follow-up schedules, adherence monitoring and imaging timing. A model trained in a bracing cohort with high adherence or strong in-brace correction may perform differently in a cohort with different brace practices or lower adherence, even when baseline radiographs appear similar. SHAP, Grad-CAM and feature-importance methods may help explain model behaviour, but they cannot substitute for transparent reporting, calibration, independent testing and genuine external or cross-platform validation.

From a clinical perspective, existing AIS progression models should be regarded as promising research tools rather than ready-to-use decision aids. Neither the primary metric-specific estimates nor the secondary combined cross-metric estimate changes this interpretation. A relatively high discrimination estimate does not demonstrate accurate calibration, clinically beneficial decision thresholds, transportability across populations and treatment settings or improvement in patient outcomes.

Clinical management involves decisions concerning observation, follow-up intervals, bracing, brace failure, referral and surgical-risk monitoring. These decisions require reliable absolute-risk estimates and evidence that performance is maintained outside the development environment. A model developed to estimate untreated progression should not be used to monitor brace failure, and a brace-treatment model should not be used to estimate spontaneous progression unless it has been validated and, where necessary, recalibrated in the target treatment context. Most available models have not met these requirements, and no model can currently be recommended for broad direct use in clinical decision-making.

The translational gap is therefore not simply a matter of improving AUC. High technical performance does not necessarily produce clinical benefit unless a model is usable, trusted, integrated into workflow and prospectively evaluated within the intended care pathway [64–66]. Clinicians need to know whether predicted probabilities are calibrated, whether using the model improves decisions compared with usual care and which threshold should trigger observation, intensified follow-up, brace initiation, brace-failure monitoring or surgical-risk referral.

Models also need to be implementable at the point of care. Statistical models should provide complete equations, coefficients, intercepts, nomograms, calculators and guidance for missing predictors. AI/ML/DL models should provide sufficiently detailed input requirements, preprocessing procedures, code, trained weights or reproducible inference pipelines when feasible. Prospective validation should evaluate performance within real workflows, including radiograph scheduling, brace prescription, adherence monitoring, referral pathways, clinician interpretation and communication with patients and families.

Future studies should define prediction targets precisely and distinguish natural progression, progression to a bracing threshold, severe curvature or surgery, brace-treatment failure, continuous future Cobb angle and short-term intervention-related change. The intended treatment context should be specified at model development, including untreated observation, pre-brace risk assessment, active bracing, post-brace monitoring or mixed-treatment follow-up.

Model development should use prespecified analysis plans and report sample-size considerations, missing-data methods, variable selection, overfitting control, hyperparameter tuning and internal validation, in line with broader concerns regarding bias and reporting quality in machine-learning prediction-model research [63]. Validation reporting should clearly distinguish apparent performance, hold-out testing, cross-validation, bootstrapping or jackknife resampling, independent testing, true external validation and cross-platform validation. Multicentre external validation should be prioritised, particularly for image-based and AI/ML/DL models, and validation cohorts should differ meaningfully from the development cohort in institution, geography, time period, imaging protocol, patient spectrum, curve severity, skeletal maturity and treatment context.

For clinical translation, future models should move beyond discrimination and report calibration, Brier score, decision-curve analysis, net benefit and clinically usable thresholds linked to specific care decisions. Studies should explain the handling of missing predictors and provide sufficient implementation information, including coefficients, equations, nomograms, calculators, source code, trained weights, preprocessing pipelines or model cards where feasible. Finally, prospective evaluation should determine whether model-guided care changes clinician decisions, improves risk communication, reduces unnecessary imaging or treatment, supports timely bracing or referral and improves patient outcomes without creating avoidable burden.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the included studies varied substantially in prediction task, outcome definition, treatment context, skeletal-maturity stage, predictors, prediction horizon and validation strategy. We addressed this heterogeneity by organising the principal synthesis according to six clinically oriented PICO-T task categories and by analysing binary-outcome AUCs and survival/time-to-event C-statistics separately in the primary quantitative analyses. Nevertheless, residual heterogeneity remained even within the same broad task category because studies used different progression thresholds, treatment milestones and follow-up periods. The DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model quantified statistical dispersion but could not resolve these conceptual differences. The combined six-estimate cross-metric analysis was therefore retained only as a secondary sensitivity analysis and does not represent a common clinical performance parameter.

Second, the final six-category clinical taxonomy and detailed operational eligibility matrix were not specified in detail in the original PROSPERO registration. They were developed as analytic refinements after full-text extraction and before quantitative pooling, when the extent of clinical and methodological heterogeneity became apparent. The criteria were applied consistently and documented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, and the conjunctive eligibility rule reduced discretionary selection. However, their post-registration status should be considered when interpreting the task classification and metric-specific quantitative results.

Third, reporting in the original studies was frequently incomplete. Treatment-context information was sometimes insufficient to determine brace type, prescribed wear time, adherence, in-brace correction, treatment response or changes in treatment during follow-up. For AI/ML/DL and image-based models, it was often unclear whether data splitting occurred at the patient, image or visit level; whether repeated observations could have crossed training and testing sets; or whether preprocessing, feature selection, augmentation and hyperparameter tuning were fully separated from validation data. These limitations reduced our ability to distinguish genuine model performance from the effects of treatment-related risk modification, overfitting or possible data leakage.

Classic PROBAST was retained as the formal risk-of-bias tool because it was prespecified in the protocol and could be applied consistently across traditional statistical and AI/ML/DL studies. A separate formal PROBAST + AI assessment was not performed. AI-specific concerns relating to data provenance, patient-level separation, leakage, preprocessing, feature and model selection, tuning, calibration, validation and implementation were instead addressed through structured extraction, interpretation of the classic PROBAST domains and narrative appraisal. This approach captured the main AI-specific concerns relevant to the overall conclusions but did not provide a complete domain-by-domain PROBAST + AI audit trail. Individual AI models might therefore have received different or more granular domain classifications under a formal PROBAST + AI assessment.

Fourth, formal PROBAST assessment was conducted at the study or report level rather than separately for all 63 models. Multi-model publications did not consistently provide sufficient information to adjudicate every signalling question independently for each model. Study-level ratings were therefore not propagated to individual models, and no formal model-level low-, high- or unclear-risk distribution was calculated. This avoids overstating model-level certainty but means that methodological differences among models reported within the same publication may be obscured by the conservative study-level summary. The sensitivity analysis restricted to estimates from studies rated at low overall risk of bias should likewise be interpreted as a source-study analysis rather than evidence of model-specific robustness.

Although chance-corrected agreement was calculated for title-and-abstract screening and full-text assessment, the original pre-consensus reviewer-level PROBAST ratings were not retained in a recoverable format. Valid Cohen’s kappa coefficients for individual PROBAST domains or the overall risk-of-bias judgement could therefore not be calculated retrospectively. Agreement was not reconstructed from final consensus ratings because doing so would have artificially inflated reliability. The chance-corrected reproducibility of the PROBAST judgements consequently remains unknown.

Fifth, quantitative synthesis was possible for only six publication-level estimates, with three estimates in each primary metric-specific analysis. Variance information was available for only 17 of the 31 models reporting an AUC or C-statistic. Models with usable variance information, particularly those included in the quantitative analyses, tended to arise from larger studies and more often used external, cross-platform or independent validation. Their median reported discrimination was also modestly higher. The pooled subset may therefore overrepresent methodologically mature and more completely reported models and may not reflect the full range of AIS prediction models.

Formal study-level PROBAST ratings were not included in this model-level availability comparison because some studies contributed multiple models and assigning the same study-level rating to each model would have introduced repeated use of the same judgement. Several nonpooled models also reported discrimination estimates higher than those in the pooled subset. The direction and magnitude of any performance-related availability bias therefore remain uncertain. These comparisons were descriptive, involved small groups and were not supported by inferential testing.

We did not contact the original investigators to request missing variance, calibration or implementation information. Potentially eligible models could therefore not enter the quantitative synthesis when variance information was unavailable or could not be reconstructed from the published report. Author contact might have recovered additional data and altered the composition of the quantitative subset. Residual availability and reporting bias cannot be excluded, and the pooled estimates should not be interpreted as representative of all AIS prediction models.

Sixth, patient-population overlap across publications could not always be determined because recruitment dates, registry identifiers and patient-level flow information were incompletely reported. Separate publications were not assumed to represent independent samples. Confirmed or potentially linked publications were grouped through a source-cohort audit, but residual dependence may remain within the Wang and Dolan publication clusters contributing to the quantitative analyses. Source-cohort-restricted sensitivity analyses yielded combined estimates close to the original secondary estimate, but each metric-specific group contained only two estimates after restriction and could not be pooled. These analyses reduce but do not eliminate concern about duplicate patient contributions. Accordingly, the 48 publications and 63 model specifications should not be interpreted as 48 independent patient cohorts or 63 independent evidence sources.

Metric type was also completely confounded with model family in the quantitatively analysable subset: all three survival/time-to-event C-statistics were reported by traditional models, whereas all three binary-outcome AUCs were reported by AI/ML/DL models. The groups also differed in prediction task, outcome severity and treatment context. The review could therefore not determine whether their numerical differences reflected metric type, modelling approach, clinical task or study design, and no independently interpretable quantitative comparison between traditional and AI/ML/DL models was possible. In addition, heterogeneity statistics have limited power when only three estimates are available; the absence of observed heterogeneity in the survival/time-to-event group should not be interpreted as evidence of true homogeneity.

Finally, the search was restricted to PubMed, Embase and Web of Science Core Collection with backward reference-list screening. Scopus, IEEE Xplore, the Cochrane Library and dedicated preprint servers were not searched, and forward citation tracking was not performed. The review may therefore have missed engineering-oriented machine-learning, computer-vision, radiomics, surface-imaging or other emerging technical studies. Exclusion of preprints, conference abstracts and reports without adequate full-text model-level information prioritised peer-reviewed and assessable evidence but may also have reduced sensitivity to recently developed models. The distribution of modelling approaches identified in this review should not be regarded as a complete census of the field.

The quantitative analyses focused primarily on discrimination. Calibration, prediction error, decision-curve analysis and net benefit were too sparsely and inconsistently reported for pooling. Together with the small number of quantitative estimates, selective variance reporting, possible cohort dependence and incomplete external validation, these limitations mean that all pooled and sensitivity analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than confirmatory evidence.

Conclusions

AIS prediction models should be interpreted within six clinically distinct prediction tasks rather than as an interchangeable body of curve-progression models. Transportability is constrained by treatment context, outcome definition, skeletal maturity and validation design. The metric-specific pooled estimates were each based on only three publication-level estimates, and the secondary combined estimate had no single clinical interpretation. These findings should not be regarded as field-level performance estimates or evidence of clinical readiness.

Current models remain promising research tools but are not ready for broad clinical implementation. Future studies should prespecify the intended clinical task and prediction horizon, distinguish internal from external validation, report calibration and clinical utility, provide reproducible implementation details and undertake multicentre prospective validation within the intended care pathway.
