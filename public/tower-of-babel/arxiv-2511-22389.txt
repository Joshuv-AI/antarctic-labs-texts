Complexity of Łukasiewicz Modal Probabilistic Logics
Daniil Kozhemiachenko, Igor Sedlár

Abstract
Modal probabilistic logics provide a framework for reasoning about probability in modal contexts, involving notions such as knowledge, belief, time, and action. In this paper, we study a particular family of these logics, extending the modal Łukasiewicz many-valued logic. These logics are shown to be capable of expressing nuanced probabilistic concepts, including upper and lower probabilities. Our main contribution is a 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} -completeness result for two variants of the local consequence problem, providing a precise computational characterisation.

Introduction

Probabilistic logics have been studied and applied in various fields for decades
[
20
,
29
]
. In particular, they provide formal languages that express information about probabilistic models in a compact yet unambiguous way, and they can be used to facilitate and verify reasoning about these models. The
many-valued approach
to probabilistic logic goes back to the work of Hájek et al.
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
, where ‘
\alpha
is probable’ is taken as an
imprecise statement
whose truth value (or truth
degree
) is identified with the probability of
\alpha
. This approach contrasts with the better-known logic of Fagin et al.
[
11
]
, which uses formulas involving linear inequalities over probabilistic terms to represent
precise statements
about probability – statements which are either true or false. Many-valued probabilistic logics are tailored for expressing
imprecise
information about probabilistic models in terms of properties that models do not simply “have” or “not have”, but which models have
to some degree
. A particular example are imprecise comparisons, indicating the degree to which two probabilities differ. Another advantage of the many-valued approach is its simpler syntax and axiomatisation. Nevertheless, as shown in
[
4
]
, the many-valued approach and the linear approach are linked by entailment-preserving translations.

In computer science, artificial intelligence and economics,
modal
probabilistic logics are of particular importance. These logics formalise reasoning about probability in the presence of modal notions such as knowledge, belief, time and action
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
10
]
. In
[
27
]
, a many-valued framework for modal probabilistic logic was introduced by combining modal Łukasiewicz logic
[
22
]
with the propositional probabilistic logics of Hájek et al. Decidability of the basic many-valued modal probabilistic logic and some of its extensions was established by reduction to basic non-probabilistic modal Łukasiewicz logic, which is known to be decidable
[
30
]
. However, the reduction is not polynomial and so it does not by itself yield complexity results for many-valued modal probabilistic logics. This paper makes two contributions to this line of research. First, using the tableaux method, we show that the problem of deciding local consequence over
finitely branching
frames is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete even for a rather expressive extension of the language used in
[
27
]
. We also show that the problem of deciding local consequence over arbitrary frames is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete if we restrict ourselves to a specific fragment of the full language. Secondly, we argue that our framework is sufficiently expressive to formalise a variety of practically relevant concepts and scenarios, including upper and lower probabilities and their imprecise comparisons. These two contributions together establish many-valued modal logics as a viable framework for formalising the interplay between probability and modality.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces our basic semantic structures, probabilistic frames, and provides several examples of these structures. It also discusses their relationship with similar structures proposed in the literature. Section
3
discusses a many-valued modal logic for reasoning about probabilistic frames. This logic is based on a modal extension of
{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}

[
9
]
, which is an expressive combination of Łukasiewicz and product fuzzy logics
[
15
]
. Section
4
shows how our logic can be used to represent and reason about a variety of practically relevant concepts and scenarios. Section
5
establishes our main technical results on the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-completeness of the local consequence problem (i) over finitely branching frames using the full language and (ii) over arbitrary frames using a specific fragment of the full language. Section
6
concludes the paper and lists some tasks left for the future.

Probabilistic frames

Recall that a
finitely additive probability measure
on a Boolean algebra
X

P:X\to[0,1]
such that (i)
P(1_{X})=1

P(x\lor y)=P(x)+P(y)
for all

x\land y=0_{X}
. A probability measure on a finite Boolean algebra is uniquely determined by its values on the atoms of the algebra.

Definition 1

(Probabilistic frames)
.

Let

The set of modal indices

in

Example 3
.

Well-known kinds of probabilistic relational structures also provide examples of probabilistic frames. Example

Example 4
.

The following is a generalisation of probabilistic frames. A

-frame
is a structure

such that

as before. Probabilistic frames correspond to the special case with only one fixed agent index

which can be omitted. Multi-agent probabilistic frames where

is finite,

and

is an equivalence relation correspond to finite semantic knowledge-belief systems of
[

]
, structures that extend finite Harsanyi type spaces
[
23
]
with S5-style knowledge operators. Intuitively, the relation

represents the knowledge state of agent

in a manner similar to Example

Remark 1
.

Probabilistic frames are similar to structures that have appeared in the literature. Aumann’s

[

]
assume that

is closed under countable unions (
\sigma

\mu_{a}(\cdot,E):w\mapsto\mu_{w,a}(E)
is an
S
-measurable mapping from
W
to

. This condition also appears in the definition of

is at least

” for

; see
[
23
,
26
,
32
]
. In this paper we focus only on probability assignments to Boolean events, and so we can use simpler structures. Fagin and Halpern
[

]
use similar
\mathsf{A}
-frames
\langle W,R,{\mathcal{P}}\rangle
with
{\mathcal{P}}(a,w)=\langle W_{a,w},S_{a,w},\mu_{a,w}\rangle
being a probability space for
W_{a,w}\subseteq W
,
S_{a,w}
a Boolean algebra of subsets of
W_{a,w}
containing
W_{a,w}
, and
\mu_{a,w}
a probability measure on

. That is, not only the probability measure but also the space of measurable events can vary from world to world.
2
2

2

We note that Fagin and Halpern’s frames do not require that

be measurable; indeed, such a requirement requires a “common” space of measurable events. Instead, they use inner measures to define probabilities of events of the form “the probability of event

Modal many-valued probabilistic logic

We have seen that probabilistic frames are a general and versatile type of semantic structure. In this section, we introduce a language based on many-valued modal logic for expressing and reasoning about properties of probabilistic frames. We build on the work of Hájek et al.
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
, who focus on variants of the well-known Łukasiewicz fuzzy logic and its combinations with product fuzzy logic. The propositional fragment of our modal language can be seen as the language of
{{\textbf{{\L}}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}
logic, a combination of Łukasiewicz and product logic and one of the most expressive propositional fuzzy languages.

Let
\mathtt{Prop}
be a countably infinite set of propositional variables. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\{\sim,\wedge\}}
be the set of Boolean terms generated via the following grammar:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\{\sim,\wedge\}}\ni\alpha

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\in\mathtt{Prop}\mid{\sim}\alpha\mid(\alpha\wedge\alpha)

We use Greek letters
\alpha,\beta
to range over
\mathcal{L}_{\{\sim,\wedge\}}
.

Definition 2
.

The modal probabilistic language
\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}^{\mathsf{A}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}^{\mathsf{A}}\ni\phi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\mid\neg\phi\mid(\phi\rightarrow\phi)\mid(\phi\bullet\phi)\mid(\phi\rightarrow_{\Pi}\phi)\mid\Box_{a}\phi\mid\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}

for
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}_{\{\sim,\wedge\}}
and
a\in\mathsf{A}
. We will assume a fixed
\mathsf{A}
from now on and omit explicit reference to it in most notation. In what follows, we will call formulas of the form
\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)

probabilistic atoms
.

Convention 1

(Notation)
.

Given
\phi\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
, we use
\mathtt{Prop}(\phi)
to denote the set of propositional variables occurring in
\phi
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{l}(\phi)
denotes the length of
\phi
, i.e., the number of occurrences of symbols in it. Similarly, for
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}

\mathsf{Pr}[\Gamma]
and
\mathcal{l}[\Gamma]
for the set of propositional variables in
\Gamma

R
on

.

Definition 3

(Modal depth)
.

Let

(
\mathcal{d}(\phi)

\mathcal{d}(p)=0

,

(with

Given
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}

\Gamma

\mathcal{d}[\Gamma]

\mathcal{d}[\Gamma]=\max\{\mathcal{d}(\phi)\mid\phi\in\Gamma\}
.

-formula is valid, it suffices to check probability distributions over Boolean terms that can be composed of its variables.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}=\langle W,R,X,\mu,E\rangle
be an SI-model. Define
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathsf{C}}_{\phi}=\langle W,R,2^{2^{\mathtt{Prop}(\phi)}},\mu^{\mathsf{C}},E^{\mathsf{C}}\rangle
s.t.

\displaystyle\forall p\in\mathtt{Prop}:E^{\mathsf{C}}(p)=\{Y\mid p\in Y,~Y\subseteq\mathtt{Prop}(\phi)\}

\displaystyle\forall w\in W,Y\subseteq\mathtt{Prop}(\phi):\mu^{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(\{Y\})=\mu_{w}\bigg(E\bigg(\bigwedge\limits_{p\in Y}\!\!p\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{q\notin Y}\!\!\neg q\bigg)\bigg)

Then
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)=\mathcal{I}_{{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathsf{C}}_{\phi}}(\phi,w)
for every
w\in W
and
\phi\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
.

Proof.

Induction on
\phi
. For the basis case, we let w.l.o.g.
\phi=\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
for some
\alpha\!=\!\bigvee\limits^{n}_{i=1}\tau_{i}
in the DNF each part of which contains all variables of
\phi
. Now,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha),w)\!=\!x
means that
\mu_{w}\left(E\left(\bigvee\limits^{n}_{i=1}\tau_{i}\right)\right)\!=\!x
, i.e.,
\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}\mu_{w}(E(\tau_{i}))\!=\!x
. From here, using the fact that
Y\vDash\tau
iff
Y=\{p\mid p\in\tau\}
, we obtain that
\mu^{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(E^{\mathsf{C}}(\tau_{i}))=\mu_{w}((\tau_{i}))
for every

. Thus,
\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}\mu^{\mathsf{C}}_{w}(E^{\mathsf{C}}(\tau_{i}))=\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}\mu_{w}(E(\tau_{i}))=x

The cases of propositional connectives can be shown by a straightforward application of the induction hypothesis. The case of
\phi=\Box\chi
follows easily since
\langle W,R\rangle
-reducts in
{\mathfrak{M}}
and
{\mathfrak{M}}^{\mathsf{C}}_{\phi}
coincide.
∎

\mathcal{L}_{\Box}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
s.t. only the probabilistic atoms of the form

{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}

ignoring measures
, i.e., treating each

as a

. Namely, given a

{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}

Proposition 2
.

For finite

If

Note that the truth degree of an implication
\phi\rightarrow\psi
is inversely proportional to the truncated difference between the truth degrees of
\phi
and
\psi
.
3
3

3

In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi\to\psi,w)=1-\max\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)-\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\psi,w),0\}
.
Thus,
\phi\rightarrow\psi
can be read as “The truth degree of
\phi
is
not much higher
than the truth degree of
\psi
”. If the truth degree of
\phi
is less or equal to the truth degree of
\psi
,
then the truth degree of
\phi\rightarrow\psi
is
1
. If, on the other hand, the truth degree of
\phi
is higher than the truth degree of
\psi
,
then the truth degree of
\phi\rightarrow\psi
is
1
minus the difference. Similarly, the truth degree of
\phi\leftrightarrow\psi

is inversely proportional to the
absolute difference
between the values of
\phi
and
\psi
. Consequently, we can read
\phi\leftrightarrow\psi
as “The truth degree of
\phi
does not
differ much
from the truth degree of
\psi
”.

Recall that, given a set
{\mathcal{P}}
of probability measures on a Boolean algebra
X
, the
lower
and
upper probability
of
x\in X
, respectively, are defined as
{\mathcal{P}}_{*}(x)=\inf\{\mu(x)\mid\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
and
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(x)=\sup\{\mu(x)\mid\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}\}

(see
[
19
]
and
[
20
, Ch. 2.3]
). Lower and upper probabilities are useful for expressing qualitative uncertainty. For example, the interval
[{\mathcal{P}}_{*}(x),{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(x)]
can be seen as putting lower and upper bounds on an agent’s ignorance concerning the probability of
x
. A crucial feature of the many-valued modal framework is that lower and upper probabilities are directly expressed by formulas of the language. Given a model
{\mathfrak{M}}
and a world
w
, the modal formula
\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
can be seen as a probabilistic term expressing the lower probability of
\alpha
with respect to
{\mathcal{P}}=\{\mu_{w^{\prime}}\mid w^{\prime}\in R_{a}(w)\}
since

\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha),w)=\inf\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha),w^{\prime})\mid w^{\prime}\in R_{a}(w)\}=\inf\{\mu_{w^{\prime}}(E(\alpha))\mid w^{\prime}\in R_{a}(w)\}={\mathcal{P}}_{*}(E(\alpha))\,.

Similarly,
\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
expresses the upper probability of
\alpha
. It follows that, on the epistemic interpretation of
a\in\mathsf{A}
, the formula
\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\leftrightarrow\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
expresses the amount of qualitative certainty of agent
a
concerning
\alpha
. In particular, the truth degree of
\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\leftrightarrow\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
is inversely proportional to the size of the interval
[{\mathcal{P}}_{*}(E(\alpha)),{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(E(\alpha))]
. Let us write
\mathsf{Cert}_{a}(\alpha)
instead of
\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\leftrightarrow\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
. We can use Łukasiewicz implication to express comparisons between the amounts of qualitative uncertainty of two agents. For instance,
\mathsf{Cert}_{a}(\alpha)\to\mathsf{Cert}_{b}(\alpha)
says that
a
is not much more certain about
\alpha
than
b
.
4
4

4

A similar interpretation applies, of course, if we read
a\in\mathsf{A}
as actions. Then
\mathsf{Cert}_{a}(\alpha)
expresses the extent to which action

“fixes” the probability of
\alpha
.

Remark 2
.

Halpern and Pucella
[
19
]
introduce a propositional probabilistic logic suitable for reasoning about upper probabilities. A Halpern-Pucella model is, using our notation, a tuple
\langle W,S,{\mathcal{P}},E\rangle
where
W
is a set,
S
is a Boolean subalgebra of
2^{W}
and
E
is a function from a set of propositional variables to
S
– similarly as in our models – and
{\mathcal{P}}
is a set of probability measures on
S
. The language Halpern and Pucella use is a variant of the non-modal linear inequality language of
[
11
]
with a primitive upper probability operator. Halpern and Pucella provide a sound and complete axiomatisation of their logic and show that the corresponding satisfiability problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete. Marchioni
[
28
]
introduces a propositional many-valued logic for reasoning about upper probabilities in semantic structures derived from Halpern-Pucella models.
5
5

5

Instead of a set of probability measures
{\mathcal{P}}
, Marchioni uses an upper probability measure
\pi:S\to[0,1]
, that is, a function that satisfies certain conditions that allow it to represent an upper probability given by a set of probability measures. The reader is referred to
[
19
,
28
]
for details.
The logic is based on Rational Łukasiewicz logic
[
13
]
and it does not contain modal operators. Marchioni provides a sound and (finitely) strongly complete axiomatisation of his logic, and he shows that the corresponding satisfiability problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Our framework can be seen as a generalisation of both
[
19
]
and
[
28
]
. Halpern-Pucella models are a
\mathsf{KD45}
-like version of our
\mathsf{K}
-like probabilistic models. Instead of having a fixed set of “globally” accessible states giving rise to
{\mathcal{P}}
, we introduce an accessibility relation which expresses the idea that each state has its own set of “locally” accessible states. This difference is well reflected in the comparison of the computational complexity: as shown below, deduction problems in our framework are
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete as opposed to
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete in the case of
[
19
]
and
[
28
]
. Another difference is in the language we are using. Instead of a primitive upper/lower probability operator, we use a combination
\Diamond\mathsf{Pr}/\Box\mathsf{Pr}
of a modal operator and the probability operator
\mathsf{Pr}
.
6
6

6

In addition, our syntax and semantics are both multi-modal, allowing for modelling multiple agents or actions.
The same syntactic approach to representing lower probabilities is taken by Corsi et al.
[
7
]
. Their semantics is a special case of our probabilistic models with only one equivalence accessibility relation (mono-modal
\mathsf{S5}
-style probabilistic models). On the other hand, Corsi et al.
[
7
]
work with a richer language that allows for the inclusion of
\Box
within the scope of
\mathsf{Pr}
. They demonstrate that their modal probabilistic framework can formalise reasoning about probability, belief functions, and lower probabilities within a unified framework. The main results of
[
7
]
are related to complete axiomatisations, and complexity is not studied.

We can also use Łukasiewicz implication to express how actions modify the probability of certain events. For example,
\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\to\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
says that action

cannot decrease
the probability of
\alpha

much
— the truth degree in

1
if
\mu_{w}(E(\alpha))\leq\inf\{\mu_{w^{\prime}}(E(\alpha))\mid w^{\prime}\in R_{a}(w)\}

\alpha
cannot decrease at all, and it is
1
minus the difference otherwise. Similarly,
\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\to\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
says that

cannot
increase
the probability of
\alpha
much. We can write
\mathsf{NoDec}_{a}(\alpha)
instead of
\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\to\Box_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
and, as before, use formulas of the form
\mathsf{NoDec}_{a}(\alpha)\to\mathsf{NoDec}_{b}(\alpha)

a
and

. Similarly for
\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)\to\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
written as
\mathsf{NoInc}_{a}(\alpha)

It is well known that in the presence of the constant
\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}
and the “arithmetic” operators
\bullet

\rightarrow_{\Pi}

\mathbf{q}
for all rational numbers

\mathbf{0.7}\to\Diamond_{a}\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)

if probability of

and has truth degree

otherwise. This means that we may define a restricted version of the “Markovian” modalities of

23

26

) in states where the probability of

and false (has truth degree

) otherwise.
7
7

7

This is only a restricted version since

In addition, if

.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
be finite and

be the least common denominator of the constants in
\Gamma
. Then there is
\Gamma^{\prime}
without constants s.t.
\Gamma
is satisfiable iff
\Gamma^{\prime}
is and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is of polynomial size w.r.t.
\max(\mathcal{l}[\Gamma],n)

As
\Gamma

exists. We can express
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
as follows. For fresh

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(q)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\underbrace{\mathsf{Pr}(q)\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(q)}_{n-1\text{ times}})\leftrightarrow\neg\mathsf{Pr}(q)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}}(q_{m})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathsf{Pr}(q_{m})\leftrightarrow(\underbrace{\mathsf{Pr}(q)\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathsf{Pr}(q)}_{m\text{ times}})

It is clear that

and

(and

, we define

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Pr}(q_{m})

\displaystyle(\heartsuit\phi)^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\heartsuit\phi^{\flat}

\displaystyle(\phi\circ\chi)^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\phi^{\flat}\circ\chi^{\flat}

(
\heartsuit\in\{\neg,\triangle,\Box\}
,
\circ\in\{\rightarrow,\rightarrow_{\Pi},\bullet\}
)

Now let
\mathcal{d}[\Gamma]=\max\{\mathcal{d}(\phi)\mid\phi\in\Gamma\}
and define

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\{\phi^{\flat}\mid\phi\in\Gamma\}\cup\{\Box^{i}\mathbf{\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}}(q)\mid i\in\{0,\ldots,\mathcal{d}[\Gamma]\}\}\cup\bigcup\limits^{\mathcal{d}[\Gamma]}_{i=0}\{\Box^{i}\mathbf{\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}}(q_{m})\mid\mathbf{\nicefrac{{m}}{{n}}}\text{ occurs in~}\Gamma\}

It is clear that
\Gamma^{\flat}
is only polynomially larger than
\Gamma
when
\mathcal{l}(\Gamma)\geq n
.

Now let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be a
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}

\phi\in\Gamma

5
Decidability and complexity

Let us now tackle the complexity of reasoning in
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
. We will consider two fundamental tasks. First,
model-checking
— given a
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
-model
{\mathfrak{M}}
, a state
w\in{\mathfrak{M}}
, and a formula
\phi
, determine the truth degree of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)
. The second task is
finitary entailment
over finitely branching models — given a finite
\Gamma\cup\{\chi\}
, decide whether
\Gamma\models^{\mathsf{fb}}_{\mathsf{Pr}}\chi
.

As expected, model-checking can be done in polynomial time.

Theorem 2
.

Given a finite model
{\mathfrak{M}}=\langle W,R,S,\mu,E\rangle
,
w\in W
, and
\phi\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{Pr}}
, it takes polynomial time w.r.t.
|W|+|S|+\mathcal{l}(\phi)
to determine the truth degree of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi,w)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\phi
. For a basis case of
\phi=\mathsf{Pr}(\alpha)
, we first determine
\|\alpha\|
which takes polynomial time w.r.t.
\mathcal{l}(\phi)+|S|
. Then, using the definition of
\mu_{w}
, we calculate

Conclusion

Building on the work of Hájek et al.
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
25
]
, this paper investigated many-valued logics for reasoning about probability in the presence of modal notions such as knowledge, belief and action. Our main technical result, extending the contributions of
[
27
]
, is that the problem of deciding local consequence over finitely branching frames is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for a rather expressive logic. Over arbitrary frames, it is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete for a specific fragment of the full logic.

Several interesting research problems remain to be tackled in the future. First, we would like to extend our complexity result for
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
beyond finitely branching frames and the result for
\mathbf{K}{{\textbf{{\L}}}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}_{\mathbb{Q}}
to stronger fragments. Interestingly enough, even for some fragments weaker than
{\textbf{K{\L}}}\bm{\Pi}\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
, this would require solving some open problems in many-valued logic. For example,
\mathbf{KP}{{\textbf{{\L}}}}^{\mathsf{Pr}}
(the fragment without
\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}}
and
\rightarrow_{\Pi}
) is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete if first-order
\mathbf{P}{{\textbf{{\L}}}}

[
24
]
is complete with respect to witnessed models. We would also like to extend our results to modal logics stronger than
\mathbf{K}
. Second, we would like to compare the expressivity of our framework with the corresponding fragment of
[
10
]
, especially when it comes to expressing statements about lower and upper probabilities.
