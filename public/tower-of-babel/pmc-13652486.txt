Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor of the psoas muscle presenting as a retroperitoneal mass: a case report

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is an uncommon mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by the proliferation of spindle-shaped fibroblastic and myofibroblastic cells within a variable inflammatory background. Initially described as a pulmonary lesion in 1937, IMT is now recognized as a distinct pathological entity that may arise in both pulmonary and extrapulmonary locations. Advances in molecular and cytogenetic studies have led to its reclassification from a presumed reactive inflammatory process to a neoplasm with intermediate biological behavior, characterized by a risk of local recurrence and a low metastatic potential[1,2].

IMT occurs predominantly in children and young adults, although cases have been reported across all age groups. It can arise in a wide range of anatomical locations, including the lung, liver, abdominopelvic cavity, gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, genitourinary system, and bone. Although the lung has traditionally been considered the most common site of involvement, more recent studies suggest that extrapulmonary IMTs, particularly those arising in the abdominopelvic cavity and liver, may be more frequently encountered in adults[3,4]. To our knowledge, only a small number of psoas muscle IMTs have been reported in the literature, highlighting the exceptional rarity of this location.

The pathogenesis of IMT remains poorly defined. Several associations have been proposed, including previous trauma, immune dysregulation, and IgG4-related disease; however, their causal relationship with IMT remains uncertain. Viral triggers, including HHV-8 and EBV, have been investigated as possible contributors, although definitive evidence remains lacking[4].

The clinical manifestations of IMT are highly variable and are largely determined by the tumor’s size and anatomical location. Patients may present with localized pain, a palpable mass, symptoms related to compression of adjacent structures, or systemic features such as fever, weight loss, and anemia[5]. Because radiological findings are often nonspecific and may resemble those of malignant soft-tissue neoplasms, establishing a preoperative diagnosis can be challenging[6]. Consequently, definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination supported by immunohistochemical analysis[7,8].

Complete surgical resection with negative margins is considered the cornerstone of treatment for localized disease and is generally associated with favorable long-term outcomes[9]. Given the rarity of psoas muscle involvement and the diagnostic difficulties posed by its nonspecific clinical and imaging characteristics, reporting additional cases is important to enhance awareness of this unusual presentation.

Herein, we describe a case of primary IMT arising from the psoas muscle in a young adult male. The lesion radiologically mimicked a malignant retroperitoneal soft-tissue tumor and was successfully treated with complete surgical excision. This case underscores the challenges associated with preoperative diagnosis, highlights the crucial role of histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, and emphasizes the importance of complete surgical resection in achieving optimal outcomes. This report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guidelines[10].

Case presentation

A 29-year-old male presented with a several-month history of progressive left flank and left iliac fossa pain. The pain had an insidious onset, was gradually worsening, and radiated to the back and left lower limb. He denied any associated constitutional symptoms, including fever, weight loss, or night sweats. His past medical, surgical, and family histories were unremarkable.

On physical examination, there was localized tenderness in the left iliac fossa associated with a palpable, firm abdominal mass. Laboratory investigations were within normal limits, including hemoglobin level of 16.4 g/dL, white blood cell count of 7.7 × 109/L, serum creatinine level of 0.85 mg/dL, and C-reactive protein level of 2.9 mg/L.

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a well-defined intramuscular solid lesion within the left psoas muscle, measuring approximately 7 × 6 cm, exhibiting marked internal vascularity. Subsequent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen confirmed a relatively well-circumscribed but irregular intramuscular soft-tissue mass located within the deep portion of the enlarged left psoas muscle at the level of L5–S1. The lesion measured approximately 5.8 × 4.7 cm in axial dimensions and 6.6 cm craniocaudally. It appeared isodense on non-contrast imaging and demonstrated avid heterogeneous enhancement in both arterial and venous phases. Prominent tortuous feeding vessels were noted predominantly at the superior and inferior poles of the lesion, arising from adjacent left lumbar arterial branches, suggesting neovascularization. There was no evidence of direct communication with the left common iliac artery or vein, which were mildly displaced medially but preserved in caliber and contour. The left ureter was also mildly displaced without signs of obstruction. Radiological features were suggestive of an intramuscular neoplastic process (Fig. 1a,b). A CT-guided core needle biopsy was performed; however, the histopathological examination was non-diagnostic. Microscopic examination of the biopsy specimens revealed fibrous tissue, skeletal muscle fibers, adipose tissue, and focal lymphocytic infiltrates. The final pathology report concluded: “Fibroconnective tissue with chronic inflammatory cell infiltration. No evidence of granulomatous inflammation or malignancy is identified. Findings are non-diagnostic, and clinicoradiological correlation is recommended.” Given the persistent symptoms, characteristic radiological findings, and inconclusive biopsy results, surgical exploration was discussed with the patient and subsequently undertaken for definitive diagnosis and treatment.

An exploratory laparotomy was performed. The procedure was performed by a consultant general and visceral surgeon with more than 15 years of experience in complex abdominal and retroperitoneal surgery. Intraoperatively, a large intramuscular mass was identified within the left psoas muscle, extending into the left paravertebral space. The descending colon was mobilized and retracted medially to facilitate exposure of the retroperitoneal tumor, with the left ureter and iliac vessels carefully identified and preserved (Fig. 2a,b). The lesion was meticulously dissected from the psoas muscle and surrounding tissues and was completely excised along with its surrounding capsule. No additional margin of macroscopically healthy tissue was resected, as the lesion appeared well circumscribed, without evidence of infiltration into adjacent organs or neurovascular structures (Fig. 2c,d). The postoperative course was uneventful. Standard perioperative intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis was administered according to institutional protocol. Oral intake was gradually reintroduced and was well tolerated. No postoperative infectious complications occurred. The surgical drain was removed on postoperative day three, and the patient was discharged in good general condition.

Histopathological examination revealed a well-circumscribed soft tissue tumor composed of proliferating oval to spindle-shaped myofibroblastic cells admixed with a chronic inflammatory infiltrate. No cytological or architectural features of malignancy were identified in the examined sections. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for vimentin and smooth muscle actin (SMA), while anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK-1) demonstrated focal weak positivity. LCA highlighted the inflammatory cell component. Stains for S100, SOX10, EMA, and CD34 were negative. The Ki-67 proliferation index was low, estimated at 5-7%. Surgical margins were free of tumor. The findings were consistent with IMT (Fig. 3). At the 5-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence. Follow-up evaluation, including physical examination and abdominal ultrasonography, was unremarkable. The patient reported complete resolution of symptoms and high satisfaction with the surgical outcome.

Discussion

IMT is a heterogeneous mesenchymal neoplasm characterized by a proliferation of fibroblastic and myofibroblastic spindle cells accompanied by a variable inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of plasma cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophils. Histologically, IMTs are classified into three distinct patterns: myxoid, hypercellular, and hypocellular fibrous, although these patterns frequently overlap within the same lesion. While most IMTs exhibit an indolent clinical course, their biological behavior is variable, with the potential for local recurrence and, rarely, distant metastasis[11]. In our patient, immunohistochemical analysis revealed focally weak ALK-1 expression, along with positivity for vimentin and SMA, findings consistent with the diagnosis of IMT.

The radiological characteristics of IMT are highly variable and lack specific features that reliably distinguish IMT from other soft-tissue tumors. These lesions are frequently detected incidentally during imaging performed for unrelated indications. On cross-sectional imaging, IMTs commonly appear as well-demarcated solid masses with heterogeneous enhancement, often raising suspicion for a malignant process[12]. As a result, IMT should be included in the differential diagnosis of retroperitoneal and intramuscular masses, particularly in younger patients.

Because of its nonspecific clinical and radiological presentation, the differential diagnosis is broad and includes spindle-cell sarcoma, liposarcoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, spindle-cell melanoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, inflammatory fibroid polyp, and other mesenchymal or malignant neoplasms[13]. Establishing a definitive diagnosis before surgery remains difficult. In the present case, CT-guided core needle biopsy excluded overt malignancy but failed to provide a conclusive diagnosis. Similar diagnostic limitations have been reported in previous studies and may be attributable to tumor heterogeneity and sampling error, highlighting the value of comprehensive examination of the resected specimen[7].

Histopathologically, IMT is characterized by a proliferation of spindle to epithelioid myofibroblasts within a background of chronic inflammatory cells, predominantly lymphocytes and plasma cells. Although significant cytological atypia is generally absent, the lesion may exhibit locally infiltrative growth involving adjacent soft tissues. Given the overlap with other spindle-cell tumors, immunohistochemical evaluation plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. Typical findings include expression of vimentin and SMA, with variable positivity for desmin, S100, and ALK, while CD117 and CD34 are usually absent, facilitating distinction from gastrointestinal stromal tumors and other mimicking entities[14].

Complete surgical excision remains the preferred treatment for localized IMT and offers the best chance of durable disease control[9]. Reported surgical approaches include open, laparoscopic, and robotic resection, with minimally invasive techniques being appropriate for carefully selected, localized lesions. However, open surgery remains the preferred approach for large, deeply seated retroperitoneal tumors or when malignancy cannot be excluded, as it provides optimal exposure, facilitates vascular control, and enables complete (R0) resection[15]. Reported recurrence rates vary according to tumor location, ranging from approximately 2% in pulmonary lesions to as high as 25% in extrapulmonary tumors. Recurrence is most commonly associated with incomplete resection or multifocal intra-abdominal disease[16]. In the present case, an open transperitoneal approach was chosen because the tumor was deeply embedded within the psoas muscle, demonstrated marked hypervascularity on preoperative imaging, and was located adjacent to the ureter and iliac vessels, while the possibility of a soft-tissue sarcoma could not be excluded preoperatively. This approach allowed safe, complete excision with histologically negative margins.

Targeted therapy directed against ALK has emerged as an effective option for patients with unresectable, recurrent, or metastatic ALK-positive IMT. However, its role in patients who undergo complete resection of localized disease remains limited[17]. Likewise, chemotherapy and radiotherapy are generally reserved for selected cases in which surgery is not feasible or when recurrent or metastatic disease develops[18]. Consequently, no adjuvant treatment was considered necessary for our patient.

A limitation of this report is the relatively short follow-up period of 5 months. Although the patient remained asymptomatic with no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence, extrapulmonary IMTs carry a recognized risk of delayed local recurrence. Despite the absence of standardized surveillance guidelines, long-term clinical and radiological follow-up is warranted to confirm sustained disease control and detect potential late relapse[16].

Given the scarcity of reported psoas muscle IMTs, their natural history and optimal management remain incompletely defined. This case adds to the limited literature by describing the clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, pathological findings, and successful surgical management of this unusual tumor. The principal clinical message is that IMT should be considered in the differential diagnosis of retroperitoneal soft-tissue masses, even when imaging findings suggest malignancy. Accurate diagnosis depends on careful histopathological and immunohistochemical assessment, while complete surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment and is associated with favorable outcomes.

Conclusion

Primary IMT of the psoas muscle is an exceptionally rare tumor that may closely mimic retroperitoneal malignancy. This case highlights the limitations of preoperative imaging and biopsy, the essential role of histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation, and the effectiveness of complete surgical excision in achieving disease control.
