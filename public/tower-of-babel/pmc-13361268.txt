A Unified Polymer Hydrogel Electrolyte Integrating Robust Adhesion, Self‐Healing, and Oxygen Permeability in Flexible Neutral Zn‐Air Batteries
Authors: Zhenyu Sun, Kaiming Liao, Wei Zhou, Zongping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolymer hydrogel electrolytes hold significant promise for Zn–air batteries due to their excellent flexibility, non‐flammability, and leak resistance. However, their practical application remains constrained by zinc dendrite growth and intrinsic trade‐offs among ionic conductivity, oxygen permeability, mechanical strength, and both interfacial and environmental stability. Herein, we report a neutral, 3D physically cross‐linked PFCD hydrogel engineered via the synergistic combination of a tight coordination network of small Fe3 + ions and a broader hydrogen‐bonding network of β‐cyclodextrin, with in situ incorporated N‐carboxyethyl chitosan. This hierarchical architecture endows the PFCD hydrogel with high adhesion (50 kPa), superior ionic conductivity (113.2 mS cm− 1), and significant oxygen permeability (4.1 Barrer). Additionally, the hydrogel exhibits autonomous self‐healing (99% efficiency in 3 h), exceptional stretchability (1600% elongation), and robust interfacial contact (withstanding 1000 folding/stretching cycles), while effectively suppressing zinc dendrite growth and resisting CO2 degradation. Assembled flexible neutral Zn–air batteries (FNZABs) achieve high power density (59 mW cm− 2) with stable operation over 3000 cycles. Furthermore, the fabrication of leakage‐free, closed‐system FNZABs demonstrates the hydrogel's dual role as both an electrolyte and an oxygen‐permeable encapsulation membrane. This integrated strategy effectively addresses critical interfacial and stability challenges essential for advanced flexible energy storage systems development.

Introduction

The miniaturization of electronics and the rise of wearable devices demand advanced energy storage solutions [1, 2, 3]. Flexible Zn‐air batteries (FZABs) offer a promising alternative, featuring high theoretical energy density (1086 Wh kg−1) and non‐flammable aqueous electrolytes [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. By utilizing atmospheric oxygen, FZABs decouple energy capacity from cathode volume, achieving densities threefold that of lithium‐ion batteries at costs below $100 kWh−1 [9, 10, 11]. Despite these advantages, practical application is hindered by the limitations of traditional alkaline electrolytes (e.g., 6 m KOH) [1, 12, 13]. The necessary open architecture exposes the electrolyte to atmospheric CO2, leading to carbonation that precipitates insoluble carbonates, clogs the porous cathode, and degrades conductivity [14, 15]. Furthermore, the corrosive nature of alkaline media exacerbates parasitic anodic reactions, specifically zinc corrosion and hydrogen evolution, which compromise safety in wearable applications [16].

To address these limitations, research has shifted toward neutral or near‐neutral electrolytes [17, 18]. While these systems effectively suppress carbonation and dendritic growth [19, 20, 21], they typically sacrifice ionic conductivity and electrode kinetics, particularly in gel electrolytes [22, 23]. Dual‐network (DN) hydrogels have emerged as a promising strategy [24, 25, 26]. Since their introduction by Gong et al. in 2003 [27], DN hydrogels have traditionally combined a rigid, chemically cross‐linked network with a soft, physically cross‐linked one [28, 29, 30, 31]. However, the irreversible nature of chemical cross‐linking often compromises self‐healing and fatigue resistance [32, 33]. As a result, recent efforts have focused on fully physically cross‐linked networks constructed from dynamic, reversible non‐covalent bonds [34, 35]. In such architectures, strong ionic coordination (e.g., with Cu2 + [36], Al3 + [37], or Zr4+ [38]) provides mechanical rigidity, while hydrogen bonding (e.g., F─H [39], N─H [40], O─H [41]) imparts viscoelasticity and self‐healing capabilities. The synergy between these interactions creates a robust, porous architecture that facilitates electrolyte storage and uptake [42, 43, 44]. Despite the above research efforts, the performance of these DN hydrogels in FZABs remains hindered by intrinsic trade‐offs between efficient mass transport (specifically ionic and O2 flux) and the simultaneous requirements for robust interfacial adhesion, mechanical strength, and long‐term stability.

In this work, we report a neutral hydrogel electrolyte, PAA‐Fe3 +‐CEC‐β‐CD (PFCD), that addresses these constraints through synergistic interactions: a small, strongly bonded Fe3 + (diameter ∼0.13 nm) coordination center paired with a large, multi‐armed β‐cyclodextrin (β‐CD, outer diameter ∼1.54 nm, inner diameter ∼0.62 nm, cavity height ∼0.79 nm) hydrogen‐bonding center. Furthermore, the in situ formation of N‐carboxyethyl chitosan (CEC), derived from the thermal reaction between acrylic acid (AA) and chitosan (CS), enhances interfacial adhesion and promotes oxygen transport. Characterized by a physically cross‐linked 3D network and high porosity, the resulting PFCD hydrogel simultaneously achieves superior O2 permeability, ionic conductivity, mechanical resilience, and self‐healing capability, overcoming the ion‐migration disorders and sluggish oxygen transfer typical of neutral electrolytes. At the anode interface, the PFCD network establishes a spatially uniform electric potential field that suppresses the tip effect and prevents zinc dendrite growth. As a result, the PFCD‐based flexible neutral zinc‐air battery (FNZAB) demonstrates a 1.46 V open‐circuit voltage, 59 mW·cm− 2 peak power density, and exceptional stability over 500 h (3000 cycles) with negligible dendrite formation. In a closed‐system FNZAB configuration where PFCD serves dual roles as electrolyte and oxygen‐permeable membrane, the device operates stably for 200 h (1200 cycles) and exhibits self‐adhesion to diverse surfaces such as clothing and skin alike. These results position PFCD‐based systems as a practical solution for wearable devices, enabling safe, long‐term operation in ambient conditions.

Results and Discussion

Integrated Design and Synthesis of PFCD Hydrogels

The design of polymer hydrogel electrolytes for practical FNZABs necessitates a delicate balance among critical parameters, including interfacial contact, ionic conductivity, oxygen permeability, mechanical strength, and environmental stability. As depicted in Figure 1a, the fabrication of the 3D physically cross‐linked PFCD hydrogel employs a three‐step polymerization strategy. Initially, a PAA Fe3+ CEC polymer network is established via ionic coordination bonding. Subsequently, β‐CD, a natural polymer rich in hydroxyl groups, is introduced as a building block to construct the secondary network through inter‐chain hydrogen bonding. The process is finalized by the thermal initiation of the system using K2S2O8. Structurally, the ionic coordination bonds between Fe3+ and carboxyl groups significantly enhance the mechanical strength and self‐healing properties of the hydrogel. Simultaneously, the hydrogen bond network formed by CEC, β‐CD, and PAA improves oxygen permeation, interfacial adhesion, and self‐healing performance.

(b) Diagram of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the hydrogel's triple‐phase interface. (c) Structural configuration of the assembled FNZAB prototype. (d) Overview of the synergistic performance advantages enabled by the PFCD hydrogel within the FNZAB.

The superior oxygen adsorption of the PFCD hydrogel is attributed to the synergistic combination of dipole‐induced dipole interactions between CEC and oxygen molecules and the rapid transport channels facilitated by the high hydrophilicity of CEC. In conventional CS, hydroxyl groups exert a strong electron‐withdrawing effect on oxygen, which reduces the charge density on amino groups. This weakens dipole induction during transport from OH to NH2 sites, hindering directional oxygen movement along the polymer chains and resulting in sluggish reaction kinetics.

By contrast, CEC, synthesized via the Michael addition of acrylic acid to chitosan amino groups, possesses bipolar functional groups (─OH and ─COOH) that generate a strong local electric field. When dissolved oxygen enters this field, the permanent dipole moment of CEC induces a distortion in the electron cloud of the nonpolar oxygen molecule, creating a temporary induced dipole. This interaction fosters a weak electrostatic attraction between the CEC permanent dipole and the oxygen‐induced dipole. Furthermore, the high density of hydrophilic groups (─COOH, ─OH, ─NH2) within CEC retains water molecules, forming aqueous‐phase channels essential for oxygen permeation. This structure establishes a highly efficient oxygen transport pathway, facilitating oxygen migration along the polymer chains to the triple‐phase (gas–liquid–solid) interface. As this interface is the active site for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), this design effectively overcomes the limitations of free diffusion (Figure 1b). Demonstrating the practical application of this mechanism, a FNZAB prototype was assembled comprising a Zn foil anode, a porous cathode, and the interposed PFCD hydrogel (Figure 1c). As illustrated in Figure 1d, this synergistic design endows the hydrogel with a superior balance of O2 permeability, ionic conductivity, mechanical strength, self‐healing capability, and zinc dendrite suppression, ultimately enabling the prototype to achieve the electrochemical performance requisite for flexible energy storage systems.

Mechanical Robustness and Interfacial Adaptability

The inherent trade‐off between adhesion and mechanical strength in hydrogel electrolytes presents a significant challenge for FNZABs. This balance is critical, as it directly governs the stability of the electrode–electrolyte interface and determines the battery's ability to withstand both tensile and compressive deformations without mechanical failure.

As shown in Figure 2a, the 3D physically cross‐linked PFCD hydrogel electrolyte (1 mm in thickness) is capable of being swollen into a balloon‐like object several orders of magnitude larger than its original size; structural integrity is retained even after the material is subjected to severe puncture by a screwdriver and cutting by a sharp blade. Moreover, tolerance to rigorous deformations, including multiple folding, twisting, rolling, and knotting, is demonstrated by a thin strip of the PFCD hydrogel sample (Figure 2b). Such performance is particularly noteworthy among 3D physically cross‐linked hydrogels. Additionally, high transparency is exhibited by the PFCD hydrogel at a length of 15–20 cm, as shown in Figure 2c and Figure S1.

As shown in Figure 2d and Figure S2, a porous structure with an average pore size of approximately 10 µm is revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations. As shown in Figures S3 and S4, the PFCD structure is observed to be smaller and more densely cross‐linked compared to those of the single physically cross‐linked hydrogels, such as PCD (PAA‐CEC‐β‐CD), PFC (PAA‐Fe3 +‐CEC), and PFD (PAA‐Fe3 +‐β‐CD). Stretching and compression in high‐stress regions are sustained by the structure through this dual coupling of ionic and hydrogen bonds. Furthermore, a uniform distribution of C, N, O, and Fe in the PFCD hydrogel is demonstrated by elemental mapping via SEM in Figure 2e, whereas relatively less uniform pore sizes and elemental distributions are shown by the PFC, PFD, and PCD hydrogels (Figures S5–S7).

Excellent tensile recovery properties are demonstrated by the PFCD hydrogel, as shown in Figure 2f: the initial length (1 cm) can be stretched to 16 cm without fracture, and recovery to 1.2 cm is observed after the external force is released. To further investigate the synergistic mechanical–adhesive performance, the hydrogel was adhered to skin and subjected to tensile testing. The results (Figure 2g) indicate that, supported by strong interfacial adhesion, the PFCD hydrogel can be stretched from 3 to 13 cm without detachment or rupture; recovery to 4 cm is achieved after unloading, confirming that outstanding mechanical integrity is maintained even in the adhered state. A systematic comparison of the mechanical contributions within the 3D physical cross‐linking network was conducted using tensile stress–strain curves, as presented in Figure S8a,b. The PFCD hydrogel demonstrates substantially higher tensile strength (40 kPa) and breaking elongation (1600%) than single‐network hydrogels (PCD: 18.8 kPa/1490%; PFC: 25 kPa/1034%; PFD: 24.5 kPa/1398%), and its elongation also exceeds that of PVA gel electrolytes (284%). The robust nature of the hydrogel is further demonstrated by its ability to support a 500 g weight without fracture (inset, Figure S8b). This mechanical enhancement is attributed to the 3D network architecture, which synergistically combines hydrogen bonds and ionic coordination bonds to simultaneously improve both tensile strength and extensibility. Exceptional compressive resistance and resilience are further highlighted in Figure S9, where it is demonstrated that the PFCD hydrogel completely recovers its original shape after being compressed by 60% and bears a 500 g weight without noticeable deformation.

Regarding interface adaptability, as illustrated in Figure 2h, the PFCD hydrogel was placed into differently shaped PTFE molds and allowed to rest for 6–12 h, during which the mold geometry was conformed to. Upon transfer to a flat surface for another 12 h, the original shape was restored. This reversible shape‐adaptive behavior is attributed to the synergistic interaction between hydrogen bonds (weak interactions) and ionic coordination bonds (strong interactions) within the 3D physically cross‐linked network: structural dynamic adjustability is provided by hydrogen bonds, while overall integrity is maintained by ionic bonds, thereby enabling interface adaptation while excessive material diffusion is suppressed. Furthermore, through SEM analysis of interfacial characteristics, it is observed that a “face‐to‐face” intimate interfacial contact is established by the PFCD 3D physically cross‐linked network (Figure 2i), in contrast to the “point‐to‐point” contact formed by single‐network hydrogels. Interfacial adaptability and adhesion strength to the electrode surface are significantly enhanced by this structure, thereby effectively increasing the electrochemically active area and improving the interfacial stability of the battery during cycling.

Robust Adhesion and Intimate Interfacial Contact

Robust interfacial adhesion is considered a pivotal factor for FNZABs, as it maximizes the electrochemically active surface area, ensures the stability of the triple‐phase interface, and achieves exceptional electrode–electrolyte mechanical integration [26]. The PFCD hydrogel electrolyte exhibits outstanding adhesive properties derived from synergistic interfacial interactions enabled by its specific molecular composition and 3D network structure [45]. As elucidated in Figure 3a, the adhesion mechanism is primarily attributed to hydrogen bonding [46] and ionic coordination [47], reinforced by physical interlocking on rough surfaces. This multimodal adhesion allows the hydrogel to establish close contact with diverse substrates, including metals (nickel, aluminum, zinc), carbon cloth, glass, and biological skin (Figure 3b). The disparity in performance indicates that the cellulose molecular chains in the PFCD network‐densely functionalized with highly reactive groups‐form abundant hydrogen‐bonding interactions with the substrate, thereby significantly enhancing adhesive strength. This synergistic effect within the 3D network is crucial for maintaining robust contact.

To quantify the influence of 3D physical cross‐linking on adhesion performance, comparative sliding tests were conducted on an inclined plate (20°) using a glass ball. As shown in Figure 3c, it was observed that the single‐network PFD hydrogel allowed for a significantly longer sliding distance compared to the 3D physically cross‐linked PFCD hydrogel, confirming the superior adhesion of the latter. Further quantitative analysis using a universal testing machine (Figure S10a) determined the initial adhesion strengths of the PFCD hydrogel against various functional materials. The results shown in Figure S10b revealed substantial adhesion values: 58 kPa for aluminum, 54 kPa for nickel, 50 kPa for zinc, 48 kPa for carbon cloth, and 40 kPa for paper.

The mechanical durability and stability of the interface were rigorously evaluated to assess practical applicability. The PFCD hydrogel demonstrated excellent load‐bearing capacity, vertically lifting a 200 g weight solely through adhesive force with a carbon cloth electrode (Figure S10c), and maintained robust adhesion strength (∼35 kPa) after four adhesion‐peeling cycles. The conventional PVA gel electrolyte exhibited low adhesive strengths: 11.7 kPa on carbon cloth and 10.5 kPa on the zinc anode (Figure S11). Consequently, by combining surface adhesion with internal cohesion, the PFCD hydrogel facilitates the construction of a “zinc anode‐electrolyte‐air cathode” assembly with a tightly bonded interface. As shown in Figure 3d, this creates a mechanically integrated structure suitable for convenient rolling or cutting, rather than a simple physical stack. Furthermore, FNZAB strips incorporating the PFCD hydrogel demonstrated remarkable mechanical resilience against twisting and folding, successfully enduring 1000 continuous bending cycles without any degradation to the electrolyte–electrode interface. By contrast, FNZAB strips utilizing the PFD gel exhibited interfacial cracking after only 200 bending cycles, with detailed observations revealing a distinct structural gap at the electrode–electrolyte boundary. These results demonstrate that the strong, tough adhesion of PFCD dynamically adapts to mechanical deformation, eliminating local current‐density hotspots or inactive “dead zones” caused by poor contact.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and optical photography confirmed perfect conformal contact between the PFCD hydrogel and electrode surfaces (Figures S12 and S13), while ultrasonic imaging revealed substantial differences between the two systems (Figure 3e). The PFCD | Carbon Cloth (CC)‐based cell exhibited strong, homogeneous ultrasonic signals indicative of intimate contact with minimal air entrapment, whereas the PFD | CC‐based cell displayed weak signals and pronounced blue regions, indicating poor contact and significant air voids.

These results demonstrate that the 3D physically cross‐linked PFCD hydrogel forms a conformal, stable interface that facilitates efficient transport of oxygen, ions, and electrons, which is critical for the electrochemical performance and structural stability of FNZABs.

Physicochemical Characterization and Transport Properties

The physicochemical properties and structural evolution of the PFCD hydrogel were elucidated through comprehensive spectroscopic and diffractometric analyses. Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy (Figure 4a) revealed characteristic peaks at 1067, 1159, and 1453 cm− 1, corresponding to the reactive polar groups (─OH, O─C─O, and COO−) in CEC [48, 49]. The significant attenuation of these peaks in the PFCD spectrum indicates the successful polymerization of CEC within the hydrogel matrix. Additionally, the persistence of the peak at 1696 cm− 1, attributed to the high‐density hydrophilic COO− groups of poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) [50], confirms the effective grafting of acrylic acid monomers onto the chitosan backbone (Figures S14 and S15). X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 4b) demonstrated a marked reduction in the intensity of the characteristic peak at ∼23°, suggesting a transition from a crystalline to a predominantly amorphous state driven by extensive hydrogen bonding between CEC and PAA [49]. As shown in Figure 4c and Figure S16, this structural integration was further corroborated by x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), where high‐resolution O 1s spectra identified peaks at 530.8 and 529.3 eV [51]. These correspond to C─O/C═O (Figure S17) and Fe─O bonds, respectively, confirming the formation of robust ionic coordination bonds between carboxyl groups and Fe3 + ions. Furthermore, Raman spectroscopy (Figure S18a) exhibited broad peaks at 659 cm− 1 (C─OH) [43] and 1091 cm− 1 (C─CH2) [52] and revealed enhanced signals for C─H (2939 cm− 1) [53] and H2O (2800–3800 cm− 1) [40] in the PFCD hydrogel, indicating that CEC and β‐CD synergistically construct a stable polymeric framework.

The hydration dynamics and ionic conductivity of the hydrogel were characterized to determine the efficacy of its internal transport channels. Detailed analysis of the Raman spectra (Figure 4d; Figure S18b) distinguished between bound water (3220 cm− 1) and free water (3405 cm− 1) [30], revealing that the 3D physically cross‐linked network ensures high water absorption and retention through the synergistic effect of high‐density hydrophilic groups on the PAA and CEC chains. This “hydrophilic skeleton” resulted in a swelling ratio of 280% after 3 h and a water retention capability exceeding 90%; moreover, it maintained a water retention rate of 77.4% after 10 days of storage at 60% relative humidity, significantly outperforming control hydrogels PCD (38.6%), PFC (40.9%), and PFD (40.6%), which retained only 38%–41% after 4 days (Figure 4e,f; Figure S19). Thermogravimetric analysis (Figure S20) confirmed that the PFCD hydrogel possessed the highest free water content, as evidenced by maximal weight loss between 0°C and 180°C. This free‐water‐rich, highly interconnected network enables efficient ion mobility, yielding an exceptional ionic conductivity of 113.2 mS cm− 1, which markedly exceeds the values observed for the PFC (25.6 mS cm− 1), PFD (44.2 mS cm− 1), and PCD (13 mS cm− 1) hydrogels (Figure 4g).

During discharge in neutral ZABs utilizing either liquid or PFCD hydrogel electrolytes, the ORR at the cathode induces a local pH increase at the electrode–electrolyte interface, which shifts the NH4 + dissociation equilibrium and generates NH3 that complexes with Zn2 + to form soluble [Zn(NH3)4]2 + (Figure S21a). As shown in Figure S21b,c, although the neutral PFCD hydrogel exhibits slightly lower ionic conductivity (113.2 mS cm− 1) than the neutral liquid electrolyte (121.4 mS cm− 1) due to electrostatic interactions between stereo‐distributed ─COO− groups and [Zn(NH3)4]2 + complexes, its uniformly cross‐linked network and continuous hydration channels synergistically facilitate ordered transport of these ions, effectively suppressing non‐uniform zinc deposition at the anode. The PFCD hydrogel's excellent interfacial adhesion and adaptability minimize interfacial ion transport resistance, resulting in an interfacial impedance of 14.1 Ω for the neutral PFCD ZAB, closely approaching the 12.7 Ω observed for the neutral liquid ZAB (Figure S21d).

The mechanism underlying the enhanced oxygen permeability was investigated through a combination of theoretical modeling and experimental validation. The incorporation of CEC facilitates charge redistribution, effectively optimizing the electrostatic field along the polymer chains. In unmodified chitosan, a significant disparity in electron cloud density between hydroxyl (0.266) and amino groups (0.172) creates a non‐uniform electrostatic field that hinders the dipole‐induced dipole interactions required for oxygen transport. In contrast, the CEC‐grafted system establishes a more homogeneous field due to the comparable electron densities of its bipolar functional groups (─OH: 0.263; ─COOH: 0.247), thereby sustaining persistent dipole‐induced dipole interactions and facilitating directional oxygen migration (Figure 4h). Binding energy calculations (Figure 4i) supported this mechanism, showing that the dipole‐induced dipole interaction energy between CEC and oxygen surpasses the adsorption energies of O2 with H2O, PAA, or β‐cyclodextrin. Experimentally, this optimized transport mechanism translated to a superior oxygen permeability of 4.1 Barrer for the PFCD hydrogel (thickness: 417 µm) in a pure air atmosphere, outperforming all control samples (Figure 4j,k).

As illustrated in Figure 4l, the stability of the neutral PFCD hydrogel against environmental carbonation was evaluated and compared to that of a conventional neutral electrolyte. After 3 h of CO2 exposure, the conventional PVA hydrogel exhibited interfacial softening and substantial mechanical degradation (Figure 4m,n). This failure stems from the CO3 2 −/HCO3 − environment disrupting the PVA network's hydrogen‐bonding structure, causing structural collapse. In contrast, the PFCD hydrogel's mechanical properties remained stable under CO2 exposure. This resilience derives from its 3D physically cross‐linked dual‐network architecture, wherein strong metal coordination bonds form the primary structural backbone while dynamic hydrogen bonds provide supplementary reinforcement. Consequently, even if hydrogen bonding becomes partially compromised, robust coordination bonds maintain carbonation resistance, a critical structural advantage of the PFCD network.

In conclusion, the convergence of spectroscopic, structural, and electrochemical analyses confirms that the PFCD hydrogel possesses a unique, interconnected aqueous network that supports rapid ionic conduction and directional oxygen permeation while offering robust resistance to CO2 poisoning.

Self‐Healing Performance of PFCD Hydrogels

Self‐healing capabilities in flexible battery electrolytes are essential for tolerating micro‐damage and autonomously repairing defects at room temperature, thereby preserving the integrity of the electrolyte–electrode interface under complex operating conditions [54]. Macroscopically, severed PFCD hydrogel fragments exhibited complete fusion after merely 10 min of contact (Figure 5a). This efficacy was corroborated microscopically; a distinctive 10 µm crack at the interface was observed to virtually disappear within the same timeframe, demonstrating rapid room‐temperature repair (Figure 5b). As illustrated in Figures S22 and S23a,b, obvious cracks are still observed on the surfaces of single‐network cross‐linked hydrogels (e.g., PFD, PCD, PFC, and PVA) after 10 min of self‐healing, indicating a slow self‐healing capability. Furthermore, the molecular evolution during this process was visualized via in situ infrared microscopy at characteristic spectral positions (Figure 5c). Initially, the fracture surface displayed a pronounced red region corresponding to the stretching vibration of exposed CEC carboxyl groups (Figure 5d). Following a 1‐h healing period, this region transitioned to green, indicating a substantial reduction in free functional groups. A concurrent recovery trend was observed in peaks associated with ether bonds, PAA carboxyl groups, and hydroxyl groups, confirming the material's rapid and spontaneous self‐healing potential. As illustrated in Figure 5e, the spontaneous healing mechanism of the PFCD hydrogel is derived from its three‐dimensional physically cross‐linked network, constructed exclusively via dynamic non‐covalent interactions such as hydrogen and ionic bonds.

Quantitative mechanical analysis further validated the time‐dependent restoration of the hydrogel's structural integrity. As presented in Figure S24a, both tensile strain and strength exhibited a positive correlation with healing duration, recovering to 12, 21, 31, and 44 kPa after 10, 20, 60, and 180 min, respectively. The self‐healing efficiency, calculated relative to the pristine sample, exhibited a rapid initial increase over the first 20 min and ultimately achieved a recovery rate of 99.5% after 180 min. In contrast, the single‐network hydrogels (PFC: 37.8%; PFD: 21.5%; PCD: 27.9%) exhibited significantly lower self‐healing efficiency at 180 min (Figure S24b,c). After 6 h of self‐healing, the PFCD hydrogel retained 1400% elongation and supported a 100 g weight, demonstrating near‐complete mechanical restoration (Figure S25).

The fundamental mechanism driving this performance is the 3D network architecture formed entirely by dynamic non‐covalent bonds. Upon re‐contacting fractured surfaces, these reversible physical bonds are driven by the dynamic network to spontaneously reorganize and “re‐stitch” the polymer matrix [53, 55]. In this synergistic process, reversible ionic coordination bonds constitute a stable skeletal framework that preserves structural integrity and prevents excessive flow, thereby providing spatial stability for bond reformation. Simultaneously, β‐CD functions as a multi‐hydroxyl cross‐linking center, rapidly reconstructing the hydrogen‐bond network with adjacent CEC and PAA chains to enhance density and connectivity. Consequently, the PFCD hydrogel achieves rapid, autonomous room‐temperature self‐healing without external stimuli while retaining original mechanical properties, a capability critical for maintaining stable interfaces under mechanical stress.

Electrochemical Performance of FNZAB Pouch Cells

Guided by the rational design of the 3D physically cross‐linked polymer network and its synergistic functionalities, FNZABs incorporating the PFCD hydrogel were fabricated and evaluated. As illustrated in Figure 6a, the pouch‐type FNZAB comprises a Co‐NCNTFs‐loaded carbon cloth oxygen electrode, a zinc foil anode, and a highly adhesive, self‐adaptive PFCD gel electrolyte sandwiched between them. These components are encapsulated within a perforated aluminum‐plastic film, with the perforated side aligned toward the oxygen electrode to facilitate air access. The assembled pouch cell demonstrated robust electrochemical performance, delivering a discharge capacity of 591 mAh g− 1 and an energy density of 852 mWh g− 1 (Figure 6b). Furthermore, the PFCD‐based FNZAB achieved an open‐circuit voltage (OCV) of 1.46 V (Figure 6c), surpassing the values obtained by control devices utilizing PFC, PFD, and PCD hydrogel electrolytes. This performance enhancement is attributed to the 3D physically cross‐linked network, which ensures sufficient dissolved oxygen availability at the triple‐phase reaction interface and thereby effectively mitigates mass‐transfer overpotential. Consequently, the FNZAB utilizing the PFCD electrolyte exhibited reduced polarization voltage compared to control systems at equivalent current densities (Figure S26) and achieved a maximum power density of 59 mW cm− 2 at 110 mA cm− 2 (Figure 6d; Figure S27), a value notably exceeding that of most reported FNZABs (Table S1).

To evaluate the contribution of CEC‐enhanced oxygen permeation to FNZAB performance, comparative experiments were conducted under varying oxygen partial pressures. Increasing oxygen supply from ambient air (21% O2) to pure oxygen (100% O2) substantially enhanced PFD‐based FNZAB peak power density from 15.3 to 33 mW cm− 2 (Figure S28), confirming that oxygen permeation kinetics limit baseline performance. Strikingly, PFCD in ambient air (59 mW cm− 2) exceeded PFD in pure oxygen, demonstrating that CEC‐induced dipole interactions facilitate directional oxygen migration and overcome permeation limitations inherent to conventional neutral hydrogels (Figure 6e). Rate capability and stability were assessed through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and discharge testing. EIS revealed that the interfacial impedance of the PFCD‐based FNZAB decreased ∼30% within 1 h as the electrolyte established adaptive electrode contact (Figure S29). Following this conditioning period, discharge testing across current densities from 0.1 to 10 mA cm− 2 showed that PFCD maintained stable voltage at 0.9 V even at 10 mA cm− 2, while control hydrogels (PCD, PFC, PFD, PVA) exhibited poor steady‐state voltages (<0.7 V) and inferior reversibility (Figure 6f; Figure S30). These results demonstrate synergistic enhancement of electrochemical performance through PFCD's combined adaptive interfacial contact and directional oxygen permeation.

Specific capacity and interfacial stability were evaluated through galvanostatic testing. As shown in Figure 6g and Figure S31, the PFCD‐based FNZAB achieved a specific capacity of 591 mAh gZn − 1, exceeding all control hydrogels: PFC (465 mAh gZn − 1), PFD (349 mAh gZn − 1), PCD (504 mAh gZn − 1), and PVA (536 mAh gZn − 1). This superior performance stems from the intimate adhesive interface between PFCD and the zinc anode, which maintains robust contact throughout discharge and prevents interfacial delamination and electrolyte desiccation. By eliminating these localized degradation pathways, PFCD enables uniform and complete zinc utilization.

Mechanical flexibility and cycling stability were evaluated under dynamic and static conditions, as shown in Figure 6h,i; Figure S32. The PFCD‐based FNZAB maintained stable charge/discharge voltage plateaus during bending, facilitated by a dynamically reversible cross‐linked network that preserves the structural skeleton while re‐establishing interfacial contact under stress. Notably, the device achieved 3000 stable cycles (500 h) under static conditions, significantly outperforming electrolytes such as PFC (290 cycles), PFD (280 cycles), PCD (180 cycles), and PVA (690 cycles). This exceptional stability is driven by the 3D network's ability to provide robust interfacial adhesion, a stable triple‐phase reaction interface, and dynamic self‐healing to regenerate mass‐transport pathways. As summarized in Table S2, the PFCD hydrogel systematically resolves critical challenges in reaction kinetics, mass transport, and interfacial stability for flexible neutral/alkaline ZABs.

To evaluate practical feasibility, multiple FNZABs were integrated into series‐connected circuits to drive various electronic loads (Figure 6j–l). The system successfully powered an LED panel and charged a smartwatch, underscoring its potential for wearable electronics. Additionally, a self‐charging power unit was established to harvest and store solar energy. Under both simulated sunlight (xenon lamp source) and natural outdoor conditions, a polycrystalline silicon solar panel provided a stable charging current to three parallel‐connected FNZABs (Figure 6m,n). The solar‐charged batteries subsequently illuminated an LED strip (Figure 6o), demonstrating the system's capability for sustainable, off‐grid energy storage.

Electrochemical Performance of Closed‐System FNZABs

Inspired by the high oxygen permeability and robust adhesion of the PFCD membrane, a closed‐system FNZAB was fabricated wherein the PFCD serves simultaneously as the hydrogel electrolyte and the oxygen‐permeable packaging membrane. As illustrated in Figure 7a,b, this device (diameter: 1.55 cm; thickness: 1.67 mm) adopts a configuration analogous to the pouch cell described previously, yet it distinctively replaces the conventional aluminum‐plastic encapsulation with the PFCD membrane. A critical advantage of this design is that the oxygen‐permeable nature of the PFCD eliminates the need to puncture the packaging for air access, a necessity in conventional aluminum‐plastic film assemblies. Furthermore, this configuration mitigates electrolyte leakage and allows for the self‐healing of damage, such as cracks, thereby restoring encapsulation integrity. By removing the inherent physical interfaces found in traditional “electrode–electrolyte–encapsulation film” stacks, the PFCD‐enabled design achieves a true all‐in‐one cell structure.

The electrochemical efficacy of this architecture was evaluated through discharge and capacity tests. As shown in Figure 7c, the PFCD‐based closed‐system FNZAB exhibited excellent rate capability with a minimal voltage drop of 43 mV across a current density range of 0.1–2 mA cm− 2. In discharge polarization and specific capacity assessments (Figure 7d,e), the device achieved a peak power density of 21.4 mW cm− 2 and a specific capacity of 755 mAh gZn − 1. These values significantly surpass those of control systems using other hydrogels, specifically PFC (11.5 mW cm− 2/445 mAh gZn − 1), PFD (12 mW cm− 2/480 mAh gZn − 1), and PCD (13 mW cm− 2/355 mAh gZn − 1). These superior performance metrics are attributed to the high oxygen permeability and ionic conductivity inherent to the PFCD membrane.

To demonstrate practical utility, the unique adhesive properties of the PFCD were leveraged to mount four series‐connected closed‐system FNZABs directly onto clothing, successfully powering an LED display (Figure 7f). Long‐term durability was subsequently assessed via galvanostatic cycling at 0.5 mA cm− 2 with 5‐min charge/discharge intervals. As depicted in Figure 7g, the PFCD‐based device maintained remarkable stability for 1200 cycles (200 h). In sharp contrast, devices employing PFC, PFD, and PCD hydrogels exhibited rapid degradation, with polarization voltage increasing abruptly after only 90 cycles (15 h), 216 cycles (36 h), and 301 cycles (50 h), respectively. This extended cycle life is ascribed to the robust interfacial contact between the PFCD hydrogel electrolyte and the electrodes (carbon cathode and Zn anode), which significantly reduces interfacial resistance and sustains charge–discharge cycling, thereby confirming the PFCD hydrogel's critical role in enhancing the stability of flexible wearable devices.

Stability and Deposition Behavior of Zn Anodes

Compared to conventional alkaline ZABs, neutral electrolyte‐based ZABs are chemically milder and more environmentally friendly, mitigating detrimental side reactions such as carbonation and zinc dendrite growth to extend cycle life. However, unregulated ion diffusion and the “tip effect” remain persistent challenges [56]. In standard neutral liquid electrolytes, concentration‐gradient‐driven random deposition of the tetraamminezinc (II) complex ([Zn(NH3)4]2 +) induces a pronounced tip effect, rendering dendrite formation practically unavoidable (Figure 8a). The PFCD system addresses this through a fundamentally different transport mechanism: weak electrostatic interactions between uniformly distributed carboxylate groups (─COO−) in the 3D hydrogel network and migrating [Zn(NH3)4]2 + complexes regulate ion movement, while these fixed, evenly distributed negative charges create a spatially uniform electric field at the electrode interface. This field uniformity counteracts the localized high electric fields induced by microscopic surface protrusions, redirecting [Zn(NH3)4]2 + ion nucleation from protruding tips toward the entire Zn anode surface. Consequently, the synergistic combination of carboxylate‐directed transport and electric field homogenization fundamentally suppresses the tip effect and dendrite growth, enabling dense, planar zinc deposition.

Comparison of Zn deposition behavior in neutral liquid and PFCD hydrogel electrolytes. (a) Schematic illustration of the [Zn(NH3)4]2 + complex transport and the corresponding Zn deposition mechanisms. (b) SEM images and digital photographs of Zn anode surfaces after 100 h of cycling. (c) In situ optical microscopy images showing the evolution of Zn dendrite growth over time.

To validate the suppression of dendrite growth, ZABs were assembled using either a neutral liquid electrolyte or the neutral PFCD hydrogel electrolyte. As depicted in Figure 8b, SEM analysis reveals that the pristine Zn anode exhibits a relatively flat morphology. Following cycling in the neutral liquid electrolyte, unregulated ion diffusion leads to the disordered deposition of zinc, and the pronounced tip effect induces the formation of a small number of dendrites on the anode surface. Notably, the Zn anode cycled in the neutral PFCD hydrogel exhibits the smoothest surface morphology. This indicates that the synergy of carboxylate‐guided mass transport and electric field homogenization achieves superior stabilization of the anode interface. Furthermore, as shown in Figure S33a,b, no Fe signals or characteristic peaks are observed on the surface of the cycled Zn electrode, confirming that Fe3+ does not exist as free ions within the PFCD system. This indicates that no significant migration or deposition of Fe species from the PFCD to the Zn anode occurs during prolonged cycling. The kinetics of dendrite growth were further elucidated through in situ optical microscopy of the electrode cross‐section (Figure 8c). Upon continuous charging at 1 mA cm− 2, uneven dendrites are observed on the Zn surface in the neutral liquid electrolyte after just 40 min. Remarkably, the Zn anode protected by the neutral PFCD hydrogel maintains a uniform deposition layer throughout the entire testing period. As shown in Figure S34, the PFCD‐based Zn symmetric cell achieves stable cycling for over 500 h, demonstrating significantly superior performance compared to the symmetric cells based on PFC (40 h), PFD (69 h), and PCD (83 h). These findings confirm that the stabilizing effect of the PFCD stems from the synergistic interaction of its 3D cross‐linked network at the anode interface. By combining chemically guided uniform nucleation, spatial confinement within the 3D network, and dynamic adaptation to volume changes, the PFCD hydrogel effectively transforms Zn deposition from a disordered, destructive dendritic growth into an ordered, compact, and reversible process.

Conclusions

We engineered a unified PFCD hydrogel electrolyte featuring a hierarchical 3D physically cross‐linked network composed of Fe3 + ionic coordination centers, β‐CD hydrogen bonding centers, and N‐carboxyethyl chitosan (CEC), which reconciles the mechanical robustness‐interfacial adaptability trade‐off through strong metal coordination bonds supplemented by dynamic hydrogen bonds, yielding high tensile strength (40 kPa), exceptional stretchability (1600%), and robust electrode adhesion (∼50 kPa). Crucially, the incorporation of CEC shifts oxygen transport from passive diffusion to dipole‐facilitated directed migration, significantly accelerating reaction kinetics at the triple‐phase interface. The hydrogel also establishes ordered ion‐transport pathways via electrostatic interactions between carboxylate groups and [Zn(NH3)4]2 + ions, creating a spatially uniform electric field that regulates zinc deposition. These structural innovations deliver exceptional physicochemical properties, including an ionic conductivity of 113.2 mS cm− 1, an oxygen permeability of 4.1 Barrer, 99% self‐healing efficiency, and 500 h of zinc dendrite suppression. Consequently, flexible, neutral Zn‐air batteries utilizing this electrolyte demonstrate outstanding performance, achieving a 1.46 V open‐circuit voltage, a 59 mW cm− 2 peak power density, and ultra‐stable cycling over 500 h (3000 cycles). Furthermore, employing the hydrogel as both the electrolyte and an oxygen‐permeable membrane in a closed‐system design yields a leakage‐free, integrated device capable of stable operation for 1200 cycles. Overall, this work resolves persistent interfacial and stability challenges in conventional Zn‐air batteries, establishing a robust paradigm for designing multifunctional hydrogel electrolytes for next‐generation wearable energy storage.
