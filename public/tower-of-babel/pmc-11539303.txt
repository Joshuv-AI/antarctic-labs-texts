Prevalence of depression and anxiety in orthopedic residents: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Javad Amini, Arman Shafiee, Mohammad Sajjad Mirhoseini, Mahdi Mohammaditabar, Seyyed AmirHossein Salehi, Ghazal Abdarian, Dina Sadeghi, Mahboobeh Tajvidi, Mahmood Bakhtiyari, Ayad Bahadorimonfared
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopedics is one of the specialized fields of medicine and its residency has always been of interest. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the prevalence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents.MethodsDigital databases such as PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were systematically searched until July 27, 2024.ResultsThe analysis included a total of 10 studies for anxiety (1,758 cases, 374 anxiety cases) and depression (2,389 cases, 381 depression cases). The pooled prevalence of anxiety was 31.6% [95% CI: 18.04–45.26%] with significant publication bias (Egger’s test p = 0.0257). After removing a potential outlier, the prevalence of anxiety was recalculated as 25% [95% CI: 16.61–34.01%]. The pooled prevalence of depression was 22.2% [95% CI: 12.08–32.51%] with significant publication bias (Egger’s test p = 0.0434). After removing a potential outlier, the prevalence of depression was recalculated as 18.6% [95% CI: 10.27–26.92%], showing no significant publication bias (Egger’s test p = 0.1229).ConclusionDepression and anxiety are highly prevalent among orthopedic residents, with significant levels of psychological distress affecting nearly one-third of this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06213-4.Keywords: Orthopedic residents, Depression, Meta-analysis, Anxiety

Introduction

Concerns about depression and anxiety among medical residents are increasing, particularly those in surgical residencies, due to the demanding nature of routine and emergent surgical procedures [1]. The high prevalence of depression and anxiety in medical residents can be linked to several factors, such as working and studying simultaneously, as well as long working hours and shifts [2, 3]. These mental health problems can have adverse effects on both residents and patients, leading to increased medical errors, poor patient care, and higher rates of suicidal ideation, sleep deprivation, substance abuse disorders, poor social health and burn out [4–6]. Majority of residents experiencing affective disorders reported that their function is affected not only in health communications but also in their personal lives [2]. Prevalence of depression and depressive symptoms among medical residents was reported about 28.8% [5]. Orthopedic residents experience a remarkably high prevalence of depression and anxiety [4, 7].

Research suggests that the stressful nature of orthopedic residency plays a significant role in the development of these mental health issues [6, 8]. A study carried out in Mexico revealed a high occurrence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents, highlighting the influence of factors like remarkable work-related stress, such as the death of a patient and perfectionism as factors associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms [9, 10]. Moreover, another study explained that high level of depression and anxiety among United States and Canadian orthopedic surgery residents is deeply associated with an intense workload [7].

To our best of knowledge, there has been no systematic review investigating the prevalence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents. Therefore, we tried to systematically examine the prevalence in order to highlight the importance of mental health support to orthopedic residents, enabling them to effectively manage their psychological challenges.

Methods

The main purpose of this study is to review the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders in orthopedic residents globally. The PRISMA [11]. checklist was followed in this study for the purpose of transparency and accuracy. The study was registered in PROSPERO [12]. with the following number: CRD42024565619 (see Fig 1).

Flow diagram studies included in the review

Search strategy

Reviewers searched various databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. All publications were collected to 27 July 2024. The search terms was conducted using the combination of the following keywords: “depress*”, “anxi*”, “sleep”, “orthoped*”, and “residen*”.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were selected based on population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO]. framework. Population (p): orthopedic residents; Intervention (I): none; Comparison (c): anxiety, depression, and sleep disorder; and Outcome (o): prevalence of anxiety, depression, and sleep disorder among orthopedic residents.

Study selection

The selected studies were reviewed by two independent reviewers. Studies were screened based on title and abstract followed by full-text assessment ensuring the inclusion of relevant data. Any conflicts were resolved by consulting a third reviewer to reach an agreement. The first two reviewers were D.S and F.M and the third reviewer was M.J.A.

Data extraction

Relevant information was extracted from the selected studies, including the author’s name, country of origin, publication date, study design, total number of patients in each study, questionnaire, and outcome data.

Quality assessment

For the quality assessment of the included articles, The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS]. was used. The NOS for cross-sectional studies includes seven items within three domains, including selection, comparability, and outcome, with an overall score of nine. The selection domain has four questions and a maximum score of five scores. The comparability domain has a maximum score of one. The outcome domain has two questions and a maximum score of three scores. A score of 7–9 indicates high quality, 4–6 indicates high risk, and 0–3 indicates very high risk of bias. Two authors independently checked the Quality assessment, and any disagreements were resolved by a third author.

Data synthesis

The extracted data was synthesized using a random effects meta-analysis, synthesizing the overall prevalence rates of anxiety, depression, and sleep disorders among orthopedic residents. The pooled prevalence meta-analysis was meticulously conducted using the R ‘meta’ package. Both fixed and random-effect models were carefully considered for calculation. A funnel plot, a widely accepted method, was utilized to identify publication bias, and an Egger’s regression test was performed when applicable. A p-value of less than 0.05, a standard threshold, was considered significant. Heterogeneity was thoroughly evaluated using the I2 statistic, with an I2 greater than 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis, a robust technique, was carried out using the leave-one-out method. At last, a new forest and funnel plot without likely outlier studies was made. The results of these two forest plots, the culmination of our meticulous methodology, were reported.

Statistical analysis

The initial analysis for anxiety involved 10 studies [4, 6, 9, 13–19]. with a total of 1758 subjects, 374 of which were anxiety subjects. The results indicated that the prevalence of anxiety is 31.6% [95%-CI [0.1804; 0.4526] /p-value < 0.0001]. The leave-one-out analysis was used to detect outliers, and it showed that all the pooled estimates after each survey remained within the 95% confidence interval of the overall estimate. However, the funnel plot and Egger’s test results indicated the presence of funnel plot asymmetry [95%-CI [1.57–9.53] /p-value = 0.0257] (Fig. 2). A potential outlier detected by sensitivity analysis was removed, and a new calculation was performed. The results, based on 9 studies with a total of 1727 subjects (348 anxiety subjects), revealed that the prevalence of anxiety is 25% [95%-CI [0.1661; 0.3401] /p-value < 0.0001] (Fig. 3). This analysis showed a more symmetric funnel plot, and the results of Egger’s test was borderline significant [95%-CI [0.61–7.22] /p-value = 0.0533]. However, it should be noted that Egger’s test may not have sufficient statistical power to detect bias when there is a small number of studies (i.e., k < 10).

The initial analysis for depression included 11 studies with a total of 2389 subjects, out of which 381 were depression subjects. The results showed the prevalence of depression is 22.2% [95%-CI [0.1208; 0.3251] /p-value < 0.0001] (Fig. 4). After conducting a leave-one-out analysis to detect outliers, it was found that all the pooled estimates after removing one survey at a time were still within the 95% confidence interval of the overall estimate. However, the funnel plot and Egger’s test results indicated the presence of funnel plot asymmetry [95%-CI [0.68–7.56] /p-value = 0. 0434]. The potential outlier detected by sensitivity analysis was omitted, and the calculation was redone. The new results, which included 10 studies with a total of 2358 subjects (362 depression subjects), revealed that the prevalence of depression is 18.6% [95%-CI [0.1027; 0.2692]/p-value < 0.0001] (Fig. 5). This analysis had a more symmetric funnel plot, and the results of Egger’s test did not indicate the presence of funnel plot asymmetry [95%-CI [-0.47 -7.34] /p-value = 0. 1229].

Discussion

All surgical residencies, particularly orthopedic residency, are known to be significantly demanding [20]. The rigorous nature of these programs has led to the hypothesis that such challenging residencies may contribute to various mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, and burnout. Research indicates that mental health disorders can lead to irreversible medical errors and increase the risk of mortality and morbidity during procedures and postoperative care [21, 22]. It is estimated that approximately 20.9–50.9% of medical residents, including orthopedic residents, may experience depressive symptoms during their training [16]. Studies have shown that students and residents who live alone or have poor relationships with their loved ones are more susceptible to anxiety and depression. In contrast, living with family and having a strong support system can help prevent or alleviate these mental health issues [23]. The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to provide context for the prevalence of anxiety and depression among orthopedic residents by reviewing all the available data on this matter.

To assess the prevalence of anxiety, we initially included a total of 10 studies comprising 1,758 cases. However, following the identification of asymmetry in Egger’s test, we narrowed it down to 8 studies for final analysis. The results indicated that the prevalence of anxiety among orthopedic residents is 25%. To investigate the prevalence of depression, we initially compiled 10 studies. Again, due to asymmetry in Egger’s test, one study was excluded from the final analysis. The pooled data suggested that the prevalence of depression among orthopedic residents is 18.6%. Our study found a 25% prevalence of anxiety among orthopedic residents, which aligns with the higher end of the reported anxiety prevalence for young adults in general populations (15-30%). On the other hand [24], The prevalence of depressive disorders among young adults has been reported to be around 10–20%. For instance, studies like the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). have found rates often in the 15% range [25]. The prevalence of 18.6% depression among orthopedic residents is higher than the lower estimates of depression in the general young adult population but may be comparable to higher-end estimates (10-20%).

The challenging nature of many medical specialties has long suggested that mental health complications may be more prevalent in this population than in the general public. Numerous studies have been conducted to further explore this issue. Alarmingly, the number of suicides reported among medical students and residents has raised serious concerns. For instance, it has been reported that in the past year, a troubling number of 16 residents took their own lives in Iran [26]. A systematic review by Gianakos et al. assessed the negative effects of bullying, discrimination, harassment, and sexual harassment on the lives of residents in surgical specialties, including orthopedic surgery, general surgery, otolaryngology, gynecology, and others. The overall prevalence of anxiety and depression reported in their study was 33%, a figure comparable to what we observed in our research. Additionally, a significant concern is that attending surgeons and senior co-residents often contribute to significant pressure [27].

Mental health issues such as depression and anxiety are not only prevalent in surgical residencies but also among other medical residency programs. For example, a study conducted by Cecilia Kwok investigated the prevalence of depression, stress, and perceived medical errors among psychiatric residents in Singapore. The findings revealed a prevalence of depression at 38.3%, diagnosed with a PHQ-9 score of ≥ 10. An important association identified in this study was that depression correlated with sleeping less than 6 h per night and working 60 h or more per week [28]. A survey by François Jaulin et al. examined anxiety and depressive symptoms among anesthesia and intensive care residents, finding that 19.8% reported anxiety symptoms while 7.8% indicated depressive symptoms [29]. Moreover, training as a general physician or in medical school can be as stressful and challenging as surgical residency programs. A systematic review by Liselotte N. Dyrbye et al. assessed the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and other indicators of psychological distress among medical students in the U.S. and Canada. Although a precise pooled prevalence of depression and anxiety was not provided, the review suggests that levels of these issues among medical students are consistently higher than those in the general population and age-matched peers [30]. Lihong Chen et al. studied the risk factors for depression among training physicians in China and the United States. Their results indicated that high work hours, stressful life events outside the training program, and low sleep hours were significantly associated with an increase in depressive symptoms in both countries. One important factor reported among Chinese residents was experiencing violence by patients or patients’ relatives, witnessing violence and fear of violence. Moreover another factor that was contributing to the occurrence of depression in China but not in the US, was younger age. This could be due to the earlier starting time of residency in China than in the US [31]. Another significant factor that can greatly amplify anxiety and depression rates among orthopedic residents is immigration. International medical graduates pursuing residency programs in the US, not only struggle with the routine pressures of the program, but also have to cope with the hardships of immigration. This matter is further discussed in a study by Fares MY et al. [32].

As with any scientific endeavor, our study has limitations. First, the number of included studies was less than optimal, primarily due to a lack of original articles addressing the prevalence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents. Second, we acknowledge that the quality of the included studies may vary, with some potentially containing biases. To address this, we evaluated the quality of each study. Third, there may be a geographical bias, as most of the original articles were conducted in developed countries. Finally, some of the included studies in the present study, were carried out during the time of Covid-19 pandemic. This could have possibly overestimated the results in those articles.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. Firstly, this article is the first systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on the prevalence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents. Secondly, we employed a comprehensive search strategy utilizing various databases to ensure thoroughness. Thirdly, we adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). flowchart to maintain transparency and rigor in our research [11]. Finally, the included studies originate from diverse backgrounds and qualities. To account for this diversity, we assessed the quality of each article using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment.

Given the limited number of studies available, future research and surveys are essential for a more accurate understanding of the prevalence of depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents. Furthermore, serious efforts to identify preventative solutions are necessary. Investigating the prevalence of other mental health challenges, particularly burnout, is also of significant importance.

In conclusion, in this study, we compiled all relevant research on depression and anxiety among orthopedic residents and analyzed the pooled data statistically. The results of our study showed that based on the pooled data, the prevalence of anxiety and depression among orthopedic residents was 25% and 18.6%, respectively. To further assess these concerning numbers, first we have to find the contributing factors to the occurrence of depression and anxiety in orthopedic residents. This might be a topic for a future study. On another hand, implementing preventative measures, identifying those affected, and providing support towards recovery is of the utmost importance. Finding and establishing such measures would also require designing of another study.
