ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) is a severe condition with high short-term mortality. Identifying key predictors of poor outcomes is critical for guiding therapeutic strategies. Mean arterial pressure (MAP), a key determinant of tissue perfusion and systemic hemodynamics, may play a pivotal role in prognosis.METHODS:We analyzed a multicenter, prospective cohort of 323 patients hospitalized with AH, with validation in an independent cohort of 290 patients. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared with the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios for mortality, adjusted for Model for end stage liver disease score. Mechanistic insights were explored through assessment of cardiovascular biomarkers.RESULTS:A baseline MAP <80 mm Hg was associated with significantly higher mortality at both 28 days (27.3% vs 8.0%) and 90 days (40.3% vs 15.6%; P < 0.001 for both). This association persisted after adjusting for Model for end stage liver disease score, age, and baseline hepatic encephalopathy. The incidence of infections during follow-up was similar across MAP groups (27.6% vs 22.8%), but acute kidney injury occurred more frequently in the MAP <80 mm Hg group (62% vs 38%, P < 0.001). In the validation cohort, patients with MAP <80 mm Hg also demonstrated significantly lower 28-day transplant-free survival. Although B-type natriuretic peptide and plasma renin levels were altered in patients with AH, they did not correlate with MAP values.DISCUSSION:A baseline MAP <80 mm Hg is an independent predictor of short-term and mid-term mortality in patients with AH. Strategies aimed at restoring hemodynamic stability may improve outcomes in this high-risk population.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) is the leading cause of advanced liver disease worldwide (1). Alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) is a life-threating form of ALD characterized by a rapid onset of jaundice and is frequently associated with liver-related decompensations. Patients with AH have high 28-day mortality rate (10%–30%) (2). The model for end stage liver disease (MELD) score at admission accurately predicts survival in these patients, and helps classify them into severe or non-severe forms (3). Identifying the main drivers of early mortality could aid in the development of novel therapies. The presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) (4), early changes in total bilirubin (5), certain histologic features (6) and severity scores (7) can predict disease outcome, yet the current treatment (i.e. corticoids) only improved 28-day survival in a subset of patients (8). Early liver transplant, a relatively new strategy, is costly and indicated only in a minority of patients, with grim outcomes for those patients considered unsuitable for transplantation (9,10). Thus, identifying clinical parameters associated with poor outcomes remains an unmet need in this deadly disease.

Prospective studies show that nearly 80% of patients with AH have underlying cirrhosis (11). Decompensated cirrhosis is characterized by profound derangement in systemic circulation because of splanchnic vasodilatation and reduced effective vascular filling. In these patients, impaired systemic hemodynamics are related to the existence of portal hypertension, cardiac dysfunction and the presence of complications such as bacterial infections and gastrointestinal bleeding (12,13).

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is a major determinant of tissue perfusion. Below a threshold, tissue perfusion becomes linearly dependent on arterial pressure (14). Most studies in septic patients consider a MAP of 85 mm Hg as a target arterial pressure to ensure an adequate tissue perfusion (15–18). Achieving this target may decrease mortality and reduce the need for renal replacement therapies (15). Importantly, MAP demonstrated prognostic value in patients with advanced liver disease (19–21), as it is a reliable indicator of hemodynamic status and a key contributor to tissue perfusion (22,23). A MAP value ≤65 mm Hg is considered a sign of hypoperfusion and a marker of poor prognosis in severely ill patients (14,24). A recent study in critically ill cirrhotic patients has shown that low MAP at admission is independently associated with higher mortality, regardless of MELD-Na score (25). Furthermore, studies suggest that timely interventions to increase MAP (e.g., albumin, vasopressors) could improve outcomes, as cumulative time spent below a MAP threshold is linked to increased mortality in critically ill patients (25,26).

Given that many patients with AH have underlying cirrhosis, and are prone to SIRS and sepsis, because of the release of bacterial products and inflammatory cytokines (27), we hypothesize that these patients are particularly susceptible to the deleterious effects of low arterial pressure. This could contribute to tissue hypoperfusion, multiorgan damage, and early death. The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of hemodynamic parameters on 28-day and 90-day transplant-free survival in AH and explore possible mechanistic pathways.

METHODS

Design, setting, and participants

This international, observational, multicenter study prospectively identified patients with AH. Eleven liver or gastrointestinal liver units from Europe, North America, and Central America participated. The overarching goal of the InTeam consortium was to identify molecular targets in AH. This ancillary study specifically aimed to investigate the impact of hemodynamic parameters on 28-day and 90-day transplant-free survival in patients with AH, as well as to explore underlying mechanistic pathways. Individual participant data will not be shared for this study.

Patient selection and definitions

The study cohort included 323 patients. Inclusion criteria for the InTeam study (NCT02075918) were: age between 18 and 70 years, active alcohol use within the last 3 months, aspartate amino transferase > alanine amino transferase, elevated total bilirubin (>3 mg/dL), and either liver biopsy-proven or clinically consistent (definite or probable) AH (7). Patients must have received less than 3 days of specific treatment (e.g., corticosteroids and/or pentoxifylline) before inclusion. Severe AH was defined by a baseline MELD score ≥ 21. Exclusion criteria included previous diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma, terminal illness, and complete portal vein thrombosis. Patients were followed until day 90, death, or liver transplant. Medical evaluations occurred at inclusion, day 7 and/or hospital discharge, and at 30, 60, and 90 days. Demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical data were recorded at each visit. Each participating center obtained approval from their institutional ethics committee (coordinating center IRB PRO17040075) (Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537).

The validation cohort included 290 prospectively collected patients from 3 international academic gastrointestinal or liver units: Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau (Barcelona, Spain), University of Alabama at Birmingham School of Medicine (Alabama, USA), and Service d’Hépatologie, DHU Digest, Hôpital Beaujon, AP-HP (Clichy, France). The same inclusion and exclusion criteria than the study cohort were applied. Each center obtained institutional ethics approval (Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537).

At all time points, data collected included clinical history (demographics, previous diseases, prior episodes of acute decompensation), physical examination, laboratory results, presence of bacterial infections, and liver-related complications at admission and follow-up, such as ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), and acute kidney injury (AKI). Disease severity was assessed using MELD, Child–Pugh, and age-bilurubin-INR-creatinine (ABIC) prognostic scores, whereas the Lille score evaluated response to standard therapy.

Baseline MAP was defined as the first MAP value recorded at ward admission, which is the reference for all MAP mentions in the study. We assessed the optimal MAP cutoff using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and selected it based on the Youden index. The values with the highest Youden index were consistently clustered around 80 mm Hg. SIRS was defined as meeting 2 or more of the following criteria: temperature >38 °C or <36 °C, heart rate >90/min, respiratory rate >20/min or PaCO2 <32 mm Hg, and white blood cell count >12,000/mm3 or <4,000/mm3 (28) (Supplementary Material, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537). Proven infection included spontaneous bacteremia with positive cultures and no other septic focus, catheter-related bloodstream infection, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis with polymorphonuclear count in ascitic fluid >250/mm3 regardless of culture result, urinary tract infection confirmed by signs, symptoms, or cultures, respiratory infection or pneumonia diagnosed by clinical criteria or chest radiography, cellulitis, or other infections based on clinical assessment. Sepsis was defined as the presence of SIRS with a confirmed source of infection. AKI was classified according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria (29). Data from Alabama patients had limited available variables.

Mechanistic studies

For the mechanistic analysis, we targeted vasoactive systems indicative of cardiac function and plasma volume by measuring serum B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and plasma renin levels across 4 groups: patients with AH (n = 59) subdivided by MAP <80 mm Hg (n = 30) and ≥80 mm Hg (n = 29), patients with ALD cirrhosis (n = 17), patients with alcohol use disorder or heavy drinking without hepatic disease (n = 20), and a healthy control cohort (n = 41). Participants were drawn from the InLi study (IRB PRO18110273, NCT03915002) and AlcHepNet studies (IRBs 20190963, 20183231; NCT04072822) (Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537). Serum BNP and renin were measured using specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (MILLIPLEX MAP Human Cardiovascular Disease Magnetic Bead Panel 1 for pro-BNP and MILLIPLEX MAP Human Kidney Injury Magnetic Bead Panel 4 for renin; both from Merck), following manufacturer protocols.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with 95% confidence intervals or medians with interquartile ranges (P25–P75). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used the Student t-test or non-parametric tests, as appropriate. Categorical differences were assessed using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test. The primary end point was 28-day and 90-day transplant-free survival. Transplant-free survival was used as the composite outcome for survival analyses and analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank Mantel-Cox tests. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression identified mortality risk factors within the AH cohort. Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate models. Results are expressed as adjusted hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals. Cox proportional hazards regression models assessed mortality differences among groups, adjusting for MELD score. Model coefficients represent the change in expected log HR relative to changes in MELD, age, or HE category, holding other predictors constant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 21; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and RStudio (version 1.1.463, rms packages; Boston, MA). Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided 5% level.

RESULTS

The majority were men, with a median age of 49 years, and up to 65% presented with ascites at baseline. MELD score, HE, and age were significantly associated with mortality at 28 and/or 90 days (Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537). In multivariate analysis, only MELD score, aspartate amino transferase levels, and MAP <80 mm Hg remained independently associated with mortality at both time points (Table 2).

Impact of MAP on transplant-free survival

Overall transplant-free survival in the study cohort was 88.1% at 28 days and 78.4% at 90 days (Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537). Using a MAP cutoff of 80 mm Hg, patients with MAP <80 mm Hg had significantly higher mortality at 28 days (27.3% vs 8%) and 90 days (40.3% vs 15.6%) (P < 0.001) (Figure 1). To determine whether mortality differences were because of liver function or MAP, a Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for MELD was performed. Consistent with the overall findings, patients with MAP <80 mm Hg had higher HRs for mortality than those with MAP ≥80 mm Hg, independent of baseline MELD, age, and HE, at both 28 and 90 days (Figure 2a,b, Supplementary Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537).

Clinical outcomes by MAP

Neither infections nor SIRS were identified as mortality risk factors, and their distribution was similar across the cohort at baseline (Table 1). To investigate causes of increased mortality linked to MAP, we analyzed complications during follow-up. Incidence of infections was comparable between groups (27.6% in MAP ≥80 mm Hg vs 22.8% in MAP <80 mm Hg), whereas the incidence of AKI was significantly more frequent in the MAP <80 mm Hg group (62% vs 38%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure 6, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537).

Role of MAP in the external validation cohort

Patients in the validation cohort exhibited higher rates of liver-related and infection-related complications at baseline, as well as higher albumin and creatinine levels, without differences in sex, age, body mass index, or MELD score. Importantly, baseline MELD scores were similar between MAP groups.

Consistent with the study cohort, patients with MAP <80 mm Hg in the validation cohort had significantly lower transplant-free survival at 28 days (75.3% vs 85.9%, P = 0.03), with a trend toward lower survival at 90 days (68.8% vs 76.1%, P = 0.1) that did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3).

Mechanistic studies

To explore mechanisms underlying higher mortality in patients with MAP <80 mm Hg, serum levels of renin and BNP—markers of cardiac and activation of vasoconstrictive factors—were measured via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay in 4 patient subsets. Although no statistically significant differences were found, BNP concentrations were elevated in both AH groups, with slightly higher renin levels in patients with AH with MAP <80 mm Hg (Supplementary Figure 7, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B537). DISCUSSION

The mortality of AH is still high, and novel therapies are urgently needed. Although most studies have focused on the role of liver and multiorgan failure as well as infections, the role of hemodynamic disturbances is poorly known (30). In patients with decompensated cirrhosis, it is well known that splanchnic vasodilation and the resulting decrease in effective blood volume leads to hypotension, hyperdynamic circulation and contributes to poor patient outcomes (22,31). This is even more evident in patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) (32,33). Because long exposure to alcohol leads to severe cardiometabolic disturbances (34), and patients with severe AH often meet criteria of ACLF (35), we hypothesized that hemodynamic disturbances at admission would predict patient survival. In particular, we assessed the role of MAP as a prognostic factor in patients with AH. We hypothesized that these patients are particularly susceptible to the deleterious effects of low arterial pressure with an impact on vital prognosis. To test this hypothesis, we performed a study in a large international cohort with both study and validation groups of patients with well documented AH. For mechanistic studies, we used plasma from the AlcHepNet and InLi cohorts.

In severe critically ill patients, a MAP value of ≤ 65 mm Hg is considered a sign of hypoperfusion and a marker of poor prognosis, critical condition and requirement of urgent resuscitation (15). Our study identified a MAP cut off of 80 mm Hg as a predictor of 28-day and 90-day mortality risk factor independently of the underlying MELD, age and presence of baseline HE. We also confirmed these finding in an international prospectively collected cohort of patients. Our data suggested that strategies to increase MAP over 80 mm Hg in patients with AH could potentially have a survival benefit in their survival outcome. These measures could include drugs such as albumin infusion or vasopressors, as studies suggest that the time spent below a MAP threshold directly impacts patient survival. Importantly, in our study, MAP was defined as the first measurement obtained at ward admission, which may partly reflect prior interventions. Nevertheless, its simplicity and accessibility provide meaningful clinical value, particularly in real-world practice. Controlled clinical trials assessing the role of albumin with or without terlipressin in the outcome of patients with severe AH are anticipated.

Low MAP in cirrhosis results from a combination of cirrhotic cardiomyopathy, alcohol-related myocardial dysfunction, and profound systemic vasodilatation driven by nitric oxide and inflammatory mediators (36–38). This hemodynamic profile is further exacerbated in ACLF, a condition frequently precipitated by alcohol and often developed in the course of AH (35), where infections or sterile SIRS amplify systemic inflammation and vascular dysregulation, leading to reduced effective arterial blood volume and renal hypoperfusion (39).

Although optimal MAP targets in this population are undefined, insights from septic shock suggest potential benefits of individualized hemodynamic management a target minimum of 65 mm Hg has been proposed (40). The ALBIOS trial showed that MAP target of 80–85 mm Hg vs 60–65 mm Hg in septic shock reduce AKI and renal replacement therapy although no clear benefits were showed on survival (15). Given the frequent coexistence of arterial stiffness and relative adrenal insufficiency in advanced cirrhosis (41), similar MAP targets may merit evaluation to mitigate renal dysfunction in ACLF, though the risk of increased vasopressor exposure must be balanced against potential benefits.

To explore potential mechanisms leading to arterial hypotension in patients with AH, we explored biomarkers indicative of cardiac function and effective blood volume. For this purpose, we studied BNP and renin that reflect heart function and arterial filling. Both parameters have been extensively studied in patients with decompensated cirrhosis (42,43). Surprisingly, although these factors were deregulated in patients with AH compared with controls, they did differ from patients with and without arterial hypotension but the administration of fluids, vasoactive substances, diuretics and other drugs as well as the relatively small sample size of the mechanistic analyses (BNP and renin) complicates the interpretation and may limit the statistical power and, consequently, the ability to detect significant differences between groups.

The role of these markers in predicting poor outcomes warrants further investigation. In particular, exploring their relationship with other markers of cardiac or vasodynamic dysfunction could help clarify their mechanistic roles. It is conceivable that other factors such as circulating cytokines, well know vasodilators, play a role in this effect (44). In fact, we previously showed that systemic inflammatory response, either infection-associated or sterile, is associated with higher mortality in these patients (27).

Several limitations have been identified in the study, being one of the most important the observational design. A randomized controlled trial would be ideal to confirm these findings and assess the impact of early interventions to raise MAP. Although the study includes a diverse international cohort, there may be regional differences in clinical practices or patient characteristics that affect the generalizability of the findings. For example, the use of corticosteroids or other treatments might vary across different healthcare systems. Nevertheless, the key point of the study is the identification of a MAP <80 mm Hg before any intervention has been performed as a transplant free-survival determinant. Although attempts were made to control for confounders such as disease severity, the study design and the sample size limited this capacity. MAP, renal dysfunction, and overall disease severity are intrinsically linked, reflecting a shared pathophysiological process characterized by systemic vasodilation, reduced effective arterial volume, and compensatory vasoconstriction. This process drives renal hypoperfusion and AKI, indicating that low MAP is not merely a marker but part of the causal pathway leading to worse outcomes.

In summary, this study demonstrates that low MAP is an independent and robust predictor of early mortality in patients with AH, independent of liver function severity (MELD score). It also underscores the need for closer monitoring and potential intervention to improve hemodynamic status in these critically ill patients. If these findings are validated in larger cohorts, MAP could become an essential parameter in the management of AH, potentially guiding therapeutic strategies aimed at improving circulatory status and reducing early mortality.
