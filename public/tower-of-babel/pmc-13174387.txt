Urine protein electrophoresis analysis in dogs with systemic inflammatory response syndrome
Authors: Rankyung Jung, Hyeona Bae, Kyu-Woan Cho, Jinho Park, Do Hyeon Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerum biomarkers can underestimate renal damage in dogs with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo identify differences in proteinuria patterns between dogs with and without SIRS using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and evaluate its value as an early diagnostic and prognostic indicator.AnimalsClient-owned, proteinuric dogs with (n = 10) and without (n = 21) SIRS.MethodsIn this retrospective study, urine electropherograms from SDS-PAGE were used to identify and quantify low molecular weight (LMW) and high molecular weight (HMW) protein bands. The LMW/HMW ratio was calculated for each dog and analyzed for correlations with the acute patient physiologic and laboratory evaluation (APPLE) full (APPLEfull) and fast (APPLEfast) scores and death within 2, 4, and 8 weeks.ResultsThe LMW/HMW ratio and the proportion of proteins with molecular weights of 20-30 kDa were significantly higher in the SIRS than in the non-SIRS group (P < .001 and P = .018). The LMW/HMW ratio correlated with inflammatory (C-reactive protein, globulin, white blood cell, and band neutrophil counts) and renal markers (blood urea nitrogen and symmetric dimethylarginine; rho = 0.381-0.786, all P < .05). The LMW/HMW ratio showed a high predictive value for death within 2 (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.844, 95%CI, 0.597-1.091), 4 (AUC = 0.867, 95%CI, 0.682-1.041), and 8 weeks (AUC = 0.944, 95%CI, 0.802-1.087).Conclusions and clinical importanceAn increased LMW/HMW ratio, reflecting elevated LMW proteinuria, can be a reliable diagnostic and prognostic indicator for early renal damage in dogs with SIRS.Keywords: multiorgan dysfunction syndrome, proteinuria, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, tubular damage

Introduction

Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) is an exaggerated immune response to stressors such as acute inflammation, ischemia, reperfusion, or malignancy. Dysregulated cytokine storms trigger a detrimental inflammatory cascade, leading to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS).1,2 Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome is a severe complication associated with poor prognosis in dogs with critical illnesses. However, there are no universally accepted diagnostic criteria for MODS in veterinary medicine.2 Renal dysfunction is a severe complication of SIRS that can result in higher fluid and vasopressor requirements, more rapid MODS progression, and higher morbidity and risk of death.3 Its prognosis is poor, with only 14% of dogs with septic peritonitis and concurrent renal dysfunction being discharged, and 45.8% of septic dogs with acute kidney injury (AKI) surviving.4

Serum creatinine (sCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) are markers of renal function. However, they are nonspecific and insensitive for early diagnosis of kidney injuries: sCr levels might not rise until approximately 75% of glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is lost, and SDMA might not increase until a 40% reduction in GFR.5,6 Moreover, tubular damage might occur without reduced GFR, further complicating early detection.7 Therefore, ongoing research in human critical-care medicine has focused on urinary biomarkers, particularly urinary proteins, as potential early indicators of renal damage and tubular injury. However, there is limited research on the relationship between proteinuria and SIRS in veterinary medicine. Proteinuria can be categorized as pre-renal, renal, and post-renal. Renal proteinuria can be subdivided based on its origin into glomerular and tubular.8 Conventional methods for diagnosing proteinuria, such as the urine dipstick, urine protein-to-creatinine (UPC) ratio, and sulfosalicylic acid (SSA) turbidimetric tests, are primarily qualitative or semiquantitative and cannot locate the origin of proteinuria.9 Location of injury is typically detected by renal biopsy or urine protein electrophoresis (UPE).9,10 Renal biopsy is the gold standard for lesion location, but its clinical application is limited by invasiveness, anesthetic requirements, risks of infection and bleeding, discomfort, and high cost. Urinary casts, such as granular, waxy, and cellular casts, and fractional excretion of electrolytes can serve as biomarkers of tubular injury, with low sensitivity and specificity.5 Contrarily, UPE using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) can help determine the origin of proteinuria based on the molecular weight of urinary proteins, thereby suggesting the damage is of glomerular or tubular origin. Urine protein electrophoresis findings might correlate with histopathological results.

This method enables differentiation of glomerular, tubular, and mixed patterns of proteinuria without the disadvantages of kidney biopsy mentioned above.11 It differentiates types of kidney injury, assesses severity, and identifies the early stages of ambiguous renal injury through qualitative and semiquantitative approaches to lesion location.10,12

We analyzed proteinuria in dogs with SIRS using UPE by evaluating the low molecular weight (LMW) to high molecular weight (HMW) protein (LMW/HMW) ratio, and identified its diagnostic and prognostic values for proteinuria by assessing relationships between the LMW/HMW ratio and clinicopathological data, disease severity, and case fatality rates.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study involved retrospective analysis of clinical characteristics, acute patient physiologic and laboratory evaluation (APPLE) full (APPLEfull) and fast (APPLEfast) scores, clinicopathological variables, and electrophoresis of urine protein of dogs assessed at the time of presentation. It was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC no., GNU-240528-D0116).

Case selection

The complete medical records, including urinalysis results, of dogs that visited the institution between 2019 and 2023 and had proteinuria were retrospectively reviewed. Urine samples were collected and stored at the time of initial presentation as part of routine diagnostic procedures, which include storing residual urine for potential follow-up testing or retrospective analysis. Cases were selected retrospectively based on the availability of these stored urine samples and the inclusion criteria described below. From these dogs, samples with proteinuria were identified, and dogs were subsequently categorized into SIRS and non-SIRS groups according to previously established SIRS criteria, which required the presence of at least 2 of the following: hypo- or hyperthermia (body temperature < 37.8°C or > 39.4°C), tachycardia (heart rate > 140 bpm), tachypnea (respiratory rate > 20 breaths/min), and leukopenia (<6000 cells/μL), leukocytosis (>16 000 cells/μL), or the presence of > 3% band neutrophils.13 Dogs with borderline or overt renal proteinuria (UPC ≥ 0.2) were included and followed for at least 2 months after the initial visit; pre- and post-renal causes were excluded based on urinalysis, clinicopathological data, and diagnostic imaging. All urine samples were collected via cystocentesis.

Cases were excluded if urinalysis revealed hematuria, pyuria, or bacteriuria.14 Hematuria was defined as gross discoloration or more than 5 red blood cells per high-power field (40× magnification) on sediment examination. Pyuria was defined as more than 5 white blood cells per high-power field (40× magnification). Bacteriuria was confirmed if bacteria were observed on sediment examination or the culture results were positive (Figure 1).

Urine sample collection and protein electrophoresis

Urine was collected aseptically via ultrasound-guided cystocentesis in order to ensure consistency in collection technique and minimize contamination. Dipstick analysis, urine cytology, culture, and UPC ratio were performed within 30 min of collection as part of routine diagnostics. The remaining urine was centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min within 1 h after collection; only the supernatant was separated and stored at −80°C. Long-term freezing of residual urine is part of the hospital’s standard sample-retention protocol, allowing for quality-controlled retrospective analyses such as UPE. The urine protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kits; Thermo Scientific; Waltham, MS, USA), and the urine was diluted with distilled water to reach a protein concentration of 100 mg/dL. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was conducted in 10% self-made polyacrylamide gels comprising 10% separation gel (distilled water, 4% resolving gel buffer, 10% SDS, monomer stock solution, 10% ammonium persulfate, and TEMED) and 4% stacking gel (distilled water, stacking gel buffer, monomer stock solution, 10% ammonium persulfate, and TEMED). Electrophoresis was performed using a Mini-Protean Electrophoresis system (BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA, USA) for 3 h at 60-80 V, and the gels were immersed in a staining solution (Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA) for 30 min. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The proteins were categorized into HMW and LMW using a reference molecular weight of 69 kDa, and the ratio of the area occupied by LMW proteins to that occupied by HMW proteins (LMW/HMW ratio) was calculated.15 The relative area of proteins with molecular weights between 20 and 30 kDa was determined by calculating the proportion of the 20-30 kDa band area relative to the total area of all bands. The HMW/LMW cut-off (42 kDa) was determined by overlaying representative electropherograms to visually identify the most consistent separation point between HMW and LMW protein bands (Figure 2).

Clinical data collection

Data, including breed, age, sex, body weight, medical history, clinical signs, physical and neurological examinations, diagnosis, APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores, and clinicopathological evaluation, were obtained from the medical records. A comprehensive laboratory examination was performed for all dogs included in the study to assess their overall clinical condition and proteinuria. The examination comprised a complete blood count (Procyte Dx Hematology Analyzer; IDEXX, Westbrook, ME, USA), acid–base balance and electrolyte concentration analyses (Nova pHOX Analyzer; Nova Biochemical, Waltham, MA, USA), serum biochemical analysis (Catalyst Dx Chemistry Analyzer; IDEXX) including C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, total protein, γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), SDMA, BUN, sCr, phosphorus, and canine pancreas-specific lipase (Spec cPL; IDEXX). The coagulation tests included partial thromboplastin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) (Coag Dx, IDEXX).

Urinalysis, including the evaluation of gross appearance, urine specific gravity (Reichert Rhino VET 360 Refractometer; Reichert Technologies; Buffalo, NY, USA), urine dipstick analysis (VetLab UA Analyzer and UA Strips; IDEXX), SSA turbidimetric test,16 urine sediment examination, urine culture, and UPC ratio (Catalyst One; IDEXX), was conducted within 30 min of sample collection.

Disease severity score and outcome

The APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores were evaluated based on data obtained at the time of admission. Both are used to assess disease severity in dogs with critical illness, with higher scores indicating greater risks of death.17 Death was assessed within 2, 4, and 8 weeks.

Statistical analysis

The sex, age, and disease distributions of the SIRS and non-SIRS groups were analyzed using multiple linear regression. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to identify statistical differences between the clinicopathological data of the non-SIRS and SIRS groups. Pearson’s correlation test was used to examine the relationships between the clinicopathological data; APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores; death within 2, 4, and 8 weeks; and LMW/HMW ratio. Sulfosalicylic acid turbidimetric test is a semiquantitative test, recorded based on numerical scores (1+ to 4+), which were then converted to corresponding numerical values (ie, 1-4) for statistical comparison and analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the predictive values of the LMW/HMW ratio and other clinicopathological variables that were significantly associated with risk of death. The cut-off value of the LMW/HMW ratio was set to 1.25 from the ROC curve, optimizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity. The cut-off values provided by IDEXX Procyte Dx Hematology Analyzer (Catalyst Dx), Nova pHOX Analyzer, and left shift for band neutrophils (defined as > 1000 nonsegmented neutrophils/μL) were used for other clinicopathological variables to assess their diagnostic values for predicting death after 2, 4, and 8 weeks. Area under the curve (AUC) was interpreted as excellent when ≥ 0.9, considerable at 0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9, fair at 0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8, poor at 0.6 ≤ AUC < 0.7, and failure at 0.5 ≤ AUC < 0.6.18 Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to compare the survival durations based on clinicopathological variables, APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores, and the LMW/HMW ratio. P-values < .05 were significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0 for Windows, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and graphical procedures were performed using GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Characteristics of animals and comparison of the clinical data

Thirty-one dogs in the non-SIRS (n = 21) and SIRS (n = 10) groups were included. Breeds in the non-SIRS group included Maltese (n = 5), Poodle (n = 4), Mixed breed (n = 2), Miniature Schnauzer (n = 2), and 1 each of 7 other breeds. The SIRS group included Maltese (n = 3), Jindo (n = 3), Poodle (n = 3), and Shih Tzu (n = 1). The mean ages (±SD) were 10.4 ± 2.8 years (range, 1-14 years) for the non-SIRS group and 7.4 ± 3.2 years (range, 2-13 years) for the SIRS group. Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the dogs in the non-SIRS group were significantly older than those in the SIRS group (P = .013). Underlying diseases in the non-SIRS group included neoplasia (n = 10: including lymphoma, mast cell tumor, and carcinoma), myxomatous mitral valve disease (n = 6), and other conditions such as liver cirrhosis and pancreatitis. In the SIRS group, diagnoses included immune-mediated diseases (n = 2), infectious diseases (n = 2; heartworm and babesiosis), lymphoma (n = 1), pancreatitis (n = 1), and other inflammatory conditions. However, there were no significant differences in sex or disease distribution between the SIRS and non-SIRS groups. The absolute band neutrophil count was significantly higher in the SIRS group compared to that in the non-SIRS group (0.320 ± 0.127 vs 0.187 ± 0.172 × 109/L, P = .001). The monocyte count was higher in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (1.8 ± 0.6 vs 1.1 ± 0.6 × 109/L, P = .004). The eosinophil count was significantly lower in the SIRS group compared to that in the non-SIRS group (0.2 ± 0.1 vs 0.4 ± 0.8 × 109/L, P = .009). The serum CRP concentration was significantly higher (P < .000), and the serum albumin concentration (P = .007) was significantly lower in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (8.6 ± 0.7 vs 1.7 ± 0.7 mg/L; 2.4 ± 0.1 g/dL vs 3.1 ± 0.1 g/dL, respectively). The sodium concentration was significantly lower (P = .012) in the SIRS group (136.9 ± 2.6) than in the non-SIRS group (147.2 ± 1.1 mmol/L) (Figure 3A). However, no significant differences were observed in other hematological, serum biochemical, acid–base, and coagulation variables between the 2 groups. The APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores were significantly higher (P = .001; P < .001) in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (16.4 ± 7.6 vs 2.5 ± 0.8; 39.0 ± 5.3 vs 8.9 ± 1.9, respectively) (Figure 3B). Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio was 1.1 ± 0.3 in the non-SIRS and 4.5 ± 1.3 in the SIRS groups (P = .003), and SSA scores were 1.8 ± 0.3 in the non-SIRS and 3.3 ± 0.5 in the SIRS groups (P = .001) (Figure 3C). However, the 2 groups showed no significant differences in the dipstick results or urine specific gravity.

Comparison of SDS-PAGE data between the groups

The LMW/HMW ratio was calculated based on the quantification of LMW and HMW proteins separated by SDS-PAGE; it was significantly higher in the SIRS group compared to that in the non-SIRS group (1.8 ± 0.6 and 0.6 ± 0.4, respectively, P < .001) (Figure 4). The relative area occupied by proteins with molecular weights of 20-30 kDa, calculated as the ratio of the 20-30 kDa band area to the total area of all bands, was significantly higher in the SIRS group than in the non-SIRS group (17.8% ± 5.6% vs 11.0% ± 7.7%, P = .018) (Figure 5). The LMW/HMW ratio significantly and positively correlated with total WBC count (ρ = 0.381, P = .038), band neutrophil absolute count (ρ = 0.411, P = .041), monocyte count (ρ = 0.667, P < .001), serum globulin concentration (ρ = 0.561, P = .003), CRP (ρ = 0.786, P < .001), BUN (ρ = 0.418, P = .034), SDMA (ρ = 0.430, P = .022), phosphorus concentration (ρ = 0.455, P = .022), and ALP (ρ = 0.376, P = .041). Contrarily, it negatively correlated with serum albumin concentration (ρ = −0.492, P = .009) and sodium concentration (ρ = −0.452, P = .011). However, other hematological indicators, including neutrophil, lymphocyte, and eosinophil counts; platelet count; and serum total protein, sCr, ALT, GGT, lactate, glucose, ionized calcium, potassium, chloride, and magnesium concentrations did not show significant correlations with the LMW/HMW ratio. Furthermore, pancreas-specific lipase test results and the coagulation variables, including citrate-aPTT and PT did not significantly correlate with the LMW/HMW ratio.

To assess disease severity, both APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores were calculated for all dogs. Both scores significantly and positively correlated with the LMW/HMW ratio (ρ = 0.540, P = .006 and ρ = 0.661, P < .001, respectively) (Table 1). The 2-week case fatality rate significantly correlated with the LMW/HMW ratio (ρ = 0.498; P = .004), APPLEfull score (ρ = 0.667; P < .001), and APPLEfast score (ρ = 0.481; P = .017). Inflammatory variables, including band neutrophil count (ρ = 0.781; P < .001) and neutrophil count (ρ = 0.519; P = .003), and kidney variables such as BUN (ρ = 0.805; P < .001), phosphorus (ρ = 0.776; P < .001), SDMA (ρ = 0.592; P = .001), and sCr (ρ = 0.462; P = .009), positively correlated with the LMW/HMW ratio.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to assess the predictive value of the LMW/HMW ratio and other clinicopathological variables for death. The serum phosphorus concentration (AUC = 1.000), APPLEfast score (AUC = 1.000), sCr (AUC = 0.969), neutrophil count (AUC = 0.938), total WBC count (AUC = 0.906), and BUN (AUC = 0.906) had excellent predictive values, while the APPLEfull score (AUC = 0.875), LMW/HMW ratio (AUC = 0.844), and SDMA (AUC = 0.813) had considerable values in predicting death at 2 weeks (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, dogs with SIRS exhibited a significantly higher LMW/HMW ratio compared to dogs without SIRS, indicating tubular proteinuria. Furthermore, this ratio demonstrated high prognostic value for predicting death in dogs with SIRS.

A significantly higher LMW/HMW ratio in the SIRS group indicated higher concentration of urinary protein originating from the tubules in dogs with systemic inflammatory conditions. This is consistent with a previous report of a greater number of LMW bands in dogs with severe inflammation.19 Systemic inflammatory response syndrome can induce tubular damage, leading to worsening LMW proteinuria.

Low molecular weight urinary proteins are valuable markers of tubular injury. Proteins such as retinol-binding protein (RBP) (21 kDa), β2-microglobulin (11.8 kDa), α1-microglobulin (27 kDa), cystatin C (13 kDa), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) (25 kDa), and lysozyme (15-16 kDa) have been identified as potential biomarkers in previous studies.20,21 In our study, analysis of protein bands, particularly in the 20-30 kDa range, revealed an higher in the protein concentration within this range in the SIRS group relative to that in the non-SIRS group. The urinary proteins with molecular weights in the 20-30 kDa range include RBP, α1-microglobulin, and NGAL.20,21 Higher urinary RBP concentration is associated with the progression of SIRS in dogs, while higher α1-microglobulin and NGAL concentrations are observed in human cases of SIRS and sepsis.19,22,23 A specific protein within the 20-30 kDa molecular weight range was not identified; however, the higher in proteins of this range might be closely associated with elevated urinary protein concentrations observed in dogs with SIRS. Moreover, the LMW/HMW ratio correlated with various inflammatory variables and renal markers of reduced renal function. Although these correlations do not directly confirm the presence of tubular injury, the observed higher in LMW proteins in the urine might suggest a disruption in tubular reabsorption. Since these dogs were affected by severe systemic inflammation, the presence of tubular proteins could reflect a combined effect of inflammatory burden and altered renal function. The LMW/HMW ratio did not significantly correlate with the serum creatinine concentration, which might be because creatinine generally rises only after more substantial declines in GFR compared with other renal biomarkers, such as SDMA.6 These findings suggests that the LMW/HMW ratio can be a novel biomarker for inflammation and renal diseases in dogs with SIRS.

The LMW/HMW ratio correlated with disease severity, indicated by the APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores. APPLE scoring models are used to assess overall disease severity in dogs with critical illness. While higher APPLE scores do not directly indicate worsening tubular proteinuria, the observed correlation in our study suggests that tubular proteinuria might increase in parallel with systemic disease severity.17 This is consistent with the results of the ROC curve analysis, which showed a significant association between the LMW/HMW ratio and death by the second, fourth, and eighth weeks.

The molecular weight criteria for distinguishing HMW (originating from the glomerulus) and LMW (originating from the tubules) proteins vary and are not well established; the values are 70, 69, 60, and 40 kDa.10,12,15,24 A threshold of 42 kDa was determined when electropherograms were overlaid. The LMW/HMW ratio at a threshold of 42 kDa also correlated positively with inflammatory markers, including monocyte count, band neutrophil count, and CRP concentration. This also positively correlated with renal indicators, such as BUN and phosphorus concentrations, and with the severity of the disease, as measured by the APPLEfull and APPLEfast scores. We set a cut-off of 0.44 for the LMW/HMW ratio, which was associated with death within 2 and 8 weeks, as determined by ROC curve analysis. This suggests that a threshold of 42 kDa (as well as 69 kDa) might be valuable for diagnosing inflammation and assessing renal damage as a cut-off value for the LMW/HMW ratio. These results suggest that tubular proteinuria is associated with disease severity and death in dogs with SIRS.

The case fatality rate of dogs with SIRS without MODS is 25%, significantly increasing to 70% when MODS is present.25 Coagulation is most frequently affected (60.5%), followed by hepatic (49.1%), respiratory (31.6%), cardiovascular (17.5%), and renal (12.3%) disorders.25 In cases of severe inflammation, kidney tubular damage can result from or be caused by ischemia, reperfusion, toxicosis, dysregulated inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin (IL)-6, and pathogenic molecules such as pathogen-associated molecular patterns, especially lipopolysaccharide. Damage to other organs can lead to the release of nephrotoxic or pro-ischemic molecules. This mechanism mimics the interorgan communication described in hepatorenal or cardiorenal syndromes and contributes to renal tubular epithelial injury.26–28 When renal epithelial cells are damaged, cellular polarity is disrupted. This leads to apoptosis, necrosis, and eventual cell death. This disruption of cellular cohesion results in cell detachment and leakage, ultimately leading to a decrease in GFR. Consequently, sCr, BUN, and SDMA levels increase. However, these serum markers lack sensitivity and specificity for early diagnosis of renal injury. Conversely, urinary proteins manifest in the before cell death phase of cellular damage, suggesting their potential value as early diagnostic indicators of renal injury.28 An increase in the urine concentrations of LMW proteins, such as NGAL, occurs before an increase in serum creatinine and BUN in cases of parvovirus infection in dogs.6 This finding supports the notion that urinary protein biomarkers are more sensitive and specific than serum biomarkers in detecting renal injury in dogs.

In humans, AKI associated with sepsis is more frequently detected because renal injury is extensively monitored using standardized AKI criteria and sensitive biomarkers.29 In our study, an increase in tubular proteinuria was observed even in noninfectious SIRS, suggesting a higher likelihood of tubular proteinuria in dogs with severe inflammation without infection, which is associated with higher case fatality rates. Although tubular cell damage such as necrosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis occurs in systemic inflammatory conditions, marked tubular proteinuria in the absence of sepsis was an unexpected finding. Histopathological examination reveals vacuolization of tubular cells and mitochondrial rarefaction, which are associated with autophagy and mitophagy, respectively. The kidney damage caused by systemic inflammatory conditions is related to cell dysfunction rather than cell death.26 Given that proteinuria increases in early stages of renal injury before cell death occurs, the degree of tubular damage was more frequently assessed in this study than in previous studies that used histopathology. Although we did not perform histopathological evaluation, our findings suggest that evidence of tubular damage might be detectable earlier by UPE than histopathology.

The dogs in the non-SIRS group had a higher mean age compared to those in the SIRS group. This age difference likely reflects the types of underlying diseases present in each group. In the non-SIRS group, approximately 47.6% (n = 10) of the dogs were diagnosed with neoplasia, while only 10% (n = 1) of the dogs in the SIRS group had this condition. Since proteinuria increases with age and is commonly observed in dogs with cancer, the presence of proteinuria in the non-SIRS group might be more closely related to age or neoplastic changes.30

Only 1 of 10 dogs with SIRS and renal proteinuria demonstrated an increase in the concentration of sCr above the reference range. No significant differences were observed in renal function indicators in the SIRS and non-SIRS groups, including sCr, BUN, SDMA levels, and urine specific gravity. However, the UPC ratio and SSA turbidimetric test results were significantly higher in the SIRS than in the non-SIRS group, indicating a higher degree of proteinuria in dogs with SIRS. This suggests that proteinuria might serve as a more sensitive indicator of renal involvement in dogs with SIRS than serum renal biomarkers such as sCr or BUN. Conventional renal biomarkers might be insufficient to diagnose renal damage; this indicates the necessity of proteinuria as an additional renal assessment variable. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing significantly elevated UPC and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratios in dogs with SIRS.19 Contrary to the significant differences detected between the UPC ratio and SSA test results of the SIRS and non-SIRS groups, there were no significant differences between the urine dipstick results in the 2 groups. Two possibilities were considered for this observation. First, dipsticks only detect albuminuria above 30 mg/dL and cannot detect lower concentrations of urinary protein. In addition, urine dipsticks can only detect albuminuria, unlike the SSA test that can detect all types of proteins including LMW proteins. Therefore, tubular proteinuria cannot be detected when urine dipsticks are used.31 Although there are several indicators that can suggest tubular kidney injury, there is currently no widely accepted, noninvasive method to definitively confirm active tubular injury in veterinary medicine. Urine casts in urine sediment and fractional excretion of electrolytes as markers of tubular injury have low sensitivity and specificity for early diagnosis of kidney injury.5 In this study, a correlation was observed between the SSA and LMW/HMW ratios (ρ = 0.480; P = .007), indicating that the SSA test can reflect the extent of tubular proteinuria. This suggests that using the SSA test to assess proteinuria, instead of relying solely on urine dipstick analysis, has greater diagnostic value in dogs with suspected tubular injury, such as those with SIRS.

In addition, a negative correlation between sodium concentration and the LMW/HMW ratio was observed when comparing the blood gas analysis results with the LMW/HMW ratio and case fatality rate. This association might reflect mechanisms similar to those described in human studies, where hyponatremia is linked to severe systemic inflammation and increased risk of death.32,33 This link might be mediated by the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6, and the release of antidiuretic hormone.33 This finding aligns with the results of our present study that sodium concentration correlated with inflammatory variables such as band neutrophil count (ρ = −0.486; P = .014), neutrophil count (ρ = −0.372; P = .043), CRP concentration (ρ = −0.546; P = .029), and albumin concentration (ρ = 0.701; P < .000), as well as case fatality rates within 2 (ρ = −0.552; P = .001), 4 (ρ = −0.577; P = .001), and 8 (ρ = −0.633; P < .000) weeks. This study proposes an association between hyponatremia and inflammatory markers, which are linked to higher case fatality rates. Regarding ionized magnesium concentrations, an increase in magnesium concentration was associated with death within 2, 4, and 8 weeks. This finding is consistent with the previous report that hypermagnesemia developed in up to 13% of dogs with critical illness and was associated with a high case fatality rate.34

Furthermore, GGT was associated with the 4- and 8-week case fatality rates. Research on the relationship between GGT and systemic inflammation in veterinary medicine is lacking, but blood GGT activity increases in humans, because of oxidative damage caused by systemic inflammation, and the increase is potentially associated with cardiovascular disease.35

The limitations of this study include the small sample size, which might increase the risk of statistical error and limit the generalizability of the findings, and absence of histopathological findings to confirm kidney damage and location. Kidney function in dogs is typically assessed using serum creatinine concentration and breed-specific reference intervals for serum creatinine were not considered. Renal biopsy is necessary to evaluate kidney function in dogs with normal creatinine concentrations but increased tubular proteinuria, as observed in this study. No evidence of infection was found in dogs with SIRS included in this study, but infectious and noninfectious SIRS could not be differentiated through additional testing. Further investigation is needed to differentiate between sepsis and noninfectious SIRS to understand the mechanism of tubular proteinuria and renal damage in systemic inflammation. Furthermore, the cases in this study had solely proteinuria levels measured upon presentation, and information on proteinuria in dogs with SIRS before the onset of systemic inflammation was lacking. In addition, the resolution was optimal for proteins with molecular weights of 18-75 kDa due to the characteristics of the 10% SDS-PAGE. This limited the detection of urine proteins with molecular weights of 20 kDa or less. Cystatin C and β2-microglobulin, with weights of 11-14 kDa, might not have been detected. Given that proteins with lower molecular weights, such as those of 12 or 15 kDa, reflect severe tubulointerstitial damage in the kidney, identifying them in dogs with systemic inflammation can be useful.14

In conclusion, proteinuria in dogs with SIRS is associated with renal tubular injury and can serve as a biomarker for the diagnosis of renal injury. In addition, proteinuria can serve as an early diagnostic indicator of renal damage and should be considered when evaluating MODS in dogs with critical illness. Furthermore, worsening tubular proteinuria can be a negative prognostic indicator in dogs with SIRS. Therefore, UPE via SDS-PAGE has potential diagnostic and prognostic values for early detection of renal damage in dogs with proteinuria.
