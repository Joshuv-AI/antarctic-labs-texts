An immuno-chemotherapeutic bone scaffold for tumor eradication and bone regeneration in drug-resistant osteosarcoma
Authors: Zuyun Yan, Yiwen He, Jin Zeng, Tao He, Dongcheng Xu, Xin Wu, Hao Zhou, Wei Tan, Xiaofang Zang, Youwen Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative management of osteosarcoma remains hindered by several critical challenges, including platinum-based chemoresistance, insufficient antitumor immune responses, and the difficulty of synchronously repairing large bone defects. Addressing these issues requires strategies capable of coordinating tumor eradication, immune modulation, and bone regeneration within the dynamic postoperative microenvironment. In this study, we developed a near-infrared (NIR)-responsive intelligent composite bone scaffold that integrates a reducible platinum (IV) prodrug (Pt (IV)) and cucurbitacin B (CuB) via hollow mesoporous silica and polydopamine interfacial engineering, enabling dual responsiveness to tumor-associated cues and exogenous photonic stimulation. Mechanistically, controlled CuB release suppresses DNA damage repair, amplifies Pt (IV)-induced DNA damage, and reverses platinum resistance, while the combined action of Pt (IV) and CuB induces immunogenic cell death and activates the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby remodeling an antitumor immune microenvironment. Concurrently, sustained Si4+ release and CuB synergistically promote angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation, establishing a regenerative niche for bone reconstruction. In vivo studies further demonstrate that the scaffold exhibits microenvironment-responsive and externally triggered regulatory capability. During the tumor suppression phase, the acidic tumor microenvironment and NIR stimulation enhance the release of Pt (IV) and CuB, leading to effective inhibition of drug-resistant osteosarcoma. As the local microenvironment progressively normalizes, the system supports osteogenesis and vascularization, ultimately achieving substantial new bone formation and bone bridging without observable systemic toxicity. Taken together, this work presents a multifunctional composite scaffold strategy characterized by microenvironment responsiveness, functional integration, and multi-pathway coordination. This approach provides an integrated solution for postoperative treatment and bone repair in drug-resistant osteosarcoma.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive primary malignant bone tumor that predominantly affects children and adolescents [1,2]. Current clinical management relies mainly on surgical resection combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy; however, overall patient outcomes have remained largely stagnant over recent decades [3,4]. On the one hand, residual tumor cells frequently acquire chemoresistance after treatment, leading to poor control of recurrence and metastasis. On the other hand, although immunotherapy has achieved substantial success in multiple solid tumors, its efficacy in OS remains limited due to low tumor immunogenicity, insufficient infiltration of effector T cells, and a strongly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [[5], [6], [7]]. Meanwhile, extensive tumor resection inevitably results in severe bone defects, which markedly compromise functional recovery and quality of life [8,9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a temporally orchestrated therapeutic strategy capable of simultaneously reversing chemoresistance, remodeling the local immune microenvironment, and promoting bone regeneration, thereby enabling integrated postoperative tumor control and bone defect repair.

Platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents remain a cornerstone of systemic treatment for OS. Among them, cisplatin (DDP) exerts its antitumor activity primarily by forming DNA adducts and crosslinks, thereby blocking DNA replication and transcription and inducing apoptosis [10,11]. In parallel, DDP-induced DNA damage can trigger the release of cytosolic DNA, leading to activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and subsequent induction of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which enhance the recruitment and effector function of tumor antigen–specific T cells and provide a mechanistic basis for chemo–immunological coupling [12,13]. However, the lack of tumor selectivity results in widespread biodistribution of DDP, frequently causing cumulative nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity, which severely restricts dosing intensity and treatment duration [14,15]. Compared with DDP, platinum (IV) prodrugs (Pt (IV)), characterized by an octahedral geometry and a coordinatively saturated structure, exhibit superior chemical stability and improved systemic safety. Within tumor cells, Pt (IV) prodrugs can be reduced by intracellular reductive conditions such as elevated glutathione (GSH), releasing highly cytotoxic platinum (II) species in situ, thereby reducing systemic toxicity while maintaining potent antitumor efficacy and amplifying immune-related signaling [[16], [17], [18]]. Nevertheless, as treatment progresses, tumor cells can still rapidly develop resistance through multiple mechanisms, including upregulation of efflux pumps, drug inactivation, target alteration, and enhanced DNA damage repair, which collectively constitute major barriers to the long-term effectiveness of platinum-based chemotherapy [19,20].

Currently, therapeutic strategies targeting chemoresistance, including small-molecule inhibitors and nucleic acid–based gene silencing approaches, can interfere with specific resistance pathways. However, their clinical efficacy is often limited by single-target effects, poor stability, and the activation of compensatory signaling networks, making it difficult to effectively halt the progression of acquired resistance [21,22]. In contrast, natural product–derived small molecules exhibit multi-target and multi-pathway regulatory capabilities, such as modulating cell survival signaling, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and DNA damage repair, thereby offering a more robust and adaptive strategy for overcoming chemoresistance [[23], [24], [25]]. Among these, cucurbitacin B (CuB), a highly bioactive natural compound, has demonstrated potent antitumor activity and has been reported to reverse chemoresistance to certain anticancer drugs in osteosarcoma [[26], [27], [28]]. Based on these findings, we propose a combination strategy integrating a Pt (IV) prodrug with CuB, aiming to achieve functional synergy between platinum-induced cytotoxicity/immunoactivation and CuB-mediated multi-target reversal of chemoresistance, thereby enhancing the elimination of drug-resistant tumor cells and improving antitumor immune responses.

In recent years, bioactive materials for tissue regeneration have evolved from purely structural support toward “externally driven regulation” [[29], [30], [31]]. Such materials not only provide mechanical support but also enable the modulation of local inflammatory responses and the immune microenvironment in response to external stimuli, thereby facilitating tissue repair [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. However, in the complex pathological context following osteosarcoma surgery, how to effectively integrate antitumor therapy and chemoresistance intervention with bone defect repair remains a critical challenge [36,37]. Existing bone repair systems, including osteogenic scaffolds and drug-loaded delivery platforms, often suffer from functional separation: conventional scaffolds primarily focus on structural support and osteoinduction but lack antitumor capability, whereas local drug delivery systems can suppress tumor growth but are insufficient to simultaneously modulate chemoresistance and the immune microenvironment [38,39]. Moreover, current “tumor therapy-bone regeneration” integrated strategies largely rely on simple functional combination rather than mechanistic integration, limiting their therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop multifunctional systems capable of integrating antitumor activity, immune modulation, and bone regeneration in a coordinated manner. Notably, beyond its antitumor effects, low-dose CuB has been reported to promote endothelial cell proliferation via VEGFR-related pathways and to enhance angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation [40], suggesting its unique advantage in simultaneously reversing chemoresistance and promoting bone regeneration.

Based on this rationale, we developed a near-infrared (NIR)-responsive 3D-printed multifunctional bone scaffold for coordinated postoperative tumor suppression and bone regeneration (Scheme 1). Specifically, a reducible Pt (IV) prodrug was first synthesized by oxidizing Pt (II) and introducing axial ligands. The Pt (IV) prodrug was then loaded into hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (HMSNs), followed by deposition of an ultrathin polydopamine (PDA) layer to improve biocompatibility and confer NIR-responsive properties. Subsequently, a CuB derivative was covalently conjugated onto the PDA surface via amide linkage, yielding a nanoplatform with synergistic antitumor and chemoresistance-reversing capabilities. Finally, this nanoplatform was immobilized onto a 3D-printed poly (L-lactic acid) (PLLA) porous scaffold via the strong adhesive properties of PDA, forming a composite scaffold capable of microenvironment-responsive and externally triggered drug release. The synergistic antitumor, immunomodulatory, and bone regenerative effects of this system were systematically evaluated in vitro and in vivo. This strategy is expected to reduce postoperative tumor recurrence while improving the local microenvironment and promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis, thereby offering a promising, translatable biomaterial-based solution for integrated osteosarcoma therapy and bone defect repair.

Results and discussion

CuB reverses DDP resistance in OS by suppressing DNA damage repair

To investigate the effect of CuB on DDP resistance in OS, we first performed cell proliferation inhibition assays. The results showed that CuB at concentrations below 0.1 μM exhibited no apparent cytotoxicity toward either parental human osteosarcoma (HOS) cells or their DDP-resistant counterpart (HOS/DDP). Treatment with DDP alone at 20 μM resulted in inhibition rates of approximately 40% and 15% in HOS and HOS/DDP cells, respectively. In contrast, co-treatment with DDP and CuB markedly enhanced the antiproliferative effect, with the strongest synergistic inhibition observed at a CuB concentration of 0.1 μM (Fig. S1A and B). To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying CuB-mediated reversal of DDP resistance, we performed 3 × 3 transcriptomic sequencing on HOS/DDP cells treated with DDP (20 μM) alone or in combination with CuB (0.1 μM). Volcano plot analysis revealed pronounced differences in gene expression profiles between the two treatment groups (Fig. 1A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated significant dysregulation of tumor-associated transcriptional functions, while Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further demonstrated that upregulated genes were primarily enriched in transcriptional regulatory activity and DNA-binding transcription factor activity (Fig. 1B and C), both of which are closely associated with DNA damage repair mechanisms [41,42]. It is well established that DDP induces DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) through direct binding to DNA, and that RAD51 and MRE11 are key proteins in the DNA damage response that play essential roles in DSB repair [44,45]. We therefore employed molecular docking simulations to evaluate the potential binding interactions between CuB and RAD51 or MRE11. The results indicated that CuB binds to RAD51 through a composite interaction mode dominated by hydrophobic interactions, supplemented by hydrogen bonding. Specifically, CuB is stabilized via strong hydrophobic interactions between its hydrophobic scaffold and residues ILE B:152, LEU B:157, PHE B:159, and ILE B:177 of RAD51, while a hydrogen-bonding network involving ARG B:153, GLY B:178, and SER B:179 precisely anchors CuB within the RAD51 binding pocket. Similarly, CuB was firmly locked within the intersubunit pocket of MRE11 through a dense hydrogen-bonding network formed with ASN B:84, TYR B:145, GLU B:185, HIS B:206, and ILE B:207, together with hydrophobic and π–π stacking interactions with PHE B:146 and PHE B:188 (Fig. 1D). We further employed the drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay to validate the interaction of CuB with RAD51 and MRE11. Under protease treatment, CuB attenuated the degradation of both proteins (Fig. S2A and B), supporting potential direct interactions of CuB with RAD51 and MRE11, in agreement with the molecular docking results. Taken together, these findings suggest that CuB has favorable binding affinity for both RAD51 and MRE11.

We next evaluated the effect of CuB on DNA damage in HOS/DDP cells using phosphorylated histone variant γ-H2AX as a marker of DSBs, assessed by immunofluorescence staining. Compared with the control group, cells treated with 0.1 μM CuB alone exhibited only weak fluorescence signals, indicating that low-dose CuB does not induce appreciable DNA damage in HOS/DDP cells. Treatment with 20 μM DDP alone resulted in relatively weak γ-H2AX signals, consistent with the DDP-resistant phenotype of this cell line. In contrast, co-treatment with CuB and DDP led to a pronounced increase in γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity, indicating a synergistic induction of DNA damage in resistant cells (Fig. 1E and F). A similar trend was observed in parental K7M2 cells (Fig. S3A and B). To further validate these observations, protein expression levels of RAD51, MRE11, and γ-H2AX were analyzed by Western blot. The results showed that combined CuB and DDP treatment significantly suppressed RAD51 and MRE11 expression in HOS/DDP cells while concomitantly upregulating γ-H2AX levels, in agreement with the immunofluorescence findings (Fig. 1G and H). A similar expression pattern was also observed in parental HOS cells (Fig. S4A and B).

To further verify the functional roles of RAD51 and MRE11 in the CuB-mediated reversal of DDP resistance, RAD51-and MRE11-overexpressing cell models were established in both HOS and HOS/DDP cells (Fig. S5A–D). Western blot analysis showed that, under DDP or CuB + DDP treatment, overexpression of either RAD51 or MRE11 partially reversed the increase in γ-H2AX levels (Fig. S6A–D), suggesting that restoration of RAD51 or MRE11 expression could, to some extent, mitigate the accumulation of DNA damage induced by the combination of CuB and DDP. Consistently, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays further demonstrated that overexpression of RAD51 or MRE11 also partially restored cell viability under DDP or CuB + DDP treatment (Fig. S7A–D). Collectively, these findings indicate that restoration of RAD51 or MRE11 expression can partially counteract the DNA damage and cytotoxic effects elicited by CuB in combination with DDP, supporting the notion that CuB-mediated reversal of DDP resistance is closely associated with suppression of RAD51-and MRE11-related DNA damage repair. As overexpression of either RAD51 or MRE11 conferred only a partial rescue effect, the precise molecular mechanism by which CuB reverses DDP resistance warrants further investigation.

Preparation and characterization of the Pt (IV) prodrug and the composite nanotherapeutic system

Based on the ability of CuB to reverse DDP resistance, we constructed a responsive-release nanotherapeutic system featuring a Pt (IV) prodrug and CuB as functional cores (Fig. 2A). In this system, the Pt (IV) prodrug was loaded into HMSNs, followed by coating with a PDA layer and subsequent covalent conjugation of CuB. The synthetic route of the Pt (IV) prodrug is shown in Fig. S8. 1H NMR characterization (Fig. 2B) revealed a characteristic peak at δ = 6.50 ppm, attributable to the amine protons coordinated to the DDP parent structure, indicating retention of the core coordination geometry. The peak at δ = 12.01 ppm corresponds to the proton signal of a free carboxyl group, while the split peaks around δ ≈ 2.30 ppm can be assigned to methylene protons of the newly introduced axial ligands. The presence and chemical shift of these signals are highly consistent with the target molecular structure, confirming the successful construction of the Pt (IV) prodrug based on the DDP scaffold [46]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectra (Fig. 2C) further identified the presence of Pt, Cl, C, N, and O elements. High-resolution Pt 4f spectra with peak deconvolution (Fig. 2D) indicated that the Pt (IV) species accounted for approximately 68.22% of the total platinum, collectively confirming the successful preparation of the Pt (IV) prodrug in terms of both elemental composition and oxidation state [47].Fig. 2Synthesis and structural characterization of the nanotherapeutic system. Subsequently, the Pt (IV) prodrug was loaded into HMSNs via a diffusion-driven strategy to obtain HMSNs@Pt (IV) (H@Pt). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis showed a Pt (IV) loading efficiency of approximately 55%. Under mildly alkaline conditions, dopamine underwent self-polymerization to form a PDA shell, yielding HMSNs@Pt (IV)@PDA (H@Pt@P). This PDA shell not only enhanced the stability and biocompatibility of the system but also provided a versatile interface for subsequent surface functionalization [48]. CuB derivatives were covalently conjugated onto the PDA surface via an amidation reaction, ultimately generating the functional nanotherapeutic system HMSNs@Pt (IV)@PDA–CuB (H@Pt@P-C). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses demonstrated that HMSNs possessed a uniform spherical morphology with a well-defined mesoporous structure, which remained intact after Pt (IV) loading and subsequent surface modifications (Fig. 2E and F and Fig. S9A–D). After PDA coating and CuB conjugation, a continuous and homogeneous shell was observed on the surface of HMSNs, with high-resolution TEM confirming a PDA layer thickness of approximately 10 nm (Fig. 2G and H and Fig. S9E and F). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and elemental mapping further verified the uniform distribution of Pt and organic functional layers throughout the nanoparticles (Fig. 2I). Together, these results confirm the successful construction of the H@Pt@P-C composite nanotherapeutic system from the perspectives of morphology, elemental composition, and spatial distribution.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that HMSNs exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 100–120 nm, falling within the optimal size range for the enhanced permeability and retention effect, which is favorable for tumor penetration and retention [49]. After PDA coating, the particle size increased markedly to 142.2 ± 2.2 nm, reflecting the typical size increment associated with PDA film formation, whereas subsequent CuB conjugation caused no significant further increase, indicating that CuB was immobilized as a molecularly thin layer on the PDA surface (Fig. 2J). The ζ-potential exhibited a stepwise evolution consistent with the sequential construction process (Fig. 2K): bare HMSNs displayed a surface potential of −27.1 ± 0.6 mV, which shifted slightly toward neutrality after Pt (IV) loading; PDA coating increased the ζ-potential to −12.0 ± 0.3 mV, consistent with the presence of phenolic hydroxyl and amine groups; CuB conjugation resulted in a slight decrease in ζ-potential, reflecting enhanced surface negativity due to the introduction of carboxyl/carbonyl groups. XPS analysis further confirmed the coexistence of O, C, N, Si, Pt, and Cl elements in HMSNs@Pt (IV)@PDA–CuB (Fig. 2L). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra showed characteristic Si–O–Si framework vibrations of HMSNs at 1065, 803, and 456 cm−1. After PDA coating, additional absorption bands appeared at 2786–3630, 1617, 1501, 1286, and 815 cm−1, confirming stable PDA deposition. Following CuB conjugation, the overall spectral profile remained similar to that of the PDA-coated group (Fig. 2M), which can be attributed to the relatively low CuB content and overlap of its amide-related vibrations with the intrinsic PDA absorptions and the strong Si–O–Si framework signals. Collectively, these results systematically verify the stepwise construction of the composite nanotherapeutic system via Pt (IV) loading, PDA coating, and CuB covalent modification.

Preparation and characterization of the composite bone scaffolds

Numerous studies have demonstrated that three-dimensional porous scaffolds can synergistically promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis [50]. Building upon this, in the present study, fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing was combined with the strong adhesive properties of PDA to fabricate PLLA scaffolds with a well-defined porous architecture and their surface-functionalized composite counterparts, including PLLA-PDA (PD), PLLA-PDA/HMSNs@Pt (IV) (PD/H@Pt), and PLLA-PDA/HMSNs@Pt (IV)@PDA-CuB (PD/H@Pt@P-C) (Fig. 3A). Macroscopic observation showed that pristine PLLA scaffolds were white in appearance, whereas scaffolds modified with PDA and the nanotherapeutic system exhibited a dark brown coloration, visually confirming successful surface functionalization (Fig. 3B). SEM analysis revealed that all scaffolds possessed an interconnected three-dimensional macroporous network with an average pore size of 500 ± 15 μm and a porosity of 65.2 ± 2.8%, which falls within the optimal range for bone tissue engineering and supports both extracellular matrix deposition and vascular ingrowth. Higher-magnification images further demonstrated uniform coverage of nanoparticles on the scaffold surface. Water contact angle measurements showed that pristine PLLA scaffolds were highly hydrophobic (103.3 ± 1.8°), whereas PDA modification significantly reduced the contact angle to 90.3 ± 1.1°. Further incorporation of H@Pt and H@Pt@P-C led to a continuous enhancement in surface hydrophilicity (Fig. 3C), which can be attributed to the introduction of hydrophilic functional groups and increased micro-/nanoscale surface roughness [51,52]. We next evaluated the in vitro degradation behavior of scaffolds with different compositions. After 4 weeks of immersion in PBS, the PLLA scaffold showed only minimal mass loss, whereas PDA modification and the incorporation of HMSNs markedly accelerated degradation, likely due to increased hydrophilicity and enhanced water penetration within the composite structure (Fig. S10A). During degradation, the pH of the surrounding medium exhibited only a slight decrease and remained close to neutral, indicating a relatively mild degradation process (Fig. S11A). Meanwhile, the zeta potential of the released nanoparticulate components gradually decreased over time, suggesting dynamic changes in surface interfacial properties (Fig. S11B). SEM observations further confirmed that the PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffold exhibited evident surface erosion and partial structural collapse after 4 weeks, demonstrating a progressive degradation profile that maintains early structural support while gradually creating space for new bone formation (Fig. S10B). Mechanical testing demonstrated that PDA modification simultaneously improved the compressive and tensile properties of the scaffolds, with further enhancement observed upon incorporation of H@Pt and H@Pt@P-C. Notably, the PD/H@Pt@P-C composite scaffold exhibited a compressive strength of 25.76 ± 1.04 MPa and a compressive modulus of 0.83 ± 0.05 GPa. In addition, its tensile properties were also enhanced (Fig. 3E and Fig. S12), which can be primarily attributed to PDA-mediated interfacial reinforcement and the formation of a rigid HMSNs-based nanocomposite surface layer [53]. These mechanical properties fall within the typical range of cancellous bone [54], indicating suitable mechanical compatibility for bone defect repair.

ICP-MS quantification revealed a loading efficiency of H@Pt@P-C as high as 92% on the PLLA scaffolds, reflecting the highly effective interfacial anchoring enabled by the catechol moieties of PDA. On this basis, the composite scaffolds exhibited favorable stimulus-responsive drug release behavior. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), Pt displayed a slow and controlled release profile, with a cumulative release of approximately 80% over 28 days. In contrast, Pt release was markedly accelerated under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) or upon NIR irradiation, indicating that the acidic TME and the photothermal effect of PDA could synergistically trigger efficient release of the Pt (IV) prodrug (Fig. 3F and Fig. S13). By comparison, CuB exhibited a stable and mild sustained-release behavior under physiological conditions, with an increased release rate under acidic conditions (Fig. 3G). Meanwhile, Si4+ released from HMSNs displayed a gradual and sustained release behavior associated with scaffold degradation, with an overall release rate lower than that of CuB, reflecting its origin from the dissolution of the inorganic framework (Fig. S14).

Photothermal performance evaluation showed that under NIR irradiation at 1.0 W cm−2, the scaffold temperature could be stably maintained at approximately 42.0 °C (Fig. 3H), which is sufficient to enhance drug release while avoiding excessive heating-induced thermal damage. These results indicate that the system enables stimulus-responsive Pt release under tumor-relevant conditions, while maintaining sustained CuB-mediated regulation under relatively neutral environments, thereby supporting the coordinated interplay between antitumor activity and microenvironmental modulation at the functional level. Good cytocompatibility is a fundamental prerequisite for bone scaffolds intended for defect repair [[55], [56], [57]]. In vitro biocompatibility assessments showed that all scaffolds exhibited favorable cytocompatibility (Fig. 3I and J). Further cell morphology analyses revealed that BMSCs displayed limited spreading and poorly developed filopodia on pristine PLLA scaffolds, whereas cells cultured on PDA- and nanotherapeutic-modified scaffolds exhibited markedly enhanced spreading, with well-organized F-actin cytoskeletons and abundant filopodia firmly anchored to the scaffold surface (Fig. 3K and L). These findings suggest that the biointerface constructed by PDA and the composite nanostructure effectively enhances cell–material interactions, facilitating cell adhesion, migration, and spreading, and thereby providing a favorable foundation for subsequent bone regeneration. Composite bone scaffolds enhance Pt-induced DNA damage by suppressing DNA repair and synergistically induce apoptosis in OS cells

Based on the favorable physicochemical and biological properties of the fabricated bone scaffolds, their antitumor efficacy was further evaluated at the cellular level (Fig. 4A). CCK-8 assays were first employed to systematically assess the in vitro cytotoxicity and antitumor activity of scaffolds with different compositions against HOS, HOS/DDP, and K7M2 cells (Fig. 4B–D, Fig. S15A–C). The experimental groups included PLLA, NIR, PLLA/HMSNs (PD/H), PD/H + NIR, PLLA/HMSNs@PDA–CuB (PD/H@P-C), PLLA/HMSNs@Pt@PDA (PD/H@Pt@P), PD/H@Pt@P + NIR, PD/H@Pt@P-C, and PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR. The results showed that the percentages of viable cells in the PLLA, NIR, PD/H, PD/H + NIR, and PD/H@P-C groups remained in the range of approximately 90–100%, indicating good biocompatibility of the prepared scaffolds. No significant differences were observed among these groups, suggesting that NIR irradiation alone, silica nanoparticles, and PDA (including its potential photothermal effect under NIR irradiation) exerted no appreciable influence on cell viability. Previous studies have shown that Pt (IV) can be reduced by intracellular GSH to generate highly cytotoxic Pt (II), thereby exerting antitumor activity [[58], [59], [60]]. Under non-irradiated conditions, the PD/H@Pt@P group exhibited relatively weak antitumor efficacy, which may be attributed to limited release of Pt (IV) caused by partial dissolution of PDA under the mildly acidic TME. Upon NIR irradiation, PD/H@Pt@P displayed moderate antitumor activity, confirming that NIR exposure effectively accelerates the controlled release of Pt (IV). Notably, following the introduction of CuB, both PD/H@Pt@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR groups showed significantly enhanced antitumor effects, with the latter exhibiting the strongest tumor cell–killing efficacy under NIR irradiation. These findings indicate a pronounced synergistic antitumor interaction between Pt and CuB when Pt release is regulated by NIR stimulation, consistent with our earlier observations that CuB enhances the antitumor activity of DDP. Composite bone scaffolds synergistically trigger ICD and activate the cGAS–STING pathway through DNA damage to elicit antitumor immunity

Accumulating evidence indicates that DNA damage is a critical trigger of immunogenic cell death (ICD) [61,62]. Upon DNA damage, such as double-strand breaks or oxidative lesions, tumor cells initiate stress responses that lead to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT) exposure, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, and extracellular ATP secretion [63]. HMGB1 is typically highly expressed in the nucleus of tumor cells, whereas ICD promotes its translocation into the extracellular space. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that K7M2 cells treated with PD/H@Pt@P exhibited low levels of HMGB1 release, while a moderate increase was observed in the PD/H@Pt@P-C group. In contrast, PD/H@Pt@P-C treatment under NIR irradiation induced pronounced HMGB1 translocation (Fig. 5A and B), consistent with the extent of DNA damage observed in earlier experiments. ELISA-based quantification of cell supernatants further confirmed that the PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR group exhibited the highest level of HMGB1 secretion (Fig. 5K). With respect to CRT exposure, immunofluorescence analysis showed weak but detectable CRT membrane localization in K7M2 cells treated with PD/H@Pt@P, exceeding control levels. The PD/H@Pt@P-C group displayed moderate CRT exposure, whereas PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR treatment induced robust CRT translocation to the cell membrane, indicating that NIR irradiation markedly enhances this process (Fig. S20A and B). A parallel trend was also observed in HOS/DDP cells (Fig. S21A and B). Flow cytometric analysis further quantitatively confirmed increased surface CRT expression on K7M2 cells (Fig. 5E). In addition, as a key “find-me” signal, ATP release assays demonstrated that PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR treatment induced significantly higher extracellular ATP accumulation in K7M2 cells compared with all other groups (Fig. 5L). These fragments act as endogenous ligands to activate the cGAS–STING pathway, thereby promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, initiating innate antitumor immune responses, and facilitating the conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors [64,65]. To evaluate the ability of Pt and CuB released from the scaffold to activate the cGAS–STING pathway, p-STING immunofluorescence staining was performed in K7M2 cells under different treatment conditions. Confocal microscopy revealed that p-STING fluorescence intensity was markedly higher in K7M2 cells treated with PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR compared with those treated with PD/H@Pt@P or PD/H@Pt@P-C alone (Fig. 5C and D). The results showed that phosphorylation levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 were all significantly upregulated in K7M2 cells treated with PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR, in agreement with the immunofluorescence observations (Fig. 5F and G). These findings demonstrate that under NIR regulation, the PD/H@Pt@P-C bone scaffold effectively activates the cGAS–STING signaling pathway through DNA damage induction. Activation of the cGAS–STING pathway has been reported to promote the release of various chemokines and cytokines from tumor cells, thereby facilitating immune cell recruitment and activation [66]. ELISA analysis revealed that expression levels of IFN-β, IL-6, and CXCL10 were significantly elevated in the supernatants of K7M2 cells treated with PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR (Fig. 5M-O). In K7M2 cells, RU.521 treatment markedly reduced the levels of p-STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3, whereas the total protein levels of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 remained largely unchanged (Fig. S23A and B). Similarly, SN-011 treatment significantly suppressed the expression of p-STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3 in K7M2 cells, while the corresponding total protein levels were essentially unaffected (Fig. S24A and B). In parallel, SN-011 intervention led to a significant decrease in the secretion of IFN-β, IL-6, and CXCL10 in the supernatants of K7M2 cells (Fig. S25A-C). Collectively, these findings further demonstrate that the cGAS-STING pathway represents an important functional mechanism underlying the DNA damage-associated immune activation induced by PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR treatment. Dendritic cells play a central role in linking innate and adaptive immunity [67]. When dendritic cells isolated from mouse bone marrow were co-cultured with pretreated K7M2 cells, flow cytometric analysis showed that the proportion of mature CD11c+CD80+CD86+ dendritic cells was significantly increased in the PD/H@Pt@P-C + NIR group (Fig. 5I and J), indicating effective promotion of dendritic cell activation. Taken together, these results demonstrate that under NIR regulation, the PD/H@Pt@P-C bone scaffold synergistically triggers ICD and activates the cGAS–STING pathway through DNA damage induction, thereby eliciting a robust antitumor immune response (Fig. 5H).

In the presence of CuB, this system effectively induces intracellular DNA damage, thereby triggering ICD and activating the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, which in turn promotes the production of type I interferons and other proinflammatory cytokines. Western blot analysis of osteogenesis-related proteins showed that after 7 days of co-culture, expression levels of runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), ALP, osteocalcin (OCN), and osteopontin (OPN) were significantly upregulated in the PD/H@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C groups (Fig. 8M and N). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed markedly enhanced expression of BMP2 and OPN, in agreement with the Western blot results (Fig. S36A and B and Fig. S37A and B). Collectively, these results indicate that the pristine PLLA scaffold possesses limited osteogenic activity, whereas the PD/H group shows partial improvement, suggesting that the incorporation of HMSNs may contribute to the optimization of the local osteogenic microenvironment. Furthermore, the PD/H@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffolds exhibit markedly enhanced osteoinductive capacity. Based on the release profiles and cellular responses, this effect is more likely attributed to the synergistic contributions of multiple factors, including the bioactive interface provided by Si-based inorganic components and their sustained release of Si4+, the osteogenic potential of CuB, and the overall modulation of the local microenvironment, rather than being driven by a single component. Importantly, under the conditions tested, the system did not exhibit any evident inhibitory effects on osteogenic cells or endothelial cells, further supporting its potential application in bone regeneration.

In vivo evaluation of the composite bone scaffolds for promoting femoral defect repair in rats

Based on the favorable biocompatibility and robust osteogenic activity observed in vitro, we further evaluated the bone regenerative performance of the composite scaffolds in vivo. A critical-sized femoral defect model was established in male Sprague–Dawley rats, followed by implantation of PLLA, PD/H, PD/H@P-C, or PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffolds, respectively (Fig. 9A). At 8 weeks post-implantation, femoral defect samples were harvested and subjected to micro–computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis. The PLLA group exhibited only limited new bone formation confined to the defect margins, while the PD/H group showed moderate improvement. In contrast, both the PD/H@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C groups demonstrated extensive and continuous new bone regeneration throughout the defect area (Fig. 9B), indicating that CuB retained strong osteoinductive activity in vivo and that the PDA interface likely mitigated the potential inhibitory effects of Pt (IV) on osteogenesis. Histological evaluation was performed on serial sagittal sections of femoral defects harvested at 8 weeks. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed sparse mineralized tissue in the PLLA group, whereas extensive new bone (NB) formation and complete bone bridging between the defect and host bone were observed in the PD/H@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C groups (Fig. 9G). Masson's trichrome staining further demonstrated abundant collagen deposition and osteoid formation in these two groups compared with the PLLA group (Fig. 9H). Immunofluorescence staining of osteogenic markers showed markedly elevated expression of BMP2 and OPN in the defect regions treated with PD/H@P-C and PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffolds, consistent with the in vitro findings (Fig. 9I). In terms of biosafety, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) revealed intact tissue architectures without evident pathological abnormalities across all groups (Fig. S38). Hematological and serum biochemical analyses further confirmed that all parameters remained within normal physiological ranges (Fig. S39A–G), demonstrating the favorable in vivo biocompatibility of the PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffold. Collectively, these results indicate that the PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffold markedly enhances osteogenesis in vivo and exhibits strong potential as an ideal bone implant material.

Overall, this study proposes a biomaterial design strategy for postoperative osteosarcoma treatment, in which microenvironment-responsive and externally triggered interfacial regulatory elements are incorporated into the scaffold system to enable coordinated functionality within a single platform. Rather than relying on simple functional combination, this approach emphasizes targeted integration aligned with distinct therapeutic and regenerative phases, offering a potentially translatable strategy for the local treatment of tumor-associated bone defects.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a multifunctional PD/H@Pt@P-C composite scaffold that integrates antitumor therapy, reversal of chemoresistance, and bone regeneration. Under near-infrared irradiation, the scaffold enabled controllable drug release. Mechanistically, CuB may enhance the sensitivity of drug-resistant osteosarcoma to Pt (IV)-based therapy by suppressing DNA damage repair, thereby augmenting Pt (IV)-induced DNA damage. In addition, scaffold treatment induced immunogenic cell death and activated the cGAS–STING signaling pathway through the accumulation of damaged DNA, which may contribute to enhanced antitumor immunity and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Meanwhile, the intrinsic bioactivity of the scaffold, together with the osteoinductive effect of CuB, promoted the osteogenic differentiation of rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and supported bone repair in vivo.

Overall, these findings identify the PD/H@Pt@P-C scaffold as a potential biomaterial strategy for the treatment of drug-resistant osteosarcoma and bone defect repair. In particular, in postoperative settings characterized by local recurrence risk, chemoresistance, and the need for bone defect reconstruction, this platform may serve as a localized therapeutic strategy capable of coordinately promoting tumor control, chemoresistance intervention, and bone regeneration, thus offering a new framework for integrated local treatment and functional reconstruction after osteosarcoma surgery. However, because the antitumor and bone-repair effects were evaluated separately in different animal models, further studies using a more clinically relevant osteosarcoma-associated bone defect model will be required to assess its integrated therapeutic and regenerative performance. Future preclinical studies should also systematically evaluate its long-term safety, degradation behavior, applicability in large-animal models, and translational feasibility.
