Finite-Size Security Bounds in Semi-Quantum Key Distribution: Spectral, Operator-Theoretic, and Entropic Perspectives
Zahidur Rezwan Ratul

Abstract
Abstract. We study Semi–Quantum Key Distribution (SQKD) with a focus on finite–size security bounds, developed through three complementary perspectives. (i) Spectral disturbance: wrong–basis Lüders updates produce closed–form spectra and purity loss, which serve as basis–independent indicators of disturbance. (ii) Operator–theoretic reduction: in Z / Z Z/Z –sifted rounds, intercept–resend attacks can be represented as an effective depolarizing channel, characterized by a fidelity–QBER relation. (iii) Entropic trade–offs: Maassen–Uffink and memory–assisted uncertainty relations certify security through X X tests and reflection rounds, even when the sifted QBER is low. The exposition provides step–by–step derivations supported by physically interpretable figures, and the framework concludes with finite–size estimates based on concentration inequalities that are suited for practical parameter estimation. Keywords: Quantum key distribution; Semi–quantum cryptography; Spectral disturbance; Depolarizing channel; Entropic uncertainty; Finite–size analysis.

3
Spectral Disturbance: Rank, Purity, and Witnesses

Spectral analysis offers a clear method for quantifying the disturbance caused by wrong–basis interventions. Rather than focusing only on error probabilities, one can study how the spectrum of the post–measurement state spreads in comparison to the original pure input. This spectral spreading directly reveals the loss of purity and serves as a basis–independent indicator of disturbance.

Consider a rotation by angle
\theta
from the computational basis
Z=\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
to the rotated basis
\{\ket{\pm_\theta}\}
, with projectors
P_{\pm}=\ket{\pm_\theta}\!\bra{\pm_\theta}
.
For a pure input
\rho=\ket{0}\!\bra{0}
, the wrong–basis Lüders update yields

\rho^{\prime}=\sum_{s=\pm}P_{s}\rho P_{s}=\cos^{2}\theta\,\ket{+_\theta}\!\bra{+_\theta}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\ket{-_\theta}\!\bra{-_\theta}.

(3.1)

The spectrum of
\rho^{\prime}
is therefore

\mathrm{spec}(\rho^{\prime})=\{\cos^{2}\theta,\;\sin^{2}\theta\},

with purity

\Tr[(\rho^{\prime})^{2}]=\cos^{4}\theta+\sin^{4}\theta<1\quad\text{for }\theta\in(0,\pi/2).

This loss of purity signals mixing induced by wrong–basis measurements.
In particular, the smallest eigenvalue

\lambda_{\min}=\min\{\cos^{2}\theta,\sin^{2}\theta\}

acts as a convenient disturbance witness that is basis–independent.

A simple numerical illustration highlights this effect: for
\theta=\pi/6
, one finds

\mathrm{spec}(\rho^{\prime})=\{0.75,0.25\}
, giving
\Tr[(\rho^{\prime})^{2}]=0.625
and
\lambda_{\min}=0.25
.
Thus even moderate basis misalignment produces a mixed state whose spectrum directly encodes the disturbance.

Finally, using the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequalities
[
10
]
,

1-F(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\;\leq\;D(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\;\leq\;\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\rho^{\prime})^{2}},

(3.2)

with fidelity

F(\ket{0}\!\bra{0},\rho^{\prime})=\matrixelement{0}{\rho'}{0}=\cos^{2}\theta,

we obtain explicit upper and lower bounds on the trace distance between the original and disturbed states.
This establishes a clear operational link between spectral eigenvalue spreading and distinguishability, showing that spectral disturbance functions as a rigorous, quantitative diagnostic for SQKD security.

4
Operator-Theoretic Reduction: Effective Depolarization

Another way to quantify disturbance is through an operator–theoretic reduction of the attack channel.
Consider a completely positive trace–preserving (CPTP) attack of the form

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\mathcal{A}(\rho),\qquad\mathcal{A}=\alpha E_{Z}+(1-\alpha)E_{X},

where
p
is the interception probability and
\alpha
interpolates between
Z
– and
X
–basis measurements.
Conditioning on
Z/Z
sifting, the observed statistics are equivalent to those produced by a single–parameter depolarizing channel,

\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(\rho)=(1-\lambda)\rho+\lambda\frac{I}{2},\qquad\lambda=2Q,

(4.1)

with
Q
the observed quantum bit error rate (QBER).

This reduction is significant because it collapses a wide class of intercept–resend strategies into the standard depolarizing model.
In Bloch vector form, the channel acts as a uniform contraction
\bm{r}\mapsto(1-2\lambda)\bm{r}
, making disturbance analysis geometrically transparent.
For an input
\ket{0}
, the output fidelity is

F=\bra{0}\,\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}(\ket{0}\!\bra{0})\,\ket{0}=1-2Q.

(4.2)

Thus, the operationally measurable quantity
Q
directly determines the fidelity of the channel.

Example.
If the observed QBER is
Q=0.05
, then
\lambda=0.10
, the Bloch vector shrinks by a factor
1-2\lambda=0.8
, and the fidelity with
\ket{0}
drops to
F=0.90
.
This clearly demonstrates that even a modest error rate corresponds to a well–defined depolarization strength. By mapping general attacks onto an effective depolarizing channel, the analysis connects observable error statistics to a straightforward noise model. This operator–theoretic viewpoint not only streamlines security proofs but also offers an experimentally accessible diagnostic: estimating
Q
is sufficient to characterize the effective channel acting on the sifted key.

5
Entropic Information–Disturbance: Line-by-Line

Entropic methods provide another way to assess disturbance by turning observed test statistics into lower bounds on Eve’s uncertainty. In contrast to spectral or operator–theoretic methods, which focus on describing the channel itself, entropic uncertainty relations make sure that conjugate–basis tests introduce unavoidable entropy into the adversary’s knowledge of the raw key. The next derivation explains this argument step by step.

Step 1 (relative-entropy form).

For the joint state
\rho_{AE}
before measurement, measuring
A
in the basis
Z
(resp.
X
) gives
\rho_{ZE}
(resp.
\rho_{XE}
).
Conditional entropy can be expressed in relative entropy form:

H(Z|E)=D\!\big(\rho_{ZE}\,\big\|\,\tfrac{I}{2}\otimes\rho_{E}\big),\qquad H(X|E)=D\!\big(\rho_{XE}\,\big\|\,\tfrac{I}{2}\otimes\rho_{E}\big).

(5.1)

Step 2 (contractivity).

By data processing under CPTP maps, the sum of conditional entropies is bounded below by the divergences of the pre–measurement states and their Hadamard–rotated counterparts:

H(Z|E)+H(X|E)\ \geq\ D\!\big(\rho_{AE}\,\big\|\,\tfrac{I}{2}\otimes\rho_{E}\big)+D\!\big((H\!\otimes\!I)\rho_{AE}(H\!\otimes\!I)^{\dagger}\,\big\|\,\tfrac{I}{2}\otimes\rho_{E}\big).

(5.2)

Step 3 (overlap constant).

For the qubit
Z
/
X
case, the basis overlap is
c=\max_{z,x}|\!\innerproduct{z}{x}\!|=1/\sqrt{2}
, giving
-\log c^{2}=1
.
This recovers the memory–assisted uncertainty relation
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
:

H(Z|E)+H(X|E)\geq 1.

(5.3)

Step 4 (from control data).

Observed statistics in the
X
basis provide concrete bounds.
If the
X
–test error rate is
Q_{X}
, then
H(X|E)\leq h(Q_{X})
, where
h(\cdot)
is the binary entropy.
Consequently,

H(Z|E)\geq 1-h(Q_{X}),

(5.4)

which gives a directly usable lower bound on the adversary’s uncertainty about the raw
Z
–key.

Equation (
5.4
) links the statistics from test rounds to privacy amplification: even if the sifted
Z/Z
error rate appears artificially small, the non–zero disturbance in the
X
basis forces Eve’s uncertainty to stay above zero. In this way, entropic uncertainty guarantees that security is not judged only from error rates but also from conjugate–basis tests, which serve as reliable entropy witnesses in SQKD.

Z/Z
error rate is small, nonzero

6
Finite-Size Analysis (Practical Bounds)

In realistic implementations, the number of signals exchanged is finite, so statistical fluctuations must be taken into account when estimating error rates and key rates.
Let
\hat{Q}
denote the observed error rate from the samples
N
and
Q
be the true underlying error rate.
Concentration inequalities provide explicit limits on deviation
|\hat{Q}-Q|
.

For the i.i.d. Bernoulli setting, Hoeffding’s inequality yields

\Pr\!\big(|\hat{Q}-Q|\geq\delta\big)\leq 2e^{-2N\delta^{2}},\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta=\sqrt{\tfrac{\ln(2/\epsilon)}{2N}},

(6.1)

where
\epsilon
is the desired failure probability.
This expression gives a simple rule of thumb for selecting
N
to achieve a specified level of precision.

Example.
Suppose that
N=10^{5}
sifted rounds are used for parameter estimation and we demand
\epsilon=10^{-5}
.
The Hoeffding bound then gives

\delta=\sqrt{\tfrac{\ln(2 \cdot 10^5)}{2\cdot 10^{5}}}\approx 0.0047,

so the observed error rate
\hat{Q}
is guaranteed, except with probability
10^{-5}
, to deviate from the true
Q
by no more than about
0.5\%
.
This illustrates how even moderate sample sizes already yield sub-percent precision in practice.

When tighter bounds are required, Chernoff inequalities that make use of the Kullback–Leibler divergence can give improved scaling, especially in the regime where
Q
is small. In fact, one can also rely on Azuma–Hoeffding bounds, which apply to martingale difference sequences and therefore continue to hold in situations with bounded but non–i.i.d. correlations. These kinds of concentration results fit naturally into standard finite–key frameworks
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, where they are used to determine the confidence intervals for parameter estimation. In practice, the choice of inequality depends on the particular noise regime: Hoeffding is often chosen for its simplicity, Chernoff gives sharper tails when the errors are small, and Azuma is used when non–i.i.d. effects are present. In all of these cases, the finite–size analysis guarantees that the entropic bounds introduced earlier remain reliable despite statistical fluctuations, linking the theoretical security proofs with the key rates that can actually be achieved in experiments.

X

Z/Z
error rate looks small. When considered together, reflections and
X

8
Unified Picture and Outlook

The three perspectives that we have developed in this work can be seen as coming together to form a single coherent framework for SQKD security. In fact, spectral witnesses that are based on eigenvalue spreading and purity loss provide indicators of disturbance that do not depend on the basis being used. At the same time, the operator–theoretic reduction shows that an intercept–resend attack can be described as a depolarizing channel with parameter
\lambda=2Q
, which gives a direct relation between fidelity and QBER. Entropic bounds, such as (
5.3
), take the control data coming from
X
tests and reflections and turn them into quantitative guarantees about the adversary’s uncertainty. When all of these tools are put together, they provide cross–checks that support each other: the spectral diagnostics reveal structural disturbance, the operator reduction connects this disturbance to effective noise models, and the entropic inequalities tie the observed data directly to estimates of the key rate. In other words, this unified picture does more than just clarify the mathematical structure that lies behind SQKD—it also suggests practical ways to bring spectral, operator, and entropic diagnostics into future experimental implementations. The following section builds on these ideas by summarizing what is new in the approach, pointing out its current limits, and considering the broader outlook for how SQKD security analysis can continue to move forward.

9
Discussion

Summary.
We have linked a simple intercept–resend model to three rigorous perspectives: (i) spectral disturbance, measured through eigenvalue spectra and purity loss; (ii) an operator–theoretic reduction that relates
Z/Z
statistics to a depolarizing channel with a fidelity–QBER law; and (iii) entropic uncertainty, including the role of quantum memory, which converts
X
–test outcomes into explicit bounds on
H(Z|E)
. Taken together, these results show how different mathematical tools converge toward a unified understanding of SQKD security.

Novelty.
The analysis emphasizes basis–independent spectral witnesses, an explicit CPTP–to–depolarizing reduction for
Z/Z
rounds, and step–by–step entropic bounds that can be directly applied in finite–size settings. This threefold approach is mathematically clear and pedagogical, focusing on clarity of derivation while maintaining rigor. By providing cross–validated diagnostics, it also supplies a toolkit that can be implemented in experimental SQKD systems.

Limitations.
The current treatment leaves aside device imperfections and potential side–channel effects. While the intercept–resend model is instructive, realistic attacks may include non–i.i.d. correlations or detector miscalibrations. A fully composable security proof would require entropy accumulation methods and more advanced parameter estimation techniques, going beyond the finite–size bounds addressed here.

Outlook.
Future work should extend this framework to noisy measurement devices, explicit side–channel models, and composable key–rate formulas derived from the entropy accumulation theorem (EAT)
[

,

]
. It will also be valuable to compare the resulting SQKD bounds with experimental performance in measurement–device–independent QKD (MDI–QKD) and twin–field QKD (TF–QKD)

Take-home message.
By combining spectral, operator–theoretic, and entropic perspectives within a finite–size framework, this work shows that SQKD security can be evaluated using mathematically transparent methods that are both theoretically rigorous and experimentally practical.
