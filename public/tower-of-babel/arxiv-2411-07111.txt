Building a Taiwanese Mandarin Spoken Language Model: A First Attempt
Chih-Kai Yang*, Yu-Kuan Fu*, Chen-An Li*, Yi-Cheng Lin*, Yu-Xiang Lin*, Wei-Chih Chen*, Ho Lam Chung*, Chun-Yi Kuan*, Wei-Ping Huang*, Ke-Han Lu*, Tzu-Quan Lin*, Hsiu-Hsuan Wang*, En-Pei Hu*, Chan-Jan Hsu, Liang-Hsuan Tseng, I-Hsiang Chiu, Ulin Sanga, Xuanjun Chen, Po-chun Hsu, Shu-wen Yang, Hung-yi Lee* indicates an equally contributing first author.

Abstract
This technical report presents our initial attempt to build a spoken large language model (LLM) for Taiwanese Mandarin, specifically tailored to enable real-time, speech-to-speech interaction in multi-turn conversations. Our end-to-end model incorporates a decoder-only transformer architecture and aims to achieve seamless interaction while preserving the conversational flow, including full-duplex capabilities allowing simultaneous speaking and listening. The paper also details the training process, including data preparation with synthesized dialogues and adjustments for real-time interaction. We also developed a platform to evaluate conversational fluency and response coherence in multi-turn dialogues. We hope the release of the report can contribute to the future development of spoken LLMs in Taiwanese Mandarin.

Introduction

This is a technical report on the practice of training large language models (LLMs) for spoken Taiwanese Mandarin.
The report serves as a record of the work completed to date.
As a work-in-progress paper, the current version is not yet ready for conference submission. However, we believe that some ideas and findings from this project may inspire future work. Therefore, we have made the report publicly available. If you find the ideas or findings in this report useful for your project, please cite this technical report.

1.1
Goal of the Project

The project aims to train a spoken LLM that can engage in real-time speech-to-speech conversations. The model takes a system prompt as input and, based on that prompt, facilitates seamless spoken interactions. Ultimately, the aim is to create an experience where conversing with the AI feels as natural as talking to a real person on Google Meet.

In terms of interaction, there are several goals we aim to achieve:

•

Speech-to-speech, multi-turn conversations

•

Mandarin conversation with a Taiwanese accent

•

The model should be capable of seamless, full-duplex communication with humans. This means the model can process and respond in real time, allowing for interruptions and simultaneous speaking and listening.

In the past, spoken dialogue has been achieved through a cascade framework, which combines automatic speech recognition (ASR), LLM, and text-to-speech (TTS) to facilitate speech-to-speech interaction. Here, we aim to build an end-to-end model. Since the model is end-to-end, we hope it can accomplish several tasks that a cascade model cannot. For example, it should be able to understand input audio beyond just the text, including prosody and speaker characteristics, and respond appropriately
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
. It should also recognize non-verbal vocalizations (e.g., laughter, crying) and respond with the corresponding non-verbal sounds. Compared to a cascade model, an end-to-end model has the potential to interpret environmental sounds. However, we did not implement or thoroughly evaluate all of these capabilities due to time constraints, so we are unsure how well the model currently performs in these areas. Nonetheless, the end-to-end model has this potential, and we can extend the current work to incorporate these abilities in the future.

Although GPT-4’s voice mode can achieve seamless interaction with speech, it is not open-sourced, so we cannot know how it is achieved. We hope this technical report can shed light on the development of spoken LLMs in the future, helping to accelerate the creation of open-source spoken LLMs for the community.
Our model will soon be open-sourced.
The open-source model most similar to our goal is Moshi
(
Défossez et al. 2024
)
, but it primarily supports English and cannot understand or generate Mandarin.

2.2
LLM Backbone

We utilized LLaMA-3.1 8B
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
as the LLM backbone of our spoken LLM and injected the speech capability into the backbone with speech-related training, which will be detailed in Section
4
.

In addition to having the ability to engage in speech-based conversations, we believe that it is essential for the users to be allowed to make the model response only in text or in speech as well, depending on their needs. It is also crucial for the model to have the ability to take on different roles and solve a wide variety of tasks. Without additional modification of the architecture of the backbone model, we surprisingly found that such controls could be achieved with proper design of system prompt format.

We begin by clarifying the “modalities” referred to in our model. It is important to note that since our LLM backbone does not directly generate waveform or mel-spectrograms, the model’s output is entirely tokens. However, to enable the generation of speech responses, special tokens representing discrete speech units have been added to the model’s vocabulary, which a decoder then uses to synthesize speech. Therefore, the term “modalities” in this context does not refer to the actual output modalities of the LLM, which are purely tokens. Instead, it refers to the different combinations of the original text tokens and the newly introduced special tokens representing speech units. We maintain this terminology throughout the paper.

There are three kinds of possible modalities for the input and output of our LLM backbone: (1)
text
: pure text tokens only; (2)
unit
: only the speech unit sequence; (3)
hybrid
: the interleaved sequences of the text tokens and the corresponding speech units. We use the system prompts to provide information on the input/output modalities of the model.

Specifically, we incorporated a prefix with a format like ‘‘Modality: {User: text, Machine: speech}’’ in the system prompts of training data, where ‘‘User’’ and ‘‘Machine’’ identified the modalities of the input and the model’s response, respectively
3
3

3

In actual implementation, we used the term “speech” for the “hybrid” modality in the system prompts.
.
Using the aforementioned example, the previously shown prefix indicated that the input from the user and the model response would be in text and speech (interleaved sequences of text tokens and speech units), respectively. We prepared the training data and ensured the modality of the input and the label of each data sample followed the specification of the corresponding prefix in the system prompt. With this methodology, our preliminary experiments showed that the model could precisely generate the output in the modality specified by the user in the system prompts during inference, demonstrating strong proficiency in modality control and enhancing the convenience and user experience.

The other crucial part of the system prompts is role control, i.e., task control. As a versatile model, an ideal spoken LLM should be able to take on various roles and solve a wide range of tasks for users, similar to an ordinary text-based LLM. Therefore, we incorporated this part into the model’s system prompts, in addition to the modality control prefix. A complete system prompt is the concatenation of the modality control prefix and the role-playing control instruction, resembling “Modality: {User: text, Machine: speech} You are a ChatBot. Have a fun chat with the user.” In this example, the modality control prefix indicates that input and output modalities are text and hybrid sequences, and the role-playing control instruction specifies the role of the model as a ChatBot whose task is having an interesting conversation with the user. A highly diverse set of role-playing instructions in Taiwanese Mandarin was employed in the training data to make the role controls more precise.

2.3
Streaming ASR and Encoder

As we aim to develop a spoken LLM that supports seamless conversation, the system should support real-time interaction. Therefore, both ASR and encoder in Figure
1
have to be streaming models, ensuring the input of the LLM backbone can be processed in real time.

Our streaming ASR model here is a streaming version of Whisper. Technical details will be explained in Section
5.1.1
.
For the speech encoder, to achieve shorter processing times, we apply a sliding window to the input audio signal. We use a non-causal, self-supervised speech encoder, with a carefully chosen short window size to ensure the model can still generate meaningful embeddings for quantization.

Whenever the ASR model generates a text token or the speech encoder produces a speech unit, it is sent to the transformer, which takes a hybrid of these text tokens and speech units as input. The interleaving of text and speech units is based on the timing of the ASR and speech encoder outputs.
During training, we aim to train the model to function with a hybrid of text and speech units, as well as with speech units alone.
We still include ASR in the complete framework to enhance its overall robustness because state-of-the-art ASR systems typically have access to much more significant amounts of speech data than what is available for our spoken LLM. However, it should be noted that our model can still obtain satisfactory performance without relying on the ASR module here, as shown in Section
6.1.4
.

2.4
Decoder

To enable the generation of both text and speech, the LLM backbone processes an interleaved sequence of text and speech units, producing a combined output. From this output, we extract the speech units, which are subsequently passed through a speech decoder to synthesize the speech signal.

Specifically, we leverage a denoising diffusion framework, which iteratively refines the noise signal, transforming it into a mel-spectrogram over multiple diffusion time steps. Inspired by
Wang et al. 2023
, we incorporate a reference speaker’s sound prompt during training. This sound prompt serves as a conditioning factor to control the timbre of the synthesized speech, allowing for higher speaker control abilities.

The framework starts from two convolution encoders, one for speech units and the other for handling reference audio prompts. The features generated by these two encoders are then concatenated and fed into a series of Wavenet-like
(
Van Den Oord et al. 2016
)
blocks to reconstruct the mel-spectrogram. Once the mel-spectrogram has been produced, we feed it into HiFi-GAN
(
Kong et al. 2020
)
, which converts the mel-spectrogram back into a high-fidelity speech waveform, completing the speech synthesis process.

2.5
Duplex Interaction

The ability of language models to engage in seamless, real-time communication has been a significant challenge in developing user-friendly voice agents. Typically, current interactions with LLMs are based on turn-based conversation, where users input a complete query and wait for the model’s full response before continuing. As shown in Figure
2(a)
, this conventional design relies on explicit end-of-turn signals to manage turn-taking behavior (such as typing “enter” in the chat interface), creating a rigid back-and-forth pattern where user input is disabled during model generation. This approach, while functional for text-based interactions, is unsuitable for fluid speech conversations that lack such explicit markers. The challenge lies in developing systems that can process partial inputs in real time, anticipate user intent, and seamlessly integrate into dynamic conversations without rigid structures. Such models must dynamically determine when to generate a response and when to continue listening, emulating natural conversation flow. Achieving this dynamic turn-taking behavior is crucial for creating natural, fluid conversations and avoiding awkward pauses or overlapping speech.

In a natural conversation, participants should be able to speak at any moment, just as humans do in face-to-face interactions. This creates two critical challenges in duplex dialogue systems: determining when to start talking (either when detecting the user has finished their turn or after a period of mutual silence) and deciding when to stop (either due to user interruption or completion of the system’s response). As shown in Figure
2(b)
, we address these challenges through a dynamic end-of-turn detection method that processes speech input in chunks. For each incoming chunk, our system employs an automatic checking process that leverages the end-of-turn token inherently present in Llama3. This allows us to detect turn completion without requiring any specific training. Figure
2(c)
illustrates this checking process in detail - the system feeds accumulated chunks of input (from “Tell me” to “Tell me a story about you”) into the model to obtain next-token probability distributions. When the probability of the end-of-turn token exceeds a predetermined threshold, the system interprets it as a completion signal and initiates response generation. Following human conversation etiquette, our system is designed to be polite and attentive - if the user interrupts during the system’s generation, the model immediately stops its current generation to process the new input, ensuring the user always has priority in the conversation. Additionally, we implement a silence counter to track periods when neither party is speaking. When the silence exceeds a significant amount of time, the model initiates conversation to maintain engagement, similar to how humans naturally fill conversational gaps.
In conclusion, we present a duplex interaction system that enables fluid conversation between users and systems. Our approach leverages Llama3’s inherent capability and rule-based method to achieve natural turn-taking behavior without requiring additional specialized training.

Data Preparation

3.1
Real Data Preparation

We used an in-house dataset with 40,000 hours of real-world speech data. To transform the raw real-world audio into structured conversations, we employed several speech-processing techniques to segment the conversation, and then we utilized an ASR model to obtain the corresponding transcriptions of the real-world audio.

3.1.1
Conversation Segmentation

Initially, speaker diarization is applied to determine the precise time intervals during which each speaker is active. Subsequently, source separation is utilized to resolve overlapping speech segments involving two or more speakers. Following this, the speech segments attributed to each speaker are concatenated by speaker verification to construct a continuous speech track for each individual. Finally, we segment the resulting speech tracks into conversations, ensuring a maximum of 25 conversational turns involving two speakers within a duration of five minutes.

For speaker diarization, we employ the off-the-shelf speaker diarization model

Plaquet & Bredin 2023

3.2
Synthetic Data Preparation

In the preliminary study, we found that training a spoken LLM solely with real-world dialogue data causes severe catastrophic forgetting of the skills present in the initialized text LLM. When trained only on real-world dialogue, the spoken LLM cannot produce semantically coherent responses. This issue likely arises because real-world dialogue has a distribution that is very different from the training data distribution of initialized text LLM. To prevent catastrophic forgetting of the initial text LLM’s skills and to expand the size of our training dataset, we used text LLMs to produce multi-turn text conversations and then transformed text dialogues into multi-turn spoken dialogues using our Text-to-Speech system.

3.2.1
Creation of Text Dialogue

We adopted strong text-based LLMs to generate diverse synthetic dialogue. Our synthetic dialogues can be classified into two types: “with” or “without” interruption and speaker overlapping. The former aims to simulate real-world full-duplex conversations in which people may interrupt each other or speak simultaneously, while the latter simply stands for turn-taking communication without any interruption or speaker overlapping. This helps us better simulate real-world conversation dynamics, where people either take turns speaking or talk over each other.

To generate high-quality and diverse synthetic dialogues, we developed a pipeline consisting of three stages: “conversation scenario generation”, “dialogue generation” and “interruption and overlapping insertion”, as shown in Figure
3
. We first generated diverse dialogue scenarios as the basis of the subsequent steps in the first stage. Next, we produced turn-taking dialogues based on these scenarios, collecting and formatting them as training data without interruptions or overlaps. Finally, in the last stage, we inserted interruptions and overlapping speech into the dialogues to create training data that simulates full-duplex conversations. As a result, we obtained two distinct types of synthetic dialogues. Below, we provide the details of our pipeline.

In the conversation scenario generation stage, we prompted GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al. 2024
)
to generate a diverse set of scenarios, i.e., the role-playing settings, of the conversations. This led to approximately 200 distinct scenarios representing various everyday conversational contexts, such as interactions between users and AI assistants, doctors and patients, teachers and students, and wedding consultants and newlyweds, among others.

Next, in the dialogue generation stage, we produced turn-taking dialogues based on the scenarios generated in the previous stage. We employed two distinct generation strategies here. The first strategy is similar to Magpie
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, a self-synthesis method that automatically generates both instructions and responses. Using this framework, we created multi-turn conversations in a turn-by-turn manner. Specifically, we generated a single turn with each instance of LLM inference, aggregating the already generated content to serve as the prefix and context for subsequent inferences. By utilizing a high temperature of 0.9, we found that the dialogues generated using this strategy were significantly diverse while maintaining coherence. For this approach, we selected LLaMA-3.1 70B due to its accessible tokenization process and strong generation capabilities. The second strategy involved handcrafting a system prompt to direct the model to function like a playwright, resulting in the generation of the entire multi-turn dialogue. For this approach, we selected GPT-4o, which demonstrated impressive abilities in producing consistent and coherent dialogues based solely on the system prompt. The dialogues generated with these strategies in this stage were collected and formatted to be the training data that did not contain interruption or speaker overlapping.

Finally, we turned the dialogues generated in the previous stage into full-duplex ones in the last stage. We again employed GPT-4o here. We provided the previously generated dialogues to GPT-4o and asked it to insert suitable interruptions or simultaneous speech contents into the existing dialogues. As part of the system prompts, a hand-crafted guideline was also provided to improve its performance. The guideline included several rules:

1.

The inserted content should be coherent with the original dialogue, which means the model should be aware of the context and should not insert irrelevant content or violate the role-playing settings.

2.

The model should be sensitive to the speaker of the turn where it inserts content, and avoid speaker inconsistency, such as making someone interrupt himself/herself, etc.

3.

If inserting text into the existing conversation would result in awkward or unclear expressions, the model is allowed to make slight adjustments to the original dialogue to maintain fluency and coherence.

With some demonstrations of each rule in the guideline, GPT-4o typically could insert natural and coherent interruptions or speaker overlapping into existing dialogues. In this stage, we collected the generated dialogue to serve as the full-duplex conversation data for training.

3.2.2
Speech Synthesis

Once the text-based conversations are generated by the LLM, we employ TTS models to transform them into speech.
However, there is currently no TTS model capable of producing natural-sounding speech in Taiwanese Mandarin.
To address this, we fine-tuned CosyVoice-300M
(
Du et al. 2024
)
using our in-house dataset, enabling it to generate speech in Taiwanese Mandarin.
There will be another technical report available soon about the efforts to fine-tune CosyVoice.
For both instructions and responses in these conversations, we utilized our fine-tuned TTS model, randomly selecting pairs of utterances to represent different speakers in the dialogue.

3.3
Interleaved Sequence Forming

We transform spoken utterances into interleaved sequences of discrete text and speech units through a three-step process. First, speech units are extracted using a streaming HuBERT model, as detailed in Section
5.1.2
. Then, we transcribe the speech into text using Whisper with word level timestamp. Finally, texts are inserted into the speech unit sequence by the corresponding timestamps, as outlined in Section
5.1.3
.

3.4
Data Filtering

We performed rule-based filtering to filter out low-quality data. The process focused on filtering out the data with the following patterns:

1.

ASR Hallucination Filtering
: Since we used the Whisper model when forming the interleaved sequences of text and speech, hallucination may occur during this process. We identified some common hallucination patterns and filtered out the data containing these patterns.

2.

Language Filtering
: As we aimed to develop a spoken LLM for Taiwanese Mandarin, we filtered out the data that were entirely in English in our real-world speech data.

3.

Missing Turn Filtering
: We observed that some real-world multi-turn dialogue data may contain missing turns. This often happens during conversation segmentation, where parts of a dialogue might be unintentionally removed, causing breaks in the natural flow. Additionally, filtering out inaccurate or hallucinated words and sentences during transcription can create further gaps, making the data less suitable for training. To avoid these issues, we chose to exclude such data from our dataset.

After the filtering, we processed the dataset for training, which will be explained in Section
4
.

4
Training

We describe the training setup of the components of our spoken LLM, including the LLM backbone and the speech decoder.

4.1
LLM Backbone Training

The training process for our spoken LLM consists of two primary phases: Pre-training and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). During pre-training, the model learns to align text and speech units, which were unseen for the backbone model before, through tasks such as Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and Text-To-Speech (TTS). The SFT phase then focuses on refining the model’s ability to handle spoken dialogue.

As previously mentioned in Section
2.2
, we aimed to equip the model with modality control ability. Thus, we processed the data in Section
3.1
and
3.2
into various input/output modalities and generated corresponding system prompts, with the format like the one in Section
2.2
, to form the training dataset of the subsequent pre-training and SFT phases. We detailed the two training phases in the following sections.

4.1.1
Pre-training

Since we use a text-based LLM, i.e., LLaMA-3.1 8B, as the initialization of the spoken language model, the speech units are new to the text-based backbone. Therefore, it is crucial for the model to get familiar with these newly added speech units and the relationship between these units and the existing text tokens before the training of spoken dialogue. This is the goal of the pre-training phase.

The pre-training phase utilizes a diverse dataset comprising 24k hours of speech data. In this stage, the model is trained to align text tokens with speech units through ASR and TTS tasks. For ASR, the model learns to recognize the text content of the speech based on a sequence of speech units and predict the text content or the interleaved sequence of text content and the speech units (hybrid modality). We did not include the hybrid modality as the input modality because this will make the task too simple to build the text-speech alignment, i.e., the model only learns to extract the text tokens from the interleaved sequence without any understanding of alignment. Nevertheless, we incorporated the hybrid modality for the labels in the hope of strengthening the text-speech alignment, since the model had to know which speech units in the given sequences represent each predicted text token.

On the other hand, TTS involves training the model to generate speech units corresponding to the given text content. Here, we defined the so-called “correspondence” as follows: a unit sequence corresponds to the text content if and only if the voice synthesized with that unit sequence can clearly convey the content. While the ASR task is discriminative, the TTS task is essentially generative. These two kinds of training can complement each other to help the model learn the alignment better. Similar to the ASR training, we include the hybrid, as well as the pure units, as a possible label modality to enhance the text-speech alignment.

The training data for the aforementioned two tasks originated from the real speech data in Section
3.1
and the synthetic dialogue data in Section
3.2
.
Specifically, we drew a subset of the prepared speech data and separated all the turns in those multi-turn dialogue data into individual single-turn data. We then used the prepared transcriptions and the units to form the datasets for ASR and TTS. We also included the system prompts containing corresponding input/output modality combinations and the role-playing instructions with the “speech recognition model” and “text-to-speech model” as the roles.

Besides ASR and TTS, this stage also included text-based training data to ensure that the model retains its language comprehension abilities.
The text-based training data consists of the transcriptions of the multi-turn spoken dialogue data (without interruption or speaker overlapping) that are approximately equivalent to 20k hours of speech, resulting in a pre-training dataset totaling about 44k hours of speech.
The equivalent duration of the purely textual data was determined by their speech counterpart. The input consists of a system prompt that incorporates both the modality control prefix, where both the input and output modalities are text tokens, and the role-playing instructions that define the roles of the model depending on the scenarios of the dialogues. We used the original multi-turn conversation template of LLaMA-3.1 to concatenate the turns, with special tokens of headers inserted between turns and turns to specify the current speaker for each turn. 4.1.2
SFT

The SFT phase focuses on enhancing the model’s proficiency in multi-turn conversation with various input/output modalities and role-playing scenarios. This stage involves fine-tuning the model on an extensive dataset with a total duration of
\sim
100k hours, ensuring the model can effectively understand and communicate in speech in diverse contexts and scenarios.

To preserve the model’s text-speech alignment capabilities developed in the pre-training phase, ASR tasks with 12k hours of speech data used in the pre-training were also incorporated into the SFT phase. This ensures the model retains its proficiency in mapping between text and speech while improving its conversational abilities.

We included multi-turn dialogue data with various input/output modality combinations in the SFT phase. Purely text-based dialogues, i.e., input and label are both in text, were employed to maintain the model’s general textual ability. Here, we only used the dialogue data from Section
3.1
and
3.2
that did not contain any interruption or speaker overlapping for purely text-based training data. The text-based data equivalently amounted to about 29.5k hours with appropriate processing like the one for the text-based pre-training data.

As for the spoken dialogue data that involved speech units, we used 55.5k hours of spoken dialogues. We restricted the possible input/output modality combinations for these data into only five different categories, with three kinds of input modalities (pure text tokens, pure speech units, and interleaved sequence of the text tokens and speech units) and two kinds of output modalities (pure text tokens and hybrid). All the possible combinations were included except for the combination with input and output modalities both in text. The output modality of pure speech units was excluded because we hope that the model can respond with speech while simultaneously outputting the corresponding text content for the user to view, making the pure speech unit sequence unsuitable here. On the contrary, The purpose of including the pure units as one of the input modalities was to make the model familiar with conversations whose inputs contain no text tokens, thus mitigating the reliance on the streaming ASR model in Figure
1
. Ideally, this can have benefits in that we can omit the ASR and directly input the extracted units into the spoken LLM, reducing the latency and allowing more real-time interaction.

We processed the transcriptions and the extracted unit sequences of the data to formulate the dialogues into an equal amount for each input/output modality combination. The process was similar to the one for the pre-training dataset but with two distinctions. The first one was that we did not restrict the conversation data to be single-turn like what we did for ASR and TTS training. Instead, we formulated them with the inherent multi-turn template of LLaMA-3.1 and used the header tokens to specify the speakers of the turns, which was similar to the processing of the text-based dialogues.

The other distinction was that we included the case of interruption and speaker overlapping in the training data of spoken dialogues, which was excluded for purely text-based dialogue training. We again used the header tokens to mark the speaker changes, and we inserted the interrupting content at the point where the current speaker’s speech was interrupted. This divided the turn of the current speaker into two parts and made it incomplete as a sudden speaker switching occurred. An illustration of this processing is demonstrated in Figure

. Although this method compressed the two simultaneous speech signals into a single stream, the model should still learn to predict the second part of the content despite the interruption. We believe this can enable the model to know what it is saying and what it intends to say next, even when someone interrupts it or speaks at the same time.

, we discuss the latency of the system. Our code is publicly available

5.1
System Components

5.1.1
Streaming ASR

For streaming ASR, we follow
Macháček et al. 2023
, converting Whisper into a streaming version.
Specifically, during real-time inference, we send an audio segment to Whisper every 0.3 seconds to get hypothesis transcriptions.
Then, we compare the current segment’s hypothesis transcription with those of previous segments to see if there are identical prefixes.
If there is an identical prefix, that prefix becomes a confirmed transcription.
All the other parts remain in the audio buffer to continue matching prefixes.

In the original paper, there are two different methods for how to trim the confirmed transcriptions.
The first method trims complete sentences, while the second one trims a fixed number of seconds.
The drawback of the first method is that if the sentence is too long, it can lead to long latency during real-time inference.
Therefore, we choose to use the second method.

Based on previous work, we have also implemented some new features:

1.

Warmup
: Our system feeds a random audio clip to Whisper for a warmup before any client connections are made.

2.

De-Hallucinate
: We use a rule-based approach to eliminate hallucinations by filtering out some commonly hallucinated patterns.

3.

Reset
: We have implemented a feature to reset the entire ASR system in real time. It resets when there is a hallucination, turn taking, or when the user types the special command ‘===’ with text.

5.1.2
Streaming Speech Units

When extracting speech units in real-time, as shown in Figure
5
, we divide the input audio into segments, each containing 0.1 seconds of audio.
Whenever we receive a new 0.1-second audio clip, we add it to the audio buffer and then send all the audio segments from the buffer to HuBERT
(
Hsu et al. 2021
)
to extract speech units.
We extract speech units for the new 0.1-second audio clip, conditioned on all the audio segments in the audio buffer.
To prevent the audio length from increasing indefinitely, a window size of 1 second is applied.
When the total length of the audio in the buffer exceeds this window size, we discard the oldest audio segment from the buffer.

Figure 5
:
An illustration of how we extract HuBERT units during real-time inference streamingly.

5.1.3
Real-time Interleaving

In real-time, we need to interleave transcriptions and their corresponding speech units before sending them to language models.
Since the latency of ASR is much longer than that of HuBERT, we choose to perform interleaving around the transcriptions.
We present a simple toy example for illustration, where the ASR model generates (1) Hi, How, followed by (2) Are You.
Specifically, as shown in Figure
6
, whenever ASR produces a new transcription, we obtain the time spans for different words within that transcription.
Based on these time spans, we take the corresponding sequences of speech units for interleaving.
This method of interleaving might discard some speech units between two transcriptions, for example, the light blue units in Figure
6
.
To address this issue, we check for any speech units that were missed between the current and the previous transcriptions and append up to 2 seconds of these missed speech units to the beginning of the interleaved sequence.

Figure 6
:
An illustration of interleaved transcription and speech units in real-time. We use a simple toy example as an illustration, where the ASR model first generates (1) Hi, How, and then generates (2) Are You. Here, “Thunder” and “Rain” refer to the background environmental sounds in the input speech.

5.1.4
Streaming Speech Unit Decoder

In Section
2.4
, we describe a diffusion-based decoder trained to synthesize output speech units back into audio. However, this decoder is designed for offline processing and does not inherently support streaming. To approximate streaming in the inference phase, we apply chunking, similar to the streaming approach in ASR, where speech unit segments are sent to the decoder in real time. Despite this, the audio generated by the decoder suffers from discontinuities at segment boundaries, affecting overall quality. Replacing it with a fully functional streaming decoder is left for future work.

Although directly fitting an offline decoder into a streaming scenario is far from perfect, we still try to mitigate the artifact by decreasing the number of total chunks. We propose a
dynamic chunking
strategy which aims to make each chunk as long as possible without increasing the latency.

Figure
7
illustrates the dynamic chunking strategy. We always chunk as late as possible, before the audio resynthesized from the previous chunk is finished playing. If the speech units are generated faster than real-time, we can expect the
k
-th chunk to be longer than the
(k-1)
-th chunk, overall decreasing the number of chunks needed and therefore the occurrence of the boundaries. Since we can calculate the time represented by each chunk in advance, we can schedule the next chunk as late as possible. Denote the time of the
k
-th chunking as
t_{k}
, the size of the
k
-th chunk as
n_{k}
, and the time represented by each unit as
t_{unit}
. The next chunking is scheduled as

t_{k+1}=t_{k}+n_{k}\times t_{unit}-\epsilon,

where one can adjust
\epsilon
manually to resynthesize the next chunk slightly earlier. Since decoder forwarding requires processing time, the design aims to prevent gaps between chunks by ensuring the next chunk is resynthesized before the previous audio chunk finishes playing.

Figure 7
:
The dynamic chunking strategy.

5.2
System Optimization

5.2.1
Turn-Taking Implementation

Unlike the offline scenario, in the streaming case, LLM requires a turn-taking protocol to determine when the user stops and starts the inference session. The determined process itself also introduces latency, as shown in Figure
8
a.

Figure 8
:
Two different turn-taking implementations. (a) The straightforward implementation. (b) The optimized asynchronous version without idle.

To mitigate the latency, note that the determined process and inference session can be performed asynchronously. The key idea is to restart a new inference session immediately after receiving any new input. The output is only confirmed when a turn-taking signal is detected, as illustrated in Figure
8
b. In this implementation, the turn-taking detection process overlaps with the inference session, allowing the algorithm to operate without idle waiting.

5.2.2
LLM Inference Acceleration

Thanks to the rapid development of LLM in recent years, numerous frameworks exist to accelerate the inference speed of LLM. In this work, we use TensorRT-LLM
9
9

9

https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-LLM
to accelerate the inference speed. After applying TensorRT-LLM with int4 weight-only-quantization, our LLM can generate about 100 tokens per second on a single Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. Since we use 25Hz speech units, the speed is fast enough for a real-time system. Note that without acceleration, our LLM can only generate about 20 tokens per second, which does not apply to building a real-time system.

5.3
System Latency

The latency is defined as the time between the user input ends and the system starts to speak. Streaming ASR with interleaving produces nearly 900ms latency, LLM needs about 400ms to generate the first token, and the decoder needs about 200ms. Additionally, the turn-taking wait time is capped at 1 second, resulting in a maximum latency of 2500 ms. By applying the technique described in Section
5.2.1
, we reduce the maximum possible latency to 1900 ms.

Such latency is still very high compared to humans, which is reported to be under 500ms
(
Jacoby et al. 2024
)
. One reason is that streaming Whisper and streaming decoder both apply a chunking algorithm, which inevitably introduces a large latency. Applying these models trained under offline scenarios for streaming harms their performance a lot and is also problematic. This suggests that all components should inherently support streaming when building a real-time system.

6.1
Multi-turn Dialogue

We design multi-turn dialogues on various topics, including expressing opinions on hip-hop music, daily discussions about pet care, casual movie chats, thoughts on social media, and relationship counseling.
These topics range from everyday conversations to more specialized discussions. The objectives of the evaluation process are:

1.

To test the model’s ability to understand the user’s speech and respond appropriately within the topic’s context.

This is because the ability to respond appropriately within the context of the topic is a fundamental metric for evaluating the model’s conversational skills.
Our goal is to maintain the original text language model’s proficiency in handling text-based content.

2.

To evaluate the intelligibility of the model’s spoken responses.

The model’s speech output forms the user’s first impression of its performance.
Even if the textual content is appropriate, low intelligibility or unnatural speech could significantly impact the user’s perception and cognition of the model’s quality.

Our methodology for constructing multi-turn dialogues involves two steps: first, selecting representative topics as conversation scenarios, followed by having human experts design structured user utterances to facilitate model interactions.
For example, we might select “restaurant reservation” as a scenario, where human experts design sequential user inputs such as asking about table availability, specifying dining preferences, and confirming reservation details.
Similarly, for a “technical support” scenario, the designed dialogue might include users describing their problems, responding to troubleshooting questions, and acknowledging solution steps.

6.1.1
Contextual Coherence and Accuracy Evaluation via LLM Score

In order to assess whether the model’s responses align with the dialogue’s context and situation, we employ the LLM-as-a-judge approach
(
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Chiang & Lee 2023
)
.
Specifically, we provide GPT-4o
10
10

10

The version we used in this work is
gpt-4o-2024-05-13
.

(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
with the dialogue topic and the entire conversation history.
The goal of this method is to use large language models to predict whether the model’s responses match the dialogue topic and maintain consistency with the conversation’s context.

The detailed evaluation prompt is presented in Appendix
A.2
.
The evaluation prompt presented in this work is designed to systematically assess the conversational performance of a voice agent.
LLM Evaluators are instructed to rate the interaction between the agent and a human user across three specific dimensions: relevance and accuracy, adherence to the system prompt, and grammatical correctness.
Each dimension is scored on a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 represents poor performance and 5 represents excellence.

The
Relevance and Accuracy
dimension aims to evaluate the agent’s ability to respond appropriately to the user’s requests.
Evaluators are asked to consider whether the responses directly address the user’s questions and provide correct, relevant information.
In the
Adherence to System Prompt
dimension, the focus shifts to whether the agent follows the instructions or role outlined in the system prompt. The evaluation ensures that the agent’s responses remain consistent with its designated role and knowledge constraints.
The
Grammatical Correctness
criterion assesses the linguistic quality of the agent’s replies, evaluating them for proper sentence structure, word usage, and overall fluency.

For each category, the LLM evaluator provides a brief justification explaining their rating.
After rating all three dimensions, an overall score is calculated by averaging the three ratings and rounding to the nearest whole number.
The overall score represents the contextual coherence of the conversation on a scale from 1 to 5. The higher overall score indicates stronger contextual coherence.
This comprehensive scoring system enables a clear, structured evaluation of the voice agent’s conversational capabilities across multiple critical aspects.

However, this pre-designed multi-turn dialogue testing method has a limitation: it doesn’t allow for real-time interactions based on the model’s responses, such as asking follow-up questions or requesting further explanations.
To address this lack of immediate interaction, we introduce a new approach in the next subsection called “Forum of Spoken Agents”.

6.2
Forum of Spoken Agents

Spoken agents are systems capable of engaging in natural spoken conversations.
This paper presents a mechanism designed to assess the performance of spoken agents through two distinct tracks: one involving role-based conversations between agents, and the other featuring direct user interactions with the agents.

6.2.1
Track 1: Conversations Between Two Spoken Agents

The first track evaluates the performance of spoken agents through role-based dialogues.
This track provides a controlled environment to compare different models and measure how effectively they fulfill assigned roles in the conversation.
We show the actual interface in Appendix
A.3
.

Here, we use two spoken LLMs (agents) to talk to each other and generate a conversation.
Each conversation takes place within a predefined scenario, with two agents assigned specific roles via system prompts.
These prompts provide guidance on how each agent should behave throughout the dialogue.
For example, in the “Fitness Trainer and Trainee” scenario, the first agent assumes the role of a professional fitness coach.
This agent is responsible for evaluating the trainee’s goals and physical condition, designing a suitable training plan, demonstrating proper exercise techniques, and offering dietary advice.
The language is expected to be encouraging, with patient responses to the trainee’s inquiries.
The second agent plays the role of a beginner in fitness.
It expresses fitness goals, such as weight loss or muscle gain, describes current lifestyle habits, and asks how to start training effectively.
This agent may also express confusion about using equipment, seeking detailed instructions and encouragement from the coach.

The evaluation process involves human assessors rating the agents’ performance across three dimensions:
(1) Intelligibility
: clarity and comprehensibility of speech output.
(2) Naturalness
: authenticity of conversational flow.

(3) Overall impression
: holistic assessment of agent effectiveness.
Human evaluators indicate their preference between the two agents in each conversation.
This comparative assessment helps identify which model performs better in maintaining natural, role-appropriate dialogue within the given scenario. Through this methodology, we combine structured dialogue scenarios with human preference evaluation to assess the models’ conversational capabilities comprehensively.
As this work is still in progress, detailed evaluation results will be made publicly available upon completion.
The detailed findings from this ongoing study will be publicly disclosed after completion.

6.2.2
Track 2: User-Driven Conversations with Spoken Agents

The second track allows users to interact directly with spoken agents.
Users begin by defining the system prompt and setting the context and expectations for the conversation.
They are then free to engage with the agent in an open-ended dialogue.
The objective is to allow users to evaluate spoken agents based on their interaction experience.

During human-agent interactions, evaluators assess the model’s performance across three key dimensions, providing feedback through upvotes and downvotes.
The first dimension is response quality, measuring how well the agent understands and provides coherent, appropriate answers.
The second dimension focuses on conversational flow, evaluating the agent’s ability to maintain natural dialogue with appropriate turn-taking and contextual awareness.
The third dimension examines task completion, assessing the agent’s overall effectiveness in meeting user needs and achieving the conversation’s objectives.

While Track 1 focuses on interactions between two spoken agents, Track 2 involves direct interaction between users and spoken agents.
Track 1’s advantage lies in its ability to automate interactions between different spoken agents, while Track 2 benefits from direct human involvement in the conversation process.
However, both tracks share a common element: human participation in the final evaluation phase.
This ensures that the scoring process better reflects human preferences and expectations.
As this work is still in progress, detailed evaluation results will be made publicly available upon completion.

7
Conclusion

This work represents an initial yet comprehensive endeavor to build a Taiwanese Mandarin spoken LLM. Leveraging an end-to-end, decoder-only transformer model, we explored the technical and implementation aspects necessary for creating an interactive, full-duplex conversational experience. Our approach incorporates innovative architectural and data-processing techniques, using a decoder-only transformer for both speech and text processing. These enhancements enable the model to handle interruptions and respond in real time.

Despite challenges in managing real-time responses with low latency, our approach optimizes components such as streaming ASR, real-time interleaving, and dynamic chunking, advancing the model’s capacity for seamless spoken exchanges to a notable extent. However, current latencies still fall short of human conversational standards. Limitations remain in latency and in the integration of components under real-time conditions, highlighting the need for further advancements in streaming architectures.

This project represents an initial exploration, with limitations that future work can address. Key areas for further research include optimizing latency, enhancing real-time processing for faster response times, and expanding the model’s versatility to handle diverse dialects and emotional expressions.
This project began in the summer of 2024 and may not continue unless we secure additional support.
At the start, resources and literature on spoken LLMs were limited; even Moshi and GPT-4o voice mode had not been released.
In contrast to text-based LLM development, which has abundant literature to guide developers as they ‘

摸著石頭過河’ (cross the river by feeling for stones), the development of spoken LLMs is like navigating largely uncharted waters. We have encountered numerous challenges in this project and learned a great deal along the way. We believe this project lays a foundation for spoken LLMs in Taiwanese Mandarin, providing groundwork on which future developments can build.
