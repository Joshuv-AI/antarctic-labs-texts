Endoscopic endonasal resection of a craniopharyngioma in a 23-month-old patient: illustrative case
Authors: Jessica Eaton, Spencer Raub, Rebecca Ronsley, Christian L. Roth, Ralph Ermoian, Seth D. Friedman, Samuel N. Emerson, Manuel Ferreira, Amy Lee, Randall A. Bly, Jacob Ruzevick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDTreatment of pediatric craniopharyngioma requires a multidisciplinary approach to counsel patients and families on the spectrum of treatment options, including biopsy, radiation, and/or resection. Gross-total resection can avoid radiation and its long-term comorbidities. In very young patients, this is of particular importance but is especially challenging because of anatomical considerations.OBSERVATIONSA 23-month-old boy was found to have a partially calcified and cystic sellar and suprasellar mass. A fully endoscopic endonasal transtuberculum and transsellar approach was performed for gross-total resection of a subdiaphragmatic adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma. Postoperatively, the patient was diagnosed with panhypopituitarism with diabetes insipidus, though without other hypothalamic dysfunction or new visual deficits. To date, there is no evidence of tumor recurrence.LESSONSIn a high-volume center with an experienced multidisciplinary skull base team, endoscopic endonasal approaches to sellar and suprasellar pathology in children younger than 2 years can be safely performed. A smaller nasal cavity and lack of sinus aeration necessitate wide exposure via bone removal and complete opening of the sinuses to enable an adequate working corridor to perform not only tumor resection but also reconstruction. Safe gross-total resection can avoid, or at least delay, radiation and its long-term morbidity when performed in a developing child. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24209

Craniopharyngioma, while relatively rare with an annual incidence of 0.5–2 cases per million persons, remains one of the most complex neurosurgical pathologies among pediatric patients.1 Its growth within the sella, suprasellar space, third ventricle, and/or ventral brainstem cisterns causes considerable morbidity, including hydrocephalus, vision decline, hypothalamic dysregulation, and pituitary dysfunction. Consensus on a gold standard for treatment has not been identified, as treatment strategies vary widely depending on institutional experience.

Endoscopic endonasal surgery in young children for midline intracranial pathology is especially challenging due to limited working corridors and underdeveloped sinus aeration.2 A conchal-type sphenoid sinus, often present until age 4 years, requires extensive drilling to optimize access to the sella with care to identify the lamina, planum sphenoidale, and paramidline anterior skull base without injury to critical structures. A narrow intercarotid distance in addition to limited visualization due to pituitary transposition limits instrumentation into the suprasellar space and third ventricle for tumor dissection or the management of iatrogenic injury. In addition, skull base reconstruction is made challenging by the small amount of mucosal surface available for a pedicled nasoseptal flap. Alternative surgical strategies to mitigate these challenges include combined transcranial and endoscopic approaches or staged endoscopic approaches.

Alternatively, fractionated radiation limits some aspects of the surgical risk profile but can contribute to panhypopituitarism, hypothalamic obesity, neurological and cognitive morbidity, delayed vascular changes, and radiation-induced neoplasms. While outcomes related to tumor control are largely limited to single-patient series and meta-analyses, current data suggest that surgical and radiation approaches offer similar tumor control rates.3, 4

Case series of craniopharyngioma treated via the endoscopic endonasal approach are limited, especially in very young children, with no reports of children younger than 2 years.3, 5–17 In this report, we describe the clinical presentation, decision-making, surgical care, and postoperative outcomes of a 23-month-old boy who underwent a fully endoscopic endonasal resection of craniopharyngioma with skull base reconstruction.

Illustrative Case

A 23-month-old boy, born 5 weeks premature, presented for a workup of nystagmus and falls. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed a giant partially calcified and cystic sellar and suprasellar mass, consistent with craniopharyngioma. There was significant mass effect on the optic chiasm. The lesion displaced the lamina terminalis but did not extend into the third ventricle, and there was no associated ventriculomegaly. There was no growth beyond the dorsum sellae into the prepontine cistern. The lateral extent of the lesion respected the posterior communicating arteries without encasement, suggesting that it had not invaded beyond the diaphragma sellae. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a midline nasal septum with a conchal-type sphenoid sinus. Advanced surgical planning was performed by analyzing imaging data, which was segmented for the lesion, paranasal sinuses, and critical neurovascular structures using Mimics software (Materialise). This allowed for three-dimensional visualization of the lesion within the patient’s anatomy, virtual endoscopy, and trajectory planning. It was determined that the lesion could be accessed transnasally and that the area of the nasoseptal flap would be adequate for reconstruction. The intercarotid distance was 1.5 cm. The lesion volume was 8.7 cm3, and approach angles relative to the hard palate to reach the entire lesion ranged from 18° to 45°. With early cystic decompression, the superior pole of the lesion was expected to descend, eliminating the need for a significant superior approach angle beyond the tuberculum sellae (Fig. 2). The patient underwent an endoscopic transsphenoidal approach, jointly performed by the otolaryngology (R.A.B.) and neurosurgery (J.R. and A.L.) teams. A right frontal external ventricular drain (EVD) was placed under navigation for temporary cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diversion. To maximize working angles, exposure included partial resection of the bilateral middle turbinates, bilateral maxillary antrostomies, bilateral anterior and posterior ethmoidectomies with exposure of the laminae, and wide drilling of the nonpneumatized sphenoid region to gain access to the planum, sellar floor, face of the sella, and tuberculum. This was necessary to increase the range of motion for maneuvering the endoscope and instruments as instruments were passed in both nares. A right-sided nasoseptal flap pedicled on the sphenopalatine artery was harvested to maximize tissue, with extension anteriorly to the caudal septal cartilage and inclusion of the entire floor of the nose (to inferolateral to the inferior turbinate). The skull base exposure included resection of the anterior sellar floor, the face of the sella, and the tuberculum. The sellar dura was opened sharply, and after coagulation of the superior intercavernous sinus, the opening was extended to the dura of the tuberculum, without breach of the anterior diaphragma. Opening the dura allowed for the immediate egress of cystic fluid, and a solid, calcified component was debulked and mobilized. The cyst wall could also be dissected from the lateral walls and resected. The infundibulum was identified and contained an obvious tumor without a clear plane and was sectioned. Following resection, there was no evidence of diaphragma breach. Although no CSF was encountered, given the need for possible radiation in the future, a complex, multilayer skull base reconstruction was performed using inlays of fat and fascia lata grafts. The previously harvested nasoseptal flap was onlayed to the demucosalized skull base and bolstered using surgical fibrin glue and absorbable packing (Fig. 3). Postoperatively, the patient was at his neurological baseline without an obvious change in vision. He developed panhypopituitarism with DI, requiring titration to long-term doses of hydrocortisone, levothyroxine, and desmopressin. His growth trajectory slowed, and serum growth factors remained low, as expected following surgery. The EVD was removed, and there was no evidence of a postoperative CSF leak or the development of delayed hydrocephalus in the follow-up period.

Postoperatively, he remained at his neurological baseline and continued to develop age-appropriate cognitive milestones. There was no evidence of hypothalamic dysfunction, and the ophthalmological examination showed an improved, now normal fundoscopic examination. Clinical endoscopy showed a well-healed surgical site without the need for additional sinus surgery. Postoperative imaging continued to show gross-total resection without evidence of recurrence at 6 months of imaging follow-up. In addition, there was a return of normal anatomical localization of the optic chiasm, without apparent distortion or injury to the hypothalamus or changes in ventricular anatomy (Fig. 4). Pathology was consistent with a World Health Organization grade 1 adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma. Next-generation sequencing analysis revealed a somatic missense mutation in exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene [NM_001904.4(CTNNB1):c.98C>T (p.Ser33Phe)] without a BRAF-V600E mutation.

Discussion

We describe a successful fully extended endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal transsellar and transtuberculum approach for resection of an adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma in a 23-month-old patient.

The treatment of pediatric craniopharyngioma remains controversial, requiring a multidisciplinary discussion of the immediate and long-term risk profiles of treatment options, especially in very young children. In the modern surgical era, the endoscopic endonasal approach affords a high-resolution trajectory view of the tumor with panoramic visualization of adjacent neurovascular structures. This has evolved from transcranial approaches requiring extensive intradural dissection, brain retraction, and resection around structures such as the optic nerve without direct visualization of sellar contents. In select cases, however, both can be employed for maximal resection, depending on the extent of lateral and superior tumor invasion. In addition, separate endoscopic transventricular approaches can be utilized to address cystic growth into the ventricular system. Though surgical approaches afford the possibility of immediate tumor decompression, this approach places the patient at immediate risk of sinonasal morbidity, postoperative CSF leak in the setting of suboptimal skull base reconstruction, likely pituitary dysfunction leading to panhypopituitarism, surgery-related injury to the parasellar carotid circulation, injury to the optic chiasm through direct injury or devascularization, or hypothalamic dysfunction.

Alternatively, treatment via radiotherapy leads to delayed resolution of tumor mass effect but does limit the immediate risks of surgery. However, cystic growth causing acute neurological symptoms can occur, and delayed and long-term morbidity from a radiotherapy approach includes similar panhypopituitarism, hypothalamic dysfunction, and visual pathway injury in addition to radiation-specific morbidity of neurocognitive effects,18, 19 vasculopathy,20 and risk of radiation-induced neoplasms.20 Highly conformal radiation techniques such as proton radiation can partially mitigate the extent of some of these risks but do not completely eliminate any of these risks.

Limited medical options exist, especially for upfront treatment. Interferon and bleomycin have been used for intracystic therapy but require surgical placement of an Ommaya reservoir and catheters within tumor cysts, either through a transventricular approach or, as recently reported, via supraorbital craniotomy.21, 22 In children, as in the presented case, adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma is the predominant variant.23, 24 In the majority of patients, this is associated with BRAF-V600E mutations in CTNNB1 (encoding β-catenin), affecting the WNT/ERK pathway, which is not detectable in papillary craniopharyngioma.24 Other signaling abnormalities include the programmed cell death and sonic hedgehog pathways. While single or combination therapy of BRAF and MEK inhibitors has shown recent success in papillary craniopharyngioma, its utility in adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma remains limited.25 Treatment with the anti-IL6 monoclonal antibody tocilizumab has shown some success in limited case series and is being further investigated in ongoing clinical trials.26

Observations

Surgical Decision-Making

Counseling of treatment options for the presented patient included discussions of the above-described options. Evaluation for surgery relied heavily on preoperative imaging and advanced surgical planning, which showed a subdiaphragmatic suprasellar mass without extension into the third ventricle or lateral extension beyond the posterior communicating arteries. This benefited an endoscopic approach, as subdiaphragmatic craniopharyngiomas arise from the most distal aspect of the infundibulum and tightly adhere to the pituitary gland, allowing for multiple protective layers from the optic chiasm and cisternal/ventricular spaces.27 In addition, the primarily cystic mass would allow for expansion of the intracranial operating corridor, allowing for instrumentation to the suprasellar component of the tumor. Evaluation of the sinus anatomy on preoperative CT showed a partially aerated sphenoid sinus, and analysis of the distance between the medial orbits following planned drilling would allow for tandem visualization using a 2.7-mm endoscope and bimanual instrumentation. Skull base drilling would include the formation of a clival recess and skeletonization of the paraclival and cavernous carotid arteries. Given the extensive enlargement of the sphenoid sinus, the surface area for the formation of a pedicled nasoseptal flap for skull base reconstruction was believed to be adequate. This was favored over a transcranial approach, as it was believed that the endoscopic approach provided the superior trajectory view of the tumor. In addition, we believed that a greater degree of tumor could be resected via the endoscopic approach while sparing the effects of craniotomy, brain retraction, and potential injury to displaced parasellar structures.

In contrast, given the patient’s young age, he was considered high risk for radiation therapy–associated morbidity. Even if a radical resection could not be achieved or a recurrence occurred in the future, our consensus view was that a surgical option would at least decompress the optic chiasm and allow time for continued brain maturation prior to any future radiotherapy interventions.

Surgical Nuances and Complication Avoidance

The surgical approach for our case relied heavily on the principles of skull base surgery, primarily maximal bony resection to optimize the working corridor and limit risks to neurovascular structures. Given the patient’s young age, he was positioned supine with his head on a padded horseshoe headholder rather than in a fixed position, as is typically our preference. Harvesting a nasoseptal flap comprising mucosa of the nasal septum and floor allowed for a wide sellar opening, with exposure of the medial cavernous sinuses. In addition, upfront harvesting and placement within the nasopharynx prevented damage to the flap from passing instrumentation within the smaller corridor. Drilling to expand the sphenoid sinus and skeletonize the skull base was assisted by intraoperative neuronavigation and early visualization of the lamina papyracea to guide the trajectory of the lateral extent of sinus exposure.

The infundibulum and pituitary gland could not be maintained given the small working corridor and obvious invasion into the infundibulum, and the sacrifice of these structures was believed to be the best approach for maximizing the extent of resection and decreasing the risk of recurrence. The bimanual technique working through both nares allowed for maximal freedom of motion of instruments. A smaller endoscope (2.7 mm) was used with a sheath for clearing the image as needed. Tapered suction was used through the left naris, while primary instrumentation was through the right. Constant communication between the primary surgeons optimized visualization to reduce the risk of complications.

Following decompression of the cyst and resection of the calcified component of the tumor, a clear boundary of the diaphragma was appreciated, and care was taken to dissect in the plane of the diaphragma without its disruption. This anatomical nuance, predicted by preoperative imaging, allowed for gross-total resection of the lesion without evidence of an intraoperative CSF leak. Despite the lack of an intraoperative CSF leak, a complex skull base reconstruction was performed to minimize the risk of a postoperative CSF leak, especially if Valsalva maneuvers occurred in the postoperative setting (given the patient’s young age) and if adjuvant radiation was needed in the future. A three-layer technique was used, including inlays of autologous fat and fascia lata. These inlays were optimized by temporarily lowering intracranial pressure by increased ventilation, increasing the degree of reverse-Trendelenburg positioning, and draining CSF from the previously placed EVD. The remaining skull base was drilled to optimize adherence of the previously harvested nasoseptal flap pedicled on the sphenopalatine artery.

Postoperative Care

In-hospital postoperative care centered on close hemodynamic and neurological monitoring. Since the infundibulum was sectioned intraoperatively, central venous access was helpful for frequent blood draws and dosing of desmopressin, which was titrated to urine output. Given the patient’s young age, a low threshold was maintained for temporary sedation to minimize elevations in intracranial pressure and optimize healing of the skull base repair. The EVD remained in place for 5 days postresection, and both clinical and radiographic evaluations were performed to confirm that no permanent CSF diversion was needed for hydrocephalus or CSF leakage.

Once the patient was out of the immediate postoperative period, several days were taken to optimally transition him to home dosing of desmopressin, taper postoperative hydrocortisone from stress dosing to a stable dose, and provide appropriate parental teaching prior to discharge. Levothyroxine was also started prior to discharge. As is our institutional practice, the replacement of growth hormone will be restarted 1 year following resection in the absence of tumor recurrence.

Lessons

Optimal management of pediatric craniopharyngioma remains a major question, with advocates of surgery and radiation therapy as primary therapy often firmly entrenched based on institutional experiences. However, what is clear is that multidisciplinary discussion optimizes patient care.

Careful study of preoperative imaging can guide the multidisciplinary discussion of the optimal upfront therapy and surgical approach.27 Radiographic findings such as a subdiaphragmatic craniopharyngioma can limit the need for pituitary transposition as the tumor fills and often expands the sella and represents a favorable target for an endoscopic approach. Conversely, tumors developing from the most superior aspect of the infundibulum often extend into the third ventricle and can significantly displace the hypothalamus. Similarly, tumors arising from the midportion of the infundibulum often extend beyond the sella into the subarachnoid cisterns. These later variants make resection more challenging as significantly more skull base must be removed to access the tumor capsule, placing critical neurovascular structures at risk. The use of nasoseptal flaps for reconstruction in patients younger than age 5 years has been debated in the literature, with most authors suggesting feasibility based on radiographic studies and clinical series.6, 28, 29 Our case illustrates that coverage can be optimized by extending 1) anteriorly to the caudal edge of the septal cartilage and 2) laterally to include the entire floor of the nose plus the tissue that extends superiorly and laterally under the inferior turbinate.

Balancing the risk of resection with upfront radiation benefits from a team discussion prior to any patient interaction so that there is consistent communication between care providers and the family. From a neurosurgical perspective, at least partial hypopituitarism should be assumed and counseled to families, as the rate of new hypopituitarism with DI exceeds 60% in most retrospective studies.10, 13, 15, 30 Although limited to small patient series, gross-total resection rates range between 43% and 100%,7, 9–12, 15, 16, 30 with the rate of vision decline following surgery ranging from 0% to 28.6%.12, 16, 30 New-onset obesity ranges from 0% to 33%.8, 10, 15–17, 30, 31 Conversely, the risk profile of radiation primarily consists of delayed effects. Vision impairment and panhypopituitarism have been reported in 39% and 36%–80% of pediatric patients after radiotherapy, respectively. Long-term effects have similarly been documented with the risk of delayed vasculopathy as high as 21% over 5–12 years and the risk of developing a secondary malignancy of 1.9% at 10 years, with more secondary malignancies expected in subsequent decades.20 Additionally, radiotherapy in patients younger than 15 years has been shown to be associated with neurocognitive impairment. As a result, while radiotherapy may offer a reasonable option to reduce tumor burden when gross-total resection cannot be achieved and spare developing neural structures acutely, its insidious effects among this population have the potential to severely affect neurological and other outcomes later in life.

In conclusion, with an experienced multidisciplinary medical and surgical team, a fully endoscopic approach that avoids perioperative complications and achieves gross-total resection is possible in a 23-month-old child.
