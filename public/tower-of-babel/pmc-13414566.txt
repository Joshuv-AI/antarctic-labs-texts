SPARC family proteins: matricellular modulators of cancer progression and therapeutic resistance
Authors: Rachel Huey Xuan Hoe, Thamil Selvee Ramasamy, Ajantha Sinniah, Ain Zubaidah Ayob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine) family represents a unique class of matricellular proteins – including SPARC, SPARCL1, SPOCK1-3, SMOC1-2, and FSTL1 – that regulate extracellular matrix (ECM) dynamics, cell signaling, and tissue homoeostasis. In cancer, their expression is frequently dysregulated, through epigenetic mechanisms, microRNAs, and interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Dysregulation of SPARC family members is most pronounced in aggressive malignancies with SPARC, SPARCL1, SPOCK1, and SPOCK2 most consistently altered. These proteins drive tumor progression through ECM remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), maintenance of cancer stemness, immune modulation, and drug resistance acquisition. Their functions are highly context-dependent, exerting either tumor-suppressive or oncogenic effects depending on tissue types and disease stage. While their secreted nature positions family members as promising serum or plasma biomarkers, challenges such as ECM protein undruggability, functional heterogeneity, and the absence of upstream regulators have so far precluded direct therapeutic targeting, with no agents advancing to clinical trials. Nonetheless, indirect strategies leveraging their biology show preclinical promise. This review synthesizes current knowledge on SPARC family structure, regulation, and context-specific functions in cancer-TME interactions, emphasizing their dual roles as modulators of progression and resistance. By integrating mechanistic insights with translational advances, we highlight the emerging utility of SPARC family proteins in precision oncology and underscore the need for deeper understanding to enable effective biomarker development and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with therapeutic resistance driving over 90% of treatment failures in metastatic cases. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy, the complex interactions between cancer cells and their microenvironment continue to fuel resistance. Central to this challenge is the dynamic remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM), orchestrated by matricellular proteins – specialized ECM modulators that regulate cell-matrix interactions rather than providing structural support. Unlike structural ECM components such as collagen, matricellular proteins are secreted in response to injury, inflammation, and cancer, acting as critical communicators between cells and the ECM. Among these, the SPARC (secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine) family proteins—including SPARC itself, SPARC-like protein 1 (SPARCL1, or Hevin), SPOCKs (SPARC, CWCV, and kazal-like domain proteoglycan 1-3, or Testicans), SMOCs (secreted modular calcium-binding proteins 1-2), and follistatin-like protein 1 (FSTL1)—play crucial roles in tissue remodeling, wound healing, inflammation, fibrosis, and cancer progression by modulating ECM composition and cellular behavior [1].

What makes these proteins particularly intriguing as therapeutic targets is their remarkable context-dependency: the same protein can act as a tumor suppressor or oncogenic driver depending on cancer type, stage, and microenvironment. For instance, SPARC is frequently overexpressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), melanoma, ovarian, colorectal (CRC) and lung cancers, yet downregulated in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), prostate cancer and B-cell lymphoma, highlighting its versatility [2–4]. Similarly, SPARCL1 may be epigenetically silenced in aggressive bladder cancer and CRC but upregulated in glioblastoma and prostate cancer [5–7]. This duality challenges the traditional therapeutic paradigm and calls for a nuanced understanding of tissue-specific cues guiding their function. Functionally, these proteins reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) through ECM remodeling, influencing cell adhesion, motility, angiogenesis, and immune interactions. Notably, SPARC can both promote proliferation and metastasis in some cancers (e.g., liver, ovarian) and suppress tumors in others. SPARCL1 and SPOCK1 influence epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and invasiveness, while FSTL1 modulates inflammation and chemoresistance. Their frequent dysregulation in cancers underscores their significance as biomarkers and putative therapeutic targets, particularly as tumor-specific and microenvironmental strategies evolve.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the SPARC family proteins as matricellular modulators in cancer with a particular emphasis on their context-dependent roles and therapeutic implications. We integrate insights from recent mechanistic, molecular, clinical, and bioinformatic studies to highlight how SPARC family proteins orchestrate tumor progression through ECM remodeling, EMT, cancer stemness and immune modulation. Furthermore, we examine their contribution to therapeutic resistance and discuss emerging strategies targeting these proteins and outline future research directions that leverage their unique properties to enhance cancer diagnosis and treatment. By understanding the molecular determinants of SPARC family protein function, we aim to provide a framework for harnessing their therapeutic potential while navigating their inherent complexity.

Overview of SPARC family proteins

The SPARC family comprises structurally related yet functionally diverse matricellular proteins. Despite sharing a conserved modular architecture, individual members exhibit distinct tissue-specific functions, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (detailed expression data are compiled in Supplementary Files 1 and 3), and are not functionally redundant, reflecting their versatility in controlling ECM dynamics and cellular signaling. The conserved domain structure of SPARC family proteins, as illustrated in Fig. 2, augments their diverse biological functions. All family members converge on two core structural elements that enable shared mechanistic roles in ECM assembly, counter-adhesion, and growth factor modulation. Individual SPARC family proteins exhibit specialized structural features that underpin their diverse physiological roles. SPARC itself represents the prototypic family member ( ~ 43 kDa), featuring a compact FS-EC tandem arrangement that serves as the structural template for the family [12]. Compared to SPARC, SPARCL1 ( ~ 71 kDa, sharing ~62% sequence homology) possesses an extended acidic N-terminal domain, enhancing its anti-adhesive properties and facilitating homodimerization in vitro, which may increase protein stability [13, 14]. SPARCL1 can undergo proteolytic processing to generate a SPARC-like fragment, and its unique N-terminus confers additional functions, including synaptogenesis through modulation of neurexin-neuroligin interactions [14]. The SPOCK subgroup (SPOCK1-3), classified as proteoglycans, ranges from ~50-60 kDa depending on glycosylation status, with core proteins of 424-439 amino acids. They contain glycosaminoglycan (GAG) attachment sites and thyroglobulin-like domains that confer protease-inhibitory activity, critically involved in the modulation of ECM integrity and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [15, 16]. These members are distinguished by their GAG compositions, with SPOCK1 carrying both heparan and chondroitin sulfate, SPOCK2 being predominantly N-glycosylated, and SPOCK3 bearing O-linked glycans with enhanced protease inhibitory capacity [1, 17, 18].

Meanwhile, SMOC proteins (SMOC1 and SMOC2, ~50 kDa each) feature two thyroglobulin domains along with a unique SMOC-specific domain, involved in bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling modulation and vascular development, with roles in tissue patterning during the developmental phase and angiogenesis [19–21]. Unlike direct BMP binding observed with other antagonists, SMOCs modulate BMP gradients through interactions with glypicans and basement membrane components [19]. FSTL1 ( ~ 35 kDa) is distinct in harboring a von Willebrand factor type C (VWFC) domain that facilitates multiprotein complex formation with ECM components and signaling molecules [22]. Through its FS and VWFC domains, FSTL1 likely provides the structural interface for BMP-4 antagonism; however, its EC domain exhibits reduced calcium-binding capacity compared to other family members, possibly limiting its collagen-modulating functions [23, 24]. This protein plays a role in regulating inflammation and macrophage-associated immune responses, executed through the toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-κB (NFκB) signaling [25]. Additionally, Magadum et al. reported that FSTL1 significantly contributes to cardiomyocyte proliferation and cardiac regeneration in a mouse model [26].

The specific domain structures and post-translational modifications of each protein equip them to coordinate ECM dynamics and cellular behaviors in a highly context-dependent manner. Understanding these structural-functional relationships is pivotal for elucidating how SPARC family proteins contribute to tumor progression and for developing targeted therapies that modulate their activity within the TME.

Roles of SPARC family proteins in cancer progression

Building on their distinct capabilities, SPARC family proteins also play crucial roles in tumor biology by modulating the ECM and the TME. Abundantly expressed in stromal cells, SPARC regulates MMP activities to influence tumor cell adhesion, proliferation and matrix remodeling [27, 28]. SPARCL1, despite structural similarity to SPARC, does not compensate for its loss and exhibits divergent roles – restricting tumor dissemination and modulating immune cell infiltration in various cancers [29–31]. SPOCK proteins intricately regulate ECM composition and protease activities that affect metastasis and neural tissues [15, 32]. SMOC members influence angiogenesis and tissue remodeling through BMP signaling [19, 20]. FSTL1’s capacity to modulate inflammation and immune responses positions it as a key regulator of tumor-associated processes such as fibrosis and chemoresistance [25, 26]. Although SPARC is well-studied, emerging evidence underscores similarly significant contributions from other family members in regulating matrix dynamics and cellular behavior within the TME. As illustrated in Fig. 3 (detailed source of evidence is compiled in Supplementary Files 2 and 3), the differential dysregulation of SPARC family proteins across a spectrum of human cancers highlights their context-dependent roles and reinforces their prognostic and therapeutic relevance. ECM remodeling

During cancer development, it is commonly reported that the ECM will be dysregulated as observed with the differential expressions of many components in the matrix. SPARC has been shown to regulate ECM deposition and turnover by interacting with various ECM components, such as collagen and influencing the activity of MMPs [33]. This regulation is crucial for maintaining the structural integrity of tissues and facilitating tumor growth as well as metastasis. In contrast to its physiological role in collagen fibrillogenesis, dysregulation of SPARC in cancer enhances ECM remodeling to support tumor invasion. In pancreatic cancer, the silencing of SPARC gene in pancreatic stellate cells suppressed ECM remodeling which otherwise, will promote collagen fiber deposition and reorganize fibronectin fibril orientation through TGF-β/Rho-Associated Kinase (ROCK) signaling and MMP activity [34]. On the contrary, overexpression of SPARC was reported to restrain the migration of basal cells expressing cytokeratin 5 (KRT5) in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis of human lung fibroblast matrix, suggesting a unique role of SPARC in regulating the migration of KRT5+ basal cells [35]. SPARCL1 also contributes to ECM dynamics as its expression is associated with heightened infiltrative and proangiogenic features in induced tumors using proneural and mesenchymal cancer stem cells (CSCs), and glioma cell lines [36].

Similarly, SPOCK proteins play critical roles in ECM remodeling during cancer progression. SPOCK1 have been reported to be upregulated and predictive of poor prognosis in various cancers such as HCC, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ovarian cancer, glioblastoma and osteosarcoma [37–41]. The overexpression of SPOCK1 enhanced cell proliferation and migration in cancer cells [37, 38]. On the other hand, SPOCK2 contributes to cell proliferation, adhesion and invasion through its downregulation in endometrial epithelial cells when transfected with short hairpin RNA [42]. Suppressed expression of SPOCK3 was also found to be associated with poor prognosis in patients with prostate cancer, possibly via multiple signaling pathways including PI3K-Akt, Ras and MAPK [43]. Likewise, lower expression of SMOC1 is linked to the malignancy of traditional serrated adenomas, conventional adenomas and CRC as demonstrated using immunohistochemical (IHC) staining in a collection of colorectal tumors and adjacent normal colonic tissues from patients [44].

Lastly, FSTL1 has been shown to be closely associated with activated fibroblasts found abundantly in advanced HCC tumors, driving malignancy and metastasis via the TLR4/Akt/mTOR/4EBP1 axis [45]. Despite that, FSTL1 was previously reported to be suppressed in NSCLC patients as compared to normal tissues, and FSTL1 treatment adequately inhibited the metastatic activity of the lung cancer cells in vivo through direct interaction with secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1)/osteopontin [46]. These findings underscore SPARC family proteins’ pivotal role in ECM remodeling—a mechanism whose impact on tumor progression varies markedly between tumor-suppressive restraint and oncogenic promotion depending on TME cues. In addition to regulating tumor cell invasion and metastasis, SPARC family–mediated ECM remodeling shapes immune cell accessibility. SPARC modulates collagen fibrillogenesis to regulate immune trafficking [33, 47], SPARCL1 and FSTL1 cooperatively enhance ECM permeability to promote immune infiltration [36, 45, 48], while SPOCK/SMOC proteins contribute to proteoglycan-rich ECM composition [44, 49]. These ECM-driven alterations establish permissive or restrictive immune landscapes within the TME [50], providing the foundation for processes such as EMT, where SPARC family proteins further modulate tumor cell behavior and invasiveness.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition

SPARC family proteins extend their influence beyond ECM remodeling to include EMT, a critical cellular process in cancer progression, which enables epithelial cells to acquire mesenchymal traits, facilitating metastatic capacity. EMT not only enhances migratory capabilities of tumor cells, but also aids in immune evasion, chemoresistance and stem-cell-like properties, hence contributing to the aggressiveness and heterogeneity of the cancer bulk [51]. In parallel, SPARC engagement of integrin αvβ3 triggers integrin-linked kinase (ILK)-mediated GSK-3β inhibition, enabling β-catenin nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation of mesenchymal genes [52].

SPARC plays dual roles in EMT, depending on the cancer type. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, SPARC is downregulated as EMT is inhibited by hypoxia inducible domain family member 1 A (HIGD1A), upregulating E-cadherin and EpCAM while suppressing vimentin to impede invasion [53]. Conversely, in prostate cancer, SPARC induces EMT by upregulating the transcription factor Zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), suppressing E-cadherin, increasing vimentin and integrin β3 levels, enhancing migration via the integrin αvβ3/ZEB1 pathway; silencing SPARC reverses these effects [54]. These observations align with the broader mechanistic framework whereby SPARC coordinates both TGF-β-dependent and integrin-dependent arms of EMT signaling.

Activation of LCN2/LOXL2/MMP9 signaling induces SPOCK1 expression, thereby promoting EMT to enhance invasion and migration in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma [55]. Given its Kazal-type protease inhibitor domain, SPOCK1 may potentially amplify EMT-inducing signals by modulating TGF-β receptor availability, though the precise mechanism warrants further investigation [56]. Additionally, Váncza and colleagues reported that silencing of the SPOCK1 gene impaired the invasiveness of HCC cells, suggesting that SPOCK1 plays a role not only in ECM remodeling but also in the regulation of EMT in HCC progression [38]. Knockdown of SPOCK2 resulted in the upregulation of MMP2 and MMP9, with increased cell proliferation, adhesion and invasion in endometrial epithelial cells, suggesting that SPOCK2 normally restrains these pro-metastatic processes [42]. SMOC2 was recently reported to be regulated by the expression of LINC01857 in PDAC through its downstream target, miR-19a-3p [57]. The overexpression of SMOC2 restored the inhibitory effects of LINC01857 silencing on the proliferative and metastatic potentials, as well as EMT activation, to a certain extent in the cells. Finally, FSTL1 has emerged as a significant player in cancer biology, possibly by promoting EMT upon TGF-β1 induction in CRC organoid adhesion, according to results obtained from gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [58]. Overall, these findings accentuate the multifaceted roles of SPARC family proteins in mediating EMT and their broader implications for cancer progression.

Cancer stemness

Cancer stemness refers to a stem-like state in cancer cells, characterized by their self-renewal ability, multilineage differentiation, tumor-initiating ability and dynamic interactions with their environment to promote tumor maintenance, progression and therapy resistance. These properties are attributed to CSCs, typically constitute a subpopulation within a tumor responsible in driving tumor growth, metastasis, resistance, eventually contributing to tumor relapse [59]. SPARC family proteins influence cancer stemness largely through modulation of TME and activation of context‑dependent signaling cascades, including MAPK and PI3K–Akt pathways, which in turn support self‑renewal, survival and tumor‑initiating capacity in cancer stem‑like cells [54–60].

SPARC contributes to cancer stemness in various tumor types. Mateo et al. demonstrated that SPARC secreted by non-CSCs enhances the metastatic potential of the CSC population in prostate cancer [60]. A recent study on limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs) represents SPARC’s function in stemness regulation, showing it promotes self-renewal and proliferative capacity in LESCs through the phosphorylation of JNK and p38-MAPK signaling pathways [61, 62]. Specifically, recombinant SPARC treatment significantly increased the expression of LESC-positive markers ABCG2, Bmi-1, and p63α, while suppressing the differentiation marker cytokeratin-3, with these effects blocked by JNK/p38 inhibitors. Although conducted in non-malignant stem cells, these MAPK-mediated mechanisms suggest SPARC may similarly support CSC maintenance and tumor-initiating capacity in cancer contexts.

Overexpression of SPARCL1 was associated with the enrichment of mesenchymal CSC-induced tumors together with increased infiltrative and neoangiogenic properties, particularly in glioblastoma models [36]. While direct evidence linking SPOCK1 to stemness regulation remains limited, its involvement in tumor metastasis suggests a potential role in this process. Distinctively, SPOCK1 has been identified as a key regulator of brain metastasis-initiating cells (BMICs), where it influences self-renewal capacity, tumor-initiating potential, and the metastatic spread from lung to brain [63]. Furthermore, analysis of a longitudinal cohort of primary lung cancer specimens revealed that SPOCK1 overexpression was predominantly observed in patients who subsequently developed bone metastases. These observations imply a potential role for SPOCK1 in sustaining stem-like characteristics that drive tumor progression.

SMOC2 has been strongly associated with stem-like properties that contribute to metastatic activity and tumor aggressiveness. Notably, SMOC2 expression is higher in CSCs as compared to non-CSCs, and its silencing restricted sphere formation ability and suppressed the expression of stemness-associated genes such as SOX2, OCT4 and NANOG in endometrial CSC [64]. A recent study by Feng and Zheng demonstrated that cancer stem-like properties in gallbladder cancer cells are reinforced through the stabilization of SMOC2 mRNA, mediated by the upregulation of histone deacetylase (HDAC) 2 [65]. This regulatory axis was shown to enhance stemness, as evidenced by increased sphere-forming capacity following SMOC2 overexpression, while its depletion impaired self-renewal potential, highlighting SMOC2’s functional role in promoting stem-like traits [65]. Collectively, these findings suggest a positive feedback loop wherein HDAC2-mediated SMOC2 stabilization reinforces stem-like transcriptional programs.

FSTL1 has been identified as a key factor secreted by glioma stem cells (GSC), which shape the TME and support the maintenance of GSC. FSTL1 has been shown to interact with tumor-associated macrophage (TAM), whereby it promotes polarization towards M2 phenotype, which in turn contributes to stem-like and malignant behaviours in GSCs [66]. Mechanistically, GSC-derived FSTL1 engages TLR2 to inhibit the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mediated endocytosis-lysosomal degradation, leading to the activation of PI3K-Akt signalling for the maintenance of self-renewal ability. This TLR2-mediated stabilization of EGFR prolongs downstream PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling, which is essential for sustaining the metabolic and biosynthetic demands of GSC self-renewal [66].

Immune modulation: evasion and infiltration

Immune evasion and altered immune cells infiltration are critical factors in enabling cancer cells to escape immune surveillance, survive, and disseminate, thus driving metastasis. Recent studies suggest that SPARC family proteins drive cancer progression by modifying the immune cell composition and function in the TME, with different members of the proteins showing distinct effects on immunosurveillance and immunosuppression as compiled in Table 1 (detailed compilation can be found in Supplementary File 3). For instance, SPARC was found to exhibit opposing roles: in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), it acts as a tumor suppressor of LUAD restricts immune evasion through the inhibition of CD276 activity, validated through IHC [67]. On the other hand, elevated SPARC levels in ovarian cancer were found to be positively correlated with increased immune infiltration, including T cell (CD8+ and CD4 + ), macrophage and neutrophil [8]. This high expression of SPARC was also demonstrated to promote cancer progression and was suggested to be used as an indicator for poor prognosis and drug resistance. Meanwhile, SPARCL1 expression is generally downregulated across many cancers, correlating with poor prognosis [68]. All three SPOCK proteins demonstrate significant association with immune cell infiltration across multiple cancer types, though with context-dependent effects on prognosis. SPOCK1 expression is associated with increased immune cell infiltration in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), indicating that SPOCK1 may promote tumor malignancy by influencing the TME and driving cancer progression [69]. Supplementally, SPOCK1 also has a positive correlation with expressions of immune checkpoint regulators and is significantly upregulated in colorectal tissues relative to surrounding healthy tissues. Alongside this, macrophage markers CD68 (M1 and M2 macrophages) and CD206 (M2-specific macrophages) also show increased expression in cancer tissues, which suggests a role for SPOCK1 in promoting an immunosuppressive TME [70]. Given these associations, SPOCK1 might serve as a prognostic marker linked to poorer outcomes in CRC. SPOCK2 expression also correlates with immune infiltration and the overall survival in PDAC patients, based on TCGA dataset, although the mechanism and corresponding outcome of SPOCK2 modulation requires further investigation [71]. Similarly, SPOCK3 correlates positively with immune score, with Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumour using Expression data (ESTIMATE) and single-set GSEA (ssGSEA) analyses revealing increased infiltration by mast cells, B cells and natural killer (NK) cells in high SPOCK3 expression group [43].

Unlike SPARC family proteins, SMOC1 is highly expressed in low-grade glioma tissues compared to normal brain tissues, negatively correlated with the levels of infiltrating immune cells like macrophages and neutrophils, and is associated with improved patient survival – positioning it as a favourable prognostic biomarker [72]. Finally, FSTL1 is reported to be associated with the activity of anti-tumor NK cells as its high expression in osteosarcoma turns out to inhibit potent NK cell production in vivo and FSTL1 induction suppresses NK cell activity partially through apoptotic pathway [73]. Another study reported that FSTL1 increased the number of apoptotic T cells to drive tumor malignancy, catalyzing resistance towards anti-PD1/PDL1 therapy in metastatic CRC cells [74]. In summary, SPARC family proteins play complex, context-dependent roles in shaping immune infiltration and evasion in TME, highlighting their potential as biomarkers and targets for cancer immunotherapy.

SPARC family proteins in therapeutic resistance

Drug resistance remains a formidable obstacle in cancer therapy, significantly contributing to tumor relapse and poor clinical outcomes. Emerging evidence implicates SPARC family proteins as critical modulators of resistance across diverse therapeutic modalities, including conventional chemotherapy (e.g., doxorubicin, gemcitabine, cisplatin), targeted molecular therapies (such as EGFR and BRAF inhibitors), and immunotherapies (including anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 agents). These matricellular proteins influence drug resistance primarily by regulating ECM remodeling and key intracellular signaling pathways, thereby altering tumor cell survival, proliferation, and interactions with the microenvironment.

Within the pancreatic TME, SPARC secreted by pancreatic stellate cells activates the ERK signaling pathway, promoting cell survival and resistance to therapeutic agents [75]. This effect is further compounded by miR-31-5p-mediated regulation of Hippo signaling, underscoring the intricate network involving SPARC in therapy evasion. Conversely, in gastric cancer cells characterized by low endogenous SPARC expression, exogenous administration enhances sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), likely reflecting differences in the TME and isoform-specific functions of SPARC [76]. Furthermore, the dysregulated high expression of HDAC10 suppresses the expression of SPARC, which conferred resistance in melanoma cells against BRAF inhibitors, a class of targeted cancer drugs commonly used to treat melanoma [3]. Similarly, SPARCL1 contributes to immunotherapy resistance; its upregulation following anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 treatment drives microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) expression and concomitant induction of IFI27, APOC1, and XIST, promoting malignant progression [77]. In bladder cancer, high SPARCL1 expression corresponds to increased IC50 values for doxorubicin, gemcitabine, and mitomycin C, indicating reduced drug susceptibility [31].

Aside from that, SPOCK1 has emerged as a key mediator of resistance across diverse cancers, including glioblastoma, lung, and gastric cancers, where its overexpression is linked to decreased sensitivity to targeted therapies such as osimertinib. Notably, silencing of SPOCK1 abrogates resistance and suppresses tumor growth in lung cancer models [40, 41, 78]. Non-coding RNAs, notably circular RNAs (circRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), serve as critical upstream regulators of SPARC family proteins, modulating therapeutic resistant phenotypes. In gefitinib-resistant NSCLC cells, radiotherapy upregulates miR-1243, which directly targets and downregulates SPOCK1. The decreased SPOCK1 levels impair pathways mediating resistance, thereby increasing the cells’ radiosensitivity [40]. Additionally, the suppression of miR-135b directly represses the expression level of SPOCK1 in CRC, leading to the sensitization of cells to 5-FU [17]. These mechanistic links position SPOCK1 as a key regulator of therapeutic resistance. Conjointly, hsa_circ_0074298 facilitates gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic cancer by sponging miR-519, a negative regulator of SMOC2; inhibition of this circRNA curtails proliferation and reverses resistance both in vitro and in vivo, effects that are rescued by SMOC2 overexpression [79]. FSTL1 mediates cisplatin resistance in LUAD through the miR-215-5p/COL5A1/FSTL1 signaling axis, suppressing autophagy and enhancing cell survival under treatment stress [80]. In breast cancer, elevated FSTL1 promotes resistance towards cisplatin and doxorubicin by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling via integrin β3, fostering a drug-resistant phenotype [81]. Taken together, these findings underscore SPARC family proteins as critical modulators driving resistance to diverse therapeutic agents by modulating essential signaling cascades, interacting with non-coding RNAs, and orchestrating ECM dynamics, thus highlighting their potential as targets for overcoming treatment refractoriness.

Clinical relevance of SPARC family proteins: biomarkers and therapeutic targets

The SPARC family of matricellular proteins presents therapeutic challenges and opportunities in cancer management due to their divergent roles in driving or restraining tumor progression. Clinical relevance spans disease monitoring and molecular intervention, requiring tumor-specific strategies.

The secreted nature of SPARC family proteins places them as serum or plasma biomarkers for cancer progression. As depicted in Fig. 3, cancers of the brain, head and neck, lung, pancreas, reproductive systems, and colon exhibit the highest degree of dysregulation across multiple SPARC family members. In HCC and cervical cancer, elevated SPARC indicates poorer prognosis and resistance to conventional therapies [82, 83]. SPARCL1 expression loss marks advanced disease and adverse outcomes in bladder and colorectal cancers, further supporting its prognostic utility [31]. Elevated FSTL1 levels track advanced liver fibrosis and HCC trajectory, while SPOCK1 serum concentrations distinguish untreated from post-chemotherapy patients in HCC and ovarian cancer, enabling precise monitoring of treatment efficacy [37, 38, 84]. Additionally, SPOCK1 expression patterns align with EMT, immune infiltration, and oxidative stress in HCC and LUAD, while higher expression of SPOCK2 and SPOCK3 associates with increased immune cell infiltration and favorable prognosis in LUAD and prostate cancer, respectively, substantiating the prognostic significance of distinct family members [8, 38, 43, 85–87].

While preclinical studies have illuminated these biomarker roles, clinical investigations thus far have predominantly emphasized the predictive potential of SPARC family proteins rather than their direct therapeutic targeting. The phase III MPACT trial (NCT00844649) represents the most comprehensive evaluation to date, examining whether stromal SPARC expression could predict response to nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine in metastatic PDAC [88]. Contrary to earlier phase I/II observations, the exploratory biomarker analysis found no significant association between SPARC levels and overall survival in either treatment arm. Such discrepancies likely reflect assay heterogeneity, the complexity of distinguishing stromal from tumoral expression, and the use of predominantly archival tissues from metastatic rather than primary sites. Nevertheless, subsequent trials have continued to explore SPARC as an exploratory biomarker across multiple cancer types, although none have yet established its reliability for guiding treatment selection.

Therapeutic strategies targeting the SPARC family proteins must therefore account for the specific tumor biology to harness their divergent functions without unintended consequences. For example, inhibiting SPARC activity counters tumor growth, metastasis, and chemoresistance driven by tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO2) [89]. Conversely, therapeutic upregulation of SPARC—such as through HDAC10 inhibition—has the potential to suppress melanoma proliferation and overcome resistance to BRAF inhibitors [3]. Furthermore, FSTL1 suppression of NK cell activity in osteosarcoma positions FSTL1 inhibitors as promising immunotherapeutic adjuvants to potentiate anti-tumor immunity [90]. However, the absence of approved SPARC family-targeted therapeutics reflects inherent drugging challenges.

The SPARC family has also been leveraged for drug delivery and resistance modulation. SPARC’s high albumin affinity enhances nab-paclitaxel drug delivery (Abraxane™), which is FDA-approved for breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers, and achieves enhanced intratumoral penetration and retention partly [91]. CRISPR-based activation of NF-κB signaling is under investigation to amplify this albumin-mediated chemotherapy delivery [92]. SPOCK1 downregulation via RBM15/m6A silencing drives osimertinib resistance in LUAD through EMT activation, which may be reversible using SPOCK1-restoring therapies, whereas SPOCK1 silencing in CRC inhibits proliferation and invasion via EMT reversal [93, 94]. Although no direct SPARC family inhibitors have yet received regulatory approval, their integral roles in drug delivery, chemoresistance, and immune modulation establish a clear clinical rationale.

Challenges and future directions

Despite their promising clinical associations, SPARC family proteins face substantial hurdles as direct therapeutic targets, primarily due to their ECM localization and intrinsically challenging druggability. These matricellular proteins function primarily through large, flat protein-protein interfaces in the extracellular space, a target class historically resistant to small molecule inhibition [95]. While crystal structures exist for individual SPARC domains [12], comprehensive structural data for other family members, their complexes, and context-specific conformations remain scarce, severely limiting rational drug design. Functional heterogeneity further compounds these issues: global inhibition risks paradoxical tumor promotion in tissues where certain members exert suppressor roles, while ubiquitous normal tissue expression raises toxicity concerns for wound healing, ECM homoeostasis and immune regulation. Few known upstream regulators have been identified for individual family members, exacerbating these difficulties, and no SPARC family-targeted agents have advanced to clinical trials — reflecting their classification as poor direct targets rather than validated opportunities.

Emerging approaches seek to circumvent these obstacles through indirect or selective strategies, though clinical proof remains elusive. Nanotechnology-based delivery systems show preclinical promise for tumor-selective payload delivery [96], while antisense oligonucleotides could silence specific members. Downstream pathways or cancer cell-intrinsic regulators offer more tractable intervention points than the proteins themselves, potentially yielding better outcomes by preserving physiological ECM roles. Similarly, leveraging SPARC’s ECM remodeling capacity to enhance drug efficacy—such as sensitizing hypoxic tumor cores to chemotherapy via co-treatments or improving immunotherapy penetration—represents a pragmatic alternative to direct inhibition [92].

Given these targeting challenges, SPARC family proteins may prove more clinically valuable as biomarkers and prognostic tools to guide personalized medicine. Their secreted nature enables non-invasive monitoring of tumor response and treatment stratification, offering superior utility over direct inhibition. However, realizing this potential requires rigorous multi-phase validation: analytical assay standardization, retrospective and prospective clinical cohorts demonstrating risk stratification, and context-of-use qualification per FDA/EMA guidelines. Addressing the three critical knowledge gaps, i.e., the molecular switches governing tissue-specific functions, intratumoral crosstalk between family members, and the lack of selective modulators, will require integrative technological approaches. Single-cell spatial transcriptomics can chart SPARC family expression gradients and resolve cell-cell interactions within the CAF-tumor niche, revealing context-dependent regulatory switches. CRISPR-based functional genomics applied to co-expression networks can systematically uncover dominant resistance phenotypes and pinpoint synergistic vulnerabilities across family members. Patient-derived organoids, coupled with high-throughput perturbation assays, provide translational platforms for modeling expression dynamics, accelerating small-molecule discovery, and testing rational combination strategies with checkpoint inhibitors. Together, these complementary approaches create a multi-layered framework to dissect SPARC biology and guide therapeutic development.

Conclusion

SPARC family proteins exemplify matricellular regulation in cancer, driving TME remodeling, stemness maintenance, immune modulation, and therapy resistance through protein-specific mechanisms (as summaried in Fig. 4). Their clinical value currently lies in biomarker and prognostic applications, as well as roles in drug delivery and resistance modulation, rather than as direct therapeutic targets. Moving forward, the field must bridge mechanistic insights with clinical translation through rigorous biomarker validation, development of selective modulators targeting downstream pathways, and combination strategies that harness—rather than inhibit—their ECM-remodeling functions.
