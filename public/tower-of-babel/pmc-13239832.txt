RescueGPT: An Automated System for Detecting Adverse Safety Events in Prehospital Emergency Medical Service Notes With a Zero‐Shot Approach With Large Language Models: A Proof‐of‐Concept Study
Authors: Tina Yi Jin Hsieh, Carl Eriksson, Garth Meckler, Matthew Hansen, Nathan Bahr, Steven Bedrick, Diego Trujillo, Kyu Seo Kim, Byeongyeon Cho, Kai Zhang, Xiaoqian Jiang, Jeanne‐Marie Guise
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction and ObjectiveTraditional adverse safety events (ASE) identification relies on domain experts to manually review and annotate charts, which hinders the scalability of processing high‐volume EMS data. This study explores the use of large language model (LLM) with a knowledge base to automate extraction of adverse safety events (ASE) from unstructured emergency medical service (EMS) notes for pediatric out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) as proof of concept.Data Sources and Study DesignPediatric OHCA records from a national EMS provider were obtained from 2017 to 2020. Leveraging the Pediatric Prehospital Adverse Safety Event Detection System (PEDS) as a foundational knowledge base, we used the LinkML framework to develop an ontology to define ASEs across six essential EMS care domains. To convert unstructured EMS narratives into structured prompts, we used the Structured Prompt Interrogation and Recursive Extraction of Semantics (SPIRES) method, which generated schema‐driven prompts to guide the GPT‐3.5 model in identifying ASEs. By mapping unstructured data into structured concepts consistent with PEDS guidelines, the model produced targeted prompts that supported effective entity extraction.ResultsWe evaluated framework effectiveness with accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, and specificity across 42 pediatric OHCA cases covering ASE‐related entities. RescueGPT showed high accuracy in detecting common ASEs (Patient Rhythm, Age, Weight, Length) but revealed challenges in rare events (Failure to Establish IV Access, Incorrect Airway Equipment Size, Failure to Ventilate Patient) likely due to more inconsistent and complex documentation.ConclusionsRescueGPT demonstrates potential in scaling automated ASE detection, but performance varies by completeness and clarity of EMS narrative, particularly with rare events. Fragmented clinical documentation limits accuracy and highlights the need for standardized collection protocols in EMS systems. Future directions will focus on implementing rebalancing strategies for rare events, applying explainability methods to improve decision‐making transparency, and refining text segmentation techniques to handle mixed outcomes to further improve performance.

Introduction

Pediatric out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is an important contributor to child mortality and contributes to the loss of disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) within the US [1]. While there have been marked improvements in survival rates from in‐hospital pediatric cardiac arrests and adult OHCAs over the past 10–15 years, survival rates for pediatric OHCA remain relatively stagnant [2]. EMS plays a critical role in the healthcare system's response to medical emergencies such as OHCAs, as timely and effective early interventions may significantly enhance the chance of survival. Our prior research in prehospital care for pediatric OHCA has identified potential mild or severe adverse safety events (ASE) related to resuscitation in up to 60% of cases [3].

Children affected by OHCA emergencies are especially prone to ASEs, which span from healthcare‐associated deaths to injuries and other complications [4, 5]. The conventional gold standard for identifying ASEs involves time‐intensive manual reviews of medical charts by highly trained physicians, a process which, even assuming short charts (1–3 pages) and highly experienced physician reviewers, requires roughly seven to 10 min per EMS chart [4, 6]. Expert manual medical chart review is not only time‐intensive but expensive. An automated approach to detect ASEs has the potential to improve the efficiency of patient safety and quality reviews in EMS, enabling rapid identification of statewide, regional, and agency‐specific opportunities to improve care and identify areas for additional training.

Developing conventional machine learning models necessitates extensive and expensive chart review and annotation by domain experts increasing cost and limiting the feasibility of developing these models. State‐of‐the‐art large language models (LLMs) create promising new avenues for analyzing unstructured free‐text data. Foundation models like GPT‐3 [7] and GPT‐4 [8] from OpenAI, which do not require task‐specific training, have been successfully applied in clinical settings to extract structured information from medical reports and synthesize evidence with limited data [9]. These models, while innovative, come with challenges such as difficulties in generalizing, issues with understanding negations [10], and a tendency to produce incorrect information, known as hallucinations [11]. To overcome these challenges, integrating LLMs with structured knowledge bases (KBs) and ontologies has been a focus of recent research [12, 13]. These frameworks store and organize domain‐specific information, enabling logical reasoning and factual validation.

Integrating the adaptability of LLMs with the precision of KBs and ontologies has led to advanced methodologies that combine entity extraction with relationship understanding, promoting a more comprehensive text analysis compared to traditional named entity recognition methods [13]. Utilizing ontologies helps validate data before incorporating it into KBs, addressing LLM limitations and enhancing adaptability of the model.

Research Focus

The objective of the proof‐of‐concept study is to develop a streamlined tool “RescueGPT,” where RESCUE stands for Recognition, Extraction, Surveillance, and Conceptualization Using EMS notes, which leverages LLMs and incorporates domain‐specific knowledge bases from clinical pediatric and emergency medicine experts for the efficient named entity recognition (NER), extraction, and conceptualization of EMS charts, targeting the identification and tracking of preventable ASEs in pediatric OHCA cases.

Methods

ASE Identification Process

We utilized the Pediatric Prehospital Adverse Safety Event Detection System (PEDS) tool [6, 14] to identify ASEs that constitute our knowledge base. The PEDS tool is a rule‐based system that incorporates expert knowledge from a clinical panel to detect ASEs in six key domains of care: (1) Assessment and diagnosis, (2) Clinical decision‐making, (3) Procedures, (4) Airway, (5) Medications, and (6) Fluids. The PEDS tool was previously developed by our team and validated in prior studies [3, 4, 5, 6]. We used a similar process as in previous studies to review medical records including using physicians board‐certified in pediatric emergency or critical care medicine, rigorous chart review training, and dual review for all charts with resolution of disagreements by consensus. EMS clinical notes were collected in partnership with a large nationwide provider of EMS transport for children < 18 years with OHCA from 2017 to 2020. The study was approved by Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center's Institutional Review Board as exempt.

Ontology Development

To transform unstructured clinical text data into structured, meaningful concepts pertinent to pediatric OHCA, we employed the LinkML framework [15] to construct our ontology schema, informed by the PEDS tool [6, 14]. This structure lays the groundwork for systematically organizing and understanding the data (Figure 1).

Workflow of development and utilization of RescueGPT algorithm.

Prompt Generation

Next, leveraging a two‐part input system, we applied the Structured Prompt Interrogation and Recursive Extraction of Semantics (SPIRES) method [12]. This involves the integration of the LinkML‐generated schema, derived from the PEDS tool, with the unstructured text from EMS notes. This process facilitates the generation of targeted prompts for the GPT‐3.5 model, a transformer‐based autoregressive language model with 175 million parameters [7], ensuring the extraction of structured information while maintaining HIPAA compliance via Azure AI. The extracted data, formatted according to the predefined LinkML schema, undergoes a validation process to ensure its alignment with the corresponding concepts within the ontology. This results in a structured representation of domain‐specific knowledge, enabling researchers and healthcare professionals to query and analyze the information effectively. The complex relations between named entities captured by the KB‐informed model enhance accuracy compared to conventional named entity recognition methods.

ASE Analysis

We then selected a sample of 42 EMS notes to analyze using RescueGPT, focusing on its capability to accurately identify and classify nine specific entities: Patient age, rhythm, weight/length, “Failure To Successfully Establish IV(Intravenous) or IO (intraosseous) Access,” “IV IO Placement Delay In Access,” “Multiple Vascular Access Attempts IV or IO,” “Failure To Ventilate Patient,” “Intubation Too Many Attempts,” and “Incorrect Airway Equipment Size Tube Size Half Size Off.” We assessed RescueGPT's performance using accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, and specificity metrics. Good Accuracy Across Entities With Identified Areas for Improvement

The performance of RescueGPT, with accuracy scores ranging from 0.74 to 0.98, showcases overall effectiveness, especially in relatively straightforward entities like “Patient Rhythm” (accuracy: 0.98) and “Patient Age” (accuracy: 0.95). However, “Failure to Successfully Establish IV or IO Access” exhibits a lower accuracy (0.74) and the highest rate of false positives (9 out of 42, Figure 2), highlighting an area for improvement. This challenge is evident when failure and success appear in the same sentence, leading to misclassifications by RescueGPT. Examples include scenarios such as “IO UNSUCCESSFUL IN R TIBIA, IV STARTED R AC 20GA. EPI GIVEN X3 AS CHARTED” and “A PEDIATRIC IO WAS ATTEMPTED IN UPPER LEFT ARM BUT WAS NOT APPROPRIATE SIZE FOR THE PT. ANOTHER IO ATTEMPT WAS MADE LEFT FEMUR AND WAS SUCCESSFUL”(Table 4), where the model incorrectly interprets the ultimate outcome as a failure. This indicates that RescueGPT struggles with contextual understanding in complex medical narratives where multiple outcomes are described in the same sentence. Enhancing RescueGPT's ability to interpret nuanced language and sequential medical procedures could reduce these false positives, improving its performance and reliability in emergency care settings.

Confusion matrices across the 9 entities.

Optimal Specificity but Poor Precision, Recall, and F1 Scores for Two Entities (Rare Events)

Two entities, “Incorrect Airway Equipment Size Tube Size Half Size Off” and “Failure to Ventilate Patient” exhibit poor precision, recall, and F1 scores metrics due to the low incidence of positive events in the dataset. For “Incorrect Airway Equipment Size,” only 5 out of 42 cases are positive, and for “Failure to Ventilate Patient,” there are only 4 positive cases (ASE present) (Figure 2), both outnumbered by the negative events (ASE not present), leading to high specificity but poor precision and recall (Figure 3). While the model efficiently identifies normal situations, it fails to accurately detect these rare yet critical events. Improving these metrics may involve dataset rebalancing, applying cost‐sensitive learning, or using anomaly detection techniques to enhance training on these essential but infrequent cases.

Discussion and Conclusions

Adverse safety events and errors that occur during the delivery of care are one of the leading causes of preventable death and morbidity. Being able to identify and characterize ASEs at scale would be a major step forward in the pathway to prevention.

Large language models are increasingly applied to automate extraction and unlock insights from unstructured or free‐text clinical narratives, with techniques like prompt engineering, zero‐shot [16, 17, 18], few‐shot [19], chain‐of‐thought [20, 21], instruction‐based named entity recognition (NER) [22], fine‐tuning [23, 24], retrieval‐augmented generation (e.g., knowledge graph‐enhanced RAG [25]), semantic similarity filtering [26], or hybrid frameworks [27, 28], among others. The SPIRES method [12] goes beyond methods that focus on classification, summarization, or NER, enabling structured knowledge‐based summary assembly though schema‐guided recursion, zero‐shot prompting and ontology grounding, without the need of fine‐tuning or retrieval systems.

To our knowledge, RescueGPT is the first tool to explore the application of the schema‐driven prompting approach (SPIRES) combined with a pediatric emergency medicine ontology to leverage expert domain‐specific knowledge in identifying pediatric ASEs through zero‐shot learning. This study represents an important step in combining LLMs with ontologies developed by experts to assist in improving emergency care by identifying opportunities for improvement in care rapidly and at low cost.

RescueGPT's performance is influenced by the quality and completeness of the ontologies it ingests, as well as the complexity of the narratives. The algorithm's ability to accurately parse and understand these narratives is critical yet currently hampered by existing data quality and structure issues. OHCA care follows a more well‐established care algorithm than many other medical conditions, suggesting that developing ontologies and establishing the gold standard for ASEs in other clinical scenarios could be more challenging. A particular challenge arises with identifying rare events, such as “Failure To Successfully Establish IV or IO Access.” These instances often result in lower F1 scores due to the model's difficulty maintaining high precision and recall amidst the dominance of negative and nonapplicable cases. RescueGPT's accuracy also suffers when clinical narratives combine successes and failures in a single sentence. To address this, we plan to improve its ability to parse complex sentences by segmenting the text and using these segments to generate a series of focused prompts based on partial schemas. This approach should enable RescueGPT to understand the sequence of events better and avoid misclassifications.

There are also notable gaps in specific clinical information within EMS notes. For instance, many notes lack critical details like tube size (required for assessment of “Incorrect Airway Equipment Size Tube Size Half Size Off”) or exact timestamps for procedures (Ex, assessing “Failure To Ventilate Patient” requires information on time), which are crucial for accurate medical assessment. This lack of specific information points to a broader issue within data collection protocols in EMS, suggesting a need for standardized, detailed clinical documentation because “the model is only as good as the data it is trained on.”

To ensure RescueGPT works effectively across different regions and patient populations, we need to validate its generalizability. Currently, the model is trained on data from a specific group of highly experienced pediatric and emergency physicians. We plan to conduct broader validation studies using real‐world EMS records from the National EMS Information System (NEMSIS) across various states. This will help us assess how well RescueGPT performs on data from different emergency care practices and diverse patient populations.

Moreover, the absence of a “Non‐Applicable” category within the model's schema has led to interpretative challenges, notably when information is missing from EMS narratives. Introducing this category could significantly improve RescueGPT's interpretive accuracy by allowing it to classify cases where specific details are not mentioned appropriately.

In addition, developing explainability methods is essential for building trust in RescueGPT's reasoning. Similar to how attention maps [29] visualize focus points in computer vision models, explainability methods in healthcare can reveal RescueGPT's decision‐making logic. This transparency is vital because, unlike other domains, potential biases or errors in RescueGPT could have severe consequences. By understanding the model's rationale, healthcare professionals can assess its trustworthiness in specific cases [30], ultimately leading to more informed patient care decisions. Explainability methods can also pinpoint areas where the model struggles, guiding further development and refinement.

Last but not least, it is important to ensure the dynamic nature of domain knowledge to ensure RescueGPT's effectiveness. Medical best practices constantly evolve, necessitating a model that adapts. To address this, we envision continuous updates through regular ontology refreshes to reflect advancing medical knowledge, active learning with human feedback for real‐world scenario refinement, and potential integration with real‐time data sources containing latest clinical guidelines [31]. Such a dynamic approach will ensure RescueGPT remains a valuable tool for pediatric OHCA analysis and contributes to improved patient outcomes in the ever‐changing healthcare landscape.

Conclusions

RescueGPT shows promise for automating the analysis of pediatric OHCA cases and it requires further refinement of its knowledge base, data collection methods, and handling of complex narratives. With these refinements, RescueGPT can be a valuable tool for efficient, accurate, and scalable identification of ASEs occurring during the care of pediatric OHCA. This integration of LLMs with structured knowledge bases unlocks vast potential in analyzing unstructured data like EMS narratives, paving the way for advancements in ASE detection and ultimately leading to better decision‐making. In the future, tools such as RescueGPT may be deployed by individual EMS agencies to reduce the burden of quality assurance work, deployed by a state or region to conduct large scale evaluations of emergency care quality, and/or with further refinements, may become embedded in electronic medical records as early warning systems.
