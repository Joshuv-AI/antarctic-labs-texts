Precision Medicine and Shared Decision-Making to Advance Gestational Diabetes Management: Genetic, Metabolic, and Gut Microbiota Factors
Authors: Joviana Farhat, Moaz Mohammed, Kenana Hatab, Gayatri Janardhan, Rand Tayyem, Dian Kusuma, Basema Saddik, Basem Al-Omari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the apparent heterogeneity of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) and the inter-individual variation, the current management follows standardised protocols that do not account for patients’ needs or underlying phenotypic differences. A search of peer-reviewed literature was conducted to focus on precision medicine (PM) and shared decision-making (SDM) approaches in GDM. A narrative synthesis of evidence from clinical studies, reviews, and conceptual frameworks was conducted to explore how integrating PM with SDM may contribute to more individualised, patient-centred GDM care. The Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) was searched to assess trends in publications over the past decade. Women with GDM exhibit diverse clinical trajectories and consistently report a desire to be involved in decisions affecting maternal and infant outcomes. PM enables further individualisation through genetic, metabolic, environmental, and emerging markers, including the gut microbiota, to refine risk stratification and guide targeted interventions. SDM supports informed, collaborative treatment choices and is associated with improved engagement, adherence, and satisfaction. Integrating PM and SDM offers a pathway toward truly individualised GDM care by aligning biologically tailored management with women’s values and priorities. Combining SDM and PM may provide a more holistic framework for individualised GDM care. This framework could potentially enhance the effectiveness, acceptability, and sustainability of GDM management and may lead to a change in current practice. Despite their promise, both approaches remain underutilised and insufficiently integrated into current GDM care models.Keywords: gestational diabetes mellitus, individual patients’ preferences, personalised medicine, shared decision making, precision medicine, gut microbiota, genetic markers

Introduction

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is one of the most common maternal complications of pregnancy.1 It is associated with increased risk of premature or caesarean-section delivery, hypertension, maternal dystocia, hyperglycaemia, and premature rupture of membranes.2 Approximately 50% of women who experience GDM may have recurrence in future pregnancies3 and face a six- to seven-fold higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) compared with healthy pregnant women.4,5 In 2021, the standardised global prevalence of GDM was estimated to be 14.0%, with the highest prevalence of GDM in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) and South-East Asia (SEA), and the lowest prevalence of GDM in Europe (EUR) and North America and Caribbean (NAC).6 This rise is likely due to changes in screening practices, diagnostic criteria, testing methods, and population demographics.7

In most cases, the management of GDM places a substantial burden on affected patients and their families.8 The diagnosis may adversely affect women’s mental health, productivity, and quality of life, potentially undermining adherence to treatment.9 The management of GDM is a multifaceted process that requires monitoring blood glucose at least 4 times daily, strict dietary changes, and pharmacologic therapy when lifestyle modification is insufficient to stabilise glycaemic levels.10,11 GDM management is also associated with increased financial burden due to higher utilisation of antenatal services, inpatient care, delivery interventions, and neonatal support.12 Furthermore, pregnancy complications associated with GDM can further increase healthcare costs.13

Recent evidence highlights the importance of incorporating personalised care strategies, particularly those that reflect patients’ individual preferences and lived experiences, to improve outcomes and promote better adaptation to GDM.14,15 Within this context, Shared Decision Making (SDM) emerged as one of the fundamental mechanisms to deliver patient-centred care. The SDM concept encourages patients to play an active role in their care, supported by their healthcare team.16 SDM also enables patients to participate in informed treatment decisions aligned with their personal values and circumstances.17 Consequently, SDM represents an important operational component of personalised medicine, translating individual patient priorities into clinical decision-making and ensuring that care is tailored beyond biological or clinical characteristics alone.

Alongside personalised medicine, the evolving field of precision medicine (PM) aligns closely with the SDM concept and aims to further individualise care by considering genetic, environmental, and lifestyle variability when designing treatment and prevention strategies.18,19 While PM provides the scientific framework tailored to clinically relevant individual differences,20 SDM ensures these insights are integrated to reflect patients’ values and preferences.21 Jointly, SDM and PM offer complementary approaches to personalised healthcare, combining data-driven risk stratification with patient-centred decision-making. This intersection is particularly relevant in GDM as it is still largely managed using a standard one-size-fits-all approach, despite substantial heterogeneity in clinical presentation and risk profiles.22 In the GDM setting, the use of PM can help identify markers affecting glycaemic control and provide targeted care and interventions to ameliorate maternal and child health.23 Furthermore, integrating PM with SDM may facilitate more individualised risk assessment, treatment selection, and T2DM prevention among women diagnosed with GDM.24 Despite the importance of these emerging and promising practices, there is limited evidence synthesis to support their integration into clinical guidelines. Accordingly, this review discusses the value of SDM and the importance of incorporating patient preferences as core elements of personalised care in GDM management. It also explores the emerging role of PM in shaping more individualised and evidence-based approaches to GDM care and research.

Methods

To assess trends in publications on the use of PM in diabetes and GDM over the past decade, a small descriptive bibliometric exercise was conducted to explore publication trends in PM-related publications on DM and GDM. Since the objective was to explore the publication trends, not to identify, critically appraise, or synthesise evidence from individual studies, formal inclusion/exclusion criteria, search strategy, duplicate removal, and screening were not required. An online search was conducted across all databases in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) to examine variation in publication trends. The database was searched on February 11, 2026. Access to the database was obtained via Khalifa University’s electronic library portal in the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The Boolean search query method was implemented. A search was conducted using the concepts “Precision medicine”, “gestational diabetes mellitus”, and “diabetes mellitus”. The WoSCC online search was conducted using key terms (“Precision medicine” OR “Precision treatment” OR “Precision management”) AND (“diabetes mellitus” OR “diabetes”) NOT (“gestational”) for PM in diabetes, and key terms (“gestational diabetes” OR “gestational diabetes mellitus” OR “pregnancy diabetes”) AND (“Precision medicine” OR “Precision treatment” OR “Precision management”) for PM in GDM.

Shared Decision-Making in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

SDM is an important component of patient-centred care, particularly in chronic and preference-sensitive conditions. In the context of GDM, SDM is highly relevant given the condition’s heterogeneity, the need for ongoing self-management, and the requirement to balance maternal and foetal outcomes throughout screening, treatment, and postpartum care. The following sections examine the role of SDM in clinical practice and explore patient preferences and challenges related to its implementation in GDM management.

Value of Shared Decision-Making in Practice

SDM is a collaborative process that enables patients and physicians to reach a mutual consensus regarding healthcare decisions.25 Through this interactive method, patients and physicians can share their experiences, motivations, cultural perspectives, and knowledge to understand each other’s preferences and implement an agreed-upon plan.26 This allows physicians to explain the natural history of the illness, risk factors, and possible complications, while supporting patient understanding and engagement.27 SDM also helps patients feel more comfortable and more willing to comply with treatment plans.28 In pregnancy, patient decision aids are proven to be effective tools to support personalised care planning and informed decision-making in women with pre-existing morbidity.29 A standardised tool for incorporating SDM in clinical practice is the SHARE (Seek, Help, Assess, Reach, and Evaluate) approach.30 This method emphasises patient participation, the assessment of their values and preferences, and the reaching of a collective decision.31 SDM has a crucial role in maternal health, given the vulnerability of pregnancy and the need to consider both maternal and foetal outcomes.32 A recent systematic review meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials suggested that SDM is an effective intervention to improve the quality of decision-making about the mode of delivery of pregnant women after caesarean section.33 This importance extends to GDM management, which requires a continuum of care encompassing screening, treatment, complication mitigation, and postpartum care.34,35 The variable presentation of this heterogeneous condition further favours SDM in personalising and individualising GDM care according to specific patient priorities and preferences.36–38 SDM promotes transparent, cooperative communication, enabling treatment strategies to be tailored to the unique requirements and desires of GDM patients.39 Through SDM, appropriate patient education, personalised care plans, and emotional support can be effectively delivered.39 Extensive counselling within this framework supports informed decision-making regarding the management of their pregnancy.40 Consequently, by actively involving women in their treatment decisions, SDM promotes patient autonomy and shared responsibility for care.41 It is believed explicitly that women with GDM are more likely to adhere to the recommended treatment regimen when involved in the decision-making process.42,43

Preferences of Gestational Diabetic Patients for Shared Decision-Making

Patients with GDM reported their preference for increased involvement in care and decision-making.44 This preference for SDM varies according to demographic and social factors, including age, marital status, education level, and personal relationship with the healthcare provider.45 It has also been reported that patients’ prior knowledge of their illness, previous healthcare experiences, and a preference for active participation further facilitate patients’ engagement.46 Educational level was positively correlated with the preference for involvement in SDM; however, women across all academic backgrounds generally favour shared participation over delegating decisions entirely to clinicians.47 Patients with varying levels of information about GDM were shown to accept the diagnosis more quickly and to develop a sustainable plan to manage it.48 Conversely, patients with lower socioeconomic status, difficulty asking questions, or reduced health literacy may be less comfortable engaging in SDM.49 In some cases, the presence of other parties, including caregivers or other family members, can disrupt SDM implementation and complicate final decisions.50 Focus group findings highlight the need for SDM, as many GDM patients felt their care was paternalistic and their opinions were often overlooked in decisions.51 They reported a lack of autonomy and personalised choices, especially regarding foetal health, which limited patients’ participation in the decision-making process and led to primarily physiological management, contributing to negative experiences and diminished trust in the healthcare team.51,52

Women have highlighted the importance of agency decisions regarding GDM screening and treatment.53 In contrast, women excluded from decision-making often assumed that the healthcare provider’s opinion was the only course of action.53 Perceptions of behavioural control by healthcare providers have also been reported, particularly when treatment plans are not collaboratively developed.53,54 From the physician’s perspective, time constraints, clinical complexity, unrealistic patient expectations, and communication barriers can limit the uptake of SDM in practice.27,55 Furthermore, the SDM process barriers are generally not different from those identified in the primary care setting, such as physicians’ time constraints, a lack of SDM culture, a paternalistic model of healthcare, patients’ passive attitude, and the belief that they are unable to make decisions.56 Some women have reported negative interactions where physicians used technical language that was difficult to understand or did not feel supported.57 In non-collaborative settings, many patients have expressed fear as they did not receive any choice about treatments and did not feel that they were genuinely involved in their treatment plan.58 Subsequently, poorly perceived SDM is associated with increased emergency department visits and poorer physical and mental health outcomes.59

Misalignment between patient expectations and clinician priorities underscores the need for broader integration of SDM in GDM care.60 Only 8% of maternal-foetal medicine physicians incorporate SDM into their pharmacotherapy plans.61 Women reported significant comfort with decision-making when given the opportunity to choose the pharmacological agent that best suited their needs, based on their priorities.62 Regardless of GDM risk, women reported that they prefer receiving multiple options and adequate information about each option to make an informed decision about their healthcare plan.44 When it comes to diabetes prevention among women with a history of GDM, most participants show a preference for active participation in their medical decisions.47 This highlights the significant role of SDM in enhancing patient satisfaction during GDM treatment.62 It is also vital to incorporate SDM when determining delivery timing in patients with GDM in the later stages of pregnancy.63 The inclusion of SDM is necessary because many variables, including biomedical, psychological, social, and environmental factors, may influence the woman’s decision.63 This also raises concerns regarding the lack of SDM inclusion in GDM protocols, which focus more on objective outcomes and evidence-based science rather than the patient’s decisions and involvement.64 Overall, women with GDM express a clear preference for active involvement in their care and meaningful participation in SDM.65

Precision Medicine in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

In recent years, the increasing affordability of genetic testing, advancements in computational methods, the development of interconnected genomic databases, and the emergence of targeted treatments have collectively enhanced the opportunities to prevent and treat diseases based on a patient’s unique genetic and metabolic profile.66 PM has been introduced as a new-age approach to tailor dietary interventions, pharmacotherapy, and lifestyle modifications,67 thereby providing specific treatments for subpopulations that share a common susceptibility to a particular disease or exhibit a similar response to a specific drug.68 In the setting of GDM, PM is considered a valuable approach, as it has a wide range of applications across disease stages.69 Current diagnostic methods implicitly suggest that the GDM population is uniform if all patients present with similar profiles and causes.70 The application of PM has helped Healthcare Professionals (HCPs) detect and evaluate inter-individual variation in prevention, diagnosis, disease progression, response to treatment, and prognosis.71 Therefore, PM has the potential to shift standard approaches from traditional one-size-fits-all to more patient-centred directions.72 PM can also help clinicians determine the suitability of standard treatments for a patient, or whether alternative or unconventional therapies should be considered.73 For example, variants in the NUS1 and GP2 genes have been associated with a higher risk of developing GDM and T2DM, illustrating the underlying genetic heterogeneity within GDM.74 It has been reported that ethnic background is associated with variations in GDM prevalence.75 These inter-individual variations make GDM a suitable candidate for PM, highlighting the potential value of using this advanced method to model GDM management based on the patient’s unique genetic, metabolic, and environmental profile (See Table 1).

In practice, the application of PM has been associated with a more rapid reduction in high blood glucose levels, offering significant benefits for both the mother and the foetus.89 Additionally, applying treatment decisions based on PM could prevent unnecessary medical interventions for women who can effectively manage their glucose levels through more conservative methods.89 Physicians can also create personalised risk predictions derived from clinical and biochemical data, thereby facilitating informed decision-making for patients.69 When these individualised risk estimates and treatment options are communicated clearly, women can actively participate in the SDM process, contributing to the creation of a customised plan that aligns with their preferences and is more likely to be followed.98 Combining SDM with personalised medicine reinforces the core principle of PM by delivering care that is both biologically tailored and preference-sensitive, ultimately guiding GDM management toward more effective, patient-centred pathways.99 Looking ahead, personalised disease risk identification through genetic sequencing could support earlier recognition of women at increased risk of GDM, enabling closer monitoring and timely preventive intervention.100,101 Algorithms that integrate genetic, metabolic, and clinical characteristics should be developed and made accessible to predict GDM risk, allowing for earlier intervention.102 Once GDM is diagnosed, pharmacogenomics information and personalised lifestyle interventions can be utilised to create programs that optimise glycaemic control in patients.23 The integration of patient information with clinical parameters through electronic records can also facilitate real-time decision support aids and promote long-term patient engagement.103

Genetic and Metabolic Determinants of Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

A compelling example of genotype-guided care in GDM involves the melatonin receptor 1B gene (MTNR1B). MTNR1B encodes the melatonin receptor expressed on pancreatic β-cells. Activation of this receptor suppresses insulin secretion, particularly in certain genetic variants of the gene, resulting in decreased insulin secretion and elevated blood glucose levels.104

Common single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in MTNR1B (such as rs10830963, rs1387153, rs2166706, and rs1447352) are strongly associated with elevated fasting glucose levels.105 Studies across multiple populations have identified the rs10830963 variant as a possible genetic risk factor for GDM, particularly among carriers of the G allele.106 The clinical impact of MTNR1B variants appears to be amplified in overweight or obese women.91 The gene interacts with pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) to predict the likelihood of requiring early insulin therapy.91 As illustrated in Figure 1, MTNR1B risk variants reduce insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, and when combined with elevated pre-pregnancy BMI and insulin resistance, this gene-environment interaction increases GDM risk and the likelihood of requiring early insulin therapy. Consistent with this framework, studies have shown that overweight women who carry the risk allele are significantly more likely to need insulin escalation, while non-carriers may be adequately managed with lifestyle modification alone.107 MTNR1B genotyping may contribute to risk stratification and could enable clinicians to tailor treatment intensity to the individual’s genetic risk profile in the future, moving beyond the conventional GDM management model.

The interaction between MTNR1B genetic variation and pre-pregnancy BMI increases the risk of GDM. The MTNR1B Gene-environment interaction reduces insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, and when combined with elevated pre-pregnancy BMI and insulin resistance, it increases GDM risk, which requires lifestyle modification/Metformin and/or early insulin therapy.

As genomic analyses and fine-mapping techniques continue to advance,108 an improved understanding of disease mechanisms may position genetic information as a valuable pillar of the SDM framework, alongside clinical evidence and patient preferences. As such, MTNR1B genotyping, particularly when interpreted in conjunction with pre-pregnancy BMI, offers a promising proof-of-concept for incorporating PM into future antenatal care pathways, pending validation in prospective clinical trials.105

These findings collectively support the potential value of PM in modelling GDM management strategies based on each patient’s unique genetic and phenotypic profile, while highlighting the need for validation in prospective studies and for integration with additional genetic risk markers.

Gut Microbiota and Emerging Precision Targets in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

Gut microbiota refers to a diverse ecosystem of micro-organisms, including bacteria, viruses and fungi, that reside in the gastrointestinal tract.109 These micro-organisms play a key role in nutrient metabolism, immune regulation and glucose homeostasis.109 Advances in microbiome research have improved the understanding of the role of gut microbiota in metabolic health.110 During pregnancy, physiological and metabolic adaptations are accompanied by increases in bacterial load and alterations in gut microbiota composition.111 These changes are minimal during the first trimester but become more pronounced in mid- to late gestation, when dysregulated microbial shifts have been associated with insulin resistance and the development of GDM.112,113

Women with GDM exhibit distinct gut microbiota profiles compared to normoglycemic pregnant women; however, the direction and magnitude of these changes vary across studies.114,115 Rather than a single pathogenic microbiota, GDM is characterised by altered microbial patterns. These include enrichment of Actinobacteria, shifts in Firmicutes-related taxa, lower microbial richness (α-diversity), and greater differences between individuals (β-diversity).112,113 These longitudinal changes are detected during the first trimester and remain present during late gestation, suggesting a sustained microbial imbalance that may affect maternal glucose regulation.114,116

At the genus and species levels, several microbial alterations have been linked to glucose metabolism in GDM. Increased abundance of Blautia, Butyricicoccus, Clostridium, and members of the Lachnospiraceae family has been reported, alongside reduced levels of Bacteroides, Collinsella, and Rikenellaceae.112,114,117,118 Some of these taxa correlate with fasting blood glucose levels and oral glucose tolerance test results, suggesting potential links with glycaemic regulation.118,119 However, these associations remain observational and may vary across populations and study designs.

Diet is a major driver of gut microbiota variability, and microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids have been implicated in insulin sensitivity, inflammatory pathways, and energy balance.120 Higher dietary fibre intake has been associated with increased abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria such as Lachnospira, which has been linked to favourable metabolic profiles in pregnancy.121 Conversely, low fibre intake promotes growth of Collinsella, a lactate-producing pathobiont linked to impaired glucose metabolism and adverse metabolic outcomes.122 Macronutrient composition also affects microbial patterns: high carbohydrate intake is positively associated with Proteobacteria and Bacteroides, while high-fat or protein intake can increase Firmicutes, Rombustia, and Lachnospira.112,123 In women with GDM, adherence to medical nutrition therapy (MNT) alters gut microbial abundance, increasing beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacteriaceae and butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae, while reducing potentially harmful taxa such as Acidaminococcaceae and Enterobacteriaceae.124,125 Dietary interventions may also influence the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, which may serve as a biomarker of energy balance and metabolic control in GDM.124 These findings suggest that diet influences the gut microbiota and its metabolic functions, including the production of short-chain fatty acids, modulation of inflammatory pathways, and regulation of glucose homeostasis. However, inter-individual variation, ethnicity, and baseline metabolic status contribute to heterogeneous microbial responses, highlighting the need for precision nutrition approaches.120,126

Probiotics and prebiotics have also been explored as potential interventions to modify gut microbiota in GDM. In particular, Lactobacillus species have been studied due to their proposed anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects.127 Clinical trials have reported modest improvements in fasting glucose, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory markers following probiotic supplementation with selected Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains.128 These effects may be partially mediated by microbially derived metabolites that influence intestinal gluconeogenesis and glucose homeostasis.129 However, other randomised controlled trials have shown no significant reduction in GDM incidence or improvement in glycaemic outcomes following probiotic or synbiotic supplementation during pregnancy.130,131 Collectively, these findings suggest that strain-specific and host-dependent effects exist, warranting cautious interpretation before routine clinical implementation.

Importantly, maternal gut microbiota is influenced by multiple factors, including ethnicity, pre-pregnancy BMI, dietary patterns, and disease severity, which may affect both microbial composition and response to dietary interventions.132,133 While current evidence highlights promising associations between microbial composition, diet, and glucose metabolism, further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are required before microbiota-targeted strategies can be integrated into routine GDM management. Overall, gut microbiota represents an emerging and biologically plausible target in GDM, offering potential avenues for PM and personalised therapeutic interventions.

Precision Medicine Research Trends in Diabetes Mellitus and Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

Despite its expansion in research, there is still limited evidence on the overall growth of PM’s application in diabetes, particularly in GDM. For PM in diabetes, the WoSCC online search returned 2,879 total hits, while the PM in GDM search returned 60 total hits. The findings indicate a gradual increase in the production of published papers about PM in DM and GDM areas. However, PM publications in DM began 4 years earlier (2013) than those on GDM (2017) and have shown a steady increase over the years. It is also worth noting that the highest number of PM publications was focused on the Endocrinology and Metabolism field, particularly on diabetes (n= 661, 22.96%) and GDM (n= 23, 38.33%) (see Figure 2). These findings highlight a gap in introducing the PM concept in the GDM area and emphasise the importance of further research development to customise and personalise the treatment in this area to improve disease management, treatment adherence, and quality of life.

Besides time constraints, communication, and attitude, the need for multidisciplinary teams, such as obstetrics, endocrinology, nutrition, and psychology, to integrate care plans may further impede the implementation of PM and SDM concepts in GDM. Therefore, a comprehensive needs assessment, resource evaluations, pilot studies, and feasibility studies should be conducted.

Strengths and Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to discuss how integrating PM with shared SDM may contribute to more individualised and patient-centred GDM care. This is also the first paper to summarise the role of PM from prevention to the postpartum stage of GDM. The paper’s narrative is based on a comprehensive literature review that discusses several aspects of PM and SDM in GDM. However, the narrative nature of the discussion could be subjective and open to different interpretations. The results regarding the PM trend in GDM and DM are not based on a comprehensive bibliometric analysis or systematic search; rather, they are based on a WoSCC databases search to identify differences in PM trends between GDM and DM. Therefore, it is recommended that the paper’s outcomes and conclusions be interpreted in light of its limitations.

Conclusion

Women with GDM face heterogeneous risks, treatment responses, and psychological needs that are not fully addressed by current one-size-fits-all management strategies. Integrating SDM into routine GDM care enables women to express their preferences, make informed choices among treatment options, and engage more fully in their care, thereby improving adherence and satisfaction. Evidence shows that women with GDM value collaborative care models and wish to be involved in decisions that affect both their own health and their infant’s health. Moreover, PM offers a complementary pathway to individualised GDM management by incorporating genetic, metabolic, and environmental information, along with emerging markers such as the gut microbiota profile, to refine risk stratification and guide targeted GDM management toward a tailored, personalised approach that considers unique phenotypic characteristics. Combining SDM and PM by aligning biologically tailored treatment with patient values and priorities may provide a more holistic framework for individualised GDM care. This framework could enhance the effectiveness, acceptability, and sustainability of GDM management and may lead to changes in current practice to improve patient care and quality of life. However, a pilot programme and a clinical trial are required before widespread adoption.
