Pairing Analogy-Augmented Generation with Procedural Memory for Procedural Q&A
K Roth, Rushil Gupta, Simon Halle, Bang Liu

Abstract
Large language models struggle to synthesize disparate pieces of information into a coherent plan when approaching a complex procedural task. In this work, we introduce a novel formalism and structure for such procedural knowledge. Based on this formalism, we present a novel procedural knowledge dataset called LCStep, which we created from LangChain tutorials. To leverage this procedural knowledge to solve new tasks, we propose analogy-augmented generation (AAG), which draws inspiration from the human ability to assimilate past experiences to solve unfamiliar problems. AAG uses a custom procedure memory store to retrieve and adapt specialized domain knowledge to answer new procedural tasks. We demonstrate that AAG outperforms few-shot and RAG baselines on LCStep, RecipeNLG, and CHAMP datasets under a pairwise LLM-based evaluation, corroborated by human evaluation in the case of RecipeNLG.



1
Introduction

Procedural knowledge, or “knowledge-how”, is a form of knowledge that involves the ability to
do
something, as opposed to propositional knowledge, which is the awareness of a fact (“knowledge-that”)
Pavese (2022)
. A hallmark of procedural knowledge is that the agent is expected not only to understand the facts but also
plan
and
reason
about actions in its environment.

The community has made efforts both theoretically
Georgeff and Lansky (1986)
and empirically via LLMs to enable information systems that explicitly handle procedural knowledge in real-world
Wang et al. (2024b)
and virtual environments
Huang et al. (2022)
;
Wang et al. (2024a)
;
Tan et al. (2024)
;
Brahman et al. (2023)
. However, these works have primarily exploited prompt engineering to leverage the model’s internal or limited external knowledge without taking inspiration from human cognition, which excels in these tasks.

LLMs consistently struggle to perform well in specialized domains not well represented in training data and require up-to-date knowledge
Kandpal et al. (2023)
, usually added through fine-tuning or as augmented external memory. On the contrary, humans demonstrate
analogical reasoning
abilities in these situations
Parsons and Davies (2022)
, adapting relevant knowledge from their past experiences in new ways to achieve a goal.

Figure 1:
An illustration of using the AAG system to generate a recipe for an unseen food.

Analogy is a central ability of human cognition and has been formally theorized by human psychologists, the most common being
Structure Mapping Theory

Gentner (1983)
. The theory defines analogy as mapping knowledge from one situation (base) into another (target). According to the theory, a situation consists of objects, their properties, and the
relationships
that characterize their interactions. It is not
object attributes
that gets mapped in an analogy; it is the similarity in the relationships between objects that plays the key role
Gentner and Smith (2012)
. The
systematicity principle
, a crucial part of the theory, emphasizes that people prefer to map predicates with higher-order relations than isolated predicates.

To ground this, consider the recipe example presented in Figure
1
. Each recipe forms a situation, with all the different ingredients and intermediate dishes constituting the objects. The relations in the situation correspond to the cooking operations, which are complex predicates involving two or more arguments. For example, consider the step, “dip the tuna in a crusting batter infused with coriander”, which could be thought of as the predicate:
Dip(tuna, Batter(Coriander, …))
, comprising one simple and one complex predicate as an argument. The LLM agent possesses knowledge of such cooking operations from examples in its memory and leverages them in the new question on sesame-crusted tuna, which would involve a similar predicate:
Dip(tuna, Batter(Sesame, …)
.

Inspired by human analogical reasoning, we propose Analogy-Augmented Generation
(AAG)
, a novel system for procedural question answering based on the
Rewrite-Retrieve-Read
framework
Ma et al. (2023)
. The AAG pipeline retrieves analogical examples from a specialized procedural memory in a multi-step process, creating a procedure that accomplishes the query (see Figure
2
).

Our contributions in this paper are the following:

•

We provide an easily extensible formalism for text-based procedural knowledge that plugs easily into existing information retrieval systems.

•

We describe a novel dataset,
LCStep
, consisting of procedures to do tasks with the LangChain Python library
Chase and contributors (2022)
. The recency of this data presents an opportunity to test procedural knowledge systems on a topic not present in some LLMs’ training data.

•

We propose a novel LLM-based system called Analogy-Augmented Generation (AAG) that capitalizes on the above-defined formalism for procedural knowledge and implements a workflow inspired by human analogical reasoning.

•

We evaluate this system on an unfamiliar domain using our LCStep dataset and again on a familiar domain using the RecipeNLG dataset
Bień et al. (2020)
. We ablate each separable component of the AAG system to measure the contribution of each. We also stretch our formal representation and system by applying it to CHAMP
Mao et al. (2024)
, a challenging math problem dataset. Through LLM-based pairwise evaluation and a blind human study, we conclude that the AAG system generates more coherent and plausible procedures for the input queries than existing baselines, including RAG.

2
Procedure Generation Task

We define a procedure as
(x,y,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}))
where:

•

x
is a string containing the available inputs,

•

y
is the string containing the output of the procedure, and

•

(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
is a sequence of ordered steps, each of which is a string.

This structure can be seen as a linear directed chain graph starting with the input as the root, the output as the leaf node, and each step
s_{i}
as an intermediate node in the chain.

Unlike some existing conceptions of procedural knowledge
Georgeff and Lansky (1986)
, our formalism depends on the text of the steps to describe any non-linear control flow. The sequential format makes it easier for LLMs to grasp task-relevant patterns, fostering analogical reasoning and the reuse of experience to tackle new tasks.
Our approach retains composability: two procedures
(x,y,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}))
and
(y,z,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}))
can be combined into
(x,z,(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k},t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell}))
. This supports the generation of
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
from
x
and
y
, for example, generating recipe steps from a title and ingredient list.

Procedural knowledge differs from knowledge graphs, as it captures skill-like sequences that can be directly translated into agent actions or code APIs, emphasizing "how" to achieve outcomes. Functionally, this structure is an improvement over plain text because it reduces noise and isolates essential procedural information, improving data quality for enhancing LLMs’ reasoning and planning.
This paper focuses on linear chains due to the complexity of dependency extraction from raw procedure data, but our future work will focus on expanding this to explicitly represent dependencies within procedures.

Under this framework, the task of procedure generation is to generate
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
given
x
and
y
. For example, a procedure generation system for food recipes will be expected to generate the cooking steps given the recipe title and ingredients.

3
LCStep Dataset

A key goal in this work is to test systems’ domain adaptation ability to answer procedural knowledge questions for domains not present or not up to date in LLM training data. Across a wide variety of tasks, the expense of model training makes it infeasible to continually retrain as new domains become relevant or as information is updated
Gao et al. (2024)
. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is popularly used to leverage external data to keep information up to date, but we expect our procedural knowledge system to provide further benefits, especially because answering procedural knowledge questions often requires information from disparate document sources.

For our experiments, we collected a dataset of procedural knowledge of the LangChain Python library, unseen by many extant LLMs. We selected LangChain as the domain for our dataset because it was published in 2022, which is later than the knowledge cutoff date for many web-scale LLMs, including GPT-3.5
OpenAI (2024)
, while also having plenty of documentation due to its popularity. Of course, any domain of public interest will quickly become incorporated into the training data for both commercial and academic models. Hence, a dataset of this nature has a limited time window in which to truly test domain adaptation. In this paper, we perform our experiments using a model with a cutoff date before 2022. This allows us to measure the extent to which systems can leverage the supplied procedural knowledge store to solve tasks in an unseen domain.

The LCStep dataset was collected from 180 tutorial pages in the Python section of the LangChain website. We used an LLM-enabled pipeline with human oversight and quality review to extract 276 procedures from these tutorials, representing each procedure in the formal structure defined in Section
2
. We include details on the collection pipeline in Appendix
A
.

4
Our Method

We propose an Analogy-Augmented Generation (AAG) system to tackle the task of procedural Q&A. Our system comprises three key components: 1)
procedural memory store:
a retrieval database for procedures; 2)
query and answer generator
: the analogy step which breaks the input query into predicates (steps) and their corresponding questions and retrieves analogous procedures from memory to answer them; and 3)
iterative self-refinement
: an LLM-based critic which evaluates the candidate response, suggests edits, and then performs them. Figure
2
shows a flow diagram of our system. We now describe each module in more detail, followed by an end-to-end description of the overall control flow. Please refer to Appendix
G
for the full prompts used in each module.

Figure 2:
Schematic view of the AAG architecture. The input and intermediate outputs are enclosed in red rounded rectangles, whereas all modules involving an LLM are enclosed in blue rectangles. Boxes 1 and 2 denote our architectural contributions, where 1 shows the query and answer generation step, and 2 shows the iterative refinement with the self-critic step. The procedural memory store holds objects structured as procedures (see Section
4.1
). The RAG and answer generator modules interact with the memory store to retrieve
K
and
N\times K
procedures relevant to the input query and all the generated questions (one at a time), respectively.

4.1
Procedural Memory

The procedural memory store embeds whole procedures by formatting the linear graph in a text template natural to the domain. Every search to the memory store finds and returns
K
nearest procedures to the search query in the embedding space. Practically, the store maintains the input, output, and steps as Python
str
,
str
, and
list[str]
types, respectively, when the procedures are retrieved, generated, or communicated from one module to another.

4.2
Query Generator

The input query to the AAG system comprises the goal (the procedure output
y
) and the resources we have access to (the procedure input
x
). Using this input query directly to search the memory can yield insufficient or noisy knowledge when the examples in the memory are indirectly related or don’t use words similar to words in the query. Humans solve this problem by analogy, developing high-level mental maps of steps to achieve the goal and consequently linking and assimilating knowledge from relevant past experiences to compose the solution
Piaget (1968)
;
Vosniadou and Ortony (1989)
.

To approximate this in our system, we first prompt the LLM to generate a high-level outline of the steps in the required procedure (similar to HyDE
Gao et al. (2022)
), followed by asking it to formulate questions for them that can be answered through analogical examples in the memory. We adapt the query rewriting prompt used by Ma et al. (
2023
) for our task and generate a maximum of
N=4
queries for a given input query. For example, given the input query: ‘
create a custom LLM agent that can interact with users in a conversational manner using a specific language style using an LLM, Serp API
’, the following four questions are generated by the query generator:

•

How to train a language model for a specific language style?

•

Best practices for integrating a language model into a conversational interface?

•

How to use the Serp API to extract relevant information from search engine results?

•

Strategy for optimizing the performance of a custom LLM agent in conversational interactions?

4.3
Answer Generator

Each question received from the query generator module is individually used to retrieve from the procedure memory. The challenge, however, is that the information required for the question is often implicit or hidden in the retrieved procedures.

Therefore, the answer generator module acts as a filter and prompts the LLM with the question and the retrieved procedures, asking it to generate a plausible answer by borrowing knowledge from the retrieved procedures. Note that this module relies heavily on LLM’s ability to understand the semantics of the retrieved procedures as it filters out helpful content from the retrieved procedures. Finally, each question, along with its generated answer, is stacked together as
"Q: <Question> [newline] A: <Answer>"
to build a high-quality context, which is used by both the Update Response and the Edit performer module.

4.4
Iterative Refinement with Self-Critic

LLMs have been shown to act as powerful self-critics for their own outputs, evaluating them across several dimensions and suggesting feedback crucial to improving them
Madaan et al. (2023)
;
Saunders et al. (2022)
. We augment the AAG system with an iterative refinement procedure guided by the same LLM-based critic for its output. As seen in Figure
2
, the critic takes the output of the Update Response module, evaluates if any edits are required, and suggests them as a bulleted list. If no edits are needed, the critic appends the string
‘NO UPDATE REQUIRED’
to its response. The LLM then performs the suggested edits with a prompt that includes the list of edits, context from the answer generator, and the current version of the output procedure steps. The LLM critic then re-evaluates this edited version, and the cycle continues for a maximum of
T=3
cycles. The output at the end of the iterative refinement is the final answer of the AAG system to the input query.

4.5
End-to-End Workflow

The AAG system takes as input a query by the user and outputs a bulleted list of steps that will lead the user to the desired goal. Using the terminologies defined in Section
2
, the input query is of the format “
<output>
using
<input>
”, where the user specifies the goal and the resources available to use to reach the goal. Assuming we have a procedural memory instantiated with the procedure objects as outlined in Sections
4.1
and
5.3
, the detailed workflow of the AAG system is as follows (refer to Figure
2
and the full trace in appendix
G
):

1.

The RAG module searches the procedural memory using the input query and retrieves the top
K=3
relevant procedures (highest cosine similarity in the embedding space) from memory.

2.

The query generator module takes the input query and breaks it into
N=4
queries about different steps of the solution.

3.

For each query, the answer generator module searches the memory and utilizes the retrieved procedures to generate an answer. All questions and corresponding answers are stacked together to form the context for downstream modules.

4.

The update response module is the first to update the RAG response using the above context and passes the updated response to the critic.

5.

The LLM critic decides if the response requires edits and suggests them in a bulleted list.

6.

The LLM edits its response using the suggestions given by the critic (same LLM) and the context from the answer generator module.

7.

The edited procedure is then re-evaluated for edits. Repeat step 5 for a maximum of
T=3
cycles. The self-refined version is then returned as the output of the system.

5
Experiments

We evaluate the effectiveness of the AAG system for procedure generation in three scenarios: an unseen domain (LCStep), a familiar domain (RecipeNLG), and CHAMP, a challenging domain for our formalism. We begin by describing the baselines used for all experiments, followed by discussing the evaluation methodology and design choices of the AAG system. We then examine the two case studies of LCStep and RecipeNLG
Bień et al. (2020)
and include the survey of CHAMP
Mao et al. (2024)
in Appendix
B
. Finally, we conclude with a comprehensive ablation study of our proposed pipeline. For completeness, we also compare inference times for our system and the baselines in Appendix
F
.

5.1
Baselines

We compare our system against widely used natural language generation approaches that involve prompting an LLM. For our experiments, we use
gpt-3.5-turbo-0125
from OpenAI as the language model for all approaches.

•

Zero-Shot
: We prompt the language model to generate the steps for a procedure, given the task instructions and the procedure’s input and output.

•

Few-Shot
: We sample
K=3
random procedures from the training set and include those in the prompt after the task instructions, which are the same as in the zero-shot case.

•

RAG
: We retrieve
k=3
procedures based on the provided input and output and then use those three procedures in the prompt like the few-shot case.

•

ReAct
: We adapt the popular ReAct system
Yao et al. (2023)
to our task by prompting the model with access to the procedural knowledge store as a “tool” which it can search, and allowing successive queries to the tool before deciding to produce a final answer.

5.2
Evaluation Methodology

We compare the proposed AAG system and the other methods under an LLM-based pairwise evaluation scheme. We prompt the LLM to evaluate the two procedures (AAG and other method) on:

•

Their ability to accomplish the specified goal;

•

The level of details, clarity, and coherence of the steps;

•

Coverage of the resources specified in user input.

The exact prompt for this evaluation can be found in the appendix
G.1
. Note that we don’t provide ground-truth steps as input in the prompt due to several reasons: 1) there can be multiple correct procedures to achieve a single task, 2) the ground-truth procedures are seldom incomplete and noisy, specifically in recipes as they are scraped from the internet, and 3) to prevent biasing the LLM towards only matching the keywords in the generated and ground-truth procedure steps.

Both procedures under comparison are generated by the same LLM; thus, LLM’s bias towards its own generation gets nullified. To further control for randomness and de-bias the LLM evaluation towards ordering procedures in the prompt, we run ten prompt requests for each sample, with five requests having the AAG procedure placed first and the other 5 have the other method placed first. Moreover, the LLM is set to a temperature of 0.7, and each of the ten calls is on a different seed of the LLM, but the ten seeds remain fixed during the evaluation of all the samples. Finally, we obtain the result for a sample using majority voting among the results of 10 LLM calls.

We must note that we report a pairwise comparison of methods and AAG in table
1
,
2
and
3
; therefore, the numbers cannot be compared across methods. A higher number for a technique means that the LLM is more
confident
that one system is better than the other in the pair, not that one system is better than the other systems not in the pair.

5.3
System Design Choices

The AAG system has no trainable parameters and relies on frozen LLMs. The temperature of the OpenAI LM is set to a value of 0.7 for all evaluations. We use the
all-mpnet-base-v2
model from sentence-transformers
Reimers and Gurevych (2019)
as the embedding model for procedural memory Weaviate store, creating dense 768-dimensional embeddings for each procedure object. All three data fields of a procedure (input, output, and steps) are embedded together in one embedding.

5.4
LCStep: An Unfamiliar Domain

Setup:
We sorted the 276 procedures in LCStep by increasing length and selected the final (longest) 56 examples as the test set, with the preceding 27 examples as a validation set. The remaining examples populate the procedural memory store. Selecting the longest examples for the test set evaluates AAG’s ability to leverage simpler procedural knowledge to propose solutions for more complicated tasks.

Results:
From Table

, it is evident that the LLM prefers the procedures generated by the AAG system over all other baselines by a substantial margin (>40%). Figure
3
shows a qualitative example of how AAG manages to provide more of the detail present in the reference procedure. While the RAG approach stops at just initializing agents, the AAG output, similar to the ground truth, takes a step ahead and encourages the user to test the implemented setup for correctness, which is a good coding practice.

5.5
RecipeNLG: A Familiar Domain

Setup:
Under our formalism, we construct a procedure using the recipe title as output, the ingredients as input, and the directions as the procedural steps. From over 2 million recipes, we select a random subset of 10000 recipes for our experiments (fixed across methods). Out of these 10000 recipes, we select 2000 as test and 1000 as validation examples. The remaining 7000 examples populate the procedural memory store.

Results:
As highlighted in Table
1
, the AAG system outperforms its counterpart for all methods on the RecipeNLG dataset. Interestingly, even though recipes are already familiar to the LLM, the analogical reasoning in the AAG pipeline further improves the quality of the generated responses according to our evaluations. Thus, while the AAG system is expected to shine better on specialized unseen domains like LCStep, it still provides clear benefits for a familiar domain.

Since cooking is more common knowledge than LangChain, we were able to conduct a blind human study to corroborate our pairwise evaluation results. We surveyed human raters to compare generations from AAG and RAG for 50 random test examples from the RecipeNLG test set. 3-6 human raters evaluated each pair of generations. The study revealed that users preferred AAG recipes in
56%
of instances and preferred RAG recipes in only 16% of examples. (28% of examples resulted in a tie.) This underscores the conclusions of the pairwise evaluation on the efficacy of the AAG system on the RecipeNLG dataset. See Appendix

5.6
Ablation Study

We perform a rigorous ablation study of our proposed AAG architecture. We experiment with removing the three architectural contributions in all permutations, i.e., the query generator, answer generator, and critic-guided iterative refinement. We describe each of the resulting modifications below:

•

AAG-NOQG
: This represents the AAG system without the query generator. The absence of a query generator also implies the absence of the pipeline’s answer generator and Update Response modules. The RAG response is directly fed into critic-based iterative refinement, to produce the final output.

•

AAG-NOAG
: This represents the AAG system without the answer generator module. For each question output by the query generator, we concatenate all the retrieved procedures to form the answer, removing the duplicates across queries and forming one big context with all the procedures.

•

AAG-NOCR
: This represents the AAG system without critic-guided iterative refinement. The Update Response module’s output is considered the system’s final output.

•

AAG-NOAG-NOCR
: This represents the AAG system without the answer generator and the critic-guided iterative refinement. The Update Response module receives the context as in AAG-NOAG; its output is the system’s final output.

We also vary the number of queries
N
generated by the query generator and include its observations in appendix
E
.

Results
: The lower half of Table
1
displays the results for the pairwise comparison between AAG and each of the ablation methods above on both datasets. From the table, it can be observed that AAG performs better than any of its ablation variants, highlighting the importance of each component in the pipeline. The numbers for the AAG-NOQG variant showcase the analogy’s significance in our system, achieved by the query and answer generator modules.
In the absence of any of the three modules, namely the query, answer generator, and iterative refinement, performance is reduced.

6
Related Work

Based on the
Rewrite-Retrieve-Read
framework
Ma et al. (2023)
, AAG differs from the conventional RAG
Lewis et al. (2021)
in three key aspects: sophisticated prompting approach, analogical reasoning, and the iterative workflow for response generation (Section
4.5
), in addition to the application in knowledge-intensive procedural QA. We refer the readers to
Gao et al. (2024)
for a survey covering the latest RAG research.

Standard Prompting Methods
: Zero-shot prompting
Chang et al. (2024)
, few-shot prompting
Brown et al. (2020)
, and RAG
Lewis et al. (2021)
are the most common LLM-based approaches to natural language generation. Few-shot prompting requires a fixed set of labeled examples, and RAG requires some supporting set of documents, not necessarily of the same form as the system’s generated text. For example, when augmenting LLMs with general-knowledge Wikipedia articles to answer factual questions. In this work, we perform a sophisticated interleaving of prompts and memory searches to retrieve finer context information from the analogical examples in procedural memory.

Analogical Reasoning in LLMs
: Many papers attempt to elicit reasoning in LLMs by various prompting methods
Zhou et al. (2024)
;
Wang et al. (2023a)
. Specific to analogical reasoning, early attempts show by case studies that LLMs can do analogical reasoning just like humans
Webb et al. (2023)
. Recent works explore analogy generation and reasoning with knowledge graphs on LLMs
Yuan et al. (2023)
;
Bhavya et al. (2023)
;
Bhavya et al. (2022)
. These works are applied to propositional knowledge and rely on large-scale external knowledge bases to store entity relationships and perform analogical reasoning. Yu et al. (
2023
) and Yasunaga et al. (
2024
) prompt the model to propose problems (and their solutions) analogous to the original problem, to inform the final solution. Unfortunately this approach is prone to failure on under-represented or unseen domains due to a lack of knowledge. Thus, in our work, we cast the problem as a domain adaptation problem where we retrieve real solutions to analogous real problems from the procedural memory, thus receiving performance benefits in both familiar and unfamiliar knowledge-intensive domains.

Iterative Pipeline
: Numerous recent works based on the traditional
Retrieve-then-Read
paradigm undertake an iterative workflow to generate the response to an input query. Asai et al. (
2023
) train a separate LM to predict reflection tokens like ‘retrieve,’ ‘relevant,’ and ‘irrelevant’ within the response, which determines the subsequent control flow to generate or refine subsequent chunks of the response accordingly. On the contrary, Shao et al. (
2023
) intersperse Generation-Augmented Retrieval (GAR) and RAG to sequentially expand the search query in the memory and obtain relevant information to plug into the final response. Similar to Shao et al. (
2023
), we expand the search query to meaningful sub-questions that help retrieve relevant knowledge from the procedural memory at the granularity of individual steps.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel formalism for procedural knowledge inspired by human reasoning and extended it to build a novel LLM-based system to perform analogical reasoning for procedural Q&A. Our experiments demonstrate its increased performance over standard RAG on three domains. The system performed especially well on our self-curated LCStep dataset, showing that AAG can better augment a frozen LLM with the knowledge needed to answer questions on unseen domains. Although recipes are highly familiar to general-purpose LLMs like GPT-3.5, the AAG system still significantly improves the model outputs even according to human review. These results show that the structured procedural memory, analogical reasoning through the combination of query and answer generator, and iterative critique/refinement all bolster the performance of our system on procedure generation tasks. Our theoretical framework provides a basis for solving high-level planning tasks more effectively in varied domains.

Limitations and Future Work

This framework deliberately ignores procedure structure that is non-linear, meaning that a non-linear procedure must express its non-linearity in the text of the steps. This is a reasonable assumption for many tasks performed by humans, but not for computer algorithms. In future work we will expand our procedure definition to allow dependencies to be represented so that analogy can be done on the procedure structure beyond just step ordering. In particular, we plan to more tightly couple our system with the formal structure we have proposed, by leveraging the composable nature of our procedure definition to perform finer-grained search and reasoning over sub-procedures. This may involve a sort of subroutine representation similar to the skill library from Voyager
Wang et al. (2023b)
.

As mentioned in Section
3
, the window is limited for using LCStep to measure system performance on unseen domains. In this work it was necessary for us to use an older LLM in order to test this for AAG. Future work can add another incremental LCStep dataset with updated instructions for a later version of LangChain, which would allow us to test how systems perform as documentation is updated.

Ethical Considerations

The use of automated systems to generate and execute plans can and will result in unintended consequences, whether humans are in the loop or on the loop
Leins and Kaspersen (2021)
. In addition, automating actions generally done by humans (here planning and reasoning) has the social effect of anthropomorphizing these systems, leading to a gradual shift in accountability from human stakeholders onto the systems themselves
Weidinger et al. (2021)
.

The domains which we have applied our system to in this work do not constitute immediate risks. For LCStep, the generated steps cannot easily be converted into code, although this could become increasingly easy as planning-based code generation continues to improve
Jiang et al. (2024)
. Mathematical problem solving poses no real-world risks.

Thanks to Google’s AI Overviews feature, there has already been at least one instance of a generative AI system producing a food recipe that would be harmful if followed
Robison (2024)
.

While it might be obvious to most that it’s a bad idea to add glue to food, it’s clear that automated recipe generation could result in worse than a botched meal.
