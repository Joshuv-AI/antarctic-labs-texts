Spironolactone versus Bicalutamide for Female Pattern Hair Loss: A 24-Month Unicenter Retrospective Comparative Study of Effectiveness and Safety
Authors: Rocío Gil-Redondo, Juan Jimenez-Cauhe, Adrián Imbernon-Moya, Diego Buendía-Castaño, Patricia Burgos Blasco, Borja Díaz-Guimaraens, Miguel Domínguez-Santas, Ángela Hermosa-Gelbard, David Saceda-Corralo, Sergio Vañó-Galván
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFemale pattern hair loss (FPHL) is a chronic, progressive condition that affects quality of life. Off-label systemic antiandrogens are increasingly used, but comparative data remain limited.MethodsTo compare the effectiveness, safety, and healthcare resource utilization of spironolactone versus bicalutamide in FPHL over 24 months, we conducted a unicenter retrospective cohort study of women with FPHL treated between 2018 and 2022. The primary outcome was mean change in Sinclair grade from baseline to month 24. Secondary outcomes included ≥ 1 grade improvement, adverse events, and healthcare utilization. Linear and ordinal logistic regression models were adjusted for baseline severity.ResultsA total of 187 women (median age 35 years, range 15–69 years) completed 24 months of follow-up. Both drugs produced significant Sinclair grade improvement (mean change −0.5, p < 0.001). Spironolactone (n = 128) was superior, with 54.4% achieving ≥ 1 grade improvement versus 37.3% with bicalutamide (n = 59) (p = 0.030). High-dose spironolactone (> 100 mg/day, median 130 mg/day) showed greater benefit than the maximum bicalutamide dose (50 mg/day). Adverse event rate was similar, but bicalutamide caused more laboratory abnormalities and required closer monitoring.ConclusionsBoth drugs are effective systemic options. Spironolactone, particularly > 100 mg/day, demonstrated greater efficacy, comparable safety, and less monitoring, supporting its potential role as first-line systemic option.

Introduction

Female pattern hair loss (FPHL) is a common, progressive condition characterized by hair thinning of the frontal, parietal, and vertex scalp, driven by androgen-mediated follicular miniaturization in genetically predisposed individuals [1]. Despite its prevalence, therapeutic options remain limited and often guided by clinical experience rather than robust evidence. Topical minoxidil, the only Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatment, shows modest efficacy, frequent local irritation, and low cosmetic satisfaction, prompting exploration of systemic alternatives [2]. Among these, oral antiandrogens have gained attention as off-label options [3, 4].

Spironolactone, a potassium-sparing diuretic with antiandrogenic properties via androgen receptor blockade and inhibition of androgen synthesis, is widely prescribed in FPHL [5]. Retrospective studies suggest efficacy at doses of 100–200 mg/day with a favorable safety profile, though potassium monitoring is recommended in women older than 45 years [1, 6, 7]. However, data are primarily observational, with few randomized trials [1, 7].

Bicalutamide, a nonsteroidal androgen receptor antagonist used in prostate cancer, lacks diuretic and anti-mineralocorticoid effects and may offer a more targeted mechanism with fewer systemic side effects. Preliminary data from small series show promising efficacy and tolerability, particularly in patients intolerant or unresponsive to spironolactone [4, 9–12].

Despite their growing use, head-to-head comparative studies remain scarce. Only one small retrospective study has compared both agents, limited to premenopausal women using concomitant topical minoxidil and short follow-up, possibly underestimating their potential [14]. Long-term safety data on bicalutamide in women are limited, raising concerns about liver enzyme monitoring, optimal dosing, and long-term risks [15, 16].

In this context, we conducted a 24-month retrospective comparative study evaluating spironolactone versus bicalutamide in a large FPHL cohort, aiming to provide real-world evidence to guide clinical decisions.

Methods

Study Design, Population, and Treatment Strategies

We conducted a retrospective cohort in a hair disorders center in Madrid, Spain, including patients with FHPL treated with spironolactone or bicalutamide between 2018 and 2022. Diagnoses and therapeutic decisions were performed by board-certified dermatologists with expertise in hair disorders. All patients were evaluated with digital trichoscopy (FotoFinder® medicam 1000). Patients with other concomitant hair disorders were excluded.

For safety analysis, we included all patients receiving at least one dose and one follow-up. Efficacy analysis required a minimum of 24 (± 6) months’ follow-up. Drug doses were classified as low (< 50 mg/day spironolactone, < 30 mg/day bicalutamide), medium (50–100 mg/day spironolactone, 30–< 50 mg/day bicalutamide), or high (> 100 mg/day spironolactone, ≥ 50 mg/day bicalutamide) on the basis of average daily dose during follow-up.

Ethical considerations were strictly adhered to throughout the study. Consent for the publication of recognizable patient photographs or other identifiable material was obtained by the authors and included at the time of article submission to the journal stating that all patients gave consent with the understanding that this information may be publicly available. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal (DER-AGA 01-2019).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the mean change in Sinclair grade between baseline and 24 months. Secondary outcomes included: the proportion of participants achieving a clinically meaningful improvement (≥ 1 grade reduction in Sinclair score); the likelihood of achieving lower Sinclair grades at 24 months according to treatment group, adjusting for baseline severity; the effect of treatment dose on Sinclair improvement; the proportion of participants who experienced any adverse event; healthcare resource utilization (mean number of dermatology visits and laboratory tests); and treatment effect on seborrhea using a patient-reported 3-point subjective scale (worsening, no change, or improvement).

For all outcomes, data nearest to 24 months were selected, applying a last observation carried forward approach. Sinclair grades and adverse events were recorded by dermatologists at each visit. When documentation was unclear, standardized scalp photographs were reviewed in a blinded manner. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate clinical improvement, we created a binary outcome defined as a reduction of ≥ 1 Sinclair grade from baseline to month 24. Proportions of patients that clinically improved were compared using the chi-squared test as a descriptive, unadjusted comparison. We conducted an ordered logistic regression analysis (proportional odds model) as the primary inferential approach to assess changes in Sinclair grade at 24 months by treatment group, adjusting for baseline severity. For subgroup analysis, we used simple linear regression to compare mean Sinclair grade changes in each subset of participants. Linear regression models were used as complementary analyses to estimate mean changes in Sinclair grade and facilitate clinical interpretation of effect size. Given the retrospective design and variability in follow-up intervals, outcomes were analyzed at a predefined clinically relevant timepoint (24 months), rather than as time-to-event variables.

Given the absence of established clinical criteria guiding treatment allocation between spironolactone and bicalutamide, major confounding by indication is unlikely in this setting. Therefore, as no baseline characteristics were hypothesized to influence treatment allocation, primary analyses were performed without covariate adjustment. However, we performed exploratory multivariable analyses adjusted for baseline characteristics (age, baseline Sinclair grade) as sensitivity analyses. We also performed sensitivity analyses on the basis of the mean daily dose received by participants in each treatment group (high versus low dose categories). Concomitant treatments received during follow-up were systematically recorded, and further subanalyses were conducted, adjusting the primary models for any concomitant treatments showing significant baseline differences between groups.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata v.18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX). Graphical figures were generated with GraphPad Prism v.10.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

Results

A total of 387 women diagnosed with FPHL initiated treatment during the study period: 273 (71%) received spironolactone and 114 (29%) received bicalutamide. Of these, 187 completed the follow-up period and were included in the primary analysis (128 on spironolactone and 59 on bicalutamide). The median follow-up was 26 months (IQR 22–31 months). Given the baseline differences in the use of topical minoxidil, subanalyses were performed including this treatment as an adjustment variable in the models, yielding results similar to those of the primary analysis (mean Sinclair difference 0.15, p = 0.144). Further analysis excluding participants receiving this concomitant treatment showed no change in the estimates (mean Sinclair difference 0.03, p = 0.847).

Figure 1 details the causes of loss to follow-up among participants who did not complete the 24 months. There were no significant differences in early discontinuations due to adverse effects or lack of efficacy. Table 4 presents the adverse events among the participants who completed the follow-up, in both groups. There were no significant differences in the incidence of adverse events among the three dose categories (p = 0.143). Hypertrichosis was the most frequent, taking into consideration that most participants received concomitant oral minoxidil. Participants with spironolactone presented more menstrual alterations, although without statistically significant differences, and those who received bicalutamide had more laboratory alterations and scalp dryness. Regarding this last finding, we analyzed the improvement in seborrhea during follow-up. Among participants with seborrhea at baseline, we observed a higher percentage of improvement in those receiving bicalutamide (85% versus 68%, p = 0.302). Although the overall incidence of adverse events was similar between groups, menstrual irregularities, breast tenderness, dizziness/orthostatic hypotension, and nocturia were numerically more frequent among patients treated with spironolactone. The latter symptoms may be related to its diuretic and mineralocorticoid receptor–antagonist properties, although their low frequency precludes firm conclusions and warrants confirmation in prospective studies (Table 5). Discussion

This retrospective cohort study represents the most extensive direct comparison of spironolactone and bicalutamide in FPHL treatment to date. Both agents stabilized or improved Sinclair grades over 24 months. However, spironolactone, especially at doses >100 mg/day, achieved significantly greater improvement. Patients receiving high-dose spironolactone more often reached at least a one-grade Sinclair reduction compared with those treated with bicalutamide 50 mg/day, the highest dose used in our cohort, suggesting dose-dependent efficacy, and supporting its use as a first-line systemic treatment in FPHL.

Our findings align with two recent meta-analyses confirming spironolactone’s efficacy. In our cohort, 51.4% of patients treated with spironolactone showed clinically meaningful improvement after 24 months, comparable to the 56.6% global improvement reported by Aleissa et al. [8]. Moreover, Wang et al. reported 81% clinical improvement across four studies evaluating oral spironolactone (25–200 mg/day), with most patients receiving 80–110 mg/day [7]. In a recent randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial in a Thai population, it was observed that the group of women who received oral spironolactone 100 mg daily plus topical minoxidil 3% showed a statistically significant improvement in the rate of moderate to marked global photographic improvement compared with the group receiving oral placebo plus topical minoxidil 3% at 24 weeks [17].

Conversely, bicalutamide’s efficacy in our study was lower than previously reported. For instance, Ismail Ferial et al. described a mean Sinclair grade reduction of 0.8 points at 24 months in a cohort where bicalutamide was combined with oral minoxidil (98%) and spironolactone (72%) [9]. Fernández-Nieto et al. reported similar reductions using 41 mg/day bicalutamide, though baseline severity in both studies was greater than in ours [11].

However, a recent study by da Silva et al. reported that bicalutamide 25 mg/day combined with minoxidil 1 mg/day did not improve hair density or the clinical appearance of FPHL compared with minoxidil 1 mg/day as monotherapy at 24 weeks, although it did lead to reductions in hair shedding and facial hypertrichosis [18].

Our results contrast with Jha et al., who reported superior outcomes with bicalutamide 50 mg/day versus spironolactone 100 mg/day over 24 weeks in premenopausal women receiving 2% topical minoxidil [14]. Although the bicalutamide group showed greater reductions in Sinclair and shedding scores, as well as higher photographic improvement, several key differences in study design may explain the divergent findings.

First, our study had a substantially longer follow-up (median 26 months versus 24 weeks), allowing for a more robust evaluation of treatment durability in a chronic, progressive condition such as FPHL. Second, dosing strategies differed notably: Jha et al. used fixed doses of spironolactone (100 mg/day), whereas a significant proportion of our cohort received higher doses (> 100 mg/day), which were associated with greater clinical benefit in subgroup analyses. Third, outcome measures varied. While Jha et al. relied on short-term photographic assessments, we employed longitudinal changes in the Sinclair scale, a validated, ordinal tool that more comprehensively captures clinical progression over time.

FPHL’s chronic nature and delayed response to treatment often limit short-term studies [19]. The largest retrospective study on bicalutamide, involving 138 patients, showed incremental Sinclair score reductions over time: 6.5% at 3 months, 17% at 6 months, 20.2% at 9 months, 24.5% at 12 months, and 28.9% at 24 months [9]. Burns and Sinclair also demonstrated spironolactone’s time-dependent efficacy, with greater benefits at 12 versus 6 months [3, 20]. Furthermore, a recent retrospective study found significant Sinclair score reductions with low-dose spironolactone (≤ 50 mg/day) over 12 months [21].

Differences in patient populations and concomitant treatments likely explain efficacy variations between studies. In Jha et al., all participants used topical minoxidil, while in our cohort, only a subset did, reflecting current trends toward oral minoxidil use in routine practice [2]. Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of combining spironolactone or bicalutamide with other treatments, mainly topical or oral minoxidil [7, 8, 20, 22–25]. Though topical minoxidil use varied between groups in our study, sensitivity analyses adjusting for this confirmed the robustness of our findings. Similarly, oral minoxidil was used in nearly all participants at comparable doses, minimizing potential confounding. Notably, the superiority of spironolactone persisted in models adjusted for topical minoxidil use and in analyses excluding patients receiving it. Lastly, Jha et al. excluded women with any prior hormonal therapy, whereas our study reflects real-world practice, where treatment combination and sequential therapies are common [14, 26]. Together, these differences suggest that the two studies address complementary clinical questions: short-term, controlled evaluation of monotherapy in a narrow population versus longer-term, real-world assessment of antiandrogen efficacy in a broader clinical context.

Interestingly, patients treated with bicalutamide showed greater improvement in seborrhea, although differences were not statistically significant. This aligns with their higher rates of scalp and facial dryness, suggesting a potentially favorable seborrhea response to bicalutamide.

Regarding safety, both studies reported similar adverse event profiles. Hypertrichosis, likely due to oral minoxidil co-treatment, was the most common adverse event in our cohort: 21.8% in the spironolactone group and 20.6% with bicalutamide. These rates are consistent with prior safety data of oral minoxidil. A meta-analysis including 2933 patients estimated a pooled hypertrichosis rate of 35% with oral minoxidil [27]. Moussa et al. reported that bicalutamide improved minoxidil-induced facial hypertrichosis, permitting an increase in minoxidil dose [28]. Nohria et al. found that oral minoxidil combined with spironolactone reduced hypertrichosis rates (17.6%) versus minoxidil monotherapy (40.5%) [29]. These findings support the hypothesis that antiandrogens may improve treatment tolerability by mitigating minoxidil-induced hypertrichosis.

Menstrual irregularities were more common in the spironolactone group, while laboratory abnormalities (mild hepatic enzyme elevation, not requiring discontinuation) and scalp dryness were more often reported with bicalutamide. Jha et al. similarly reported dizziness and menstrual changes with spironolactone, and mild transaminase elevation in patients treated with bicalutamide. No cases of hyperkalemia were reported in the spironolactone arm in either study [14]. Importantly, bicalutamide patients underwent more frequent laboratory monitoring and dermatologic visits, indicating higher healthcare utilization.

This study is limited by its retrospective design, lack of randomization, widespread co-administration of oral minoxidil, and limited generalizability beyond similar clinical settings. Although concomitant therapies and individualized dose adjustments may have introduced residual confounding, these treatment strategies reflect routine clinical practice. Importantly, predefined dose-stratified analyses and sensitivity analyses adjusting for topical minoxidil produced estimates consistent with the primary findings. As Sinclair grades were recorded by treating dermatologists during routine care, some outcome misclassification cannot be excluded. However, this reflects real-world practice. Although the Sinclair scale is an ordinal variable, we reported mean changes to facilitate clinical interpretation. However, to appropriately account for the ordinal nature of the outcome, we prioritized ordered logistic regression models. Importantly, all main findings were consistent across ordinal and linear modeling approaches. Nonetheless, strengths include a relatively large sample size, clinically relevant follow-up duration, and real-world clinical data.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that spironolactone may provide a clinically meaningful advantage over bicalutamide in achieving long-term improvement in FPHL, particularly at doses > 100 mg/day, with similar tolerability but fewer laboratory tests and medical visits. Our data suggest that spironolactone may be a reasonable first-line systemic antiandrogen in FPHL and underscore the importance of adequate dosing and follow-up duration to properly evaluate its efficacy. Bicalutamide remains a viable alternative for patients who are unable to tolerate or do not respond to spironolactone and may provide additional benefits in patients with seborrhea, although it requires closer clinical follow-up and more frequent laboratory monitoring. Future randomized trials are needed to validate these findings and to evaluate the cost-effectiveness and broader dermatologic effects of antiandrogens in FPHL.
